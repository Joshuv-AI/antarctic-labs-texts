Refining caudal catheterization for anorectal surgery: a comparative study of a technique to optimize sensory block and minimize motor impairment
Authors: Haixuan Wu, Zhengying Li, Simin Zhang, Shuailong Yu, Ling Liu, Ning Liufu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe application of caudal epidural catheterization for anorectal surgery has not been systematically investigated.MethodsWe performed a retrospective chart review of adult patients undergoing elective anorectal surgery under caudal epidural anesthesia at our institution. 163 patients who received conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia (20 mL of 5 mg/mL ropivacaine) and 62 patients who received our refined catheterization technique were included. In the refined group, an initial height-based dose of 5 mg/mL ropivacaine (10–16 mL according to patient height) was administered through a Tuohy needle, followed by 2 cm cephalad catheterization; additional 5 mg/mL ropivacaine was then titrated via the catheter until complete sensory blockade of the surgical site was achieved. To compare the two techniques while minimizing selection bias, we performed inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Anesthetic efficacy, sensory block level, lower limb motor block, and procedure-related complications were assessed.ResultsThe refined technique demonstrated comparable anesthetic efficacy to conventional caudal anesthesia (Grade I anesthetic efficacy: 86.8% vs. 85.6%; OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.46–2.66, p = 0.820). The refined technique significantly improved preservation of lower extremity motor function, with a higher rate of no motor block (84.7% vs. 71.7%; OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.01–4.71, p = 0.047) and higher level of Sensory block (median S2 vs. S3; OR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.33–3.77, p = 0.002).ConclusionCompared with conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia, the refined caudal catheterization regimen provides non-inferior anesthetic efficacy, superior preservation of lower extremity motor function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03881-1.

Introduction

Epidural anesthesia is one of the most widely used anesthetic techniques in clinical practice worldwide [1]. Caudal epidural block, as an important type of sacral epidural anesthesia, can provide effective and reliable intraoperative and postoperative analgesia for patients undergoing anorectal surgery, and has now become a commonly used anesthetic method for this type of surgery [2–5].

Compared with spinal anesthesia, which is a commonly used neuraxial technique for anorectal surgery, caudal epidural block offers several potential advantages for anorectal surgery. A recent randomized controlled trial by Chen et al. demonstrated that ultrasound‑guided caudal epidural block significantly improved patient satisfaction compared with spinal anesthesia, primarily by providing longer postoperative analgesia, reducing opioid consumption, and lowering the incidence of phantom limb syndrome, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and intraoperative hypotension [6]. Similarly, Bozkurt et al. reported that in patients undergoing perianal surgery, caudal block preserved lower extremity motor function, whereas spinal anesthesia produced extensive motor block [7]. Furthermore, caudal block avoids dural puncture, thereby eliminating the risk of post-dural puncture headache — a well-recognized complication of spinal anesthesia [8]. Collectively, these findings suggest that caudal epidural block is a favorable alternative to spinal anesthesia for anorectal procedures.

However, in clinical practice, conventional single-injection caudal epidural regimens exhibit limitations. The resulting range of neural blockade is susceptible to multiple factors, including patient height, anatomical heterogeneity, drug dosage, and administration method, making precise regulation difficult [9, 10]. Consequently, this approach sometimes produces unpredictable cephalad spread, leading to excessively extensive blockade that compromises lower extremity motor function and contravening the principles of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) [11]. When analgesic efficacy proves suboptimal, rescue intravenous anesthetic agents become necessary, which not only escalates anesthesia-related risks including respiratory depression but also imposes additional economic burdens on patients [12, 13].

Caudal epidural catheterization enables anesthesiologists to titrate local anesthetic administration according to analgesic efficacy and surgical progression, thereby facilitating modulation of the sensory block level. Nevertheless, the application of this technique for perioperative anesthetic management in anorectal surgery has not been systematically investigated. Given the potential advantages of catheter-based titration, a systematic exploration of caudal catheterization protocols tailored for anorectal surgery holds substantial practical value.

The present study was therefore designed to conduct a clinical exploration of caudal epidural catheterization in anorectal surgical procedures, with the objective of providing evidence-based guidance for clinical implementation.

Method

Study population

Following approval by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shenshan Medical Center, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and waiver of informed consent, this study was conducted as a retrospective chart review. Prior to surgery, all patients received standard preoperative counseling regarding anesthetic options. Spinal anesthesia was discussed as a conventional alternative to caudal epidural anesthesia. The decision to proceed with caudal epidural anesthesia was made jointly by the anesthesiologist and the patient based on clinical considerations and patient preference following informed consent for the chosen anesthesia method.

Refined caudal epidural catheter placement (CECP) group

We initially identified patients who underwent elective anorectal surgery under caudal epidural anesthesia with caudal catheterization between February 2025 and November 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) elective anorectal surgery (hemorrhoidectomy, fistulectomy, or perianal abscess drainage) performed under caudal epidural anesthesia with caudal catheterization; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; and (3) age ≥ 18 years. The exclusion criteria were: (1) ultrasonographic evidence of sacral stenosis or atresia (anteroposterior diameter of the sacral hiatus < 1.6 mm); (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) psychiatric disorders or poor compliance; (4) known allergy to local anesthetics; and (5) contraindications to caudal epidural anesthesia, including coagulopathy, severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, infection or surgical history in the sacrococcygeal region, pre-existing neuralgia, or significant spinal pathology. A total of 62 patients were included in the refined CECP group.

Conventional caudal anesthesia group

To evaluate the potential advantages of the modified technique, we additionally reviewed electronic medical records of patients who underwent elective anorectal surgery under conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia during the same study period (February 2025 to November 2025). The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. A total of 163 patients were included as the conventional control group.

Refinement of the CECP technique

Standard ASA monitoring and intravenous access were established prior to the procedure. Following preparation, patients were positioned in the prone position and received supplemental oxygen at 3 L/min via nasal cannula. All procedures were performed by an experienced anesthesiologist, including caudal puncture, catheterization, and subsequent sensory block level assessment. According to our department’s standard operating procedure (SOP), preoperative ultrasound measurements of sacral anatomical parameters — including the width of the base of the sacrum (BSW), sacral hiatus length in the sagittal plane (SHLS), and anterior-posterior diameter of the sacral hiatus at its apex (SHAP) — were recorded for all patients. Additionally, the SOP mandated real-time documentation of catheter insertion depth, the occurrence of the retraction sign, all incremental local anesthetic doses, and sequential sensory block assessments as part of routine anesthetic record-keeping.

After sonographic identification and evaluation of the sacral hiatus, the linear-array ultrasound transducer (5–12 MHz; Mindray 7, Shenzhen, China) was covered with a sterile plastic sheath. Rigorous aseptic preparation of the puncture site was performed. Following subcutaneous infiltration of 2% lidocaine at the intended puncture site, an 18-gauge Tuohy needle was inserted from the caudal aspect of the transducer. Under continuous real-time ultrasound guidance using an in-plane approach, the Tuohy needle was advanced to penetrate the sacrococcygeal ligament, with loss-of-resistance technique employed to confirm entry into the caudal epidural space. Upon successful cannulation, an epidural catheter was threaded through the needle and advanced to retain appropriate length within the sacral epidural space. Intravascular and subarachnoid intubation was excluded by epidural catheter aspiration, a 2mL test dose of 1% lidocaine was administered. Then the catheter was secured with adhesive tape and covered with a transparent waterproof dressing.

In the initial cohort (n = 9), an epidural catheter was advanced in a cephalad direction, with 5 cm of the catheter length positioned within the caudal epidural space. The initial dose was administered at 0.5 mL/s based on patient height: 10 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine for height < 160 cm, 12 mL for 160–169 cm, 14 mL for 170–179 cm, and 16 mL for height ≥ 180 cm. Ten minutes after the initial injection, surgical site sensation was assessed using a blunt needle, and supplemental doses of 0.5% ropivacaine were titrated as needed until complete sensory blockade of the surgical site was achieved. In this cohort, 5 patients (55.6%) required > 30 mL of local anesthetic to achieve complete surgical analgesia; however, these patients demonstrated excessively high sensory block levels extending to L3 or even T12, accompanied by significant lower extremity motor blockade.

Subsequently, 32 patients underwent catheter placement with the tip positioned 2 cm within the caudal epidural space, employing the same dosing regimen. Under this modified protocol, 23 patients (71.9%) achieved adequate surgical analgesia with only 15–20 mL of local anesthetic without significant lower extremity motor blockade. Nevertheless, 9 patients (28.1%) still exhibited cephalad spread beyond the desired level, with the highest sensory block reaching L1 and associated with notable lower extremity motor blockade.

To minimize cephalad spread of local anesthetic and preferentially establish sacral epidural blockade, we modified the anesthetic protocol. In the subsequent 62 patients, the catheter insertion depth of 2 cm was maintained; however, prior to catheter insertion, the Tuohy needle was rotated to direct the bevel caudally for the initial dose administration and subsequently rotated to direct the bevel cephalad before threading the epidural catheter in a cranial direction. Following administration of the lidocaine test dose, supplemental local anesthetic was titrated through the catheter as required to achieve adequate analgesia at the surgical site. After confirmation of complete sensory blockade of the surgical site following the initial and any supplemental doses, standard skin preparation and draping were performed, and the surgical procedure commenced.

Conventional caudal anesthesia protocol

According to our department’s SOP, preoperative ultrasound measurements of sacral anatomical parameters — including BSW, SHLS, and SHAP — were recorded for all patients using the same ultrasound system and measurement protocol as described for the refined CECP group.

After sonographic identification of the sacral hiatus and aseptic preparation and subcutaneous infiltration of 2% lidocaine, a 18-gauge Tuohy needle was inserted under real-time ultrasound guidance. The needle was advanced to penetrate the sacrococcygeal ligament, and loss-of-resistance technique was used to confirm entry into the caudal epidural space. After negative aspiration for blood or cerebrospinal fluid, 20mL of 5 mg/mL ropivacaine was injected. The needle was then withdrawn, and the puncture site was compressed and covered with sterile dressing. After confirmation of complete sensory blockade of the surgical site, standard skin preparation and draping were performed, and the surgical procedure commenced.

Outcome measures

The outcome measures were defined as follows: (1) Anesthetic efficacy: Caudal anesthesia was classified as successful (Grade I) if complete sensory blockade of the S3–S5 dermatomes and adequate analgesia with relaxation of the anal sphincter were achieved, necessitating no rescue interventions—including supplemental opioids, conversion to general anesthesia, or local infiltration by the surgeon—throughout the surgical procedure, and if the patient remained pain-free prior to discharge from the recovery unit. Grade II was assigned if intraoperative intravenous analgesics or supplementary local infiltration was required to ensure a pain-free surgical experience. Grade III was designated if general anesthesia with intravenous agents (e.g., ciprofol) was necessary despite the aforementioned rescue measures. (2) Sensory block level: The highest dermatome exhibiting loss of pinprick sensation. (3) Lower extremity motor blockade: Assessed using the modified Bromage scale: 0 = able to move hip, knee, ankle, and toes; 1 = unable to move hip, able to move knee, ankle, and toes; 2 = unable to move hip and knee, able to move ankle and toes; 3 = unable to move hip, knee, and ankle, able to move toes; 4 = unable to move hip, knee, ankle, and toes. (4) Procedure-related complications: High resistance during catheter insertion, unintentional dural puncture, intravascular or intrathecal local anesthetic injection, and infectious complications including postoperative spinal infection.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean and standard deviation, median and interquartile range, or number and percentage, as appropriate. To compare the refined CECP technique with conventional caudal anesthesia while minimizing selection bias and preserving the full sample size, we performed inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using propensity scores. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included the following baseline covariates: age, sex, height, weight, ASA status, BSW, SHLS, and SHAP. Stabilized weights were calculated, and a truncation threshold of 5 was applied to avoid extreme weights. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.1 indicating acceptable balance. Weighted outcome analyses were performed using survey-weighted logistic regression for binary outcomes (Grade I anesthetic efficacy, absence of motor block, sensory level ≤ S1) and survey-weighted proportional odds logistic regression for the ordinal sensory block level. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1).

Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included the following baseline covariates: age, sex, height, weight, ASA status, BSW, SHLS and SHAP. After IPTW, all covariates achieved good balance except SHAP, which remained mildly imbalanced (SMD = -0.272). The weighted post-balance results are presented in Table 2.

After IPTW adjustment, the refined CECP technique demonstrated comparable anesthetic efficacy to conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia. The weighted Grade I anesthetic success rate was 86.8% in the refined group versus 85.6% in the conventional group (OR = 1.107, 95% CI: 0.461–2.661, p = 0.820).

The refined CECP technique showed superior preservation of lower extremity motor function. The weighted rate of no motor block (Bromage 0) was significantly higher in the refined CECP group compared to the conventional caudal anesthesia group (84.7% vs. 71.7%; OR = 2.181, 95% CI: 1.011–4.705, p = 0.047). Consistently, the weighted rate of any motor block (Bromage ≥ 1) was significantly lower in the refined CECP group (15.3% vs. 28.3%; OR = 0.459, 95% CI: 0.213–0.989, p = 0.047).

Regarding sensory block characteristics, the refined CECP technique achieved significantly higher sensory block levels. The weighted median sensory block level was S2 (Interquartile Range: S3-S1) in the refined CECP group compared to S3 (Interquartile Range: S3-S1) in the conventional caudal anesthesia group. Proportional odds logistic regression confirmed that the refined CECP technique was associated with significantly higher sensory block levels (OR = 2.240, 95% CI: 1.332–3.767, p = 0.002). The proportion of patients achieving sensory block level ≤ S1 was comparable between the two groups (83.7% vs. 84.9%; OR = 0.915, 95% CI: 0.405–2.069, p = 0.830). No procedure-related complications, including dural puncture, intravascular injection, or spinal infection, were observed in either group. The weighted outcome results are summarized in Table 3.

In this refined CECP technique involving 2-cm insertion depth with cephalad advancement, we consistently observed a distinct clinical phenomenon during drug administration through the epidural catheter, which we designated as the “retraction sign” (see online supplemental video). This sign was characterized by immediate, visible posterolateral bulging and retraction of the peripuncture tissues, including the sacrococcygeal ligament, following drug injection. The occurrence of the retraction sign demonstrated a close association with catheter insertion depth: the phenomenon was reproducibly observed in all patients with 2 cm catheter placement, whereas no tissue retraction was detected in patients with deeper catheter insertion. Consequently, we propose that the retraction sign holds potential value as an auxiliary indicator for assessing catheter insertion depth, as its absence may serve as a warning sign of excessively deep caudal catheter placement.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 225 patients undergoing elective anorectal surgery, we demonstrated that our refined CECP technique offers distinct clinical advantages over conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia. After IPTW to minimize selection bias, the refined CECP technique achieved significantly better preservation of lower extremity motor function and provided more reliable sacral sensory blockade, while maintaining comparable anesthetic efficacy. These findings address the existing evidence gap regarding optimal catheter insertion depth and drug administration strategy for caudal epidural anesthesia in anorectal surgery and provide quantitative support for the clinical utility of this refined technique.

The perianal region is richly innervated by nerve endings predominantly derived from the S2–S5 spinal segments, rendering it exquisitely sensitive to nociceptive stimuli [14–16]. Consequently, achieving reliable sacral nerve blockade is particularly critical for surgical procedures in this anatomical region [16–18]. Caudal epidural anesthesia permits extensive distribution of local anesthetic within the epidural space, potentially encompassing the sacrococcygeal, lumbar, and even thoracic dermatomes [19, 20]. Precise modulation of the sensory blockade enables optimal analgesia for the anus, rectum, and perineum without compromising lower extremity motor function, thereby facilitating accelerated postoperative rehabilitation, shortening hospital stays, and underscoring the clinical value of this anesthetic approach in the perioperative management of anorectal surgery [21].

In contrast, conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia often produces unpredictable cephalad spread of local anesthetic, leading to unintended lumbar or thoracic sensory blockade and consequent lower extremity motor impairment. Caudal epidural catheterization overcomes this limitation by permitting titration of local anesthetic according to analgesic efficacy and surgical progression, thereby facilitating precise modulation of the sensory block level.

Appropriate catheter depth and drug dosage are critical for achieving an optimal sensory block level. Ropivacaine was selected for its well‑described sensory‑motor separation profile. To determine the initial drug dosage, we referred to a study investigating the 90% minimum effective volume of 5 mg/mL ropivacaine for ultrasound‑guided caudal epidural block in anorectal surgery [22]. However, no published evidence exists regarding the optimal caudal catheter insertion depth for this type of surgery; therefore, we initiated a clinical exploration of catheter depth.

We first attempted a conventional epidural catheter insertion depth of 5 cm. In this initial cohort, 55.56% of patients developed an excessively high sensory block level (extending to L3 or even T12), accompanied by significant lower extremity motor impairment. We then reduced the insertion depth to 2 cm. With this modification, 71.86% of patients achieved a sensory block level at or below S1, nevertheless, 28.13% still experienced lower extremity motor block. We hypothesized that the injection modality might contribute to this observation. Previous studies have demonstrated distinct patterns of local anesthetic distribution between injection through an epidural catheter and injection through a Tuohy needle, with one report showing that catheter injection can produce four additional cephalad segments of sensory blockade compared with needle injection at equivalent flow rates [23].

To minimize cephalad spread of local anesthetic, we further refined the technique as follows: the initial local anesthetic dose was administered through the Tuohy needle with the bevel oriented caudally, after which the catheter was advanced cephalad for 2 cm, followed by incremental titration of local anesthetic as needed. The key advantage of this refined technique is that the caudally directed needle injection concentrates local anesthetic in the caudal portion of the sacral canal, thereby ensuring adequate coverage of the perianal surgical area (S2-S5). In addition, catheter placement allows intraoperative titration according to surgical progression and patient response, enabling true “on-demand” dosing.

Under this refined protocol, 62 consecutive patients were treated. Among them, 95.16% exhibited no lower extremity motor block, and 83.87% achieved a satisfactory sensory block level at or below S1. To further quantify the clinical advantage of this technique, we compared it with 163 patients who received conventional single‑injection caudal anesthesia during the same period using IPTW. The results showed that, while maintaining non-inferior anesthetic efficacy, the refined technique significantly reduced the incidence of lower extremity motor block and provided a more reliable sensory block level.

Advanced imaging guidance has become increasingly important for catheter placement and confirmation. According to published reports, ultrasound enables direct visualization of the catheter at individual vertebral levels superior to the sacrum during advancement when the catheter is advanced through the caudal space to reach lumbar or thoracic segments [24, 25]. However, in the sacral canal region, the sacrum constitutes a continuous, completely fused bony structure that precludes ultrasonic penetration and visualization of catheter position, thereby limiting the value of ultrasound guidance in this anatomical region. Fluoroscopy combined with contrast agent may alternatively be employed to confirm appropriate catheter placement; nevertheless, this technique entails risks of radiation exposure and depends upon specialized equipment and radiology support, restricting its applicability in primary healthcare institutions or bedside emergency surgical scenarios, and rendering it unsuitable for routine localization [26, 27]. In contrast, the “retraction sign” identified in the present study offers a simple, noninvasive, and intuitive adjunctive method for rapid bedside assessment of caudal catheter depth. The presence of this sign reliably indicates that the catheter tip is not positioned excessively distant from the sacral hiatus. The underlying mechanism is directly related to tissue mechanical effects following drug injection—when the catheter tip is not advanced excessively into the sacral canal, the local pressure generated by drug injection can be effectively transmitted to superficial peripuncture tissues including the sacrococcygeal ligament, producing observable posterolateral bulging and retraction. This clinical sign requires no complex imaging equipment and can be assessed solely through visual observation during intraoperative drug administration, providing a practical bedside adjunct for precise localization of caudal epidural catheters.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design and the relatively limited sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the exclusive inclusion of adult patients undergoing elective anorectal surgery restricts the applicability of our results to pediatric populations, obese patients, and other special subgroups, such as those with significant spinal abnormalities. Third, the retrospective nature of this investigation precludes direct causal inference regarding the superiority of the refined technique over conventional caudal anesthesia. While our IPTW analysis provided robust comparative data, a prospective randomized controlled trial is warranted to confirm the benefits of our approach. Future studies should address these aspects to further validate the clinical utility of this refined CECP technique.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that our refined CECP regimen achieved significantly better preservation of lower extremity motor function and provided more reliable sacral sensory blockade than conventional single-injection caudal anesthesia. Additionally, the “retraction sign” serves as a simple, non-invasive bedside indicator for appropriate catheter depth, addressing the imaging limitations in the sacral region.
