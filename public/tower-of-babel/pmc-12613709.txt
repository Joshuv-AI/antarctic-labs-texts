RNA sequencing analysis in chicken spleen infected with Newcastle disease virus reveals genotype-specific immune response
Authors: Haile Berihulay, Wei Luo, Chuxiao Lin, Ainong Lao, Endashaw Jebessa, Xian Zou, Jian Ji, Hao Qu, Manshan Cai, Dingming Shu, Chenglong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, the Newcastle disease virus (NDV) is a seriously important pathogen and results in substantial economic losses in the poultry industry. To mitigate this pathogen, the poultry industry has employed intensive breeding programs aimed at selecting genetic Lines that promote resistance and enhance immune response. Resistance to infection occurs at multiple levels and involves genotype-specific polymorphisms in the host. To date, the influence of genetic variations on the immune response within the same chicken Lines at the genotype-specific level is not fully understood. Therefore, it is important to understand the host genetic resistance that plays a role after NDV infection. This study aimed to investigate genotype-specific immune responses in chicken spleens following NDV infection at 7 days post-infection (7dpi), Samples were analyzed for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using high-throughput RNA sequencing approaches.ResultsTwelve cDNA Libraries from LNH chicken spleens were compared with those of NDV-infected birds across the AA and GG genotypes. A total of 9886 genes were compared, of which 1348 were upregulated (833) and downregulated (515). Of these, 552 genes, with 415 upregulated and 137 downregulated genes, were differentially expressed (DE) between the two pairwise comparison groups: Control_AA vs. Immune_AA (AA group) and Control_GG vs. Immune_GG (GG group). Higher number of differentially expressed genes (407 DEGs), with 349 upregulated and 58 downregulated genes, were identified in chickens with the GG genotype, whereas 142 genes, with 63 upregulated and 79 downregulated genes, were observed in chickens with the AA genotype. In this study, a set of seven candidate genes (LECT1, MR1, MYH3, USH1C, NMU, BG8, and CCN3) was identified associated with the immune response in chickens. Further analysis revealed that DEGs in the spleen were enriched in various signaling pathways, such as neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, tight junctions, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways, suggesting their potential role in immune resilience.ConclusionsRNA-seq analysis revealed immune-related genes and pathways involved in the immune response in the chicken spleen. The results indicate that chickens with the GG genotype exhibited higher levels of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), potentially linked to NDV responses. Our findings also offer valuable transcriptomic insights for the comparative analysis of molecular mechanisms underlying immune response in LNH chickens and have practical implications for enhancing chicken breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12078-8.Keywords: Chicken, Genotype, Immune response, Newcastle disease, RNA-seq, Spleen

Background

Globally, chicken is a potential source of lean, nutrient-dense protein and serves as a valuable model for understanding various biological processes, including basic biology, vertebrate evolution, immunity, and host–pathogen interactions [1]. Newcastle disease virus (NDV) is a highly contagious viral infection that affects various bird species, particularly chickens [2]. It is a single-stranded, nonsegmented, negative-sense RNA virus belonging to the family Paramyxoviridae [2, 3]. The virus leads to high mortality and morbidity rates in infected flocks [2]. The outbreak of the disease can lead to substantial economic losses for poultry producers by disrupting production, affecting growth rates, and decreasing egg production [4]. It is well known that poultry production (broilers and layers) yielded higher results in commercial settings than in backyard chicken farming [5]. However, the intensive confinement of chickens in commercial settings poses a high risk of pathogen spread. Like other vertebrates, chickens can resist pathogens by activating their immune systems, which provide defense against viruses, bacteria, and parasites, as well as specialized responses to specific diseases [6].

Disease primarily occurs when the immune system cannot adequately protect the body from injuries inflicted due to insufficient immune activation, dysregulated responses, or pathogen evasion strategies [7]. When the host becomes infected, both innate and adaptive immune responses (antibody production and cytotoxic T-cell responses) are triggered in a cascade, aiming to restrict viral spread, suppress pathogen replication, clear infected cells, and reduce further transmission [8]. In chicken. The spleen serves as a site of specific variation for innate and adaptive immune cell interactions and is one of the central organs of the immune system, which is essential for investigating immune responses [9, 10]. RNA-seq profiling is tissue-specific and breed-dependent responses to NDV infection, providing insights into the complex host–pathogen interactions and potential targets for improving disease resistance in chickens [1, 7]. This technique is effective for identifying genotype-specific gene expression, alternative splicing events, novel transcripts, and exploring immune-related genes in differentially expressed (DE) genes at different stages of infection [11], thereby offering valuable insights into genetic variants that impact gene regulation and contribute to traits [12]. These immune factors reflect immune responsiveness, which could be correlated with susceptibility or resistance to pathogens.

Disease resistance is characterized as a polygenic trait, indicating that multiple genes contribute to their immune response and enhancing the capacity to combat NDV infections [6, 7]. In chicken, genetic variation affects susceptibility to diseases [7], with some breeds or individuals displaying natural resistance due to polymorphisms in immune-related genes. For example, commercial chickens like the Lingnanhuang (LNH), are cross breed lines synthesized from both exotic and native breeds and exhibit desirable traits like disease resistance [13].

Furthermore, genetic variations in gene expression among different chicken lines highlight the importance of genetic factors that determine susceptibility or resistance to NDV [14]. These insights are crucial for selecting chickens with enhanced immune resistance, benefiting both the poultry industry and the birds themselves [15]. In this regard, identifying the specific genetic markers and how gene expression changes can shed light on the molecular mechanisms of immune response to NDV. For example, genetic differences in susceptibility to pathogens were observed between inbred lines like Fayoumi and Leghorn [1]. Moreover, previous association studies on the ROBO2 gene showed that the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) 1418G > A is significantly higher in Huiyang Bearded chicken (HB) × fast-growing Chinese yellow broiler (FCYB) F2 cross chicken populations [16], suggesting that the GG genotype was strongly selected during NDV resistance compared to the AA genotype. To date, despite decades of research and development on the immune responses towards differential expression, there is still a lack of studies that explore the specific chicken genotypes that confer immune response against NDV infections.

In the current experiment, we used RNA-seq to assess genotype-specific immune responses in the spleen of LNH broiler chickens after NDV infection, hypothesizing that the GG genotype provides greater disease resistance than the AA genotype. Therefore, the specific objective of this study is to investigate host immune response to NDV infection and identified potential candidate genes/signaling pathways in LNH Chinese chicken at a genotype-specific level. To our knowledge, this study is the first effort to investigate DEGs and genetic variants within chicken at a comparative genotype-specific level.

Results

Quality control and mapping of RNA-seq data

In present study, genes with low expression value (RPKM < 1) were removed and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified within the LNH chickens based on two factors: treatment (control or immune) and genotype (AA or GG). Twelve cDNA Libraries were prepared and sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. The raw sequences in each RNA-seq Library varied from 22.8—38.9 million reads and the total mapped reads ranging from 37.61% % to 65.44% (Additional file 1: Table S1). The average quality score of the sequence reads across all samples was high, with most samples exhibiting 89.93% at the Q20 level and 83.50% at the Q30 values, and consistent GC content ranging from 42.11% to 45.53%, which is relatively consistent across all samples, suggesting a stable GC composition in the sequenced regions (Additional file 1: Table S1). Following filtration, each Library yielded more than 20 million clean reads. Under immune conditions, chickens with the GG genotype (148,258,288) clean reads compared to chicken with AA genotype (141,433,224) (Additional file 1: Table S1). Similarly, the GG group yielding 166,808,602 clean reads, in contrast the AA- group contains 153, 285, 014 clean reads, under control condition. The proportion of total mapped sequences/reads varied from 54.60%—65.44% of the clean reads. Uniquely mapped reads (URs) constituted 54.07% to 64.85% of clean reads, while multiple mapped reads comprised 0.53%—0.93% of the total (Additional file 1: Table S2). The error rate in the sequencing data was remarkably moderate, ranging between 0.03 and 0.11, exhibiting consistent distribution patterns (Additional file 1: Table S2). When the clean reads were aligned to the chicken genome, 59.7% were successfully mapped to the Gal4 reference genome, 51.06% were mapped to exonic regions, 17.54% were mapped to intergenic regions, and 31.40% were mapped to intronic regions (Additional file 2: Figure S1). These discrepancies imply the presence of genotype- and condition-specific biases in read mapping or sequencing efficiency, which may have implications for subsequent analyses.

Generally, the sequencing data illustrates consistent mapping rates, with minor variations Likely attributable to experimental factors. The data also compared and identified a total of 2,208 genes across all chromosomes and chromosome 1 also harbors 275 genes (250 mb) whose spliced variants differ significantly between the experimental groups (Additional file 3: Figure S2). Alternative splicing revealed the existence of such splicing events in each sample, highlighting the intricacies of gene expression regulation (Additional file 1: Table S3). These genes demonstrate substantial enrichment for molecular functions, including interleukin-1 receptor activity, interleukin-1 binding, and transition metal ion binding. Gene expression quantification employed RPKM, and the Pearson correlation coefficients between matched specimens and their biological replicates exceeded 0.92, indicator of experimental reliability and the rationality of sample selection. The Pearson correlation coefficients of chickens across the different genotypes and experimental conditions were greater than 0.8 (Fig. 1A-D). Furthermore, the antibody titers against NDV measured by ELISA at 7 dpi indicated that chickens carrying with the GG genotype produced significantly higher levels of antibody titer than AA genotype (Fig. 1D). P < 0.05 or less is considered significant. Furthermore, Shapiro–Wilk tests also confirmed normality (R = 0.92, p = 0.0.45), supporting strong statistical validity. At 49 days post-immunization (7 dpi), birds with the GG genotype within the immune group (Immune_GG) exhibited significantly higher antibody titers (P < 0.001) than that of all other groups, with peak values recorded at 1.142 ± 0.340 (range:0.55–1.45) (Additional file 1: Table S4). Conversely, the Immune_AA group demonstrated moderate tiers of 0.747 ± 0.102 (range:0.6–0.85) at p < 0.03. Under control conditions, birds with the GG genotype (Control_GG) exhibited titers of 0.523 ± 0.131, marginally surpassing those of the AA genotype (Control_AA), which recorded titers of 0.368 ± 0.072.

Detection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

In the current study, RPKM-based hierarchical clustering revealed distinct differentiation between immune-challenged and control groups, with H-cluster line charts illustrating the temporal expression trends of subclustered genes (Fig. 2A–C). Analysis of 9,886 genes from the LNH chicken spleen revealed that 1,348 DEGs (accounting for 13.6% of the total) were successfully annotated with known gene symbols, comprising 833 up- and 515 downregulated (p < 0.05, |log₂FC|> 1) at 7dpi (Table 1). Among these, 549 DEGs were identified when comparing the immune conditions between the AA and GG groups (Fig. 3A-C). Of the 549 DEGs, 412 were up- and 137 downregulated. In the AA group, 142 known DEGs were identified, of which 62 were upregulated and 80 were downregulated. Among the upregulated genes, 44 had a fold change between 2 and 4, and 18 had a fold change > 4. Among the downregulated genes, 62 had a fold change between − 2 and − 4, and 18 had a fold change < − 4. In contrast, in the GG group, 350 genes were upregulated and 57 were downregulated. Among the upregulated genes, 224 had a fold change between 2 and 4, and 126 had a fold change > 4, while for the downregulated genes, 48 had a fold change between − 2 and − 4, and 9 had a fold change < − 4.

Differentially expressed associated to immune-related genes

Genetic background plays a crucial role in poultry health and disease management because difference in immune-related genes between the different genotypes can have biologically significant effects on the pathogenic response. Immune-related genes between genotypes under different conditions can have biologically significant effects on pathogen response, it was expected that some immune related genes would be differentially expressed between the genotypes (AA vs. GG) within the LNH chickens. Therefore, this data revealed a total of 195 immune related genes over the course of infection, with 117 genes in chicken with immune GG and 78 genes with immune AA at P < 0.05, and fold change ≥ 1. After comparison, the analysis discovered for the top 20 immune-related DEGs and found that 9 immune related genes were found. To understand the difference in the host gene expression response across the genotypes under different conditions (immune and control birds), two contrasts were made comparing Con_AA vs. Imm_AA (AA) and Con_GG vs. Imm_GG (GG) birds at 7 dpi. After comparison, including with the novel gene ID, the AA group revealed 12 genes with 6 up and 6 down-regulated genes (Additional file 1: Table S5), while the GG group comprises 9 most significant up- and down-regulated DEGs with 4 up- and 5 down regulated genes (Additional file 1: Table S6). Finally, we exclude the unannotated (novel ID) and include only genes with annotated (Ensemble ID) were retained for further investigation. Therefore, differential expression analysis identified four upregulated and three downregulated immune related genes that were consistently differentially expressed and uniquely expressed in GG genotypes (Table 2).

RNA-seq data validation via qRT-PCR

Following the RNA-Seq results of DE uniquely expressed in LNH chickens with the GG genotype, we selected seven genes (LECT1, MR1, MYH3, USH1C, NMU, BG8, and CCN3) using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The log2 fold change (log2FC) values obtained from DESeq2 and qRT-PCR showed a significant correlation (R = 0.82, p = 0.003; Fig. 7), further supporting the reliability of RNA-seq data. Although the fold changes in the differential expression of the selected DEGs determined by RNA sequencing and qRT-PCR were not always the same, the directions of the changes were always the same. This high concordance underscores the strength of the transcriptomic findings and reinforces the credibility of the identified DEGs.

Discussion

Recent advances in genetics, genomics, and RNA sequencing have facilitated a more detailed study of how a specific polymorphism shapes immunogenetic variation and the diversity of immune responses across or within chicken lines [17]. RNA-seq is a technique that uses deep sequencing technology to profile the transcriptome and analyze changes in gene expression and signaling pathways [14]. Understanding the molecular basis of disease resistance or susceptibility has become a valuable current research direction and is important for the control of avian pathogens [10]. Comparative transcriptomic analysis of resistant and susceptible chicken phenotypes following NDV infection can identify key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with avian resistance [9]. Although genetic associations, antibody titers, and immune responses have been used to evaluate NDV resistance [13, 16], reliable phenotyping remains a challenge. In this study, we employed RNA-seq profiling to investigate genotype-specific immune responses in LNH chicken spleens following NDV infection at 7dpi, aiming to characterize host resistance mechanisms, identify critical DEGs and pathways, and provide insights for breeding disease-resistant chickens. This result will be used as a reference for understanding the molecular basis of the chicken immune response and identifying various genes and pathways involved in disease resistance.

Typical differential expression analysis of transcriptome data produces a list of hundreds of DEGs, requiring further analysis to construct a high-level overview of changes between the different compared groups [9]. In this study a total of 1348 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and processed. More importantly, following NDV infection, the spleen of LNH broilers in the GG group exhibited 393 differentially expressed unigenes (DEGs), whereas the AA group yielded about 128, indicating significantly more DEGs in the GG group than in the AA group. expressions of these genes also appeared to be positively related to NDV antibody titters in the spleen, with the GG genotype being significantly higher than the AA genotype. These results were consistent with NDV expression in the spleen. Prior studies have reported significant differences in antibody titters between Fayoumi and Leghorn chickens at 2 and 6 dpi following NDV infection under non-heat stress conditions, suggesting genetic variations in their early humoral immune responses [1].

Similarly, recent studies have identified potential genetic markers linked to immune responses in chickens, offering promising avenues for enhancing disease resistance and other genetic traits in breeding programs. For instance, Wang et al. [16] employed an association-based approach and found that allele G (1418G > A) and allele T (2462T > C) were strongly linked disequilibrium between these alleles, suggesting their potential as genetic markers for improved immune responses to NDV in different Chinese indigenous breeds. Pathway analysis of the DEGs identified between the treated and untreated birds was performed to determine the enriched signaling pathways. The LNH chickens with the GG genotype had two significant KEGG pathways: tight junctions and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways (P < 0.05), suggesting their potential role in immune response [18]. This result indicated that the aforementioned two pathways related to tight junctions and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway should deserve more attention in further research. Previous research supports our findings; the pathway involved in regulating aggressive pecking behavior by inducing neurodegeneration immune cell functions in ducks [19]. Efforts targeting this pathway have potentially positive implications in poultry farming for its welfare and productivity, as they reduce health concerns associated with stress and promote immune response to viruses like avian leukosis virus [20]. Tight junctions play a vital role in maintaining the integrity of epithelial barriers in the gut, respiratory tract, and other epithelial membranes and serve as the main entry point for NDV infection [21]. In chickens, tight junctions can be used as markers of gut health and integrity [22]. Following NDV infection, host cells alter many metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, amino acid synthesis, and niclosamide synthesis [23]. Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are key metabolic pathways that play pivotal roles in enhancing the energy metabolic availability of immune cells and supporting stress responses in chickens [24, 25]. Identifying the signaling pathways and genes involved in chicken resistance to NDV infection may aid in controlling NDV in chickens.

Several immune-related genes (LECT1, MR1, MYH1B, USH1C, NMU, BG8, and CCN3) in chickens with the GG genotype at 7 dpi, highlighting their significance in antiviral immunity and inflammatory regulation [26–30]. Therefore, the upregulation of these genes is an expected observation following antibody challenge, further validating the spleen as an appropriate tissue for studying gene expression responses and genetic resistance to NDV infection. Based on differential expressions, leukocyte cell-derived chemotaxin is of gene interest and plays a pivotal role in mediating interactions within the subset of the gene network and is involved in various immune functions, including inflammation, immune cell activation, and immune surveillance [26]. It may be an activator of the NF-κB pathway that induces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines to assist the immune system in combating NDV. Furthermore, LECT1 can promote the regulation of the integrity of tight junctions through its interactions with a panel of cell surface glycoproteins, which stabilize the structural integrity of epithelial barriers, including those in the gut or respiratory tract, which are important routes of entry for the virus during infection. These results suggest that LECT1 may facilitate the initial recognition of NDV by the innate immune system in epithelial cells and trigger the activation of immune effector functions in the spleen for subsequent viral clearance. LECT plays versatile roles in immune diseases and attenuates tumorigenesis by modulating inflammatory responses in various tissues, including several crucial tissues like kidney, liver, and lung [27]. It also plays an important role in inflammatory regulation and immune regulation. Inflammation and immune regulation are closely related to a variety of diseases, such as bacterial infection, liver cirrhosis [27].

Major histocompatibility (MR1) was among genes significantly upregulated in chickens with the GG genotype. The genetic linkage of MHC class I genes, whose products deliver cytosolic peptides to the cell surface, with TAP, tapasin, and proteasome genes, which encode molecules that generate peptides in the cytosol and transport them into the endoplasmic reticulum, suggests that the entire MHC has been selected during evolution for antigen processing and presentation [28]. When cells are treated with the interferons IFN-α, -β, or -γ, there is a marked increase in the transcription of MHC class I α-chain and β2-microglobulin genes, as well as the proteasome, tapasin, and TAP genes. Interferons are produced early in viral infections as part of the innate immune response; therefore, this effect increases the ability of cells to process viral proteins and present the resulting peptides at the cell surface. This helps activate the appropriate T cells and initiate the adaptive immune response against the virus. The coordinated regulation of the genes encoding these components may be facilitated by the linkage of many of them to the MHC. Previous report indicated that, in chicken, the MHC expression and appearance of peripheral T cells, and a is central role for the adaptive immune responses [28] and has wider role within immunity and disease resistance [29].

MYH3, the other upregulated gene, encodes a skeletal muscle-specific myosin heavy chain isoform, which is biologically important for muscle contraction and development. Skeletal muscle comprises various tissues, including muscle fibers, connective tissue, and adipose tissue [30]. It is a critical component of muscle physiology; its expression is not exclusive to disease or inflammatory states but is also part of normal developmental processes [30]. In chickens, MYH1B (alongside other myosin isoforms such as MYH1D) are dynamically expressed during embryonic muscle development, particularly in fast-twitch fibers [30, 31]. Previous investigations in chickens (e.g., Tibetan vs. broiler breeds) showed that the differential expression of myosin genes (including MYH3) was tied to natural variation in muscle growth rates, not necessarily inflammation [30]. Previous reports have also indicated that three MyHCs are present at the protein level in developing rat and mouse skeletal muscles: MyHC-emb (MYH3), MyHC-neo (MYH8), and MyHC-slow [32, 33].

Immune cell functions play a critical role in maintaining bursal follicle integrity during thermal stress, while also mitigating inflammation [34]. Notably, in this study, the USH1C gene emerged as one of the significantly differentially expressed (DE) genes, highlighting its potential association with stress response pathways. Monson et al. (2018) identified USH1C as a key component of the Usher type 1 (USH1) protein network in Fayoumi chickens and observed its marked upregulation in the bursa under stress conditions [34]. This tissue-specific response, coupled with stable basal expression, suggests a specialized role for USH1C in maintaining cellular homeostasis during stress, possibly through its scaffold function in protein interactions. Furthermore, studies have also demonstrated that USH1C plays a crucial role in intestinal border development in chickens exhibiting high feed efficiency chickens [34].

The molecular mechanism of the NMU (neuromedin U) gene in inflammation, stress responses, and immune cell function involves a complex interplay between neuropeptide signaling, immune cell activation, and tissue-specific adaptations [35]. The gene interacts with neuroactive ligands and receptors, thereby modulating the activation of immune cells and stress response [36]. The NMU interacts with G protein-coupled receptors, NMU-R1 and NMU-R2 [36], which play a crucial role in regulating energy metabolism, appetite, and other physiological processes. This gene encodes a multifunctional peptide that integrates various physiological processes, helping to maintain homeostasis and adapt to changing internal and external environments in chickens [36]. In this regard, the expression of NMU in the spleen of NDV-infected chickens may play a role in modulating inflammatory responses during infection, enhancing immune function and response. Moreover, BG8 is a member of the butyrophilin (BTN) gene family and plays a key role in regulating the immune response in chickens. BG antigens found in the B12 haplotype are integral components of the immune response and have significant implications for immune regulation, disease resistance, and evolutionary biology [37]. The gene is also located within the family of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region, which contains numerous genes related to the immune system and disease resistance [29]. Specific allelic variations in BG genes, particularly the dominant BG8 allele in the B12 haplotype and the BG13-like allele in the B2 haplotype, are associated with differential resistance or susceptibility to certain viral infections in chickens.

The CCN3 gene is one of the top driver transcripts associated with immune traits in chickens. The CCN3 gene, also known as the nephroblastoma overexpressed gene previously identified in day-old chicks, is a member of the CCN family of matricellular proteins [38] and a critical regulator of a variety of diseases, including immune-related diseases. Research has shown that CCN3 inhibits extracellular matrix accumulation and fibrosis, potentially by modulating the TGF-β signaling pathway [39]. The potential involvement of CCN3 in stress-induced immunosuppression, as evidenced by the regulation of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in response to corticosterone administration, suggests a potential link to chicken immunity [40, 41]. Generally, the expression levels of LECT1, MR1, MYH3, USH1C, NMU, BG8, and CCN3 were significantly different after NDV infection, which was proven to be induced throughout the infection process by qRT-PCR and RNA seq results.

Conclusion

Newcastle disease virus is a significant threat to global poultry health. Comparative RNA-seq analysis of the LNH chicken lines revealed genotype-dependent immune responses to NDV, with the GG genotype exhibiting significantly different gene expression and antibody production compared to the AA genotype. While our findings suggest potential genetic markers for NDV resistance, the limitations of this study, including a small sample size, single time-point analysis, and a focus on spleen tissue, limit the interpretation of the results. Further validation through larger sample sizes, multi-tissue studies, and functional assays is required to confirm their roles in disease resistance and breeding applications.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Animal Care Committee at the Institute of Animal Science, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Guangzhou, People's Republic of China, under approval number GAAS-IAS-2009–73. All animals in the study were euthanized humanely, as needed, to minimize their suffering.

Animals and experimental design

Twelve 42-day-old broiler chickens were obtained from the Institute of Animal Science, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Guangzhou, China). The chickens were randomly assigned to two genotypes (AA and GG) and two treatment groups (control and immune challenge), with three animals per genotype × treatment combination (n = 3 biological replicates). The immune-challenged group was inoculated with 200 µL of the LaSota lentogenic strain of Newcastle Disease Virus (NDV) (10⁷ EID₅₀ per bird) via nasal and ocular routes (50 µL per nostril and per eye). The control group received an equivalent volume (200 µL) of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using the same method of administration. At 7 days post-inoculation (7dpi), all chickens were euthanized via intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital. Spleen tissues were harvested, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequence

Total RNA was extracted from individual chicken spleen tissue samples using TRIzol Reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and quantity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 8000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), with RNA integrity assessed using the RNA Integrity Number (RIN ≥ 7). To conduct RNA-seq, 3 µg of RNA per sample was used, with the remaining RNA stored at −80°C for future validation assays. The assembly of the cDNA Library employed the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit from Illumina, following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. Magnetic beads Linked with poly-T oligo were used for mRNA purification from total RNA. After screening 300-bp cDNA fragments, the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA) was employed for library fragment purification. Fragments of DNA, possessing adaptors at both extremities, were selectively enriched through PCR amplification (15 cycles) utilizing the Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail. In the final step, PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system, and the Bioanalyzer 2100 system was employed to appraise the quality of the libraries.

Sequence reads, filtering, and mapping

After quality assessment of raw reads using FastQC (version 0.10.1, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), version 0.11. Raw data was evaluated for Q20 and Q30 content. The purified high-quality reads were then aligned to the galGal4 reference genome and Ensemble annotation using default settings. This alignment was performed using TopHat2 [42], utilizing its default settings, followed by the application of HISAT2 v2.1.085 for read counting [43].

Identification of differential expressed

Gene abundance was analyzed using Feature Counts v1.5.0-p3, and expression levels were normalized by reads per kilobase per million (RPKM), comparing the immune and control groups. The fold-change in gene expression was calculated using formula log2(RPKM-control/RPKM-treatment). The method of Benjamini–Hochberg was employed to manage the false discovery rate (FDR) and calculate the adjusted P-value (padj). The level of gene expression between groups was analyzed using DESeq2 (1.16.1), with thresholds of P-values < 0.05 and |log2(fold change) |≥ 1 [44]. Volcano plots illustrating significantly regulated genes were created using the R package "ggplot2" (v3.5.0), as previously described by Wang et al. [45]. Heat maps for all DEGs were generated using the gplots R library [46]. Additionally, a Venn diagram was used to illustrate the number of DEGs between the two sample groups and to identify their common elements using the Venny plot 2.1 tool (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/).

GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs

Enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology (GO) was employed to annotate and analyze the DEGs [47]. GO terms categories: molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP) were enriched at (http://www.geneontology.org/). The benchmark for GO annotation was set using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.05. The evaluation of pathway enrichment involved the use of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [48], which is a comprehensive database resource designed for the analysis of DEGs. Results showing FDR < 0.05 were considered significant.

Validation of RNA-seq through qRT-PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed to assess the differential expression of target genes. A subset of seven candidate genes was selected from the statistical analysis of RNA-seq data, underwent qRT-PCR analysis. GAPDH was utilized as the reference gene to normalizing gene expression. The 2−ΔΔCT method was employed to analyzing relative gene expression level. The results are displayed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). The primers used in this study are listed in (Table 3). Quantification was carried out using the TransScript® II Green Two-Step qRT-PCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, China). The qRT-PCR reactions were conducted under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. All procedures were conducted in triplicate with distinct samples. Fold changes were log transformed (log2FC) and used to determine pairwise correlations and the R2 (linear fit) between RNA-Seq of top 7 up and down regulated genes and qRT-PCR using SRplot [49].

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance of the difference was analyzed by one way ANOVA and student t-test to compare the differences between mean values of different groups. The value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean and standard deviation (Mean ± SD).
