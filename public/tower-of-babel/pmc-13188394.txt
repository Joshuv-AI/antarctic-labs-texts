Can music activity training improve self-control among preschool children? Evidence from an 8-week intervention experiment
Authors: Chuanhui Zhong, Weigang Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-control is crucial for healthy individual development and environmental adaptation, with the preschool period being a critical stage for its emergence. This study aimed to examine the effects of music activity interventions on self-control among 4- to 5-year-old preschool children.MethodsIn a 3×2 mixed experimental design, 67 children were randomly assigned to one of three groups: a percussion intervention group (n = 20), an integrated music intervention group (n = 20), or a control group (n = 27). The intervention groups underwent an 8-week music teaching intervention with two sessions per week. The Children’s Self-Control Teacher Rating Questionnaire was used to assess the children’s performance across four dimensions—consciousness, persistence, continence, and delay of gratification—before and after the intervention.ResultsAnalysis of covariance revealed that after controlling for baseline differences, both intervention groups scored significantly higher than the control group did in terms of total self-control, persistence, continence, and delay of gratification, but no significant difference was found in the dimension of consciousness. No statistically significant differences were detected between the two music interventions; however, owing to the limited statistical power for this comparison, these null findings should be considered inconclusive rather than evidence of equivalence.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that both percussion and integrated music activities positively affect preschool children’s self-control. These findings provide empirical support for the role of structured music activities in fostering self-control in young children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04534-1.Keywords: Music activity training, Self-control, Preschool children

Introduction

Humans live in complex and multifaceted environments and receive a vast amount of information daily. To achieve goal-directed behavior, individuals must effectively inhibit information that conflicts with their goals, resist the temptation of irrelevant stimuli, and regulate inappropriate behavioral patterns. This capacity to override interfering influences, alter or inhibit dominant response tendencies, and adjust thoughts, emotions, and actions is referred to as self-control [1]. As a core function of the self, self-control is crucial for maintaining good health, achieving accomplishments and well-being, and enhancing adaptability to environmental demands [2, 3]. It serves as a strong predictor of health behaviors [4], and self-control ability among children and adolescents is associated with better adaptability, social competence, prosociality, and success in school settings [5]. A meta-analysis examining the significance of self-control in childhood for later life outcomes revealed that self-control early in life predicts subsequent achievement, interpersonal behavior, mental well-being, and physical health [6]. Researchers suggest that early interventions aimed at promoting self-control in children may yield long-term individual and social benefits [7].

Within the theoretical framework of self-control, researchers distinguish between the ‘cool’ and ‘hot’ executive function systems [8]. The ‌“cool system”‌ is primarily cognitive, supporting ‌“calm, strategic, and rule-driven information processing”‌. It involves tasks requiring inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility within “‌relatively abstract, low-affect contexts‌”. Conversely, the ‌“hot system”‌ is ‌“emotionally driven”‌, activated in situations demanding the regulation of affective, motivational, and desire-based responses—‌particularly when rewards or socioemotional cues are salient‌. This theoretical distinction advances our understanding of ‌the functional architecture and developmental trajectories‌ of self-control.

The preschool period is a critical stage for self-control

Self-control exhibits distinct developmental characteristics throughout different stages of life. Pener-Tessler et al. [9] investigated changes in self-control from early childhood to early adolescence and reported that middle childhood represents a potential transitional period with significant shifts in self-control. Emerging genetic factors around the age of 6.5 may partially account for these changes. Some researchers argue that the stability observed in self-control largely emerges as early as age 7 [10]. Vazsonyi and Jiskrova [11] proposed that self-control increases significantly from preschool years to mid-adolescence but tends to stabilize between the ages of 8.5 and 10.5. A multigroup longitudinal study examining the development of self-control between ages 13 and 19 confirmed that self-control increases throughout adolescence, reflecting a maturation process [12]. These findings suggest that significant developmental changes in self-control occur before age 6 and during mid-to-late adolescence. A long-term study of preschool children in China revealed that the critical period for the overall development of self-control occurs between 3.5 and 4.5 years of age [13]. The development of executive functions, which form the cognitive foundation of self-control, includes core components such as inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [14]. Zelazo et al. reported that ages 3–5 represent the stage with the most rapid development of executive functions in young children, particularly in terms of cognitive flexibility and inhibitory control [15]. The preschool period is a stage of rapid development in terms of inhibitory control [16]. This implies that early interventions aimed at enhancing self-control can be effectively implemented before the age of 5.

Intervention approaches for enhancing children’s self-control

Researchers have developed various interventions to enhance self-control in children. Studies have shown that mindful yoga can significantly improve self-control among preschool children, with notable effects, particularly on attention, delay of gratification, and inhibitory control [17]. Yoga interventions have also been shown to enhance behavioral and emotional self-control among economically disadvantaged young children [18]. Moderate levels of aerobic exercise can strengthen inhibitory control and support cross-domain transfer effects, such as by improving dietary self-control [19]. Martial arts training [20] and creative dance and movement programs [21] have also demonstrated positive effects. Additionally, specialized intervention programs such as the Stop Now And Plan (SNAP) have been developed to target self-control in children, with the results confirming their effectiveness in enhancing self-control and reducing problem behaviors [22]. However, Miles et al. tested the effects of a six-week practice regimen on a range of self-control measures—including laboratory tasks and real-world assessments—and reported that repeated practice in self-control tasks did not lead to broad, generalized improvements in self-control [23]. A meta-analysis indicated insufficient evidence to support that repeated inhibition of dominant responses is a key mechanism for training effects [24]. These findings suggest that while self-control in children can be improved through early intervention, its effectiveness depends on factors such as the type of intervention strategy and specific implementation procedures. Researchers argue that although executive functions can be enhanced through training, the effects are often task specific (e.g., computerized working memory training improves working memory but does not transfer to inhibitory control). Aerobic exercise alone shows limited benefits for executive functions unless combined with cognitive challenges, as seen in practices such as martial arts or yoga. Integrated approaches that combine cognitive, emotional, and social components tend to be more effective than single-domain training is [25]. Activities designed on the basis of children’s intrinsic interests and motivations are more likely to sustain their engagement, thereby enhancing intervention adherence. Therefore, researchers suggest that interventions should leverage children’s intrinsic motivation to maximize their effectiveness [26].

The unique advantages of music training in enhancing self-control

The engaging and structured nature of music training offers unique advantages in interventions aimed at promoting self-control in children. Compared with other training methods, such as physical exercise or meditation, music training tends to be more enjoyable and motivating, thereby stimulating children’s intrinsic interest and more effectively supporting the development of executive functions [27]. Research findings indicate that early music training emphasizing bilateral coordination through gross motor activities can enhance inhibitory control performance in complex tasks [28]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that instrumental music lessons can positively impact children’s executive functions [29]. Consistent with this, cross-sectional research has revealed associations between sustained instrumental training and enhanced cognitive performance in domains such as working memory and inhibitory control, suggesting a possible cognitive advantage linked to long-term music engagement [30]. From a cognitive processing perspective, musical activities require children to integrate auditory, visual, and motor information simultaneously, strengthening the attentional allocation and information integration abilities essential for executive function [31]. With respect to physiological mechanisms, some researchers suggest that music-based training may accelerate the development of inhibitory control and related neural networks during early childhood [32]. Although most studies confirm the beneficial effects of musical activities on the development of self-control in children, a small number of studies suggest that isolated instrumental training, such as orchestral music instruction, has no significant effect on executive functions in young children [33].

Music training is not a homogeneous entity; various forms exist that aim to enhance executive functions in preschool children. A recent systematic review categorized music training into two broad types: comprehensive music training, which typically integrates multiple musical elements and activities into a complete curriculum, and training, which focuses on specific musical components, such as rhythm, pitch, instrumental practice, musical games, music creation, integrative approaches, or specific pedagogical methods, such as Orff-Schulwerk [31]. Different forms of music training may influence the development of self-control through distinct cognitive processes. Percussion training, such as ensembles playing with drums or maracas, centers on explicit, rule-based rhythmic execution and precise sensorimotor control. It requires children to inhibit or initiate movements at exact time points while adhering strictly to collective rhythmic rules. This type of training is believed to primarily challenge and strengthen “cool” executive function, such as inhibitory control and working memory [28, 34]. In contrast, rhythm-based integrated music activities, such as body movement, singing, and contextual performance in response to music, place greater emphasis on holistic self-control within dynamic, emotionally engaging, and socially interactive contexts. These activities involve not only rhythmic synchronization but also strong emotional expression, social synchrony (e.g., coordinating movements with peers), and motivational systems. Thus, theoretically, these two types of activities may differentially affect distinct aspects of self-control. By comparing these two models, we can not only examine the overall effect of music training but also preliminarily explore the associations between specific components of musical activities and particular dimensions of self-control.

Current study

In summary, the existing research has established two key points of consensus: First, self-control is highly malleable during the preschool period, making early intervention critically important; second, structured and engaging music activities are associated with the promotion of self-control development in young children. However, direct empirical comparisons concerning how different forms of music training may differentially influence specific dimensions of self-control among preschoolers are lacking. Such a comparison is highly important for the refined and targeted design of music intervention programs.

To investigate which type of music intervention is more conducive to enhancing self-control among children, this study employed two distinct intervention models: percussion training and rhythm-based integrated music training. Percussion training focuses on precise rhythmic execution and creative expression through instrument combinations, requiring children to control the intensity, speed, and rhythm of hand movements. Its core objectives include developing a sense of rhythm, motor control, and collective coordination, which are often implemented in music activities as standalone modules or integrated with other activities. Rhythm-based activities, on the other hand, center on rhythmic patterns formed through variations in pitch, duration, and dynamics, often involving repetitive or evolving sequences that support emotional expression. The key distinction lies in the emphasis of percussion training on accuracy and instrumental skill, whereas rhythm-based training prioritizes rhythmic repetition, variation, and affective engagement. Both approaches cultivate rhythmic awareness, but percussion training further enhances hand‒eye coordination and creativity, whereas rhythm-based activities emphasize whole-body movement and emotional experience.

The hot/cool theoretical framework of self-control corresponds to the two types of music interventions examined in this study. Percussion training, which emphasizes precise rhythmic execution, rule adherence, and motor control in structured contexts, primarily involves the cool executive function system. In contrast, rhythm-based integrated music activities, which incorporate emotional expression, social synchrony, and whole-body movement within dynamic contexts, may more strongly activate the hot executive function system. By comparing these two intervention approaches, this study aims to preliminarily explore whether different components of musical activities are differentially associated with these two systems of self-control.

Researchers suggest that music training initiated during early childhood (particularly between ages 3 and 7) yields more pronounced benefits, especially when activities are enjoyable and generate positive emotional experiences [35]. Such intrinsic motivation—such as the pleasure derived from engagement—enhances learning efficacy potentially through dopaminergic pathways. On the basis of these findings, this study selected children aged 4–5 years as participants and implemented an 8-week music intervention to examine the effects of different programs on self-control. This study proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Compared with children in the regular kindergarten routine (control group), children in both the percussion training group and the rhythm-based integrated music activity group may demonstrate higher levels of self-control after the 8-week intervention.

Hypothesis 1: Compared with children in the regular kindergarten routine (control group), children in both the percussion training group and the rhythm-based integrated music activity group may demonstrate higher levels of self-control after the 8-week intervention.

Hypothesis 2: The two music interventions may have differential effects on specific dimensions of self-control. Specifically, percussion training, with its emphasis on rhythmic precision and motor control, may have relatively stronger effects on persistence and continence, whereas rhythm-based integrated music activities, with their emphasis on emotional expression and social interaction, may have relatively stronger effects on delay of gratification.

Hypothesis 2: The two music interventions may have differential effects on specific dimensions of self-control. Specifically, percussion training, with its emphasis on rhythmic precision and motor control, may have relatively stronger effects on persistence and continence, whereas rhythm-based integrated music activities, with their emphasis on emotional expression and social interaction, may have relatively stronger effects on delay of gratification.

This study aims to contribute in the following ways. At the theoretical level, comparing the differential effects of the two intervention models will be valuable. It may help to preliminarily reveal how two potential mechanism paths are respectively related to the “cool” (cognitive) and “hot” (emotional) systems of self-control. The first path is “rhythmic precision and motor control”, central to percussion training; the second is “rhythmic integration and emotional modulation”, central to integrated music activities. This could enrich theoretical models of how music training influences executive function. At the practical level, the findings provide kindergarten teachers and educational administrators with an empirically grounded basis for curriculum selection, clarifying which form of music activity may be more targeted in enhancing specific self-control abilities in young children, thus facilitating the optimization and refinement of preschool intervention programs.

Methods

Study design, aim, and setting

This study employed a 3 × 2 mixed experimental design, incorporating one between-subjects factor (group: percussion, integrated music, and control) and one within-subjects factor (time: pretest and posttest). The primary aim was to examine whether compared with regular kindergarten routines, two types of structured music activities—percussion training and rhythm-based integrated music activities—have a positive effect on preschool children’s self-control. The secondary aim was to explore, on the basis of hot/cool framework of executive function, whether these two intervention approaches yield differential effects on specific dimensions of self-control (i.e., consciousness, persistence, continence, and delay of gratification). The study was conducted from March to June 2023 in a public kindergarten in Yongchuan District, Chongqing. All interventions and assessments took place in regular school settings to maximize ecological validity.

Participants

Participants were recruited from two middle classes (for children aged 4–5 years) at the participating kindergarten. Following communication with the kindergarten administration, the research team introduced the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and data usage to all parents during a parent–teacher meeting. It was clearly communicated during the meeting that all the participating children, regardless of group assignment, would undergo two assessments (pretest and posttest) and that only some children would receive the music activity intervention. Ultimately, a total of 67 children and their parents consented to participate in this study. All parents of these 67 children (33 boys, 34 girls) provided written informed consent for their children’s participation in the study, including both the assessments and, if assigned, participation in the music activities. Additionally, the researchers explained the study procedures to the children using age-appropriate language and obtained their oral assent prior to each music session; no child refused to participate during the study period.

The required sample size was calculated via G*Power 3.1. We used repeated-measures ANOVA and within-between interactions to calculate the sample size. With α set at 0.05, statistical power at 0.80, and a medium effect size (f = 0.25) [36], the minimum sample size required was 42 children, with 14 per group. Although a larger sample could increase the robustness of the findings, increasing the class size could also raise practical challenges in classroom management and reduce individual engagement in music activities. To ensure instructional quality and adequate experiential participation, each intervention group was limited to approximately 20 children. Finally, these 67 children were assigned to one of three groups according to the following randomization procedure: the percussion intervention group (n = 20), the integrated music intervention group (n = 20), or the control group (n = 27).

The participant allocation procedure involved renumbering the 67 children’s student IDs from 1 to 67. From these numbers, 20 were first randomly selected for the percussion intervention group, followed by another random selection of 20 for the integrated music intervention group. The remaining 27 children were assigned to the control group. The details are presented in Table 1.

Measurement of self-control

Children’s self-control was assessed via the Children’s Self-Control Teacher Rating Questionnaire developed by Yang et al. [37]. The questionnaire consists of 22 items (see Supplementary Material Table S3 for details) measuring four dimensions of self-control: consciousness, the child’s ability to follow rules without direct supervision. An example item is “He/She follows game rules even when the teacher is not around.” Persistence: The child’s capacity to persevere through challenges to achieve a goal. An example item is “When faced with a somewhat difficult task, he/she persists within his/her ability until it is completed.” Continence is the ability to inhibit impulses and resist immediate temptation. An example item is “He/She completes tasks assigned by the teacher even if he/she does not like them.” Delay of gratification: The orientation toward forgoing immediate rewards for more valuable long-term outcomes, along with the self-control exhibited while waiting. Example item: “If told that receiving one small red flower can be exchanged for a small toy now, but waiting for two flowers will yield a better toy, he/she chooses to wait.”

This instrument has been validated repeatedly for more than a decade and has proven suitable for the multidimensional assessment of self-control among Chinese preschool children [38, 39]. Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Never” (1) to “Always” (5), with higher scores indicating higher levels of self-control. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s α for the questionnaire was 0.963 at the pretest, with subdimension α values of 0.911 (consciousness), 0.842 (persistence), 0.881 (continence), and 0.811 (delay of gratification). At posttest, the overall α was 0.921, with subscale α values of 0.843, 0.732, 0.741, and 0.750, respectively.

Assessments were conducted at two time points for all three groups of children: one week prior to the intervention (pretest) and one week after the intervention concluded (posttest). To ensure temporal consistency across groups, all pretest assessments were completed within the same three-day window, and all posttest assessments were also completed within the same three-day window. The researchers provided teachers with a standardized assessment schedule and reminded them to complete the ratings for all the children in their respective classes within the designated timeframe.

The ratings were completed by the lead classroom teachers of each participating class. Each kindergarten class has one lead teacher who interacts with the children for approximately 8 h per day (5 days per week) and is responsible for observing and documenting children’s behavioral and developmental progress. Consequently, the lead teachers had an in-depth understanding of each child’s typical behavioral performance across various daily situations (including structured learning activities, free play, transition periods, and daily care) for both the intervention and control group children. Importantly, the lead classroom teachers did not participate in implementing the music interventions. The music activities were conducted by specialist music teachers, who were specifically involved in this study. This role separation was designed to mitigate the risk that teachers’ ratings might be influenced by their own involvement in the intervention.

Prior to the pretest assessment, all the lead classroom teachers participated in a two-hour training session led by the research team. The training content included the following: (1) An introduction to the purpose and structure of the questionnaire; (2) A detailed explanation of each item, supplemented with behavioral examples illustrating the meaning of different rating levels; (3) Practice rating using video-recorded child behaviors, followed by group discussions to calibrate the rating standards; (4) The emphasis should be placed on ratings based on observed behaviors rather than on subjective impressions or comparisons with other children.

Teachers were informed that this study was a longitudinal investigation concerning “early childhood psychological development,” aimed at “understanding children’s developmental characteristics at different time points to inform educational practices”. The specific focus on self-control and the hypotheses regarding the music interventions were not disclosed to the teachers. However, we acknowledge a limitation: owing to the practical circumstances of the school environment, teachers were likely aware of which children in their classes were participating in the music activities (as these children regularly left the regular classroom to participate). Therefore, while teachers remained blind to the study’s hypotheses, they were not fully blind to group assignment. This constitutes a potential source of expectancy bias. We address this limitation in the discussion section.

Intervention protocol

The two experimental intervention groups received separate music instruction led by teachers specializing in percussion and rhythm-based activities. Both held bachelor’s degrees in music education and had at least three years of experience teaching music to preschool-aged children. Neither the teacher was affiliated with the participating kindergarten, and neither had prior contact with the participating children nor their classroom teachers. The specialist teachers were informed that they were implementing a “music enrichment activity” as part of a study on child development. They were not informed about the specific hypotheses regarding self-control or the purpose of comparing the two intervention approaches. They did not participate in the assessment of children’s self-control and had no communication with the classroom teachers during the intervention period, except for necessary logistical coordination (e.g., scheduling). During the music activity sessions, the lead classroom teachers remained in the classroom with the control group children, who engaged in regular daily classroom activities (free play, drawing, story reading). This ensured that the control condition represented “business as usual” rather than a deprived state.

The intervention spanned 8 weeks, with two 30-minute sessions per week. Each week featured a distinct thematic focus (for detailed content, see Supplementary Material Table S1 and Table S2). The two weekly sessions shared the same theme: the first introduced and practiced core activities, whereas the second reinforced and extended learning. The instructional approach was guided by established principles of music pedagogy, both internationally and domestically recognized, including “The development of musical thinking arises from hands-on musical engagement,” [40] “Bodily movement serves as the foundation for music learning,” [41] and “Imitation is integral to the initial stages of musical acquisition.” [42] Teaching content drew upon relevant classic examples and activity structures.

The percussion intervention was adapted from exemplary teaching activities developed by the research team of Xu [43] at Nanjing Normal University and was tailored to support the self-control development objectives of this study. The program consisted of eight thematic percussion units implemented over 8 weeks for a total of 16 sessions. Prior to each session, the instructors prepared all the necessary instruments and props. Each lesson began with familiarizing the children with the musical piece, followed by rhythmic practice without instruments, and gradually introduced percussion performance. Teachers adjusted the complexity of the ensemble pieces according to the children’s real-time engagement and ability. A key instructional emphasis was placed on auditory monitoring and self-control—children were consistently reminded to stop playing as soon as the music ceased. When instances of low self-discipline were observed, dedicated exercises were implemented to practice appropriate handling and storage of instruments. The teaching approach primarily employs guided exploration, emphasizing rule compliance, rhythmic accuracy, and fundamental percussion skills. The percussion activities in this study were designed with full consideration of the developmental characteristics of preschool children. Compared with high-intensity, technique-oriented instrumental instruction that emphasizes formal skill training from an early age, the activities in this study feature lower technical difficulty, an emphasis on playful exploration, and flexibility suitable for group implementation.

The integrated music intervention was adapted from the program “Swing with Me: Music Curriculum for Young Children in Diverse Contexts” by Chen [44] of the Shanghai Conservatory of Music. A total of eight comprehensive music game activities were designed and delivered over 8 weeks, comprising 16 sessions in total. The curriculum was structured around rhythm-based integrated learning, combining elements such as reading notation, singing, auditory discrimination, instrument playing, and performance with bodily movement. Instruction began with fundamental musical elements—rhythm, melody, harmony, structure, and timbre—guiding children to experience, perceive, imitate, and explore music through kinesthetic engagement. The activities were designed to be highly contextualized and engaging, emphasizing both enjoyment and experiential learning. The teaching methodology blended guided exploration with opportunities for free discovery, allowing children to interact creatively with musical materials in a supportive environment.

Data analysis methods

Data analysis was performed via SPSS 26.0. This study employed a 3 × 2 mixed factorial design, for which repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) would typically be the optimal statistical method. The participants in this study were recruited from two natural classes and were randomly assigned to three groups according to a randomization procedure. We further examined whether the three groups had consistent initial levels of self-control using ANOVA. A one-way ANOVA conducted on the baseline self-control scores revealed significant differences among the three groups in the following dimensions: continence (F(2, 64) = 3.172, p = 0.049) and delay of gratification (F(2, 64) = 4.135, p = 0.020). These results indicated that these groups were not homogeneous at baseline, violating the sphericity and homogeneity assumptions required for repeated-measures ANOVA. To control for the influence of preintervention self-control levels on postintervention outcomes, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed, following the method recommended by Vickers and Altman [45]. The pretest self-control scores were included as covariates, with posttest self-control scores serving as the dependent variable.

In accordance with the prerequisite conditions for ANCOVA, we examined whether a linear relationship existed between the covariate and the dependent variable within each group. Scatter plots revealed that a linear relationship was present within each group. Simultaneously, we checked whether the residuals for each group, after controlling for the covariate, followed a normal distribution. The Shapiro‒Wilk test yielded p values greater than 0.05, indicating that the data met the normality assumption. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances revealed no significant differences in the variances of the residuals across groups, satisfying the condition of homogeneity of variances. Most importantly, we analyzed the homogeneity of regression slopes by testing for an interaction between the group factor and the covariate; the results revealed no significant interaction in any case. These findings indicate that the data obtained in this study are suitable for analysis using the ANCOVA method.

Homogeneity test of baseline self-control levels across the experimental and control groups

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine the baseline differences in self-control among the three groups prior to the intervention. The results are presented in Table 2. Significant differences were observed across multiple dimensions of self-control, indicating that these groups were not homogeneous at baseline. These preexisting differences suggest that initial self-control levels could confound postintervention outcomes. Therefore, to statistically control for the effect of baseline scores, the pretest results were included as covariates in subsequent analyses. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed to evaluate the intervention effects on posttest self-control levels. We also analyzed whether there were significant differences in age and sex at baseline. Analysis of variance revealed no significant differences in age among the three groups (F = 2.067, df = (2,64), p = 0.135). A chi-square test revealed no significant sex differences among the three groups (χ² = 3.401, p = 0.183). Therefore, we did not include age or sex as confounding variables to control for.

Notably, after switching to ANCOVA, there was a discrepancy between the ideal sample size calculated on the basis of a priori power analysis (e.g., a total sample size of approximately 159 participants required for an effect size of f = 0.25) and the actual effective sample size (N = 67). We conducted a post hoc power analysis to assess the testing ability of the current sample. In our actual analysis, the observed between-group effect sizes (partial η² = 0.048 for consciousness, 0.184 for persistence, 0.107 for continence, 0.121 for delay of gratification, and 0.159 for total self-control, converted to f values of 0.225, 0.475, 0.346, 0.371, and 0.435, respectively) reached moderate or higher levels for all dimensions except consciousness. The results of the post hoc power analysis (conducted using G*Power 3.1 software with the following parameters: F tests - ANCOVA: fixed effects, one-way; number of groups = 3; number of covariates = 1; α = 0.05; total sample size = 67) indicate that, for the actual effect sizes observed in this study, the current statistical power reached acceptable levels (> 0.80) for most dimensions. Specifically, except for consciousness, which had a power of 0.39, indicating insufficient statistical power, the statistical power for the remaining dimensions was > 0.80 (0.97 for persistence, 0.80 for continence, 0.85 for delay of gratification, and 0.94 for total self-control). This suggests that although the sample size did not meet the ideal scale for detecting small effects (f = 0.1) or typical medium effects (f = 0.25), the current statistical results hold certain values for the actual effect sizes observed in this study.

We also recognize the limitations of this study in terms of statistical power. Post hoc power analysis reveals that the current sample size is indeed insufficient for detecting smaller effect sizes (f < 0.3). This means that if subtle but important intervention effects exist, our analyses may fail to detect them. Therefore, we have adopted a cautious approach in interpreting null findings in the discussion and have emphasized the reporting of effect sizes and their confidence intervals. We recommend that future research, when conditions permit, increase the sample size and strictly follow a priori power analysis results for recruitment to more robustly test intervention effects. We also suggest considering multicenter studies or collaborative efforts to accumulate larger samples.

Discussion

This study tested how percussion and rhythm-based music activities affect self-control among 4–5-year-olds. After 8 weeks of training, both intervention groups scored significantly higher than the control group on total self-control, persistence, continence, and delay of gratification, but no significant difference was found in the dimension of consciousness. In addition, no statistically significant differences were detected between the two music intervention approaches. These nonsignificant results should be interpreted with caution, taking into account the characteristics of the study design and statistical power considerations.

Music training directly engages the three core components of executive function by requiring children to understand and follow musical rules (e.g., rhythm, note recognition), inhibit impulsive behaviors (e.g., silent singing), and flexibly switch between tasks (e.g., role-playing) [27]. Inhibitory control is the most critical component of self-control. Extensive research has demonstrated that music training leads to significant improvements in children’s executive functions, particularly in the domain of inhibitory control [46–48]. Music activities demand the simultaneous processing of auditory, visual, and motor information, enhancing the attentional allocation and information integration capacities essential for executive function [31]. Furthermore, music training can induce adaptive changes in brain structure and function [35]. The preschool period is characterized by high neural plasticity, and music training provides a physiological foundation for the development of self-control during this critical stage.

From a cognitive development perspective, music activities may positively influence young children’s attention, memory, and impulse inhibition. Human processing of musical rhythm relies on the precise temporal coordination of the brain’s sensory, cognitive, motor, and reward systems. This involves core neural structures that form the foundation for executive control and emotion regulation [34]. During music interventions, whether they participate in rhythmic movement or percussion activities, children must concentrate on following rhythms, memorizing movement sequences, and coordinating responses. This sustained cognitive engagement helps strengthen executive function networks in the brain, supporting the ability to suppress irrelevant distractions and regulate behavior, thereby fostering self-control. From an emotional regulation standpoint, music inherently soothes emotions and creates a positive atmosphere. Children in a pleasant emotional state are better able to inhibit impulsive behaviors and engage in activities as instructed, which indirectly supports the development of self-control. Research has shown that musical synchrony—rhythmic synchronization and bodily coordination—promotes interpersonal similarity and cooperation, fostering a “sense of community”. As a flexible medium of communication, music can regulate emotions, allow individuals to assign personal meaning, and provide a safe space for imitation and interpersonal interaction [49]. The findings of this study provide support for the research hypothesis by indicating that participation in both forms of musical activity is associated with higher levels of self-control among preschool children. On the basis of these results and prior literature, we propose the following potential mechanisms through which music interventions might influence self-control: On the one hand, elements such as rhythm perception and melody appreciation stimulate the auditory system and promote neural refinement, strengthening connections between brain regions involved in behavioral regulation. On the other hand, in group-based activities such as percussion ensembles, children learn to restrain their actions and follow directions to achieve harmonious collective performance. This practice of self-control in a social context may generalize to everyday behaviors and may thereby contribute to the development of overall self-control. Notably, these proposed mechanisms require further empirical investigation in future studies.

The two types of music interventions may be associated with these two systems through different pathways. Percussion training emphasizes precise rhythmic execution, rule adherence, and motor control, requiring children to continuously monitor their own behavior and align with the group in a structured context. Such cognitive demands may primarily involve the cool executive function system. In the present study, the percussion group showed the greatest effect size on the persistence dimension (d = 1.12), and persistence—defined as the ability to sustain cognitive effort when facing difficulties—is consistent with the functional characteristics of the cool system.

In contrast, rhythm-based integrated music activities incorporate emotional expression, bodily movement, and social interaction, emphasizing holistic participation in dynamic, emotionally rich musical contexts. Such activities may more strongly involve the hot executive function system. Research by Kirschner and Tomasello [50] revealed that 4-year-old children’s participation in group music activities (such as singing and dancing) increased subsequent spontaneous cooperation and helping behavior; the proposed mechanism is that music activities prompt participants to maintain “audiovisual representations of collective intentions and shared goals,” thereby satisfying the “innate desire to share emotions, experiences, and activities with others”. In the present study, compared with the percussion group (d = 0.65), the integrated group had a slightly greater effect on the continence dimension (d = 0.73). Continence, which involves the ability to inhibit impulsive behavior, may be related to the regulatory function of the hot system in emotionally charged contexts.

Notably, neither music group demonstrated scores superior to those of the control group on the consciousness dimension. Consciousness refers to the ability to follow rules in the absence of supervision, a dimension that may involve deeper processes of rule internalization. According to self-determination theory [51], for external rules to become truly integrated as part of self-regulation, individuals’ need for autonomy must be satisfied—that is, individuals need to understand the meaning of the rules and voluntarily endorse them. Although the music activities in this study were highly structured and engaging, they were conducted primarily in teacher-led and group-supervised environments, which may not have provided children with sufficient autonomy support to truly internalize the rules as self-directed behavior. These findings suggest that future interventions aimed at enhancing consciousness should pay greater attention to creating transitional contexts “from supervised to unsupervised” and provide children with more opportunities for autonomous choice and self-monitoring.

A secondary aim of this study was to explore whether percussion training and rhythm-based integrated music activities have differential effects on specific dimensions of self-control. In this study, no statistically significant differences were found between the two music groups on any outcome measure. However, several noteworthy trends can be observed.

Persistence. Compared with the integrated music group, the percussion group had a higher adjusted mean (3.94 vs. 3.82) and a substantially larger effect size (d = 1.12 vs. 0.75). This pattern aligns with the theoretical prediction that percussion training, with its emphasis on sustained attention, rule adherence, and precise motor control, may be particularly beneficial for developing persistence—the ability to sustain effort toward goal achievement when facing challenges.

Delay of gratification. Similarly, the percussion group had a slightly greater adjusted mean (4.09 vs. 4.01) and a greater effect size (d = 0.88 vs. 0.62) on delay of gratification. This finding was somewhat unexpected, as we initially hypothesized that integrated activities, with their emphasis on emotional expression and social interaction, might more strongly activate the “hot system” and thus show an advantage in this affectively salient dimension. One possible explanation is that percussion training emphasizes waiting for precise moments to play and stopping immediately when the music ceases to directly train delay-related skills in a structured context.

Continence. In contrast, the integrated music group showed a slightly higher adjusted mean on continence (4.12 vs. 4.10) and a marginally larger effect size (d = 0.73 vs. 0.65). Continence involves inhibiting impulses and resisting immediate temptations in emotionally charged situations. Integrated activities, which incorporate emotional expression, social synchrony, and peer interaction, may provide more opportunities to practice impulse control in socially and emotionally rich contexts, potentially explaining this trend.

Consciousness. Neither intervention resulted in meaningful changes in consciousness—the ability to follow rules without supervision. The adjusted means for both music groups were similar to each other and only slightly above those of the control group. These consistent null findings across both interventions suggest that structured, teacher-led music activities alone may be insufficient to promote the deep internalization of rules required for unsupervised rule adherence.

This study has several limitations. The first limitation of this study is the absence of an active control group. Consequently, the observed effects cannot be attributed completely and uniquely to the specific components of the music activities; the contribution of nonspecific factors (such as additional adult attention and the structured experience of group activities) cannot be overlooked. Future research should introduce active control conditions—for example, by including a group of children who participate in nonmusical but equally structured and engaging group activities (such as picture book reading or construction games)—to precisely isolate the specific and nonspecific effects of music interventions, thereby more rigorously examining the unique mechanisms through which music activities influence young children’s self-control. Second, the baseline levels of self-control among the three groups were unequal prior to the intervention. This baseline nonequivalence poses a certain threat to internal validity. We used analysis of covariance to control for this factor. Future research is needed to further verify the internal validity of these findings. Third, in the current study, questionnaires were completed by the lead classroom teacher. Given that each class comprises approximately 30 children, the teacher’s ability to consistently and meticulously observe specific behavioral changes in each child may have somewhat influenced the accuracy of the assessments. Future research could employ multisource assessments (e.g., involving multiple teachers or parents) or incorporate objective behavioral tasks (such as the day‒night Stroop task or gift delay task) to increase the reliability of the results. Additionally, owing to the practical circumstances of the school environment, teachers were likely aware of which children in their classes were participating in the music activities (as these children regularly left the regular classroom to participate). Therefore, while teachers remained blind to the study hypotheses, they were not fully blind to group assignment. This constitutes a potential source of expectancy bias. Furthermore, cross-cultural comparisons could be conducted to investigate whether the influence of music training on young children’s self-control follows consistent patterns across different sociocultural contexts. The absence of individual-level process data (e.g., observations of children’s engagement during musical activities, teacher ratings of participation, or children’s self-reports of enjoyment) is another of the limitations of this study. It is necessary for future research to examine the potential influence of these individual factors on outcomes.

Although the design limitations of this study require caution when causal conclusions are drawn, the findings nonetheless offer several practical insights for early childhood education:

(1) Integrating structured music activities into daily routines. The observation that children in both music groups scored higher than those in the control group did on multiple self-control dimensions at posttest suggests that structured, engaging music activities can serve as a valuable component of kindergarten curricula. Educators may consider incorporating brief, regular music activities—such as percussion games or rhythmic movement exercises—into weekly schedules as a complement to existing social-emotional learning practices.

(2) Leveraging the distinct strengths of different music activities. Percussion training demonstrated a particularly large effect size for persistence (d = 1.12), suggesting that activities emphasizing rule adherence, motor control, and sustained attention may be especially beneficial for supporting children’s ability to persist when they face challenges. Rhythm-based integrated music activities showed relatively balanced effect sizes across dimensions, suggesting that they may provide broad-based support for self-control development. Educators may refer to these patterns when designing or selecting music activities for specific educational objectives.

(3) Addressing the challenge of rule internalization. The finding that neither music intervention enhanced consciousness—the ability to follow rules without supervision—underscores the importance of intentionally fostering rule internalization in instructional design. Simply engaging children in structured, teacher-led activities may not be sufficient for them to internalize rules as personal behavioral standards. Educators need to supplement such activities with autonomy-supportive practices, such as explaining the reasoning behind rules, offering children choices, and gradually reducing external supervision, to support children’s transition from external control to self-control.

Conclusions

An 8-week intervention using either percussion training or rhythm-based integrated music activities had positive effects on children aged 4–5 years. Whether the effects of the two intervention approaches differ in terms of self-control remains inconclusive.
