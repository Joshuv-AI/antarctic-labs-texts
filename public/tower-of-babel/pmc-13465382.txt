A methodological framework for conducting diagnostic meta-analyses using large literature corpora
Authors: Jennyfer Portilla-Yela, José-Rafael Tovar-Cuevas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe exponential growth of biomedical literature challenges the feasibility, reproducibility, and bias control of diagnostic meta-analyses based on manual screening.MethodsWe propose a scalable framework integrating automated topic modeling (Latent Dirichlet Allocation, LDA) for thematic pre-screening with hierarchical multivariate meta-analysis to jointly synthesize sensitivity and specificity. Abstracts from eight databases were processed using linguistic normalization, lemmatization, and probabilistic topic modeling to prioritize diagnostically relevant studies. Selected studies were synthesized using bivariate hierarchical random-effects models on the logit scale, allowing incorporation of methodological and clinical moderators.ResultsApplied to dengue diagnostic algorithms in Latin America, the framework reduced 5, 766 retrieved records to 10 studies contributing 94 algorithms. Machine-learning-based models showed significantly higher joint diagnostic performance than traditional statistical models (logit coefficient = 1.5657, p < 0.001), while external validation was associated with a significant loss of performance (−0.7962, p < 0.001). Estimated sensitivity ranged from 37.97% to 86.66% and specificity from 63.26% to 94.81% across algorithm types and phases.ConclusionsThe proposed workflow offers a methodologically rigorous and scalable approach to diagnostic evidence synthesis that is adaptable to clinical domains characterized by rapid literature growth and heterogeneous diagnostic evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02873-6.Keywords: Diagnostic accuracy, Hierarchical meta-analysis, Topic modeling, LDA, Machine learning in healthcare, Systematic review automation, Dengue diagnosis

Introduction

Global scientific production is growing at an accelerated pace, which increasingly complicates the process of conducting systematic reviews. For example, [1] reports that in 2023 more than 2 million articles were published annually; illustrating that traditional manual screening methods are becoming progressively less feasible. This volume of literature requires innovation in automated tools such as text mining and artificial intelligence to accelerate the prescreening of relevant studies and to avoid unnecessary overlaps and duplications [2].

This challenge is even greater in the context of meta-analyses, where researchers must filter and assess thousands of publications to extract useful and relevant information. In this regard, topic modeling using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) has proven to be an effective tool for classifying articles into latent thematic structures and thereby narrowing the initial search space. Mo et al. [3] showed that representing documents in an LDA-based thematic space improves the automatic detection of relevant studies in systematic reviews. Similarly, [4] applied this technique to facilitate thematic prescreening in diagnostic studies, particularly in the context of Dengue. In the present framework, this approach is adopted to reduce the manual burden during the exploratory stage of a meta-analysis.

Once studies have been preselected, the statistical synthesis of results becomes central. At this stage, bivariate or multivariate hierarchical models with a logit link offer significant advantages over classical approaches. Montero [5] notes that traditional meta-analyses tend to produce independent pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity, with artificially narrow confidence intervals and symmetric, theoretical ROC curves extrapolated beyond the observed range. Such approaches ignore diagnostic threshold variability and underestimate between-study heterogeneity. An alternative to address these limitations is the use of hierarchical regression models with random-effects estimation, which provide more realistic estimates that are coherent with the correlation between sensitivity and specificity and offer greater clinical utility across diverse contexts.

Given this context, there is a clear need for structured methodological approaches that enable rigorous and scalable evidence synthesis when the volume of literature is large and heterogeneous. This article has two objectives. First, we propose a generalizable framework that combines automated thematic pre-screening using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) with hierarchical multivariate meta-analysis for the joint synthesis of diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. This framework is designed to be transferable across clinical domains characterized by rapid publication growth and methodological diversity. Second, we illustrate the framework through its application to the synthesis of evidence on dengue diagnostic algorithms developed in Latin America, using this case to demonstrate how each methodological component operates in practice and what substantive insights the workflow can generate. Where relevant, the manuscript distinguishes between general framework recommendations and specific decisions adopted for the dengue application.

This document is organized into four main sections. First, the methodological guide developed to conduct systematic reviews and meta-analyses focused on diagnostic performance is presented, detailing its key stages: formulation of inclusion criteria, automated text mining, thematic classification, and article evaluation. Second, the application of this guide to the dengue case is described, focusing on the identification and analysis of diagnostic algorithms proposed in Latin America. The third section presents the results of the selection, characterization, and quantitative synthesis of the algorithms using a hierarchical multivariate meta-analysis model. Finally, the conclusions are provided.

Materials and methods

This section presents the methodological framework in its general form, describing each stage as a set of recommended procedures applicable to any clinical domain with large and heterogeneous diagnostic literature. The specific implementation of this framework for the dengue case study, including the search strategy, LDA configuration, eligibility criteria, and meta-analytic modeling decisions, is documented within each subsection and clearly distinguished from the general recommendations. Readers interested solely in the framework may follow the prescriptive guidance; those interested in the applied case will find the specific decisions embedded in the same structure.

Identification and thematic preselection of articles

This stage aims to ensure a broad and systematic retrieval of the available literature, minimizing selection bias and establishing a solid documentary basis for subsequent analysis. The systematic search of scientific literature should begin in recognized databases such as PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Ovid Medline, and other sources relevant to the research topic. It is recommended to design a search string combining terms related to the topic of interest. Each database may require specific adjustments in search fields (title, abstract, and keywords) or in the syntax used. In some cases, it may be appropriate to apply filters such as full text, original articles, or publications in indexed academic journals. Unless there are clearly justified methodological reasons, no restrictions by language or year of publication should be applied.

The results should be consolidated in a platform that allows reference management and curation. Tools such as [6] enable: detection and removal of duplicates, transparent definition of inclusion and exclusion criteria, assisted screening, tagging of articles by relevance or reason for exclusion, among other functions. The number of articles retrieved from each database should be recorded, and the selection process should be represented using a PRISMA-compliant flow diagram. This allows transparent documentation of the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and finally included in the synthesis. Figure 1 illustrates this step-by-step process.

Joint estimation of sensitivity and specificity using multivariate meta-analysis

The objective of this stage is to jointly synthesize the sensitivity and specificity reported by multiple diagnostic studies, taking into account the inherent correlation between both metrics as well as the heterogeneity across studies and evaluated algorithms. Unlike traditional univariate approaches, which analyze these measures separately and may produce inconsistent estimates or artificially narrow confidence intervals, multivariate meta-analysis yields more realistic and clinically interpretable inferences regarding overall diagnostic performance.

To simultaneously synthesize sensitivity and specificity from multiple diagnostic tests or models, a bivariate hierarchical random-effects meta-analytic approach is recommended, following the model proposed by [7] and implemented using the rma.mv function from the metafor package in R [8]. Results

The following results correspond to the application of the proposed methodological framework to the synthesis of evidence on dengue diagnostic algorithms developed in Latin America. Each subsection maps directly onto a stage of the framework presented in Section 2, illustrating how the general procedures translate into concrete analytical decisions and findings. This application is intended as a proof-of-concept demonstration of the framework, and the results should be interpreted within that scope.

Meta-analysis of classification studies using algorithms to identify dengue cases

A literature review was conducted to identify diagnostic algorithms proposed for detecting dengue cases. This exercise revealed several relevant methodological challenges. First, the growing volume of publications dispersed across multiple indexing platforms hinders the comprehensive and systematic identification of relevant studies. Second, the available studies exhibit marked heterogeneity in clinical approaches considered, the variables included, the statistical methods employed, and the criteria used to evaluate diagnostic performance, which limits direct comparability across studies. To address these limitations, a search was conducted across eight recognized bibliographic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, among others).

In the first stage, the search equation was designed to include terms such as “clinical algorithm*”, “classification algorithm*”, “clinical tools”, “statistical techniques”, “data science”, and “predictive scoring”, which were intersected with descriptors such as “dengue”, “breakbone fever”, and equivalent clinical variants. In no case were restrictions imposed by year of publication or by language, to maximize retrieval sensitivity.

LDA model applied to the dengue diagnostic literature

The objective of this stage is to thematically structure the retrieved literature corpus to identify and prioritize studies with a diagnostic focus relevant to the meta-analysis. Given the volume and thematic diversity of the identified articles, the use of automated topic modeling techniques allows for a reduction in the complexity of the document set and supports decision-making during the prescreening phase, complementing traditional manual filtering.

To identify the optimal number of topics, models with k ranging from 2 to 7 were fitted using Gibbs sampling (seed = 1234, burn-in = 1,000 iterations, 5,000 total iterations). Perplexity was used as the primary fit criterion and decreased steadily across all values of k; however, the rate of improvement became marginal from k = 4 onward. The final selection of k = 3 was made on the basis of both perplexity and the semantic interpretability of the topic-term distributions. The k = 4 solution was considered less adequate because one of its topics merged diagnostic and computational prediction content into a single thematic cluster without a coherent conceptual boundary. The three-topic solution, in contrast, yielded three clearly differentiated themes: Diagnosis, Epidemiology, and Virology, each dominated by distinct and contextually meaningful terms. Hyperparameters α and β were kept at their Gibbs sampler default values throughout; no systematic exploration of alternative specifications was conducted (Figs. 3 and 4).

Based on this classification, priority was given to the Diagnosis topic, and potentially relevant articles were identified according to the following criteria: i) primary focus on dengue, ii) proposal or validation of quantitative tools or predictive models, iii) reporting of performance metrics using real data, and iv) innovative contribution to diagnostic methods. This filtering resulted in a preselection of 76 articles, of which 23 met the predefined clinical–diagnostic criteria and were subjected to full-text review.

During the detailed assessment, additional criteria of regional relevance and methodological availability were applied. Studies that did not include clinical variables, those whose full text was inaccessible, and one study focused exclusively on coinfections were excluded. Through a manual citation tracking strategy (forward and backward citation tracking), two complementary articles were added, yielding a total of ten studies selected for quantitative synthesis. Table S2 (Supplementary Material) provides the complete PRISMA-compliant selection flow and the detailed exclusion reasons at the full-text stage. Briefly, of the 76 articles preselected by the LDA model, 23 met the predefined clinical-diagnostic criteria and were retrieved for full-text review. Of these, 13 were excluded for the following reasons: 9 did not meet the Latin American geographic scope criterion; 2 proposed algorithms based exclusively on laboratory variables, without incorporating clinical signs or symptoms; 1 could not be retrieved in full text; and 1 focused exclusively on dengue coinfection without reporting standalone diagnostic performance metrics. Two additional studies were identified through forward and backward citation tracking and, after full-text review, both met all inclusion criteria. The final set comprised ten studies.

Finally, the identified algorithms were classified according to the nature and type of variables used in their construction, distinguishing six categories: (i) S–S, based exclusively on clinical signs and symptoms; (ii) S–S–E, which additionally incorporate epidemiological linkage information; (iii) S–S–L, which include variables derived from laboratory tests; (iv) S–S–E–L, corresponding to integrated models combining clinical, epidemiological, and laboratory information; (v) L, based exclusively on laboratory variables without clinical signs or symptoms; and (vi) L–E, combining laboratory and epidemiological variables. Although categories L and L–E do not follow the individual clinical diagnostic focus adopted as the primary framework of this review, they were retained in the analysis because they were proposed within the included studies alongside clinical algorithms, and their exclusion would have introduced an additional selection criterion not prespecified in the protocol. Algorithms based solely on epidemiological variables were excluded, as they do not constitute individual clinical diagnostic tools.

Regarding geographic origin, Colombia contributes the largest share of algorithms, accounting for nearly half of the total, followed by Paraguay and Brazil. This territorial concentration reflects both the regional interest in early dengue diagnosis and the availability of local clinical and epidemiological data. The participation of Mexico, although more limited, complements the Latin American landscape considered in this review. Temporal analysis reveals that the most productive years were 2020 and 2024, with 25 and 30 algorithms proposed, respectively. In contrast, older studies such as those from 2006 continue to contribute a significant proportion of the evidence. This distribution highlights the recent dynamism in algorithm development, possibly associated with advances in data infrastructure, statistical techniques, and increased awareness of the dengue burden in the region.

Regarding the development phase, most algorithms (81.9%) are in the construction stage. This imbalance reveals a frequent methodological weakness in diagnostic studies: the lack of performance evaluation in independent clinical settings, which limits the generalizability of the results and their potential implementation in real-world scenarios. With the type of variables used, algorithms integrating signs, symptoms, and laboratory data predominate (53.2%), followed by exclusively clinical models (21.3%). Only a small group of studies includes explicit epidemiological variables as model components, suggesting an underexplored area with relevant potential for context-aware diagnosis.

Regarding the target population, algorithms were primarily designed for mixed populations (59.6%), followed by pediatric populations (31.9%), while a minority did not clearly specify the age group. Fitting meta-analysis models for the overall literature

The objective of this stage is to identify the multivariate model structure that best describes the joint diagnostic performance of the included algorithms, considering different combinations of clinical, methodological, and contextual moderators. For this purpose, multiple fixed- and random-effects specifications were evaluated in order to achieve an appropriate balance between goodness of fit, model parsimony, and reduction of residual heterogeneity.

Based on the methodology described in Section 2.3, multiple fixed- and random-effects structures were evaluated to identify combinations of moderators that optimized model fit and reduced residual heterogeneity. According to the information criteria (AIC) and heterogeneity tests (Test_Het), the most competitive formulas were: measure + Phase + Country - 1, measure + Phase + Year + Age - 1, measure + Phase + Year + technique - 1, measure + Phase + technique + Country - 1, and measure + Phase + Type + Year + Age - 1.

These structures yielded the lowest AIC values (range: 634.31–635.90) and consistent heterogeneity values (QE range: 298,517.5–300,392). Accordingly, each of these models was fitted individually to explore the relative contribution of the variables validation phase, algorithm type, technique used, country, year, and age to the combined sensitivity and specificity. The five multivariate models showed significant residual heterogeneity (p < 0.0001) in all cases, reflecting between-study variability. The moderators validation phase, country, and Machine Learning technique were the most influential (p < 0.05), highlighting their relevance to diagnostic performance. In contrast, the variables year of publication, pediatric age, and signs-and-symptoms-based algorithm type did not reach statistical significance, suggesting a limited influence on diagnostic performance variability.

Given that some moderators were not statistically significant, a comparison was performed between the models measure + Phase + technique - 1 and measure + Phase - 1, which yielded AIC values of 644.5521 and 654.8503, respectively, and heterogeneity statistics of 302,118.2433 and 302,690.6271. These results show that including the technique variable slightly improves model fit, with lower AIC and residual heterogeneity values. The likelihood ratio test (LRT = 12.4559, p = 0.0004) confirmed that this improvement is statistically significant, indicating that the inclusion of the technique moderator explains an additional portion of between-study variability. The final model was measure + Phase + technique - 1.

The fitted multivariate model showed significant residual heterogeneity (QE = 302,118.24, p < 0.0001), indicating substantial unexplained variability across the analyzed studies and algorithms. The included moderators were statistically significant (QM = 170.39, p < 0.0001), suggesting that variables such as validation phase and technique contribute substantially to explaining differences in diagnostic performance. The model reflects the presence of structural heterogeneity among diagnostic algorithms, although the analyzed moderators significantly improve its overall fit.

The substantial residual heterogeneity observed (QE = 302,118.24, p < 0.0001) reflects the complexity of the diagnostic landscape for dengue in Latin America and is attributable to multiple sources beyond the moderators included in the final model. First, differences in study design and patient populations are likely contributors: the included studies encompass both pediatric and mixed-age cohorts, with algorithms developed in hospital-based settings ranging from primary care to referral centers across Colombia, Brazil, Paraguay, and Mexico. These contextual differences in case mix, disease severity, and dengue serotype circulation may introduce variability in diagnostic performance that cannot be fully captured by the available covariates. Second, the heterogeneity of predictor variables across algorithms is considerable: some algorithms rely exclusively on clinical signs and symptoms, others incorporate laboratory parameters such as platelet and leukocyte counts, and others combine both sources. The threshold values used to dichotomize continuous variables also vary across studies, creating additional variability in the sensitivity–specificity trade-off. Third, temporal factors may play a role, as the included studies span from 2006 to 2024, a period during which dengue epidemiology, circulating serotypes, and clinical management practices in the region have evolved substantially. Finally, the concentration of algorithms in a small number of highly productive studies, particularly Echeverría et al. [11], Caicedo-Borrero et al. [12], and da Silva Ferreira et al. [13], which together contribute nearly 50% of all included algorithms, may introduce cluster-level heterogeneity not fully addressed by the algorithm-level random effects.

This finding is consistent with the phenomenon of loss of fit or overfitting, which occurs when models lose predictive capacity when applied beyond the dataset used for their development.

The Machine Learning Technique variable, in turn, showed a significant positive effect (1.5657, p < 0.0001), indicating that algorithms built using machine learning methods achieve superior average performance compared with traditional statistical methods. This effect reflects the ability of machine learning techniques to capture nonlinear relationships and complex patterns in the data, resulting in higher specificity and sensitivity in both training and validation scenarios. Although machine learning models show clear advantages in overall performance, the validation phase remains a determining factor in reducing diagnostic accuracy. This reinforces the importance of external validation in the evaluation of clinical algorithms, ensuring that results obtained in controlled environments remain stable when applied to real-world populations.

Discussion

What this study adds

This study makes three contributions to the methodological literature on diagnostic evidence synthesis. First, it proposes and demonstrates a formal integration of Latent Dirichlet Allocation-based topic modeling as a reproducible pre-screening stage within a diagnostic meta-analytic pipeline, providing an auditable and scalable alternative to purely manual literature screening in contexts where the retrieved evidence base is large and heterogeneous. Second, it applies a hierarchical multivariate meta-analytic model that jointly synthesizes sensitivity and specificity across multiple algorithms from the same studies, explicitly addressing within-study dependence through a unique algorithm identifier and an unstructured covariance matrix - a modeling choice that avoids artificially narrow confidence intervals while preserving the bivariate structure of diagnostic accuracy data. Third, it provides a quantitative synthesis of dengue diagnostic algorithms developed in Latin America, covering 94 algorithms from 10 studies across four countries, and identifies validation phase and statistical technique as significant moderators of diagnostic performance. Together, these contributions offer a proof-of-concept demonstration that the proposed framework is both methodologically and empirically applicable and that it is designed to be transferable to other clinical domains.

The integration of automated text mining and hierarchical meta-analysis proposed here builds on and extends several recent methodological contributions to evidence synthesis. Robleto and Shehadeh [1] presented a rule-based screening protocol in which artificial intelligence assists exclusively with code generation, transforming what they describe as “the 6–24-mo manuscript preparation process” into a one-week workflow; however, they explicitly note that “meta-analysis and statistical synthesis remain manual processes” requiring human expertise, and their approach relies on keyword matching rather than probabilistic topic modeling. Natukunda and Muchene [2], who applied LDA to automated title and abstract screening, concluded that “unsupervised title and abstract screening has the potential to reduce the workload” of systematic reviews without requiring pre-labelled training data, achieving sensitivity of 69.83% and specificity of 77.37% in one case study; however, their framework focused exclusively on the screening stage and did not integrate downstream statistical synthesis. Montero [5] argued that traditional meta-analytic approaches “often yield biased and clinically misleading estimates” and demonstrated through a simulated dataset that hierarchical random-effects models are essential to preserve the correlation between sensitivity and specificity and to avoid underestimating between-study heterogeneity. Compared with these contributions, the present framework is distinctive in formally integrating LDA-based thematic pre-screening as a reproducible upstream stage within a diagnostic meta-analytic pipeline, and in demonstrating this integration in a real-world application involving 94 dengue diagnostic algorithms from Latin America.

A key contribution of this work lies in the use of Latent Dirichlet Allocation as a formal preselection mechanism rather than as an exploratory visualization tool. By transforming abstracts into a low-dimensional thematic space, the framework enables objective and transparent prioritization of diagnostically relevant studies. This approach mitigates selection bias arising from purely manual screening and provides an auditable trail from the initial corpus to the final analytical dataset. In contrast to conventional keyword-based filtering, topic modeling captures semantic structure.

At the synthesis stage, the use of hierarchical multivariate meta-analysis provides an essential statistical complement to automated screening. Diagnostic studies frequently report multiple algorithms, thresholds, or validation scenarios within the same publication, a feature of diagnostic evidence that standard independence assumptions do not accommodate. By explicitly modeling both within-study and between-algorithm variability, the proposed framework avoids artificially narrow confidence intervals and preserves the correlation between sensitivity and specificity. This is especially important when the goal is not merely to estimate average performance, but to compare methodological strategies and assess how study design choices influence diagnostic accuracy.

The dengue case study illustrates how these methodological components interact in practice. Automated topic modeling reduced a corpus of several thousand articles to a diagnostically coherent subset that could be reviewed and synthesized quantitatively. The hierarchical model then revealed systematic performance differences associated with validation phase and modeling technique, demonstrating that the framework can generate substantive insights while preserving methodological rigor. Importantly, these findings emerge from the structure of the model rather than from ad hoc subgroup analyses, highlighting the advantage of embedding moderators directly within a joint multivariate framework.

In the context of the dengue case study, the results of the meta-analytic model offer some findings that may be relevant to clinical and methodological decision-making, though they should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of this proof-of-concept application. The observed reduction in diagnostic performance between the construction and validation phases suggests that algorithms evaluated exclusively on the data used to develop them tend to overestimate their accuracy, which underscores the importance of external validation before any clinical adoption. A recent systematic review of machine learning and deep learning approaches for dengue diagnosis identified 48 distinct algorithms across 32 studies selected from an initial corpus of 2,723 records, underscoring the methodological heterogeneity that characterizes this research domain [21]. Support vector machine algorithms were the most frequently applied, appearing in 25% of the reviewed studies, while the best-performing model achieved sensitivity of 99.75% and specificity of 99.09% under construction-phase conditions.

The heterogeneity of methodological approaches and the variability in reported performance across these studies reinforce the need for systematic synthesis frameworks capable of organizing and statistically integrating evidence from large and diverse diagnostic corpora. The difference in performance associated with machine learning techniques, while statistically significant within this dataset, should not be interpreted as a definitive recommendation for their use, but rather as a hypothesis-generating finding that warrants confirmation in prospective studies with independent samples. More broadly, the framework itself may be of practical value in settings where evidence needs to be synthesized rapidly from large corpora, as it provides a structured and auditable pathway from literature retrieval to quantitative synthesis. Whether these findings translate into changes in clinical practice remains to be determined through further validation in different epidemiological contexts.

The present study has implications at two complementary levels. For methodologists and systematic reviewers, the proposed framework demonstrates that automated topic modeling and hierarchical meta-analysis can be integrated into a coherent, auditable pipeline for diagnostic evidence synthesis, offering a structured alternative to purely manual screening in research contexts characterized by large and heterogeneous corpora. For researchers working on dengue diagnosis in Latin America, the results highlight two findings with practical relevance: first, that diagnostic performance tends to be substantially lower in external validation than in algorithm construction, which argues for caution when interpreting accuracy estimates derived from single-dataset studies; and second, that machine learning approaches show a consistent performance advantage in this corpus, a finding that warrants prospective confirmation in independent samples before it can inform clinical recommendations. In both cases, the value of the present work lies not in providing definitive answers but in offering a reproducible methodological pathway through which such answers can be systematically pursued.

Limitations

First, the thematic pre-screening stage relies on abstract-level information, which may incompletely reflect the full content of a study and could lead to the exclusion of relevant articles whose diagnostic contribution is not evident from the abstract alone. This limitation is particularly relevant for studies that report diagnostic performance metrics as secondary outcomes, or whose abstracts use non-standard terminology not captured by the dominant topic vocabulary. As a result, the sensitivity of the LDA-based pre-screening stage may be lower than that of a fully manual review, and some potentially eligible studies may have been missed. To mitigate this risk, the present framework incorporated a manual citation tracking strategy that recovered two additional studies not identified through automated screening.

Second, formal risk-of-bias assessment tools, such as QUADAS-2 or PROBAST, were not applied to the included studies. This decision was consistent with the primary objective of the work, which was to demonstrate the methodological feasibility of the proposed framework rather than to produce a clinical practice guideline. However, the absence of formal quality assessment limits the interpretability of the comparative results, particularly the observed advantage of machine learning algorithms over traditional statistical methods, since performance differences may partly reflect differences in study quality rather than true methodological superiority. Future applications of this framework in clinical guideline development should incorporate formal risk-of-bias evaluation as an integral component of the pipeline.

Third, the geographic scope of the included evidence is concentrated in four Latin American countries, Colombia, Brazil, Paraguay, and Mexico, with Colombia alone contributing nearly half of all included algorithms. This concentration reflects both the regional interest in dengue diagnosis and the availability of local clinical data, but limits the generalizability of the findings to other endemic regions with different epidemiological profiles, healthcare infrastructure, and circulating serotypes.

Fourth, despite the hierarchical structure of the meta-analytic model, residual dependence among algorithms from the same study may not be fully captured by the algorithm-level random effects. Studies contributing large numbers of algorithms, particularly Echeverría et al. [11] with 22 algorithms and Caicedo-Borrero et al. [12] with 15, may exert disproportionate influence on the pooled estimates.

Future directions

Several directions for future research emerge from this work. The LDA-based pre-screening stage could be extended to incorporate full-text content rather than abstract-level information alone, substantially improving the sensitivity of the automated selection process. Hybrid approaches combining automated topic modeling with manual validation of a subset of excluded records would allow estimation of the screening error rate and further strengthen the transparency of the workflow.

Recent work has explored the integration of artificial intelligence tools with traditional topic modeling approaches in biomedical literature. Ma et al. [22] applied both LDA and BERTopic to a corpus of 1,837 PubMed abstracts on opioid-related cardiovascular risks in women, using MALLET for LDA implementation and BioBERT embeddings for BERTopic. Their comparison showed that both models generated coherent and interpretable topics with similar levels of accuracy in expert validation, 89.8% for LDA and 84.9% for BERTopic, while BERTopic produced more semantically distinct clusters as evidenced by higher UMass coherence scores and better-separated t-SNE visualizations. The authors concluded that LDA remains valuable for large-scale biomedical text analysis, particularly in resource-constrained settings, while BERTopic offers advantages in semantic coherence when integrated with large language models. These findings are consistent with the use of LDA in the present framework and suggest that transformer-based approaches such as BERTopic may offer complementary advantages in future implementations where computational resources permit.

Kumari et al. [23] provide a comprehensive review of topic modeling techniques applied to healthcare, bioinformatics, and scientific literature, covering methods from LDA and probabilistic latent semantic analysis to more recent approaches such as Top2Vec and BERTopic. Their analysis highlights that LDA is particularly well-suited for large, high-dimensional, and sparse datasets, characteristics that precisely describe the corpus of dengue diagnostic literature processed in the present framework, while acknowledging that LDA may have difficulty with noise filtering and may not be the best choice when fine-grained topic distinction is required. The authors also note that BERTopic offers advantages in interpretability and handling of sparse data, but at a higher computational cost. These observations are consistent with the methodological decisions adopted in this study and reinforce the rationale for exploring hybrid or transformer-based approaches in future implementations, as the choice of topic modeling technique should be guided by the specific characteristics of the target corpus.

The proposed framework is readily adaptable to other clinical domains characterized by large and heterogeneous diagnostic evidence bases, such as tuberculosis, malaria, or COVID-19 diagnosis. Validation of the framework in these contexts would provide evidence of its generalizability beyond the dengue application presented here. From a statistical perspective, future implementations could explore the integration of formal risk-of-bias tools, such as QUADAS-2 or PROBAST, directly into the meta-analytic pipeline as additional moderators, allowing quality-adjusted estimates of diagnostic performance.

Overall, this study demonstrates that the combination of automated literature mining and hierarchical statistical modeling constitutes a promising and structured approach to modern diagnostic meta-analysis. As biomedical literature continues to expand, such integrated frameworks may prove essential for ensuring that evidence synthesis remains rigorous, transparent, and scalable.

Conclusions

This study provides a methodological contribution to diagnostic evidence synthesis by introducing an integrated framework that combines automated topic modeling with hierarchical multivariate meta-analysis. The proposed workflow addresses two of the main bottlenecks of contemporary diagnostic meta-analysis: the scalability limitations of manual literature screening and the statistical inconsistency of synthesizing sensitivity and specificity using independent or univariate approaches.

Through its application to dengue diagnostic algorithms, the framework demonstrates its capacity to organize large, heterogeneous corpora into analytically coherent subsets and to generate statistically valid joint estimates of diagnostic performance while accounting for study-level and algorithm-level heterogeneity. In this sense, the case study serves as a proof of concept illustrating how complex diagnostic evidence from a specific regional context can be transformed into structured, transparent [10], and interpretable quantitative summaries.

Beyond the specific application to dengue, the framework is designed to be transferable to other clinical and epidemiological domains characterized by rapid publication growth and methodological diversity, although further validation in different contexts is needed.
