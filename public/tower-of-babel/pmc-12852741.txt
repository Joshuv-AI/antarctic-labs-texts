Climate-driven habitat shifts And niche overlap of overexploited trees Cordia africana Lam. and Terminalia brownii Fresen in Ethiopia
Authors: Daniel Melese, Muluye Asnakew, Ashebir Awoke, Atnafu Tesfaw, Yibeltal Meslie, Yibelu Yitayih Hailie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCordia africana and Terminalia brownii, indigenous Ethiopian multipurpose trees of high ecological and socioeconomic value, are increasingly threatened by overexploitation and climate change. Understanding how climatic and anthropogenic factors shape their distribution is critical for sustainable management and conservation. This study identified key environmental drivers, predicted suitable habitats under current and future climates, and assessed shifts in distribution, geographic range, and niche overlap. Future climate projections were assessed under two scenarios: the medium-emission scenario (SSP2-4.5) and the high-emission scenario (SSP5-8.5), for two time horizons: the 2050s and 2070s. An ensemble modeling framework was applied using seven algorithms: boosted regression trees, random forest, generalized linear model, generalized additive model, maximum entropy, support vector machine, and multivariate adaptive regression splines. Each model was run with ten sub-sampled replicates. We evaluated model performance using the area under the curve (AUC) for C. africana (0.84) and T. brownii (0.82), and the true skill statistic (TSS), which was 0.60 for both species. Predicted suitable habitat for C. africana (20.1%) was concentrated in the western, central, and southwestern regions, while T. brownii (19.9%) was mainly distributed in the northern, eastern, and southeastern lowlands. Under future climate scenarios, suitable areas are projected to decline sharply, shrinking to 8.68% and 2.07% of Ethiopia’s land area, respectively, by the 2070s. Suitable habitats for both species are expected to contract, with potential refugia for C. africana shifting toward highland areas. Geographic and environmental overlap between the two species was minimal. Given their multipurpose use, increasing vulnerability, and limited niche and geographic overlap, our results show that the projected habitat contraction, the marked decline of T. brownii within its natural range, and the upslope shift of C. africana refugia require species-specific conservation actions. C. africana needs refugia protection and climate-adapted management, while T. brownii requires targeted measures to support its long-term viability. Conservation efforts should focus on safeguarding existing habitats, regulating harvesting, strengthening community-based forest management, and maintaining ecological connectivity. Introduction

Indigenous multipurpose trees play a vital role in sustainable agriculture and agroforestry systems, offering a wide range of ecological, economic, and social benefits1,2. Across different regions, these species are managed for multiple functions. For example, Corylus avellana L. has been reported as a valuable component of agroforestry landscapes in Europe3, while Syzygium cuminii (L.) SKEELS is widely utilized in Indian systems for its diverse uses4. In South Africa, species such as Adansonia digitata L., Prunus africana (Hook.f.) Kalkman, and Warburgia salutaris (Bertol. f.) Chiov. provide not only subsistence and livelihood benefits but also carry significant ethnomedicinal and cultural importance1. In Ethiopia, indigenous tree species such as Cordia africana Lam. and Terminalia brownii Fresen are managed for multiple purposes, including the provision of fruit, fodder, fuel wood, timber, and cash income (Fig. 1). Beyond these direct uses, they contribute to ecosystem health by maintaining soil fertility, preventing erosion, reclaiming degraded lands, supplying bioactive compounds from by-products, and conserving biodiversity5,6.

C. africana occurs mainly in mid- to high-elevation regions, whereas T. brownii is predominantly found in lowland areas. Both species are closely associated with water bodies, and C. africana is also recorded on the Lake Tana peninsula. Cordia africana is a small to medium-sized tree, generally reaching up to 10 m in height, though in forest habitats it may occasionally attain 18 m. The leaves are alternate and thick-textured, while the bisexual flowers are sessile and sweetly scented. The fruits are yellow, subglobose to ovoid, glabrous, and contain a sweet, sticky pulp15. Cordia africana is a highly valuable indigenous tree with multiple uses. Its edible fruits are consumed as food or processed into juices, while other parts are used for medicine, fodder, fuelwood, and various cultural purposes5,7,8. The fruit was found to be a source of vitamin C, an antioxidant that helps meet part of the daily nutritional requirement9. Agriculturally, C. africana provides excellent mulch and can be integrated into mixed cropping systems on cropland and pastureland, where it improves soil fertility and microclimatic conditions10. It also supports apiculture: the tree flowers, serving as a key forage source for honeybees. The tree is also an important source of high-quality timber, commonly used in Ethiopia for furniture, doors, windows, and mortars. As one of the country’s major commercial timber species, it contributes significantly to household income while also supporting biodiversity conservation10.

Terminalia brownii is a semi-deciduous tree, typically attaining a height of 10–15 m. It forms a rounded, flat-topped, spreading crown and often branches near the base. The young leaves are sericeous-tomentose, whereas the flowers are white to cream, strongly fragrant, and glabrous externally. The fruits are ellipsoid to broadly ellipsoid, reddish-purple to crimson at maturity, and glabrous16. Terminalia brownii is a drought-resistant tree species that plays a crucial role in supporting the livelihoods of rural households in Eastern Kenya. It is highly valued for its versatility, serving multiple purposes, including medicinal applications, wood carving, fuelwood, construction material, and various cultural uses11,12. In northern Ethiopia, it is primarily utilized for fumigation to promote the health and strength of mothers during childbirth, as well as animal fodder, and as an insect repellent in homes13. It is also used to treat bacterial, fungal, and allergic infections, as well as malaria. In southern Ethiopia, farmers use it to improve soil fertility14.

Consequently, their multi-purpose nature makes these tree species more vulnerable to extinction than species grown for a single use. Anthropogenic pressures, including selective logging, harvesting for commercial purposes, and traditional uses, combined with climate change, further increase their risk17. Species-specific factors play a major role in driving overexploitation: Wanza (C. africana) is particularly valued for its high-quality timber, which is sought after for furniture, construction, and other commercial products, resulting in intensive local and commercial logging. Weyiba (T. brownii), on the other hand, is heavily harvested for fumigation and smoke production, practices widely applied in households and modern beauty salons, creating high localized demand. The combination of these targeted uses and the species’ widespread utility makes both wanza and weyiba especially vulnerable to unsustainable exploitation7,13,18,19.

To ensure sustainable use, promote agroforestry development, and guide investment opportunities, as well as to understand the impacts of overexploitation on C. africana and T. brownii, it is crucial to model their potential suitable habitats under changing climate conditions in Ethiopia. Species Distribution Models (SDMs) have become valuable tools for assessing potential species ranges[20,21,22] and predicting how climate change influences habitat suitability88–90. By integrating species occurrence records with relevant environmental predictors, SDMs provide robust projections of possible habitat shifts under changing climatic conditions. In Ethiopia, only a limited number of studies have used SDMs to assess potential plant species distributions in response to climate change91–93, and none has focused on C. africana and T. brownii despite their ecological and socioeconomic importance. This lack of species-specific modelling for these key multipurpose trees represents a significant knowledge gap. To address this, we applied ensemble species distribution modelling (eSDM), a technique that combines multiple algorithms to improve predictive accuracy and reliability compared to single-model approaches40,51,94.

Using an ensemble species distribution modelling (eSDM) approach, our objectives were to: (1) identify the key environmental variables shaping the distribution of C. africana and T. brownii; (2) predict their potential suitable habitats under current and future climate scenarios; and (3) assess spatiotemporal distributional shifts, geographic overlap, and niche overlap between the two species. Given that these multipurpose tree species are increasingly threatened by anthropogenic pressures and climate change, understanding their potential distribution patterns is essential. The findings of this study are expected to provide actionable insights that support climate-resilient spatial planning and promote the sustainable use of C. africana and T. brownii.

Results

Model performance and variable importance

Seven SDM algorithms (BRT, RF, GLM, GAM, SVR, MaxEnt, and MARS) were used to build an ensemble model based on their predictive performance, evaluated using AUC and TSS. The average AUC and TSS values across all algorithms and scenarios were 0.82 ± 0.04 and 0.60 ± 0.03 for C. africana, and 0.84 ± 0.02 and 0.60 ± 0.02 for T. brownii. No remarkable differences (standard deviation, SD ≤ 0.06) were observed between the AUC and TSS values across the predicted models, indicating that the dataset was sufficiently accurate for predicting the habitat suitability of C. africana and T. brownii. Overall, our prediction remained consistent across the model complexity levels, runs, and datasets (Table 1).

Uncertainty in future projections was quantified based on the number of pixels classified as suitable habitat under medium and worst climate scenarios. Both the standard deviation across scenarios and the difference in suitable pixels between the worst and medium scenarios were calculated for the 2050 s and 2070s. The results show that median uncertainty is very low for both C. africana (0.001073 in the 2050 s; 0.001531 in the 2070 s) and T. brownii (0.000635 in the 2050 s; 0.000667 in the 2070 s), indicating that the projections are highly consistent across scenarios (Supplementary Table S3).

Notably, the mean temperature of the driest quarter (BIO9, 23.85%), precipitation of the warmest quarter (BIO18, 13.85%), and precipitation seasonality (BIO15, 9.23%) were the most influential bioclimatic variables determining the suitable habitats of C. africana. Similarly, suitable habitats of T. brownii were primarily influenced by the mean temperature of the driest quarter (BIO9, 19.9%), temperature annual range (BIO7, 12.9%), and precipitation of the warmest quarter (BIO18, 10.3%). Additionally, non-climatic factors such as land use (19.62%) and soil type were important for predicting the habitat suitability of C. africana, and soil pH (13.3%) and land use (8.6%) were significant for T. brownii (Fig. 2).

Spatio-temporal dynamics of potential habitats of C. africana and T. brownii

Current habitat suitability prediction

Under current climate conditions, the ensemble model predicted 223,605.49 km² (20.01%) as suitable habitat for C. africana and 222,457.78 km² (19.9%) for T. brownii (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S4). Suitable habitats for C. africana are mainly concentrated in the central, western, southwestern, eastern, and southeastern parts of Ethiopia, including the floristic regions of Shewa, Hararge, Arsi, Bale, Welega, Sidamo, Illubabor, and Kaffa. In contrast, suitable habitats for T. brownii are distributed in the northern, eastern, and southeastern regions, particularly within the Shewa, Borena, Wollo, Gambella, and Hararge floristic zones. For both species, suitable habitats are smaller in extent and fragmented in the northern floristic regions (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4).

Across both current and future climate conditions, highly suitable habitats for C. africana are consistently concentrated in the southeastern and southwestern regions of Ethiopia. However, future projections indicate a potential upward and northward shift in distribution, with suitable habitats becoming increasingly restricted to the central highlands (Fig. 4). In contrast, T. brownii is projected to experience severe range contractions, losing much of its current distribution, with only fragmented suitable habitats persisting in the central parts of the country (Fig. 5).

Geographic and niche overlap between C. africana and T. brownii

Habitat suitability overlap between C. africana and T. brownii was consistently observed across all projection periods, although spatial shifts in distribution were evident. Overlap was primarily concentrated along the habitat margins of the two species, particularly in northern Ethiopia (Tigray and Wollo regions), as well as in the eastern, southern, and southeastern zones (Borena areas). The lowest habitat suitability overlap was projected under the 2070 s worst-case scenario (SSP5-8.5), whereas the highest overlap occurred under current climatic conditions (Table 3; Fig. 6).

Discussion

In Ethiopia, C. africana and T. brownii are among the most important multipurpose tree species. This study is the first to evaluate climatically suitable habitats for these species across the country by considering both climate change and anthropogenic pressures under present and future scenarios. Developing accurate and up-to-date distribution maps and identifying areas at risk of loss is crucial for guiding effective management and conservation strategies. Our study shows that strong performance across all averaged evaluation metrics improves the ability to identify species distributions and priority suitable habitat areas accurately. The average AUC and TSS values derived from the ensemble models were consistent with those reported for well-fitted species distribution models and showed no significant differences from earlier studies51,91,93, confirming their reliability in distinguishing suitable from unsuitable habitats. High AUC values indicate strong discriminatory power, while high TSS values demonstrate balanced sensitivity and specificity, confirming that the models performed well across both presence and absence predictions51,59. The use of an ensemble modelling framework further enhanced model performance. Ensemble averaging is widely recognized for reducing the uncertainty associated with individual algorithms, improving robustness, and stabilizing predictions across different modelling techniques60,61. This approach is particularly advantageous in ecological modelling, as it minimizes algorithm-specific biases and provides more reliable estimates of species distributions under current and future climate scenarios. Consequently, the eSDM method improves the accuracy and ecological realism of distribution predictions for valuable tree species vulnerable to climate change, including C. africana and T. brownii. Moreover, the low standard deviation and minimal differences between pixels representing future suitable habitats indicate very low uncertainty across scenarios. This consistency suggests that the projected shifts in suitable areas are highly reliable, allowing conservation planning and habitat management strategies for C. africana and T. brownii to be implemented with strong confidence in the stability of future habitat predictions.

Consistent with a previous study, our results indicate that climatic, anthropogenic, and edaphic factors contribute significantly to the decline of multipurpose tree species62. Among the bioclimatic variables, the mean temperature of the driest quarter, precipitation of the warmest quarter, and precipitation seasonality were the key determinants shaping the suitable habitats of C. africana. In regions with seasonal rainfall and reliable soil moisture, the climatic conditions align with the ecological requirements of C. africana, a fast-growing tree that thrives in fertile, well-drained soils and depends on adequate precipitation for successful establishment and natural regeneration. This species exhibits high light demand, moderate tolerance to shade in early stages, and is highly sensitive to drought and frost, which restricts its distribution to areas with favorable climatic and edaphic conditions63.

Likewise, T. brownii was primarily influenced by the mean temperature of the driest quarter, temperature annual range, and precipitation of the warmest quarter. Its distribution was more strongly shaped by temperature-related factors, particularly the mean temperature of the driest quarter and temperature annual range, together with soil pH as a key edaphic determinant. This species is well adapted to semi-arid environments, tolerating prolonged dry periods through deep rooting and physiological drought resistance. Its growth is constrained by large temperature fluctuations and unfavorable soil chemistry, performing best in slightly acidic to neutral soils that facilitate nutrient uptake and stable regeneration16,64.

Temperature and precipitation are critical determinants of plant growth, development, and physiological performance65,66. Water stress caused by insufficient moisture limits growth by reducing photosynthetic activity, leading to stunted development, leaf shedding, and dieback, which increase stress and mortality67,68. Precipitation supports tree development, seed dispersal, and the establishment of favorable habitats, making it a key factor in species distribution69,70. In contrast, high temperatures during the warmest quarter accelerate transpiration and evaporation. When combined with irregular or reduced rainfall, this lowers plant vigor and increases susceptibility to pests and diseases71,72. The interaction of favorable precipitation and moderated temperature is therefore essential for sustaining growth, photosynthetic performance, and the long-term survival of C. africana and T. brownii in their native habitats.

Overexploitation has severely impacted C. africana, largely because of its highly valued for the high-quality wood. In many parts of Ethiopia, local communities harvest this species unsustainably, without regard for its regeneration capacity or ecological importance. The strong demand for its durable timber, especially for house construction and furniture production, has resulted in excessive cutting that surpasses natural replenishment rates. Consequently, populations of C. africana have been declining, threatening the species’ long-term survival7,18.

Similarly, T. brownii has been significantly affected by human activities, particularly through the use of different parts of the plant for producing smoke in beauty practices. This trend is especially prominent in urban centers. Field observations in Dessie, Kombolcha, and Addis Ababa (2023) confirmed that numerous beauty salons burn T. brownii (locally known as “yeweyba chesi”) to generate smoke believed to enhance beauty. These salons appear at short intervals across the city, and the same practice is widespread in other large Ethiopian cities. Such continuous and large-scale use, without due concern for sustainability, has placed increasing pressure on the species and may accelerate its depletion in the wild13. Significantly, soil pH has also influence on the predicted suitable habitats and distribution of T. brownii. Variations in soil pH in lowland areas, influenced by flooding, irrigation, and sediment deposition, can strongly affect plant occurrence by altering nutrient availability and the composition and activity of soil organisms, which in turn impact nutrient cycling and plant health73,74,96.

Suitable habitats for C. africana are distributed across different parts of the country, with primary concentrations in the southeastern and southwestern moist evergreen regions (MAF), as well as in the northern, northwestern, and eastern dry Afromontane highlands (DAF). The species is considered characteristic of these regions, where moderate temperatures and adequate rainfall create suitable conditions for its growth95. Its sensitivity to extreme temperatures and cold stress restricts it to areas with reliable moisture availability. This pattern of distribution under current climatic conditions is consistent with previous studies, which also document its natural presence in these floristic regions at elevations ranging from 700 to 2,550 m a.s.l. 15,63. However, in northern Ethiopia, the distribution of suitable habitats is highly fragmented, mainly due to human activities, particularly deforestation, which significantly reduces habitat suitability. As a result, populations in northern Ethiopia are often confined to the edges of protected forests and to remnant forest patches surrounding churches23–25.

Under current climatic conditions, suitable habitats of T. brownii are mainly concentrated in the lowlands to midlands of the eastern, central, southeastern, and northern regions, with a smaller presence in the lowlands of the southwestern regions. The species occurs at elevations ranging from 300 to 2,000 m a.s.l., including the lowlands of Somalia. It thrives in Acacia-Commiphora, Acacia-Combretum, and Combretum-Terminalia woodlands, as well as in wooded grasslands and bushlands on a wide variety of soils. The species is commonly found in rocky areas, cultivated lands, riverbanks, and dry riverine forests16. Within valley systems, well-developed riverine forests commonly occur along major waterways, indicating that T. brownii benefits from the enhanced moisture availability in these areas95.

Projected climate change is expected to substantially alter the distribution of C. africana and T. brownii, leading to shifts in suitable habitats. Both climatic factors and human-driven habitat modifications are likely to shape their future ranges, highlighting critical considerations for conservation planning56,75. Under the worst-case climate scenario (SSP5-8.5), the range of C. africana is projected to decline by 56.62%, while T. brownii may experience an even greater reduction of 89.61%. For C. africana, habitat losses are concentrated in the southern, eastern, central, and northwestern regions, with populations persisting mainly in highland refugia such as the Bale Highlands. As noted by Thuiller et al78., global warming often prompts directional range shifts, although some species may exhibit localized redistributions in response to changing climatic conditions. In contrast, T. brownii is projected to disappear from the eastern, southeastern, and northern regions, leaving only small remnant populations in central Ethiopia. These patterns demonstrate species-specific responses to climate change, underscoring the need for scenario-based conservation strategies76,77.

Our results indicate that the environmental niche overlap between C. africana and T. brownii is generally low, with current habitat suitability overlap amounting to only about 5.6%. At present, the two species share suitable areas primarily in marginal regions of northern and southeastern Ethiopia; however, this geographic overlap is projected to decline further under high-emission future scenarios. Interestingly, their environmental niches show a contrasting pattern: despite reduced geographic co-occurrence, environmental niche overlap may increase under some future climate conditions, reflecting shifts in how the species partition environmental space79.

Because both geographic and niche overlaps remain low across all scenarios, the two species appear to maintain largely distinct ecological strategies, with only a slight increase in niche similarity under SSP5-8.5. This small rise does not suggest strong competition but indicates a modest shift toward occupying somewhat more similar climatic conditions, meaning any ecological interactions are likely to remain weak and localized. The contrast between low geographic overlap and slightly increasing environmental overlap highlights the complex ways species respond to climate change80. This pattern has important implications for conservation planning, as relying solely on geographic projections may overlook subtle but meaningful shifts in climatic requirements. Integrating both geographic and environmental dimensions is therefore essential for designing effective protected areas, anticipating climate-driven habitat changes, and prioritizing conservation actions aimed at sustaining ecosystem stability and reducing the risk of local extinctions.

Conclusion

This study assessed the habitat suitability of C. africana and T. brownii in Ethiopia under current and future climate and anthropogenic pressures. The ensemble model identified the mean temperature of the driest quarter, precipitation of the warmest quarter, temperature annual range, land use, and soil pH as the most influential variables shaping their distributions. Projections indicate that suitable habitats for both species will contract under all future climate scenarios. Although the two species show minimal geographic and ecological overlap, climate change is expected to further reduce areas of co-occurrence, while their niche overlap increases as habitat availability declines. C. africana may gain suitable habitat in some highland regions, which makes the identification and monitoring of potential microrefugia crucial. Given the sensitivity of both species to climate and human pressures, further research on seed germination, regeneration, and establishment is essential. Conservation should prioritize protecting existing habitats from unsustainable use, together with regulating tree harvesting, promoting community-based forest management, enforcing habitat protection, supporting natural regeneration, and maintaining ecological connectivity. Because C. africana and T. brownii occupy distinct ecological niches, conservation strategies must be developed separately for each species. These findings emphasize the urgency of proactive and climate-informed measures, including habitat protection and restoration, safeguarding refugia, regulation of human use, and consideration of assisted migration, to ensure the long-term survival of Ethiopia’s indigenous tree species under accelerating climate change.

Methods

Species occurrence data

Occurrence data for C. africana and T. brownii in Ethiopia were compiled from herbarium specimens, field observations, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; https://www.gbif.org), and published literature. For C. africana, the dataset included field surveys (n = 26), herbarium specimens (n = 30), GBIF records (n = 37)81 and published literature (n = 8) 23–25. For T. brownii, the dataset comprised field surveys (n = 4), herbarium specimens (n = 40), and GBIF records (n = 38)82. Field surveys were conducted between October 2023 and October 2024 across ecologically diverse sites encompassing both biodiversity-rich habitats and cultivated crop areas. The study sites included districts in northeastern Ethiopia (South Wollo Zone: Kalu and Tehuledere) and southwestern Ethiopia (Yeki District and its surroundings). Plant materials originated from farmlands, home gardens, forests, and markets, with a minimum distance of 1 km maintained between sampling sites to reduce spatial clustering (Fig. 8). Collection was conducted with official permission from the Department of Plant Biology and Biodiversity Management, Addis Ababa University, which issued a formal support letter presented to the respective local administrations. In addition, oral consent was obtained from local authorities and community members prior to sample collection.

Voucher specimens C. africana and T. brownii (collection numbers DMA001 and DMA002) were obtained from selected sites in the South Wollo area, subsequently pressed, labeled, and transferred to the National Herbarium of Addis Ababa University for identification. They were then compared with authenticated herbarium samples, identified, and their classification was confirmed by taxonomic expert Melaku Wondafrash. All field collections and research activities were conducted in strict compliance with institutional, national, and international guidelines. In particular, the work adhered to the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction (with reference to the Guidelines on the Scientific Collecting of Threatened Species) and the provisions of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES). These measures ensured that the study contributed to the conservation and sustainable use of the species without causing harm to natural populations.

The GBIF data were carefully screened and validated against herbarium records. Only entries with precise geographic coordinates were retained, while those with uncertain locations were excluded. Occurrence points from the 1980 s onwards were included, as older specimens often lack reliable location data26. A multi-step data cleaning and rarefaction process was implemented to ensure data reliability and minimize sampling bias, excluding records with missing or questionable geographic coordinates (checked with ArcGIS and Google Earth), removing those with taxonomic uncertainty or inconsistencies with the known distribution of C. africana and T. brownii (based on the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea), and deleting duplicates before spatial filtering; spatial rarefaction was then applied at a 1 km² resolution using SDM Toolbox v2.6 27 to reduce spatial autocorrelation28. Following spatial rarification, 101 unique records for C. africana and 82 for T. brownii were retained for modeling (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Ecological predictor variables

Various ecological predictors, including bioclimatic variables, topographic attributes, land use and land cover, and edaphic factors, were considered for habitat suitability modeling. The bioclimatic variables (n = 19, BIO1–BIO19) were obtained from the WorldClim 2.1 database version 1 (www.worldclim.org/bioclim) at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km²), based on interpolated climate data from 1970 to 200029. Topographic (elevation, slope, and aspect) attributes were derived from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) digital elevation model (https://srt.csi.cgiar.org/), with the slope layer specifically calculated from the same DEM.

To account for human land-use impacts on the distribution of Cordia africana and Terminalia brownii, we used a global land cover dataset from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/). This dataset classifies the Earth’s surface into 22 categories based on the UN FAO Land Cover Classification System, including cropland, broad-leaved evergreen and deciduous forest, shrubland, grassland, urban areas, bare land, and water bodies. Soil variables (pH and soil type) were obtained from Ethiopia’s National Soil Information System (https://nsis.moa.gov.et/). All predictor layers were reprojected, cropped, and resampled in R version 4.3.3 to match the coordinate reference system (CRS), spatial extent, and resolution of the bioclimatic variables, using the bioclimatic data’s CRS as the reference.

We extracted continuous ecological variable values from species occurrence points and 10,000 randomly generated background points, which encompassed the full range of environmental conditions across the study area to improve variable selection and reduce overfitting. To minimize collinearity, we retained only predictors with pairwise correlation coefficients < 0.7 and variance inflation factors (VIF) < 529,30. A stepwise procedure implemented in the “usdm” package in R31 was then used to iteratively remove highly correlated variables. In total, 11 ecological predictors were selected for modelling habitat suitability, consist of 9 continuous and 2 categorical variables (Table 4).

We used the HadGEM3-GC global circulation model (GCM) from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), developed by the UK Met Office Hadley Centre32, for future projections. Two shared socioeconomic pathways were selected: SSP2-4.5, representing intermediate emissions with CO₂ peaking around 2050 before declining to net zero by 2100, and SSP5-8.5, representing high emissions with CO₂ levels tripling by 210033. These scenarios were used to project the future suitable habitats of C. africana and T. brownii for the years 2050 and 2070. Following the approach of34,51, we used current land cover types, soil characteristics, and topographic features as fixed variables to project the habitat suitability of the species.

Ensemble species distribution modeling (eSDM)

To predict habitat suitability, we implemented an ensemble species distribution modeling (eSDM) framework that integrates complementary modeling strategies, including profile-based approaches, regression techniques, and machine learning algorithms35. Regression-based models, such as Generalized Linear Model (GLM), Generalized Additive Model (GAM), and Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS), capture linear and nonlinear relationships between species occurrences and environmental variables, while machine learning methods, including Boosted Regression Trees (BRT), Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM), enhance predictive power by identifying complex patterns in the data36,37. By combining these approaches, the eSDM leverages its complementary strengths to produce more robust and reliable habitat suitability predictions. These modeling techniques are widely used in ecological research and conservation planning because of their high predictive accuracy38–40. We selected these algorithms based on their compatibility with our dataset and their demonstrated effectiveness in ecological modeling41,42.

BRT were included because even a relatively small number of trees can substantially improve the predictive power of ecological models38. RF were selected for their strong performance with minimal parameter tuning and their well-documented resistance to overfitting83. GLM were incorporated as they provide a robust statistical framework capable of handling complex, non-linear ecological data structures84. GAM were used because they are widely applied in ecological research, accommodate non-normal distributions85, and have demonstrated strong reliability with small sample sizes86. MARS were included to model flexible, nonparametric relationships using piecewise linear basis functions to capture complex ecological responses87. Finally, SVMs were incorporated as a powerful non-parametric machine-learning method capable of handling both regression and classification tasks48. Collectively, this ensemble approach leverages the complementary strengths of each algorithm, enhancing overall predictive performance and ecological inference.

We used case weights during model calibration to give appropriate emphasis to underrepresented presence points and reduce sampling bias, thereby addressing the imbalance between presence and pseudo-absence data43. Model performance was further enhanced by testing and refining both default and customized parameter settings. This optimization involved fine-tuning parameters such as the method for generating background points, the number of iterations, the learning rate, feature selection, and the number of decision trees. We used the “sdm package” in R44, which supports ensemble modeling with multiple algorithms for habitat suitability modeling. Model training was performed using 70% of the occurrence data, with the remaining 30% used for model evaluation. This random subsampling procedure was repeated 10 times to account for variation due to data partitioning45. Models were fitted using an ensemble of algorithms (BRT, RF, GLM, GAM, MaxEnt, SVM, and MARS).

We applied algorithm-specific configurations to improve model reliability and performance. A learning rate of 0.05, 500 trees, and a bag fraction of 0.75 were used for BRT, while the RF model was also run with 500 trees46,47. GLM and GAM were fitted using a binomial family48. The MaxEnt model was executed with 10,000 iterations and a regularization multiplier of 149. SVM used a radial kernel with a cost parameter of 1, and the MARS model was configured with degree equal to 2 and nprune equal to 30. To provide pseudo-absence data, we randomly sampled 10,000 background points across the study area using the gRandom method within the sdmData function. Because no true absence records were available, these background points were used as pseudo-absences for algorithms requiring binary input and as background data for MaxEnt, together with the 101 presence records for species C. africana and 82 presence records for species T. brownii.

Model evaluation, averaging, and Spatial mapping

We used both threshold-independent and threshold-dependent evaluation metrics for model performance evaluation. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2) and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate overall model discrimination, while true skill statistic (TSS), and the sum of maximum sensitivity and specificity provided threshold-based insights50,51. AUC values range from 0 to 1. A score of 0.5 indicates random performance; values between 0.7 and 0.8 indicate fair performance; 0.8 to 0.9 represent good performance; and scores above 0.9 are considered excellent. Ensemble predictions were produced by averaging outputs from all algorithms, weighted by their respective TSS52. It predicts the current suitable habitats and distribution ranges of C. africana and T. browni, providing a baseline for evaluating future climate change impacts.

To classify habitat as suitable or unsuitable, we first identified an optimal presence–absence threshold for each model by selecting the value that maximized the sum of sensitivity and specificity (max ((se + sp)), which is equivalent to maximizing the True Skill Statistic (TSS), a widely used metric for evaluating model performance53,54. For each model, the threshold corresponding to the maximum TSS was recorded. To increase robustness across models, we calculated a weighted mean threshold, where each model’s threshold was weighted by its TSS value55,97. This weighted threshold was then applied to the continuous habitat suitability predictions: pixels with predicted values above the threshold were classified as suitable, and those below were classified as unsuitable. Binary habitat maps were generated based on this classification. Suitable habitat distribution of C. africana and T. brownii across Ethiopia was then mapped, and spatial analyses were carried out using ArcGIS 10.8. Future projections for 2050 and 2070 were generated under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways: SSP2-4.5 (intermediate) and SSP5-8.5 (high emissions). Spatial changes were quantified in terms of suitable, unsuitable, lost, gained, or unchanged areas to evaluate range shifts and assess the potential effects of climate change on a species56.

Geographic and niche overlap

To assess geographic overlap between the predicted distributions of C. africana and T. brownii, habitat suitability polygons were generated independently for each species. The INTERSECT tool in ArcGIS was applied to these layers to identify areas where suitable habitats coincide, producing an overlap polygon. From the intersect results, we then derived the other categories as follows: (1) C. africana only: areas from the original C. africana polygon that do not intersect with T. browni, (2) T. brownii i only: areas from the original T. brownii polygon that do not intersect with C. africana, and (3) Overlap: areas resulting from the intersection of the two species’ polygons, and (4) Unsuitable for both: remaining areas within the study region that are not part of any suitable polygon.

Niche overlap was evaluated using two complementary similarity indices: Schoener’s D and Hellinger’s I. These indices quantify the degree of similarity between the environmental niches of the two species, ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap)57. In addition, the centroid distance between the niches was calculated, providing a measure of the separation between the centers of the species’ environmental niches in multivariate environmental space. The values were based on normalized habitat suitability scores from the SDMs of C. africana and T. brownii, allowing us to assess both the overall similarity and the relative positioning of their ecological requirements.

Following the categories proposed by 57 and58, niche overlap based on D values was interpreted as follows: 0–0.2 indicates no or very limited overlap, 0.2–0.4 indicates low overlap, 0.4–0.6 indicates moderate overlap, 0.6–0.8 indicates high overlap, and 0.8–1.0 indicates very high overlap.
