Isolated Gamma‐Glutamyltransferase Elevation During Statin Therapy: A Diagnostic Pitfall and a Stepwise Management Framework to Prevent Unnecessary Discontinuation
Authors: Jun Yang, Yanling He, Zhengyong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe management of patients with isolated gamma‐glutamyltransferase (GGT) elevation during statin therapy remains a common clinical dilemma due to the unclear relevance to hepatotoxicity. We report a case of a patient with coronary artery disease who developed persistent isolated GGT elevation (> 100 U/L) while on atorvastatin, with normal alanine and aspartate aminotransferase levels. Critically, the elevated GGT persisted after statin withdrawal, and a comprehensive workup revealed uncontrolled cardiometabolic risk factors. We conclude that isolated GGT elevation in this context is more likely a biomarker of underlying cardiovascular risk than of statin‐induced liver injury. Based on this case analysis, we propose a stepwise decision framework to guide evaluation and management in such scenarios. Through literature review and case analysis, we conclude that isolated GGT elevation is not a reliable indicator of statin hepatotoxicity and may be associated with cardiovascular risk. Therefore, clinicians should not hastily discontinue statins. Instead, a comprehensive assessment should be initiated to identify potential causes and personalize treatment.Keywords: cardiovascular risk, gamma‐glutamyltransferase, management framework, stain

Introduction

Statins, foundational pharmacotherapy for cardiovascular disease, exert their primary benefit by inhibiting HMG‐CoA reductase and reducing low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), thereby mitigating major adverse cardiovascular events [1, 2, 3]. Despite their proven efficacy, concerns regarding potential hepatotoxicity, often triggered by asymptomatic liver enzyme elevations, can lead to premature drug discontinuation. Isolated gamma‐glutamyltransferase (GGT) elevation poses a particular diagnostic challenge due to its multifactorial etiology, which extends beyond drug effects to encompass metabolic syndrome and systemic oxidative stress.

This ambiguity in clinical interpretation underscores the need for clearer management guidelines. Through a detailed case report integrated with a critical literature review, this article addresses three pivotal questions: First, what is the clinical significance of isolated GGT elevation during statin therapy? Second, what is its correlation with genuine hepatotoxicity? Third, could this biochemical anomaly reflect underlying cardiovascular risk? Our objective is to translate these insights into a practical, evidence‐based framework for clinicians, aiming to preserve the cardiovascular benefits of statin therapy while ensuring patient safety.

Case Report

A 69‐year‐old female presented in May 2022 with recurrent chest tightness and palpitations. She was diagnosed with coronary artery disease (CAD), arrhythmia (paroxysmal atrial fibrillation), grade 3 hypertension (very high risk), and primary hypercholesterolemia. The patient denies a history of alcohol use. Admission laboratory tests confirmed dyslipidemia: LDL‐C 3.61 mmol/L, total cholesterol 5.79 mmol/L, and triglycerides 1.4 mmol/L. Baseline liver function tests showed normal alanine aminotransferase (ALT, 29.0 U/L) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, 25.0 U/L) levels, alongside a mildly elevated GGT of 79.0 U/L (reference range ≤ 50 U/L). Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a normal sonographic appearance of the liver, gallbladder, spleen, and pancreas. Atorvastatin (20 mg daily) was initiated for lipid management. Other medications included aspirin, levoamlodipine, and losartan, but no agents that can cause myopathy.

In October 2023, due to suboptimal LDL‐C control (2.84 mmol/L), evolocumab (140 mg biweekly) was added. During a follow‐up in March 2024, the patient reported new‐onset myalgia. Laboratory results revealed suboptimally controlled LDL‐C (1.91 mmol/L), persistently normal ALT/AST, but a further elevated GGT (105.0 U/L). Suspecting statin‐associated myopathy, atorvastatin was discontinued, and the patient continued on evolocumab monotherapy. By September 2024, the myalgia persisted. Lipid control remained effective (LDL‐C 1.7 mmol/L), transaminases were normal, but the GGT elevation persisted. A diagnosis of statin‐intolerant primary hypercholesterolemia was maintained. Discussion

The mechanism of statin‐induced hepatotoxicity is less well‐elucidated. Induction of caspase activity, triggering of apoptosis, reduction of coenzyme Q10, and generation of free radicals have been reported [4, 5]. However, statin‐induced hepatotoxicity is typically characterized by elevations in AST and ALT, whereas isolated GGT elevation is uncommon [6]. Serum GGT which is a cell‐surface enzyme, has been traditionally used as an indicator of alcohol intake or liver dysfunction [7]. Previous studies have shown serum GGT is proposed as an early and sensitive marker of oxidative stress [8], and elevated GGT levels serve as a significant predictor for hypertension [9], diabetes mellitus [10], cardiovascular disease [11], and metabolic syndrome [12]. In the present case, the patient demonstrated a distinctly elevated GGT level in isolation, with persistently normal ALT and AST. This pattern strongly argues against a classic statin‐related hepatotoxic mechanism. Consequently, a comprehensive differential diagnosis for isolated GGT elevation was pursued, encompassing non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), biliary pathology, and effects of concomitant medications. Through detailed clinical history and abdominal ultrasonography, these common causes were systematically excluded. This diagnostic approach shifts the interpretation of GGT elevation away from classical hepatocellular injury towards pathophysiological processes more closely aligned with the patient's underlying metabolic and cardiovascular risk profile.

The Clinical Significance of Isolated GGT Elevation During Statin Therapy

The epidemiological evidence further complicates a direct causal attribution. A large‐scale study found exceedingly low rates of significant, drug‐attributable transaminase elevation among statin users, though it did not assess GGT [13]. More instructively, research in a metabolically vulnerable NAFLD population that included GGT measurement found no adverse impact of statins on liver enzyme profiles [14]. These data suggest that in populations where background GGT elevation is common, statins do not appear to be a predominant driver. Isolated case reports of pan‐enzyme elevation exist [15], but they represent a distinct, acute pattern unlike the chronic, isolated GGT elevation seen here. We contend that the weight of evidence indicates isolated GGT elevation is a poor surrogate for genuine hepatotoxicity. Its diagnostic value is minimal without concomitant transaminitis, urging clinicians to look beyond the liver for its etiology—often towards underlying metabolic or cardiovascular risk.

Assessing Correlation With Genuine Hepatotoxicity

The first and most pressing question in this case was whether the observed GGT elevation represented statin‐induced hepatotoxicity. Formal assessment using established criteria for drug‐induced liver injury (DILI), such as the RUCAM scale [16], yielded a score of 2 (possible), argue against this diagnosis. The patient presented with two critical objective findings: first, a complete absence of elevation in ALT, AST, or bilirubin, which fails to meet the biochemical threshold for DILI; and second, and more decisively, the persistence of elevated GGT after drug withdrawal. This latter point is inconsistent with the expected course of a drug‐related injury, where improvement upon removal of the offending agent is typically anticipated. Therefore, both the cross‐sectional pattern (isolated elevation) and the longitudinal response (lack of normalization post‐cessation) provide strong clinical evidence that this was not a case of statin hepatotoxicity, corroborating the low risk profile indicated in current studies [17, 18].

GGT as a Biomarker of Underlying Cardiovascular Risk

Understanding the etiology of GGT elevation requires a paradigm that looks beyond pharmacologic triggers to encompass systemic metabolic and cardiovascular health. While markedly high levels unequivocally signal hepatobiliary pathology such as cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [19], a moderately elevated GGT—particularly in isolation—often conveys a different clinical narrative. Crucially, a robust body of evidence positions GGT as an independent risk factor for coronary artery disease (CAD) [20]. A meta‐analysis confirms serum GGT as an independent harbinger of both cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality in CAD patients [21]. We interpret this to suggest that GGT may act as a biomarker of underlying vascular oxidative stress and LDL oxidation, mechanisms which also explain its observed correlation with coronary lesion complexity and long‐term mortality in stable CAD [22]. Consequently, in clinical practice, an elevated GGT should prompt a rigorous assessment for metabolic syndrome, with which it demonstrates strong, direct correlations across components like dyslipidemia, hypertension, and dysglycemia [23, 24]. This pathophysiological link clarifies why elevated GGT frequently accompanies and predicts conditions like type 2 diabetes [25, 26]. Although extrahepatic expression in renal tissue exists, its inconsistent association with chronic kidney disease (CKD) incidence underscores that GGT's primary clinical utility, in the context of statin therapy, likely resides in reflecting cardiometabolic risk rather than renal or isolated hepatic injury [27, 28].

For this patient, exhaustive clinical assessment during atorvastatin treatment excluded concurrent hepatic, renal, glycemic, or other hepatobiliary pathologies. The most instructive follow‐up finding emerged after drug withdrawal: both the elevated GGT and myalgia persisted unchanged for 7 months. When this temporal dissociation from statin exposure is weighed against the established literature, the argument for a direct drug‐induced hepatotoxicity becomes tenuous. Instead, a more parsimonious and evidence‐aligned explanation points to the patient's baseline cardiometabolic risk profile—encompassing underlying dyslipidemia and progressive coronary atherosclerosis—as the probable drivers of the persistent GGT elevation. Hence, the subsequent management strategy for this patient will focus on lipid‐lowering therapy, rigorous blood pressure control, glycemic monitoring, as well as weight reduction combined with dietary and exercise counseling.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that isolated GGT elevation in patients on statins can be a misleading indicator of hepatotoxicity and may instead serve as a red flag for underlying or persistent cardiometabolic risk. A knee‐jerk reaction to discontinue statins is therefore unwarranted and potentially harmful to cardiovascular outcomes. The stepwise framework derived from this case—involving verification of ‘isolated’ elevation, exclusion of alternative hepatobiliary causes, consideration of a monitored drug holiday for diagnostic clarity, a systematic evaluation of cardiovascular risk factors, and subsequent management strategy—provides clinicians with a rational approach to navigate this common dilemma. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate the utility of this management framework. Ultimately, this approach aims to shift clinical focus from a narrow concern over liver safety towards a broader optimization of global cardiovascular risk management.
