Source-Condition Analysis of Kernel Adversarial Estimators
Antonio Olivas-Martinez, Andrea Rotnitzky

Abstract
In many applications, the target parameter depends on a nuisance function defined by a conditional moment restriction, whose estimation often leads to an ill-posed inverse problem. Classical approaches, such as sieve-based GMM, approximate the restriction using a fixed set of test functions and may fail to capture important aspects of the solution. Adversarial estimators address this limitation by framing estimation as a game between an estimator and an adaptive critic. We study the class of Regularized Adversarial Stabilized (RAS) estimators that employ reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) for both estimation and testing, with regularization via the RKHS norm. Our first contribution is a novel analysis that establishes finite-sample bounds for both the weak error and the root mean squared error (RMSE) of these estimators under interpretable source conditions, in contrast to existing results. Our second contribution is a detailed comparison of the assumptions underlying this RKHS-norm-regularized approach with those required for (i) RAS estimators using ℒ 2 \mathcal{L}^{2} penalties, and (ii) recently proposed, computationally stable Kernel Maximal Moment estimators.



1
Introduction

In many scientific and policy-relevant applications, target parameters depend on nuisance functions identified through conditional moment restrictions. Estimating such functions often leads to ill-posed inverse problems, where small perturbations in intermediate quantities can cause large estimation errors. These settings arise in causal inference with unmeasured confounding
(
Santos, 2011
;
Severini and Tripathi, 2012
;
Escanciano and Li, 2013
;
Freyberger and Horowitz, 2015
;
Miao et al., 2018
;
Tchetgen et al., 2020
;
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Bennett et al., 2022
;
Bennett et al., 2023b
;
Cui et al., 2024
)
and in analyses of missing-not-at-random data
(
Miao and Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2016
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Miao et al., 2024
)
.

Classical approaches—such as series or sieve-based Generalized Method of Moments (GMM)—replace the infinite set of implied moment equations with a finite system, obtained by testing against a pre-chosen set of functions. While computationally straightforward, these methods can miss key structural components of the solution when they lie in directions poorly represented by the chosen test space.

Adversarial estimators
(
Bennett et al., 2019
;
Muandet et al., 2020b
;
Muandet et al., 2020a
;
Dikkala et al., 2020
;
Liao et al., 2020
;
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Mastouri et al., 2021
;
Bennett et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Bennett et al., 2023a
;
Bennett et al., 2023b
;
Park et al., 2024
)
address this limitation by framing estimation as a two-player zero-sum game: a candidate solution is pitted against an adaptive critic that searches for the most challenging directions of violation. Within this framework, Regularized Adversarial Stabilized (RAS) estimators apply Tikhonov regularization to stabilize estimation. Of particular interest are variants that use reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) for both estimation and testing. When regularized via the RKHS norm—hereafter referred to as KRAS estimators—they admit closed-form solutions without the numerical instability suffered by their
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
–penalized RAS counterparts. The latter, while also closed-form, require solving nearly ill-conditioned systems and can be computationally fragile.

Theoretical analysis of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
–penalized RAS estimators, hereafter referred to as LRAS estimators, has yielded sharp finite-sample bounds under interpretable source conditions that link solution smoothness to operator smoothing properties. In contrast, existing theory for KRAS estimators is limited: available bounds rely on abstract ill-posedness measures that are hard to interpret and impose uniqueness of the solution.

This paper has two goals of equal importance. First, we develop a new theoretical framework for KRAS estimators that yields finite-sample bounds for both weak error and RMSE under transparent source conditions, without requiring uniqueness of the inverse problem solution. These conditions describe the smoothness of the minimal-norm solution relative to the smoothing effect of the composite operator formed by the conditional expectation and the RKHS kernel integral operator. Second, we place KRAS estimators in the broader landscape by systematically comparing their assumptions underpinning their error bounds, and the convergence rates that each deliver, with those of LRAS estimators and with those of recently proposed, computationally stable Kernel Maximal Moment (KMMR) estimators. This comparative perspective situates our results within the spectrum of available methods, clarifying the trade-offs each entails and the contexts in which their respective assumptions are most plausible.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the general class of target parameters and formulates their identification through conditional moment equations, which often give rise to ill-posed inverse problems. Section
3
reviews the debiased machine learning (DML) framework for estimating the target parameter, emphasizing the importance of controlling both weak error and RMSE in the estimation of nuisance functions. Section
4
outlines the broad class of regularized estimation strategies, with a particular focus on adversarial estimators. Section
5
presents our main theoretical results: finite-sample bounds on weak error and RMSE of KRAS estimator under interpretable source conditions, without requiring uniqueness of the solution to the inverse problem. Section
6
provides comparative analysis of KRAS, LRAS and KMMR estimators. We conclude in Section
7
with a discussion of open problems.

Target Parameter

Let
O_{1},\dots,O_{n}
be independent copies of a random vector
O
with unknown probability distribution
P_{0}
assumed to belong to a class
\mathcal{P}
—to be specified later—of mutually absolutely continuous laws
P
on some sample space to be defined later. Let
W:=(W^{\prime},X)
and
Z:=(Z^{\prime},X)
be two subvectors of
O
, where
X
contains the variables common to both
W
and
Z
, if any, and
W^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
contain the remaining, non-overlapping variables. We assume
W^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
are both non-empty, so that
W
and
Z
are distinct but may share the components collected in
X
.

For any random vector
V
distributed according to
P_{V}
, we let
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{V})
denote the space of real-valued measurable functions on the sample space of
V
with finite second moment under
P_{V}
. We use calligraphic upper case letters to denote the, assumed common, support of random variables under any law
P
in
\mathcal{P}
, i.e. if
V
is a subvector of
O
,
\mathcal{V}
denotes the common support of
V
under any
P
in
\mathcal{P}
. We denote with
\|\cdot\|_{2}

and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
the norm and the inner product in the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
space with respect to the relevant probability law. For any real-valued map
f:\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}}|f(v)|
. We let
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denote the empirical mean operator.

We are concerned with inference based on
O_{1},\dots,O_{n}
about the value taken at
P_{0}
of functionals
P\mapsto\theta(P)
that admit the following triple representation:

\theta(P)=\Psi_{P}(h_{0,P})=\Phi_{P}(g_{0,P})=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{r(O)h_{0}(W)g_{0,P}(Z)\right\}

(1)

where

1.

\Psi_{P}(h):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{\widetilde{m}(O;h)\right\},\Phi_{P}(g):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{m(O;g)\right\}

with
\widetilde{m}:\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
m:\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are known mappings such that for each
o
, the maps
h\mapsto\tilde{m}(o;h)
and
g\mapsto m(o;g)
are linear, and the functionals
h\mapsto\Psi_{P}(h)
and
g\mapsto\Phi_{P}(g)

are bounded (and linear) on
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
and
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
respectively, with Riesz representers denoted by
\tilde{\rho}_{P}(W)
and
\rho_{P}(Z)
; and

2.

h_{0,P}
and
g_{0,P}
solve respectively the integral equations

\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{r(O)h(W)|Z\right\}=\rho_{P}(Z)\quad a.e.(P_{Z}),

(2)

and

\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{r(O)g(Z)|W\right\}=\tilde{\rho}_{P}(W)\quad a.e.(P_{W}),

(3)

where
r
is a known measurable function of
O
satisfying
\|r\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

We make no additional assumptions on the data generating process other than the existence of solutions to equations (
2
) and (
3
) at
P=P_{0}
. Therefore, the aforementioned model
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all laws
P
on the sample space of
O
such that equations (
2
) and (
3
) have a solution.
Importantly, we assume that solutions to these equations exist but we do not require these solutions to be unique. Nevertheless, when both equations admit solutions, the parameter
\theta(P)
is well-defined, in the sense that its value remains the same regardless of the specific solutions
h_{0,P}
and
g_{0,P}
that are used in (
1
). This well known fact (see, e.g.
Severini and Tripathi, 2012
) can be seen as follows. Let
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
and
\mathcal{T}^{*}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
denote the operators

\mathcal{T}h(Z):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{r(O)h(W)|Z\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{T}^{*}g(W):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{r(O)g(Z)|W\right\}.

The operator
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{T}_{P}
.
If
h_{0,P}
and
h^{\diamond}_{0,P}
are two solutions of equation (
2
), then
h_{0,P}-h^{\diamond}_{0,P}
is in the null space of
\mathcal{T}_{P}
and consequently is orthogonal to the range of the adjoint operator
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{*}
. On the other hand, the existence of a solution to equation (
3
) implies that
\tilde{\rho}_{P}(W)
is in the range of the operator
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{*}
, and consequently is orthogonal to
h_{0,P}-h^{\diamond}_{0,P}
. Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}_{P}\{\widetilde{m}(O;h_{0,P})\}-\mathbb{E}_{P}\{\widetilde{m}(O;h^{\diamond}_{0,P})\}=\mathbb{E}_{P}\{(h_{0,P}-h^{\diamond}_{0,P})\tilde{\rho}_{P}(W)\}=0.

The same reasoning can be used to argue the uniqueness of
\mathbb{E}_{P}\{m(O;g_{0,P})\}
regardless of the solution
g_{0,P}
of (
3
).

For some estimands
\theta(P_{0})
, see for instance Example
2

below, it just happens that it is a-priori known that for some non-empty subset
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subsetneq\mathcal{X}
it holds that
\rho_{P}(Z)=0
a.e.
(P_{Z})
if and only if
Z=(Z^{\prime},X)
a.e.
(P_{Z})
and
X\notin\mathcal{X}_{0}
. In such a case, to enforce this restriction we will restrict attention to solutions of equation (
2
) of the form
h_{0,P}(w)=I_{0}(x)h_{0,P}^{\dagger}(w)
where
h_{0,P}^{\dagger}
is any function in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
that satisfies
\mathcal{T}_{P}(h)(z)=\rho_{P}(z)
whenever
z=(z^{\prime},x)
and
x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, where here and throughout

I_{0}(x):=1\quad\text{if }x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{0}(x):=0\quad\text{otherwise.}

We follow the same rule for defining the solutions
g_{0,P}
whenever the Riesz representer
\tilde{\rho}_{P}(W)
has structural zeros at some subset of the support of
X
.

Estimands of the form
\theta(P_{0})
are ubiquitous in causal inference, including settings with unobserved confounding—where identification is achieved by means of instrumental variables
(
Santos, 2011
;
Severini and Tripathi, 2012
;
Escanciano and Li, 2013
;
Freyberger and Horowitz, 2015
)
or by means of proxy variables
(
Miao et al., 2018
;
Tchetgen et al., 2020
;
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Cui et al., 2024
)
—as well as in missing-not-at-random data scenarios, where shadow variables enable identification
(
Miao and Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2016
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Miao et al., 2024
)
. We review three examples next. Inference for general estimads
\theta(P_{0})
, under weak assumptions on
h_{0,{P_{0}}}
and
g_{0,{P_{0}}}
, has been the focus of extensive recent work
(
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Bennett et al., 2022
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
;
Bennett et al., 2023b
)
. The general approach adopted in these papers, often referred to as debiased machine learning (DML), is reviewed in the next section.

Examples

In this section, we provide several examples of parameters satisfying the representation (
1
).

Example 1

(Proximal Inference for Generalized Average Causal Effects)
.

Suppose that
O=(L,A,Z^{\prime},W^{\prime},Y)
, where
A
is a discrete or continuous treatment,
Y
an outcome, and
L
a vector of observed baseline covariates, and
Z^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
are correlates of an unmeasured confounder
U
, referred to as negative control treatments and negative control outcomes, respectively, satisfying
Z^{\prime}\perp Y\mid A,L,U
and
(A,Z^{\prime})\perp W^{\prime}\mid L,U
. For each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, let
Y(a)
denote the potential outcome under treatment level
a
and
Y(\cdot):=\left\{Y(a):a\in\mathcal{A}\right\}
. Let
O_{\text{full}}=(L,A,Z^{\prime},W^{\prime},Y,Y(\cdot))
. Let
P_{0}
and
P_{\text{full},0}
denote the unknown distributions of the factual random vector
O
and the factual/counterfactual vector
O_{\text{full}}
. Suppose the goal is to estimate a parameter
\gamma(P_{\text{full},0}):=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{full},0}}\left\{Y(A)\pi(A,L)\right\}
where
\pi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{L}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a given specified weighting function. In particular, when
A
is binary and
\pi(a,l)=2a-1
,
\gamma(P_{\text{full},0})
is the average treatment effect (ATE), and when
\pi(a|l)=a
,
\gamma(P_{\text{full},0})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{full,0}}\{Y(1)\}
. Under the consistency assumption
Y(A)=Y
and additional regularity conditions which essentially ensure that
Z^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
are strong proxies of the unmeasured confounder
U
, it can be shown (see
Miao et al., 2018
;
Tchetgen et al., 2020
;
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Cui et al., 2024
) that
\gamma(P_{\text{full},0})
is identified and equal to
\theta(P_{0})
of the form (
1
), with
W=(L,A,W^{\prime})
,
Z=(L,A,Z^{\prime})

\tilde{m}(o;h)=\pi(a,l)\cdot h(w)

denotes the conditional density or probability mass function of

(L,W^{\prime})

\rho_{P_{0}}
does not have structural zeros,

is binary and
\pi(a,l)=a

\tilde{\rho}_{P_{0}}(w)=0

Example 2

(Proximal Inference for Policy Shift Interventions)
.

Suppose that under the same assumptions as in Example
1
, with

\gamma(P_{\text{full},0}):=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{full},0}}\left[Y\{q(A)\}\right]

(Missing Not At Random Data with Shadow Variables)

Suppose that

3
Debiased Estimation of
\theta(P_{0})

In this section we review the general debiased-machine learning estimation strategy for estimating parameters of the form
\theta(P_{0})
as defined in (
1
).
It is well known that if an estimator
\hat{h}
is obtained using flexible statistical methods, the plug-in estimator
\hat{\Psi}(\hat{h}):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m(O_{i},\hat{h})
does not generally achieve
\sqrt{n}
–rate convergence to
\theta(P_{0})
even when
\hat{h}
converges in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{0,W})
to a solution
h_{0,P_{0}}
. The main reason is that the dependence of

\Psi_{P_{0}}(\hat{h})-\Psi_{P_{0}}(h_{0,P_{0}})=\int(\hat{h}-h_{0,P_{0}})(w)\>\tilde{\rho}_{P_{0}}(w)\>dP_{0}(o)

on
\hat{h}-h_{0,P_{0}}
causes the plug-in estimator to inherit the bias of
\hat{h}
. Writing

\displaystyle\int(\hat{h}-h_{0,P_{0}})(w)\>\tilde{\rho}_{P_{0}}(w)\>dP_{0}(o)

\displaystyle=\int\hat{h}(w)\tilde{\rho}_{P_{0}}(w)dP_{0}(o)-\int m(o,g_{0,P_{0}})dP_{0}(o)

\displaystyle=\int r(o)\hat{h}(w)g_{0,P_{0}}(z)dP(o)-\int m(o,g_{0,P_{0}})dP_{0}(o)

suggests estimating the bias of the plug-in estimator with

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{r(O_{i})\hat{h}(W_{i})\hat{g}(Z_{i})-m(O_{i},\hat{g})\}
,
where
\hat{g}
is an estimator converging to a solution
g_{0,P}
constructed using flexible statistical methods. Subtracting this estimated bias from the plug-in estimator, yields the bias-corrected estimator of
\theta(P_{0})
given by
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(O_{i};\hat{h},\hat{g})
, where for any
h
and
g
,

\phi(O;h,g):=\tilde{m}(O;h)+m(O;g)-r(O)h(W)g(Z).

The map
o\mapsto\phi(o;h_{P_{0}},g_{P_{0}})-\theta(P_{0})
corresponds to an influence function at
P_{0}
of the functional
P\mapsto\theta(P)
in model
\mathcal{P}
(see, e.g.,
Kennedy, 2024
and references therein).

The preceding debiased estimator may still fail to be asymptotically linear when flexible machine learning methods are employed to compute
\hat{h}
and
\hat{g}
due to overfitting. A popular approach to addressing this issue is to integrate debiasing with a technique called cross-fitting. Specifically, the sample is partitioned into
K
roughly equal sized subsamples, the data in all but one sample, say the
k^{th}
sample, is used to compute the estimators of the nuisance functions, denoted as
\hat{h}^{(-k)}
and
\hat{g}^{(-k)}
, and the estimator of
\theta(P_{0})
is computed as

\hat{\theta}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\>\sum_{i\>\text{in sample}\>k}\phi(O_{i};\hat{h}^{(-k)},\hat{g}^{(-k)}).

This estimator belongs to the class of DML estimators
(
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
)
, and it is also commonly described as an influence-function-based one-step estimator with cross-fitting
(
Robins et al., 2008
)
.

The following theorem establishes the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{\theta}
, a result that is now standard in the literature on semiparametric and DML estimation, and whose proof we consequently omit (see
Robins et al., 2008
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
and
Kennedy, 2024
).

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
h_{0,P_{0}}
and
g_{0,P_{0}}
solve equations (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, at
P=P_{0}
. Suppose that
\int\{\phi(o;\hat{h}^{(-k)},\hat{g}^{(-k)})-\phi(o;h_{0,P_{0}},g_{0,P_{0}})\}^{2}dP_{0}(o)=o_{P}(1)
holds for each
k\in\{1,...,K\}
. Then,
\sqrt{n}\{\hat{\theta}-\theta(P_{0})\}=\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\phi(O;h_{0,P_{0}},g_{0,P_{0}})-\theta(P_{0})\right]+\sqrt{n}\>R_{n}+o_{P}(1)
, where

R_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\>\int r(o)\left\{\hat{h}^{(-k)}(w)-h_{0,P_{0}}(w)\right\}\left\{g_{0,P_{0}}(z)-\hat{g}^{(-k)}(z)\right\}dP_{0}(o),

with
n_{k}
denoting the size of the
k^{th}
subsample. In particular, if
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\{\phi(O;h_{0,P_{0}},g_{0,P_{0}})^{2}\}<\infty
and
R_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, then
\sqrt{n}\{\hat{\theta}-\theta(P_{0})\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\tau_{0}^{2}),

where
\tau_{0}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[\{\phi(O;h_{0,P_{0}},g_{0,P_{0}})-\theta(P_{0})\}^{2}]
.

Theorem
1
highlights the importance of constructing estimators
\hat{h}^{(-k)}
and
\hat{g}^{(-k)}
that converge sufficiently quickly to solutions
h_{0,P_{0}}
and
g_{0,P_{0}}
of the integral equations (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, in order to ensure that
R_{n}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. As noted in
Bennett et al., 2022
and
Chernozhukov et al., 2023
, a sufficient condition on the convergence rates of the nuisance estimators to ensure that
R_{n}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
can be derived from the following derivation:

\displaystyle|R_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\>\Bigg|\int\Bigg[\int r(o)\left\{\hat{h}^{(-k)}(w)-h_{0,P_{0}}(w)\right\}dP_{0}(o|z)\Bigg]\left\{g_{0,P_{0}}(z)-\hat{g}^{(-k)}(z)\right\}dP_{0}(z)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\left\|\mathcal{T}_{P_{0}}(\hat{h}^{(-k)}-h_{0,P_{0}})\right\|_{2}\left\|\hat{g}^{(-k)}-g_{0,P_{0}}\right\|_{2}

where the inequality follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. A symmetric bound can be obtained by integrating first with respect to
dP_{0}(o|w)
. Combining both bounds, yields:

|R_{n}|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\min\{\|\mathcal{T}_{P_{0}}(\hat{h}^{(-k)}-h_{0,P_{0}})\|_{2}\|\hat{g}^{(-k)}-g_{0,P_{0}}\|_{2},\|\mathcal{T}^{*}_{P_{0}}(\hat{g}^{(-k)}-g_{0,P_{0}})\|_{2}\|\hat{h}^{(-k)}-h_{0,P_{0}}\|_{2}\}

Thus, we upper bound
R_{n}
with the minimum of two products: the weak error of one estimator times the RMSE of the other–by weak error, say of estimator
\hat{h}^{(k)}
, we mean
\|\mathcal{T}_{P_{0}}(\hat{h}^{(k)}-h_{0,P_{0}})\|_{2}
. We can also upper bound
R_{n}
using the looser inequality:

|R_{n}|\leq\|r\|_{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\left\|\hat{g}^{(-k)}-g_{0,P_{0}}\right\|_{2}\left\|\hat{h}^{(-k)}-h_{0,P_{0}}\right\|_{2}

This later bound is less sharp than the former, since weak errors tend to be substantially smaller than RMSEs. This observation highlights a limitation of the existing analyses of KMMR estimators because they provide only bounds for RMSE: relying exclusively on RMSE bounds can lead to overly stringent smoothness conditions when establishing
\sqrt{n}
–consistency of the DML estimator of
\theta(P_{0})
.

4
Adversarial Estimation of the Nuisance Functions

For the remainder of the paper, we focus on estimating the nuisance function that solves equation (
2
). The same analysis applies to equation (
3
) after an appropriate redefinition of the relevant objects. Furthermore, to simplify notation, throughout we will omit the subscript
P
from the operator
\mathcal{T}_{P}
, the solution
h_{0,P}
, the Riesz representer
\rho_{P}
, and all other operators and quantities, e.g. expectations, defined throughout that depend on
P
.

Estimating solutions to integral equations is inherently challenging due to their ill-posed nature: small perturbations in the input function
\rho
can lead to large deviations in the solution. A naive way to circumvent this instability is to restrict attention to a sufficiently small subspace
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
in which the inverse problem becomes well-posed. When
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, the resulting estimator is computationally tractable, but the approach is theoretically unsatisfying as it relies on the strong assumption that a solution lies in the small subspace
\mathcal{H}
.

A broadly applicable alternative strategy is to regularize the estimation problem. This can be done by either allowing the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}
to grow with the sample size, as in sieve-based methods, or by penalizing the complexity of functions in an infinite-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}
, such as an RKHS. Both strategies yield estimators that approximate the solution of the original ill-posed problem as the level of regularization is gradually reduced
(
Carrasco et al., 2007
;
Cavalier, 2011
)
. From a unified perspective, several regularization approaches can be viewed as targeting solutions to surrogate regularized optimization problems of the form

\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\|\rho-\mathcal{T}I_{0}h\|^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h-h^{\blacklozenge}\|^{2}\right\},

(4)

where
h^{\blacklozenge}
is a fixed reference function, typically set to zero. These estimators differ in their choice of function class
\mathcal{H}
(typically a subset of a normed space), in whether they include a penalization term (i.e., whether
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}
is positive), and in the choice of norms used for both the loss term
\|\rho-\mathcal{T}I_{0}h\|^{2}
and the regularization term
\|h-h^{\blacklozenge}\|^{2}
.

When
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}=0
, a common choice is to take
\mathcal{H}
as a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by the first
p
elements of an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
and to use the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
–norm for the loss. In this setting, regularization is relaxed by allowing the dimension
p
to grow with the sample size. Estimators constructed in this way are referred to as sieve-based and include methods proposed in
Newey and Powell, 2003
and
Ai and Chen, 2003
;
Ai and Chen, 2007
. In contrast, when
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\neq 0
, the approach is referred to as Tikhonov regularization. An influential earlier example of this approach is the work of
Chen and Pouzo, 2012
, who consider nonparametric conditional moment models and apply Tikhonov regularization by penalizing the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
–norm of the solution in a sieve space.

In more recent proposals,
\mathcal{H}
is typically taken to be an infinite-dimensional function class, often restricted via a norm constraint, or a very high-dimensional normed space, like shape constrained functions, neural networks (NN), RKHSs, and random forests. The penalty parameter
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}
is strictly positive and regularization is relaxed by letting it decrease to
0
. For instance,
Dikkala et al., 2020
allow
\mathcal{H}
to be any of the aforementioned spaces;
Ghassami et al., 2022
focus on RKHSs; and
Bennett et al., 2022
consider both RKHSs and NNs. In all of these papers the loss is measured with the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
–norm, while the penalty is defined via the norm of the function space
\mathcal{H}
with
h^{\blacklozenge}=0
. By contrast, other recent approaches use the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
–norm for both the loss and the penalty terms. This includes
Liao et al., 2020
, who choose
\mathcal{H}
as NNs; and
Bennett et al., 2023b
, who consider norm-constrained high-dimensional linear models, RKHSs, and NNs.
The latter also considers an iterated Tikhonov approach, starting with an initial estimator targeting the solution
h_{\lambda}^{(0)}
to the regularized problem with
h^{\blacklozenge}=0
. At each subsequent step, the estimator targets the solution
h_{\lambda}^{(k)}
to the same problem but with
h^{\blacklozenge}
set to the previous iterate
h_{\lambda}^{(k-1)}
. This process is repeated for a fixed number of iterations or an adaptively chosen stopping point.

In the Maximal Moment Restriction (MMR) proposals of
Muandet et al., 2020a
;
Mastouri et al., 2021
;
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Zhang et al., 2023
; and
Park et al., 2024
, the surrogate problem takes the form:

\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\left[\mathbb{E}\{m(O;g)-h(W)g(Z)\}\right]^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|^{2}.

We discuss the MMR proposals in Section
6.2
.

In the next subsection, we review the class of RAS estimators. To avoid notational burden, we will drop the superscript
(-k)
from
\hat{h}^{(-k)}
and the subscript
k
from
n_{k}
. Furthermore, we will use
\mathbb{E}_{n}
to denote the empirical mean operator calculated with data in the entire sample but the
k^{th}
sample.

4.1
Motivation and Rationale for Regularized Adversarial Stabilized Estimation Strategies

In addition to the statistical advantages discussed in the introduction—most notably, their ability to adaptively focus on directions where the conditional moment restriction is most difficult to satisfy—adversarial estimation strategies offer two key computational benefits. First, they bypass the need to estimate the conditional mean operator
\mathcal{T}
and their convergence does not depend on smoothness assumptions on the conditional distribution of
W
given
Z
. Second, they do not require prior knowledge of the functional form of the Riesz representer
\rho
, which facilitates greater automation of the estimation procedure. Methods that do not require knowledge of the functional form of the Riesz representer are referred to in the literature as auto-debiased machine learning (auto-DML). They were first proposed in the case where
X=W=Z
by
Chernozhukov et al., 2022a
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2022b
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2022c
, and later extended to the setting where
W
and
Z
are distinct subvectors of
O
by
Bennett et al., 2022
;
Bennett et al., 2023b
.

The RAS estimation strategies we introduce next, unlike the alternative MMR class of adversarial estimators studied in
Muandet et al., 2020a
;
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Mastouri et al., 2021
; and
Park et al., 2024
, have the advantage that existing analytical results provide bounds for the weak error. As discussed in Section
3
, this makes it possible to establish root–
n
consistency of the resulting estimator of
\theta(P_{0})
under weaker conditions on the solutions to equations (
2
) and (
3
).

Quite generally, RAS estimators are of the form
\hat{h}(W)=I_{0}(X)\tilde{h}(W)
where

\displaystyle\tilde{h}:=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\{m(O;g)-I_{0}(X)r(O)h(W)g(Z)-c^{2}I_{0}(X)g(Z)^{2}\}-\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\|g\|^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|^{2}]

(5)

for some constant
c\neq 0
and,
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
specified function classes
(
Muandet et al., 2020b
;
Dikkala et al., 2020
;
Liao et al., 2020
;
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Bennett et al., 2022
;
Bennett et al., 2023b
)
. As noted earlier,
Bennett et al., 2023b
also consider iterated Tikhonov estimators with penalties centered at the estimator from the previous iteration.
The aforementioned proposals differ on the choice of norms
\|\cdot\|
for
g
and
h
. Additionally, some proposals set
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}=0
and/or
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}=0
.

To motivate the RAS approach we first observe that for any
g\in L^{2}(P_{Z})
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}[m(O;g)]=\mathbb{E}[\rho(Z)g(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[r(O)h(W)g(Z)]
,
where the first equality is because
\rho
is the Riesz representer, and the second is by
h_{0}
satisfying equation (
2
). In particular, this implies that for any
g\>\in\>\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
, the residual

m(O;g)-I_{0}(X)r(O)g(Z)h(W)

(6)

has mean equal to 0 at
h=h_{0}^{\dagger}
where
h_{0}=I_{0}h_{0}^{\dagger}
is a solution of equation (
2
). Consequently,

Q_{\mathbb{J}}(h):=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbb{J}}\left\{\mathbb{E}[m(O;g_{j})-I_{0}(X)r(O)g_{j}(Z)h(W)]\right\}^{2}

for given functions
g_{j},j=1,\dots,J
where
\mathbb{J}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, attains its minimum at
h_{0}^{\dagger}
. One can then use the empirical version of
Q_{\mathbb{J}}(h)
as the basis for deriving estimators of
h_{0}^{{\dagger}}
–a strategy that underlies the classical GMM approach. The functions
g_{j}
are known in the machine learning literature as
test functions
. The question then is: how many and which test functions do we choose? The following result sheds light into the answer. In what follows, given a subspace
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
, we let
I_{0}\>\mathcal{G}:=\left\{I_{0}\>g\>:\>g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}

and we let
\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
denote its closure in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
\{g_{j}=I_{0}g_{j}^{\dagger}:g_{j}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{G},j\in\mathbb{J}\}
is an
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
–orthonormal basis of
\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})\rightarrow\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
denote the projection operator. Then

Q_{\mathbb{J}}(h)=\|\mathcal{P}_{\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}}\mathcal{T}I_{0}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)\|_{2}^{2}.

To understand the relevance of this result avoiding distracting heavy notation, suppose for the purpose of this discussion that
I_{0}=1
and that the integral equation (
2
) admits a unique solution
h_{0}
lying in a known function class
\mathcal{H}
. Proposition
1
implies that estimators minimizing
Q_{\mathbb{J}}(h)
over
\mathcal{H}
implicitly target solutions to the projected equation

\mathcal{P}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}}}\circ\mathcal{T}\>h=\mathcal{P}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}}}\>\rho,

(7)

where
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
denotes the closure of the linear span of the test functions
\{g_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{J}}
. If
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
is a strict subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
, then any function of the form
h_{0}+h^{\diamond}
, with
\mathcal{T}h^{\diamond}\in\text{Null}(\mathcal{P}_{\overline{\mathcal{G}}})
, also solves (
7
). Consequently, minimizing
Q_{\mathbb{J}}
does not distinguish
h_{0}
from these alternatives.

This ambiguity cannot be resolved by selecting the minimum norm solution of (
7
), as this solution will generally differ from
h_{0}
, the unique solution of the original problem (
2
). To see this, suppose that
W
and
Z
have no overlap (i.e.,
X
is null), in which case
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
is compact. Let
(\mu_{i},\varphi_{i},\psi_{i})
for
i=1,2,\dots
denote the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{T}
. Since, by assumption, equation (
2
) has a solution, then the Riesz representer
\rho
lies in the range of
\mathcal{T}
, and can therefore be written as

\rho=\sum_{i:\mu_{i}\neq 0}\langle\rho,\psi_{i}\rangle_{2}\>\psi_{i}
.
The minimum-norm solution to the projected equation (
7
) is then
h_{0}^{\text{proj}}=\sum_{i:\>\mu_{i}\neq 0\>,\>\psi_{i}\in\overline{\mathcal{G}}}\>\mu_{i}^{-1}\>{\langle\rho,\psi_{i}\rangle_{2}}\>\varphi_{i}
,
while the solution of the equation of interest (
2
) is
h_{0}=\sum_{i:\mu_{i}\neq 0}\>\mu_{i}^{-1}\>{\langle\rho,\psi_{i}\rangle_{2}}\>\varphi_{i}
.
If
\rho\notin\overline{\mathcal{G}}
, then there exists some
i
for which
\langle\rho,\psi_{i}\rangle_{2}\neq 0
and
\psi_{i}\notin\overline{\mathcal{G}}
, meaning that
h_{0}^{\text{proj}}
omits the corresponding direction
\varphi_{i}
. In the machine learning literature, this is referred to as
misspecification of the test class

\mathcal{G}
. In particular, if
\rho
has nonzero projection onto infinitely many directions
\psi_{i}
, then any finite-dimensional test class
\mathcal{G}
will necessarily be misspecified. This is the case in sieve-based GMM estimation, where the dimension of
\mathcal{G}
increases with sample size, and the test functions
\{g_{j}\}
are chosen to minimize approximation error. However, for any fixed sample size, the sieve space cannot capture all directions—especially the high-frequency components of
\rho
—and as a result, the corresponding estimator will fail to recover those components of the true solution
h_{0}
.

To avoid misspecification, the test class
\mathcal{G}
must be dense in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
. However, when
Z
has continuous components, this requires indexing the class by the entire natural numbers, i.e.,
\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{N}
, implying that the objective function
Q_{\mathbb{J}}
involves an infinite sum. As a result, the empirical version of this criterion becomes computationally infeasible, since one cannot evaluate or minimize an infinite sum in practice. Remarkably, when
\mathcal{T}(h_{0}-h)
lies in
\mathcal{G}
there exists an equivalent reformulation that avoids direct evaluation of the infinite sum. Specifically, let
c
be any non-zero constant and define

M_{P}(h):=4c^{2}\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{m(O;g)-I_{0}(X)r(O)h(W)g(Z)-c^{2}I_{0}(X)g(Z)^{2}\right\}.

Proposition 2
.

For any constant
c\neq 0
, any
h_{0}^{\dagger}
such that
h_{0}=I_{0}h_{0}^{\dagger}
is a solution of equation (
2
), and any
h\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
, it holds that:

1.

If
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
such that
(2c^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{T}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)\in\mathcal{G}
then
M_{P}(h)=\|I_{0}\mathcal{T}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)\|_{2}^{2}.

2.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
. Let
I_{0}\mathcal{G}:=\left\{I_{0}g\>:\>g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
and let
\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
denote its closure in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
. Let
\{g_{j}=I_{0}g_{j}^{\dagger}:g_{j}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{G},\>j\in\mathbb{J}\}
denote an
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
–orthonormal basis of
\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
where
\mathbb{J}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. If
\mathcal{P}_{\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}}\circ\mathcal{T}_{P}I_{0}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)\in\ I_{0}\>\mathcal{G}
, then
Q_{\mathbb{J}}(h)=M_{P}(h)
.

Proposition
2
is ultimately a consequence of the Fenchel conjugate formulation of the square function
(
Borwein and Lewis, 2006
)
. Part 1 of this proposition has been shown by
Dikkala et al., 2020
and
Kallus et al., 2021
. Part 2 of the proposition has been established in the finite-dimensional setting by
Bennett et al., 2019
and
Muandet et al., 2020b
, for the specific case
m(O;g)=Yg(Z)
, with
Y
and
Z
denoting distinct components of
O
. To the best of our knowledge, the extension of this result to infinite-dimensional function classes has not yet been reported in the literature. Proposition
2
has the following key implication.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
and let
c\neq 0
be a constant. If there exists
h_{0}^{\dagger}
such that
h_{0}=I_{0}h_{0}^{\dagger}
solves equation (
2
), and for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
it holds that
(2c^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{T}_{P}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)\in\mathcal{G}
, then

\displaystyle\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\|\rho-\mathcal{T}I_{0}h\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big[\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left\{m(O;g)-I_{0}(X)r(O)h(W)g(Z)-c^{2}I_{0}(X)g(Z)^{2}\right\}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|^{2}\big].

It is now clear from Corollary
1
that when
\mathcal{G}
is a linear space, under the
closeness
condition

\mathcal{T}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)\in\mathcal{G}\quad\text{holds for every}\>h\in\mathcal{H},

(8)

the RAS estimators (
5
) with
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}=0
are estimating solutions of surrogate problems of the form (
4
) that employ the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
–norm for the loss term.
If
\mathcal{H}
is a normed space and it is known than
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}<B
for some constant
B
, then the outer minimization can be restricted to functions in
\mathcal{H}
with norm at most
B
. In this case, the closeness condition (
8
) only needs to hold over that restricted subset.

On the other hand, when the closeness condition (
8
) does not hold, part 2 of Proposition
2
tells us that the RAS estimators can still be interpreted as regularized GMM estimators over a class of test functions that are dense in the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
–closure of the class
I_{0}\>\mathcal{G}
. The requirement that the projection of
\mathcal{T}_{P}I_{0}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)
onto
\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
lies in
I_{0}\mathcal{G}
is equivalent to requiring that the maximum over
\mathcal{G}
of the expected stabilized residuals exists. Existing finite-sample error bounds for RAS estimators have been established only under the stronger closeness condition (
8
), but the preceding discussion on test-class misspecification suggests a potential relaxation: if one is willing to assume that
\rho\in\mathcal{G}
, then it may be possible to derive analogous bounds under the weaker condition that the projection of
\mathcal{T}I_{0}(h_{0}^{\dagger}-h)
onto
\overline{I_{0}\mathcal{G}}
belongs to
I_{0}\mathcal{G}
.

KRAS estimators
\tilde{h}
of the form (
5
) with
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}>0
,
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}>0
,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
taken as RKHSs, and penalization based on the corresponding RKHS norms were first studied by
Dikkala et al., 2020
in the special case
m(o;g)=y\cdot g(z)
, and by
Ghassami et al., 2022
in the more general setting
m(o;g)=s(o)g(z)
, where
s(\cdot)
is a known function satisfying
\|s\|_{\infty}<\infty
. In both works, the set
\mathcal{X}_{0}
was taken to be the entire domain
\mathcal{X}
, equivalently assuming
I_{0}\equiv 1
. Choosing
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
as RKHSs and penalizing via their respective RKHS norms is attractive because it yields computationally stable estimators that admit a closed-form expression. In contrast, omitting the
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
penalty or replacing RKHS norms with empirical
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
norms for the penalization of
h
may still result in closed-form estimators, but their computation typically involves inversion of nearly ill-posed
n\times n
matrices. Including the
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
penalization and using RKHS norms for penalizing
h
leads instead to inversion of well-posed matrices, thus stabilizing the computation. See
Dikkala et al., 2020
and
Ghassami et al., 2022
for the closed form expression of KRAS estimators.

Dikkala et al., 2020
and
Ghassami et al., 2022
provided a finite-sample analysis of KRAS estimators. Their analyses begin by showing that, under suitable regularity conditions and with appropriately chosen tuning parameters
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}
, the weak error
\|\mathcal{T}(\hat{h}-h_{0})\|_{2}
is of order
O(\delta_{n})
, where
\delta_{n}
is an upper bound on the critical radius of the classes
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
, and
h_{0}
is any solution to equation (
2
). They then assume that the solution
h_{0}
to equation (
2
) is unique and derive a bound on the RMSE of the form

\|\hat{h}-h_{0}\|_{2}\leq\tau(\delta_{n}),

(9)

where, for any
\delta>0
,

\tau(\delta):=\sup\left\{\|h\|_{2}:h\in\mathcal{H},\ \|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 2B,\ \|\mathcal{T}h\|_{2}\leq\delta\right\}

with
B
being a universal constant such that
\|\hat{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
and
\|h_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
. Note that
\tau(\delta)
is a measure of the ill-posedness of equation (
2
) that depends both on the data generating process and on
\mathcal{H}
.

Unfortunately, if there exists a nonzero function
h^{(0)}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{T})\cap\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{T})
denotes the null set of the conditional mean operator
\mathcal{T}
, then the bound (
9
) becomes vacuous; that is,
\tau(\delta_{n})>C>0
for some universal constant
C
. Indeed,

h^{(0)^{*}}:=B\>{h^{(0)}}/{\|h^{(0)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}}

satisfies
\mathcal{T}h^{(0)^{*}}=0
and
\|h^{(0)^{*}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=B<2B
, implying that
\tau(\delta)\geq\|h^{(0)^{*}}\|_{2}=:C
for all
\delta>0
. Similar comments were made by
Kallus et al., 2021
and
Bennett et al., 2023b
.

Even under the condition
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{T})\cap\mathcal{H}={0}
, there is no known general methodology for expressing or bounding
\tau(\delta)
as a function of
\delta
.
Dikkala et al., 2020
obtained such a bound in a particular setting, relying on a strong structural assumption on the data-generating process that, in effect, requires
Z
to be a strong predictor of
W
. While this result illustrates that bounding
\tau(\delta)
is possible in certain special cases, to our knowledge no general approach is currently available.

In contrast, more satisfactory finite-sample analyses exist for the computationally unstable RAS estimators that employ
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
as RKHSs with
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
penalties
(
Liao et al., 2020
;
Bennett et al., 2023b
)
. These analyses adopt a different notion of ill-posedness that does not require uniqueness of the solution to the integral equation, focusing instead on the minimum
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
–norm solution satisfying the so-called
\beta
–source condition
.

To review the definition of the
\beta
–source condition and its implication for controlling the regularization bias in complete generality, consider an arbitrary bounded linear operator

\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{E}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}
,
where
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are Hilbert spaces, and suppose we aim to solve the equation

Let

–source condition imposed on the solution with minimal

–norm. Crucially, we do not assume
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{T})\cap\mathcal{H}={0}
, nor do we rely

In Section
6
we argue that while adversarial stabilized estimators with

penalties can achieve faster convergence rates under comparable smoothness assumptions, these rates rely on a strong condition that KRAS does not require—and may fail entirely when that condition is violated. 6
Contrasting Kernel based Adversarial Estimation Strategies

In this section we investigate the advantages and limitations of the three prominent recently proposed kernel-based adversarial approaches:
(i) LRAS estimators, i.e. kernel adversarial stabilized estimators with
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
penalization;
(ii) KRAS estimators, i.e. kernel adversarial stabilized estimators with RKHS penalization; and
(iii) the kernel maximal moment restriction (KMMR) estimators studied in
Muandet et al., 2020a
;
Mastouri et al., 2021
;
Kallus et al., 2021
; and
Zhang et al., 2023
.
We compare these methods in terms of the assumptions they impose, the scope and sharpness of their available finite-sample error bounds, and—most importantly—the nature of the inverse problems they implicitly solve. This perspective brings into focus the trade-offs between statistical convergence rates, computational stability, and the degree of ill-posedness each method must confront.
To keep the notation light, we assume throughout this section that
I_{0}=1
.

6.1
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
-penalized Kernel Regularized Adversarial Stabilized Estimators

An LRAS estimator using RKHS classes
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{LRAS}:=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{(D)}}\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\Big[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{m(O;g)-r(O)h(W)g(Z)-r(X)g(Z)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{g(Z)^{2}\right\}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left\{h(W)^{2}\right\}\Big],

where
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}:=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\|h\|\leq D\}
for some constant

and some norm
\|\cdot\|
, and where we set

for simplicity.

In their description of LRAS estimators for arbitrary classes
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
,
Bennett et al., 2023b
state that the argmin is taken over a “norm-constrained” class, but do not specify which norm defines the constraint. At the same time, their main theorems establishing error bounds for LRAS estimators require the function class to be uniformly bounded, i.e., that there exists
B^{\prime}
such that
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{(D)}}\|h\|\infty<B^{\prime}
.

When, as we shall assume here,
\mathcal{H}
is an RKHS, this uniform boundedness is guaranteed if the norm used in the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
is either the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
or the RKHS norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
. By contrast, uniform boundedness is not ensured if
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
is defined using the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
or
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{n})
norm. For this reason, in what follows we assume that the norm in
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
is either
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
or
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.

When
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}
is negligible in relation to
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}
, and the closeness condition (
8
) holds, the LRAS estimator aims at the solution to the surrogate regularized problem:

\displaystyle h_{\lambda_{\mathcal{H}},LRAS}:=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H_{(D)}}}\|\mathcal{T}h-\rho\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|_{2}^{2}.

However, as indicated in our discussion in Section
4.1
, the argmin over
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
need not exist. Note that if the norm defining
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
is the

norm, we cannot ensure that

exists even if a solution

with minimal
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
–norm or a solution with minimal RKHS–norm falls in
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
. When the norm in the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{(D)}
is the sup norm, then it is even harder to justify the existence of
h_{\lambda_{\mathcal{H}},LRAS}
since
\mathcal{H}
is not a Hilbert space when endowed with the sup norm.

Bennett et al., 2023b
derived bounds for
\|\tilde{h}_{LRAS}-h_{0,LRAS}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\mathcal{T}(\tilde{h}_{LRAS}-h_{0,LRAS})\|_{2}^{2}

under the critical assumption:

(22)

Under this assumption,
h_{\lambda_{\mathcal{H}},LRAS}
exists and is equal to
\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})}\|\mathcal{T}h-\rho\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|_{2}^{2}

2

(23)

Unlike for KRAS estimators, establishing bounds for LRAS does not require the realizability condition in Assumption
1
that posits the existence of a solution to equation (
2

\mathcal{H}
. However, bounds for LRAS estimators rely critically on the validity of condition (

\mathcal{H}
—only on the true solution of the integral equation itself. In contrast, the analysis of LRAS estimators demands this smoothness property to hold for the solutions of infinitely many surrogate regularized problems, making it a less natural and generally more stringent condition.

The
\beta
–source conditions (
16
) or (
18
) assumed for KRAS estimators also differ from the source condition (
23

\mathcal{H}
, whereas in the LRAS case, it is the minimum-norm solution in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
in the KRAS case, and
\mathcal{T}
in the LRAS case. For the same value of

in the definition of

We note that the finite-sample bounds for estimators that use optimal tuning parameters in

have the same functional form as those in Corollary

\beta
in Assumption (

in Assumption (

•

Bennett et al., 2023b

4

•

Bennett et al., 2023b
also analyzed an adaptive version of the iterated Tikhonov LRAS estimator, deriving weak and strong error bounds with exponents

We conjecture that an analogous result holds for adaptive iterated KRAS estimators. We note that while adaptive procedures represent a notable theoretical advance, their practical implementation introduces additional computational challenges that have yet to be fully examined.

6.2
Kernel Adversarial Maximal Moment Restriction Estimators

Like regularized adversarial stabilized estimation strategies, maximal moment restriction methods also leverage the fact that the residual (
6

g
in a given function class
g\in\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})

A kernel maximal moment estimator (KMMR) estimator (

\hat{h}_{KMMR}:=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}:\|g\|_{\mathcal{G}}\leq 1}\left[\mathbb{E}_{n}\{m(O;g)-h(W)g(Z)\}\right]^{2}+\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

\mathcal{H}
and

and

and

Park et al., 2024
adopts a similar strategy to construct solutions to integral equations needed for estimation of target parameters that fall outside the class defined in (
1

To facilitate the comparison with KRAS estimators we will re-express the surrogate problem that the KMMR estimator targets in terms of the weak error relative to some operator. To do so, we start by noting that an immediate generalization of the results in
Zhang et al., 2023

\displaystyle\max_{g\in\mathcal{G},\|g\|_{\mathcal{G}}\leq 1}\left[\mathbb{E}\{m(O;g)-h(W)g(Z)\}\right]^{2}=\|T_{\mathcal{G}}\left(\rho-\mathcal{T}h\right)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{G}}

As in the KRAS case, Assumption (

of the solution of the transformed equation (

Let us now compare the advantages and limitations of the KRAS and KMMR estimators.

•

KRAS estimators implicitly require equation (
17
) to admit a solution in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{W})
, whereas KMMR estimators require a solution to equation (
27
). The former condition implies the latter, but not conversely—unless the integral operator
T_{\mathcal{G}}
is injective. Injectivity holds when the kernel
K_{\mathcal{G}}
is Matérn, but not necessarily otherwise.

•

Unlike KMMR estimators, deriving bounds for KRAS estimators requires Assumption
5
on closeness and boundedness, which, as noted earlier, can be hard to justify.

•

Even when
T_{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
is injective, so that equations (
17
) and (
27
) share the same solutions, the formulation (
27
) poses a more ill-posed problem. This is because
T_{\mathcal{G}}^{1/2}:\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
is compact when
\mathcal{G}
is endowed with the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
–norm. Thus,
T_{\mathcal{G}}^{1/2}
in equation (
27
) applies additional smoothing beyond
\mathcal{T}\circ T_{\mathcal{H}}^{1/2}
. Intuitively, solving a more ill-posed problem to a given level of accuracy demands stronger smoothness assumptions on the true solution. This added difficulty is reflected in the source condition imposed on the minimal
\mathcal{H}
–norm solution: for the same value of
\beta
, the source condition condition (
31
) (condition (
28
)) entails stronger smoothness than the source condition in Assumption
4
(Assumption
16
), because the former is formulated in terms of the fractional power of an operator that smooths more strongly than the operator appearing in the latter.

•

To the best of our knowledge, the sharpest available bounds for the RMSE of
\hat{h}_{KMMR}
under Assumption (
28
) were obtained by
Park et al., 2024
, refining the earlier analysis of
Mastouri et al., 2021
. Specifically,
Park et al., 2024
showed that, with an appropriately chosen
\lambda_{\mathcal{H}}
,

\left\|\hat{h}_{KMMR}-h_{0,KMMR}\right\|_{2}=O_{p}\left(n^{-\frac{\min\{\beta,2\}}{2(1+\min\{\beta,2\})}}\right).

At present, no results provide sharper bounds for the weak error
\|\mathcal{T}(\hat{h}_{KMMR}-h_{0,KMMR})\|_{2}
. This may be due to the fact that applying Theorem
2
yields bounds on the weak regularization error with respect to the transformed operator
T_{\mathcal{G}}\circ\mathcal{T}
(as in the second inequality in (
30
) rather than
\mathcal{T}
itself). The lack of a sharper bound on the weak error has the following consequence. Suppose we estimate the solutions to equations (
2
) and (
3
) using KMMR estimators, and that the minimal RKHS-norm solutions satisfy the
\beta
–source condition (
28
) or (
29
) with parameters
\beta_{h}^{\text{KMMR}}
and
\beta_{g}^{\text{KMMR}}
respectively. Let
\hat{\theta}_{KMMR}
be the resulting DML estimator of
\theta_{0}
. Using the existing finite-sample bounds we can establish that
R_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
if
\min\{\beta_{h}^{KMMR},2\}/(1+\min\{\beta_{h}^{KMMR},2\}+\min\{\beta_{g}^{KMMR},2\}/(1+\min\{\beta_{g}^{KMMR},2\})>1
, Thus, we cannot ensure that
R_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
and thus, that
\hat{\theta}_{KMMR}
is
\sqrt{n}
–consistent when, for instance,
\beta^{KMMR}=1
where
\beta^{KMMR}:=\max\left\{\beta_{h}^{KMMR},\beta_{g}^{KMMR}\right\}
.

In contrast, suppose that to estimate the solutions of equations (
2
) and (
3
) we use KRAS estimators with the RKHSs in the inner maximization for the two estimation problems having the same critical radius
\delta_{n}
. Suppose that the minimum RKHS norm solutions satisfy the
\beta
–source condition of Assumption
16
or Assumption
4
with parameters
\beta_{h}^{\text{KRAS}}
and
\beta_{g}^{\text{KRAS}}
. Let
\hat{\theta}_{KRAS}
be the DML estimator of
\theta_{0}
using the optimal choice of tuning parameters as given in Corollary
2
. Then, using the bounds in that corollary we can establish that
R_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
if

\delta_{n}^{1+\frac{\min\left\{\beta^{KRAS},1\right\}}{1+\min\left\{\beta^{KRAS},1\right\}}}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})

(32)

where
\beta^{KRAS}:=\max\{\beta_{h}^{KRAS},\beta_{g}^{KRAS}\}
. As noted in Section
5
, when the inner maximization RKHSs are Gaussian, condition (
32
) is always satisfied because
\delta_{n}=O(\sqrt{\left\{log(n)/n\right\}})
. When these are
d
-dimensional RKHSs with Sobolev kernels of order
\nu=(l+d)/2
where
l>d
is a positive integer, then
\delta_{n}=O(n^{-\nu/(2\nu+d)})
, which yields
R_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
whenever
\beta^{KRAS}\in(d/l,1]
.

A rigorous comparison of the smoothness requirements under which the remainder
R_{n}
is
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
for
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KMMR}}
and
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KRAS}}
is not possible for the following reasons:

1.

Qualitatively, we expect that a given
\beta^{\mathrm{KRAS}}
corresponds to a smaller
\beta^{\mathrm{KMMR}}
for roughly the same smoothness of the minimal
\mathcal{H}
–norm solution, though the exact correspondence between the two parameters remains unknown.

2.

The absence of sharp bounds on the weak error of the KMMR nuisance parameter estimators means that current error analysis for
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KMMR}}
relies on bounding the product of their RMSEs. In contrast, the analysis for
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KRAS}}
relies on bounds on the minimum of two terms, each being the product of the weak error of one nuisance estimator and the RMSE of the other. Since weak errors are typically much smaller than RMSEs, yet-unavailable sharper bounds on the weak error of the KMMR nuisance estimators could potentially lead to a substantially different error characterization for
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KMMR}}
.

3.

Beyond the lack of weak error bounds, it also remains an open question whether existing RMSE bounds for KMMR estimators can be improved. This is because, the current RMSE bounds, derived by
Mastouri et al., 2021
and
Park et al., 2024
, were obtained via an application of the Bennett–Bernstein inequality in Hilbert spaces (Theorem 6.14 of
Steinwart and Christmann, 2008
), which provides a global bound that does not account for variation in the complexity of the RKHS induced by different kernels. In contrast, following the approach of
Bennett et al., 2023b
, we instead leveraged the critical radius—a localized measure of complexity—which enabled bounds that adapt to the effective size of the RKHS. This raises the question of whether a similarly localized analysis could yield sharper convergence rates for the RMSE of KMMR estimators.

4.

All that said, we conjecture that no refinement of the current analysis will show that
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KMMR}}
estimators outperform
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{KRAS}}
estimators in terms of rates of convergence of the remainder
R_{n}
, as the fundamental issue remains: KMMR estimators target the solution of a more severely ill-posed problem, inherently limiting their precision relative to KRAS estimators.

7
Discussion

We have introduced a new theoretical framework for analyzing KRAS estimators arising from an auto-DML scheme. Our analysis provides finite-sample bounds on both their weak error and RMSE, under source conditions. A key strength of this framework is that it avoids reliance on hard-to-quantify measures of ill-posedness and does not require the solution to be unique.
Several important directions for future research remain open.

First, our analysis has focused on single-step Tikhonov regularization. As indicated in Section
6.1
, we conjecture that under our source conditions it will be possible to derive results for (adaptive and non-adaptive) iterated Tikhonov KRAS estimators, along the lines of
Bennett et al., 2023b
.

Second, our current analysis of KRAS estimators—and of all RAS estimators for that matter—relies on the closeness and boundedness condition in Assumption
5
. It remains an open question whether this Assumption can be relaxed, perhaps by exploiting part 2 of Proposition
2
.

Third, to our knowledge, the minimax optimality of LRAS estimators under source conditions has not yet been established. Likewise, it would be important to investigate if KRAS estimators are minimax optimal under our proposed source conditions.

Finally, while RAS estimators have gained popularity in recent years, the literature offers little guidance on how to tune their hyperparameters in practice. When hyperparameter selection strategies are discussed, they are often heuristic and lack theoretical guarantees. Developing principled, data-driven methods for selecting regularization parameters—and establishing their statistical properties—is a critical step toward making these estimators truly practical.

so the map

is an isometry between
(\mathcal{G}_{B_{1}},\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{0}^{q}))
and
(m\circ\mathcal{G}_{B_{1}},\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{0}))

Observe that the Rademacher complexity is preserved under reparameterization: if

,
\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Z}_{n}\sim P_{0}^{q}
, and
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}

,

\delta>0

m\circ\mathcal{G}_{B_{1}}
under
P_{0}
equals the critical radius of
\mathcal{G}_{B_{1}}
under
P_{0}^{q}
. The result follows because the critical radius of an RKHS class is preserved up to multiplicative constants under changes of measure. That

is star shaped follows because
o\mapsto m(o,g)=g(q(z))
is linear in

and
\mathcal{G}_{B_{1}}
is star-shaped.

\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{H}
admits the representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\left\{h=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{j}\varphi_{j}\>:\>\text{ for some }(\gamma_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\text{ with }\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\gamma_{j}^{2}}{\eta_{j}}<\infty\right\},

(35)

where if

\mathcal{L}^{2}(P_{0,W})
-metric.
Additionally, its inner product decomposes as (see Theorem 11.3 of

Assertion (b) follows from the representation of
\mathcal{H}
in (
35

(36)

To show Assertions (d) and (e), we use that

such that

. Then,

, which contradicts the definition of

. We thus conclude that

.

Next, since

is measurable for all
f\in\mathcal{S}
, and the map

is continuous for all

, Lemma A.3.17 of
Steinwart and Christmann, 2008
ensures that the mapping

is measurable on
\mathcal{O}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}
. Then, the map
\underset{\sim}{o}\mapsto\sup_{f\in\mathcal{S}}g(\underset{\sim}{o};f)
is also measurable because

is countable. Hence,
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{S}}g(O_{1},\dots,O_{n};f)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}g(O_{1},\dots,O_{n};f)

is a well-defined random variable. This completes the proof.

\displaystyle=|t_{I_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\cdot f}(o)-t_{I_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\cdot f^{\prime}}(o)|+\mathbb{E}\big\{\big|t_{I_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\cdot f}(O)-t_{I_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\cdot f^{\prime}}(O)\big|\big\}
