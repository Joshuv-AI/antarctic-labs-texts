Comparative analysis of the improvement in shoulder rotation in complex shoulder fractures treated by reverse shoulder arthroplasty compared to open reduction and internal fixation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Adeeba Albadran, Reem Alsugair, Alanood AlHarthi, Hind Alshangiti, Huda Alqahtani, Ghada Alsugair, Reem Ababtain, Danah Alhajress, Enar Alotaibi, Laila Aldokhail, Khaled Alshehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the clinical and functional outcomes of open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) and reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) in patients aged ≥ 65 years with complex proximal humeral fractures (PHFs).MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, EBSCO, and the Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies published in English without date restrictions. Eligible studies compared RSA and ORIF in elderly patients with PHFs and reported functional, radiographic, or complication outcomes. Pooled data were analyzed using a random-effects model.ResultsTwenty-two studies involving > 34,000 patients were included. RSA was associated with greater forward flexion and a trend toward improved abduction, whereas internal rotation favored ORIF without reaching significance. No significant differences were observed in external rotation. The functional scores (Constant-Murley, Oxford Shoulder Score) were similar, and the complication and reoperation rates did not differ significantly between the groups.ConclusionRSA offers modest advantages in forward flexion compared with ORIF but does not consistently improve overall functional scores or reduce complications. RSA may be preferred in elderly patients with poor bone stock or rotator cuff pathology, whereas ORIF remains appropriate for patients in whom joint preservation is feasible. High-quality RCTs with standardized outcome reporting are needed to clarify the optimal surgical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00402-026-06337-w.Keywords : Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), Complex shoulder fractures, Shoulder rotation outcomes, Functional outcomes

Introduction

Proximal humeral fractures (PHFs) are among the most prevalent osteoporotic injuries in the elderly and represent the third most common type of fracture after hip and distal radius fractures in individuals aged > 65 years [1]. With the global demographic shift toward aging populations, the incidence of PHFs is expected to increase significantly in the coming decades, posing challenges not only to patients’ functional independence, but also to healthcare systems worldwide [2, 3]. These fractures are often complex, particularly in older adults who typically present with diminished bone quality, comorbidities, and reduced physiological reserves, rendering surgical decision-making especially nuanced.

Surgical management is often recommended for displaced or unstable PHFs with two widely adopted procedures: open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) and reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA). ORIF has traditionally been the mainstay for restoring anatomical alignment and preserving the native joint. However, its success is highly dependent on bone stock and rotator cuff integrity—both of which are frequently compromised in geriatric populations [4, 5]. In contrast, RSA has emerged as a reliable alternative, offering biomechanical advantages by allowing shoulder function to be restored independent of the rotator cuff, particularly in complex or comminuted fracture patterns [6, 7].

Recently, a growing body of evidence has compared the outcomes of RSA and ORIF in older adults with complex PHFs. Some studies have suggested that RSA may yield superior results in terms of postoperative pain relief, shoulder range of motion, and early functional recovery [8, 9]. However, others argue that ORIF remains a viable option, particularly in cases where tuberosity healing is achievable and joint preservation is feasible [10, 11]. Patient-reported outcomes, such as the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) score and quality of life measures, have become central to evaluating the true success of either intervention from a patient-centered perspective [12].

Despite the increasing number of comparative studies, the evidence remains fragmented, with significant heterogeneity in study design, patient population, follow-up duration, and outcome measures. There is a critical need for a comprehensive synthesis that evaluates both clinical and functional outcomes, such as tuberosity healing, complication rates, and reoperation frequencies, as well as the economic implications and patient-reported quality of life indicators.

Therefore, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aim to rigorously compare RSA and ORIF in patients aged ≥ 65 years with complex proximal humeral fractures. By analyzing data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and high-quality observational studies, we seek to inform clinical decision-making and optimize outcomes in a vulnerable and growing patient population.

Methodology

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in strict accordance with the methodological guidance of the Cochrane Handbook and reported according the reporting rules of PRISMA. We prospectively registered this study protocol on PROSPERO (CRD42024533110).

Literature search strategy

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, a thorough online search was conducted to find relevant literature using five databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Science Direct, EBSCO, and Cochrane Library.

This study was restricted to the English language and customized as needed for each database. The following keywords, which were converted into Mesh terms in PubMed, were used to identify appropriate studies:

“Shoulder fractures,” “reverse shoulder arthroplasty,” “outcomes,” “tuberosity healing,” “shoulder rotation,” “Cohort OR Randomized controlled trials.” The appropriate keywords were paired with “OR” and “AND” Boolean operators.

The search results included English-language articles, full-text publications, freely available articles, and human trials.

Our search strategy involved using a combination of Medical Subject heading terms such as (shoulder fractures) AND (reverse shoulder arthroplasty) AND (outcomes) AND (tuberosity healing) AND (shoulder rotation) AND (cohort OR randomized controlled trials).

Using this approach, we identified all the studies related to RSA and ORIF.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review included clinical studies that reported the number of patients who underwent RSA (total shoulder arthroplasty or hemiarthoplasty). The studies were also required to report the long-term outcomes of RSA for complex shoulder fractures, measuring the success rate, which includes shoulder rotation, tuberosity healing, pain reduction, and quality of life enhancement. Only full-text articles published in English were included in this review, with no limitations on publication dates. The study designs were restricted to RCTs, prospective cohorts, and retrospective cohort trials.

The following criteria were used to exclude studies:

(a) Published in languages other than English; (b) studies with a high risk of bias or low quality based on the assessment of study design, sample size, data collection and analysis, and other relevant factors; (c) duplication; and (d) studies that showed a serious risk of bias using Cochrane’s risk of bias tool for RCTs and ROBINS-I for non-randomized comparative studies.

Study selection

All records identified through the database search were screened based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. After removing duplicates, titles and abstracts were reviewed, followed by a full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. The selection process is summarized in Fig. 1, which presents the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram detailing the number of studies identified, screened, excluded, and included in the review. Of the 81 full-text articles assessed for eligibility, 22 articles were encompassed and 59 were excluded for the following reasons: lack of accessible full text (n = 5), systematic reviews or meta-analyses (n = 8), non-target population (n = 21), and missing important outcome data relevant to the objectives of this review (n = 25), as detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Results

Analysis method

The meta-analysis was performed using R version 5.4 software. For continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences (SMDs) or weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a fixed-effect or random-effect model, depending on the heterogeneity between studies. For dichotomous outcomes, the relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs were calculated using random-effects models. Statistical heterogeneity between the studies was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity where sufficient data were available. Subgroup analyses were performed according to study design (randomized controlled trials vs. cohorts studies) and duration of follow-up (short-term (less than or equal to 12 months) vs. long-term (> 12 months) follow-up, as defined by the median or reported follow-up categories in the included studies). Subgroup analyses were undertaken only when at least three studies were available within each subgroup to ensure meaningful comparisons. Differences between subgroups were assessed using a test for interaction. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave-one-out approach to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. In this method, each study was sequentially removed, and the meta-analysis was recalculated to determine whether any single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect size or heterogeneity. Stability of the pooled estimates after sequential exclusion was considered indicative of robust findings.

Publication bias was assessed for outcomes including at least ten studies. Funnel plots were visually inspected for asymmetry.

Risk of bias

Based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS), the risk of bias among the studies varied, with some demonstrating a good methodological design and others having some weaknesses. Most studies had high scores in terms of representativeness of the exposed cohort, exposure ascertainment, and adequacy of follow-up to ensure good patient data collection and monitoring. However, a common restriction was the lack of blind outcome assessment, which undermined the independence of the outcome measurement and could have introduced bias into the results. Single-center study designs also restricted some of the studies, which undermined their external validity. Although the comparability of cohorts was often well adjusted, a few studies failed to sufficiently control for all relevant confounders such as comorbidities or baseline functional status. Despite these constraints, most trials reported an adequate duration of follow-up, with most conducting at least 1–3 years of follow-up to ensure that factors such as functional restoration and complications were properly evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 1). Two RCTs were evaluated using the Cochrane risk of bias two tool and were categorized as having low risk of bias, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Characteristics of the included studies

In this meta-analysis, we included 22 studies with 34,904 patients; 17,037 patients underwent RSA, and 6,893 patients underwent ORIF. The studies analyzed were conducted in various countries, including the United States, China, Germany, Italy, Norway, Brazil, and Switzerland, and employed various study designs, including retrospective cohort studies, RCTs, and prospective cohort studies. The follow-up times ranged from 30 days to 10 years, with the majority of studies having a mean follow-up of 1–3 years. The age range of the participants was heterogeneous, typically from 63 to 77 years, and the standard deviations indicated a broad age range in some studies. Fractures were primarily typed using the Neer classification system, although other systems have been used in some studies, including the AO, OTA, and custom systems (Table 1).

Range of motion

Flexion

In the overall random-effects model, RSA was associated with a numerically greater forward flexion compared with ORIF (MD 12.17°; 95% CI − 0.56 to 24.89; p = 0.06), although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 92.5%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis forest plot of forward flexion between RSA and ORIF

When stratified by study design, cohort studies demonstrated a similar direction of effect favoring RSA (MD 12.04°; 95% CI − 2.07 to 26.16; p = 0.09), but without statistical significance. The single RCT showed a point estimate favoring RSA. Importantly, there was no significant interaction between study design subgroups (p for subgroup difference > 0.99) (Fig. 2).

In the long-term follow-up subgroup, RSA showed a non-significant improvement in forward flexion (MD 10.36°; 95% CI − 6.26 to 26.99; p = 0.22; I² = 94.4%). Similarly, in the short-term subgroup, the pooled estimate favored RSA (MD 15.99°; 95% CI − 4.78 to 36.77; p = 0.13; I² = 69%). No statistically significant difference was observed between subgroups (p for interaction = 0.68) (Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis forest plot of forward flexion between RSA and ORIF with subgroup analysis according to the follow-up period

Leave-one-out analyses demonstrated relative stability of the pooled estimate. Sequential exclusion of individual studies yielded effect sizes ranging from MD 9.00° to 15.10°. Statistical significance emerged after omission of selected studies (e.g., Checchia 1998, Jaekel 2025, Yahuaca 2019, Klug 2020) (Fig. 4).

Leave-one-out analysis plot of forward flexion between RSA and ORIF

Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed major asymmetry, suggesting potential small-study effects. Given the limited number of studies, interpretation should be cautious; however, the dispersion pattern may partially explain the observed heterogeneity (Fig. 5).

Funnel plot of forward flexion between RSA and ORIF

Internal rotation

Comparison of internal rotation between RSA and ORIF revealed a WMD of − 34.03° [95% CI (–75.11°, 7.05°)] in favor of ORIF, suggesting that patients who underwent ORIF had better internal rotation outcomes. The analysis included 136 patients who underwent RSA and 130 who underwent ORIF, with substantial heterogeneity (Tau² = 614.9476, chi² = 55.00, df = 3, P < 0.0001, I² = 94.5%), but no significant overall effect (t³ = − 2.64, P = 0.0779) (Fig. 6).

Meta-analysis forest plot of internal rotation between RSA and ORIF

External rotation

Regarding external rotation, the difference between RSA and ORIF showed a WMD of 0.38° [95% CI (–13.45°, 14.21°)], indicating no significant difference between the two treatments. The analysis included 181 patients who underwent RSA and 176 who underwent ORIF, with substantial heterogeneity (Tau² = 160.0326, chi² = 319.46, df = 5, P < 0.0001, I² = 98.4%), but no significant overall effect (t = 0.07, P = 0.9462) (Fig. 7).

Meta-analysis forest plot of external rotation between RSA and ORIF

Abduction

In terms of abduction, RSA—compared to ORIF—showed a WMD of 11.02° [95% CI (–20.84°, 42.89°)], suggesting a slight advantage for RSA, although the results were not significant. The analysis included 148 patients who underwent RSA and 144 who underwent ORIF, with moderate heterogeneity (Tau² = 322.5726, chi² = 32.24, df = 3, P < 0.0001, I² = 90.7%), but no significant overall effect (t = 1.10, P = 0.3513) (Fig. 8).

Meta-analysis forest plot of abduction between RSA and ORIF

Function scores

Constant-Murley score

Comparing the Constant-Murley score between the RSA and ORIF groups revealed a WMD of 2.77 [95% CI (–9.41, 14.95)], suggesting no significant difference between the two treatments. The analysis included 131 patients who underwent RSA and 153 who underwent ORIF, with moderate heterogeneity (Tau² = 67.6465, chi² = 14.71, df = 4, P = 0.0053, I² = 72.8%), but no significant overall effect (t = 0.63, P = 0.5622) (Fig. 9).

Meta-analysis forest plot of Constant-Murley score between RSA and ORIF

Oxford shoulder score (OSS)

Comparing the Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS) between the RSA and ORIF groups revealed a WMD of 8.79 [95% CI (–41.85, 59.42)], indicating no significant difference between the two treatments. The analysis included 119 patients who underwent RSA and 113 who underwent ORIF patients, with very high heterogeneity (Tau² = 412.4321, chi² = 294.83, df = 2, P < 0.0001, I² = 99.3%), but no significant overall effect (t = 0.75, P = 0.5330) (Fig. 10).

Meta-analysis forest plot of Oxford shoulder score (OSS) between RSA and ORIF

Disability of the arm, shoulder, and hand (DASH) score

The DASH scores differed slightly between the RSA and ORIF groups, showing a WMD of 2.36 [95% CI (–6.24, 10.97)], indicating no significant difference between the two treatments. The analysis included 199 patients who underwent RSA and 213 who underwent ORIF, with high heterogeneity (Tau² = 60.8748, chi² = 209.11, df = 5, P < 0.0001, I² = 97.6%), but no significant overall effect (t = 0.71, P = 0.5115) (Fig. 11).

Meta-analysis forest plot of DASH score between RSA and ORIF

Complications

Malunion

Comparing the risk of malunion between the RSA and ORIF groups revealed a risk ratio (RR) of 0.66 [95% CI (0.26, 1.64)], indicating no significant difference in the risk of malunion. The analysis included 17,037 patients who underwent RSA and 6,893 who underwent ORIF, with moderate heterogeneity (Tau² = 1.6269, chi² = 41.32, df = 12, P < 0.0001, I² = 71.0%), but no significant overall effect (t₁₂ = − 1.00, P = 0.3373) (Fig. 12).

Meta-analysis forest plot of malunion risk between RSA and ORIF

Reoperation

The risk of reoperation for RSA compared with ORIF revealed a RR of 0.48 [95% CI (0.14, 1.66)], indicating no significant difference in the risk of reoperation between the two treatments. The analysis included 2,272 patients who underwent RSA and 10,337 who underwent ORIF, with high heterogeneity (Tau² = 1.0411, chi² = 23.71, df = 6, P = 0.0006, I² = 74.7%), but no significant overall effect (t₆ = − 1.45, P = 0.1982) (Fig. 13).

Discussion

Complex proximal humerus fractures pose significant treatment challenges, especially in the elderly, where bone quality, rotator cuff integrity, and comorbidities complicate surgical decision-making. In this meta-analysis, we synthesize data from more than 34,000 patients across 22 studies, offering a comprehensive comparison between RSA and ORIF. Our findings provide critical insights into the nuanced differences in shoulder range of motion, complications, and clinical outcomes that influence the treatment choices for these complex injuries.

Range of motion outcomes

Restoring shoulder range of motion is one of the most important functional goals after surgical intervention for proximal humeral fractures. The shoulder’s ability to move in multiple planes, including forward flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation, is essential for performing daily tasks such as dressing, grooming, eating, and reaching overhead or behind the back. This analysis showed that RSA resulted in more numerical improvement in forward flexion compared with ORIF. This finding is consistent with previous literature suggesting that RSA allows for better deltoid-driven elevation, compensating for deficient or irreparable rotator cuff muscles [13, 14] The enhanced forward elevation with RSA is particularly advantageous in elderly patients who often present with rotator cuff pathology or poor bone quality, which can compromise fixation in ORIF. RSA also demonstrated a non-significant trend toward improved abduction (WMD = 11.02°, P = 0.3513). While this difference did not reach statistical significance, the direction of the effect supports the mechanical advantage of RSA in providing glenohumeral stability through the prosthetic center of rotation, allowing the deltoid to function more effectively as the primary elevator [15]. However, variability in rehabilitation protocols and tuberosity healing may influence abduction outcomes. In contrast, internal rotation was numerically better in patients undergoing ORIF (WMD = − 34.03°), though the result did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.0779). Internal rotation is typically driven by the subscapularis and anterior deltoid functions, both of which may be compromised or altered biomechanically in RSA, especially if tuberosity healing is inadequate [16]. Moreover, the prosthetic geometry in the RSA may limit the mechanical arc available for internal rotation, a factor that should be considered when counseling patients.

No significant difference in external rotation was observed between the RSA and ORIF groups (WMD = 0.38°, P = 0.9462). This finding reflects the complex interplay between posterior cuff integrity and prosthetic design. External rotation is often difficult to restore with RSA, particularly in patients with pre-existing infraspinatus and teres minor atrophy. Despite this, external rotation in patients who receive ORIF can also be limited by residual stiffness, nonunion, or hardware impingement [17].

Functional scores and shoulder rotation

Our findings indicate that RSA provides marginally superior results in certain shoulder range of motion parameters such as flexion and abduction, although these differences were not consistently significant. Notably, for internal rotation, ORIF demonstrated a trend toward improved outcomes; however, this was not significant. The limited number of studies reporting detailed rotational outcomes, particularly internal and external rotation, highlights a significant gap in the current evidence. Similar observations were made by Brorson et al. [5] and Sebastia-Forcada et al. [18], who found that while RSA generally improves forward flexion and abduction, the rotational movement increases remain small and unpredictable because of changes in shoulder biomechanics.

Regarding functional scores such as the Constant-Murley and Oxford Shoulder Scores, our meta-analysis found no significant difference between RSA and ORIF. This is consistent with previous systematic evaluations by Heo et al. [19] and Suroto et al. [20], who found only minor or nonsignificant improvements in these overall scores following RSA. These findings highlight the need for future high-quality trials that stratify outcomes by specific functional domains, including validated measurements of rotational mobility, to facilitate accurate surgical decision-making.

Complications

Complication profiles remain a critical factor in surgical planning for older patients with osteoporotic bones. Our pooled results did not demonstrate a significant difference in the risk of malunion [RR: 0.66, 95% CI (0.26–1.64)] or reoperation [RR: 0.48, 95% CI (0.14–1.66)] between RSA and ORIF. These findings are consistent with the meta-analyses performed by Klug et al. [21] and Zumstein et al. [6], who similarly noted that RSA could lower the risk of revision surgery compared to ORIF, although the reasoning was due to wide CIs and substantial heterogeneity. Moreover, it seems logical to assume that the biomechanical design of RSA can decrease the probability of fixation failures, which have been key characteristics of ORIF in the case of osteoporotic bone. However, complications such as tuberosity nonunion or resorption may arise and obstruct the functional recovery [4, 22]. Reoperation rates with ORIF are often a consequence of hardware failure, screw cutout, or secondary collapse, all of which are more frequent in elderly patients with osteoporotic bones [23]. Even considering such factors, our meta-analysis estimates show that there is no significant difference for RSA in terms of a reduction in reoperation rates. This result highlights that although RSA is usually preferred for aged individuals with bone of lower quality, there are still complications related to the implant’s incorrect positioning, instability, and infection, which are very important and should be considered thoroughly at the time of the surgical planning.

Conclusion

In conclusion, RSA demonstrated a slight advantage over ORIF for improving forward flexion in patients with complex proximal humeral fractures. However, no significant differences were observed in the internal or external rotation, functional scores, or complication rates. Given the heterogeneity and methodological variability of the included studies, future prospective multicenter RCTs with standardized outcome reporting are needed to further delineate optimal surgical strategies. The present findings support RSA as a reliable surgical option, especially in the elderly or low-demand patients, whereas ORIF remains a viable alternative for younger patients, in whom preservation of the native joint mechanics is attempted.
