A multicentre evaluation of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment of piperacillin and tazobactam and the association with clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with sepsis and septic shock
Authors: Helmi Sulaiman, Saskia A Wölky, Muhammad Azrai Rozali, Santosh K S Adiraju, M Shahnaz Hasan, Maria Patricia Hernandez-Mitre, Xin Liu, Mohd-Basri Mat-Nor, Mohd Zulfakar Mazlan, Zeti Norfidiyati Salmuna, Steven C Wallis, Jiao Xie, Jason A Roberts, Mohd H Abdul-Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo characterize the population pharmacokinetics and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment of piperacillin and tazobactam in critically ill patients with sepsis or septic shock in Malaysian intensive care units and to use the population pharmacokinetic model to estimate individual target attainment and explore associations with clinical outcomes.MethodsSerial blood samples were collected on days 1 and 3 of therapy in this prospective multicentre study. Total plasma piperacillin and tazobactam concentrations were quantified using a validated chromatographic assay. Population pharmacokinetic analysis and Monte Carlo dosing simulations were performed using Monolix. Therapeutic exposure was defined as achieving 100% fT>16 mg/L for piperacillin and 85% fT>2 mg/L for tazobactam while remaining below the piperacillin toxicity threshold (<160 mg/L).ResultsForty-five critically ill adults with sepsis or septic shock were recruited (median age, 61 years [range: 18–88]; median creatinine clearance (CLcr) 70 mL/min [range: 30–161]; 20 females). A one-compartment model with first-order elimination best described the pharmacokinetics for both drugs. Estimated CLcr significantly influenced drug clearance. Pre-defined therapeutic exposures were achieved in 54% and 44% on days 1 and 3, respectively. Patients attaining exposures were older and had lower CLcr. Clinical cure and ICU survival were numerically different across exposure groups, although these exploratory comparisons were not statistically significant. Simulations indicated that a 4.5 g loading dose followed by 4.5 g every 6 h as a continuous infusion achieved effective and safe exposures in patients with CLcr 50 to 130 mL/min.ConclusionContinuous infusion is the most reliable strategy for achieving early piperacillin/tazobactam therapeutic exposures in patients with sepsis or septic shock.

Introduction

Piperacillin, which is usually co-administered with the beta-lactamase inhibitor tazobactam, has time-dependent bactericidal activity, with efficacy linked to maintaining free drug concentrations above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for at least 40% to 50% of the dosing interval (40%–50% fT>MIC).1 In critically ill patients with severe infections, higher exposures ranging from 100% fT>MIC to 100% fT>4–6×MIC are increasingly recognized as being necessary to improve patient outcomes.2 Achieving these exposures is challenging due to profound pharmacokinetic variability in this vulnerable patient population. In the DALI study, one-third of patients receiving piperacillin failed to achieve 50% fT>MIC and across the full cohort of patients receiving beta-lactam antibiotics, and failure to achieve this exposure was associated with worse clinical outcomes.3 Additional complexity arises in infections caused by extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing organisms, which are commonly encountered in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with sepsis.4 In such cases, tazobactam is essential to restore piperacillin activity by suppressing its enzymatic degradation. In vitro infection models suggest that effective beta-lactamase inhibition requires maintenance of free tazobactam concentrations above a concentration threshold (CT) of 2 mg/L for at least 85% of the dosing interval (85% fT>2 mg/L).5

Optimizing dosing strategies for both drugs has therefore become critical particularly in patients with sepsis and septic shock. Evidence shows that prolonged (either extended or continuous) infusions of piperacillin/tazobactam consistently improve pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) target attainment, and these altered dosing strategies have been associated with lower mortality in recent meta-analyses6 and randomized controlled trials,7–9 when compared with intermittent infusion. Additionally, the MERINO trial reported higher mortality with intermittent infusion of piperacillin/tazobactam compared to meropenem in ceftriaxone-resistant Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae bloodstream infections.10 Although the trial did not directly evaluate the relationship between PK/PD target attainment and patient outcomes, the findings have raised concerns that sub-optimal exposures of piperacillin/tazobactam with intermittent infusion may have contributed to treatment failure.

To date, most population pharmacokinetic studies of piperacillin/tazobactam in critically ill patients have focused on piperacillin alone without simultaneous PK/PD analysis of both drugs. Although recent pooled population pharmacokinetic models have sought to describe piperacillin/tazobactam pharmacokinetics across different age groups (neonates to elderly patients),11–13 critically ill patients with sepsis or septic shock remain underrepresented. Additionally, dose optimization must account for possible drug toxicity. For piperacillin, concentrations above 160 mg/L have been associated with neurotoxicity in one study,14 while another reported a threshold above 360 mg/L.15

To address this gap, we conducted a multicentre study to characterize the population pharmacokinetics of piperacillin/tazobactam in critically ill patients with sepsis or septic shock. Using the population pharmacokinetic model, we estimated individual PK/PD target attainment, investigated key patient factors that influenced target attainment, and explored the association between PK/PD target attainment and patient outcomes. Finally, we sought to develop clinically relevant piperacillin/tazobactam dosing nomograms designed to optimize PK/PD target attainment while reducing the likelihood of toxicity.

Methods

Study design and population

A prospective multicentre pharmacokinetic study was conducted between March 2017 and March 2018 in three Malaysian ICUs: (i) Sultan Ahmad Shah Medical Centre, (ii) Hospital Universiti Sains Malaysia and (iii) University of Malaya Medical Centre. Eligible patients were adult (≥18 years) ICU patients receiving piperacillin/tazobactam with sepsis or septic shock as defined by the Sepsis-3 criteria,16 had confirmed or suspected bacterial infections, had a central venous or arterial line in place to facilitate blood collection for pharmacokinetic sampling, and provided informed consent to participate in the study. Ethical approval was obtained from each participating institution (UMMC Medical Research Ethics Committee Reg. No 2016725-4020; USM Human Research Ethics Committee Reg. No USM/JEPeM/16110528; IIUM Research Ethics Committee Reg. No IREC 2017-062). The study was registered with and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee Malaysia (NMRR-16237231675).

Patients were excluded if they had a known or suspected hypersensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics, were pregnant or lactating, or had pre-existing renal impairment, which was defined as receipt of renal replacement therapy or an estimated creatinine clearance (CLcr) of less than 30 mL/min or plasma creatinine concentration of above 200 μmol/L.

Drug dosing and sample collection

Drug dosing was per standard of care at the discretion of the treating physician. Pharmacokinetic sampling was performed on two occasions: Occasion 1 (Day 1 of antibiotic therapy) and Occasion 2 (Day 3 of antibiotic therapy). Each patient underwent up to seven blood samplings on each occasion. Samples were collected from a central venous or arterial line into heparinized tubes at pre-determined time points according to the dosing regimen (Text S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online).

Data collection

In addition to pharmacokinetic sampling, relevant clinical data were collected using a standardized case report form. These included patient demographics, clinical characteristics, severity of illness assessed using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II)17 and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)18 scores, antibiotic dosing details, and baseline biochemistry (e.g. serum albumin and creatinine).

Clinical outcomes were assessed by independent clinicians who were not involved in patient care in the participating ICUs. Outcomes were adjudicated according to the definitions in Table S2, either on the day of piperacillin/tazobactam cessation or on Day 14 following study enrolment, whichever occurred first. Mortality at ICU discharge was also recorded.

Bioanalysis

Total plasma concentrations of piperacillin and tazobactam were measured using a validated ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) method on a Shimadzu 8030 + triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan).19 All analyses were performed in accordance with the US FDA guidance for industry on bioanalysis20 and is described in Text S3.

Population pharmacokinetic analysis

Monolix software (version 2021R2, LIXOFT, Antony, France) was used for the population PK analysis, utilizing a nonlinear mixed-effect modelling approach. All analyses and evaluations are described in detail in Text S4.

Individual PK/PD target attainment and therapeutic exposure

For each patient, the %fT>MIC for piperacillin and %fT>CT for tazobactam in plasma were calculated using individual predicted free drug concentrations. PK/PD target attainment was assessed using epidemiological cut-off (ECOFF) values defined by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST). Target attainment was evaluated on days 1 and 3 of therapy. For piperacillin, 100% fT>MIC was chosen as the target using EUCAST ECOFF MIC value of 16 mg/L for Pseudomonas aeruginosa (100% fT>16 mg/L). Although earlier in vivo animal PK/PD studies suggest that 50% to 60% fT>MIC may be sufficient for beta-lactam bactericidal activity,1 100% fT>MIC for piperacillin was selected in-line with international guidelines for critically ill patients in the ICU.21,22 As organism-specific MIC data were not available for most patients in this study, which is consistent with routine practice in many clinical settings, EUCAST ECOFF values were used as pragmatic surrogate MIC thresholds to assess empiric dosing adequacy. The P. aeruginosa ECOFF MIC of 16 mg/L was selected as a conservative threshold reflecting the least susceptible pathogen that could be encountered during therapy. For tazobactam, 85% fT>2 mg/L was chosen as the target. The target was based on in vitro infection models, which demonstrated that maintaining unbound tazobactam concentrations above CT of 2 mg/L for at least 85% of the dosing interval achieved a ≥ 2 log10 reduction in bacterial counts of high-level CTX-M-15–producing Escherichia coli strains.5 A conservative toxicity threshold was applied for piperacillin, defined as a trough (Cmin) or steady-state (Css) concentration of >160 mg/L,14 which has been associated with an increased risk of neurotoxicity when co-administered with tazobactam. Therapeutic exposure was defined as achieving both 100% fT>16 mg/L for piperacillin and 85% fT>2 mg/L for tazobactam while remaining below the toxicity threshold (<160 mg/L). This classification was used as a pragmatic approach to assess empiric PK/PD target attainment rather than to define patient-specific therapeutic exposure targets.

Dosing simulations and probability of target attainment

Monte Carlo simulations (n = 1000) were performed using Simulx 2024R1 (Lixoft SAS, a Simulations Plus company) to evaluate 15 piperacillin/tazobactam dosing regimens (Text S5). For both drugs, the unbound (free) concentrations were predicted assuming 30% protein binding, consistent with values reported in critically ill patients.23

Using the same efficacy and toxicity targets as the individual PK/PD target attainment analysis, the probability of target attainment (PTA) for efficacy was defined as 100% fT>MIC for piperacillin combined with 85% fT>2 mg/L for tazobactam. For each dosing regimen, the PTA for efficacy was calculated as the proportion of simulated patients achieving 100% fT>MIC for piperacillin (for a given MIC) and 85% fT>2 mg/L for tazobactam. PTA was calculated on Days 1 and 3 of therapy, and a regimen was considered optimal if it achieved ≥90% PTA on both days. A total trough (Cmin) or steady-state (Css) concentration of piperacillin >160 mg/L,14 which was associated with an increased risk of neurotoxicity, was used as the toxicity threshold. The optimal dosing regimen was defined as the regimen that provided the greatest probability of achieving exposures associated with efficacy while minimizing the risk of toxicity.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as means (SD) or medians (IQR or range), as appropriate, and categorical data as counts (percentage). Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous data and the Pearson’s chi-square or the Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, as appropriate. The association between PK/PD target attainment and patient outcomes was exploratory and the study was not powered for formal exposure-outcome analyses. A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. Analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study population

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Forty-five patients with sepsis or septic shock receiving piperacillin/tazobactam were enrolled; the median age was 61 years (interquartile range, IQR 36–72) and 56% were male. The median APACHE II and SOFA scores on ICU admission were 14 (IQR 11–17) and 6 (IQR 3–9), respectively. Cockcroft-Gault CLcr on admission ranged from 30 to 160 mL/min. Pneumonia (71.1%) was the most common infection. Twenty-nine patients (64%) had at least one causative pathogen identified before or during treatment, with P. aeruginosa (n = 7) and K. pneumoniae (n = 7) the most frequently isolated pathogen. Most patients (82%) received 4.5 g piperacillin/tazobactam every 6 h, with continuous infusion (40%) being the most common method of administration.

Pharmacokinetic model building

The time course of piperacillin and tazobactam in plasma was best described by a one-compartment model with first-order elimination. The model was parameterized as clearance (CL in L/h) and volume of distribution (V in L) for both drugs separately. All parameters were log transformed. Between-subject variability (BSV) and between-occasion variability (BOV) were estimated for CL and V. Residual unexplained variability was described by a combined error model.

During covariate testing, the inclusion of Cockcroft-Gault CLcr effect (normalized to the population median value of 71 mL/min) on piperacillin CL and tazobactam CL improved the model fit (by decreasing the objective function value, OFV) by 103.19 points as well as reducing BSV on CL from 70% to 24% for piperacillin and from 75% to 30% for tazobactam). PK/PD target attainment and patient outcomes

Comparison of clinical characteristics and treatment-related variables between patients with pre-defined therapeutic and non-therapeutic (i.e. sub-therapeutic or toxic) concentrations on Occasion 1 and Occasion 2 are presented in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. Across both sampling occasions, pre-defined therapeutic piperacillin/tazobactam concentrations were achieved in approximately half of the patients (54% on Occasion 1 and 44% on Occasion 2), whereas about one-third were sub-therapeutic and only a small proportion exceeded the toxicity threshold. Achieving pre-defined therapeutic concentrations on Occasion 1 was significantly associated with the achievement on Occasion 2 (P < 0.001). Patients with therapeutic concentrations were typically older and had lower CLcr than those who did not. Continuous infusion most consistently achieved therapeutic concentrations while intermittent infusion was frequently associated with sub-therapeutic concentrations (P < 0.001). Clinical cure and ICU mortality were numerically different across exposure groups, but these exploratory comparisons did not achieve statistical significance (Tables 3 and 4). Patients who achieved pre-defined therapeutic concentrations on both occasions showed numerically higher clinical cure (75% [6/8] vs. 56% [5/9]; P = 0.402) and lower ICU mortality (13% [1/8] vs. 33% [3/9]; P = 0.302) when compared with those who did not, although these exploratory comparisons were not statistically significant (Tables 3 and 4).

Dosing simulations and PTA

PTA rates for efficacy and toxicity were comparable between Days 1 (Figure 5) and 3 (Table S9) of therapy. Simulated regimens with higher total daily doses and more frequent dosing intervals increased the PTA for efficacy, whereas PTA markedly declined with increasing CLcr. Across simulated scenarios, the PTA for efficacy improved progressively from intermittent to extended to continuous infusion dosing regimens. At both the EUCAST ECOFF MICs of 8 mg/L for Enterobacterales and 16 mg/L for P. aeruginosa, optimal PTA for efficacy was achieved only with continuous infusion regimens in patients with CLcr ≤110 mL/min, and with a loading dose of 4.5 g followed by either 4.5 g every 4 or 6 h as a continuous infusion in those with CLcr 130 mL/min. Toxic concentrations were generally uncommon (<5% for most regimens), but the probability increased substantially with higher total daily doses (≥18 g/day) or reduced CLcr (≤50 mL/min).

Discussion

This multicentre study is the first to characterize both the population PK of piperacillin and tazobactam as well as the combined PK/PD target attainment for these agents in critically ill adults with sepsis or septic shock. Although more than 20 population PK studies of piperacillin/tazobactam have been published, only six have characterized both components simultaneously,13,24–28 and none has specifically focused on sepsis or septic shock ICU patients with preserved renal function, nor been conducted in Southeast Asian ICUs. Despite receiving dosing regimens that were considered ‘standard’ at the time of study, pre-defined PK/PD target concentrations were achieved in only about half of the cohort, with sub-therapeutic concentrations most frequently observed in patients receiving intermittent infusions and those with elevated CLcr. Achieving the pre-defined therapeutic exposures was associated with numerically higher clinical cure and lower ICU mortality, although these exploratory findings should be interpreted cautiously. These observations are consistent with the rationale for early and optimized dosing strategies for this high-risk population. Importantly, toxicity thresholds were incorporated into all analyses providing a comprehensive assessment of both safety and efficacy. Continuous infusion demonstrated the best balance between safety and efficacy across renal function strata and should therefore be preferred, not only over intermittent but also extended infusion, in sepsis and septic shock patients.

The primary pharmacokinetic parameters for piperacillin (V 25.53 L, CL 8.78 L) and tazobactam (27.15 L, 8.74 L) were generally consistent with previously reported estimates (piperacillin: V 12–34 L, CL 6–13 L/h; tazobactam V 8–40, CL 6–12) but showed greater inter-patient variability, as typically observed and anticipated in patients with sepsis or septic shock. When compared with other studies,13,24–28 CL for both drugs was at the lower end of the reported range, likely reflecting reduced renal function, whereas the V was comparable despite the lower average body weight of the present cohort. Consistent with previous studies, CLcr was a significant covariate that influenced drug CL underscoring the importance of renal function in determining beta-lactam antibiotic exposures in critically ill patients. Incorporating BOV on CL for both drugs significantly improved model performance, highlighting the dynamic physiological changes during critical illness that have important dosing implications. Antibiotic exposures in this population can fluctuate over the course of treatment as organ function, particularly renal function, changes. Consequently, dosing requirements should be regularly reviewed as a fixed dosing approach may quickly become inadequate. For piperacillin/tazobactam, our findings suggest that routine implementation of continuous infusion supported by therapeutic drug monitoring (if available) may be crucial to maintain therapeutic exposures over the course of therapy.

Sub-therapeutic piperacillin/tazobactam exposures were common, consistent with previous large multinational pharmacokinetic studies.3,29,30 Approximately half of the patients in this study failed to achieve the pre-defined therapeutic target, particularly younger individuals and those with higher CLcr, highlighting the well-recognized, but frequently underestimated, impact of augmented renal clearance on beta-lactam antibiotic underexposure. Patients who achieved pre-defined therapeutic concentrations particularly on Day 1 of therapy demonstrated numerically higher rates of clinical cure and lower ICU mortality. However, these exploratory comparisons were not statistically significant and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal exposure-outcome relationship. Early optimal beta-lactam antibiotic exposure remains clinically relevant in sepsis and septic shock as the initial phase of therapy is a critical period for evaluating initial dosing adequacy. This exploratory exposure-outcome analysis approach is consistent with previous large multinational ICU pharmacokinetic studies, including DALI3 and SMARRT,30 in which early antibiotic exposures were related to clinical outcomes. However, our findings should be interpreted cautiously because piperacillin/tazobactam exposure was assessed on Days 1 and 3 whereas clinical outcomes were assessed later. Therefore, early exposure should be viewed as a marker of early PK/PD target attainment rather than a surrogate for exposure throughout the entire treatment course. These findings should be regarded as hypothesis generating and require confirmation in larger studies designed and powered to specifically evaluate exposure-outcome relationships. Based on our dosing simulations, conventional intermittent infusion and even extended infusion regimens are unlikely to consistently achieve the pre-defined PK/PD targets.

Although the 100% fT>MIC PK/PD target is consistent with contemporary guidelines for critically ill patients in the ICU,22,31 more aggressive alternative targets (e.g. 100% fT>4×MIC) have also been proposed for this population.21 A highly relevant recent population pharmacokinetic study by Cojutti et al.27 similarly evaluated both piperacillin and tazobactam exposure in ICU patients receiving continuous infusion with TDM-guided dose optimization but used a more aggressive joint PK/PD target (fCss/MIC ≥4 for piperacillin and fCss/CT ≥1 for tazobactam). Cojutti et al.27 reported that higher continuous infusion doses of piperacillin/tazobactam of 22.5 g/day may be required to improve PK/PD target attainment in patients with preserved renal function (≥100 mL/min). Although these dosing conclusions slightly differ from ours, the findings are complementary rather than contradictory and likely reflect differences in PK/PD target selection (standard vs. more aggressive ICU targets), sampling scheme (early serial sampling vs. steady-state sampling during continuous infusion), renal function estimation (Cockcroft-Gault CLcr vs. CKD-EPI equation), and study population (sepsis or septic shock vs. heterogeneous ICU populations). Consistent with Cojutti et al.,27 applying these higher targets would likely reduce target attainment, particularly with intermittent and extended infusions, while also increasing the likelihood of excessive beta-lactam exposure and concentration-related toxicity. The increasing use of aggressive beta-lactam exposure targets may have contributed, at least in part, to the increasing recognition of beta-lactam toxicity in critically ill patients32 and further supports the need to balance safety and efficacy ideally through therapeutic drug monitoring. Therefore, the optimal piperacillin/tazobactam exposure target that balances safety and efficacy in critically ill patients with sepsis or septic shock remains to be defined.

Our dosing simulations demonstrated that continuous infusion of piperacillin/tazobactam achieved the most consistent PTA for efficacy across all renal function categories. When restricted to the clinically relevant EUCAST ECOFF MICs for Enterobacterales (8 mg/L) and P. aeruginosa (16 mg/L), >94% of continuous infusion regimens achieved the PTA for efficacy on both simulated days compared with <27% and <17% for extended and intermittent infusions, respectively. A loading dose of 4.5 g followed by 4.5 g every 6 h as a continuous infusion showed the best balance between safety and efficacy in patients with CLcr of 50–130 mL/min. These findings are particularly relevant to patients with preserved renal function or augmented renal clearance (CLcr ≥130 mL/min) as PTA for efficacy declined with increasing CLcr up to the highest simulated value of 130 mL/min. Although patients with CLcr >130 mL/min were not evaluated in our simulations, such patients would be expected to be at even greater risk of sub-optimal exposure and may require higher-than-standard dosing (e.g. 22.5 g/day as continuous infusion),27,33 preferably guided by therapeutic drug monitoring where available. In patients with CLcr ≤30 mL/min, intermittent (e.g. 2.25 g every 4 h or 4.5 g every 6 h) or extended infusion (e.g. 4.5 g every 8 h as a 4-h infusion) regimens can be considered. The risk of toxic concentration was low across most simulated scenarios and remained low at standard total daily doses. It is important to highlight that we have chosen the lower piperacillin toxicity threshold described in the literature,14,15 Cmin/Css of 160 mg/L rather than 360 mg/L, to provide a conservative safety assessment in our analysis.

Optimized beta-lactam antibiotic dosing strategies (e.g. extended or continuous infusion) may improve the probability of achieving effective exposure when piperacillin/tazobactam is used empirically. Improved clinical outcomes have been reported with continuous infusions in several studies, including our previous work,34 the BLING III trial,8 and recent meta-analyses.6 Our simulations further suggest that sub-optimal PK/PD target attainment with intermittent dosing may have contributed, at least in part, to the poorer outcomes observed in the MERINO trial.10 This notion is supported by the recently presented preliminary PeterPen data (presented at ESCMID Global 2026),35 in which extended infusion of piperacillin/tazobactam was non-inferior to extended infusion of meropenem for treatment failure in patients with ceftriaxone-resistant Enterobacterales infection, suggesting that early and optimized dosing strategies may have an important role in patients with severe Gram-negative infections.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted exclusively in Malaysian ICUs, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions with different demographics, healthcare systems, or dosing practices. Second, only plasma concentrations were measured, which may not fully reflect concentrations at infection sites (e.g. lungs). Third, total rather than free concentrations were measured, which are more relevant for evaluating the pharmacologic activity of a drug, although free concentrations are relatively predictable for both drugs.36 Fourth, this study was not powered for formal exposure-outcome analyses, and the relatively small sample size may have limited the ability to detect robust relationships between piperacillin/tazobactam concentrations and clinical outcomes. Conversely, any observed associations should be interpreted with caution given the potential overestimation due to statistical uncertainty. Fifth, the relationship between piperacillin/tazobactam concentrations and clinical outcomes was exploratory because concentrations were assessed only on Days 1 and 3 whereas clinical outcomes were assessed later. Therefore, early exposure should not be assumed to represent exposure throughout the full treatment course. Nevertheless, assessing PK/PD target attainment during the early phase of therapy is clinically relevant in sepsis and septic shock, where inadequate early antimicrobial exposure may compromise treatment outcomes. Sixth, organism-specific MIC data were not available and were therefore not used in the analysis. Instead, EUCAST ECOFF values were used as pragmatic surrogate MIC thresholds. Although this approach supports empiric dosing assessment, it may misclassify individual PK/PD target attainment when the actual MIC differs from the assumed ECOFF and may therefore not fully reflect the relationship between exposure and patient outcomes. Seventh, although every effort was made to collect pharmacokinetic samples on both occasions, sampling was imbalanced between Days 1 and 3 due to clinical and logistical constraints inherent to ICU care. Repeat sampling was not always feasible due to competing medical procedures, clinical deterioration, or death. This may have limited our ability to estimate the PK/PD target attainment on Day 3 and assess how changes in exposure over time related to clinical outcomes. Eighth, toxicity assessment was restricted to piperacillin, as no clinically validated or proposed threshold for tazobactam toxicity currently exists. Finally, renal function was estimated using Cockcroft-Gault CLcr, which may be less accurate than measured urinary CLcr in critically ill patients.

Conclusion

Standard intermittent dosing of piperacillin/tazobactam, and even extended infusion in some scenarios, was insufficient to achieve pre-defined PK/PD targets across a range of renal function categories, particularly at clinically relevant MICs for Enterobacterales and P. aeruginosa. By contrast, continuous infusion with an appropriate loading dose consistently achieved pre-defined PK/PD targets and offered the most favourable balance between efficacy and safety in critically ill patients with sepsis and septic shock. The association between PK/PD target attainment and patient outcomes was investigated, but these analyses were exploratory and not powered for formal exposure-outcome evaluation.
