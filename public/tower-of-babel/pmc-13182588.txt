Spondyloptosis is a rare and severe spinal condition characterized by the complete anterior displacement of a vertebra, which typically occurs at the lumbosacral junction (L5–S1). This extreme form of spondylolisthesis results in serious spinal instability, often followed by neurological deficits.1 Revision surgery in these cases can be complex and challenging, with little available literature to guide approaches. We describe the first pediatric use of a sacral pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) to salvage a revision spondyloptosis case with severe scoliosis.

Illustrative Case

A 14-year-old healthy adolescent female presented with a long history of hamstring tightness, significant swayback hyperlordotic posture, and bilateral extensor hallucis longus weakness. She had an L5–S1 spondyloptosis with a 80° olisthetic scoliosis (Fig. 1). Her pelvic parameters were as follows: lumbar lordosis (LL) 90°, pelvic index (PI) 66°, pelvic tilt (PT) 37°, and sacral slope 25°. She underwent a posterior spinal fusion from T3 to her pelvis with the Bohlman technique and decompression (Fig. 2). Six months postoperatively, she had a broken rod that was treated with simple rod exchange and bone morphogenetic protein augmentation. Initially she did well, but 6 months later (1 year from the index surgery) she broke both rods and developed progressive sagittal malalignment. FIG. 1.Preoperative whole-spine radiographs. To address the progressive sagittal imbalance, lumbosacral kyphosis, and associated high shear stresses, a sacral PSO was performed. This osteotomy was performed proximal to the previous Bohlman dowel, which was well fused. The dura and S1 nerve roots were identified. There was already an absence of sacral lamina. The pedicles were removed with a drill while protecting the roots with a Penfield 4. Utilizing navigation, we used an ultrasonic bone scalpel with a thin long blade to create an osteotomy across the vertebra above the dowel. Bone from the osteotomy was removed piecemeal using pituitaries, Kerrison rongeurs, and a drill (Fig. 3). The L5 vertebral body was reduced over the sacrum with a multirod construct including four rods and a kickstand rod (Fig. 4). The patient subsequently did well with good restoration of her sagittal profile. Her postoperative pelvic parameters were PI 77°, PT 52°, and LL 55° (Table 1). She had some transient L5–S1 neuropathies that resolved over time. Unfortunately, at 18 months postoperatively, a single rod in her multirod construct fractured, but there was no change in her global sagittal alignment or clinical symptoms. Her single-rod fracture was monitored without intervention. FIG. 3.Sagittal CT images demonstrating planned resections of PSO (dotted line) and sacral dome osteotomy (solid line). This case illustrates a complex symptomatic spondyloptosis and olisthetic scoliosis. A sacral PSO in contrast to a revision lumbar PSO for restoration of sagittal alignment can be utilized as a surgical salvage technique with the use of the kickstand rod. A sacral PSO is a potentially feasible option in pediatric patients to ensure a stable construct for fusion, correction of deformity, and, ultimately, prevention of any progressive neural element compromise.

Discussion

Observations

Spondyloptosis is a condition that generates significant shear force at the site of displacement, which makes surgical correction and stabilization challenging.2 Current surgical treatments for spondyloptosis include posterior spinal fusion, anteroposterior fusion, and sacral PSOs, which specifically aim to correct spinal alignment and aid in the mechanical stability of the spine.3,4 Additionally, multirod constructs and reduction techniques have improved outcomes by reducing the risk of instrumentationre failure while optimizing the sagittal balance.5,6 These surgical options are important because there is a high risk of instrumentation failure due to the complexity of this deformity.3,7

Scoliosis has been identified in 43% of cases of spondylolisthesis in male soldiers.8 Guo et al. reported that 56% (28/50) of adolescent patients with dysplastic spondylolisthesis had concomitant scoliosis.9 Crostelli and Mazza separated spondylolisthesis and scoliosis into three categories.10 They stated that scoliosis and spondylolisthesis can be separate entities. Scoliosis can also be a spasm in response to the spondylolisthesis. The final category is an olisthetic scoliosis. The rotational deformity of the spondylolisthesis results in the uneven base and resultant scoliosis.10 Guo et al. recommended a treatment algorithm for cases of scoliosis and spondylolisthesis, treating them as separate conditions if the scoliosis is not at the lumbar level.9 Sudarshan et al. reported on 4 cases of spondyloptosis and scoliosis. The scoliosis resolved in all cases with surgical management of the spondyloptosis.11 Srivastava et al. also reported a case of high-grade spondylolisthesis and scoliosis.12 In their case, the scoliosis resolved after L4–S1 posterior spine instrumentation and fusion.12 A literature search was performed in the PubMed database on February 2, 2026, using the keywords “scoliosis” and “spondyloptosis.” Twelve articles were found, and none reported treatment of spondyloptosis and concomitant scoliosis. The current report is novel for describing a case of spondyloptosis and severe scoliosis that required surgical management of both conditions.

PSO is a procedure used to correct sagittal deformities of the spine, particularly in the lumbar or thoracic regions.13 Originally indicated for the treatment of ankylosing spondylitis, PSO is now performed for a variety of conditions including degenerative diseases and spondylolisthesis.14,15 During this procedure, a wedge-shaped section of the vertebral body along with posterior elements is resected to shorten the spine and correct the sagittal deformity.15,16 PSO is often combined with other osteotomies or resection techniques to improve correction, especially in multilevel deformities or more severe cases. Safaee et al. described a case of using an S1 PSO for a 47-year-old female with a grade 4 spondylolisthesis at L5–S1 that was previously fused.4 Safaee et al. noted a 23%–27% neurological risk from this procedure.4 An S1 PSO can correct for pelvic imbalance due to lumbosacral deformities. We conducted a PubMed search using the keywords “spondyloptosis” and “pedicle subtraction osteotomy” and found 1 result, which was a technique review. This is the first case of an S1 PSO performed for L5–S1 spondyloptosis with severe scoliosis in a pediatric patient.

Lessons

Deformities involving the sacrum, such as this patient’s spondyloptosis, necessitate a more robust fixation, such as through multirod constructs and kickstand rods to maintain alignment and avoid future complications such as instrumentation failure. There are limited reports about the use of sacral PSO for spondyloptosis. However, it proves to be a valuable approach in complex revision surgeries where other techniques may not yield the desired correction and sagittal balance.
