The efficacy of ionomycin - induced artificial oocyte activation in preventing fertilization failure in an ICSI cycle: an eight - year retrospective study
Authors: Ming Li, Nan Zhang, Yuehao Sun, Jin Huang, Qin Li, Junsheng Li, Ping Liu, Rong Li
Published: 2025

Background

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) technology was a major breakthrough in assisted reproductive technology and is used successfully to treat male infertility caused by oligoathenoteratozoospermia [1, 2]. The application of ICSI has also achieved good results as a re-treatment for cases that experienced fertilization failure in conventional in vitro fertilization (IVF) [3–8]. Unfortunately, although ICSI is a highly effective solution for fertilization failures in conventional IVF, ICSI is also associated with a failure rate of 1−5% [9, 10]. Artificial oocyte activation (AOA) procedure was first applied in 2006 to address this effect [11].

AOA may facilitate fertilization and is performed by the addition of calcium ionophores or the application of electrical stimuli; these treatments may achieve normal fertilization and prevent fertilization failure following ICSI [12]. At present, the AOA procedure is mainly used for patients with previous fertilization failure following ICSI (ICSI-failure) and cycles with severe teratozoospermia [9, 13–15]. In our clinical work, we also observed that immotile sperm derived from thawed testicular tissue significantly elevated the risk of fertilization failure in ICSI (in 55 cycles, 39 (70.91%) cases experienced fertilization failure (unpublished data)); a scenario in which AOA can also be used.

Although many studies have reported that AOA may reduce the possibility of fertilization failure following ICSI and can result in healthy babies, its efficacy has been a topic of some debate [16–19]. A prior review indicated that the efficacy of AOA was confirmed [20], whereas another review suggested that both the efficacy and safety of AOA remained controversial [21]. However, this review did not include one randomized controlled trial conducted in 2018, which showed that AOA could significantly improve clinical outcomes in cycles with previous ICSI fertilization failure and with sperm malformations [22]. In the study by Fawzy and colleagues, the total live birth rate (LBR) was 36%; this was comparable to that observed generally in ICSI cycles and much higher than that in the control group (LBR = 18%). This study suggested that AOA was effective in reducing the incidence of fertilization failure following ICSI and can achieve a normal LBR. However, there were some limitations to this previous study; first, this study did not separately analyze the effects of AOA when used for different clinical reasons; for example, there was no comparison between the results of cycles with previous ICSI-failure and cycles involving severe teratozoospermia. Second, this study reported a LBR of 36% in the AOA group, and noted that half of these AOA cycles may not require the use of AOA because 18% of cases produced a live birth using ICSI alone (the control group). Therefore, it is possible that AOA may have been over-used in this particular study and it may be important that we avoid the excessive use of AOA. Third, the LBR in the AOA group was comparable to that for general ICSI cycles, thus suggesting that AOA could completely avoid fertilization failure and obtain a good clinical outcome; this seems too perfect and warrants further investigation.

Therefore, there are several questions that need to be answered: whether AOA is effective; whether AOA exerts differential effects when used for different clinical scenarios, including cycles with a history of failed fertilization after ICSI, cycles involving severe teratozoospermia, and cycles involving immotile post-thaw testicular sperm (sperm-immotile); whether the excessive use of AOA exerts adverse effects on cycles that could achieve fertilization by ICSI alone; and whether AOA could produce a healthy baby after overcoming ICSI fertilization failure. To address these issues, we designed a retrospective analysis cohort study in a single infertility center; using sibling oocytes from the female patient for each case, half of the recovered oocytes underwent AOA (half-AOA) and the other half underwent conventional ICSI (c-ICSI) alone.

Methods

Study population and design

This was a retrospective cohort study involving 267 AOA cycles at Peking University Third Hospital from October 2015 to December 2022. Using sibling oocytes from the female patient for each case, half of the oocytes recovered underwent AOA and the other half underwent c-ICSI. Of the 267 cycles, 240 cycles underwent half-AOA due to the high possibility of fertilization failure after ICSI. These 240 cycles served as the subjects of this study. They were either diagnosed with severe teratozoospermia or sperm-immotile, or were from patients who had experienced ICSI-failure (defined as a fertilization rate ≤ 30% with at least five oocytes injected). It is important to note that in the present study, the primary indications for initial ICSI application in the previous ICSI-failure group were as follows: oligozoospermia/asthenospermia with normal sperm morphology (96 cases), and utilization of fresh testicular spermatozoa with normal sperm morphology (3 cases).

Study definitions

Based on the fertilization results, the included cycles were separated into three groups: (1) when the 2PN rate was ≤30% following AOA, the cycle was recognized as invalid and was assigned to an AOA-invalid group; (2) when the 2PN rate was > 30% following AOA and the 2PN rate was ≤ 30% after c-ICSI, the cycle was recognized as valid and was assigned to the AOA-effect group; and (3) when the 2PN rate was > 30% after AOA and the 2PN rate was > 30% after c-ICSI, then the cycle was recognized as unnecessary and assigned to an AOA-unnecessary group.

Embryos were defined as day 3 cleavage-stage embryos with ≥4 cells and ≤30% fragmentation. Embryos that developed to the blastocyst stage were scored using the Gardner grading system [23]. Blastocysts were defined by blastocysts reaching a score of ≥3 BB. The cumulative LBR (c-LBR) was calculated as the ratio of the number of first delivery cycles (>24 weeks of gestation, including fresh and frozen embryo transfer cycles) with at least one live birth, to the number of oocyte retrieval cycles. The delivery of a singleton, twin, or other multinomial gestation was recorded as one live birth event. The conservative c-LBR was defined by the assumption that patients who did not resume treatment were presumed to have no live birth outcome. This metric was calculated as the number of cycles with live births divided by the total number of oocyte retrieval cycles [24]. Only one patient without a live birth did not return to treatment in this study; therefore, the conservative c-LBR was used in this study.

Clinical protocol

Women underwent controlled ovarian hyperstimulation under a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist or GnRH antagonist protocol. Ovarian follicle development was monitored based on serum estradiol (E2) levels and transvaginal ultrasonographic measurements. When at least one follicle reached a mean diameter of 18 mm and the E2 concentration exceeded 500 pg/ml, 10,000 units of urinary human chorionic gonadotropin [hCG] (Serono, Aubonne, Switzerland) were administered before ultrasonography-guided oocyte retrieval. Luteal support was initiated on the day after oocyte retrieval using60 mg of progesterone [25]. Between 36 and 38 hours after the administration of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), oocytes were retrieved and placed in a humidified incubator at 37 °C (type 3131; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), 6% CO2, in G-IVF PLUS™ (Vitrolife, Goteborg, Sweden) medium for at least 1−2 hours, after which the cumulus cells were stripped, and ICSI was performed. After ICSI, the surviving oocytes were combined. Subsequently, under a stereomicroscope with 10 × magnification (SMZ1000, Nikon, Japan), the oocytes were split “equally” and “without bias” (potentially differing in quantity by one oocyte between groups) to the AOA group and the non-AOA group. Embryologists only selected the surviving oocytes and did not evaluate them in detail, aiming to reduce any potential bias in the quality of oocytes between the two groups. In short, the surviving oocytes after ICSI were evenly divided into two Petri dishes containing the same medium, one for the AOA and the other for the ICSI alone (the non-AOA group). For AOA, oocytes were transferred into 10 μmol/L of ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in G-IVF PLUS™ and were cultured for 5 minutes. Subsequently, the oocytes were thoroughly rinsed with G-IVF PLUSTM medium and incubated for 30 minutes. Following this 30-minute incubation period, the oocytes were transferred into 10 μmol/L of ionomycin dissolved in G-IVF PLUS™ medium for 5 minutes again. Finally, the AOA oocytes were thoroughly again rinsed with G-IVF PLUS™ medium and were transferred to G-1 PLUS™ (10128; Vitrolife) medium and cultured in a 6% CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C, until fertilization examination.

Normal fertilization was identified by the presence of two pronuclei and two polar bodies 16 −18 hours after insemination. Either one or two embryos were transferred on day 3 or days 5−6 after oocyte retrieval. Serum hCG concentration was measured approximately 2 weeks after embryo transfer (ET) and clinical pregnancy was defined as the presence of a gestational sac on ultrasound examination at approximately 5 weeks after transfer. Supernumerary embryos were vitrified on day 3 or were further cultured to the blastocyst stage and then vitrified.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables are described as the number of cases and percentages [n (%)], and comparisons were made using Pearson’s chi-squared test. In the logistic regression model of this study, the three categories of indications for AOA application (previous ICSI-failure; severe teratozoospermia; sperm-immotile) were all incorporated as independent binary covariates for statistical analysis. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated to evaluate the effects according to the indications for AOA was applied (previous ICSI-failure; severe teratozoospermia; sperm-immotile) on fertilization results (AOA-invalid, AOA-effect, and AOA-unnecessary) using multinomial logistic regression, with or without adjustment for confounding factors (including female age, female BMI, male age, infertility type, and infertility cause). In order to identify whether AOA could ultimately deliver live infants after solving the fertilization problems, binary logistic regression was employed to assess the effects of AOA indications on cumulative live birth, with or without confounding factors (including female age, female BMI, male age, infertility type, and infertility cause) adjusted in the AOA-effect group. To investigate the effects of the excessive use of AOA (ICSI without AOA can also achieve fertilization), we compared fertilization and embryo development between the AOA group and c-ICSI group in the AOA-unnecessary cases. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Of the 240 patients, 197 cycles achieved normal fertilization following AOA part and 68 cycles following c-ICSI part. There were a higher number of normal fertilization cycles in the AOA group when compared to the c-ICSI group (P<0.001). Discussion

Main findings

The findings of this study indicate that the AOA procedure may potentially prevent the occurrence of ICSI fertilization failure. However, for patients who experienced previous ICSI-failure, AOA may be relatively ineffective. In cases where ICSI fertilization failure is caused by sperm-related factors (severe teratozoospermia, sperm-immotile), AOA appears to be more effective. Nevertheless, when considering the final c-LBR, even if AOA can overcome the fertilization failure caused by sperm factors, its effect on such patients remains rather limited, as the c-LBR still remains at a relatively low level.

Our analysis detected a higher number of normal fertilization cycles following AOA when compared to c-ICSI and demonstrated that AOA was generally effective in avoiding the occurrence of fertilization failure following ICSI; this is consistent with previous study [17, 19, 21]. In addition, there were 131 (54.58%) AOA-effect cycles with a 2PN rate for AOA oocytes >30%, and the 2PN rate for c-ICSI oocytes was ≤30%, thus confirming that AOA did prevent the occurrence of fertilization failure after ICSI in these patients. Furthermore, we identified 66 AOA-unnecessary cycles, thus indicating that these cycles did not require AOA, and that ICSI alone also led to successful fertilization. Notably, AOA may not be fully able to prevent the occurrence of fertilization failure, as demonstrated by the identification of 43 AOA-invalid cycles.

We performed multinomial logistic regression on the three groups of infertile patients undergoing AOA to investigate the factors that might influence the result of AOA. We found that AOA tended to be ineffective for cases with previous ICSI-failure, but was effective for cases involving severe teratozoospermia and sperm-immotile in order to avoid fertilization failure.

In order to investigate whether AOA ultimately produced live infants after solving the fertilization problem, we analyzed only AOA-fertilized embryos transferred in 109 AOA-effect cycles without fertilized embryos from c-ICSI, finally resulting in 32 (29.36%) cycles to obtain live births. Fifteen (45.45%) cycles resulted in live birth in cases who had previously experienced fertilization failure after ICSI, six (16.67%) cycles with sperm-immotile, and 11 (27.50%) cycles with severe teratozoospermia. Logistic regression demonstrated that previous fertilization failure was more likely to result in live birth, while sperm-related defects maybe less likely to result in live birth.

In the AOA-unnecessary group, the rate of blastocyst formation in the AOA component was lower than for c-ICSI (non-AOA), thus indicating that the excessive use of AOA may have potential adverse effects on embryonic development when ICSI can achieve normal fertilization.

Interpretation

Previous studies have discussed the efficacy of AOA to prevent ICSI fertilization failure; however, these results were inconsistent [10, 16–19, 21, 22, 26]. The most noteworthy of these previous studies was the 2018 RCT study, which found that AOA was effective and significantly improved the rates of live birth when compared with ICSI alone [22]. In this study, of 240 “half-AOA” cycles, we found that 197 cycles achieved normal fertilization following AOA compared to 68 cycles following c-ICSI; therefore, AOA may effectively avoid the occurrence of fertilization failure for patients with ICSI-failure, patients with severe teratozoospermia, and cycles of sperm-immotile.

Many studies have shown that fertilization failure is associated with phospholipase Cζ (PLCζ) deficiency in sperm. The main reason is that the defective PLCζ cannot trigger the characteristic oscillatory pattern of intracellular calcium ions (Ca2⁺). These oscillations play a crucial role in activating specific molecular pathways within the oocyte and promoting the resumption and completion of meiosis [27–29]. In addition, studies have also found that AOA is effective in preventing fertilization failure caused by phospholipase Cζ (PLCζ) deficiency in sperm [10]. In this study, among the 99 “half-AOA” cases involving patients with ICSI-failure, the number of fertilization cycles in the AOA part reached 76, while the number of fertilization cycles in the c- ICSI part was 40, and the number of fertilization cycles in the AOA part was significantly higher than that in the c-ICSI group (P < 0.001). Therefore, AOA is effective for patients with ICSI-failure. However, in the multinomial logistic regression model, our study findings indicate that for patients with ICSI-failure, AOA appears to be ineffective. This is because, among these patients, more outcomes presented as “AOA-invalid” and “AOA –unnecessary”. With regards to the “AOA-invalid” outcome, if the fertilization failure cycle was caused by calcium oscillation defects (mainly due to phospholipase Cζ (PLCζ) deficiencies in sperm), AOA may be effective; however, if fertilization failure was caused by other factors (mainly oocyte activation defects) [10, 30–32], AOA may have resulted in the “AOA-invalid” situation. Thus, it can be seen that some of the reasons underlying fertilization failure in patients with ICSI-failure stems from oocyte-related factors. With regards to oocyte-related activation defects, some study points out that the use of alternative AOA initiators that are capable of inducing meiotic resumption and maturation-promoting factor (MPF) inactivation may successfully solve the problem. Such agents include WEE2 complementary RNA, N,N,N',N'-tetrakis (2 - pyridylmethyl) ethane - 1,2 - diamine (TPEN), cycloheximide, and roscovitine [10]. Therefore, for patients with ICSI-failure, if feasible, relevant molecular tests should be carried out on patients to clarify the possible causes of activation defects, and corresponding activation strategies should be adopted accordingly. If the cause of fertilization failure cannot be determined, the adoption of “half-AOA” is recommended. It is also worth noting that in the fertilization failure cycles following ICSI, we did encounter the “AOA-unnecessary” outcome. In these cycles, normal fertilization can be achieved by using c-ICSI alone. One possible reason for this is that the oocytes were not fully matured in the previous oocyte retrieval cycle, while in this cycle, the patient may have been in a better physiological ovulation cycle, or that a modified ovarian stimulation protocol and appropriate trigger timing had been adopted. All these factors may have increased the fertilization rate [10].

For cycles involving cases with severe teratozoospermia and sperm-immotile, although we observed the situations of “AOA-invalid” and “AOA-unnecessary”, this study indicates that AOA is actually effective in such cycles. This phenomenon is likely attributed to the association between fertilization failure and abnormal calcium oscillations within these cycles. It is worth noting that the c-LBR in these cycles was low. Why is the c-LBR relatively low in patients with sperm-related factors (severe teratozoospermia, sperm-immotile)? Many studies indicate that sperm not only play a dominant role in the process of sperm-oocyte fertilization but also serve a crucial function during post-fertilization embryonic development [33, 34]. In patients with severe teratozoospermia, there is a correlation between sperm head malformation and genetic abnormalities [35]. Some genetic abnormalities may not only cause fertilization failure but also potentially impair subsequent normal embryonic development, even if normal fertilization is achieved [35]. In summary, sperm with severe teratozoospermia may not only harbor defects that lead to fertilization failure but also be associated with subsequent embryonic developmental impairment. While the AOA procedure may overcome fertilization obstacles, it fails to address issues related to embryonic development, ultimately resulting in a relatively low c-LBR in this patient population. In treatment cycles using immotile thawed testicular sperm (sperm-immotile), accumulating evidence has confirmed that the freeze-thaw process inevitably induces damage to a subset of sperm, and may even result in their demise—persistent immobility of testicular sperm following thawing typically indicates that the sperm sample has sustained severe freeze-thaw injury with impaired functionality. These functional defects may not only compromise the fertilization process but also exert adverse effects on subsequent embryonic development [36]. Although AOA procedure can overcome fertilization obstacles caused by sperm damage, it fails to thoroughly ameliorate subsequent embryonic developmental impairment. This maybe constitutes a key factor contributing to the relatively low c-LBR in treatment cycles using sperm-immotile. While the specific mechanisms underlying these phenomena require further in-depth investigation, our study findings have shown that when considering the c-LBR, it was clear that the efficacy of AOA was limited for patients with sperm-related defects.

There may also be a situation in which AOA procedure is being over-used when ICSI can achieve normal fertilization alone; thus, it is important that we take steps to prevent the overuse of AOA. A previous RCT, reported by Fawzy and colleagues, found that the LBR was 36% in the AOA group, while the LBR was 18% in the non-AOA group, thus indicating that 50% of live birth cycles in the AOA group potentially did not need AOA and that these cycles utilized AOA procedure when they did not need to do so. In this study, out of 197 normal fertilization cycles, 66 (33.50%) were classified as AOA-unnecessary. Therefore, in clinical practice, the excessive use of AOA procedure indeed merits our attention. Although there are currently no relevant studies on the excessive use of AOA in humans, an experimental study in mice previously reported that excessive application of the AOA procedure elevates the frequency and amplitude of calcium oscillation patterns. This perturbation disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism homeostasis, triggering an increase in reactive oxygen species production and a reduction in adenosine triphosphate levels. Ultimately, these cascading effects enhance DNA damage and impair blastocyst formation [37]. In this study, the rate of blastocyst formation following AOA was lower than that in the c-ICSI group in the AOA-unnecessary group, thus indicating that the overuse of AOA may represent a potential risk for embryonic development. When comparing the AOA-effect and AOA-unnecessary groups, patients with sperm-immotile or severe teratozoospermia were more likely to result in an AOA-effect outcome; however, this was not the case for patients who experienced previous fertilization failure after ICSI. Thus, when considering cycles with a history of ICSI-failure, it is necessary to consider the potential overuse of AOA, and consider adopting a half-AOA strategy.

The safety of AOA is another general concern. A previous study found that 6.3% of babies had congenital malformations following AOA, although this was not significantly difference from a c-ICSI group with a 4.1% rate [38]. Most previous studies found no detectable congenital defects in AOA babies [39–41]. Recently, two meta-analyses from Shan et al. (2021) [42] and Long et al. (2020) [43] and confirmed these results by finding no significant difference in terms of congenital malformations risk between AOA babies and c-ICSI babies. Another follow-up study on children conceived via AOA has shown that this procedure does not affect the neurodevelopment of early childhood, suggesting that no definite safety risks have been identified for the technology [44]Current data from human studies demonstrate that AOA has no reported harmful effects on pre - and post - implantation development, live births, epigenetic effects, or altered methylation patterns of imprinting genes [10, 45]. In this study, there was no health issue detected in any of the AOA babies, including 34 babies (30 singlets, 4 twins) in the AOA-effect group. In the AOA-effect group, the babies were only fertilized by AOA procedure, thus indicating that there was no significant health risk attributed to AOA. However, due to the limited number of cases from human studies, the safety of AOA babies remains undefined and further study is necessary [10].

Clinical implications

The rational application of AOA in clinical practice is a complex issue. The findings of our present study provide some specific operational guidance to facilitate the management of AOA. We need to adopt different AOA strategies for different patients. First, when testing conditions are available, for patients with ICSI-failure, molecular tests such as genetic testing should be carried out to clarify the cause of fertilization failure. If the fertilization failure is caused by calcium oscillation-related defects (such as defects in the sperm-related PLCZ1 gene), AOA can be used in its entirety. If the cause is clearly related to the oocytes, other corresponding oocyte activation strategies can also be adopted. If the cause of fertilization failure cannot be determined, “half-AOA” is recommended. If there are no relevant molecular testing methods, “half-AOA” is recommended for patients with Previous ICSI-failure. Second, for patients with severe teratozoospermia, relevant genetic testing can also be carried out, as obvious head malformations are associated with genetic abnormalities [35]. Third, for patients with sperm-related factors, it is recommended to use AOA in its entirety. However, although AOA can overcome fertilization failure, the c-LBR is low. Therefore, for patients with teratozoospermia, sperm with a close to normal morphology should be selected as much as possible. For cycles involving thawed testicular sperm, motile sperm should be selected for ICSI injection as much as possible. At the same time, it is also recommended to use sperm motility stimulants to assist in identifying motile sperm. The standard for selecting sperm quality should not be lowered just because AOA procedure can effectively prevent fertilization failure caused by sperm-related factors. Fourth, since the excessive use of AOA may affect embryo development, the over-application of AOA procedure should be avoided.

The uncertainty of the efficacy and safety of AOA is another issue that must be faced in clinical practice. Clearly AOA cannot completely prevent fertilization failure; we found that cases of previous ICSI-failure did not respond effectively to AOA. Further study has found that for certain cycles (severe teratozoospermia; sperm-immotile), even if AOA solves the problem fertilization failure after ICSI, it cannot achieve a normal LBR. The safety of babies born by AOA still remains uncertain. In short, AOA is not a routine technique in an assisted reproductive laboratory. Due to the uncertainty and safety of AOA procedure, it is necessary to fully consider the actual treatment situation, be fully informed with the patient, and obtain their consent before carrying out AOA procedure. Informed consent for implementing AOA procedure should encompass the following aspects. First, there is a high probability that AOA will prove ineffective in preventing fertilization failure. Second, even when AOA resolves the problem of fertilization disorders, it cannot ensure the normal development of subsequent embryos and a live-birth outcome (noting a low live-birth rate, especially among patients with sperm-related factors). Third, it is advisable that patients undergo relevant genetic tests to attempt to identify the cause of fertilization failure, and subsequently initiate targeted treatment. Fourth, babies born through AOA may inherit the fertilization-disorder-related defects of their parents. For instance, if fertilization disorders occur due to genetic defects in the parents, these defects might be transmitted to the offspring via AOA procedure. Thus, genetic counseling is of particular necessity and importance. The consultation content may include, but not be restricted to, inquiries such as whether it is essential to examine parental genetic factors contributing to fertilization disorders, and whether pre-implantation genetic testing (PGT) technology could impede the inheritance of these abnormal genes. Take, for example, a case in which fertilization disorders are caused by a mutation in the father’s PLCZ1 gene; this mutation could potentially be passed on to the offspring through the AOA procedure, thus suggesting that PGT technology might be utilized to block the inheritance of this PLCZ1 mutation. Fifth, the impact of the AOA process on offspring remains unclear. Therefore, the safety of the AOA procedure itself is uncertain, and patients need to be fully informed of this.

In clinical practice, AOA appears to be predominant with which to prevent fertilization failure after ICSI; therefore, AOA is still recommended for use when fertilization failure after ICSI may occur. It is important that the patient is kept fully informed at all times.

Study implications

The reasons for ICSI fertilization failure are complex, and may be due to sperm or egg factors [46]; AOA may only partially solve fertilization failure after ICSI [47]. In this study, we found that AOA may solve the fertilization problem where fertilization failure after ICSI was caused by sperm-related issues, but could not achieve a good LBR outcome. To address these issues, relying solely on clinical laboratory morphological study and case analysis is very limited and the ultimate solution still needs to rely on relevant basic study [10, 48].

Interestingly, 52.63% of cycles with previous ICSI-failure were fertilized by ICSI alone in this study, thus indicating that previous ICSI-failure may be related to ovarian condition, the procedures used for controlled ovarian hyperstimulation, and trigger time, in the previous cycle. In summary, fertilization failure after ICSI is a particularly complex problem, which may be a congenital genetic issue or caused by assisted reproductive therapy. Further study is required to investigate these possibilities further.

Strengths and limitations

This study had the following advantages: first, it performed half AOA and half c-ICSI treatment on each patient’s oocytes; thus, it was easy to compare the effect of AOA with that of c-ICSI. This strategy helped us to identify which cycles specifically required AOA and which did not require cycles of AOA as normal fertilization can be achieved merely by using c-ICSI. Second, we analyzed the efficacy of using AOA in different situations (previous ICSI-failure; severe teratozoospermia; and sperm-immotile). Previous study did not focus on these issues but noted that the efficacy of AOA may vary depending on the reasons for its application. However, our study still had some limitations that need to be considered. First, our study was a single-center retrospective study and involved a limited number of cases; we acknowledge that the data present here is only suggestive and more case collection and more in-depth study are needed. Second, all of the AOA cases in our study used ionomycin to activate oocytes; thus, our findings only applied to ionomycin activation. In addition, due to limited data availability, this study did not directly calculate the live birth rate; instead, the cumulative live birth rate was used as a surrogate indicator, which also constitutes one of the limitations of this research.

Conclusions

In this study, we showed that AOA was effective in preventing fertilization failure after ICSI; however, the effect of AOA was limited, as evidence by the c-LBR, and this was particularly the case for patients with sperm-related defects. The use of AOA remains a complex clinical problem. The effects of AOA differed for cases with previous ICSI-failure; severe teratozoospermia, and sperm-immotile. Half-AOA is recommended for cases with previous ICSI-failure and AOA for all eggs is recommended for patients with severe teratozoospermia and sperm-immotile. It is important to avoid the excessive use of AOA as much as possible and half-AOA may be used for uncertain situations. To summarize, AOA is not a routine procedure in IVF laboratories; it is therefore necessary to fully inform patients of the efficacy and potential risks of AOA before commencing treatment.
