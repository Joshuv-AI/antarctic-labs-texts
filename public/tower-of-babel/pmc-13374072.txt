Nutritional disparities between urban poor and non-poor Malaysian children under five in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Minn Yin Choo, Lucy Chai See Lum, Muhammad Irfan Abdul Jalal, Kai Shen Ooi, Callie Foo, Azriyanti Anuar Zaini, Nik Mazlina Mohammad, Muhammad Yazid Jalaludin, Amnasewary Ramakrishnan, Amnasewary Ramakrishnan, Nurshadia Samingan, Jing Yuan Wong, Li Ren See, Zheng Wei Lee, Shi Ting Loo, Vaneeda Sok One, Sheng Hong Siah, Nisha Adriana Joachim, Nurul Afifah Kamruldzaman, Kai Voon Heng, Chin Aun Tan, Abqariyah Yahya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid urbanisation in Asia has widened intra-urban nutritional disparities, particularly among urban-poor children during periods of compromised food security.MethodsThis cross-sectional study aimed to investigate nutritional disparities among children under five in an urban locality during the COVID-19 pandemic. We recruited 717 children aged 6–59 months from July 2020 to June 2023 across three sites in Selangor, Malaysia. Preterm or small-for-gestational-age children and those with incomplete data were excluded. Dietary intake was assessed by 24-h recall adapted from WHO-IYCF and FAO-WDD tools. Anthropometric indices were weight-for-age, length/height-for-age, and weight-for-length/height z-scores. Malnutrition indicators were WHO-defined underweight, stunting, wasting, and overweight. Associations of sociodemographic factors and minimum dietary diversity with malnutrition indicators were analysed.Results357 urban-poor and 360 urban non-poor children were recruited. Urban-poor children had lower mean weight and height z-scores than urban non-poor children (-0.77 [SD 1.20] vs -0.46 [SD 1.17], p < 0.001; -0.73 [SD 1.06] vs -0.46 [SD 1.21], p = 0.002). Underweight prevalence was higher among urban-poor children (13.2% vs 7.0%, p = 0.009). Among children aged 6–23 months, dietary diversity scores were lower in the urban-poor group (mean DDS 5.02 vs 5.60, p < 0.001), with lower breastfeeding prevalence (47.5% vs 59.8%, p = 0.007). Intake of sugar-sweetened beverages and foods was higher in both urban-poor age groups (52.9% vs 30.0%, p < 0.001; 80.4% vs 59.7%, p = 0.003). In multivariable models, urban poverty increased the odds of underweight (adjusted OR 1.99 [95% CI 1.19, 3.32], p = 0.008). Higher birth weight z-scores were protective against underweight (adjusted OR 0.65 [95% CI 0.47, 0.91], p = 0.013) and wasting (adjusted OR 0.68 [95% CI 0.49, 0.94], p = 0.018), while higher birth length z-scores were protective against stunting (adjusted OR 0.65 [95% CI 0.49, 0.87], p = 0.004). Achieving minimum dietary diversity was protective against underweight (adjusted OR 0.46 [95% CI 0.23, 0.92], p = 0.029).ConclusionUrban-poor children had lower anthropometric z-scores, higher underweight prevalence, poorer early-childhood dietary diversity, and higher intake of sugar-sweetened beverages and foods. Targeted socioeconomic and nutrition support is needed for urban-poor families.

Background

Malnutrition, which comprises undernutrition (underweight, stunting, wasting) and overnutrition (overweight and obesity), remains an underlying contributor to under-five mortalities in children in developing countries. Most countries are off track to meet the World Health Assembly global nutrition targets for 2025, which include reducing childhood stunting and wasting, preventing an increase in childhood overweight, and improving other maternal and infant nutrition indicators, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) target 2.2 for 2030, which aims to end all forms of malnutrition by 2030, with recent data revealing an upward trend in under-five stunting [1, 2]. The prevalence of stunting in Malaysia increased from 17.7% in 2015 to 21.2% in 2022, while wasting remained high at 11% in 2022 [2].

At the same time, Asia has the fastest rate of urbanization; by 2030 and 2050, 60% and 68% of the world's population, respectively, will dwell in urban areas [3]. In major cities, intra-urban disparities predominate; the poorest urban disadvantaged children, as opposed to 'urban advantage', have a higher rate of malnutrition and under-five mortality than their rural counterparts [3]. Studies across countries in Asia consistently demonstrate that poor dietary diversity and household food insecurity were associated with stunting, with the highest rates observed in children below 24 months of age [4]. The 2022 Malaysian National Health and Morbidity Survey observed that approximately 70% of children under-five reside in urban regions [2]. Among Malaysian children residing in low-cost flats in the capital city, the prevalence of food insecurity, malnutrition, and deprivation are above the national average [5]. This challenging condition was further aggravated during and immediately after the COVID-19 pandemic. A Malaysian pilot study conducted by Rahman et al. revealed that the COVID-19 impact on employment and business was significantly associated with stunting and wasting in children under-five [6].

Infants and young children are among the groups most vulnerable to nutritional deprivation, especially during the commencement of complementary feeding, which needs to be timely, adequate, and appropriate to meet the nutritional demands of a rapidly growing child. Dietary diversity is crucial to increase the probability of receiving sufficient essential nutrients during the critical period when brain development is at its peak. Dietary diversity, defined as the number of food groups consumed within a given reference period, is the key dimension of dietary quality and several studies have demonstrated its positive association with nutritional status, micronutrient density and nutritional adequacy in young children [7–10]. In different settings, dietary diversity can be impaired in times of disaster or pandemic illness outbreaks when food access is affected. Nutritional disruption during the first 1000 days of life can impair a child’s cognitive and physical development, leading to a lifelong impact on health, future educational attainment, and risk of non-communicable diseases later [11].

In this study, we compared the growth and nutritional status of children under-five from poor and non-poor households using enrollment data from a community project conducted over a three-year period. The specific objectives were to 1) compare the sociodemographic characteristics, growth and nutritional status between urban-poor and non-poor children, 2) compare dietary intake between the two groups, and 3) identify the sociodemographic determinants of nutritional status. This study was conducted during the period coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. The studies were conducted by the Child Health Clinic (CHC), Universiti Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC), a tertiary university hospital, in collaboration with the Yayasan Generasi Gemilang, a local non-governmental organization, and the Ministry of Health, Malaysia.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study using the enrolment data collected between July 2020 to June 2023 drawn from a project “Dietary Diversity of Young Children during COVID-19 Outbreak: A longitudinal study.” The extended recruitment period reflected the operational constraints of community-based face-to-face enrolment during the COVID-19 pandemic and early post-pandemic period, including movement restrictions, phased site access, and the logistics of recruitment across clinical and community sites.

The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, ID: NCT04447209, date of registration 27 September 2021. Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Research and Ethics Committees of Universiti Malaya Medical Center (MREC ID no: 2020410–8500) and the Ministry of Health, Malaysia (RSCH ID-24–00439-YN1) and registered under the National Medical Research Registry, Malaysia (NMRR ID-24–00395-OSQ). The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. The study was conducted at three urban sites in Selangor, a state on the west coast of Peninsular Malaysia that forms part of the Greater Kuala Lumpur metropolitan region. The first study site was the Child Health Clinic (CHC) located within Universiti Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC) in Kuala Lumpur, the second was PPR Lembah Subang 1, a government low-cost public housing site under Malaysia’s People’s Housing Programme (locally known by the Malay acronym PPR), and the third was a maternal and child health clinic in Kelana Jaya. The second and third sites are in an urban township within Petaling Jaya city, Selangor.

Study participants

We recruited healthy children aged six to 59 months through a nutritional program promoted through leaflet distribution and facilitated by volunteers from the residential committee. Written consent was provided by caregivers. Children from all three study sites were categorized into different income groups. Specifically, the urban-poor group was defined as families whose incomes fell within the lowest forty percent of the economy (B40) according to the national economic statistics [12]. In contrast, the urban non-poor group comprised families with incomes above the B40. Participants who were born preterm, defined as less than 37 completed gestational weeks, small-for-gestational-age (SGA) with a birthweight less than 2.5 kg, and had incomplete birthweight or gestational age data were excluded. The exclusion criteria were necessary to assess the effect of socioeconomic demographic factors on nutritional status without the confounding influence of prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction, or missing baseline data. The results were reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting in Observational Studies in Epidemiology—Nutritional Epidemiology (STROBE-Nut) guideline [13].

Data collection

Data collection methods are summarized below, with further methodological details available in a previously published paper from this study [16]. We obtained a written informed consent from caregivers before enrolment. A questionnaire consisting of sociodemographic information, including ethnicity, and a 24-h dietary recall history was administered by trained research assistants in a face-to-face twenty-minute interview during study enrolment. As part of the nutritional program, trained researchers offered nutritional counselling through individualized recommendations, group education, culinary demonstrations, and recipes, and assisted illiterate caregivers in completing the questionnaire. To mitigate the potential misreporting and misclassification biases, data discrepancies were cross-verified by the research team, and in cases where discrepancies were identified, participants were contacted via telephone to clarify. The collected data on food groups was also verified by a second operator to ensure consistency.

The 24-h food recall questionnaire was adapted from Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) practices by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Women's Dietary Diversity (WDD) scale as per the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations [17, 18]. The food types included in the questionnaire were adjusted according to the local settings. For children aged six to 23 months, the dietary diversity score was calculated using eight food groups, with a minimum dietary diversity (MDD) defined as a score of at least five as per the IYCF guideline [17]. For children aged 24 to 59 months, dietary diversity scores (DDS) are based on the WDD scale, comprising nine food groups, with the MDD similarly defined. The food groups for the two respective age groups are shown in Table 2. The questionnaire included sugar-sweetened beverages and foods (SSBF) containing free sugar based on the operational definition utilized in our previous research [16]. Parental weight and height parameters were obtained during the face-to-face interview for parents who attended the nutritional program. Weight and height were measured using the SECA digital weighing scale and SECA 217 stadiometer, respectively. For parents who were not present, information on weight and height was obtained based on recall from the accompanying parent. Outcome measures

Anthropometric measurements were performed by trained research team members at study enrolment. Weight was measured using a SECA scale and height as the recumbent length for children up to two years of age using a SECA 417 mobile measuring board, while the height of children aged two to five years was measured using a SECA 217 stadiometer (SECA, Hanover, USA). Identical equipment was used at the three research sites. Research assistants were trained according to the WHO Child Growth Standards: Training Course on Child Growth Assessment [19]. Data were interpreted using the WHO z-score definitions; weight-for-age z-score (WAZ), length/height-for-age z-score (LAZ/HAZ), and weight-for-length/height z-score (WLZ/WHZ) [20].

Primary outcomes were the WHO-defined indices of malnutrition; underweight (weight-for-age z-score < −2 SD), stunting (length/height-for-age z-score < −2 SD), and wasting (weight-for-length/height z-score < −2 SD) [20]. Potential confounders were birthweight, socio-economic factors, and dietary intake, identified a priori from previous literature [21]. All data were recorded on paper-based clinical report forms and stored in a filing system before being transferred to digital formats.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS software (Version 25, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). For the baseline characteristics of study participants, the data were descriptively summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normal data for continuous variables or frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Shapiro-Wilks test and Fisher’s coefficient of skewness (threshold ± 1.96) were used to assess the numerical data’s normality. Variables with 5% to 40% missing data were imputed using fully conditional specification, with 20 imputed datasets generated [22]. The imputation model included study group, age group, sex, ethnicity, anthropometric z-scores, parental body mass index, parental education, parental employment, breastfeeding status, number of children in the household, dietary diversity variables, and malnutrition indicators. Continuous variables were imputed using regression-based methods, while categorical variables were imputed using logistic, multinomial, or ordinal logistic models according to their measurement scale. Estimates were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules [23].

Comparisons of sociodemographic factors and nutritional status between the urban-poor and urban non-poor groups were performed using the independent t- or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson’s Chi-Square test for categorical variables. Simple logistic regression analyses were used to screen predictors of malnutrition indices, using a more liberal p-value cut-off (p < 0.25) to avoid unintentional exclusion of clinically significant predictors. For multivariable logistic regression analyses, only predictors with p-values < 0.25 in the simple logistic regression analyses were included. For the maternal and paternal education predictors, they were collapsed into three categories, namely no formal or primary education, secondary education, and tertiary education, including undergraduate and postgraduate education, to reduce sparse-data problems in the regression models. Significant predictors in the regression analysis were determined using sequential model-building strategies, in which predictors were forced into the multivariable model at pentry = 0.05 and premoval = 0.10. Ethnicity was summarised descriptively but was not included in the logistic regression models since it was considered an upstream sociocultural characteristic that may influence anthropometric outcomes indirectly through urban poverty status and dietary practices. Its role would therefore be more appropriately examined in a separate mediator or pathway-based analysis, rather than by routine adjustment in the present regression models. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Of these, 360 children were from the urban non-poor group, in which 296 were under two years and 64 were aged between 24 and 59 months. Birth weight z-scores did not differ significantly between the two groups (median −0.52 [IQR 1.02] vs −0.44 [IQR 1.10], p = 0.389). However, enrolment weight z-scores were significantly lower in the urban-poor group than in the urban non-poor group in the age-combined analysis (−0.77 [IQR 1.20] vs −0.46 [IQR 1.12], p < 0.001). Although birth length z-scores were significantly higher in the urban-poor group, enrolment height z-scores were significantly lower in the urban-poor group than in the urban non-poor group in the age-combined analysis (−0.73 [IQR 1.06] vs −0.46 [IQR 1.21], p = 0.002). Males (56.1%) predominated in the urban non-poor group, while females were more common in the urban-poor group (53.5%) (p = 0.011). The ethnic distribution also differed significantly between the two groups, with a higher proportion of Malay children in the urban-poor group and a higher proportion of Chinese children in the urban non-poor group (p < 0.001). The urban non-poor group had a considerably greater prevalence of breastfeeding among children under two years of age (59.8% vs 47.5%, p = 0.007).

Significant disparities were observed between the two groups in parental education, employment, body mass index (BMI), and number of children in the household. Compared with the urban non-poor group, mothers in the urban-poor group had lower educational attainment, with a higher proportion having secondary education as their highest completed level (66.6% vs 13.4%) and a lower proportion having tertiary undergraduate education (26.5% vs 74.1%) or tertiary postgraduate education (0.6% vs 12.5%) (p < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed among fathers, with a higher proportion in the urban-poor group having secondary education as their highest completed level (77.4% vs 20.5%) and a lower proportion having tertiary undergraduate education (14.1% vs 66.8%) or tertiary postgraduate education (0.3% vs 11.6%) (p < 0.001). Maternal employment also differed significantly between the two groups, with a higher proportion of mothers in the urban-poor group not working (69.7% vs 15.1%) (p = 0.001). Maternal BMI distribution differed significantly between the two groups, with obesity being more common among mothers in the urban-poor group (33.3% vs 14.1%) (p < 0.001). Paternal employment differed significantly between the two groups, with fathers in the urban-poor group having higher proportions of self-employment (25.4% vs 18.1%) and unemployment (6.6% vs 0.3%) (p < 0.001). Paternal BMI distribution also differed significantly, with obesity being more common among fathers in the urban-poor group (35.0% vs 18.0%) (p = 0.001). The urban-poor group also had a larger household size, with a median of three children compared with two children in the urban non-poor group (p < 0.001).

Similarly, the prevalence of stunting (11.0% vs. 8.4%) and wasting (17.9% vs. 10.7%) was also higher in the urban-poor group, although the differences were not statistically significant. Discussion

Our research highlights the intra-urban health disparity among children and the socioeconomic determinants associated with a higher prevalence of suboptimal growth, malnutrition, and poorer-quality diets among urban-poor children. Higher birth weight and length were protective against underweight and stunting, respectively, while larger household size increased the odds of stunting. Achieving minimum dietary diversity decreased the odds of being underweight. To our knowledge, our study is one of the few comprehensive studies in the region during the COVID-19 pandemic to examine and compare urban-poor and non-poor under-five children in an upper-middle-income country, integrating analyses of growth, dietary intake, and nutritional status.

Despite comparable birth weight z-scores, urban-poor children had lower enrolment weight z-scores and nearly twice the prevalence of underweight compared with their urban non-poor counterparts. Suboptimal growth was evident in early childhood among the urban-poor, and these deficits widened and became more pronounced in later childhood, as demonstrated by our age-stratified analyses of WAZ. Similar observations from the National Family Health Survey in India showed lower mean WAZ among urban-poor children, a gap that was persistent across every age interval in children under-five, indicating the socioeconomic disparities between the two groups within the same urban setting [15].

Using the 2022 Malaysian National Health and Morbidity Survey as a contextual national benchmark, the prevalence of wasting was higher in the urban-poor group (17.9% vs. 11.0%), whereas the prevalence of underweight (13.2% vs. 15.3%) and stunting (11.0% vs. 21.2%) was lower than the national estimates [2]. This comparison should be interpreted cautiously because the present study was conducted in selected urban sites in Selangor over a recruitment period spanning the pandemic and early post-pandemic period, whereas the national survey provides population-level estimates. Furthermore, wasting, which indicates acute or recent nutritional deprivation, could be linked to the high prevalence of food insecurity and child hunger, 92.5% and 58.4% respectively among the urban-poor community [16]. In contrast, stunting represents a more chronic form of nutritional deprivation. The urban population has better access to healthcare and food aids, which may mitigate the chronic growth faltering. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of this study, capturing children aged 6 to 59 months in varying stages of nutritional compromise, during a period of socioeconomic instability, may reflect how an immediate crisis (wasting) could co-exist with long-term structural poverty (stunting). The higher prevalence of wasting compared to that of stunting among the urban-poor population may reflect a greater exposure to recent nutritional changes rather than prolonged chronic deprivation. The intra-urban growth deficits observed in our study were underlined by their socioeconomic circumstances. Urban poverty was found to be an independent risk factor, increasing the odds of underweight by almost twofold in our study. Victora et al. show that children from the poorest households in the low and middle-income countries (LMICs) suffer from a greater burden of undernutrition compared to their wealthier counterparts [24].

Our results align with findings from the UNICEF Children Without study conducted pre-pandemic in the low-cost housing of Kuala Lumpur, in which both datasets highlight a substantial burden of malnutrition among the urban-poor children. Unexpectedly, the prevalence of malnutrition among the urban-poor children in our study was lower than that reported in UNICEF’s Children Without study, despite our study being conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic [5]. A possible explanation is that the urban-poor children in our study predominantly came from the PPR group that had been actively engaged in community interventions and food assistance programs whereby these initiatives may have partly safeguarded them from the nutritional impact of the pandemic.

Parental education appeared protective in the univariable analysis, this effect was lost after adjustment, suggesting that the adverse economic impact of the COVID-19 pandemic may have diminished the protective effect of education. The National Family Health Survey in India found maternal education represented a major contributor to nutritional inequality among children under-five [15]. Larger household size has been demonstrated as a contributory risk factor for malnutrition, whose effect may be exaggerated during the pandemic, due to resource dilution, thereby increasing household food insecurity.

Noticeably, urban-poor and urban non-poor children ate differently. This difference should be interpreted in relation not only to socioeconomic position, but also to the ethnic and cultural diversity of Malaysian food practices. Not surprisingly, starchy staples (e.g. rice, noodles, and rice porridge) ranked highest among the consumed foods since they are the pillars of Asian cuisine and are often eaten together with protein sources and vegetables. Notwithstanding that, plant proteins are integral to the local cultures, but the consumption of this food group ranked low in the overall group, an indication of a transition away from a traditional to a more industrialized diet. In contrast, children from South Asia whose customary diets consist mostly of flatbread with lentils are more aligned with the EAT-Lancet Commission's planetary health diet recommendations [25]. We also noted a low rate of consumption of internal organs, which are rich in proteins, iron and other micronutrients, again signifying a departure from traditional norms. Future work could examine whether ethnicity influences child nutritional outcomes directly through dietary practices or indirectly through socioeconomic position and food access.

Consistent with most research, urban non-poor children had a higher dietary diversity score and greater consumption of vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables. The MDD of urban-poor children under two in our population (69·2%) falls below the pre-pandemic urban rate of 78·0% [26]. To make matters worse, the high unemployment among urban-poor households, reported by UNICEF's Families on the Edge, further compromises the affordability of quality foods, adversely affecting dietary diversity [27]. Dietary diversity emerged as a protective factor against underweight in the multivariable statistical model, consistent with reports from Ethiopia and India, underscoring the need to maintain access to varied and nutritious foods, especially during pandemic disruptions [28, 29].

During the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown, the SEANUT II study reported a positive shift in food consumption among Malaysian urban children, i.e., increased daily intake of fruits and vegetables and reduced consumption of SSBF. However, the urban poor group in our study did not experience these dietary improvements [30]. Our study shows that overall intake of free sugar increased with age and in older urban children, even surpassing essential components of the diet such as animal and plant proteins, fruits, and vegetables. Unsurprisingly, the trend is more pronounced among the urban-poor, consistent with a previous report on this group of children in the pre-pandemic era [31]. Rapid urbanization is associated with the proliferation of food deserts replacing traditional markets with supermarkets and neighborhood convenience stores selling ultra-processed foods and SSBF [32]. Cash food aid subsidies that can only be redeemed for packaged foods with barcodes will, by default, limit fresh vegetables and fruits. Popular fallacies that fruit cordials are made from fruits and thus contain healthy vitamins and minerals, and chocolate malt drinks are healthy energy drinks for children, must be addressed by appropriate authorities. Furthermore, breastfeeding was lower in prevalence among the urban-poor compared to their non-poor counterparts, consistent with current literature whereby disadvantaged socioeconomic status adversely affects the initiation and duration of breastfeeding. Key factors include higher maternal education being associated with greater initiation of breastfeeding, while informal employment, food insecurity and non-supportive working conditions hinder breastfeeding practices [33].

We identified several study limitations. First, the findings should be interpreted in light of the study setting and design. Although participants were recruited from both clinical and community sites, the study was conducted in selected urban sites in Selangor and may not be generalisable to all Malaysian children under five, particularly those in rural areas or other states. Second, because this was a cross-sectional analysis without a pre-pandemic comparator group, the observed nutritional disparities cannot be causally attributed directly to COVID-19. Rather, the pandemic and early post-pandemic period should be understood as the broader socioeconomic and public health context in which the data were collected. Although ethnicity was described in the baseline characteristics, the present analysis did not formally evaluate causal pathways linking ethnicity, socioeconomic position, dietary diversity, and anthropometric outcomes. Third, overestimation or inaccurate recollection of the 24-h diet by caregivers may potentially bias the findings. While three-day or seven-day dietary diaries or quantitative analysis of dietary intake may reduce recall variability and improve accuracy, such data collection may not be feasible during the COVID-19 pandemic due to the lockdown restrictions and logistical constraints. Nevertheless, all children who were excluded from the primary analysis still received comprehensive, individualized food-based dietary recommendations with closely monitored progress, comparable to those in our study participants, consistent with our primary aim of advocating for healthy dietary habits and growth promotion among young children.

Our recommendations are, first, there is an urgent need to prioritize the identification of faltering growth in early infancy and to implement targeted nutritional interventions to prevent further deterioration, with emphasis on traditional home-cooked foods and healthy diets among pregnant and breastfeeding mothers. Second, breastfeeding should be promoted up to two years and beyond in line with the WHO’s recommendation for optimal health for both baby and mother, alongside high-quality antenatal care [34]. The COVID-19 pandemic should serve as a lesson in preparation in the event of future recurrence, as it highlights the fragility of early childhood growth in vulnerable populations. Third, food aid in the form of food baskets consisting primarily of convenient items such as biscuits, sugar, cooking oil, and flour should be replaced with fresh poultry, fruits, and vegetables or less processed foods such as canned sardines, meat, and beans. These may be useful in emergency food security situations while retaining adequate nutritional value and dietary diversity.

Some of these strategies have been incorporated into The National Plan of Action for Nutrition Malaysia (NPANM) III 2016–2025, led by the Nutrition Division of the Ministry of Health Malaysia, and included nutritionists from other established institutions. It is a master plan that outlines the framework for action to address food and nutrition challenges for all strata of the population in the country [35]. In addition, we recommend a strategic multistakeholder approach that advocates for home food availability and positive parental modelling of dietary behaviors to address the household-level of malnutrition and to ensure its sustainability. Other stakeholders, including family medicine physicians, pediatricians, obstetricians and town planners, should also be at the forefront for the implementation of a national-level coordination of nutrition strategies. Dietary guidelines should evolve as a response to the specific public health challenges of the triple burden of malnutrition which is amplified by the pandemic.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to demonstrate the gap in socioeconomic position, dietary practices, and growth between two groups of young children, revealing the deprivation of urban-poor children that coincides with the COVID-19 pandemic when a nationwide movement control order was implemented. This silent epidemic of public health concern occurring in the wealthiest state of Malaysia highlights the urban paradox. In line with the SDG to end hunger, achieve food security, improve nutrition and promote sustainable agriculture, faltering growth in early childhood should be prioritized for routine identification and at-risk children should be directed to early intervention and sustainable food-based nutrition programs [36].
