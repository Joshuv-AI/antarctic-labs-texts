Optimization and validation of traction performance for an eight-axle heavy-haul locomotive during gradient starting
Authors: Yongfeng Liu, Xueliang Wu, Pingle Ma, Xuancheng Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To validate the traction performance of two newly developed eight-axle heavy-haul electric locomotives prior to their formal commissioning, a restart test was carried out. The test required each locomotive to individually initiate movement of a 4,700-ton freight train on a track with a 13 promille gradient. During the initial trial, Type B locomotive experienced traction failure, underscoring the need for prompt resolution of its traction deficit. The systematic resolution of this issue involved three key steps. First, a co-simulation framework tailored for Type B was constructed, integrating multibody dynamics and adhesion control models, and the framework was verified by experimental data to ensure reliability. Second, based on the validated framework, optimized solutions were developed—including improved electrical compensation parameters and a novel adhesion control strategy based on the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF). Simulation results showed that this optimized control scheme increased the overall traction performance of Type B by approximately 10% under low constant-speed conditions. Third, to further reduce starting resistance, driver operating procedures were refined through in-depth resistance analysis. Following the comprehensive optimization of both the control strategy and operating protocols, Type B locomotive successfully passed the gradient restart test. This study delivers a practical, model-driven solution for maximizing traction utilization, offering a critical reference for heavy-haul locomotive engineering.

Introduction

AC heavy-haul locomotives have become the preferred solution for overland freight transport due to their energy efficiency, environmental benefits, low energy consumption, high traction power, and high speed. Around 2006, China began mass-producing eight-axle AC freight locomotives, including two Harmony series models, which were widely deployed. These locomotives feature a single axle power of 1200 kW, an axle load of 25 tonnes, and a starting tractive effort of 760 kN, enabling them to haul 4500 tonnes on gradients of up to 13 promille under all weather conditions. They have since played a vital role in supporting China’s economic growth.

With continued economic development and increased emphasis on energy conservation and emissions reduction, rail freight volume has grown significantly. To further improve transport efficiency, single-locomotive haulage capacity has been raised from 4500 to 4700 tonnes. Based on the original platforms, a new generation of AC electric locomotives—Type A and Type B—has been developed. While maintaining a 25-tonne axle load, the new models offer an increased starting tractive effort of 785 kN and achieve a starting adhesion coefficient of 0.4 (defined as the ratio of wheel-rail tractive force to normal force). Their maximum design speed remains 100 km/h. The locomotive draws electric power from an AC grid. This power is first stepped down in voltage by the main transformer and then converted into direct current via a rectifier. The DC power is subsequently inverted into three-phase AC with adjustable voltage and frequency to drive three-phase asynchronous traction motors. The output torque from each motor is transmitted to the corresponding wheelset through a single-stage spur gear system, which provides speed reduction and torque amplification. Each traction motor is equipped with an independent inverter and adhesion control unit, enabling individual axle control.

Prior to being commissioned for service, these locomotives are required to undergo rigorous hill-start performance validation. Specifically, each locomotive must demonstrate its capability to restart a 4700‑tonne train from a complete standstill on a gradient of 13 promille. This test criterion is derived from actual operational demands on China’s high‑density rail networks, where trains may need to stop and restart on graded sections due to signalling constraints, track maintenance, or other operational contingencies. The ability to restart autonomously under such conditions is critical; failure to do so would necessitate rescue operations, resulting in significant disruptions to network efficiency and schedule reliability.

Hill-starting tests impose stringent requirements on locomotive traction performance, attributable to the following factors: (a) After a train stops, substantial braking force must be maintained to prevent rollback due to grade-induced inertia. (b) Steep gradients introduce significant resistance to motion. (c) During gradient startup, the locomotive must overcome not only the inherent resistance but also the initial static inertia of the train. (d) Brake release is accompanied by delays in air pressure propagation through the train’s pneumatic system, leading to highly nonlinear train resistance. This effect is exacerbated by coupler slack between the locomotive and trains, which induces oscillatory forces. Consequently, hill-starting tests provide critical validation of locomotive dynamic performance and wheel–rail adhesion characteristics under demanding operational conditions.

Related work

During Gradient starting, the traction force output of locomotives is subjected to extremely high demands. In the pursuit of maximizing locomotive traction, advanced adhesion control systems play a critical role. By continuously monitoring dynamic parameters such as wheel creep speed and acceleration, these systems enable precise regulation of the locomotive’s traction force output1. Various control strategies have been applied in this field, including combined correction methods2, PID control algorithms3,4, sliding mode control5, disturbance observers6, predictive control7, and unscented Kalman filter8. Although the algorithms vary in form, their core objective remains consistent: to achieve optimal utilization of traction force under complex and varying wheel-rail contact conditions. However, due to the extremely high requirements for system reliability and stability, the industry still tends to adopt well-established strategies such as combined correction methods and PID control in practical applications2, to address the nonlinear characteristics of wheel-rail contact relationships and the challenges posed by all-weather locomotive operation.

In recent years, with the rapid development of data-driven technologies and the deepening understanding of wheel-rail adhesion mechanisms, a number of intelligent adhesion control algorithms have gradually entered the experimental and application stages9,10. It should be noted that the realisation of adhesion coefficient is limited by friction coefficient. Therefore, under low-adhesion conditions, sanding devices are commonly used to actively increase the friction coefficient between the wheel and rail, thereby effectively enhancing the traction performance. However, during Gradient starting, as the locomotive needs to apply extremely high traction forces at near-zero speeds, even when sanding is applied, the adhesion-enhancing medium struggles to penetrate the wheel-rail contact patch. Instead, it tends to accumulate in front of the contact patch, leading to increased running resistance, which adversely affects the locomotive’s Gradient starting performance.

Besides adhesion control, axle load transfer is another key factor constraining the exertion of locomotive traction11,12. Particularly under heavy-haul locomotive Gradient starting conditions, the increase in traction force intensifies the axle load transfer phenomenon. Specifically, axle load transfer refers to the redistribution of axle loads under dynamic conditions when high traction forces are applied, causing the locomotive body to pitch and resulting in uneven axle load distribution13. If the same traction force is applied to all axles under such conditions, the lighter-loaded wheelsets are prone to slipping or skidding due to excessive adhesion coefficients, thereby triggering traction force reduction mechanisms. Meanwhile, the heavier-loaded wheelsets, due to their lower adhesion coefficients, fail to fully utilize the adhesion potential between the wheel and rail. The overall consequence is that the total traction force of the locomotive cannot reach an optimal level.

To address this issue, traction forces are typically allocated differentially based on the actual axle loads of each wheelset. That is, under the same driver’s handle notch, the motors corresponding to heavier-loaded wheelsets output higher torque, while those corresponding to lighter-loaded wheelsets output lower torque. This approach aims to maximize adhesion utilization for each wheelset and is often implemented by setting electrical compensation coefficients14. However, since all traction motors of the locomotive are of the same model and performance, the setting of electrical compensation coefficients must remain within the power redundancy limits of each motor, which, to some extent, constrains the adjustability of this method. Moreover, the effective implementation of this strategy relies on an important prerequisite: the friction conditions at each wheel-rail interface must be consistent or highly similar to ensure that the electrical compensation coefficients function as intended. Under Gradient starting conditions, as the locomotive needs to apply extremely high traction forces, the axle load transfer phenomenon reaches its peak, thereby imposing higher demands on the accuracy and condition-matching of the electrical compensation coefficients.

Based on their adjustment characteristics, the setting of electrical compensation coefficients can be categorized into fixed and dynamic modes. Although simulation studies suggest that dynamic coefficient methods coupled with adhesion control can achieve higher adhesion limits15,16, in engineering practice, the easily implementable fixed coefficient method is more widely adopted13.

Recently, some researchers have begun to focus on the gradient-start problem of heavy-haul locomotives. For instance, Wu et al.17 investigated the gradient-start issue for 24-axle heavy-haul locomotives in China. Longitudinal train dynamics simulations were conducted to evaluate the impact of five driving strategies on train speed and coupler forces. The results indicated that while locomotives could successfully start on gradients, the maximum coupler force between vehicles exceeded the yield strength of the couplers. Zhu et al.18 developed a longitudinal dynamics model for twelve-axle locomotives. This model was employed to study the influence of train air pipe pressure on the success of gradient-starting for a 10,000-ton train hauled by two twelve-axle locomotives on a 11.4 promille gradient, leading to recommendations for optimizing the timing of brake release during gradient-starting. Much of the research on longitudinal train dynamics during gradient starting has focused on coupler forces, operational safety, and numerical modelling19. However, comparatively less attention has been directed toward the systematic maximization of tractive effort during locomotive startup.

In summary, the gradient starting of heavy-haul locomotives represents a systemic engineering challenge involving the integrated management of adhesion control, axle load transfer, electrical compensation, and driver operation. This challenge is further accentuated by the ongoing increase in design adhesion coefficients—exemplified by the development of locomotives with coefficients as high as 0.42 in China—raising the critical question of how to reliably utilize such high adhesion potential in practice. While previous research has often focused narrowly on algorithmic sophistication in areas like axle load transfer or adhesion control, reliability remains the foremost priority in industrial application. To bridge this gap, this paper presents a systematic study that integrates electrical compensation, adhesion control, and operational strategies. Based on gradient-starting tests of newly manufactured locomotives, detailed field data, and numerical simulations, a series of coherent optimization measures were proposed. Practice has confirmed that the proposed approach effectively resolves gradient-starting failures and ensures reliable locomotive operation, offering a more systematic and engineering-oriented solution compared to existing methods.

Content of the paper

This paper conducts a systematic investigation into the gradient-starting performance of two type of heavy-haul locomotive under a 4700-ton freight load on a 13 promille gradient. Test results indicated that while Type A locomotives successfully initiated movement, Type B locomotives experienced failure. Through analysis of field test data, the causes of failure were systematically summarized. To implement targeted optimization, a multi-body dynamics model of the locomotive incorporating adhesion control was developed and validated via experimental tests. Key optimization measures were implemented in three areas:

1) The electrical compensation coefficient of Type B locomotive was optimized through axle load transfer calculations, following a reliability‑based tuning principle. This approach integrated theoretical analysis with field‑test data to strategically reduce compensation within the adhesion limit, thereby enhancing system durability under sustained operational conditions.

2) To mitigate frequent adhesion loss under low-speed and high-traction conditions, the unscented Kalman filter was employed to achieve high-precision estimation of its core parameter—the reference speed. Based on this estimation, the traction control strategy under low-adhesion conditions was thereby optimized.

3) By analyzing operational behaviors in failure cases, the driver’s Gradient-starting procedure was refined according to the principle of minimal resistance.

These three improvements functioned synergistically, ultimately enabling the successful gradient-starting of the Type B locomotive.

Gradient starting tests

Types A and B locomotives differ significantly in their mechanical structure. Notably, despite being produced by different manufacturers, both locomotives utilize the same traction control system. Therefore, variations in traction systems have minimal impact on the findings discussed in this paper. Before gradient-starting testing, both locomotives were configured with identical electrical compensation coefficients, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Specifically, when the axle 1 of the locomotive acted as the guiding axle during traction, torque reduction averaged 3% for wheelsets 1 and 5, and 1.5% for wheelsets 2 and 4. Conversely, torque increased for the remaining four wheelsets.

Electrical compensation coefficient setting.

The pre-determined gradient starting operation procedure before the test was as follows: (a) After the entire train came to a standstill on the 13 promille gradient, the traction force was gradually decreased to zero. Simultaneously, the train pipe pressure was reduced to approximately 120 kPa to ensure secure stopping on the gradient. (b) After maintaining zero speed for a while, the locomotive initiated hill-starting by gradually increasing the traction force to about 750 kN and maintaining it for about 5 s. After confirming the absence of wheel slip, the train pipe pressure was increased and the train brakes were released. (c) When the entire train has attained initial forward momentum (typically above 1 km/h), the traction force should be progressively increased to its maximum level to enhance the train’s acceleration.

Type A locomotive

Figure 2 illustrates the gradient-starting process of the Type A locomotive, plotting time against traction force (left axis) and speed (right axis). In Fig. 2(a), the traction force reaches about 750 kN, taking roughly 50 s to achieve 1 km/h. By 70 s, it rises to 785 kN, enabling a successful start under normal adhesion. Speed oscillations during startup result from longitudinal dynamics caused by: (1) asynchronous brake release due to air transmission delays, and (2) coupling slack between vehicles. After a 4700 t test, the load was increased to 5000 t. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the locomotive achieved 775 kN and started successfully, but acceleration slowed significantly, reaching only 1.6 km/h after 120 s. Overall, the Type A locomotive demonstrated full success in gradient-starting.

Type B locomotive

Figure 3 presents the traction force and speed characteristics of a Type-B locomotive during a 4,700-ton gradient starting operation. The measured data demonstrate that the peak traction force attains approximately 750 kN. Approximately 30 s after initiation of the starting procedure, a progressive reduction in traction force is observed, accompanied by significant velocity oscillations. These combined effects ultimately lead to failure of the gradient starting maneuver. Notably, the acquired test data are unsigned in type. Speed fluctuations centered around zero indicate instances of reverse motion, implying longitudinal oscillation of the locomotive. This phenomenon was distinctly observed by onboard technical staff during testing. For failure analysis, Fig. 3 (b) illustrates the traction force distribution among axles in the leading section. The experimental data reveal that wheelset 1 initially demonstrates a declining traction force trend (marked by the red dashed box in Fig. 3(b)). Correspondingly, the secondary section of the locomotive exhibits a comparable behavior, with the guiding axle (axle 5) displaying initial reductions in traction force.

Since the first and fifth axles experienced premature traction reduction, inducing fluctuations in aggregate adhesion force and resulting in gradient-starting failure, whereas Type A locomotives achieved successful starts under identical conditions, technical analysis confirmed that the Type B locomotive’s failure originated from a mismatch between axle load transfer and electrical compensation. Based on experimental data, the electrical compensation coefficients for the Type B locomotive were re-optimized from initial values of − 3%, − 1.5%, + 1.5%, and + 3% to final values of − 4%, − 3%, + 3%, and + 4%, with subsequent verification testing of gradient-starting performance.

The operator increased the traction command to a maximum of 785 kN; whereas upon reaching traction stabilization, an adhesion force reduction occurred at 50 s, culminating in another gradient-starting failure. This sequential failure pattern demonstrates persistent incompatibility between the locomotive’s axle load transfer and the revised electrical compensation parameters. Analysis of potential failure-inducing factors

The identified failure mechanisms are summarized as follows:

1) During initial gradient-start attempts, wheelsets 1 and 5 showed reduced traction. After adjusting electrical compensation, wheelsets 1 and 5 had a 1% decrease while wheelsets 3 and 7 increased by 1.5%. In the next attempt, wheelsets 3 and 7 then showed traction loss, indicating wheel-rail adhesion was near friction limits. Fine-tuning compensation may have caused uneven adhesion and wheel slip. Further analysis of axle load transfer and coefficient re-optimization is needed.

2) During gradient-start wheel slip, excessive adhesion force reduction limited full use of available wheel-rail adhesion. For example, Wheelset 1 traction fell below 30 kN at 32 s (Fig. 3 (b)), while the third wheelset dropped to zero at 61 s (Fig. 4 (b))—both inconsistent with actual rail conditions. The adhesion control algorithm requires optimization to maximize adhesion during gradient starts.

3) Higher traction was applied to Type B locomotives than to other types. While this improves acceleration, it raises adhesion demands. When adhesion control activates, gradient-start failure risk increases. Operational procedures need refinement.

To address these, a co-simulation model combining adhesion control and multibody dynamics was developed. This enabled re-optimizing electrical compensation and adhesion control, leading to successful Type B locomotive gradient-start tests.

Simulation and experiments

Numerical modeling and experimental validation

Numerical modeling

The multi-body dynamics models for individual Type A and Type B locomotives were established using SIMPACK software, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Each locomotive model comprises one car body, two bogies, four wheelsets, four motors, and two traction rods. The car body, bogies, and wheelsets are modeled with six degrees of freedom (DOFs), while motors are constrained exclusively to yaw DOF. Drawbars incorporate both yaw and pitch DOFs. It should be noted that the key differences between the dynamic models of Type A and Type B locomotives lie in two aspects: (1) Different suspension parameters. For instance, the primary vertical stiffness and secondary vertical stiffness of Type A locomotive are 2.4 kN/mm and 0.8 kN/mm, respectively, while those of Type B locomotive are 1.2 kN/mm and 7.8 kN/mm, respectively. It is found that Type A locomotive has a higher primary vertical stiffness but a significantly lower secondary vertical stiffness compared with Type B locomotive; (2) Type B locomotive adopts an inclined drawbar, whereas Type A locomotive is equipped with a horizontal drawbar.

Figure 6 shows the locomotive traction control logic. The driver’s command is converted into a target torque by the network control system and sent to the adhesion control unit. This unit adjusts the motor torque according to wheel–rail creep, then forwards it to the motor control unit for execution. In the model, this process is simplified: the command is directly mapped to the output torque, and torque pulsation is neglected. This is justified because high-power traction motors deliver smooth torque output, and field data confirm that pulsation is not critical to the studied gradient-start failures. Note that electrical compensation is implemented in the network control module. In contrast, the adhesion control unit executes the core signal processing task of adhesion control: the Kalman filter-based speed optimization algorithm studied subsequently in this paper.

To optimize adhesion control for Type B locomotives, an adhesion control module was integrated. The adhesion control implements a hybrid control strategy utilizing both creep velocity and acceleration. Angular acceleration of locomotive wheelsets is initially measured, enabling derivation of wheelset velocity and acceleration. Locomotive velocity is determined according to the following criterion: during traction, the minimum wheelset velocity is selected, whereas the maximum value is adopted during braking. Locomotive acceleration is subsequently computed from the derived velocity.

The wheel-rail adhesion curve exhibits three characteristic phases20,21 (Fig. 7): (i) Linear growth phase, where the adhesion coefficient increases nearly linearly with small creepage; (ii) Saturation phase, where the coefficient plateaus at its maximum value with increasing creepage; and (iii) Decline phase, where frictional heating induced by spinning beyond critical creepage reduces the adhesion coefficient, resulting in a negative Gradient. Therefore, adhesion control is required to regulate the traction force at the optimal creepage range. The typical adhesion control process under traction conditions is illustrated in Fig. 8. During normal operation, the linear velocity of the wheelset generally slightly exceeds the locomotive speed due to creep phenomena at the wheel-rail interface. When wheelset spin occurs, its linear velocity exhibits a rapid increasing trend, significantly surpassing the locomotive speed. In this scenario, the adhesion control detects wheel spin based on two primary criteria: (i) whether the velocity difference between the locomotive and the wheelset exceeds a predefined threshold, or (ii) whether the wheelset acceleration surpasses the acceleration threshold. Upon identification of wheelset spin, the adhesion control immediately executes a rapid reduction of traction motor torque and maintains this reduced torque state until the spin is effectively suppressed. Following restoration of adequate wheel-rail adhesion conditions, the system progressively recovers the output torque of the traction motors. Detailed adhesion control methodology is provided in Reference2.

Experimental validation

To validate the reliability of the dynamic model, field tests were conducted on a Type B locomotive. As shown in Fig. 9, displacement sensors were mounted between bogie frames and axle box linkages to monitor vertical dynamic displacements of primary suspension springs in real time. Acquired experimental data were compared with simulation results for model validation. The experimental procedure comprised the following steps: (1) Electrical compensation coefficients were initialized to zero to ensure uniform traction force distribution. (2) A six-locomotive consist provided braking forces to the test locomotive. 3)The test locomotive’s throttle was progressively advanced to maximum position. After traction force stabilization, the system was returned to neutral. 4)This sequence was repeated three times to ensure data repeatability.

Fig. 9Field experiment on the displacement of primary suspension.

Field experiment on the displacement of primary suspension.

In this test, the axle traction forces acquired from the locomotive traction system are derived from the motor control unit based on motor characteristic curves, rather than directly measured values. Consequently, these data inherently contain systematic errors. However, since all involved motors, associated hardware, and control software are from the same batch, these errors are consistent across different axles and exhibit high systematicity. Therefore, in the comparative analysis of traction forces conducted in this test, such errors are considered acceptable.

Figure 10(a) presents traction forces (right axis) from the traction control system and corresponding primary suspension vertical displacements (left axis). Measurements indicate compressive deflection in Axles 1 and 3 primary springs versus tensile deflection in Axles 2 and 4 under traction loading, with excellent repeatability across three trials.

Numerical simulations were conducted using the established dynamic model by applying equivalent braking forces at the coupler location and a 98-kN traction force per axle. Comparative analysis between the averaged maximum displacement data from three tests (Fig. 10(a)) and simulation results (Fig. 10(b)) revealed consistent variation trends, though simulated values were marginally higher. The identified error sources may include: inherent measurement errors in traction force estimation from motor feedback signals, discrepancies between modeled design parameters and actual locomotive parameters, and unaccounted-for flexible deformations in the rigid-body dynamics model. For engineering applications, however, the dynamic model provides sufficient accuracy. It should be noted that reliability validation was explicitly performed only for Type B locomotives. Given identical modeling methodologies for Types A and B, Type A model reliability was inferred through methodological consistency rather than direct experimental validation.

Electrical compensation parameters

The determination of electrical compensation coefficients necessitates comprehensive evaluation of the following key aspects: (1) It should align with axle load transfer trends to maximize adhesion utilization; (2) As electrical compensation alters inter-axle traction distribution, the resulting force variations must comply with transmission component reliability criteria—encompassing gears, motors, and bearings—while also considering the uniform material parameters across all axle drive systems. Excessive compensation may accelerate fatigue degradation in specific components, leading to uneven service life and increased lifecycle maintenance costs; (3) While meeting adhesion requirements, the electrical compensation coefficients should be maintained at minimal levels to mitigate disproportionate load concentrations and preserve system durability.

Based on the successful gradient-starting performance demonstrated by the Type A locomotive, the electrical compensation parameters of the Type B locomotive were optimized in reference to its adhesion characteristics.

1) According to the field test results shown in Fig. 3, wheelset 1 may experience wheel slip when its traction force exceeds 91 kN. Therefore, it is critical to ensure that the traction force of wheelset 1 never surpasses 91 kN. Let F1,max denote the maximum traction force of wheelset 1 without slip (91 kN) and F1,base denote its base traction force (98 kN). The electrical compensation coefficient for wheelset 1 (C1) is defined as the ratio of the difference between the maximum allowable traction force and the base traction force to the base traction force, i.e., Ci =(Target adjusted traction force − Base traction force)/ Base traction force. 2) For the second wheelset, let Δmn represent the axle load transfer rate of wheelset n (the ratio of axle load change to the original axle load, with positive values indicating increased axle load). Given its modest + 3.7% increase in axle load transfer (Δm2 = + 3.7%), a positive compensation coefficient is set to match the improved load-carrying capacity. 3) Based on axle load transfer calculations shown in Fig. 11, wheelset 4 requires maximum electrical compensation, given that wheelset 3 demonstrates minimal load transfer (i.e., Δm3 = min(Δm1, Δm2, Δm3, Δm4) and Δm4 = max(Δm1, Δm2, Δm3, Δm4)). Optimization of reference speed based on UKF

Under low-speed and heavy-haul operating conditions of locomotives, accurate estimation of the actual locomotive speed is recognized as a critical challenge. In such scenarios, due to the lack of direct support from effective speed sensors, the locomotive speed is generally inferred indirectly based on rotational speed information from individual wheel-sets. However, under high traction forces, significant creep phenomena are frequently observed in the wheel-sets, and wheel spin may occur, leading to severe distortion in the speed estimation derived from wheel-set rotations. The accuracy of locomotive speed estimation directly affects adhesion control performance. Unreliable speed information can result in degraded adhesion control, which in turn causes fluctuations in traction force output and compromises operational stability. The Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) is commonly employed in adhesion control for optimal traction regulation4,25 and estimating adhesion coefficients26,27. In this paper, it is applied to locomotive speed estimation. To account for wheel-rail creep characteristics, the filter is specifically tailored to assign the highest weighting to the axle with the lowest rotational speed during traction.

Considering the difficulty in achieving strict synchronization of wheel-set rotational speeds between the front and rear vehicles of the locomotive, the rotational speeds of four wheel-sets from a single locomotive section are selected as observational inputs in this study. This approach enables the development of a speed estimation strategy suitable for low-speed and heavy-haul conditions. During gradient-starting operations of Type B locomotives, the wheelset system is subjected to substantially increased traction torque loads. When subjected to low-adhesion rail conditions or external disturbances, the wheelset exhibits a rapid surge in transient angular acceleration, triggering abrupt adhesion force reduction, which in turn reduces rail surface adhesion utilization efficiency and compromises gradient-starting success rates. The proposed optimization strategy comprises two key aspects:

Driver operations

The success rate of heavy-haul locomotive starting on gradients is significantly affected by the operational proficiency of the crew. For a 4700-tonne freight train, the gradient resistance is calculated using the formula:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_R}=\left( {{G_t}+{G_l}} \right) \times g \times i$$\end{document}

According to the literature28, the unit starting resistance is reported to be 5 N/kN for locomotives and 3.5 N/kN for freight vehicles. Under simultaneous starting conditions, the starting resistance of the freight vehicles is calculated as 3.5 × 4700 × 9.8/1000 = 161.21 kN, while the locomotive exhibits a starting resistance of 5 × 200 × 9.8/1000 = 9.8 kN. The total resistance during synchronous acceleration is determined to be 624.26 + 161.21 + 9.8 = 795.27 kN, which exceeds the maximum designed tractive effort of the locomotive. Consequently, the initial resistance must be minimized to ensure effective gradient starting.

The optimized operational strategy is composed of two key aspects: 1)The buffering mechanism of the couplers should be utilized by engaging regenerative braking during gradient stops to maintain coupler compression. This allows sequential acceleration of freight vehicles during starting, thereby substantially reducing peak starting resistance. 2)Since the gradient resistance component (624.26 kN) represents the dominant opposing force, the tractive effort should be maintained at 50–60 kN (e.g., 680 kN) above this value before brake release. The corresponding state transition of the coupler during the optimized hill-start process is illustrated in Fig. 20. Collectively, these measures provide an adequate traction margin to prevent rollback while mitigating the excessive longitudinal forces and adhesion loss typically associated with aggressive starting efforts. It should be emphasized that when the coupler compression strategy is implemented, the locomotive brake must remain applied and should not be released until tractive force is established.

Post-optimization gradient starting tests

Following the implementation of the three aforementioned optimization measures, subsequent gradient-starting tests were conducted with a 4700-ton freight train. As illustrated in Fig. 21, the experimental data demonstrate that during the gradient-stopping phase, the traction force was initially reduced to zero by the operator. At approximately 107 s, regenerative braking was applied by the locomotive, achieving a maximum braking force of 92 kN, which was maintained for approximately 20 s before being discontinued. This procedure effectively facilitated the compression of train couplers.

During the gradient-starting process, the traction force was increased to 685 kN prior to the release of the air brake, whereupon train movement was initiated. The traction force was subsequently incrementally raised to 702 kN and 725 kN by the operator. Minor fluctuations in traction force were observed during this process; however, their magnitude was negligible and stability was rapidly restored. As the locomotive speed gradually increased, the traction force was further enhanced, ultimately reaching its peak value of 785 kN. The gradient-starting test was thus successfully completed.

Conclusions

Two 25-ton axle-load heavy-haul electric locomotives, designed for a maximum starting traction of 785 kN, underwent gradient-starting tests (4700-ton trainload, 13 promille grade). Type A achieved 100% success, while Type B initially failed. Through numerical simulations and experimental investigations, the failure mechanisms were identified, with key conclusions as follows:

1) Type B’s gradient-starting failures stem from three core factors: mismatched axle load transfer and electrical compensation parameters, insufficient adhesion control, and excessive starting fundamental resistance.

2) Adhesion control was optimized using an Unscented Kalman Filter (for locomotive speed estimation) alongside adjusted control strategies, leading to ~ 10% improvement under low constant-speed conditions in simulations.

3) Employing regenerative braking during Gradient parking maintains coupler compression; during restart, the resulting buffer travel enables sequential (rather than simultaneous full-consist) vehicle starting, reducing fundamental starting resistance to the single-vehicle level.
