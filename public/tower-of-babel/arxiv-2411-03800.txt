Perturbational Decomposition Analysis for Quantum Ising Model with Weak Transverse Fields
Youning Li, Junfeng Huang, Chao Zhang, Jun Li

Abstract
This work presented a perturbational decomposition method for simulating quantum evolution under the one-dimensional Ising model with both longitudinal and transverse fields. By treating the transverse field terms as perturbations in the expansion, our approach is particularly effective in systems with moderate longitudinal fields and weak to moderate transverse fields relative to the coupling strength. Through systematic numerical exploration, we characterized parameter regimes and evolution time windows where the decomposition achieved measurable improvements over conventional Trotter decomposition methods. The developed perturbational approach and its characterized parameter space may provide practical guidance for choosing appropriate simulation strategies in different parameter regimes of the one-dimensional Ising model.



I
Introduction

Quantum simulation has emerged as a crucial tool for understanding complex quantum systems, with applications ranging from quantum chemistry to condensed matter physics
Childs and Su 2019
;
Cao et al. 2019
;
Altman et al. 2021
. Recent work has demonstrated significant theoretical advances in simulation algorithms
Childs et al. 2021
;
Haah et al. 2021
;
Tran et al. 2021
, while experimental achievements have shown the potential of quantum simulators in various platforms
Semeghini et al. 2021
;
Ebadi et al. 2021
;
Mi et al. 2022
, highlighting their practical significance in exploring quantum phenomena. In most physical systems of interest, the Hamiltonian can be expressed as a sum of local terms:

H=\sum_{k=1}^{M}H_{k},

(1.1)

where each
H_{k}
acts non-trivially only on a limited number of degrees of freedom. This local structure naturally leads to simulation strategies based on the Trotter-Suzuki decomposition. The theoretical foundation was established by Suzuki’s pioneering work on generalized Trotter formulas
Suzuki 1976
, further developed through quantum analysis methods
Suzuki 1997
, and extended to higher-order product formulas
Hatano and Suzuki 2005
. The most basic form of such decomposition approximates
Lloyd 1996
;
Trotter 1959
the evolution operator as:

e^{-itH}\approx\left(e^{-itH_{1}/n}e^{-itH_{2}/n}\cdots e^{-itH_{M}/n}\right)^{n},

(1.2)

where
n
is the number of Trotter steps.

Conventional quantum simulation methods typically maintain a crucial constraint: the sum of coefficients in the decomposed terms exactly matches the original Hamiltonian generating the evolution. This approach, while mathematically natural, may not always yield optimal simulation accuracy. Recent theoretical developments have further expanded our understanding of efficient product formulas and their applications
Childs and Su 2019
;
Tran et al. 2021
;
Childs et al. 2021
. Higher-order decomposition schemes have been developed to improve simulation accuracy
Hatano and Suzuki 2005
, though their effectiveness can be limited by the increasing complexity of implementation. Recent work has shown that careful consideration of the system’s structure and parameter regimes can lead to more efficient simulation strategies
Low 2019
;
Roetteler et al. 2017
;
Low and Wiebe 2018
.

A particularly important model in quantum many-body physics is the quantum Ising model with both longitudinal and transverse fields
Coldea et al. 2010
;
Sachdev 1999
;
Dutta et al. 2015
. This model not only serves as a paradigmatic example for studying quantum phase transitions but also represents a practical testing ground for quantum simulation techniques
Bernien et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
King et al. 2018
. The quantum Ising model has been extensively studied both theoretically and experimentally
Simon et al. 2011
;
Lanyon et al. 2011
;
Struck et al. 2011
, providing crucial insights into quantum criticality and many-body phenomena.

The applicability of Ising-type models extends far beyond traditional condensed matter physics. In biological physics, these models have been successfully applied to understand protein folding dynamics and to infer protein structure from sequence data
Ferguson et al. 2013
;
Morcos et al. 2011
;
Weigt et al. 2009
. In collective behavior studies, they have helped explain the emergence of coordinated motion in biological systems
Cavagna et al. 2010
. The model has also found applications in studying complex biological networks and their dynamics
Bialek et al. 2012
;
Mora et al. 2016
.

Recent developments in machine learning and artificial intelligence have further expanded the reach of Ising-type models
Carleo and Troyer 2017
;
Mehta et al. 2019
;
Torlai et al. 2018
. These connections have not only provided new computational tools for studying quantum many-body systems but have also established bridges between quantum physics and modern data science. In many of these applications, the transverse field terms appear as perturbations to the primary Ising interactions, creating a natural hierarchy in the energy scales of the system.

Building upon the perturbation composition framework, this work presented a decomposition approach that deliberately deviates from the conventional coefficient-matching constraint. We focused specifically on simulating quantum evolution under the one-dimensional Ising model, treating transverse field strength as perturbations in the expansion. Our approach exploits the natural parameter hierarchy in systems where the transverse field strength is moderate relative to the coupling strength. For certain evolution time ranges and within appropriate parameter regimes, our method achieves measurable improvements in simulation fidelity compared to conventional approaches.

Our numerical exploration mapped out the evolution of time windows across different field strength combinations, providing a comprehensive visualization of where this method offers noticeable improvements over conventional decomposition techniques. This systematic investigation not only extends recent theoretical understanding of Trotter decomposition
Childs et al. 2021
to practical quantum simulations, but also reveals the practical boundaries and limitations of our perturbational approach within the parameter space of the one-dimensional Ising model.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we review the fundamental concepts and establish our notation. Section III presents our main analysis of the perturbational decomposition method and its optimization results. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the implications and potential applications of our results.

II
Preliminary

We begin by examining the optimization of unitary evolution simulation under a single-qubit Hamiltonian:

H=\alpha\sigma_{x}+\sigma_{z},

(2.1)

where
\alpha
represents the relative strength of the transverse field.

The Suzuki-Trotter decomposition
Suzuki 1976
provides a fundamental approach to simulating quantum evolution. Its simplest form, known as the second-order Trotter formula, gives:

e^{-it\alpha\sigma_{x}-it\sigma_{z}}\approx e^{-it\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{x}}e^{-it\sigma_{z}}e^{-it\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{x}}+O(t^{3}).

(2.2)

The accuracy of this decomposition deteriorates for finite time intervals due to the non-commuting nature of
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{z}
. This motivates us to introduce an optimization parameter
\lambda
to improve the simulation fidelity.

Let
U=e^{-it\alpha\lambda\sigma_{x}-it\sigma_{z}}
denote the target evolution and
V=e^{-it\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{x}}e^{-it\sigma_{z}}e^{-it\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{x}}
represent the Trotter decomposition. The optimization problem can be formulated as:

\max\limits_{\lambda}|\textrm{Tr}(UV^{\dagger})|^{2}/2^{2}.

(2.3)

This optimization can be achieved either by direct expansion or by differentiating both sides with respect to
\alpha
and equating them:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}(e^{-it\alpha\lambda\sigma_{x}-it\sigma_{z}})

\displaystyle=\frac{de^{\Phi}}{d\Phi}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\alpha},

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}(e^{-it\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{x}}e^{-it\sigma_{z}}e^{-it\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{-it}{2}(\sigma_{x}e^{\Phi}+e^{\Phi}\sigma_{x})=\frac{-it}{2}e^{\Phi}(e^{-\Phi}\sigma_{x}e^{\Phi}+1)\sigma_{x},

(2.4b)

where
\Phi(\alpha)=-it\alpha\lambda\sigma_{x}-it\sigma_{z}
.

To proceed, we need to calculate
\frac{df(A(\alpha))}{d\alpha}
, taking into account the non-zero commutator between
\Phi(\alpha)
and
d\Phi(\alpha)
. Following
Suzuki 1997
;
Hatano and Suzuki 2005
, we briefly review the key steps:

{df(A(\alpha))}/{d\alpha}
must be expressed in terms of
A
and the commutation relation of
A
, or the ”inner derivation”:

\delta_{A}\equiv[A,].

(2.5)

It can be directly obtained from the above definition:

\displaystyle\delta_{aA+bB}

\displaystyle=a\delta_{A}+b\delta_{B},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle[A^{m},\delta_{A^{n}}]

\displaystyle=0.

(2.6b)

By induction of the power, one may obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{A^{n}}

\displaystyle=A^{n}-(A-\delta_{A})^{n},

(2.7a)

\displaystyle e^{xA}Be^{-xA}

\displaystyle=e^{x\delta_{A}}B,

(2.7b)

\displaystyle dA^{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{(A+hdA)^{n}-A^{n}}{h}=(A^{n}-(A-\delta_{A})^{n})\delta_{A}dA.

(2.7c)

Therefore,

\frac{d(A^{n})}{dA}=\frac{A^{n}-(A-\delta_{A})^{n}}{\delta_{A}}=\frac{\delta_{A^{n}}}{\delta_{A}},

(2.8)

Here, while
\delta_{A}
in the denominator appears formally ill-defined, it cancels out during the expansion of the numerator.
By Taylor expansion, Eq(
2.8
) could be generalized to

\frac{df(A)}{dA}=\frac{f(A)-f(A-\delta_{A})}{\delta_{A}}=\frac{\delta_{f(A)}}{\delta_{A}}.

(2.9)

For the special case
f(A)=e^{A}
, Eq(
2.9
) reads

\frac{de^{A}}{dA}=\frac{e^{A}-e^{A-\delta_{A}}}{\delta_{A}}=e^{A}\frac{1-e^{-\delta_{A}}}{\delta_{A}}.

(2.10)

Substituting Eq(
2.8
) and Eq(
2.10
) into Eq(
2.4
) and equating them obtain:

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\alpha}=\frac{-it}{2}\delta_{\Phi}\frac{1+e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}{1-e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}\sigma_{x}.

(2.11)

In the perturbative limit
\alpha\ll 1
, we obtain

\lambda\sigma_{x}=\frac{-it\sigma_{z}}{2}\frac{1+\exp(it\delta_{\sigma_{z}})}{1-\exp{it\delta_{\sigma_{z}}}}\sigma_{x},

(2.12)

The optimization yields the optimal parameter:

\lambda=\frac{t}{\tan(t)},

(2.13)

which provides enhanced simulation accuracy for finite evolution times.

After relabeling the parameters, we can express the unitary evolution for single-qubit rotation in a more compact form. Our perturbative method yields:

e^{-it\alpha^{\prime}\sigma_{x}-it\sigma_{z}}\approx e^{-it\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\frac{\tan t}{t}\sigma_{x}}e^{-it\sigma_{z}}e^{-it\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\frac{\tan t}{t}\sigma_{x}}+O(\alpha^{\prime 2}).

(2.14)

We compare the performance of our perturbative method with the traditional Trotter decomposition shown in Eq.(
2.2
). As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the perturbative method matches the performance of Trotter decomposition in the small
t
regime while exhibiting superior fidelity for larger evolution times
t

III
Perturbational Composition for Ising Model

Extending our previous analysis of single-qubit optimization, we now investigate a more complex system: a one-dimensional Ising chain with periodic boundary conditions under both longitudinal and transverse fields. The Hamiltonian of this system can be expressed as:

H=\sum_{k=1}^{2n}J_{k,k+1}Z^{(k)}Z^{(k+1)}+g_{k}Z^{(k)}+h_{k}X^{(k)},

(3.1)

where
J_{k,k+1}
represents the nearest-neighbor coupling strength, while
g_{k}
and
h_{k}
denote the longitudinal and transverse fields respectively. We focus on the regime where
h_{k}\ll J_{k,k+1},g_{k}
. The system satisfies periodic boundary conditions through
2n+1\equiv 1
.

This Hamiltonian naturally decomposes into four distinct components:

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}J_{2k-1,2k}Z^{(2k-1)}Z^{(2k)},

(3.2a)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}J_{2k,2k+1}Z^{(2k)}Z^{(2k+1)},

(3.2b)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2n}g_{k}Z^{(k)},

(3.2c)

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2n}h_{k}X^{(k)}.

(3.2d)

Thus, we have
H=A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}+B_{2}
. However, unlike the simpler case discussed in Section
II
, we cannot simultaneously remove
A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}
. This limitation arises because
\delta_{A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}}^{2}B_{2}
inevitably generates terms such as
Z^{(k-1)}X^{(k)}Z^{(k+1)}
or
Z^{(k-1)}X^{(k)}+X^{(k-1)}Z^{(k)}
, necessitating a sequential removal approach.

To address this new challenge in many-body systems, let us first examine a simplified but illustrative example. Consider the exponential product form:

e^{-it\left(J_{1,2}Z^{(1)}Z^{(2)}+g_{1}Z^{(1)}+g_{2}Z^{(2)}+h_{1}X^{(1)}+h_{2}X^{(2)}\right)}.

Following the methodology of
Hatano and Suzuki 2005
, we analyze:

\displaystyle e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\alpha(B+C)}e^{-itA}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\alpha(B+C)}=e^{-i\Phi(\alpha,t)}=e^{-it(A+\alpha p(t)+O(\alpha^{2}))},

(3.3)

where we define
A=J_{1,2}Z^{(1)}Z^{(2)}
,
B=g_{1}Z^{(1)}+g_{2}Z^{(2)}
, and
C=h_{1}X^{(1)}+h_{2}X^{(2)}
, with
\alpha
serving as a small parameter.

By differentiating both sides of Eq.(
3.3
) with respect to
\alpha
, we obtain:

\frac{d}{d\alpha}e^{-it(A+\alpha p(t)+O(\alpha^{2}))}=\frac{de^{\Phi}}{d\Phi}\frac{\partial\Phi(\alpha,t)}{\partial\alpha}=e^{\Phi}\frac{1-e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}{\delta_{\phi}}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\alpha},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\alpha(B+C)}e^{-itA}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\alpha(B+C)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-it}{2}e^{\Phi}\left(e^{-\Phi}(B+C)e^{\Phi}+(B+C)\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-it}{2}e^{\Phi}(e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}+1)(B+C).

Equating these expressions yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\alpha}=\frac{-it}{2}\delta_{\Phi}\frac{1+e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}{1-e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}(B+C),

(3.6)

Direct calculation of
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\alpha}
gives:

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\alpha}=-itp(t).

(3.7)

Consequently:

p(t)=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\Phi}\frac{1+e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}{1-e^{-\delta_{\Phi}}}(B+C),

(3.8)

For
\alpha\ll 1
, we can obtain a Taylor expansion:

p(t)=\frac{-it\delta_{A}}{2}\frac{1+e^{it\delta_{A}}}{1-e^{it\delta_{A}}}(B+C)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(-it)^{k}\delta_{A}^{k}(B+C),

(3.9)

where
a_{0}=2
.

Through careful analysis, we find:

\displaystyle\delta_{A}(B+C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[A,B+C]=[A,C]=2iJ_{1,2}(h_{1}Y^{1}Z^{2}+h_{2}Z^{1}Y^{2}),

(3.10a)

\displaystyle\delta_{A}^{2}(B+C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2iJ_{1,2}[A,h_{1}Y^{1}Z^{2}+h_{2}Z^{1}Y^{2}]=4J_{1,2}^{2}(h_{1}X^{1}+h_{2}X^{2})=4J_{1,2}^{2}C,

(3.10b)

\displaystyle\delta_{A}^{3}(B+C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4[A,C]=8iJ_{1,2}^{3}(h_{1}Y^{1}Z^{2}+h_{2}Z^{1}Y^{2}),

(3.10d)

\displaystyle\cdots.

Moreover, since
p(t)
satisfies the symmetry relation
p(-t)=p(t)
, we can neglect all odd terms in the expansion, yielding:

p(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(2B+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(-2it)^{k}\right)=B+\frac{1}{f(J_{1,2}t)}C,

(3.11)

where
f(t)=\frac{\tan(t)}{t}
is the scaling function.

This leads to the following equivalent expression:

\displaystyle e^{-it(A+\alpha^{\prime}B+\alpha^{\prime}C)}=e^{-i\frac{t}{2}\alpha^{\prime}(B+f(J_{1,2}t)C)}e^{-itA}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\alpha^{\prime}(B+f(J_{1,2}t)C)}.

(3.12)

Following the same procedure iteratively, we obtain the optimized exponential product formulas:

\displaystyle e^{-it\left(A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}+B_{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle e^{-i\frac{t}{2}(A_{2}+B_{1}+B^{(1)}_{2})}e^{-itA_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}(A_{2}+B_{1}+B^{(1)}_{2})},

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle e^{-i\frac{t}{4}(B_{1}+B^{(2)}_{2})}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}A_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}(B_{1}+B^{(2)}_{2})}e^{-itA_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}(B_{1}+B^{(2)}_{2})}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}A_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}(B_{1}+B^{(2)}_{2})}

(3.13b)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}B_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}A_{2}}

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}B_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}e^{-itA_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}B_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\frac{t}{2}A_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}}e^{-i\frac{t}{4}B_{1}}e^{-i\frac{t}{8}B^{(3)}_{2}},

(3.13c)

where the modified operators
B_{2}^{(k)}
incorporate the optimization parameters:

\displaystyle B_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2n}f(J_{2k-1,2k}t)h_{2k-1}X^{(2k-1)}+f(J_{2k-1,2k}t)h_{2k}X^{(2k)},

(3.14a)

\displaystyle B_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2n}f(J_{2k,2k+1}t)f(J_{2k-1,2k}t)h_{2k}X^{(2k)}+f(J_{2k,2k+1}t)f(J_{2k+1,2k+2}t)h_{2k+1}X^{(2k+1)},

(3.14b)

\displaystyle B_{2}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2n}f(g_{2k}t)f(J_{2k,2k+1}t)f(J_{2k-1,2k}t)h_{2k}X^{(2k)}+f(g_{2k+1})f(J_{2k,2k+1}t)f(J_{2k+1,2k+2}t)h_{2k+1}X^{(2k+1)}

(3.14c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2n}f(g_{k}t)f(J_{k-1,k}t)f(J_{k,k+1}t)h_{k}X^{(k)}.

Notably, Eq(
3.13c
) provides a more accurate expansion compared to the conventional second-order Trotter expansion:

e^{-it\left(A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}+B_{2}\right)}\approx e^{-i\frac{t}{2}B_{2}}e^{-it\left(A_{1}+A_{2}+B_{1}\right)}e^{-i\frac{t}{2}B_{2}}.

(3.15)

IV
Conclusion

This work presented a comprehensive analysis and extension of quantum simulation schemes based on perturbational decomposition. Our investigation revealed the following results.

We extended single-qubit optimization techniques to many-body systems by treating transverse field strength as perturbations in the expansion.
Our numerical exploration mapped out the evolution time windows across a continuous range of longitudinal and transverse field strengths, revealing systematic trends in the parameter space.

The central achievement lay in our application of perturbational decomposition to the one-dimensional Ising chain with competing longitudinal and transverse fields. Through optimization of the decomposition, we achieved noticeable improvements in simulation fidelity compared to conventional approaches for systems with typical coupling ratios (
g_{k}/J_{k,k+1}\approx 0.2
).

However,this advantage exhibits clear parameter boundaries. The method performs optimally in weak transverse field regimes, with time windows systematically shrinking as transverse field strength increases. While improvements persist at higher longitudinal field strengths, the relative enhancement becomes marginal beyond certain thresholds.

These findings contribute to both theoretical understanding and practical implementation of quantum simulation techniques. Our method offers a well-characterized tool for precise evolution within specific, physically relevant parameter regimes of the one-dimensional Ising model. The identification of favorable parameter regimes and evolution time windows provides useful guidance for practical applications. We expect our perturbational decomposition strategy to inspire new simulation approaches for other quantum many-body systems.
