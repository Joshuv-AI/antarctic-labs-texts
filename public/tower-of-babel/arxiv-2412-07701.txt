For a prime

Aside from cases where genus theory applies in some form or other, we

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{n,\ell}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-\delta},

h_{3}(K)\ll|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-\delta}

whenever
K

Thorne, Tsimerman and Zhao
[
2

h_{2}(K)\ll_{n,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-\delta_{n}+\varepsilon},

\delta_{n}=1/2n

One can do more under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH).
With this assumption
Ellenberg and Venkatesh
[

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{n,\ell,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/2\ell(n-1)+\varepsilon},

(1.3)

\varepsilon>0

\ell

with
n=p^{r}

1.2

\delta(\ell,p)>1/2\ell(n-1)

for large enough
r

Our goal in this paper is to
investigate the extent to which one can relax, or indeed remove, the
hypothesis of the GRH from (
1.3

quadratic and cubic fields.

We begin by mentioning one almost trivial result. This considers
“pure” cubic fields
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d})
, where
d>1
is a
cube-free integer.

Theorem 1
.

For a pure cubic field we have

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/(4\ell)+\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
, and any prime
\ell
.

The principle behind this bound has been known for some time,
in lectures by Wang in particular, but we feel the result merits explicit
mention here.

In the context of Theorem
1
we should note that in the
case
\ell=3
one has the stronger bound

h_{3}(K)\ll 9^{\omega(d)}\ll_{\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{\varepsilon},

(1.4)

for any
\varepsilon>0
and any pure cubic field
K
,
where
\omega(k)
is the
number of distinct prime factors of
k
. This follows from Gerth

[
3
, Theorem 2.7 & Proposition 3.2]
. In the notation of

[
3
]
, the 3-rank of
\mathrm{Cl}(K)
is denoted
\mathrm{rank}\;S_{L}
, and
according to Theorem 2.7 this is at most a certain quantity

\mathrm{rank}\;{}_{N}D
. Proposition 3.2 gives a formula for

\mathrm{rank}\;{}_{N}D
, which shows that it is at most
r+t-1
, where

r
is the 3-rank of the class group of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
in our case
(so that
r=0
), and
t
is the number of ramified primes in the
extension
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d},\sqrt{-3})/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
. Thus
t
is at
most
2\omega(d)+1
, and (
1.4
) follows.

Ellenberg and Venkatesh’s proof of (
1.3
) builds on ideas of
Soundararajan
[
10
]
to give the following result, which is a special case
of Ellenberg and Venkatesh
[
1
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
n
, let
\varepsilon>0
, let
\ell
be a prime, and
let
\varpi<1/2\ell(n-1)
. Suppose there are
M
unramified prime
ideals of
K
, all of degree 1, and having norm at
most
|\Delta_{K}|^{\varpi}
. Then

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{n,\ell,\varepsilon,\varpi}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2+\varepsilon}M^{-1}.

Soundararajan restricted his attention to imaginary quadratic
fields. The extension to general number fields by Ellenberg and
Venkatesh required a significant new idea to handle infinite unit
groups.

To deduce Theorem
1
from Lemma
2

it suffices to observe that if

p\equiv 2\;(\text{mod}\;3)
is any prime not dividing
\Delta_{K}
, then
(p)

splits over
K
as a product
P_{1}P_{2}
of distinct prime ideals, where

P_{1}
has degree 1. This provides
M\gg|\Delta_{K}|^{\varpi}/\log|\Delta_{K}|

suitable prime ideals, and the result follows on taking

\varpi=1/(4\ell)-\varepsilon
, and re-defining
\varepsilon
.

In order to obtain (
1.3
) from Lemma
2
Ellenberg and
Venkatesh call on a strong form of the Chebotarev Density Theorem
which assumes the GRH. The principal theme of this paper is to ask
whether one can get by without assuming such a strong hypothesis
as GRH. We shall confine our attention to situations in which it
suffices to work with Dirichlet
L
-functions, although some of the ideas
apply to more general Artin
L
-functions. This means that we will
assume
K
to be either quadratic or cubic.

When
K
is quadratic we
might seek rational primes
p
for which
\chi(p)=+1
, where

\chi
is the quadratic character associated to
K
.
Any such prime splits in
K
into two unramified first-degree prime
ideals. The study of such primes is hampered by the possibility of

L(s,\chi)
having an exceptional real zero,
very close to
s=1
. We can largely avoid this via the following easy
variant of Lemma
2
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
n
, let
\ell
be a prime, let
\varepsilon>0
and
let
\varpi<1/2\ell(n-1)
. Suppose there are
M
integral
ideals
I
of
K
, having square-free norm
N(I)
coprime to
\Delta_{K}
, and
with
N(I)\leq|\Delta_{K}|^{\varpi}
. Then

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{n,\ell,\varepsilon,\varpi}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2+\varepsilon}M^{-1}.

We will prove this later, in Section
2
. Lemma
3
allows
us to work with
L(s,\chi)
directly, rather than with its logarithmic
derivative.
We will prove the following result.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\ell\geq 3
be prime. Let
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
and

0<\xi<\frac{2\theta-\theta^{2}}{4\ell}.

Suppose that
K
is a quadratic number field with corresponding quadratic
character
\chi
, and assume that

L(\sigma+it,\chi)\ll|\Delta_{K}|^{\xi},\;\;\;(\sigma\geq 1-\theta,\;|t|\leq 1).

Then

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\theta,\xi,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/(2\ell)+\varepsilon},

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. The implied constant above is ineffective.

The Generalized Lindelöf Hypothesis would permit us to
take any
\xi>0
, for any
\theta
, so that the assumptions required
above are vastly weaker even that this. Indeed an examination of the proof
shows that it would suffice to have a suitable bound on
L(s,\chi)
on a rather
shorter interval than
|t|\leq 1
. We also remark that the proof can be adapted
to fields
K
of higher degree, given a suitable assumption on the size of

\zeta_{K}(s)/\zeta(s)
.

When
K
is a cyclic cubic field the way
p
splits is
determined by a cubic character
\chi_{K}
whose conductor is composed
of primes that divide
\Delta_{K}
. In this situation any prime
p

for which
\chi_{K}(p)=1
will split over
K
into three unramified
first-degree prime ideals. (The character
\chi
is only defined
up to conjugation.) On the other hand, for a non-cyclic cubic field the
discriminant
\Delta_{K}
will be a non-square, and any prime
p\geq 3

with

\left(\frac{\Delta_{K}}{p}\right)=-1

factors over
K
as a product of two prime ideals, one having degree 1
and the other with degree 2. Thus it is enough for us to find a good
supply of rational primes
p
for which
\chi(p)
has a prescribed
value, where
\chi
is a quadratic or cubic character. The conductor
q

of
\chi
only involves primes dividing
\Delta_{K}
, and the argument
of Heath-Brown
[
5
, foot of page 271]
allows us to deduce that

q\ll|\Delta_{K}|
. Our situation is similar to Vinogradov’s problem on
the least quadratic non-residue modulo
q
, but our task involves
finding many suitable primes, not just one. We attack this by
considering zero-free regions of
L
-functions.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\ell
be prime, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is an effective constant

C_{1}(\ell,\varepsilon)
with the following property.

Let
K
be a cubic number field. If
K
is
cyclic let
\chi
one of the primitive cubic characters associated to
K
, and
if
K
is non-cyclic cubic field let
\chi
be the primitive quadratic
character which induces the Jacobi symbol
(\Delta_{K}/*)
.

Suppose that
1\leq\vartheta\leq\log|\Delta_{K}|
and that
L(s,\chi)

has no zeros in the disc
|s-1|\leq\vartheta/\log|\Delta_{K}|
, apart possibly
from a single real zero in the case in which
\chi
is quadratic. Then if

\vartheta\geq C_{1}(\ell,\varepsilon)
one has

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\vartheta,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/(4\ell)+\varepsilon}

with an effective implied constant.

We should emphasize that the assumption here is vastly weaker
than the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The standard zero-free
region produces an absolute constant
C>0
such that the
disc
|s-1|\leq C/\log q
has the required property, where
q
is the
conductor of
\chi
. Hence, for example, we may generalize
Theorem
1
to cover any sequence of non-cyclic cubic
fields
K
whose discriminants factor as
\Delta_{K}=a_{K}b_{K}^{2}
with

\log|a_{K}|/\log|\Delta_{K}|\to 0
.

We may obtain zero-free regions via a
q
-analogue of a result which
is familiar in the context of the Riemann Zeta-function, see Titchmarsh

[
11
, Theorem 3.10]
, for example.

Theorem 6
.

There is an effectively computable constant
C_{2}>0
with the following
property. Suppose that
\chi
is a complex Dirichlet character
modulo
q
, and that the estimate

|L(\sigma+it,\psi)|\leq e^{\phi},\;\;\;(\forall\sigma\geq 1-\theta,\;\;\forall|t|\leq 3),

(1.5)

holds both for
\psi=\chi
and for
\psi=\chi^{2}
, where
\theta
and

\phi
are parameters satisfying
\phi e^{-\phi}\leq\theta\leq 1\leq\phi

and
\phi\geq\theta(1+\log\log 3q)
.
Then
L(s,\chi)
has no zeros with

\sigma\geq 1-C_{2}\theta/\phi,\;\;|t|\leq 1.

(1.6)

Moreover, if
\chi
is a character modulo
q
of order 2, and the
bound (
1.5
) holds for
\psi=\chi
, then
L(s,\chi)
has at most
one zero in the region (
1.6
).
If this zero exists, it is a simple real zero of
L(s,\chi)
.

Finally, if
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are distinct characters modulo
q
,
both of order 2, such that the
bound (
1.5
) holds for
\psi=\chi_{1}
,
\psi=\chi_{2}
and

\psi=\chi_{1}\chi_{2}
, then
L(s,\chi_{1})
and
L(s,\chi_{2})
cannot both have
zeros in the region (
1.6
).

The proof is entirely straightforward and we do not claim any
novelty. See Graham and Ringrose
[
4
, Lemma 6.2]
for a special
case of the result.

As an immediate corollary of Theorems
5
and
6
we have
the following.

Corollary 7
.

For each prime
\ell\geq 3
and small real number
\varepsilon>0
there is an
effective constant
C_{3}(\ell,\varepsilon)
with the following property.

Let
K
and
\chi
be as in Theorem
5
, and suppose that

L(\sigma+it,\chi)\ll|\Delta_{K}|^{\xi},\;\;\;(\sigma\geq 1-\theta,\;|t|\leq 3)

for some
\theta\in(0,1)
and
\xi\leq C_{3}(\ell,\varepsilon)\theta
. Then

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\theta,\xi,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/(2\ell)+\varepsilon}.

The implied constant above is effective.

For the proof it is enough to take
C_{3}(\ell,\varepsilon)=C_{2}/C_{1}(\ell,\varepsilon)
,
which will be sufficient when
|\Delta_{K}|
is large enough.

It is interesting to compare Theorem
4
with Corollary
7
.
The former is ineffective, but has a precise condition on
\xi
, while
the latter is effective, with an inexplicit requirement for
\xi
. The
distinction between the ranges
|t|\leq 3
and
|t|\leq 1
has no
significance. One could easily prove the results with either range.

One situation in which we are able to say something useful about
bounds for
L(s,\chi)
is that in which we restrict to smooth values of
q
.
The following is a trivial variant of Theorem 5 of Graham and Ringrose

[
4
]
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\chi
be a non-principal character to modulus
q
. Let
p
be
the largest prime factor of
q
and write
r
for the largest
square-full factor of
q
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and write
L=2^{k+3}-2
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{M<n\leq M+N}\chi(n)

\displaystyle\ll_{r}

\displaystyle M^{1-(k+3)/L}q^{1/L}d(q)^{(3k^{2}+11k+8)/(2L)}(\log q)^{(k+3)/L}\sigma_{-1}(q)p^{(k^{2}+3k+4)/(4L)}

for any positive integers
N\leq M
.
The implied constant above depends only on
r
, and is effective.

This is proved by the
q
-analogue of van der
Corput’s method. It corresponds to the
(k+3)
-rd derivative
estimate, see Titchmarsh
[
11
, Theorem 5.13]
for example.

The procedure for deriving estimates for
L(s,\chi)
from bounds for
the corresponding character sum is standard, and we immediately get
the following corollary. For our purposes a
relatively crude result is sufficient.

Corollary 9
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
8
, for any fixed

\varepsilon>0
we will have

L(s,\chi)\ll_{r,k,\varepsilon}q^{1/L+\varepsilon}p^{(k^{2}+3k+4)/(4L)}

throughout the region
\sigma\geq 1-(k+3)/L
,
|t|\leq 3
.

We may feed this into Theorem
4
to
produce the following.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose we are given a prime
\ell
. Then there is a corresponding
effective constant
\delta_{2}(\ell)>0
with the following property.

Let
K
be a quadratic number field such that

p\leq|\Delta_{K}|^{\delta_{2}(\ell)}
for every
prime divisor
p
of
\Delta_{K}
. Then

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/(2\ell)+\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
, with an ineffective implied constant. For example,
one may take
\delta_{2}(5)=1/343
.

As in Heath-Brown
[
5
, foot of page 271]
, if
\chi
is a character
of bounded order, its conductor
q
has bounded square-full part, so that

r\ll 1
in the notation of Theorem
8
. If
p\leq|\Delta_{K}|^{\delta}

then Corollary
7
allows us to take

\theta=\frac{k+3}{2^{k+3}-2}\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\xi=\frac{1+\delta(k^{2}+3k+4)/4}{2^{k+3}-2}-\varepsilon

for any
\varepsilon>0
. One then sees that
\xi/\theta
can be made arbitrarily
small, by choosing
k
large enough and
\delta
small enough.
Indeed one may check that the values
k=15

and
\delta=1/343
are sufficient.

Similarly, combining Corollary
7
with Theorem
5

yields:-

Theorem 11
.

Suppose we are given a prime
\ell
. Then there is a corresponding
effective constant
\delta_{3}(\ell)>0
with the following property.

Let
K
be a cubic number field such that

p\leq|\Delta_{K}|^{\delta_{3}(\ell)}
for every

\Delta_{K}
. Then

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/(2\ell)+\varepsilon},

for any

Quadratic Fields — Proof of Theorem
4

We begin by proving Lemma
3
.
This is done by the obvious modification of the argument given
by Ellenberg and Venkatesh in proving
[
1
, Lemma 2.3]
. All that is
necessary for Lemma
3
is to note that if
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are
ideals as in the lemma, such that
I_{1}^{\ell}I_{2}^{-\ell}=(u)
for some

u
in a proper subfield
K_{1}
of
K
, then
I_{1}=I_{2}
. For if
u
is not a
unit, then
v_{P}(u)\not=1
for some prime ideal
P
of
K_{1}
, so that

P
divides
at least one of
I_{1}
or
I_{2}
. This however is impossible since

N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(P)=N_{K_{1}/\mathbb{Q}}(P)^{[K:K_{1}]}

whereas
N(I_{1})
and
N(I_{2})
are square-free.

Now let
K
be a quadratic field, as described in Theorem
4
,
and take
\chi

to be the associated real character, so that
\chi
has conductor

q=|\Delta_{K}|
.
The parameters
\ell
,
\theta
and
\xi
will be fixed throughout
our argument, and
we allow all our order constants to depend on them without further mention.

We define

f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu^{2}(n)\prod_{p|n}(\chi_{0}(n)+\chi(n))=\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-s},

so that
a_{n}
is the number of integral ideals of
K
of norm
n
, if
n
is
square-free and co-prime to
\Delta_{K}
, and
a_{n}=0
otherwise. Using
Euler products we see that we may write

f(s)=L(s,\chi_{0})L(s,\chi)f_{0}(s)

where
f_{0}(s)
has an Euler product which is absolutely convergent for

\sigma>\tfrac{1}{2}
, with
f_{0}(s)\ll 1
uniformly for
\sigma\geq\tfrac{3}{4}
, say.

Our argument now introduces some rather severe Gaussian weights, which
are needed because we only have information for the narrow range
|t|\leq 1
.
We consider

I=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2-i\infty}^{2+i\infty}f(s)\exp(s^{2}y)ds=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi y}}\sum_{1}^{\infty}a_{n}\exp\{-(\log n)^{2}/4y\}

(2.1)

with
y=\kappa\log q
, where
\kappa\in(0,1)
is a constant to be chosen
later. We let
\delta\in(0,1)
be another constant, also to be chosen later,
and move the path of
integration so that it runs from
1+\delta-i\infty
to
1+\delta-i

to
1-\theta-i
to

1-\theta+i
to
1+\delta+i
to
1+\delta+i\infty
. There is a pole at
s=1
,
which contributes

\prod_{p|q}(1-p^{-1})L(1,\chi)f_{0}(1)e^{y}\gg_{\delta}q^{-\delta}e^{y},

(2.2)

by Siegel’s Theorem. The implied constant here
is ineffective. The section of the integral from
1+\delta+i
to

1+\delta+i\infty
produces

\ll_{\delta}\int_{1}^{\infty}\exp((1+\delta)^{2}y-t^{2}y)dt\leq e^{(1+\delta)^{2}y}\int_{1}^{\infty}\exp(-ty)dt=\frac{e^{(2\delta+\delta^{2})y}}{y},

and similarly for the section from
1+\delta-i\infty
to

1+\delta-i
. When

1-\theta\leq\sigma\leq 1+\delta
and
t=\pm 1
we have

f(s)\ll|L(s,\chi_{0})|q^{\xi}\ll_{\delta}q^{\xi+\delta}

and

|\exp(s^{2}y)|=\exp(\sigma^{2}y-y)\leq\exp((2\delta+\delta^{2})y),

whence the integrals from
1+\delta-i
to
1-\theta-i
and from

1-\theta+i
to
1+\delta+i
contribute

\ll_{\delta}q^{\xi+\delta}e^{(2\delta+\delta^{2})y}.

Finally the integral from
1-\theta-i
to
1-\theta+i
is

\ll_{\delta}q^{\xi+\delta}\exp\{(1-\theta)^{2}y\}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-t^{2}y}dt\ll_{\delta}q^{\xi+\delta}\exp\{(1-\theta)^{2}y\}.

Since
\theta<\tfrac{1}{2}
we therefore deduce that

I=\prod_{p|q}(1-p^{-1})L(1,\chi)f_{0}(1)e^{y}+O_{\delta}\left(q^{\xi+\delta}\exp\{(1-\theta)^{2}y\}\right)

provided that
\delta<\tfrac{1}{10}
, say. Comparing the error term here
with the lower bound (
2.2
) for the main term, we deduce that

I\gg_{\delta}q^{\kappa-\delta},

(2.3)

provided that

\delta\leq\tfrac{1}{3}(\kappa(2\theta-\theta^{2})-\xi),

(2.4)

for example. We can always choose a suitable
\delta
whenever

\xi<\kappa(2\theta-\theta^{2})
.

We now examine the infinite sum in (
2.1
) and show that terms with

n
much larger than
e^{2y}
make a negligible contribution. For
the range
n>e^{8y}
we note that
a_{n}\leq n
, whence

\displaystyle\sum_{n>e^{8y}}a_{n}\exp\{-(\log n)^{2}/4y\}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\int_{e^{8y}}^{\infty}x\exp\{-(\log x)^{2}/4y\}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{8y}^{\infty}\exp\{2u-u^{2}/4y\}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{8y}^{\infty}\exp\{4y-(u-4y)^{2}/4y\}du.

However
(u-4y)^{2}\geq 4y(u-4y)
for
u\geq 8y
, so that

\sum_{n>e^{8y}}a_{n}\exp\{-(\log n)^{2}/4y\}\ll e^{4y}\int_{8y}^{\infty}\exp\{-(u-4y)\}du=1.

This is negligible compared to
I

) holds.

e^{(2+\eta)y}<n\leq e^{8y}
,
but using the bound
a_{n}\ll_{\delta}n^{\delta}

\eta>0

\displaystyle\sum_{e^{(2+\eta)y}<n\leq e^{8y}}a_{n}\exp\{-(\log n)^{2}/4y\}

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta}

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta}\int_{e^{(2+\eta)y}}^{e^{8y}}\exp\{-(\log x)^{2}/4y\}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta}\int_{(2+\eta)y}^{8y}\exp\{u-u^{2}/4y\}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta}\int_{(2+\eta)y}^{8y}\exp\{y-(u-2y)^{2}/4y\}du

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta+y}\int_{(2+\eta)y}^{8y}\exp\{-\tfrac{\eta}{4}(u-2y)\}du

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta,\eta}

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta+y-\eta^{2}y/4}.

In the same way

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq e^{(2-\eta)y}}a_{n}\exp\{-(\log n)^{2}/4y\}

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta}

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta}\int_{0}^{e^{(2-\eta)y}}\exp\{-(\log x)^{2}/4y\}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta}\int_{0}^{(2-\eta)y}\exp\{u-u^{2}/4y\}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta}\int_{0}^{(2-\eta)y}\exp\{y-(u-2y)^{2}/4y\}du

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta+y}\int_{0}^{(2-\eta)y}\exp\{-\tfrac{\eta}{4}|u-2y|\}du

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta,\eta}

\displaystyle e^{8y\delta+y-\eta^{2}y/4}.

We now make the choice
\eta=\delta^{1/3}
. The bound (
2.3
) then
shows that the sums for
n>e^{(2+\eta)y}
and
n\leq e^{(2-\eta)y}
are
negligible compared to
I

when
\delta<\kappa
and
8\kappa\delta-\delta^{2/3}\kappa/4<-\delta
.
We therefore impose the additional condition that

\delta\leq\frac{\kappa^{3}}{65(1+8\kappa)^{3}}.

(2.5)

Thus, under the conditions (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) it follows that

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi y}}\sum_{e^{(2-\eta)y}<n\leq e^{(2+\eta)y}}a_{n}\exp\{-(\log n)^{2}/4y\}\gg_{\kappa,\theta,\xi}q^{\kappa-\delta},

whence

\sum_{n\leq e^{(2+\eta)y}}a_{n}\gg_{\kappa,\theta,\xi}q^{\kappa-\delta}\exp\{(2-\eta)^{2}y/4\},

In order to apply Lemma

we choose

\varpi=1/(2\ell)-\delta
and
\kappa=\varpi/(2+\eta)
, where

\eta=\delta^{1/3}
. We then obtain

M\gg_{\ell,\delta,\theta,\xi}q^{\kappa(1+(2-\eta)^{2}/4)-\delta},

so that we obtain
h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\varepsilon}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-1/2\ell+\varepsilon}

by choosing
\delta
sufficiently small.

Cubic Fields — Proof of Theorem
5

For our proof of Theorem
5
we write
q
for the conductor of

\chi
. We will introduce a constant
\delta
in the range

0<\delta<1/(4\ell)
.
All the order constants in this section will be effective, and will be
allowed to
depend on
\ell
and
\delta
, but any other dependencies will be explicitly
mentioned. We define
y=|\Delta_{K}|^{1/4\ell-\delta}
and

\displaystyle f(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\int_{y^{1-\delta}}^{y}\int_{z^{1-\delta}}^{z}u^{s-1}\frac{du}{u}\,\frac{dz}{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\delta)y^{s-1}-(2-\delta)y^{(1-\delta)(s-1)}+y^{(1-\delta)^{2}(s-1)}}{(s-1)^{2}}.

We see that

f(1)=\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(1-\delta)(2-\delta),

that
f(s)\geq 0
for all real
s
, and that
f(s)\ll|s-1|^{-2}

when
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leq 1
.

If we now define

I=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2-i\infty}^{2+i\infty}\left\{-\frac{L^{\prime}(s,\chi)}{L(s,\chi)}\right\}f(s)ds

it follows from termwise integration that

I=\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<n\leq y}\frac{\chi(n)\Lambda(n)}{n}\min\left(\log(n/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/n^{1-\delta})\right).

By the functional equation we have

\frac{L^{\prime}(-\tfrac{1}{2}+it,\chi)}{L(-\tfrac{1}{2}+it,\chi)}\ll\log q(|t|+2).

Hence on shifting the line of integration to
\sigma=-\tfrac{1}{2}
we
find that

I=-\sum_{\rho}f(\rho)+O\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\log q(|t|+2)\frac{dt}{1+t^{2}}\right)=-\sum_{\rho}f(\rho)+O(\log q).

Zeros
\rho
with
|\gamma|\geq 1
contribute
O(\log q)
, whence

I=-\sum_{\rho,\,|\gamma|\leq 1}f(\rho)+O(\log q).

A precisely analogous argument using
\zeta(s)
in place of

L(s,\chi)
yields

\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<n\leq y}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n}\min\left(\log(n/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/n^{1-\delta})\right)=f(1)(\log y)^{2}+O(1),

since
\zeta(s)
has a simple pole at
s=1
, but no zeros with

|\gamma|\leq 1
. We may remove terms
n=p^{e}
with
e\geq 2
at a cost

O(1)
; and also terms
n=p
for which
p|q
, at a cost
O(\log q)
,
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y}\frac{\chi(p)\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)

(3.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\rho}f(\rho)+O(\log q)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y}\frac{\chi_{0}(p)\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(0)(\log y)^{2}+O(\log q).

We proceed to consider the contribution to (
3.1
) arising from zeros
for which
|\gamma|\leq 1
, and we begin by considering the case in
which
\chi
is cubic. The relevant region
may be covered by annuli
R/\log q<|\rho-1|\leq 2R/\log q
, where
R

runs over powers of 2 such that

\frac{\vartheta}{\log|\Delta_{K}|}\ll\frac{R}{\log q}\ll 1.

(3.2)

According to Linnik’s “Density Theorem” (see Prachar

[
9
, Page 331]
, for example) each such annulus contains

O(R+1)
zeros, and each zero contributes
O(R^{-2}(\log q)^{2})
to
(
3.1
). If we let
R
run over powers of 2 in the range (
3.2
)
we therefore find that the overall contribution to (3.1) is

\ll\vartheta^{-2}(\log|\Delta_{K}|)^{2}+\vartheta^{-1}(\log|\Delta_{K}|)(\log q)\ll\vartheta^{-1}(\log|\Delta_{K}|)^{2}.

It follows that

\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y}\frac{\chi(p)\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)\ll\vartheta^{-1}(\log|\Delta_{K}|)^{2}.

By complex conjugation the same estimate holds when
\chi
is
replaced by
\overline{\chi}
, and we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y}\frac{(\chi_{0}(p)+\chi(p)+\overline{\chi}(p))\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(1)(\log y)^{2}+O(\vartheta^{-1}(\log|\Delta_{K}|)^{2}).

We recall now that
y=|\Delta_{K}|^{1/4\ell-\delta}
, and that our
implied constants are allowed to depend on
\ell
and
\delta
, but
not on anything else.
Thus, if
\vartheta
is sufficiently large in terms of
\ell
and
\delta

we will have

3\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y\\
\chi(p)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)\gg(\log y)^{2},

and hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y\\
\chi(p)=1\end{subarray}}p^{-1}\gg 1

and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y\\
\chi(p)=1\end{subarray}}1\gg y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}.

Thus Lemma
2
, with
\varepsilon=\delta
, produces

h_{\ell}(K)\ll_{\ell,\delta}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2-(1-\delta)^{2}(1/4\ell-\delta)+\delta}.

Taking
\delta
suitably small gives the required conclusion, when

\chi
has order 3.

When
\chi
is quadratic we follow the same argument, but bearing in
mind that
L(s,\chi)
may have real zeros in the disc

|s-1|\leq\vartheta/\log|\Delta|_{K}
. What we find is that

\displaystyle\sum_{y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y}\frac{(\chi_{0}(p)-\chi(p))\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(0)(\log y)^{2}+\sum_{\rho\;\mathrm{real}}f(\rho)+O(\vartheta^{-1}(\log|\Delta_{K}|)^{2}).

Since
f(\rho)\geq 0
for real
\rho
we deduce as above that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y\\
\chi(p)=-1\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p}{p}\min\left(\log(p/y^{(1-\delta)^{2}})\,,\,\log(y^{1-\delta}/p^{1-\delta})\right)\gg(\log y)^{2},

and then that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}<p\leq y\\
\chi(p)=-1\end{subarray}}1\gg y^{(1-\delta)^{2}}.

The argument may now be completed as before.

Proof of Theorem
6

As explained in the introduction, the proof we shall give is a
straightforward amalgam of familiar arguments. We begin with a result
due to Landau
[
8
]
, see Titchmarsh
[
11
, Lemma
\beta
,
page 56]
.

Lemma 12
.

There is an effectively computable constant
A_{1}>0
with the following
property. Suppose
f(s)
is regular on the disc
|s-s_{0}|\leq r
, and
satisfies

\left|\frac{f(s)}{f(s_{0})}\right|\leq e^{M}

there, for some
M>1
. Suppose further that
f(s)
has no zeros in
the right hand half of the disc
|s-s_{0}|\leq r
. Then

-\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{f^{\prime}(s_{0})}{f(s_{0})}\right\}\leq\frac{A_{1}M}{r}-\sum_{|\rho-s_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}r}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{s_{0}-\rho}\right\},

where
\rho
runs over zeros of
f
, counted according to
multiplicity. In particular,

-\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{f^{\prime}(s_{0})}{f(s_{0})}\right\}\leq\frac{A_{1}M}{r}.

The form of the result is not precisely as stated by
Titchmarsh, but it is given as
[
11
, top of page 57]
.

We begin our treatment of Theorem
6
by considering the case in
which
\chi
has order 2, and
|t|\leq c\theta/\phi
for a suitable
numerical constant
c
. We will use parameters
\varphi
and

\sigma_{0}
which we will
specify in due course so as to satisfy the conditions

\varphi\geq\phi+1\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;1+e^{-\varphi}\leq\sigma_{0}\leq 1+\tfrac{1}{4}\theta.

(4.1)

We proceed to examine

-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})}{\zeta(\sigma_{0})}-\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0},\chi)}{L(\sigma_{0},\chi)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\chi(n)\right)\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{\sigma_{0}}}\geq 0.

For the first term we have

\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})/\zeta(\sigma_{0})=-1/(\sigma_{0}-1)+O(1)
.
For the second term we apply Lemma
12
to the function

L(s,\chi)
, taking

s_{0}=\sigma_{0}
and
r=\theta
, so that
L(s,\chi)
has no zeros in
the right hand half of the disc
|s-s_{0}|\leq r
. This disc is
contained in the region considered in (
1.5
), and

L(\sigma_{0},\chi)^{-1}\leq\zeta(\sigma_{0})\leq(\sigma_{0}-1)^{-1}+1\leq e^{\phi}+1\leq e^{\varphi}.

so that

\left|\frac{L(s,\chi)}{L(s_{0},\chi)}\right|\leq e^{\phi+\varphi}\leq e^{2\varphi}.

We may therefore apply Lemma
12
with
M=2\varphi
, allowing us to
conclude that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})}{\zeta(\sigma_{0})}-\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0},\chi)}{L(\sigma_{0},\chi)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2A_{1}\varphi}{\theta}+O(1)+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\sum_{|\rho-\sigma_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\rho}\right\}.

Since
\varphi\geq\phi\geq 1\geq\theta
it follows that

0\leq\frac{A_{2}\varphi}{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\sum_{|\rho-\sigma_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\rho}\right\}

for a suitable constant
A_{2}\geq 1
.

However

\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\rho}\right\}=\frac{\sigma_{0}-\beta}{(\sigma_{0}-\beta)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

This is always non-negative. Moreover, if

|\gamma|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)
we have

|\gamma|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-\beta)
and hence

\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\rho}\right\}\geq\frac{\sigma_{0}-\beta}{(\sigma_{0}-\beta)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}(\sigma_{0}-\beta)^{2}}=\frac{4/5}{\sigma_{0}-\beta}.

It follows that

0\leq\frac{A_{2}\varphi}{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\rho-\sigma_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\\
|\gamma|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)\end{subarray}}\frac{4/5}{\sigma_{0}-\beta}.

If the sum includes two real zeros with

1-\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)
, or a pair of complex conjugate
zeros in the above region, we deduce that

0<\frac{A_{2}\varphi}{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\frac{16/15}{\sigma_{0}-1},

and therefore that

\sigma_{0}-1>\frac{\theta}{15A_{2}\varphi}.

We can therefore obtain a contradiction by making the choice

\sigma_{0}=1+\frac{\theta}{15A_{2}\varphi}

provided that this satisfies the constraints (
4.1
).
Since
0<\theta\leq 1\leq\phi\leq\varphi
and
A_{2}\geq 1
it is automatic that

\sigma_{0}\leq 1+\tfrac{1}{4}\theta
. Moreover, in view of our assumption that

\theta\geq\phi e^{-\phi}
we will have

\sigma_{0}\geq 1+\frac{\phi e^{-\phi}}{15A_{2}\varphi}\geq 1+e^{-\varphi}

provided that we choose
\varphi=\phi+\kappa
with a constant

\kappa\geq 1
so large that

e^{\kappa}\geq 15A_{2}(1+\kappa)\geq 15A_{2}(1+\kappa/\phi).

We conclude from the above that there can be at most one zero
lying in the disc
|\rho-\sigma_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta
such that

1-\beta<\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)
and
|\gamma|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)
.
Any zero satisfying these latter two conditions will automatically have

|\rho-\sigma_{0}|\leq\sigma_{0}-\beta+|\gamma|\leq 2(\sigma_{0}-1)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta,

in view of (
4.1
). Since

\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)=\frac{\theta}{30A_{2}\varphi}=\frac{\theta}{30A_{2}(\phi+\kappa)}\geq\frac{\theta}{30A_{2}(\kappa+1)\phi}

this suffices for the theorem, with
C_{2}=(31A_{2}(\kappa+1))^{-1}
,
when
\chi
is real and
|\gamma|\leq C_{2}\theta/\phi
.

The argument for the remaining cases of the theorem follows along very
similar lines, and so we shall be brief.
We look next at the situation in which either
\chi
is real with

|\gamma|\geq C_{2}\theta/\phi
, or
\chi
is complex. Suppose
that
L(s,\chi)
has a zero
\beta+i\gamma
with
|\gamma|\leq 1
.
We choose
\sigma_{0}
and
\varphi
as in (
4.1
), and consider
the familiar inequality

-3\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})}{\zeta(\sigma_{0})}-4\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+i\gamma,\chi)}{L(\sigma_{0}+i\gamma,\chi)}\right\}-\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}{L(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}\right\}\geq 0.

(4.2)

We again apply Lemma
12
to the function
L(s,\chi)
, taking

r=\theta
as before, but this time choosing
s_{0}=\sigma_{0}+i\gamma
.
The previous argument then shows that

\displaystyle-3\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})}{\zeta(\sigma_{0})}-4\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+i\gamma,\chi)}{L(\sigma_{0}+i\gamma,\chi)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8A_{1}\varphi}{\theta}+O(1)+\frac{3}{\sigma_{0}-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-4\sum_{|\rho-s_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\rho}\right\}.

If
\rho=\beta+i\gamma
is
included it contributes

(\sigma_{0}-\beta)^{-1}
to the sum. Moreover any other zero will make a
non-negative contribution, so that

\displaystyle-3\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0},\chi_{0})}{L(\sigma_{0},\chi_{0})}\right\}-4\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+i\gamma,\chi)}{L(\sigma_{0}+i\gamma,\chi)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8A_{1}\varphi}{\theta}+O(1)+\frac{3}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\frac{4}{\sigma_{0}-\beta},

provided that
\sigma_{0}-\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta
.

It remains to consider the third term in (
4.2
). When
\chi
is
complex we may apply
Lemma
12
to
L(s,\chi^{2})
, taking
s_{0}=\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma

and
r=\theta
. This produces

-\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}{L(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}\right\}\leq\frac{2A_{1}\varphi}{\theta}.

Alternatively, if
\chi
is real and
|\gamma|\geq C_{2}\theta/\phi
,
then
\chi^{2}=\chi_{0}
and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}{L(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{1}^{\infty}\chi_{0}(n)\Lambda(n)n^{-\sigma_{0}-2i\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma)}{\zeta(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma)}\right|+\sum_{p|q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log p}{p^{k\sigma_{0}}}.

Here we have

\left|\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma)}{\zeta(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma)}\right|=\frac{1}{|\sigma+2i\gamma-1|}+O(1)\leq\frac{1}{2|\gamma|}+O(1)\leq\frac{\phi}{2C_{2}\theta}+O(1)

and

\sum_{p|q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log p}{p^{k\sigma_{0}}}\leq\sum_{p|q}\frac{\log p}{p-1}\ll\log\log 3q.

(4.3)

In this case, since
\phi\leq\varphi
we have

-\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}{L(\sigma_{0}+2i\gamma,\chi^{2})}\right\}\leq\frac{\varphi}{2C_{2}\theta}+O(\log\log 3q).

Thus, in either case we may conclude that

0\leq\frac{A_{3}\varphi}{\theta}+\frac{3}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\frac{4}{\sigma_{0}-\beta}+O(\log\log 3q)

for a suitable constant
A_{3}
, provided that
\sigma_{0}-\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta
.
We assume in Theorem
6
that

\phi\geq\theta(1+\log\log 3q)
, whence

0\leq\frac{A_{4}\varphi}{\theta}+\frac{3}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\frac{4}{\sigma_{0}-\beta}

with a further constant
A_{4}
. If
1-\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{4}(\sigma_{0}-1)

\sigma_{0}=1+\frac{\theta}{16A_{4}\varphi}.

We can then complete the argument as before, taking
\varphi=\phi+\kappa

with a suitable constant
\kappa\geq 1
, and checking that the assumption

1-\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{4}(\sigma_{0}-1)
ensures that we have
\sigma_{0}-\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta
.
We conclude that, for the two cases under consideration, there are no zeros
with
\beta\geq 1-C_{2}^{\prime}\theta/\phi
, for a new constant
C_{2}^{\prime}>0
.

Finally we consider two different non-principal real characters

\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
, using the inequality

-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})}{\zeta(\sigma_{0})}-\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0},\chi_{1})}{L(\sigma_{0},\chi_{1})}-\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0},\chi_{2})}{L(\sigma_{0},\chi_{2})}-\frac{L^{\prime}(\sigma_{0},\chi_{1}\chi_{2})}{L(\sigma_{0},\chi_{1}\chi_{2})}\geq 0.

If
L(s,\chi_{1})
and
L(s,\chi_{2})
have real zeros at
\beta_{1}

and
\beta_{2}
respectively,
both in the range
\sigma_{0}-\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\theta
, then the
previous arguments lead to the bound

0\leq\frac{6A_{1}\varphi}{\theta}+O(1)+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-1}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\beta_{1}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}-\beta_{2}}.

If
1-\beta_{1}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)
and

1-\beta_{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{0}-1)
we then obtain a
contradiction as before, on making a suitable choice of
\sigma_{0}
.
This shows that
there can be at most one real character
\chi
with a zero in the
range
\beta\geq 1-C_{2}\theta/\phi
, thereby completing the proof of the theorem.
