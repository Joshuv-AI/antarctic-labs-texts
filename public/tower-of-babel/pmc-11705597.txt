Validation of a mail-in delayed semen analysis protocol developed for home collection
Authors: Karen Racicot, Denny Sakkas, Brent C Barrett, Kenneth Chiang, Charles Jenkins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Infertility is a global problem. Approximately 15% of reproductive-age couples are unable to conceive after 1 year of unprotected intercourse (1, 2). Male factor infertility can be a contributing cause in 40%–50% of cases and be the sole cause in up to 30% of cases (1). Semen analysis is recommended as part of the initial evaluation by the World Health Organization (WHO) (3), American Urological Association, and American Society for Reproductive Medicine (2). It is typically the first laboratory test a clinician will order after performing a detailed history and physical examination (4) and provides important details regarding semen quality including sperm motility, concentration, viability, and morphology.

The WHO Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen, Fifth Edition, which was the current version at the time of this study, details a specific process for the collection and processing of semen samples (3). According to these guidelines, sample analysis should ideally commence within 30 minutes after collection but at least within 60 minutes because of the lability of the samples. To avoid fluctuations in temperature and minimize the time between collection and analysis, it is, therefore, necessary that the sample be collected at, or within close proximity of, a site with a clinical laboratory equipped to perform the analysis (3). These time-sensitive protocols can limit the availability of these services in some rural and underserved areas of the country. In addition, for many patients, the process of semen collection at a clinical site can be uncomfortable or embarrassing, preventing some from seeking fertility evaluation even when these services are locally available (5, 6).

To address the need for broader access to semen analyses, a number of home-based semen collection and analysis tools have been developed. At-home analysis platforms such as SpermCheck Fertility Home Test Kit (SpermCheck; Fairfield, OH) and Trak Male Fertility Testing System (Sandstone Diagnostics, Inc.; Livermore, CA) can provide sperm concentration estimates, whereas others such as SwimCount Sperm Quality Test (FKA Brands; Commerce Township, MI) and FertilitySCORE kit (FertiPro; Beernem, Belgium) provide estimates of progressively motile sperm (5, 6, 7). There are also several smartphone-based optical tracking devices including SEEM (Recruit-Lifestyle Co.; Tokyo, Japan), Yo Home Sperm Test (Medical Electronic Systems; Los Angeles, CA), and ExSeed Sperm Test Kit (ExSeed Health; London, England) (5, 6, 7). Advantages of these tests include convenience and reduced cost compared with laboratory-based analysis; however, they do not measure all recommended semen parameters and have questionable reliability because of the extent of patient processing and the limitations of these portable technologies.

Alternatively, there are fewer kits that support home collection of a sample that is then mailed to a clinical laboratory (i.e., mail-in home collection kits). These services, including offerings by Fellows, Legacy, and ReproSource, are meant to combine the convenience and privacy of collecting at home, with the accuracy and comprehensiveness of a semen analysis performed in a clinical laboratory. Developing this type of service is challenging because of the difficulty in maintaining sperm sample integrity for an extended timeframe and under varying conditions throughout transportation to the laboratory. To address these challenges, we developed a proprietary preservation medium and temperature-controlled transportation protocol, designed to maintain semen sample integrity for up to 26 hours after collection, thus providing enough time for sample shipping from most parts of the United States. We validated this mail-in delayed semen analysis service in both a controlled laboratory (study A) and real-world commercial setting (study B).

Materials and Methods

Study protocol A

The objective of study A was to evaluate the performance of a delayed semen analysis protocol in a controlled laboratory environment. Participants (n = 68) were patients seeking fertility assessment at a fertility center within close proximity of the clinical reference laboratory performing the study. After patients collected the semen specimen at the clinical site, the deidentified remnant sample was immediately transferred to the clinical laboratory; 1 aliquot of the specimen was used for the reference analysis, initiated within 1 hour of collection, whereas another 500-μL aliquot of the specimen was combined with 150 μL of Delayed Semen Analysis Medium (DSAM), a refrigeration medium with TEST-yolk extenders customized for optimal performance using our semen analysis protocol. This sample was immediately stored in a monitored refrigerator at 4 °C where it was maintained for 26 hours before analysis. The time point of 26 hours was selected because it is inclusive of the time it takes for our clinical laboratory to receive specimens shipped commercially from most continental United States. The 26- and 1-hour analyses were compared, providing the baseline effect of the delayed analysis protocol on semen parameters in a controlled laboratory environment (Fig. 1A, comparison 1).

Schematic representation of study protocols A and B. DSAM = Delayed Semen Analysis Medium.

Study protocol B

The objective of study B was to test the performance of a delayed semen analysis protocol using real-world conditions, including on-site sample preparation and commercial shipment to the reference laboratory. Participants (n = 232) were patients seeking fertility assessment at a US fertility center. After patients collected the semen specimen, the deidentified remnant sample was provided to the clinical research team for use in study protocol B. Immediately after collection, a 500-μL aliquot of the deidentified remnant sample was combined with 150 μL of DSAM, and the sample was shipped, overnight, in a temperature-controlled and monitored container to the offsite clinical laboratory to undergo the delayed analysis at 26 hours after collection. The remaining specimen was analyzed within 1 hour of collection at the clinical site, using the clinic’s standard operating procedures. Because the 1-hour semen analysis was performed at the clinical site, the 1-hour sample was split and underwent 2 independent analyses to provide a baseline for interoperator variation. These results were compared with provide a baseline measure of interoperator variation (Fig. 1B, comparison 2). The mean of the results from the 2 independent 1-hour analyses was then used for comparison with the paired 26-hour analysis performed at the reference laboratory (Fig. 1B, comparison 3).

Monitored shipment protocol

In study B, the 26-hour specimens were shipped to the reference laboratory in a temperature-monitored shipping container that is provided as part of the mail-in semen analysis service. Initial validation experiments determined that 2–10 °C was the ideal transport temperature required to maintain specimen integrity in DSAM (data not shown). Given the dramatic changes that can occur during real-world shipping scenarios, a shipping container system was designed to carefully control specimen temperatures to ensure specimen integrity and adherence to the validated testing protocol. This shipping system included phase change materials, frozen gel packs, and a foil-lined polystyrene cooler. Additionally, each shipping container included a temperature monitoring and logging device that recorded the temperature profile of the specimen through transit. On receiving each container, temperature profile data were assessed to ensure that the specimen remained within the validated temperature range throughout the shipping process. A random sample of external and internal temperature measurements for specimens shipped in the coldest and hottest months is illustrated in Supplemental Figure 1 (available online).

Semen analysis

Samples evaluated at the clinical reference laboratory underwent a complete semen analysis following the WHO standards (Supplemental Table 1, available online). Physical evaluation of the semen specimen was performed to determine liquefaction, viscosity, appearance, and pH, whereas flow cytometry was used to analyze viability, using recombinant annexin V conjugated to green-fluorescent Alexa Fluor 488 dye and SYTOX Green nucleic acid stain and for analysis of CD45+ white blood cells. All results of these analyses were consistent between the paired 1- and 26-hour analyses and within the normal range (data not shown). To evaluate sperm concentration (reported as 106 cells/mL) and motility/progression (reported as total motility, % normal), samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour and then applied to Cell-Vu slides, with a Mini-Therm plate, and then analyzed by an andrologist using MetaMorph software. Although no postanalysis adjustment was needed for our 26-hour samples to pass validation, a minor adjustment algorithm was found to further improve consistency between the 1- and 26-hour motility values and was, therefore, applied to the 26-hour motility values after analysis. All motility results represented postadjusted motility values. For assessment of sperm morphology, samples were applied to slides and dried before being fixed with paraformaldehyde. Samples then underwent rehydration via sequential ethanol immersion (100%–50%) and were stained using hematoxylin. Morphology was graded on the basis of strict Kruger guidelines (reported as % normal) by an andrologist using MetaMorph software.

Samples evaluated at the clinical site (1 hour × 2, study B) were performed by 2 experienced clinic andrologists, using the site’s standard operating procedure that is based on the standard WHO protocol. Microscopic assessments of sperm concentration and motility were reported as 106 cells/mL and total motility (% normal), respectively. The clinical site used a morphology protocol that differed from that used by the clinical laboratory performing the 26-hour assessment, and therefore, morphology measures could not be compared between 1 hour and 26 hours in the study B convenience sample.

Data analysis

The relationship between the 1- and 26-hour paired semen analyses were represented by the standard clinical laboratory validation measures, coefficient of variation (%CV = [SD/mean] × 100), and bias (%bias = (1 hour − 26 hours)/1 hour × 100), representing the difference of the experimental measure relative to the referent measure. Sample size estimation determined that for detecting a 15% difference in paired comparisons with a power = 0.80, n = 52. The %CV results presented here represent intrasample %CV, a measure of precision for the repeated analysis of a split sample. The D’Agostino-Pearson and Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated that %CV data had a nonnormal distribution and, therefore, %CVs were further characterized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). Clinical concordance (CC) was determined using the cutoff values established by the WHO Fifth Edition: sperm concentration normal, ≥15.0 × 106/mL; total motility normal, ≥40%; and morphology (Kruger, strict) normal, ≥4% (Fig. 3). College of Anatomical Pathologist (CAP) survey results were collected for the study period and included sperm concentration, SEMA-2016, SEM-02 (preprepared cells); sperm motility SPCD-A 2016, SPCD-02 (premade video); and sperm morphology, SEMA-2016, SEM-06 (premade slides).

Clinical concordance (%CC) and percentage of normal or abnormal outcomes for motility (A), concentration (B), and morphology (C). The %CC represents the percentage of paired analyses that provided matching stratification of results into a predetermined “normal” or “abnormal” category that was defined using the fifth centile clinical reference ranges for that semen parameter. The percentage of normal and abnormal outcomes for each sample is represented graphically, and the %CC is included for each study comparison (study A, comparison 1, 1 hour vs. 26 hours; study B, comparison 2, 1 hour, andrologist 1 (And1), vs. 1 hour, andrologist 2 (And2); study B, comparison 3, 1 hour vs. 26 hours).

Institutional review board

Both study protocols A and B used remnant clinical samples that were deidentified before being received by the clinical research team. There was complete separation between the sample analysis used for patient management (not part of research study or conducted by study team) and the research study analyses. Researchers had/have no access to identifiable information or sample keys linking patient data to the research samples, and therefore, the study did not qualify as human subject research and did not require institutional review board approval.

Results

Study A: %CV and %bias for delayed semen analyses in a controlled laboratory setting

The 1- and 26-hour results were consistent for each semen parameter in study A; the %CVs were 9.0% for total motility, 4.0% for concentration, and 3.0% for morphology with %bias values of −3.8, −0.3, and −0.3, respectively (Table 1). To further illustrate the assay performance over the broader study sample, the median %CV and IQR are also presented (Fig. 2). For study A (comparison 1), the median %CVs (upper IQRs) for total motility, concentration, and morphology were 6.6 (10.4), 2.3 (4.4), and 0 (5), respectively, which were in concordance with the results presented in Table 1 and demonstrated the consistency between the 1- and 26-hour measures over the broader study sample (Fig. 2A to C, %CV1).

Study B: %CV and %bias for delayed semen analyses in real-world conditions including commercial shipment to reference laboratory

In study B, the 1-hour (referent) semen analysis was performed at the clinical collection site, whereas the experimental fraction was shipped in a temperature-controlled container to the reference laboratory, where it was analyzed at 26 hours after collection. Because the 1-hour semen analysis was not performed at the reference laboratory, an additional comparison was added to study B to provide a baseline of the interoperator variation at the clinical site. To perform this, the 1-hour sample was split and analyzed by 2 andrologists at the clinical site, independently, and those results were compared (study B, comparison 2). The mean intrasample %CVs for the 2 analyses of the same specimen at 1 hour were 15% for total motility, 17% for concentration, and 21% for morphology, with %bias values of −6.0, 8.0, and 29.5, respectively (Table 1, study B, comparison 2). Notably, the %CV was higher for the 2 1-hour analyses performed at the clinical site than that for the 1- and 26-hour analyses in study A (Table 1). Next, the mean of the 2 1-hour semen analyses was compared with the 26-hour analysis performed at the reference laboratory after being shipped, commercially, using a monitored temperature-controlled shipping container. The mean intrasample %CVs were 15% for total motility and 27% for concentration, with %bias values of 3.4 and 10.9, respectively (Table 1, study B, comparison 3). Morphology could not be compared because of the use of disparate methodologies at the clinical site and reference laboratory. Interestingly, all 3 comparisons had considerably less variation compared with that measured between laboratories using controlled materials as a part of CAP survey testing; the %CVs for CAP testing during the study period were 31% for motility (n = 862), 37% for concentration (n = 900), and 76% for morphology (n = 70) (Table 1).

To further illustrate the assay performance over the broader study sample, the median %CV and IQR are also presented (Fig. 2). In alignment with Table 1, comparison 2 was more variable than study A, comparison 1, with median %CVs (upper IQRs) for total motility, concentration, and morphology of 8.8 (18.8), 10 (23), and 16 (28), respectively (Fig. 2A to C, %CV2). For comparison 3, the median %CV for total motility was 9.3, which was higher than that for comparison 2, yet comparison 2 had a larger IQR (15.8 > 11.8) and was, therefore, more variable overall (Fig. 2A, %CV2/%CV3). The median %CV (upper IQR) for concentration was 22.5 (38) for comparison 3, which was higher than that for comparison 2 and more variable (Fig. 2B, %CV2/%CV3).

Study B: performance of monitored shipment device

In study B, the 26-hour specimens were shipped to the reference laboratory in a temperature-monitored shipping container that was developed specifically as part of the mail-in semen analysis service. Given the dramatic changes that can occur during real-world shipping scenarios, the container system was designed to carefully control and monitor specimen temperatures to ensure specimen integrity and adherence to the validated testing protocol. On receiving each container, temperature profile data were assessed to ensure that the specimen remained within the validated temperature range throughout the shipping process. To demonstrate the performance of this system, a random sample of specimens shipped during the coldest (n = 15, January) and hottest (n = 15, August) months were selected, and the system’s external and internal temperature measurements were represented graphically, over time of shipment (Supplemental Fig. 1). External temperatures ranged from 30 °C to − 10 °C, whereas, as designed, the system maintained the sample temperature between 2 and 4 °C throughout shipment (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Study A and study B: CC

Finally, the %CC was calculated for each comparison using the cutoff values established by the fifth edition WHO manual for semen analysis: sperm concentration normal, ≥15.0 × 106/mL; total motility normal, ≥40%; and morphology (Kruger, strict) normal, ≥4% (Fig. 3). The %CC represented the percentage of paired analyses that provided matching stratification of results into a predetermined “normal” or “abnormal” category that was defined using the fifth centile clinical reference ranges for that semen parameter. The percentage of normal and abnormal outcomes for each sample is represented graphically, and the %CC is included for each comparison (Fig. 3). Similar to the other outcome measures, the study A comparison between the 1- and 26-hour measures yielded the most concordant results for total motility (CC, 94.2%), concentration (CC, 100%), and morphology (CC, 98.5%) (Fig. 3). In study B, comparisons 2 (1 hour/1 hour) and 3 (mean, 1 hour/26 hours) had similar CC values for total motility (CC, 87.5% vs. 86%) and concentration (CC, 95.2% vs. 93.1%) (Fig. 3). Interestingly, comparison 3 concentration had high CC and, therefore, did not appear affected by the increased variation illustrated in Figure 2B.

Discussion

Here, we present the results from 2 prospective cohort studies, demonstrating the clinical utility of a mail-in delayed semen analysis protocol. We validated a comprehensive semen analysis of specimens 26 hours after collection, reporting several key measures of male fertility, including total motility, concentration, and morphology. The delayed semen analyses were highly consistent with the 1-hour referent analyses in both controlled and real-world settings, which included commercial shipment of specimens to the reference laboratory before analyses.

The objective of study A was to characterize semen parameters before and after storage in proprietary DSAM, between 2 and 8 °C, for 26 hours to determine how these conditions affect sample integrity. Comparative semen analyses are inherently challenging because of the considerable interoperator and interlaboratory variations associated with the technique, despite the availability of standardized protocols (4, 5, 6, 7, 8). It is, therefore, critical that comparative and/or repeat semen analyses be performed in an environment with standardized protocols and minimal external sources of variation. For these reasons, in our initial validation study, all analyses were performed in the same reference laboratory, using a standardized WHO-based protocol. Importantly, the 1- and 26-hour comparisons made in study A were highly precise (mean/median %CV, <10%; maximum IQR, 10.4; and CC, >94%, for total motility, concentration, and morphology parameters), and we could conclude that semen specimen integrity could be maintained for 26 hours in these conditions.

Study B was designed to test the performance of our delayed semen analysis protocol in conditions that better mimic a mail-in “real-world” service, which are inherently more variable. This validation study demonstrated that the delayed semen analysis service performed with acceptable accuracy and greater accuracy than that between laboratories, yet these results most likely underestimate the assay’s true performance as a result of the interlaboratory variation in semen analyses. The differences in interassay precision between the 2 study sites can be glimpsed when comparing the results of study A, comparison 1, with those of study B, comparison 2. Comparison 2, performed at the clinical site, was between 2 aliquots of the same sample at the same time point (1 hour), yet it was more variable than comparison 1, performed at the reference laboratory, between 1- and 26-hour analyses. The CAP surveys can also provide an estimate of interlaboratory variation in semen analyses; indeed, CAP survey results during the study period were more variable than any of the study comparisons, even with the addition of interlaboratory variation in study B. Therefore, although the delayed semen analysis still passed validation in study B with a CC of 0.87 (above the Food and Drug Administration requirement of 0.8) and %CV well below CAP measures for all parameters, the additional interlaboratory variation most certainly reduced the 1- and 26-hour concordance, meaning that our results most likely underrepresent assay performance.

These studies have several important strengths. First, our study sample consisted of men seeking fertility assessment, which is the target population for this testing option. As expected, sampling men seeking fertility assessment provided a range of results, ensuring that our analysis was validated over a spectrum of normal and abnormal samples. Another key strength of our study was the inclusion of a validation sample that tested the effect of real-world commercial shipping conditions. This is especially important for a commercial test provided in the United States, which will be shipped over a range of 3 time zones and climates that can range from subfreezing temperatures to desert and subtropic conditions (demonstrated in Supplemental Fig. 1). Finally, the use of a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–approved commercial laboratory was an important strength that demonstrates the rigor involved in test validation and the feasibility of providing this as a commercial offering.

Alternatively, as is the case with most research using convenience samples, the current study also has limitations. For example, because we used real-world samples from fertility clinics in our second study, we could not include comparisons of morphology because of differences in protocols. This design also led to additional variation between the 1- and 26-hour assessments in study B, due to differences between the clinic and reference laboratory protocols for semen analysis, including the use of computer-assisted sperm analysis in 1 protocol and manual analysis in the other. This meant that differences in the performance of the delayed semen analysis in study B could be attributed to not only the effects of the processing/shipping of the 26 hours sample but also the additional interoperator and interlaboratory variation associated with performing the analyses at different sites. Finally, we did not have any demographic or clinical variables, which may have been used to further characterize and stratify our sample.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a mail-in delayed semen analysis protocol and demonstrate that it is a robust and clinically validated tool. Although the advantages of mail-in home collections kits are clear, there are significant challenges in maintaining sample integrity for an extended timeframe and throughout transportation to the laboratory. Therefore, the validation of this protocol represents an important step toward increasing access to comprehensive male fertility diagnostics.
