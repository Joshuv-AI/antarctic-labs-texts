SORCS2 serves as a tumor suppressor and associates with immune infiltration in ovarian cancer
Authors: Yiling Qiu, Zhijing Chen, Xiaoqin Chen, Xiaolan Lai, Cuiliu Zhao, Le Chen, Shaotao Zhong, Ying Xie, Yanping Jiang, Yan Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer (OC) is a highly aggressive gynecological malignancy, with a poor 5-year survival rate of less than 50%. Identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets is crucial to improving patient prognosis. Sortilin-related VPS10 domain-containing receptor 2 (SORCS2) regulates various biological processes and is implicated in carcinogenesis, yet its specific role in OC remains unclear.Materials and methodsMultiple bio-informatics analyses were used to analyze SORCS2 expression and potential role in OC. Cell proliferation, migration assays and flow cytometry were performed to assess the effects of SORCS2 on OC cells. Further, Xenograft mouse models were built to identify its function in OC cell growth in vivo.ResultsIn this study, we first identified that SORCS2 expression is down-regulated in OC tissues, correlating with adverse clinical characteristics and poor clinical outcomes, emphasizing its potential as a prognostic marker for OC. In vitro, SORCS2 over-expression suppressed cell proliferation and migration, induced cell cycle arrest at the S/G2 phase via the p21/CCNA1 pathway, and induced apoptosis by regulating PIDD1 and CASP9. Xenograft mouse models further validated the tumor-suppressive effects of SORCS2 in vivo. Additionally, SORCS2 was found to participate in tumor-specific immune responses by promoting T cell activation and release of immune factors.ConclusionOur findings underscore SORCS2 as a tumor suppressor in OC development and highlight its potential as a prognostic marker for OC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01822-z.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC), one of the most common gynecological malignancy, is a heterogeneous group of cancers originating from ovarian cells. Epithelial ovarian cancers, accounting for approximately 90% of ovarian malignancies, can be classified into five subtypes: low-grade and high-grade serous cancers, endometrioid, clear cell, and mucinous cancers [1]. In 2022, OC accounted for 3.4% of all new cancer diagnoses and 4.8% of cancer-related deaths worldwide [2]. Two decades ago, the incidence of OC was particularly high in North America and Northern Europe but has since declined, while cases have increased in parts of East Asia and Eastern Europe [3]. Unfortunately, the majority of OC cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 50%, due to its asymptomatic nature and the absence of effective early screening methods [4]. Although OC typically responds to platinum-based chemotherapy, relapse and metastasis following surgery and chemotherapy often leads to treatment failure [5]. Despite recent advances in endocrine therapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the long-term prognosis for advanced OC remains poor. Identifying novel biomarkers and understanding the mechanisms underlying OC carcinogenesis and progression are crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Sortilin-related VPS10 domain-containing receptor 2 (SORCS2), a member of the VPS10 domain family, is located on chromosome 4p16.1 and encodes a transmembrane receptor crucial for cell sorting, signaling, and intercellular communication [6, 7]. During embryogenesis, SORCS2 expression is dynamically regulated and is notably abundant in the central nervous system and kidneys [8, 9]. SORCS2 is implicated in several biological processes, including growth-cone collapse, synaptic plasticity, dopaminergic innervation, and apoptosis [10–14]. Emerging research highlights the potential role of SORCS2 in carcinogenesis and tumor progression, suggesting it may serve as a prognostic marker and biomarker in colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and OC [15–17]. Bioinformatics analysis has also linked SORCS2 to immune response modulation through TGF-β signaling in OC [17]. However, the precise biological role and regulatory mechanisms of SORCS2 in OC remain unclear.

This study utilized multiple bioinformatics databases and analyzed 96 pairs of OC samples to assess SORCS2 expression levels and their correlations with clinicopathological characteristics and patient outcomes. Functional enrichment analyses and the construction of a gene network were utilized to assess the potential role of SORCS2. Additionally, the associations between SORCS2 and tumor immune infiltration, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), immunostimulators, and immunoinhibitors, were examined. In vitro experiments were performed to evaluate the influence of SORCS2 on cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, migration, apoptosis and immune evasion in OC cells. Xenograft models were established to examine the impact of SORCS2 on tumor growth in vivo. This study aims to elucidate the function of SORCS2 in OC carcinogenesis and progression, suggesting its potential as both a prognostic marker and a tumor suppressor.

Methods

Bioinformatic data and analysis

The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA), which integrates and normalize data from the GTEx and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases by transcripts per million, was utilized to assess the expression levels of SORCS2 across different cancer types, with a particular emphasis on OC [18]. To validate SORCS2 expression in both normal ovarian and OC tissues, three datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were utilized: GSE12470 (including 10 normal ovarian and 43 °C samples), GSE18520 (comprising 10 normal ovarian and 53 °C samples), and GSE54388 (featuring 6 normal ovarian and 18 °C samples). Co-expressed genes linked to SORCS2 were identified utilizing the cBioPortal database, applying stringent criteria (|Spearman’s correlation coefficient| >0.5 and P-value < 0.05) [19]. These co-expressed genes underwent functional enrichment analysis via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, with visualizations created using the R package “ggplot2.” Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out to identify significantly enriched gene sets and pathways between high- and low-SORCS2 groups. The gene set was considered as an enriched group when the nominal p value and FDR value < 0.05. Additionally, a gene network map and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of SORCS2 were generated using the GeneMANIA and STRING platforms to explore the relationships between SORCS2 and its neighboring genes [20, 21].

Tumor immune infiltration analyses

TISIDB, a comprehensive web portal for exploring tumor-immune interactions, was utilized to examine the correlations between SORCS2 and TILs (B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, macrophages, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, and neutrophils), as well as immunostimulators and immunoinhibitors [22]. RNA sequencing data for OC samples were obtained from TCGA, with data updated as of September 2024. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were applied to assess the relationships between SORCS2 expression and the gene markers for TILs and immune checkpoints.

OC tissue sample acquisition and patient follow-up

OC and adjacent ovarian tissue samples were collected from 96 patients diagnosed with OC at Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital (GRCH) and Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (GPPH) between January 1 st, 2020, and December 31 st, 2023. All patients provided informed consent and were followed up until December 31 st, 2024 with a median follow-up time of 28.5 months. Each tissue sample was evaluated and diagnosed as OC by two independent pathologists. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to the last follow-up or death, while progression-free survival (PFS) was measured from treatment initiation to tumor progression. Correlation analyses were performed between SORCS2 expression and clinicopathological data, including age, carbohydrate antigen (CA125) levels, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, lymphovascular invasion, tumor differentiation, and International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics stage. Based on the median H scores, patients were categorized into high-SORCS2 and low-SORCS2 groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to assess the prognostic significance of SORCS2 expression in these groups. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were applied to identify independent prognostic factors for OC. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and granted by the Ethics Committees of GRCH and GPPH, with the number of ethical approval as 2024-484-01.

Cell culture and transfection

Normal human ovarian epithelial cells, IOSE80 and OC cell lines including A2780, SKOV3, ES-2, OVCAR3, CAOV-3, and HO-8910, were obtained from Pricella Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). All OC cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Servicebio, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. To achieve SORCS2 overexpression, the Puro-CMV-SORCS2-3×FLAG vector (OBiO Technology, China) was transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015) at a ratio of 4:3:1 (SORCS2 plasmid : psPAX2 : pMD2.G). Viral particles were harvested 72 h post-transfection following the manufacturer’s protocol (System Biosciences, San Francisco, US). For lentiviral infection, OC cells were seeded and infected with the concentrated virus in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, H9268) with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) as 20. After 24 h, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium and positive cells were selected by 2 µg/mL puromycin for one week. Two siRNAs (Genomeditech, China) targeting SORCS2 were used to knock down SORCS2 expression in OC cells. The transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, 13778150) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at 60–70% confluence with 50 nM siRNA. The siRNA-lipid complex was prepared in Opti-MEM medium (Gibco) and added to the cells. The medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 6 h post-transfection. Cells were harvested for RNA and protein analysis 48 h after transfection. The sequences of the siRNAs are as follows:

T cell co-culture experiment

To co-culture OC cells with T cells, peripheral blood from healthy donors was diluted and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Yuningwei Biotechnology, China). T cell subsets were isolated from PBMCs using the Dynabeads™ Untouched™ Human T Cell Kit (Thermo Fisher, 11344D), activated with Dynabeads™ Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher, 11131D) and then cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. T cells were seeded in the upper Transwell chamber and OC cells were seeded in the lower chamber at a ratio of 5:1 (T cells : OC cells) for co-culture. After 5 days, T cells and culture supernatant were collected and subjected to further studies.

In vitro functional experiments

Cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis assays were conducted to evaluate the proliferative and migratory abilities of OC cells. For the CCK-8 assay, 1200 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of CCK-8 solution. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and absorbance at OD450 was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h using a microplate reader. For the colony formation assay, 2000 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured at 37 °C for 10 days. After removing the culture medium, cells were fixed with 75% ethanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The number of colonies formed was counted to evaluate cell proliferation. For Transwell assays, 1 × 10⁵ cells were plated in the upper chambers of Transwell plates with 200 µL of serum-free medium, while the lower chambers contained medium with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the cells in the lower chambers were fixed with 75% ethanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Further flow cytometry was employed to evaluate the cell cycle and apoptosis. OC cells were harvested, fixed in 75% ethanol at 4 °C overnight, and stained with PI & RNase Staining Buffer (Servicebio, China) for cell cycle analysis. For apoptosis analysis, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC (Servicebio, China). Flow cytometry (Novocyte D2060R, Agilent) was utilized to assess the DNA content and determine cell cycle stages and apoptotic status. The proportion of cells in different phases of the cell cycle and the proportion undergoing apoptosis were calculated.

Animal experiments

To establish xenograft models, eight-week-old female Balb/c nude mice (OBiO Technology, China) were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6) and subcutaneously injected with 5 × 10⁵ SKOV3/SORCS2-OV or control cells. The investigators were aware of the group allocation during the animal experiments. Mouse body weights and tumor volumes were monitored and recorded weekly. After five weeks, the mice were euthanized, and the subcutaneous tumors were harvested, weighed, and subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis for further evaluation. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare & Ethics Committee of GRCH (Approval No. 2024-484-01). The humane endpoints included rapid loss of weight, loss of appetite and tumor diameter exceeding 20 mm.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), Western blot, IHC and Elisa

RT-qPCR was utilized to evaluate the mRNA expression levels of target genes. Total RNA was isolated by TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, China). Then, RCP was performed with SYBR Green mix (Servicebio, China) and specific primers (Table S1) and the 2−∆∆Ct method was used to quantify the relative mRNA expression of target genes. Western blotting and IHC were employed to analyze the protein expression of the target molecule. For western blotting, protein concentrations from cells and tissues were measured utilizing the BCA protein assay. Proteins were then separated by 8–10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking with rapid blocking buffer (Servicebio, China) for 15 min, the membrane was incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation with secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized utilizing the Amersham Imager 680 system (Cytiva, USA). The quantitative densitometry of protein bands was analyzed by Image J. For IHC staining, paraffin-embedded mouse xenograft samples and human OC tissues were deparaffinized, blocked, and incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The stained samples were imaged and the H-scores were calculated by AIPATHWELL software (Servicebio, China) automatically. The H-scores were generated as follow: H-SCORE=∑(pi×i)=(percentage of weak intensity×1)+(percentage of moderate intensity×2)+(percentage of strong intensity×3). The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2. The following procedures were performed for the detection of IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α levels using ELISA: Firstly, the supernatant from co-cultured T cells and OC cells was collected and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. Next, the levels of IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α were measured according to the instructions provided in Human IL-2 ELISA Kit (SEKH-0008), Human IFN-γ ELISA Kit (SEKH-0046) and Human TNF-α ELISA Kit (SEKH-0047) from Solarbio company.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed utilizing the Student’s t-test and ANOVA, while categorical variables were compared utilizing the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was applied to evaluate associations between variables. Each experiment was performed independently three times and the quantitative data were shown as mean ± standard deviation unless specified otherwise. Statistical analyses and data visualizations were carried out utilizing R software 4.3.2 and GraphPad Prism 8.3.0. The P-value < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance unless otherwise noted.

Results

SORCS2 is down-regulated in OC

Differential analyses were performed based on public databases to explore the function of SORCS2 in OC tumorigenesis. GEPIA revealed that SORCS2 expression was downregulated across various cancer types, with a particularly significant reduction in OC (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1A). Validation using three GEO datasets (GSE12470, GSE18520, and GSE54388) confirmed lower SORCS2 expression in OC tissues compared with normal ovarian tissues (Fig. 1B). RT-qPCR analysis of 25 paired fresh OC and normal ovarian tissue samples further corroborated the decreased mRNA levels of SORCS2 in OC (Fig. 1C). Additionally, IHC analysis of 96 paired OC and adjacent normal ovarian tissues demonstrated lower H scores in OC tissues (Fig. 1D-E).

Loss of SORCS2 is associated with adverse clinical characteristics and outcomes in OC

Based on H scores, 96 OC patients were classified into high-SORCS2 and low-SORCS2 groups. Lower SORCS2 expression was associated with adverse clinical features, including elevated CA125 levels, lymph node metastasis, poor tumor differentiation, and advanced TNM stage (Fig. 2A; Table 1). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with low SORCS2 expression had poorer OS (P = 1.181e-05, HR = 0.43) and DFS (P = 2.737e-02, HR = 0.56) versus those with high SORCS2 expression (Fig. 2B-C). Furthermore, Cox regression analysis identified low SORCS2 expression as an independent prognostic risk factor for both OS and PFS in OC (Fig. 2D-E; Tables 2 and 3). Collectively, these findings highlight SORCS2 as a potential prognostic biomarker for OC.

Functional enrichment analyses and neighbor gene network of SORCS2 in OC

To further examine the potential role of SORCS2 in OC, 307 co-expressed genes linked to SORCS2 were identified utilizing the cBioPortal database and subjected to functional enrichment analysis via DAVID 6.8. GO analysis indicated that SORCS2 is involved in key biological processes such as cell population proliferation, cell cycle regulation, angiogenesis, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and multiple oncogenic pathways, such as Wnt, Notch, and TGF-β pathways (Fig. 3A). KEGG analysis further suggested that SORCS2 is implicated in biological processes related to focal adhesion, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and cancer-related pathways (Fig. 3B). As shown in Fig. 3C, GSEA identified significantly enriched biological processes in high- and low-SORCS2 groups, including adhesion chemokine signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, TGF-β pathway, EMT, apoptosis, and immune responses. Additionally, network analysis through GeneMANIA and STRING platforms revealed that SORCS2 (central node) was closely linked to multiple genes, including BDNF, NTF3, NGF, NGFR, SORT1, SORCS1, SORCS3, PMEL, GPNMB, SORL1, KIAA0319L, TMEM130, PKD1L1, KIAA0319, ZNF608, PCOLCE, LRP1, PKDJ, NCALD and NPTX2 (Fig. 3D). The PPI network suggested that SORCS2 is functionally associated with BDNF, NGFR, VPS26A, VPS35, PSRC1, CELSR2, SORT1, GGA2, NTF3 and NGF (Fig. 3E).

SORCS2 inhibits the proliferation and migration of OC cells

RT-qPCR was utilized to assess the mRNA expression levels of SORCS2 in normal human ovarian epithelial cells and various OC cell lines. The results demonstrated that SORCS2 expression was low in SKOV3 cells but relatively high in OVCAR3 cells (Fig. 4A). To explore the functional role of SORCS2, gain-of-function experiments were performed in SKOV3 cells with stable SORCS2 overexpression (SKOV3/SORCS2-OV), while loss-of-function experiments were conducted in OVCAR3 cells using two siRNAs targeting SORCS2 (OVCAR3/SORCS2-KD). RT-qPCR and western blot analyses confirmed successful modulation of SORCS2 expression (Fig. 4B-C). The effect of SORCS2 on cell proliferation and viability was assessed using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. Overexpression of SORCS2 significantly suppressed SKOV3 cell proliferation, while SORCS2 knockdown enhanced the proliferative capacity of OVCAR3 cells (Fig. 4D-E). Additionally, Transwell assays demonstrated that SORCS2 overexpression reduced the migratory capacity of SKOV3 cells, while SORCS2 silencing enhanced OVCAR3 cell migration (Fig. 4F). These results suggest that SORCS2 acts as a negative regulator of OC cell proliferation and migration.

SORCS2 is associated with immune infiltration in OC

The tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly TILs, plays a vital role in OC tumorigenesis and progression [23]. The TISIDB database was utilized to examine the association of SORCS2 with tumor immune infiltration. As depicted in Fig. 5A, SORCS2 exhibited a predominantly positive correlation with TILs in OC. Notably, SORCS2 showed the strongest positive correlations with the abundance of type 1 T helper (Th1) cells (R = 0.403, P = 2.24e-13), central memory CD8+ (Tcm CD8) T cell (R = 0.382, P = 6.15e-12), macrophage (R = 0.374, P = 1.68e-11), plasmacytoid dendritic cell (R = 0.372, P = 2.22e-11), central memory CD4+ (Tcm CD4) T cell (R = 0.372, P = 2.22e-11) and mast cell (R = 0.369, P = 3.47e-11) (Fig. 5B). For immunostimulators, SORCS2 exhibited the highest positive correlations with NT5E, CXCL12 and CD276 (Fig. S2A-B). Among immunoinhibitors, SORCS2 showed strong correlations with TGFB1, KDR and IL10 (Fig. S2C-D). Additionally, SORCS2 was positively correlated with gene markers of general T cell (CD2), CD8+ T cell (CD8A and CD8B), general macrophage (CD11b), M1 macrophage (CD80), as well as immune checkpoint markers including PD-1 and TIM3 (Fig. 5C-D). Subsequently, we co-cultured SKOV3 cells with T cells in Transwell chambers and tested the components of the supernatant of the culture medium (Fig. 5E). As revealed by western blot, SKOV3 cells over-expressing SORCS2 co-cultured with T cells increased the expression of activation markers CD25, CD44, and CD69 compared to the control group (Fig. 5F). Elisa assays showed the levels of immune factors, including IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in the supernatant of SORCS2 over-expressed group were higher compared with the control group (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that SORCS2 may participate in tumor-specific immune responses by promoting T cell activation and release of immune factors.

SORCS2 arrests the cell cycle and promotes apoptosis of OC cells

Given that previous functional enrichment analyses suggested the involvement of SORCS2 in cell proliferation and apoptosis, flow cytometry was employed to further elucidate its role in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. Overexpression of SORCS2 led to cell cycle arrest at the S/G2 phase in SKOV3 cells, while SORCS2 downregulation increased the proportion of OVCAR3 cells in the S phase (Fig. 6A). Consistent with these findings, data from the GEPIA database showed a positive link between SORCS2 expression and P21, a key cell cycle inhibitor, while a negative correlation was observed with CCNA1, a critical cell cycle promoter (Fig. 6B) [24]. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses further validated that SORCS2 overexpression upregulated P21 and downregulated CCNA1 in SKOV3 cells (Fig. 6C-D). Flow cytometry analysis also revealed that the proportion of apoptotic SKOV3 cells increased following SORCS2 overexpression, whereas SORCS2 knockdown reduced apoptosis in OVCAR3 cells (Fig. 6E). Positive correlations were identified between SORCS2 expression and apoptotic regulators PIDD1 and CASP9 (Fig. 6F), both of which are known to promote apoptosis [25, 26]. Upregulation of PIDD1 and CASP9 was further confirmed in SORCS2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6G-H). In summary, these findings suggest that SORCS2 induces S/G2 cell cycle arrest and promotes apoptosis.

Over-expression of SORCS2 inhibits OC tumor growth in vivo

To further ascertain the influence of SORCS2 on tumor growth in vivo, BALB/c-nude mice (n = 6) were employed to establish xenograft by subcutaneously injecting SKOV3/SORCS2-OV and control cells. As indicated in Fig. 7A-B, the overexpression of SORCS2 significantly inhibited tumor growth, with the tumor growth rate notably reduced in the SORCS2-OV group compared to the control group at the end of the experiment. Tumor weight was also significantly lower in the SORCS2-OV group (Fig. 7C), further supporting the inhibitory effect of SORCS2 on tumor progression. IHC assays on tumor tissues revealed reduced expression levels of Ki-67 and CD34 in the SORCS2-OV group, while TUNEL staining was markedly increased (Fig. 7D). These findings indicate that SORCS2 overexpression significantly suppressed tumor growth, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in OC treatment.

Discussion

Discovering novel biomarkers and understanding the molecular mechanisms driving carcinogenesis and progression is essential for developing effective therapeutic targets and improving patient outcomes in OC. Our study first identified the downregulation of SORCS2 in OC tissues, which was linked to adverse clinical features, such as elevated CA125 levels, lymph node metastasis, poor tumor differentiation, and advanced TNM stage. After multivariate adjustment, low expression of SORCS2 was identified as an independent prognostic factor for patients’ survival, emphasizing its potential as a prognostic marker for OC. Although the cell lines used in vitro experiments (SKOV3, OVCAR3) have differing origins and TP53 status, it’s indicated that SORCS2 overexpression inhibited cell proliferation and migration, caused cell cycle arrest at the S/G2 phase via the p21/CCNA1 pathway, and promoted apoptosis through the regulation of PIDD1 and CASP9 in OC cells. Furthermore, SORCS2 was linked to the abundance of TILs and specific immune gene markers, suggesting its potential involvement in tumor-specific immune responses. SORCS2 over-expression promoted T cell activation and release of immune factors, implying a potential role in inhibiting immune evasion. In vivo validation in xenograft mouse models further confirmed the tumor-suppressive effects of SORCS2. Collectively, these findings position SORCS2 as both a tumor suppressor and a potential prognostic biomarker in OC.

Cell cycle dysregulation and impaired apoptosis are fundamental biological characteristics underlying abnormal cell proliferation and tumorigenesis in malignancies [27, 28]. Our functional enrichment analyses highlighted that SORCS2 and its associated genes play roles in critical processes such as cell proliferation, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis. Flow cytometry results indicated that SORCS2 overexpression blocked S/G2 cell cycle progression and promoted apoptosis in OC cells. Cell cycle checkpoints, including cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), act as DNA surveillance mechanisms that delay cell cycle progression and induce cell death in response to irreparable DNA damage [29]. P21, also known as p21, is a CDK inhibitor that arrests cell cycle progression by inhibiting cyclin/CDK complexes, including CCNA/CDK2, CCNE/CDK2, and CCND/CDK4 [24]. Our bioinformatic analysis showed a positive correlation between SORCS2 and P21, while a negative correlation with CCNA1 was observed. In SKOV3 cells, SORCS2 overexpression increased P21 mRNA and protein levels while decreasing CCNA1 expression, yet the exact signaling mechanism-such as whether the intracellular domain of SORCS2 directly triggers a pathway that upregulates p21 is still unknown. Future rescue or blocking experiments would be essential to establish causality. Previous studies have identified SORCS2 as an extracellular receptor for proneurotrophin ligands that trigger apoptotic pathways [10]. In neuronal cells, the ternary complex of the SORCS2/Sortilin/p75 neurotrophic receptor has been well-studied for its role in inducing apoptosis and regulating cell fate [10]. Our study extends these findings, showing that SORCS2 overexpression in OC cells increases the expression of two key apoptotic regulators, PIDD1 and CASP9. PIDD1, a pro-apoptotic protein identified in 2000, is known to activate the NF-κB pathway and initiate mitochondrial cell death via CASP2 activation [25, 30]. The pro-apoptotic function of PIDD1 is attributed to its death domain at the C-terminal end [31]. Our results suggest that SORCS2 regulates PIDD1 levels in OC cells, although the specific interaction and binding sites between SORCS2 and PIDD1 require further investigation.

Advances in understanding the TME and TILs have significantly contributed to the development of immunotherapies aimed at enhancing anti-tumor immune responses. These therapies utilize various strategies, including tumor antigen vaccines, cytokines, and monoclonal antibodies targeting immunosuppressive ligands [32]. However, OC remains one of the few cancers for which immunotherapy has shown limited effectiveness [33]. The primary challenges to successful immunotherapy in OC are an extremely immunosuppressive TME and a notably low mutational burden [34]. Studies have indicated the pivotal role of TILs, including T cells, macrophages, and NK cells, within the TME, in improving responses to immunotherapy [32]. Specifically, the presence of TILs, especially CD8 + T cells, is associated with improved clinical outcomes [35, 36]. Enhancing anti-cancer T-cell responses within the TME is essential for improving the effectiveness of immunotherapies. In our study, GSEA revealed SORCS2 was enriched in immune response pathways. The expression of SORCS2 was positively linked to the abundance of macrophages, central memory CD8 + T cells, central memory CD4 + T cells, and the expression of their associated gene markers. Beside, we found the over-expression of SORCS2 resulted in increased activation of T cells and the release of immune factors, which may inhibit tumor immune evasion, growth, and metastasis. To evade immune detection, tumor cells up-regulate immune checkpoints such as PD-1, CTLA4, and TIM3, which inhibit TIL activation and prevent excessive immune responses, thereby enabling immune escape [37]. Despite immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) being among the most investigated therapeutic strategies for OC, the clinical benefits of ICI monotherapy in OC have been unsatisfactory. As a result, ICIs are not yet recommended as a standalone treatment in clinical guidelines for OC. Combination therapies involving ICIs, PARP inhibitors, anti-angiogenic drugs, and other immunotherapies are currently being evaluated to enhance anti-tumor immunity in OC [33]. Our study also identified correlations between SORCS2 expression and immune checkpoint markers, including PD-1 and TIM3. Further research is necessary to explore the role of SORCS2 in regulating immune infiltration and immune checkpoint expression in OC.

Our work is the first one to reveal the low-expression of SORCS2 in OC and its tumor suppressive effect in vitro and in vivo. Although the upstream regulation on SORCS2 remains unclear, the low-expression of SORCS2 in OC might be attributed to the loss of heterozygosity or promoter hypermethylation. There are some limitations in our study. Firstly, external validations and prospective studies are needed to verify the prognostic predictive ability of SORCS2 in OC. Secondly, an additional OC cell line used in vivo experiments will strengthen the role of SORCS2 as a tumor suppressor in OC. Thirdly, although functional assays demonstrate tumor-suppressive effects of SORCS2 in OC, the direct evidence supporting regulatory molecular mechanism remains lacking. Finally, the xenograft model employed nude mice, which lack functional T cells, precluding in vivo validation of the role of SORCS2 in modulating tumor immune infiltration. Future studies using immunocompetent mouse models or direct analysis of immune cell markers (e.g., CD8) alongside SORCS2 in human OC tissue sections are warranted to define its role conclusively in TME.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that down-regulation of SORCS2 is associated with adverse clinical characteristics and poor outcomes in OC. SORCS2 arrested the S/G2 cell cycle progression via the p21/CCNA1 pathway and induced apoptosis through regulation of PIDD1 and CASP9. SORCS2 promoted T cell activation and release of immune factors to inhibit immune evasion. Overall, SORCS2 functions as a tumor suppressor and a prognostic marker in OC.
