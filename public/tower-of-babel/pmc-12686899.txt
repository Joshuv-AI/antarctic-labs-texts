AAV-mediated gene therapy for the CNS: The case for early treatment
Authors: Luanna E Liebscher Vidal, Jose M Martinez-Navio
Published: 2025

Main text

Gene therapy has emerged as a powerful tool for the treatment of a wide range of genetic and acquired diseases, and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have become the vector of choice for such therapies.1 Because of its capability to transduce neurons, relatively low immunogenicity, and potential for long-term expression, recombinant AAV has been exploited to treat a variety of neurological disorders, including many different types of central nervous system (CNS) diseases.2 AAV can be used to deliver endogenous therapeutic proteins, such as those used in protein replacement therapies in which a missing or malfunctioning protein is replaced with a functional copy; or non-endogenous therapeutic proteins, such as those intended for genome editing and/or the permanent correction of unwanted genetic mutations. An example of the latter are clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-associated (Cas) proteins. Despite remarkable potential, any gene therapy approach requires careful consideration of its limitations, particularly regarding the safety and precise delivery of exogenous CRISPR components. In a recent issue of Molecular Therapy Methods and Clinical Development, Duba-Kiss and Hampson3 have shared valuable insights about some of these limitations, providing information about the immunology, including neuroinflammation, and expression levels of AAV-mediated delivery of Cas9 in the CNS using a mouse model.

The study investigated the immune responses and neurotoxicity induced by AAV-delivered Cas9 in both the neonatal and adult mouse brain, showing that immune responses can be mitigated through early postnatal administration. In neonatal mice, Cas9 expression was well tolerated, with persistent expression even after 3 months and minimal neuroinflammation. The study used a lower AAV dose relative to intravenous administrations and injected directly in the brain. In contrast, adult administration of AAV-Cas9 led to significant neuronal loss, with microglial and astrocyte activation, and T cell and antibody immune responses, which likely contributed to the elimination of Cas9-expressing cells (see Figure 1). These findings emphasize the different neuroinflammation outcomes in two different stages of life, helping in our understanding of how the CNS deals with the expression of foreign proteins. It also highlights that performing gene therapy interventions as early as possible after birth is critical for the successful treatment of most neural disorders through gene editing.

There is cumulative evidence demonstrating that neonates, due to their immature immune system, are inclined to develop tolerance instead of immunity to transgene products, unlike adults.4,5 For instance, in a canine model of mucopolysaccharidosis type I, tolerance was described during AAV-mediated delivery of the enzyme α-l-iduronidase (IDUA) systemically in neonates.4 No antibodies were detected against the IDUA enzyme, and subsequent vector re-administration in the CNS (intrathecally) a month later resulted in robust IDUA expression, leading to efficient therapeutic activity and resolution of brain lesions.4 In the same report, AAV administered systemically at birth in rhesus monkeys exhibited tolerance to the transgene product (IDUA) that continued even after intrathecal AAV administration a month later.4 In line with this, a recent study showed sustained expression of potent and broadly neutralizing anti-HIV antibodies for more than 3 years after a single intramuscular administration of AAV in neonatal monkeys, which translated into effective protection from Simian-Human Immunodeficiency Virus infection.5 The study also showed that newborn rhesus macaques exhibited higher levels of AAV-mediated antibody expression than older infants and juveniles, indicating a correlation with age.5 Duba-Kiss and Hampson3 and these studies are consistent with the principles of neonatal tolerance and highlight the benefits of performing gene therapy treatment of neurological diseases (or any other gene-therapy approach) early in life.

A general concern regarding AAV administrations is the potential inflammation produced by the vector and/or the transgene product. Duba-Kiss and Hampson3 highlighted that the CNS is not a totally immune-privileged compartment: neonate mice expressing Cas9 presented a transitory activation of microglia, while adult mice did not express Cas9 and neuronal injury was observed, suggesting that adaptive immune responses led to the partial elimination of transduced cells. Interestingly, the authors performed a vector re-administration in the neonatal mice once they had become adults, injecting now the AAV-Cas9 in the opposite hemisphere of the brain. The expression of Cas9 protein in these mice was low, not detectable in all mice, associated with microglia activation, and with an observed reduction in the number of neurons. The findings suggest that the initial AAV-Cas9 administration post birth was able to reduce the inflammatory effects of the adult administration but could not entirely eliminate it. This highlights the difficulties associated with achieving sustained expression in vivo of exogenous proteins such as Cas9, an enzyme of bacterial origin. Other AAV-Cas9 studies have also reported inflammation and cellular senescence post vector administration, as well as increased levels of CD8+ T cells in the target tissue, which can mediate the elimination of transduced cells.6,7,8

The generation of antibodies to the transgene product can pose challenges. Humoral immune responses to AAV-delivered CRISPR systems have been reported in adult mice but were uncommon in neonates.7 Duba-Kiss and Hampson3 detected high levels of anti-Cas9 antibodies post AAV9-Cas9 administration in both neonatal and adult mice. Other studies revealed negligible levels of Cas9 in plasma of neonatal mice receiving low-dose AAV-CRISPR and absence of antibodies in cerebrospinal fluid of dogs and rhesus neonates treated with AAV-IDUA.4,9 Cas nucleases, like Cas9, used in CRISPR-based gene editing are derived from bacteria; therefore, they are exogenous and potentially immunogenic in mammals. They are widespread, found in some gut microbiomes and some common pathogens, meaning prior exposure may lead to pre-existing anti-Cas antibodies and cellular responses that can help clear transduced cells, reducing therapeutic efficacy and raising safety concerns.8,10 This underscores the challenges associated with delivering CRISPR-Cas systems for gene edition. Although it is unclear whether pre-existing anti-Cas9 antibodies contributed to the Cas9 levels observed in the Duba-Kiss and Hampson3 study, their work showed that the neonatal immune system was able to learn to tolerate Cas9.

The versatility in terms of routes of administration, safety, and efficiency renders AAV the current vector of choice for gene therapy applications, including the treatment of CNS diseases. Reaching the CNS is challenging due to the presence of the restrictive blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the complexity of the neural networks. Although some AAVs can cross the BBB, systemic administration of high doses is usually required; therefore, direct inoculation in the brain or CNS may be better suited in some cases.

While versatile and efficient, AAV has a limited capacity to receive large transgenes (about 4.7 kb).8 Incorporating CRISPR-Cas components (guide RNA, Cas9 protein, and their regulatory elements) into AAV is therefore difficult and may require the use of multiple expression cassettes. Some Cas9 proteins, like the one employed by Duba-Kiss and Hampson,3 are originally from Staphylococcus aureus and shorter (about 3.2 kb) than others like the one from Streptococcus pyogenes (which is about 4.1 kb).8 In any case, the administration of several AAV vectors combined can be considered.

Treating neonates with a genome-editing therapy using transient expression is desirable and can mitigate potential immune responses. Transient expression can be achieved by using non-viral delivery technologies, such as liposomes, and nanoparticles, but delivery may be limited.8 An approach to control AAV’s continuous expression is the use of inducible promoters, which represent a promising strategy to produce the protein only when desired.

AAV-mediated gene therapy shows great promise for treating CNS diseases (and other acquired or genetic disorders), but immune responses, especially against exogenous CRISPR-Cas components like Cas9, pose important challenges, particularly in adults. Early-life or neonatal administration can help overcome such challenges and achieve durable expression with minimal immunogenicity, paving the way for safer and more effective gene-editing treatments.
