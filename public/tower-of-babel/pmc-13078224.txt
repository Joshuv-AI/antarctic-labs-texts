The case for an Epstein–Barr virus vaccine: Lessons from its link to systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Adewunmi Akingbola, Courage Idahor, Abiodun Adegbesan, Tosin Fakiyesi, Opeyemi Aikulola, Samuel Tundealao, Petra Mariaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGrowing evidence has strengthened the longstanding hypothesis that Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) contributes directly to the development of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). While earlier work established epidemiologic associations and immune dysregulation in EBV-exposed individuals, recent multi-omic and single-cell studies now demonstrate mechanistic pathways that link viral infection to autoreactive B-cell activation. Notably, EBV has been shown to preferentially infect CD27+ CD21− memory B cells enriched for nuclear antigen-specific receptors, reprogramming them into potent antigen-presenting cells that drive T-cell activation and autoantibody formation. These findings align with genetic studies implicating EBNA2 binding at autoimmune risk loci and reinforce a credible causal model connecting EBV latency, lytic reactivation, and lupus pathogenesis. Together, these advances renew the urgency of developing a prophylactic EBV vaccine capable of blocking early infection, reducing viral latency, and limiting downstream autoimmune activation. Although no licensed vaccine currently exists, progress in multimeric antigen design, nanoparticle platforms, and adjuvants such as Matrix-M has created promising avenues for future clinical development. An effective EBV vaccine could not only reduce infectious mononucleosis and EBV-associated malignancies but also meaningfully lower the global burden of SLE. This review synthesizes emerging evidence supporting EBV vaccination as a preventive strategy for autoimmune disease.KEYWORDS: Epstein–Barr virus, systemic lupus erythematosus, autoreactive B cells, vaccine development, viral latency, autoimmune disease prevention

A long-suspected link gains molecular credibility

Epstein–Barr virus has long been suspected of contributing to the development of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), yet the mechanisms linking a ubiquitous viral infection to a relatively uncommon autoimmune disease have remained difficult to define. Recent advances in immunology, multi-omic profiling, and gene regulatory mapping have transformed this discussion. These developments culminate in the Science Translational Medicine study by Younis and colleagues, which provides the clearest mechanistic evidence to date that EBV directly reshapes autoreactive B cell behavior in ways that support lupus pathogenesis.1 This evidence shifts the field from an association model toward a credible causal framework.

Work over the past decade has strengthened the notion that individuals who eventually develop lupus exhibit immune irregularities long before clinical symptoms appear. Studies of antinuclear antibody-positive but otherwise healthy individuals have shown that early transcriptional and immunophenotypic differences can predict progression risk, suggesting that lupus evolves from a distinct preclinical state shaped by dysregulated B-cell and interferon pathways.2 In parallel, modern clinical analyses continue to show that patients with established SLE demonstrate heightened EBV-specific immune activity and intermittent viral reactivation, providing epidemiological and immunological context for a mechanistic relationship.3

Important advances also emerged from the application of genome-wide regulatory science. Harley and colleagues first showed that EBNA2, a key EBV transcription factor, binds to human regulatory DNA regions that overlap with autoimmune risk loci, including multiple SLE susceptibility regions.4 These findings suggested that EBV might act directly on the molecular architecture that governs immune cell activation and tolerance. Additional work by Jenks et al. demonstrated that dysregulated TLR7 signaling generates expanded populations of extrafollicular B cells, including the DN2 subset, which are highly prone to autoreactivity and central to the formation of lupus autoantibodies.5 Together, these studies established a strong theoretical foundation but lacked cell-specific, infection-specific confirmation.

The recent work by Younis et al. provides this missing link. Using single-cell multiomics targeted to EBV-infected cells, the authors demonstrated that EBV preferentially infects CD27+21− low memory B cells, a compartment enriched for autoreactive and nuclear antigen-specific receptors in lupus.1 Once infected, these cells undergo extensive transcriptional reprogramming. They upregulate antigen processing and presentation pathways and acquire the features of activated antigen-presenting cells, including increased expression of CD70, ZEB2, and key costimulatory molecules.1 EBNA2 binding to enhancers and promoters controlling these pathways appears central to this transformation and is consistent with earlier findings on gene regulation.1,4

These infected cells also demonstrate autoantigen cross-reactivity, classical ANA staining patterns, and the capacity to activate peripheral T helper cells, all of which support the formation and persistence of autoreactive B cell responses.1 Contemporary analyses of B cell biology in lupus have emphasized that autoreactive B cells are not passive participants but active drivers of disease progression through antigen presentation, cytokine production, and sustained interaction with T cell partners.6 In this context, the ability of EBV to convert autoreactive memory B cells into potent antigen-presenting cells provides a coherent explanation for its long-debated role in SLE. These findings offer a compelling mechanistic rationale for the development of an EBV vaccine, particularly for individuals at elevated risk of autoimmune disease.

Why an EBV vaccine is suddenly more relevant than ever

The prevention of EBV infection with early viral replication suppression would lead to a significant decrease in viral latency establishment, as EBV primarily exists in a latent form within infected lymphoid tissues.7 The reduction of early viral activity results in lower B-cell entry into the latent reservoir, which decreases the ongoing immune response that triggers autoimmune diseases, including lupus. Evidence suggests that EBV infection contributes to the development and onset of lupus symptoms, and therefore, controlling viral persistence may prevent autoimmune diseases from occurring.8 The approach to blunt early EBV replication is key to reducing the production of immunogenic viral antigens, such as EBNA1, which is linked to molecular mimicry and contributes to the development of cross-reactive autoantibodies in patients with SLE.3 By preventing or reducing early replication, fewer reactivation events occur, leading to lower lytic-phase antigen production and reduced autoimmune responses triggered by immune system activation.9 NK cells, which initiate immune responses by targeting infected cells early through lytic mechanisms, are essential in the defense against viral infections. However, this early immune response can disrupt immune tolerance and promote autoimmune reactions.10 Additionally, host B cells help maintain EBV persistence by utilizing epigenetic and regulatory mechanisms to keep the virus in a latent state. However, the first replication cycle weakens these latency-stabilizing pathways, which may lead to viral reactivation and the onset of autoimmune disease.11 The development of EBV prophylactic vaccines is crucial, as they can prevent viral entry and control initial viral replication, thus reducing both permanent viral latency and the development of autoimmune diseases like SLE.12

In this context, EBV vaccination is a critical public health intervention as it protects against acute infections, can play a role in preventing autoimmune diseases and EBV-related cancers, and can help reduce economic affectation. For example, EBV infection commonly leads to infectious mononucleosis, which causes extended periods of fatigue, resulting in students missing school and workers losing valuable time at work, all of which contribute to economic disruption.12 An effective EBV vaccine would help alleviate these issues by reducing the burden of infectious mononucleosis and preventing long-term complications, such as autoimmune diseases and cancer, that are linked to chronic EBV infection.12 By addressing these health risks, EBV vaccination is essential in minimizing the broader social and economic impacts of the virus.

EBV infection causes mononucleosis as its main symptom, but there is a strong link between EBV and multiple sclerosis (MS), with seroconversion occurring before almost all cases, identifying the virus as a key causative agent.13 The prevention of first EBV infections through vaccination would result in a significant reduction of multiple sclerosis cases because it has limited possibilities to halt neurological disorder disability progression.14 The EBV virus causes different types of cancer, which include Hodgkin lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, EBV-positive gastric carcinoma, and occasional cases of colorectal cancer.15 Vaccination efforts to reduce viral persistence will lower cancer development rates, resulting in similar long-term health benefits as HPV vaccination.14 Evidence shows that there is a link between EBV and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with high viral levels, compromised latency regulation, and immune system reactions that produce autoantibodies that drive disease development.16 It is worth noting that standard vaccination protocols do not increase the risk of developing SLE.17

Review of current evidence on EBV vaccine

For over 40 y, prophylactic EBV vaccines have been under intensive development to prevent primary EBV infection. Since the 1970s, numerous EBV vaccine studies have been conducted using subunit vaccines, epitope vaccines, DNA vaccines, nanoparticle-based vaccines, viral vector vaccines, virus-like particles (VLPs), and dendritic cell (DC) vaccines.14 So far, although many vaccines have completed clinical trials, none of them has been able to effectively prevent EBV infection in humans.18 This is because EBV itself contains multiple antigens that are carcinogenic, and it is difficult to amplify EBV through in vitro cultivation; hence, it is not possible to obtain inactivated or attenuated vaccines against EBV.14 An ideal prophylactic vaccine against EBV should be able to elicit potent neutralizing antibodies against EBV infection in both B lymphocytes and epithelial cells, which requires careful selection of antigens during vaccine design.19 Currently, there is no licensed vaccine for EBV, according to the National Institutes of Health.20 Nonetheless, it is important to mention that post-vaccine seroconversion has been documented in literature, but the vaccine’s ability to prevent EBV infection, particularly asymptomatic cases, remains limited; thus, integrating various antigens would increase the effectiveness of the vaccine.21

It’s essential to select the appropriate adjuvant to ensure an effective and long-lasting immune response. These adjuvants include alum, Syntex adjuvant formulation [SAF-1], immune-stimulating complexes, Freund’s adjuvant, and glucopyranosyl lipid A.14 Now, a new unique adjuvant called Matrix-M is made up of two saponin-based components.22 Matrix-M has demonstrated safety and high efficacy in a recent clinical trial.22 A multimeric gp350 EBV vaccine trial in animals yielded a more robust immune response than a monomeric gp350 vaccine and mice that were immunized with the tetrameric gp350 vaccine resulted in a more immune response than mice that received a monomeric gp350 vaccine.14 Another novel approach is using an adenovirus or vaccinia virus as a viral vector to deliver the code for gp350. This vaccinia viral vector was used in numerous animals to immunize against gp350, eliciting a humoral response in marmosets, tamarins, and rabbits.14

A future vaccine will most likely have to include more epitopes than just gp350. The vaccine may also have to include gH/gL, gB, and gp42 glycoproteins. Anti-gHgL antibodies seem to be 18% more effective than antibodies against gp350 in preventing EBV from infecting B cells.14 A recent vaccine trial launched by the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases has begun exploring a preventative vaccine for EBV. This vaccine specifically targets the gp350 protein present on both the surface of the EBV virus and on the surface of cells infected by the virus.23 While gp350-based vaccine candidates have shown promising results in reducing the severity of symptomatic disease, they have not yet fully prevented EBV infection21. However, recent advancements in vaccine design have introduced modern multivalent and nanoparticle-based approaches, which represent a conceptual leap over earlier subunit vaccine designs. Multivalent vaccines incorporate multiple viral antigens, enhancing the breadth of the immune response and improving the chances of providing more robust protection. Nanoparticle-based vaccines, which utilize virus-like particles (VLPs) or other nanoparticle systems, offer a more efficient method of antigen delivery and presentation, potentially increasing the immunogenicity and durability of the immune response19. These new strategies are designed to target various stages of the EBV lifecycle, providing a broader defense mechanism against both symptomatic and asymptomatic infection. Further research and work need to be done to fully understand how to make a proper EBV vaccine and curb the mortality and morbidity associated with it.

Limitations

While the review provides evidence for the role of EBV in the pathogenesis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), several important gaps remain that warrant consideration. Firstly, it remains unclear why only a minority of individuals infected with EBV ultimately develop SLE, despite the ubiquitous nature of the virus. This variability in disease progression highlights the need for further exploration of genetic and environmental factors that may contribute to susceptibility. Additionally, while the mechanistic plausibility of EBV’s role in lupus is strongly supported by the data, more work is needed to establish definitive causation. The review also does not fully address the complex interplay of host genetic susceptibility and environmental cofactors, which are likely essential in determining whether EBV infection triggers SLE in susceptible individuals. These factors represent critical areas for future research, which could enhance the understanding of the pathogenesis of SLE and improve the development of targeted prevention strategies.

Conclusion

Growing molecular and immunologic evidence now supports a credible causal link between EBV and systemic lupus erythematosus. Studies showing that EBV can reprogram autoreactive B cells provide a clear mechanism that aligns with long-standing epidemiologic observations. These insights strengthen the case for an EBV vaccine that could reduce both acute infection and downstream autoimmune disease. Although scientific challenges remain, advances in multi-antigen design, viral vectors, and modern adjuvants offer real promise. Together, current findings suggest that an effective EBV vaccine could meaningfully reduce the global burden of lupus and other EBV-associated conditions.
