Transperineal ultrasonography assisted examination improves maternal and neonatal outcomes of forceps delivery: a propensity-score-matched analysis
Authors: Shin Hashiramoto, Hiroko Takita, Tatsuya Arakaki, Yuka Yamashita, Mayumi Kaneko, Ryu Matsuoka, Akihiko Sekizawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransperineal ultrasonography (TUS) is increasingly recognized for evaluating labor progression, but its contribution to delivery outcomes, particularly in forceps delivery, remains uncertain. This prospective cohort study investigated whether TUS-assisted examination improves maternal and neonatal outcomes in forceps deliveries. We included term singleton pregnancies undergoing trial of forceps at a tertiary perinatal center in Japan between 2019 and 2022. Participants were divided into two groups based on whether TUS was performed before delivery. The maternal composite outcome comprised cesarean section after failed forceps, ≥ 3 tractions required, third- or fourth-degree perineal tear, or postpartum hemorrhage > 1500 mL. The neonatal composite outcome included umbilical artery pH < 7.0, base excess < − 12, 5-minute Apgar score < 7, trauma, or NICU admission. Propensity-score matching (1:1) was applied to balance covariates. Among 403 eligible patients, 216 matched cases were analyzed. Both maternal (relative risk [RR] 0.53, 95% CI 0.29–0.97; number needed to treat [NNT] 8.9; p = 0.03) and neonatal (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.22–0.99; NNT 10.7; p = 0.04) composite outcomes were significantly lower in the TUS group, with fewer cases requiring ≥ 3 tractions (p = 0.01). Introduction

The global decline in vaginal instrumental delivery rates can be partially attributed to the perceived safety of cesarean deliveries, leading practitioners to favor cesarean delivery over potentially complex instrumental procedures1,2. However, instrumental delivery offers significant advantages, including reduced delivery time, maternal infection, and neonatal respiratory morbidity compared to cesarean deliveries in the second stage of labor3–6, underscoring the importance of maintaining these skills in obstetric practice.

While both vacuum and forceps are viable options for instrumental delivery, they present distinct prerequisites, technical requirements, and complication profiles. Although forceps delivery has been associated with increased rates of obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI)7, it demonstrates superior outcomes in terms of successful vaginal delivery rates8,9, reduced central nervous system injuries10, and requires less time to complete the delivery compared to vacuum delivery11,12. The success and safety of either technique largely depend on appropriate judgement of indication and technical execution, highlighting the need for precise labor progression assessment.

Sonographic measurements have been increasingly investigated across obstetrics and gynecology, including uterocervical angle assessment in mid-trimester pregnancy13, prediction of labor induction outcomes14, and prediction of pain during diagnostic hysteroscopy15. Transperineal ultrasonography (TUS) has emerged as a promising tool for evaluating the progression and fetal rotation during labor16–18. Several studies described the role of TUS before instrumental delivery19,20. Hinkson et al.21 demonstrated the efficacy of ultrasound guidance during Kielland’s rotational forceps, and had no cases miss diagnosing the fetal head rotation. A recent meta-analysis including seven studies22, showed that the angle of progression (AoP) at second stage of labor during maternal pushing had a sensitivity of 91% (95% CI 85–94%) and specificity of 83% (95% CI 69–92%) for uncomplicated operative vaginal delivery. However, the low event rate of unfavorable outcomes caused by the instrumental delivery have prevented many previous researches from yielding positive results23–25.

Most studies investigating the efficacy of TUS in instrumental deliveries, evaluate vacuum and forceps collectively, despite their distinct risk-benefit profiles. Although forceps delivery is associated with a relatively higher complication rate, it also offers certain advantages that can outweigh the risks. Therefore, establishing methods to improve its safety is truly worthwhile, and TUS has the potential to serve as a valuable tool in this regard. This study specifically investigates the impact of TUS guidance on maternal and neonatal outcomes in forceps delivery, addressing a critical gap in current literature where the efficacy of TUS in forceps delivery remains inadequately explored.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This was a prospective cohort study conducted at a single tertiary perinatal center in Japan. The study period was January 2019 to December 2022. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee in our facility (approval number M3389). Eligible for inclusion were those undergoing a trial of forceps with a live singleton fetus in cephalic presentation and gestational age ≥ 37 weeks, irrespective of the fetal head rotation. We excluded patients with multiple pregnancy, preterm delivery, and fetal congenital diseases. We divided the patients into two groups, TUS group and no-TUS group, based on whether TUS was performed before the trial of forceps or not.

Outcome measurement

We set two primary outcomes for this study, maternal and neonatal composite outcome. The maternal composite outcome comprised the followings: cesarean delivery after failed attempt of forceps, three or more tractions required to complete the delivery12, third or fourth-degree perineal tear according to Sultan’s classification, and severe postpartum hemorrhage (blood loss > 1500 ml)26. Neonatal composite outcome consists of the followings: umbilical artery (UA) pH < 7.0, base excess (BE) < -12, 5-minute Apgar score < 7, significant neonatal trauma caused by the forceps delivery, and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission. Significant neonatal trauma included intracranial hemorrhage, skull fracture, severe injury to the central or peripheral nervous system, ocular injury (e.g. retinal hemorrhage, cataract), fracture of the long bones, injury to the liver or spleen, seizures, and neonatal death. The outcomes were determined prior to the study. Cases in which multiple individual outcomes occurred simultaneously were counted separately for each outcome, but counted as a single case in the composite outcome.

The individual components of the composite outcomes were also assessed as secondary outcomes.

Data collection

Numerous clinical data were recorded, including maternal age, parity, gestational age at delivery, height, weight, body mass index, maternal weight gain during pregnancy, use of labor analgesia (mostly combined spinal-epidural anesthesia), duration of first and second stages of labor, indication for instrumental delivery, number of tractions, mode of delivery, maternal vaginal or anal sphincter tear, postpartum hemorrhage, birthweight, Apgar scores, umbilical artery blood pH at delivery, birth trauma, and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit due to labor related indication.

Intrapartum ultrasound protocol before trial of forceps

When the patients were admitted for delivery, they were assigned to a labor-delivery-recovery (LDR) room in sequential order. During the study period, two of the five LDR rooms were equipped with ultrasound devices at all times, so that the accessibility to ultrasound machines was systematic. Intrapartum ultrasound was performed by an obstetrician using a Voluson P8 ultrasound machine (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) with 4 C-RS convex probe. Angle of progression (AoP)27, head direction (HD)28, and midline angle (MLA)29 images were obtained by TUS, before instrumental delivery. Ultrasound measurements were obtained following the previously published methods27–29. All of the parameters were measured both at rest and during pushing.

In every forceps delivery, a senior obstetrician, who is a board-certified member of Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology and underwent simulation training of TUS at least twice per year, performed or supervised the TUS. The senior obstetrician approved the TUS images at the time when the images were taken. The decision to attempt forceps delivery was made based on digital examination and standard clinical criteria before TUS. TUS was then used to guide the application strategy, including confirmation of fetal head position and rotation and optimization of blade application and traction direction. The decision of forceps indication was not changed after the TUS. In the event of a significant discrepancy between the digital examination and ultrasound findings, a senior obstetrician reevaluated the digital examination findings to determine how the forceps should be applied. Thus, TUS was used to assist the technical aspects of forceps and to gain a better understanding of the fetal position. There were no certain sonographic thresholds to perform the forceps.

Protocol of forceps delivery

At our institution, both forceps delivery and vacuum extraction are available for operative vaginal delivery. The choice of instrument is determined according to fetal head station, position, urgency of delivery, and operator judgment. Forceps delivery is generally selected when rapid and reliable delivery is required and the prerequisites for forceps delivery are fulfilled. The prerequisites for forceps delivery were as follows; cervix fully dilated and retracted, membranes ruptured, digital station > + 2, pelvis thought to be adequate for vaginal birth, maternal bladder emptied. Forceps delivery was performed by obstetricians who have more than 4 years of experience in instrumental delivery. We aimed to complete the delivery within five minutes from the first traction. Naegele forceps was the basic option of forceps. Kielland forceps was used as a second option by a skilled operator in existence of malrotation even after attempting manual rotation. Mediolateral perineal incision was routinely performed for every case in forceps delivery.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was based on the forceps-related complication rates observed at our institution during the year preceding study initiation. The baseline rates for maternal and neonatal composite outcomes were 24.2% and 25.4%, respectively. In a large proportion of the cases with complications, the underlying cause were due to insufficient evaluation of the fetal head station and the inaccurate estimation of the difficulty of the forceps. Therefore, we hypothesized that TUS assistance would reduce these incidences by at least 50%. Using a power of 80% and an alpha level of 0.05, we calculated that 97 patients per group would be required. To account for potential participant reduction due to propensity-score matching, which could not be predetermined, we estimated a 50% decrease in eligible participants. Therefore, we aimed to include 400 patients in total.

We used propensity-score matching to minimize potential confounding effects of covariates. We estimated the propensity scores by a multivariable logistic regression model with the following covariates; maternal age, BMI, maternal weight gain during pregnancy, parity, gestational age, labor analgesia, obstetricians’ experience, and neonatal birthweight. Selection of variables included in the multivariable models was performed a priori on the basis of clinical relevance. Fetal head rotation was not included in the primary propensity-score model because we rarely applied the forceps in malrotation cases, and inclusion of this sparse variable could have destabilized the matching model. Moreover, fetal head rotation may be subject to differential measurement accuracy between the TUS and no-TUS groups, because ultrasound provides more objective assessment than digital examination alone. Therefore, we addressed the potential confounding effect of fetal head rotation by conducting a prespecified sensitivity analysis restricted to cases with occiput anterior position. We matched the patients in the group with and without TUS at a 1:1 ratio based on the propensity-scores using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a caliper of 0.20. Covariate balance between the groups was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values below 0.1 indicating good balance.

Additionally, considering the differences in complication rates according to fetal head rotation, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to cases with occiput anterior position.

Group characteristics were examined by means of descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher’s exact test and chi-square test. Continuous variables using independent t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test, according to the normality of the variables. Two-tailed P value below 0.05 was considered significant. We also conducted the Benjamini–Hochberg to exploratory analyses of individual outcome components. An adjusted p-value (q-value) < 0.10 was considered statistically significant for exploratory analyses of individual outcome components. This threshold was selected to balance the risks of false-positive and false-negative findings given the limited number of events for each adverse outcome. The statistical analyses were performed using JMP data analysis software (JMP®ฎ Pro, Version 17.0.0, SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

During the study period, out of the 5,110 total deliveries, 428 patients underwent forceps delivery. After excluding preterm delivery, multiple pregnancy, and congenital fetal disease cases, 403 cases remained. TUS before forceps was performed in 207 of them, while 196 were not. After applying the 1:1 ratio propensity-score matching, 108 patients remained in both groups (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are that both maternal and neonatal outcomes improved with the use of TUS before forceps, contrary to the limited efficacy reported in previous studies23–25. Although most of the components of the composite outcomes did not have a statistically significant difference between the two groups, all of the components had a lower complication rate in the TUS group. Among these, we observed a significant reduction in cases requiring three or more tractions with TUS utilization which contributed to the significant findings of the main outcome.

This study focuses specifically on forceps delivery within instrumental deliveries, offering more refined data compared to studies combining forceps and vacuum extraction. Although forceps are becoming an obsolete technique, it still has its roles in modern obstetrics30. Addressing safety concerns remains crucial to preserve the benefits of forceps delivery, and our findings suggest TUS as a valuable tool for enhancing safety.

Regarding maternal complications, we observed a significant reduction in cases requiring three or more tractions with TUS utilization. While there is no consensus on the optimal number of forceps tractions, the necessity for two tractions may be justified by insufficient maternal pushing or traction direction adjustment. However, cases requiring three or more tractions likely indicate either inappropriate forceps indication or inadequate technique. Multiple tractions, particularly three or more, correlate with increased time to complete the delivery, neonatal traumas, and severe perineal lacerations31,32. TUS assistance enables precise understanding of fetal position and rotation, facilitating more accurate assessment of the fetal passage route beyond what digital examination alone can provide33–35. Henrich et al.28 compared the computed tomographic and the intrapartum ultrasound images to estimate the location of the ischial spine in order to assess an accurate position of the fetal head which was a factor of successful vacuum extraction. Although TUS requires brief additional time, even in cases of fetal bradycardia, we consider this investment warranted given the enhanced safety outcomes for both mother and fetus. In our study, the reduction of the number of forceps tractions accounted for a large proportion of the significant reduction of maternal composite outcome, which emphasizes the importance of evaluating the indication of forceps beforehand.

The neonatal composite outcome also showed significant improvement with the use of TUS. Given that indications for forceps delivery were comparable between groups with matched background characteristics using propensity-score matching, these improved outcomes likely reflect enhanced evaluation before forceps execution. More accurate assessment of fetal head position and rotation may facilitate appropriate blade placement and traction direction, thereby reducing fetal trauma. In addition, fewer tractions and potentially shorter forceps procedures may contribute to a lower risk of neonatal metabolic compromise and low Apgar scores. For example, one failed forceps case in the no-TUS group was accompanied by severe metabolic acidosis, with a base excess of − 16.7, and retinal hemorrhage (case 1, Supplementary Table S1). Although such individual cases cannot establish causality, they highlight how inaccurate assessment of fetal head position or procedural difficulty may contribute to failed or traumatic forceps attempts. Obtaining the MLA with TUS has been shown to be a simple method for reducing the misdiagnosis of fetal head rotation19,23. In cases with prominent caput succedaneum, accurate assessment by digital examination alone can be challenging23,36. Moreover, such cases may often be more suitable for forceps than vacuum extraction37, underscoring the clinical relevance of TUS evaluation. In the present study, TUS may have reduced complications by reducing the ambiguity of digital examination and allowing correction of the application strategy when discrepancies were identified (Supplementary Table S3).

Interestingly, in this study, no maternal or neonatal complications occurred in cases with fetal malrotation. Misidentification of the sagittal suture can be critical when applying rotational forceps, as it may lead to severe complications such as ocular injury in the neonate. Measuring the MLA enables objective recognition of inaccurate fetal head rotation that cannot be reliably assessed by digital examination alone23. Theoretically, this may be advantageous for the application of rotational forceps. However, given the limited number of malrotation cases, the fact that fetal head rotation was not included in the propensity-score matching model, and the absence of complications in this subgroup, this study was unable to determine the specific utility of TUS in malrotation cases. Accordingly, the applicability of our findings to forceps deliveries involving fetal malrotation should be interpreted with caution.

Labor analgesia is currently expanding rapidly worldwide, especially in Japan. With its increased adoption, an increase in instrumental delivery is evident38. We demonstrated that TUS assistance increases the safety of both mother and the neonate by providing objective and precise information of the fetal position. Thus, we expect TUS to become a mandatory technique to ensure the safe performance of instrumental procedures, particularly forceps deliveries. However, further study is necessary to confirm this.

This study investigated whether intrapartum ultrasound, when used as an adjunct to digital examination, could positively influence delivery outcomes. The utility and threshold values of individual parameters, however, were not addressed. Future research should focus on identifying optimal ultrasound parameters for each complication associated with forceps delivery to develop a prediction model. Greater emphasis to safety is necessary to maximize the benefits of forceps delivery.

Our study’s primary strength lies in its large sample size, which specifically focused on forceps delivery. This was enough to achieve adequate statistical power even after propensity-score matching procedure. In investigations involving medical interventions, it is sometimes ethically challenging to design randomized studies. Therefore, observational studies conducted under adherence to a standardized protocol are considered an ethically appropriate approach. A propensity-score-matched analysis is a suitable method for situations where the proportion of intervention cases differs between comparative groups. It ensures the validity of findings in observational studies involving clinical interventions. While the use of propensity-score matching reduces the number of eligible cases, the relatively short observational period required to accumulate a sufficient number of cases contributed to strict protocol adherence.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, a trial design that is not blinded nor randomized cannot completely eliminate selection and operator bias. To minimize this, we performed a propensity-score matching to adjust the background characteristics. Additionally, we implemented a sequential admission process for patients into the LDR rooms and avoided discretionary choices in performing ultrasound examinations. Although, we could not account for cases where cesarean deliveries were performed without attempted forceps delivery.

Second, as a single-center study in Japan with predominantly Japanese participants, generalizability requires careful consideration. While Asian ethnicity is considered a distinctive risk factor for OASI7, our observed OASI rates were comparable to19 or lower than24,28 previously reported studies. Our relatively low complication rate of forceps implies that our forceps technique meets the international standards, and our results are not overstated. Nevertheless, external validity should be carefully evaluated when applying these findings to different clinical settings.

Another important limitation is that mediolateral episiotomy was routinely performed in all forceps deliveries at our institution. Because episiotomy policy can influence the risk of OASI and other perineal outcomes, the observed maternal outcomes may not be directly generalizable to centers with restrictive episiotomy policies. In addition, our findings were obtained in a single tertiary center where forceps deliveries were performed or supervised by experienced obstetricians under a standardized protocol. Therefore, external validity should be assessed carefully in settings with different patient demographics, operator experience, episiotomy policies, or preferences for vacuum extraction versus forceps delivery.

Finally, we could not balance all of the variables by propensity score matching procedure, and some variables remained with a minor imbalance. However, the SMD was less than 0.2, and we believe it did not substantially affect the conclusions of this study.

For the conclusion, TUS-assisted examination before forceps delivery was associated with lower rates of maternal and neonatal composite outcomes. This association appeared to be driven mainly by a reduction in difficult forceps procedures requiring three or more tractions, rather than by statistically significant reductions in individual severe maternal morbidities. Further studies are warranted to determine whether TUS directly reduces major maternal and neonatal morbidity.
