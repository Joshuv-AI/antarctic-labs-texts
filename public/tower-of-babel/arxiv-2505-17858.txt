Identifying cobordisms using kernel persistence
Y. B. Bleile, L. Fajstrup, T. Heiss, A. M. Svane, S. S. Sørensen

Abstract
Motivated by applications in chemistry, we give a homlogical definition of tunnels, or more generally cobordisms, connecting disjoint parts of a cell complex. For a filtered complex, this defines a persistence module. We give a method for identifying birth and death times using kernel persistence and a matrix reduction algorithm for pairing birth and death times.



1
Introduction

Matter is built up by connected atoms. Depending on elemental constituents, temperature, pressure, kinetics, and thermodynamics, the atoms form chemical structures
[
17
]
. At the nanoscale, this presents itself as bonds between atoms, yet these connections also form larger structures which for many inorganic solids are represented as networks of atoms on the scale of nano- to micrometres. It is well-known that the packing of the atoms into dense or void-filled structures directly affect physical and chemical properties of materials
[
6
]
. This is particularly relevant in applications where atoms need to move, e.g., solid state battery materials where lithium ions travel through “tunnels” in a fixed network or gas adsorption where e.g. CO
2
needs to be adsorbed to a surface within a porous material
[
1
,
10
,
16
]
. However, identifying and characterizing packing of atoms beyond the very nearest neighbours, for example tunnels in the void space where atoms can move through, is inherently difficult. This is particularly true in materials which are non-crystalline (i.e., they lack the atomic periodicity from crystals exploited e.g. in
[
13
]
) such as glasses. To overcome this, the development of new methods is needed. A previous approach applied a reeb graph reconstruction of the space between atoms
[
12
]
, which, however, was computationally expensive. Persistent homology, has the advantage of being computationally efficient and has previously proven useful for characterizing glass materials
[
8
,
15
]
. Thus, in this paper, we take a homological approach to modelling tunnels between atoms.

We model the atoms of a material as a point cloud in a 3D box and look for tunnels through the void space connecting the top and bottom of the box, see left part of Figure
1
for a 2D-visualization. More precisely, if we place balls of radius
r
around each atom, a tunnel of width
r
will be a connected component in the complement of the balls intersected with the box that intersects both top and bottom of the box, see right part of Figure
1
. Equivalently, such a tunnel can be represented by a 2-dimensional surface in the union of balls that surrounds the tunnel. We give a homological definition way of counting such tunnels. When the radius
r
of the balls increases, such
tunnels appear and disappear again — they are born and die again. The birth time of a tunnel is interpreted as the first radius where the tunnel becomes separated from an existing tunnel or from the unbounded component except for the opening at the top and the bottom. The death time is the first radius where there is no longer a passage through the tunnel,
corresponding to the “bottleneck thickness” of the tunnel, i.e. the largest radius of a ball that is able to roll through the tunnel.
We give an algorithm for computing pairs of birth and death times closely related to kernel persistence
[
3
]
. The algorithm will be set up more generally for detecting
(k+1)
-dimensional open cobordism-like structures connecting disjoint subcomplexes of a filtered cell complex.

The paper is structured as follows. We define a homological way of representing cobordisms inside a cell complex
in
Section

2
. Then, in
Section

3
, we consider the situation where the cell complex is filtered and define a persistence module that captures the births and deaths of these open cobordisms. The birth and death times can be identified using kernel persistence
[
3
]
. However, pairing the birth and death times requires a new algorithm, which is presented in
Section

4
.

A homological way of representing cobordisms

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a finite cell complex, by which we will mean that
\mathbb{X}
is a regular CW complex with finitely many cells.
Regularity means that not only the interior of each
k

k

[
7

Simplicial and cubical complexes
[
9

as a topological space and sometimes as a combinatorial object hoping this causes no confusion.

Let

\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
. Then,
\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}
is a non-empty subcomplex of

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
understood) induced by the inclusion maps

\displaystyle\iota^{\mathbb{A}}_{\ast}:{}

\displaystyle H_{*}\left(\mathbb{A}\right)\rightarrow H_{*}\left(\mathbb{X}\right),

\displaystyle\iota^{\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}:{}

\displaystyle H_{*}\left(\mathbb{B}\right)\rightarrow H_{*}\left(\mathbb{X}\right),

\displaystyle\iota^{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}:{}

\displaystyle H_{*}\left(\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}\right)=H_{*}\left(\mathbb{A}\right)\oplus H_{*}\left(\mathbb{B}\right)\rightarrow H_{*}\left(\mathbb{X}\right).

(1)

We can then define our main object of interest.

\Phi

2.2
More on the point cloud case

For our application in chemistry, we consider a point cloud
P\subseteq[0,1]^{3}
representing the position of atoms. The union of closed balls of radius
r
centered around the points in
P
is denoted
P_{r}
. Let
\mathbb{X}_{r}\subseteq P_{r}
be the associated alpha-complex at fixed filtration value
r
, see e.g.
[
4
]
. This is a simplicial complex which deformation retracts onto
P_{r}
. To model the top and bottom of
\mathbb{X}_{r}
, we let
A=[0,1]^{2}\times[1-\varepsilon,1]
and
B=[0,1]^{2}\times[0,\varepsilon]
be slices of
[0,1]^{3}
at the top and bottom, respectively.
The subcomplexes
\mathbb{A}_{r}
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}
are the simplicial complexes formed by all simplices of
\mathbb{X}_{r}
with vertices in
P\cap A
and
P\cap B
, respectively.
A representative of a non-trivial element in
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{1}\right)
is a 2-dimensional simplicial surface in
\mathbb{X}_{r}
whose boundary is the union of non-trivial 1-cycles in
\mathbb{A}_{r}
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}
. There must be a free passage from
A
to
B
through the surface, since otherwise, the element would be a sum of an element from
H_{2}(\mathbb{X}_{r},\mathbb{A}_{r})
and one from
H_{2}(\mathbb{X}_{r},\mathbb{B}_{r})
and thus trivial in
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{1}\right)
by (
4
). We think of this passage as a tunnel. A path through this passage can always be pushed to the complement of
P_{r}
, which means that it has distance at least
r
to all points in
P
. Equivalently, one can roll a ball of radius
r
along the path. Thus, we say that the tunnel has thickness at least
r
. By adding closed cycles and relative cycles with boundary in only one of
\mathbb{A}_{r}
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}
, we can obtain an equivalent representative for the class in
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{1}\right)
that bounds one or more tunnels connecting
\mathbb{A}_{r}
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}
. On the other hand, the boundary of such a tunnel will be represented by an element of
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{1}\right)
.

In order for a cobordism from
\mathbb{A}_{r}
to
\mathbb{B}_{r}
to be non-trivial in
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{1}\right)
, its boundaries must be non-trivial in
H_{*}(\mathbb{A}_{r})
and
H_{*}(\mathbb{B}_{r})
. Typically, this will happen when a tunnel exits at the top and botton of the box, but degenerate cases may occur, see Figure
4
. In fact, the definition of tunnels is quite sensitive to the choice of
\mathbb{A}_{r}
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}
. Choosing the thickness
\varepsilon
of the top and bottom slices is a non-trivial problem in practice. On the one hand,
\varepsilon
must be large enough to contain the boundary of the tunnel. On the other hand, a very large
\varepsilon
increases the risk of degenerate tunnels.

Since we are really trying to model the void space between atoms, a natural alternative approach would be to model the void space directly via the dual Voronoi complex. Indeed, recall the duality between the Voronoi and Delauney complex associated to a point cloud
P
, see e.g.
[
4
]
. Let
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
denote the cell complex consisting of all Voronoi cells whose dual Delauney cell is not in
\mathbb{X}_{r}
. Then the complement of
P_{r}
deformation retracts onto
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
, see Appendix
A
for a rigorous statement. Thus, it is natural to think of a tunnel as a path from top to bottom in
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
(with a suitable definition of top and bottom in
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
).
Such tunnels can be identified by computing
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{0}\right)
for
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
. However, since working with the alpha-complex is most common in topological data analysis, we use the alpha-complex formulation in this paper.

3
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi\right)
as persistence module

Assume that
\mathbb{X}
is a filtered cell complex with a filter map
f
that to each cell
\sigma
in
\mathbb{X}
assigns a real number
f(\sigma)
such that
\tau\subseteq\sigma
implies
f(\tau)\leq f(\sigma)
. We assume for simplicity that
f
is injective. The filter map defines a filtration of
\mathbb{X}
into cell complexes
\mathbb{X}_{r}=f^{-1}((-\infty,r])
with inclusion maps
h_{r,s}:\mathbb{X}_{r}\to\mathbb{X}_{s}
for
r<s
inducing maps on homology
(h_{r,s})_{*}:H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{r})\to H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{s})
. For ease of notation, we assume that the image of
f
is
\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Since the topology only changes when a new cell is added to
\mathbb{X}_{r}
, all relevant information is contained in the following persistence module

H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{0})\xrightarrow{(h_{0,1})_{*}}H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{1})\xrightarrow{(h_{1,2})_{*}}\dotsm\xrightarrow{(h_{N-1,N})_{*}}H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{N}).

By the injectivity assumption on
f
, the dimension can only jump up or down by 1 at each step. In this case, we say that a birth or death happens, respectively. The filtration value when a birth or death happens is referred to as a birth or death time. A death time
d
is paired with the birth time
b
of the earliest born class that dies at time
d
. Unpaired birth times correspond to infinite cycles, i.e. classes that remain alive at time
N
.

The filter map
f
induces a filter map on
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}
, which means that the inclusion maps induce maps of persistence modules

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{0})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{N})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{*}(\mathbb{A}_{0})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{*}(\mathbb{A}_{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{*}(\mathbb{A}_{N})}

etc. As noted in
[
3
]
, the kernels of the vertical maps again form a persistence module, which we refer to as
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota_{*}^{\mathbb{A}}\right)
, with filtration
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota_{*}^{\mathbb{A}}\right)_{i}=\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota_{*}^{\mathbb{A}_{i}}:H_{*}(\mathbb{A}_{i})\to H_{*}(\mathbb{X}_{i})\right)
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Similarly, the images and cokernels of the vertical maps again form persistence modules. Extending this reasoning, we see that also
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi\right)
becomes a persistence module with filtration
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi\right)_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. The birth and death times of
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi\right)
are identified in the next section.

Example 2
.

For the point cloud example in Section
2.2
, the Delauney complex is a filtered by alpha-filtration. The filtered complex at filtration value

becomes exactly
\mathbb{X}_{r}

.

A class in

\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}\right)

\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}}_{\ast}\right)
and
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}\right)
(Case F in
Table

1
). A class in
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi\right)
dies when a class is born in
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}}_{\ast}\right)
and
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}\right)
, and hence in
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}\right)
(Case C in
Table

1
), or when a class is born in precisely one of
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}}_{\ast}\right)
and
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}\right)
, but not in
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}_{\ast}\right)

For the proof, we recall from
[
3
]
how births and deaths happen in
\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}}_{k}\right)

[c]\in\operatorname{Ker}\left(\iota^{\mathbb{A}}_{k}\right)_{b}

k
-cycle in
\mathbb{A}_{b-1}
which becomes a boundary in
\mathbb{X}_{b}
when adding a (

)-cell

of filtration value

. That is, there is a
(k+1)
-dimensional chain
c_{1}
in
\mathbb{X}_{b-1}
with
\partial(c_{1}+\Delta)=c
.
Moreover, no
(k+1)
-chain
c_{2}
in
\mathbb{X}_{b-1}
with
\partial c_{2}=c
existed before adding
\Delta
(because then
[c]
would have been born earlier). Note that
\Delta
cannot lie in

4.3
Discussion of representatives

Finding representatives of cobordism equivalence classes is relevant in chemistry, where such representatives carry information about the atomic structure around tunnels.
Theorem
4.1
tells us, which cells give birth and death to a cobordism. For the point cloud in Section
2.2
, the birth cell is the one that first seperates the tunnel from an existing tunnel or from the unbounded component. The death cell is the one that closes the tunnel off at its most narrow passage. Its filtration value is the bottleneck width of the tunnel. From Algorithm
2
we further get the following representations of cobordisms.

Corollary 4.2
.

Algorithm
2
provides the following representatives of cobordisms:

1.

If a cobordism is killed by
\tau
, the sum of the two
\tau
-columns in
D^{\Phi}
provides a representative of the cobordism that is alive just before death.

2.

If a cobordism is killed by
\tau
, the second
\tau
-column in
R^{\Phi}
gives a representative of the cobordism when it was born.

3.

A representative for a cobordism that lives forever can be read off from the birth column in
V_{im}^{\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}}
.

Proof.

1. was observed in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
To realize 2., suppose we run the reduction algorithm. If
\tau\in\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}
corresponds to a double column, the second column stores a representative of the tunnel to begin with. If
\tau\notin\mathbb{A}\cup\mathbb{B}
corresponds to a double column, when the algorithm gets to the second column, it will start by adding the first
\tau
column since both have their lowest 1 in the
\tau
row. The second column now stores a representative of the tunnel that dies at time
\tau
. By adding columns to the left of
\tau
, we can get all equivalent representations of the tunnel. By pushing the lowest 1 as far up as possible, we get a representative that is born as early as possible. The lowest 1 after the reduction marks the birth cell and the whole column gives a representation of the tunnel at birth.
∎

We note that the representatives found by Algorithm
2
for the birth and death tunnel between
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
is by no means unique and there is no guarantee that the algorithm finds the most natural choice of representative. For instance, for the point cloud in Section
2.2
,
the representative needs not form the surface of the tunnel. There might be some cells inside the representative.

5
Discussion and future directions

In this paper, we have considered surfaces connecting two spaces. We believe that this could easily be extended to three or more spaces by an iterative procedure. We leave this for future investigation.

As mentioned in Section
2.2
, in the specific case of a point cloud
P
, it might make sense to model the Voronoi dual complex
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
and identify tunnels by computing
\operatorname{Cok}\left(\Phi_{0}\right)
. This would have the advantage that the representatives found by the algorithm are paths connecting top and bottom, which would be more easily interpretable. We have chosen to work with the alpha-complex in this paper since this is most common, however, the dual perspective seems promising.

The results in the point cloud case are quite sensitive to the thickness of top and bottom slices. If they are too thin, they are unlikely to contain the boundary of a tunnel, but if they are too thick,
degenerate cases from Figure
4
are more likely to occur. One way of solving this problem is to choose thick slices in top and bottom and remove 2-cells completely contained in top or bottom
because these 2-cells are not necessary for forming the desired cobordisms (see Figure
4
a), but they are necessary for forming the degenerate cases (see Figures
4
b, c and d).
Another possibility is to consider the Voronoi dual, where only a set of potential start and end points are needed as
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
.

Atomic point clouds are often simulated with periodic boundary conditions (i.e. on a 3-torus) to avoid boundary effects. Thus, it would make sense to identify the left and right side of the box and the front and back side (forming a 2-torus times an interval) before computing the alpha-complex. This would avoid problems with tunnels exiting the sides of the bounding box. However, new problems may occur due to the non-trivial topology of the torus. For instance, a tunnel through a solid box as in Figure
6
would not be counted since the top and bottom cycle would be trivial in
H_{*}(\mathbb{A})
and
H_{*}(\mathbb{B})
, respectively. See also
[
5
,
11
]
for more on the challenges with periodic boundary conditions.

-cell
\sigma
in
\mathbb{X}
corresponds to a dual
(d-k)
-cell
\sigma^{*}
in
\mathbb{V}
and vice versa. The complexes are dual in the sense that if
\tau\subseteq\sigma
in
\mathbb{X}
then
\sigma^{*}\subseteq\tau^{*}
in
\mathbb{V}
. Let
f
be the filter function on
\mathbb{X}
that defines the alpha-complexes
\mathbb{X}_{r}=f^{-1}(\infty,r]
for
r\geq 0
.
Define the filter function
f^{*}
on
\mathbb{V}
by
f^{*}(\sigma^{*})=-f(\sigma)
. Then
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{V}
are dual filtered complexes in the sense of
[
2
]
. In particular, the boundary matrix for
\mathbb{V}
is found by transposing the boundary matrix for
\mathbb{X}
and reversing the order of the rows and columns. Thus, also their persistent homology are closely related
[
2
]
.

For a fixed filtration value
r
, it is well-known that
P_{r}=\bigcup_{p\in P}\overline{B}_{r}(p)
, where
\overline{B}_{r}(p)
denotes the closed ball around
p
of radius
r
, deformation retracts onto
\mathbb{X}_{r}
. We claim that also the complement of
P_{r}
is homotopy equivalent to
\mathbb{V}_{-r}=(f^{*})^{-1}(-\infty,-r)
. To make this statement rigorous, we have to be a bit careful about what happens at infinity. The precise statement we show is the following.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
B
be a box in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
containing
P
such that the distance from
\partial B
to
P
is greater than
r
. Then,
B\setminus P_{r}

deformation retracts onto
\partial B\cup(\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cap B)
. Moreover,
S^{d}\setminus\mathbb{X}_{r}
deformation retracts onto
\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\infty
, where
{S}^{d}
is the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\{\infty\}
is the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{V}^{k}
denote the
k
-skeleton of
\mathbb{V}
. We construct the deformation retraction inductively by pushing
(B\cap\mathbb{V}^{k})\setminus P_{r}
onto
(\partial B\cup(B\cap(\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\mathbb{V}^{k-1})))\setminus P_{r}
.

We start with
k=d
. Each Voronoi
d
-cell
\sigma
contains exactly one point
p_{\sigma}
from
P
. Then
B\setminus P_{r}
is the union of the sets
(B\cap\sigma)\setminus\overline{B}_{r}(p_{\sigma})
for all Voronoi
d
-cells
\sigma
.
Since both
(B\cap\sigma)
and
\overline{B}_{r}(p_{\sigma})
are convex, we can push
(B\cap\sigma)\setminus\overline{B}_{r}(p_{\sigma})
radially along line segments from
p_{\sigma}
towards
\partial(B\cap\sigma)\setminus\overline{B}_{r}(p_{\sigma})
. Repeating this for each
\sigma
yields a deformation retraction onto
(\partial B\cup(B\cap\mathbb{V}^{d-1}))\setminus P_{r}
. Note that
\mathbb{V}^{d-1}
can also be written as
\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\mathbb{V}^{d-1}
because
\mathbb{V}_{-r}\subseteq\mathbb{V}^{d-1}
for
r\geq 0
as all Delaunay vertices have filter value
0
(and hence there are no
d
-dimensional Voronoi cells in
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
).

Now suppose we have deformed
(B\cap\mathbb{V})\setminus P_{r}
onto
(\partial B\cup(B\cap(\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\mathbb{V}^{k})))\setminus P_{r}
. Let
\sigma
be a
k
-cell of
\mathbb{V}
whose interior is intersected by
P_{r}
in at least one point
p_{\sigma}
. Any point in
P_{r}\cap\sigma
has equal distance less than or equal to
r
to the nearest
d-k+1
points. Let
p
be any of these points. Then,
P_{r}\cap\sigma=\overline{B}_{r}(p)\cap\sigma
, which is again convex containing
p_{\sigma}
. Again, we may push
\sigma\setminus P_{r}
radially from
p_{\sigma}
towards
\partial(B\cap\sigma)\setminus P_{r}
. A
k
-cell
\sigma
that is not intersected by
P_{r}
is member of
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
and hence left untouched. This completes the inductive step. When we have pushed
(B\cap\mathbb{V})\setminus P_{r}
all the way to
(\partial B\cup(B\cap(\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\mathbb{V}^{0})))\setminus P_{r}
, we are done, because
(\partial B\cup(B\cap(\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\mathbb{V}^{0})))\setminus P_{r}=\partial B\cup(B\cap\mathbb{V}_{-r})
. To see this, note that
(\mathbb{V}_{-r}\cup\mathbb{V}^{0})\setminus P_{r}=\mathbb{V}_{-r}
, because the Voronoi vertices that are not in
P_{r}
are in
\mathbb{V}_{-r}
.

The second statement is proved similarly by noting that pushing points radially out also works for the one-point compactification of the unbounded Voronoi cells.

∎
