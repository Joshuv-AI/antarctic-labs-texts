Sustainable high-performance injection geopolymers, role of GGBFS and water-to-binder ratio in strength, microstructure, and predictive modeling
Authors: Ufuk Tunç, Zulkuf Kaya, Ali İhsan Çelik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work designed and optimized geopolymer injection mixtures suitable for curing under ambient conditions using fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) as industrial waste-based binders. Water/binder (w/b) ratios were systematically tested in the range of 0.50–1.50, and 12 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) together with sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃) were used as activators, with the activator/binder and Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratios fixed at 0.4 and 2.5, respectively. Sustainable industrial by-products have been combined with a systematic mix design to provide environmental benefits under mechanical efficiency and practical curing conditions. The effects of GGBFS content and w/b ratio on setting time, flow time, and compressive strength have been evaluated to determine optimal mixes for practical injection applications. To achieve early-age strength without requiring high-temperature curing, GGBFS was selected. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was applied to validate the parameters. The Marsh flow cone test was conducted to evaluate the injectability of the mixtures, and flow times were recorded. Compressive strength tests demonstrated that mechanical performance was greatly enhanced by increasing the GGBFS content, especially at low w/b ratios. With a strength of roughly 67 MPa, the GP-0.5 mixture (w/b = 0.50, 50% GGBFS) produced the best results regarding binder interaction and dense matrix formation. On the other hand, greater porosity was found to cause a loss in strength at high w/b ratios (> 1.0). Microstructural experiments (FE-SEM and EDX) revealed that compact gel phases occurred, and Ca-Si-Al-rich phases improved structural strength. The formation of aluminosilicate networks and the chemical evolution of the binder matrix were supported by the distinct vibration bands observed in FTIR and the crystal/phase analyses determined by XRD.Keywords: Geopolymer, Fly ash, GGBFS, Mechanical properties, Microstructural analysis, Alkali activator, Grouting, ANOVASubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Geopolymer mixtures are attracting increasing interest in research in the field of civil engineering today. Due to their superior mechanical, chemical, and microstructural properties, geopolymers are considered an environmentally friendly and sustainable alternative to Ordinary Portland cement (OPC). In this context, it is believed that geopolymers could serve as a serious alternative to cement-based materials in terms of both strength and durability performance1–5. Concrete, one of the most widely used materials worldwide, is also increasing the demand for OPC, and cement production is expected to grow even further in the coming years. However, this process also contributes to global warming and environmental problems. In particular, the carbon dioxide emissions released during cement production contribute significantly to climate change, accounting for a substantial portion of greenhouse gases. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate waste and by-products without harming the environment and to minimize environmental impacts6–11.

In geotechnical engineering, an essential sub-discipline of the construction industry, mixtures containing OPC are generally preferred in soil improvement applications. Such additives increase the bearing capacity of soils and improve their settlement behavior, enabling the creation of a more stable structure. Traditionally, OPC-containing mixtures have delivered effective results in soil improvement projects and demonstrated successful performance from an engineering perspective12–16. However, it is also known that the production process of OPC has significant adverse environmental impacts. Similarly, utilizing waste materials in concrete applications has emerged as a method that enhances environmental sustainability while maintaining mechanical strength. Using industrial by-products instead of cement reduces carbon emissions and supports recycling17. In particular, high energy consumption and carbon dioxide emissions during production seriously burden the environment. This situation necessitates the development and use of sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative binders18–21.

Geopolymers have emerged as effective grouting materials for geotechnical applications due to their ability to improve soil properties and structural stability. Unlike traditional cement-based grouts, geopolymer grouts offer tailored rheological behavior, enhanced compressive strength, and reduced shrinkage. Studies have shown that properly activated geopolymers exhibit high strength, low permeability, and excellent chemical resistance, offering a sustainable alternative to conventional cementitious materials22–24. Incorporating industrial by-products, such as fly ash, slag, and red mud, optimizes grout performance by enhancing fluidity, reducing setting time, and improving mechanical strength, while contributing to sustainable waste utilization. These characteristics make geopolymer grouts essential for underground engineering, disaster prevention, and other soil stabilization applications25–27.

In this context, many studies evaluate geopolymers, among the alternative binders that can be used as an alternative to traditional cementitious additives, for soil improvement and stabilization purposes in geotechnical engineering. Geopolymers are an attractive research topic due to their environmental advantages and their improvements in soil properties.

Previous studies have demonstrated that geopolymer injection significantly enhances pile performance in sandy soils, thereby substantially improving soil-pile interaction and foundation stability28. Studies have shown that Na₂SiO₃-based injections can significantly increase bearing capacity and foundation stability by stabilizing highly compressible organic soils29. Industrial by-products, such as stone dust and bottom ash, have been shown to enhance soil strength in both injection and deep mixing applications, with bottom ash providing particularly high performance30. Cold-bonded mixtures with FA and silica fume have exhibited cement-like rheological behavior, indicating that geopolymer injections with alternative binders can be effective in various applications31. MK-based geopolymers face rheological and shrinkage limitations; however, incorporating fine sand can improve their strength and workability, thereby broadening their application potential32.

When examining studies on FA and GGBFS, it is observed that mechanically activated geopolymers with different GGBFS/FA ratios and NaOH concentrations exhibit improvements in fresh mix and rheological properties33. Another study investigated the fresh and post-setting properties of geopolymer injection mortars made with FA and GGBFS. Increasing the GGBFS ratio improved flowability and strength, while NaOH had a more pronounced effect on setting time and viscosity. The optimum values were 0.8 for the GGBFS/FA ratio and 2.0 M for the NaOH concentration34. High water content in geopolymer mortars can reduce viscosity; however, depending on the Na₂SiO₃ concentration and the type of precursor material (FA or GGBFS), it can negatively impact durability and microstructure35. Jet-mixed columns produced using alkali-activated fly ash can achieve high strength with a low stabilizer content, offering an environmentally friendly alternative to cement by significantly reducing CO₂ emissions36. These results demonstrate that highly reactive materials, particularly GGBFS, are superior for gepolymers cured at room temperature in terms of mechanical performance and early strength increase. However, they are also more environmentally friendly.

The addition of nano-silica (NS) has been shown to improve the rheological, fresh, and mechanical properties of geopolymer mortars, with an optimum water/binder ratio of 1.0026. In innovative jet grouting systems, modified cement slurries produced denser and more extensive soil regions, while groundwater adversely affected column geometry and strength in water-saturated sands37. High-polymer cement grout (HPCG) outperformed RMPG and metakaolin-based grouts38. Some studies have demonstrated that additives such as calcium aluminate cement (CAC) and silica fume (SF) play a critical role in the early-age reactions and mechanical performance of geopolymer binders39. In addition to classic pozzolanic additives, other materials have also been used in studies and have shown positive results; however, industrial wastes and pozzolanic additives are more economical, while alternative additives are generally more costly.

Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of optimization techniques in designing sustainable binding systems. Methods such as Taguchi-Grey Relational Analysis (GRA), Response Surface Methodology (RSM), and ANOVA statistically evaluate the effects of different parameters on the properties of fresh and hardened concrete, and determine the optimum mix proportions. ANOVA results indicate that the water/binder or water/solid ratio is the most influential parameter, followed by the contents of activator and additive. These approaches not only improve mechanical and durability properties but also contribute to reducing costs and environmental impacts40–42. Within the scope of this study, the ANOVA method was utilized to determine the optimal mixture ratios.

Research significance

This study aims to develop an environmentally friendly and sustainable geopolymer injection material as an alternative to traditional soil improvement mixtures used in geotechnical applications. Deep mixing is essential for improving poor soils with binding materials in situ. However, cement-based materials are typically used in this method, leading to high energy consumption and significant CO₂ emissions. In this context, the proposed geopolymer-based injection mixture represents an essential alternative due to its low carbon emissions and applicability in field conditions. The developed mixture is expected to promote the use of environmentally friendly and sustainable materials in deep mixing applications, offering a new approach to improving poor soils. Materials and methods

Materials properties

This study presents various mixture ratios developed for injection applications. In the preparation of the mixtures, the w/b varies between 0.50 and 1.50, and NaOH and Na₂SiO₃ are used as alkaline activators. Na₂SiO₃ is selected at 50 °Bé and 2 modulus values. This choice was made to optimize the strength and workability properties of geopolymer binders. Additionally, a 12 M concentration of NaOH solution was used because the literature indicates that 12 M NaOH provides high alkalinity as an alkaline activator, effectively dissolving aluminosilicates and supporting gel formation43–46. The Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio was fixed at 2.5, and the alkali activator/binder ratio was set at 0.445–50. The binding components used in the mixtures were selected as FA and GGBFS. The F-class FA used in the study was obtained from the Zonguldak Çatalağzı Thermal Power Plant. GGBFS was obtained from the İskenderun Iron and Steel facilities, which OYAK Çimento, located in İskenderun, owns. The chemical compositions of GGBFS and FA are shown in Table 1.

This study will use industrial wastes such as FA and GGBFS as binding materials. NaOH and Na₂SiO₃ will be preferred as alkali activators. The Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio will be determined at an optimum level of 2.5, based on the workability and final strength of the fresh mixture50. Additionally, the alkali activator/binder ratio will be set at 0.4, the optimum value recommended in many studies45,47–49. The main reason for using GGBFS is that the samples in this study will be cured at ambient temperature (ambient curing). As the literature states, geopolymer systems using only fly ash typically require heat curing to achieve high strength55,56. However, since high-temperature curing applications (e.g., 60–90 °C) are impractical in the field, mixtures suitable for ambient curing have been preferred. For this reason, GGBFS has been included in the mix due to its positive effects on early strength development and setting time in ambient curing50,57. In this way, the aim is to obtain an environmentally sustainable and applicable GP injection mixture.

The water/binder (w/b) ratio in GP injection mixtures is a critical parameter regarding fresh mixtures’ workability and mechanical performance. In the literature, the w/b ratio generally varies between 0.4 and 1.5, with the 0.5–1.0 range being preferred for injection applications50,58. In this study, the w/b ratio will be adjusted between 0.50 and 1.50 to form the basis for experimental studies.

The GGBFS and FA ratios used as binders are crucial for achieving optimal performance and strength development. It has been reported that the optimum range for GGBFS is 10–20% in terms of early age strength and setting time. It is known that GGBFS has a positive effect on strength and setting time, especially in systems cured at ambient temperature. Therefore, in this study, the GGBFS/binder ratio was selected as 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 50%; in addition to the commonly used 5–15% range in the literature, the 20% and 50% ratios were also chosen to evaluate performance across a broader range. The FA content ranges from 50% to 95%, while the GGBFS ratio ranges from 5% to 50%. These ranges were selected based on previous studies reporting their effects on early-age strength, setting time, and workability of geopolymer mixtures47,50, and were gradually adjusted to optimise the mixtures’ rheological properties and injection performance. Experimental program

An experimental method was employed to prepare, cure, and evaluate the mechanical performance of geopolymer mixtures composed of FA and GGBFS. The primary reason for using GGBFS is that the samples in this study will be cured at ambient temperature (ambient curing). As the literature notes, geopolymer systems using only fly ash typically require heat curing to achieve high strength55,56. However, since high-temperature curing applications (e.g., 60–90 °C) are impractical in the field, mixtures suitable for ambient curing have been preferred. For this reason, GGBFS has been included in the mix due to its positive effects on early strength development and setting time in ambient curing50,57. The experimental process was conducted in four main stages, material procurement, mixture preparation, moulding and curing methods, and strength testing. The experimental programme is shown in the flow chart in Fig. 2. The binding materials used in the experiments, namely fly ash and blast furnace slag, were procured in the first stage. The alkali activator solutions were prepared using 12 M NaOH and (Na₂SiO₃).

Flowchart of the experimental program.

In the second stage, all components were mixed with a fixed-speed mechanical mixer to obtain a homogeneous mixture. The alkali activators Na₂SiO₃ and NaOH were mixed in predetermined proportions and left to rest for 24 h to ensure sufficient homogenization without affecting the mixture’s performance and to complete the preliminary chemical reactions. Then, FA and GGBFS were dry-mixed for 3 min, with the activator solution gradually added to the mixer. The mixture was stirred at a low speed for approximately 3 min, allowed to stand for a short period, and then stirred at a constant speed for 3 min at high speed to obtain a homogeneous mixture. This procedure ensures that the initial reactions of geopolymerization occur effectively, aligning with the methods recommended in the literature59.

In the third stage, the fresh injection mixtures were cast into moulds and cured under controlled laboratory conditions at ambient temperature for 28 days. The Marsh cone flow test was conducted on the fresh mixtures to evaluate their flowability, and the corresponding flow times were recorded. Upon completion of the curing period, the hardened samples were demoulded and subjected to compressive strength testing to assess their mechanical performance. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant standards. For each mixture type, three specimens were tested, and the average values were reported for analysis.

Microstructural analysis techniques

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis

XRF analysis was performed to determine the chemical composition of the samples. This method was preferred for quickly and accurately determining the major oxide components and trace element contents of the samples. Before analysis, the samples were prepared according to the TS EN 196-2 and ASTM C114 standards60. In this context, the samples were dried in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h, finely ground to ensure homogeneity, and pressed for analysis. The major oxide components of the samples, silicon dioxide (SiO₂), aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), calcium oxide (CaO), iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃), magnesium oxide (MgO), potassium oxide (K₂O), and sodium oxide (Na₂O), were determined by the XRF method. The results were reported as weight% (wt%) of oxides. The calibration protocols and standard measurement principles recommended by the instrument manufacturer were strictly followed during the measurements. The analyses were carried out in the Erciyes University Technology Research and Application Center (TAUM) laboratories.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

FE-SEM was used to study the surface morphology and microstructural properties of the samples. FE-SEM analysis evaluated the distribution of structures, voids, and microcracks formed on the fracture surfaces. Before analysis, the specimens were coated to prevent surface loading and improve conductivity, then made suitable for imaging. During FE-SEM imaging, the recommended operating voltage and magnification values were selected by the instrument manufacturer, and images were captured from different regions of each sample. EDX measurements were also performed along with FE-SEM to determine the local chemical composition of the samples. The EDX method allowed the determination of the elemental distribution in specific regions observed on the surface. The SEM and EDX analyses were performed according to the general principles specified in ASTM E150861. Tests were conducted at the TAUM of Erciyes University.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was performed. FTIR analysis is used to determine whether the geopolymerization process is successful and to perform structural characterization of the reaction products62–64. The relevant analyses were conducted within TAUM.

XRD (X-ray diffraction)

XRD analysis determines the crystal structure and phases of solid materials. X-rays directed at crystal structures scatter at specific angles when they hit atomic planes, creating diffraction patterns. By analyzing these patterns, information can be obtained about which crystal phases the material contains, the arrangement of atoms, and certain crystal defects65–67.

Marsh flow cone test (MFV)

The MFV test was used to determine the flow properties of the mixtures in their fresh state. This method is preferred for evaluating the viscosity and flow time of injection grouts and similar fluid mixtures. The injection mixture prepared using a Marsh funnel with a volume of 1500 mL and an outlet diameter of 4.76 mm was passed through the funnel, and the flow time was measured. To compare the consistency of the injection mixture with water, the Marsh flow time measured for water was approximately 25 s. Before the test, the inner surface of the funnel was cleaned and confirmed to be dry. The prepared mixture was filled into the upper part of the funnel without leaving any air pockets and allowed to flow freely. The time it took for the mixture to empty was measured using a stopwatch after the cap at the bottom of the funnel was opened. Measurements were taken in accordance with ASTM C93968, following the method specified in the standard. At least two replicates were taken for each mixture, and the average value was calculated. The results obtained were used to evaluate the pumpability and workability properties of the mix.

Setting time (ST)

The setting time was determined to evaluate the workability and suitability of geopolymer injection mixtures for field conditions. The final setting time was measured under ambient curing conditions based on the EN 196-3, 201669 standard and adapted to geopolymer systems. This method reliably indicates the time it takes for the mixture to transition from a plastic to a rigid state.

Compressive strength test (CST)

In the study, cube samples measuring 70 mm x 70 mm x 70 mm were prepared to determine the compressive strength of the injection mixtures. The samples were poured into molds and cured under laboratory ambient temperature conditions throughout the curing period. After completing the curing process, each specimen was tested for compressive strength using a hydraulic press device (Fig. 3). During the test, the loading rate of the device and the specimen placement procedure were carried out in accordance with the ASTM C109/C109M-21 standard. Compressive strength values were calculated by dividing the maximum applied load by the surface area of the specimen70.

Compressive strength test samples and testing equipment.

The data obtained was analysed to compare the effect of different mixture ratios on compressive strength. The effects of water/binder ratio, alkali activator quantity, and binder component ratios on compressive strength were examined within the scope of this analysis.

Design, ANOVA, and TOPSIS

The full factorial design has been selected. This design approach systematically includes all possible combinations of each level of all factors involved in an experiment. Thus, the main effects of the factors and the interactions between factors can be evaluated comprehensively71–73. The effects of mixture parameters on geopolymer performance were successfully investigated using MINITAB, and meaningful results were obtained74. The main objective is to determine the optimal parameter combinations with fewer experiments systematically. In this context, the experimental full factorial design method was chosen to evaluate the effects of water/binder ratio and GGBFS/binder ratio variables on the properties of fresh and hardened concrete in cement-based mixtures. The experiments were planned according to the full factorial array created using Minitab software. The experimental factors and levels are summarized in Table 3, where factor A represents the water/binder ratio and factor B represents the GGBFS/binder ratio, each consisting of five levels (Level 1–5).

Within the scope of this experimental design, two different factors were considered at five levels each, resulting in a total of 25 distinct experimental conditions that were defined and applied. The flow time (seconds), one of the properties of the fresh mixture, and the compressive strength (MPa) obtained after 28 days of curing were measured for each experimental mixture. These two output variables were evaluated as dependent variables, and the effect of both factors on these outputs was analysed. Although the experimental design followed a full factorial approach, the data were analyzed within the framework of the full factorial design. The effects of each factor were compared across their levels, and the parameter combinations that provided optimal performance were identified. Additionally, using ANOVA and effect plots, the contribution ratios of each factor on the dependent variables were statistically determined. As a result, significant findings were obtained for designing a mixture that is both highly workable and mechanically strong75.

To identify the mixture exhibiting the best performance when multiple responses are evaluated simultaneously, the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) method was applied. Three criteria were considered in the analysis, compressive strength (benefit criterion), flow time (cost criterion), and setting time (cost criterion). The data were first normalized using the vector normalization method, and then an equal weight was assigned to each criterion to obtain the weighted normalized matrix76,77. The ideal solution was defined as the condition where compressive strength is maximized and flow time and setting time are minimized, while the negative perfect solution was defined conversely. For each mixture, the Euclidean distances to the ideal (S⁺) and negative ideal (S⁻) solutions were calculated, and the relative closeness coefficient was determined using Eq. (1).

Experimental consequences and discussions

Compressive strength test

Experimental findings show that an increase in the water/binder ratio negatively and significantly affects compressive strength. Table 4 shows compressive strength according to water/binder and GGBFS content. With the water/binder ratio increasing from 0.50 to 1.25, a sharp decline in compressive strength was observed. The highest strength was observed at a ratio of 0.50, approximately 67 MPa, while this value decreased to approximately 1 MPa when the ratio reached 1.50. Samples with water/binder ratios of 1.25 and 1.50 exhibited extremely low compressive strength after 28 days of curing. This trend suggests that an increase in water content enhances the porosity of the mixture, weakening the microstructure and significantly reducing its mechanical strength78,79. Therefore, it is essential to maintain a low water/binder ratio to achieve high-pressure resistance.

The most effective parameter in terms of compressive strength is the water/binder ratio. It has been found that even small changes in the ratio dramatically affect strength. The GGBFS/binder ratio also has a strength-enhancing effect; however, this effect can be relatively limited. To achieve the highest strength, it is preferable to use low water/binder ratios (e.g., 0.50–0.75) and high GGBFS ratios (e.g., 50%).

The effect of varying GGBFS contents with different w/b ratios on compressive strength is presented in Fig. 4. The findings reveal a general trend of a significant increase in compressive strength as GGBFS content increases. However, the decisive role of the water/binder ratio in this effect is noteworthy. As an example from the data in the bar charts, the expression GGBFS/Binder 5% indicates the blast furnace slag/binder ratio. Among the examples given on the horizontal axis is GP-0.5, where GP is an abbreviation for the word “geopolymer,” and the value 0.5 indicates the water/binder (w/b) ratio. The percentages indicated by red arrows (%) represent the increase in compressive strength of the mixtures compared to the previous mix.

Compressive strength variation graph.

When a low w/b ratio (0.5) was used, the increase in GGBFS content significantly increased the strength. In particular, the strength value, which reaches approximately 69 MPa at a 50% GGBFS content, indicates that this ratio provides optimal binding for the system. The low water content has made the matrix more compact, and it is believed that GGBFS, along with its reaction products, has increased the mechanical strength of the system. Based on the obtained data, increasing the GGBFS ratio improves the mechanical performance of the mixture. The pozzolanic properties of GGBFS and increased reactivity can explain this within the binder system. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies57,80–83.

When the w/b ratio is increased to 0.75, the increase in strength continues, but the absolute values decrease significantly. At these ratios, it is thought that the porosity level of the system increases, thereby reducing the continuity of the binder phase. At a w/b ratio of 1.00, the GGBFS additive still positively affects strength, but this effect is limited. The finding that increased water content weakens the internal structure of the binder system and reduces strength is consistent with previous literature84. It has been observed that an increase in the water/binder ratio leads to a significant decrease in the compressive strength of the mixtures. At w/b ratios of 1.25 and 1.50, the compressive strength was relatively low in all GGBFS contents. This indicates that high water ratios weaken the geopolymerisation reaction, negatively affect the formation of the binder structure, and prevent the matrix from gaining strength. It was stated that an increase in the w/b ratio increases the porosity of the matrix, thereby reducing the compressive strength. In contrast, very low w/b ratios impair workability and lead to a non-homogeneous mixture. By contrast, increasing the GGBFS/binder ratio significantly improved compressive strength, likely due to increased calcium-aluminosilicate hydrate phases densifying the matrix. The compressive strength results obtained in this study are notably similar to the values reported in the literature85,86.

Figure 5 presents the compressive strength values obtained from three replicate samples (N = 3) with standard deviation (SD) error bars. This clearly demonstrates the reproducibility of the data. Additionally, the coefficient of variation (CV%) is provided to illustrate the relative distribution of the results. The findings indicate that the experimental results are statistically consistent, as CV values were low (below 5%) for most mixture ratios, with only a slight increase observed due to lower strength values at high water/binder ratios. These results support the reliability and statistical robustness of the obtained data. Based on these findings, it is recommended that the water/binder ratio be maintained between 0.50 and 0.75, and the blast furnace slag/binder ratio be maintained at approximately 50% to achieve the optimal flow time specified in the ASTM C93968 standard and ensure sufficient pressure resistance.

Comparison of compressive strength between GGBFS and binder.

The graph confirms trends in the literature, a low w/b ratio and high GGBFS content provide higher compressive strength. However, showing different w/b ratios together provides a more mechanistic understanding for optimizing fresh and post-set performance. In particular, at low water-to-binder ratios, the increase in strength rises dramatically when the GGBFS content is in the 20–50% range, shedding light on the optimal mix design for field application.

Marsh flow test

One of the factors with the most significant effect on flow time is the water/binder ratio. Experimental data show that as the water/binder ratio increases from 0.50 to 1.50, there is a significant decrease in average flow time (Table 5). This indicates that an increase in the water content improves the workability of the mixture by increasing its fluidity, thereby enabling the material to pass through the flow chute in a shorter time. This finding is consistent with traditional knowledge in the literature; high water content is generally associated with lower viscosity and shorter flow time.

Setting time

The ST in injection mixtures is a critical parameter in terms of ease of application and the ability of the injection to spread homogeneously on the ground. The data obtained in this study correspond to the final ST values. As shown in the figure, the ST values of the GP-0.5, GP-0.75, and GP-1.00 mixtures at different GGBFS replacement ratios were compared (Fig. 6). The results indicate that the ST decreases in all mixtures as the GGBFS ratio increases. While the GP-0.5 series offers the shortest values, at approximately 40–55 h, longer initial times were obtained in the GP-0.75 and GP-1.00 mixtures. However, a significant decrease was observed with increasing replacement ratio. This indicates that the GGBFS additive accelerates the geopolymerization process and that the GP-0.5 and GP-0.75 series, remarkably, can be evaluated as suitable ranges for injection mixtures. In mixtures with higher water/binder ratios (GP-1.25 and GP-1.50), ST values have increased significantly, reaching approximately 150–180 h. This delay can be attributed to the dilution effect of excess water, which reduces the concentration of reactive species and slows down the geopolymerization process under curing conditions.

ST values of GGBFS-based grout.

Evaluation of compressive strength and flow time

The effect of GGBFS content was also analysed. When the GGBFS/binder ratio was increased from 5 to 50, a slight decrease in the average flow time was observed. This trend indicates that GGBFS can positively affect the rheology of the mixture when used as a substitute for cement. The fact that GGBFS is less reactive than cement may cause hydration products to form more slowly and the mixture to remain more fluid in the short term.

Based on the findings, it can be stated that the most dominant variable affecting flow time is the water/binder ratio. Although the effect of the GGBFS/binder ratio is more limited, it has been observed to contribute to reducing the flow time. The flowability value of the injection mixtures is considered normal for mixtures with a flow time of up to 35 s, while values measured above 35 s are evaluated as low viscosity68,87,88. Compressive strength and flow time are jointly evaluated in the graphs presented in Fig. 7.

Relationship between flow time and compressive strength.

Maintaining a high w/b ratio and preferably a high GGBFS/binder ratio (e.g., 50%) can be considered an appropriate approach to achieve optimum flow behavior. However, this should not be interpreted in isolation; instead, it should be evaluated in conjunction with compressive strength data. Based on the results, it is recommended that the w/b ratio be maintained between 0.50 and 0.75 and that the GGBFS binder ratio be approximately 50%. While this ratio may not appear compatible with traditional additives mentioned in the literature, sufficient flow time is achieved at lower w/b ratios in the recommended geopolymer mixture.

Microstructural analysis

FE-SEM and EDX analysis

The EDX spectrum and elemental composition table reveal the element distribution in the surface region of the matrix belonging to the FA and GGBFS-based, environmentally cured geopolymer injection mixtures. ADDIN EN.CITE63,89,90. Fig. 8 shows the results of the EDX analysis. The sample IDs used in the images were obtained from GP-0.5 samples with a GGBFS/Binder ratio of 50%, with the highest compressive strength. The element with the highest weight% is oxygen (O), at 42.93%, which reflects the presence of both silicate and aluminate phases due to the FA effect, indicating that the geopolymerization reactions have been completed. Silicon (Si) and aluminum (Al) are present in significant amounts at 21.08% and 7.66%, respectively. This situation supports the binding mechanism of FA and GGBFS, which is essentially based on their silica (SiO₂) and alumina (Al₂O₃) content. The Si/Al ratio is approximately 2.75, which indicates a GP structure of the sodium aluminosilicate hydrate (N-A-S-H) type63,91. Calcium (Ca) was detected at a rate of 14.25%, an indicator of the GGBFS additive. The Si, Al, Ca, and Na contents clearly detected in the spectrum (21.08%, 7.66%, 14.25%, and 7.27%, respectively) indicate the coexistence of N-A-S-H and C-(A)-S-H gel phases with the addition of GGBFS. This confirms the formation of a high-performance hybrid matrix, as emphasized in the title, which differs from classical FA-geopolymers. In particular, the high Ca content (14.25%) has supported early and intensive gel development even under curing conditions at ambient temperature. This provides direct evidence to explain the high compressive strength results obtained. The Na content (7.27%) facilitates alkaline activation, while the Ca content contributes to the system transforming into a more dense network structure. This explains why mixtures with a low water/binder ratio develop a tighter microstructure. EDX results reveal the presence of phases and their ratios, demonstrating the effects of GGBFS addition and changes in the water/binder ratio on matrix chemistry. Thus, microstructural analyses go beyond confirming known phase formations to uncover the chemical compositional mechanisms underlying high strength.

The image shows that the geopolymer matrix has developed well and that the reaction products are clearly visible. The image shows that the N-A-S-H phases, which are amorphous in appearance, loosely structured, and low in density, and the C-A-S-H phases, which are denser and have fibrous or needle-like morphologies, have formed distinctly63,90. FE-SEM image-1 showing the GP-0.5 sample’s microstructure.

In addition, the dense geopolymer matrix regions, which spread over large areas in the image, support the reaction’s homogeneity and the matrix’s compact structure. This confirms the effectiveness of the activation at ambient temperature during the 28-day curing period and, in particular, the role of the GGBFS additive in increasing the matrix density. The high calcium content of GGBFS promotes C-A-S-H gel formation, thereby enhancing the material’s engineering properties such as mechanical strength and impermeability.

The FE-SEM image in Fig. 10 clearly shows the heterogeneous geopolymer matrix of the FA-GGBFS-based geopolymer injection mixtures, which were cured at ambient temperature. Although unreacted FA particles92 and some voids are considered disadvantages, the dense geopolymer matrix regions, the localized formation of C-A-S-H gel phases, and the observed microporosity demonstrate the decisive effects of mixing ratios and curing conditions on the microstructure. This is because this area, which shows the heterogeneous nature of the microstructure, is a local cross-section. However, the high compressive strengths obtained at low w/b ratios can be reconciled with these observations for the following reasons. It is not the individual voids that determine the strength, but the continuity of the gel phase (N-A-S-H/C-A-S-H) and the low connected capillary porosity. The voids observed in Fig. 10 are mostly isolated and closed, remaining within a gel network that does not interrupt load transfer. Partially unreacted FA particles exhibit a core-shell morphology surrounded by the reaction boundary and behave like “micro-aggregates,” increasing packing density and reducing the critical defect size. This enhances strength in conjunction with the densification of the gel volume/solid skeleton at low w/b ratios. Low w/b and appropriate activator chemistry transfer macro-capillary voids to the gel/mesopores (disconnectivity increases). From a mechanical behavior perspective, the reduction in connected large pores is more decisive than total porosity. Local voids observed in SEM are relatively less influential at this scale.

FTIR and XRD analysis

This study performed FTIR analysis to evaluate the chemical structure transformation of the prepared geopolymer injection mixtures and the formation mechanism of the binder matrix. FTIR analysis is used to understand whether the geopolymerization process has been completed and to characterize the structural properties of the reaction products62–64. The sample used in the FTIR analysis is the GP-0.5 sample, which has the highest compressive strength and an optimized binder content with 50% GGBFS content. This optimal mixture was determined based on the compressive strength results obtained by testing different GGBFS/Binder and water/binder ratios. The analysis results are shown in Fig. 11.

FTIR spectra of the GP-0.5 geopolymer sample.

FTIR analysis evaluated the effectiveness of the chemical structure transformation of the geopolymer injection mixture and the geopolymerization reaction. Various characteristic peaks related to bond structures and the phases formed were observed in the spectrum obtained from the analysis. The peak attributed to Si–O–T stretching at 959.93 cm⁻¹ has been confirmed by FTIR and SEM–EDX analyses, which indicate an increasing amorphous phase content and the development of a dense gel matrix, and is confirmed as an indicator of geopolymer gel formation. The broad and intense peak detected at approximately 959.93 cm⁻¹ corresponds to Si–O–T (T = Si or Al) asymmetric stretching vibrations, indicating the formation of an amorphous N-A-S-H gel phase within the geopolymer matrix63,64,93–95. This peak position suggests that the geopolymerization reaction has occurred effectively. The bands observed in the wavelength ranges of 1300–900 cm⁻¹, 800–600 cm⁻¹, and 600–400 cm⁻¹ indicate the presence of aluminosilicate compounds, particularly the amorphous N–A–S–H gel phase95,96. The peak observed at 1639.5 cm⁻¹ indicates the presence of physically adsorbed water in the H–O–H structure. This suggests that a certain amount of water remains in the material at the end of the curing process and is particularly associated with microporous structures64,93,97. The peak observed at a wavelength of 1396.8 cm⁻¹ corresponds to carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻) and indicates that environmental surface carbonation has occurred on the sample surface. Such reactions are commonly observed in alkaline activator systems that come into contact with air62,64,97. The peak at 753.10 cm⁻¹ corresponds to Si–O–Al bonds. This finding indicates the presence of silicate-based components that have not fully reacted in the structure63,93,96–98. The FTIR spectrum obtained confirms that the geopolymer injection mixture has developed a chemically stable structure and that the geopolymerization process has been completed with the formation of N-A-S-H gel.

XRD analysis results provide important information regarding the structural characterization of the geopolymer binder system. The broad amorphous dome observed at 20°–35° indicates the presence of C-A-S-H and N-A-S-H gel phases (Fig. 12). Broad amorphous reflection regions characterize these phases. The elemental distribution data determined by EDX are consistent with the XRD and FTIR findings, supporting the formation of the binder by calcium- and sodium-based aluminosilicate gels99–101.

Conclusions and recommendations

Within the scope of this study, the effects of different w/b and GGBFS/binder ratios on the mechanical, rheological, and microstructural properties of geopolymer injection mixtures were investigated in detail. The findings clearly revealed that the w/b ratio is a decisive parameter for both strength and flow behavior. At high w/b ratios, the integrity of the microstructure weakened, resulting in significant decreases in compressive strength. In contrast, low w/b ratios in the 0.50–0.75 range supported the formation of a denser matrix and enabled the achievement of high strength values.

The mixture containing 50% GGBFS with a w/b ratio of 0.50, which satisfied the requirement of remaining below the maximum flow time for injection applications, exhibited the highest compressive strength of 66.87 MPa and was therefore identified as the optimum geopolymer injection grout. The compressive strength of the GP-0.5 mixture increased by 69%, rising from 20 MPa to 67 MPa, as the GGBFS content increased from 5% to 50%. Similarly, the GP-0.75 mixture also exhibited a 25% increase in compressive strength, rising from 21 MPa to 28 MPa, when the GGBFS/binder ratio was increased from 5% to 50%. Although increasing GGBFS content improved strength across all ratios, the best results were obtained at low w/b ratios.

Flow tests indicated that increases in both w/b ratio and GGBFS content generally reduced flow time. Marsh flow times showed a decreasing trend with increasing GGBFS/binder ratio; this decrease was approximately 32% for GP-0.5, 13% for GP-0.75, 8% for GP-1.00, and 4.5% for GP-1.25.

The setting time of injection mixtures decreases with increasing GGBFS replacement, indicating that GGBFS accelerates the geopolymerization process. The GP-0.5 and GP-0.75 series represent suitable ranges for practical injection applications. In this study, the TOPSIS multi-criteria decision-making method was used to evaluate multiple parameters simultaneously. Compressive strength, flow time, and setting time were considered with equal weights. According to the analysis results, the mixture with a water-to-binder ratio of 0.50 and 50% GGBFS (Sample 5) showed the highest relative closeness coefficient (Ci* = 0.910) and demonstrated the best overall performance.

Increasing GGBFS content, particularly in combination with low w/b ratios, significantly enhanced the system’s strength. This effect is attributed to the pozzolanic properties of GGBFS and the formation of a hybrid structure in which C-A-S-H and N-A-S-H phases coexist. XRD analysis revealed a broad amorphous peak between 20° and 35°, indicating the formation of C-A-S-H and N-A-S-H gel phases. At the same time, EDX elemental distribution confirmed the presence of calcium- and sodium-based aluminosilicate gels in the binder. FE-SEM and FTIR analyses confirmed that these gel phases contribute to the development of a dense, homogeneous, and continuous binder matrix. Microstructural analyses further showed that low water ratios reduce matrix porosity, resulting in a more durable structure. Regarding flow behavior, the w/b ratio was observed to play a dominant role, while the GGBFS ratio had a limited but positive effect.

To optimize both rheological and mechanical performance, it is recommended that the w/b ratio be maintained between 0.50 and 0.75 and the GGBFS ratio around 50%. ANOVA analysis indicated that the w/b ratio is the most statistically and practically influential parameter. While the GGBFS/binder ratio also has a significant effect, its contribution is comparatively smaller. High R² and low error variance values support the model’s validity and the reliability of the experimental design. In this study, the method was effectively applied both as an experimental design and as a prediction tool, reducing the number of required experiments and allowing accurate predictions from the data.

The results obtained not only explain material performance at the laboratory level but also provide a foundation for practical applications. In geotechnical engineering, there is a growing need for alternatives with low environmental impact and sustainable carbon footprints to replace cement-based injection mixtures, which are widely used in soil improvement applications such as liquefaction control, settlement reduction, and increased bearing capacity. In this context, geopolymer injection mixtures produced from industrial wastes, such as fly ash and blast furnace slag, emerge as an environmentally friendly and high-performance option. The study findings indicate that these mixtures offer adequate mechanical and microstructural performance, making them a viable alternative to traditional systems. In conclusion, careful optimization of the w/b and GGBFS ratios in geopolymer injection mixtures allows the simultaneous achievement of high strength and sufficient flowability. The data demonstrate that geopolymer-based injection systems offer a technically and environmentally viable alternative, providing a scientific basis for their application in advanced geotechnical projects. The findings of this study have demonstrated the applicability of geopolymer injection mixtures in terms of various engineering properties.
