Lipid profile alterations in pulmonary tuberculosis patients with and without diabetes mellitus: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Samuel Yahaya, Ibrahim Anyass Goumboundi, Roland Osei Saahene, Kwabena Dankwa, Samuel Acquah, Richard K. D. Ephraim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis systematic review and meta-analysis explored lipid alterations associated with tuberculosis-diabetes comorbidity. We quantitatively synthesised the lipid parameters, comparing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and total cholesterol among patients with TB-DM comorbidity, TB-only, and DM-only.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines and a registered protocol (PROSPERO: CRD420261355915), we searched PubMed (n = 150), Scopus (n = 679), and Google Scholar (n = 70) through April 7, 2026. Observational studies reporting lipid parameters were included. Methodological quality was appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE. Pooled mean differences and pooled means were estimated using random-effects models, with between-study variance estimated by REML and Hartung-Knapp-adjusted confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic and τ2, and 95% prediction intervals were also calculated.ResultsNine studies comprising 1,494 participants were included. Compared to the TB-only group, patients with TB-DM exhibited higher concentrations of LDL-C (MD = 14.99 mg/dL; 95% CI: 1.28–28.71), TG (MD = 30.13 mg/dL; 95% CI: 17.58–42.67), and TC (MD = 17.29 mg/dL; 95% CI: 5.42–29.16), while HDL-C showed no significant difference (MD = −1.15 mg/dL; 95% CI: −5.07–2.77). A principal sensitivity analysis showed that TG elevation remained robust, whereas LDL-C and TC findings lost statistical significance. In the secondary comparison, TB-DM patients exhibited significantly lower concentrations across all four lipid parameters relative to the DM-only group. Heterogeneity was substantial for the primary comparison (I2 >70%) but ranged from 1% to 67% in the secondary comparison. Exploratory subgroup analyses showed statistical differences for LDL-C, TC, and TG, whereas no subgroup difference was found for confounder adjustment, geographic region, or study design. The overall certainty of evidence was rated as very low across all outcomes.ConclusionThe findings suggest that TB-DM may be associated with a higher TG concentration compared to patients with TB alone. Significant associations with LDL-C and TC were also observed but were not robust to sensitivity analyses, while no significant difference was found for HDL-C. Conversely, TB-DM may be associated with a relative reduction in HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and TC compared to DM-only.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14453-6.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a major health threat globally. It continues to be the foremost cause of death from a single infectious agent, which claimed approximately 1.23 million lives globally in 2024 [1]. Concurrently, diabetes mellitus (DM) affects an ever-increasing share of the population. Modern estimates indicate that approximately 589 million adults (aged 20 − 79) live with diabetes globally, with a projected 45% increase by 2050 [2]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), there is a significant overlap between these two conditions. Compared to TB patients without diabetes, those with TB-DM comorbidity face greater risks of relapse, treatment failure, and mortality [3]. This overlap between TB and diabetes mellitus emphasises the necessity of exploring their mutual effects on disease mechanisms and management at a population scale.

TB and diabetes mellitus converge through metabolic and immune pathways, with lipids as a significant nexus. The cell wall of Mycobacterium tuberculosis is rich in lipids, and the components of the lipid molecules can alter host lipid metabolism and immune responses during infection. This induces lipid-laden macrophage formation, which the bacterium harnesses as an energy reservoir [4, 5]. Diabetic patients, especially those with type 2 diabetes, are likely to experience atherogenic dyslipidaemia, marked by elevated triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) but reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [6], which commonly arises alongside insulin resistance and sustained hyperglycaemia [7]. This underlying hyperglycaemia impairs macrophage and lymphocyte activity, slowing bacterial clearance and worsening TB disease [8]. In essence, the co-existence of diabetes mellitus and TB establishes a bidirectional syndemic, where each disease influences the outcomes of the other.

The clinical significance of lipid alterations in TB-DM comorbidity is increasingly recognised. Active TB is traditionally viewed as a catabolic disease that leads to wasting and a general reduction in circulating lipid levels [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that the presence of DM in TB patients may shift the metabolic profile toward a pro-atherogenic state, which has been linked to exacerbated lung injury and heightened systemic inflammation [10]. Given that hyperlipidaemia affects nearly 70% of diabetes patients [11], a quantitative understanding of the lipid profile in comorbid patients is essential. To our knowledge, no previous systematic review has quantitatively compared lipid profiles among TB-DM, TB-only, and DM-only patients. Therefore, this study aimed to compare key lipid parameters, including HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides, and total cholesterol, between patients with TB-DM comorbidity, those with tuberculosis (TB) only and those with only DM. Such a synthesis is relevant for improving risk stratification and developing targeted metabolic management strategies for this vulnerable patient population.

Methods

Study design

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12]. The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration Number CRD420261355915).

Eligibility criteria

The review included published studies involving adult patients (18 years and above) diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis. The exposure of interest was the presence of diabetes mellitus, defined as a comorbid condition among patients with pulmonary tuberculosis (TB-DM comorbidity). The primary comparator group was pulmonary TB patients without diabetes mellitus (TB-only group). A secondary comparator group, diabetes mellitus (DM-only group), was also included. The primary outcome was the difference in mean lipid levels (HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides, and total cholesterol) between pulmonary TB patients with diabetes and those without diabetes. The secondary outcome compared the difference in mean lipid levels between TB-DM patients and DM-only patients. Eligible designs were observational studies, including cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort studies (prospective or retrospective). Reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, randomised controlled trials, case reports, animal studies, and non-English studies were excluded. Studies not published in English were excluded due to resource constraints preventing reliable translation and data extraction.

Information sources and search strategy

The following databases were searched for the retrieval of relevant data: Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar. No date restrictions were applied. The search strategy was developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords to identify studies examining lipid parameters in tuberculosis patients. All databases were last searched on April 7, 2026. For PubMed, (“Tuberculosis, Pulmonary”[Mesh] OR “pulmonary tuberculosis”[tiab] OR tuberculosis[tiab] OR TB[tiab]) AND (“Diabetes Mellitus”[Mesh] OR “Diabetes Mellitus”[tiab] OR diabetes[tiab] OR “type 2 diabetes”[tiab] OR “type 1 diabetes”[tiab]) AND (“Dyslipidaemias”[Mesh] OR dyslipidaemia[tiab] OR dyslipidemia[tiab] OR “lipid profile”[tiab] OR cholesterol[tiab] OR triglycerides[tiab] OR HDL[tiab] OR LDL[tiab] OR “high-density lipoprotein”[tiab] OR “low-density lipoprotein”[tiab]).

For Google Scholar, results were sorted by default relevance ranking, and the first seven pages of results were screened, with screening discontinued once the relevance of successive results declined. The full search strategies for all databases have been provided in the supplementary materials (Table S3).

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into an online reference management software (Rayyan) for study screening and selection. Two reviewers independently screened study titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full-text screening was subsequently performed independently by the same reviewers using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Screening and selection discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

The data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Extracted data included primary author, year of publication, country, study design, setting, sample size, groups reported (TB-DM, TB-only, DM-only), participant age, sex distribution, BMI, TB history, diagnostic criteria for pulmonary TB and diabetes mellitus, DM type, DM status, use of lipid-lowering drugs, fasting status, and control for confounding factors. The primary outcomes for extraction were the concentrations (mean and SD) of the key lipid parameters: high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC). For studies that reported lipid levels as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or full ranges [13–16], the mean and standard deviation (SD) were estimated using the validated statistical formulas of Wan et al. [17] and Luo et al. [18]. All lipid parameters reported in mmol/L [13, 16, 19, 20] were converted to mg/dL by multiplying them by a factor of 38.67 for TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C, and 88.57 for TG. For studies that presented data across multiple subgroups [16, 19], a single representative mean and SD for the entire group were calculated using the sample-size-weighted pooled mean and SD formula [21].

Risk of bias assessment

This review utilised the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for assessing the quality of observational studies. To ensure accuracy across different study designs, we used the NOS adaptation for cross-sectional studies, given its established use for observational studies involving non-cohort designs, and the original NOS for cohort studies [22]. Detailed descriptions of the specific domains, items, and the coding manual used for these adaptations are provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary File 3). Each study was assessed across three specific domains: selection (maximum of four stars), comparability (maximum of two stars), and outcome (maximum of three stars), yielding a total score of 9. Studies scoring 7–9 were classified as low risk of bias, 5–6 as moderate risk, and fewer than 5 as high risk. The assessment was performed by two reviewers independently, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2. Meta-analyses were conducted specifically using the meta package to synthesise the data extracted from the included studies. The primary measure of effect for comparing the lipid parameters across the study groups was the mean difference (MD), reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and 95% prediction intervals (PIs). Given the small number of included studies and substantial heterogeneity, the Hartung-Knapp (HK) adjustment was applied to all pooled random-effects estimates to provide more conservative CIs. Random-effects models were used throughout, with between-study variance (τ2) estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic.

For each lipid parameter, pooled mean concentrations with corresponding 95% CIs and 95% PIs were also calculated separately for the TB-DM, TB-only, and DM-only groups. Both subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted only for the primary comparison (TB-DM vs. TB-only). Subgroup analyses explored potential sources of heterogeneity based on study design, geographic region, control for at least one confounding factor, and fasting status. For the sensitivity analysis, the primary comparison was repeated, restricted to studies not requiring median-to-mean transformation, studies with low risk of bias, studies that excluded patients on lipid-lowering drug use, and a full leave-one-out analysis. Finally, Egger’s regression tests for asymmetry and funnel plots were used to evaluate publication bias for all four lipid parameters in the primary comparison. For all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of the evidence for each pooled lipid outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) framework. We utilised the GRADEpro GDT online software to facilitate the assessment for both the primary and secondary comparisons. As the included studies were non-randomised observational designs, the evidence for each outcome initially started at a “low” certainty level. The evidence was further evaluated and potentially downgraded based on five specific domains (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias) or upgraded based on three specific domains (large effect, plausible confounding, and dose-response gradient). The overall certainty for each lipid parameter was then classified into one of four categories: high certainty, moderate certainty, low certainty, or very low certainty.

Risk of bias was assessed based on the proportion and weight of contributing studies with serious limitations in a specific NOS domain, rather than relying on total score classification alone. Inconsistency was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with outcomes showing substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) downgraded accordingly. Indirectness was assessed by evaluating whether the population, exposure, comparator, and outcome definitions across the included studies aligned directly with the review question. Imprecision was assessed based on the width of the 95% CI relative to the point estimate and the number of contributing studies, since no minimal clinically important difference for these lipid parameters was prespecified. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test and funnel plot asymmetry for the primary comparison. This assessment was not conducted for the secondary comparison given the smaller number of contributing studies (n = 6).

Results

Study selection

The literature search identified 899 potentially relevant studies. After removing 126 duplicates, 773 articles remained for screening. Title and abstract screening yielded 26 articles for full-text assessment. Of these, 2 full texts could not be retrieved, and 15 did not report the outcomes of interest. Characteristics of included studies

Nine observational studies published between 2017 and 2026 met all eligibility criteria (Table 1). These studies comprised a total of 1,494 participants across Africa and Asia. The study designs were cross-sectional and cohort studies. Participants were categorised into three primary groups: TB-DM, TB-only, and DM-only. While all nine studies provided data for the TB-DM and TB-only comparison, six studies [14, 15, 19, 20, 23, 24] also included a DM-only group. The study populations were generally middle-aged. In all the studies, except Vrieling et al. [20] and Dong et al. [16], the TB-only groups tended to be younger than their TB-DM counterparts. Seven (7) of the nine studies [13–15, 19, 23–25] utilised fasting blood samples for the determination of lipid levels. Only four (4) studies [15, 16, 19, 20] controlled for confounding variables such as age, sex, BMI or history of lipid treatment through matching or multivariable statistical adjustment. Diabetes status was mixed (newly and previously diagnosed) in most studies. Only Vrieling et al. [20] and Gui et al. [19] reported inclusion of participants on lipid-lowering drugs. Subgroup analysis

All subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature. Confounding subgroup analysis (Figure S2C) examined whether the mean difference (MD) in lipid concentrations between TB-DM and TB-only groups differed according to whether studies controlled for confounding factors. Formal tests for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance for any lipid parameter (HDL-C: p = 0.6289; LDL-C: p = 0.6093; TG: p = 0.2604; TC: p = 0.4255). In studies that did not adjust for confounders (n = 5), pooled MD for TG (MD = 36.12 mg/dL; 95% CI: 33.00–39.25; τ2 = 0) and TC (MD = 21.21 mg/dL; 95% CI: 4.42–38.00; τ2 = 82.34) reached statistical significance, whereas in adjusted studies (n = 4), all four lipid parameters lost statistical significance (p ≥ 0.05). Notably, TG in unadjusted studies showed 0% heterogeneity alongside a narrow PI (26.63–45.61), presenting as the most internally consistent estimate across this analysis.

The subgroup analysis based on geographic region (Figure S2B) examined whether the MD in lipid concentrations between TB-DM and TB-only groups differed between Asia (n = 7) and Africa (n = 2). Formal tests for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance for any lipid parameter (HDL-C: p = 0.4730; LDL-C: p = 0.1723; TG: p = 0.1850; TC: p = 0.9489). In the Asian subgroup, only the MD for TG (MD = 26.61 mg/dL; 95% CI: 10.90–42.31; I2 = 75.2%; τ2 = 220.12) and TC (MD = 17.19 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.99–33.40; I2 = 79.3%; τ2 = 231.54) reached statistical significance, however, with substantial heterogeneity. The non-significant tests for subgroup differences in the above analyses indicate no confirmed evidence that confounder adjustment or geographic region modifies the MD in lipid concentrations between TB-DM and TB-only patients. Hence, the observed significance for TG and TC in the unadjusted and Asian subgroups likely reflects differences in statistical power and heterogeneity between strata, rather than a genuine effect of confounder adjustment or geographic region.

Study design subgroup analysis (Figure S2A) examined whether the MD in lipid concentrations between TB-DM and TB-only groups differed according to study design, cohort (n = 4) versus cross-sectional (n = 5). Formal tests for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance for HDL-C (p = 0.2790), LDL-C (p = 0.6524), or TC (p = 0.8213). TG approached but did not reach significance (p = 0.0546), suggesting a possible, though unconfirmed, difference by study design for this parameter. TG was significantly elevated in TB-DM relative to TB-only in cross-sectional studies (MD = 39.22 mg/dL; 95% CI: 31.64–46.80; I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0), with a narrow PI (24.80–53.64), whereas the cohort estimate did not reach significance (MD = 20.52 mg/dL; 95% CI: −9.19–50.24; τ2 = 293.46) and carried a substantially wider PI (−42.52–83.57) alongside high heterogeneity (I2 = 88.9%). Given the borderline subgroup test result for TG, this difference signals a need for further investigation rather than a confirmed effect of study design. For TC, only the cross-sectional (MD = 18.49 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.92–36.07; I2 = 60.9%; τ2 = 101.48) estimate reached statistical significance. As the formal subgroup test was non-significant, this difference in significance is more likely attributable to differences in precision between the two strata than to a genuine effect of study design. HDL-C and LDL-C did not reach statistical significance in either subgroup, and PIs for both parameters crossed the null in both strata, indicating no stable association with TB-DM status regardless of study design.

Fasting subgroup analysis (Figure S2D) examined whether the MD in lipid concentrations between TB-DM and TB-only groups differed by fasting status, fasting (n = 7) versus non-fasting (n = 2). Formal tests for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance for HDL-C (p = 0.9523), LDL-C (p = 0.7245), and TC (p = 0.7103). TG showed a statistically significant subgroup difference (p = 0.0385), indicating that fasting status may genuinely modify the pooled mean difference for TG. For TG, the fasting subgroup showed a significant elevation (MD = 27.21 mg/dL; 95% CI: 12.17–42.25; I2 = 81.4%; τ2 = 208.15), though its PI crossed the null (−11.66–66.09). The non-fasting subgroup showed a larger, non-significant estimate (MD = 44.92 mg/dL; 95% CI: −30.74–120.59; I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0). Since the non-fasting subgroup contains only two studies, this estimate is strictly interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory, despite the significant subgroup test. For TC, the fasting subgroup reached significance (MD = 16.52 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.51–32.53; I2 = 77.8%; τ2 = 220.77). HDL-C and LDL-C did not reach significance in either subgroup.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), evaluating each according to the domains of selection, comparability, and outcome. Overall, all included studies were classified as having either a low or moderate risk of bias, with no study rated as high risk (Table 4). Six (6) studies [13, 16, 19, 20, 23, 24] achieved scores of 7 or 8, indicating a low risk of bias. The remaining three studies [14, 15, 25] were rated as having a moderate risk, with scores of 6. Five studies [13, 14, 23–25] scored zero on this domain despite three of them [13, 23, 24] being classified as low risk of bias based on total score. Certainty assessment

Among the primary comparison groups (TB-DM vs. TB-only), all four lipid outcomes were rated with very low certainty of the evidence. Evidence for all four outcomes was downgraded for risk of bias, since five of the nine contributing studies showed no control for confounding factors regardless of their overall NOS classification. The outcomes were each additionally downgraded for inconsistency, given substantial to high heterogeneity (72.6%, 92.2%, 77.7%, and 73.5% respectively), and for imprecision, owing to wide CIs (Table S1A). All upgrading domains were not applicable. The very low certainty across these outcomes suggests that while TB-DM patients generally exhibit a more pro-atherogenic profile compared to those with TB alone, the evidence is highly uncertain.

Similarly, all four lipids were rated with very low certainty of evidence in the secondary comparison (TB-DM vs. DM-only). Evidence for all four outcomes was downgraded for risk of bias, since three of the six contributing studies showed no control for confounding factors, and for imprecision, given wide CIs and the small number of contributing studies. HDL-C and TG were additionally downgraded for inconsistency, given substantial heterogeneity (66.9% and 56.5% respectively) (Table S1B). The very low rating suggests that while active TB in diabetic patients appears to be associated with a relative reduction in lipid levels compared to DM-only, the evidence is similarly uncertain.

Publication bias

Supplementary Figure S3 shows the funnel plots and results of Egger’s regression test for asymmetry for the TB-DM vs TB-only comparison group. No statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry was detected (p > 0.05); however, publication bias could not be reliably assessed because fewer than ten studies were available.

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis examined the metabolic interface between tuberculosis (TB) and diabetes mellitus (DM) across nine observational studies. Building upon the broad understanding of diabetes-related multimorbidity [2, 11], our findings suggest an association between TB and DM with a distinct pattern of lipid alterations, differing from the traditionally catabolic profile of TB alone [9] and the profound dyslipidaemia typical of DM alone [2, 7]. However, the interpretation of these findings requires caution due to the substantial to high heterogeneity observed across the analyses. The results reflect population-level associations and should not be interpreted as evidence of a confirmed causal biological mechanism.

In the comorbid TB-DM group, concentrations of LDL-C and total cholesterol (TC) were significantly higher than in patients with TB alone (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that the presence of DM may be associated with a lipid profile that appears to counter the hypocholesterolaemia traditionally observed in active TB [9, 26]. Substantial heterogeneity, however, limits confidence in these results, and the LDL-C association in particular was not robust across our sensitivity analyses restricted to non-transformed data (Fig. 4) and low risk of bias studies (Fig. 5). Potential confounders may include the systematic difference in age and nutritional status between study populations. TB-only participants were consistently younger and had lower BMIs than comorbid patients (Table 1). Given that advancing age is associated with higher circulating cholesterol [27], these demographic factors likely influenced the absolute magnitude of the lipid elevations observed. Although TC showed a significant difference in unadjusted studies (Figure S2C), our formal test for subgroup differences did not confirm confounder adjustment as a statistically significant modifier for either LDL-C or TC, and this pattern more likely reflects the smaller number and greater heterogeneity of adjusted studies than a confirmed confounding effect. Consequently, while the comorbid state may be associated with a pro-atherogenic trend, the extent to which DM independently offsets the catabolic changes associated with TB alone remains to be confirmed in age-matched, confounder-adjusted prospective studies.

Our pooled results demonstrated that TB-DM comorbidity was associated with significantly higher triglyceride (TG) concentrations compared to patients with TB alone, while HDL-C levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 2). The observed elevation in TG is consistent with the established metabolic profile of type 2 diabetes, in which insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia drive the hepatic overproduction of large, triglyceride-rich very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) particles [7, 28]. Unlike LDL-C and TC, the TG association remained significant and consistent across every sensitivity analysis performed, indicating this is the most robust lipid finding in our primary comparison. Nonetheless, interpretation must still account for the substantial heterogeneity observed in the TG analysis. Because age and BMI influence circulating lipid levels [27, 29], the younger, leaner profile of TB-only participants likely reduced the absolute TG concentrations observed. Regarding HDL-C, the lack of a significant difference suggests that DM does not appear to further reduce its levels beyond the effect of TB alone. Because TB can lower HDL-C through weight loss and inflammation [9, 30], and diabetic dyslipidaemia is also associated with low HDL-C [6, 20], it is plausible that the combination does not produce an additional decrement beyond the effect of TB alone. However, the reliance on observational data and the presence of confounding variables necessitate a cautious interpretation.

The differential robustness of the lipid findings observed may be partly explained by the distributional properties of each parameter. TG concentrations are well documented to follow a right-skewed distribution at the population level [31], reflecting a physiological pattern in which most individuals maintain relatively low concentrations while a subset exhibits markedly elevated values, often driven by insulin resistance and hepatic lipid overproduction [7], or acute phase metabolic changes during active infection [9]. This skewness means that the mean and standard deviation, whether directly reported or derived through median-to-mean transformation, may be more sensitive to a small number of extreme values than would be the case for a less skewed parameter [17, 18]. This may partly explain why the LDL-C and TC pooled estimates, despite reaching significance in the full dataset, proved less stable once restricted to studies not requiring median-to-mean transformation, while the TG association remained robust throughout. These considerations suggest that future primary studies could prioritise reporting of raw means and standard deviations, or at minimum sufficient distributional detail such as sample size, minimum, and maximum values, to allow more appropriate statistical handling of skewed lipid parameters.

Our secondary analysis evaluated lipid profile alterations between patients with comorbid TB-DM and those with diabetes mellitus alone (DM-only). Comorbid patients exhibited significantly lower concentrations across all lipid parameters compared to the DM-only group (Fig. 3). This trend may be partly attributable to the intracellular utilisation of host-derived lipids by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which relies on host cholesterol and fatty acids as an intracellular carbon source during infection [4, 10, 26], alongside a broader systemic inflammatory response that increases whole-body metabolic demands [7]. This proposed intracellular mechanism operates at the level of infected macrophages and should not be equated with the systemic reduction in circulating lipid concentrations observed in blood samples, which reflects a more complex, whole-body interplay of metabolic and inflammatory processes rather than a direct depletion of circulating lipids by the pathogen [4]. With this distinction in mind, these processes may plausibly contribute to attenuating the hypertriglyceridemia and hypercholesterolemia typically associated with type 2 diabetes [7, 26], consistent with reports of reduced serum cholesterol accompanying inflammation during tuberculosis treatment [30, 32].

However, while the comorbid state was associated with lower concentrations of atherogenic lipid fractions relative to DM-only, it was also associated with significantly lower HDL-C (Fig. 3). Both TB and DM are independently associated with reduced HDL-C, through inflammatory and catabolic pathways [6], and through insulin resistance-driven metabolic changes [9]. However, as HDL-C did not differ significantly between TB-DM and TB-only in the primary comparison, this reduction relative to DM-only is more likely attributable to the TB component of the comorbid state predominating, rather than a synergistic or potentiating interaction between the two conditions. This suggests that active TB, rather than the combination of both conditions, may be the primary driver of the lower HDL-C observed in comorbid patients relative to those with DM alone. However, these findings must be interpreted cautiously as population-level associations derived from observational evidence. Certainty assessments for all outcomes in this study were rated very low, underscoring the need for more robust, high-quality research to establish definitive effect estimates.

Clinical and research implications

Given the very low certainty of evidence, substantial heterogeneity, and reliance on unadjusted estimates across the included studies, these findings should be regarded strictly as hypothesis-generating and not as a basis for clinical practice change. The pro-atherogenic metabolic profile associated with TB-DM comorbidity raises questions worth investigating in future research. Whether routine lipid screening in this population would meaningfully improve clinical outcomes is entirely unproven and would require dedicated prospective evaluation. Future research should assess whether identifying dyslipidaemia in comorbid patients has any value in risk-stratifying individuals for cardiovascular or pulmonary complications. Similarly, whether the observed dyslipidaemia represents a viable target for host-directed therapy remains an open question for future mechanistic and interventional research.

This review does not provide evidence to support lipid-lowering pharmacological intervention in patients with TB-DM. The efficacy and safety of such interventions remain entirely untested in this population, and the very low certainty of the current evidence, combined with the possibility that additional lipid depletion could be harmful in malnourished patients with active TB [30, 32, 33], means that any consideration of lipid-directed therapy would be premature. Given that only four of the included studies adjusted for relevant confounders such as age, sex, BMI, or lipid-lowering treatment, and that these associations were not confirmed by formal subgroup difference testing, residual confounding cannot be excluded as a contributor to the observed associations. Rather, these findings underscore the need for dedicated prospective clinical trials and longitudinal studies with adjustment for confounding, to first establish whether a genuine, clinically meaningful lipid alteration exists in this population before any therapeutic implication can be responsibly considered.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide, to our knowledge, one of the first quantitative syntheses of the metabolic interface between TB and DM, offering a comprehensive characterisation of lipid alterations across three distinct patient populations (TB-DM, TB-only, and DM-only). A major strength of this study is its rigorous methodology, which ensures transparency and minimises bias in the review process. The inclusion of three-way comparisons provides a more complete metabolic picture than previous reports [8, 10]. Furthermore, the use of random-effects models, REML, and GRADE certainty assessments enhances the clinical interpretability and critical evaluation of the pooled evidence.

However, several important limitations must be acknowledged to contextualise these findings. First, all included studies were observational, which precludes the establishment of biological causation. Second, substantial to high heterogeneity was observed across all primary lipid outcomes. While subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, they showed that clinical diversity contributed to the pooled results. Third, because pooled mean differences were derived from unadjusted group-level lipid concentrations, residual confounding by these and other factors, including nutritional status, diabetes control, antidiabetic and lipid-lowering therapy, HIV status, smoking, and TB disease severity, cannot be excluded. Fourth, a potential geographic bias may exist, as the majority of included studies were conducted in Asia, which may limit the generalisability of these findings to other regions. Finally, several methodological factors may introduce additional uncertainty: the lack of standardisation in lipid assay methods across international laboratories, the exclusion of non-English publications, which may have introduced a degree of language bias, and the omission of major databases such as Embase and Web of Science, which were not accessible through our institution during the study period, and may have resulted in some relevant studies being missed.

A further limitation concerns the median-to-mean transformation applied to several studies reporting medians with interquartile ranges or ranges, using the formulas of Wan et al. [17] and Luo et al. [18]. These formulas assume that the underlying data are approximately normally distributed. This assumption is particularly questionable for TG, which are well recognised to follow a right-skewed distribution [31]. We instead addressed this limitation through a principal sensitivity analysis restricted to studies not requiring this transformation, which confirmed that the TG finding was robust to this concern, while the LDL-C and TC findings were not, and should be interpreted with corresponding caution. Diagnostic approaches for DM varied across the included studies, with some relying on fasting plasma glucose and others on HbA1c thresholds (Table 1). This may represent an additional potential source of heterogeneity, since active TB has been shown to affect the reliability of HbA1c as a diagnostic marker, likely due to TB-associated anaemia and altered erythrocyte turnover affecting glycated haemoglobin measurement [34].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that patients with comorbid TB-DM may occupy a distinct metabolic position relative to each condition alone. Compared to TB-only, TB-DM was associated with significantly higher TG, a finding that remained robust across sensitivity analyses and represents the most consistent result of this review. Associations with LDL-C and TC were less stable, losing statistical significance when the analysis was restricted to studies not requiring median-to-mean transformation. Compared to DM-only, TB-DM was associated with significantly lower concentrations across HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and TC, suggesting that active TB may attenuate the lipid profile typically seen in DM alone. Together, these comparisons indicate that TB-DM comorbidity is associated with a pro-atherogenic shift relative to TB alone, and a relative lipid reduction compared to DM alone. However, the results reflect population-level associations and should not be interpreted as evidence of a confirmed causal biological mechanism. Future research using age-matched, confounder-adjusted prospective cohorts, standardised raw data reporting, and well-powered clinical trials is essential to confirm these metabolic trajectories and to determine whether targeted lipid management or screening can safely improve TB-related outcomes in this vulnerable population.
