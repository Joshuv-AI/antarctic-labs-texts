Economic evaluation of low-dose computed tomography for lung cancer screening among high-risk individuals – evidence from Hungary based on the HUNCHEST-II study
Authors: Péter Rózsa, Anna Kerpel-Fronius, Mátyás Péter Murányi, Ágnes Rumszauer, Gabriella Merth, Zsolt Markóczy, Péter Csányi, Krisztina Bogos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer (LC) is the leading cause of cancer-related death in Hungary. Early diagnosis of LC contributes to delivering survival benefits to patients. Low-dose computer tomography (LDCT) is an imaging technology that can be used to identify smaller nodules. The aim of this study was to investigate the cost-effectiveness of introducing organised LDCT screening in Hungary among individuals aged 50 to 74 years with high-risk for developing LC using clinical effectiveness and resource utilisation inputs based on the recent HUNCHEST-II clinical trial.MethodsWe estimated costs and outcomes in a cost-utility analysis framework over the time horizon of 30 years to compare annual and biennial screening for LC with LDCT against standard screening. The economic evaluation simulated a cohort of current and former smokers aged 50 to 74 years with a smoking exposure of at least 25 pack-years, using data from HUNCHEST-II, a multicentre study to evaluate the impact of LDCT screening on early detection of LC. Complementary data were retrieved from published studies and interviews with local experts.ResultsThe results of the analysis are favourable from an economic perspective: the introduction of biennial screening for LC with LDCT yielded an incremental effectiveness of 0.031 QALYs as well as an increase in costs of 306 764 HUFs over the 30-year -time horizon when compared to standard screening. The value of the base case ICER (9 908 100 HUF/QALY) of biennial screening for LC with LDCT over standard screening was below the relevant cost-effectiveness threshold. Applying an annual screening strategy using LDCT yielded even more favourable cost-effectiveness results (ICER = 7 927 455 HUF/QALY) compared to biennial screening. Notably, the cost-effectiveness of biennial screening was extendedly dominated by annual screening.ConclusionsAlong with the mature data on its effectiveness, our analysis confirms that using LDCT for LC screening among high-risk individuals is a cost-effective alternative of standard screening in Hungary. Funding a nationwide lung screening program that uses LDCT is a justified decision in economic terms; annual screening would be the optimal strategy to maximize health benefits; however in case of limited financial resources, biennial LDCT screening could offer a cost saving alternative for marginally less health gains than annual screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11828-w.Keywords: Lung cancer, Low-dose computed tomography, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Respiratory-gated imaging techniques, Smokers

Background

Cancers of the respiratory tract have been and are still among the leading causes of death in upper-middle income countries such as Hungary. Five-year survival rates approach 70% with surgical resection of stage IA disease; however, more than 75% of individuals have incurable locally advanced or metastatic disease at diagnosis, the latter having a 5-year survival of less than 5% [1]. Provided that the diagnosis is made early in the course of the disease, patients should benefit from receiving early treatment interventions. A possible key to the early diagnosis of lung cancer (LC) (and thus, to maximise the impact of treatments) is introducing targeted screening programmes for high-risk individuals such as current and former smokers at an older age.

Using low-dose computerised tomography (LDCT) imaging, healthcare providers can identify smaller nodules than by using chest radiograph. High-quality evidence has been available on the use of LDCT for LC screening for more than 2 decades: a recent synthesis of the available evidence showed that it is associated with a reduction in LC-related mortality in high-risk individuals, more precisely, those over the age of 40 years with a significant smoking history [2]. This effect was consistent in comparison with alternative strategies of opportunistic screening offered by health care providers (for example, mobile LC screening units or enrolment in occupational health and safety programmes).

The relevance of economic evaluations harbours strong ties to their local context, with respect to the cost estimates emerging from local healthcare settings. There has already been an attempt to assess the economic aspects, in particular, budget impact [3], as well as the cost-effectiveness of using LDCT for LC screening in Hungary [4]. The authors concluded that introducing a nationwide targeted screening programme for LC with LDCT compared with standard screening could be cost-effective for the high-risk target group of current smokers aged between 50 and 74 years as well as between 55 and 74 years. However, results also showed that neither annual, nor biennial screening with LDCT in the general population is cost effective compared with no screening. The authors of the earlier analysis also noted that data was somewhat limited concerning the characteristics of consecutive screening rounds, as well as on expected survival, resource use and utility values. Eventually, the earlier analysis had to rely on international data to populate some model parameters. Therefore, additional data collection and economic analyses are needed to further establish the future introduction of a nationwide targeted LC screening program with LDCT.

The HUNCHEST-II study was a prospective, multicentre, single-arm screening study conducted in Hungary that evaluated the efficiency of LDCT for detecting LC among asymptomatic high-risk individuals of current and former smokers aged 50–74 years, with smoking exposure of at least 25 pack-years. Researchers reporting on the HUNCHEST-II study results highlighted a significantly greater proportion of early-stage LC detected with LDCT than observed in local archives or national cancer registries in Hungary: LC was significantly more often diagnosed in stage I–IIIA patients with surgically removable tumour compared to those in the National Korányi Institute of Pulmonology database (78.8% vs. 30.5%; p < 0.001) and the Hungarian National Cancer Registry (78.8% vs. 40.6%; p < 0.001), respectively. Likewise, stage IIIB-IV cancer was diagnosed in more than half of the patients in the Hungarian National Cancer Registry (59.5%), whereas only 21.2% of the HUNCHEST-II screening-detected cases of LC were diagnosed in stage IV (p < 0.001) [5].

The aim of this study was to further elaborate on the cost-effectiveness of using LDCT for LC screening among high-risk individuals of former and current smokers in Hungary by using the most recent data available from the HUNCHEST-II study, and to estimate the incremental number of LC patients to be identified with LDCT.

Methods

Model design and structure

To represent the characteristics of disease progression as well as to ensure comparability with earlier analysis, we designed a Markov cohort state-transition model with a cycle length of 28 days to conduct the cost-utility analysis with a time horizon of 30 years, taking into account the mean age of the target population (61.32 years) and the corresponding median residual life expectancy (approximately 20 years) in the general population to capture cost and outcomes over the lifetime of the cohort. The economic evaluation was set up considering the local guideline on conducting economic evaluations in health, and policy parameters such as the discount rate, time horizon of the analysis, and the cost-effectiveness threshold were adapted accordingly [6]. The local guidelines on economic evaluations in healthcare also explicitly specify the cost-effectiveness thresholds for health technologies relative to their incremental QALY gains. The guideline recommends using the ratio of the incremental QALY gains to the expected total QALYs provided by the intervention being assessed for selecting the appropriate cost-effectiveness threshold (that is, 1.5x the nominal GDP per capita if the aforementioned ratio is below 0.25; 2x the nominal GDP per capita if the value of the ratio is between 0.25 and 0.60; 3x the nominal GDP per capita if the value of the ratio is above 0.60).

Using a cost-utility analysis framework enables to estimate the economic impacts of introducing LDCT screening for LC through the effect of interventions (for example, earlier diagnosis contributing to better outcomes and increased resource use over the lifetime of the cohort) on several outcomes in a single evaluation. Results on outcomes and costs were both discounted by an annual rate of 3.7%. “Standard screening” was selected as the comparator in this evaluation, representing the reference screening strategy in the current local practice, containing invitation-based screening activities with voluntary participation, as well as discovery of LC by showing symptoms. The model structure consists of the following health states: alive (no diagnosis of cancer), localised small cell lung cancer (SCLC), advanced SCLC, localised non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and advanced NSCLC, as well as death as a terminal, absorbing health state (final health state of patients, as they can only enter this health state). Localised disease was defined as being Tis/T1-T3A, N0, M0 according to the classification system describing the size and spread of cancer into nearby tissue and lymph nodes, as well as any potential metastases. Advanced disease was defined as any other stage that does not match the definition of localised disease. To account for overdiagnosis (defined as overdetection of lung nodules that were never going to cause harm, abnormalities that do not progress, that progress too slowly to cause symptoms or harm during the remaining lifetime, or that resolve spontaneously [7]), the model also contains a health state to capture indeterminate cases of LC in the LDCT arm; the data on LDCT test performance included several rounds of screening, as well as the final diagnosis set up by a multidisciplinary team. The impact of LDCT and discovery of LC on costs and outcomes are both captured by the respective tunnel health states (that is, patients can only spend one cycle, and cannot remain in this health state and moves to the next state). False positive and indeterminate cases were assumed to reduce utility (by inducing the disutility values of 0.291 and 0.030, respectively) and induce further cost of conducting additional examinations (i.e. bronchoscopy, histology), and assumed to return to the alive (no diagnosis of cancer) health state. Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 was used to program the economic model (Redmond, WA).

Our economic evaluation followed a cohort of former and current smokers aged between 50 and 74 years with a smoking history of at least 25 pack-years and without a history of LC. Patients with a mean age of 61 years entered the simulation were followed on the time horizon of the evaluation (30 years); screening for LC with LDCT was set up to start immediately, and terminated once the mean age of the cohort reached 75 years (implemented by setting the probabilities associated with LC discovery by LDCT screening to zero, while individuals may be diagnosed with LC by showing clinical symptoms). The concept of the economic model assumes that LC cases develop at a given rate in the simulated cohort (see Supplementary Material for details), and may be discovered by different screening strategies, at a different rate depending on the screening strategy (that is, false negative cases may remain detectable by showing clinical symptoms, but undiscovered by LDCT screening). The cohort enters the simulation in the alive (no diagnosis of cancer) health state and moves toward the discovery of LC under the “standard screening” strategy, followed by health states describing the type of LC. When simulating the “screening for LC with LDCT” strategy, patients in the simulated cohort may also enter the health states describing the type of LC via LDCT screening (that is, their disease is discovered by LDCT in an asymptomatic stage), although they can also be classified as false positives or indeterminate cases and return to the alive (no diagnosis of cancer) health state. The impact of organised LDCT screening for LC is depicted by more patients detected in the early stages of LC yielding a higher chance for resection and thus enabling better survival in exchange for higher lifetime costs. A cure assumption was not introduced for our analysis. Patients diagnosed with LC may stay alive (while being diagnosed with cancer) or die of the disease at different rates depending on the particular health state. Death from other causes may occur in any health state of the model, according to the initial distribution of the cohort by age and gender. LC death was modelled by estimating a per-cycle transition probability constant in each health state, i.e. no duration of time was assigned as the terminal phase. Death was accounted for as a one-time cost of dying, and the utility of those dying was assumed to drop immediately to zero. In the base case, the evaluation compared two scenarios: one that assumed annual or biennial targeted screening for LC with LDCT and diagnosis by further examinations against another that assumed standard screening for LC (meaning that patients can still be diagnosed with LC either by opportunistic screening offered in Pulmonology Outpatient Offices (incorporating the current Hungarian practice of sending an invitation letter to everyone over the age of 40 years who is legally entitled to have a chest radiograph) or by showing symptoms and being referred to undergo chest X-rays). The frequency of screening for LC with LDCT (i.e. biennial or annual) was assumed to have no impact on relative test performance, although the absolute number of diagnosed patients depends on screening frequency.

The estimated size of the target population – 50–74 years of age, current or former heavy smokers – can be estimated at 644 400 people based on census data and a national public health survey from Hungary. The size of the target population was used in interim calculations to estimate participation in invitation-based screening activities with voluntary participation (as data on screening participation was reported as absolute numbers). LDCT screening uptake was assumed to be 50%, whereas the probability of responding to screening invitation per cycle in the “standard screening” strategy was assumed to be 42,80%, an estimate based on participation in LC screening programs between 2018 and 2020.

Model inputs

The clinical input parameters of the economic evaluation were sourced from the HUNCHEST-II study for the efficiency of screening, while data on the epidemiology and clinical features of LC (incidence, diagnosis, histology-specific survival), as well as utility values for each health state were collected from secondary data sources identified by targeted literature review (see Supplementary Material for details). Estimates on the survival of the cohort (such as changes in life expectancy, disease-specific mortality) were obtained from the mortality.org database and a published analysis of local retrospective data on lung cancer patients [8]. Estimates of resource use and unit costs were retrieved from clinical experts and from the analysis of cost databases and retrospective claims data of the National Health Insurance Fund Manager, respectively (see Supplementary Material for details). Components of direct costs were diagnostics, treatment (including surgery, medication, radiation therapy and respective follow-up), and hospice costs funded by the public payer. Model outcomes

We aggregated our estimates on outcomes by combining utility values assigned to health states with the time spent in the given health state to calculate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), while costs were expressed in Hungarian Forints, i.e. the local currency (HUFs, with the approximate exchange rate of 380 HUF / EUR). The economic model also enabled to estimate interim outcomes, such as the proportion of diagnosed LC cases.

Scenarios and sensitivity analyses

To observe the structural- and parameter uncertainties in the economic evaluation, we conducted both deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses and probabilistic sensitivity analyses, respectively. For the deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses, we evaluated the impact of each included variable on the cost-effectiveness results over a range of possible input values (altering the value of each included variable by ± 10% as the example suggests in the local guidelines on economic evaluations) while holding all other variables constant and visualised the results on a tornado chart. Probabilistic sensitivity analyses using a second-order Monte-Carlo simulation for 1 000 trials to generate random sets of input parameter values were carried out to assess the impact of parameter uncertainty (see Supplementary Material for details).

Results

Base case analysis

In the base case, the cost-effectiveness analysis of biennial screening for LC using LDCT among former and current smokers aged between 50 and 74 yielded incremental costs of 306 764 HUF and an incremental effectiveness of 0.031 QALYs over standard screening for LC on the time horizon of 30 years, yielding an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of 9 908 100 HUF / QALY. The annual screening strategy with LDCT versus biennial screening with LDCT resulted in incremental costs ( 174 404 HUF) and incremental QALYs (-0.022), and favourable estimates of cost-effectiveness (7 927 455 HUF / QALY) when compared to biennial screening (see Table 2); the combination of no screening and annual screening demonstrated extended dominance over the biennial screening strategy. The less frequent screening consequentially demonstrated a lower proportion of LC patients identified with LDCT (71.67% vs. 54.22%, respectively).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were carried out using the annual screening frequency scenario. Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses conducted on the cost-effectiveness model showed that input variables related to the diagnosis of localised LC, as well as corresponding utility estimates are influencing the results the most, possibly having a significant impact on the cost-effectiveness conclusion (Figs. 2 and 3). To evaluate the impact of LDCT implying further costs due to false positive and indeterminate cases, we investigated the increase in the unit costs that is needed for the cost-effectiveness conclusion to change. Our results indicate that the unit costs of false positive and indeterminate cases would need to double for the cost-effectiveness conclusion to change.

Discussion

Among its Sustainable Development Goals, the United Nations set an ambitious target of reducing premature mortality from noncommunicable disease by one third by 2030, through means of prevention, treatment and promotion of mental health and well-being [17]. With LC being one of the leading causes of death in upper-middle income and high-income countries, all interventions addressing the burden of disease such as public health screening programmes may rightfully receive the attention of several stakeholders. Government decision-makers may see using LDCT for LC screening as a socially justifiable intervention that delivers good value in exchange for the scarce resources available for health, whereas health technology developers might expect further demand for innovative treatment options, possibly even earlier in the disease trajectory. Our study provides economic evidence on using LDCT for annual and biennial LC screening among high-risk individuals of former and current smokers. While biennial screening is cost-effective relative to no screening, biennial screening was extendedly dominated by the combination of no screening and annual screening, meaning it is less cost-effective than a mix of these two strategies. It is our firm belief that this piece of evidence benefits all stakeholders, but most importantly may provide access of high-risk patients to high-quality care.

The conclusion on the cost-effectiveness of using LDCT for LC screening is in line with that reported earlier by Nagy and colleagues [4], as well as the conclusion of a recent review on the topic that highlights the favourable cost-effectiveness of LDCT observed among individuals with high-risk for LC [18]. Along with the earlier estimates on the budget impact of introducing a national screening programme for LC patients with LDCT published by Nagy and colleagues [3], this study amends the growing body of economic evidence in the Hungarian setting. Our study builds on experience from earlier investigations as it also uses data collected to provide resource use estimates (data collection from clinical experts and analyses of the local claims database) as well as used recently published data on the survival of local LC patients, contributing to the validity, and therefore the generalisability of our findings in Hungary.

This research addresses several challenges highlighted by previous studies, and therefore can be seen as addressing evidence gaps. First, we included data not only on a single round, but also on consecutive rounds of screening. This approach helps researchers determine the complete public health impact of using LDCT for LC screening on both costs and outcomes. Moreover, data from this evaluation inform on the economic aspects of taking false positive cases and also the effect of overdetection as indeterminate cases into account, an important phenomenon considered to be prevalent in studies investigating LDCT screening [19]. Second, our modelling approach enables greater granularity on analysing the association between screening frequency and the change of detectable LC cases over time, incorporating some aspects of the natural history of LC to the economic evaluation and contributing to better alignment of methods [20].

Nevertheless, our study has limitations that are essential to interpret its conclusions accurately. Most importantly, some of the underlying data on health outcomes (disease-specific survival estimates and utility values) were sourced from publications in other healthcare settings. We reply to this argument by referring to the recent research of Vokó and colleagues [21], who concluded that the five-year age-standardised net survival among LC patients in Hungary was comparable to that in most European countries; thus, the impact of this limitation on the validity of our findings can be seen modest. In addition to data collection issues, quality of life data was not collected in the HUNCHEST-II study, whereas such information is essential for estimating QALYs for cost-utility analysis. Therefore, our analysis included input values from large, multicentre cohort studies that collected data consistent with the health states defined in the economic model. Another possible limitation of this research is that we implicitly assumed subjects in the cohort to show up for screening at a constant rate over the whole time horizon of the analysis. Although attrition rate can reasonably be expected to rise over time, reaching double-digit values in high-income countries [22], therefore we anticipated a gradual increase in LDCT screening uptake in Hungary in consecutive years as a result of public health interventions. The estimate used in our analysis (50% screening uptake in the target population) may be seen high in light of recent research [23], however, we argue by referring to the observations made in the Lung Screen Uptake Trial [24], as well as to the assumptions of earlier cost-effectiveness analyses in the local setting [4]. On the long run, maintaining a screening uptake of 30% in practice would be a great achievement from a public health policy perspective. We also refer to a systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 cohort studies that found the pooled LC screening adherence rate was 55% [25]. In the “NELSON” Dutch-Belgian multicentre randomized clinical trial the cumulative reduction of death from lung cancer at 10 years was 24% and 33% among men and women, respectively. Considering the similarities in the methodology and the target population between the NELSON trial and the Hungarian LDCT LC screening program planned, similar reduction of lung cancer mortality rate might be achieved in Hungary via the LDCT LC screening program planned [9]. Modelling the effectiveness of the intervention should be discussed from a different perspective. We assumed test performance to be the same for the annual and biennial screening strategies, as well as for the initial and subsequent rounds of screening in the target population. We anticipate that different screening intervals may not lead to substantial difference in test characteristics since the natural history of the disease development may not change to a large extent. However, those individuals in the target population who will participate in the 2nd, 3rd and subsequent screenings may have more onset and more exposure to risk factors, which can affect the test characteristics. We reply to this observation by highlighting that relevant data that could be used for extrapolations are lacking, therefore introducing assumptions, as well as matching updates to the model structure would possibly introduce ambiguity to the economic evaluation.

Concerning the economic modelling methods used in this evaluation, one may highlight that recent developments regarding microsimulation and natural-history models enable taking advantage of microsimulation methods, as well as natural-history models, enabling better comparability with analyses conducted in other healthcare settings. The authors selected the modelling approach based on the availability of earlier economic models, availability of data for economic modelling and enhancing the relevance of the analysis in the local setting, yet this have come at the price of compromising on comparability with analyses conducted in different environments. Additionally, the authors decided to select a less sophisticated approach to model treatment pathways following the diagnosis of LC compared to evaluations carried out in the same scope. We argue that the impact of different screening strategies on costs and outcomes is captured to some extent in the current model structure, yet this might have implied a conservative estimate of cost-effectiveness results. As a consequence, the difference in effectiveness (i.e. the estimate on incremental QALYs) is small, therefore potentially prone to uncertainty in parameter estimates and model assumptions, calling for some caution when interpreting the results. Further segmentation of the patient cohort according to treatment pathways could possibly demonstrate additional effectiveness for the organised LDCT screening strategy.

Although the possible limitations of this research cannot be neglected, we are convinced about the cost-effectiveness of LDCT lung cancer screening being demonstrated among high-risk individuals in Hungary, especially in light of the synthesis of the available evidence from a set of different geographical settings as well as diverse healthcare system configurations [18]. We also view the current cost-effectiveness results conservative, as the public health burden of LC also manifests outside the healthcare system.

Both the annual and the biennial screening frequency was shown to be cost-effective, yet the authors argue that implementing the latter strategy has a number of advantages from budgetary and organisational perspective if we consider past research efforts [3] on this subject. Our expectation is that adapting a biennial screening frequency would result in a budget impact that is easier to manage; also, implementing this screening strategy would not imply generating significant upfront investment costs to set up the screening capacities.

Conclusions

Based on the results of our economic evaluation, using LDCT for annual and biennial LC screening among high-risk individuals of current and former smokers aged 50–74 years is a cost-effective alternative to standard screening in Hungary. When compared to no screening, the biennial screening strategy is extendedly dominated. This piece of economic evidence can be seen as confirmatory of earlier cost-effectiveness analyses conducted in the same setting. Along with the more mature local data on the effectiveness of using LDCT for LC screening, resource use and disease prognosis, our economic evaluation provides further arguments for health care policy decision makers to allocate the necessary resources for the nationwide implementation of a LC screening program with LDCT in Hungary. Nevertheless, organisational issues related to the implementation of such a public health intervention should be addressed to maximise health gains and maintain favourable cost-effectiveness results.

RP conceptualised the study and developed the draft version of the manuscript. PC was a major contributor to the writing of the manuscript. GM carried out the analysis of the study data. AK, KB, ZM and ÁR provided clinician input to the interpretation of study results and critically revised the manuscript. The economic analysis, and interpretation of the results were carried out by MPM. PR supervised the development of the economic evaluation and critically revised the manuscript. All the authors read and approved the final manuscript.

MediConcept Ltd. was commissioned by the National Korányi Institute of Pulmonology (Budapest. Hungary) to carry out an economic evaluation of screening for lung cancer with low dose computed tomography in Hungary.
