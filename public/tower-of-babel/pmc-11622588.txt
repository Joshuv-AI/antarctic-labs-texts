Pneumococcal disease in children in the Middle East and Northern Africa: A systematic literature review of clinical burden, serotype distribution, and vaccination programs
Authors: Dzhumber Ugrekhelidze, Sameh Anis, Julianna Sępek, Maciej Grys, Marta Zalewska, Izabela Pieniążek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBacterial diseases caused by S. pneumoniae are an important public health problem in Middle Eastern and North African countries. The burden of S. pneumoniae is poorly documented in these regions, local surveillance programs are lacking. This systematic review was conducted in Medline and Embase to obtain a comprehensive overview of the clinical burden of pneumococcal disease and pneumococcal vaccination status in the pediatric population in Middle East and Northern Africa. Overall, 255 articles and gray literature sources were included in the study, with the majority representing Israel (n = 82), Türkiye (n = 40), Iran (n = 27), and Saudi Arabia (n = 13). The incidence of pneumococcal disease varied based on time frame and country. The dominant IPD serotypes varied, with serotypes 19F and 14 the most prevalent in the Middle East region and serotypes 19F, 14, and 6B the most prevalent in North Africa. The PCV introduction status was identified in most countries of interest except Egypt, Jordan, and Syria. This review sheds light on the significant impact of PCV on the burden of S. pneumoniae-related diseases. Serotypes 19F, 14, and 6B are still prevalent in the countries of MENA region despite inclusion in licensed PCVs. Genomic analysis could elucidate what the drivers of this persistence are, and future work could be developed around this. Continuous monitoring of serotype epidemiology is important for policy decisions on pediatric pneumococcal vaccination recommendations.

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae or pneumococcus) is the most frequent cause of severe pneumonia and one of the leading causes of vaccine-preventable death worldwide. Although S. pneumoniae was responsible for 95% of all cases of pneumonia1 in the pre-antibiotic era, vaccination has considerably decreased S. pneumoniae-induced pneumonia cases worldwide. However, it remains a major cause of pneumonia in low-income countries, causing numerous hospitalizations and deaths.2 Recent reports indicate that morbidity rates for childhood pneumonia have steadily declined, but the burden remains substantial.3

Childhood pneumonia is associated with significant treatment and healthcare costs. Currently, several effective and relatively inexpensive interventions exist to control serious diseases such as pneumonia. For example, a global analysis of 180 countries using ecological and economic models has shown more than 1.18 million deaths and 457 million cases of disease per year before vaccination among children younger than 5 years.4 In addition, the analyses revealed that vaccination could prevent 34% of global deaths and 12% of disease cases.4 Despite the rapid global expansion of PCV use, with around 142 countries having introduced the vaccine by 2018,5 the adoption of childhood immunization in MENA region remains inadequate, reflecting significant gaps in coverage and accessibility.6

The clinical burden of pneumococcal disease in the majority of countries of the Middle East and Northern Africa (MENA) region is not well known or documented in the pediatric population. This systematic literature review aims to summarize clinical burden of pneumococcal disease (represented as S. pneumoniae serotype distribution, incidence and case fatality rates) and status of vaccination programs in children in MENA region.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and data sources

Extensive bibliographic searches were performed in Medline (via PubMed) and Embase (via Ovid) on February 22, 2022 (search strategy provided in Supplementary Table S14), to identify observational studies assessing status of pneumococcal vaccination programs and the clinical burden of pneumococcal disease in the pediatric population under 18 years of age including: S. pneumoniae serotype distributionIncidence rate of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) and other pneumococcal diseasesProportion of patients with S. pneumoniaeCase fatality rate (CFR)

To investigate the burden of IPD disease in MENA region, publications from the following countries were searched:

Middle East: Bahrain, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, State of Palestine, Syrian Arab Republic/Syria, Turkiye, United Arab Emirates, Yemen

North Africa: Algeria, Djibouti, Libya, Morocco, Sudan, Tunisia.

Additional searches were performed on national government/ministry of health websites and other gray literature sources for individual countries.

Study selection and data extraction

The initial search results were compiled into a common literature citation database. All articles were screened by two reviewers according to the PICOS criteria (provided in Supplementary Table S1) in a 2-step process, (1) title/abstract and (2) full-text review, to develop a final list of included articles (Supplementary Table S1). Reasons for excluding articles for both screening steps (title/abstract and full-text) were recorded. A four-phase flow diagram from Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) was completed during the literature search, implementing the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The flow diagram reported the number of studies initially retrieved, the number of studies excluded at each selection step, and the number of studies eventually included in the systematic literature review. Relevant data were extracted from the articles included, including study/patient background characteristics and relevant outcomes. The extracted data were checked for quality by a second reviewer.

Study quality assessment

The critical assessment of the included studies was performed by a single reviewer utilizing the JBI Critical Appraisal Tools. The selection of this tool was guided by the nature of the review and the types of studies included. Given the inclusion of observational studies encompassing various study types, it was essential to employ a tool that facilitates the evaluation of multiple study types. The JBI Critical Appraisal Tool was also chosen for its transparency and simplicity, which aligns with the requirements of this review.

Quality assessment (QA) was performed by a single investigator who made critical appraisal of articles based on questions from the JBI tool. Assessment of the trustworthiness, relevance and results of published papers was executed using miscellaneous checklists for each study type (e.g. cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, case–series studies, cohort studies and prevalence studies).

This review is based on data identified through a SLR and is limited to descriptive analysis, without the inclusion of quantitative data synthesis. The selection process was based on the PRISMA guidelines8 (Suplementary Figure S1).

Results

Study characteristics

Two hundred twenty-nine articles and 26 governmental and WHO websites were included in this SLR. The countries with the largest number of publications covering all outcomes of interest were Israel (n = 82), Türkiye (n = 40), Iran (n = 27), Saudi Arabia (n = 13) [Supplementary Table S2-S3]. The most frequently reported endpoints among the included publications were epidemiological outcomes and serotype distribution. The complete list of all included studies can be found in Supplementary Table S4.

Study quality

Quality assessment (QA) was performed for 209 of the 255 included publications. Quality of articles available only as abstracts or studies with designs not compatible with the JBI tool were not assessed.

Five main study designs were distinguished from among identified publications: cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, prevalence studies, case–control studies, and case series. Pneumococcal vaccination status

Implementing vaccination programs at the national level is key to managing vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) in the overall population. The pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV), derived from a capsular polysaccharide, was the first pneumococcal vaccine to be developed. In order to analyze the introduction of PCV immunization among the countries of interest, data were exported from the WHO website and compiled using Microsoft Excel. Among the countries of interest, the first country that introduced PPV vaccination into its immunization programs was Bahrain, wherein PCV vaccination has been administered throughout the country since 2002. Subsequently, vaccination was introduced in Qatar, Iraq, Israel, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Libya, and Iran.9

The PCV introduction status was identified in most countries of interest except Egypt, Jordan, and Syria. It should be noted that in some countries of MENA region the access to pneumococcal vaccines is provided with the help of Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, namely Djibouti, Sudan, and Yemen are Gavi-eligible countries with PCV introduced in their immunization programs. Details regarding access conditions and specific periods in which PCV was available in the vaccination programs in each country are shown in Figure 1. The main data source was the WHO website.9 Figure 1.Introduction status of PCV in the national immunization programs.9

Introduction status of PCV in the national immunization programs.9

According to the data derived from the WHO/UNICEF joint reporting process, PCV vaccination was initially available only to specific risk groups in 2002 in Bahrain. However, in 2008, PCV was administered throughout the entire country. Similarly, vaccines were primarily available only for specific risk groups in Israel, Kuwait, and Qatar before extending access to the entire population. PCV is still available only to specific risk groups in Iran. In Egypt, Jordan, and the Syrian Arab Republic, the pneumococcal vaccine is not in the national immunization schedule for routine services. In Jordan, all PCVs have been found privately on the market since 2000. The pneumococcal conjugate vaccines are not yet part of national immunization programs (NIP), although an urgent need for PCVs was discussed and approved at the Ministry of Health of Jordan.

Based on data from the WHO/UNICEF Joint Reporting Form on Immunization (JRF), the standard immunization schedule commonly involves administering a sequence of three doses to children under one-year-old, typically administered at 2, 4, and 12 months of age. However, in Kuwait, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia, the recommended schedule comprises four doses, with each dose administered at specific intervals: 2, 4, 6, and between 12 and 18 months, respectively. Additionally, in Bahrain, Israel, and Saudi Arabia an extra dose is provided to children identified as being at higher risk.

Individual serotypes distribution

The top 10 most frequent serotypes among IPD patients in the region were reported in 28 publications, with most publications reporting data from Israel, Saudi Arabia, and Iran. Specific serotype prevalence varied by cohort, country, and data collection period, with serotypes 19F and 14 the most prevalent in the Middle East region and serotypes 19F, 14, and 6B being the most prevalent in North Africa. The methodology of sample collection method and targeted strains is presented in Supplementary Table S6.

Among children with non-specified IPD, variation is observed in the distribution of leading serotypes. Serotype 19F is most prevalent in Kuwait and Tunisia, and ranks second in studies from Iran, Oman, and Algeria, with its distribution in these regions ranging from 12.9%35 to 28%.41 In Kuwait, serotype 19F was the predominant strain until 2013,23 after which serotype 12F became the most common.24 A similar pattern is observed in Oman, where 12F is also the leading serotype.25 In Iran, serotype 23F is the most prevalent, with rates ranging from 18.9% to 24.5%, as confirmed by three publications from 2013 to 2019.11,12,14 In contrast, serotype 14 is the most frequently reported in Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Algeria, and Morocco, with prevalence ranging from 16.25% to 32.5%.36 However, it is worth mentioning that the data from Saudi Arabia indicate a large variation in the leading serotypes depending on the time of data collection and age.27–30,45

The top 10 most frequent serotypes among non-IPD patients was reported in 2 publication from Iran11 and Tunisia.37 Serotypes 19F (range 12.5%–23%); 3 (9%–15%), 14 (7.5%–12.5%) were the most frequent serotypes across non-IPD patients. The top 10 highest reported serotypes among acute otitis media (AOM) patients were assessed in 3 publications from Israel20 Serotype 19A/F (range: 6.5% to 27%), 17F (33.3%), and 3 (range: 4% to 22.2%) were among the most frequent serotypes. Among children with meningitis, serotype 19F predominated in Morocco38 and Turkiye,33,34 serotype 23F in Iran.12

Epidemiology

The SLR identified 178 publications reporting epidemiological outcomes, which can be distinguished as follows:

Ø The incidence rate of patients with IPD, specific diseases among IPD (meningitis, AOM, bacteremia, bacterial pneumonia, non-bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia), and all-cause pneumonia.

Ø Proportion of patients with S. pneumoniae infection case fatality due to IPD.

The majority of publications presenting epidemiological data originated from Israel (58 articles), and Türkiye (31 articles). Median number of publications for all countries is 3.5.

Incidence rate of pneumococcal disease in children

The incidence of pneumococcal disease in children across the Middle East and North Africa, was reported in 33 publications, most of which were from Israel. The incidence of pneumococcal diseases varied considerably based on the time frame and country (Supplementary Tables 7–12).

The incidence of non-specified IPD was reported in six countries of interest: Israel, Morocco, Oman, Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates and Türkiye (Supplementary Table S12). In Israel, the incidence of IPD decreased from 49.6 in 2004 (pre-PCV period) to 17,3 in 2018 (post-PCV13) in children under 5 years old.7

The incidence rate of pneumococcal bacteremia among children <18 years of age decreased from 250 per 100,000 in the pre-PCV era to 115 in the post-PCV era in Israel.49 Among children in Oman incidence ranged from 7.7 to 17.4 in 2006.50

Meningitis incidence rate for the general population <18 years of age was reported from Israel, Oman, Qatar, and Türkiye. However, post-PCV data were captured only from Türkiye and Israel (Supplement Table S7). In Turkiye, the annual incidence of pneumococcal meningitis decreased from 0.70 cases per 100,000 in 2005–2006 period (pre-PCV) to 0,18 in 2017–2018 period.51 In Israel, children in their first year of life vs. children 2–5 years of age had a higher incidence of meningitis (11 vs. 1.7 per 100,000, respectively).

Ben-Shimol et al. compared the incidence rate of bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia among children <5 in the pre-PCV and the post-PCV era in Israel (Supplement Table S9), and the data indicated a decreasing trend after the introduction of PCV vaccine in the national immunization program (7 vs. 16.7 per 100,000).52

The incidence of AOM was reported in Türkiye, and in children <18 years old, a trend for a slight increase from 4067 per 100,000 in 2011 to 4711 per 100,000 in 2011 was observed. The incidence of AOM in children ≤5 years of age was found to be significantly decreasing from 10,700 per 100,000 in 2011 to 4712 per 100,000 in 2017.53

Proportion of patients with S. pneumoniae

Data on S. pneumoniae proportion both among healthy patients and those with IPD was reported in 116 identified publications covering 18 countries of interest, with a high number of articles from Israel (n = 40), Türkiye (n = 22), and Iran (n = 16). Apart from the country of origin, the data collected was also analyzed in terms of age and the data collection period.

Proportion of pneumococcal carriage was found in 24 publications (Supplementary Table S12). Pneumococcal carriage across countries ranged from 2% to 77.7% with the lowest value was reported in 2014 in Iran54 and the highest in Salfeet region of State of Palestine between 2012–2013.55

Proportion of S. pneumoniae cases among non-specified IPD population were found in studies from Israel and Saudi Arabia (Supplement Table S13). In a study from Saudi Arabia, the rate of S. pneumoniae infections among IPD cases was 7.5% in the pre-PCV era.27 Evaluation of S. pneumoniae cases in Israel in a 10-year period (2009–2019) points to a peak incidence between 2016 and 2017, both among children <5 and those between 5 and 18 years old (an increase from 9.22% to 41.38%).56 According to Ashkenazi et al.15 the proportion of cases <1 year old with S. pneumoniae in Israel was 9%.

All five papers reporting S. pneumoniae cases among children with pneumococcal bacteremia were from Israel (Supplement Table S13). Berger et al.49 reported the outcomes for the population <1 year old, where a significant decrease in carriage was observed after the introduction of the PCV vaccine. Similar conclusions can be drawn from the study by Dabaja-Younis et al.19 where proportion of cases was calculated for the wider pediatric population (<18). Reduction in proportion of cases was also observed in the early PCV era (2010–2012).57

S. pneumoniae cases were frequently reported in the patient population with meningitis (Supplemental Table S13). Based on the studies identified, S. pneumoniae carriage was reduced in Israel after PCV was introduced. However, a similar trend was not reported by studies from Türkiye.51 In Türkiye,58 pathogen carriage was higher among children aged 2–5 vs. children ≤1 year old. Data for S. pneumoniae carriage from Egypt, Iraq, Oman, Kuwait, Qatar, the State of Palestine, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen were collected before PCV introduction or during the early PCV era.

Case fatality rate

In a study from Lebanon, the mortality rate among children <5 years increased from 6.6% in the PCV7 era to 17.8% in the PCV13 era.46 The highest mortality was observed among children between 6 months and 5 years old.46 CFR varies depending on the invasive or noninvasive status of pneumococcal disease.46,59 Data from Egypt showed that the overall pneumococcal annual CFR among children <5 years of age was 33.3% and was higher in IPD (75%) than in non-IPD (12.5%) cases.

Discussion

The SLR aimed to evaluate the burden of pneumococcal disease in children in the Middle East and Northern Africa. We have identified studies across these regions that provide valuable information on the impact of PCV on children’s immunization. Epidemiological outcomes and serotype distribution were the most commonly reported endpoints among the included studies. The serotypes with the highest proportion among all children <18 years old were serotypes 14, 19A, 19F and 3 despite inclusion in licensed PCVs. Genomic analysis could elucidate what the drivers of this persistence are and future work could be developed around this.

The introduction of PCV7 and PCV13 vaccines contributed to a reduction in bacteremic and non-bacteremic pneumococcal pneumonia and other pneumococcal diseases, which also reduced hospitalizations and mortality due to pneumonia. PCV7/PCV13 sequential introduction into the childhood NIP led to a 67% decline in the incidence of IPD in Israel in children aged younger than 5 and 5–17 years, comparing the late-PCV13 period (6–9 years after PCV13 implementation, 2016–2019) with the pre-PCV period.7 The proportion of PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 serotypes decreased in children aged <18 years old between the pre- and post-PCV periods, so there is generally a downward trend for these serotype groups. However, while the proportion of PCV7 serotypes has declined by 30–50% across various countries in the post-PCV era, PCV13 serotypes have persisted at elevated levels (≥50%) among children under 2 years of age in Saudi Arabia, Turkiye, Morocco, and Lebanon, despite the introduction of PCV13 vaccines in these regions. In Israel non-PCV13 IPD incidence rates increased by 193% and 70% in children younger than 5 and 5–17 years, respectively, when comparing the pre-PCV and the PCV13 periods.7

A decrease in carriage of vaccine serotypes was observed in a meta-analysis encompassing four randomized trials evaluating the impact of PCV vaccination on children aged <2 years old.60 However, the overall carriage remained unchanged, attributed to a concurrent increase in NVT serotypes.60 The significance of PCV on disease outcomes has been proposed to be discernible through pneumococcal carriage studies, as newly acquired serotypes are implicated in disease development.61 Notably, the five most prevalent serotypes identified in that review correspond to seven serotypes causing the majority of global invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in children. Specifically, serotypes 19F and 14 emerged as among the most prevalent in 14 out of 21 and 13 out of 21 studies, respectively.61 These findings align with our SLR, which also indicates that serotypes 14 and 19F exhibit the highest incidence among children >18 years old.

Limitations of this SLR stem from a variety of included studies which differ in quality, some of them not reaching the highest level of rigor. Furthermore, disparities in defining outcomes and heterogenicity of populations can hinder result comparisons. The diversity of included studies results from a less conservative approach to the study design within the eligibility criteria. This approach was dictated by the expectation of low number of studies on PCV burden in the pediatric population in the MENA region. This study sought to identify as many studies as possible conducted in the MENA region that met the eligibility criteria, to present the current state of knowledge on the available data, and to assess how the burden has evolved over time in specific countries within the region. However, as a result, the SLR also contains non peer-reviewed papers as a source of information, including approximately 25% of studies/data sources unearthed during a gray literature search, potentially introducing lower-quality evidence compared to studies retrieved through database searches. In addition, conference abstracts were considered relevant, if they encompassed data not identified in other publications, which can also be recognized as sources leading to an underestimation of result reliability due to lacking precise methodological descriptions. Nevertheless, applying such an approach revealed that the MENA region is poorly described in peer-reviewed publications, and while much data was identified on the pneumococcal disease burden, the majority of them pertained to past years, not providing knowledge on the situation in the most recent years. Our SLR showed that new studies in the MENA region are needed to understand the current burden of pneumococcal disease and its change over time, with particular attention to the identification of persistent serotypes and the reasons of their persistence (vaccine escape, incomplete vaccination campaigns, etc.).

In summary, pneumococcal vaccination must remain a priority because the disease burden persists due to suboptimal vaccination rates and serotypes not included in currently available vaccines. While pediatric immunization initiatives have notably decreased the incidence of S. pneumoniae, the disease burden remains a significant challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries. As our systematic review has shown, the incidence of PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 is declining while the incidence of NVT serotypes is increasing. Serotypes 22F and 33F, identified among the top 10 serotypes in certain studies included in our analysis, are encompassed within the higher valence PCV15 vaccine, presenting a promising option to further reduce the burden of disease in children.

Expert opinion

Implementation of vaccination programs at the national level is crucial to managing vaccine-preventable diseases caused by S. pneumoniae in the general population. Although pediatric vaccination programs have significantly reduced the incidence of S. pneumoniae, the disease burden remains a major challenge globally. Invasive bacterial disease is a major public health concern in this region. The 10- and 13-valent PCVs have been used in Northern Africa and the Middle East through government-funded national immunization programs (NIPs) and in collaboration with the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunization (Gavi) since 2010.5 As for most developing countries, a three-dose schedule for basic PCV vaccination without booster vaccination has recently been introduced and has been shown to be highly immunogenic, leading to elevated levels of antibodies during the initial year of life. After the introduction of the 3 + 0 infant vaccination schedule in African countries, infant morbidity, and mortality significantly reduced.62 Our systematic review included over 300 studies conducted in MENA region, confirming the reduction in disease burden after introducing PCV.

While the systematic review provides valuable insights into the impact of pneumococcal vaccination in the targeted regions, further research is imperative. Addressing the challenges of declining herd immunity, suboptimal vaccination rates, and the emergence of NVT serotypes requires ongoing investigation. The study lays the groundwork for optimizing vaccination schedules in Northern Africa and the Middle East, emphasizing the continued importance of pneumococcal vaccination as a public health priority.
