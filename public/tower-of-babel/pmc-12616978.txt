Exploring healthcare professionals’ perspectives on barriers and enablers of antiplatelet adherence after PCI: a multisite qualitative study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulaziz M Alodhailah, Louisa Lam, Ashwaq A Almutairi, Shorok Hamed Alahmedi, Ahmed Alsadoun, Mohammed Almutairi, Monirah Albloushi, Amjad M Almutairi, Kimberley Crawford
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence to antiplatelet therapy is critical for preventing stent thrombosis and recurrent ischemic events following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Despite its importance, non-adherence remains a global challenge, particularly in settings where sociocultural factors, health system limitations, and interprofessional practices intersect. Limited research has examined how nurses and other healthcare professionals perceive these challenges in the Saudi context.AimTo explore nurses’ and other healthcare professionals’ perspectives on barriers and enablers to antiplatelet medication adherence among post-PCI patients in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted in two tertiary cardiac centres in Riyadh. Using purposive sampling, 24 healthcare professionals (13 nurses, 8 cardiologists, 3 pharmacists) participated in semi-structured interviews. Data—reflecting healthcare providers’ perspectives—were analyzed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s framework. Analytical transparency and trustworthiness were ensured through triangulation, member checking, and reflexive journaling.ResultsThree overarching themes emerged: (1) patient- and family-related barriers, including misconceptions, cultural influence, and psychological factors; (2) healthcare system and communication challenges, including language barriers, fragmented continuity of care, and affordability issues; and (3) interprofessional collaboration and professional roles, highlighting nurses as trust builders, physicians as clinical anchors, and pharmacists as regimen simplifiers.ConclusionFindings reflect healthcare professionals’ perceptions of multifactorial barriers and enablers influencing adherence. Strengthening family-inclusive education, multilingual communication strategies, structured follow-up, and equitable access to medicines—supported by interprofessional collaboration—may enhance post-PCI outcomes in Saudi Arabia.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04067-9.Keywords: Medication adherence, Antiplatelet therapy, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Nurses, Interprofessional collaboration

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the foremost cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with significant implications for healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become a cornerstone in managing acute coronary syndromes (ACS), yet its long-term success critically depends on patients’ adherence to antiplatelet therapy [2]. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), typically comprising aspirin and a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor, is essential for preventing stent thrombosis and recurrent ischemic events [3]. However, adherence rates remain suboptimal: one population-based cohort study found that only 50% of post-PCI patients maintained adequate medication refill and availability during the first year, with similar declines observed in continuation of P2Y₁₂ inhibitors and statins [4]. Factors associated with non-adherence included longer hospital stays and out-of-pocket prescription costs; conversely, regular follow-up appointments significantly improved adherence [5].

The PARIS registry-a large multicentre study in North America and Europe demonstrated that approximately 10% of patients discontinued DAPT within the first year after PCI, with discontinuation rates rising to 14% by year two [6]. Discontinuation was linked to increased cardiovascular risk, and reasons varied between patients and physicians: patients commonly cited miscommunication and lack of continuity of care, while physicians often pointed to cost concerns.

Meanwhile, recent cross-sectional data from Saudi Arabian physicians and pharmacists paint a vivid picture of adherence management practices: common strategies include direct patient inquiry and education, yet standardized tools such as the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale are used less than 15% of the time. Key obstacles include workload pressures, brief consultations, inadequate tracking systems, and insufficient training in behavioural strategies [7, 8].

In Saudi Arabia, though quantitative evaluations suggest moderate to high self-reported adherence among patients using socioeconomic factors such as insurance coverage and income significantly affect adherence levels [9–11]. These insights reveal a gap between reported intent and actual adherence behaviours, and they underscore the necessity of understanding underlying facilitators and barriers within the healthcare system and patient context.

Despite this, there is limited research exploring the perspectives of healthcare professionals, especially within Saudi Arabia, on how antiplatelet adherence is promoted, supported, or hindered [12, 13]. Published literature from the region largely centres on patient viewpoints or systemic aspects of secondary prevention, such as cardiac rehabilitation. For instance, qualitative explorations with policymakers revealed limited availability of rehabilitation services, staff shortages, and gaps in awareness among cardiologists as key barriers to delivering secondary prevention after PCI [14–16].

Taken together, these studies suggest that promoting antiplatelet adherence is a multifaceted endeavour influenced by individual behaviours, communication quality, systemic processes, interprofessional roles, and sociocultural context.

Aim of the current study

To address this knowledge gap, the aim of this current qualitative study was to explore nurses’ and other healthcare professionals’ perspectives on the barriers and enablers of antiplatelet medication adherence following PCI in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore the perspectives of nurses and other healthcare professionals on antiplatelet medication adherence following PCI in Saudi Arabia. Qualitative description was chosen to capture firsthand experiences and insights from healthcare providers working in cardiac care settings, without imposing theoretical constructs that could obscure practical realities. This approach is particularly suited to applied health research where the aim is to inform policy or practice [17]. The study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and completeness.

Study setting and recruitment

This study was conducted in two large tertiary cardiac centres located in Riyadh, the capital city of Saudi Arabia. These hospitals provide advanced interventional cardiology services and serve a broad population base, making them ideal sites for studying real-world adherence challenges in post-PCI care. The hospitals have also established interprofessional teams managing cardiac rehabilitation, discharge planning, and follow-up care, offering a rich environment for exploring interprofessional perspectives.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure the inclusion of healthcare professionals directly involved in post-PCI patient management. Initial contact was made through departmental leads and clinical educators, who distributed study information sheets and invitation letters. Healthcare professionals expressing interest were then contacted directly by the first author to confirm eligibility and schedule interviews.

Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point where no new themes or significant variations were emerging in subsequent interviews. Saturation was achieved after 21 interviews, but three additional interviews were conducted to confirm thematic redundancy. The final sample consisted of 24 participants.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured face-to-face interviews. Interviews were conducted in private meeting rooms within the hospital premises to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality. All interviews were conducted by the first author, a registered nurse with qualitative research training and clinical experience in cardiovascular nursing. His familiarity with the hospital context and professional language of participants facilitated rapport and deeper probing of emergent ideas.

Interviews followed a semi-structured guide developed based on the study’s aim and research questions. Sample prompts included:

“In your experience, what factors influence whether patients continue their antiplatelet medications after PCI?”

“Can you describe how your role or your team’s role supports medication adherence?”

Each interview lasted between 25 and 60 min, depending on the participant’s availability and the depth of the discussion. With participant consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Recording and transcription

Digital voice recorders were used for audio capture, and files were securely stored on password-protected devices. Transcriptions were completed by a professional service fluent in both Arabic and English, ensuring preservation of linguistic and cultural nuances. The research team cross-checked transcripts against recordings to ensure accuracy. Identifiers were removed and pseudonyms used during analysis and reporting.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [18], was used to identify, interpret, and report recurring patterns in the data (Fig. 1). NVivo software (version 12) facilitated coding and theme organization. The first author led the initial analysis, reading transcripts multiple times for immersion, generating initial codes, and developing a codebook. A second qualitative researcher independently reviewed a subset of coded transcripts and participated in theme refinement. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion .

Systematic data analysis process

Codes were clustered into candidate themes such as “patient misconceptions,” “family influence,” and “interprofessional communication.” Themes were iteratively reviewed against the dataset and refined into three major thematic categories, each with subthemes. Representative quotations were selected to illustrate and validate each theme.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards at two sites (IRB No. 17/0174/IRB and IRB No. 17-019E). All participants received a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature prior to participation. Written informed consent was obtained before each interview. Confidentiality was strictly maintained by anonymizing transcripts and limiting access to data to the core research team. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without consequence.

Themes, Subthemes, and descriptions

Patient- and family-related barriers

Misconceptions and limited knowledge

Healthcare professionals consistently highlighted patients’ lack of accurate understanding regarding the purpose and duration of antiplatelet therapy. Many patients assumed that medication was only required temporarily after discharge, or that symptom relief signalled a reduced need for adherence. Participants linked these misconceptions to limited health literacy and insufficient prior education about PCI outcomes.

One nurse explained:

Some patients stop their tablets after a few weeks because they think the stent is already fixed. They don’t realize the medicine is for life, not just for the first days. (Nurse, Participant 6)

Similarly, a pharmacist described the challenge of medication misinterpretation:

They often confuse blood thinners with painkillers. If they feel well, they think they can skip doses, which is dangerous. (Pharmacist, Participant 12)

These accounts illustrate how misconceptions act as a fundamental barrier to sustained adherence, emphasizing the importance of structured, plain-language education at discharge.

Cultural and family influence

Cultural norms and family involvement played a dual role, functioning both as facilitators and barriers. On one hand, supportive families encouraged adherence by reminding patients to take medications regularly. On the other hand, relatives occasionally discouraged continued use due to fear of side effects, financial burden, or reliance on traditional healing practices. This subtheme reflects the wider sociocultural context of Saudi Arabia, where family decision-making exerts a strong influence on individual health behaviours.

A cardiologist noted:

In some families, the son or wife will make sure the patient never misses a dose. But in other cases, they advise the patient to stop when there is minor bleeding, because they are afraid. (Cardiologist, Participant 18)

Another nurse highlighted the effect of traditional beliefs:

Some families tell patients to use herbal remedies instead of medicines. They don’t trust chemicals, and this can lead to stopping the pills early. (Nurse, Participant 9)

These insights underscore the cultural embeddedness of adherence decisions and the need for family-inclusive education strategies.

Psychological and emotional factors

Participants emphasized the psychological and emotional dimensions of adherence behaviour. Fear of side effects, particularly bleeding, contributed to medication discontinuation. Others mentioned medication fatigue, stress from polypharmacy, or anxiety about dependency. Emotional responses often outweighed rational explanations provided by professionals.

One pharmacist observed:

Even when we explain that the bleeding risk is low, patients remain anxious. Their fear is stronger than the information we provide. (Pharmacist, Participant 20)

A nurse added:

Some patients feel overwhelmed with too many pills, not just the blood thinners. They give up because it is too stressful to manage every day. (Nurse, Participant 4)

These findings highlight how emotional and psychological factors act as hidden barriers that are less visible than knowledge gaps but equally impactful on adherence outcomes. Addressing these concerns requires empathy, reassurance, and follow-up beyond routine discharge instructions.

Healthcare system and communication challenges

Language barriers and health literacy

Language emerged as a significant barrier to effective patient education and medication adherence. Participants noted that the multicultural healthcare workforce, whilst bringing valuable expertise, created communication challenges with Arabic-speaking patients. The magnitude of this challenge was described by a nurse:

The biggest barrier we face is language. Most of our staff are expatriates and don’t speak Arabic fluently. When we try to explain medication instructions to Saudi patients, especially the elderly, there’s often a communication gap. (Nurse B2)

A cardiologist from a non-Arabic speaking background explained the daily challenges:

Every day I see patients where I know I’m not explaining things as clearly as I could if we shared the same language. I can see in their faces that they don’t fully understand, but I’m limited in how much I can explain in Arabic. (Cardiologist B1)

Participants described various strategies to overcome language barriers, including the use of interpreters, written materials in Arabic, and involving Arabic-speaking colleagues:

When we have language difficulties, we call our Arabic-speaking colleagues to help explain things to patients. We also have brochures in Arabic that explain the medications and their importance. (Pharmacist B2)

However, participants noted that these solutions were not always available or sufficient:

Sometimes there’s no Arabic-speaking staff available when you need them, and the patient leaves without fully understanding their medication regimen. This definitely affects adherence. (Nurse B5)

Fragmented continuity of care

Another major barrier was the lack of seamless continuity between inpatient discharge and outpatient follow-up. Participants noted that time pressures during hospitalization limited the opportunity for thorough patient education, while outpatient services were often overstretched, leading to inconsistent reinforcement of adherence messages. The absence of systematic follow-up calls or structured transition-of-care programs was also described as problematic.

A cardiologist reflected:

We discharge patients quickly, but there is no guarantee they will return for follow-up. If they miss the first appointment, no one checks on them, and that is when adherence falls apart. (Cardiologist, Participant 19)

Similarly, a nurse observed:

Patients receive good advice inside the hospital, but after they leave, no one continues the education. Without follow-up, everything is forgotten. (Nurse, Participant 3)

These accounts underscore how fragmented systems weaken the reinforcement of adherence behaviors, suggesting the need for integrated follow-up strategies.

Medication access and affordability

Participants consistently identified medication availability and affordability as significant barriers, especially for expatriates and patients without full insurance coverage. While Saudi citizens typically accessed medications through government subsidies, expatriate patients often had to purchase antiplatelet drugs at their own expense, leading to skipped doses or early discontinuation.

A pharmacist noted:

For Saudi nationals, the cost is covered, but expatriates face a real struggle. Some cannot afford to buy medicines every month, so they reduce the dose themselves. (Pharmacist, Participant 20)

A nurse added:

Patients tell us they want to continue, but the financial situation doesn’t allow it. They choose between buying medicines and other family needs. (Nurse, Participant 7)

These reflections reveal how financial inequities create systemic disparities in adherence outcomes, reinforcing the need for policies that address access barriers for vulnerable patient groups.

Interprofessional collaboration and professional roles

Nurses as patient educators and trust builders

Participants consistently described nurses as central figures in patient education and ongoing adherence support. Nurses were viewed as having more frequent and extended contact with patients and families, which enabled them to build rapport and trust. This trust created opportunities to address misconceptions, encourage family involvement, and reinforce adherence messages after physicians’ brief consultations.

One nurse explained:

We spend more time with patients, so they tell us their worries about side effects or costs. When they trust us, they share things they might not say to the doctor. (Nurse, Participant 5)

A cardiologist acknowledged this dynamic:

Nurses are often the bridge. They repeat the instructions and make sure the patient and family really understand. Without them, much of what we explain is forgotten. (Cardiologist, Participant 14)

These accounts highlight how nurses serve as trust builders and patient educators, positioning them as key players in reinforcing adherence within the interprofessional team.

Physicians’ anchoring risk–benefit discussions

Physicians, particularly cardiologists, were described as anchoring the clinical rationale for antiplatelet therapy. Their role centred on explaining the pathophysiology, risk-benefit balance, and consequences of discontinuation. Participants emphasized that patients often attached the greatest weight to physicians’ authority when making decisions but noted that time pressures sometimes limited the depth of discussions.

A cardiologist reflected:

Patients want to hear from the doctor why the medicine is needed. Our words carry weight, but in a busy clinic we cannot spend long explaining everything. (Cardiologist, Participant 22)

A nurse added:

When the doctor says it clearly, the patient listens. But the details and reminders fall to us afterward. (Nurse, Participant 10)

This subtheme underscores the physician’s role as the authoritative anchor in adherence discussions, complemented by other professionals who sustain the message through ongoing reinforcement.

Pharmacists as gatekeepers of regimen simplification

Pharmacists were described as crucial in clarifying complex regimens, identifying drug–drug interactions, and providing strategies to simplify medication schedules. Their expertise was particularly valued in reducing pill burden and addressing patients’ concerns about side effects, which directly influenced adherence.

One pharmacist explained:

We adjust timings, suggest combination pills, and explain possible interactions. When patients see the plan simplified, they are more likely to continue. (Pharmacist, Participant 12)

A nurse echoed this by noting pharmacists’ role in practical adherence support:

Patients often ask the pharmacist if two pills can be taken together or if timing can be adjusted. This advice makes it easier for them to follow the regimen. (Nurse, Participant 9)

These insights portray pharmacists as “gatekeepers” who translate complex pharmacological plans into manageable routines, bridging the gap between prescription and daily adherence.

Discussion

This study explored nurses’ and other healthcare professionals’ perspectives on barriers and enablers to antiplatelet medication adherence among post-PCI patients in Saudi Arabia. Three overarching themes emerged: (1) patient- and family-related barriers, (2) healthcare system and communication challenges, and (3) interprofessional collaboration and professional roles. Together, these findings illustrate the complex interplay of individual, sociocultural, and systemic factors influencing adherence and underscore the importance of team-based approaches to sustain long-term antiplatelet therapy.

Patient- and family-related barriers

Consistent with international evidence, healthcare professionals in this study identified patient misconceptions and limited knowledge as major determinants of non-adherence. Misunderstanding the chronic necessity of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) has been widely reported, with patients often discontinuing treatment prematurely once symptoms subside or after perceived recovery from PCI [19, 20]. In line with our findings, a US registry reported that nearly one in five patients discontinued P2Y12 inhibitors within six months, primarily due to misperceptions about duration and necessity [21].

Family influence was another significant factor shaping adherence behaviours. In Saudi Arabia, where family structures and collective decision-making are central, relatives often act as gatekeepers of health decisions. Our participants described family support as both a facilitator and a barrier: supportive families encouraged adherence through reminders, while others discouraged medication use due to fear of side effects or reliance on traditional remedies. This duality echoes research from Middle Eastern and Asian contexts, where family members play a decisive role in chronic disease management [22]. Interventions targeting adherence must therefore extend beyond the individual patient to include family education and culturally tailored strategies [23].

Emotional and psychological barriers, such as anxiety about bleeding and medication fatigue, were also highlighted. Similar concerns have been documented in European and North American cohorts, where fear of adverse events remains a leading cause of discontinuation [24]. Addressing these factors requires not only factual education but also empathetic communication and psychological support, delivered consistently across the care continuum.

Healthcare system and communication challenges

Continuity of care was another systemic challenge. Participants described fragmented discharge planning and limited follow-up, which mirror findings from previous Saudi studies reporting that weak transitions between inpatient and outpatient services undermine adherence [25]. International research also demonstrates that structured post-discharge interventionssuch as early follow-up visits, pharmacist-led counselling, and telephone reminders enhance adherence and reduce cardiovascular events [26]. Our findings reinforce the need to implement standardized follow-up pathways in Saudi cardiac centres.

Medication access and affordability were particularly problematic for expatriate patients without insurance coverage. Although Saudi citizens benefit from subsidized healthcare, expatriates often face out-of-pocket costs, leading to skipped doses or treatment discontinuation. Similar disparities have been reported in other Gulf countries, where non-citizen populations encounter inequities in access to essential cardiovascular medications [27, 28]. Addressing these systemic inequities is critical for improving outcomes and reducing preventable complications.

Interprofessional collaboration and professional roles

Our findings emphasize the complementary roles of nurses, physicians, and pharmacists in promoting adherence. Nurses were described as trust builders and educators, providing repeated, simplified explanations and identifying barriers through close patient contact. This aligns with global evidence that nurse-led interventions improve medication adherence by fostering therapeutic relationships and tailoring education to patient needs [29].

Physicians were seen as anchoring risk–benefit discussions, with patients attaching high authority to their explanations. While time constraints limited depth, physician endorsement remained pivotal in reinforcing the importance of adherence. This echoes findings from qualitative studies in Europe and North America, where physician recommendation was the strongest predictor of long-term adherence to antiplatelet therapy [16, 30, 31].

Pharmacists were identified as gatekeepers of regimen simplification, reducing pill burden and clarifying drug interactions. Evidence from randomized controlled trials shows that pharmacist-led medication reconciliation and counselling significantly improve adherence and reduce adverse events in cardiovascular patients [32]. Integrating pharmacists into discharge planning and follow-up services may therefore represent a practical, evidence-based strategy to strengthen adherence in Saudi settings [10, 13, 33].

Taken together, these findings highlight that no single profession holds the solution to adherence challenges. Instead, adherence emerges from the synergy of interprofessional roles. Nurses, physicians, and pharmacists each contribute unique but interdependent functions, reinforcing the importance of coordinated, team-based approaches in cardiac care.

Implications for practice and policy

This study offers several implications for practice. First, family-inclusive education should be embedded into discharge planning, recognizing the strong cultural influence of family in Saudi Arabia. Second, multilingual, plain-language educational materials and interpreter support should be standardized to address linguistic diversity. Third, structured follow-up systems, including early outpatient visits, telephone reminders, and pharmacist-led counselling should be institutionalized to strengthen continuity of care. Finally, policy interventions are needed to reduce inequities in medication access for non-citizens, potentially through expanded insurance coverage or subsidized programs for essential cardiovascular drugs.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its multisite design, incorporating perspectives from nurses, physicians, and pharmacists across two tertiary centres, enhancing transferability. The inclusion of multiple professional groups allowed for data triangulation and a more comprehensive view of adherence challenges. However, the study has limitations. First, it focused solely on healthcare professionals and did not include patient perspectives, which may limit understanding of lived adherence experiences. Second, findings are specific to tertiary centres in Riyadh and may not capture variations in other regions or smaller hospitals. Third, as with all qualitative studies, findings reflect context-specific interpretations and are not intended for statistical generalization.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifaceted nature of antiplatelet adherence after PCI in Saudi Arabia, shaped by patient misconceptions, family influence, psychological barriers, language and systemic challenges, and financial inequities. Nurses, physicians, and pharmacists each play essential but complementary roles in supporting adherence, underscoring the value of interprofessional collaboration. Future interventions should prioritize family-inclusive education, multilingual communication strategies, structured follow-up, and equitable access to essential medications. By addressing these factors, healthcare systems can improve adherence, reduce preventable complications, and enhance outcomes for post-PCI patients in Saudi Arabia.
