Potential involvement of the KLF2–GPX4 axis in ferroptosis during S.aureus-induced osteomyelitis
Authors: Jian Sun, Xingbo Cai, Junhui Qi, Hao Xia, Qilin Zhao, Zhe Yin, Yi Cui, Yongqing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreasing evidence suggests that ferroptosis plays a pivotal role in Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)-induced osteomyelitis. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) in osteomyelitis remain poorly understood. This study aimed to identify key FRGs and elucidate their regulatory roles in osteomyelitis.MethodsKey FRGs were identified using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithms based on transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) were isolated, characterized, and infected with S. aureus protein A (SpA) to construct an in vitro model. Cell viability, osteogenic differentiation, inflammation, and gene expression were assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Alizarin Red S staining, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). In vivo, a mouse model of S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis was established, and the role of KLF2 was examined by micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), ELISA, histological staining, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and qRT-PCR.ResultsA total of 683 differentially expressed FRGs were identified. Ten candidate biomarkers were screened using LASSO and SVM-RFE, of which TXN, KLF2, HSPA8, CCT3, and AKR1C3 were consistently validated across training and validation datasets. These genes were associated with immune regulation, protein synthesis, and multiple ribosome- and metabolism-related pathways. In vitro, SpA treatment increased inflammation response, reduced BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, upregulated HSPA8, TXN, and CCT3, and downregulated KLF2 and its putative downstream target GPX4. In vivo, KLF2 overexpression alleviated S. aureus-induced bone loss, inflammation, and ferroptosis, while promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis, in part through modulation of GPX4.ConclusionThis study highlights KLF2 as a potential protective factor in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis, possibly by regulating GPX4 and ferroptosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00851-5.

Introduction

Osteomyelitis is a serious bone disorder primarily caused by Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) infection [1, 2], posing significant clinical challenges and economic burdens [3]. It is characterized by severe pain, restricted mobility, and, in some cases, life-threatening complications [4]. The causes of osteomyelitis include contiguous spread from a local source (e.g., trauma or surgical contamination) [2], secondary infections due to vascular insufficiency or neuropathy such as diabetic foot ulcers [5], and acute hemorrhagic osteomyelitis [6]. Typically, antimicrobial therapy is the mainstay of treatment for acute osteomyelitis, while chronic osteomyelitis often requires surgical debridement [2]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, there is no effective therapy yet for S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis. Therefore, it is essential to further explore the underlying pathological mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets with both antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties to improve treatment outcomes.

Recent studies have revealed novel mechanisms underlying S. aureus pathogenesis and its persistence within bone tissue, including the formation of implant-associated biofilms, abscesses, and invasion into the osteocyte lacuno-canalicular network [4]. S. aureus induces bone destruction by orchestrating bacterial proliferation, disrupting immune homeostasis, modulating bone metabolism, promoting biofilm formation, and targeting osteocytes [7]. First, S. aureus promotes immune evasion and abscess formation by accelerating bacterial growth and impairing the immune microenvironment [8, 9]. Second, it contributes to vascular damage and reduced oxygen levels, leading to enhanced biofilm resilience [10]. Moreover, S. aureus disrupts the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts [11–13], penetrates cortical bone to reduce osteocyte viability, and inhibits the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) [14]. These mechanisms highlight the importance of early diagnosis and the development of targeted therapies to control infection and prevent bone destruction.

Ferroptosis is a form of programmed cell death (PCD) characterized by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation. It is tightly regulated by multiple cellular metabolic pathways, including redox homeostasis, iron metabolism, mitochondrial function, and the metabolism of amino acids, lipids, and sugars, as well as various disease-related signaling cascades [15]. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis. Clinical studies have shown that the impaired osteogenic capacity observed in patients with chronic inactive osteomyelitis (CIOM) is linked to ferroptosis in osteoblasts, triggered by S. aureus internalization. Notably, melatonin has been shown to preserve osteogenic function in CIOM by modulating the Nox4/ROS/p38 signaling axis [15]. Additionally, the N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) demethylase FTO protects against S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis by inhibiting ferroptosis in BMSCs [16]. Similarly, PTGS2 exerts a protective effect in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis by inhibiting the IL-17 A signaling pathway in vitro [17], whereas the transcription factor FOS aggravates ferroptosis and inflammation in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis in vitro [18]. Furthermore, in tissue engineering contexts, ferroptosis inhibition has shown promise in combating S. aureus infection [19]. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis in the treatment of S. aureus-related osteomyelitis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms, particularly those involving transcriptional regulation of ferroptosis in this context, remain largely unclear.

In this study, we applied bioinformatics analyses to identify novel ferroptosis-related targets implicated in the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis. To validate these findings and elucidate their functional relevance, we performed comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments using S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis cell and animal models. Our results aim to clarify the role of ferroptosis in bone destruction and to assess whether modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways represents a viable therapeutic approach for osteomyelitis.

Materials and methods

Data collecting and processing

Microarray datasets GSE6269, GSE16129, and GSE30119 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE6269 included blood leukocyte samples from 44 pediatric patients with Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumonia) infection and 7 healthy controls [20]. GSE16129 included blood samples from 97 patients with Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) infections and 29 healthy controls [21]. And GSE30119 comprised whole blood samples from 39 patients with osteomyelitis and 44 healthy controls [22]. GSE6269 and GSE16129 were merged using “Perl” (http://www.perl.org/) and normalized using the “sva” (version 3.44.0) [23] and “limma” (version 3.26.8) [24]. Batch effects were corrected using the “ComBat” function [25], and the merged data were defined as the training cohort. GSE30119 served as the independent validation cohort.

Identification differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes (DE-FRGs)

A total of 834 ferroptosis related genes (FRGs, Table S1) were obtained from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/) [26]. Differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes (DE-FRGs) between S. aureus -induced osteomyelitis and healthy controls were screened using “Limma” package in R, with thresholds of |log fold change (FC)| value >1 and p-value < 0.05. The top 50 DEGs were visualized using a heatmap generated by the “pheatmap” package in R [27].

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO; biological process, BP), cellular component, CC), and molecular function, MF) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of DE-FRGs were conducted using “clusterprofiler” R package version 3.18.0 [28].

Identification of a FRG signature based on LASSO and SVM-REF algorithms

The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithms were employed to identify a FRG signature for osteomyelitis based on the DE-FRGs. LASSO regression was performed with ten-fold cross-validation using the “glmnet” package (version 4.1-7) [29], and SVM-RFE with five-fold cross-validation using “e1071” package (version 1.7–13) [30]. The intersecting genes from both algorithms were defined as the final FRG signature.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess diagnostic performance in both the training (merged datasets) and validation (GSE30119) cohorts. Expression differences of the FRG signature between osteomyelitis and control groups were further evaluated using the Wilcoxon test.

Construction of a nomogram

A nomogram integrating the expression levels of the FRG signature genes was constructed using the “rms” R package (version 6.3-0) [31] to predict osteomyelitis risk. Its diagnostic performance was evaluated by ROC curves and AUC values.

Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using “clusterProfiler” package [32]. Genes were ranked by Spearman correlation with the hub FRG signature to identify significantly enriched functions and pathways in osteomyelitis (p < 0.05).

Construction of a transcription factor (TF)-mRNA regulatory network

KLF2 is a transcription factor (TF) known to regulate downstream gene expression [33], and GPX4,a key regulator of ferroptosis, has been implicated in various diseases [34–37]. Potential regulatory interactions between KLF2 and GPX4 were predicted using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net) [38]. KLF2 binding motifs within the GPX4 promoter region were identified and further validated using both JASPAR and the NCBI database (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cell (BMSC) isolation, culture, and treatment

Bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of mice under sterile conditions and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Medium was refreshed 48 h after seeding and every 3 days thereafter. Passage 3 BMSCs were used for experiments. For treatments, cells were stimulated with recombinant Staphylococcus aureus protein A (SpA, Abcam, MA, USA) and collected for downstream assays including gene and protein expression analyses.

Flow cytometry assay

BMSC identity was confirmed by flow cytometry using positive markers (CD105, CD44, CD29) and negative markers (CD45, CD11b). Fluorophore-conjugated antibodies were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Data were acquired on an ACEA NovoCyte flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo (v10).

Multi-lineage differentiation of BMSC in vitro

BMSC differentiation potential was assessed by osteogenic and adipogenic induction using commercial differentiation kits (Procell) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Osteogenesis was confirmed by Alizarin Red S staining, and adipogenesis by Oil Red O staining [39].

Cell viability analysis

BMSC viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with graded concentrations of recombinant Staphylococcus aureus protein A (SpA, Abcam) to determine the IC₅₀. For time-course experiments, BMSCs were treated with the IC₅₀ concentration of SpA for 0, 48, 72, and 96 h. After each treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added, incubated for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay

Following cell treatment, culture supernatants and serum samples were collected. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 20 min at room temperature. Levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TNF-α) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Culturing of S. aureus, lentiviral transfection, and establishing an animal model of an osteomyelitis animal model

S. aureus (#ATCC25923; ATCC, VA, USA) was cultured on blood agar and in nutrient broth, and bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL for model establishment. KLF2-overexpressing lentivirus was generated by transfecting HEK293T cells with KLF2-expressing and packaging plasmids; viral supernatants were harvested for mouse transduction.

Thirty-six male C57BL/6 mice (10–12 weeks, 20–25 g) were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 9 each): control, S. aureus, S. aureus + OE-NC, and S. aureus + OE-KLF2. Osteomyelitis was induced by creating a distal femur bone cavity and injecting bacterial suspension, followed by insertion of a Kirschner wire to seal the canal. Treatment groups received daily intraperitoneal injections of KLF2-overexpressing or control lentivirus (100 µL, 1.5 × 10⁸ TU/mL) for 4 weeks; control mice received saline. Body temperature was monitored twice weekly. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. YNZQSW-PM-R024).

Microbiological assessment

Femoral bones were collected under sterile conditions, homogenized in PBS, serially diluted, and plated on blood agar and tryptic soy agar (TSA). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h to assess bacterial colony formation.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay

Levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TNF-α, and PCT), osteogenic markers (BALP, BGP), bone resorption marker (CTX-I), Fe²⁺, and lipid peroxide (LPO) in culture supernatants, serum, and bone marrow were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT)

Left femurs (n = 3 per group) were scanned by micro-CT. Images were reconstructed and analyzed using SkyScan CT-Analyser software. Trabecular bone parameters, including bone volume to tissue volume ratio (BV/TV; %), bone surface area to tissue volume ratio (BS/TV; mm), bone surface area to bone volume ratio (BS/BV; mm), trabecular number (Tb.N; mm), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th; mm), bone mineral density (BMD; mgHA/cm³), and the region of interest (ROI) data.

17 histology staining, immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence staining

Femurs were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, decalcified in 10% EDTA, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm) were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess inflammation. For immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, antigen-retrieved, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies against KLF2, GPX4, TF, and ACSL4, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB staining. Images were quantified using ImageJ software (Wayne Rasband, USA). For immunofluorescence, sections were incubated with CD31 primary antibody, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI nuclear staining. Sections were imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissue using VeZol Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) to assess mRNA expression levels, normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used were as follows: TXN, 5’-AACAGTGCGATTTGGGGTTAT-3’ and 5’-AGCCCACTTATAGATGTTCTCA-3’. KLF2, 5’-CTCAGCGAGCCTATCTTGCC-3’ and 5’-CACGTTGTTTAGGTCCTCATCC-3’. HSPA8, 5’-TCTCGGCACCACCTACTCC-3’ and 5’-CTACGCCCGATCAGACGTTT-3’. CCT3, 5’-GGACCTGCTTGGGACCTAAAT-3’ and 5’-CGGGATGCTGGACTTGAATC-3’. β-actin, 5’-GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG-3’ and 5’-CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT-3’.

Statistically analysis

In this study, bioinformatics analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.3). Experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test for multiple groups, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed ferroptosis-related genes (DE-FRGs) in osteomyelitis

To identify ferroptosis-related genes (DE-FRGs) associated with osteomyelitis, mRNA profiles from GSE6269 and GSE16129 (97 S. aureus patients, 29 controls) were analyzed. After batch correction, samples separated clearly into osteomyelitis and control groups (Fig. 1A). Among 834 FRGs from FerrDb, 683 were differentially expressed (DE-FRGs) (Fig. 1B-C, Table S2).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that DE-FRGs were enriched in immune and metabolic processes, including leukocyte activation, response to hypoxia, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and purine metabolism (Fig. 1D-E, Figure S1A-C). KEGG analysis showed enrichment in programmed cell death pathways (ferroptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, mitophagy), iron ion transport, and inflammatory/metabolic signaling (HIF-1, NOD-like receptor, Foxo, PI3K/AKT, carbon metabolism) (Fig. 1F-G, Figure S1D). These findings suggest that DE-FRGs may contribute to the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis by modulating inflammation, metabolism, and various forms of programmed cell death.

Identification and evaluation of a predictive signature for osteomyelitis

To assess the diagnostic potential of the DE-FRGs in osteomyelitis, we applied both LASSO regression and SVM-RFE algorithms to construct a predictive signature (Fig. 2A-B). LASSO regression identified 37 potential biomarkers, including ALKBH5, PARP10, ATG3, NFE2L1, STAT6, NUP62, BRD7, HMGCL, EGLN2, TP53, MEN1, ELAVL1, PEX6, PPARD, PHKG2, ACSF2, AQP3, IRF3, USP11, SMPD1, ATF2, MFN1, OSBPL9, SNX5, NCOA3, MEF2C, ENO3, AEBP1, METTL3, TSC1, AKR1C3, SLC38A1, USP7, BCAT2, CCT3, HSPA8, and GOT1 (Table S3). Meanwhile, the SVM-RFE algorithm identified 15 biomarkers, including GLS, MAP3K14, ICA1, ATF3, NPC1, KRT18, PHKG2, METTL3, HNRNPL, CASP3, KLF2, ATF4, AEBP1, EPHA2, and AKR1C3 (Table S4).

By integrating the results of both algorithms, we selected ten overlapping biomarkers with strong classification performance, including TXN (AUC = 0.824), AEBP1 (AUC = 0.0.796), AKR1C3 (AUC = 0.845), ATF4 (AUC = 0.819), CCT3 (AUC = 0.821), HSPA8 (AUC = 0.797), IRF3 (AUC = 0.802), KLF2 (AUC = 0.817), METTL3 (AUC = 0.866), and S100A8 (AUC = 0.804) (Fig. 2C). A nomogram model incorporating these biomarkers was constructed to predict osteomyelitis risk by summing the gene-specific scores, where a higher total score indicated a higher risk (Fig. 2D). A higher total score represented a higher risk of osteomyelitis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed excellent predictive performance of the combined model (AUC = 0.931) (Fig. 2E). Five hub biomarkers (TXN, KLF2, HSPA8, CCT3, and AKR1C3) were validated in an independent external dataset (GSE30119), with AUCs of 0.797, 0.821, 0.899, 0.752, and 0.796, respectively. Notably, TXN was consistently upregulated, whereas KLF2, HSPA8, CCT3, and AKR1C3 were downregulated in both the training and validation cohorts (Figure S1E-F). These findings suggest that TXN, KLF2, HSPA8, CCT3, and AKR1C3 are important diagnostic biomarkers and may play critical roles in the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis.

Biological functional enrichment analysis of TXN, HSPA8, CCT3, KLF2, and AKR1C3

To further elucidate the biological roles of TXN, HSPA8, CCT3, KLF2, and AKR1C3 in osteomyelitis, single-gene Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed. TXN and HSPA8 were mainly associated with DNA replication, mismatch repair, primary immunodeficiency, ribosome, RNA processing, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 3A, C). KLF2 was primarily involved in immune regulation, complement and coagulation cascades, NOD-like receptor and Toll-like receptor signaling, lysosome, and cytoskeleton regulation (Fig. 3B). CCT3 and AKR1C3 were enriched in pathways including immune responses (allograft rejection, T/NK cell signaling), ribosome, RNA processing, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 3D-E). These findings suggest that these genes may play critical roles in osteomyelitis by regulating immune responses, protein synthesis, and multiple ribosome- and metabolism-related pathways.

Previous study has reported that Staphylococcus aureus infection impairs osteoblast proliferation, with staphylococcal protein A (SpA) acting as a key mediator in the interaction between S. aureus and osteoblasts [40]. In this study, an in vitro osteomyelitis model was established by treating BMSCs with 50 µg/mL recombinant Staphylococcus aureus protein A (SpA, IC₅₀ = 54.83 µg/mL). SpA significantly inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 4C-D), increased inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TNF-α (Fig. 4E-F), and suppressed osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 4L).

Based on binding motif analysis from the JASPAR database, GPX4 was predicted to be a downstream target of KLF2. GPX4 is a critical regulator of ferroptosis [15] and is involved in various diseases [16–18]. Therefore, we examined the expression levels of the KLF2, HSPA8, TXN, CCT3, and GPX4 in response to SpA treatment in vitro. We found that SpA treatment upregulated HSPA8, TXN, and CCT3, while downregulating KLF2 and its predicted downstream target GPX4 (Fig. 4G-K). These findings suggest that S. aureus may modulate osteomyelitis progression by altering the expression of key regulatory genes involved in inflammation, ferroptosis, and osteogenesis.

KLF2 attenuates S. aureus-induced cortical bone destruction and loss in vivo

Given the potential protective role of KLF2 and its downstream target GPX4 demonstrated in vitro, we further investigated the effects of KLF2 in a S. aureus-induced murine model of osteomyelitis. A KLF2-overexpressing lentiviral vector was constructed, and S. aureus cultures were prepared for the infection experiments (Fig. 5A-B). After four weeks of treatment, micro-CT analysis revealed that S. aureus infection caused cortical bone destruction, reduced bone mineral density (BMD), and decreased bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) ratio (Fig. 5A-H). KLF2 overexpression partially reversed these changes, though differences did not reach statistical significance. Similar trends were observed for bone surface/total volume (BS/TV), bone surface/bone volume (BS/BV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) (Fig. 5C-H). These findings suggest that KLF2 may attenuate S. aureus-induced bone loss in osteomyelitis.

KLF2 inhibits S. aureus-induced bacterial growth, inflammation, and bone resorption in vivo

Next, we investigate the effects of KLF2 on bactericidal capacity and inflammation. KLF2 overexpression significantly inhibited S. aureus-induced bacterial proliferation (Fig. 6A-B). Serum levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-10, TNF-α, and PCT, which were elevated after infection, were markedly reduced by KLF2 (Fig. 6C-E). H&E staining showed that KLF2 alleviated immune cell infiltration, periosteal abscess formation, and bone necrosis observed in the S. aureus group (Fig. 6I). Additionally, S. aureus decreased bone formation markers, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP) and bone Gla protein (BGP), and increased bone resorption marker C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-1). These alterations were reversed upon KLF2 overexpression (Fig. 6F-H). These findings suggest that KLF2 protects against osteomyelitis by limiting bacterial growth, inflammation, and bone resorption.

KLF2 promotes angiogenesis in vivo

Enhancing osteogenesis and angiogenesis, while suppressing inflammation and osteoclastogenesis, is crucial for effective bone defect repair [41]. CD31-positive cells, an angiogenic marker, were reduced in S. aureus-infected mice but restored upon KLF2 overexpression (Fig. 7A-B). These results suggest that KLF2 promotes angiogenesis and contributes to the protection against osteomyelitis.

KLF2 inhibits S. aureus-induced ferroptosis in vivo by targeting GPX4

Given the protective effects of KLF2 in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis, we further explored whether KLF2 exerts its function through interaction with GPX4. Overexpression of KLF2 partially restored S. aureus-induced downregulation of KLF2 and GPX4 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8A-F). Ferroptosis markers, including Fe²⁺ and lipid peroxides (LPO), were elevated in infected mice but significantly reduced upon KLF2 overexpression (Fig. 8I-J). Similarly, Transferrin (TF) and Acyl-CoA Synthetase Long Chain Family Member 4 (ACSL4) upregulation was reversed by KLF2 (Fig. 8G-H, K-L). These findings suggest that KLF2 inhibits S. aureus-induced ferroptosis through GPX4, contributing to its protective role in osteomyelitis.

Discussion

Growing evidence indicates that ferroptosis activation exacerbates Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)-induced pathology in various diseases, including endometritis and mastitis [42–44]. In S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis, ferroptosis contributes to bone destruction by promoting bacterial colonization [45], inflammatory responses [46], and disrupting the survival and differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [16]. However, whether key regulators of ferroptosis play protective roles in osteoblasts during bacterial infection remains unclear. Therefore, identifying and validating ferroptosis-related regulatory pathways could yield novel insights into the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis and provide promising therapeutic targets.

In the present study, we identify five ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs), including TXN, KLF2, HSPA8, CCT3, and AKR1C3, as the potential diagnostic biomarkers for osteomyelitis. These genes were found to regulate immune responses, protein synthesis, and multiple ribosome- and metabolism-associated pathways, supporting their critical roles in immune regulation and metabolic alterations in osteomyelitis [47]. Furthermore, to validate these bioinformatics findings, we constructed an in vitro osteomyelitis cell model by stimulating mouse BMSCs with S. aureus staphylococcal protein A (SpA). Functionally, SpA treatment suppressed BMSC proliferation and osteogenesis while triggering inflammation. Importantly, we observed upregulation of HSPA8, TXN, and CCT3, along with downregulation of KLF2 and its downstream target GPX4, suggesting their involvement in ferroptosis regulation during S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis.

While ferroptosis has been extensively studied in infection-related diseases such as endometritis [48, 49] and mastitis [50, 51], its role in osteomyelitis remains largely unexplored. Our findings extend the field by identifying novel FRGs implicated in S. aureus-induced bone pathology, thereby distinguishing this work from existing ferroptosis-infection studies that have primarily focused on soft tissues.

Specifically, we provide the first evidence that Heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 8 (HSPA8) is upregulated in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis. As a constitutive member of the HSP70 family, HSPA8 is known to mediate autophagic protein degradation [52, 53] and regulate ferroptosis in bacterial and viral infections [54, 55]. Our data indicate its potential role as a ferroptosis-related biomarker in osteomyelitis. Similarly, thioredoxin (TXN), a key regulator of redox homeostasis [56], has been linked to ferroptosis in cancers [57–59], and neurodegenerative diseases [60, 61]. Notably, inhibition of TXN reductase reduces osteoclast activity and bone loss in osteoporosis models [62], suggesting that TXN may also mitigate S. aureus-induced bone destruction via ferroptosis regulation. Chaperonin-containing TCP1 subunit 3 (CCT3) is a constituent of the TCP1 ring complex (TRiC) family [63] and has been reported as a prognostic biomarker in multiple cancers [64–66]. Our data indicated that its relevance to infectious bone disease, implicating it as a novel ferroptosis regulator in osteomyelitis.

Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) is a zinc finger transcription factor, exerting multifaceted protective effects [67–69]. Previous research has linked KLF2 to osteoclastogenesis [70, 71], osteoblast differentiation and bone regeneration [72, 73], chondrocyte survival [35], and angiogenesis [74], as well as ferroptosis regulation in osteoarthritis [35]. Here, we demonstrate for the first time that KLF2 protects against S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis by upregulating GPX4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis. In vivo, KLF2 overexpression reduced bacterial burden and inflammation, preserved bone integrity, and promoted osteogenesis and angiogenesis, underscoring its therapeutic potential.

Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a key lipid hydroperoxide scavenger with three isoforms, including cytosolic (cGPX4), mitochondrial (mGPX4), and nuclear (nGPX4) [75]. It has been widely studied in cancer and degenerative diseases but not in bacterial bone infections [34, 76]. Additionally, GPX4 plays a master role in maintaining the redox homeostasis by blocking ferroptosis by eliminating phospholipid hydroperoxides [77]. Our study confirms its role as a downstream effector of KLF2, linking ferroptosis regulation directly to S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis for the first time. Together, these findings establish a novel KLF2-GPX4 axis in the context of osteomyelitis, distinguishing this work from prior ferroptosis-infection studies and providing new insights into the interplay between ferroptosis, immunity, and bone remodeling. This mechanistic understanding not only highlights KLF2 as a promising therapeutic target but also lays the foundation for developing ferroptosis-based interventions in osteomyelitis.

In summary, our study comprehensively integrated bioinformatics analysis, bacterial culture, and both in vitro and in vivo experiments to identify and validate ferroptosis-related biomarkers in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis. Among them, KLF2 emerged as a key protective factor, exerting antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-osteoclastogenic, and pro-osteogenic/angiogenic effects through regulation of the GPX4-mediated ferroptosis pathway. Despite these promising findings, several limitations remain. The precise molecular interaction between KLF2 and GPX4 has not been fully clarified, and rescue experiments are required to confirm their regulatory relationship. Future studies should therefore focus on delineating the mechanistic basis of the KLF2–GPX4 axis and its therapeutic potential in S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified ferroptosis-related predictive biomarkers for the classification of osteomyelitis and elucidated the role and underlying molecular mechanisms of KLF2 in a S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis mouse model. Our findings highlight KLF2 as a promising therapeutic target and offer new insights into the early diagnosis and treatment of S. aureus-related bone infections, which may help prevent disease progression and reduce infection-related complications.
