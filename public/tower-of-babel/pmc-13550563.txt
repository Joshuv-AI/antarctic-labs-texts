Identification of heat-tolerant tomato genotypes through integrated phenotypic, biochemical, molecular, and GGE biplot analyses
Authors: Jagesh Kumar Tiwari, Nagendra Rai, Manish K. Singh, Rajat Singh, Tushar Kant, Yagyat Singh, Suresh Reddy Yerasu, Achuit K. Singh, Rajesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High temperature stress is a major constraint affecting tomato productivity and fruit quality worldwide. The present study aimed to identify heat-tolerant tomato genotypes using integrated morphological, biochemical, molecular, and GGE (Genotype plus Genotype-by-Environment) biplot analyses under controlled high temperature stress conditions (40 ± 2 °C, day) compared with optimal conditions (26 ± 2 °C, day). Thirty-five tomato genotypes were evaluated for horticultural, reproductive, biochemical, and molecular traits. Heat stress significantly reduced fruit weight, pollen viability, fruit set, total soluble solids, and yield (g/plant). However, substantial genotypic variation was observed among the evaluated lines. Based on Heat Tolerant Index (HTI), ten genotypes namely VRT-06, VRT-67, Superbug, VRT-494, H-88-78-1, VRT-458, VRT-500, ToDVAR-5, VRT-34, and VRT-613 were identified as highly heat-tolerant. Biochemical analysis demonstrated higher proline accumulation, antioxidant enzyme activities like superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), chlorophyll content, and relative water content along with lower malondialdehyde content and electrolyte leakage in tolerant genotypes. GGE biplot and MGIDI (Multi-Trait Genotype-Ideotype Distance Index) analyses identified VRT-06, VRT-67, Superbug, VRT-494, and H-88-78-1 as stable and superior performers across environments. Gene expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of heat-responsive genes (HSP70, HSP90, and HSFA2) and antioxidant defense genes (CAT and SOD1) in tolerant genotypes, particularly VRT-06 and VRT-67, indicating coordinated molecular defense mechanisms under heat stress. Overall, the study identified elite heat-tolerant tomato genotypes and demonstrated the effectiveness of integrating morphological, biochemical, molecular, and multivariate analyses for thermotolerance screening and breeding of climate-resilient tomato cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-66041-y.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is among the most widely cultivated vegetable crops worldwide, contributing substantially to nutritional security, agro-industry, and farmers’ livelihoods. As a thermosensitive crop of tropical origin, tomato exhibits optimal growth and reproductive performance within a relatively narrow temperature range (21–27 °C)1,2. However, in recent decades, the accelerated pace of global warming has resulted in more frequent, prolonged, and intense heat waves, particularly in tomato-growing regions of Asia, Africa, and southern Europe. High temperature episodes exceeding 35–40 °C have been reported to cause severe physiological disruptions, leading to flower abortion, impaired pollen development, reduced fruit set, and significant yield penalties3,4. Consequently, identifying and developing heat-resilient tomato cultivars has become a critical component of climate-smart agriculture.

Heat stress triggers an intricate array of morphological, physiological, biochemical and molecular changes in tomato plants. Phenotypically, heat-tolerant genotypes typically maintain acceptable levels of pollen viability, fruit set, and yield under high-temperature episodes5–7. These traits are particularly important as reproductive processes are the most heat-sensitive phase in tomato. In contrast, susceptible genotypes exhibit floral drop, malformed fruits, reduced seed set, and premature senescence8. Understanding these phenotypic responses helps establish baseline screening criteria for differentiating tolerant and sensitive genotypes.

At the biochemical level, high temperatures lead to excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing lipid peroxidation, membrane disintegration, protein denaturation, and metabolic dysfunction6,7. To counteract these effects, heat-tolerant genotypes activate a suite of antioxidative defense mechanisms. Osmolytes such as proline play a crucial role in osmoprotection and stabilizing proteins and membranes. Lower levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) reflect restricted lipid peroxidation, indicating better membrane stability under stress. Enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes—superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX)—help mitigate oxidative damage by detoxifying ROS6,9. Additionally, maintenance of total chlorophyll content, relative water content (RWC), and lower electrolyte leakage (EL) are reliable biochemical markers of stress resilience7,10. Integrating these parameters provides a mechanistic understanding of heat-stress adaptation and improves precision in selecting tolerant lines.

At the molecular level, heat tolerance is regulated by a complex transcriptional network involving heat-shock transcription factors and downstream heat-shock proteins, which protect cellular structures by acting as molecular chaperones11. Other stress-responsive genes associated with ROS scavenging, cell wall remodeling, signal transduction, and reproductive development also contribute to thermotolerance11. Differential gene expression profiling and molecular marker-based characterization enable the identification of allelic variants and candidate genes associated with heat tolerance, strengthening the foundation for molecular-assisted breeding12. Such molecular insights are indispensable for understanding genotype-specific tolerance mechanisms and for advancing precision breeding techniques10.

One of the major challenges in heat tolerance breeding is the pronounced genotype × environment (G × E) interaction, which complicates the consistent identification of stable genotypes across diverse environments13. Traditional selection methods often fail to capture the multidimensional nature of this interaction. In recent years, GGE biplot analysis has emerged as a powerful statistical tool for deciphering G and GE components simultaneously13. The GGE biplot facilitates visualization of discriminative and representative testing environments, identifies high-performing and stable genotypes, and allows “which-won-where” interpretation14. Its integration into heat-tolerance studies enhances the reliability of genotype selection by enabling breeders to identify both specifically adapted and broadly stable genotypes across multi-year or multi-season trials.

Considering the complexity of heat-stress responses, there is a critical need for integrated approaches that combine phenotypic and biochemical attributes along with robust multivariate analysis. Such comprehensive evaluations not only deepen our understanding of heat tolerance mechanisms in tomato but also accelerate the identification of elite donor lines for breeding programs targeting high-temperature environments15,16. In this context, the present study aims to assess: i) phenotypic variability among tomato genotypes for key reproductive and yield-related traits under high-temperature conditions, ii) elucidate biochemical and physiological mechanisms contributing to heat tolerance, and iii) identify stable and superior heat-tolerant genotypes using GGE biplot analysis. Overall, this integrated approach is expected to generate valuable insights into the biological basis of heat tolerance and provide a scientific foundation for developing climate-resilient tomato varieties suited to warming agro-ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental conditions

A total of 35 tomato genotypes were used in this study (Supplementary File S1). The experiment was conducted during the summer seasons (February–May) of 2025 and 2026 under controlled environmental conditions at the ICAR–Indian Institute of Vegetable Research (ICAR-IIVR), Varanasi, India (25.18° N latitude, 82.83° E longitude). Two independent polycarbonate-sheet-covered polyhouse facilities were used to establish contrasting temperature regimes. The heat stress environment was maintained in a temperature-controlled polyhouse equipped with electric heaters, where the day time and night time temperatures were regulated at 40 ± 2 °C and 25 ± 2 °C, respectively. The optimal (control) environment was maintained in a separate air-conditioned polyhouse with temperatures ranged from 26 ± 2 °C during the day and 20 ± 2 °C at night. The relative humidity during the full crop cycle (Feb-May) under the heat stress treatment ranged from 25–30% during the day and 65–80% at night, whereas under the optimal environment it ranged from 25–40% during the day and 55–75% at night. Both environments were maintained under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 400–600 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1. Other environmental parameters like soil parameters were not actively controlled.

Seedlings were raised under standard nursery conditions and transplanted into 12-inch diameter earthen pots filled with a potting mixture of soil and well-decomposed farmyard manure (FYM) in a 3:1 (v/v) ratio. Plants were allowed to establish under optimal conditions for 20 days after transplanting (DAT), after which one set of plants was transferred to the heat stress polyhouse, while the other set remained under optimal conditions. Both treatments were maintained until crop maturity and final harvest (120 DAT). The experiment was laid out in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three biological replications. Each replication consisted of five plants per genotype, resulting in 15 plants per genotype under each temperature regime. All recommended agronomic practices, including irrigation, nutrient management, and plant protection measures, were uniformly followed throughout the experimental period.

Phenotypic and biochemical characterization

Morphological data were collected from all five plants per genotype per replication as described earlier17 for traits such as i) fruit weight (g), ii) yield (g/plant), iii) fruit length (cm), iv) fruit width (cm), v) pericarp thickness (cm), vi) total soluble solids (TSS) (°Brix), vii) Pollen viability (%), and viii) Fruit set (%). Fruit weight was calculated from an average of ten fruit weight. Pericarp thickness was measured with a Vernier caliper. TSS was determined using a hand refractometer. Pollen viability (%) was estimated using the acetocarmine staining, whereas fruit set (%) was determined by direct counting and calculated as Fruit set (%) = (Number of fruits set/Total number of flowers) × 100.

Leaf samples were harvested at flowering/fruiting stages for biochemical analyses under heat stress. Biochemical assays were performed following the original methods described by Thimmaiah18. Proline content (μg/g FW) was measured following the original protocols of Bates et al.19. Lipid peroxidation (MDA, malondialdehyde content) (μmol/g FW) was quantified using the Heath and Packer20 method. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (μmol/g FW) levels were determined by the approach of Velikova et al.21. Antioxidant enzyme activities were assessed: superoxide dismutase (SOD) (units/mg protein)22, catalase (CAT) (μmol H2O2 oxidized/min/mg protein)23, and ascorbate peroxidase (APX)24. Electrolyte leakage (EL) (%) was measured following Blum & Ebercon25 to evaluate membrane stability. Pigment analyses included total chlorophyll (mg/g FW)26 and carotenoids (mg/g FW)27. Relative water content (RWC) (%) was determined according to Barrs and Weatherley28.

Heat tolerance index

A modified Heat Tolerance Index (HTI) was developed to quantify the relative performance of tomato genotypes under heat stress compared with normal conditions. The index integrates pollen viability, fruit set, and yield retention, which are recognized as key indicators of thermotolerance in tomato29–31. Genotypes were classified based on the HTI value as i) Highly heat-tolerant (HHT: ≥ 70 HTI with ≥ 70% pollen viability and ≥ 40% fruit set), ii) Moderately heat-tolerant (MHT: 50–69 HTI with 50–69% pollen viability and 25–39% fruit set, and iii) Heat sensitive (HS: < 50 HTI with < 50% pollen viability and < 25% fruit set). Gene expression analysis by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The expression profiles of five genes including three heat-responsive genes- HSP70 (Heat shock protein 70), HSP90 (Heat shock protein 90) and HSFA2 (Heat shock transcription factor A2), and two antioxidant-related genes- CAT (Catalase), and SOD1 (Superoxide dismutase 1)- were analysed by qRT-PCR. The leaf tissues of heat-tolerant tomato genotypes along with the heat-susceptible control Punjab Chhuhara grown under heat stress conditions were used. Total RNA was extracted from fresh leaf tissues using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of the isolated RNA were assessed prior to complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA using the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Reagents Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a qTOWER3 Real-Time PCR System (Analytik Jena GmbH + Co. KG, Jena, Germany) with gene-specific primers targeting heat stress-responsive and antioxidant genes (Supplementary Table S2)32. Each reaction was performed using three biological replicates, with three technical replicates per biological sample. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of 50 °C/2 min, 95 °C/10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C/15 s, 60 °C/1 min, and 72 °C/30 s with melting curve analysis to confirm the specificity of the PCR products. The tomato Actin-2 gene (SlACT2; Solyc11g005330) was used as the endogenous reference gene for normalization of transcript abundance. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (2⁻ΔΔCt) method33. Relative gene expression values (fold change) were normalized against the internal reference gene in treatment versus control (Punjab Chhuhara) under stress conditions.

Statistical analysis

The mean data recorded over two years were used for statistical analyses. Phenotypic, physiological, and biochemical data were subjected to pooled analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the OPSTAT statistical software34. The homogeneity of error variances across years was tested using Bartlett’s Chi-square test, and only traits showing homogeneous variances were included in the combined analysis. The pooled data were subsequently analyzed using a completely randomized design (CRD) model. Mean comparisons among genotypes were performed using the Critical Difference (CD) test (P ≤ 0.05) to determine significant differences among genotypes, treatments, years, and their interactions. To identify genotypes with consistent performance across years, pooled mean values were used to estimate stable and favorable responses under contrasting temperature conditions. Furthermore, data from the optimal and heat stress environments were subjected to Genotype plus Genotype × Environment (GGE) biplot analysis to evaluate genotype performance, stability, and genotype × environment interactions. The Multi-trait Genotype–Ideotype Distance Index (MGIDI) analysis was performed to identify superior genotypes using the default algorithm in the ‘metan’ package in R (version 4.6.1).

Under stress conditions, fruit weight ranged from 21.33 g in VRT-101A to 87.17 g in VRT-06, with a population mean of 45.94 g, whereas under optimal conditions the mean fruit weight increased to 66.27 g. Similarly, fruit length and fruit width showed overall reductions under stress, although some genotypes maintained comparatively stable fruit morphology. Genotypes such as VRT-458, VRT-494, ToDVAR-5, and VRT-500 exhibited comparatively higher fruit size and pericarp thickness under heat stress, suggesting better reproductive stability and sink strength under elevated temperature conditions. Total soluble solids (TSS) also declined under heat stress, with mean values decreasing from 5.56°Brix under optimal conditions to 3.92°Brix under stress. Nevertheless, certain genotypes including VRT-06, VRT-500, VRT-494, and ToLCV-32 maintained relatively higher TSS values under stress, indicating their ability to preserve fruit quality traits under adverse thermal environments (Table 1).

High temperature stress severely affected reproductive traits, particularly pollen viability and fruit set percentage. Mean pollen viability declined from 84.93% under optimal conditions to 54.49% under stress, while mean fruit set decreased from 43.62% to 34.03%. Among the evaluated genotypes, VRT-06 (82.35%), VRT-458 (83.08%), VRT-494 (81.10%), VRT-500 (79.17%), H-88–78-1 (77.93%), ToDVAR-5 (74.50%), and VRT-613 (74.67%) recorded comparatively higher pollen viability under stress conditions. Likewise, higher fruit set percentages under stress were observed in VRT-67 (64.42%), VRT-613 (63.58%), VRT-494 (62.67%), ToDVAR-5 (63.17%), and VRT-06 (62.43%), demonstrating superior reproductive resilience under elevated temperature conditions. Yield per plant was drastically reduced under stress conditions, with the population mean decreasing from 1482.83 g under optimal conditions to 318.77 g under heat stress. Despite this reduction, several genotypes maintained relatively higher yield under stress, including VRT-06 (846.19 g), VRT-67 (596.33 g), Superbug (583.42 g), VRT-34 (551.84 g), and VRT-494 (548.25 g), indicating their superior adaptation to high temperature stress (Table 2).

Discussion

High temperature stress is among the most severe abiotic constraints limiting tomato productivity worldwide and is expected to intensify under future climate change scenarios. Temperatures exceeding the optimum range (25–30 °C) during the reproductive stage adversely affect pollen development, fertilization, fruit set, assimilate partitioning and ultimately marketable yield31,32. In the present study, substantial phenotypic variation was observed among the 35 tomato genotypes under contrasting temperature regimes, demonstrating the existence of considerable genetic variability for heat tolerance. The consistent performance of elite genotypes (VRT-06, VRT-67, Superbug, VRT-494, H-88–78-1, VRT-458, VRT-500, ToDVAR-5, VRT-34 and VRT-613) under heat stress indicates that these genotypes possess effective heat tolerance mechanism. Similar levels of genetic variation for thermotolerance have been reported in tomato germplasm32,35,36, highlighting the potential of exploiting natural genetic diversity for breeding climate-resilient cultivars.

Heat stress markedly reduced fruit weight and yield in susceptible genotypes, reflecting the detrimental effects of elevated temperature on photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism and assimilate translocation towards developing fruits3. High temperatures reduce Rubisco activity, accelerate photorespiration and impair source–sink relationships, resulting in poor fruit development37. Nevertheless, highly heat-tolerant genotypes such as VRT-06, VRT-67, Superbug, VRT-494, H-88-78-1 and VRT-458 maintained significantly higher fruit weight and yield under stress, suggesting greater photosynthetic resilience and more efficient assimilate partitioning7. Maintenance of sink strength during reproductive development has been recognized as a major determinant of yield stability under heat stress38. The superior performance of these genotypes therefore indicates enhanced physiological capacity to sustain carbon assimilation and reproductive growth under adverse environmental conditions.

Among the reproductive traits evaluated, pollen viability and fruit set emerged as the most reliable indicators of thermotolerance29,30. Tomato pollen development is particularly sensitive to elevated temperatures because meiosis, tapetal development and pollen maturation are highly susceptible to heat-induced oxidative damage31. The tolerant genotypes maintained significantly higher pollen viability and fruit set compared with the susceptible genotype, resulting in superior yield under heat stress. Furthermore, the strong positive association observed among pollen viability, fruit set and yield demonstrates that preservation of male reproductive function is fundamental to heat tolerance. Similar conclusions have been reported by many researchers who identified pollen fertility as one of the strongest predictors of fruit yield under elevated temperatures29–31,39,40. These findings suggest the utility of pollen viability and fruit set as rapid and reliable screening criteria in tomato breeding programmes targeting heat tolerance.

The HTI effectively classified genotypes into highly heat-tolerant, moderately heat-tolerant and heat-sensitive categories by integrating reproductive and productivity traits under stress. The HTI genotypes exhibited superior pollen viability, fruit set and yield. Unlike conventional indices that rely solely on yield performance29, the HTI method employed in this study integrates reproductive and yield traits, thereby providing a more biologically meaningful assessment of thermotolerance. Similar integrated stress indices have been successfully used for identifying stable tomato genotypes under high-temperature environments41,42. Hence, this study demonstrate HTI method for screening of tomato genotypes for thermotolerance.

Physiological and biochemical analyses further elucidated the mechanisms underlying heat tolerance39,41. Heat stress induces excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative damage, membrane destabilization and impaired cellular metabolism39. The heat-tolerant genotypes accumulated significantly higher concentrations of proline than the susceptible control (Punjab Chhuhara). Proline serves multiple protective functions by acting as an osmolyte, molecular chaperone, ROS scavenger and stabilizer of proteins and cellular membranes43. Concurrently, lower malondialdehyde (MDA) content and reduced electrolyte leakage observed in tolerant genotypes indicated diminished lipid peroxidation and superior membrane thermostability43. Similar responses have been documented in heat-tolerant tomato genotypes by Rajametov et al.44 and Osei-Bonsu et al.45, confirming that maintenance of membrane integrity is a critical parameters of thermotolerance.

Enhanced antioxidant defence also contributed significantly to heat tolerance11. The higher antioxidant activity observed in tolerant genotypes suggests more efficient detoxification of ROS generated under heat stress. Antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) constitute the primary enzymatic defence system by converting superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide and subsequently detoxifying hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen10,11. Efficient ROS scavenging minimizes oxidative damage, preserves chloroplast integrity and maintains metabolic homeostasis under stress. Consequently, tolerant genotypes observed in our study sustained greater reproductive success and yield under elevated temperatures. These observations agree with previous reports describing enhanced antioxidant capacity as a hallmark of thermotolerant tomatoes9,44.

The integration of multivariate analyses substantially improved genotype discrimination46. GGE biplot analysis revealed that VRT-06, Superbug and VRT-67 combined high mean performance with remarkable stability across optimal and heat-stress environments. The "which-won-where" pattern further confirmed VRT-06 and VRT-67 as winning genotypes for the target environments, indicating broad adaptation and stable performance. Complementarily, MGIDI analysis simultaneously integrated multiple agronomic, reproductive and physiological traits into a single selection index, identifying VRT-06, Superbug, VRT-494, VRT-67 and H-88-78-1 as the most desirable genotypes. The combined application of GGE biplot and MGIDI provides a powerful framework for multi-trait selection because it simultaneously considers genotype performance, stability and ideotype proximity, thereby enhancing selection efficiency in tomato breeding programmes14. Thus, our study identifies superior and stable performing genotypes for high temperature tolerance.

At the molecular level, highly tolerant genotypes exhibited coordinated activation of heat-responsive and antioxidant genes, providing genetic evidence for the observed phenotypic differences9. The up-regulation of genes (e.g. HSP70 and HSP90) indicates activation of molecular chaperone systems that stabilize proteins, prevent aggregation of denatured proteins and facilitate refolding during thermal stress43. Likewise, elevated expression of HSFA2, a master regulator of acquired thermotolerance, suggests activation of downstream heat-responsive regulatory networks that sustain prolonged stress adaptation10. Simultaneously, increased expression of CAT and SOD1 reflects enhanced antioxidant defence capable of mitigating ROS accumulation and protecting cellular components from oxidative injury. Similar coordinated transcriptional responses have been documented by Karkute et al.9 and Fernández-Crespo et al.32. The coordinated induction of heat shock proteins and antioxidant enzymes in elite genotypes such as VRT-06, VRT-67 and VRT-494 demonstrates that thermotolerance in tomato is governed by an integrated molecular network involving protein protection, ROS detoxification and maintenance of cellular homeostasis.

Overall, the integration of morphological, reproductive, physiological, biochemical and molecular analyses provides a comprehensive understanding of heat tolerance mechanisms in tomato. The superior performance of VRT-06, VRT-67, Superbug, VRT-494 and H-88–78-1 across multiple evaluation criteria demonstrates their considerable potential as donor parents for developing heat-resilient tomato cultivars. These genotypes also represent valuable genetic resources for dissecting the molecular basis of thermotolerance and for deployment in marker-assisted and genomics-assisted breeding programmes47 aimed at sustaining tomato productivity under future climate change scenarios48.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated considerable genetic variability among tomato genotypes for morphological, reproductive, biochemical, and molecular traits under high temperature stress conditions. Heat stress significantly reduced fruit yield, pollen viability, fruit set, and overall physiological performance. Based on HTI Multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index, GGE biplot, MGIDI analysis, and biochemical and molecular characterization, the genotypes VRT-06, VRT-67, Superbug, VRT-494, H-88-78-1, VRT-458, VRT-500, ToDVAR-5, VRT-34, and VRT-613 were identified as highly heat-tolerant. The tolerant genotypes maintained higher pollen viability, fruit set, yield stability, chlorophyll content, relative water content, and antioxidant enzyme activities, along with lower lipid peroxidation and electrolyte leakage under stress conditions. Furthermore, enhanced expression of heat-responsive genes (HSP70, HSP90, and HSFA2) and antioxidant defense genes (CAT and SOD) indicated the presence of coordinated molecular defense mechanisms contributing to thermotolerance. The strong positive association among pollen viability, fruit set, and yield further highlighted the importance of reproductive traits as reliable selection criteria for heat tolerance breeding. The integration of morphological, biochemical, molecular, GGE biplot, and MGIDI analyses provided a robust and comprehensive framework for identifying stable heat-tolerant tomato genotypes. Among the evaluated lines, VRT-06 emerged as the most promising genotype due to its superior yield performance, reproductive stability, biochemical resilience, and enhanced expression of stress-responsive genes under high temperature stress. The elite genotypes identified in this study can serve as valuable genetic resources for developing heat-tolerant tomato cultivars and may be effectively utilized in the future breeding programs.
