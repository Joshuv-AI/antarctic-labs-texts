On the Fourier dimension of fractional Brownian graphs
Cheuk Yin Lee and Samy Tindel

Abstract
. In this note we prove that the Fourier dimension of the graph G ⁡ ( B ) G(B) of a fractional Brownian motion B B with Hurst parameter H ∈ ( 0 , 1 / 2 ) H\in(0,1/2) is equal to 1. This finishes to solve a conjecture by Fraser and Sahlsten [ 6 ] . It also yields an exact formula for the gap dim H ( G ⁡ ( B ) ) − dim F ( G ⁡ ( B ) ) \dim_{\rm H}(G(B))-\dim_{\rm F}(G(B)) between the Hausdorff dimension and the Fourier dimension of G ⁡ ( B ) G(B) . The proof is based on an intricate combinatorics procedure for multiple integrals related to the covariance function of the fractional Brownian motion.



1.
Introduction

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Borel set and
\mathcal{P}(A)
denote the set of all Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu(A)=1
.
For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A)
, the Fourier transform of
\mu
is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot x}d\mu(x),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The Fourier dimension of
A
is then defined by

(1)

\dim_{\rm F}A=\sup\left\{\beta\in[0,n]:\exists\,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A),|\hat{\mu}(\xi)|\lesssim|\xi|^{-\beta/2}\right\},

where
f(x)\lesssim g(x)
means that there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that
f(x)\leq Cg(x)
for all
x
.
The relevance of this definition is ensured by a number of geometric properties. Among those, let us mention the following:

(i)

The Fourier dimension provides a lower bound of the Hausdorff dimension. Specifically, for a measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(2)

\dim_{\rm F}A\leq\dim_{\rm H}A.

(ii)

For manifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-vanishing curvature, inequality (
2
) becomes in fact an equality.

(iii)

The notion of Fourier dimension is especially relevant for stochastic processes, since they can be easily related to local time properties.

The concept of Fourier dimension also encodes a number of challenging arithmetic properties. We refer to
[
9
]
for more details about these aspects of the theory.

With those motivations in mind, observe that one of the key questions for a random set
A
is to know if its Hausdorff dimension coincides with its Fourier dimension. A set with such a property is called a
Salem set
, a name coined by Kahane in
[
8
]
. Whenever
A
is not a Salem set, a natural question is then to quantify the difference

\dim_{\rm H}(A)-\dim_{\rm F}(A)
.
There has been a recent burst of studies carrying out this type of program for the most common (mostly Gaussian) processes. Among the classes of sets which have been considered, let us mention the following:

(1)

Images of compact sets
A\subset[0,1]
by a process
X:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
with parameter
H\in(0,1)
, one gets that
B^{H}(A)
is a Salem set with

\dim_{\rm F}\big(B^{H}(A)\big)=\min\left\{1,H^{-1}\dim_{H}(A)\right\}.

This result was established in
[
7
]
, with further generalizations to Gaussian fields in
[
11
]
.

(2)

The level sets of a fractional Brownian motion were considered in
[
10
]
. Specifically, it is proved therein that the zero set of a fractional Brownian motion is almost surely a Salem set.

(3)

The graph of a function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

(3)

G(f)=\left\{(t,f_{t});\,t\in[0,1]\right\}.

For most natural stochastic processes, this type of set is not Salem. Indeed, a general result in
[
5
]
asserts that for any continuous function
f
one has

(4)

\dim_{\rm F}G(f)\leq 1\,,

while for a fractional Brownian motion, the article
[
1
]
states that
\dim_{\rm H}G(B)=2-H>1
.

Due to the phenomenon mentioned above, graphs of stochastic processes have attracted a lot of attention in recent years. More specifically, in order to quantify the difference
\dim_{\rm H}(G(B))-\dim_{\rm F}(G(B))
for a generic process
B
and recalling relation (
3
), one wishes to prove that
\dim_{\rm F}(G(B))=1
for most cases of interest. Relevant results in this direction include
Fraser and Sahlsten
[
6
]
, who proved that the Fourier dimension of the graph of the standard Brownian motion is almost surely equal to 1, and conjectured that the Fourier dimension of the graph of fractional Brownian motion is also equal to 1. Then
Lai and Lee
[
9
]
confirmed part of this conjecture by proving that the Fourier dimension of the graph of fractional Brownian motion with
H\in[1/2,1)
is equal to 1.
Whether the same holds for
H\in(0,1/2)
remained an open problem; see
[
9
, Open Problem 1.3]
.
The main result of this paper fully confirms this conjecture:

Theorem 1.1
.

The Fourier dimension of the graph of a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
is almost surely 1.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on an intricate integration by parts procedure involving combinatorics tools.
The strategy employed in our proof is detailed in Section
2.1
.
However, one can already highlight a few key points at this stage:

(i)

A proper study of the Fourier dimension of the graph measure can be reduced to an evaluation of the moments of the following quantity (see (
9
) for more context):

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(\xi_{1}t+\xi_{2}B_{t})}dt\quad\text{for $\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$.}

The goal is to obtain a precise rate of decay for the moments. In our case we will discover (see (
81
) below) that

\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|^{2q}]\lesssim|\xi_{1}|^{-q}
.

(ii)

Some scaling properties and moment computations for the fractional Brownian motion
B
reduce our estimates to the following problem: consider
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{2q}
,
T>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
We wish to extract powers of
T
and
\lambda
from an integral
\mathcal{I}^{\lambda}_{T}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]
over the simplex
\Delta_{T}=\{0<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{2q}<T\}
:

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\lambda}_{T}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]=\int_{\Delta_{T}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda\langle\varepsilon,u\rangle}e^{-\pi\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^{2q}\varepsilon_{i}B_{u_{i}})}du.

(iii)

Extracting powers from (
6
) will be our main endeavor. It will be achieved in the lengthy and technical Lemma
3.1
.
Roughly speaking, one starts from (
6
) and performs integration by parts in order to extract negative powers of
\lambda
. Then one has to carefully choose the integrations by parts in order to sort out two problems: (a) The combinatorics related to the variables
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
; and (b) The singularities created by the differentiation of
u\mapsto\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^{2q}\varepsilon_{i}B_{u_{i}})
. Those singularities are particularly challenging in our current case of study
H\in(0,1/2)
.

The remainder of the paper details the strategy laid out above, with a particular emphasis on integration by parts.

2.
Preliminary results

This section is devoted to recalling some basic facts about Fourier dimension computations. They are mostly borrowed from
[
9
]
and serve as a basis for our computations in the rough case of a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H<1/2
.

2.1.
Reduction to variance estimates

The processes considered in this paper are generically denoted by
B=\{B_{t}\,,t\geq 0\}
.
Those will be fractional Brownian motions with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1/2)
.
Let us recall that
B
is thus a centered Gaussian process defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
, with covariance function

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{s}B_{t}]=\frac{1}{2}(|s|^{2H}+|t|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H})\,,\qquad\text{for $s,t\geq 0$.}

For this process we consider the graph measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{G}(A)=\int_{0}^{1}{\bf 1}_{\{t\in[0,1]:(t,B_{t})\in A\}}dt\,,\qquad\text{for every Borel set $A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}$.}

Note that one can also write

(8)

\displaystyle\mu_{G}(A)=\iint_{A}{\bf 1}_{[0,1]}(t)\,\delta_{x}(B_{t})\,dx\,dt,

so that formally
\mu_{G}
can be recast as

\displaystyle\mu_{G}(dt,dx)={\bf 1}_{[0,1]}(t)\,\delta_{x}(B_{t})\,dt\,dx.

With this relation in hand, introduce the Fourier transform of
\mu_{G}
as

(9)

\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i(\xi_{1}t+\xi_{2}x)}\mu_{G}(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(\xi_{1}t+\xi_{2}B_{t})}dt.

In the sequel we will also consider the image measure of the process
B
.
We recall its definition below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
B
be a fractional Brownian motion, with covariance function given by (
7
).
Recall that the graph measure
\mu_{G}
of
B
is given by (
8
).
Then the image measure of
B
is defined on Borel sets
F\subset\mathbb{R}
by

(10)

\displaystyle\nu(F)=\mu_{G}([0,1]\times F)=\int_{0}^{1}{\bf 1}_{\{B_{t}\in F\}}\,dt.

Its Fourier transform
\hat{\nu}
is given for
\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}(\xi_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i\xi_{2}B_{t}}dt.

A standard way of finding Fourier dimension of the image of a stochastic process is through computing moments of the image measure
\nu
.
This method was first used by Kahane
[
7
]
.
General statements for this approach can be found in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will use the following specific version in Lai and Lee
[
9
]
that is adapted to our current setting for getting lower bounds on the Fourier dimensions of graphs.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
9
, Proposition 2.3]
.
For a generic process
B
, recall that the graph and image measures
\mu_{G}
and
\nu
are respectively defined by (
8
) and (
10
).
Suppose for every
q\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
C>0
such that

(i)

(vertical bound)
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\nu}(\xi_{2})|^{2q}]\leq C|\xi_{2}|^{-\gamma_{2}q}
for all
\xi_{2}\neq 0
; and

(ii)

(horizontal bound)
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|^{2q}]\leq C|\xi_{1}|^{-\gamma_{1}q}
for all
\xi_{1}\neq 0
and
\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Then
\dim_{\rm F}G(B)\geq\min\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}
a.s.

Within the landmark of Proposition
2.2
, we thus wish to establish vertical and horizontal bounds for the fractional Brownian motion.
A first remark in this direction is that the vertical bounds are already known (see (
80
) below).
Therefore, we will concentrate our efforts on the horizontal bounds in Proposition
2.2
.
To this end, we will follow the combinatorial framework in Lai and Lee
[
9
]
.
Let us first recall some notation and tools introduced in
[
9
]
, starting from the definition of simplex:
for
q\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
T>0
, we consider the simplex
\Delta_{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2q}
as

(11)

\Delta_{T}=\{u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{2q})\in\mathbb{R}^{2q}:0<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{2q}<T\}.

We will also use sets of Rademacher type variables given as follows:

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2q}=\Big\{\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{2q})\in\{-1,1\}^{2q}:\sum_{j=1}^{2q}\varepsilon_{j}=0\Big\}.

Then for any
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{A}_{2q}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and any integrable function
G
on
\Delta_{T}
, define

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\varepsilon,G]=\mathcal{I}^{\lambda}_{T}[\varepsilon,G]:=\int_{\Delta_{T}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda\langle\varepsilon,u\rangle}G(u)du.

The following formula for the even moments of
\hat{\mu}_{G}
has been obtained in
[
9
]
using Kahane’s decomposition and the scaling property of fractional Brownian motion.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
9
, Lemma 5.1]
.
Let
B
be a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
, and recall that the Fourier transform of
\mu_{G}
is given by (
9
). Then for any
q\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|^{2q}\right]=\frac{(q!)^{2}}{|\xi_{2}|^{2q/H}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{A}_{2q}}\mathcal{I}_{T}^{\lambda}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}],

where the parameters
\lambda,T
and the function
G_{\varepsilon}
are chosen as

(15)

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\xi_{1}}{|\xi_{2}|^{1/H}},\qquad T=|\xi_{2}|^{1/H},\quad\text{ and }\quad G_{\varepsilon}(u)=e^{-\pi\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^{2q}\varepsilon_{i}B_{u_{i}})}.

With formula (
14
) in hand, and keeping in mind that we wish to get the horizontal bound

\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|^{2q}\right]\leq\frac{C_{q}}{|\xi_{1}|^{q\gamma_{1}}}

with
\gamma_{1}=1
, it is readily checked that it is sufficient to bound the integrals
\mathcal{I}_{T}^{\lambda}[\varepsilon,G]
in (
13
) as follows:

(16)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{T}^{\lambda}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]|\leq\frac{C_{q}}{|\lambda|^{q}}T^{q}

where we recall from (
15
) that
\lambda=\xi_{1}/|\xi_{2}|^{1/H}
and
T=|\xi_{2}|^{1/H}
.
As mentioned in the introduction, the bound (
16
) will be achieved by combining Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
3.1
below,
through an intricate integration by parts and combinatorial procedure.
In particular, it should be clear from (
16
) that we need
q
integrations by parts in
\mathcal{I}_{T}^{\lambda}[\varepsilon,G]
if we wish to achieve the bound
|\lambda|^{-q}T^{q}
.
Calibrating those
q
integrations by parts in the
2q
-dimensional simplex
\Delta_{T}
is of fundamental importance.

Let us set up the stage for our combinatorial formula for the integral
\mathcal{I}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]
in (
14
) which has been obtained in
[
9
]
via integration by parts.
Note that whenever integration by parts is performed on a simplex like
\Delta_{T}
, one has to take care of several boundary terms. In our case, three terms are produced for each integration by parts.
Two of those terms involve evaluation of a variable at a boundary value and hence that variable is removed.
In order to describe the formula, we need to extend the definition of
\mathcal{I}[\varepsilon,G]
in (
13
) to the case that
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{2q})\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\ast\})^{2q}
, where
\ast
indicates the removal of an entry of
\varepsilon
. Below we give a definition of a related operation on Rademacher type variables.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\ast\})^{2q}
with
q\geq 1
, and let
j\in\{1,\dots,2q\}
.
Suppose that
\varepsilon_{j-1},\varepsilon_{j+1}\neq\ast
if
j\not\in\{1,2q\}
;

\varepsilon_{2}\neq\ast
if
j=1
;
\varepsilon_{2q-1}\neq\ast
if
j=2q
.
The operation
\Phi_{j}^{-}
on
\varepsilon
is defined by

\Phi_{j}^{-}(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{j-1}+\varepsilon_{j},\underbrace{\ast}_{\text{$j$-th position}},\varepsilon_{j+1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{2q})&\text{if $j>1$,}\\
(\ast,\varepsilon_{2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{2q})&\text{if $j=1$.}\end{cases}

In the same way, the operation
\Phi_{j}^{+}
is defined by

\Phi_{j}^{+}(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{j-1},\underbrace{\ast}_{\text{$j$-th position}},\varepsilon_{j+1}+\varepsilon_{j},\cdots,\varepsilon_{2q})&\text{if $j<2q$,}\\
(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{j-1},\ast)&\text{if $j=2q$.}\end{cases}

Observe that the operations
\Phi_{j}^{-}
and
\Phi_{j}^{+}
also define an action on a function
G:\mathbb{R}^{2q}\to\mathbb{R}^{2q}
. Namely we set

(17)

\Phi_{j}^{-}(G)(u_{1},\cdots,u_{2q})=\begin{cases}G(u_{1},\cdots,u_{j-1},\underbrace{u_{j-1}}_{\text{$j$-th position}},u_{j+1},\cdots,u_{2q})&\text{if $j>1$,}\\
G(0,u_{2},\cdots,u_{2q})&\text{if $j=1$,}\end{cases}

and the action
\Phi_{j}^{+}
is defined by

(18)

\Phi_{j}^{+}(G)(u_{1},\cdots,u_{2q})=\begin{cases}G(u_{1},\cdots,u_{j-1},\underbrace{u_{j+1}}_{\text{$j$-th position}},u_{j+1},\cdots,u_{2q})&\text{if $j<2q$,}\\
G(u_{1},\cdots,u_{2q-1},T)&\text{if $j=2q$.}\end{cases}

Before stating our main formula for integrations by parts on simplexes, we label some more notation for indices, domains and integrals:

(i)

Our integration by parts will involve differentiations or evaluations on functions.
In order to perform integration by parts multiple times in a systematic way and to obtain a tractable formula, we will only integrate by parts with respect to the odd-indexed variables
u_{1},u_{3},\dots,u_{2q-1}
.
For this reason, we consider the following set of operations:

(19)

\Sigma(q)=\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{3}\times\cdots\times\Sigma_{2q-1},\quad\text{where}\quad\Sigma_{j}=\{\Phi_{j}^{-},\Phi_{j}^{+},\partial_{u_{j}}\}.

(ii)

For each
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3},\dots,\sigma_{2q-1})\in\Sigma(q)
,
we may partition the indices of
\sigma
as follows:

\displaystyle\{1,3,\dots,2q-1\}=J_{1}(\sigma)\cup J_{2}(\sigma)\cup J_{3}(\sigma),

where the sets
J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}
are subsets of
\{1,3,\dots,2q-1\}
defined by

J_{1}(\sigma)=\{j:\sigma_{j}=\Phi_{j}^{-}\},\quad J_{2}(\sigma)=\{j:\sigma_{j}=\Phi_{j}^{+}\},\quad J_{3}(\sigma)=\{j:\sigma_{j}=\partial_{u_{j}}\}.

Let us also write

(20)

\displaystyle J_{1}(\sigma)\cup J_{2}(\sigma)=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}.

Note that for
j\in J_{1}(\sigma)\cup J_{2}(\sigma)
, the action
\sigma_{j}
is introduced in Definition
2.4
. One can then compose the actions and form
\alpha:=\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{2q}
. We get

(21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha\in\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3,\ast\}^{2q}\quad\text{and}\quad\{j:\alpha_{j}=\ast\}=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}.\end{split}

Note that all non-
\ast
entries of
\alpha
do not exceed
\pm 3
because we have chosen to apply operations
\sigma_{j}\in\Sigma_{j}
only to the odd-indexed entries
j=1,3,\dots,2q-1
.
For example, when
q=2
, the maximal value
\pm 3
may be achieved for
\alpha=\Phi_{3}^{-}\Phi_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon)=(\ast,\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3},\ast,\varepsilon_{4})
.

(iii)

Recall that the simplex
\Delta_{T}
is defined by (
11
).
For
\alpha
given as in (
21
), we let
\Delta_{\alpha,T}
denote the simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{2q-m}
with the coordinates
u_{k_{1}},\dots,u_{k_{m}}
removed, i.e.,

(22)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha,T}=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k_{1}-1},u_{k_{1}+1},\dots,u_{k_{m}-1},u_{k_{m}+1},\dots,u_{2q}):0<u_{1}<\cdots<u_{2q}<T\}.

(iv)

For
\alpha\in(\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\ast\})^{2q}
, the definition of the multiple integral
\mathcal{I}[\varepsilon,G]
in (
13
) can be extended to the partial simplex
\Delta_{\alpha,T}
by setting

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\alpha,G]=\mathcal{I}^{\lambda}_{T}[\alpha,G]:=\int_{\Delta_{\alpha,T}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda\langle\alpha,u\rangle}G(u)du,

which is a
2q-m
iterated integral over
\Delta_{\alpha,T}
and
\langle\alpha,u\rangle
is identified as an inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{2q-m}
with
\ast
positions removed.

.

and

,

and

\sigma_{1}

’s.

We now recap the combinatorial integration by parts formula obtained in

]
through integrations by parts with respect to the variables

.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
9
, Proposition 3.4]

For any
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{2q}
, let
G_{\varepsilon}
be as defined in (
15
) and
\mathcal{I}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]
as introduced in (
13
). Also recall that
\Sigma(q)
is the set given by (
19
).
Then

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i\lambda}\right)^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{q}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2j-1}}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(q)}\mathcal{I}[\sigma;\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}].

Here,
\mathcal{I}[\sigma;\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]
is defined by

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\sigma;\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]:=(-1)^{\#J_{2}(\sigma)}\mathcal{I}[\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon)\,,\,\sigma_{2q-1}\cdots\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}(G_{\varepsilon})],

where the indices
k_{1},\dots,k_{m}
are given by (
20
) above,
\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon)
is defined through the actions of
\sigma_{j}\in\Sigma_{j}
on
\varepsilon
introduced in Definition
2.4
,
\sigma_{2q-1}\cdots\sigma_{1}(G_{\varepsilon})
is defined through the actions of
\sigma_{j}
on functions given by (
17
) and (
18
),
and
\mathcal{I}[\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon)\,,\,\sigma_{2q-1}\cdots\sigma_{1}(G_{\varepsilon})]
is defined according to (
23
) above.

In order to estimate (
24
), we need to recall more notation and properties of
\mathcal{I}[\sigma;\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]
defined in (
25
) for any
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{A}_{2q}
and
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3},\dots,\sigma_{2q-1})\in\Sigma(q)
.
Label and order the indices
k_{1}<\cdots<k_{m}
and
k^{\prime}_{1}<\cdots<k^{\prime}_{\ell}
such that

(26)

\displaystyle J_{1}(\sigma)\cup J_{2}(\sigma)=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{3}(\sigma)=\{k^{\prime}_{1},\dots,k^{\prime}_{\ell}\},

where we remark that

(27)

\displaystyle m+\ell=q.

Then
\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon)
has
m
entries that are equal to
\ast
and (
25
) can be written as

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\sigma;\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]=(-1)^{\#J_{2}(\sigma)}\int_{\Delta_{\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon),T}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda\langle\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon),u\rangle}\sigma_{2q-1}\cdots\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}G_{\varepsilon}(u)du,

where the integral is a
2q-m
iterated integral
and
\Delta_{\sigma_{k_{m}}\cdots\sigma_{k_{1}}(\varepsilon),T}
is defined by (
22
).
We are now ready to give a simpler expression for the right hand side of (
25
).

Lemma 2.7
.

[
9
, Lemma 6.3]

For
q\geq 1
, let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{2q}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma(q)
as defined in (

s_{I}
first, then
s_{I-1}
, and so on. Furthermore, for a
fixed
P\in\mathscr{P}_{2}=\mathscr{P}_{2}\{p_{1},\dots,p_{\ell}\}
, we define the parameters

\displaystyle r=r(P):=\#\{B\in P:\#B=2\},

\displaystyle n=n(P):=\#\{B\in P:\#B=1\}=\ell-2r.

With this notation in hand, the integrals in (
51
) can be estimated thanks to the following facts:

\bullet

If
s_{j}=s_{p_{i}}
with
\{p_{i}\}\in P
, then the integral can be bounded by a factor of
(2H)^{-1}T^{2H}
when integrating
s_{p_{i}}
(or
s_{p_{i}+1}
respectively) if
\theta_{i}=-1
(if
\theta_{i}=1
respectively), and there are
n
such factors.

\bullet

If
s_{j}=s_{p}
with
B=\{p^{\prime},p\}\in P
for some
p^{\prime}<p
, then the singularity
(s_{p}-s_{p^{\prime}})^{2H-2}
seems to be a non-trivial obstacle to integrability when
H\in(0,1/2)
. However, recall that for such a
B
we have seen that

p^{\prime}\leq p-2
and
s_{p^{\prime}+1}
is not a differentiation variable (by property
(ii)
above). This means that the diagonal is avoided here, and we have

(52)

\displaystyle\int_{s_{p-1}}^{T}(s_{p}-s_{p^{\prime}})^{2H-2}ds_{p}\leq\frac{1}{1-2H}(s_{p-1}-s_{p^{\prime}})^{2H-1}\leq\frac{1}{1-2H}(s_{p^{\prime}+1}-s_{p^{\prime}})^{2H-1}.

Furthermore later, when it comes to integrating
s_{p^{\prime}+1}
, the factor
(s_{p^{\prime}+1}-s_{p^{\prime}})^{2H-1}
from (
52
) will be the only factor that involves
s_{p^{\prime}+1}
, which is not a differentiation variable, so a simple factor of
(2H)^{-1}(1-2H)^{-1}T^{2H}
is produced. There are
r
such factors.

\bullet

If
s_{j}
is none of the above cases,
then a factor of
T
is produced, and there are
I-\ell
such factors.

With those considerations in mind, plugging the above estimates into (
51
), for
0\leq T\leq 1
we have obtained

(53)

\displaystyle U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)\leq C^{n+r}T^{I-\ell}T^{2H(n+r)}\leq C^{q}T^{I-\ell}\,,

for some
C<\infty
that depends only on
H
. Otherwise stated, we have proved (
48
) for
0\leq T\leq 1
.

Step 4: Large
T
estimate, initiating the recursion
.
It remains to show that (
48
) holds for
T\geq 1
.
We estimate
U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)
by first integrating the variable
s_{I}
, taking now the exponential factor into account.
To this aim, recall the type
\tau(s_{i})
of variables defined in property
(i)
, and also recall from property
(ii)
that
\tau(s_{I})=0
.
Next from (
42
), we see that
I\in J^{*}
since

. Hence summarizing our considerations on the
s_{I}
variable, we are left with the following two cases:

(a)

If
\tau(s_{I-1})=\partial
,
\{I-1\}\in P
, and
\theta_{I-1}=1
, then the variable
s_{I}
appears in one differentiation in (

).
Therefore we use the bound

(54)

\int_{s_{I-1}}^{T}(s_{I}-s_{I-1})^{2H-1}e^{-K^{q}(s_{I}-s_{I-1})^{2H}}ds_{I}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}s_{I}^{2H-1}e^{-K^{q}s_{I}^{2H}}ds_{I}\leq C^{q};

(b)

In all other situations, the variable
s_{I}
is not differentiated. Hence we simply use

(55)

\int_{s_{I-1}}^{T}e^{-K^{q}(s_{I}-s_{I-1})^{2H}}ds_{I}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-K^{q}s_{I}^{2H}}ds_{I}\leq C^{q}.

Putting together (
54
) and (
55
),
it follows that the computation of
U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)
is reduced to an integration on an
(I-1)
-dimensional simplex:

(56)

\displaystyle U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)\leq C^{q}U(I_{0},P_{0},J_{0}^{*},\theta^{(0)}),

where
C<\infty
is a constant depending only on
H
and the new parameters
I_{0},P_{0},J_{0}^{*},\theta^{(0)}
are defined as

(57)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&I_{0}=I-1,\quad P_{0}=\{B\in P:B\subset\{1,\dots,I-1\}\},\quad J^{*}_{0}=\{j\in J^{*}:j\leq I-1\},\\
&\text{and}\quad\theta^{(0)}=\begin{cases}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{I-2},0)&\text{if $\theta_{I-1}=1$,}\\
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{I-2},\theta_{I-1})&\text{if $\theta_{I-1}\neq 1$.}\end{cases}\end{split}

Note that the last entry of
\theta^{(0)}
is set to be 0 if
\theta_{I-1}=1
because the factor
(s_{I}-s_{I-1})^{2H-1}
is removed after the integration in (
54
). In particular,
\theta^{(0)}_{I-1}
is either
-1
or
0
.

Next, we continue with the estimate iteratively by integrating two variables at a time and updating
(I_{0},P_{0},J_{0}^{*},\theta^{(0)})
after each iteration. Define
\nu
, the number of iterations, by

(58)

\displaystyle\nu=\begin{cases}I_{0}/2&\text{if $I_{0}$ is even;}\\
(I_{0}-1)/2&\text{if $I_{0}$ is odd.}\end{cases}

For any
k\in\{1,\dots,\nu-1\}
, given
(I_{k-1},P_{k-1},J_{k-1}^{*},\theta^{(k-1)})
, we set
i=I_{k-1}
and we update the set of parameters by defining

(59)

I_{k}=I_{k-1}-2=i-2,\quad L_{k}=\{j\in\{i-1,i\}:\tau(s_{j})=\partial\},\quad\ell_{k}=\#L_{k},

as well as

P_{k}=\{B\setminus L_{k}:B\in P_{k-1}\},\quad\text{and}\quad J^{*}_{k}=\{j\in J^{*}:j\leq I_{k}\}.

Note that
P_{k}
is a partition of
P\cap\{1,\dots,I_{k}\}
.
We also update the parameter
\theta
by setting

(60)

\displaystyle\theta^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\big(\theta^{(k-1)}_{1},\dots,\theta^{(k-1)}_{i-3},0\big)&\text{if $\theta^{(k-1)}_{i-2}=1$;}\\
\big(\theta^{(k-1)}_{1},\dots,\theta^{(k-1)}_{i-3},\theta^{(k-1)}_{i-2}\big)+{\bf e}_{j}&\text{if $\{j,i-1\}\in P_{k-1}$ for some $j<i-2$;}\\
\big(\theta^{(k-1)}_{1},\dots,\theta^{(k-1)}_{i-3},\theta^{(k-1)}_{i-2}\big)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
{\bf e}_{j}
denotes the vector
(0,\dots,1,\dots,0)
whose
j^{\text{th}}
entry is 1, and 0 otherwise.
Observe that the last entry of
\theta^{(k)}
is either
-1
or
0
, and that according to (
59
) we have

(61)

\displaystyle\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{k}=\#\{j:\ i-1\leq j\leq I-1\text{ and }\tau(s_{j})=\partial\}.

Our next step will be to detail the case
k=1
in (
59
). That is we claim that there exists
C<\infty
depending only on
H
such that

(62)

\displaystyle U(I_{0},P_{0},J_{0}^{*},\theta^{(0)})\leq C^{q}T^{2-\ell_{1}}\,U(I-3,P_{1},J^{*}_{1},\theta^{(1)}).

Before that we will explicit our methodology on an example.

Step 5: Example of integration.

In order to make our integration procedure more concrete, let us see what we obtain following Example

. In this case, recall that we consider the expression (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&U\left(I=4,P=\big[\{1,3\}\big],J^{*}=\{2,4\},\theta=(0,0,0,0)\right)\\
&\lesssim\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<s_{3}<T}\exp\left(-K^{4}(s_{2}-s_{1})^{2H}\right)(s_{3}-s_{1})^{2H-2}ds_{1}ds_{2}ds_{3},\end{split}

which corresponds in (

\displaystyle I_{0}=3,\quad P_{0}=\big[\{1,3\}\big],\quad J_{0}^{*}=\{2\},\quad\theta^{(0)}=(0,0,0).

Now, anticipating on Step 6, observe that we just have

for

, that is

. Moreover, looking at the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<s_{3}<T}\exp\left(-K^{4}(s_{2}-s_{1})^{2H}\right)(s_{3}-s_{1})^{2H-2}ds_{1}ds_{2}ds_{3}

This removal of singularities owing to the fact that singular variables are not contiguous in the simplex will be ubiquitous in the sequel. The current case corresponds to the estimate (

) below (see also (

)).

Step 6: Analyzing (

In order to prove (

where we note that the case

. We now further analyze those 3 cases separately.

Case (1)
:
\tau(s_{i-1})=\partial
,

. In this case,

.
There are two subcases (here recall that

(1a)

(1b)

First consider case (1a).
Thanks to property

above, at least one of

If

\displaystyle\int_{s_{i-2}}^{T}ds_{i-1}\int_{s_{i-1}}^{T}ds_{i}\,|s_{i-1}-s_{i-1+\theta_{i-1}}|^{2H-1}e^{-K^{q}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})^{2H}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s_{i-2}}^{T}ds_{i-1}\int_{s_{i-1}}^{T}ds_{i}\,\left[(s_{i}-s_{i-1})^{2H-1}+(s_{i-1}-s_{i-2})^{2H-1}\right]e^{-K^{q}(s_{i}-s_{i-1})^{2H}}

Similarly, if

\displaystyle\int_{s_{i-2}}^{T}ds_{i-1}\int_{s_{i-1}}^{T}ds_{i}\,|s_{i-1}-s_{i-1+\theta_{i-1}}|^{2H-1}e^{-K^{q}(s_{i-1}-s_{i-2})^{2H}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s_{i-2}}^{T}ds_{i-1}\int_{s_{i-1}}^{T}ds_{i}\,\left[(s_{i}-s_{i-1})^{2H-1}+(s_{i-1}-s_{i-2})^{2H-1}\right]e^{-K^{q}(s_{i-1}-s_{i-2})^{2H}}

We now wish to extend our Step 6, which established a relation between

Step 8: Conclusion.

Putting together (

\displaystyle\begin{split}U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)&\leq C^{q}U(I_{0},P_{0},J_{0}^{*},\theta^{(0)})\\

We can now proceed to apply the general case (

We end up with

\displaystyle\begin{split}U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)&\leq C^{2q}T^{2-\ell_{1}}U(I_{1},P_{1},J^{*}_{1},\theta^{(1)})\\
&\,\,\,\vdots\\

.

\displaystyle\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{\nu}=\#\{j\leq I-1:\,\tau(s_{j})=\partial\}=\#\{j\leq I:\,\tau(s_{j})=\partial\}=\ell.

Plugging this information into (

\displaystyle U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)\leq C^{qI}T^{I-1-\ell}\leq C^{q^{2}}T^{I-\ell}.

(ii)

If
I
is even, that is, if
I_{0}
is odd. In this situation we can still start from (
74
) and invoke (
73
) successively.
Similarly to (
75
), we get

(76)

\displaystyle\begin{split}U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)&\leq C^{2q}T^{2-\ell_{1}}U(I_{1},P_{1},J^{*}_{1},\theta^{(1)})\\
&\,\,\,\vdots\\
&\leq C^{q\nu}T^{2(\nu-1)-(\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{\nu-1})}U(3,P_{\nu-1},J^{*}_{\nu-1},\theta^{(\nu-1)})\\
&\leq C^{q(\nu+1)}T^{2\nu-(\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{\nu})}U(1,P_{\nu},J_{\nu}^{*},\theta^{(\nu)}).\end{split}

The slight difference here is that we still have to evaluate
U(1,P_{\nu},J_{\nu}^{*},\theta^{(\nu)})
above.
Specifically, owing to the definition (
58
) of
\nu
, we still have one variable to integrate in
U(1,P_{\nu},J_{\nu}^{*},\theta^{(\nu)})
.
On the other hand, all singularities have been removed thanks to the repeated applications of (
73
).
We thus discover that

(77)

\displaystyle U(1,P_{\nu},J_{\nu}^{*},\theta^{(\nu)})\leq\int_{0}^{T}1\,ds\leq T.

Furthermore, observe that

(78)

\displaystyle\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{\nu}=\#\{j:\ 2\leq j\leq I,\tau(s_{j})=\partial\}=\begin{cases}\ell&\text{if $\tau(s_{1})=0$;}\\
\ell-1&\text{if $\tau(s_{1})=\partial$,}\end{cases}

and
2\nu=I-2
.
Hence gathering (
77
) and (
78
) into (
76
), it follows that

(79)

\displaystyle U(I,P,J^{*},\theta)\leq C^{q(\nu+2)}T^{I-1-(\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{\nu})}\leq C^{q^{2}}T^{I-\ell}.

Now, we may combine the odd case (i) and the even case (ii) to obtain (
48
).
Then, putting (
48
) back into (
44
) yields
|\mathcal{I}[\sigma;\varepsilon,G_{\varepsilon}]|\leq C^{q^{2}}T^{2q-m-\ell}
.
Finally, recall the relation
m+\ell=q
(see (
27
) above) to finish the proof.
∎

We now gather everything to prove the main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

This result has been proved for
H=1/2
by
[
6
]
and for
H\in(1/2,1)
by
[
9
]
.
It remains to prove it for
H\in(0,1/2)
.
We will prove it by verifying the hypothesis of Proposition
2.2
.
Define
\nu
as the image measure
\nu(A)=m\left\{t\in[0,1]:B_{t}\in A\right\}
as alluded to in Section
2.1
.
It is known
[
7
]
that

(80)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\nu}(\xi_{2})|^{2q}\right]\lesssim|\xi_{2}|^{-q/H}\quad\text{for all $\xi_{2}\neq 0$ and $q\in\mathbb{N}_{+}$.}

We thus focus on the horizontal bounds of Proposition
2.2
.
To this aim, for
H\in(0,1/2)
, we may apply Lemma
3.1
to (
24
) and put it into (
14
) to deduce that there exists
C_{q}<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|^{2q}\right]=\frac{(q!)^{2}}{|\xi_{2}|^{2q/H}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{A}_{2q}}\left(\frac{3}{2\pi\lambda}\right)^{q}C^{q^{2}}T^{q}\leq\frac{C_{q}T^{q}}{|\xi_{2}|^{2q/H}|\lambda|^{q}}

for all
q\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
\xi_{1}\neq 0
and
\xi_{2}\neq 0
. Recall (
15
) for the values of
\lambda
and
T
, and use this to obtain from the above that

(81)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\mu}_{G}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|^{2q}\right]\leq C_{q}|\xi_{1}|^{-q}\quad\text{for all $\xi_{1}\neq 0$ and $q\in\mathbb{N}_{+}$.}

Hence, the estimates (
80
) and (
81
) together allow us to apply Proposition
2.2
and deduce that
\dim_{F}G(B)\geq\min\{H^{-1},1\}
a.s. Since
H<1/2
, it follows that
\dim_{F}G(B)\geq 1
. But
B_{t}
is continuous, so we know that
\dim_{F}G(B)\leq 1
(see
[
5
]
).
This completes the proof.
∎
