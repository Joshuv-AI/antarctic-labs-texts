Racial and Ethnic Disparities in Medication Adherence Among Medicare Advantage Enrollees With Diabetes
Authors: Utibe R. Essien, Eric T. Roberts, Dominic A. Ruggiero, Jasmyn J. Tang, Sindhu Raghava, John Z. Ayanian, Renuka Tipirneni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study assesses racial and ethnic disparities in medication adherence among Black, Hispanic, and White Medicare Advantage (MA) enrollees diagnosed with diabetes and examines factors that may contribute to or mitigate such disparities.

Introduction

More than 34 million people in the US have a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes,1 with prevalence increasing among older adults, among whom 1 in 4 aged 65 years or older has a diabetes diagnosis.1,2 Diabetes is associated with elevated morbidity, mortality, and health care costs for older adults.1 Furthermore, Black and Hispanic adults have 47% and 31% higher rates of diabetes than White adults, respectively, as well as poorer outcomes associated with diabetes, including inadequate glycated hemoglobin control.3,4,5

Effective medications, including insulin, metformin, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, are critical to diabetes management. However, cost-related medication nonadherence is common,6,7 with prior research demonstrating adherence disparities by race, ethnicity, and income.3 These data include Medicare analyses demonstrating that Black individuals had 39% lower adherence to diabetes medication than White individuals, contributing to disparities in long-term diabetes complications.8,9

A growing number of Medicare beneficiaries, including a disproportionate share of Black and Hispanic individuals, are enrolled in Medicare Advantage (MA) plans, which provide Medicare Parts A and B services typically along with Part D drug coverage.10 Within MA, multiple factors have potential to mitigate medication adherence disparities, such as publicly reported performance measures,11 reduced cost sharing for generic drugs, and specialized cost-assistance plans, such as dual-eligible special needs plans (D-SNPs), that serve individuals enrolled in Medicare and Medicaid.12 Conversely, high out-of-pockets costs and MA utilization management could exacerbate adherence disparities for groups unable to pay for medications or navigate coverage requirements. Furthermore, other factors may be associated with adherence differences across racial and ethnic groups, including enrollment in the Part D Low-Income Subsidy (LIS), which reduces Part D cost sharing,13 and community-level characteristics linked to access to prescribers and pharmacies.14

Little is known about the extent to which adherence to diabetes medications varies by race and ethnicity among enrollees in MA plans. Furthermore, few studies have examined the factors associated with such differences at the individual, plan, and community levels, knowledge of which could identify areas for policy intervention. We sought to examine disparities in medication adherence among MA enrollees with diabetes and assess the contributions of various factors to these disparities. We hypothesized that adherence to diabetes medications would differ by race and ethnicity among MA enrollees.

Methods

Data and Sample

In this cohort study, we analyzed a national sample of 20% of individuals with MA and Part D coverage in 2019 who had an established diabetes diagnosis. Our primary data sources included the Master Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF), MA encounter data, and Part D prescription drug event (PDE) files. The MBSF provided demographic and enrollment information, including identifiers for MA plans. We used MA encounter data from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2018, to identify beneficiaries with a diabetes diagnosis using the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW) algorithm. PDE files from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2019, were analyzed to measure drug prescription fills during the study year (2019) and a 1-year look-back period to identify standing supplies. This study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania institutional review board, which granted a waiver of informed consent because this study used deidentified data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for study design, execution, analysis, interpretation, and reporting.

We included enrollees of preferred provider organizations (PPOs), health maintenance organizations (HMOs), and D-SNPs.15 We excluded private fee-for-service plans, employer- or union-sponsored plans, Medicare-Medicaid plans, and Programs of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly, which have different benefit designs and serve specialized populations. The sample was then limited to include beneficiaries enrolled in the same MA and Part D plans for all months while alive in 2019. We further limited it to enrollees with MA contracts (ie, ≥1 plans operated by an insurer) with at least 200 Black, Hispanic, and White enrollees each in the 20% sample in 2019; these contracts accounted for nearly 85% of national MA enrollment in 2019.

We implemented 3 additional inclusion criteria. First, we identified enrollees who filled at least 1 prescription for metformin, insulin, an SGLT2 inhibitor, or a GLP-1 RA in 2019. Second, we excluded plans for which MA encounter data were considered unreliable due to having incomplete or unusually low per-capita health utilization and diagnoses reported (eMethods 1 in Supplement 1). Third, among plans retained by this step, we analyzed those with at least 50 non-Hispanic Black and 50 non-Hispanic White beneficiaries (Black and White enrollee disparities analysis) or at least 50 Hispanic and 50 non-Hispanic White beneficiaries (Hispanic and White enrollee disparities analysis) meeting inclusion criteria. eTable 1 in Supplement 1 describes the sample retained at each inclusion step; eTable 2 in Supplement 1 shows the final samples for which we analyzed adherence to metformin, insulin, and SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 RA medications.

Study Outcomes

We separately examined medication adherence for beneficiaries who used at least 1 medication in the following classes: (1) metformin, (2) insulin, or (3) a combined group of SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 RA therapies (eMethods 2 in Supplement 1). We focused on these classes because they were prescribed with sufficient frequency to assess adherence differences by race and ethnicity. We analyzed individuals prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs together as a pooled sample of individuals using higher-cost diabetes medications. We measured adherence as the proportion of days covered by medications in a class. The numerator was the number of days with at least 1 prescription in the class, calculated from the days’ supply variable in the PDE file, using 2018 as a look-back period to establish standing supplies. The denominator was the number of days that a beneficiary was alive in 2019. Beneficiaries with at least 80% of days covered were considered adherent.16

Study Covariates

We used the MBSF to assess beneficiaries’ age, sex, race and ethnicity, enrollment in Medicaid or the LIS, reason for Medicare entitlement, and county of residence. Race and ethnicity were assessed from the Research Triangle Institute–enhanced race and ethnicity variable in the Medicare data and categorized as non-Hispanic Black (hereafter, Black), Hispanic, and non-Hispanic White (hereafter, White); while these were not self-reported, prior work has found high consistency between the RTI variable and self-reported race and ethnicity.17 Additional racial and ethnic groups were not included due to small samples. We constructed a binary indicator for Medicaid or LIS enrollment, as these programs reduce or eliminate cost sharing for Medicare services and Part D drugs.18 We used 2017 to 2018 encounter data to identify diagnoses for 18 chronic conditions based on CCW algorithms and categorized beneficiaries into quartiles of the number of chronic conditions.

We included the following plan-level variables: plan type (HMO vs PPO status with D-SNP designation), quartiles for the percentage of plan enrollees who were either Black or Hispanic,19 and plan for-profit or nonprofit status. We linked public data on contract-level star ratings for 2019 and categorized contracts as having ratings of 4.5 stars or higher, 4 stars, or below 4 stars.20 To characterize community-level factors, we included zip code–level variables for Rural-Urban Commuting Area codes and the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), both categorized in deciles.21 ADI was mean-centered by state to capture within-state differences in community-level socioeconomic status and was included to consider whether factors such as poverty, housing, and transportation were associated with medication adherence disparities.

Statistical Analysis

We separately analyzed Black and White enrollee and Hispanic and White enrollee adherence disparities by drug class and conducted 3 sets of analyses. The number of White beneficiaries differed across samples due to differences in the number of MA plans meeting minimum sample size criteria for each racial and ethnic group comparison. First, we carried out a descriptive analysis of population characteristics and unadjusted racial and ethnic adherence disparities by medication class. We then decomposed disparities into components arising within vs between plans. Within-plan disparities represent adherence differences between racial and ethnic groups in the same plan; between-plan disparities reflect mean differences in adherence across plans serving proportionally more vs fewer Black or Hispanic enrollees.22,23

Second, for each medication class, we examined the extent to which regression-adjusted Black and White enrollee or Hispanic and White enrollee adherence disparities varied across plans serving proportionally more vs fewer Black or Hispanic enrollees. We estimated multilevel linear regression models (beneficiaries nested within plans) and regressed beneficiary-level adherence on beneficiary-level Black race or Hispanic ethnicity, plan-level share of Black or Hispanic enrollees (categorized into quartiles), and their interaction. Separate models were estimated to examine Black and White enrollee disparities (using plan quartiles defined by share of Black enrollees) and Hispanic and White enrollee disparities (using plan quartiles defined by share of Hispanic enrollees). Each model adjusted for the aforementioned factors and county fixed effects (to control for geographic differences in factors such as clinician availability and composition of available MA plans). We tested for Black and White enrollee and Hispanic and White enrollee differences in adherence by plan quartile and used estimated margins to report adjusted adherence rates by race and ethnicity and plan quartile.

Third, we performed the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition analysis to estimate the contributions of observed factors (included covariates) vs unobserved factors (other potentially influential factors not available for inclusion in analysis) to Black and White enrollee or Hispanic and White enrollee adherence disparities.24 This method decomposes a disparity between 2 groups into explained (based on contribution of observed factors) vs unexplained (based on difference remaining after accounting for observed factors) components. The explained component represents the disparity associated with differences across racial and ethnic groups in observed factors (beneficiary demographics, MA plan characteristics, and community-level attributes), including interactions between those factors and racial and ethnic differences in their association with adherence. The unexplained component represents all differences not explained by observed factors.25 We expected factors such as insurance plan factors that reduce patients’ out-of-pocket costs to be associated with improved adherence. We used this decomposition analysis to assess whether such factors are associated with higher adherence rates for Black and Hispanic individuals compared with White individuals. To account for geographic region, all factors were mean-centered at the county level.

We graphically summarized estimates for the explained component both overall and by the contributions of individual factors. Negative estimates indicate that a factor was associated with smaller disparities than would be expected if Black or Hispanic beneficiaries had the same characteristics as White beneficiaries. Positive estimates indicate that a factor was associated with larger disparities than would be expected had Black or Hispanic beneficiaries’ characteristics equaled those of White beneficiaries. To facilitate interpretation, in the text we describe each factor’s contribution to the explained component as a percentage of the total Black and White enrollee disparity or Hispanic and White enrollee disparity.

Statistical significance was assessed using 2-sided tests with P < .05. Analyses used Stata, version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC). Analyses were conducted between December 2024 and November 2025.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The total sample of 587 716 beneficiaries (mean [SD] age, 72.11 [8.85] years) included 80 586 Black beneficiaries (63.6% female, 36.4% male) and 233 505 White beneficiaries (49.1% female, 50.9% male) in the sample for Black and White enrollee disparities analysis and 83 116 Hispanic beneficiaries (53.9% female, 46.1% male) and 190 509 White beneficiaries (48.9% female, 51.1% male) in the Hispanic and White enrollee disparities analysis (Table 1). Compared with White enrollees, Black and Hispanic enrollees were more likely to be younger than 65 years, qualify for Medicare because of a disability, have Medicaid or LIS enrollment, and reside in major metropolitan areas or socially disadvantaged communities. Compared with White beneficiaries, Black and Hispanic enrollees were more likely to be enrolled in MA contracts with a star rating of less than 4 stars. eTables 3, 4, and 5 in Supplement 1 show sample characteristics by medication class.

Adherence to Diabetes Medications by Race and Ethnicity and Disparities Within vs Between Plans

Adherence rates were suboptimal (<60%) among all 3 racial and ethnic groups for each category of diabetes medication, and they were consistently lower for Black and Hispanic beneficiaries compared with White beneficiaries. Among SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA users, a mean (SE) of 31.4% (0.4%) of Black beneficiaries and 40.4% (0.3%) of White beneficiaries met the threshold of 80% adherence or higher (Table 2), representing an unadjusted disparity between Black and White individuals of 9.0 percentage points (pp) (P < .001). Among SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA users, the mean (SE) unadjusted adherence rate among Hispanic beneficiaries (33.9% [0.5%]) was 8.1-pp lower than among White beneficiaries (42.0% [0.3%]) (P < .001). Among insulin users, mean (SE) adherence was 8.0-pp lower among Black beneficiaries (40.9% [0.3%]) compared with White beneficiaries (48.9% [0.2%]) (P < .001) and 2.8-pp lower among Hispanic beneficiaries (47.8% [0.3%]) compared with White beneficiaries (50.6% [0.2%]) (P < .001). Mean (SE) adherence to metformin was 10.5-pp lower among Black beneficiaries (46.0% [0.2%]) compared with White beneficiaries (56.5% [0.1%]) (P < .001) and 3.6-pp lower among Hispanic beneficiaries (54.0% [0.2%]) compared with White beneficiaries (57.6% [0.1%]) (P < .001).

In the analysis of Black and White individuals, within-plan disparities accounted for 74.6% of unadjusted disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA drugs, 85.3% of disparities in insulin adherence, and 84.5% of disparities in metformin adherence (Figure 1). Conversely, between-plan differences accounted for 25.4% of unadjusted disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs, 14.7% of disparities in insulin adherence, and 15.5% of disparities in metformin adherence. In the analysis of Hispanic and White individuals, within-plan disparities explained 62.5% of unadjusted disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs, 39.1% of disparities in insulin adherence, and 66.1% of disparities in metformin adherence. Across these medication classes, between-plan differences accounted for a mean of 37.5% of unadjusted Hispanic-White disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs, 60.9% of disparities in insulin adherence, and 33.9% of disparities in metformin adherence.

Regression-Adjusted Analyses of Adherence by Beneficiary Race and Ethnicity and Plan-Level Proportion of Black or Hispanic Enrollees

Regression-adjusted medication adherence rates varied by race, ethnicity, and plan-level proportion of Black or Hispanic enrollees (Figure 2). Disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs between Black and White enrollees and Hispanic and White enrollees differed across quartiles of plans grouped by the share of Black or Hispanic individuals enrolled. For example, among plans in the lowest quartile of Black individuals enrolled, regression-adjusted adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs was 36.1% among White beneficiaries and 35.8% among Black beneficiaries (P = .97 for difference); among plans in the highest quartile of Black individuals enrolled, adherence to these medications was 33.6% among White beneficiaries and 27.0% among Black beneficiaries (P < .001). Among plans in the lowest quartile of Hispanic individuals enrolled, adherence was 47.2% among White enrollees and 45.5% among Hispanic enrollees (P = .76), whereas in plans in the highest quartile of Hispanic individuals enrolled, adherence was 37.7% among White enrollees and 30.4% among Hispanic enrollees (P < .001).

Regression-adjusted adherence to both insulin and metformin were lower among Black enrollees than among White enrollees across all plan quartiles defined by the share of Black individuals enrolled. For example, among plans in the lowest quartile of Black individuals enrolled, insulin adherence was 48.1% among White enrollees and 33.8% among Black enrollees (P < .001); in the highest quartile, adherence was 46.3% among White enrollees and 39.2% among Black enrollees (P < .001). Decomposing Contributions to Disparities

Figure 3 depicts findings from the decomposition analyses, including the overall and individual contributions of individual-, plan-, and community-level factors to adherence disparities between Black and White enrollees and Hispanic and White enrollees. For SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs, Medicaid or LIS enrollment was associated with reductions in adherence disparities between Black and White enrollees of −2.9 pp (95% CI, −3.2 pp to −2.5 pp), or 32.3% of the total disparity, and between Hispanic and White enrollees of −3.4 pp (95% CI, −3.9 pp to −2.8 pp), or 41.3% of the total disparity, compared with what would have been expected if Black and Hispanic beneficiaries’ enrollment in these programs were as low as that of White beneficiaries. Similarly, Medicaid or LIS enrollment was associated with reductions in disparities in insulin adherence of −1.8 pp (95% CI, −2.0 to −1.6 pp) between Black and White enrollees, or 22.0% of the total disparity, and −1.8 pp (95% CI, −2.1 to −1.5 pp) between Hispanic and White enrollees, or 63.9% of the total disparity. These estimates reflect greater enrollment in Medicaid or LIS among Black and Hispanic beneficiaries than among White beneficiaries and the association between enrollment in these programs and higher adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs and to insulin (eFigures 1, 2, 4, and 5 in Supplement 1). Conversely, for metformin, Medicaid or LIS enrollment explained little of the disparity in adherence (eFigures 3 and 6 in Supplement 1).

Plan-level characteristics explained a modest and variable proportion of disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs and to insulin and little of the disparity in adherence to metformin. Plan-level characteristics were estimated to account for 18.6% (95% CI, 14.8%-22.4%) of the total disparity between Black and White beneficiaries in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs and 31.0% (95% CI, 25.1%-36.9%) of the disparity between Hispanic and White beneficiaries. For metformin, plan characteristics explained only 2.6% (95% CI, 1.5%-3.7%) of disparities in adherence between Black and White enrollees and did not explain a statistically significant share of the disparity between Hispanic and White enrollees (2.1%; 95% CI, −2.7% to 6.9%). Living in a socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhood explained 6.7% (95% CI, 1.1%-12.3%) of disparities in insulin adherence between Hispanic and White beneficiaries and 3.4% (95% CI, 0.9%-5.8%) of the disparity between Black and White beneficiaries. Neighborhood socioeconomic status did not explain a statistically significant share of the disparity in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs between Black and White beneficiaries (2.0%; 95% CI, −1.9% to 6.0%) or Hispanic and White beneficiaries (0.8%; 95% CI, −2.4% to 4.0%). ADI also did not explain a significant share of the difference in adherence to metformin between Black and White (0.2%; 95% CI, −1.0% to 1.4%) or Hispanic and White (0.1%; 95% CI, −2.6% to 2.9%) beneficiaries. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of adults enrolled in MA plans nationally, we observed significant differences between Black, Hispanic, and White beneficiaries in adherence to medications for diabetes. First, adherence to metformin, insulin, and high-cost SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs was suboptimal among all MA enrollees and markedly lower among Black and Hispanic enrollees compared with White enrollees. Second, within-plan differences contributed substantially to overall adherence disparities. Third, Medicaid or LIS enrollment was associated with smaller disparities in SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA adherence and insulin adherence between Black and White enrollees and Hispanic and White enrollees than would be expected if Black and Hispanic beneficiaries’ enrollment in these programs matched the lower enrollment rates of White beneficiaries. Fourth, plan characteristics explained a variable proportion of disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs and to insulin but were not associated with metformin adherence disparities. Fifth, community-level socioeconomic status, as measured by ADI, accounted for a small proportion of disparities in adherence to insulin and SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 RA medications.

Although prior studies examined the role of MA enrollment in diabetes medication initiation and adherence,26,27 there has been limited evidence on variations in adherence among MA enrollees. Our study expanded on prior work by examining the role of individual-, plan-, and community-level factors that may mitigate or exacerbate disparities in adherence owing to differences in the prevalence of these factors across racial and ethnic groups and their association with adherence rates. We found that enrollment in Medicaid and LIS was associated with substantially reduced disparities in adherence to insulin and to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs, owing both to the positive association between enrollment in Medicaid or LIS and adherence to these medication classes and the greater proportion of Black and Hispanic beneficiaries than White beneficiaries enrolled in these programs. This finding underscores the importance of maintaining programs that reduce out-of-pocket costs for diverse Medicare beneficiaries with low income.16,28

Furthermore, contextual factors may be linked to adherence via their association with access to prescribers or pharmacies. We analyzed the ADI as a summary measure of contextual factors and found that living in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods explained up to 6.7% of disparities in insulin adherence and 2.0% of disparities in adherence to SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA medications. These findings expand on prior work that documented structural barriers to medication access, including pharmacy closures.29 Other factors not included in the ADI, such as health literacy, quality of patient-clinician communication, complexity of dosing regimens and medication adverse effects, and presence of social support systems, have been associated with adherence disparities.30 However, we were unable to examine those factors due to a lack of administrative data characterizing them.

Our analysis sought to examine underexplored factors at the plan level to help identify future policy-level targets to optimize diabetes medication adherence. Overall, we found that adherence disparities were largely within the same plans rather than between plans enrolling more vs fewer Black or Hispanic individuals. However, our decomposition analysis identified 2 plan-level factors that were associated with adherence disparities. First, higher enrollment of Black and Hispanic beneficiaries in MA contracts with lower star ratings (a summary measure of quality) was associated with greater disparities in adherence to insulin and to SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 RAs between Black and White enrollees and Hispanic and White enrollees. Prior work highlighted that disparities in access to high-rated plans were associated with disparities in enrollment in plans that may provide higher-quality care.31 Second, we found that HMO vs PPO enrollment was associated with greater disparities in adherence between Black and White enrollees and Hispanic and White enrollees. However, the contribution of these plan-level factors to adherence disparities was small compared with the contribution of Medicaid or LIS enrollment.

One notable finding of this study was the lower adherence to metformin, a relatively low-cost medication, among Black beneficiaries compared with White beneficiaries.32,33 Prior studies have identified patient concerns about long-term medication adherence, adverse effects, and personal beliefs about the medication’s effectiveness as reasons for metformin nonadherence.34,35 These unmeasured factors may explain the high rate of metformin nonadherence that was comparable with the rate of nonadherence for the other diabetes therapies examined.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. First, because this was an observational study, the decomposition analysis reflects estimated associations between covariates and outcomes and remains susceptible to residual confounding. Second, we focused on Black, Hispanic, and White beneficiaries, limiting the generalizability of our study findings to other racial and ethnic groups with insufficient sample sizes for inclusion in our analysis. Third, the detection of all chronic comorbidities may be limited based on the CCW algorithm and beneficiaries’ MA enrollment period. Fourth, whereas proportion of days covered is the standard measurement for medication adherence,36 it may not reflect individuals’ actual use of prescribed medications. Fifth, our analysis did not include classes of diabetes medications other than the ones mentioned, as they were among the most common drug classes used.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of MA beneficiaries, we observed suboptimal diabetes medication adherence overall, along with marked racial and ethnic disparities in adherence. Disparities in adherence to insulin, SGLT2 inhibitor, and GLP-1 RA medications were partially mitigated by Medicaid and prescription drug subsidies. However, disparities in adherence were also associated with other factors, including plan characteristics. The findings suggest that additional insurance- and community-level strategies are needed to improve medication adherence overall and in disadvantaged patients and communities.
