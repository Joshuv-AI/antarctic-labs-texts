Dendrobium mixture ameliorates hepatic injury induced by insulin resistance in vitro and in vivo through the downregulation of AGE/RAGE/Akt signaling pathway
Authors: Shuting Zhuang, Xian Zhou, Xiaowen Yang, Dennis Chang, Tao Chen, Yibin Sun, Chenxiang Wang, Chutian Zhang, Jichao Jiang, Yong Chen, Xiaohui Lin, Xiaoning Wang, Wenzhen Yu, Xinjun Lin, Caigu He, Yanfang Zheng, Jieping Zhang, Hong Shi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Dendrobium mixture (DM) is a patented Chinese herbal medicine which has been shown to ameliorate type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in vivo and in vitro. We aimed to investigate the underlying mechanism of DM as a therapeutic agent in attenuating liver steatosis in relation to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).DM (16.2 g/kg/d) was administered to db/db mice for 4 weeks. The db/m mice and db/db mice in the control and model groups were given normal saline. Additionally, DM (11.25 g/kg/d) was administered to Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, and the serum was collected and used in an experiment involving palmitic acid (PA)-induced human liver HepG2 cells with abnormal lipid and glucose metabolism.In db/db mice, the administration of DM significantly alleviated liver steatosis, including histological damage and cell apoptosis. DM was found to prevent the upregulation of the RAGE and AKT1 proteins in liver tissues. The underlying mechanism of DM was further studied in PA-induced HepG2 cells. Post-DM administration serum from SD rats reduced lipid accumulation and regulated glucose metabolism in HepG2 cells. Consequently, it inhibited RAGE/AKT signaling and restored autophagy activity. The upregulated autophagy was associated with the mTOR-AMPK signaling pathway. Furthermore, post-DM administration serum reduced apoptosis of hepatocytes in PA-induced HepG2 cells. Our study supports the potential use of DM as a therapeutic agent for the treatment of NAFLD in T2DM. The mechanism underlying this therapeutic potential is associated with the downregulation of the AGE/RAGE/Akt signaling pathway.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a prevalent chronic liver condition affecting a significant proportion of the global population, with approximately 25 % of the adult being affected [1]. It refers to a condition of excessive fat accumulation in the liver without a history of excessive alcohol consumption [2]. If left untreated, NAFLD can progress to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), a severe form of the disease characterised by fat accumulation, inflammation and cirrhosis [3]. Ultimately, NASH can lead to hepatocellular carcinoma and even mortality [4]. Individuals with NAFLD often have concomitant cardiovascular diseases and metabolic syndrome, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hyperlipidemia, and hypertension [2]. Notably, obesity and insulin resistance have been identified as significant risk factors of NAFLD [5]. Moreover, a meta-analysis incorporating data from 20 observational studies revealed that NAFLD is associated with a two-fold increased risk of developing T2DM [2]. However, the resolution of NAFLD has been shown to decrease the risk of T2DM [6]. These observations suggest the presence of common underlying factors linking the pathogenesis of NAFLD and T2DM [7].

Recent studies have highlighted the role of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) as co-factors in the connection between T2DM and NAFLD [8]. AGEs are products of non-enzymatic reactions between reducing sugars and proteins, lipids or nucleic acids [9]. Their production increases with high-fat diet and hyperglycemia [10]. Notably, AGEs exhibit a weak to moderate correlation with liver function and damage indices in NAFLD patients [10]. In experimental models of NAFLD, AGEs activate the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), triggering downstream oxidative and inflammatory pathways [8]. RAGE is expressed in various tissues and immune cells and serves as a link between the innate and adaptive immune systems, participating in the onset and maintenance of inflammation. High AGE levels can worsen liver injury by upregulating inflammation, oxidative stress, and activating hepatic stellate cells involved in fat storage and fibrosis, thereby disrupting glucose and lipid metabolism [8]. Consequently, the AGE-RAGE signaling axis has emerged as an important therapeutic target for NAFLD [11].

Dendrobium mixture (DM) is a patented Chinese herbal formula (patent. no. ZL201110408411.0) composed of various ingredients, including Huang Qi [Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bge.], Dan Shen (Salvia miltiorrhiza Bge.), Wu Wei Zi [Schisandra chinensis (Turcz.) Baill.], Niu Xi (Achyranthes bidentata Bl.), Shi Hu (Dendrobium nobile Lindl.), and Zhi Mu (Anemarrhena asphodeloides Bge.), among others. For over three decades, DM has been utilised in clinical practice in China as a therapeutic agent for abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism [12]. It has also been demonstrated potential in mitigating complications associated with diabetic nephropathy [13] and diabetic cognitive dysfunction [14], in animals. Our recent study has highlighted the therapeutic potential DM in ameliorating T2DM with NAFLD in an integrated study of bioinformatics analysis and experimental verification [15]. The underlying molecular mechanism was associated with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor ϒ (PPAR-ϒ) activation. In addition, the improved liver function and insulin resistance by DM was also associated with the modulation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, leading to improved liver function and insulin resistance [12]. Since DM is large formulation with various ingredients, the therapeutic effects may be linked with multiple targets [13].

This study aimed to further investigate the underlying mechanisms of action of DM in the treatment of NAFLD induced by T2DM in vitro and in vivo.

Material and methods

DM preparation

The preparation of DM decoction in this study followed our previous research [14]. The herbal ingredients, totaling 108 g of raw materials (daily dosage) were soaked in 800 mL of cold water and boiled for 1 h, followed by simmering on low heat for 15 min. The resulting decoction was filtered, and the remaining solution was cooked with an additional 400 mL of water for another hour. Subsequently, the DM decoction was condensed and concentrated to a density of 1.08 g/mL. The main marker compounds identified in DM included catalpol, harpaside, puerarin, timosaponin BII, astragaloside IV, tanshinone IIA and schisandrin [14].

Animal model and experimental intervention

The study design for the animal model and experimental intervention followed our previous study [15]. Ten male non-diabetic db/m mice (8 weeks of age, weighing 18–20 g) and 20 male obese and T2DM db/db mice (8 weeks of age, weighing 36–38 g) were purchased from Changzhou cavens experimental animal Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in the Barrier System at the Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Laboratory Animal Center. The db/m mice were used as the control group (n = 10). The db/db mice with comparable blood glucose levels and body weights were randomly divided into two groups: the model group (n = 10) and the DM-treated groups (n = 10). The control and the model group received normal saline via oral gavage once a day for four weeks. The DM-treated group received DM via oral gavage at a dosage of 16.2 g/kg/d based on body weight, once a day for four weeks. The dosage of DM was calculated based on the recommended clinical dosage which is 108 g of the raw materials per day. At the end of the treatment period prior to the commencement of experiments, all animals underwent a 10 h-fasting period, but free access to water. Anesthesia was then induced by intraperitoneal injection of 5 % urethane before subjecting to the following assays. This study was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: FJTCM IACUC2021079) and adhered to the ARRIVE Guidelines and guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the Chinese Council on Animal Research.

Electron microscopic analysis

Fresh liver tissues were collected within 3 min from each mouse after they have been anesthetised. The tissue was then immediately placed in a culture dish containing electron microscopy fixative solution [0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4 containing 1 % osmium tetroxide]. The tissue was cut into approximately 1 mm3 pieces and then transferred to an eppendorf tube containing fresh electron microscopy fixative for further fixation, storage, and transportation at 4 °C. The tissue was rinsed three times for 15 min each with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4). Sequentially, the liver tissue was immersed in 30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 95 %, and 100 % ethanol for 20 min each for dehydration, and rinsed twice in 100 % acetone for 15 min each. The tissue was embedded in EMbed 812 Resin (1:1, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China, 10000418) under 37 °C for 2–4 h, acetone: EMbed 812 Resin (1:2) under 37 °C overnight, followed by 100 % EMbed 812 Resin under 37 °C for 5–8 h. Finally, the tissue sections were embedded in the plate filtered with Embed 812 Resin overnight under 37 °C. After staining with 2 % uranium acetate saturated alcohol solution (Sigma, USA), ultrathin sections of liver were prepared and viewed by transmission electron microscopy (HT7800/HT7700, HITACHI, Japan). For morphometric analysis, a minimum of five random fields per mouse were taken and examined in blinded manner at × 10000 magnification.

Histological staining of liver

After anesthesia, the intact liver tissues were removed, washed and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde/phosphate-buffered saline. The histological staining of liver follows the steps in our previous study [15]. The specimens were embedded in paraffin and cut into thin slices (5 μm thick). The slices were dehydrated using a gradient alcohol series before deparaffinized with xylene and embedded with wax. The wax section was then cut to 4 μm thickness and dewaxed by xylene solutions again, followed by the hydration with descending ethanol. Subsequently, the specimens were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 15 min. The slide was then mounted using neutral balsam with a cover slip secured using glue. The sections were then observed and photographed using a Nikon 55i microscope (Nikon, Japan). The lipid accumulation areas in liver tissues were analysed through ImageJ software.

Treatment of post-DM administration serum in palmitic acid-induced HepG2 cells

Human hepatoma HepG2 cells were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium (Gibco, USA, 11995065) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA, 10099141) and penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA,15140163) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. To induce cell steatosis and insulin resistance, palmitic acid (PA, Solarbio, China, H8780) dissolved in 40 % bovine serum albumin was added to the cells for different time points (12 h, 24 h, 36 h and 48 h) in a serial dilution manner. The optimal concentration and time point were determined for subsequent bioassays.

Forty male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from Changzhou cavens experimental animal Co., Ltd. Fifteen rats were randomly assigned to the normal saline, while the remaining rats were given DM (11.25 g/kg/d, once a day). All rats were fed for 7 days. On the last day, after fasting for 12 h with free access to water, the rats were anesthetised with 20 % urethane. Blood samples were collected by removing the eyeball and centrifuged at 4 °C, 10,000 g for 15 min. The serum was pooled within the same group, sterilized in a 56 °C water bath, filtered using a 0.22 μm filter, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. HepG2 cells were treated either with DMEM media alone (blank control) or DMEM media containing PA at 250 μM for 24 h. Subsequently, the PA-induced cells were treated with diluted serum from either the normal group of SD rats or the DM treated group of SD rats. The cell and cell supernatants were subjected to the following bioassays.

Measurements of apoptotic biomarkers

After 24 h of PA stimulation, HepG2 cells were treated with serum samples obtained from the DM treated groups in SD rats, using serial dilution as follows. The control group was supplemented with medium containing 10 % FBS, while the experimental group was mixed with serum containing 2.5 %, 5 %, and 10 % of serum-containing DM, and cultured for 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h. Cell viability was determined at 450 nm using the CCK8 kit (C6005, New Cell and Molecular Biotech, China). Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry using the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-Annexin FITC/7-AAD kit (4A Biotech, Beijing, China) [16]. Briefly, HepG2 cells were seeded overnight in 6-well plate until reaching confluency. The cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and suspended in binding buffer (100 μL). Then, Annexin V FITC (10 μL) and 7-AAD (5 μL) were added to the cell suspension and incubated for 15 min in the dark prior to examination using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX LX, Beckmancoulter, America).

Glucose and lipid metabolism analyses

The glucose content in HepG2 cells was determined using the colorimetric glucose oxidase method, following a previously established protocol [17]. Briefly, HepG2 cells treated with various conditions were incubated in serum- and phenol red-free DMEM supplemented with 4.5 g/L d-Glucose (Gibco, USA, 21063029) for 3 h. After incubation, the cell supernatant (5 μL) was collected and mixed with glucose oxidase reagent (195 μL, APPLYGEN, China, E1011). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 Pro, Tecan, Australia). The glucose content was calculated using a glucose standard curve. Glucose consumption was determined by subtracting the glucose concentration in the sample wells from that of the blank control wells (medium only).

Oil O red staining

The method follows a previous study [15]. Frozen sections of liver tissue obtained from mice and crawling slices of HepG2 cells treated with various post-administration DM serum were subjected to fractionation using 60 % isopropanol for 10 min. Subsequently, staining with oil red O (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 0625) were performed for 10 min, followed by washing with PBS and additional fractionation with 60 % isopropanol for 15 s. Hematoxylin (Solarbio, China, H8070) staining was then conducted, and the crawling slices were blow-dried and mounted using glycerol gelatin. The films were sealed and photographed. Glycerol gelatin (Beyotime, China, C0187) was used for mounting the films. The films were observed and photographed using a light microscope (Nikon 55i microscope, Japan). The lipid accumulation areas in hepatocytes were analysed through Image Pro plus 6.0 software.

Western blot analysis

Liver tissue and HepG2 cells were lysed using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, China, P0013B) and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The total protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (PC0020, Solarbio, China). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by electrophoresis, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and blocked with 5 % skim milk. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against specific proteins, including anti-AMPK (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, #5832S), anti-Phospho-AMPK (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, cat. no. 2535S), anti-AKT1 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, cat. no. 75692S), anti-mTOR (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, cat. no. 2983), anti-Phospho-mTOR (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, cat. no. 5536), anti-LC3B (1:1,000, Abcam, USA, cat. no. ab48394), anti-RAGE (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, cat. no. 55222), anti-Bax (1:1,000, Proteintech, USA, cat. no. 50599-2-Ig), anti-Bcl2 (1:1,000, Proteintech, USA, cat. no. 26593-1-AP), anti-Casepase3/p17/p19 (1:1,000, Proteintech, USA, cat. no. 19677-1-AP), anti-GAPDH (1:3,000, Proteintech, USA, cat. no. 60004-1-Ig), and anti-β-actin (1:3,000, Proteintech, USA, cat. no. 2D4H5). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including Goat anti-rabbit (1:5,000, Proteintech, America, cat. no. 10366-1-AP) and Goat anti-mouse (1:5,000, Proteintech, America, cat. no. 3H9D1) for 1 h. Image Lab software (version 3.0) was used to analyze intensity of the protein bands, and the relative expression levels were calculated by comparing the target protein to the reference protein.

Statistical analysis

The data are shown as mean ± standard deviation. The comparison among different groups was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's test. P < 0.05 was set statistically significant. ROUT test was conducted to exclude outliers.

Results

DM attenuated liver damage and steatosis in db/db mice

Macroscopical differences were observed between the control and db/db model group, with the liver in the model group showing hepatomegaly (Fig. 1a). In addition, the distribution pattern of steatosis was observed to across various regions of the liver lobule. However, the macroscopical appearance of the liver in the DM group appeared similar to that of the control group with few regions displayed steatosis or lesion. Statistical analysis (Fig. 1b) revealed that the liver weight in the model group was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.0001). The liver weight in the DM group was significantly lower than that of the model group (p < 0.001). Hematoxylin staining of the liver in control mice exhibited normal cytoarchitecture of periportal hepatocytes. In contrast, the model group of db/db mice displayed microvesicular and macrovesicular steatosis in hepatocytes, indicative of disturbed lipid metabolism. Quantification revealed that the liver in the diabetic model group had significantly higher areas of lipid droplets compared to the control group. However, the DM treatment group exhibited significantly lower areas of the hepatocytes with lipid droplets (p < 0.001), suggesting a significant decrease in steatosis. DM downregulated RAGE-AKT1 pathway and prevented early apoptosis in liver tissues of diabetic db/db mice

In the model group, there was a significant increase in RAGE (p < 0.01) and AKT1 protein expressions (p < 0.001) compared to the control group, suggesting the activation of the RAGE-AKT1 pathway in the liver tissues of the diabetic group (Fig. 2a and b). However, DM treatment significantly reduced the expressions of both protein expressions (p < 0.01). The downstream molecular targets in the RAGE-AKT1 pathway, including apoptotic markers, were quantified. The changes in apoptosis induced by DM were assessed by examining the B-cell lymphoma protein 2 (Bcl-2)-associated X (Bax) and the protein expression of cleaved-caspase 3. Although there was no significant change in the Bcl2/Bax ratio between the control and model group (Fig. 2c and d), the protein expression of cleaved caspase-3 was significantly increased in the model group (p > 0.05). In contrast, there was an evident increasing trend in the Bcl2/Bax ratio in the DM group, and the protein expression of cleaved caspase-3 in the DM group was significantly lower compared to the model group (p < 0.01). After observing autophagosome formation in the liver treated with DM, Western blot analysis was performed to investigate the autophagy-related mechanism (Fig. 3b). As shown in Fig. 4a, PA concentrations of 125 and 250 μM did not induce significant cytotoxicity at 12 h and 24 h. However, at 36 h, the 250 μM concentration of PA resulted in significant cell viability inhibition (p < 0.001). At 48 h, both 125 μM (p < 0.01) and 250 μM (p < 0.001) concentrations showed significant cell viability inhibition. On the other hand, PA concentration of 500, 800 and 1000 μM caused significant cytotoxicity at all tested time points (p values < 0.05). Based on these results, a 24 h's co-incubation of PA at 125 and 250 μM was selected for further glucose content and glucose consumption tests (Fig. 4c). The co-incubation of PA at 250 μM for 24 h significantly increased the glucose level and reduced glucose consumption (p < 0.05) compared to the control group. The effects observed with the 250 μM concentration were greater than those with 125 μM concentration. Post-DM administration serum regulated the glucose metabolism in PA-induced HepG2 cells

In the study, the cell viability of HepG2 cells was assessed using the CCK8 assay with the co-incubation of serum containing DM at various dilutions. Fig. 4b shows that all the selected dilutions of serum-containing DM (2.5 %, 5 %, 10 % and 20 %) did not significantly affect cell viability at the incubation time points of 12 h, 24 h and 36 h. However, at 48 h, the 10 % and 20 % dilutions of DM demonstrated significant cytotoxicity (p < 0.01 and p < 0.0001). Therefore, the 24-h co-incubation of serum-containing DM at a 2.5 % dilution, which showed no cytotoxicity, was selected for the glucose metabolism test. Fig. 4d shows the results of the glucose content and glucose consumption tests. The addition of saline, used as a vehicle, did not significantly affect the glucose content and consumption, suggesting its minimal influence. The co-incubation of PA (250 μM) resulted in a significantly higher glucose content (p < 0.05) and lower glucose consumption (p < 0.05), suggesting glucose metabolism disorder. However, the serum-containing DM treatment corrected the glucose metabolism disorder induced by PA, leading to significantly reduced glucose content (p < 0.01) and restored glucose consumption level (p < 0.001). Furthermore, Oil Red O staining was performed to evaluate lipid accumulation in insulin resistant HepG2 cells with or without DM treatment (Fig. 4e). The co-incubation of PA and PA + saline groups for 24 h induced noticeable intracellular lipid accumulation, as evidenced by strong red staining. The statistical analysis in Fig. 4f revealed that the percentage of lipid droplets was approximately 5 times higher in the model group compared to the control group (p < 0.001). However, the serum-containing DM group exhibited a significant reduction in lipid droplets, which was significantly lower than that of the model group (p < 0.01). These findings suggest that DM treatment effectively attenuated lipid accumulation in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells.

Mechanisms of action of post-DM administration serum in PA-induced HepG2 cells

In the PA-induced HepG2 cells, the protein expression levels of RAGE and AKT1 were examined using Western blot analysis to further investigate the pharmacological action of post-administration DM in serum.

Flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining were performed to assess the occurrence of apoptosis in HepG2 cells (Fig. 5b and c). The results showed a significant increase in the percentage of cells undergoing both early and late apoptosis in the model group compared to the control group (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, the PA and saline groups also exhibited a significantly higher apoptotic level compared to the control group (p < 0.001), suggesting that the effect of saline alone was not significant. In contrast, the DM group showed a significantly lower apoptotic level than the PA group (p < 0.001), suggesting that the post-DM administration serum treatment reduced apoptosis in the PA-induced HepG2 cells.

The autophagy activity in HepG2 cells affected by the post-DM administration serum was examined. In Fig. 5d and e, the PA group showed a decreasing trend in the LC3II/LC3I ratio, suggesting that PA induced insulin resistance and glucose metabolism disorder in HepG2 cells were associated with an inhibited autophagy activity. However, in the post-DM administration serum group, there was a significant increase in the LC3II/I expression (p < 0.01), suggesting that the action of PA was partially related to the restored autophagy activity by DM. Moreover, PA treatment led to a significant increase in p-mTOR/TOR levels and a decrease in p-AMPK/AMPK levels, while the post-DM administration serum corrected both of these levels. This finding suggests that the regulated autophagy activity by DM in HepG2 cells may be mediated through the mTOR-AMPK pathway.

Discussion

The high prevalence of obesity and T2DM is closely associated with the development of NAFLD. The relationship between T2DM and NAFLD is complex and involves bi-directional regulation [18]. One of the key signaling pathways that links these two diseases is the AGE/RAGE/Akt pathway. In our study using db/db mice, we observed that DM treatment protected liver tissues from lipid accumulation and steatosis. Therefore, the use of DM treatment is considered a promising herbal approach to mitigate NAFLD in individuals with T2DM. However, it is important to note that safety concerns have been reported regarding herbal products [19]. While no obvious adverse events were observed in animals or cytotoxic effects in cellular models, a comprehensive assessment of DM's safety profile on liver function should be thoroughly investigated in future studies.

The underlying mechanism of this protective effect was associated with the downregulation of the AGE/RAGE/Akt signaling pathway. This downregulation led to reduced apoptosis and upregulated autophagy in the liver tissues of the db/db mice. To further investigate the mechanism of action, we conducted experiments using serum samples from SD rats treated with DM and tested them on PA-induced HepG2 cells. The post-DM administration serum continued to exhibit protective effects on HepG2 cells by regulating glucose and lipid metabolism, reduced apoptosis and restored autophagy. These effects were consistent with the suppression of the AGE/RAGE/Akt signaling pathway.

Numerous studies have highlighted the central role of AGE/RAGE pathway in the development and progression of NAFLD [20]. AGEs, which are formed through the reaction of reducing sugars with protein or lipids, activate RAGE and contribute to the accumulation of fat in the liver, leading to steatosis and progression to NASH [21]. The soluble form of RAGE (sRAGE) has been considered as a potential therapeutic agent for NAFLD and T2DM. sRAGE can block the interaction between AGE and RAGE, thereby potentially mitigating the harmful effects of AGE/RAGE signaling. Some studies have explored the possibility of using stem cells or monocytes transfected with sRAGE for auto-transplantation to treat NAFLD and T2DM. However, there are challenges associated with the clinical application of sRAGE. These challenges include the accessibility, heterogeneity and cross-talk with other RAGE ligands, which limit the development of sRAGE as a widely applicable therapeutic agent in clinic [8]. Given these challenges, it is of great significance to continue searching for potential therapeutic agents that can inhibit the AGE/RAGE signaling pathway for the treatment of NAFLD. Discovering alternative approaches or identifying novel compounds that can effectively modulate the AGE/RAGE pathway may hold promise for the development of future therapeutic strategies targeting NAFLD and related metabolic disorders.

The study conducted by Xu Q et al. demonstrated the glucose- and lipid-lowering effects of DM through the regulation of approximately 1000 functional genes associated with glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as cell survival [22]. Our recent publication suggested that one of the associated mechanisms of DM to attenuate NAFLD was the upregulation of the PPARγ [15]. This highlights the multi-component and multi-target potential of DM. Studies have further supported the beneficial effects of DM on hypoglycemia and complications such as diabetic nephropathy [23] and diabetic cognitive impairment [14] using diabetic animal models. In particular, a 12-week treatment of DM in diabetic rats was shown to regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis and histo-morphological changes in the liver, with the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway being implicated in the mechanism of action [12]. These findings motivated the present study to explore the pharmacological action and mechanisms of DM in treating NAFLD in T2DM, both in vitro and in vivo. The results of the present study demonstrated that DM attenuated liver injury in db/db mice, as evidenced by reduction in liver weight, as well as reduced lipid droplets and lipid accumulation in the liver tissues. One important and novel finding from our study is that DM effectively regulated the activated AGE-RAGE pathway by inhibiting the over-expression of RAGE and AKT1 [24]. It has been reported that the activation of AGE/AKT1 induces apoptosis which is mediated by caspases and Bcl-2 family proteins [25]. In line with the previous report, our study observed an upregulation of caspase-3 protein expression in the liver tissue, suggesting early apoptotic events.

In addition, the DM group showed a high level of autophagosomes in the liver tissue under the electron-microscope. Autophagy has emerged as a promising target for therapeutic interventions, as it regulates lipid metabolism, insulin resistance, and protects hepatocytes from injury and cell death [26]. The study by Porcu et al. (2018) suggested the activation of autophagy mediated by mTOR-AMPK pathway played a protective role against liver steatosis induced by a high-fat diet [27]. Conversely, acetaminophen was found to inhibit autophagy associated with the AMPK/mTOR pathway, exacerbating fat accumulation in NAFLD [28]. Therefore, the upregulated autophagy observed in the present study may contribute to the beneficial actions of DM in attenuating NAFLD in T2DM. However, question may arise whether the upregulation of autophagy by DM was associated with the downregulation of AGE/RAGE signaling or it is an independent molecular event which warrants further investigation.

To further investigate the molecular mechanism of action of DM after the administration in the body, serum samples from SD rats were collected after oral administration of DM. These samples were then applied to PA-induced human liver HepG2 cells. The post-administration DM in serum regulated glucose metabolism dysfunction in HepG2 cells. It corrected the abnormalities in glucose content and consumption, alongside reduced lipid accumulation in hepatocytes as evidenced by Oil O red staining. These findings suggested that the amelioration of liver steatosis by DM was likely to be attributed to the regulation of glucose content as one of the multi-target mechanisms of DM. We continued to observe that post-administration DM in serum regulated protein expressions of RAGE and AKT1 in HepG2 cells. Consistently, the data suggest that DM exhibited its liver-protective effects through the downregulation of the AGE/RAGE/Akt signaling pathway. Similarly, the reduced apoptosis and restored autophagy activities were observed in hepatocytes which was in line with the findings from in vivo.

The identification of the bioactive compounds presents in serum DM that carry the bioactivity was important to further uncover the mechanisms of action within this complex herbal formulations. Several key bioactives, including kaempferol, luteolin, baicalein, diosgenin, wogonin and quercetin, have been independently shown to be effective against NAFLD in cellular or animal studies. These compounds have demonstrated efficacy in preventing the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis or protecting against the “two hit” in NAFLD [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Nevertheless, the interactions among these bioactive compounds within DM and their effects on key gene and protein targets of the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway and upregulated autophagy remain unknown. Furthermore, it is important to determine whether these bioactive compounds are present in the post-administration DM in serum and whether they reach target organs at concentrations that can elicit meaningful pharmacological effects. Future pharmacokinetic studies are needed to unravel these puzzles and advance our understanding of the pharmacological actions of DM.
