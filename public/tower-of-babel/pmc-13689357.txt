B vitamins and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD): a systematic review of observational studies
Authors: Mohammad Safargar, Sara Arefhosseini, Helda Tutunchi, Seyed Rafie Arefhosseini, Mehrangiz Ebrahimi-Mameghani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundB vitamins are crucial cofactors in metabolic regulation; however, their role in the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) remains unclear. Evidence for individual B vitamins is often contradictory. This systematic review evaluated the findings of observational studies examining the association between B vitamin intake and MASLD risk, severity, and outcomes.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for studies published up to January 2026, adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. All included studies were observational in design, encompassing cross-sectional, case-control, cohort, and Mendelian randomization (MR) studies that assessed dietary, supplemental, or biological B-vitamin status in relation to MASLD diagnoses. Study quality was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS). No quantitative meta-analysis was performed and the evidence was narratively synthesized.ResultsFifty-nine studies were included (38 cross-sectional, 13 case-control, 6 cohort, and 2 MR studies). Folate has been extensively studied, with most evidence suggesting a protective role for serum folate and 5-mTHF against MASLD and fibrosis. However, this was contradicted by positive associations for RBC folate and unmetabolized folic acid (UMFA). Cohort studies have linked higher folate status to reduced all-cause mortality. Vitamin B12 findings were highly controversial, with studies reporting protective, detrimental, and null associations; MR studies suggested a positive causal link with MASLD risk. Choline intake was inversely associated with steatosis but positively correlated with fibrosis in specific subgroups, including men and postmenopausal women. Evidence for niacin and pyridoxine was inconsistent, though often suggesting an inverse relationship with MASLD.ConclusionObservational evidence suggests significant, albeit contradictory, associations between specific B-vitamins (particularly folate and B12), choline, and MASLD progression. However, given the massive methodological heterogeneity and heavy reliance on cross-sectional data, the certainty of these findings is very low. The inherent uncertainty of these associations rather than concluding a probable clinical benefit should also be emphasized. Rigorous prospective cohorts and randomized controlled trials are required to establish causality and clarify the true clinical utility of B-vitamin status in MASLD.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02420-w.

Background

The liver, a central nexus of metabolic homeostasis, is profoundly affected by environmental, genetic, and epigenetic factors [1, 2]. Steatotic liver disease (SLD) has replaced Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) spectrum over the past few years encompassing alcohol-related subsets, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [1, 3]. The term MASLD represents liver steatosis accompanied by at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, affecting almost 38% of the general population [4, 5]. The ‘Multiple Hits Theory’ clarifies the intricate association between insulin resistance (IR) and pro-inflammatory-oxidative pathways, gut microbiome dysbiosis, and hormonal dysfunction, leading to the progression of the disease to more severe forms, including fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [6–9].

There is growing recognition toward the role of diet in MASLD and hence, lifestyle modifications (a combination of caloric restriction, specific dietary patterns and physical activity) are the only approved treatment [10, 11]. From a nutritional perspective, not only energy and macronutrient intake but also micronutrients have represented promising roles in MASLD [10–12]. In this regard, B vitamins, as water-soluble micronutrients, exert a plethora of physiological functions in metabolic regulation through one-carbon transfer and methylation reactions [11, 13]. To the best of our knowledge, B vitamin deficiency, particularly in terms of folate, B12, and their active metabolites, has been attributed to multi-metabolic conditions such as cardiovascular disease (CVD), obesity, and cancer via genetic regulation and acting as cofactors in enzymatic reactions [13, 14].

B vitamins have been explored in relation to MASLD pathogenesis, including redox pathways, inflammatory responses, and lipid metabolism [15]. Furthermore, several large-scale observational studies have investigated the relationship between dietary intake of B vitamins, their status, and metabolites with MASLD, MASH, related outcomes, and even mortality and morbidity risk, particularly using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [16–19]. Meanwhile, most of these studies have only investigated a single vitamin or a small number of vitamins [20, 21], and the findings are contradictory, showing considerable variation resulting from different methodologies and diagnostic methods, which highlights the need for a precise conclusion on the overall effect. To address this research gap, the present comprehensive systematic review of observational studies aimed to assess the relationship between B vitamins and MASLD.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22]. While the review followed a predefined framework for literature search and data extraction, a formal protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or another registry. In order to explore the related studies, we conducted a systematic search in PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar until January 1st 2026, along with a review of the reference lists of the included articles and previously published reviews to minimize the loss of further studies. The search strategy combined MeSH terms with keywords related to B vitamins and MASLD. The full search strategy for each database is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included in the present systematic review based on the following predefined eligibility criteria, structured around the Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) process [23]:

“Populations,” No restrictions were placed on population, age, or health status; “Intervention/ Exposure,” The status of the B vitamins, their metabolites, and choline was defined as either (1) dietary or supplemental intake assessed via validated methods (such as food frequency questionnaires (FFQ), 24-hour recalls, and food records), (2) measurements in biological samples (such as serum) using any validated biochemical assay (e.g., liquid chromatography, chemiluminescence, or radioimmunoassay), without restriction to a specific detection method to ensure maximum inclusivity of the epidemiological data, or (3) genetic predisposition; “Comparator,” The comparison was between individuals with different levels of vitamin B status, such as comparing the highest versus the lowest quantile of intake or comparing individuals with sufficient versus deficient serum concentrations; “Outcomes,” The primary outcome was a diagnosis of MASLD (or previous terminologies, such as MAFLD and NAFLD). Given the extensive timeframe and observational design of the included studies, a pragmatic acceptance principle regarding diagnosis was adopted: The diagnostic modalities and thresholds were accepted as defined and validated by the original authors of each respective study. Specifically, accepted criteria included: (1) Liver histology via biopsy (considered the gold standard for MASH and fibrosis staging); (2) Imaging techniques, including Ultrasonography (typically defined by standard criteria such as increased hepatic echogenicity) and FibroScan (utilizing established kilopascal cut-offs for liver stiffness and Controlled Attenuation Parameter [CAP] scores for steatosis); (3) Validated non-invasive clinical scoring indices, such as the Fatty Liver Index (FLI, typically utilizing a threshold of ≥ 60), United States FLI (US-FLI), and Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI); or (4) Administrative ICD/Medicare data. Specialists generally approve the application of MASLD nomenclature to populations defined as NAFLD [1]; “Study Design”, Included observational studies, specifically cross-sectional, case-control, cohort, and Mendelian randomization (MR) studies. No language or publication date restrictions were applied during the search. Studies lacking sufficient details, as well as editorials, letters, commentaries, research involving non-human subjects, abstracts, review articles, and duplicate studies, were not included.

Study selection

The identified studies were imported into the EndNote reference manager for duplicate removal. Two authors, [MS] and [SA], independently reviewed all the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the articles to determine their eligibility. Controversies at either the title-abstract or full-text screening stages were resolved through discussion and consensus. A third author [HT] was consulted to resolve any discrepancies. The study selection process is documented in the PRISMA 2020 flowchart.

Screening, data extraction, and quality assessment

Two authors [MS, SA] separately extracted the necessary data using predefined data extraction forms. The extracted information comprised the first author’s name, publication date, country, study design, age, study population, sample size, B vitamin type and assessment, disease diagnosis, and outcomes. The quality of the included studies was evaluated by two reviewers using a 9-point scoring system for case-control and cohort studies based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and its modified version with a 10-point scoring system specific to cross-sectional studies [24–26]. In accordance with the foundational framework of the NOS [27] and widely adopted methodological standards for observational evidence synthesis in nutritional epidemiology [28], the total scores were categorized to reflect the risk of bias. Specifically, studies achieving a score of 7 to 9 were classified as high quality (indicating a low risk of bias), whereas scores of 4 to 6 and 0 to 3 were considered moderate and low quality, respectively. While a formal inter-rater reliability statistic (such as Cohen’s kappa) was not retrospectively calculable due to the immediate recording of consensus-only scores, the independent evaluation process was strictly maintained. Any initial scoring discrepancies between the primary reviewers were rigorously resolved through direct discussion or formal arbitration by a third senior [HT] to finalize the scores.

Data synthesis

The literature exhibited profound clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Specifically, the included studies utilized inherently diverse epidemiological designs, different diagnostic modalities for MASLD, and heterogeneous methods of B-vitamin assessment. Because these studies measured varying exposures against varying diagnostic thresholds, quantitatively pooling their effect sizes would violate the core assumptions of meta-analysis and yield mathematically unreliable, clinically misleading results. Consequently, formal statistical pooling, as well as quantitative assessments of inter-study heterogeneity and publication bias could not be reliably performed. Instead, a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence was conducted to ensure accurate, context-dependent interpretation of the findings.

Results

The initial systematic search yielded 12,471 records (3,596 from PubMed, 3,651 from Web of Science, and 5,224 from Scopus). Next, 6,965 duplicates were removed, and after excluding studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 65 full-text articles of potentially relevant studies remained (two were identified by searching reference lists). Six further articles were eliminated following an examination of the publications, leaving 59 studies for this review (Fig. 1). Three of the excluded studies assessed a combination of nutrients or a mixed dietary pattern [29–31], two had insufficient or irrelevant data [32, 33], and one study was an abstract [34].

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 59 studies met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review. The characteristics (number of studies) of the included literature, encompassing study design, geographic distribution, dietary and biochemical assessment methodologies, diagnostic tools, and specific clinical outcomes have been consolidated alongside their corresponding citations in Table 1. Briefly, the evidence base comprises predominantly cross-sectional designs (n = 38) supplemented by case-control and prospective cohort studies spanning from 2005 to 2025. The sample sizes varied widely from 43 to 84,024 participants (aged 5 to 80 years), with a heavy geographical reliance on North American datasets, particularly the NHANES studies. Evidence on the association between B vitamins and MASLD

Folate (Vitamin B9)

Findings from cross-sectional studies

According to the findings, 20 studies showed a negative association [14, 15, 18, 39, 44–46, 48–52, 54, 58, 59, 61, 66, 67, 75, 76], three showed a positive link [36, 50, 53], and seven failed to document any relation between folate and MASLD [35, 36, 55, 63, 72, 73, 86]. For instance, Peng et al. [54] identified a non-linear, inverse association between serum folate levels and MASLD; while lower serum folate concentrations correlated with increased disease incidence, individuals in the highest quartile of serum folate exhibited a 46.3% reduction in the odds of MASLD compared to the lowest quartile (OR: 0.537, 95% CI: 0.364–0.793). Moreover, decreased serum folate and increased RBC folate were also related to mild [53] and biopsy-proven severe MASLD [65], while one study failed to document any differences in subjects with biopsy-proven MASLD [63]. In this regard, the Korean NHANES study on adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) found no overall relationships between folate and MASLD [35, 36, 55]. Moreover, Liao et al. [36], in the most recent study on 1,321 FibroScan-proven patients with MASLD, identified a significant dose-dependent positive association between RBC folate and MASLD; compared to the lowest quartile, individuals in the highest RBC folate quartile exhibited a 44.3% increased risk of MASLD (OR: 1.443, 95% CI: 1.113–1.872), while serum folate showed no independent association. Evidence in non-Hispanic white participants has revealed a U-shaped relationship between serum folate and steatosis, suggesting that both low and high serum folate levels could be detrimental [46]. Several studies have also examined the MASLD spectrum in relation to folate metabolites (serum 5-mTHF and THF, UMFA, and RBC folate), and most of them reported negative relationships [14, 15, 18, 39, 44–46, 48–52, 54, 58, 59, 61, 66, 67]. Here, 5-mTHF, as the bioactive systemic form of folate, showed an inverse relationship with MASLD; specifically, comparing the highest serum concentration quartile to the lowest revealed a profound 71% reduction in the odds of MASLD (OR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.21–0.41) [39, 48, 50]. This relationship was considered non-linear, as Yao et al. [51] reported protective effects of serum folate and 5-mTHF, plateauing at a concentration of approximately 60 nmol/L in 2,489 patients with MASLD [51]. Furthermore, another NHANES study (n = 6,610 adults) suggested that 5-mTHF is a protective marker, unlike UMFA, where elevated serum concentrations ($\ge0.6$ nmol/L) were significantly associated with increased odds of both MASLD (OR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.20–1.89) and advanced fibrosis (OR: 1.48, 95% CI: 1.01–2.19) [50].

Numerous studies have explored dietary folate intake. Sheng et al. [38] in 2025 documented a non-linear correlation between dietary folate intake and hepatic steatosis, with an inflection point of 410 µg/day. Similar results were observed in patients with MASH [68] and postmenopausal women with FibroScan-proven MASLD [15]. Low folate intake has also been attributed to elevated serum aminotransferase levels and is considered the most significant dietary determinant of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) concentration. In turn, higher folate intake (exceeding 268 µg/day) is correlated with normalized liver enzymes [67]. In this context, adding folate to prediction models improved diagnostic accuracy for various liver fat contents [61]. Patients with higher serum folate and 5-mTHF levels also exhibited substantial protection against advanced hepatic fibrosis; for example, high serum folate concentrations correlated with a 53% reduction in the odds of advanced fibrosis (OR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.35–0.63) [44], while elevated 5-mTHF independently reduced fibrosis likelihood (OR: 0.666, 95% CI: 0.499–0.888) [39], corroborating the negative correlations observed with histological fibrosis severity [59]. However, this association was not comprehensive, as two studies on different populations failed to find any link between dietary folate or serum folate and liver fibrosis [15, 52]. Despite this evidence, some studies have focused on different subgroups. For instance, cross-sectional studies on patients with MASLD have shown a more significant protective role of folate in women [45] and subjects with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25, highlighting the potential role of adiposity in the association [49, 66].

Evidence from case-control studies

Among the five case-control studies on folate status, two reported lower dietary intake of folate in patients with MASLD than in controls [74, 75], one showed similar results for serum folate [78], and two showed no inter-group differences in serum [79] or dietary folate [80]. Three out of the seven studies that evaluated dietary folate intake found a protective correlation with hepatic steatosis [70, 75, 76]. For example, Cheng et al. [70] linked lower dietary folate intake to an almost 20-fold increase in the odds of hepatic steatosis in 105 obese patients with liver disease. Another study also observed a marked negative link between folate intake and MASLD in men only [76]. Conversely, two studies failed to address the relationship between MASLD and the index of nutritional quality (INQ) [73] and dietary folate intake (assessed by FFQ in the Southern Community Cohort Study (SCCS)) [72], respectively.

Evidence from cohort studies

Two large-scale cohort studies using reported the protective effect of folate on the risk of all-cause mortality in adults with existing MASLD [84, 85]. Dong et al. [84] assessed dietary folate intake in 3,266 adults with MASLD and indicated a 31% lower risk of all-cause mortality over a 10-year period following high folate intake (Q4 vs. Q1 HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.51–0.94) [84]. In addition, evidence from NHANES III over a follow-up period of almost 26 years showed a marked relationship between higher serum folate levels and a lower risk of all-cause mortality [85]. Hence, limited cohort evidence on US populations links higher folate status to a reduced risk of all-cause mortality in individuals with pre-existing MASLD, although data on MASLD prevention or progression are currently lacking.

Evidence from MR studies

One dedicated MR study [86] and one cross-sectional study incorporating an MR analysis [18], investigated the causal link between folate and MASLD. Both studies failed to report any causal link; Liu et al. [86] in an analysis of European MASLD datasets failed to report a causal association between serum folate and MASLD, further supported by Chen et al. [18], who found no significant causal relationship in the primary and secondary MR analyses, despite observing a significant connection in the cross-sectional analysis of their study. In conclusion, the evidence for folate’s role in MASLD is complex. While most studies suggest a protective role of folate against MASLD and even all-cause mortality, this is contradicted by opposing findings for UMFA and RBC folate. The most consistent evidence comes from cohort studies linking higher folate status to reduced mortality. However, a critical gap remains, as no study has established whether folate influences the initial development or progression of MASLD.

Cobalamin (Vitamin B12)

Evidence from cross-sectional studies

Vitamin B12 has been explored throughout 13 studies, mainly suggesting its protective role in SLD and liver fibrosis [15, 40, 42, 58, 59]. Higher serum B12 levels have been associated with lower odds of MASLD [58] while robust multivariate analyses demonstrate significant negative correlations between serum B12 concentrations and both liver fibrosis severity (standardized beta coefficient: -0.623, p < 0.001) and MASH histological activity (standardized beta coefficient: -0.183, p = 0.039) [59]. However, Polyzos et al. [63] found no association between B12 and liver histopathology. In contrast, recent transient elastography data demonstrated a significant positive correlation between serum B12 levels and liver stiffness in MASLD patients (men: r = 0.31, p < 0.001; women: r = 0.15, p = 0.016), revealing that every 25 pg/mL increase in circulating B12 elevates the odds of F4 cirrhosis by 6% (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.03–1.10) [40]. Similar results were observed in case of biopsy-proven hepatic steatosis/fibrosis in obese subjects undergoing bariatric surgery [56].

Regarding dietary B12 intake, Li et al. [15] found no association with liver fibrosis in postmenopausal women with MASLD. Meanwhile, a NHANES analysis of 4,352 adults linked the highest tertile of dietary B12 intake to a 49% increase in the odds of liver fibrosis compared to the lowest tertile (OR: 1.49, 95% CI: 1.01–2.18), though this association was not observed for hepatic steatosis [55]. Additionally, two recent studies described non-linear correlations between dietary and serum B12 levels and liver fibrosis based on both FibroScan findings [38] and the FLI scoring index [54], respectively. Overall, this association seems to be discrepant and complicated by reports of non-linear relationships, which prevent a clear conclusion.

Evidence from case-control studies

Most case-control studies revealed lower serum B12 levels in patients with MASLD [71, 77, 78, 81]. For example, two studies reported reduced B12 levels in relation to increased steatosis severity (especially in grades II to III), serum ALT [78], and more advanced stages of MASLD [81]. In contrast, another study on patients with biopsy-proven MASH found higher serum B12 levels in the cases than in the controls [79]. The clear associations observed for serum B12 levels were notably absent when dietary intake was examined. Three case-control studies on Iranian and Korean populations consistently reported no significant differences in B12 intake [73, 74, 76]. These null findings could be attributed to the use of varied diagnostic criteria and dietary assessment methods.

Evidence from cohort studies

A prospective cohort study of 2,125 participants with MASLD proposed a protective effect of higher serum B12, demonstrating that individuals in the third and fourth quartiles experienced a 42% (HR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.86) and 28% (HR: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.54–0.96) reduced risk of all-cause mortality, respectively, compared to the lowest quartile [85]. Furthermore, Wang et al. [17] reported that while neither dietary nor serum B12 was independently associated with mortality, elevated serum MMA, a functional marker of B12 deficiency, was independently associated with a 35% increased risk of all-cause mortality (adjusted HR: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.12–1.62). Hence, assessing functional B12 status using markers such as MMA may be more clinically relevant than measuring serum B12 alone. Notably, the combination of high B12 and MMA levels showed the highest risk for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, indicating that high serum B12 in the presence of functional deficiency is a marker of particularly high risk. Furthermore, high serum folate and vitamin B12 exerted a synergistic effect on their U-shaped link with mortality risk, a methodologically strong finding from a cohort with a follow-up period of approximately 26 years on ultrasound-based MASLD [85].

Evidence from MR studies

Two MR studies have reported a genetic correlation between higher circulating B12 levels and an increased risk of MASLD (OR = 1.30, 95% CI:1.13–1.48 per standard deviation increase) [88], with positive causal associations [86]. This evidence supports a potential bidirectional relationship between genetic liability for MASLD and increased B12 levels [88].

Niacin (Vitamin B3)

Evidence from cross-sectional studies

Cross-sectional evidence on dietary niacin intake is inconsistent. Two studies documented an inverse relationship between higher niacin intake and MASLD prevalence [16, 47]. Chen et al. [16] in 2025 evaluated niacin intake in 4,113 patients with MASLD (assessed by scoring indices) and reported a negative dose-response association with the risk of disease [16]. In contrast, another study on 1,385 adults with MASLD (assessed by FibroScan) revealed a U-shaped pattern, as the MASLD risk decreased with niacin intake up to a threshold of 23.6 mg/day, and a higher intake was associated with increased odds, suggesting that both low and excessive niacin intakes could be unfavorable [41]. It is noteworthy that the results of the U-shaped relationship seem more reliable due to the use of FibroScan findings. Other studies did not report any significant associations between niacin and liver outcomes [15, 38, 55].

Evidence from case-control studies

Antentas et al. [69] failed to observe any differences in niacin intake between the patients with MASLD and healthy controls (n = 222 in each group), while a non-linear, negative dose-response association was found for B3 or its equivalent intake [69]. This finding was more pronounced for intakes below 35 mg/day [69]. Three other studies on MASLD assessed niacin as part of a panel of multiple B vitamins but reported no associations with niacin [74, 80] or the INQ of niacin [73]. In summary, with only four case-control studies available and three of them reporting null findings, the evidence seems to be insufficient.

Evidence from cohort studies

Only one retrospective cohort study in 2024, with a follow-up period of approximately 9 years on 4,315 patients with MASLD, demonstrated a negative link between higher dietary niacin and all-cause mortality (highest vs. lowest tertile HR = 0.70, 95%CI:0.50–0.96) [19].

Pyridoxine (vitamin B6)

Evidence from cross-sectional & case-control studies

Numerous studies have suggested that a higher vitamin B6 intake or status is associated with improved liver health [38, 54, 60]. A NHANES study in 2025 found an inverse correlation between dietary B6 and both liver steatosis and fibrosis [38], subsequently confirmed by another study on MASLD with focus on liver fat, inflammation, Controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) score and ALT levels, excepting the participants in the obese subgroup [60]. Two case-control studies also illustrated lower dietary intake of B6 in cases with MASLD than controls [80] as well as inverse associations between the INQ of B6 and the odds of MASLD [73]. According to MASH, Federico et al. [68] compared 124 biopsy-proven MASH patients with 2,326 healthy controls and reported a lower dietary intake of B6 in the cases. One study reported a linear negative correlation between serum B6 and the risk of MASLD (higher levels linked to a 47% lower risk) [54]. Despite these findings, some studies did not find any link between B6 and liver outcomes [15, 55].

Evidence from MR studies

An MR analysis of European individuals using three datasets found no evidence of a causal association between genetically predicted B6 levels and MASLD risk [86].

Other B vitamins

Evidence on other B vitamins, assessed as part of a dietary panel, is limited and highly inconsistent. Li et al. [15] stated non-linear negative associations of thiamine and riboflavin intake with liver steatosis and no associations with liver fibrosis in postmenopausal subjects. In contrast, patients with MASH had a higher intake of B1 than healthy controls [68]. Other studies have not found any associations between B vitamin intake and FibroScan findings [38, 55]. In addition, four case-control studies have evaluated B vitamins in relation to MASLD [73, 74, 76, 80]; Vahid et al. [73] identified a negative link between the INQ of several B vitamins and MASLD risk, specifically in terms of pantothenic acid (B5) and biotin [73]. However, another report was in contrast and failed to document any associations in the case of thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid and biotin based on 24-hour recall [53]. In particular, a large cohort study in 2025 assessed 84,000 veterans with MASLD and identified a primary association between increased total vitamin B intake (assessed by FFQ) and decreased fibrosis progression (assessed by Fibrosis-4 index), while no significant association was found on the time to developing cirrhosis [83].

Choline

Evidence from cross-sectional studies

The evidence on the association between choline and MASLD is mixed, relying on the liver condition (steatosis vs. fibrosis) and study population. Cross-sectional studies only addressed dietary intake and reported an inverse relationship with the presence or severity of steatosis [15, 43, 64]. An NHANES analysis of FibroScan-proven MASLD inversely connected higher choline intake to hepatic steatosis and MASLD risk in both sexes [43]. Similar findings were also elucidated in a large sample of over 20,000 participants assessed using the FLI [57] as well as in the postmenopausal population [15]. Other research has also provided insight into other areas. For example, Yu et al. [62] in a population-based study on 56,195 Chinese participants indicated an association between higher choline intake and lower risk of MASLD in both sexes with an emphasis on the interaction of BMI in women, where higher choline intake was protective only in normal-weight women and not in the overweight/obese ones. However, these findings are limited by the use of self-reported ultrasonography for diagnosis [62]. Low dietary choline intake was also related to increased hepatic injury markers, and total choline intake > 233 mg/day was associated with normalized AST levels [67].

In contrast, some studies have reported a direct association between choline intake and an increased risk of liver fibrosis in specific subgroups [37, 55]. For example, despite the lack of a link between dietary choline and steatosis in the NHANES analysis, the highest intake tertile was associated with higher odds of fibrosis [55]. Similarly, another study in 2025 evaluated choline intake in 5,687 participants with MASLD (2,019 with fibrosis) and found no association with steatosis but positive associations with fibrosis only in men [37]. The same relationship with severe fibrosis was also observed in postmenopausal women with MASLD [64].

Evidence from case-control studies

The case-control studies were limited to only two studies. One study on the Iranian population found no association between choline INQ and MASLD [73]. In contrast, Chang et al. [70] reported that lower dietary choline was associated with increased odds of steatosis, with an approximate 81% protective effect of combined intake of choline and betaine [70].

Evidence from cohort studies

Yiannakou et al. [82] in a most recent study on 1,414 participants from the Framingham Heart Study with an approximate six years follow-up duration, indicated an inverse relationship between dietary choline intake (evaluated by FFQ) and MASLD incidence. More precisely, a 31% lower risk of MASLD development was observed, suggesting that adequate choline consumption is a key modifiable factor [82]. These findings show that while sufficient choline intake could prevent the initial fat accumulation, in the case of established disease or in certain high-risk groups, higher choline intake may be linked to the progression of fibrosis.

Qualitative sensitivity analysis: impact of study quality and diagnostic modality

Because the clinical heterogeneity of the included literature precluded a quantitative meta-analysis, a formal statistical sensitivity analysis could not be performed. However, to rigorously evaluate the reliability of the observed associations, a qualitative sensitivity analysis stratifying the evidence strictly by diagnostic modality and methodological quality (NOS ≥ 7) was conducted.

When stratifying by diagnostic modality, significant divergences emerge. Large-scale cross-sectional studies relying on non-invasive indices (e.g., FLI, HSI) generally report inverse, protective associations between B-vitamin status (namely folate and vitamin B12) and hepatic steatosis. Conversely, when restricting the analysis strictly to the highest-quality evidence utilizing liver biopsy (the clinical gold standard) the narrative shifts. In biopsy-confirmed MASH and advanced fibrosis cohorts, circulating B12 levels frequently correlate positively with disease severity.

Furthermore, when stratifying strictly by study quality, restricting the synthesis exclusively to high-quality studies (NOS ≥ 7) eliminates cohorts with severe methodological flaws, but the core epidemiological contradictions remain unchanged. High-quality prospective cohorts and Mendelian randomization studies continue to demonstrate mixed, paradoxical results. This confirms that the conflicting associations reported in the broader literature are not the results of poor-quality study execution, but are instead primarily driven by true clinical variations in disease stage and diagnostic accuracy.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to investigate the association between B vitamins and MASLD. Although pioneering studies have examined the role of individual B vitamins, the exact relationship between all B vitamin families needs to be clarified. Specifically, the roles of folate and vitamin B12 seem to be particularly prominent, as most existing studies have focused on these two vitamins. To provide a comprehensive overview, 59 observational studies were included and evaluated according to their methodological rigor and level of evidence.

Folate and MASLD

Folate is the principal mediator of methylation reactions. The current body of evidence suggests a protective role of folate in terms of dietary and serum folate, particularly 5-mTHF, against MASLD, fibrosis, and all-cause mortality [14, 45, 83]. However, this protective effect is opposed by several inconsistencies, such as the positive association of RBC folate and UMFA with the risk of MASLD and fibrosis [50, 53]. This relationship is further complicated by the findings of non-linear associations, where the protective effect of folate appears to plateau or follow a U-shaped pattern in certain populations. Indeed, this conclusion could be weakened by a heavy reliance on U.S. data, different methodologies, and geographic locations. Moreover, the biological link may depend entirely on the form of folate (serum or its metabolites) and the individual’s capacity to metabolize folate. MR studies have not mentioned any causal link between folate and liver disease, making it open to further investigation. Mechanistically, folate is crucial for one-carbon metabolism, RNA/DNA synthesis, and epigenetic regulation. Adequate folate activates the liver kinase B1/AMP-activated protein kinase pathway, improving glycemic and lipid profiles [18]. Conversely, folate deficiency stimulates hepatic lipogenesis, induces oxidative stress, and upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), driving MASLD progression [35, 44, 89]. Furthermore, folate facilitates phosphatidylcholine and VLDL synthesis, preventing hepatic triglyceride accumulation. Finally, folate deficiency disrupts mitochondrial β-oxidation and causes hyperhomocysteinemia, both of which strongly promote steatosis and fibrosis [44, 89, 90]. This biochemical dualism explicitly explains the paradox observed in our results: while adequate systemic levels of natural, bioactive folate (e.g., 5-mTHF) are hepatoprotective, the accumulation of UMFA and high RBC folate often reflects excessive synthetic supplementation or impaired hepatic metabolization (such as pseudo-MTHFR deficiency) [91]. When the damaged liver cannot process these excess forms, they accumulate systemically, paradoxically acting as pro-inflammatory triggers that may accelerate MASLD [53, 89, 92].

Cobalamin and MASLD

The liver is the main storage site for vitamin B12. Current evidence on the link between B12 and MASLD is controversial, comprised both positive and negative associations based on SLD severity. Notably, the positive associations could be driven from impairments in hepatic B12 metabolism, leading to higher circulating levels which don’t reflect a healthy status [14] while a clear negative association has been proved throughout case-control findings [73, 78]. Hence, elevated B12 in patients with MASH is a critical outlier that may suggest its different role in more advanced inflammatory stages of MASLD. In addition, impaired intestinal absorption or, more likely, altered hepatic uptake and metabolism affect circulating B12 concentrations. It should be noted that genetically predicted higher circulating B12 is causally and even bi-directionally associated with an increased risk of MASLD, a finding from MR studies. Additionally, there is a U-shaped correlation between the mortality risk and the combination of serum folate and B12 levels [85].

At the mechanistic level, vitamin B12 acts as a vital cofactor in single-carbon transfer and the regulation of odd-chain fatty acids [42]. B12 deficiency leads to MMA accumulation, which suppresses mitochondrial β-oxidation and promotes insulin resistance and dyslipidemia [42, 58]. Furthermore, B12 deficiency elevates serum homocysteine, upregulating key lipogenic genes (e.g., SREBP) and promoting hepatic fat infiltration [20, 53]. In advanced SLD, impaired hepatic clearance can paradoxically elevate plasma B12 while the liver itself remains functionally deficient, explaining the phenomenon of ‘vitamin B12 insensitivity’ seen in severe disease stages [17, 40, 42].

Niacin and MASLD

Evidence on the link between niacin and MASLD encompasses the preventive effects observed in cross-sectional studies and U-shaped or null associations reported by case-control designs and studies with a stronger methodology, hindering any firm overall conclusion. The hepatoprotective mechanisms of niacin primarily involve redox homeostasis and lipid regulation. Niacin replenishes NAD+ levels, mitigating oxidative stress. It also exhibits hypolipidemic effects by suppressing hepatic diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2, thereby reducing triglyceride synthesis. Additionally, niacin activates the AMPK cascade and reduces free fatty acid efflux from adipocytes, limiting VLDL accumulation. Beyond lipid regulation, niacin prevents fibrosis by reducing collagen formation and exerting anti-inflammatory actions through the suppression of NF-kB and specific interleukins [93–95].

Pyridoxine and MASLD

The current findings primarily suggest an inverse relationship between dietary vitamin B6 intake and the risk and severity of MASLD/MASH. Studies on serum B6 are extremely limited, and MR findings have failed to document any causal association. Vitamin B6 plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism, specifically in fatty acid elongation and phospholipid methylation. It acts as a cofactor in synthesizing eicosanoids and prostaglandins, which help reduce hepatic lipid deposition. B6 deficiency alters the systemic lipid profile, contributing to dyslipidemia. Furthermore, similar to folate and B12, B6 deficiency disrupts homocysteine metabolism; the resulting elevated homocysteine induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and upregulates de novo lipogenesis (via SREBP1c), explicitly linking low B6 status to MASLD pathogenesis [96]. Other B vitamins and MASLD

Knowledge on other B vitamins is preliminary, with no significant associations in most publications, requiring further exploration. As for the relationship between thiamine, riboflavin, pantothenic acid, and biotin intake and MASLD risk, a number of studies have found conflicting results. An observational study in 2025 reported a primary relationship between increased total vitamin B intake and reduced fibrosis progression, providing a basis for future studies. Vitamin B1 contributes to the catabolic pathways of carbohydrates by enhancing the activity of thiamine pyrophosphate -dependent enzymes and decreasing hepatic fat content, specifically under high-calorie exposure. Meanwhile, the exact mechanistic effect on MASLD is poorly elucidated [97]. Riboflavin is also involved in redox reactions, cellular respiration and catabolic pathways of carbohydrate and fatty acid metabolism. Recent findings have focused on its regulatory functions in immunity and the gut microbiome as critical determinants of MASLD progression [98, 99]. Despite the findings on the association between B5 and a lower risk of MASLD, the related mechanistic effects remain unclear. Biotin, like the majority of B vitamins, lowers cholesterol by inhibiting lipogenic enzymes such acetyl-CoA carboxylase and fatty acid synthase. It is noteworthy that the gut microbiota, which plays a role in biotin synthesis, associates MASLD with biotin insufficiency [100–102].

Choline and MASLD

The established role of choline in MASLD is paradoxical and varies significantly with disease severity. Collectively, cross-sectional and cohort methodologies suggest the role of choline in preventing the initial onset of MASLD, but its role in more advanced stages of SLD, particularly fibrosis, is detrimental. Mechanistically, choline is essential for synthesizing phosphatidylcholine, a critical component of cell membranes and VLDL particles. Consequently, choline deficiency impairs hepatic VLDL secretion, leading directly to triglyceride accumulation and steatosis. It also triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress and hepatic apoptosis, driving fibrosis. Another crucial mechanism involves the gut-liver axis; altered gut microbiota metabolize unabsorbed choline into trimethylamine, which the liver oxidizes into TMAO—a pro-inflammatory metabolite strongly linked to MASH and lobular inflammation [15].

Overweight and obesity in terms of BMI are other contributing factors in the link between choline and MASLD, and in turn, choline intervention facilitates adipocyte and hepatic lipolysis through mitochondrial oxidative demethylation. Moreover, dietary cholesterol intake can mask the association between choline and MASLD. Taken together, choline deficiency is linked to MASLD via induced steatotic, pro-inflammatory, and fibrotic pathways, whereas adequate intake seems to be hepatoprotective. The mentioned effect depends on gender, menopausal status, as well as obesity [82].

Synthesis with previous literature

To the knowledge, previously published systematic and narrative reviews have predominantly focused on isolated micronutrients rather than the broader B-vitamin spectrum. For example, studies evaluating vitamin B12 and homocysteine in MASLD highlighted similarly contradictory evidence regarding B12 concentrations, confirming our observation that systemic B12 levels may not accurately reflect hepatic functional status in advanced disease stages [20]. Furthermore, narrative reviews exploring one-carbon metabolism have hypothesized that folate and choline deficiencies drive hepatic lipid accumulation via impaired VLDL secretion and altered epigenetic regulation [21]. Our systematic synthesis of 59 observational studies corroborates these mechanistic hypotheses on a population level, demonstrating consistent inverse associations between adequate folate/choline status and initial steatosis. However, this study uniquely synthesizes the paradoxical risks associated with specific metabolites (e.g., unmetabolized folic acid) and disease stages (e.g., choline’s positive association with fibrosis).

Qualitative assessment of evidence strength

While a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible, applying the qualitative principles of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework reveals insights into the certainty of the current evidence [103]. Because the 59 included studies are entirely observational, the baseline certainty of evidence inherently begins at ‘Low.’ For folate and choline, while there is a consistent observational trend suggesting inverse associations with early-stage steatosis, the certainty of this evidence must be downgraded to ‘Very Low.’ This downgrade is strictly driven by the reliance on cross-sectional designs (which introduces temporal bias) and indirectness regarding diagnostic modalities. For vitamin B12, the strength of evidence is similarly ‘Very Low’; it is significantly downgraded for inconsistency, as the primary literature presents highly contradictory functional outcomes (protective in early stages vs. detrimental in advanced fibrosis) heavily confounded by the stage of hepatic necrosis. Overall, while the biological plausibility linking B-vitamins to MASLD is robust, the qualitative strength of the epidemiological evidence remains ‘Low’ to ‘Very Low,’ further underscoring the urgent need for rigorously designed randomized controlled trials to establish causal certainty.

Knowledge gaps, limitations, and future directions

A primary limitation of this review is the heavy reliance on cross-sectional evidence, which strictly precludes causal inference. Consequently, any discussion of “protective” or “detrimental” effects must be interpreted solely as epidemiological associations. This design introduces a profound risk of reverse causality, particularly regarding single-timepoint biological status markers [104]. Furthermore, pervasive residual confounding limits the observational literature. B-vitamin and choline intake are intertwined with higher socioeconomic status and overall healthy lifestyle behaviors. As detailed in Supplementary Table S5, multivariable adjustments varied significantly across primary studies. Crucially, the literature largely fails to stratify synthetic supplemental intake from natural dietary sources, and rarely adjusts for highly prevalent medications. In addition, because studies reporting significant associations are more likely to be published, the inherent risk of reporting bias may be considered.

Methodological and diagnostic heterogeneity represents a second major limitation. The included studies utilized diagnostic modalities with vastly different sensitivities. While liver biopsy was rarely used, widespread reliance on ultrasonography likely misclassified individuals with mild steatosis into healthy control groups. Furthermore, non-invasive scoring indices (such as FLI) heavily incorporate BMI; because B-vitamin status is also linked to adiposity, using these indices introduces significant confounding. Analytical heterogeneity in biochemical detection (ranging from LC-MS/MS to standard immunoassays) further introduces measurement variability. Compounding this variance is the heavy reliance on overlapping NHANES survey cycles across multiple independent publications which may duplicate participant populations, artificially inflating the perceived consistency of the evidence base and overrepresenting the U.S. demographic.

Finally, significant translational and genetic gaps remain. The overarching literature lacks systematic sub-ethnic stratification, preventing the evaluation of critical genetic variables such as MTHFR polymorphisms, which dictate intrahepatic folate metabolism and vary drastically across global ethnicities. While MR studies attempt to bypass traditional confounding, their causal claims are limited by reliance on European-centric GWAS databases, limiting global generalizability. Moreover, the highly specific biochemical pathways discussed originate from murine models and in vitro experiments. Future, rigorously designed randomized controlled trials, stratified by gender, BMI, and genetic polymorphisms, are urgently required to establish true clinical utility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, while the current observational evidence suggests that specific B-vitamins, particularly folate, vitamin B12, and choline are associated with MASLD and its progressive stages, these findings must be interpreted with substantial caution. Given the highly contradictory nature of the literature, the heavy reliance on cross-sectional data, and the high risk of residual confounding, the certainty of these epidemiological associations remains very low. Consequently, we emphasize the inherent uncertainty of these relationships rather than concluding a probable clinical benefit. While plausible biological mechanisms exist to explain both the inverse correlations with early steatosis and the paradoxical positive correlations with advanced fibrosis, the current observational evidence is strictly insufficient to establish causality or advocate for targeted supplementation. Future rigorously designed randomized controlled trials are urgently required to resolve these uncertainties.
