A fluorescence-off based approach for sensitive determination of bepotastine in pharmaceutical and biological matrices considering human safety aspects: sustainability assessment
Authors: Badriah Saad Al-Farhan, Mohamed A. M. Ali, Anis Ahmad Chaudhary, Safaa F. Saleh, Angum M.M. Ibrahim, Reem H. Obaydo, Ahmed Abdulhafez Hamad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A pioneering spectrofluorimetric approach was established to facilitate the precise and environmentally considerate measurement of Bepotastine (BPST), a pharmaceutical compound utilized as a novel anti-allergy treatment. The study presents a highly dependable, sensitive, and green analytical procedure for the investigation and validation of this drug. The described methodology capitalizes on the rapid electrostatic interaction that occurs between Eosin Y and Bepotastine, a non-hazardous dye approved for biological staining use, under somewhat acidic pH settings. This interaction leads to fluorescence quenching of the bio-dye, predominantly at an emission wavelength of 556 nm. Outstanding analytical considerations were achieved, including detecting and measuring thresholds of 30.8 ng/mL and 93.5 ng/mL, respectively, across the linear working range of 100–1900 ng/mL. Extensive optimization studies were conducted to refine the conditions impacting the complexation mechanism between molecules of both the drug and the bio-dye. Rigorous validation demonstrated full compliance with ICH standards, confirming the method’s accuracy and precision. The developed method demonstrated high analytical accuracy, with recovery values ranging from 98.63% to 100.15% (relative error: -0.15–1.37%), and excellent precision, reflected by SD and RSD values < 2.0%. The method also exhibited remarkable ruggedness and robustness (RSD ≤ 1.64%), confirming its reliability and suitability for advanced analytical applications. This novel procedure was effectively employed for the evaluation of BPST in its raw state, pharmaceutical formulations, and various matrices. From an ecological viewpoint, the procedure was thoroughly assessed for sustainability using advanced green chemistry tools. Comprehensive evaluation using eight internationally recognized green assessment tools (Analytical Eco-Scale, AGREE, MoGAPI, ComplexMoGAPI, CaFRI, CACI, BAGI, and AGSA) confirmed the superior greenness and analytical reliability of the proposed method, establishing it as a sustainable and efficient choice for routine determination of bepotastine in pharmaceutical and biological samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29550-w.

Introduction

Clinical importance of bepotastine

Bepotastine (BPST, Fig. 1), chemically known as -[4-[(S)-(4-chlorophenyl)-pyridin-2-ylmethoxy]piperidin-1-yl]butanoic acid, was formulated as a pharmacological agent specifically targeting allergic reactions, particularly ocular itching. This drug functions through three key mechanisms: acting as a selective, non-sedating H1 receptor blocker, mast cells stabilizing, and inhibiting eosinophil migration into inflamed tissues, thereby mitigating tissue impairment and preventing the exacerbation of allergic conjunctivitis. Accurate quantification of BPST in ocular formulations is essential to balance therapeutic efficacy with minimized systemic absorption, which can lead to unintended side effects such as dry mouth or dizziness in vulnerable populations like children and the elderly. Typically, BPST is taken as eye droplets to relieve symptoms like redness, swelling, excessive tearing, and itchiness caused by allergens1–3. The precise determination of drug dosage in human aqueous humor has been critical for attaining therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Suboptimal BPST levels in ocular tissues can result in inadequate symptom relief, exacerbating allergic conjunctivitis and impairing patients’ quality of life. Consequently, the demand for robust and reliable analytical techniques has grown.

Brief review of reported analytical methods

Despite this, limited spectroscopic methods4–6 for BPST quantification have been reported, with most relying on chromatographic approaches6–11. However, these chromatographic methods are often hindered by extended analysis times, the consumption of significant volumes of high-grade organic solvents, and substantial economic and ecological drawbacks12. Spectroscopic techniques (photometry and fluorimetry) have emerged as attractive alternatives in pharmaceutical analysis and quality control, primarily due to their cost-effectiveness, ease of operation, and reduced environmental impact. While photometric-based procedures have been less suitable for imitated bio-matrices due to their reasonably poorer sensitivity, spectrofluorimetry has been favored for its rapid analysis, heightened sensitivity, cost-effectiveness, simplicity, and negligible sample preparation requirements13.

In light of the previously reported analytical efforts, several analytical techniques have been developed for the analysis of bepotastine (BPST) in therapeutic and biological matrices, yet most exhibit limitations in sensitivity, environmental impact, or operational simplicity. RP-HPLC and UPLC-ESI-MS/MS methods achieved good quantification ranges and sensitivity but relied on toxic organic solvents and costly instrumentation, limiting their sustainability9,10. HPTLC and UHPLC techniques offered moderate to excellent sensitivity yet required multiple solvents and long preparation times, reducing practicality for routine use. UV–Vis spectrophotometric techniques were simple and low-cost but lacked sufficient sensitivity for trace or biological analyses6.

Earlier fluorimetric approaches attained satisfactory sensitivity (LOD ≈ 0.04–0.05 µg/mL) but employed non-green reagents such as surfactants and phosphoric acid5, or were limited to ophthalmic products without biological validation.

In response to the challenges outlined above, the developed spectrofluorimetric method provides enhanced sensitivity (LOD = 30.8 ng/mL; LOQ = 93.5 ng/mL), a wider linear range (100–1900 ng/mL), and a short analysis time (< 5 min). Using the biocompatible dye EY in an aqueous medium, the method eliminates hazardous solvents and demonstrates strong matrix tolerance through validation in spiked plasma, urine, and artificial aqueous humor. These attributes collectively highlight its analytical robustness, environmental safety, and superior applicability, positioning it as a sustainable alternative among reported BPST determination methods.

Knowledge gap and need for the current method

Fluorescent xanthene-based bio-dyes have been widely explored as probes through ion-pairing mechanisms14–16. However, dyes like merbromin, containing extremely toxic mercury, were deemed unsuitable due to mercury’s association with irreversible neurological damage, posing significant occupational and environmental health risks. Similarly, Rhodamine 6G and related analogs couldn’t be employed due to their inability to form electrostatic interactions with BPST. In contrast, Eosin Y (EY, Fig. 1) has been extensively used as a fluorescent sensor thanks to its exceptional sensitivity in detecting various analytes13,17–19.

In the development of the proposed method, eosin Y (EY) was selected due to its high fluorescence stability and excellent biocompatibility, making it an appropriate reagent for sensitive pharmaceutical and analytical applications. Its use enhances the safety and reliability of the analytical procedure by avoiding hazardous substances, in accordance with the principles of Green Analytical Chemistry, which promote safer and more sustainable analytical practices and protect human health20–22.

The developed study utilized Eosin Y for BPST analysis by forming an electrostatic complexation of the analyte to the EY dye. In a moderately acidic environment, the substituted amine moiety in BPST became protonated, creating a positive species that interacted electrically with the negative dye molecules. An acetate buffer controlled the pH of the reaction medium. This complex significantly quenched the fluorescence of Eosin Y, enabling quantification via spectrofluorimetric analysis. From sustainability aspects, the increased awareness of environmental apprehension in the scientific population has underscored the need for analytical methodologies that are both effective and environmentally sustainable. Green methods like this EY-based approach not only reduce ecological footprints but also safeguard human health by minimizing exposure to toxic reagents and solvents—such as acetonitrile in HPLC—throughout the drug analysis lifecycle—from sample preparation to quantification. This study examined the proposed technique by combining its metrological performance and ecological impact, following the guidelines and principles set forth in green and sustainable Analytical Chemistry. The incorporation of spectrofluorimetry into pharmaceutical analysis enhances analytical efficiency and reliability, facilitating precise quantification and rigorous quality evaluation of therapeutic agents, which, in turn, indirectly supports patient safety by ensuring accurate dosing and consistent formulation quality, essential for effective allergy management and the prevention of adverse outcomes.

Notably, few fluorimetric methods4,5 for BPST determination have been reported, highlighting a critical knowledge gap. This gap necessitates a spectrofluorimetric approach over chromatographic methods, as it offers superior sensitivity for trace-level detection in complex matrices like aqueous humor, while addressing the environmental and operational limitations of existing techniques.

Study objectives

The primary goal of this research was to design a novel, sustainable, selective, and sensitive fluorescence-anchored method for quantifying BPST in diverse matrices, such as pure samples, pharmaceutical formulations, and aqueous humor. By ensuring precise BPST quantification, this method supports personalized medicine, tailoring doses to individual patient needs and reducing the burden of allergic diseases on healthcare systems. The inherent fluorescence of Eosin Y was proportionally quenched by BPST, facilitating its simple and accurate measurement in ophthalmic drops and simulated aqueous humor. The significant quenching effect enabled the fluorimetric detection of BPST with a remarkably sensitive detection limit. Such sensitivity is crucial for detecting trace BPST levels in pediatric patients, where even minor dosing errors can have disproportionate health impacts. Robustness and practical applicability were verified through effective quantification in pharmaceutical and simulated biological matrices. This newly constructed spectrofluorimetric technique offers a practical and efficient alternative to traditional analytical applications. The procedure was validated under the International Council for Harmonization (I.C.H.) standards23, ensuring its reliability for both quality and quantity BPST assessment. Compliance with ICH guidelines reinforces the method’s suitability for global analysis adoption, ensuring that allergy treatments meet safety benchmarks for diverse patient populations. The developed method proved to be effective, trustworthy, and highly sensitive for pharmaceutical form testing. The validation practice evaluated considerations such as sensitivity limits, linearity, accuracy, and precision, all of which conformed to regulatory standards. This approach offered notable advantages over existing BPST analysis methods, including simpler and quicker sample preparation, reduced resource consumption, and minimal operational effort. Moreover, the method required easily operable instruments, making it accessible to researchers with varied expertise.

Experimental

Apparatus

The fluorescence peaks were acquired using a SCINCO fluorometer (South Korea) fitted with a 150 W X-arc lamp. The scanning speed was maintained at 574 nm/min (to ensure high precision in data collection without distortion, being a relatively slow speed to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio in fluorescence measurements), and the time of integration was at 50 ms. An SC-101TH (bath sonicator) was used to sonicate the solutions. The pH measurements of all solutions were recorded by the electronic pH meter (AD11P) supplied by Adwa (Romania).

Materials and reagents

Bepotastine, in its pure form (99.80%), was generously provided by Rameda for Therapeutic Industry, Ltd., located in Giza (6th of October City), Egypt. This material was used as received without additional purification. Commercial ophthalmic formulations, including Corenfresh® (15 mg/mL) and Lergeblock® (1.5%), were procured from local pharmacies. Eosin Y (developed in distilled water), a dye reagent of 0.020% w/v concentration, was sourced from the UK, Market Harborough.

Solvents and solutions

All solvents and chemicals required for the procedure were obtained from El-Nasr company for pharmaceutical and Industrial Chemicals, Cairo, Egypt. The solvents involved acetonitrile, dioxane, alcohols, acetone, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and dimethylformamide (DMF). Additionally, hydrochloric acid liquid and sodium hydroxide scales were employed as auxiliary reagents.

Buffer solutions and compositions

Several buffer solutions, such as acetate, Britton-Robinson, McIlvaine, and Teorell-Stenhagen buffers, were explored to identify the optimal pH for the reaction medium.

Britton–Robinson24 (0.04 M each of acetic, phosphoric, and boric acids adjusted with NaOH), McIlvaine25 (0.2 M disodium hydrogen phosphate and 0.1 M citric acid), an acetate26 (0.1 M sodium acetate and 0.1 M acetic acid), and Teorell–Stenhagen27,28 (acetic, phosphoric, and boric acids with NaOH). Each buffer was examined for its pH stability and influence on the fluorescence quenching efficiency of the BPST–dye system.

Preparation of stock and working solutions

A parent stock solution of BPST was prepared by dispersing 5 mg of BPST drug in ethanol within a 50 mL volumetric flask. The resulting solution was further diluted to obtain the required working range for investigation and then kept at 2–8 °C, protected from light, to maintain stability. The resulting solution was further diluted to the required working concentrations for analysis, and then stored, protected from light, at 2–8 °C to preserve stability.

General analytical steps

Standard procedure for BPST determination

An aliquot of 1.0 mL of the BPST solution, covering the working concentration range of 100–1900 ng/mL, was transferred into a series of 10.0 mL calibrated volumetric flasks. All flasks received 1.8 mL of acetate buffer (pH 3.8) and 2.0 mL of the Eosin Y biological dye solution. The volume was made up to the calibration mark using distilled water. The mixtures were allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min to ensure complete interaction. The fluorescence response was then recorded at an emission wavelength of 556 nm (excitation at 302 nm).

Calibration curve construction

The alteration in the dye’s intrinsic fluorescence amplitude (ΔF) was calculated by subtracting the fluorescence intensity of the test solutions from that of the blank solution (ΔF = Fblank – Fsample), since the interaction between BPST and Eosin Y involves fluorescence quenching. A calibration plot was then constructed by plotting the obtained ΔF values against the corresponding BPST concentrations within the studied range.

Blank and control experiments

A blank solution was prepared under identical conditions, excluding BPST, and served as the reference. Control experiments were conducted to confirm the absence of interference from excipients or matrix components, ensuring the selectivity and reliability of the method.

Assay of BPST in ophthalmic formulations

A measured volume of Corenfresh® ophthalmic solution equivalent to 5.0 mg of BPST was diluted with injectable water. The solution was subjected to the procedural steps described in the “General Analytical Steps” section. Recovery percentages were determined using the calibration curve generated during method validation.

Measurement of BPST in synthetic aqueous humor

A synthetic aqueous humor liquid was prepared following a previously reported method to closely imitate the human aqueous humor composition29.

To mimic the native composition of human aqueous humor, an artificial medium was prepared in two separate portions. Part I consisted of sodium chloride (7.4 mg), potassium chloride (0.40 mg), sodium bicarbonate (2.2 mg), and disodium phosphate (0.43 mg), each dissolved in water suitable for injection. Part II contained calcium chloride dihydrate (3.85 mg), glutathione disulfide (4.6 mg), dextrose (23.0 mg), and magnesium chloride hexahydrate (5.0 mg) per milliliter. For the final preparation, 10 mL of Part II was carefully blended with 240 mL of Part I, and the pH of the resulting solution was finely adjusted to 7.4 using sodium hydroxide or hydrochloric acid, as required29. A 1.0 mL aliquot of this synthetic sample was transferred to a chain of 10 mL volumetric flasks, followed by the addition of measured volumes of BPST. The analysis was carried out following the steps listed in the “General Analytical Steps” section. The percentages of recoveries were determined based on the calibration graph constructed during the study.

Results and discussion

In an aqueous environment, the fluorescence emission of Eosin Y was tracked at a wavelength of 556 nm upon excitation at 302 nm (Fig. 2). A significant quenching in the dye’s inherent fluorescence intensity occurred when BPST was introduced into the system. This decline in the inherent fluorescence amplitude was attributed to the construction of an electrostatic complex developed between the BPST molecules and the dye, EY. A direct correlation was established between the BPST drug concentration and the degree of the quenching response observed.

Method validation

The analytical approach was validated under carefully controlled conditions, adhering to the guidelines established by ICH guidelines.

Linearity and range

The approach linearity was evaluated by measuring the responses of standard solutions containing varying doses of the analyte. Fluorescence intensity reductions (∆RFI) were recorded for these solutions, and a calibration chart was constructed to link the net quenching effect to the respective concentrations of BPST. A linear regression analysis was performed, yielding critical analytical metrics detailed in Table 2. The correlation coefficient (r²), determined to be 0.9996, demonstrated remarkable linearity. This indicated that the net quenching effect was directly proportional to BPST concentrations across the 0.10 to 1.9 µg mL⁻¹ range.

Sensitivity evaluation

The sensitivity of the method was checked by determining the detection limit (LOD) and quantification limit (LOQ). These values were calculated using the formulas LOQ = 10σ/S and LOD = 0.333LOQ, where σ represents the standard deviation of the y-intercept and S denotes the slope of the calibration chart. The LOD was determined to be 0.0308 µg mL⁻¹, while the LOQ was calculated as 0.0935 µg mL⁻¹ (Table 2).

Evaluation of the method in spiked biological matrices

Given the high sensitivity, selectivity, and remarkable signal stability of the developed method, its applicability in biological environments was further evaluated through analyses of exogenously spiked biological samples, including plasma and urine, as well as synthetic aqueous humor, which closely simulates the actual ocular milieu. The method demonstrated excellent analytical performance, characterized by stable fluorescence signals (RSD < 1.98%) and high recovery values in all tested matrices, 98.03–99.70% for spiked plasma, 99.61–101.92% for spiked urine, and 98.06–101.76% for synthetic aqueous humor (Table 6). These results confirm the method’s robustness against potential matrix interferences and its strong capability to maintain analytical accuracy and reproducibility within complex biological systems.

Overall, the proposed method combines exceptional analytical sensitivity and reliability, rendering it suitable for applications involving bio analytical and pharmaceutical evaluation in biologically relevant matrices. The optimized fluorometric design, based on the stability and selectivity of the formed BPST–EY complex, enabled superior performance compared to previously reported spectrofluorimetric methods, particularly regarding signal stability, reproducibility, and accurate quantification of low concentration levels down to 93.5 ng/mL (LOQ).

The successful application of the method to spiked biological matrices highlights its broad analytical applicability and promising potential for future use in the quantitative evaluation of ophthalmic formulations and in monitoring trace drug levels within complex biological environments. This demonstrates the analytical robustness and sustainability of the proposed approach as a reliable tool in modern ocular drug analysis.

Molar ratio assessment

The stoichiometry of the interaction between BPST and Eosin Y dye was elucidated using Job’s method of continuous variation30. Equimolar solutions of the two species were prepared, and varying mole fractions ratios between 0.1 and 0.9 were used to create a chain of solutions while maintaining a constant total amount of 1.0 mL. These solutions were moved to 10 mL volumetric flasks, filled with water to the prim, and subjected to analysis under the optimized experimental conditions. Blank corrections were applied to all measurements. The results, depicted in Fig. 5, established a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio between BPST and Eosin Y in the complex.

Comparative evaluation of existing analytical methods for BPST

Several analytical methods have been reported for the determination of bepotastine (BPST) in pharmaceutical and biological matrices, each with distinct analytical performance and environmental implications (Table S2). The RP-HPLC method quantified BPST over a linear range of 50–200 µg/mL with LOD = 0.0543 µg/mL and LOQ = 0.01646 µg/mL, but relied heavily on hazardous organic solvents such as acetonitrile and methanol, increasing both operational cost and environmental burden9‎. The UPLC-ESI-MS/MS technique achieved excellent sensitivity (LOD = 0.06 ng/mL; LOQ = 0.2 ng/mL) and a total run time of about 2 min, yet it required expensive instrumentation and toxic reagents (e.g., ethyl acetate, formic acid), which limit its sustainability10‎.

The HPTLC method covered a linear range of 0.5–5 µg/band with LOD = 0.0415 µg/spot and LOQ = 0.124 µg/spot, but involved a 20-minute chamber saturation time and used multiple non-eco-friendly solvents, reducing its greenness6.UHPLC offered ultra-low detection limits (LOD = 0.00038 µg/mL; LOQ = 0.00115 µg/mL) and short retention times (~ 3 min), but its application remained limited to pharmaceutical formulations only6. UV-Vis spectrophotometric methods were simple and low-cost (LOD = 2.2–3.66 µg/mL; LOQ = 6.6–11.0 µg/mL) but lacked the sensitivity required for trace-level or biological analyses6.

Earlier fluorimetric methods achieved satisfactory sensitivity (LOD = 0.046 µg/mL; LOQ = 0.14 µg/mL) using SDS as a micellar enhancer, but involved non-green reagents like phosphoric acid and surfactants5. Another study using the safe food dye erythrosine B obtained LOD = 39 ng/mL; LOQ = 118 ng/mL, though it was restricted to ophthalmic formulations without validation in biological matrices4‎.

In contrast, the present spectrofluorimetric method demonstrated a broader linear range (100–1900 ng/mL) and superior sensitivity (LOD = 30.8 ng/mL; LOQ = 93.5 ng/mL), while maintaining a rapid analysis time (< 5 min). The method is also simpler, safer, and greener, employing the biocompatible fluorescent dye eosin Y in an aqueous medium free from hazardous solvents. Moreover, it was validated in spiked plasma, urine, and synthetic aqueous humor, confirming its robustness, matrix tolerance, and wide bio analytical applicability. Thus, the proposed approach uniquely combines high sensitivity, operational simplicity, and environmental sustainability, establishing it as a promising and eco-friendly alternative for the reliable quantification of BPST in pharmaceutical and biological contexts.

Fluorescence quenching mechanism

A proposed structure for the BPST-EY complex is illustrated in Fig. 1. EY, a diacidic dye (H₂L), predominantly existed in its monoanionic form (HL⁻) at pH 3.8 due to its pKa values of 2.80 and 4.931. The hydroxyl group, positioned near a strongly electron-withdrawing bromine moiety on the xanthene framework, was preferentially deprotonated under acidic settings. Concurrently, the amino group of BPST (-NH-) was protonated forming the cationic species BPST-H⁺. Electrostatic interactions between BPST-H⁺ and HL⁻ led to the formation of a neutral 1:1 ion-pair complex. The complex stability was conferred by electrostatic attraction and hydrophobic interactions. Specifically, a hydrogen bond was proposed between the protonated nitrogen species of BPST and the deprotonated hydroxyl group within the EY molecule (Fig. 1). These interactions altered the electron distribution within Eosin Y’s conjugated π-electron system, triggering a fluorescence quenching response.

The reduction in fluorescence intensity observed upon adding BPST was attributed to quenching mechanisms involving energy transfer, collisional interactions, and excited-state reactions. To elucidate the predominant quenching mechanism, the Stern-Volmer equation was applied:

The studying of BPST-EY system stability

To evaluate the temporal characteristics of the developed BPST–EY fluorometric system, a comprehensive investigation was performed focusing on both the reaction completion time and the stability profile of the resulting complex. This assessment aimed to establish the optimal measurement window and ensure the analytical reliability of the proposed method. At ambient temperature, the BPST–EY complex formation was initiated immediately after mixing the reactant solutions and reached completion within approximately 5 min, as confirmed by the stabilization of the fluorescence quenching response. The obtained signal remained nearly constant for more than 60 min, indicating excellent temporal stability of the developed system (SI, Fig. 2). Such stability ensures that accurate and reproducible measurements can be performed within this time frame without significant signal drift, thereby confirming the robustness and suitability of the BPST–EY system for routine analytical applications.

Temperature-dependent behavior of the BPST–eosin Y quenching system

The influence of temperature on the fluorescence quenching behavior of the BPST–Eosin Y system was systematically investigated over the temperature range of 298–333 K under identical experimental conditions. As presented in Fig. 6, increasing the temperature caused a consistent and progressive decrease in fluorescence quenching efficiency (ΔF). A accompanied by a corresponding decline in the Stern–Volmer constant (KSV) from 4.115 × 102 M-1 at 298 K, to 1.862 × 102 M-1 at 333 K. This monotonic decline in KSV values clearly indicates that the quenching mechanism is predominantly static, governed by the formation of a ground-state ion-pair complex between Eosin Y and BPST33,34.

The observed temperature dependence reflects the partial dissociation of the preformed ground-state complex at elevated temperatures, leading to diminished quenching efficiency14. The calculated binding constants exhibited a similar trend, showing a gradual decrease with rising temperature, which further corroborates the static quenching mechanism and confirms that thermal agitation weakens the intermolecular interactions responsible for complex stabilization. These findings align well with established photo physical principles describing static quenching in dye–drug systems, where complex formation in the ground state precedes photon absorption and emission processes33,35.

From an analytical standpoint, the temperature-dependent behavior of this system carries significant implications. Maintaining room-temperature conditions (298 ± 2 K) was found to yield the highest quenching magnitude and Stern–Volmer constant, ensuring maximal analytical sensitivity and reproducibility. Conversely, performing the assays at elevated temperatures (above 318 K) leads to decreased complex stability, reduced fluorescence quenching, and consequently poorer precision and sensitivity of the analytical method36,37. Thus, it can be concluded that optimal analytical performance of the BPST–Eosin Y system is achieved under ambient laboratory conditions, where the static ion-pair complex remains sufficiently stable to provide robust and reproducible fluorescence responses38.

Spectral characterization and reaction confirmation of the BPST–eosin Y complex

The formation of the BPST–Eosin Y complex was thoroughly characterized and spectroscopically confirmed through comprehensive absorption and fluorescence behavior. In an aqueous acidic medium, free Eosin Y exhibited a characteristic absorption maximum (λabs) at approximately 515 nm and an emission peak (λem) at 550─560 nm upon excitation at around 302 nm (Fig. 2). Upon the gradual addition of BPST, a systematic decrease in both absorption and fluorescence intensities was observed, accompanied by a slight bathochromic shift in the absorption spectrum (Fig. S3). Such spectroscopic behavior provides strong evidence of molecular interaction between the cationic form of BPST and the anionic xanthene chromophore of EY. Additionally, these spectral alterations offer direct evidence of a ground-state interaction rather than a transient collisional process, thus supporting the static quenching mechanism.

This quenching phenomenon arises from electrostatic attraction between the protonated tertiary amino group of BPST and the anionic xanthene chromophore moiety of Eosin Y, leading to the formation of a stable ion-pair complex. The complexation perturbs the π–π* electronic transitions of the dye and reduces its radiative emission probability, resulting in the observed decrease in fluorescence intensity. Similar ion-pair formation between xanthene dyes and cationic drugs has been reported as a characteristic pathway associated with static quenching processes33.

Bathochromic shift in Eosin Y is a well-documented phenomenon arising when the dye interacts with other molecules — such as proteins, metal ions, or basic drugs — or when aggregation occurs. The shift of the absorption maximum may be attributed to electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and charge-transfer processes that extend π-electron delocalization and reduce the HOMO–LUMO energy gap of the dye molecule39.

Further confirmation of complex formation was obtained through UV–Vis spectral analysis, where new, less intense absorption bands appeared with increasing BPST concentration, indicating the formation of a distinct molecular entity with altered electronic properties. Additionally, temperature-dependent Stern–Volmer analysis revealed decreasing quenching constants (KSV) at elevated temperatures, confirming the ground-state nature of the complex. The stoichiometric ratio, determined via Job’s continuous variation method (1:1), further supports this conclusion.

Collectively, these findings confirm that the BPST–Eosin Y complex is formed through a robust and stable electrostatic ion-pair association in the ground state, responsible for the fluorescence quenching observed. This characterization not only validates the proposed quenching mechanism but also highlights the analytical robustness, sensitivity, and reproducibility of the developed fluorometric system.

Insights on the greenness and sustainability concepts

Over the past decade, Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC) has drawn significant academic interest, aiming to develop environmentally conscious methodologies that reduce ecological harm. This approach focused on minimizing or eliminating the use of hazardous substances and solvents to decrease waste generation, employing carefully structured strategies and evaluation tools to design GAC frameworks40. However, GAC alone was insufficient to address all the ecological challenges associated with chemical analysis. In response, a broader concept called White Analytical Chemistry (WAC) was introduced by Nowak et al. in 202141. WAC provided a more inclusive sustainability perspective by addressing GAC’s limitations, integrating both ecological and operational attributes within its assessment model.

This study aimed to establish an environmentally friendly analytical procedure for determining BPST, guided by the principles of green chemistry and incorporating advanced sustainability platforms to promote eco-friendly pharmaceutical analysis strategies.

Evaluation of the procedure sustainability (comprehensive evaluation)

Growing awareness of environmental concerns in the scientific community necessitated that analytical methodologies be designed to meet ecological sustainability requirements while ensuring their effectiveness. Evaluations of these methods were performed by incorporating metrological data quality alongside ecological impacts, applying established criteria and tools as defined in Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC) principles.

Approach assessment via eco-scale platform

The Analytical Eco-Scale is a quantitative tool for evaluating the environmental impact of analytical methods, starting with a base score of 100, from which penalty points are subtracted based on reagent/solvent consumption, energy consumption, occupational hazards, and waste generation42. Methods with scores ≥ 75 are classified as excellent green methods, 50–75 as acceptable green methods, and < 50 inadequate green methods. The proposed method scored exceptionally (95/100), classifying it as an excellent green method (≥ 75). Limited quantities of solvents were used: 1.8 mL of EY solution, 1.8 mL of acetate buffer (pH 3.8), and water for preparing and diluting the standard solutions. Limited penalty points (5 points) included reagent use (1 point), waste generation (2 points), and moderate energy consumption by the spectrofluorimeter (2 points). The method completely avoided toxic organic solvents, relied on a simple aqueous medium, and required no harsh derivatization steps.

In contrast, the published method achieved a good score (82/100) but with a narrower margin. It used 1 mL of SDS solution (1% w/v), phosphoric acid solution (0.1 M), and larger quantities of ethanol. It incurred larger penalty points (18 points) due to the more intensive use of unsafe chemical reagents and the need for more careful waste management43,44. The large difference (+ 13 points) reflects a stronger commitment to green chemistry principles in the proposed method (Table 7).

Sustainability analysis through AGREE design

This comprehensive metric evaluates the twelve principles of Green Analytical Chemistry using a visual circular pictogram with graded colors, with an overall score (0–1)45. The proposed method achieved an excellent score (0.78), surpassing the published method (0.66) by a notable margin (+ 0.12). The proposed method complied with several key principles: Principle 1 (direct analysis) requiring only very simple sample treatment limited to dilution; Principle 2 (small sample size) using only 1 mL; Principle 7 (waste minimization) generating less than 10 mL waste per sample; Principle 10 (reagent type; use of safe re and bio-based reagents) using FDA-approved biological-grade dye Eosin Y. The circular pictogram of the proposed method showed clear predominance of green color in most sectors, with limited yellow ratings in aspects such as sampling procedure (off-line analysis), positioning of the analytical devise (off-line), degree of automation and miniaturization (manual operation), and energy consumption. In contrast, the published method displayed a balanced mixture of green and yellow colors, with some orange ratings in criteria for waste generation and reagent use, reflecting good, though not optimal, environmental performance (Table 7).

MoGAPI (Modified green analytical procedure Index)

This metric combines a colored visual assessment and an overall quantitative score by evaluating five main aspects through 15 specific criteria46. The proposed method scored excellently (86/100), surpassing the published method by a moderate margin (+ 3 points). The proposed method excelled in multiple criteria: off-line sample collection without the need for special preservation or storage under exceptional conditions; very simple treatment without complex extraction or derivatization steps; use of less than 10 mL of solvents per sample; notably low-toxicity reagents (NFPA health hazard score ≤ 1); low safety hazards (NFPA flammability/instability ratings ≤ 1); low energy consumption (≤ 0.1 kWh/sample); limited waste generation (1–10 mL) with appropriate treatment applied. The pentagonal pictogram showed a clear predominance of the green color in most sections, with some yellow areas in energy consumption and waste. In contrast, the published method achieved a comparable excellent rating but scored slightly lower due to reduced performance in several criteria: use of different reagents, inclusion of an organic solvent (ethanol), moderate toxicity (NFPA health hazard 2–3), moderate safety hazards, slightly higher energy consumption, and elevated safety concerns associated with SDS handling and phosphoric acid use. The pentagonal pictogram displayed a balanced mixture of green and yellow colors, with some red areas in the safety and reagent criteria (Table 7).

ComplexMoGAPI

The newly developed tool extends the MoGAPI framework to include a comprehensive evaluation of preanalytical processes, such as reagent preparation and material synthesis, through the addition of a hexagonal section dedicated to assessing synthesis, purification, and reagent preparation stages47. The assessment extended to pre-analytical processes, where the proposed method achieved 92/100 compared to 85/100 for the published method, demonstrating a 7-point advantage. This metric adds a hexagonal section evaluating synthesis, purification, and reagent preparation stages. The proposed method excelled in this additional dimension by utilizing commercially available bio-safe EY dye requiring no complex synthesis, achieving high yield (> 89%), requiring minimal purification steps, and demonstrating excellent E-factor performance (total waste to product ratio). The published method, while using commercially available reagents (SDS and phosphoric acid), scored lower due to moderate yield (70–89%), requirement for standard purification procedures, and higher waste generation during the complete analytical lifecycle from reagent preparation through final analysis (Table 7).

CaFRI (carbon footprint reduction index)

CaFRI is an innovative metric focusing primarily on the carbon footprint of analytical methods. It evaluates several key criteria including: energy consumption, CO₂ emissions, sample storage, transportation, personnel requirements, waste management, recycling, and reagent use. Methods with scores ≥ 75 are classified as excellent in carbon footprint reduction48. The proposed and published methodologies achieved an identical and excellent score of 85/100 on the CaFRI scale, signifying that both are highly suitable for routine application and exhibit strong sustainability credentials. This equivalent final rating confirms that the strengths and weaknesses of each method are essentially balanced. Both methods demonstrated high performance in common criteria, such as low energy consumption for the analytical instruments (< 0.1 kW per sample) and limited personnel requirements (one to two persons), which are key factors for operational suitability. Furthermore, neither method required special sample handling, such as deep freezing or high-pressure conditions, nor did they necessitate extensive sample transportation, thereby contributing significantly to their high scores. Both methods also achieved a favorable assessment regarding environmental inputs, specifically through the use of reagents with a low carbon footprint and a low emission factor (< 0.1 kg CO₂ per kWh). The visual “footprint” pictogram for both methodologies likewise showed a clear predominance of green color across most evaluated criteria, underscoring their comparable green profiles. However, this equal final score results from similar trade-offs in both procedures; both methods exhibited shared weaknesses, such as moderate waste generation (≈ 10 mL per sample) and the use of specialized auxiliary substances that require specific waste-treatment measures (such as SDS or EY), which prevented either method from achieving a perfect green score (Table 7).

CACI (Click analytical chemistry index)

According to the Click Analytical Chemistry Index (CACI), this innovative metric emphasizes the practicality and real-world applicability of analytical methods by assessing several essential criteria that reflect operational efficiency and sustainability49. The proposed method achieved an excellent CACI score (81/100), surpassing the published method by three points (78/100). It demonstrated superior performance across most parameters, including minimal sample volume (< 1 mL), extremely simple sample preparation, readily available commercial reagents, and instrumentation commonly accessible in routine analytical laboratories. Furthermore, it exhibited very low operational cost, accurate quantitative performance, exceptionally high sensitivity (LOD = 30.8 ng/mL), and a remarkably fast total analysis time (≤ 5 min for both preparation and measurement). The method therefore demonstrates high overall applicability across the core criteria, further enhancing its environmental and practical profile. In contrast, the published method scored comparably well in most of these criteria but showed slightly lower performance in terms of sensitivity (LOD = 46 ng/mL), overall cost, and safety or availability of reagents and chemicals, in addition to a slightly longer analysis time. Collectively, these findings confirm that the proposed method offers a more efficient, rapid, and eco-compatible analytical alternative, aligning well with the CACI framework for sustainable analytical chemistry (Table 7).

BAGI (blue applicability grade index)

This complementary metric evaluates ten practical criteria related to the applicability aspects of White Analytical Chemistry. It assesses ten attributes, including type of analysis, number of analytes, instrumentation requirements, simultaneous sample treatment, sample preparation scale, sample throughput, reagent availability, preconcentration needs, degree of automation, and sample amount50. BAGI focuses on practical applicability aspects, where the proposed method scored 77.5/100 compared to 72.5/100 for the reported method, representing a 5-point advantage. The proposed method demonstrated superiority in: quantitative and confirmatory analysis capabilities, simple instrumentation available in most quality control laboratories, simple treatment of samples using standard laboratory equipment, higher throughput, commonly available commercial reagents, no preconcentration requirement meeting sensitivity criteria directly, and small sample volume. The reported method achieved a slightly lower score, primarily due to diminished performance across several criteria, including the use of different reagents, the incorporation of an organic solvent (ethanol), moderate toxicity levels (NFPA health hazard 2–3), increased safety risks, marginally higher energy consumption, and additional safety concerns related to the handling of SDS and phosphoric acid (Table 7).

AGSA (analytical green star area)

AGSA evaluates all twelve principles of Green Analytical Chemistry in a visual star format51, where the proposed method achieved 70.83% compared to 63.89% for the published method, representing a 7% advantage. The proposed method demonstrated strong performance across multiple principles: Principle 1 (moderate sample treatment with simple dilution), Principle 2 (sample size less than 1 mL), Principle 3 (measurements in local laboratory facilities), Principle 4 (moderate process integration with separate but streamlined steps), Principle 5 (semi-automated and semi-miniaturized procedures), Principle 6 (no derivatization required), Principle 7 (waste generation less than 100 mL per sample with proper disposal), Principle 9 (energy consumption below 1.5 kW), Principle 11 (low toxicity with ≤ 2 hazard pictograms), and Principle 12 (high operator safety requiring minimal personal protective equipment). The published method scored lower particularly in Principles 7, 10, 11, and 12 due to larger waste volumes, non-renewable reagent dependence, moderate toxicity (3–5 hazard pictograms), and increased safety precautions required for handling SDS and concentrated phosphoric acid (Table 7).

The comprehensive and systematic assessment using eight internationally recognized green metrics demonstrated consistent and clear superiority of the proposed method across all environmental assessment dimensions, with an overall average improvement of 6.25 points/units (+ 13, + 0.12, + 3, +7, + 0, +3, + 5, +7%).

Based on the comprehensive multi-metric assessment, the proposed eosin Y-based fluorimetric method demonstrates unequivocal environmental superiority and is strongly recommended for adoption in pharmaceutical quality control laboratories. The method achieves optimal balance between analytical excellence (LOD: 30.8 ng/mL, linear range: 100–1900 ng/mL), environmental sustainability (average improvement: +6.25 points across all metrics), and practical applicability. The consistent superiority across all eight internationally recognized green metrics (Analytical Eco-Scale, AGREE, MoGAPI, ComplexMoGAPI, CaFRI, CACI, BAGI, AGSA) provides robust evidence supporting its implementation as the method of choice for routine bepotastine determination in eye drops and biological matrices. This method represents a paradigm for sustainable analytical chemistry, successfully integrating the principles of green chemistry, white analytical chemistry, and click analytical chemistry into a unified, practical, and environmentally responsible analytical approach suitable for modern pharmaceutical analysis (Table 7).

Conclusion

The study developed a sustainable spectroscopic method for bepotastine quantification, adhering to green chemistry standards. This approach not only minimizes environmental impact but also safeguards human health by eliminating volatile organic solvents, reducing occupational exposure risks for laboratory personnel and potential toxicity to patients. The method exhibited high sensitivity, selectivity, and accuracy within a range of 100–1900 ng/mL, facilitated by an ion-coupling complex formed under faintly acidic conditions. Such precision ensures reliable therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), critical for preventing under- or overdosing in vulnerable populations like pediatric or elderly patients, who are more susceptible to adverse effects such as sedation or cardiovascular complications. Its single-step process, using water as the solvent, avoided volatile organic solutions and lengthy extractions, emphasizing simplicity, eco-friendliness, and efficiency. The elimination of toxic reagents aligns with goals for sustainable healthcare, promoting safer pharmaceutical practices, and reducing long-term public health risks from chemical pollution. The overall assessment employing eight internationally recognized green evaluation tools — Analytical Eco-Scale, AGREE, MoGAPI, ComplexMoGAPI, CaFRI, CACI, BAGI, and AGSA — confirmed the remarkable environmental performance, analytical efficiency, and innovative nature of the proposed method. The consistent superiority across all metrics substantiates its potential as a sustainable, reliable, and eco-conscious approach for the routine determination of bepotastine in pharmaceutical formulations, biological samples and quality control.
