Exploring the experiences of GPs in establishing and operating the chronic disease management programme in clinical practice in Ireland. A qualitative study
Authors: Stephen Buckley, James O’ Flynn, Tony Foley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of chronic diseases, in the context of an ageing population and increasing life expectancy, is one of the major challenges facing the Irish health system. In 2020, a chronic disease management programme [CDM] was introduced in Irish general practice as part of a transformation in health policy to meet the changing needs of the population.ObjectivesTo explore GPs’ experiences and perceptions of the impact of the introduction of CDM on general practice in Ireland.Methods18 semi-structured interviews were conducted with GPs in clinical practice from across Ireland. GPs were purposefully recruited to capture the differing experiences faced by GPs in large and small practices in both urban and rural settings. Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsGPs were enthusiastic about aspects of CDM, which they felt provided an improved framework for their approach to the management of chronic diseases. However, they described several challenges to implementation, especially around capacity constraints. CDM could have unintended consequences for aspects of routine GP care. GPs described how practice nurses had taken on a central role in both clinical and administrative aspects of the programme.ConclusionsGPs value the framework provided by CDM for their approach to the management of chronic disease. However, challenges around practice capacity and its impact both on the delivery of CDM and aspects of routine care highlight the importance for policy makers to provide continued support to strengthen the general practice infrastructure.

Introduction

In Ireland, chronic diseases are among the leading causes of both death and disability [1,2]. The increasing prevalence of chronic diseases both in Ireland and across Europe occurs in the context of ageing populations and presents a major challenge for health systems [3,4]. The WHO advocates a primary and community care led integrated approach to managing the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases [5]. By international comparison, Ireland has historically had a ‘hospital-centric’ health system, with underdeveloped and ‘fragmented’ community care services, leading to higher levels of hospital admissions from chronic diseases [3,4]. The 2017 ‘Sláintecare Report’ outlines a 10-year plan for the development of the Irish health service, with re-orientation away from the historic ‘hospital-centric’ model, towards an integrated care model, based on the development of primary and community care services to meet the changing healthcare demands of the Irish population [3,4]. The Chronic Disease Management Programme (CDM) for general practice, launched in 2020, was developed as part of this new policy [6].

The introduction of CDM marks a paradigm shift in both the public funding model and clinical approach to chronic disease management for general practice in Ireland. GPs in Ireland are self-employed and operate through a mixed funding model, with patients either paying out-of-pocket to see their GP or via General Medical Services Card (GMS) and Doctor Visit Card (DVC) schemes, which are means tested and allow holders to access GP services for free at the point of care, covering approximately 43% of the population [7,8]. Public funding for general practice through these two schemes has historically been in the form of capitation grants to GPs, paid out of the national annual health budget, for the provision of acute care. This funding model has not incentivised the proactive management of chronic disease in general practice and resulted in high levels of hospital admissions from chronic diseases [3,4]. CDM introduces a fee-for-service funding model to develop the management of chronic disease in general practice.

CDM is a structured programme for the management of seven chronic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, COPD and ischaemic heart disease [4,6]. The programme provides for six monthly reviews with both the patient’s GP and practice nurse to monitor treatment, review medications, and agree an individual care plan (see Table 1) [9]. Since January 2022 CDM is open to patients aged 18 and over with GMS or DVC cards who have a diagnosis of at least one of the qualifying conditions. Although a recent development in Irish general practice, similar programmes are well established internationally. Whilst highlighting potential clinical benefits, the published literature reveals that there is no single optimum approach to organising a CDM programme, as significant variation exists between countries and CDM programmes tend to reflect unique characteristics of the local health system from which they evolve [10,11]. Health systems are complex and reorientation of health infrastructure away from secondary care and towards primary care can have unintended consequences, requiring ongoing evaluation and interaction between the stakeholders involved [11,12].

This study explores the experience of GPs, based across Ireland, of the introduction and operation of CDM and their perceptions of its impact on the day-to-day operation of general practice.

Methods

Design of the study

This qualitative study utilises an ‘experiential qualitative’ theoretical approach to explore the research topic [13]. This approach focusses on the lived experiences of participating GPs and was chosen to use these experiences to gain insight into the practical realities of operating CDM within general practice [13]. Data was collected via one-to-one semi-structured interviews conducted between May and July 2023. Study findings are reported using The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research [14].

Sampling and recruitment

A purposeful sampling approach was utilised to recruit GPs in order to capture the variety of general practice from across Ireland, in terms of size (as measured by number of GPs per practice) and geographical location (urban vs rural) [15]. SB and TF initially contacted GPs in the south, east and north-west of the country via email using the directory of the Irish College of General Practitioners and the professional networks of the researchers. Following this snowballing sampling was utilised for further recruitment, with participating GPs given details of the study and contact information of the researchers to pass on. GPs received a written description of the project via email and those expressing an interest in participating were subsequently contact by phone or email to plan the interview. No compensation was offered for participation.

Data collection

Due to practical difficulties, including the busy schedules and geographic dispersion of study participants, one-to-one semi-structured interviews were conducted virtually using online video calls. An interview topic guide was developed by the research team following literature review of studies examining the introduction of similar programmes internationally [10,16–20]. This interview guide was reviewed and updated in an iterative process as interviews were conducted, with minor changes (Table 2). For the purposes of this research project, ‘data saturation’ in effect meant ‘inductive thematic saturation’ [21]. Following discussion amongst the research team it was felt after 16 interviews the data began to repeat itself. Two further interviews were conducted in which no new data emerged and it was deemed by the researchers that ‘inductive thematic saturation’ had been met.

The three authors [SB, TF, JOF] are GPs in clinical practice. All the interviews were conducted by [SB]. It is important to recognise the roll the researcher’s pre-existing subjective experiences play in the collection, analysis and interpretation of data and is an inherent part of the qualitative research process [13]. Reflexivity was practiced by [SB] when conducting the interviews to recognise and ‘bracket’ these subjective experiences and viewpoints in order to minimise the risk of bias, both through self-awareness and through open discussion with [TF], who is an academic GP with significant experience in qualitative research [22,23].

Data analysis

A reflexive thematic analysis was utilised to analyse the data set using the six phase approach described by Braun and Clarke [24]. This study used ‘semantic coding’ to generate an in-depth summary of the dataset [24]. No codes or themes were specified in advance of analysis. To ensure rigour, coding of all interviews was conducted independently by two researchers [SB & JOF]. An inductive and iterative approach was taken to coding the dataset [24]. Once coding was complete hard copies of the codes were made to facilitate the creation of coding clusters following discussion between [SB & JOF] from which an initial thematic map was generated. Themes were generated from the thematic map in an iterative process following discussion between the whole research team, linking back to the original interviews, to generate consensus on the final themes (see Supplementary Material).

Ethics

Ethical approval was sought and granted in advance from the UCC School of Public Health Social Research Ethics Committee (SREC log number 6). The research rationale, qualitative research process and participant anonymity were explained to participants in advance of participating and written information provided. Written consent was sought to participate in the study. Audio recordings were made of these interviews, with the consent of the interviewee, for subsequent transcription and analysis. The audio files were deleted by the researcher following transcription. The interviews were transcribed verbatim by [SB]. All identifiable data was removed during the transcription process to ensure anonymity.

Results

Eighteen GPs participated in 1:1 semi-structured interviews, lasting on average 28 min (range 18–36 min). 17 of 18 study participants were GP practice partners, whilst the single non-partner was a senior clinician in a practice not structured on the partnership model. Participating GPs self-identified their practice location as urban, rural or mixed. Participants were based across three of the four Irish provinces (Connacht, Leinster, Munster) and had been applying the programme since 2020, with some minor variation due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Improved framework for chronic disease management

Most participants commented favourably on the impact of CDM on the organisational approach to the management of the covered conditions. GPs reflected that whilst they undertook a lot of chronic disease management before the introduction of CDM it was less formalised. CDM provided for a more structured approach to care, resulting in a more guideline based management of chronic disease, which GPs felt would be of clinical benefit to their patients.

We were doing a lot of this work anyway, but it wasn’t so structured, it wasn’t so complete and we are, I think it does make a big difference. Now it is a box ticking exercise to be fair, to a certain extent. But it is practicing good medicine. GP 7

As part of the perceived improvements in the approach, several GPs commented that the CDM reviews allowed for a more patient-centred medication review. Reported benefits included reducing prescription errors, deprescribing and adjusting medications to improve management of the CDM conditions.

I think they [patients] are safer because I think there are more checks and balances when it comes to their medications. [.] looking for good responses to treatment rather starting somebody on something and then kind of just going on and on and nobody really checking back to see are we actually gaining ground here? Is this benefitting this person? GP 17

Many GPs valued having designated, planned appointments to review patients’ chronic diseases, which was felt would improve communication and patient education.

[.] for what I have realised was such a big part of our job that was crammed into someone when they were sick. Like you were reviewing their COPD when they were breathless and coughing and feverish. I mean it’s just you know, what’s the chance of someone taking in anything there, […] So it’s nice yeah. GP 15

Organisational change

The process of establishing the CDM programme was seen as a significant organisational undertaking, requiring the involvement of all practice staff and stakeholders. Several participants reported increasing clinical staff in advance of establishing CDM in practice, primarily nursing staff, but also occasionally additional GP staff.

Yeah, so we had taken on a new nurse in anticipation of it. GP 6

There were differing views regarding the workload impacts of CDM. Some GPs experienced a significant increase in their workload due to CDM.

[.] I think it has hugely increased the workload and made appointments harder to get. GP 10

Others felt the impact on GP workload was more equivocal, as they were doing much of the work before and could combine elements of routine care with CDM reviews. In contrast most GPs recognised that the CDM programme had increased the demand for practice nurse appointments, with much of the responsibility for overseeing and administrating the CDM programme delegated to practice nursing staff.

[…] the nurse time would be the most significant time, as in she does, you know from the protocols, a lot of the work and we [GPs] really just put the finishing touches on it, so it’s all about nurse space and time. GP 5

This suggests the CDM structures have increased the clinical role of practice nurses. Indeed, in a couple of practices, participants reported that nurses performed most of the clinical reviews under the supervision of the GP.

[.] So our nurse is also a nurse prescriber, so either it is fully completed by the nurse once the bloods are back [.] Or they are offered kind of an in-person review or phone consultation with the doctor. GP 13

Unintended consequences from capacity constraints

Some GPs commented that their practices are operating at capacity and the loss of any staff member involved in running CDM would impair practice ability to sustain the volume of CDM reviews.

So we need everybody that we have at the moment in order to keep that going [CDM reviews] because you’re also running all of your other work alongside it. […] our nurses especially, if we lost a nurse there is no way we would be able to keep going at the same level. GP17

Smaller practices in particular, in both rural and urban areas, reported struggling with capacity to expand registration for the CDM programme as eligibility increased.

[…] opening up CDM for everyone in adulthood [.] that’s just not something that we could ever dream of here. I don’t know how any practices could really do it properly unless they made significant increases in their workforce you know. GP 5

Occasionally GPs admitted that there were times when pressures of the acute workload increased to such an extent that despite their enthusiasm for the programme, CDM reviews had to be postponed to facilitate routine appointments.

[.] it’s hard to fit in. We are trying to obviously make sure that we get to do it, but there are times when you are just too busy doing everything else that it just has to go by the wayside. GP 12

This increased demand for nursing time from CDM, in particular for morning nurse appointments due to the phlebotomy requirements of CDM could impact on routine practice activity, such as vaccinations.

So it has kind of displaced […] baby immunisations etc. Now we only do them in the afternoons and that didn’t suit some parents [.] GP 15

Rigidity of the electronic pro-forma

There were conflicting opinions expressed regarding the ‘checklist’ nature of the electronic pro-forma used to collect data during CDM reviews. Some GPs found this helpful, serving as an ‘aide memoire’ to the management of complex conditions.

The checklists are something that’s probably to everyone’s benefit [.] and you know there are questions that we would probably be asking anyway but it’s just easier for the doctor and patient to go through them. GP 1

However, it was observed by other GPs that there was a balance to be struck between this checklist approach and a more holistic art of medicine. Some mentioned they felt that the pressure to collect data and complete the electronic form acted as a barrier, diverting focus away from the patient and their concerns during the consultation.

I suppose my biggest fear about it is the disruption to the doctor patient relationship. I know I keep talking about it, but you are distracted, you are not really there, […] You’ve got to get everything down on the computer you know. GP 11

Most participants commented negatively about the how the individual care plan was currently generated by the electronic pro-forma. GPs felt it was too long, poorly formatted and elicited a poor response from patients. Some GPs observed that the written format of the care plan may create barriers for patients who had poor literacy or whose first language wasn’t English.

[.] so maybe have more something someone could watch on a phone. Send them a link to an education piece or something like that I think would work better. Different languages, having that available [.] it would be great if they could do that. GP 6

Desire for adaptability

From their experiences, some GPs advocated for a change in the rigid visit structure to permit greater flexibility in scheduling CDM reviews. GPs identified some low-risk, stable patients who may not require six monthly follow up whilst others, with multimorbidity may require more frequent reviews.

[.] I think maybe there should be something set up for extra visits […] if you need to, because some of them have more going on from the multi-morbidity point of view. GP 14

The number of blood tests required for CDM was frequently raised by participants. Their viewpoint was influenced by prior clinical practice, with some commenting that the CDM has provided a means for rationalising the number of blood tests performed within the practice. More commonly GPs felt that they were required to order excessive blood tests for CDM and desired greater flexibility to tailor the investigations to the clinical needs of patients. Several respondents complained of having to spend time following up on test results which they did not feel were clinically indicated.

[…] I would say we’re doing a significant amount more [blood] tests than we used to, maybe 30% more [.] GP 4

Participants sought some flexibility around aspects of the protocols based on their practical clinical experiences and hoped for engagement about future development and evolution of the programme.

I would hope that there would be engagement, that there would be flexibility to review it and see what is working and what is not working and that it wouldn’t get sort of set-in stone. GP 13

Discussion

Main findings

This study demonstrated enthusiasm among GPs for aspects of CDM, who felt it improved the organisational approach to the management of chronic disease in general practice. However, the study also revealed staffing and capacity challenges faced by GPs in operating the programme. These challenges appeared to limit a practice’s ability to fully implement the programme, or could result in the demands of CDM having unintended consequences for the delivery of routine care. In response to staffing and capacity limitations, many GPs described the increasingly prominent role played by practice nurses in both the clinical and administrative aspects of CDM. GPs called for greater flexibility to tailor the rigid structures of CDM to the differing needs of their patients.

Strengths and limitations

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first qualitative study of GPs’ views on the development and roll-out of CDM in Ireland. This study used qualitative methods to capture a rich summary and provide an in-depth analysis of GPs’ experiences of establishing and operating CDM in Irish general practice. By interviewing GP partners, the researchers sought to capture the views of senior clinicians who are responsible for the roll out of CDM and able to influence how the programme is organised in individual general practices. Purposeful sampling was used to gather a broad range of experiences, reflecting the varying types of GP practices that exist in Ireland. This approach was taken to capture the differing challenges faced by large and small practices in both urban and rural settings.

In terms of study limitations, all members of the research team [SB, TF, JOF] are practicing GPs and were identified as such during the interview process. There is the risk that qualitative research interviews conducted between fellow clinicians may introduce social desirability bias, a fear of ‘being judged’, thus limiting the responses of interviewees [25]. However, research has shown that this common background and understanding of the subject matter can allow for a more open and detailed sharing of ‘rich and intuitive’ experiences by study participants [22,25].

Another potential limitation is interview length, averaging 28 min, is at the shorter-range for a qualitative study. This may have resulted from purposefully sampling GP partners. These senior clinicians have significant pressures on their availability which may have impacted on the length of some interviews. Lack of triangulation with other stakeholders in CDM may be considered a study limitation. The views of practice nurses and patients would add further valuable insight. However, the focus of this study was an in-depth exploration of GP experiences and perceptions of CDM and our findings need to be considered within this context.

Interpretation

GPs were positive about their experiences of operating CDM in general practice. While many observed a lot of this work was being done before, GPs felt that CDM improved their clinical practice by providing a structure with regular reviews that helped coordinate their approach to the management of chronic disease. In particular GPs valued having specific time dedicated to reviewing patients’ chronic conditions and medications, which allowed them to take a more holistic approach especially in the context of managing patients with several chronic conditions. GP prioritisation of holistic care for patients with complex needs is in-keeping with the findings of similar research among GPs in Denmark [20]. In an Irish context, given that CDM forms one part of a larger policy transition towards the delivery of more care in the community, the enthusiasm of GPs for the clinical aspects of CDM despite the challenges they faced, is an important finding as their support is vital to the viability of the policy.

Our findings around capacity need to be considered in the context of a workforce shortage within Irish general practice [26]. Several GPs reported difficulty in expanding CDM among the younger age cohort or maintaining the volume of CDM reviews. This study highlights how capacity deficits could result in the demands of acute or routine care limiting a practice’s ability to fully implement CDM and is in keeping with the findings of a cross sectional survey of Irish GPs that highlighted staffing as a potential barrier to full implementation of CDM [19]. However, the findings of this study also illustrate how the demands of CDM could have inadvertent consequences for aspects of routine care. Demand for morning practice nursing appointments due to the phlebotomy requirements of CDM displaced routine nursing activity, including vaccinations, to the afternoon. This potential for unintended consequences for aspects of routine GP care from the introduction of structured fee-for-service programmes has been previously highlighted in the published literature [12]. Given existing capacity constraints, the conditions exist for CDM to have further inadvertent consequences for routine care in Irish general practice that merits careful ongoing evaluation as the programme continues to expand and develop in the coming years.

The central role of practice nurses in both administering and operating the CDM programme in clinical practice was a significant finding of the study. In a small number of practices, both urban and rural, nurses conducted a majority of CDM reviews under GP supervision. Reports on the future of general practice in Ireland have highlighted the central role of practice nurses in delivering high quality care in the management of chronic disease, calling for a doubling in their number [26,27]. Our findings suggest that given capacity constraints within general practice, practice nurses’ roles will continue to expand, as they assume further clinical responsibilities under CDM. This highlights the need for workforce planning to ensure bespoke training and active recruitment of practice nurses with the requisite skill set for chronic disease management. An increase in the supports available to GPs to employ practice nurses during recent contractual negotiations suggests further recognition at a policy level of the vital role of practice nurses [28]. From international experience, the introduction of QOF in the UK resulted in increasing delivery of chronic disease management in nurse-led clinics within general practice [18]. Findings from our study suggest that the introduction of CDM may have accelerated that process in Irish general practice and is an important area for future research.

GPs in our study called for greater flexibility to meet the different clinical needs of their patients, by, for example, scheduling more frequent reviews for those with more complex multimorbidity. The management of patients with multimorbidity, who constitute over 40% of the patients registered for CDM, is viewed as particularly challenging by GPs [6,17]. This desire for greater clinical flexibility, to meet the more complex needs of patients with multimorbidity, reflects the findings of previous qualitative research both in Ireland and internationally [16,29]. Adapting the level of supports and intervention based on patient needs is a core component of integrated care models for chronic disease [4,30]. However, given existing capacity constraints, extra visits for some patients may need to be balanced by reducing the frequency of visits for other more stable patients, which may prove controversial and undermine prevention aspects of CDM.

Implications

CDM forms part of the ‘Sláintecare’ policy of transitioning away from a hospital-centric model and towards an integrated model of care, to address the changing and future health needs of the Irish population [4]. This will involve increased delivery of appropriate care within the community and general practice settings. The enthusiasm expressed by GPs for aspects of CDM in this study suggests a level of support among GPs for this transition. However, several findings in this study highlight systemic weaknesses that may have inhibited the roll out of CDM and may influence the long-term viability of this policy. Our findings around unintended consequences for aspects of routine practice activity carries important implications for both policy and practice, warranting careful consideration as the programme continues to expand and develop in coming years. The central role of practice nurses in both administrative and clinical aspects of CDM is a significant finding, however, their views and experiences are not captured in this study and represent an important area for future research.

Conclusions

GPs in this study perceived the framework provided by CDM improved the practice approach to the management of chronic disease. However, capacity constraints impacted both on the delivery of CDM and aspects of routine care, highlighting the importance for policy makers to provide continued support to strengthen the general practice infrastructure.
