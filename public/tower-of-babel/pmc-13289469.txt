IGF2BP2-driven serine metabolism promotes the progression of thyroid carcinoma via m6A-PHGDH
Authors: Yuhang Zhang, Zhifu Xiao, Xuqin Dong, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThyroid cancer (TC) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the endocrine system, and patients with advanced and metastatic TC have a poor prognosis. Serine metabolism is an important component of tumor cell metabolic reprogramming. However, the molecular mechanism underlying the upregulation of serine metabolism in TC remains poorly understood.MethodsImmunohistochemistry was performed to observe the expression of IGF2BP2 and its target molecules in TC and adjacent tissues. RNA interference and lentivirus infection of TPC-1 and 8505 C cells were used to downregulate and overexpress key molecules, respectively. CCK8, colony formation, EdU incorporation, transwell migration and invasion, scratch wound-healing and flow cytometry assays were performed to detect the changes in cell function among the different groups. LC‒MS was used to detect serine and related metabolites in TC cells. Transcriptome sequencing, RIP, m6A-RIP, FISH, RNA stability and luciferase reporter assays were performed to explore the molecular mechanism of IGF2BP2 and its targets. Finally, the molecular axis was validated in a mouse model.ResultsWe observed high expression of IGF2BP2 in TC tissues, and its expression increased with the tumor stage and was associated with a poor prognosis. The inhibition of IGF2BP2 suppressed proliferation, invasion, migration and serine synthesis and metabolism in TC cells. Additionally, supplementation with serine partially rescued the proliferation defect in IGF2BP2-knockdown cells. RNA-seq indicated that PHGDH is a target of IGF2BP2, and its expression was positively correlated with IGF2BP2 expression in TC. Moreover, RIP, m6A-RIP, FISH, RNA stability, LC‒MS and luciferase reporter assays showed that IGF2BP2 can increase serine synthesis and metabolism by promoting PHGDH expression in an m6A-dependent manner. The overexpression of PHGDH partially rescued the IGF2BP2 knockdown-induced attenuation of TC cell proliferation. Finally, mouse experiments verified that the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis promoted the formation of TC.ConclusionsOur results further reveal the pathogenesis of TC and provide new insights for controlling the progression of TC from the perspective of tumor metabolism in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08316-6.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is the ninth most common malignant tumor worldwide [1], its incidence is increasing worldwide [2], and it is one of the most common malignant tumors among teenagers and adults under 40 years old [3]. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common histological type and is a differentiated form of TC, accounting for approximately 84% of TC cases in patients. The prognosis is relatively good, but patients with distant metastasis or resistance to radioactive iodine have a significantly lower survival rate, resulting in a poorer prognosis [4]. Anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) is extremely malignant, accounting for only 1% of TC cases, and has a very poor prognosis; the median survival time is only 6.5 months [5]. The prognosis of medullary thyroid cancer is between that of PTC and ATC [6]. Although molecular targeted therapy can significantly prolong the progression-free survival of patients with advanced TC, the effect of targeted therapy on overall survival is still unclear [7]. Toxic side effects and the development of drug resistance further affect therapeutic efficacy and the quality of life of patients, ultimately leading to disease progression and death [8]. Therefore, further exploration of the pathogenesis of TC and the identification of new and more effective treatment strategies are urgently needed.

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play a role in regulating mRNA splicing, stability and translation [9]. Posttranscriptional regulation mediated by RBPs plays a crucial role in tumor occurrence and development [10]. IGF2BPs, members of a highly conserved RBP family that recognize single-stranded RNA, participate in the regulation of RNA processing and metabolism and in various pathological and physiological processes in cells [11, 12]. Studies have shown that IGF2BP2 expression is significantly correlated with lymph node metastasis, the tumor stage and the survival of patients with head and neck tumors [13, 14]. Specifically, IGF2BP2 increases the stability or translation of mRNAs and noncoding RNAs by recognizing m6A modifications, and subsequently affects the proliferation, apoptosis, metabolic reprogramming, and immune escape of cancer stem cells to promote tumor progression and metastasis [15–18]. Therefore, targeting IGF2BP2 and disrupting the tumor cell state and tumor microenvironment may inhibit tumor growth.

Serine (Ser) metabolism plays a crucial role in tumor development. Tumor cells typically exhibit an excessive demand for serine [19]. Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), a key enzyme in Ser biosynthesis, plays a critical role in the metabolism and growth of solid tumors [20, 21]. PHGDH can catalyze the conversion of 3-phosphoglycerate to 3-phosphohydroxypropionate, which is subsequently converted to Ser through the catalytic activities of phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1) and phosphoserine phosphatase (PSPH). Ser, a metabolic substrate, can be used to synthesize reduced NADPH and glycine (Gly). Additionally, Ser and Gly can interconvert through a reaction catalyzed by serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT1/2) [22]. Gly is the raw material for the synthesis of glutathione (GSH), and GSH plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of tumors [23]. Although numerous studies have shown that PHGDH is closely related to tumor growth and treatment resistance [24–26], the specific mechanisms of PHGDH, serine synthesis and metabolism in TC remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Here, we comprehensively utilized online databases and a TC tissue array to investigate the IGF2BP2 expression pattern in TC. Moreover, we performed functional experiments and liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry to determine the effects of the abnormal expression of IGF2BP2 on the malignant behavior of and Ser metabolism in TC cells. RNA-seq, RIP, and m6A RIP assays revealed the mechanism by which IGF2BP2 promotes TC progression through the regulation of PHGDH expression and Ser metabolism. Additionally, animal experiments further confirmed the role of the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis in promoting TC progression in vivo. This study reveals a novel mechanism of TC pathogenesis from the perspective of abnormal tumor cell metabolism, providing new ideas for the clinical control of this disease.

Materials and methods

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

TC tissue microarrays (TMAs) (ETG8010b, Taibsbio, China) were stained with antibodies targeting IGF2BP2 (1:200, 11601-1-AP, Proteintech) and PHGDH (1:500, 14719-1-AP, Proteintech). The clinicopathological characteristics of the clinical cohort are listed in Table S1. For the mouse xenograft model, tissue slices were also stained with anti-Ki67 antibodies (1:500, GB111499-100, Servicebio, China). Both the TMAs and the mouse xenograft slices were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI scanning microscope (3DHISTECH, China) and analyzed using Aipathwell® analysis software (Servicebio, China). IHC staining was scored independently by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinical data. The histochemical scores (H-scores) were calculated and compared between different groups. The following formula was used: H score = Σ(pi×i)= (percentage of weak intensity × 1) + (percentage of moderate intensity × 2) + (percentage of strong intensity × 3).

Cell lines

The TPC-1 cell line was purchased from AoyinBio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the 8505 C cell line was previously preserved in our laboratory. Both of the cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling and were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using Hoechst staining and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free throughout the study. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin.

RNA interference and lentivirus infection

The small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting IGF2BP2, METTL3 and METTL14 that were used in this study were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) was mixed with the siRNAs, and the cells were transfected for 48 h. The transfection efficiency was measured using qRT‒PCR and western blotting.

The luciferase-shIGF2BP2/shPHGDH and oePHGDH lentiviruses were constructed by TsingKe Biotech Ltd. (Beijing, China). The vector and sequences of the siRNAs and shRNAs are presented in Table S2. Stable shIGF2BP2 and shPHGDH cell lines were obtained by selecting the cells with 10 µg/ml puromycin, and shIGF2BP2-oePHGDH cells were subsequently selected by culturing them with 1000 µg/ml geneticin (Solarbio, China).

RNA extraction, real-time PCR and RNA-seq analysis

An R1200 Total RNA Extraction Kit (Solarbio, China) was used to extract RNA. Then, 2× One-Step SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme) was used for qRT‒PCR according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used were synthesized by TsingKe Biotech Ltd. (Table S3). β-Actin was used as the internal control gene, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

For RNA sequencing, RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), preserved at -80 °C, and sent to Seqhealth Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for sequencing. For library preparation, libraries were prepared using a KC™ stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth Ltd., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The enriched libraries with fragments ranging from 200 to 500 bp were quantified and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform using paired-end 150 bp mode. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR software (version 2.5.3a) with the default parameters. Reads mapped to the exon regions of each gene and each transcript were counted using featureCounts (Subread-1.5.1; Bioconductor), after which expression was calculated. Differentially expressed genes and transcripts between groups were identified using the edgeR package (version 3.40.2), and a p value cutoff of 0.05 and a fold-change cutoff of 2 were used. A Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was implemented using KOBAS software (version 2.1.1), with a p value cutoff of 0.05, to determine statistically significant enrichment. The raw sequence data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (accession number HRA015057).

Western blot

The treated cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for quantification using a BCA assay. Then, 5× loading buffer was added, and proteins were denatured in a 100 °C water bath for 5 min. Afterward, the samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE on a 10% gel (Epizyme, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were loaded in each lane. After separation the proteins were transferred to a membrane, incubated with a primary antibody at 4℃ overnight and then incubated with a secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The specific information on the antibodies and dilution ratios is listed in Table S4. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Bio-Rad, USA).

CCK8 and colony formation assays

A total of 2 × 103 cells were seeded in each well of a 96-well plate, and 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well at different times (0, 24, 48, and 72 h). After a 4 h incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SynergyLX, Biotek, USA).

For the colony formation assays, 400 cells were seeded in a 60-mm dish with 10 mL of fresh medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After two weeks, the dishes were washed with PBS and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Solarbio, China).

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay

A BeyoClick™ EdU-594 Kit (Beyotime, China) was also used to detect cell proliferation. Briefly, cells were seeded into a 6-well plate, and 1× EdU working solution (10 µM) was added and incubated for 2 h. Afterward, the cells were washed, fixed and permeabilized. After the click additive and reaction solution were added, the plate was incubated for 30 min in the dark. The cell nuclei were stained with 1× Hoechst 33,342 for 10 min in the dark. After three washes with PBS, the cells in the plate were observed with an FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Transwell migration and invasion experiments

Transwell chambers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) inserted in 24-well plates were used for migration and invasion assays. Cells were seeded in the upper chamber with medium without FBS, and the lower zone contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS. For invasion assays, the chambers were pretreated with Matrigel (BD, USA). After 48 h, the cells in the chambers were washed with PBS, fixed with cold ethyl alcohol and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Solarbio, China).

Scratch wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 70–80% and transfected with siRNAs for 48 h. Afterward, a 200 µL pipette tip and a ruler were used to draw a straight line at the bottom of the plate. At different time points (0, 24, and 48 h), the scratches were photographed using an EVOS M7000 microscope (Thermo Fisher).

Flow cytometry

A Cell Cycle Detection Kit (CA002, SAB, USA) and an Annexin V-FITC/PI Kit (CA001, SAB, USA) were used to detect differences in the cell cycle and apoptosis among the different groups. All the experimental procedures and details were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman, USA) was used for detection.

Liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (LC‒MS)

The levels of serine and related metabolites in cells were detected by LC‒MS using an Agilent 1290 + AB SCIEX 3200 QTRAP. For sample preparation, 1 × 106 cells were harvested and washed with 200 µL of a 150 mM NH4Ac solution. An aliquot of 20 µL was used to determine the protein concentration using a BCA assay (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The remaining 180 µL was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min to pellet the cells. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of cold 80% methanol and incubated at − 80 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation to obtain the supernatant, another 50 µL of cold 80% methanol was added for the second extraction. The total supernatant was dried and then preserved at − 80 °C until detection.

For LC‒MS detection, mobile phases A and B were 50 mL of 200 mmol/L ammonium formate solution mixed with 450 mL of water or acetonitrile, respectively. The MS conditions were as follows: electrospray ionization source; multiple reaction monitoring of both positive and negative ions at 5500 V and − 4500 V; and ion source temperature of 550 ℃, and Gas1, Gas2, CUR and CAD pressures of 55, 60, 35 and 9 psi respectively. For chromatography, a Kinetex HILIC (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.6 μm) column was used. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, the column temperature was 40 °C, the injection volume was 2 µL, and a gradient program was adopted.

The raw data were processed using MultiQuant™ software. The peak areas were normalized using the respective protein concentrations (as determined with a BCA assay), and the resulting peak areas were subjected to relative quantification. The final relative abundance is reported as the normalized peak area per µg of protein.

RNA stability assay

Cells were seeded in a 6-well plate, and 5 µg/mL actinomycin (Sigma‒Aldrich) was added and incubated with the cells for different durations (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h). Total RNA was extracted as described above, and qRT‒PCR was subsequently performed to compare mRNA expression among the different groups.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-RIP

A RIP Assay Kit (MBL Life Science, Japan) was used to assess the binding of IGF2BP2 to the PHGDH mRNA. Briefly, cells were seeded in 15-cm dishes and transfected with siRNAs for 48 h. The cells were subsequently washed and harvested in nuclease-free PBS. Then, the precleared cell lysate was added to preprocessed Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads supplemented with anti-IGF2BP2 or IgG and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the RNA was separated from the bead complexes and subjected to qRT‒PCR. For m6A-RIP, total mRNA was extracted with a polyA Spin mRNA Isolation Kit (NEB, China), and an m6A antibody (Active Motif, China) was used to obtain RNAs with m6A modifications. After separation from the bead complexes, the RNA was subjected to qRT‒PCR.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For FISH and IF costaining, probes targeting the PHGDH mRNA (Servicebio, China) and anti-IGF2BP2 antibodies were used. Cells were seeded in 15-mm confocal dishes and incubated with the probes at 37 °C for 4 h and anti-IGF2BP2 antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Afterward, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. Finally, images were captured with an FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) sequences containing the predicted m6A modification sites within the PHGDH gene (as predicted by the SRAMP database) were cloned and inserted into the pmirGLO vector. Moreover, the IGF2BP2 cDNA was cloned and inserted into the PCDNA3.1 vector. The detailed sequences are listed in Table S5. In the reporter assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cotransfected with 1 µg of pmirGLO-PHGDH-WT/MUT and PCDNA3.1-IGF2BP2 or PCDNA3.1 vector along with 5 µl of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). At 48 h after transfection, the reporter activity was assessed using a Dual Luciferase Assay Kit (RG027; Beyotime).

Mouse experiments

Sample sizes were determined based on preliminary data and a power analysis using G*Power software (version 3.1). Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d > 1.5) based on our in vitro results and pilot studies, with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.8, the calculated minimum sample size was 5 mice per group. In accordance with institutional animal ethics guidelines and the 3R (reduction, refinement, and replacement) principles to minimize animal usage, we used 5 mice per group. Female BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old) were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5) and subcutaneously injected with TPC-1 cells infected with different lentiviruses, namely, shCtrl, shIGF2BP2, shPHGDH, shIGF2BP2 + vector and shIGF2BP2 + oePHGDH. After the tumors formed, their size was measured using Vernier calipers every three days. The tumors were observed with an IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer) three weeks after injection. The investigators were blinded to the group assignments. The protocol for the animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using a blind testing approach to minimize researcher bias. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used for statistical analyses. For all continuous data, we first performed the Shapiro‒Wilk test to assess normality. For data with a normal distribution, we used parametric tests. For comparisons between two groups, we used the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. For comparisons involving more than two groups (multigroup comparisons), we used one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by an appropriate post hoc test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The data are presented as the means ± SDs of three biological replicates. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

High expression of IGF2BP2 in thyroid cancer is related to the tumor stage

We first determined the expression pattern of IGF2BP2 in TC tissues using the GEPIA database. The data from 512 tumor tissues and 59 tissues adjacent to TC revealed significantly higher expression of IGF2BP2 in TC tissues than that in normal tissues (Fig. 1A). We further verified this result in TC tissues from our cohort, which included 10 adjacent tissues and 70 TC tissues of different clinical stages, including 28 stage I TC tissues, 17 stage II TC tissues, 18 stage III TC tissues, and 7 stage IV TC tissues. IHC staining and statistical analysis showed that IGF2BP2 expression was significantly higher in TC tissues than in adjacent tissues, and its expression level significantly increased with increasing tumor stage (Fig. 1B, C). We further compared these results with those of other online databases, including the TNMplot and UALAN databases. Both results indicated that IGF2BP2 was expressed at higher levels in TC than in normal tissue (Fig. 1D, E) and might be related to lymph node metastasis (Fig. 1F). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier survival curves generated using the KMplot database revealed that high IGF2BP2 expression was significantly associated with shorter relapse-free survival of TC patients (Fig. 1G).

The downregulation of IGF2BP2 expression inhibits the proliferation, invasion and migration of TC cells

We selected two common TC cell lines, TPC-1 and 8505 C, as tools for a series of functional experiments designed to explore the effect of high IGF2BP2 expression in TC on disease progression. First, we knocked down the expression of IGF2BP2 in TPC-1 and 8505 C cells using two siRNAs and verified the knockdown effect at the mRNA and protein levels by qRT‒PCR and WB (Fig. 2A, B). The results of the CCK8 experiments showed that compared with that of the control group, the proliferation rate of cells in which IGF2BP2 was knocked down was significantly lower at 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig. 2C). The results of the colony formation assays indicated that the number of colonies of TC cells with IGF2BP2 knockdown was less than one-third of that of the control group, and their clonogenic ability was significantly reduced (Fig. 2D). EdU staining showed that knocking down IGF2BP2 expression significantly inhibited the proliferation of TC cells (Fig. 2E). Additionally, we evaluated the effects of IGF2BP2 overexpression on TC cell invasion and migration by performing scratch wound healing and transwell experiments. The results showed that the speed at which TC cells with IGF2BP2 knockdown healed the scratch wound was significantly slower (Fig. 2H), and the number of cells that passed through the transwell chamber was significantly reduced regardless of the presence of Matrigel (Fig. 2F, G). Moreover, we used flow cytometry to observe the effects of IGF2BP2 overexpression on the apoptosis and cell cycle progression of TC cells. The results revealed that inhibiting IGF2BP2 expression significantly increased the apoptosis rate of TC cells (Fig. 2I) and tended to arrest TC cells in G0/G1 phase (Fig. 2J). These results indicate that IGF2BP2 overexpression in TC cells can promote cell proliferation, invasion, and migration.

IGF2BP2 facilitates serine biosynthesis and metabolic processes in TC cells

We conducted transcriptome sequencing on two TC cell lines in which IGF2BP2 was knocked down to investigate how IGF2BP2 promotes the proliferation of TC cells and disease progression. IGF2BP2 knockdown led to the differential expression of many genes in both TPC-1 and 8505 C cells (Supplementary Data). Heatmaps and volcano plots of some of the most significantly differentially expressed genes were constructed. The Venn diagram shows 139 overlapping upregulated genes and 105 overlapping downregulated genes in the two TC cell lines (Figure S1). We found that IGF2BP2 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of PHGDH in both cell lines (Fig. 3A, B). We performed a KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes, and the results showed that these genes were significantly enriched in the amino acid synthesis and metabolism pathways (Fig. 3C). Since PHGDH is the primary rate-limiting enzyme in the serine synthesis pathway, we performed qRT‒PCR and WB assays to detect the expression of several important enzymes in the serine synthesis pathway in TC cells from different treatment groups. IGF2BP2 knockdown inhibited the expression of PHGDH, SHMT1, PSAT1, PSPH and SRR to varying degrees (Fig. 3D, E). Moreover, we used LC‒MS to determine the effects of IGF2BP2 knockdown on the steady-state levels of the main metabolites of the serine synthesis pathway, including Ser, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), Gly, GSH, and norvaline, in TC cells. The graphs showing the Ser peaks are shown in Fig. 3F, and the graphs for the SAM, Gly, GSH and norvaline peaks are shown in Figure S2. The calculated normalized peak areas can be used to compare the differences in the relative contents of metabolites in different treatment groups. The results showed that the steady-state levels of Ser, SAM, Gly and GSH in TC cells with IGF2BP2 knockdown were significantly reduced (Fig. 3G). Additionally, serine supplementation partially rescued the proliferation defect in the IGF2BP2-knockdown cells (Fig. 3H). These results suggest that high expression of IGF2BP2 in TC cells may promote disease progression by inducing serine synthesis and metabolic pathways.

IGF2BP2 stimulates PHGDH expression by recognizing the m6A-modified site in the PHGDH mRNA in TC cells

We first determined the expression levels of PHGDH in TC and adjacent tissues to investigate whether IGF2BP2 promotes the expression of PHGDH in TC cells and its underlying mechanism. The results from the TNMplot, UALAN, and GEPIA databases all indicated that the expression level of PHGDH in TC tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent tissues (Fig. 4A, B, C) and was positively correlated with IGF2BP2 expression (Fig. 4D). We then conducted IHC staining and an analysis of our TC tissue array, and the results showed that PHGDH expression increased in TC tissues with increasing clinical stage (Fig. 4E, F) and was significantly correlated with IGF2BP2 expression (Fig. 4G). Based on the function of IGF2BP2 as an RBP, we hypothesized that it might promote gene expression by interacting with the PHGDH mRNA. RNA stability experiments demonstrated that in TC cells in which IGF2BP2 was knocked down, the expression level and half-life of the PHGDH mRNA were significantly reduced (Fig. 4H). Studies have indicated that IGF2BPs may regulate mRNA stability by recognizing m6A modification sites and that the METTL3–METTL14–WTAP complex is the core methyltransferase. Therefore, we transfected TC cells with siRNAs targeting METTL3 and METTL14, and the results revealed that METTL3 knockdown did not significantly affect PHGDH expression (Fig. 4I), whereas METTL14 knockdown significantly inhibited PHGDH expression and simultaneously suppressed the expression of enzymes such as SHMT1, PSAT1, PSPH, and SRR to varying degrees (Fig. 4J, K). Additionally, the results of the m6A-RIP assay indicated that the content of the m6A-modified PHGDH mRNA was significantly decreased after METTL14 was knocked down (Fig. 4L), and the results of the RIP assay showed that the content of the IGF2BP2-bound PHGDH mRNA was also significantly decreased after METTL14 was knocked down (Fig. 4M). Based on these results, we used the SRAMP database to predict potential m6A sites on the PHGDH. Five sites with a confidence score of “very high confidence” were identified, all of which were located in the CDS region (Figure S3). By combining eCLIP-seq data (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/), we analyzed the binding of IGF2BP2 to the PHGDH mRNA, selected two of the most likely m6A sites (the first and third sites are shown in Figure S3) and used a dual-luciferase vector for verification. As shown in Fig. 4N, IGF2BP2 overexpression significantly increased the luciferase activity of the WT reporters but not the MUT reporters. Moreover, FISH and IF costaining experiments demonstrated that IGF2BP2 and the PHGDH were colocalized in TC cells (Fig. 4O). The above results indicated that IGF2BP2 may promote the expression of PHGDH by recognizing the m6A modification sites of the PHGDH mRNA.

PHGDH overexpression partially rescued the IGF2BP2 knockdown-induced attenuation of proliferation in TC cells

We conducted a series of rescue experiments to clarify the logical role of the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis in promoting the progression of TC. First, we overexpressed PHGDH in TC cells in which IGF2BP2 was knocked down and verified the effect using qRT‒PCR and WB. After the overexpression of PHGDH, the expression of IGF2BP2 did not change significantly, whereas the expressions of SHMT1 and PSAT1 in the serine synthesis pathway partially recovered, and the expression of PSPH did not change significantly (Fig. 5A, B). The results of the CCK8 assays indicated that overexpressing PHGDH partially counteracted the inhibitory effect of IGF2BP2 knockdown on cell proliferation (Fig. 5C). Moreover, after overexpressing PHGDH, the colony formation ability of the TC cells also significantly recovered (Fig. 5D). EdU experiments revealed that the proliferation of TC cells overexpressing PHGDH was significantly higher than that of cells in which IGF2BP2 was knocked down (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the results of the scratch wound healing and Transwell assays indicated that PHGDH overexpression significantly restored the migration of TC cells with IGF2BP2 knockdown and partially restored their invasion ability (Fig. 5F, G). Flow cytometry was used to observe the apoptosis and cell cycle distribution of cells in the different treatment groups. Compared with the TC cells with IGF2BP2 knockdown alone, those with PHGDH overexpression exhibited reduced apoptosis and an alleviation of cell cycle arrest (Fig. 5H, I). Additionally, LC‒MS detection of the steady-state levels of serine metabolites in different groups indicated that PHGDH overexpression largely counteracted the inhibition of serine synthesis and metabolism in TC cells caused by IGF2BP2 knockdown, and the steady-state levels of Ser, SAM, Gly and GSH were all restored (Fig. 5J).

The IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis increases TC tumor growth in vivo

We determined the role of the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis in the progression of TC in vivo by conducting subcutaneous tumor formation experiments using groups with stable knockdown of IGF2BP2 or PHGDH and groups in which PHGDH expression was restored in TC cells. Since all the cells were labeled with luciferase, we observed tumor formation in vivo using a small animal live imaging instrument three weeks after the tumor cell injection and removed the tumors four weeks after the injection. The results showed that knocking down IGF2BP2 or PHGDH inhibited the tumor-forming ability of TC cells, whereas overexpressing PHGDH essentially counteracted the tumor-suppressing effect caused by knocking down IGF2BP2 (Fig. 6A, B). We constructed and plotted the tumor growth curves based on the tumor sizes measured every three days. The results indicated that compared with that of the control group, the tumor growth rate of the groups in which IGF2BP2 or PHGDH was knocked down was significantly reduced, while PHGDH overexpression partially restored the tumor-suppressing effect caused by IGF2BP2 knockdown (Fig. 6C). We weighed the excised tumors and found that the tumor weights of the mice in the groups in which IGF2BP2 or PHGDH was knocked down were significantly lower, while the tumor weights in the PHGDH-overexpressing group were significantly greater than those in the group in which IGF2BP2 was knocked down (Fig. 6D). In addition, we evaluated the expression of IGF2BP2, PHGDH, and Ki-67 in tumors and found that the expression of Ki-67 in mice in which IGF2BP2 or PHGDH was knocked down was significantly reduced, whereas the expression of Ki-67 in mice in which PHGDH was overexpressed was partially recovered (Fig. 6E, F). We maintained the mice shown in Fig. 6A for 60 days after the tumor cell injection and observed that the mortality rate of the mice in the groups in which IGF2BP2 or PHGDH was knocked down was less than 50%, whereas all the mice in the PHGDH-overexpressing group died after 54 days (Fig. 6G). These experimental results indicate that the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis promotes the progression of TC in vivo.

Based on the aforementioned experimental results, we propose the following molecular mechanism: In TC, high expression of IGF2BP2 can promote the expression of the PHGDH gene by recognizing the m6A modification sites of the PHGDH mRNA and induces cell proliferation and disease progression by promoting serine synthesis and metabolism.

Discussion

TC is one of the most common malignant tumors of the endocrine system, and its incidence has significantly increased over the past few decades [27]. Although most patients with TC have a favorable prognosis, some patients (especially those with advanced and metastatic TC) respond poorly to traditional treatment methods; thus, further clarification of the pathogenesis of TC and the exploration of new treatment strategies are needed [28]. Metabolic reprogramming is one of the important characteristics of cancer, and metabolic pathways are altered in tumor cells to meet their rapid proliferation needs [29]. Ser metabolism is an important component of tumor cell metabolic reprogramming and is closely related to the proliferation, survival and invasion of tumor cells [30]. In this study, IGF2BP2, which is abnormally highly expressed in TC, was used as an entry point to explore the mechanism by which it regulates serine metabolism in tumor cells through PHGDH, providing new insights for controlling the progression of TC from the perspective of tumor metabolism in clinical practice.

Increasing evidence suggests that high expression of IGF2BP2 in TC may promote tumor progression through various molecular pathways and physiological processes. The long noncoding RNA MALAT1 regulates the miR-204/IGF2BP2/m6A-MYC signaling pathway in TC to exert a carcinogenic effect [31]. Inhibiting IGF2BP2 can reduce the expression and stability of HAGLR in TC cells, induce apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, and inhibit the occurrence of TC [32]. In PTC, FTO inhibits the expression of apolipoprotein E through the m6A modification mediated by IGF2BP2 and inhibits the glycolytic metabolism of PTC through regulation of the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [33]. Furthermore, FTO/IGF2BP2 can also regulate the expression of CHD12 through the m6A modification and mediate the EMT, thereby promoting the metastasis of TC [34]. Lymph node metastasis is the main cause of PTC recurrence. Existing studies have shown that IGF2BP2 promotes PTC lymph node metastasis in an m6A-dependent manner by stabilizing DPP4, providing a new strategy to block the lymph node metastasis pathway [35]. In this study, we found that IGF2BP2 can increase Ser synthesis and metabolism by promoting PHGDH expression in an m6A-dependent manner, promoting the progression of TC. Our results provide a new perspective on the role of IGF2BP2 in promoting TC and further suggest that inhibiting IGF2BP2 may help control the progression of TC. In addition, radioactive iodine-refractory PTC (RR-PTC) is a major clinical problem. Several studies have shown that the inhibition of IGF2BP2 can promote the differentiation of RR-PTC cells, providing a new treatment method and potential marker for PTC differentiation [36, 37]. Overall, inhibiting the expression of IGF2BP2 is likely to benefit patients with PTC, ATC, or RR-PTC. Further studies should consider the combined strategy of the targeted inhibition of IGF2BP2 with other therapies to achieve better therapeutic effects in patients with TC.

Ser/Gly, the main one-carbon unit donors in single-carbon metabolism, are the most consumed substances in cancer metabolism apart from glucose and glutamine [38]. Currently, the function of PHGDH in Ser synthesis is the most extensively studied in cancer research. PHGDH is highly expressed in various solid tumors and hematological tumors [39]. T PHGDH is expressed at the highest levels in malignant melanoma, and knocking down PHGDH can inhibit the progression of melanoma [40]. In triple-negative breast cancer tissues, the expression levels of PHGDH and Ser metabolism-related products are closely related to the clinical prognosis of patients [41]. In addition, several studies have shown that inhibiting PHGDH can reduce the occurrence of clinical resistance and further improve the therapeutic effect on cancer [42–44]. This study also revealed that PHGDH is highly expressed in TC tissues and leads to the significant upregulation of serine-related metabolic products in tumor cells, further indicating that Ser synthesis and metabolism are activated and upregulated in TC.

Notably, the data from TCGA database indicated a weak correlation between the expression of IGF2BP2 and that of PHGDH at the mRNA level, whereas we observed a strong and statistically significant positive correlation between the protein expression of IGF2BP2 and that of PHGDH. The discrepancy between the weak mRNA correlation and the strong protein-level correlation in our TMA cohort may reflect the importance of posttranscriptional regulation. Although RIP, m6A-RIP and dual luciferase assays revealed that IGF2BP2 may exert posttranscriptional regulatory effects by binding to the m6A sites of the PHGDH mRNA, CLIP-based approaches could further strengthen site-specific binding evidence in future studies. In addition, different effects were observed between METTL14 knockdown and METTL3 knockdown. Although METTL3 and METTL14 typically function as a core heterodimeric complex, they can have independent or nonredundant functions, and their substrate specificity or dependency on other interacting partners can vary in different cellular contexts. The differential effects we observed might reflect such context-specific roles in TC cells or could be related to the efficiency and off-target effects of the specific siRNAs used.

In addition, studies have shown that the expression of the amino acid metabolism enzyme PSAT1 is upregulated in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, colorectal cancer, ER-negative breast cancer, and ovarian cancer and can promote the proliferation and metastasis of malignant tumor cells, thereby leading to a poor prognosis [45–47]. Additionally, high PSPH expression is related to a poor prognosis of liver cancer [48], and it can promote the growth and metastasis of melanoma [49]. High expression of metabolic enzymes in the Ser synthesis pathway may be important for maintaining the occurrence and development of various malignant tumors. Preliminary preclinical studies have shown that dietary restriction of Ser and Gly inhibited tumor cell proliferation in mouse models of intestinal cancer and lymphoma, as well as in PDX colon cancer models [50]. Recent studies have shown that restricting the dietary intake of Ser and Gly leads to the accumulation of deoxyceramide in tumors, which has an adverse effect on tumor growth [51]. In this study, the enzymes PSAT1, PSPH, SHMT1, and SRR, which are involved in the Ser metabolism pathway, were upregulated to varying degrees in TC. The SRAMP database revealed that the SHMT1, PSAT1, PSPH, and SRR mRNAs all contain potential m6A modification sites in their 3’UTRs or CDS regions. Moreover, previous studies have reported that IGF2BPs can directly bind to and stabilize the mRNAs encoding these enzymes in various cancer types [52, 53]. However, additional experiments are needed to confirm their direct regulation by the m6A modification. Although the upstream mechanism is not completely clear, the coordinated regulation of multiple enzymes by IGF2BP2 highlights its role as a master modulator of serine metabolism in TC, and these results suggest that targeting Ser metabolism may be a potentially effective approach to control the progression of TC.

The present study had several inherent limitations. First, our findings are primarily based on two established TC cell lines, which may not fully capture the genetic and histological heterogeneity of human thyroid tumors. Future studies utilizing or primary patient-derived cells will be crucial to validate the clinical relevance and generalizability of the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis in a more physiologically and genetically diverse context. Second, although our data demonstrate that IGF2BP2 binds to the PHGDH mRNA and prolongs its half-life, we have not determined whether this process occurs through direct protection from degradation by specific endo- or exoribonucleases, recruitment to subcellular compartments that favor stability, or competition with destabilizing factors. Elucidating these detailed mechanistic details represents an important direction for future research. Third, subcutaneous xenografts do not fully recapitulate the native tumor microenvironment of the thyroid gland, including the unique extracellular matrix composition, stromal cell interactions, and immune landscape that influence tumor progression. Future studies employing orthotopic injection, patient-derived xenograft and genetically engineered models will be essential to evaluate the function of IGF2BP2 in the authentic thyroid microenvironment. Finally, the metabolomic data presented in this study were obtained using LC‒MS, which measures steady-state metabolite levels rather than direct metabolic flux. Although changes in steady-state levels often correlate with alterations in pathway activity, they do not necessarily reflect true metabolic flux, which will require studies employing ¹³C-serine tracing and comprehensive isotopic targeted mass spectrometry in the future to elucidate the precise metabolic nodes affected by IGF2BP2 knockdown.

In summary, we determined the high expression of IGF2BP2 and PHGDH in TC tissues, showed that the inhibition of IGF2BP2 suppressed proliferation, invasion and migration; determined the steady-state levels of the main metabolites of the serine synthesis pathway in TC cells; and showed that the IGF2BP2–PHGDH axis promoted tumor formation in a mouse model. Mechanistically, IGF2BP2 may increase Ser synthesis and metabolism by promoting PHGDH expression in an m6A-dependent manner, promoting the progression of TC.
