Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), a high-grade primary brain tumor, presents a formidable challenge in neuro-oncology because of its aggressive nature, infiltrative growth, and limited response to treatment.1, 2 Typically affecting the elderly, with a median age of 64 years at presentation, GBM has a higher incidence in men and in White patients.1 While this malignancy can manifest in various intracranial locations, its occurrence within the septum pellucidum is exceedingly uncommon, adding unfamiliarity to its already complex clinical diagnosis and management. The septum pellucidum is a delicate structure located between the lateral ventricles, with a crucial role in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation.3–7 The rarity of GBM within the septum pellucidum presents unique clinical, radiological, and therapeutic considerations.2

Guided by a unique case, this systematic review seeks to comprehensively analyze the existing literature on septum pellucidum GBMs, providing insights into their clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, management strategies, and outcomes. By juxtaposing the 5 cases of this malignancy documented in the literature with contemporary diagnostic and therapeutic modalities, this review seeks to elucidate the diverse clinical scenarios that clinicians encounter when faced with GBMs in this distinctive anatomical location.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation

A 69-year-old male with a previous history of prostate carcinoma presented to an outside hospital with a 2-week history of a “trance-like state” and cognitive decline. Initial head computed tomography (CT) showed prominent ventricles without distinct mass lesions. Subsequently, the patient underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, which revealed hydrocephalus and a brain mass within the ventricular septum and fornix. These findings prompted his transfer for further neurosurgical evaluation.

Upon admission to our institution, the patient was awake and alert but exhibited profound memory loss. MRI was performed with and without contrast, showing an enhancing, diffusion-restricting intraventricular mass within the inferior septum pellucidum extending to the third and both lateral ventricles (Fig. 1).

Preoperative axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) postcontrast T1-weighted MRI showing a heterogeneously enhancing mass at the base of the septum pellucidum with extension into both lateral ventricles.

Operative Findings and Clinical Course

On hospital day 6, the patient underwent a brain biopsy as well as a septostomy and placement of a right frontal ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt for CSF diversion. Intraventricular endoscopic biopsy findings indicated a GBM, World Health Organization (WHO) central nervous system tumor grade 4, with immunopositivity for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and a Ki-67 labeling index of 60%–70% (Fig. 2). Postoperative head CT on hospital day 7 showed increased dilation of the lateral ventricles, with the left ventricle showing greater dilation than the right ventricle. Secondary analysis would reveal that the lesion was isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) wildtype; however, IDH1/IDH2 status as well as O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase promoter methylation analysis could not be determined due to an insufficient biopsy quantity.

A and B: Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the biopsied lesion from the septum pellucidum, confirming the diagnosis of GBM, WHO grade 4. C: Immunohistochemical staining for GFAP, demonstrating extensive and near-complete positivity (approximately 100%) within the tumor cells, consistent with a glial origin. D: Immunohistochemical staining for Ki-67, revealing a high proliferative index (labeling index: 60%–70%) indicative of the aggressive nature of the tumor. The patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, with a worsening mental status. Over the next few days, he became intermittently arousable, lost the ability to spontaneously open his eyes, became nonverbal, and ultimately was unable to follow commands. Faced with these profound deficits, the patient’s family opted for comfort measures rather than pursuing further treatment. On hospital day 11, the patient experienced respiratory distress and cardiopulmonary arrest, ultimately dying that morning. Postmortem examination revealed a mass within the septum pellucidum and caudate nucleus, with invasion of the lateral and third ventricles. Contributing to marked ventricular dilation, the mass measured 3.5 cm × 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm with areas of hemorrhage.

Discussion

Previous Cases in the Literature

To find similar cases, a systematic search of the PubMed and Embase databases was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 3). The search terms were “glioblastoma,” “GBM,” and “septum pellucidum,” with no restriction on the publication date. Only 5 case reports fit the criteria (Table 1).

The earliest description of a GBM manifesting in the septum pellucidum, reported by Chusid and De Gutierrez-Mahoney3 featured a patient who had presented with a 1-month history of nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., vomiting and diarrhea) in whom GBM was ultimately diagnosed at autopsy. In contrast, the most recently published case, reported by Takigawa et al., highlighted the more common scenario of asymptomatic discovery during an evaluation for chronic headaches.7 The cases described by Epstein and Epstein and Hariri et al. were marked by fluctuating states of consciousness and headache, with rapid progression and cognitive decline over several weeks in the latter case, similar to the case presented here.4,6 Finally, Asano et al. reported the case of a patient presenting with low-back pain, numbness in the lower extremities, diplopia, vomiting, and headaches.5

The diagnostic evolution, traced from postmortem identification to contemporary imaging techniques, underscores the challenges and advances in early detection. In the earliest case, the mass was identified postmortem, indicating the challenges in early diagnosis during the mid-20th century.3 The remainder of the cases demonstrate the evolution of intracranial imaging from pneumoventriculography, revealing diffuse septum pellucidum widening, to modern-day CT and MRI, which allow visualization of the tumor.4–7 The advancements of genomic sequencing and molecular profiling technologies have allowed the use of molecular markers to further categorize different subtypes of GBM. The early articles do not report any molecular markers.3–5 Of those reported, both cases were positive for GFAP with a high Ki-67 proliferative index.6, 7 Both of these markers are expected in immunohistochemical staining of GBM.8 The most current articles documented results of additional genetic testing, including TP53 and NFKBIA deletions and categorization as IDH wildtype, which is inherent in the GBM diagnosis per 2016 WHO classification guidelines.7, 9

In terms of management, early cases were managed with no treatment or palliative measures with subtemporal decompression and ventricular drainage.3, 4 Overall, 3 cases required CSF diversion, with permanent VP shunt placement in 2. Three of the cases underwent various degrees of resection and radiation, with the most recent managed aggressively per the current standard of care with gross-total resection followed by chemotherapy and radiation.5–7 Notably, even with current advancements in therapy, 2 cases imminently pursued palliative care with comfort measures.

Not surprisingly, the outcomes of septum pellucidum GBM were typically poor in the reviewed cases. While the first reported cases resulted in rapid death, outcomes improved over time, with 2 cases reporting almost a year of preserved mental status before death at 13 months after diagnosis.3–6 In contrast, the most recently published case demonstrated complete remission, with a preserved quality of life at 61 months after treatment, suggesting that the evolution of contemporary multimodal therapies can yield favorable outcomes.7 Along with the regrettably brief 11-day survival observed in our case, these published reports underscore the diverse historical and contemporary trajectories of GBM within the septum pellucidum. Addressing this formidable challenge emphasizes the requirement for tailored, multidisciplinary approaches, with the potential for improved outcomes through the continual evolution of treatment modalities for this devastating diagnosis.

Observations

This illustrative case study of a 69-year-old male with GBM in the septum pellucidum offers additional insights into the clinical spectrum of septum pellucidum GBMs when compared to the 5 previously documented cases. While sharing some similarities, there are notable differences in the clinical presentation, workup, and management.

Our case stands out as the first report of a septum pellucidum GBM in a male patient, deviating from the exclusively female occurrences in the previously published cases. Despite the well-documented male predominance of GBM, this anomaly suggests the need for further investigation into any gender-related aspects of septum pellucidum GBMs.1 Additionally, the tendency of GBMs to affect an older population aligns with our case, and the majority of the reviewed cases were patients older than 40 years.1, 9

In a diagnostic pattern consistent with some of the previously reported cases, the clinical manifestation of cognitive decline in our patient prompted cranial imaging, leading to diagnosis.5–7 It is noteworthy that GBMs can manifest with a variety of symptoms, as evidenced by prior cases reporting headaches, altered consciousness, and focal neurological deficits.4–6 Advanced imaging techniques, such as diffusion-weighted MRI, played a crucial role in characterizing the lesion in our case, emphasizing the importance of sophisticated diagnostic tools in elucidating the nature of these intracranial malignancies.

In the clinical management of our case, the patient underwent a brain biopsy, septostomy, and placement of a VP shunt, highlighting the significance of CSF diversion in addressing GBMs and mass lesions in the unique anatomical location of the septum pellucidum. Furthermore, the use of stereotactic guidance and endoscopy during the procedure demonstrates the evolving landscape of neurosurgical techniques, showcasing their dual role in both diagnosis and treatment. Despite these interventions, the patient’s rapid deterioration sheds light on the challenging nature of septum pellucidum GBMs and underscores the need for further research into the underlying factors influencing their occurrence and management.

Lessons

This illustrative case and review summarize the uncommon occurrence of GBM in the septum pellucidum, emphasizing the necessity for special considerations in managing such cases. Our findings underscore the heterogeneity in clinical presentations and management strategies for septum pellucidum GBMs, highlighting the formidable nature of GBMs within this region. Even with prompt intervention and the continued evolution of advanced neurosurgical techniques, septum pellucidum GBMs require individualized treatment plans and a multidisciplinary approach to effectively address the complexities of this rare and challenging intracranial malignancy.
