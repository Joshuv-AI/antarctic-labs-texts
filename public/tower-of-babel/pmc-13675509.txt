Integrated multitissue analysis reveals distinct oxidative stress responses during recovery from cold shock in the Amazonian fish Colossoma macropomum
Authors: Naiara Melo, Isaac Konig, Stefania Priscilla de Souza, Maria Fernanda Aquino Freitas, Daniella Aparecida de Jesus Paula, Ronald Kennedy Luz, Priscila Vieira Rosa, Luis David Solis Murgas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTambaqui (Colossoma macropomum) is a tropical fish widely cultivated in South America. The increasing demand for this species has heightened interest in its cultivation in subtropical regions with greater thermal variability, where cold shock may induce oxidative stress. This study evaluated the effects of cold shock and subsequent recovery on antioxidant enzyme activity, which included superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase (GR), glutathione S‐transferase (GST) and oxidative damage biomarkers (lipid peroxidation and protein carbonyl) in different tissues of juvenile tambaqui exposed to cold shock at 20°C for 24 h. Samples were collected before temperature reduction (baseline) and at 1, 24 and 48 h after returning to 28°C (recovery phase). Because sampling was restricted to the recovery period, the biomarkers primarily reflected post‐stressor physiological reorganization. Recovery responses were strongly tissue‐specific. The liver exhibited the broadest biomarker modulation, whereas the gills showed the highest integrated disturbance and the earliest response according to Integrated Biomarker Response version 2 (IBRv2). The brain displayed the clearest tendency towards restoration of basal redox homeostasis, while the intestine and muscle retained oxidative demands during later recovery. These findings demonstrate that post‐cold recovery in juvenile tambaqui is a dynamic and tissue‐dependent process and highlight the importance of multibiomarker and multitissue approaches for physiological monitoring and thermal stress assessment in tropical aquaculture species.Keywords: antioxidant enzymes, cold shock, integrated biomarker response (IBRv2), oxidative stress, Tambaqui

INTRODUCTION

Water temperature is one of the main limiting factors for fish because, as ectothermic animals, it determines the rate of biochemical reactions and, consequently, the energy balance available for functions such as feeding, digestion, locomotion, growth and reproduction (Reid et al., 2022; Volkoff & Rønnestad, 2020). In addition to the absolute value, the rate of thermal change is also crucial, as rapid changes tend to produce more severe effects than gradual ones, as they reduce the possibility of timely physiological adjustment (Donaldson et al., 2008; Reid et al., 2022). Thus, abrupt temperature drops over a short period characterize cold shock, which can trigger stress responses and physiological imbalances (e.g., changes in cortisol and glucose levels, ionic/acid–base disturbances, oxidative stress and increased maintenance costs). This is often accompanied by reduced locomotor performance and impaired reflexes/activity, with implications for vulnerability and mortality (Donaldson et al., 2008; Islam et al., 2021; Michie et al., 2020; Szekeres et al., 2014). This type of event can occur both under natural conditions and in anthropogenic situations, such as handling, transport and aquaculture systems with low thermal inertia (Donaldson et al., 2008; Reid et al., 2022; Szekeres et al., 2014).

Tambaqui (Colossoma macropomum) is one of the most important native species for inland aquaculture in South America, with production concentrated in tropical regions characterized by low annual thermal amplitude and typically warm waters (Agudelo et al., 2022; Hilsdorf et al., 2022; Valladão et al., 2018). Endemic to the Amazon and Orinoco basins, the species evolved in an environment driven by extreme seasonal fluctuations in dissolved oxygen and water chemistry (Val & Wood, 2022). To thrive in such heterogeneous habitats, C. macropomum exhibits remarkable phenotypic, biochemical and physiological plasticity (Val & Almeida‐Val, 2026). A classic example of its resilience is its high tolerance to severe hypoxia, characterized by the reversible expansion of the lower lip to facilitate aquatic surface respiration (Fagundes et al., 2026; Val & Wood, 2022). This morphological adaptation is supported by profound metabolic reprogramming, including the activation of the hypoxia‐inducible factor (HIF) pathway, downregulation of aerobic pathways and enhanced glycolytic flux to sustain local energy demands (Fagundes et al., 2026).

Although the species is recognized for its robustness in relation to some management stressors and these natural environmental extremes, it is a tropical fish with marked restrictions at low temperatures, which limits its performance and the feasibility of cultivation in subtropical regions (Agudelo et al., 2022). Thermal deviations from its optimal range strongly challenge its homeostasis and redox balance (Val & Wood, 2022), altering its antioxidant status and inducing severe cell damage (Amanajás et al., 2024). For instance, evidence from farming under subtropical conditions reports episodes of total mortality after consecutive days with minimum temperatures around 15°C (Meurer & Filho, 1997). Several studies indicate that water temperatures below approximately 15–18°C during the cold season constitute a limit for the establishment and expansion of the species, and that suboptimal ranges (below ~23°C) are associated with reduced performance (Woynárovich & Van Anrooy, 2019). In contrast, the range considered ideal/optimal for culture and performance is around 28–30°C, with experimental evidence of better growth and feed efficiency close to 30°C (Chu Koo et al., 2011; Hilsdorf et al., 2022; Woynárovich & Van Anrooy, 2019).

In addition to classical stress responses (endocrine and metabolic), rapid cooling and/or exposure to suboptimal temperatures can impact redox balance, as thermal changes alter mitochondrial function and the efficiency of aerobic metabolism, favouring the formation of reactive species and requiring adjustments in the antioxidant system (Lu et al., 2019; Reid et al., 2022; Tseng et al., 2011). Studies in teleosts, such as Danio rerio, demonstrate that cold shock can induce oxidative damage and tissue‐specific antioxidant responses, showing that different organs (e.g., liver, gills and brain) may display distinct patterns of lipid peroxidation, antioxidant enzyme expression/activity and apoptosis following acute cold exposure (Tseng et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2015).

For C. macropomum, studies have already shown that sudden reductions in temperature trigger haematological and metabolic alterations and modulate antioxidant components. However, much of the available knowledge still focuses on single‐tissue analyses and sampling restricted to the cold exposure period (dos Santos Silva et al., 2024; Paulino et al., 2026). For example, in juveniles subjected to a sudden decrease from 28 to 18°C, changes in plasma markers and liver antioxidant activities were reported within 1–24 h after the onset of the cold shock (dos Santos Silva et al., 2024). In parallel, studies with juvenile tambaqui reinforce that oxidative biomarkers and antioxidant defences are strongly tissue‐dependent, showing contrasting profiles among liver, intestine, gills, muscle, brain and plasma (Melo et al., 2024).

Considering these knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to expose juvenile C. macropomum to a rapid temperature decrease maintained for 24 h and to evaluate antioxidant responses and oxidative damage biomarkers in different tissues in the recovery phase. This approach allows the comparison of the magnitude and temporal dynamics of the responses, as well as the identification of which tissues are more responsive and which return more rapidly to baseline patterns.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study site and ethical approval

The present study was conducted in the laboratories of the Department of Animal Science at the Federal University of Lavras (UFLA), Brazil. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Committee for the Ethical Use of Animals under protocol no. 29/17.

Experimental animals and rearing conditions

The animals used in this study were obtained from induced reproduction carried out at the Aquaculture Laboratory of the Federal University of Minas Gerais (LAQUA‐UFMG). Five‐day post‐hatch larvae were fed with Artemia salina for 15 days, followed by a powdered diet containing 46% crude protein up to 70 days post‐hatching (Santos et al., 2021). During this early phase, fish were reared in 28 L tanks connected to a Recirculating Aquaculture System (RAS) at a stocking density of 50 larvae L−1, with water temperature maintained at 28°C. Subsequently, the juveniles were transferred to 800 L tanks in a RAS, following routine maintenance protocols (Ananias et al., 2024; Santos et al., 2021). In this phase, the fish were kept at a density of 2000 individuals per tank at 28°C and fed with a powdered diet at 10% of their biomass, mixed with an extruded commercial feed (1.3–1.5 mm in diameter, Wean Prime line, Total Rações, Três Corações, Brazil) containing 45% crude protein.

Juvenile tambaqui (C. macropomum) with an initial body weight of 75.57 ± 7.13 g and a total length of 16.83 ± 0.92 cm were distributed among three 400 L tanks connected to a RAS equipped with mechanical and biological filtration, temperature control and supplemental aeration. Fish were acclimated to the experimental conditions for 15 days. The photoperiod was maintained at 12 h light:12 h dark (12L:12D) using a timer (Brasfort 8769). During acclimation, fish were kept under the following conditions: temperature of 28.12 ± 0.25°C, dissolved oxygen of 6.91 ± 0.27 mg L−1 and pH of 6.91 ± 0.27. Fish were fed three times daily with an extruded commercial diet (AQUOS Tropical; 2–4 mm; 320 g kg−1 crude protein, 7.5 g kg−1 lipids, 120 g kg−1 ash and 4.5 g kg−1 fibre). The animals were fasted for 24 h before the start of the experiment.

Cold shock protocol and recovery

Cold shock was induced by a controlled decrease in water temperature in the RAS from 28 to 20°C by water replacement using two auxiliary reservoirs with a combined volume of 600 L, consisting of one 400 L reservoir and one 200 L reservoir. The RAS consisted of three 400 L tanks containing fish. The water in the auxiliary reservoirs was cooled to 12°C using ice, which was added only to these reservoirs and not directly to the tanks containing fish, thereby preventing direct contact between ice and fish.

To induce the temperature decrease, the water outlet of the tanks was opened, allowing part of the 28°C water to leave the system while cooled water from the auxiliary reservoirs entered the RAS and mixed with the water in the tanks. Water movement during this procedure was driven by a Sarlo Aquafortis 20000 submersible pump, with a nominal flow capacity of 19,000 L h−1. Under the experimental configuration, the measured water flow was 22.0 L min−1 tank−1. Considering the three tanks, the total measured flow was 66.0 L min−1. The target temperature of 20°C was reached after 11 min, corresponding to an average cooling rate of 0.727°C min−1, equivalent to 43.6°C h−1. During the cooling procedure, continuous water circulation and supplemental aeration were maintained to promote water mixing and reduce thermal heterogeneity. Water temperature was monitored during the cooling process until the target temperature was reached.

Fish were maintained at 20°C for 24 h. After this period, the RAS temperature controller was reset to 28°C, allowing the water temperature to gradually return to the initial condition without abrupt heating. Recovery began after the 24 h exposure at 20°C with the adjustment of the thermal controller to 28°C. Temperature was gradually restored under continuous recirculation and supplemental aeration until reaching 28°C. Recovery time 0 h was defined as the moment when the water temperature reached 28°C, from which sampling was performed at 1, 24 and 48 h.

Sampling and tissue collection

Baseline sampling (0 h) was performed before the induction of thermal stress. After the system returned to 28°C (recovery phase), additional samplings were conducted at 1, 24 and 48 h. At each time point, six fish were collected per tank, totalling 18 fish per sampling point. Fish were euthanized by spinal cord section, followed by dissection and collection of brain, gills, intestine, liver and a portion of white muscle from the dorsal region. The tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until sample processing. For statistical analyses, the tank was considered the experimental unit. Six fish were sampled from each tank at each time point (18 fish per time point in total), and fish from the same tank were treated as biological subsamples. Individual biomarker values were averaged within each tank, and the resulting tank mean (n = 3 tank means per time point) was used for all statistical analyses.

Tissue homogenization and protein quantification

Tissue samples were initially weighed using an analytical balance (Shimadzu AUW220D) and homogenized in ice‐cold saline buffer (0.138 M NaCl; 0.0027 M KCl; pH 7.2) at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The homogenates were centrifuged at 7000 × g for 15 min at 4°C (Multifuge X1R, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The resulting supernatant was carefully collected, divided into four 500 μL aliquots and stored at −80°C until biochemical analyses.

Total protein concentration in the homogenates was determined following the Bradford method (Bradford, 1976). For this purpose, 10 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 200 μL of Bradford reagent, and a standard curve was constructed using bovine serum albumin as the reference protein. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader. Protein concentrations were used to normalize the activities of antioxidant enzymes and the levels of oxidative damage biomarkers described below. All analyses were performed in triplicate, and the interval between tissue sampling and completion of the assays was approximately 1 week.

Antioxidant enzymes and oxidative damage biomarkers

Antioxidant enzyme activities were determined in tissue homogenates. Catalase (CAT, EC 1.11.1.6) activity was measured by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 240 nm according to Aebi (1984). Superoxide dismutase (SOD, EC 1.15.1.1) activity was determined based on the inhibition of pyrogallol auto‐oxidation following the method of Madesh and Balasubramanian (1997), with absorbance measured at 570 nm. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx, EC 1.11.1.9) activity was quantified by monitoring NADPH oxidation at 340 nm in a coupled reaction system containing reduced glutathione (GSH) and glutathione reductase (GR), according to Günzler et al. (1984). GR (EC 1.8.1.7) activity was also determined by monitoring NADPH oxidation at 340 nm, following the method of Günzler et al. (1984). Glutathione S‐transferase (GST, EC 2.5.1.18) activity was measured at 340 nm using 1‐chloro‐2,4‐dinitrobenzene (CDNB) as a substrate according to Habig et al. (1974).

Oxidative damage was evaluated through lipid peroxidation and protein oxidation markers. Lipid peroxidation was estimated by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), using malondialdehyde (MDA) as the reference compound, with absorbance read at 530 nm (Buege & Aust, 1978). Protein oxidation was assessed by determining protein carbonyl (PC) content after derivatization with 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) according to Reznick and Packer (1994).

Detailed descriptions of the enzymatic assays and oxidative damage determinations are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Integrated biomarker response version 2

Integrated biomarker response version 2 (IBRv2) was calculated according to the methodology proposed by Beliaeff and Burgeot (2002) and later refined by Sanchez et al. (2013). The index was calculated separately for each tissue, using the basal group as the reference (X 0j ). For each biomarker (j) and sampling time (i), the mean values (X ij ) were normalized relative to the basal condition and expressed as a logarithmic ratio (Y ij = log(X ij /X 0j )). Subsequently, for each biomarker, the mean (μ j ) and standard deviation (s j ) of Y ij were calculated considering all evaluated time points (0, 1, 24 and 48 h), and the values were standardized using a z‐score transformation (Z ij = (Y ij − μ j )/s j ). The deviation relative to the reference group was then obtained as A ij = Z ij − Z 0j . The IBRv2 for each time point was calculated as the sum of the absolute values of these deviations (IBRv2 i = Σ j |A ij |). The A ij values were represented using star plots, in which positive and negative values indicate induction or inhibition of each biomarker relative to the basal condition, respectively. Finally, the integrated index for each tissue throughout the experiment was obtained by summing the IBRv2 values across all experimental time points.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Minitab® 23 (Minitab LLC, State College, PA, USA), and graphs were generated using Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The tank was considered the experimental unit, with each value corresponding to the mean of one tank. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test. When necessary, data were transformed using the Box‐Cox procedure to meet the assumptions of parametric analysis. After transformation, normality and homogeneity of variances were reassessed. For each tissue, differences among experimental time points were analysed using one‐way ANOVA. When significant effects were detected, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey's post hoc test. The level of significance was set at 5%. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation using untransformed data for biological interpretation.

DISCUSSION

In fish, thermal changes can destabilize redox balance and promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, so the activation of antioxidant defences is a central component for maintaining homeostasis and mitigating damage to lipids and proteins (Lushchak, 2011; Reid et al., 2022). A key aspect of our experimental design is that sampling was conducted exclusively during the recovery phase, so the analysed biomarkers primarily reflect physiological reorganization after cold shock and the redox costs associated with metabolic resumption. This is relevant because recovery can be biologically critical and not necessarily milder than exposure to the stressor, and redox challenges may persist even after thermal restoration (Sánchez‐Nuño et al., 2018). From an applied perspective, understanding the recovery dynamics after cold shock in C. macropomum, a tropical species with limitations to low temperatures, can support thermal management strategies and physiological monitoring aimed at reducing sublethal damage, mortality and production losses in aquaculture.

The cerebral response to cold shock results from the integrated action of different antioxidant systems (Wu et al., 2015). SOD, which constitutes the first line of defence, and GR maintained stable activities in the brain, suggesting preservation and maintenance of thiol redox status. This interpretation is consistent with studies in D. rerio and Carassius auratus, where GSH metabolism in brain tissue was preserved even under acute cold exposure (Lushchak & Bagnyukova, 2006a; Tseng et al., 2011). Additionally, the early mobilization of GST at 1 h suggests an immediate response in the detoxification of secondary oxidation products (Lushchak & Bagnyukova, 2006b). An efficient antioxidant system is important since this organ has high metabolic demand and elevated levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids, making it naturally vulnerable to ROS (Papoutsoglou, 2012; Ratko et al., 2022). The effectiveness of the antioxidant defence is confirmed by the absence of protein carbonylation, corroborating findings in Astyanax lacustris (Ratko et al., 2022). In the literature, this protection of the neuronal proteome has been attributed to the induction of heat shock proteins, which act as molecular chaperones, preventing protein misfolding and oxidation (Li et al., 2024). In parallel, the reduction in MDA levels at 24 h suggests the neutralization of peroxides, metabolically supported by the pentose phosphate pathway, whose activation of the enzyme G6PDH provides NADPH essential for GSH recycling, protecting membranes and preventing apoptosis (Ratko et al., 2022; Toni et al., 2019).

The integration of all these biomarkers through the IBRv2 index allows visualization of the overall adaptive effort of brain tissue during recovery. The expansion of the area occupied by the index at 24 h characterizes what the literature defines as the physiological reorganization window, representing the moment of greatest mobilization to achieve a new homeostatic state (Campos et al., 2019; Joy et al., 2017). Mathematically, this peak at 24 h is driven by specific variables that act as vectors within the index (Beliaeff & Burgeot, 2002), with the activity of glutathione system enzymes, together with the reversal of MDA levels, being the main factors expanding the radar chart area at this time point. The decline toward baseline levels at 48 h indicates that the brain established effective compensatory mechanisms, with adaptive plasticity and recovery capacity after cold shock, without compromising the functional integrity of the organ. This reflects a maximal physiological mobilization effort to neutralize ROS before the onset of permanent damage (Campos et al., 2019; Li et al., 2024).

In cultured tropical species such as C. macropomum, cold stress can rapidly compromise gill function, as this tissue acts as the primary interface with the environment and is highly susceptible to structural alterations and redox imbalance induced by low temperatures (Refaey et al., 2023; Schleger et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2018). In our study, the recovery phase was accompanied by a biphasic antioxidant response in this tissue. In the first hour of recovery, reduced CAT and GST activities were observed, while SOD activity remained unchanged, suggesting a lower capacity for peroxide removal, consistent with the transient modulation of antioxidant enzymes already described for gills under acute thermal challenges (Lushchak & Bagnyukova, 2006a; Rossi et al., 2017). In subsequent time points, increased activities of SOD, GPx and GST were observed, while CAT remained reduced and GR activity showed no change, suggesting a greater contribution of the glutathione‐dependent antioxidant system. This pattern is consistent with the literature, which describes increased participation of these enzymes during recovery after thermal challenges (da Silva et al., 2024; Schleger et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2018).

The maintenance of PC levels in the gills, together with the reduction in MDA levels, is consistent with the limitation of oxidative damage in gill tissue throughout recovery. Overall, the data suggest that although the initial phase of recovery involves modulation of enzymes associated with peroxide removal, C. macropomum exhibits antioxidant adjustment capacity in the gills, favouring the reorganization of the redox response during recovery (Duan et al., 2024). The gill IBRv2 index complements these findings by highlighting the overall adaptive effort of the organ through a rapid response in the first hour of recovery, associated with initial changes in CAT and GST and a reduction in MDA (Devin et al., 2014). At 24 h, the maintenance of the index indicates persistence of the adaptive effort, associated with increased SOD, GPx and GST, reflecting biochemical adjustments aimed at stabilizing redox status (Wen et al., 2018). Finally, the reduction of the index at 48 h indicates progressive stabilization and return to homeostasis, suggesting that compensatory mechanisms were effective in restoring gill function and preserving the functional integrity of the fish after cold shock (Campos et al., 2019; Schleger et al., 2024).

Although the intestine is often addressed in feeding trials and studies of nutritional stress, this tissue also deserves attention under cold stress conditions, as it plays a central role in nutrient digestion and absorption and acts as a physico‐immunological barrier (Guerreiro et al., 2023; Ramos‐Oliveira et al., 2026). This functional combination results in high metabolic activity of the epithelium, making the intestine particularly sensitive to changes in redox balance (Chen et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). In our study, we observed a compensatory antioxidant response within the first 24 h, evidenced by increased activities of SOD, GPx, GST and GR, while the maintenance and subsequent reduction of CAT activity suggest a greater contribution of the glutathione‐dependent antioxidant system in peroxide removal (Lv et al., 2021). This pattern is consistent with that described in teleost species such as Brachymystax lenok tsinlingensis and D. rerio (Chu et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2023).

Regarding oxidative damage in the intestine, no changes were observed in PC levels. In contrast, lipid peroxidation increased at 24 h, peaking at 48 h, indicating that the antioxidant response was not sufficient to prevent the progression of oxidative damage. The high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids in intestinal cells, resulting from lipid absorption, makes this tissue particularly susceptible to oxidation (Chen et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Magouz et al., 2022). This pattern is consistent with reports in teleosts exposed to acute stressors, showing increased intestinal MDA levels (Chen et al., 2023; Dawood et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022). Complementing these findings, the IBRv2 index identified a peak in physiological reorganization at 24 h and a decline at 48 h, indicating that, despite the delayed peak in MDA levels, the overall set of compensatory mechanisms was effective in stabilizing homeostasis (Lv et al., 2021).

In studies investigating different types of stress in fish, the liver is among the main tissues evaluated due to its central role in metabolism, biotransformation and maintenance of homeostasis (Figueiredo‐Fernandes et al., 2006). This high energetic demand makes hepatic tissue particularly susceptible to ROS production under thermal challenge conditions (Sun et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019). In our study, during the recovery phase after cold stress, the liver of C. macropomum showed increased activities of SOD, GPx and GR within the first hour of recovery. This pattern is consistent with a higher oxidative demand in hepatic tissue during post‐cold physiological reorganization, a condition that, in other studies, has been associated with intensified oxidative metabolism and electron leakage in the respiratory chain (Brand, 2016; Mailloux, 2018; Qi et al., 2013).

In this context, the absence of an increase in MDA in the liver compared to baseline is consistent with an initial limitation of oxidative damage to lipids, a pattern also described in juveniles of C. macropomum subjected to thermal challenges (dos Santos Silva et al., 2024). On the other hand, the maintenance of the hepatic antioxidant response throughout recovery appears to depend, in part, on the activity of GPx and GR and on the supply of NADPH required for the regeneration of GSH from GSSG (Fan et al., 2014). In other species, the literature indicates that, under acute thermal challenges, this demand may be associated with the involvement of the pentose phosphate pathway and G6PDH (Dalvi et al., 2017; Guillen et al., 2019; Laiz‐Carrión et al., 2002). The reduction in SOD, GPx and GR activities, together with increased protein carbonylation, indicates diminished hepatic antioxidant capacity at later stages and enhanced protein damage, consistent with studies showing that proteins are preferential targets under thermal stress (Grim et al., 2010; Malek et al., 2004; Wen et al., 2018). Unlike the pattern observed in the IBRv2 in other tissues, the liver exhibited an immediate response followed by a progressive decline in subsequent hours (Campos et al., 2019; Devin et al., 2014). In this specific context of hepatic tissue, this reduction does not indicate homeostatic recovery, but rather a possible limitation in sustaining the antioxidant response over time, suggesting greater susceptibility to the progression of structural oxidative damage (da Silva et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019). This behaviour corroborates studies identifying the liver as a highly demanding organ under thermal stress, in which prolonged exposure may compromise the antioxidant system and affect cellular integrity (Rossi et al., 2017; Roychowdhury et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2020).

Skeletal muscle represents the largest fraction of fish body mass, constitutes a functionally diverse tissue, and responds sensitively to metabolic and environmental changes, with direct implications for growth, performance and final product quality (Chu et al., 2021; Dettleff et al., 2020; Johnston et al., 2011). In this context, cold stress imposes significant physiological and metabolic challenges on teleost muscle tissue (Guderley, 2004). During the recovery phase, we observed modulation of the antioxidant response in this tissue throughout the experimental period, with changes in enzymatic activities but no evidence of a sustained protective antioxidant response. In contrast to the enzymatic profile, muscle showed reduced lipid peroxidation (MDA), suggesting stability of the lipid component, possibly associated with membrane remodelling and reduced propagation of peroxidation at low temperatures (Chu et al., 2021; Grim et al., 2010; Malek et al., 2004). On the other hand, there was a progressive increase in protein carbonylation, indicating a greater incidence of oxidative damage to proteins during recovery, a pattern also described in other species (Pereira et al., 2023).

Overall, the data indicate a dissociation between MDA and PC, with greater vulnerability of the protein component in muscle. This pattern is represented by the IBRv2 as a scenario of metabolic effort and vulnerability. Muscle reached its peak mobilization at 24 h, maintaining a high plateau up to 48 h. The expansion and maintenance of the radar chart area illustrate the continuous effort of the antioxidant system in response to the challenge imposed by recovery after cold shock (Campos et al., 2019). However, consistent with the dissociation observed in damage biomarkers, the persistence of this elevated index indicates that the muscle response capacity was directed and limited (Grim et al., 2010; Kammer et al., 2011). Although mobilization was effective in protecting membrane lipid structures, it was not sufficient to fully neutralize ROS, allowing the progression of oxidative stress and leading to protein carbonylation (Corrêa et al., 2024; Melo et al., 2024).

In the context of tropical aquaculture species, cold shock episodes represent critical events, as they may compromise physiological functions even after thermal restoration. In C. macropomum, recovery after cold shock was tissue‐specific, with a temporal redistribution of redox costs among organs: gills responded more promptly, the liver concentrated the integrated response in the early phases, while brain and intestine expressed a relevant part of this reorganization at later time points, and muscle showed a slower recovery. This pattern is consistent with the tissue variability described in juvenile tambaqui and in other species subjected to different stressors (Corrêa et al., 2024; Melo et al., 2024) and reinforces IBRv2 as a useful tool for integrating complex redox responses.

CONCLUSION

The present study demonstrates that post‐cold recovery in juvenile C. macropomum is a dynamic, time‐dependent and tissue‐specific process. The liver stood out for the greatest amplitude of biomarker changes, suggesting high redox reactivity and lower sustainability of the antioxidant response throughout recovery. In parallel, the gills responded immediately, while the muscle and intestine showed more delayed oxidative damage. The brain, in turn, showed a greater tendency to restore basal homeostasis. The multitissue approach, combined with IBRv2, allowed the integration of antioxidant responses across different tissues, revealing that recovery involves biological costs even after thermal restoration. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating the recovery phase into experimental assessments of cold stress in tropical fish and provide a physiological basis for management and monitoring strategies applied to aquaculture.
