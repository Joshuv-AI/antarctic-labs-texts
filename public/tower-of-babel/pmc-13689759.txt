Critical Aortic Stenosis With Rapid Multiorgan Decompensation Reversed by Emergency TAVR
Authors: David G. Gent, Majid Jafar, Marwa Daghem, Kully Sandhu, Warkaa Shamkhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCritical aortic stenosis can deteriorate abruptly from compensated disease to low-output shock and multiorgan dysfunction. We describe rapid multiorgan decompensation from severe bicuspid aortic stenosis with recovery following emergency transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).Case SummaryA previously active 69-year-old man with severe bicuspid aortic stenosis re-presented to the emergency department with pulmonary edema and rapidly progressive breathlessness. Although systolic blood pressure was preserved, he had severe hypoperfusion with lactate 8.6 mmol/L, alanine aminotransferase 5,379 IU/L, international normalized ratio 2.8, creatinine 189 μmol/L, and bilirubin 48 μmol/L. Repeat echocardiography demonstrated new biventricular dysfunction, functional mitral regurgitation, and a high probability of pulmonary hypertension. He underwent emergency transfemoral TAVR, which reduced the gradient from 129 to <10 mm Hg. The biochemical abnormalities improved within 48 hours, and he was discharged well 8 days later.DiscussionThis case illustrates rapid, reversible multiorgan decompensation caused by critical aortic stenosis.Take-Home MessagesDeteriorating critical aortic stenosis should prompt urgent reassessment and expedited intervention. Emergency TAVR can restore forward flow and facilitate rapid physiologic recovery.

History of Presentation

A previously active 69-year-old man with no comorbidities attended the emergency department of the local hospital with progressive exertional breathlessness. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed, revealing critical aortic stenosis (AS) (Vmax: 5.0 m/s), moderate aortic regurgitation (AR), and mild left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). The patient was hemodynamically compensated with no signs of volume overload and was referred to cardiology for urgent outpatient follow-up. He remained physically active, attending the gym 3 times weekly. Over the next 6 weeks (Figure 2), he re-presented to the emergency department with progressive exertional breathlessness and was treated with oral diuretics pending outpatient cardiology review. A computed tomography (CT) scan of his aortic valve and aorta was performed to help guide planned intervention. The CT showed a heavily calcified bicuspid valve with suitable transfemoral access and no significant coronary artery disease (Figure 3). Given the progressive symptoms, he was referred for urgent surgical outpatient assessment. He then presented in extremis to the emergency department, 6 weeks after his initial presentation, with normotensive cardiogenic shock (SCAI [Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions] stage C-D). His blood pressure was stable at 110 mm Hg systolic with profound biochemical evidence of hypoperfusion: alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 5,379 IU/L, international normalized ratio (INR) 2.8, lactate 8.6 mmol/L, creatinine 189 μmol/L, and bilirubin 48 μmol/L. The working diagnosis was ischemic hepatitis, cardiogenic acute kidney injury, and global low-output physiology (Figure 4). Past Medical History

The patient had no significant past medical history and was previously physically active.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included acute severe AR, acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia, infective endocarditis, and aortic dissection, The index of suspicion for endocarditis and dissection was low, and there were no supportive clinical features, vegetations, abscess formation, or aortic root dissection present on cardiac imaging.

Management

After stabilization, the patient underwent emergency transfemoral TAVR with anesthetic support and pre-emptive temporary pacing. A 26-mm Sapien 3 valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was selected, with +1 mL overfill planned to ensure adequate expansion.

Right radial access was used for monitoring, and right femoral access was obtained under ultrasound guidance with 2 ProGlides (Abbott). After crossing the valve, a 22-mm balloon predilatation was performed. A 26-mm Sapien 3 Ultra valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was deployed, with +1 mL added. The predeployment gradient was 129 mm Hg; the postdeployment residual gradient was <10 mm Hg with an AR index of 38, indicating excellent relief of obstruction and no significant residual AR. Angiography confirmed no paravalvular leak or vascular complications. The pacing wire and femoral sheath were removed with successful closure. The patient was transferred to the cardiac care unit on intravenous furosemide and enoximone, with plans for routine blood tests, electrocardiography, and echocardiography the following morning.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Within 48 hours, the ALT, creatinine, INR, lactate, and bilirubin normalized (Figure 4). The biventricular function started to improve, with reduction in MR and pulmonary hypertension. He was discharged on day 8 after TAVR, with near-complete resolution of shock physiology. A follow-up echocardiogram showed stable valve function and improved biventricular performance (Video 4).

Discussion

Cardiogenic shock in the context of severe AS carries substantial mortality, and urgent valve intervention remains the only therapy with survival benefit. Although the safety and success of urgent TAVR to treat patients with severe aortic stenosis and cardiogenic shock has been described previously, it remains under-reported.1, 2, 3 Contemporary registry and meta-analysis consistently demonstrate that emergent TAVR, although higher risk than elective procedures, is superior to medical management alone in cardiogenic shock.4, 5, 6, 7 This case supports this evidence, by demonstrating complete reversal of multiorgan dysfunction, including ischemic hepatitis, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury, within 72 hours of TAVR. Importantly, the patient's initial echocardiogram 6 weeks earlier showed only mild to moderate AR, preserved right ventricular function (RV), mild LV systolic dysfunction, no pulmonary hypertension, and normal mitral and tricuspid valve function. The subsequent development of severe biventricular dysfunction, functional MR and tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, and multiorgan shock thus represents a physiological inflection point rather than the expected disease trajectory. This underscores how rapidly biventricular dysfunction with a global low-output state can develop in the setting of critical AS, prompting urgent reassessment when symptoms escalate.

In critical AS, the LV operates at the limits of compensatory reserve. The sudden decompensation of LV systolic function in a noncompliant, hypertrophied LV results in a rapid elevation in afterload and LV end-diastolic pressure, acute functional MR, and postcapillary pulmonary hypertension, with increased RV afterload, RV dilatation and dysfunction, and severe tricuspid regurgitation, as demonstrated in this case.

The significant biochemical derangements, ALT >5,000 IU/L, INR 2.8, lactate 8.6 mmol/L, and creatinine 189 μmol/L, are characteristic of global low-output hypoperfusion and fulfill criteria for stage C-D normotensive cardiogenic shock, despite a preserved blood pressure of 110/50 mm Hg. In contemporary shock classification, elevated lactate and end-organ dysfunction define true cardiogenic shock, whereas hypotension is not required. The rapid and near-complete normalization of hepatic, renal, and coagulation parameters within 48 to 72 hours after restoration of forward flow with TAVR strongly supports a purely hemodynamic mechanism. This dramatic normalization highlights an important concept: multiorgan dysfunction caused by valvular obstruction can be fully reversible when forward flow is promptly restored.

This case underscores another important learning point: In patients with known severe AS, presentations with shortness of breath or escalating symptoms should prompt urgent reassessment with repeat echocardiography, irrespective of how recently the prior study was performed. Acute deterioration in ventricular function may occur abruptly in a nonlinear fashion and significantly accelerate hemodynamic deterioration.

Finally, this case reinforces the expanding role of salvage TAVR in hemodynamically unstable patients with anatomy suitable for transfemoral access. Although cardiogenic shock has historically been associated with poor outcomes after TAVR, emerging evidence, and the complete recovery demonstrated here, supports its use when the underlying mechanism is valve-driven and rapidly reversible.

Conclusions

Rapid progression of biventricular dysfunction in critical aortic stenosis can precipitate sudden multivalvular failure and fulminant multiorgan collapse. In selected patients with valve-driven shock and suitable anatomy, emergency TAVR can rapidly reverse severe shock physiology when performed promptly.
