SWATH-MS based proteomics reveals the role of photosynthesis related proteins and secondary metabolic pathways in the colored leaves of sweet olive (Osmanthus fragrans)
Authors: Cheng Zhang, Kailu Zhang, Min Zhang, Daowu Zhang, Qi Ye, Xianrong Wang, Takashi Akagi, Yifan Duan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractColored leaves, a notable horticultural trait, have high research and ornamental value. The evergreen sweet olive (Osmanthus fragrans), one of the top ten traditional flowers in China, has been cultivated for more than two thousand years. However, in recent years, an increasing number of O. fragrans cultivars with colored leaves have been cultivated for their ornamental value. To study the molecular mechanism underlying the observed changes in leaf color, we selected O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ (Y), which has yellow-white leaves, and O. fragrans ‘Sijigui’ (S), which has green leaves, as materials. Pigment content measurement showed that the chlorophyll, carotenoid and anthocyanin contents in Y were lower than in S. According to the SWATH-MS sequencing results, a total of 3,959 proteins were quantitatively identified, 1,300 of which were differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), including 782 up-regulated and 518 down-regulated proteins in Y compared to S. Functional enrichment analysis of DEPs revealed that down-regulated expression of photosynthesis related proteins may lead to the inhibition of chlorophyll synthesis in Y, this may be the main cause of leaf color change. Moreover, a protein interaction prediction model also showed that proteins such as PetC, PsbO, PsbP, and PsbQ were key proteins in the interaction network, and the up-regulated proteins participating in the anthocyanin and carotenoid pathways may be related to the formation of yellow-white leaves. Taken together, our findings represent the first SWATH-MS-based proteomic report on colored leaf O. fragrans and reveal that chlorophyll synthesis and secondary metabolism pathways contribute to the changes in leaf color.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10867-1.Keywords: Osmanthus fragrans, Leaf color, Chlorophyll, Carotenoid, SWATH-MS

Background

Leaves are the main organs involved in plant photosynthesis and can use solar energy to convert CO2 into carbohydrates, fixing CO2, and providing a material basis for plant growth and development [1]. Leaf color mutants can help us understand the genetic mechanisms of plant photosynthesis, Chl biosynthesis, Chl development, Chl degradation, and tetrapyrrole synthesis [2]. Moreover, plants with colored foliage are often used for architectural landscaping or in aesthetic applications due to their high attractiveness, high environmental adaptability, and great economic value [3]. For example, Ginkgo biloba, which has yellow leaves [4]; Saururus chinensis, which has white leaves [5]; Padus virginiana, which has purple leaves [6]; and Anthurium andraeanum, which has red leaf cultivar [7], are widely used in horticulture.

Leaf color is mainly caused by three kinds of pigments, including chlorophylls, flavonoids and anthocyanins, and their content or distribution determines the color of leaves [8, 9]. For instance, VAL1 (virescent-albino leaf 1) is localized in the chloroplast and encodes a phosphoribosylamine-glycine ligase (PurD), the second enzyme in the purine biosynthesis pathway, involved in regulating chloroplast development and chlorophyll metabolism, and the mutant of rice (Oryza sativa) that produces dynamic green-revertible albino and narrow-leaf phenotypes [10]. In Populus deltoids, three structural genes (4CL, CHS, and F3H) and six transcription factors that may be involved in the anthocyanin synthesis pathway were identified in colored leaf cultivars, leading to the abnormal anthocyanin accumulation and making the leaves bright red [11]. Long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) libraries for yellow-leaf and normal green-leaf ginkgo trees has been sequenced, and lcnRNAs and their target genes participate in the chloroplast thylakoid membrane subcategory, and photosynthesis may result in yellow-leaf ginkgo [12]. Transposon insertion leads to alternative splicing of the LsGLK (GOLDEN2-like) gene, which is involved in chlorophyll development, generating a large number of nonfunctional transcripts and decreasing the expression of the LsGLK gene, resulting in light green lettuce (Lactuca sativa) leaves [13]. Although there are various reasons for leaf color variation, most of these factors affect the color of leaves by regulating the synthesis or degradation of the three types of pigments mentioned above.

As a woody ornamental flower plant, the evergreen Osmanthus fragrans is one of the top ten traditional flowers and has been cultivated in China for more than two thousand years. O. fragrans is widely distributed from south of the Qinling Mountains to north of the Nanling Mountains in China, and is also cultivated in Japan, Vietnam and other countries. To date, there are more than 200 cultivars of O. fragrans, and new cultivars are constantly being cultivated [14]. All the cultivars are mainly divided into five major groups, namely, the Albus group (greenish white to yellowish white flowers), Luteus group (yellow flowers), Auranticus group (light orange to orange-red flowers), Asiaticus group (flowering in multiple seasons a year) and the Color group, which is a newly established cultivar group. Color group is usually artificially selected from other groups and propagated by cuttings and grafting. In contrast to those of the other four groups, which mainly focus on flower viewing and fragrance, the leaves of the Color group cultivars exhibit distinctive color variation and high ornamental value [15]. Among them, O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ is a typical cultivar in the Color group [16]. However, to date, there are few studies on the mechanism of leaf color change in Osmanthus Color group cultivars.

In this study, we used the typical cultivar O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ with yellow-white leaves and O. fragrans ‘Sijigui’ with green leaves as materials to detect the difference in pigment content, and used quantitative proteomics to identify the differences in protein expression; GO and KEGG analysis of the differentially expressed proteins revealed important pathways affecting the color of leaves. Our results lay the foundation for a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanism of leaf color variation in Osmanthus cultivars.

Results

Leaf color and pigment content

The colored leaf cultivar O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ (Y) was derived from the Asiaticus group cultivar, and its young leaves were purplish-red or pink at the edges and light red in the middle. The leaf color changed with growth, and the mature leaves were irregular yellow-white and green overall (Fig. 1A). The young leaves of O. fragrans ‘Sijigui’ (S) were similar to those of ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ (Y), with an overall purplish-red or pink color, but the mature ‘Sijigui’ were all green leaves (Fig. 1B).

Phenotype of leaves and four pigments content. (A) Mature leaves of O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ (Y). (B) Mature leaves of O. fragrans ‘Sijigui’ (S). C-F. Content of chlorophyll a, b (Chl a, b), carotenoid and anthocyanin in Y and S, respectively

The contents of chlorophyll a and b (Chl a and b) and carotenoids in leaves of ‘Sijigui’ were dramatically higher than those in the leaves of ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’. Even though the content of anthocyanin was also higher than in ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’, the difference was not significant (Fig. 1C-F). In ‘Sijigui’, the content of chlorophyll a (Chl a) was the highest at 9.02 mg/g FW (Fresh Weight), and the content of anthocyanin was the lowest at 0.99 mg/g FW. In ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’, the content of chlorophyll a (Chl a) was the highest, reaching 3.58 mg/g FW, and the content of carotenoids was the lowest, at 0.79 mg/g FW. The total pigment content of the ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ cultivar was 6.52 mg/g FW, which was much lower than that of the ‘Sijigui’ cultivar (15.73 mg/g FW). Interestingly, the main pigment was chlorophyll, and the Chl a content accounted for more than 50% of all the contents in both cultivars; however, the relative proportion of the carotenoid and anthocyanin content in Y was higher than that in S, respectively (Table 1).

Quality of SWATH-MS and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) identification

Principal component analysis (PCA) can visually display the differences between different samples through the spatial distribution of data, the closer the spatial distribution is, the smaller the data differences. According to the PCA results, the Y and S duplicate samples were clustered in relatively concentrated regions and could be distinguished by the first principal component (PC1, 49.55%) (Fig. 2A). Correlation analysis can be used to visually demonstrate the similarity of the total protein quantification across samples. The correlation between the three replicates of the same cultivar was greater than 0.84, while the correlation between Y and S was less than 0.74, indicating a difference in protein expression patterns between the two cultivars (Fig. 2B) and the sequencing quality was sufficiently high to warrant further analysis.

(C) The volcano plot of the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), the green dots represented down-regulated proteins and red dots indicated up-regulated proteins in Y. (D) Heatmap of DEPs

In this study, a total of 3,959 proteins were identified (Table S1), and proteins with log2(fold change) values greater than 1 or less than − 1 (P < 0.05) were considered differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). There were a total of 1,300 differentially expressed proteins, among which 782 were up-regulated and 518 were down-regulated in Y compared to S, as shown in the volcano plot and heatmap (Fig. 2C-D).

Enrichment analysis and functional annotation of DEPs

The results of the GO enrichment analysis can be divided into three categories, namely, molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC) and biological process (BP), which describing the roles of genes from different perspectives. The top terms corresponding to the up-regulated DEPs of MF/CC/BP were aminoacyl-tRNA ligase activity, plastid, and small molecule metabolic process (Fig. 3A), whereas the top terms corresponding to the down-regulated DEPs of MF/CC/BP were response to chlorophyll binding, photosystem and photosynthesis, light reaction (Fig. 3B). According to the CC classification, both up-regulated and down-regulated DEPs were enriched in chloroplast and membrane related proteins. These DEPs related to chloroplast structure and function may affect pigment synthesis and photosynthesis in chloroplasts, potentially leading to changes in pigment content in chloroplasts and thus affecting leaf color. In addition, according to the BP annotations, the up-regulated DEPs were involved in various metabolic processes, and the down-regulated DEPs were mainly concentrated in the photosynthesis and pigment binding processes. Changes in DEPs during metabolic processes and photosynthesis affect the synthesis of different types of pigments in leaves, and the combination of pigments and proteins to form photosynthetic complexes has an impact on the stability of photosynthesis and pigments, all of which determine the final presentation of leaf color. We speculated that the DEPs identified in these enrichment results may be closely related to the variation in leaf color.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the up and down-regulated DEPs. Orange, blue and green terms represent the top 10 BP, CC and MF, respectively

We also performed KEGG pathway analysis on the DEPs. All the up-regulated DEPs in Y were mapped and enriched in 57 pathways, most of which were related to different amino acid metabolism pathways, and there were also several secondary metabolism pathways, such as flavonoid biosynthesis and carotenoid biosynthesis, which may also play important roles in the color change in leaves (Table S2). All the down-regulated DEPs were mapped to 16 pathways, and multiple pathways associated with photosynthesis, including photosynthesis, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, photosynthesis proteins and carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, were significantly enriched (Fig. 4A). Moreover, according to the MapMAN BIN analysis results, photosynthesis was also enriched, and almost all the proteins involved in photosynthesis and the photosynthetic antenna proteins were down-regulated (Fig. 4B). In addition to the photosystem, photosynthetic electron transport (Pet) and F-type ATPase related proteins were also significantly down-regulated in Y (Fig. 5A). However, proteins involved in the porphyrin synthesis pathway, including HemB, HemC, HemF and ALB, were all upregulated in Y (Fig. 5B). In addition, the secondary metabolic pathways in which DEPs are involved can be divided into three parts: the phenylpropanoid (Fig. 6A), flavonoid (Fig. 6B) and carotenoid biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 6C). A total of 18 proteins were found to be up-regulated in these pathways. These DEPs may be the key response after or before the color change (Table S3).

Protein‑protein interaction (PPI) analysis of DEPs

Proteins do not function alone, and the exercise of their function also depends on the regulation and mediation of other proteins. In protein interaction networks, highly clustered proteins may have the same or similar functions, and proteins with high connectivity may be key nodes influencing related signaling pathways or metabolic pathways. In this study, proteins identified by SWATH-MS were subjected to BLAST searches against the protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana, and those with more than 60% identity were subjected to protein‑protein interaction network analysis with STRING. In PPI results, a total of 867 DEPs with 15,782 interactions were predicted (Table S5), and these DEPs were mainly associated with metabolic pathways such as photosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism and flavonoid biosynthesis (Table S3). Among the most critical protein interaction modules screened by MCODE, there were 106 DEPs, including five transcription factors, 22 DEPs involved in photosynthesis, and 79 DEPs involved in other metabolic processes (Fig. 7). Furthermore, DEPs related to photosynthesis, such as ofr.gene43417 (PetC), ofr.gene14553 (PsbP), ofr.gene41337 (GDH1), ofr.gene10958 (PsbO), ofr.gene12380 (FNR1) and ofr.gene7266 (PsbQ), were the key proteins in the interaction network module according to cytoHubba.

In addition to photosynthesis-related proteins, proteins involved in secondary metabolic pathways also play important roles in the pigment synthesis and degradation pathways and are affected by changes in leaf color. The genes encoding the proteins ofr.gene10375 (FLS) and ofr.gene56056 (CHI), which are involved in the flavonoid pathway, as well as the gene encoding proteins ofr.gene11033 (ZEP), ofr.gene26243 (CCD1), and ofr.gene55135 (ZDS), which are involved in the carotenoid metabolic pathway, were quantified to validate the reliability of the proteome data. Discussion

Photosynthesis-related proteins were down-regulated in Y

Chloroplasts are the sites where plants undergo photosynthesis, and the most important parts are photosystem I (PS I) and photosystem II (PS II). These photosystems consist of two components: the reaction-center complex and the light-harvesting complex (LHC) [17]. For example, LHCA is the outer antenna system of photosystem I and can combine with photosynthetic pigments to harvest light and transfer the excitation energy to the reaction center in PS I. Low expression of LHCA also results in sufficient assemble of PS I in albino leaves of Pseudosasa japonica [18, 19]. Knockout of the PsaD gene in Arabidopsis blocks photosynthetic electron flow, resulting in a significant decrease in chlorophyll content, and the mutants exhibit yellowish leaves and slow growth [20, 21]. In PS II, PsbO was reported to protect PS II from photodamage, and the low expression of PsbO1 can also lead to a decrease in the chlorophyll content [22, 23].

Photosynthetic electron transport (Pet) and F-type ATPase related proteins were also significantly down-regulated in Y (Fig. 5A). In the petc and atpd mutants, the total chlorophyll concentration drastically decreased, consistent with the light-green coloration of the leaves. Moreover, in petc-2 mutants, the level of antenna proteins of both photosystems decreased to only approximately 35–40% of that of the wild type [24]. This suggested a complex interaction between photosynthetic proteins and photosynthetic antenna proteins, and our protein interaction prediction results also showed that proteins such as PetC, PsbO, PsbP, and PsbQ were key proteins in the interaction network (Fig. 5). The total chlorophyll content of Y (4.75 mg/g FW) was only 38.87% of that of S (12.22 mg/g FW) (Table 1). Thus, we hypothesized that the down-regulated expression of photosynthesis-related proteins was the main reason for the reduced chlorophyll content.

Porphyrin, the precursor for chlorophyll synthesis

Mutations in any of the genes in the chlorophyll synthesis pathway will result in a loss of synthesis and cause a change in leaf color [25]. Porphyrin is an important precursor for chlorophyll synthesis. In this study, the expression of almost all proteins in the porphyrin synthesis pathway of Y were increased (Fig. 5B). In the early steps of chlorophyll biosynthesis, ERS catalyzes the attachment of glutamate to tRNA (Glu); subsequently, HemA encodes a protein with glutamyl-tRNA reductase (GluTR) activity that catalyzes the NADPH-dependent reduction of Glu-tRNA (Glu) to glutamate 1-semialdehyde (GSA) with the release of free tRNA (Glu) [26]. Overexpression of AtHemA1 in tobacco increased GluTR levels, and seedlings showed a chlorotic phenotype, and chlorotic or variegated cotyledons. Electron microscopy images have shown that plastid structure and membrane topology are altered in mature leaves [27]. ALB catalyzed the insertion of magnesium ions into protoporphyrin IX to yield Mg-protoporphyrin IX. The synthesis from Mg-protoporphyrin IX to the final chlorophyll also requires a complex series of steps. Although the porphyrin synthesis pathway was facilitated, other subsequent steps also affected the final synthesis of chlorophyll, resulting in a decrease in the total chlorophyll content in Y.

DEPs in secondary metabolic pathways involved in the alteration of leaf color

In most plants, green leaves are characterized by high levels of chlorophyll, while colored leaves are determined primarily by the contents of anthocyanins and carotenoids [28]. For instance, anthocyanins and flavonoids are present at high levels in purple leaf cultivars of Camellia sinensis [29], and dark-purple coloration of leaves in spring is caused by a high amount of anthocyanins in Acer palmatum [30]. In Y, although the contents of carotenoids and anthocyanins were lower than those in S, their relative proportion of total pigments was greater, which was closely related to the up-regulated proteins involved in secondary metabolic pathways.

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) is a key enzyme for both phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways; it catalyzes phenylalanine to form cinnamic acid [31] and is closely related to the accumulation of anthocyanins, as reported in Pennisetum setaceum [32] and C. sinensis [33]. Chalcone isomerase (CHI) catalyzes the intramolecular cyclization of chalcone to colorless naringenin [34, 35] and is positively correlated with flavonoid accumulation [36, 37]. The up-regulated DEPs that participated in the carotenoid biosynthesis pathway were zeta-carotene desaturase (ZDS), cytochrome P450 superfamily protein (LUT5, LUT1) and zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP), and their up-regulated expression led to the accumulation of carotenoids, resulting in a yellow leaf color (Fig. 6C) [4, 38]. Taken together, proteins involved in the anthocyanin and carotenoid pathways may be responsible for the formation of yellow-white leaves.

Conclusions

The variation in plant leaf color in nature is very high, and colored leaf plants have high ornamental value. In recent years, an increasing number of cultivars of colored leaf osmanthus have been selected and bred, and O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ is one of the earliest cultivars of colored leaf osmanthus. In this study, we used SWATH-MS based quantitative proteomics and physiological characterization to identify the major molecular mechanisms related to leaf color variation in O. fragrans. A total of 3,959 proteins were identified, including 782 up-regulated and 518 down-regulated proteins in Y compared to S. Functional annotation of DEPs revealed that down-regulated proteins were mainly concentrated in photosynthesis and pigment binding processes, and PetC, PsbO, PsbP, and PsbQ were key proteins in the interaction network. These photosynthesis-related proteins may lead to the inhibition of chlorophyll synthesis and a severe decrease in chlorophyll content in Y leaves, which was consistent with the pigment measurement results. In addition, the up-regulated proteins were mainly involved in various metabolic processes, such as the porphyrin, anthocyanin and carotenoid pathways, which may be responsible for the formation of yellow-white leaves. However, the reason why yellow and white coloration only appear at the edge of leaves is still unknown, and further research is needed. Taken together, our findings provide a valuable basis for understanding the molecular and genetic mechanisms underlying leaf change in O. fragrans Color group cultivars.

Methods

Sample collection and pigment measurements

In this study, we used O. fragrans ‘Sijigui’ (S) and the Color group cultivar O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ (Y) as the experimental materials. Mature leaves were collected from Nanjing Forestry University. Leaf samples were collected, and some were utilized for pigment content measurements and some were frozen with liquid nitrogen for proteome extraction and quantitative validation experiments.

To measure the chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, 0.1 g of leaves were extracted with 5 ml of 95% ethanol overnight and quantified spectrophotometrically (Shanghai Matash Instruments Co., Ltd., China) at 470, 649 and 665 nm. The total chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents were calculated as described by Wellburn [39]. Similarly, anthocyanin was extracted from 0.1 g of fresh leaves with acidic methanol (0.1% hydrochloric acid) and then measured at spectrophotometrically 530 and 657 nm. The anthocyanin content was quantified according to Kanemaki [40]. Three biological replicates were performed for each sample measurement.

Protein and peptide preparation for SWATH‑MS

Leaf samples from the two cultivars were fully ground in liquid nitrogen, and 2.5% SDS/100 mM Tris-HCl lysis buffer was added for ultrasonic treatment. After 20 min of centrifugation at 16,000 × g, acetone was added to the supernatants to clean and precipitate the proteins, and the proteins were subsequently dissolved in Tris-HCl solutions and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to the supernatants to a final concentration of 10 mM and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After the concentration reached 40 mM, iodoacetamide (IAM) was added, and alkylation was carried out at room temperature in dark to seal the sulfhydryl group. The protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method after the addition of 100 mM Tris-HCl solution. After the samples were oscillated overnight at 37 °C, trypsin was added, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min and desalted with C18 columns. The desalted peptide solutions were prepared for sequential windowed acquisition of all theoretical mass spectra (SWATH-MS) detection.

SWATH‑MS detection and proteomic data analysis

A Triple TOF 5600 (Sciex, USA) LC/MS system was used to analyze the peptide samples. After SWATH scanning, the mass spectral data were processed by DIA-Umpire to obtain the secondary mass spectrum files for database search. TPP software was used for database retrieval, the retrieved results were used as a spectral library, and the OpenSWATH algorithm was used for SWATH targeting extraction with a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 1% set as the selection criterion [41]. The protein quantitative intensity information was subjected to log2 transformation, data filling, and data normalization using the imputation algorithm in Perseus software for difference comparison and t-test analysis. Proteins with log2(fold change) values above 1 or below − 1 (P < 0.05) were considered differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in this study.

The corrplot R package and pheatmap R package were used to evaluate the correlation of protein quantification and to construct the heatmap of DEPs, respectively. A volcano plot was also generated to display the DEPs between the two cultivars. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEPs were performed with TBtools [42]. The DEPs were subjected to BLAST searches for homologous proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana, and the MapMAN BIN system was used for functional classification (http://ppdb.tc.cornell.edu/dbsearch/searchacc.aspx). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of DEPs was carried out using String (https://string-db.org/), the clustering function modules were screened by the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plugin and visualized by Cytoscape v3.10.0 [43, 44], and key proteins in the interaction network module were identified with cytoHubba [45].

Quantitative real‑time PCR analysis

Total RNA from O. fragrans ‘Yinbi Shuanghui’ and O. fragrans ‘Sijigui’ was extracted with an RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, 2 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed with Evo M-MLV RT Premix for qPCR (AG11706, ACCURATE Biotechnology (HUNAN) Co., Ltd., ChangSha, China) for cDNA synthesis. A SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701, ACCURATE Biotechnology (HUNAN) Co., Ltd., ChangSha, China) was used for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments with OfACT as the reference. Quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using an ABI StepOnePlus System (Applied Biosystems, USA), and the reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S4.
