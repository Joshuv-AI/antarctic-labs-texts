How and by whom are multiple language-related issues managed in transnational qualitative research using content analysis? A scoping review
Authors: Stefania Chiappinotto, Alvisa Palese, Jessica Longhini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultilingual, transnational qualitative research is increasingly common across the health and social sciences. However, it presents significant methodological challenges related to translation, validity and data interpretation, which remain insufficiently explored in the literature. Although some recommendations exist regarding research protocol translation, the role of interpreters and the timing of translation, no comprehensive approaches have been established to guide consistent language management across all phases of the research.ObjectiveTo map the strategies researchers use to manage languages during data collection preparation, data collection, and content analysis in transnational qualitative studies.Information sourcesMEDLINE-PubMed, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, PsycInfo, and Scopus were searched up to October 2024.MethodsA scoping review of qualitative studies using content analysis, conducted in various countries and languages. Data were extracted and summarised according to how languages were managed across three phases of the research process: data collection preparation, conduction, and content analysis. Using both detailed and overarching approaches, key patterns in researchers’ practices were synthesised narratively.ResultsBetween 2001 and 2024, 123 transnational qualitative studies were published. Only 35 described language management during preparation for data collection, revealing two patterns: developing the interview guide in a single language (typically English) and then translating it, or developing it simultaneously in all study languages. During data collection, local languages were predominantly used, while 33 studies reported who conducted the interviews, involving either general research personnel or individuals with explicit language expertise. Language management during content analysis was described in nearly half of the studies. Overall, only 11 studies reported practices across all three phases, most commonly following a process of instrument development in English, data collection and transcription in local languages, and translation into English for analysis.ConclusionsTransnational qualitative research using content analysis is increasing, yet language management issues remain insufficiently reported. An analysis of the methods described in available studies identified recurring patterns across the three main phases of the research process. These patterns may represent a first step towards a critical discussion on developing methodological guidelines, reporting standards, and best practices to promote harmonised methodological guidance and strengthen the validity and reliability of transnational qualitative research.RegistrationNot registered.

Background

In an increasingly interconnected world, multilingual and multicultural qualitative research is essential for capturing the complex experiences and needs of diverse populations. Researchers across disciplines, particularly in the health and social sciences, including nursing, are increasingly conducting transnational qualitative studies to provide widely transferable results (Arriaza et al., 2015; de Jesus-Espinosa et al., 2024). However, incorporating multiple languages into qualitative research across countries with diverse languages presents substantial methodological challenges, especially in ensuring the validity, reliability, and cultural fidelity of collected data and their analysis (Esposito, 2001; Halcomb et al., 2007; Premji et al., 2020). Nevertheless, despite the importance of how language and communication shape the research process, related issues remain underexplored (Hennink, 2008), and no guidelines are available to assist researchers in designing, conducting, and reporting language management in transnational qualitative research. In this growing field, mapping strategies documented in available studies may highlight existing practices and inform the development of methodological guidelines, standards, and best practices.

Managing multiple languages in translational qualitative studies

The methodological complexities of translation in qualitative research have been acknowledged for decades. In 1997, Twinn highlighted how translation may affect data interpretation by introducing language-based variations that can undermine the validity of qualitative studies (Twinn, 1997). As international and global qualitative research has expanded in recent years to involve patients (e.g., Rosendahl and Larsson, 2025), their families (e.g., Wallis et al., 2026), and healthcare professionals (e.g., Gerchow et al., 2021), language has become a key challenge compared with single-site or domestic qualitative studies.

The use of multiple languages reflecting the countries involved, alongside the shared language chosen by the international research team, presents several challenges: in developing data collection tools; in ensuring accurate understanding during data collection, particularly when researchers conducting interviews are not native speakers of the participants’ language (Al-Amer et al., 2015; Wallin and Ahlström, 2006); and in the analysis and interpretation of the data. Despite its relevance, no conceptual and procedural definition of “language management” has been established to date in transnational qualitative research. Some authors have addressed specific issues (e.g., Dolan et al., 2023; Specht et al., 2024; Squires, 2009; Squires, Sadarangani and Jones, 2020), focusing on “language equivalence”, which refers to the point at which “the translator provides a technically and conceptually accurate translated communication of a concept spoken by the study participant” (Jandt, 2003). As a result, interpreters have been shown to play an essential role, translating not only linguistic content but also the cultural meanings embedded within it.

Squires (2008) and Larkin and colleagues (2007) have stated that language is inseparable from culture, and that translation is always a negotiation of meaning across cultural boundaries. Therefore, the inclusion of interpreters introduces additional challenges related to the authenticity and accuracy of conveyed meanings, especially with culturally specific expressions that may lack equivalent terms in the target language (Al-Amer et al., 2015; Wallin and Ahlström, 2006; Younas et al., 2022). Several authors have underscored that language translation can result in the loss of nuanced meanings, as some culturally specific concepts cannot be accurately rendered in another language (de Jesús-Espinosa et al., 2024; Larkin, Dierckx de Casterlé & Schotsmans, 2007; Premji et al., 2020; van Nes et al., 2010). De Jesús-Espinosa and colleagues (2024) have emphasised the importance of robust translation protocols, particularly in cross-cultural qualitative research, where preserving the original context is crucial to ensure data validity (de Jesús-Espinosa et al., 2024).

Few structured methods, such as back-translation and expert panels, have been developed to address idiomatic expressions, metaphors, and culturally bound terms, emphasising accuracy in preliminary translation but lacking specific strategies for translating and maintaining interpretive fidelity during coding (Al-Amer et al., 2015; Chen & Boore, 2010). Younas and colleagues (2022) have suggested that presenting both native and translated quotes could enhance transparency and rigour in qualitative research reporting, promote greater cross-cultural understanding, and mitigate some interpretive challenges (Younas et al., 2022). Moreover, regarding the timing of translation within the research process, Santos and colleagues (2015) have noted that deciding whether to translate immediately after data collection or during the analysis phase can significantly influence data interpretation. However, guidance on how to make this choice remains limited (Santos, Black and Sandelowski, 2015).

Overall, a significant research gap remains in approaches that systematically guide data analysis in transnational qualitative studies. This gap is especially relevant to content analysis, one of the most widely used methods (Im et al., 2023). Elo and Kyngäs (2008) define content analysis as a systematic method for analysing written, verbal, or visual communication, aimed at distilling extensive information into thematic categories to identify relevant meanings within a text. With reference to the different stages to follow, in the first so-called “preparation stage”, researchers select an appropriate unit of analysis (such as a word or sentence) and engage deeply with the data to gain a comprehensive understanding. Multiple readings help differentiate between explicit (manifest) and implicit (latent) content, laying the groundwork for coding. In the subsequent “organising stage”, data are coded and categorised; with an inductive approach, codes emerge directly from the data, while a deductive approach uses a predefined framework to guide categorisation. These codes are then grouped into broader thematic categories. In the “reporting stage”, findings are presented with examples or quotations that directly relate to the data, enhancing transparency (Elo and Kyngäs, 2008). From a methodological perspective, applying content analysis in cross-language research introduces interpretive challenges that go beyond linguistic and cultural differences, requiring a structured framework to support the process and preserve linguistic and cultural integrity (Moretti et al., 2011). Although this issue is primarily methodological, it also has a strong conceptual foundation, given the central role of language and communication in health science research. Communication practices in healthcare are not merely instrumental; they can profoundly shape participants’ understanding, meaning-making, and access to information, particularly in contexts involving complex health conditions and emotional vulnerability. As communication occurs through language, it shapes the co-construction of meaning between researchers and participants and influences the rigour and validity of findings, especially in cross-cultural contexts. Effective communication is therefore essential to ensure methodological robustness and trustworthy knowledge production (Chiappinotto et al., 2026; McDonald, Granger and Remedios, 2025). Incorporating this perspective enriches the discussion by situating multilingual research within broader patterns of health communication and transnational knowledge exchange.

In conclusion, although the literature does not provide specific guidelines, the available methodological recommendations emphasise rigorous translation procedures, collaborative and culturally sensitive approaches, the timing of translation, and the role and competence of translators (Chen & Boore, 2010; Larkin, Dierckx de Casterlé & Schotsmans, 2007; Santos et al., 2015; Squires, 2008; Yunus et al., 2022). Most recommendations focus primarily on the accuracy of the initial translation rather than on consistent language management throughout the different stages of data collection preparation, conduction and analysis (Chen & Boore, 2010; Larkin, Dierckx de Casterlé & Schotsmans, 2007; Santos et al., 2015; Squires, 2008). This results in fragmented procedures, which are difficult to adapt to content analysis methods, as they often treat translation as a one-off process instead of a dynamic, iterative component of content analysis that addresses cross-language equivalence.

Aim

To map the strategies researchers use to manage languages during data collection preparation, data collection, and content analysis in transnational qualitative studies. By identifying these strategies and summarising their main patterns, the study sought to clarify documented practices while preserving linguistic validity in translation, understood as the extent to which participants’ intended meanings are accurately represented, and linguistic reliability, referring to the transparency and consistency of the translation process in transnational qualitative studies.

The review addressed the following research questions:(a)What are the main characteristics of published transnational qualitative studies using content analysis?(b)What strategies have been reported for managing language issues in these studies?(c)What overarching process has been documented in studies that describe language management strategies across preparation for data collection, conduct and analysis?

For the purpose of this review, strategies used to manage language issues were defined as all language-related practices adopted by researchers and reported in studies across the three main phases of qualitative research: preparation for data collection, data collection, and data analysis (van Nes et al., 2010) using content analysis.

Methods

Design

We conducted a scoping review, reported here in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1) (Tricco et al., 2018).

Search methods and screening process

MEDLINE-PubMed, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), PsycInfo, and Scopus were searched to identify eligible studies published since October 2024. No limits in terms of time or language were applied, and the search was not restricted to any scientific discipline. The keywords used and combined to structure the search strings are reported in Supplementary Table 2. Two researchers (blinded for review) independently screened the results, first analysing the titles and abstracts, then the full texts. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a senior researcher (blinded for review). Given the intent of the study, and the number of primary studies identified, the reference lists of the included studies were not searched for additional eligible articles. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they (1) had a qualitative design; (2) used content analysis; (3) included interviews, focus groups, or other qualitative data collection methods; (4) were transnational, defined as studies conducted in different countries and different languages; and (5) reported information on language management processes.

Studies were not eligible if they (1) had a quantitative design or were reviews; (2) were editorials, books, or commentaries; (3) used thematic analysis; or (4) were conducted in only one country with different languages (e.g., in the Swiss cantons) or in different countries with the same language (e.g., in Germany and Switzerland). These were excluded because this review focused specifically on transnational qualitative research, in which linguistic diversity is inherently linked to cross-country collaboration and related methodological complexities, such as coordination across research teams and the use of a shared analytical language. In contrast, multilingual studies within a single country often reflect heterogeneous forms of linguistic variation, including officially recognised languages (e.g., Switzerland), regional dialects, or partial language differences (e.g., words or grammar rules; Harkness, Van de Vijver and Mohler, 2003). As that variability makes it difficult to define and consistently operationalise what constitutes ‘linguistic diversity’ within single-country contexts, and given the aim of the study, which mapped language management strategies in transnational studies involving different countries with different languages, these were not eligible.

Search outcome and data abstraction

A grid designed for data extraction was piloted with three studies, and no changes were necessary. Two researchers (blinded for review) independently extracted data and completed the grid with the following information: (1) author(s), title, journal, and year of publication; (2) author affiliations; (3) countries involved; (4) study aim(s); (5) main research topic(s); (6) qualitative design, method, or framework; (7) data collection method(s) (e.g. interviews); (8) the languages used in the development of the data collection methods, in the conduct of data collection, and in data analysis; and (9) the language management strategies adopted. This last point was divided into three phases: (1) how researchers documented the preparation of interviews or focus groups, that is, the process in which all tools (such as interview questions) for data collection from different populations in various languages were developed; (2) how researchers documented the conduct of interviews or focus groups, which includes all activities where researchers interact with study participants and collect data; and (3) how they reported the analysis of interviews or focus groups, referring to the strategies applied for content analysis. For each phase, we extracted data on how language was managed (e.g. development of a guide in one language followed by translation) and who was involved in the process (e.g. native speakers, bilingual researchers, official interpreters), as reported in the included studies. This information was collected in light of the extensive debate on the crucial role of interpreters in ensuring linguistic and cultural validity in translations (Al-Amer et al., 2015; Wallin & Ahlstrom, 2006). For content analysis, the methodological approach used was identified and extracted from the references cited by the authors. Disagreements were resolved through consultation with the senior researcher (blinded for review) until consensus was reached.

Rigor

In line with the aim and the scoping review design (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005), no quality appraisal was conducted for the included studies. To ensure methodological rigour, researchers (see authors) with extensive experience in translational research, and who are therefore critically engaged with language and cultural issues, were involved. Data extraction was based on information reported in the original studies; when information was missing, additional data were not sought from the study authors. This decision was made to preserve the integrity of data as it was originally accessible to readers. All steps of the mapping process were double-checked to ensure accuracy and transparency. During interpretation of the findings, the researchers’ expertise was integrated with the existing literature to support a coherent, theoretically informed analysis and discussion.

Data synthesis

According to the review questions, a narrative synthesis was used (Popay et al., 2006), first describing the main study features, including year of publication, journal, researcher affiliations, countries involved, study aims, design, and data collection methods. In addition, using a free automatic online programme (https://www.wordclouds.com/), a word cloud of the main topics, based on their frequency in the studies, was produced. Specifically, the keywords representing the topic of each paper – listed in the keywords section of the manuscripts – were extracted by two researchers and entered into the programme to create the word cloud.

Data extracted regarding the transnational process used in managing languages were summarised in their main patterns according to three phases: (1) preparation of data collection (e.g., interview questions), (2) its conduction, and (3) content analysis of the data collected. Two methods were used in the data synthesis: (1) a granular detailed approach, describing the richness of the approaches used by researchers applying an analytical perspective and identifying the main patterns in the data inductively; and (2) a comprehensive overarching approach, describing and synthesizing the main patterns that emerged. For studies that provided information for all three phases, an overarching view of the process as conducted in these studies was mapped and summarized.

Results

Main characteristics of included studies

A total of 10,844 records were identified. After removing duplicates, 3871 studies were screened. During title and abstract screening, 3546 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Full texts were then reviewed and, ultimately, 123 articles were included (Fig. 1).

The 123 studies (see Supplementary Table 3 for all references) were published between 2001 and 2024, mostly after 2017, with nearly half published after 2020 (n = 55; 44.5%). These studies appeared in 92 different journals, most frequently in BMC Health Services Research (n = 7; 5.7%), the Journal of Advanced Nursing (n = 5; 4.1%), and BMC Public Health (n = 4; 3.2%). Authors were primarily affiliated with academic institutions (n = 25; 20.3%), non-academic institutions (n = 8; 6.5%), or both (n = 88; 71.5%). Studies focused on different keywords, as shown in Supplementary Figure 1, with health issues and public health topics being the most prevalent. All included studies were in English.

The studies included between two (e.g., Bø et al., 2022) and 52 countries (Brizuela et al., 2019), mainly the United Kingdom (n = 28; 22.8%), followed by Germany (n = 22; 17.9%), Sweden (n = 20; 16.3%), Italy (n = 19; 15.4%), and the Netherlands (n = 17; 13.8%).

Overall, studies used between two (e.g., Kalaitzi et al., 2019) and seven languages (Cardenas et al., 2021), although many articles did not explicitly report the languages used, often referring only to “local languages”.

In most studies, interviews were used as the data collection method (n = 84; 68.3%), followed by focus groups (n = 18; 14.6%) or both (n = 20; 16.3%). Two studies used observations (Granel-Gimenez et al., 2022; Hamel and Vossing, 2017), while one study each used a workshop (Homer et al., 2018), email (Bido et al., 2015), or written comments (Manninen et al., 2020).

Overall, patients were involved in 92 studies (74.8%); health professionals (e.g., nurses, doctors) in 67 (54.5%); families, informal caregivers, and significant others in 41 (33.3%); students and trainees in 19 (15.4%); policy and decision-makers and system-level stakeholders in 28 (22.8%); community and lay populations in 23 (18.7%); and others (e.g., experts, pharmaceutical industry) in 28 (22.8%). Details of the main characteristics of included studies are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Data collection preparation

Of the 123 studies, 88 (71.5%) did not document the process used to prepare the data collection tools. In the remaining 35 (28.5%), two main patterns emerged: studies in which a guide was developed in English or a single language before being translated into local languages (n = 32), and studies in which the guide was developed directly in all languages used, simultaneously (n = 3) (Adolfsson, Johnson and Nilsson, 2018; Egholm et al., 2019; Schulenkorf, Sørensen and Okan, 2022), with some minor context-related changes.

Among the 32 studies where English was the starting point, different processes were used for subsequent translation of the guide, as summarised below:(a)translation into the required local languages (n = 19; e.g., Broda et al., 2017). In one of these studies (Ribic et al., 2022), the guide was developed interactively and then translated based on the meaning of the questions rather than word for word;(b)translation into another language, followed by a pilot for linguistic and semantic assessment (n = 4; Bastiaens et al., 2007; Caravaca-Morera and Padilha, 2017a; Caravaca-Morera and Padilha, 2017b; Cárdenas et al., 2021);(c)consensus on a definitive version in English, followed by translation into local languages (n = 1; Bressan et al., 2021);(d)translation into local languages, followed by back-translation and a pilot test for contextual acceptability (n = 1; Santini, Andersson and Lamura, 2016);(e)validation with a pre-study, followed by translation into local languages (n = 1; Ruoranen et al., 2015); and(f)translation into other local languages, followed by adaptation to the context (n = 1; Ivanova et al., 2016).

In other cases (n = 3; Berg, Kaltiala-Heino and Välimäki, 2011; Friedman et al., 2020; Oliver et al., 2018), the process began with a pilot test without defining the starting language. Afterwards, in one study (Friedman et al., 2020), the guide was translated into local languages and checked for accuracy; in another (Berg, Kaltiala-Heino and Välimäki, 2011), the guide was translated, piloted again, and then adjusted; in the last case (Oliver et al., 2018), the guide was adapted to the context and then translated.

Additionally, some studies addressed translation accuracy (n = 2; Dancet et al., 2012; Harvey et al., 2019). One study (Dancet et al., 2012) used reciprocal translation (Fumimoto et al., 2001), which includes a double back-translation procedure with five main steps: independent forward translation by two native speakers; reconciliation of the forward translations by another native speaker not involved in the forward translation process; back-translation of the reconciled version by a native speaker; review by four bilingual experts; and spelling and grammatical verification of the new forward translation in preparation for pre-testing. The other study (Harvey et al., 2019) used a back-translation process (Peterson, 2009).

Overall, 17 studies (Bastiaens et al., 2007; Berg, Kaltiala-Heino and Välimäki, 2011; Bido et al., 2015; Bressan et al., 2021; Broda et al., 2017; Cárdenas et al., 2021; Friedman et al., 2020; Graham-Rowe et al., 2023; Ivanova et al., 2016; Kaae et al., 2020; Petricek et al., 2019; Ribic et al., 2022; Ruoranen et al., 2015; Silva et al., 2016a; Sommer et al., 2017; Stephan et al., 2018; Swigris et al., 2021) reported who contributed to developing and validating the interview or focus group guides. These contributions generally followed two main patterns: (1) organisational and research roles, including core or country study teams, multidisciplinary teams, country research teams or researchers, and partner institutions; and (2) contributors with language-related expertise, including native speakers, mother-tongue authors, bilingual team members, language experts, and professional translators.

Data collection

Overall, 47 of 123 studies (38.2%) did not describe the data collection process in relation to language. Of the remaining studies, 42 (e.g. Caravaca-Morera and Padilha, 2017a) conducted interviews and focus groups in local languages, while others (n = 27; e.g. Berg, Kaltiala-Heino and Välimäki, 2011) used both English and another local language. In some cases (n = 2; Hämel et al., 2017; Shiroiwa et al., 2022), local languages were used, but not the countries’ primary official language. Some studies used only English for data collection (n = 4; Foulds et al., 2023; Silva et al., 2016a; Silva et al., 2016b; Strøm et al., 2018) or English with some translated terms (n = 1; Apon et al., 2022), even when other languages were used in other parts of the research process.

Overall, 33 studies reported who conducted the data collection (e.g. Putri et al., 2022), and two main patterns emerged: (1) the involvement of general research roles, including authors or principal investigators, teachers or mentors, local moderators and researchers, trained doctoral students, and experienced research assistants; or (2) the involvement of individuals with specified language competence or mediation roles, including bilingual investigators, translators or interpreters, interviewers with documented language proficiency (e.g. at least C1 level according to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages), interviewers fluent in the local language, and other proficient speakers. Some studies also explicitly referred to native speakers, such as native-speaking authors or experienced patient interviewers who were native speakers.

Content analysis of the data

Among the 123 studies, 78 (63.4%) cited a reference specific to the method adopted for content analysis, resulting in 18 unique citations related to frameworks or methods used (all references: Supplementary Table 5). In total, 24 different methods were used, as some of the 18 unique methods above were combined. The most frequently used were those of Elo and Kyngäs (2008) and Hsieh and Shannon (2005) (n = 16; 13% each), followed by Graneheim and Lundman (2004) (n = 11; 8.9%) (Supplementary Figure 5). Forty-five studies did not report a specific method, instead citing a general reference for qualitative research.

Overall, 24 studies did not report information about the content analysis method. The remaining studies (n = 99; e.g. Egerod et al., 2023) mainly reported transcribing interviews or focus groups in local languages, followed by translation into English as a shared language across the included countries. In some cases, additional steps were taken, such as cultural interpretation (n = 1; Doukani et al., 2021), independent reverse translation (n = 1; Oliver et al., 2018), or spot-checking (n = 1; Podgorica et al., 2022).

In nine studies (e.g. Dancet et al., 2012), interviews were transcribed in local languages and analysed using an English coding tree. One study (Fagerdahl et al., 2020) described the process as transcription in the local language, followed by translation into English, resulting in bilingual coding. In three studies (Becker et al., 2020; Egerod et al., 2023; Naef et al., 2021), interviews were transcribed in local languages, with only the most important and meaningful quotes translated. Another reported process (Durante et al., 2022) involved transcription in local languages with coding in a grid using English themes.

In the remaining articles, only partial information on the process was provided, such as transcription in local languages (n = 7; e.g. Van Wezemael et al., 2010), analysis in local languages (n = 6; e.g. Ruoranen et al., 2015), written transcription in English (n = 1; Van Stappen et al., 2018), production of an English report (n = 1; Tomasik et al., 2011), or translation of audiotapes into English (n = 1; Gusdal et al., 2009).

Different individuals were involved in data analysis, as reported in 64 studies (e.g., Gunnarsdottir et al., 2017). Two main patterns emerged: (1) the involvement of general researchers, including members of research, study, or country teams, authors, local researchers, moderators, and research assistants; or (2) the involvement of individuals with explicit language expertise. In the latter case, reporting was generally more detailed and structured, including bilingual investigators or authors, native speakers, staff fluent in local languages, and individuals with specified language proficiency (e.g. at least B2 level according to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages). Several studies also described institutionalised language-management structures, such as professional translators employed by international organisations (e.g., WHO-employed translators), approved or qualified translators, external translation or professional service providers, and additional coordination or support roles (e.g. programme officers or specialised teams).

Methodologically, three references were cited in the methods sections of the included studies to support language management during data analysis, in addition to the content analysis methodology itself. Squires (2009) was cited to inform practices related to conceptual equivalence, translator qualifications and roles, and translation methods. Al-Amer et al. (2015) was cited to emphasise the importance of involving qualified bilingual and bicultural researchers with expertise in qualitative research who share participants’ language, culture, and, where relevant, religious background. The authors also recommended prioritising semantic and content equivalence over literal translation, conducting debriefing sessions between translators and researchers to enhance data accuracy and credibility, documenting translators’ decisions, addressing slang, jargon, and regional linguistic variations, and maintaining a record of atypical terms not captured in dictionaries. Finally, Santos et al. (2015) was cited to support the use of translation during the early stages of the research process to facilitate collaborative data analysis between researchers and translators, overcome limitations associated with researchers’ lack of proficiency in the source language, and maintain an audit trail of translation decisions to enhance the transparency and trustworthiness of data interpretation.

Language management across data collection preparation, conduction and analysis: overarching patterns

Although only a limited number of studies reported data for all three phases (n = 11), their analysis is of interest because it provides a comprehensive overview of patterns across the entire process (Table 2). The first, and most common, pattern involves the initial development of research instruments in English or another language; translation into local languages; data collection (via interviews or focus groups) in participants’ native or preferred language; transcription in those languages; and subsequent translation into English for analysis (n = 8: Bido et al., 2015; Cabrera et al., 2021; Dancet et al., 2012; Janzik et al., 2024; Kaae et al., 2020; Karki et al., 2024; Petricek et al., 2019; Shiroiwa et al., 2022). In Dancet and colleagues the analysis was conducted deductively, based on an English coding tree (Dancet et al., 2012). In two other studies, the process was the same, except that the analysis was conducted in the local language (Ruoranen et al., 2015; Van Wezemael et al., 2010). The second pattern, reported in one study (Berg, Kaltiala-Heino and Välimäki, 2011), reverses the sequence by developing the instrument in local languages, followed by translation into English. Discussion

Main characteristics of the included studies

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to map how authors of transnational qualitative studies using content analysis have practically managed linguistic issues across the three research phases – preparation, data collection, and analysis – and to collate the strategies they applied to ensure rigour in their research. The findings indicate that qualitative studies employing content analysis, conducted in more than two countries with different languages, have increased over the past ten years and have been published in a wide range of journals, suggesting that researchers from diverse disciplines are interested in transnational qualitative research. Author affiliations also suggest that both academic and non-academic researchers are involved in transnational studies, highlighting broad engagement with these approaches.

Regarding the countries involved, some (e.g., Brazil, India, South Africa, Ethiopia, Uganda) appear less frequently, whereas most studies focus on Europe, particularly Western and Northern Europe, with dominant contributions from the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, Italy, the Netherlands, and Spain. The presence of European-level grants funding transnational research, as well as shared geopolitical contexts (e.g., Europe), may have influenced the research trends observed to date (Scherngell et al., 2024).

Regarding the languages used, many authors do not clearly state which languages are involved, assuming that readers know the language of the country in which the study was conducted, or simply referring to “local languages” without providing further details.

Data collection methodologies generally follow classic approaches, such as individual interviews or focus groups. Transnational data management is considerably more complex in face-to-face data collection than in simpler methods (e.g., email or written reports), underscoring the inherent complexity of this research (Cuoco et al., 2022). This complexity is also reflected in the diversity of the populations involved. While investigations including healthcare professionals (who account for approximately half of the studies included) may benefit from the use of technical or shared language, studies involving patients, family members, and caregivers face additional challenges. Differences in language proficiency, the use of dialects, and varying levels of education and literacy require adaptation processes that can further increase the complexity of managing linguistic issues across the different phases of the research process.

Data collection preparation

Language-related issues should be considered from the outset when developing data collection tools (Im et al., 2016). In practice, however, most studies neglect these aspects; in the majority of cases, data were not reported, and only 28.5% indicated the strategies used for language management, with just three developing guides in all study languages and only two explicitly reporting the process for managing translations (Fumimoto et al., 2001; Peterson, 2009). Data collection guides are an integral part of methodological development and should therefore be reported in all studies. Nevertheless, the two patterns identified indicate that the choice of a dominant language within the transnational research team forms the foundation on which other translations are subsequently built, while direct translation into all languages is used less frequently.

In most cases, a one-way translation process is used, and only in limited instances are reciprocal translation or back-translation employed. Although this one-way process saves researchers time, it does not guarantee language equivalence, which requires a clear and transparent methodological structure. Moreover, 10 studies (e.g., Berg, Kaltiala-Heino and Välimäki, 2011) reported some form of adaptation, piloting, consensus-building or adjustment, suggesting some attention to semantic and cultural adequacy. In a transnational context, such aspects are recognised as important for adapting the interview or focus group directly to participants’ cultural context, thereby ensuring richer and more meaningful data; however, their explicit reporting is often lacking, which reduces the overall quality of the studies, in line with the most recent guidelines (Sinha et al., 2024) for reporting qualitative studies, where insufficient attention is devoted to language issues. Documenting the pilot phase is also relevant in this context: piloting the interview in the language common to all researchers is important to establish the guide before data collection, but there is a risk of failing to identify language-specific or culturally relevant issues that could be detected by piloting the translated versions locally.

Researchers appear to exercise considerable flexibility, involving external experts in some studies (e.g., Friedman et al., 2020); however, the largely informal or undocumented approach may introduce significant inconsistencies, potentially affecting accuracy and validity. In contrast, in quantitative data collection, language-related issues are more systematically addressed, particularly when standardised questionnaires are used (e.g., Karimali et al., 2026; Zucco et al., 2025), for which linguistic and cultural validation is typically required (Beaton et al., 2000; Sousa and Rojjanasrirat, 2011).

Data collection

Regarding data collection, information was missing in approximately 38.2% of studies, suggesting that researchers are more likely to describe this process than the development of data collection tools. Most studies reported that data collection was conducted in local languages but rarely provided details about who conducted the interviews, the interviewers’ linguistic proficiency, or their cultural competence. Although the use of local languages can enhance data quality by facilitating participant engagement and inclusion in the research process, it is unclear whether this practice adequately safeguards data quality overall, particularly as this aspect is often poorly documented (Santin and Jenkins, 2023). The local language appears to serve as the dominant language of the research encounter, rather than a lingua franca such as English or the language of the research team. This may introduce additional challenges for data quality, including issues related to translation accuracy, interviewer interpretation, and consistency across data collection processes.

Content analysis of the data

In the analysis phase, more detailed descriptions were provided than in previous phases. However, regarding the methods used, nearly half of the included studies did not explicitly state the methodological framework underpinning their content analysis. Among the 18 unique methodological references identified, there appears to be a temporal trend in their adoption. Since around 2010, the most frequently cited references have been Elo and Kyngäs (2008), Graneheim and Lundman (2004), and Hsieh and Shannon (2005), reflecting alignment with monolingual studies and suggesting no clear differences in method choice between single-country and cross-national research. This indicates a progressive consolidation of methodological standards in content analysis, although consistency in their application remains variable.

A large proportion of studies transcribed interviews in local languages and then translated them into English for coding and interpretation. Various strategies, such as back translation, spot checking, or bilingual coding, were used in a limited number of studies. Furthermore, only three studies explicitly cited references (Al-Amer et al., 2015; Santos et al., 2015; Squires, 2008) to guide translation at the analysis stage, highlighting a lack of systematic integration of available recommendations. Regarding timing, authors who translated all interviews or focus groups before analysis favoured a unified analysis but risked losing the richness of cultural and linguistic meaning if accurate translation was not ensured. Conversely, those who chose to analyse in the local language and then translate preserved greater richness in linguistic meaning, but risked producing a highly fragmented and context-dependent analysis.

Whereas in previous phases the characteristics of individuals conducting the process were described only in general terms, in this phase the authors provided a more detailed explanation of the people involved, including their level of language knowledge and relative proficiency. This underscores the importance the authors place on language management in content analysis.

Language management across data collection preparation, conduction and analysis phases: overarching patterns

Only a limited number of articles described how language was managed across all three phases: data preparation, conduct, and analysis suggesting an overall fragmented approach in language management issues. In the studies that covered all three phases, the main trend was to develop a guide in English or another language, translate it into the local language, conduct the interview in the local language, transcribe it in the local language, and then translate the transcripts into English for analysis. The translation process was generally documented at some stages, but with varying levels of detail. Santos and colleagues (2015) noted that translation can be introduced at different stages – before data collection, during data preparation, analysis, or dissemination – each with distinct implications that should be acknowledged. Several gaps also emerged, as raised by Squires (2008), who emphasised that failing to explicitly address language barriers or translation processes undermines the credibility, transferability, and dependability of cross-language qualitative research. There was also a lack of transparency regarding who conducted each phase. Squires (2008) highlighted the important distinction between translators and interpreters, noting that both roles extend beyond literal translation to include cultural mediation, and that their qualifications directly affect conceptual equivalence.

Implications for research

Implications for research are discussed here in relation to two main directions: (1) what our review identified as not documented in current practices of translational qualitative studies and (2) what these studies documented overall, interpreted here as possible draft recommendations in the field.

(1) Poor documentation of the language management process at various stages of research and at the overarching level has emerged, suggesting issues in how linguistic validity and reliability are addressed in available transnational studies using content analysis. Insufficient description of how languages are used, translated, or negotiated during data preparation, collection, and analysis does not in itself reflect poor methodology, but it limits transparency for readers. When language decisions are inadequately reported, it may be difficult to assess the validity and reliability of findings, as potential shifts in meaning, translation choices, and researcher positions remain largely invisible. This lack of clarity can obscure how participants’ voices are represented in the findings and how interpretations may have been shaped by multilingual practices.

In addition, inadequate documentation of language management has broader implications for the contribution and cumulative value of qualitative research. Poor transparency may affect, for example, attempts to summarize findings in reviews that include qualitative studies and limit opportunities for methodological learning across different research approaches. Moreover, it may compromise the validity and reliability of a study’s findings, given that these depend on how linguistic validity and reliability are themselves managed, leading to conceptual discrepancies and loss of meaning. Future transnational studies should adopt transparent, standardized language management protocols and report them comprehensively, thereby enabling greater methodological rigor in preserving linguistic validity and reliability throughout the entire research process.

(2) Drawing on the researcher practices documented across studies to date, combined with available literature in the field (Chen & Boore, 2010; Larkin, Dierckx de Casterlé & Schotsmans, 2007; Santos et al., 2015; Squires, 2008), a set of recommendations can be drafted (Fig. 2) for harmonisation and guidelines to enhance rigour in transnational qualitative studies.Fig. 2A proposed set of recommendations for language management in transnational multilingual qualitative studies using content analysis: preparation for data collection, data collection conduct, and content analysis.

A primary consideration is the preparation of the data collection process. A shared working language within the research team should be clearly defined. Guides are often developed in English for efficiency within the research team and then translated into local languages, but this process requires careful attention. The English proficiency and cultural knowledge of bilingual translators should be clearly documented. Whenever possible, data collection guides should be collaboratively developed in all study languages rather than drafted in one language and later translated. When translation is required, it should follow rigorous and transparent procedures, including pilot testing, back-translation, and review by bilingual expert panels, as recommended by Chen and Boore (2010), to ensure both semantic and cultural equivalence. Pilot testing is essential not only to refine wording but also to verify clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness within each setting, thus establishing the consistency of the interview guide.

During data collection, particular attention should be given to conducting interviews or focus groups in participants’ preferred language, ensuring both communicative effectiveness and ethical inclusivity. All language-related procedures, such as the use of interpreters, language choices, and any challenges encountered, should be systematically documented.

In the analytical phase, researchers must make explicit decisions regarding the timing of translation (e.g., before, during, or after coding (Santos et al., 2015)) and the language(s) used for coding and analysis. These decisions should be justified in relation to the study’s epistemological approach and analytical goals. Where appropriate, bilingual or multilingual coding strategies can be adopted to preserve nuance and enhance interpretive depth.

Across all phases, particularly in data collection and translation, translators should be regarded as active members of the research team, contributing to conceptual clarity and cultural sensitivity (Larkin et al., 2007). In line with this view, translators and interpreters should be appropriately qualified, with sociolinguistic competence and, ideally, professional certification. Their active role in co-constructing meaning should be explicitly acknowledged, managed and reported, as emphasised by Squires (2008). Data collection and the translation of both interview guides and verbatim transcripts should not be treated as purely technical tasks but as collaborative, interpretive processes. In addition, cultural reflexivity should be integrated into every stage of the research. Culture is not a background variable but a constitutive element that shapes study design, data collection and interpretation. Researchers should document how cultural factors influence their methodological choices, using diaries and memos, and extend these tools to translators when they are not part of the research team. Maintaining detailed audit trails of translation decisions, including how idioms, colloquialisms and culturally embedded terms were handled, can further strengthen transparency. As Santos et al. (2015) suggested, such practices enhance interpretive clarity and allow readers to assess the robustness of the analytical process.

Limitations

This methodological study has several limitations. First, our synthesis was intentionally restricted to information explicitly reported in the included studies to highlight the strategies used for language management. While this decision preserves transparency and reproducibility, it likely undercaptures strategies that were implemented but not described. A lack of reporting in a study does not necessarily mean these procedures were not conducted by the researchers. This distinction should be considered when interpreting the results and could be further explored by contacting the authors in future work. Second, by scoping review design, we focused on content analysis, although thematic analysis is another widely used approach in transnational qualitative research (Vaismoradi, Turunen and Bondas, 2013); excluding it may have led us to miss additional language management strategies specific to theme development and interpretive cross-language work. In this sense, the present review may be considered a first step: a focused mapping within content analysis that can be progressively complemented with evidence from studies using thematic analysis and other approaches to build a more comprehensive mapping of the approaches used. Third, the review protocol was not formally registered. Although protocol registration would have enhanced transparency, reduced the risk of selective reporting, and strengthened the methodological rigor of the review, all review procedures were defined a priori and consistently applied throughout the study. Fourth, no formal methodological quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted, consistent with the purpose and design of a mapping review. Finally, the literature search was conducted up to October 2024. The large body of eligible studies identified required extensive analysis and synthesis. Consequently, studies published after this date may report additional or different language management strategies. Future reviews are encouraged to update this mapping as approaches evolve, thereby informing developments in methodological advances in the field.

Conclusions

Transnational qualitative research using content analysis is increasing, particularly across Europe, with 123 studies published between 2001 and 2024. Despite this growth, which could inform practice and support the development of guidelines, studies remain methodologically heterogeneous in their reporting of language management issues, especially during the preparation and conduct of data collection, where decisions are less standardized and more inconsistently reported. In contrast, during the content analysis of data, more description and clarity have emerged, although inconsistently across studies, likely because guidelines are lacking to adequately support researchers in this area.

The lack of transparency in language management may lead to misinterpretation of participants’ lived experiences, limit the ability to fully represent meaning across contexts, and weaken transnational research investigations. Furthermore, variability in who is responsible for translation and the frequent absence of information about their language proficiency raise concerns. The timing of translation also emerges as a critical but underreported issue, with important implications for both the depth of analysis and the preservation of meaning. These findings highlight the urgent need for harmonized and robust methodological guidance capable of addressing linguistic validity and reliability across all phases of transnational qualitative research.
