Vishwa Prakash HV Ruta Mehta Chennai Mathematical Institute University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign vishwa@cmi.ac.in rutameht@illinois.edu Prajakta Nimbhorkar Chennai Mathematical Institute prajakta@cmi.ac.in

Abstract
We study the problem of fair allocation of a set of indivisible goods among n n agents with k k distinct additive valuations, with the goal of achieving approximate envy-freeness up to any good ( α ​ - ​ EFX \alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX} ). It is known that EFX allocations exist for n n agents when there are at most three distinct valuations [ 16 ] . [ 5 ] showed that a 2 3 ​ - ​ EFX \frac{2}{3}\text{-}\mathrm{EFX} allocation is guaranteed to exist when number of agents is at most seven. In this paper, we show that a 2 3 ​ - ​ EFX \frac{2}{3}\text{-}\mathrm{EFX} allocation exists for any number of agents when there are at most four distinct valuations. Secondly, we consider a relaxation called EFX \mathrm{EFX} with charity , where some goods remain unallocated such that no agent envies the set of unallocated goods. [ 1 ] showed that for n n agents and any ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 2 ] \varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right] , there exists a ( 1 − ε ) ​ - ​ EFX (1-\varepsilon)\text{-}\mathrm{EFX} allocation with at most 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ( n / ε ) 1 / 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}((n/\varepsilon)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}) goods to charity. In this paper, we show that a ( 1 − ε ) ​ - ​ EFX (1-\varepsilon)\text{-}\mathrm{EFX} allocation with a 𝒪 ~ ​ ( k / ε ) 1 / 2 \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(k/\varepsilon)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}} charity exists for any number of agents when there are at most k k distinct valuations.

Introduction

The problem of discrete fair division arises whenever a collection of
indivisible
goods must be assigned to a group of agents, each with their own preferences over these goods. These agents may be people, autonomous software programs, or embodied agents such as robots. The goods in question are not divisible–there is no meaningful way to split a university seat or a domain name in half. In some settings, we are dividing public housing, school seats, or government resources among human participants. In others, we are allocating compute tokens, digital licenses, or scheduled access to systems among artificial agents. The underlying challenge in each of these settings is the same—how can we allocate scarce, indivisible goods in a fair manner?

The quintessential notion of fairness is that of envy-freeness
[
12
,
21
]
, which requires that each agent prefers the bundle assigned to them over that of any other agent. While compelling, this notion is often too strong in settings with indivisible goods, where such allocations may fail to exist even in simple cases. This has led to the study of relaxed fairness guarantees
[
17
,
8
,
20
,
2
]
that retain the spirit of envy-freeness while accommodating the combinatorial nature of discrete settings. Among these, envy-freeness up to any good (
\mathrm{EFX}
) and its approximation,
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
, have emerged as two of the central solution concepts.

An allocation is said to be
envy-free up to any good
(
\mathrm{EFX}
) if each agent prefers the bundle assigned to them over any strict subset of the bundle assigned to any other agent. Does every instance of the discrete fair division problem admit an
\mathrm{EFX}
allocation? This seemingly simple question has turned out to be surprisingly difficult to answer. The answer to this question is known under a range of additional assumptions—such as restrictions on the number of agents, the structure of their valuation functions, or the number of unique valuation functions present in the instance. For example,
\mathrm{EFX}
allocations are known to exist when there are at most three agents
[
11
]
with additive valuations

This result is known when two agents have arbitrary monotone valuations and one agent has
MMS-feasible
valuation
[

. More recently, it has been shown that
\mathrm{EFX}
exists for three

[

. That is, for any number of agents provided that the number of distinct valuation functions is at most three. For general valuations,
\mathrm{EFX}

[

and more generally for two types of agents
[

In light of the challenges surrounding exact
\mathrm{EFX}
allocations, the literature has developed along several distinct directions—each relaxing the problem in a different way. One such direction considered a multiplicative approximation of
\mathrm{EFX}
, known as
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
, which requires that each agent prefers their bundle over any strict subset of the bundle assigned to any other agent up to a factor of
\alpha

\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Plaut and Roughgarden 2020
showed the existence of
0.5\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
for any number of agents under general monotonic valuations. Under additive valuations,
Amanatidis et al. 2020
, showed that a
0.618\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
exists for any number of agents. More recently,
Amanatidis et al. 2024
showed that a
\frac{2}{3}\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}

Another line of work

considers partial
\mathrm{EFX}
allocations, under an additional constraint that no agent envies the set of unallocated goods—often referred to as the

Berger et al. 2022

A third direction combines these two relaxations, seeking partial allocations that are approximately

Instances with Few Types of Agents.

In many practical scenarios, it is reasonable to assume that agents can be grouped into a small number of types based on various shared characteristics or needs. For example, Consider the allocation of disaster relief resources. Here the beneficiaries can often be classified by demographic groups such as men and women, or by age brackets such as adults, children, and the elderly. In public health interventions, patients are grouped based on risk profiles. Similarly, in public housing assignments, applicants are frequently categorized into types based on household composition—such as single adults, families with children, or senior citizens—with each group having distinct preferences. These kinds of structured populations give rise to instances with a limited number of valuation types, although the total number of agents is large.

2.1
Fairness and Efficiency Notions:

The fairness criteria relevant to our setting are introduced below. We start with the standard
envy-freeness
notion, and then present some of the relaxations of it, namely
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
and
\mathrm{EFX}
.

Definition 1

(Envy, Envy-Freeness,
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
)
.

Given an allocation
X
, a constant
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and two agents
a,b\in\mathcal{N}
, we say:

•

Agent
a
does not
\alpha
-envy agent
b
or, equivalently, agent
a
is
\alpha
-envy-free towards agent
b
if
v_{a}(X_{a})\geq\alpha\cdot v_{a}(X_{b})
.

•

Agent
a
does not
\alpha
-envy agent
b

up to any good
or, equivalently, agent
a
is
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
towards agent
b
if
v_{a}(X_{a})\geq\alpha\cdot v_{a}(X_{b}\setminus h)
for all
h\in X_{b}
.

An allocation
X
is
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
if every agent is
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
towards every other agent. When
\alpha=1
, we simply say that the allocation is
\mathrm{EFX}
.

Another relaxation of envy-freeness is
\mathrm{EFX}
with charity, which allows some goods to remain unallocated, as long as no agent envies the set of unallocated goods. More formally, it is defined as follows:

Definition 2

(
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
with Charity,
[
10
]
)
.

A partial allocation
X
is said to be
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
with charity
\mathcal{P}(X)
, if
X
is an
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
allocation in which no agent envies the set
\mathcal{P}(X)
. That is, for every agent
a\in\mathcal{N}
,
v_{a}(X_{a})\geq v_{a}(\mathcal{P}(X))
.

In the context of EFX allocations,
Chaudhury et al. 2024
showed that one can assume without loss of generality that the input instances are
non-degenerate
, meaning that no agent values two different bundles equally. This assumption simplifies the analysis and is often used in the literature. Throughout this paper, we too assume the instances to be non-degenerate. We recall this definition below.

Definition 3

(Non-degenerate Instance,
[
11
,
1
]
)
.

An instance
\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{V}\rangle
of fair allocation is called non-degenerate if no agent values two different bundles equally. That is, for any agent
a\in\mathcal{N}
and any two bundles
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, if
v_{a}(S)=v_{a}(T)
, then
S=T
.

They show that for any positive integer
n
, if every non-degenerate instance of fair division on
n
agents admits EFX allocations, then every the instances, including degenerate ones, admit EFX allocations. We note that this result also holds for
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
allocations. For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof of this result in Appendix
A.1
.

We next define the
Pareto optimality
criterion, which is a standard efficiency notion, as well as a measure of progress of iterative procedures in the fair allocation literature.

Definition 4

(Pareto Domination and Optimality)
.

Given two allocations
X
and
Y
, we say that
X

Pareto dominates

Y
, denoted by
X\succ Y
, if for every agent
a\in\mathcal{N}
,
v_{a}(X_{a})\geq v_{a}(Y_{a})
and there exists at least one agent
b\in\mathcal{N}
such that
v_{b}(X_{b})>v_{b}(Y_{b})
. An allocation is said to be
Pareto optimal
if it is not Pareto dominated by any other allocation.

Envy Graphs:

An important tool in the study of fair allocation of indivisible goods is the notion of
envy graphs
, which captures the envy relationships among agents in a given allocation. We now define various types of envy graphs used in this paper. In these definitions, an allocation may be complete or partial.

Definition 5

(Envy Graph
G
)
.

Given an allocation
X
, the envy graph
G(X)=(\mathcal{N},E(X))
, is a directed graph on the set of agents. There exists a directed edge
(a,b)
if and only if agent
a
envies agent
b
, that is,
E(X)=\{\,(a,b)\mid v_{a}(X_{a})<v_{a}(X_{b})\}
.

Definition 6

(Reduced Envy Graph
G_{r}

[
5
]
)
.

Given an allocation
X
, the
reduced envy graph on
X
, denoted by
G_{\mathrm{r}}(X)=(\mathcal{N},E_{\mathrm{r}}(X))
, is a sub-graph of the envy graph
G(X)
, obtained by removing each edge
(a,b)
, such that
|X_{a}|>1
,
|X_{b}|=1
, and
a
is
\frac{2}{3}
-envy-free towards
b
.
That is
E_{\mathrm{r}}(X)=E(X)\setminus\{\,(a,b)\mid|X_{a}|>1,|X_{b}|=1,\text{ and }v_{a}(X_{a})\geq\frac{2}{3}v_{a}(X_{b})\}
.

Definition 7

(Enhanced Envy Graph
G_{e}

[
5
]
)
.

Given an allocation
X
, the
enhanced envy graph on
X
,
G_{\mathrm{e}}(X)=(\mathcal{N},E_{\mathrm{e}}(X))
, is a super-graph of the reduced envy graph
G_{\mathrm{r}}(X)
, obtained by adding edges of the form
(a,s)
, where
s
is a source (i.e., vertex of in-degree 0) in
G_{\mathrm{r}}(X)
,
|X_{a}|=1
,
|X_{s}|>1
, and
v_{a}(X_{s})\geq\frac{2}{3}v_{a}(X_{a})
. These edges are referred to as the
red edges
.

Given an allocation, the
Envy-Cycle-Elimination
(ECE) procedure of
Lipton et al. 2004
iteratively finds a directed cycle in the envy graph and cyclically permutes the bundles along the edges of the cycle. This strictly increases the utility of agents in on the cycle and does not decrease anyone’s utility, resulting in a Pareto dominating allocation. The process repeats until the envy graph becomes acyclic. Importantly, if the initial allocation is
\alpha
-EFX, then every intermediate and the final allocation produced by ECE is also
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}

[
20
]
, and the final envy graph is acyclic. Moreover,
Amanatidis et al. 2024
show that the ECE procedure maintains
\frac{2}{3}\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
when run on either the extended or the reduced envy graphs. In Section
C.1
, we identify a small error in the proof of termination of the ECE procedure in
[
5
]
and provide a corrected proof.

Critical Goods:

Markakis and Santorinaios 2023
showed that, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
and any
\alpha\in(0,1)

Definition 8

(Critical Good,
[
19
]
)

Given a partial allocation

Lemma 1

(
[
19
]
)
.

Given a partial
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
allocation

in which no agent has a
\beta

In this work, one of our primary interests is in finding complete

k

Our main focus is on instances with
k

k
-type instances. Such instances are considered in literature, e.g. in

16

[
16
]
. An instance

of fair allocation is called a

-type instance
if the agents in
\mathcal{N}
can be partitioned into
k

\mathcal{N}^{i}=\{a_{1}^{i},a_{2}^{i},\ldots,a_{p_{i}}^{i}\}

Leading Agents and the Ordering Invariant:

Given an allocation
X
for a
k
-type instance, we denote the bundle assigned to agent
a_{i}^{t}\in\mathcal{N}^{t}
as
X_{i}^{t}
. Furthermore, we assume, without loss of generality, that within each group, the bundles are sorted in non-decreasing order of their valuations. That is, for any two agents
a_{j}^{t},a_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{N}^{t}

j<\ell
, then
v_{t}(X_{j}^{t})\leq v_{t}(X_{\ell}^{t})
. This is referred to as the
ordering invariant
. As a consequence, for any
t\in[k]
, agent
a_{1}^{t}
is the agent with the least valued bundle in
\mathcal{N}^{t}
, and is referred to as the
leading agent
of the group
\mathcal{N}^{t}
.

Given a
k
-type instance, we denote the set of leading agents as
\mathcal{L}=\{a_{1}^{1},a_{1}^{2},\ldots,a_{1}^{k}\}
. The leading agent of group
\mathcal{N}^{t}
is denoted by
a_{1}^{t}
. The following proposition captures some properties of
k
-type instances and partial allocations, which will be useful in our proofs.

Proposition 1

(Properties of
k
-types Instances and Partial Allocations
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{V}\rangle
be a
k
-types instance, and let
X
be a partial allocation in which every agent receives at least one good. Then the following hold:

1.

The envy graph
G(X)
has at most
k
sources, and every source is a leading agent of its group.

2.

For any group
\mathcal{N}^{t}
and any non-leading agent
a_{j}^{t}\in\mathcal{N}^{t}
(
j>1
), if
a_{j}^{t}
envies a bundle
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, then the leading agent
a_{1}^{t}
also envies
S
.

The following corollary is a simple observation that follows from the ordering invariant:

Corollary 1
.

For any group
\mathcal{N}^{t}
and any non-leading agent
a_{j}^{t}\in\mathcal{N}^{t}
(
j>1
), if a good
g\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is

\beta

Our second result deals with the problem of finding a
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
allocation with bounded charity.
Chaudhury et al. 2021a

Definition 9

(Rainbow cycle number

9
]
)
.

Given an integer

is the largest

there exists a
k

with the following properties:

•

each part has at most d vertices, i.e.,

, and

•

every vertex has at least one incoming edge from every part other than the one containing it,

•

there exists no cycle

that visits each part at most once.

We refer to cycles that visit each part at most once as “rainbow” cycles.

The following theorem relates the rainbow cycle number with approximate

Theorem 1

(Theorem 4,
[
9
]
)
.

Let
h(d)=d\cdot R(d)
and

. Let

be the smallest integer such that

. Then there is a
(1-\varepsilon)
-EFX allocation
X
and a set of unallocated goods

such that

|\mathcal{P}(X)|=O\big(\frac{4n}{\varepsilon\cdot h^{-1}(\frac{2n}{\varepsilon})}\big)
, and no one envies
\mathcal{P}(X)
.

Chaudhury et al. 2021a
show that

and thus show the existence of a

allocation with
O((\frac{n}{\varepsilon})^{4/5}

The following theorem from
[

]
gives an almost tight upper bound on the rainbow cycle number:

Theorem 2

(Theorem 3,
[
1
]
)
.

Given any integer

.

This implies the existence of
(1-\varepsilon)
-EFX allocations with
\tilde{O}((\frac{n}{\varepsilon})^{1/2})
charity.

The
\textsc{3PA}^{+}
Algorithm of
[

Our algorithm for computing a
\frac{2}{3}\text{-}\mathrm{EFX}
allocation builds on some of the techniques introduced in
[
5

Given an instance of fair division, if there are at least as many agents as the number of goods, then an allocation of size at most one, is trivially EFX. Therefore, one can safely assume that there are more goods than agents. That is,
m>n
. To begin with, consider a partial allocation with exactly one good per agent. If there is an envy-cycle in the allocation, resolve it using the ECE procedure, and make the envy graph acyclic. This allocation is called a
seed

Amanatidis et al. 2024
proposed an way to compute a
Property Preserving Partial Allocation
, via an algorithm referred to as
the
\textsc{3PA}^{+}
algorithm
(Algorithm
1
). Some of the details regarding the subroutines of the algorithm is deferred to Appendix
B
. The algorithm takes a seed allocation as input and returns a (possibly partial) allocation that satisfies a specific set of properties, listed below:

Desired properties of a partial allocation
X

C.1
Corrected Proof of Lemma
9

We show that the
All Cycles Resolution
subroutine terminates in pseudo-polynomial time.

Given an allocation, define a potential function as follows.

Definition 13

(Potential Function
\phi
)
.

Let
w:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a weight function defined as:

w(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x=1,\\
\frac{3}{2}&\text{if }x\geq 2.\end{cases}

Given an instance
\mathcal{I}
, and an allocation
X
for
\mathcal{I}
,

\phi(X)=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{N}}w(|X_{a}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{a}),

Proof.

Due to
[
17
]
, it is known that the algorithm terminates when the input graph is the envy graph
G(X)
. Therefore we only need to show that the algorithm terminates when the input graph
\tilde{G}(X)
is either the reduced envy graph
G_{\mathrm{r}}(X)
or the enhanced envy graph
G_{\mathrm{e}}(X)
.

Given an allocation
X
and its graph
\tilde{G}(X)
, let
X^{\prime}
be the allocation obtained after resolving one cycle
C
in
\tilde{G}(X)
. We show that
\phi(X^{\prime})>\phi(X)
.

For an agent
a
, her contribution to the potential function is
w(|X_{a}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{a})
. Note that the agents who are not part of the cycle, retain their bundles, and hence their contributions to the potential remain unchanged. Thus, we only need to consider the agents who are part of the cycle
C
.

Consider two consecutive agents
a
and
b
in the cycle
C
. If
(a,b)
is a red edge in
\tilde{G}(X)
, then by the definition of the red edge, we have
|X_{a}|=1
,
|X_{b}|>1
and
v_{a}(X_{b})\geq\frac{2}{3}v_{a}(X_{a})
. When the cycle is resolved, agent
a
receives the bundle of agent
b
. Therefore, the new contribution of agent
a
to the potential function is

\textstyle w(|X_{a}^{\prime}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{a}^{\prime})

\textstyle=w(|X_{b}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{b})

\textstyle\geq\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}v_{a}(X_{a})

\textstyle\geq w(|X_{a}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{a})

If
(a,b)
is not a red edge in
\tilde{G}(X)
, and if either
|X_{a}|=1
or
|X_{b}|>1
, then, on receiving the bundle of agent
b
, agent
a
has a new contribution to the potential function strictly increases as
w(|X_{b}|)\geq w(|X_{a}|)
and
v_{a}(X_{b})>v_{a}(X_{a})
.

Finally, if
(a,b)
is not a red edge in
\tilde{G}(X)
, and if both
|X_{a}|>1
and
|X_{b}|=1
, then, by the definitions of the reduced envy graph and enhanced envy graph, we know that
v_{a}(X_{b})>\frac{3}{2}v_{a}(X_{a})
. Therefore, the new contribution of agent
a
to the potential function is
w(|X_{b}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{b})>\frac{3}{2}v_{a}(X_{a})=w(|X_{a}|)\cdot v_{a}(X_{a})
.

As red edges go from agents with singleton bundles to agents with bundles of size at least two, a cycle must contain at least one non red edge. Therefore, at least one agent in the cycle has a contribution to the potential function that strictly increases after the cycle resolution, while no agents contribution strictly decreases. Thus,
\phi(X^{\prime})>\phi(X)
. Finally, since for any allocation
X
, the maximum value of the potential is
\phi(X)\leq\max\limits_{a\in\mathcal{N}}\left(\frac{3}{2}\cdot v_{a}(\mathcal{M})\right)^{n}
, the algorithm terminates in pseudo-polynomial time. This completes the proof.
∎
