Differentiated risk factors for stroke recurrence in anterior versus posterior circulation intracranial atherosclerosis: A high-resolution vessel wall imaging study
Authors: Caiya Yuan, Lin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeThe risk factors for recurrent stroke in intracranial atherosclerosis (ICAS) may differ between the anterior and posterior circulations. This exploratory study aimed to investigate and compare these risk factors, leveraging plaque characteristics identified by high-resolution vessel wall imaging (HR-VWI).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 166 patients with ICAS-related ischemic stroke, categorized into ACIS (n = 123) and PCIS (n = 43) groups. Each group was subdivided into recurrent (ACIS: 25; PCIS: 11) and non-recurrent (ACIS: 98; PCIS: 32) cohorts based on follow-up (mean: 16.69 ± 10.18 months). Clinical and imaging risk factors were compared, with multivariable Cox regression analysis employed to identify independent predictors of recurrence. Predictive performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC).ResultsFor ACIS, older age, multiple infarcts, and significant plaque enhancement were associated with recurrence (all q < 0.05). For PCIS, diabetes mellitus and intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH) were associated with recurrence (all q < 0.05). Multivariable analysis confirmed older age (>55.5 years), multiple infarcts, and significant plaque enhancement as independent risk factors for ACIS recurrence, whereas diabetes mellitus and IPH were independent predictors for PCIS recurrence. A combined predictive model for ACIS achieved a fair-to-good predictive performance (AUC: 0.821, 95% CI: 0.72–0.92), while the model for PCIS yielded a more uncertain performance (AUC: 0.770, 95% CI: 0.61–0.93) with a wide confidence interval, reflecting the small sample size.ConclusionThe drivers of recurrent stroke in ICAS appear to differ between the anterior and posterior circulations. These exploratory findings suggest that integrating clinical data with HR-VWI plaque features may enhance risk stratification. However, due to significant limitations, these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, prospective cohorts before being applied to clinical practice. This study underscores the potential need for circulation-specific secondary prevention strategies.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Anterior circulation, Posterior circulation, Recurrence, Intracranial atherosclerosis, Vessel wall imaging

Introduction

Intracranial atherosclerosis (ICAS) is a leading cause of ischemic stroke worldwide, accounting for up to 50% of cases in Asian populations and carrying a high annual recurrence rate of approximately 15% despite optimal medical therapy (Kim et al., 2012; Qureshi and Caplan, 2014). Effective secondary prevention hinges on accurate risk stratification, which requires a deep understanding of the factors driving these recurrent events. While traditional clinical risk factors are well-established, attention has increasingly turned to the direct role of vessel wall pathology in predicting stroke recurrence (Prabhakaran et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022).

High-resolution vessel wall imaging (HR-VWI) has emerged as a powerful tool for this purpose, enabling non-invasive characterization of plaque morphology and composition beyond simple luminal stenosis (Choi et al., 2015). Features of plaque vulnerability, such as significant post-contrast enhancement and the presence of intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH), have been identified as potent imaging biomarkers associated with plaque instability and an increased risk of future ischemic events (Kim et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2022). Indeed, a recent meta-analysis confirmed that plaque enhancement is one of the strongest predictors of downstream ischemic events in patients with ICAS (Song et al., 2021).

However, the cerebrovascular system is not a monolith. The anterior and posterior circulations possess distinct embryological origins, anatomical structures, and hemodynamic properties (Zürcher et al., 2019; Libman et al., 2001). For instance, post-mortem studies have revealed that atherosclerotic plaques in the basilar artery (posterior circulation) exhibit less neovascularization and macrophage infiltration compared to those in the middle cerebral artery (MCA, anterior circulation) (Yang et al., 2017). These underlying biological differences strongly suggest that the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis and the mechanisms of stroke recurrence may differ between the two territories. While some studies have reported a higher long-term recurrence risk in posterior circulation stroke (Zhang et al., 2020) and others have noted divergent risk factor profiles (Varghese et al., 2024), few have systematically compared the predictive value of HR-VWI plaque characteristics between anterior circulation ischemic stroke (ACIS) and posterior circulation ischemic stroke (PCIS).

This knowledge gap is clinically significant, as a “one-size-fits-all” approach to secondary prevention is likely suboptimal. Differentiating the key drivers of recurrence in ACIS versus PCIS could pave the way for more tailored and effective therapeutic strategies. Therefore, this study was designed as a hypothesis-generating effort to leverage HR-VWI to identify and compare the distinct clinical and imaging-based risk factors for recurrence in patients with ICAS-related ACIS and PCIS.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. We consecutively enrolled patients who were diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke secondary to ICAS and underwent HR-VWI at our institution between November 2019 and June 2022.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) diagnosis of unilateral ACIS or PCIS confirmed by diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) within 4 weeks of symptom onset; (3) presence of a culprit atherosclerotic plaque (defined as the plaque at the site of maximal stenosis or the one most likely responsible for the downstream infarct) in the corresponding vascular territory on HR-VWI; (4) complete clinical, laboratory, and follow-up data; and (5) at least one major atherosclerotic risk factor (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, or current smoking).

Exclusion criteria were: (1) stroke of other determined etiology (e.g., cardioembolism, vasculitis, moyamoya disease); (2) significant (≥50%) stenosis in the ipsilateral extracranial carotid or vertebral artery, which could be a competing source of embolism; (3) previous history of stroke or transient ischemic attack in the same vascular territory; and (4) poor quality MR images that precluded reliable analysis.

After applying these criteria, a total of 166 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 123 patients with ACIS and 43 with PCIS.

Clinical and laboratory assessment

Baseline demographic data, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking status), and laboratory results were collected from electronic medical records. Neurological severity on admission was quantified using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Functional outcome at 90 days was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), with a score >2 defined as a poor outcome.

Follow-up and endpoints

Patients were followed up via outpatient clinic visits or telephone interviews at 3, 6, and 12 months post-discharge, and annually thereafter. The primary endpoint for this study was recurrent ischemic stroke, defined as a new focal neurological deficit lasting more than 24 h and confirmed by new ischemic lesions on follow-up MRI within the vascular territory of the original culprit artery. The mean follow-up duration was 16.69 ± 10.18 months.

MR image acquisition

All imaging was performed on a 3.0 T MRI scanner (Achieva, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) using an 8-channel head coil. The standardized protocol included conventional sequences (DWI, FLAIR) and a 3D time-of-flight MR angiography (3D-TOF-MRA). The specific acquisition parameters for the conventional sequences were as follows: (1) axial DWI: repetition time (TR) = 3000 ms, echo time (TE) = 82 ms, field of view (FOV) = 230 × 230 mm2, matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 5 mm, and b-values = 0 and 1000 s/mm2; (2) axial FLAIR: TR = 9000 ms, TE = 130 ms, inversion time (TI) = 2500 ms, FOV = 230 × 230 mm2, matrix size = 256 × 256, and slice thickness = 5 mm; (3) 3D-TOF-MRA: TR = 21 ms, TE = 3.5 ms, FOV = 200 × 200 mm2, matrix size = 320 × 232, flip angle = 18°, and slice thickness = 1.0 mm.

For vessel wall assessment, the HR-VWI protocol consisted of pre- and post-contrast 3D T1-weighted SPACE (Sampling Perfection with Application optimized Contrasts using different flip angle Evolution) sequences. The parameters for the 3D T1-SPACE sequence were: TR = 800 ms, TE = 22 ms, FOV = 200 × 200 mm2, matrix size = 332 × 302, acquired voxel size = 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 mm3 (isotropic), and echo train length = 45. Post-contrast images were acquired 5 min after the intravenous administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent (gadopentetate dimeglumine, 0.1 mmol/kg) using identical sequence parameters.

Image analysis

Image analysis was performed independently by two experienced neuroradiologists (with 6 and 10 years of experience, respectively) who were blinded to all clinical information. A dedicated workstation (Syngo.via, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) was used for all measurements. Any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved by consensus.

The culprit plaque was identified and the following qualitative and quantitative characteristics were evaluated:

1.Significant Plaque Enhancement: This was assessed qualitatively and defined as a post-contrast signal intensity of the plaque that was clearly greater than or equal to the signal intensity of the pituitary stalk on the same T1-weighted sequence, which served as an internal reference for avid enhancement (Song et al., 2020).

Significant Plaque Enhancement: This was assessed qualitatively and defined as a post-contrast signal intensity of the plaque that was clearly greater than or equal to the signal intensity of the pituitary stalk on the same T1-weighted sequence, which served as an internal reference for avid enhancement (Song et al., 2020).

2.Intraplaque Hemorrhage (IPH): This was identified as a distinct region of high signal intensity (>150% of the signal from adjacent pons tissue) within the plaque on the pre-contrast T1-weighted images (Zhu et al., 2018).

Inter-rater reliability for the assessment of these categorical imaging features was excellent, with Cohen's Kappa coefficients exceeding 0.80 for all variables.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. To address the issue of multiple comparisons in the univariable analyses, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR), with an FDR-adjusted P-value (q-value) < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To identify independent predictors of stroke recurrence, variables with a P-value <0.10 in the univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model. A backward stepwise selection method was used to build the most parsimonious model. The proportional hazards assumption was verified for all final models. Hazard ratios (HR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated.

Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the predictive performance of the independent risk factors, both individually and combined in a predictive model. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and optimal cutoff points were determined using the Youden index. We acknowledge that this cross-sectional predictive model is not a time-to-event prediction and serves as an exploratory tool. To address the potential for over-optimism in model performance given the sample size, an internal validation was performed using a bootstrap procedure with 1000 resamples to obtain a bias-corrected estimate of the AUC.

Results

Baseline characteristics and follow-up

Of the 166 patients included, 123 (74.1%) were classified into the ACIS group and 43 (25.9%) into the PCIS group. During the mean follow-up period of 16.69 ± 10.18 months, a total of 36 patients (21.7%) experienced a recurrent ischemic stroke. The recurrence rate was 20.3% (25 of 123) in the ACIS group and 25.6% (11 of 43) in the PCIS group, a difference that was not statistically significant (P = .45).

Comparison of risk factors for recurrence in ACIS

In the ACIS cohort, univariable analysis showed that patients in the recurrent group were significantly older, had a higher prevalence of multiple infarcts on initial DWI, and were more likely to demonstrate significant plaque enhancement and irregular plaque surfaces on HR-VWI when compared to the non-recurrent group (all q < 0.05 after FDR correction). Independent predictors of stroke recurrence

To isolate the most critical predictors, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed separately for each circulation.

For ACIS, the final model identified older age (HR: 3.15; 95% CI: 1.28–7.76; P = .013), the presence of multiple infarcts (HR: 2.89; 95% CI: 1.15–7.26; P = .024), and significant plaque enhancement (HR: 2.77; 95% CI: 1.09–7.04; P = .032) as independent predictors of stroke recurrence (Table 3).

Predictive performance of risk models

We then assessed the predictive utility of these independent risk factors using ROC curve analysis. The interpretation of these results should be cautious, given the study's exploratory nature and small sample size.

For ACIS, the individual AUCs were modest: 0.699 for age, 0.664 for multiple infarcts, and 0.619 for plaque enhancement. However, a combined model incorporating all three factors demonstrated fair-to-good predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.821 (95% CI: 0.72-0.92). At an optimal cutoff, this model yielded a sensitivity of 92.00% and a specificity of 67.30%. Bootstrap internal validation confirmed the model's robustness, yielding a bias-corrected AUC of 0.805.

For PCIS, the individual AUCs were 0.692 for diabetes mellitus and 0.678 for IPH. A combined model of these two factors yielded an AUC of 0.770. However, the wide 95% confidence interval (0.61-0.93) suggests considerable uncertainty in this estimate. The model produced a sensitivity of 72.70% and a specificity of 65.60%. The bootstrap-corrected AUC was 0.751, indicating only fair predictive performance, which must be considered with extreme caution due to the very small sample size of the recurrent group (n = 11).

Discussion

This exploratory, hypothesis-generating study, utilizing HR-VWI, suggests that the key risk factors for recurrent ischemic stroke in patients with ICAS may differ based on the location of the culprit plaque. For patients with ACIS, a combination of clinical (older age), embolic-pattern (multiple infarcts), and plaque-activity (significant enhancement) markers were predictive of recurrence. In stark contrast, for patients with PCIS, a metabolic factor (diabetes mellitus) and a marker of advanced plaque vulnerability (IPH) were the sole independent predictors. These distinct profiles, while requiring significant further validation, underscore the potential clinical importance of a circulation-specific approach to risk stratification and secondary prevention.

A central observation of our study is that significant plaque enhancement independently predicted recurrence only in the ACIS group. Plaque enhancement on HR-VWI is a well-established surrogate marker for inflammation and active neovascularization, processes integral to plaque destabilization (Kim et al., 2016; Song et al., 2021). While our results align with previous studies linking enhancement to stroke risk (Song et al., 2020), the novel contribution here is the demonstration of its circulation-specific predictive power. This is likely explained by underlying biological differences; post-mortem studies have shown that atherosclerotic lesions in the MCA tend to have more prominent inflammatory cell infiltration and neovascularization compared to those in the basilar artery, which are often more fibrotic or calcified (Yang et al., 2017). This suggests that inflammation-driven plaque activity, as captured by enhancement, is a more dominant mechanism of recurrence in the anterior circulation.

Conversely, IPH emerged as a powerful predictor exclusively in the PCIS group. IPH is a hallmark of advanced, unstable plaques, resulting from the rupture of fragile microvessels within the plaque core, which can rapidly expand plaque volume and precipitate thrombosis (Zhu et al., 2018; Alkhalil et al., 2020). This differential role may also be rooted in vascular anatomy. Arteries of the posterior circulation, particularly the basilar artery, possess a thinner media and fewer smooth muscle cells compared to the MCA, potentially rendering them more susceptible to the mechanical stress and disruptive effects of an intramural hematoma (Niu et al., 2016). Our findings thus corroborate other reports that have specifically linked IPH to recurrent events in the posterior circulation (Yu et al., 2015).

Beyond imaging biomarkers, our study confirmed the differential role of clinical risk factors. Diabetes mellitus was a strong independent predictor for PCIS recurrence but not for ACIS, a finding consistent with population-based studies showing a higher prevalence of diabetes in patients with posterior circulation stroke (Varghese et al., 2024). Diabetes induces profound endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and a pro-inflammatory state, which may exert a more pronounced deleterious effect on the unique vascular environment of the posterior circulation (Mosenzon et al., 2023). Furthermore, the presence of multiple infarcts on initial imaging, suggestive of an artery-to-artery embolic mechanism, was predictive only for ACIS recurrence. This may reflect the higher propensity of complex, inflamed plaques in the MCA to rupture and release embolic debris.

Limitations

This study has several significant limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. First, and most importantly, this is a retrospective, single-center study with a small sample size, particularly in the PCIS recurrent group (n = 11). This severely limits the statistical power of our analysis for the posterior circulation, makes the findings susceptible to overfitting and Type II errors, and reduces the generalizability of our conclusions. Consequently, the findings, especially regarding PCIS, must be considered strictly exploratory and are in urgent need of validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Second, our predictive models were not validated in an independent external cohort, which is a critical step for assessing model performance and generalizability. The internal bootstrap validation provides some measure of stability but cannot replace external validation.

Third, the predictive models were constructed using variables from a cross-sectional analysis and do not represent a formal time-to-event prediction, which limits their prognostic utility.

Fourth, our analysis did not account for several potential confounding factors. Although patients reportedly received dual antiplatelet therapy, we did not analyze treatment adherence, duration of therapy, level of risk factor control (e.g., blood pressure, lipid levels), or other potential changes in medical management during the follow-up period. These unmeasured factors could have significantly influenced stroke recurrence rates.

Finally, while we applied FDR correction to mitigate the risk of false positives from multiple comparisons, the exploratory nature of the analysis means some findings could still be due to chance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this exploratory study suggests that the clinical and imaging-based predictors of recurrent stroke in patients with ICAS may differ between the anterior and posterior circulations. Plaque enhancement appears to be a key marker of risk in ACIS, while IPH and diabetes may be dominant risk factors in PCIS. However, given the significant limitations, including the small sample size and lack of external validation, these findings should be interpreted with considerable caution and are best viewed as hypothesis-generating. They highlight the heterogeneity of intracranial atherosclerosis and support a paradigm shift towards circulation-specific risk stratification, but large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are imperative to validate these preliminary findings before they can inform clinical practice.
