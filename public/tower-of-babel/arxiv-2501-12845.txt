Modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group
Md Hasan Ali Biswas and Sundaram Thangavelu

Abstract
. In this article we show how certain irreducible unitary representation Π λ \Pi_{\lambda} of the twisted Heisenberg group ℍ λ n ​ ( ℂ ) \mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}) leads to the twisted modulation spaces M λ p , q ​ ( ℝ 2 ​ n ) . M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}). These Π λ \Pi_{\lambda} also turn out to be irreducible unitary representations of another nilpotent Lie group G n G_{n} which contains two copies of the Heisenberg group ℍ n . \mathbb{H}^{n}. By lifting Π λ \Pi_{\lambda} we obtain another unitary representation Π \Pi of G n G_{n} acting on L 2 ​ ( ℍ n ) . L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}). We define our modulation spaces M p , q ​ ( ℍ n ) M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}) in terms of the matrix coefficients associated to Π . \Pi. These spaces are shown to be invariant under Heisenberg translations and Heisenberg modulations which are different from euclidean modulations. We also establish some of the basic properties of M λ p , q ​ ( ℝ 2 ​ n ) M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}) and M p , q ​ ( ℍ n ) M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}) such as completeness and invariance under suitable Fourier transforms.



1.
Introduction

We begin by recalling the definition of the modulation spaces
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
introduced by Feichtinger in
[
7
]
and a detailed set of results were published in
[
8
]
. The left regular representation of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the translation operator
\tau(x)
whose action is simply given by
\tau(x)f(\xi)=f(\xi-x)
for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
By conjugating with the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
which acts unitarily on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we get another representation
e(y)=\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\circ\tau(y)\circ\mathcal{F}
which are the modulation operators with action
e(y)f(\xi)=e^{iy\cdot\xi}f(\xi).
These two families of operators do not commute but lead to the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
whose group law is

(1.1)

(x,y,t)(u,v,s)=\big(x+u,y+v,t+s+\frac{1}{2}(u\cdot y-v\cdot x)\big).

The Schrödinger representation
\pi(x,y,t)
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined by

(1.2)

\pi(x,y,t)\varphi(\xi)=e^{it}\,e^{i(x\cdot\xi+\frac{1}{2}x\cdot y)}\,\varphi(\xi+y),\,\,\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We observe that this family
\pi(x,y,t)
contains both translations and modulations. Recall that the modulation space
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),1\leq p,q\leq\infty
is defined to be the class of tempered distributions
f
for which the Fourier-Wigner transform
V_{g}f(x,y)=\langle f,e(y)\circ\tau(x)g\rangle
belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})

for some Schwartz function
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

In terms of the operator
\pi(x,y)=\pi(x,y,0)
we can also express
V_{g}f
as

V_{g}f(x,y)=e^{-\frac{i}{2}x\cdot y}\,\langle f,\pi(y,-x)g\rangle.

Consequently,
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if the matrix coefficient
\langle f,\pi(y,-x)g\rangle\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

It is well known that the above definition of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is independent of the ‘window function’
g.
When
g
is chosen to be the standard Gaussian
e^{-\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}}
it turns out that

V_{g}f(x,y)e^{\frac{i}{2}x\cdot y}=Bf(x-iy)e^{-\frac{1}{4}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}

where
B
is the Bargmann transform which takes
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
unitarily onto the Fock space
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}).

Recall that
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
is the weighted Bergman space of entire functions corresponding to the weight
w(x+iy)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})}.
Thus
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if the function

F(x,y)=Bf(x+iy)\sqrt{w(x+iy)}\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We have a perfect analogy when
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is replaced by the twisted Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
leading to the twisted modulation spaces

M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

The left regular representation
\mathbb{H}^{n}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
gives rise to a family of twisted translations
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi),\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for each non-zero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.
These are unitary operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
whose action can be transferred to the twisted Fock spaces
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
studied in
[
12
,
15
]
. This is achieved by means of the twisted Bargmann transform
B_{\lambda}
since
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
is exactly the image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
under
B_{\lambda}.
The resulting unitary operator
\rho_{\lambda}(\xi)
initially defind for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
has a natural extension to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}.
Thus for each
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
we have a unitary operator
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)
on
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}).
For
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},
the operators
\rho_{\lambda}(\eta)
and
\rho_{\lambda}(i\eta)
are unitarily equivalent: there is a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
so that
\rho_{\lambda}(i\eta)=U^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\eta)\circ U.

By defining
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)\circ B_{\lambda}
we get a family of unitary operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
it happens that
\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi)=\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)
and by defining
e_{\lambda}(\eta)=\Pi_{\lambda}(i\eta),\,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
we get another family of unitary operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

In analogy with translations and modulations on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
leading to
\mathbb{H}^{n},
the operators
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)
and
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
lead to the twisted Heisenberg group

\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})={\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
introduced by one of us in
[
23
]
, see Section 3.1. The group law of
\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is given by

(\zeta,t)(\zeta^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\big(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]\big).

see Section 2.3 for the notation. In terms of
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)
we can build a unitary representation
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,t)
of the twisted Heisenberg group.

We will show that the unitary operator
U_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ U\circ B_{\lambda}
intertwines
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
and
\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)
: thus
e_{\lambda}(\eta)=U_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)\circ U_{\lambda}.
This is reminiscent of the well known relation
e(y)=\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\circ\tau(y)\circ\mathcal{F}
where
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
In view of this, the operators
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
are qualified to be called twisted modulations. In this article we are interested in knowing what kind of spaces we will end up with if translations and modulations on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are replaced by twisted translations
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)
and twisted modulations
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for a given non-zero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.
We are led to the twisted modulation spaces
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
consisting of tempered distributions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for which
the matrix coefficients
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)g\rangle
belong to the mixed norm space
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for some Schwartz function
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

As in the classical setting, the above definition can be shown to be independent of the window function
g.
Moreover, when we choose

g(x,y)=p_{1/2}^{\lambda}(x,y)
where
p_{t}^{\lambda}
is the heat kernel associated to the special Hermite operator
L_{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
it turns out that

(1.3)

d_{n}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)g\rangle=B_{\lambda}f(\xi+i\eta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)}

where
c_{\lambda}=(4\pi)^{-n}\lambda^{n}(\sinh\lambda)^{-n}
and
d_{n}
is a constant. Here
w_{\lambda}
is the weight function appearing in the definition of
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}).
In view of this we note that
f\in M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
if and only if

B_{\lambda}f(\xi+i\eta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)}\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

In this article we study some of the basic properties of these modulation spaces.
Another goal is to give a reasonable definition of modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group. In order to define them we make use of the twisted modulation spaces.

The family of unitary operators
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})

satisfy the composition formula

(1.4)

\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})=\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime})e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}((\coth\lambda)\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})-\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}])}

where
[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]
is the obvious extension to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
of the symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

This suggests that we introduce a group structure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n+1}={\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times{\mathbb{C}}
by the rule

(\zeta,s)(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})=(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime},s+s^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]+\frac{i}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})).

We denote this group by
G_{n}
and note that
G_{n}
contains two copies of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
as subgroups. We get a family of unitary representations of
G_{n}
by defining

\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)=e^{i\lambda(\operatorname{Re}s-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}s)}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta).

These representations are irreducible as the family
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)
is irreducible as shown in
[
12
]
.

The operators
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)
initially defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
can be lifted to a unitary operator
\Pi(\zeta,s)
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
When
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
it can be shown that

\Pi(\xi,s)=\tau(\xi,s)
the left translation on the Heisenberg group.
There is a unitary operator
U:L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
which intertwines
\Pi_{\lambda}(\eta,s)
with
\Pi_{\lambda}(i\eta,s)
for
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
Thus the operator
e(\eta,s)=\Pi_{\lambda}(i\eta,s)
satisfies
e(\eta,s)=U^{\ast}\circ\tau(\eta,s)\circ U.
For this reason we can consider
e(\eta,s)
as analogues of modulations on the Heisenberg group.
We look for Banach spaces of distributions on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
that are invariant under the Heisenberg translations and modulations or more generally under
\Pi(\zeta,s)
for
s\in\mathbb{R}.

The representation
\Pi(\zeta,s)
fails to be square integrable but by considering a modified matrix coefficient we get an interesting identity. Indeed, we can show that for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle|^{2}\,d\zeta\,ds=c_{n}\,\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|f(h)|^{2}\,dh.

Here
p_{t}
is the heat kernel associated to the sublaplacian on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
T
is a certain Fourier multiplier operator acting on the central variable.
The above identity follows from the relation

d_{n}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\lambda s}\,\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle\,ds=B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}

Taking the clue from the equivalent definitions of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
M^{p,q}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
in terms of the Bargmann/twisted Bargmann transforms we make the following definition. We say that a tempered distribution
f
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
belongs to
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
if and only if for every
\lambda\neq 0,
its inverse Fourier transform
f^{\lambda}
in the central variable belongs to
M^{p,q}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
in such a way that

\|f\|_{(p,q)}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\cdot)}\,B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\cdot)\|_{L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})}^{2}\,d\lambda<\infty.

The logic behind this particular definition is explained in Section 4.3, see also Remark
4.10
. We will show that these are Banach spaces invariant under the action of
\Pi(\zeta,s),s\in\mathbb{R}
and also under
U
when
p=q.
Some more basic properties of these spaces will also be established.

Many important function spaces, such as modulation spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or Bergman spaces on the unit ball in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
occur as coorbit spaces defined in terms of matrix coefficients associated to irreducible unitary representations of certain Lie groups. In particular, the case of nilpotent Lie groups have received considerable attention in the works of Beltita and Beltita
[
3
,
4
]
and Grochenig
[
14
]
. In these works we find detailed study of modulation spaces associated to representations of nilpotent Lie groups which are square integrable modulo the center. Some of our results on twisted modulation spaces can be read out from these papers, e.g Theorem 3.3 from
[
3
]
and Theorem 3.7 from
[
4
]
. However, our motivation for the study of twisted modulation spaces comes from twisted Fock spaces and a search for a reasonable definition of modulation spaces on Heisenberg groups.

We are not the first to consider modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group. We would like to bring the work of Fischer-Rottensteiner-Ruzhansky
[
9
]
to the attention of the readers. In this very interesting work the authors have defined and studied modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group which they denote by
E^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
in the unweighted case. These spaces are defined in terms of the matrix coefficients associated to a square integrable representation of the Dynin-Folland group
\mathbb{H}_{n,2}
in very much the same way
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are defined in terms of the Schrödinger representation
\pi
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
This group studied by Dynin
[
6
]
and Folland
[
11
]
is the semidirect product of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2n+2}
which contains
\mathbb{H}^{n}
as a subgroup. Compare this with the fact that
\mathbb{H}^{n}
can be considered as the semidirect product of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.
As a set
\mathbb{H}_{n,2}
can be identified with
\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
whose elements are written as
(P,Q,S).

There is a family of irreducible unitary representation
\pi_{\lambda}
of
\mathbb{H}_{n,2}
whose action on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is explicitly given by

\pi_{\lambda}(P,Q,S)f(X)=e^{2\pi i\lambda(S+(Q,X))}\,f(XP).

The similarity between the Schrödinger representation is obvious. These representations turn out be square integrable modulo the centre and hence there is a canonical way of defining the modulation spaces. Note that
\pi(P,0,0)
is the right translation by elements of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\pi(0,Q,S)
is the usual modulation on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}.
Thus the modulation spaces
E^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n+1})
are invariant under the Heisenberg translations and euclidean modulations. Though at present we do not know how to compare these modulation spaces with our
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),
the following remarks are in order.

Despite there is an underlying nilpotent group
G_{n}
in our definition, we are motivated by the twisted modulation spaces which are associated to a square integrable representation
\Pi_{\lambda}
of the twisted Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}).
Even though the representation
\Pi
of
G_{n}
is built in terms of
\Pi_{\lambda}
it is not square integrable even if we go modulo the centre. In defining
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
we have used the modified matrix coefficients of
\Pi
restricted to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}\subset G_{n}.
The resulting spaces are invariant under Heisenberg translations
\tau(h)
and Heisenberg modulations
e(h)
which are different from the standard euclidean modulations. Roughly speaking, our modulation spaces are direct integrals of twisted modulation spaces.

The plan of the paper is as follows. After recalling the basic representation theory of the Heisenberg group, twisted Bargmann transform and twisted Fock space in Section 2, we move on to section 3 where we introduce the twisted Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
and study the matrix coefficients of a representation
\Pi_{\lambda}
realised on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Later we define the twisted modulation spaces
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and study some basic properties of the same. In Section 4 we define the group
G_{n}
and introduce the representation
\Pi
which is realised on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
Based on properties of the matrix coefficients of
\Pi
we define the modulation spaces
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and investigate some of its basic properties.

We conclude this introduction with a warning about the convention we have followed about constants that are not important. Apart from the well known convention that constants
C
in various inequalities are not necessarily same, we also use, by abuse of notation, the same constant
c_{n}
or
d_{n}
in several equations to avoid keeping track of their not so important exact values.

2.
Heisenberg groups and twisted Heisenberg groups

2.1.
Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and the representations
\pi_{\lambda}

We begin by recalling some well known facts about the Heisenberg groups
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and their representations. For more details we refer to the monographs
[
10
,
21
]
. The group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is just
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
equipped with the group law

(z,t)(w,s)=(z+w,t+s+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}(z\cdot\bar{w})).

Note that if we let
z=x+iy,w=u+iv,
then
\operatorname{Im}(z\cdot\bar{w})=(u\cdot y-x\cdot v)
is nothing but the symplectic form
[\xi,\eta]
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
between
\xi=(x,u)
and
\eta=(y,v).
Therefore, the group law can also be written in the form

(\xi,t)(\eta,s)=(\xi+\eta,t+s+\frac{1}{2}[\xi,\eta]).

The group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a step two nilpotent Lie group, it is therefore unimodular and the Haar measure is simply given by the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}.
The spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
are defined with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The convolution between two functions
f,g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is defined in the usual way

f\ast g(\xi,t)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f((\xi,t)(\eta,s)^{-1})\,g(\eta,s)\,d\eta\,ds.

The convolution on the Heisenberg group gives rise to a family of convolutions for functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
known as twisted convolutions.

To define them let us set up some notation.
For a function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
let us denote by
f^{\lambda}(x,u)
the inverse Fourier transform of
f
in the central variable: thus

f^{\lambda}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\xi,t)\,e^{i\lambda t}\,dt.

Since
(\eta,s)^{-1}=(-\eta,-s)
a simple calculation shows that

(f\ast g)^{\lambda}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f^{\lambda}(\xi-\eta)\,g^{\lambda}(\eta)\,e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}[\xi,\eta]}\,d\eta.

The right hand side defines what is known as the
\lambda
-twisted convolution of
f^{\lambda}
with
g^{\lambda}
denoted by
f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}g^{\lambda}.

By defining the
\lambda
-twisted translation
\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)=\tau_{\lambda}(y,v)
by the equation

(2.1)

\displaystyle\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)g^{\lambda}(\xi)=g^{\lambda}(\xi-\eta)\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}[\xi,\eta]}

we see that the twisted convolution
f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}g^{\lambda}
is given by the following integral

(2.2)

f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}g^{\lambda}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f^{\lambda}(\eta)\,\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)g^{\lambda}(\xi)\,d\eta.

Taking partial Fourier transform in the central variable is a useful technique which reduces problems on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
to problems on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

We now briefly recall the representation theory of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which is needed in order to define the group Fourier transform. For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\lambda\neq 0,
there is an irreducible unitary representation
\pi_{\lambda}
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
explicitly given by (see
[
10
]
)

\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,t)f(\xi)=e^{i\lambda t}e^{i\lambda(x\cdot\xi+\frac{1}{2}x\cdot y)}f(\xi+y),\,\,\,f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We use the notation
\pi_{\lambda}(x,y)
for the operator
\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,0)
and when
\lambda=1
we simply write
\pi(x,y)
instead of
\pi_{1}(x,y).
We observe that
\pi(x,y)
is factored as

\pi(x,y)=e^{\frac{i}{2}x\cdot y}\,e(x)\circ\tau(-y)
where
\tau(y)
and
e(x)
are the standard euclidean translation and modulation. The Fourier transform a function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is the operator valued function

\hat{f}(\lambda)=:\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f(\xi,t)\,\pi_{\lambda}(\xi,t)\,\,d\xi\,dt.

Recalling the definition of
\pi_{\lambda}
and
f^{\lambda}
we see that
\hat{f}(\lambda)=\pi_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda})
where

\pi_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda})=:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\,f^{\lambda}(\xi)\,\pi_{\lambda}(\xi)\,d\xi

is known as the Weyl transform of
f^{\lambda}.
Properties of the Fourier transform
f\rightarrow\hat{f}
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
are proved by studying the Weyl transform.

The Weyl transform initially defined on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
has an extension to the whole of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
as a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.
It takes
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
onto the the space
\mathcal{S}_{2}
of Hilbert -Schmidt operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
The inversion formula for the Weyl transform reads as

(2.3)

f(x,u)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\,|\lambda|^{n/2}\,\operatorname{tr}(\pi_{\lambda}(-x,-u)\pi_{\lambda}(f)).

It can be shown that
\pi_{\lambda}(f\ast_{\lambda}g)=\pi_{\lambda}(f)\,\pi_{\lambda}(g).
For easy reference we record the following relation between the two families
of unitary operators
\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)
and
\pi_{\lambda}(x,u):

\pi_{\lambda}(a,b)\pi_{\lambda}(x,u)=\pi_{\lambda}(x+a,u+b)e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}(u\cdot a-x\cdot b)}

which follows from the fact that
\pi_{\lambda}
is a representation of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Another easy calculation shows that

\pi_{\lambda}(a,b)\pi_{\lambda}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x,u)\pi_{\lambda}(x+a,u+b)e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}(u\cdot a-x\cdot b)}dx\,du.

Hence the Weyl transform of the twisted translation of a function is given by the relation

\pi_{\lambda}(a,b)\pi_{\lambda}(f)=\pi_{\lambda}(\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)f)

which translates into the property

(2.4)

\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)(f\ast_{\lambda}g)=\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)f\ast_{\lambda}g

for the
\lambda
-twisted convolution of
f
with
g
in view of the relation
\pi_{\lambda}(f\ast_{\lambda}g)=\pi_{\lambda}(f)\,\pi_{\lambda}(g).

2.2.
The twisted Bargmann transform and the twisted Fock spaces

As we have remarked in the introduction the classical modulation spaces can be defined in terms of the Bargmann transform
B
which takes
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
unitarily onto the Fock space
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
consisting entire functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
that are square integrable with respect to the Gaussian measure. It is well known that Bargmann transform is closely related to the Schrödinger representation
\pi
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
In a similar way, we define the twisted modulation spaces in terms of a unitary representation of the twisted Heisenberg group. The motivation for this group comes from certain unitary representation of the twisted Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
which is the image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
under the twisted Bargmann transform
B_{\lambda}.
Hence we begin by recalling the necessary background material to state relevant results on
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}).
The basic reference for this section is
[
15
,
12
,
23
]
. We also refer the reader to
[
20
,
21
]
for results related to Hermite and special Hermite operators.

Consider the scaled Hermite operator
H(\lambda)=-\Delta+\lambda^{2}|x|^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is a positive operator with an explicit spectral decomposition given in terms of the Hermite functions. We
let
e^{-tH(\lambda)},t>0
stand for the semigroup generated by
H(\lambda).
We also consider the special Hermite operator
L_{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
generating another semigroup
e^{-tL_{\lambda}}
with the associated kernel

p_{t}^{\lambda}(x,u)=(4\pi)^{-n}\,\lambda^{n}(\sinh t\lambda)^{-n}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda(\coth t\lambda)(|x|^{2}+|u|^{2})}

which has a holomorphic extension
p_{t}^{\lambda}(z,w)
to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}.
Let us write
c_{\lambda}=(4\pi)^{-n}\,\lambda^{n}(\sinh\lambda)^{-n}.
The twisted Bargmann transform
B_{\lambda}
is defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
by

(2.5)

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}f(z,w)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\,|\lambda|^{n/2}\,c_{\lambda}\,\,p_{1}^{\lambda}(z,w)^{-1}\,\operatorname{tr}\left(\pi_{\lambda}(-z,-w)\pi_{\lambda}(f)e^{-\frac{1}{2}H(\lambda)}\right).

In view of the inversion formula (
2.3
) for the Weyl transform, using the fact that
\pi_{\lambda}(p_{t}^{\lambda})=e^{-tH(\lambda)},
the twisted Bargmann transform is also given by

B_{\lambda}f(z,w)=\,c_{\lambda}\,\,p_{1}^{\lambda}(z,w)^{-1}\,f\ast_{\lambda}p_{1/2}^{\lambda}(z,w).

The image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
under
B_{\lambda}
is known to be a weighted Bergman space with the explicit weight function

w_{\lambda}(\zeta)=c_{\lambda}\,\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\coth\lambda)|\zeta|^{2}}\,e^{\lambda[\operatorname{Re}\zeta,\,\operatorname{Im}\zeta]},\,\,\zeta=(z,w).

Here
[\xi,\eta]
is the symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
defined by
[(x,u),(y,v)]=(u\cdot y-v\cdot x).

The twisted Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
is defined as the space of all entire functions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
square integrable with respect to the measure
w_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,d\zeta
where
d\zeta
is the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}.
When equipped with the inner product

\langle F,G\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}F(\zeta)\,\overline{G}(\zeta)\,w_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,d\zeta,

the space
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
becomes a reproducing kernel Hilbert space and the Bargmann transform

B_{\lambda}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
becomes a unitary operator. The reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
is known explicitly and given by

(2.6)

K_{\zeta}(\zeta^{\prime})=d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}\,\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,(\overline{\zeta}\cdot\zeta^{\prime})}\,e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}\,[\overline{\zeta},\zeta^{\prime}]}

where
d_{n}
is a constant depending only on the dimension. We refer the reader to
[
15
,
12
]
for more details of this transform and further properties of
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}).
We note that when
\lambda=0
the twisted Fock space reduces to the classical Fock space
\mathcal{F}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}).

The operator
U
defined on
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
by
UF(\zeta)=F(-i\zeta)
is a unitary operator since the weight function
w_{\lambda}
and the Lebesgue measure
d\zeta
are invariant under the map
\zeta\rightarrow-i\zeta.
Consequently, as
B_{\lambda}
is unitary, there exists a unitary operator
U_{\lambda}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
defined by the relation

U\circ B_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}\circ U_{\lambda}.
In
[
12
]
the authors have calculated the operator
U_{\lambda}
explicitly and shown that for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})

(2.7)

\displaystyle U_{\lambda}f(x,u)=c(\lambda)^{n}\,\widehat{f}(c(\lambda)(x,u))

where
c(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\coth\frac{1}{2}\lambda)
and
\widehat{f}
is the euclidean Fourier transform of
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
The operator
U_{\lambda}
becomes the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
when
\lambda
becomes
0.
The function
c(\lambda)
occurs in many places whose properties are investigated in Lemma
4.3
.

2.3.
Twisted Heisenberg group and a unitary representation

We begin by setting up some notations. The symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
defined by
[(x,u),(y,v)]=(u\cdot y-v\cdot x)
has a natural extension to
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
given by

[\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}]=[(z,w),(z^{\prime},w^{\prime})]=(w\cdot z^{\prime}-z\cdot w^{\prime}),\,\,\zeta=(z,w),\zeta^{\prime}=(z^{\prime},w^{\prime}).

For any
\lambda\neq 0,
the twisted Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
is defined as the manifold
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
equipped with the group law

(\zeta,t)(\zeta^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\big(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]\big).

Note that when
\lambda=0
the twisted Heisenberg group reduces to the standard Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{2n}={\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
with the group law

(\zeta,t)(\zeta^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\big(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})\big).

The twisted Heisenberg group is an example of a Mackey group, see
[
16
]
.

To every real
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrix
\Theta
there is an associated nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{H}_{\Theta}
which is just
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}
equipped with the following group law:

(x,e^{it})(y,e^{is})=(x+y,e^{i(s+t+\frac{1}{2}\langle x,\Theta y\rangle)}).

The universal covering of this group is
G_{\Theta}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
with the group law

(x,s)(y,t)=(x+y,s+t+\frac{1}{2}\langle x,\Theta y\rangle).

Note that Heisenberg groups are of the form
G_{\Theta}
for a suitable
\Theta.
More generally, as proved by Arhancet et al in
[
1
]

G_{\Theta}
is a Metivier group if and only if
\Theta
is invertible and a H-type group if and only if
\Theta
is orthogonal.

Identifying
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and writing
\zeta=\xi+i\eta,\,\xi=(x,u),\eta=(y,v)
the group product
(\zeta,t)(\zeta^{\prime},t^{\prime})
of two elements from
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
is given by

\big(\xi+\xi^{\prime},\eta+\eta^{\prime},\,t+t^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)(x^{\prime}\cdot y-y^{\prime}\cdot x+u^{\prime}\cdot v-v^{\prime}\cdot u)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,(x^{\prime}\cdot u-u^{\prime}\cdot x+y^{\prime}\cdot v-v^{\prime}\cdot y)\big).

In terms of
I_{n},
the
n\times n
identity matrix, and the matrix

\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}
we define the matrix
\Theta_{\lambda}
by

\Theta_{\lambda}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{J}&-(\coth\lambda)I_{2n}\\
(\coth\lambda)I_{2n}&\mathbf{J}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
becomes a Mackey group since the group law can be written in the form

\big((\xi,\eta)+(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}),t+t^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\langle(\xi,\eta),\Theta_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\rangle\big).

It is clear that
\Theta_{\lambda}
is skew-symmetric and easy to check that it is invertible but not orthogonal. Thus
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
is a Metivier group but not of Heisenberg type.

The twisted Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
supports a representation
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta,t)
of the twisted Heisenberg group which we describe now. We consider the following family of unitary operators defined on the twisted Fock space by

(2.8)

\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})F(\zeta)=F(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})}\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}\,[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]}\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\coth\lambda)|\zeta^{\prime}|^{2}}\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}[\operatorname{Re}\zeta^{\prime},\,\operatorname{Im}\zeta^{\prime}]}.

As shown in
[
12
]
this family of operators satisfy the composition law

\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})=\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime})\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}((\coth\lambda)\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})-\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}])}.

By defining
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime},t^{\prime})=e^{-it^{\prime}}\,\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})
it is not difficult to show that
\rho_{\lambda}
is indeed a unitary representation of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}).
In fact, the group law of
\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{n}
itself was suggested by the above composition law.
In
[
12
]
the authors have shown that
\rho_{\lambda}
is an irreducible unitary representation of the twisted Heisenberg group, see also
[
23
]
. As the twisted Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
is isometrically isomorphic to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
this representation leads to another representation realised on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
The action of this representation which we denote by
\Pi_{\lambda}
can be calculated explicitly in terms of twisted translations and twisted modulations.

3.
Twisted modulations spaces

3.1.
Twisted translations and modulations

We now proceed to define twisted modulations. By conjugating
\rho_{\lambda}
with the unitary operator
B_{\lambda}
we get a representation
\Pi_{\lambda}
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
realised on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Thus we define

\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,t)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta,t)\circ B_{\lambda}.

Note that
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,t)=e^{-it}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)
where
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)=\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,0)
which can be calculated explicitly. With
\zeta=\xi+i\eta,
sometimes we use the notation
\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)
for
\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta).
An easy calculation shows that the operator
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)=\rho_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)

can be factored as

\rho_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)=e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,\xi\cdot\eta}\,\rho_{\lambda}(i\eta)\,\rho_{\lambda}(\xi)\,.

In view of this, conjugation with the twisted Bargmann transform
B_{\lambda}
leads to the relation

(3.1)

\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,\xi\cdot\eta}\,e_{\lambda}(\eta)\,\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)\,

where
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\xi)\circ B_{\lambda}
and
e_{\lambda}(\eta)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(i\eta)\circ B_{\lambda}.
These operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
can be calculated explicitly.

From the definition of
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)
and
U
it is easy to check that
U\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)\circ U^{\ast}=\rho_{\lambda}(i\zeta)
and hence recalling that
U\circ B_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}\circ U_{\lambda}
we obtain the relation

\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ U^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(i\eta)\circ U\circ B_{\lambda}=U_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(i\eta)\circ B_{\lambda}\circ U_{\lambda}.

This simply means that
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
and
\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)
are unitarily equivalent to each other:

(3.2)

e_{\lambda}(\eta)=U_{\lambda}\circ\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)\circ U_{\lambda}^{\ast}.

As we show below
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)
turned out to be nothing but the
\lambda
-twisted translation. Since
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
is unitarily equivalent to
\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)
we call them
\lambda
-twisted modulations.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
we have the following:

(3.3)

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(a,b)\circ B_{\lambda}=\tau_{\lambda}(a,b),\,\,

is just the
\lambda
-twisted translation whereas the
\lambda
-twisted modulations are given by

(3.4)

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}(a,b)f(x,u)=f\big(x-(\tanh\frac{\lambda}{2})b,u+(\tanh\frac{\lambda}{2})a\big)\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\frac{\lambda}{2})(x\cdot a+u\cdot b)}.

Proof.

If we let
F=B_{\lambda}f,
then by the inversion formula (
2.3
) we see that

F(x,u)=c_{\lambda}\,p_{1}^{\lambda}(x,u)^{-1}\,f\ast_{\lambda}p_{1/2}^{\lambda}(x,u).

Recalling the action of
\rho_{\lambda}(a,b)
on
F
we get

\rho_{\lambda}(a,b)F(x,u)=e^{-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\coth\lambda)(a^{2}+b^{2})}\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(a\cdot x+b\cdot u)}\,\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)F(x,u).

Using the relation (
2.4
) we calculate that

(\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)F(x,u)=p_{1}^{\lambda}(x-a,u-b)^{-1}\,p_{1}^{\lambda}(x,u)\,B_{\lambda}(\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)f)(x,u).

Using this in the expression for
(\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)F(x,u)
and simplifying we get

\rho_{\lambda}(a,b)(B_{\lambda}f)(x,u)=B_{\lambda}(\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)f)(x,u).

We calculate
e_{\lambda}(a,b)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(i(a,b))\circ B_{\lambda}
by making use of the relation (
3.2
) verified above. As
U_{\lambda}
is essentially the Fourier transform, the expression for
e_{\lambda}(a,b)
is obtained after an easy calculation.
∎

As the representation
\Pi_{\lambda}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is unitarily equivalent to the irreducible representation
\rho_{\lambda}
on
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}),
it is also irreducible. We now show that it is square integrable modulo the center of the twisted Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{\lambda}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}).
The following proposition is also true in the more general context of Metivier groups, see
[
3
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}),
the matrix coefficient
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Moreover,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta=d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}^{-2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|f(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|g(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\right).

Proof.

Recall that
\pi(\xi,\eta)=\pi_{1}(\xi,\eta,0)
where
\pi_{\lambda}
are the Schrödinger representations of
\mathbb{H}^{2n}
realised on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
It is enough to show that

(3.5)

\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle=\langle f,\pi(\eta^{\prime},-\xi^{\prime})g\rangle,\,\,\,(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})=\Lambda(\xi,\eta)

where the matrix
\Lambda
is defined by

(3.6)

\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}I_{2n}&\tanh(\lambda/2)\mathbf{J}\\
(\lambda/2)\mathbf{J}&-(\lambda/2)\coth(\lambda/2)I_{2n}\end{pmatrix}.

In fact, once we have the above identity it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|F\circ\Lambda(\xi,\eta)|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta

where
F(\xi,\eta)=\langle f,\pi(\eta,-\xi)g\rangle.
Since
\mathbf{J}^{2}=-I,
an easy calculation shows that the determinant of
\Lambda
is
(-\lambda/2)^{2n}\big(\coth(\lambda/2)-\tanh(\lambda/2)\big)^{2n}=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sinh\lambda}\right)^{2n}
and hence
\Lambda
is invertible. Therefore,

(3.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta=|\det\Lambda|^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|F(\xi,\eta)|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta.

But it is well known that the representation
\pi_{1}
of
\mathbb{H}^{2n}
is square integrable modulo the center and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|F(\xi,\eta)|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta=(2\pi)^{2n}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|f(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|g(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\right).

Thus we are left with proving the identity (
3.5
).
With
\xi=(x,u)
and
\eta=(y,v)
let us compute the matrix coefficient

\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle=e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\xi\cdot\eta}\langle f,e_{\lambda}(\eta)\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)g\rangle.

Recalling the definitions of
\tau_{\lambda}(x,u)
and
e_{\lambda}(y,v)
after a simple calculation we get

e_{\lambda}(\eta)(\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)g)(a,b)=g(a-x^{\prime},b-u^{\prime})\,e^{i(a\cdot y^{\prime}+b\cdot v^{\prime})}\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}\tanh(\lambda/2)(\xi\cdot\eta)}.

Here we have defined the new coordinates by
(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})=\big(x+\tanh(\lambda/2)\,v,u-\tanh(\lambda/2)\,y\big)
and

(y^{\prime},v^{\prime})=-(\lambda/2)\big(-u+\coth(\lambda/2)\,y,x+\coth(\lambda/2)\,v\big).
If we write
\xi^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},u^{\prime}),\eta^{\prime}=(y^{\prime},v^{\prime}),
then we see that
(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})=\Lambda(\xi,\eta)
where the matrix
\Lambda
is as in the proposition.
We can now rewrite the above expression for
e_{\lambda}(\eta)\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)g
in terms of ordinary translations and modulations and hence in terms of the representation
\pi(\xi,\eta).
By making use of the relation

2\coth\lambda=\coth(\lambda/2)+\tanh(\lambda/2)
and simplifying we get

\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g(a,b)=\pi(\eta^{\prime},-\xi^{\prime})g(a,b)

and hence
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle=\langle f,\pi(\eta^{\prime},-\xi^{\prime})g\rangle=F(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
proving the identity (
3.5
).
∎

3.2.
Twisted modulation spaces

Let
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
stand for the space of all measurable functions
F(\xi,\eta)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for which

\|F\|_{p,q}^{q}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|F(\xi,\eta)|^{p}\,d\xi\right)^{q/p}d\eta

is finite.
We say that a tempered distribution
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
belongs to the twisted modulation space
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
if the matrix coefficient

\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle

belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for some Schwartz class function
g.
We equip
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
with the norm

\|f\|_{(p,q)}^{q}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle|^{p}\,d\xi\right)^{q/p}d\eta.

In view of (
3.5
) we immediately see that
M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})=M^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for any
\lambda.
But for
p\neq q,M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
could be different from
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

It follows from the definition of
\Pi_{\lambda}
that for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
we have

\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle=\langle B_{\lambda}f,\rho_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)B_{\lambda}g\rangle

and hence
f\in M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
if and only if
\langle B_{\lambda}f,\rho_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)B_{\lambda}g\rangle
belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for some Schwartz class function
g.
This relation is very useful in studying certain properties of the twisted modulation spaces.

Theorem 3.3
.

The definition of
M^{p,q}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is independent of
g.
That is to say if
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle
belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for some
g
then
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g^{\prime}\rangle
also belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for any other
g^{\prime}.
Different windows
g
define equivalent norms.

To prove this result, we need some preparation. Let us define
V_{g}^{\lambda}f(\xi,\eta)=\langle f,e_{\lambda}(\eta)\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)g\rangle
which is the twisted analogue of the short-time Fourier transform used in the classical theory of modulation spaces. In view of (
3.1
) it follows that
f\in M^{p,q}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
if and only if
V_{g}^{\lambda}f\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Moreover, the identity in Proposition
3.2
holds for
V_{g}^{\lambda}f
which after polarization gives

(3.8)

c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\langle V_{g}^{\lambda}f,V_{g^{\prime}}^{\lambda}h\rangle=d_{n}\,\langle f,h\rangle\langle g^{\prime},g\rangle.

We use the above identity to obtain an inversion formula for
V_{g}^{\lambda}
. Towards this end, we define the following operator on
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
which takes a function
F
into the functional

\langle\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}F,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}F(\xi,\eta)\,\langle e_{\lambda}(\eta)\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)g,\varphi\rangle\,d\xi\,d\eta,

for
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. In the following lemma we record some important properties of
\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
and
g,h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. Then

V_{h}^{\lambda}\circ\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}
maps
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
into itself and the following inequality holds:

(3.9)

\|V_{h}^{\lambda}\circ\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}F\|_{p,q}\leq\|F\|_{p,q}\|V_{g}^{\lambda}h\|_{1,1}.

In particular, when
F=V_{g}^{\lambda}f,
we have the inversion formula

(3.10)

c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,\widetilde{V_{h}^{\lambda}}(V_{g}^{\lambda}f)=d_{n}\,\langle h,g\rangle\,f.

Proof.

Since
V_{g}^{\lambda}h
can be written in terms of the classical short-time Fourier transform of
g
and
h
it is clear that
V_{g}^{\lambda}h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Note that
|V_{h}^{\lambda}g(\xi,\eta)|=|\langle g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle|
and hence it is enough to consider

\langle\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}F,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}F(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\,\langle e_{\lambda}(\eta^{\prime})\tau_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime})g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle\,d\xi^{\prime}\,d\eta^{\prime}.

Using (
3.1
) and the fact that
\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)^{\ast}=\Pi_{\lambda}(-\xi,-\eta)
we can rewrite the above as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}F(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\xi^{\prime}\cdot\eta^{\prime})}\,\langle g,\Pi_{\lambda}(-\xi^{\prime},-\eta^{\prime})\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle\,d\xi^{\prime}\,d\eta^{\prime}.

As
\Pi_{\lambda}
is a projective representation of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}
from the above we obtain

(3.11)

|V_{h}^{\lambda}\circ\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}F(\xi,\eta)|\leq|F|\ast|V_{g}^{\lambda}h|(\xi,\eta).

This yields (
3.9
) by Young’s inequality for mixed norm spaces. In order to prove (
3.10
) it is enough to show that

c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,\langle\widetilde{V_{h}^{\lambda}}(V_{g}^{\lambda}f),\varphi\rangle=d_{n}\,\langle h,g\rangle\,\langle f,\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
But this is certainly true for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
as a consequence of (
3.8
). Thus for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
we have the inversion formula

d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}^{-2}\,\langle g,h\rangle\,\varphi=\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}(V_{h}^{\lambda}\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}V_{h}^{\lambda}\varphi(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\,e_{\lambda}(\eta^{\prime})\tau_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime})g\,d\xi^{\prime}\,d\eta^{\prime}.

From the above by taking inner product with
f
and simplifying we get

d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}^{-2}\,\langle h,g\rangle\,\langle f,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}V_{g}^{\lambda}f(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\,\langle e_{\lambda}(\eta^{\prime})\tau_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime})h,\varphi\rangle\,d\xi^{\prime}\,d\eta^{\prime}.

The change of order of integration is justified as
g,h
and
\varphi
are all Schwartz. As the right hand side of the above is nothing but
\langle\widetilde{V_{h}^{\lambda}}(V_{g}^{\lambda}f),\varphi\rangle
(
3.10
) is proved.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
:
Assume that
F=V_{g}^{\lambda}f\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and take any Schwartz function
g^{\prime}.
By (
3.10
) we have
d_{n}\,\|g\|_{2}^{2}\,\,f=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,\widetilde{V_{g}^{\lambda}}(V_{g}^{\lambda}f)
and hence using (
3.9
) we get

d_{n}\,\|g\|_{2}^{2}\,\|V_{g^{\prime}}^{\lambda}f\|_{p,q}\leq c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\|V_{g}^{\lambda}f\|_{p,q}\|V_{g}^{\lambda}g^{\prime}\|_{1,1}.

This proves that
V_{g^{\prime}}^{\lambda}f\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and hence the definition of
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
independent of
g.
Moreover, it is clear that
\|V_{g^{\prime}}^{\lambda}f\|_{p,q}
is equivalent to
\|V_{g}^{\lambda}f\|_{p,q}.

An interesting consequence of Theorem
3.3
is the following result which is a very useful alternate definition of
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Let
w_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)
which appears in the definition of
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n}).

Corollary 3.5
.

A function
f\in M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
if and only if the function
F
defined by

F(\xi,\eta)=\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)}\,\,B_{\lambda}f(\xi+i\eta)
belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

Proof.

In view of the above theorem,
f\in M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
if and only if
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle
belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
As
B_{\lambda}
is unitary,
B_{\lambda}\,p_{1/2}^{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)\circ B_{\lambda},\,
we have
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle=c_{\lambda}\,\langle B_{\lambda}f,\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)1\rangle
where
\zeta=\xi+i\eta.
From the definition of
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)
given in (
2.8
) we see that
\langle B_{\lambda}f,\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)1\rangle
is given by the integral

e^{-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\coth\lambda)|\zeta|^{2}}\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}[\operatorname{Re}\zeta,\,\operatorname{Im}\zeta]}\,\,\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}B_{\lambda}f(\zeta^{\prime})e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})}\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}\,[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]}\,w_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})\,d\zeta^{\prime}.

Recalling the expression for the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
given in (
2.6
), namely

d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}\,\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})}\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}\,[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]}=\,\overline{K_{\zeta}(\zeta^{\prime}}),

the above integral is seen to be just
B_{\lambda}f(\zeta).
Therefore,

d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}\,\,\langle B_{\lambda}f,\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)1\rangle=\,B_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\coth\lambda)|\zeta|^{2}}\,e^{\frac{\lambda}{2}[\operatorname{Re}\zeta,\,\operatorname{Im}\zeta]}.

Recalling the definition of the weight function
w_{\lambda}
we can rewrite the above as

d_{n}\,\,c_{\lambda}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,\langle B_{\lambda}f,\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)1\rangle=B_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}.

This proves the corollary.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We record the following relation established above for future use:

(3.12)

d_{n}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle=B_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}.

From the above relation we immediately obtain the identity

c_{\lambda}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|f(x,u)|^{2}dx\,du

which plays an important role in the definition of modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group.

In the following theorem we list some basic properties of the twisted modulation spaces.

Theorem 3.7
.

(i) The space
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is a Banach space for any
1\leq p,q\leq\infty.
(ii) When
p
and
q
are finite the Schwartz space is dense in
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and the dual is given by
M_{\lambda}^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
(iii) The inclusion

M_{\lambda}^{p_{1},q_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\subseteq M_{\lambda}^{p_{2},q_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

holds whenever
1\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\infty
,
1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\infty
.

Proof.

The proofs are very similar to the classical case. For example, to see the completeness of
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
let
(f_{k})
be a Cauchy sequence. Then by definition,
F_{k}=V_{g}^{\lambda}f_{k}
is Cauchy in
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
where
g
is a fixed Schwartz function
with
\|g\|_{2}=1.
By the completeness of
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}),
we get
F\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
to which the sequence
(F_{k})
converges. In view of (
3.10
) we have
c_{\lambda}\,f_{k}=\widetilde{V}_{g}^{\lambda}F_{k}
and by defining
c_{\lambda}f=\widetilde{V}_{g}^{\lambda}F
we see that

c_{\lambda}\,\|V_{g}^{\lambda}(f_{k}-f)\|_{p,q}=c_{\lambda}\,\|V_{g}^{\lambda}\circ\widetilde{V}_{g}^{\lambda}(F_{k}-F)\|_{p,q}\leq C\,\|F_{k}-F\|_{p,q}

in view of the estimate (
3.9
). This proves that
(f_{k})
converges to
f
in
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
The duality is proved as in the classical case. We refer to
[
13
]
for the details. To prove the density of the Schwartz space, we require the easy to prove fact that
\widetilde{V}_{g}^{\lambda}F
is Schwartz whenever
F
is compactly supported or rapidly decreasing. In order to see the inclusion, take
f\in M^{p_{1},q_{1}}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. Then using the inversion formula (
3.10
) and the estimate (
3.11
), we get

|V_{g}^{\lambda}f(\xi,\eta)|\leq c_{n}\,c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\langle g,g\rangle^{-1}|V_{g}^{\lambda}f|\ast|V_{g}^{\lambda}g|(\xi,\eta).

Now choose
1\leq r,s\leq\infty
such that

1/p_{1}+1/r=1+1/p_{2},~~\text{ and }1/q_{1}+1/s=1+1/q_{2}.

By Young’s inequality for mixed Lebesgue spaces we obtain

(3.13)

\|V_{g}^{\lambda}f\|_{p_{2},q_{2}}\leq c_{n}\,c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\langle g,g\rangle^{-1}\|V_{g}^{\lambda}f\|_{p_{1},q_{1}}\|V_{g}^{\lambda}g\|_{r,s}.

Since
g
is a Schwartz function,
V_{g}^{\lambda}g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and hence the desired inclusion follows from the above estimate.
∎

In the classical setting, the spaces
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are invariant under translations and modulations. Moreover, when
p=q
they are also invariant under Fourier transform. In the twisted setting we have the following invariance properties.

Theorem 3.8
.

The spaces
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
are invariant under
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)
and
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
for all
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
When
p=q
they are invariant under
U_{\lambda}
defined in (
2.7
).

Proof.

The invariance under
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)
and
e_{\lambda}(\eta)
follows from the definition. From the definition of
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)
we easily calculate that
U\circ\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)\circ U^{\ast}=\rho_{\lambda}(i\zeta)
from which follows

U\circ B_{\lambda}\circ\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)\circ B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ U^{\ast}=\rho_{\lambda}(i\zeta).

The property
U\circ B_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}\circ U_{\lambda}
translates the above into

U_{\lambda}\circ\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)\circ U_{\lambda}^{\ast}=B_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\rho_{\lambda}(i\zeta)\circ B_{\lambda}=\Pi_{\lambda}(-\eta,\xi).

If
f\in M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
so that
\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle\in L^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
then the equation

\langle f,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g\rangle=\langle f,U_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\Pi_{\lambda}(-\eta,\xi)\circ U_{\lambda}g\rangle=\langle U_{\lambda}f,\Pi_{\lambda}(-\eta,\xi)U_{\lambda}g\rangle

shows that
U_{\lambda}f\in M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
proving the invariance of
M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
under
U_{\lambda}.

∎

Remark 3.9
.

It is well known that the modulation space
M^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})=M^{1,1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is a Banach algebra under pointwise multiplication. As
M^{1,1}
is invariant under the Fourier transform,
M^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is a Banach algebra under convolution as well. We can also show that
M^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is a Banach algebra under
\lambda
-twisted convolution. To see this, recall (
2.2
) which reads as

f\ast_{\lambda}g(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(\eta)\,\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)g(\xi)\,d\eta.

Since
M^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is invariant under
\tau_{\lambda}(\eta)
by Minkowski’s integral inequality we have

\|f\ast_{\lambda}g\|_{(1,1)}\leq\|f\|_{1}\,\|g\|_{(1,1)}\leq\|f\|_{(1,1)}\,\|g\|_{(1,1)}.

In the last inequality we have used the continuous the inclusion
M^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

The same property holds for
M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
as long as
1\leq p\leq 2
as show below. The proof makes use of the curious property enjoyed by the twisted convolution, namely

(3.14)

\|f\ast_{\lambda}g\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}\,\|g\|_{p}\,

valid for
1\leq p\leq 2.
The above is clearly true for
p=1
and for
p=2
it is a consequence of the fact that Weyl transform is an isometry from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
onto the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators. For
1\leq p\leq 2
it is proved by interpolation, see Proposition 3.4 in
[
17
]
and Theorem 1.1 in
[
19
]
for details.

Theorem 3.10
.

For any
1\leq p\leq 2
the space
M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})=M^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is a Banach algebra under twisted convolution.

Proof.

Assuming that
f,g\in M_{\lambda}^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}),
with
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
consider

\langle f\ast_{\lambda}g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(\xi^{\prime})\,\langle\tau_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime})g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle\,d\xi^{\prime}.

Since
\tau_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime})=\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime},0)
a simple calculation using (
1.4
) and the fact that
\Pi_{\lambda}
is a representation of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}})
gives us

\tau_{\lambda}(-\xi^{\prime})\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi-\xi^{\prime},\eta)\,e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\xi^{\prime}\cdot\eta}\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}[\xi,\xi^{\prime}]}.

Therefore, by defining
f_{\eta}(\xi^{\prime})=f(\xi^{\prime})\,e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\xi^{\prime}\cdot\eta}
and
G_{\eta}(\xi^{\prime})=\langle g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi^{\prime},\eta)h\rangle\
we can write

\langle f\ast_{\lambda}g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)h\rangle=f_{\eta}\ast_{\lambda}G_{\eta}(\xi).

We now make use of (
3.14
) to conclude that

\|\langle f\ast_{\lambda}g,\Pi_{\lambda}(\cdot,\eta)h\rangle\|_{p}\leq\|f_{\eta}\|_{p}\,\|G_{\eta}\|_{p}.

Since
\|f_{\eta}\|_{p}=\|f\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{(p,p)}
integrating the
p
-th power of the above with respect to
\eta
we complete the proof.
∎

4.
Modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group

4.1.
Translations and modulations on the Heisenberg group

The family of unitary operators
\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)

satisfy the composition formula

\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})=\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime})e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}((\coth\lambda)\,\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})-\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}])}.

This suggests that we introduce a group structure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n+1}={\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times{\mathbb{C}}
by the rule

(\zeta,s)(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})=(\zeta+\zeta^{\prime},s+s^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\overline{\zeta^{\prime}}]+\frac{i}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\overline{\zeta^{\prime}})).

We denote this group by
G_{n}
and note that
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
and
i\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
are subgroups of
G_{n}
each isomorphic to
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
If we let

\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)=e^{i\lambda(\operatorname{Re}s-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}s)}\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta),

we easily verify that
\Pi_{\lambda}(g)\Pi_{\lambda}(g^{\prime})=\Pi_{\lambda}(gg^{\prime})
for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G_{n}.
Thus we have a family of unitary representations of
G_{n}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
These are also irreducible as the family
\rho_{\lambda}(\zeta)
is irreducible as shown in
[
12
]
. The operators
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)
initially defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
can be lifted to unitary operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
when
s
is real. We simply define

(4.1)

\Pi(\zeta,s)f(x,u,t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\,\,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)f^{\lambda}(x,u)\,d\lambda.\\

As
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)
acts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
as unitary operators, it is clear that
\Pi(\zeta,s)
is an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
An easy calculation shows that
\Pi(\zeta,s)^{\ast}=\Pi(-\zeta,-s)

and hence
\Pi(\zeta,s)
is in fact unitary. Moreover, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
it follows that

\Pi(\xi,s)f(\eta,t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\,e^{i\lambda s}\,\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)f^{\lambda}(\eta)\,d\lambda=\tau(\xi,s)f(\eta,t)

where
\tau(g)f(h)=f(g^{-1}h),\,g,h\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the left translation on the Heisenberg group. The family of operators
U_{\lambda}
also gives rise to the operator

\widetilde{U}f(\xi,t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\,\,U_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\xi)\,d\lambda

which is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
For
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
let us define

e(\eta,s)f(\xi,t)=\Pi(i\eta,s)f(\xi,t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\,e^{i\lambda s}\,e_{\lambda}(\eta)f^{\lambda}(\xi)\,d\lambda.

As verified in (
3.2
),
U_{\lambda}\circ\tau_{\lambda}(a,b)\circ U_{\lambda}^{\ast}=e_{\lambda}(a,b),
and hence it follows that
\widetilde{U}\circ\tau(g)\circ\widetilde{U}^{\ast}=e(g)
for any
g\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Thus we may consider
e(g)
as modulation operators on the Heisenberg group.

Remark 4.1
.

When
f
is a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
it is clear that
\tau(g)f
is also Schwartz for any
g\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.
We are interested in knowing if the same thing is true for
e(g)f.
In view of the relation

\widetilde{U}\circ\tau(g)\circ\widetilde{U}^{\ast}=e(g),
this property holds if
\widetilde{U}
leaves the Schwartz class invariant. In the following proposition we show that this is indeed true.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any Schwartz function
f
on
\mathbb{H}^{n},\,\widetilde{U}f
is also Schwartz.

Proof.

Recalling the definition of
\widetilde{U},
and
U_{\lambda}
we are required to show that

U_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\xi)=c(\lambda)^{n}\,\mathcal{F}_{2n}f^{\lambda}(c(\lambda)\xi)

is a Schwartz function of
(\xi,\lambda)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}.
Here
\mathcal{F}_{2n}
is the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
It is therefore enough to consider
F(\xi,\lambda)=c(\lambda)^{n}\,g(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda)
when
g
is Schwartz. We will show that for any multi-indices
\alpha
and
\beta
and non-negative integers
j
and
p
the functions
F_{\alpha,\beta,j,p}(\xi,\lambda)=\lambda^{p}\partial_{\lambda}^{j}\,\xi^{\beta}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}F(\xi,\lambda)
are bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}.
Note that

\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}g(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda)=c(\lambda)^{|\alpha|}\,\,(\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}g)(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda),

\partial_{\lambda}\,g(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda)=(\partial_{\lambda}g)(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda)+\frac{d}{d\lambda}c(\lambda)\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\xi_{j}(\partial_{\xi_{j}}g)(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda)

and therefore a moment’s thought reveals that
\partial_{\lambda}^{j}\,\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}F(\xi,\lambda)
is a finite linear combination of terms of the form

c(\lambda)^{l}\,\frac{d^{m}}{d\lambda^{m}}c(\lambda)\,\xi^{\mu}\,(\partial_{\xi}^{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}g)(c(\lambda)\xi,\lambda).

The boundedness of
F_{\alpha,\beta,j,p}(\xi,\lambda)
follows immediately once we have the following estimates on
c(\lambda)
and its derivatives.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and a non-negative integer
m
we have
|c(\lambda)|\geq c_{1}
and

|\frac{d^{m}}{d\lambda^{m}}c(\lambda)|\leq c_{2}(1+|\lambda|)^{m+1}.

Proof.

As
c(\lambda)=\lambda\coth\lambda
it is clear that the lemma is true for
m=0.
By defining
\varphi(\lambda)=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sinh\lambda}\right)
and
\psi(\lambda)=\varphi(\lambda)^{-1}
we note that

\psi^{(m)}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}t^{m}\,e^{\lambda t}dt

which shows that
\psi^{(m)}(\lambda)
grows like
\cosh\lambda.
From the equation
c(\lambda)\psi(\lambda)=\cosh\lambda
we get

c^{(m)}(\lambda)\,\psi(\lambda)=\cosh\lambda-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{m!}{j!(m-j)!}\,c^{(j)}(\lambda)\,\psi^{(m-j)}(\lambda)

when
m
is even. (For odd
m
the above formula holds after changing
\cosh\lambda
into
\sinh\lambda
on the right.) By induction we get the estimates on the derivatives of
c(\lambda).
The lower bound for
c(\lambda)
follows from
c(\lambda)^{-1}=\psi(\lambda)(\cosh\lambda)^{-1}.

∎

4.2.
Matrix coefficients associated to the representation
\Pi

Recall that the modulation spaces
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are defined in terms of the matrix coefficients
\langle f,\pi_{1}(x,y,t)g\rangle
associated to the Schrödinger representation of the Heiseberg group. In particular, the identification
M^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a consequence of the fact that
\langle f,\pi_{1}(x,y,t)g\rangle
is square integrable modulo the centre of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and the identity

(4.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|\langle f,\pi_{1}(x,y,t)g\rangle|^{2}\,dx\,dy\,=c_{n}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|g(x)|^{2}\,dx\right).

We now consider the matrix coefficients
\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)g\rangle
of the representation
\Pi
of
G_{n}
where
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and see if an identity of the above form is true. For
\zeta=\xi+i\eta
and
s\in\mathbb{R},

\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)g\rangle=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}\,\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

By Plancherel theorem for the Fourier transform in the
s
variable we get

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)g\rangle|^{2}\,d\zeta\,ds=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4n}}|\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g^{\lambda}\rangle|^{2}\,d\xi\,d\eta\right)d\lambda.

The inner integral has been already calculated in Proposition
3.2
. Using that we obtain

(4.3)

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)g\rangle|^{2}\,d\zeta\,ds=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sinh\lambda}{\lambda}\right)^{2n}\|f^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|g^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}\,d\lambda.

Thus we see that the representation
\Pi
of
G_{n}
fails to be square integrable.

Since the modulation spaces
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are also defined in terms of the Bargmann transform which corresponds to
\langle f,\pi_{1}(x,y,t)q_{1/2}\rangle
where
q_{t}
is the euclidean heat kernel, let us try to see the behaviour of
\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle
where
p_{t}
is the heat kernel associated to the sublaplacian on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Recall the relation (
3.12
), namely

d_{n}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle=B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}

established in the course of the proof of Corollary
3.5
. We also have

(4.4)

\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}\,\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

Let
T
stand for the Fourier multiplier operator corresponding to
\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}
acting on
f
in the central variable. Then we have

(4.5)

\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}\,B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\,\,d\lambda.

As
B_{\lambda}
is an isometry we obtain the identity

(4.6)

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle|^{2}\,d\zeta\,ds=c_{n}\,\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|f(h)|^{2}\,dh.

Note that
\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle=T\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,\cdot)p_{1/2}\rangle(s)
and hence the modified matrix coefficients
T\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,\cdot)p_{1/2}\rangle(s)
are square integrable over
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}.

Something interesting happens if we replace the heat kernel
p_{t}
associated to the sublaplacian by the heat kernel
h_{t}
for the full Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}}
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Since
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+\mathcal{L},
it follows that
h_{t}^{\lambda}(x,u)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}}\,p_{t}^{\lambda}(x,u)
and hence

(4.7)

\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)h_{1/2}\rangle=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}}\,B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\,\,d\lambda.

From the above expression it is clear that the function
s\rightarrow\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)h_{1/2}\rangle
has a holomorphic extension to the whole of
{\mathbb{C}}.
By defining

(4.8)

B_{\mathbb{H}}f(\zeta,s)=e^{\frac{1}{4}s^{2}}\,\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)h_{1/2}\rangle

we observe that
B_{\mathbb{H}}f(\zeta,s)
is nothing but the classical Bargmann transform of the function
\lambda\rightarrow B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}
evaluated at
-is.

Consequently, we have the identity

(4.9)

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}}|B_{\mathbb{H}}f(\zeta,s)|^{2}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}|s|^{2}}\,ds=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|^{2}\,w_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,d\lambda.

Since the image of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
under the Bargmann transform is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space we also have the pointwise estimate

(4.10)

|B_{\mathbb{H}}f(\zeta,s)|^{2}\leq c_{n}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}|s|^{2}}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|^{2}\,w_{\lambda}(\zeta)\,d\lambda.

In view of (
4.5
) the above gives the following estimate valid for
s\in\mathbb{R}
:

(4.11)

|B_{\mathbb{H}}f(\zeta,s)|^{2}\leq c_{n}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}|s|^{2}}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle|^{2}\,ds.

This inequality and the identity (
4.6
) motivate our definition of modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group.

We would like to extend the definition of the matrix coefficients
\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)g\rangle
when
f
is a tempered distribution and
g
a Schwartz class function. This can be done once we know that
\Pi(\zeta,s)g
is Schwartz whenever
g
is.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and
(\zeta,s)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R},
the function
\Pi(\zeta,s)g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Proof.

We have already noted in Remark
4.1
that both
\Pi(\xi,s)
and
\Pi(i\eta,s)
for
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
preserve the Schwartz class as they are translations and modulations. In view of the definition (
4.1
) and the relation (
3.1
) we have

\Pi(\zeta,s)g(x,u,t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\,e^{i\lambda s}\,e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,\xi\cdot\eta}\,e_{\lambda}(\eta)\,\tau_{\lambda}(\xi)\,g^{\lambda}(x,u)\,d\lambda.

It is therefore enough to show that the function

g_{0}(x,u,t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}\,e^{i\lambda s}\,e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\,\xi\cdot\eta}\,g^{\lambda}(x,u)\,d\lambda

is Schwartz. But this is immediate in view of the properties of
c(\lambda)=\lambda/2\,\coth\lambda/2
proved in Lemma
4.3
. Note that it is important to assume
s
is real.
∎

4.3.
Modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group

We look for Banach spaces of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which are invariant under translations
\tau(h)
and modulations
e(h)
for all
h\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
or equivalently invariant under the operators
\Pi(\zeta,s),\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n},s\in\mathbb{R}.
We look for a definition of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
which reduces to
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
when
p=q=2.
We also like to have the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
contained in
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

We therefore, begin by investigating the behaviour of
\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle
when
f
is a Schwartz class function.

Let us try to get some pointwise estimates for
B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)
when
f
is Schwartz. Since the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})
is given by (
2.6
) it follows that

|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|^{2}\leq\|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}\|_{\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}({\mathbb{C}}^{2n})}^{2}\,K_{\zeta}(\zeta)=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|f^{\lambda}(x,u)|^{2}\,dx\,du\right)w_{\lambda}^{-1}.

Therefore, we get the pointwise estimate

|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\leq c_{\lambda}\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}|f^{\lambda}(x,u)|^{2}\,dx\,du\right)^{1/2}\leq c_{\lambda}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{2}\,dt.

Defining
f_{m}(x,u,t)=(1-\partial_{t}^{2})^{m}f(x,u,t)
and noting that
(1+\lambda^{2})^{m}\,f^{\lambda}(x,u)=f_{m}^{\lambda}(x,u)
we can improve the above estimate as

(4.12)

|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\leq c_{\lambda}\,(1+\lambda^{2})^{-m}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|f_{m}(\cdot,t)\|_{2}\,dt.

We would also like to get an estimate of
B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}
involving decay in the
\zeta
variable. Such an estimate has been proved in
[
18
]
when
\lambda=1.
As we need an estimate involving both
\zeta
and
\lambda
variables, we have to keep track of the constants that depend on
\lambda.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
a=\lambda/2\,\coth(\lambda/2)
and
b=i\lambda/2
and define
A_{j}=\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-ax_{j}\big)
and
B_{j}=\big(-\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}+au_{j}\big)
for
j=1,2,...,n.
Then we have the following relations:

-(a^{2}+b^{2})z_{j}\,B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=B_{\lambda}\big(aA_{j}-bB_{j}\big)f^{\lambda}(z,w),

(a^{2}+b^{2})w_{j}\,B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=B_{\lambda}\big(bA_{j}+aB_{j}\big)f^{\lambda}(z,w).

Proof.

By setting
e^{-\frac{1}{2}L_{\lambda}}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}p_{1/2}^{\lambda}(z,w)
we have

e^{-\frac{1}{2}L_{\lambda}}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}^{\prime}}f^{\lambda})(z,w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}^{\prime}}f^{\lambda}(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})\,p_{1/2}^{\lambda}(z-x^{\prime},w-u^{\prime})\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}(x^{\prime}\cdot w-u^{\prime}\cdot z)}\,dx^{\prime}\,du^{\prime}.

Integrating by parts and collecting the terms we get

(-az_{j}+bw_{j})e^{-\frac{1}{2}L_{\lambda}}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}L_{\lambda}}A_{j}f^{\lambda}(z,w).

In a similar way we can also prove the identity

(bz_{j}+aw_{j})e^{-\frac{1}{2}L_{\lambda}}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}L_{\lambda}}B_{j}f^{\lambda}(z,w).

Now the lemma follows from these two identities.
∎

From the lemma we infer that
z_{j}B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=B_{\lambda}(P_{j,\lambda}f^{\lambda})(z,w)
and
w_{j}B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(z,w)=B_{\lambda}(Q_{j,\lambda}f^{\lambda})(z,w)
where
P_{j,\lambda}
and
Q_{j,\lambda}
are first order differential operators with monomial coefficients. The constants occurring in these differential operators are one of the following:
a(a^{2}+b^{2})^{-1},\,a^{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})^{-1},\,b(a^{2}+b^{2})^{-1}
and
ab(a^{2}+b^{2})^{-1}.
By inspection we can convince ourselves that these constants are bounded by
(1+|\lambda|)\,e^{|\lambda|}.
More generally, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2n}
with
|\alpha|=N
we have

\zeta^{\alpha}\,B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)=B_{\lambda}(Pf^{\lambda})(\zeta),\,\,\,P=\sum_{|\mu|+|\nu|=N}c_{\mu,\nu}(\lambda)\,(x,u)^{\mu}\partial_{(x,u)}^{\nu}

where the constants are bounded by
(1+|\lambda|)^{n}\,e^{N|\lambda|}.
As before this gives the estimate

(4.13)

|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\leq Cc_{\lambda}^{2}\,(1+\lambda^{2})^{N/2}\,e^{N|\lambda|}\,(1+|\zeta|)^{-N}\sum_{|\mu|+|\nu|=N}\|(x,u)^{\mu}\partial_{(x,u)}^{\nu}f^{\lambda}\|_{2}.

Once again as
f
is Schwartz, the last term in the above inequality can be estimated independent of
\lambda.
Combining (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) we get

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
f
be a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Then for any
m,N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\leq C_{m,N}(f)\,c_{\lambda}\,(1+\lambda^{2})^{-m}\,e^{N|\lambda|}\,(1+|\zeta|)^{-N}

Remark 4.7
.

The decay in the
\zeta
variable seems to be optimal. But we do not know if the exponential growth in
\lambda
can be improved as it is a result of the behaviour of the coefficients
a
and
b
in the previous lemma.

We have two candidates for the modulation spaces on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
The most natural definition is the one where the Schrödinger representation
\pi_{1}
of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is replaced by the representation
\Pi
of the group
G_{n}.
Thus we may define
M_{0}^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
as the space of all tempered distributions
f
such that
B_{\mathbb{H}}f(\zeta,0)=\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,0)h_{1/2}\rangle,\zeta=\xi+i\eta
belongs to the mixed norms space
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

In view of (
4.10
) it follows that
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\subset M_{0}^{2,2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
However, it is not clear if
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})=M_{0}^{2,2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

The identity (
4.6
) suggests that we can also define the modulation spaces in terms of the modified matrix coefficients
\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)h_{1/2}\rangle
and their
L^{2}
norms with respect to the
s
variable.

Let
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
stand for the space of functions
F(\xi,\eta,\cdot)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}
taking values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
equipped with the obvious norm. We say that a tempered distribution
f
belongs to
M_{1}^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
if the function
\langle Tf,\Pi(\xi+i\eta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle
belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n},L^{2}(\mathbb{R})).
In view of (
4.5
) this is the same as saying that the function

(4.14)

\|\langle Tf,\Pi(\xi+i\eta,\cdot)p_{1/2}\rangle\|_{2}=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)|^{2}\,w_{\lambda}(\xi+i\eta)\,d\lambda\right)^{1/2}

belongs to
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
With this definition it is clear that
M_{1}^{2,2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})=L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
Moreover, we also have the inclusion
M_{1}^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\subset M_{0}^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
which is a consequence of (
4.11
). However, we are not able to prove the completeness of
M_{1}^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

We get another definition by interchanging the roles of the mixed norms in
(\xi,\eta)
and the
L^{2}
norm in
\lambda.
This amounts to requiring that the
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
norm of
B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}
is square integrable with respect to
\lambda.
Note that this does not alter the
p=q=2
case but seems to be a reasonable definition as we plan to demonstrate now.

In order to make the definition
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),
let examine the modified matrix coefficients more closely. By polarising the identity (
4.6
) and using the fact that
\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle=\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle
we get

(4.15)

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle\,\langle\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2},g\rangle\,d\zeta\,ds=c_{n}^{\prime\prime}\,\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f(h)\,\overline{g(h)}\,dh.

The above identity gives us the following inversion formula:

(4.16)

f(h)=c_{n}\,\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle\,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}(h)\,d\zeta\,ds.

Let us set up some notations. Let
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}=L^{p,q,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R})
be the mixed norm space equipped with the norm
\|F\|_{p,q,2}.
For a function or a tempered distribution
F(\xi,\eta,s)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
let
F^{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)
stand for its inverse Fourier transform in the last variable. We then define

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}=\{F\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}):F^{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)\in\mathcal{H}_{p,q}\}

equipped with the norm
\|F\|_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}}=\|F^{\lambda}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{p,q}}=\|F^{\lambda}\|_{p,q,2}.

Remark 4.8
.

A word of caution about the notation. When
f
is a tempered distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{m+1},
the partial inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{m+1}f
is again a tempered distribution. When this distribution is given by a function, we write
f^{\lambda}(x)
instead of
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{m+1}f.

We need the completeness of the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
proved in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Both
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
are Banach spaces. (The norm of
F\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
is the norm of
F^{\lambda}
in
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}.
)

Proof.

The completeness of
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}
is immediate since it is a mixed norm space, see
[
5
]
. To prove the completeness of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q},
let
F_{k}
be Cauchy. Then by definition
F_{k}^{\lambda}
is Cauchy in
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}.
Hence
F_{k}^{\lambda}
converges to some
F\in\mathcal{H}_{p,q}.
It is enough to show that there exists
G\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
such that
F(\zeta,\lambda)=G^{\lambda}(\zeta).
In order to show this we make use of the following well known fact.

Let
\Phi_{\alpha},\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
be the family of Hermite functions on
\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Then every tempered distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
has a formal expansion

F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}}\langle F,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle\,\Phi_{\alpha}

in the sense that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
the action of
F
on
\varphi
is given by the absolutely convergent series

(F,\varphi)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}}\langle F,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle\,\,\langle\varphi,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle.

This is due to the fact that
F
is tempered if and only if
|\langle F,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle|\leq C\,(1+|\alpha|)^{N}
for some
N
whereas
\varphi
is Schwartz if and only if

|\langle\varphi,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle|\leq C_{j}\,(1+|\alpha|)^{-j}
for every
j.

Since the tempered distribution
F
is given by a function
F(\xi,\eta,\lambda)
we have the expansion

F(\xi,\eta,\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{4n}}\langle F,\Phi_{\alpha}\otimes\Phi_{j}\rangle\,\Phi_{\alpha}(\xi,\eta)\,\Phi_{j}(\lambda).

As
F
is tempered,
\langle F,\Phi_{\alpha}\otimes\Phi_{j}\rangle
have polynomial growth and hence the formal expansion

G(\xi,\eta,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{4n}}(-i)^{j}\,\langle F,\Phi_{\alpha}\otimes\Phi_{j}\rangle\,\Phi_{\alpha}(\xi,\eta)\,\Phi_{j}(t)

defines a tempered distribution. Since
\widehat{\Phi_{j}}(\lambda)=(-i)^{j}\,\Phi_{j}(\lambda)
it is immediate that
G^{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=F(\xi,\eta,\lambda)
proving that
G\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.

∎

For the sake of simplicity of notation, let us set

Vf(\zeta,s)=\langle f,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle.
We also write
V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)
in place of
(Vf)^{\lambda}(\zeta).

We note that
V
takes
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
into
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{2,2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}).
This is a consequence of (
4.5
) and the fact that
B_{\lambda}
takes
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
isometrically into
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
This suggests that we make the following definition of modulation spaces on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.

We say that a tempered distribution
f
belongs to
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
if
Vf(\zeta,s)
belongs to
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
In other words the function
V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\in L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and
the integral

\|f\|_{(p,q)}^{2}=:\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|V_{\lambda}f\|_{p,q}^{2}\,d\lambda=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}\sqrt{w_{\lambda}}\,\|_{p,q}^{2}\,d\lambda<\infty

is finite. We equip
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with the norm
\|f\|_{(p,q)}
defined above.

Remark 4.10
.

Observe that when
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),
the function
B_{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}
belongs to the mixed norm space
L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
for a.e
\lambda.
Hence
f^{\lambda}\in M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and we may be tempted to define
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
by replacing
p_{1/2}
in the definition by any Schwartz class function
g
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Thus we may say that
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
if and only if the inverse Fourier transform of
\langle Tf,\Pi(\zeta,s)g\rangle
in the
s
variable belongs to
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
in such a way that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c_{\lambda}\,\|\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)g^{\lambda}\rangle\|_{p,q}^{2}\,d\lambda<\infty.

This is certainly a better definition if we can show that it is independent of the choice of
g.
Unfortunately, it is not the case- if the above integral is finite for a
g
there is no guarantee that it will be finite for other choices of
g.
Moreover, only with
g=p_{1/2}
we can get the desired identification
M^{2,2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})=L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

The identity (
4.16
) suggests that we define an operator
\widetilde{V}
acting on functions
F
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
as follows:

(4.17)

\widetilde{V}F(h)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\zeta,s)\,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}(h)\,d\zeta\,ds.

It is then clear from (
4.16
) that
\widetilde{V}(Vf)=f.
We will show that
\widetilde{V}F
makes sense for all
F\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
Consider the functional

(4.18)

\langle\widetilde{V}F,g\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\overline{V_{\lambda}g(\zeta)}\,\,d\lambda\,d\zeta

where
g
is a Schwartz class function on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
From (
4.5
) we have

\langle g,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}B_{\lambda}g^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\sqrt{w_{\lambda}(\zeta)}\,d\lambda

and hence we can use the estimate proved in Proposition
4.6
to get the estimate

|\langle\widetilde{V}F,g\rangle|\leq C_{N}(g)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|F^{\lambda}\|_{p,q}\,e^{N|\lambda|}\,d\lambda

where
C_{N}(g)
is a Schwartz seminorm. Therefore, if we assume that
F^{\lambda}
is compactly supported in
\lambda,
then
\widetilde{V}F
is seen to be a tempered distribution. We can take
(
4.18
) as an equivalent definition of
\widetilde{V}F.

Lemma 4.11
.

The operator
V\circ\widetilde{V}
initially defined on the subspace of band limited functions in the
s
variable has an extension to the whole of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
Moreover,
\widetilde{V}(Vf)=f
holds for all
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Proof.

For
F\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
which is band limited in the
s
variable, we consider
(V\circ\widetilde{V})F.
This given by the integral

(4.19)

\langle\widetilde{V}F,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})\,\langle\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})Tp_{1/2},\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle\,d\zeta^{\prime}\,ds^{\prime}.

The above integral has to be interpreted as the action of the tempered distribution
\widetilde{V}F
acting on the Schwartz function
\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}
on
\mathbb{H}^{n},
see Lemma
4.4
.
As
\Pi(\zeta,s)
is defined in terms of
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)
we see that

\langle\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})Tp_{1/2},\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c_{\lambda}\,\,\langle\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

As
\Pi_{\lambda}
is a representation of the group
G_{n}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})^{\ast}=\Pi_{\lambda}(-\zeta^{\prime},-s^{\prime})
the above becomes

\langle\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})Tp_{1/2},\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}((-\zeta^{\prime},-s^{\prime})(\zeta,s))p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

Recalling the group law and the definition of
\Pi_{\lambda}
we see that the above integral becomes

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\lambda(s-s^{\prime})}e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\lambda\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}]-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}))}c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

Thus the inverse Fourier transform of
\langle\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})Tp_{1/2},\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle
in the
s^{\prime}
variable evaluated at
\lambda
is equal to

e^{i\lambda s}\,e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\lambda\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}]-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}))}\,c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle.

Rewriting the integral on the right hand side of (
4.19
) using Plancherel in the
s^{\prime}
variable we can express the function
G(\zeta,s)=\langle\widetilde{V}F,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle
as

(4.20)

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F^{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})\,e^{i\lambda s}\,e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\lambda\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}]-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}))}\,c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle d\zeta^{\prime}\,d\lambda.

By defining
K_{\lambda}(\zeta)=c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle,
the above leads to the inequality

|G^{\lambda}(\zeta)|\leq\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}|F^{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})|\,\,|K_{\lambda}(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})|\,d\zeta^{\prime}\,.

We claim that
K_{\lambda}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}),
uniformly in
\lambda.
Assuming the claim for a moment, by Young’s inequality for mixed norm spaces we get

\|G^{\lambda}\|_{p,q}\leq\|K_{\lambda}\|_{1,1}\,\|F^{\lambda}\|_{p,q}\leq C\,\|F^{\lambda}\|_{p,q}.

Once we have the above, taking
L^{2}
norm in the
\lambda
variable we get the estimate

\|(V\circ\widetilde{V})F\|_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}}\leq C\,\|F\|_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}}.

The important point to note is that the constant
C
in the above inequality is independent of the support of
F^{\lambda}.
Let
\varphi
be a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}
whose Fourier transform is supported on
[-2,2]
and identically 1 on [-1,1]. Defining
\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)=\epsilon^{-1}\varphi(\epsilon^{-1}t)
let

F_{\epsilon}(\zeta,s)=:F\ast_{3}\varphi_{\epsilon}(\zeta,s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\zeta,s-t)\,\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\,dt.

Then
F_{\epsilon}
is band limited and converges to
F
in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
We can then define
(V\circ\widetilde{V})F
as the limit of
(V\circ\widetilde{V})F_{\epsilon}.

Returning to our claim, the required property of the kernel
K_{\lambda}
is proved by explicit calculation. Indeed, in view of (
3.12
), as
B_{\lambda}p_{1/2}^{\lambda}=c_{\lambda},
we obtain

(4.21)

K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=d_{n}\,c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda(\coth\lambda)(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda[\xi,\eta]}.

With
\xi=(x,u)
and
\eta=(y,v)
we have
[\xi,\eta]=(u\cdot y-v\cdot x)
and hence we can rewrite

K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,e^{-\frac{1}{8}\lambda\left(\coth\lambda+1\right)((x+v)^{2}+(u-y)^{2})}\,e^{-\frac{1}{8}\lambda\left(\coth\lambda-1\right)((x-v)^{2}+(u+y)^{2})}.

By introducing the new variables
x^{\prime}=x+v,v^{\prime}=x-v,u^{\prime}=u-y,y^{\prime}=u+y,\,\xi^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})
and
\eta^{\prime}=(y^{\prime},v^{\prime})
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)d\xi\,d\eta=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}e^{-\frac{1}{8}\lambda\left(\coth\lambda+1\right)|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{8}\lambda\left(\coth\lambda-1\right)|\eta^{\prime}|^{2}}\,d\xi^{\prime}d\eta^{\prime}.

Evaluating the integral on the right hand side we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)d\xi\,d\eta=c_{n}\,\lambda^{2n}(\sinh\lambda)^{-2n}\big(\lambda^{2}(\coth\lambda+1)(\coth\lambda-1)\big)^{-n}=c_{n}.

This proves the required property of
K_{\lambda}.

Finally, we need to show that
\widetilde{V}(Vf)=f
for all
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
With notation as above, note that
V(f\ast_{3}\varphi_{\epsilon})=Vf\ast_{3}\varphi_{\epsilon}
and hence
f\ast_{3}\varphi_{\epsilon}
converges to
f
in
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
We can therefore assume that
f
is band limited in the
t
variable so that
Vf(\zeta,s)
is band limited in the
s
variable. To prove
\widetilde{V}(Vf)=f
let us check the action of the tempered distribution
\widetilde{V}(Vf)
on a Schwartz function
g.
Thus

\langle\widetilde{V}(Vf),g\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\,\,\overline{V_{\lambda}g(\zeta)}\,\,d\lambda\,d\zeta.

Interchanging the order of integration, the integral over
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
can be evaluated using the properties of the operators
V_{h}^{\lambda}
proved in Lemma
3.4
. Indeed, by recalling the respective definitions and taking
h=p_{1/2}^{\lambda}
we easily calculate that

(4.22)

V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)=c_{n}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}(\coth\lambda)\xi\cdot\eta}\,V_{h}^{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)

and a similar expression for
V_{\lambda}g.
By appealing to the orthogonality relation (
3.8
) we

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\,\,\overline{V_{\lambda}g(\zeta)}\,\,d\zeta=c_{n}\,c_{\lambda}^{-1}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f^{\lambda}(x,u)\,\overline{g^{\lambda}(x,u)}\,dx\,du.

A simple calculation using the explicit expression for
p_{1/2}^{\lambda}
shows that
\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle=d_{n}c_{\lambda}
and hence

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)\,\,\overline{V_{\lambda}g(\zeta)}\,\,d\zeta=c_{n}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f^{\lambda}(x,u)\,\overline{g^{\lambda}(x,u)}\,dx\,du.

Integrating the above with respect
\lambda
we obtain
\langle\widetilde{V}(Vf),g\rangle=c_{n}\,\langle f,g\rangle
proving the result.
∎

From the above lemma we infer that the operator
T=V\circ\widetilde{V}
is bounded on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
We now show that
T
is self-adjoint, a property which will be used later in computing the dual of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Lemma 4.12
.

For any
F\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
and
G\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}
let
F_{0}=TF,G_{0}=TG.
Then

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle F_{0}^{\lambda},G^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle F^{\lambda},G_{0}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

Proof.

We have already computed
F_{0}(\zeta,s)
in (
4.19
) in the proof of Lemma
4.11
. From (
4.20
) we obtain the following expression:

F_{0}^{\lambda}(\zeta)=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}F^{\lambda}(\zeta^{\prime})\,e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\lambda\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}]-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}))}\,c_{\lambda}\,\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\zeta^{\prime}\,.

Integrating
F_{0}^{\lambda}(\zeta)
against
\overline{G^{\lambda}(\zeta)},
changing the order of integration and simplifying we can easily check that
\langle F_{0}^{\lambda},G^{\lambda}\rangle=\langle F^{\lambda},G_{0}^{\lambda}\rangle
which proves the lemma.
∎

With the above lemmas in hand, we can now establish some basic properties of the modulation spaces
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Theorem 4.13
.

M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
are Banach spaces for any
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
which are invariant under
\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})
for all
(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}.
Moreover,
M^{p,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
are invariant under
U.

Proof.

Let
(f_{k})
be Cauchy in
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
so that
F_{k}=Vf_{k}
is Cauchy in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
Then by the completeness of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
there exists
F\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}
such that

F_{k}\rightarrow F
in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
In view of Lemma
4.11
,
f=\widetilde{V}F\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and hence

\|V(f_{k}-f)\|_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}}=\|V\circ\widetilde{V}(F_{k}-F)\|_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}}\leq C\|F_{k}-F\|_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}}.

which shows that
f_{k}\rightarrow f
in
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
proving the completeness. To prove the invariance of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
under
\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime}),
let
g=\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})f
and consider

Vg(\zeta,s)=\langle\Pi(\zeta^{\prime},s^{\prime})f,\Pi(\zeta,s)Tp_{1/2}\rangle.

As
\Pi
is defined in terms of
\Pi_{\lambda}
it is easy to check that

Vg(\zeta,s)=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}\,e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\lambda\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}]-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}))}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime})p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda.

In other words, we have the equality

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Vg(\zeta,s)\,e^{i\lambda s}ds=e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\lambda\operatorname{Re}[\zeta,\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}]-(\coth\lambda)\operatorname{Im}(\zeta\cdot\bar{\zeta^{\prime}}))}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Vf(\zeta-\zeta^{\prime},s)\,e^{i\lambda s}ds

from which follows the stated invariance of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
To prove the invariance of
M^{p,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
under
U
we make use of the relation

\langle f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)g^{\lambda}\rangle=\langle f^{\lambda},U_{\lambda}^{\ast}\circ\Pi_{\lambda}(-\eta,\xi)\circ U_{\lambda}g^{\lambda}\rangle=\langle U_{\lambda}f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(-\eta,\xi)U_{\lambda}g^{\lambda}\rangle.

We also use the fact that
U_{\lambda}p_{1/2}^{\lambda}=p_{1/2}^{\lambda}
which follows from the relation
U\circ B_{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}\circ U_{\lambda}.
As
U
is defined in terms of
U_{\lambda}
we have

\langle T(Uf),\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle=c_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda s}\,\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,\langle U_{\lambda}f^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)U_{\lambda}p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,d\lambda=\langle Tf,\Pi(-i\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle.

This proves that
Uf\in M^{q,p}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
whenever
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

∎

Remark 4.14
.

We can rewrite the equality
\langle T(Uf),\Pi(\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle=\langle Tf,\Pi(-i\zeta,s)p_{1/2}\rangle
obtained in the proof of Theroem
4.13
as

V(Uf)(\zeta,s)=V(f)(-i\zeta,s).

The above equality is the analogue of the fundamental identity of the time-frequency analysis.

We have seen inclusion between twisted modulation spaces in Theorem
3.7
. In the following theorem we lift this property to the case of modulation spaces on the Heisenberg group.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
1\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\infty
and
1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\infty
. Then we have the inclusion

M^{p_{1},q_{1}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\subseteq M^{p_{2},q_{2}}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Proof.

Let
f\in M^{p_{1},q_{1}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
so that
V_{\lambda}f\in L^{p_{1},q_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. Since we have the relation
|V_{\lambda}f(\zeta)|=\sqrt{c_{\lambda}}\,|V_{p_{1/2}^{\lambda}}^{\lambda}f^{\lambda}(\zeta)|
, by using (
3.11
) with
g=p_{1/2}^{\lambda}
we get

\|V_{g}^{\lambda}f^{\lambda}\|_{p_{2},q_{2}}\leq c_{n}\,c_{\lambda}\,\|V_{g}^{\lambda}f^{\lambda}\|_{p_{1},q_{1}}\|V_{g}^{\lambda}g\|_{r,s},

where
r,s
are as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
. Once we show that
c_{\lambda}\,\|V_{g}^{\lambda}g\|_{r,s}
is a bounded function of
\lambda
for any
1\leq r,s\leq\infty
, the desired inclusion follows from the above inequality by taking the
L^{2}
norm in the
\lambda
variable.
Now recall that we have already computed the function
c_{\lambda}\,|V_{g}^{\lambda}g(\xi,\eta)|=c_{\lambda}\,|\langle p_{1/2}^{\lambda},\Pi_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle|
in (
4.21
). It is explicitly given by

K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda(\coth\lambda)(|\xi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda[\xi,\eta]}.

Writing
\xi=(x,u)
and
\eta=(y,v)
, the expression for
K_{\lambda}
takes the form

K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)=c_{\lambda}^{2}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda(\coth\lambda)(x^{2}+u^{2}+y^{2}+v^{2})}e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda(u\cdot y-v\cdot x)}.

An easy calculation evaluating the Fourier transform of a Gaussian shows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}r\lambda(\coth\lambda)x^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}r\lambda\,v\cdot x}\,dx=c_{n,r}\,(\lambda^{-1}\tanh\lambda)^{n/2}\,e^{\frac{1}{4}r\lambda(\tanh\lambda)v^{2}}.

With a similar calculation for the
L^{r}
norm in the
u
variable we obtain

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\,K_{\lambda}(\xi,\eta)^{r}\,dx\,du\right)^{1/r}=c_{n,r}\,\,(\lambda^{-1}\tanh\lambda)^{n/r}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{4}(\coth\lambda-\tanh\lambda)(y^{2}+v^{2})}.

Now by calculating the
L^{s}
norm of the above in the
\eta=(y,v)
variable, we get

\|K_{\lambda}\|_{r,s}=c_{n,r,s}\,\lambda^{-n/s}\,\,c_{\lambda}^{2}\,(\lambda^{-1}\,\tanh\lambda)^{n/r}(\coth\lambda-\tanh\lambda)^{-n/s}.

The right hand side in the above expression simplifies to a constant multiple of

c_{n,r,s}\,(\lambda/\sinh\lambda)^{2n(1-1/s)}\,(\lambda/\tanh\lambda)^{-n/r}(\lambda\coth\lambda)^{n/s}.

Since
\tanh\lambda
and
\coth\lambda
tend to 1 as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty,
noting that
1-1/s>0,
the exponential growth of
\sinh\lambda
allows us to conclude that the above is bounded.
∎

In the next theorem we identify the dual space of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
The result is again easy consequence of the corresponding result for the twisted modulation spaces.

Theorem 4.16
.

For any
1\leq p,q<\infty,
the dual of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is
M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Proof.

In proving this theorem we make use of the fact that
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})^{\ast}=M_{\lambda}^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).
Recall that the duality between
M_{\lambda}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and
M_{\lambda}^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is given by

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\langle\varphi,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle\,\overline{\langle\psi,\Pi_{\lambda}(\zeta)p_{1/2}^{\lambda}\rangle}\,d\xi\,d\eta

where the integral on the right hand side is the duality between the corresponding mixed norm spaces. Given
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and
g\in M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),
we define the duality bracket

\langle f,g\rangle_{0}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle f^{\lambda},g^{\lambda}\rangle_{\lambda}\,d\lambda.

By Hölder and Cauchy-Schwarz we get the estimate
|\langle f,g\rangle_{0}|\leq\|f\|_{(p,q)}\,\|g\|_{(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})}.
Thus for any
g\in M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
the functional
\Lambda_{g}(f)=\langle f,g\rangle_{0}
belongs to
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})^{\ast}.
This proves the inclusion
M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\subset M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})^{\ast}.
In order to prove the theorem we need to show that every member of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})^{\ast}
is of the form for some
g\in M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

We start with the following observation. For any function
F
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R},
let
\mathcal{F}_{c}F
stand for its Fourier transform in the last variable. Then as we have noted in Lemma
4.9
,
\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\ast}:\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{p,q}
is an isometric isomorphism. Hence
\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda\circ\mathcal{F}_{c}^{\ast}
sets up a one to one correspondence between the duals of
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}
is a mixed norm space, we have
\mathcal{H}_{p,q}^{\ast}=\mathcal{H}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}
and hence
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}^{\ast}=\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}.
If we let
W_{p,q}
stand for the image of
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
under
V,
then it is a closed subspace of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
Given a bounded linear functional
\Lambda
on
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),
the functional
\Lambda\circ\widetilde{V}
belongs to
W_{p,q}^{\ast}
which by Hahn-Banach theorem gives rise to an element
\widetilde{\Lambda}\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}^{\ast}.
Hence there exists
G\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}
such that

(4.23)

\widetilde{\Lambda}(F)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}}F^{\lambda}(\zeta)\,\overline{G^{\lambda}(z)}\,d\zeta\right)\,d\lambda

for any
F\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{p,q}.
By defining
g=\widetilde{V}G
we note that
g\in M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and
(V\circ\widetilde{V})G=Vg.
For any
f\in M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
the function
F=Vf
satisfies
(V\circ\widetilde{V})F=F.
Therefore, by taking
F=Vf
in (
4.23
) and appealing to Lemma
4.12
we obtain

\widetilde{\Lambda}(Vf)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle V_{\lambda}f,V_{\lambda}g\rangle\,d\lambda.

Since
\Lambda(f)=\widetilde{\Lambda}(Vf),
recalling the definition of the duality bracket, we obtain

\Lambda(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle f^{\lambda},g^{\lambda}\rangle_{\lambda}\,d\lambda=\langle f,g\rangle_{0}.

This proves the reverse inclusion
M^{p,q}(\mathbb{H}^{n})^{\ast}\subset M^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and hence the theorem follows.
∎
