Challenges and Lived Realities of Patients With Chronic Kidney Disease in Ghana: A Qualitative Inquiry
Authors: Alorkorbu Vinolia, Evans Appiah Osei, Edward Obeng Amoah, Emuejevoke Fuafugo, Nancy Yaa Durowaa, Mbanefo Onyinye, Lillian Agyemang, Zainab Seidu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) are rising public health concerns in low- and middle-income countries such as Ghana, where late diagnosis and limited access to renal replacement therapy are common. Qualitative evidence on their lived experiences in Ghana remains limited.AimTo explore the lived experiences and challenges of patients with CKD and ESRD receiving hemodialysis in Ghana.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive qualitative study was conducted with 17 patients with CKD/ESRD on hemodialysis for at least six months at Ho Teaching Hospital, Ghana. Data were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews (July–August 2025), audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using conventional content analysis. Reflexivity and team-based coding ensured rigor.ResultsThree themes emerged: (1) Structural and health system failures shaping illness trajectories, encompassing delayed and misdiagnosis, perceived neglect, limited dialysis availability, and severe financial hardship; (2) Fear of death and loss of self, reflecting persistent mortality anxiety, and identity disruption and (3) Endurance through relationships, faith, and treatment-related hope, highlighting the critical role of family support, spiritual coping, and temporary symptom relief from dialysis in sustaining resilience.ConclusionPatients with CKD/ESRD in Ghana face significant structural, financial, and psychosocial challenges that affect their illness experience and treatment engagement. Although family support, faith, and symptom relief promote resilience, they do not compensate for gaps in renal care. Improving early diagnosis, equitable dialysis access, psychosocial integration, and policy reform is essential to enhance outcomes and quality of life.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD)/ESRD ranks among the leading causes of mortality worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems. 1 The later stages of CKD, particularly end-stage renal disease (ESRD), carry significant health and economic consequences. ESRD is defined by a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 and requires life-sustaining interventions, such as dialysis or kidney transplantation, to prevent death. 2 Globally, an estimated 788 million adults were living with CKD/ESRD in 2023. 3 Despite the high prevalence, many patients in need of treatment do not have access to adequate care, highlighting a critical global health inequity. 4

Preventive strategies, early detection, and affordable treatment options are urgently needed to reduce the incidence and progression of CKD/ESRD, especially among younger adults aged 20 to 59 years. 5 Studies have shown a higher burden of CKD/ESRD attributable to high fasting plasma glucose in males over recent decades, reflecting the growing impact of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and glomerular diseases as leading causes of CKD/ESRD.2,6 Furthermore, the rising global burden of CKD/ESRD is linked to population growth and aging. As CKD progresses to kidney failure, kidney replacement therapy (KRT) becomes necessary; however, access to such therapies remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).7-9

The costs of managing CKD/ESRD are prohibitive. Median annual costs for kidney replacement therapies range from approximately US$19,000 for hemodialysis to nearly US$27,000 for the first year following kidney transplantation. 10 While public funding often supports hemodialysis in high-income countries, such coverage is less common and less effective in LMICs, where access to nephrologists and specialized care is also limited. 11

In Africa, the burden of CKD/ESRD is increasing exponentially, disproportionately affecting younger, economically productive adults particularly males and rural populations. 12 Compounding this is the rarity of kidney transplantation and peritoneal dialysis, which are standard treatments in wealthier regions. 13 Africa faces unique challenges, including the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria, weak epidemiological data, poor recognition of CKD/ESRD in primary care, and resultant late diagnoses with poor outcomes. 14 Financial barriers are substantial, as average incomes are low, health insurance is often unavailable, and out-of-pocket expenses can be catastrophic for families. 15

In Ghana, CKD/ESRD are typically diagnosed at advanced stages among relatively younger patients, with a median age of approximately 45.5 years, significantly younger than in high-income countries.16,17 The true incidence of CKD/ESRD remains unclear due to limited data, but evidence suggests rates higher than those reported in Europe. 18 Despite this burden, CKD/ESRD receives less public health attention in Ghana compared to other noncommunicable diseases such as hypertension and diabetes. 19 Few studies have explored the lived experiences of individuals with CKD/ESRD in Ghana, particularly the psychosocial and physical challenges they face during treatment, including depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation. 20

Although a previous qualitative study by Abu Bonsra et al 21 explored the lived experiences and coping strategies of patients with chronic renal failure across three teaching hospitals in Ghana, important gaps remain in understanding the specific challenges faced by patients receiving hemodialysis within individual treatment settings. The present study was not a follow-up to the previous investigation but an independent study conducted at Ho Teaching Hospital with a different sample of participants. While the earlier study broadly examined lived experiences and coping strategies across multiple sites, the current study focused specifically on the structural, health system, financial, and psychosocial challenges shaping the lived realities of patients with CKD/ESRD receiving hemodialysis. By providing a context-specific examination of patients’ experiences within a major regional dialysis center, this study extends existing knowledge and offers insights to inform targeted clinical, psychosocial, and policy interventions in Ghana. Hemodialysis remains the predominant treatment, as Ghana lacks a national kidney transplant program and has only limited transplant services. 17

Given the growing burden of CKD/ESRD in Ghana and the unique socio-cultural and healthcare system challenges, there is an urgent need for in-depth qualitative research to understand the lived realities of patients managing CKD/ESRD. Such understanding is critical for informing culturally sensitive interventions, improving healthcare access, and guiding policies tailored to the Ghanaian context.

Method

Design

This study employed an exploratory descriptive qualitative design to gain an in-depth understanding of the challenges and lived realities of patients with chronic kidney disease in Ghana. This approach is particularly suitable for exploring experiences, perceptions, and meanings in areas where limited knowledge exists. 22 It facilitates rich descriptions of participants’ subjective experiences and provides insight into the “who,” “what,” and “where” of human experiences within their natural contexts. 23 Guided by a constructivist perspective, the study acknowledges that reality is socially constructed, context-dependent, and shaped by individuals’ unique experiences and interpretations.

Context

The study was conducted at Ho Teaching Hospital, a regional referral and teaching hospital in Ghana that provides dialysis services for patients with chronic kidney disease. The hospital receives referrals from the Volta Region and surrounding areas and offers specialized renal care, making it a suitable setting for exploring the experiences and challenges of patients receiving dialysis care.

Participants

The study included patients with CKD/ESRD receiving hemodialysis at Ho Teaching Hospital, Ghana. Eligible participants had been on hemodialysis for at least six months, had a diagnosis of chronic kidney disease, and were able and willing to provide informed consent. Patients who were critically ill, physically too weak to participate, or psychologically unstable were excluded.

Data Collection Tool

Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed by the first author (A.V.) under the supervision of the second author. The guide was informed by the study objectives and relevant literature and was reviewed by all co-authors to ensure content relevance, clarity, and appropriateness. Prior to the main study, the interview guide was piloted with one male and one female patient with chronic kidney disease at the study site who met the inclusion criteria. The pilot interviews assessed the clarity, comprehensibility, flow, and relevance of the interview questions.

The pilot interviews were conducted to assess the clarity, relevance, and flow of the interview guide. Findings from the pilot resulted in minor refinements, primarily the addition of probing questions to facilitate richer and more detailed responses, while the core interview questions and study objectives remained unchanged. Because the pilot participants met the study eligibility criteria and the data generated were relevant to the research aims, the pilot interviews were retained and included in the final analysis. This approach is considered acceptable in qualitative research when modifications to the interview guide are minor and do not substantially alter the focus or content of the data collected. 24

Sampling Technique and Recruitment

Participants were purposively recruited from the dialysis unit at Ho Teaching Hospital, which had approximately 50 patients receiving hemodialysis at the time of the study. A purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure the inclusion of participants with relevant knowledge and experiences related to the phenomenon under study. 25 Purposive sampling was used to identify individuals who met the inclusion criteria, including the relevant clinical condition and the required duration of diagnosis.

Recruitment was conducted by A.V. between July 10 and August 24, 2025, through regular visits to the unit two to three times per week. Unit nurses assisted in identifying eligible participants and screening out patients who were critically ill or otherwise unable to participate at the time of each visit. During each recruitment visit, at least two eligible patients were approached. The researcher explained the purpose of the study, answered participants’ questions, and obtained verbal consent from those who expressed willingness to participate before proceeding with data collection.

Data Collection

Data collection was conducted by A.V. between July and August 2025 and occurred concurrently with participant recruitment. Interviews commenced on July 11, 2025. Eligible participants received detailed information about the study purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature of participation and were given the opportunity to ask questions before providing written informed consent. All interviews were conducted by A.V. Throughout the data collection process, A.V. received methodological guidance and supervision from the second author (E.A.O.), who has extensive experience conducting qualitative research and using similar study designs. Before each interview, the researcher reconfirmed their willingness to participate. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in English and lasted 30–60 minutes. Of the 30 patients approached, five were ineligible due to recent dialysis initiation or critical illness, and four declined participation. Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was achieved, with no new themes or insights emerging from successive interviews. 26 Saturation was reached by the 15th interview; two additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation, and data collection concluded after 17 interviews. Saturation was independently verified by A.V. and E.O.A.

Interviews were conducted in a designated private space within the hospital provided by the nursing staff, with only the researcher and participant present to ensure confidentiality. All interviews were audio-recorded. The research interviewer followed the interview guide and used probes to elicit deeper insights and clarify participants’ responses.

During one interview, a participant became emotionally distressed and began to cry while recounting his experience. The interview was paused, and the participant was offered emotional support and the option to discontinue or reschedule. After approximately 15 minutes, the participant chose to continue with the interview. All participants who consented and attended the interview completed the data collection process. At the conclusion of each interview, participants were thanked for their time and valuable contributions.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the [Blinded] Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. [Blinded]). Administrative permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the hospital management. To protect patient privacy and confidentiality, hospital staff did not disclose patients’ personal health information to the researcher. Instead, nurses in the unit acted as gatekeepers by identifying patients who met the eligibility criteria and introducing the study to them. Patients who expressed interest in learning more about the study were referred to the researcher, who subsequently explained the study purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits, addressed any questions, and obtained written informed consent prior to participation. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their care.

Reflexivity

All authors are nurses, with the exception of one social worker, and each has five or more years of clinical experience caring for patients with chronic illnesses. Four authors are doctoral candidates in Nursing, and one is a doctoral candidate in Social Work, with prior experience using similar qualitative methods and study designs. These professional backgrounds informed the research process, including participant engagement, data interpretation, and reflexive awareness of how clinical and disciplinary perspectives may have shaped the study.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive qualitative content analysis, which enables the systematic identification of patterns, categories, and themes from textual data. 27 The first three authors (A.V., E.A.O., and E.O.A.) independently transcribed the interviews verbatim, while the remaining authors reviewed the transcripts for accuracy and completeness. To achieve immersion in the data, transcripts were read repeatedly by the research team.

Meaning units relevant to the study aim were identified, condensed, and assigned codes. Similar codes were grouped into categories based on conceptual similarities, and categories were subsequently abstracted into broader themes and subthemes that reflected participants’ experiences. Coding was conducted independently by the first five authors, followed by regular team meetings to compare interpretations, refine codes, and resolve discrepancies until consensus was reached. The analysis generated three overarching themes and ten subthemes that captured the challenges and lived realities of patients with chronic kidney disease.

To enhance trustworthiness, the research team engaged in reflexive discussions throughout the analytic process. Given the team’s nursing background, potential assumptions regarding patient care, treatment adherence, and health-seeking behaviors were acknowledged and critically examined to ensure that the findings remained grounded in participants’ perspectives.

Themes and Subthemes

Theme 1: Structural and Health System Failures Shaping Illness Trajectories

This theme captures how systemic gaps in healthcare delivery negatively influence the course of chronic kidney disease in patients. Participants describe struggles with diagnosis delays, perceived neglect experiences, inadequate treatment options, and financial burdens faced during treatment.

Subtheme 1: Delayed and Misdiagnosis of CKD/ESRD

Patients often faced long delays and incorrect diagnoses, resulting in advanced disease stages by the time kidney failure was identified. This delay caused emotional trauma and significant financial strain due to ineffective treatments and sudden dialysis costs.“By the time they finally found out it was my kidneys, I was already too weak to walk by myself. The swelling had spread all over my body, and I was gasping for breath. If they had discovered it earlier, maybe I wouldn’t have reached this stage” (P8)“I kept going to different hospitals because I was always weak, swollen, and vomiting, but they kept on giving me a different diagnosis all the time until it had kept long. It was only when I could hardly pass urine and my body was completely swollen that they said it was kidney failure. By then, I was already in a very bad stage and had to start dialysis immediately” (P3)

Subtheme 2: Perceived Neglect and Abandonment in the Health System

Many participants expressed feelings of neglect and abandonment within the healthcare system. They described inadequate communication from healthcare providers, limited access to specialist care, insufficient information about their condition, and a lack of support in navigating treatment decisions. Participants felt that their concerns were sometimes overlooked and that the healthcare system offered limited assistance beyond dialysis treatment, leaving them feeling isolated and unsupported.“I felt like nobody really explained what was happening to me. They just kept giving me medications and sending me home. By the time I understood how serious my condition was, it had already become very bad. Sometimes it felt like I was facing this illness alone” (P12).“When I started dialysis, I had so many questions about what would happen to me and my family, but there was no one to guide me. I felt abandoned because once the treatment started, it seemed like I was expected to figure everything else out on my own” (P7).

“I felt like nobody really explained what was happening to me. They just kept giving me medications and sending me home. By the time I understood how serious my condition was, it had already become very bad. Sometimes it felt like I was facing this illness alone” (P12).

Participants also felt that healthcare providers focused primarily on the physical management of kidney disease, particularly dialysis, while paying limited attention to their emotional, psychological, and financial needs. This lack of holistic support contributed to feelings of neglect and abandonment within the healthcare system.“Most of the time, the focus is only on the dialysis. Nobody asks about how I am coping emotionally or financially. It makes me feel like my struggles beyond the machine do not matter” (P14).

Theme 2: Fear of Death and Loss of Self

This theme explores the profound emotional and psychological impact of chronic kidney disease on patients, focusing on their fear of dying and the erosion of their sense of identity. Participants describe living with constant anxiety and grief over the changes to their bodies, roles, and futures.

Subtheme 1: Perceived Eventual Death/Illness as Terminal

Patients often perceive their illness as incurable and terminal, accepting death as an inevitable outcome. This perception creates a daily struggle to cope with the harsh reality of their diagnosis and limited treatment options.“For me, kidney failure is something that cannot be cured. I know I will live with it until the end, and sometimes I just think it will eventually take my life” (P17)“When I was finally diagnosed, they said it was already at the last stage. That word ‘end-stage’ alone broke me because it sounded final. I believe this sickness has no cure, and even when they talk about kidney transplant, I know it is not something easily available for people like me. So, I see this condition as a long journey that will eventually end in death, no matter how much I fight. It is a hard truth to live with every day” (P10)

Subtheme 2: Living in Constant Fear

Many participants experience ongoing fear and anxiety, haunted by the possibility of sudden health decline or death. This pervasive worry affected their emotional well-being and daily life.

“Sometimes I just sit alone and cry because I know this sickness will be with me until I die. It makes me feel like my life has stopped, it make me live everyday in fear of dying” (P4)

Theme 3: Endurance Through Relationships, Faith, and Treatment-Related Hope

This theme illustrates how patients draw strength and resilience from their families, spiritual beliefs, and the relief dialysis provides. Despite the hardships, these sources of support foster hope and motivate continued adherence to treatment.

Subtheme 1: Family Support in Dialysis Treatment and Guilt About Dependence

Patients rely heavily on family members for physical and emotional support during treatment, which sustains their survival and coping. However, many also experience guilt for the burden their illness places on loved ones.“If not for my family, I don’t think I would still be alive today. They are the ones who take me for dialysis, pay for the sessions, and make sure I eat well. On days when I feel like giving up, they remind me why I should keep fighting” (P9)“My wife has been my strength. She organizes everything for me—my drugs, my hospital visits, even my diet. Without her, I would not be able to manage this condition. The illness has become a family issue, not just mine alone” (P7)

“If not for my family, I don’t think I would still be alive today. They are the ones who take me for dialysis, pay for the sessions, and make sure I eat well. On days when I feel like giving up, they remind me why I should keep fighting” (P9)

A few participants also expressed feelings of guilt about being overly dependent on their families, describing the strain the illness placed on loved ones:“Sometimes I feel like a burden because my children contribute money every week for my treatment. I know it is not easy for them, but they never complain. Still, I feel guilty because I was supposed to be the one taking care of them, not the other way around” (P13)

Given that all participants were Christians, faith in God served as a key source of comfort, hope, and strength. Discussion

This study looked at the lived experiences of 17 patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in Ghana. It revealed a complex relationship of structural, psychological, and relational factors that shape their illness trajectories. The findings show how systemic failures, fear of death, identity disruption, and reliance on family and faith converge to influence coping, treatment adherence, and overall well-being of patients. There were three (3) overarching themes, i.e., (1) structural and health system failures, (2) fear of death and loss of self, and (3) endurance through relationships, faith, and treatment-related hope.

Structural and Health System Failures as Determinants of Illness Trajectories

Participants consistently described prolonged and frustrating pathways to diagnosis, often involving repeated healthcare visits, misdiagnoses, worsening symptoms, and eventual presentation with advanced CKD/ESRD requiring immediate dialysis. Beyond the clinical consequences, these delays were experienced as emotionally distressing and financially devastating. Many participants expressed shock upon learning the severity of their condition and reflected on how earlier diagnosis might have altered their illness trajectory. Their accounts suggest that delayed diagnosis was not perceived merely as a medical issue but as a life-altering experience that intensified physical suffering, emotional distress, and financial hardship. Similar experiences of delayed recognition and late presentation of CKD/ESRD have been reported in Ghana and other sub-Saharan African countries, where patients frequently enter care at advanced stages of disease.14,16

Participants also described feeling neglected and unsupported within the healthcare system. These perceptions stemmed not only from delays in diagnosis but also from inadequate communication, limited information about their condition, and a perceived lack of emotional and psychosocial support. While dialysis addressed the immediate physical demands of kidney failure, many participants felt their broader concerns, including uncertainty about the future, financial strain, and emotional well-being, received little attention. These experiences suggest that participants valued holistic care and desired greater engagement, guidance, and support throughout their treatment journey. Similar concerns regarding fragmented CKD/ESRD care and limited patient support have been documented in settings with constrained nephrology resources and healthcare infrastructure. 28

The challenges described by participants extended beyond diagnosis and communication to include broader health system barriers such as the high cost of dialysis, long travel distances, treatment delays, and limited access to kidney transplantation. For many, these barriers reinforced feelings of vulnerability and uncertainty about their future. Participants’ narratives illustrate how CKD/ESRD management is shaped not only by the disease itself but also by the availability and accessibility of healthcare resources. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that financial constraints and limited access to specialized kidney care remain significant challenges for individuals living with CKD/ESRD in low-resource settings.14,29

Participants described financial hardship as a pervasive challenge that shaped their ability to adhere to treatment recommendations. Many reported delaying or missing dialysis sessions, reducing medication use, and struggling to follow dietary advice because of the high cost of care. These financial pressures extended beyond the individual, often affecting entire households and creating feelings of guilt, dependence, and uncertainty about continued treatment. Participants’ accounts illustrate how economic constraints were not merely barriers to care but ongoing stressors that influenced daily decision-making and their ability to manage CKD/ESRD effectively. Similar financial challenges among patients receiving dialysis have been reported in low- and middle-income countries, where treatment costs frequently result in substantial household financial burden.10,13 In Ghana, where dialysis is not routinely covered under the National Health Insurance Scheme, the cost of hemodialysis remains a significant obstacle to sustained treatment. 30

Participants also expressed a sense of hopelessness regarding access to kidney transplantation, which many viewed as financially and geographically unattainable. Rather than perceiving transplantation as a realistic treatment option, participants often regarded it as beyond their reach, reinforcing feelings of uncertainty about their future. Some described their efforts to sustain dialysis treatment despite severe financial hardship as a continuous struggle with limited prospects for long-term improvement. These experiences reflect broader inequities in access to advanced kidney care and are consistent with reports highlighting the limited availability and affordability of transplantation services in Ghana. 17

Beyond financial challenges, participants described significant barriers in accessing dialysis services, including long travel distances, treatment delays, long waiting times, and occasional equipment shortages. These challenges disrupted treatment schedules and heightened anxiety about disease progression and symptom recurrence. For many participants, especially those residing outside major urban centers, obtaining dialysis required substantial time, effort, and financial resources. Participants’ narratives suggest that the burden of CKD/ESRD was compounded by the practical difficulties of accessing consistent care. Similar barriers have been documented in Ghana, where renal services are concentrated in urban facilities and resource constraints continue to affect the delivery of dialysis care.31,32

Fear of Death and Profound Loss of Identity

Participants in this study commonly viewed CKD/ESRD as a terminal condition and described living with a persistent awareness of death. For many, receiving a diagnosis of “end-stage” kidney disease was interpreted as confirmation that their illness was incurable and would eventually lead to death. This perception shaped how participants understood their future and contributed to feelings of uncertainty, sadness, and hopelessness. Rather than viewing dialysis as a pathway to recovery, many saw it as a means of prolonging life in the face of an illness they believed would ultimately be fatal. Similar perceptions of mortality and existential distress have been reported among individuals living with CKD/ESRD in other settings, 33 suggesting that awareness of the life-limiting nature of the condition can profoundly influence patients’ emotional experiences.

Beyond concerns about death, participants described living in a constant state of fear and vigilance. Everyday symptoms such as pain, fatigue, or physical discomfort were often interpreted as signs of impending deterioration or death, creating ongoing psychological distress. Participants’ narratives suggest that fear was not limited to specific health events but became embedded in daily life, influencing their thoughts, emotions, and outlook on the future. These findings align with previous studies showing that anxiety, uncertainty, and psychological distress are common among individuals receiving dialysis and living with CKD/ESRD. 34 In the present study, however, participants’ fears appeared closely linked to their perceptions of limited treatment options and uncertainty about disease progression.

Participants also described a profound sense of losing their former selves as a result of CKD/ESRD and its treatment demands. The inability to work, fulfil family responsibilities, travel freely, or maintain previous levels of independence led many to feel disconnected from their identities prior to illness. Feelings of dependence on family members for financial, physical, and emotional support often generated guilt and a sense of being a burden, particularly among those who had previously served as primary providers for their households. These accounts highlight that the impact of CKD/ESRD extended beyond physical symptoms to encompass significant disruptions in self-concept, social roles, and personal autonomy. Similar experiences of identity loss, dependency, and diminished self-worth have been documented among individuals living with CKD/ESRD, particularly within collectivist cultures where family and caregiving roles are highly valued. 35

Endurance Through Relationships, Faith, and Treatment-Related Hope

Participants consistently described family support as essential to their ability to cope with and manage CKD/ESRD. Family members provided financial assistance, transportation to dialysis appointments, help with daily activities, and emotional encouragement during periods of physical and psychological distress. For many participants, the illness was experienced as a shared family challenge rather than an individual burden. At the same time, participants expressed feelings of guilt and sadness about their increasing dependence on relatives, particularly when family members made financial sacrifices to support their treatment. These findings highlight the complex role of family relationships, which served as a source of strength and resilience while simultaneously generating concerns about being a burden to loved ones. Similar patterns have been reported in African settings, where chronic illness management is often embedded within family and community networks. 21

Faith emerged as another important source of endurance for participants. All participants identified as Christians and described prayer, trust in God, and acceptance of illness as helping them cope with uncertainty, suffering, and fears about the future. Participants often interpreted their illness through a spiritual lens, viewing it as part of God’s plan or will, which appeared to provide comfort and a sense of meaning despite the challenges associated with CKD/ESRD. For many, faith did not eliminate distress but enabled them to endure it and maintain hope in the face of ongoing physical, emotional, and financial difficulties. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that spiritual beliefs can play an important role in helping individuals with chronic illness make sense of their experiences and cope with existential concerns. 36

Despite describing dialysis as physically demanding, time-consuming, and emotionally exhausting, participants viewed it as a vital source of relief and hope. Improvements in symptoms such as breathlessness, swelling, weakness, and fatigue following treatment provided reassurance that their condition could be managed, even if only temporarily. Participants’ accounts suggest that these periods of relief helped sustain their motivation to continue treatment despite its burdens. Rather than viewing dialysis solely as a medical procedure, participants often described it as a means of regaining a sense of normalcy and control over their daily lives. This cyclical experience of symptom burden followed by temporary relief underscores the central role dialysis played in shaping participants’ perceptions of hope and endurance while living with CKD/ESRD. 37

Limitations and Strengths

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The sample size was relatively small and drawn from a single dialysis center, which may limit its transferability to other regions. The predominance of Christian participants restricts the exploration of diverse spiritual perspectives in this study. Additionally, the cross-sectional design captures experiences at a single point in time, limiting insights into how coping and perceptions evolve across the illness trajectory. Patients who were severely ill may have been underrepresented because of the challenges of participating in interviews.

Implications for Practice, Policy, and Research

The findings of this study suggest several implications for clinical practice, policy, and future research. Participants described substantial psychological distress characterized by persistent fear of death, anxiety, crying, feelings of hopelessness, and concerns about being a burden to family members. These experiences indicate the need for routine psychological assessment and psychosocial support as part of CKD/ESRD care. Integrating regular screening for depression and anxiety, counseling services, and psychoeducation regarding disease progression, coping strategies, and treatment expectations may help address the emotional burden associated with living with CKD/ESRD. In addition, participants frequently relied on faith as a source of strength and endurance, suggesting that incorporating spiritual care services and facilitating access to chaplaincy support may provide culturally meaningful support. The perceived lack of guidance and support beyond dialysis also highlights the importance of multidisciplinary care teams that include nurses, nephrologists, social workers, psychologists, dietitians, and spiritual care providers to address patients’ complex physical, emotional, social, and financial needs.

The findings also point to important policy priorities. Participants reported delayed and missed diagnoses, long travel distances to dialysis centers, equipment shortages, and limited access to specialist care, indicating a need to strengthen CKD screening and early detection initiatives within primary healthcare settings. The severe financial hardship described by participants, including reducing dialysis frequency, skipping medications, and relying heavily on family members for treatment costs, underscores the urgent need for expanded financial protection mechanisms, including insurance coverage for dialysis and related medications. Furthermore, decentralizing dialysis services, increasing nephrology workforce capacity, and developing alternative renal replacement therapies such as peritoneal dialysis may improve access to care, particularly for individuals residing in rural and underserved communities. Funding programs targeted toward economically disadvantaged patients may also reduce treatment interruptions and improve adherence.

Future research should build upon these findings by examining how patients’ experiences evolve over time through longitudinal studies. Given the concentration of dialysis services in urban areas, further research is needed to explore regional variations in CKD/ESRD experiences and access to care across Ghana. Participants’ strong reliance on faith and family support also suggests the need for studies examining spiritual coping mechanisms and family caregiver burden. Finally, research evaluating the cost-effectiveness and feasibility of alternative service delivery models, including decentralized dialysis and peritoneal dialysis programs, may help inform sustainable approaches to improving renal care in resource-constrained settings. Future research should build upon these findings by examining how patients’ experiences evolve over time through longitudinal studies. Additionally, intervention studies are needed to evaluate strategies aimed at reducing psychological distress, improving treatment adherence, enhancing coping, and strengthening psychosocial support for individuals living with CKD/ESRD.

Conclusion

This study highlights the intersecting burdens faced by patients with CKD/ESRD in Ghana, including health system challenges, economic hardship, psychological distress, and identity disruption. Despite these challenges, patients demonstrated remarkable resilience through family support, spiritual faith, and the temporary relief provided by dialysis. However, resilience alone cannot compensate for persistent health system and service-related limitations, including delayed diagnosis, barriers to dialysis access, financial strain, and inadequate psychosocial support. Improving outcomes requires comprehensive policy reform, strengthened clinical support systems, and culturally grounded psychosocial care. The participants’ voices underscore the urgency of developing equitable, accessible, and sustainable renal care services in Ghana.
