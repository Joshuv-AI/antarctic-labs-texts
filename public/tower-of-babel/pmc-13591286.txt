Politicising Recovery: Why Early Intervention Must Move Beyond the Imperative of Functioning
Authors: Jordan Sibeoni, Jeanne Aquili, Sovady Bora, Naya Tarabichi, Solène Carbonnel, Augustin Behaghel, Anne Revah‐Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo articulate the politicisation of recovery as a conceptual and clinical framework for early intervention in psychosis and to argue why the field must move beyond the imperative of functioning.ObservationEarly intervention for ultra‐high‐risk states and first‐episode psychosis has improved symptomatic outcomes, yet a persistent gap remains between remission and functional recovery, while social and environmental determinants shape both onset and course. Introduction

Early intervention services have produced substantial gains in reducing the duration of untreated psychosis and improving symptomatic outcomes (Fusar‐Poli et al. 2017). Yet a persistent gap remains between symptomatic remission and functional recovery: a recent meta‐analysis shows that while around half of young people achieve symptomatic remission, less than a third reach functional recovery (Catalan et al. 2021). Environmental and social determinants (urban density, socioeconomic deprivation, discrimination, migration) shape both the onset and the course of psychosis (Jongsma et al. 2019; Kirkbride et al. 2024), operating not only through individual exposure but through the relational systems (families, peer networks, communities) within which young people with ultra‐high‐risk (UHR) states or first‐episode psychosis (FEP) are embedded.

Early intervention is not a bystander to these findings. It was born as a reform movement: it contested the therapeutic pessimism attached to psychosis, redesigned services around young people rather than institutions, campaigned for policy change and integrated peer practitioners and lived experience into service planning and research; its leading voices now name the societal ‘megatrends’ such as economic precarity, climate change, unregulated digital environments, that erode youth mental health (McGorry et al. 2024). In this sense, early intervention has been doing politics from the start; yet like Molière's Monsieur Jourdain, who had been speaking prose all his life without knowing it, the field practises a politics it rarely names. Our hypothesis is that naming it, politicising recovery, is not a rupture with the early intervention paradigm but its next step.

What Politicising Recovery Means

The recovery paradigm has already moved the field beyond symptomatic endpoints, toward the subjective determination of what constitutes a meaningful life (Davidson 2003). Yet critical and survivor‐led scholarship documents how recovery has been progressively co‐opted: individualised, stripped of its collective dimension and converted into an obligation to become functional again (Harper and Speed 2012; Recovery in the Bin 2016). This drift is intelligible within what Moulier‐Boutang (2011) terms cognitive capitalism, an economy in which value is extracted from cognition, communication and sociality themselves. In such an economy, the functioning of a young mind is not only a clinical outcome but an economic resource and indicators such as NEET status (not in employment, education or training) measure re‐entry into circuits of productivity rather than a recovered capacity to act. What these indicators reward is autoplastic success, in Langer's dialectic, the adaptation of the self to reality, as opposed to the alloplastic transformation of reality to fit the self (Langer et al. 1981). An unbalanced, autoplastic model of recovery risks becoming complicit in the very conditions that produce or maintain the distress it treats.

By politicisation of recovery, we therefore do not mean political militancy, nor the capacity of patients to produce effective change in social structures. We mean the expansion of clinical and institutional practices that support the young person's capacity to act within, interpret and sometimes transform the conditions shaping their lived world. The primary object is the restoration of a capacity for agency; wider collective or social change is a possible extension, never the criterion by which recovery is judged. This proposal belongs to an established lineage—psychiatry's political mission (Priebe 2015), recovery understood as the recovery of citizenship (Rowe and Davidson 2016)—and it has a theoretical foundation: eco‐phenomenological accounts of psychosis describe selfhood as constituted in and through its world (Sass and Parnas 2003; Parnas and Sandsten 2024). A world materially impoverished, spatially hostile or marked by discrimination is not merely a stressful backdrop; it actively works against the reconstitution of a coherent self. This does not mean that the world causes the disorder; it means that neither the disorder nor its resolution can be understood apart from the world in which they unfold, so that recovery requires attending simultaneously to both poles.

Three Levels of a Politicised Clinic

Politicised recovery is not a new programme, but a re‐description made explicit and pushed further into three levels of what early intervention, at its best, already does.

The first level is the therapeutic relationship and in particular case management. When a case manager helps a young person negotiate a housing situation, contest a discriminatory decision, mediate a family conflict or weigh a treatment option in shared decision‐making, they are not preparing the young person for agency: they are practising it with them. These small, accompanied acts upon the immediate environment count fully as politicised recovery.

The second level is the group, following Pichon‐Rivière's (1971) grupo operativo, understood not primarily as a vehicle for individual skill acquisition but as a small‐scale world. Existing group‐based interventions (psychoeducation, social‐skills training, cognitive remediation, multifamily groups) already provide shared experience, mutual recognition and belonging; what a politicised reading adds is attention to the group as the place where agency is rehearsed: expressing an opinion, having it contradicted, naming a shared condition, deciding something together. From there, acting collectively upon the wider environment, for instance the urban milieu, whose experiential role in psychosis is documented and modifiable (Söderström et al. 2016; Baumann et al. 2020), becomes one possibility among others, never an implicit goal.

The third level is institutional. Peer practitioners and the involvement of lived experience in service design, policy and research already embody a politicisation of the clinic. Its corollary is reflexivity: an ongoing scrutiny, by clinicians and institutions, of their own interpretive frameworks, of what is recognised as worthy of being heard and of which experiences (racism, precarity, stigma) are too readily pathologised.

This framework has a limit case that reveals whether it is taken seriously. A politicised understanding of recovery must be able to hear a young person's refusal of psychosocial rehabilitation (i.e., the refusal of the proposed pathway back to functioning) as a possible expression of agency to be explored and not only as avolition or lack of insight. A politicisation of recovery that could not tolerate this refusal would be nothing more than a new, better‐disguised demand for compliance.

Perspectives

Three tensions must be navigated and they generate the research agenda. First, the distribution of responsibility: the burden of transforming structural determinants lies with institutions and policies, not with young patients; this framework must never licence divestment from services on the grounds that users should become the agents of their own social change. Second, stratification: if agency becomes an implicit standard, it risks reproducing, under a critical vocabulary, the same logic this framework denounces in functioning, privileging those already fluent in negotiation and self‐expression; hence our insistence that minimal, accompanied acts count. Third, dependency: relational dependency is a universal feature of human existence, not a symptom to be eliminated; recovery‐as‐agency is not an event but a process, unfolding across attachment relationships and developmental time, within which compromise remains a clinical and ethical necessity, preventing politicised recovery from hardening into an imperative as alienating as the one it seeks to replace.

Consequently, outcome measurement should complement functional indicators with instruments sensitive to perceived control, empowerment and meaningful participation: existing tools such as the Questionnaire about the Process of Recovery (Neil et al. 2009), alongside developmentally appropriate measures of functional recovery (Iyer et al. 2022), while recovery‐oriented instruments capturing the re‐appropriation of the inhabited world remain to be developed, building on the phenomenological groundwork of the Examination of Anomalous World Experience (EAWE; Sass et al. 2017).

A further research agenda may build on existing work on modifiable environments (Söderström et al. 2016; Baumann et al. 2020) to test whether interventions upon the lived world alter recovery trajectories and thereby contribute to redefining the outcome itself. Early intervention services, with their intensive follow‐up of well‐characterised cohorts, are uniquely positioned to generate this evidence.

The clinician's task, throughout, is not to lead the young person toward a predetermined emancipation, but to maintain the conditions in which movement between adaptation and transformation remains possible. Early intervention has been speaking this political prose from the beginning. Saying so, pioneering once more, is, we believe, how the field can move beyond the imperative of functioning.
