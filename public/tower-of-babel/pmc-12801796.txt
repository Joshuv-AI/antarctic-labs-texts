A comparative assessment of data completeness of the adverse events following immunization (AEFI) in DHIS2 at the early implementation stage in Bangladesh
Authors: Afroza Akter, Imam Tauheed, Md Golam Firoj, Sabina Shahnaz, Md Shahinur Rahaman, Md Tanvir Hossen, Firdausi Qadri, Fahima Chowdhury
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData completeness is crucial in making evidence-based decisions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). A total of 130 countries have adopted the District Health Information System (DHIS2) platform in the health sector to generate good-quality data. In 2021, Bangladesh implemented DHIS2 as a platform for monitoring and surveillance of adverse events following immunization (AEFI). This study aimed to assess the level of completeness of AEFI data reported in DHIS2 during the early implementation phase through existing AEFI surveillance systems in Bangladesh.MethodsWe conducted a facility-based cross-sectional study. Based on a desk review of the 2023 AEFI data, we selected three divisions (Chattogram, Sylhet, and Rangpur) of Bangladesh based on high-, medium-, and low-performing groups according to the AEFI reporting rate. A total of 97 AEFI cases were found in the DHIS2 system for the assessment from six selected health facilities, which were reported between January and December 2023. The AEFI reporting form contains 50 variables to be completed by health assistants on paper-based forms (established method), and the DHIS2 tracker contains the identical variable set as a paper-based AEFI form. A comparative analysis was conducted between these two data sources, field by field, with the number count and percentages using McNemar’s test.ResultsNinety-five paper-based AEFI reporting forms were found to be consistent with the DHIS2 AEFI tracker system (two hard copies were misplaced in two sites), and these matched AEFI cases were assessed for overall data completeness in the paper-based forms and the DHIS2 system. We found that 75.04% of the variable entries were available in paper-based forms, whereas 67.87% were available in the DHIS2 system (p < 0.001). The name of the patient, date of birth, AEFI (signs and symptoms), and date of AEFI onset were documented appropriately in the paper-based AEFI and the DHIS2 Tracker. Other variables were found to be either overreported or underreported in the DHIS2.ConclusionAt this early stage of the DHIS2 implementation, the data completeness of mandatory field entries was moderate. We recommend periodic supportive supervision by health management teams, including regular data feedback to improve completeness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01174-w.Keywords: AEFI, DHIS 2, Data completeness, Bangladesh

Background

Immunization is one of the most effective public health tools for protecting people from vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs), and this tool has saved millions of lives globally [1]. The safety of these vaccines is of great concern for all recipients, and this safety is monitored by identifying adverse events following immunization (AEFI). AEFI monitoring is vital in ensuring vaccine safety by tracking and evaluating any health events that arise after vaccination [1]. AEFI refers to any untoward medical occurrence that follows vaccination, which may not necessarily have a causal association with the vaccine used [2]. AEFI are typically mild, although they may be life-threatening on rare occasions, which is called serious AEFI, with an estimated rate of one serious AEFI case per 1,000,000 population/year [3].

The Government of Bangladesh’s Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) intends to protect all eligible recipients from VPDs by administering life-saving vaccines through routine immunization services from 1979 and continuous AEFI surveillance at the facility level in 2003 and the community level in 2006, which includes systematic monitoring and reporting of AEFI cases as part of its commitment to vaccine safety [4, 5]. Surveillance of AEFI by the EPI is an effective process for ensuring immunization safety, detecting and responding to AEFI promptly, reducing the negative impact on health, maintaining public confidence in the immunization program, and contributing to the overall quality of immunization services [2]. An effective vaccine safety surveillance system also supports informed decision-making, helping authorities decide whether a vaccine should continue to be used, modified, or withdrawn in cases where adverse events are deemed linked [2].

Globally, the District Health Information System Version 2 (DHIS2) platform was established in 1994, and this platform has improved health information data in 130 countries [6]. DHIS2 supports low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) by generating high-quality health data for evidence-based decision-making, monitoring, and policy implementation [7] and promotes consistent data collection, processing, and utilization [8]. In 2009, the DHIS2 platform was introduced for Bangladesh’s healthcare system, and the amount of data reported through DHIS2 has increased exponentially [9]. EPI, with the technical support of the World Health Organization (WHO), established an online-based AEFI reporting system within the software DHIS2 in 2019 to improve AEFI surveillance [1]. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, this application was executed in April 2021 [1]. EPI, Bangladesh, is also maintaining a paper-based AEFI reporting system parallel to the DHIS2 system in collaboration with the WHO at this initial implementation phase of the DHIS2 [1]. The AEFI surveillance in Bangladesh is both health facility and community-based passive surveillance. The doctor and other staff available in the facility are responsible for facility-based AEFI reporting. The field workers are responsible for community-based reporting. Initially, the hard copy is filled out by the responsible staff, and then the data from the hard copy is transferred to the DHIS2 system. The paper-based AEFI report was the established method before the DHIS2 introduction, and it remains an officially recognized arrangement under the current national regulatory guidelines. This paper-based system provides redundancy to ensure data integrity during the transition to full digital reporting, which confirms the safeguard against connectivity between the hard and digital copies. Robust AEFI surveillance has become increasingly critical in the post-COVID-19 era because of heightened public sensitivity to vaccine safety and hesitancy surge [10, 11]. However, the effectiveness of such systems heavily depends on the completeness, accuracy, and timeliness of the data entered [12]. Additionally, low reporting rates and data quality issues may erode public trust and reduce vaccine uptake [13]. The AEFI reporting rate was also found significantly lower in Pakistan than UK in 2021 after COVID-19 vaccines; this was due to the early implementation stage of the national pharmacovigilance system in Pakistan [14]. Bangladesh revealed several challenges, including a lack of automated aggregated data reporting from the AEFI individual reporting forms, the collection of redundant data, and staff apprehension to fill out long forms. Although the DHIS2 platform has been widely adopted in Bangladesh, and identified issues related to data entry accuracy, completeness, and limited use of data for decision-making [15].

Although DHIS2 helped improve timeliness and completeness of data reporting but substantial challenges remained, like insufficient training of human resources, slow internet connectivity, and shortage of devices. Day by day, DHIS2 has been widely adopted across LMICs; most existing studies have focused on its national-level performance or long-term implementation outcomes. There is a lack of empirical data describing the data completeness during the early phases of DHIS2 rollout for the AEFI reporting systems. Understanding the data gaps is vital for strengthening immunization programs and meeting global vaccine safety standards. Accurate and quality AEFI data will ensure the safety and effectiveness of immunization programs, contribute to community well-being by improving vaccine coverage, which is vital for protecting the health of individuals at the community level. This study aims to assess the completeness of AEFI data reported in DHIS2 during the early implementation phase through existing paper-based AEFI surveillance systems. This study examined the data completeness at the case level, whereas previous studies have primarily focused on aggregate DHIS2 data. This analysis also revealed common data gaps or missing variables in AEFI reporting forms and in the DHIS2 platform.

Methods

Site selection

Initially, we reviewed the 2023 AEFI data record of the EPI, which was obtained from the WHO through the EPI program head, to select the data quality assessment (DQA) sites for this study. The desk review was completed in the second week of February 2024 to calculate the AEFI reporting rate for this facility-based cross-sectional study. The AEFI reporting rate was measured as follows:

The survival infants were calculated with the national Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) using the following formula. Surviving infants = Live births×(1 − IMR). We selected three divisions, Chattogram, Sylhet, and Rangpur, from the eight divisions of Bangladesh, using percentile cut-offs based on the AEFI reporting rate across all divisions to ensure inclusion of both higher- and lower-reporting sites, which were reported four or more AEFI cases in 2023.

The multistage cluster sampling technique was used with three stages of clustering: division, district, and upazila, and random selection was not applied at any stage since divisions, districts, and upazilas were purposively chosen based on accessibility, security, and AEFI reporting rate. First, the divisions were categorized into high-, medium-, and low-performing groups according to the AEFI reporting rate. The AEFI reporting rate > 66th percentile was defined as a high-performing division, that within the > 33rd to 66th percentile was defined as a mid-performing group, and that ≤ 33rd percentile was defined as a low-performing group. Chattogram, Sylhet, and Rangpur were selected as the high-, medium-, and low-performing divisions, respectively. One district was then chosen from each division based on the highest AEFI count and not less than four AEFI cases, and two upazilas were selected from each selected district, one urban and one rural site. A total of 97 AEFI-reported cases were found in the WHO dataset after an initial desk review of these six selected sites (Fig. 1). We moved to these six selected study sites for data collection between March 2024 and June 2024.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval of the protocol (PR 24011) was obtained from the research review committee on 8 February 2024 and the ethical review committee on 9 March 2024 of the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr, b). We also collected permission letters from the EPI headquarters, Bangladesh, before visiting the study sites to collect secondary data extraction without any personal identification. Written informed consent was obtained from the authorized person at the specific sites before initiation of the data quality and completeness assessment.

Data collection

Before initiation of the study, the DQA tool was piloted in Gazipur Sadar Upazila Health Complex on 20 March 2024; that site was not a part of the main study. This piloting ensured the feasibility of data collection in the TAB (Samsung Galaxy A7 lite) from both the paper-based AEFI form and the DHIS2 system by the trained data collectors under the supervision of the study investigators. We collected the photocopies of the paper-based AEFI forms as references. Two independent evaluators checked the same data to ensure consistency and reliability of the collected data using the DQA tool. The findings from each evaluator were compared to identify discrepancies. In case of discrepancy, the paper-based forms were considered as authoritative method.

An individual case-based DQA form (Supplementary material 1) was used to assess AEFI data completeness through the paper-based AEFI forms and the DHIS2 AEFI tracker capture at the selected sites. This case-based DQA form was prepared on the basis of the AEFI reporting form, taken from the latest approved form and available in the operational guidelines for AEFI surveillance and response, Bangladesh [16]. A TAB-based program in offline mode was prepared for data entry and linked with an SQL server. This system was developed in the Android operating system with an SQL server. Data from paper-based AEFI forms and the DHIS2 AEFI tracker capture app were separately entered into the TAB. During our site visit, we collected photocopies of all the paper-based AEFI forms for data validation by the data management team. Our data management team also collected the DHIS2 system raw data from the WHO statistician for data validation. Once all the case information was validated, the identifiable data were exported to an Excel file and underwent a cleaning process before analysis. A comparative analysis was conducted to determine the differences between the paper-based forms and the DHIS2 AEFI tracker app to assess the data quality through data completeness via the standard software R (version 4.3.3). All electronic data were encrypted and stored in password-protected files accessible only to the study team. According to our study protocol, the information will be retained for up to three years to allow for quality review of the research report or publications, after which the dataset will be permanently deleted.

Data analysis process

Data completeness refers to the extent to which data elements are recorded fully, partially, or not entered with any required value. We presented the variables as filled completely, partially filled, and missing (blank field) for all mandatory and not mandatory fields. This analysis aimed to identify the similarities and dissimilarities between the routine immunization-related AEFI case-based data entered into the DHIS2 tracker app and those recorded on paper-based AEFI reporting forms (existing standard system). The AEFI reporting form contains seven sections: patient details, reporter details, vaccine center details, vaccine details, types of adverse events, dates of AEFI onset, and AEFI investigations and classifications. Among these, the initial six sections contained 50 variables (38 mandatory fields, 7 not mandatory fields, and 5 fields related to diluent-only used for BCG and MR recipients) to be completed by the health assistants in the paper-based AEFI form (Supplementary material 2), and these data were subsequently entered into the DHIS2 system by a data entry operator. We presented the types of adverse events and the dates of AEFI onset together in a single table, as they belong to the same category, where the types of adverse events and their onset dates, and reporting dates were mentioned.

A comparative analysis was conducted between the two data sources, field by field, with the number count and percentages, as the DHIS2 tracker contains the identical variable set as a paper-based AEFI form. Hence, the percentage difference in completeness between the DHIS2 AEFI tracker capture app and the paper-based reporting form was measured. These processes were performed by subtracting the percentages of completeness in DHIS2 from the percentages of completeness in the paper-based AEFI report form for each field. The differences between the paper-based AEFI data and the DHIS2 data were classified as consistent reporting, underreporting, or overreporting.

Consistent reporting was defined as the data elements that were documented appropriately in the paper-based AEFI reporting form and the DHIS2 Tracker. Underreporting was defined as the data elements that were completed on the paper-based reporting AEFI forms but not completed in the DHIS2 Tracker. Overreporting means that the data elements were completed in the DHIS2 Tracker but not in the paper-based AEFI reporting forms.

Discordant counts between paper-based records and DHIS2 entries were assessed to evaluate data completeness. Differences between these two sources were analyzed using McNemar’s test for paired samples, applying the exact version of the test when cell counts were small. Section-wise completeness was summarized using the median percent of completeness with Interquartile Range (IQR) for both data sources. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed to compare per-case completeness distributions between paper-based forms and DHIS2. We also applied a generalized estimating equations (GEE) model with an exchangeable correlation structure to account for repeated fields per case, adjusting for site, and reported the odds ratio (OR) of completeness in DHIS2 compared to the paper-based form with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values. A 5% significance level was used for all statistical tests. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2.

Results

A total of 97 reported AEFI cases (97/1212, 8% of national AEFI caseload) were identified in DHIS2 after the completion of DQA at the six selected study sites between January to December 2023. However, we found that ninety-five (95) paper-based AEFI reporting forms were consistent with the DHIS2 AEFI Tracker system (two hard copies were not found at two sites, one form Chattogram CC Zone 5 and one form Thakurgaon Sadar Upazila), and these matched AEFI cases were utilized for this data completeness assessment. We calculated the overall data completeness in the paper-based forms and the DHIS2 AEFI system and found 75.04% of the variable entries in the AEFI paper-based reporting forms compared to 67.87% in the DHIS2 AEFI system was a statistically significant difference (7.17% higher in paper form; 95% CI: 5.07, 9.28; p < 0.001) (Table 1). Very few variables (1/3610, 0.03%) were partially filled (considering 38 mandatory fields) in the AEFI paper-based reporting forms, but no variable was partially filled in the DHIS2 AEFI application.

The DQA showed both over- and underreporting regarding completeness across six sections of the AEFI report. The phone number, village, ward number, union, name of the vaccine center, mohalla of vaccine center, union of the vaccine center, vaccine name, date of vaccination, batch/lot number of vaccine, expiry date of vaccine, diluent name, batch/lot number of diluent, expiry date of diluent, date of reconstitution, and time of reconstitution were found as ≥ 20% under-reported among all variables.

DHIS2 demonstrated significantly lower data completeness (65.79%) compared to the paper-based AEFI form (68.42%), with an OR of 0.68 (95% CI: 0.60–0.77) when calculating section-wise per case data completeness. The median completeness was significantly lower in DHIS2 for ‘Patient’s details’ (69.23% vs. 84.62%, OR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.51–0.71), ‘Vaccine center details’ (57.14% vs. 85.71%, OR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.40–0.75), and ‘Vaccine and diluent details’ (0% vs. 66.67%, OR: 0.26, 95% CI: 0.15–0.44). Conversely, DHIS2 showed higher completeness in the ‘Reporter’s details’ Sect. (85.71% vs. 42.86%, OR: 1.93, 95% CI: 1.40–2.65). For ‘AEFI details’, both systems had equal median completeness (80%), but DHIS2 had a slightly higher proportion of completely filled cases (p = 0.019, OR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.09–1.74), indicating better performance in this section (Supplementary material 3).

In the “Patient Details” section, the data completeness of the mandatory variables (n = 13) dropped significantly (95% CI: 4.89, 11.71; p < 0.001) from 81.54% (paper-based) to 73.20% (DHIS2) (Table 1). Only two variables, patient name and date of birth, were consistently documented across both the paper-based AEFI reporting forms and the DHIS2 AEFI tracker application. Several key demographic fields, including mothers’ name, phone number, house number, village, ward number, and union, were found to be underreported in the DHIS2 system. Conversely, fields such as sex, father’s name, upazila, district, and division appeared to be overreported in the DHIS2 system compared with the original paper forms (Table 2).

A review of the “Vaccine and Diluent Details” section, the data completeness of the mandatory variables (n = 6) dropped significantly (95% CI: 26.08, 37.43; p < 0.001) from 63.86% (paper-based) to 32.11% (DHIS2) (Table 1). Site-wise analysis (Supplementary Material 4) showed variation in data completeness across divisions and facilities. Chattogram CC Zone 5 demonstrated moderate completeness (paper: 60.5%, DHIS2: 57.9%, p = 0.052), while Banshkhali, Sylhet Sadar, and Fenchuganj showed higher paper-based completeness (92–95%) compared to DHIS2 (79–82%, p < 0.05). Overall, facilities with higher case volume (e.g., Chattogram) tended to show lower DHIS2 completeness.

Discussion

The missing or incorrect data elements within the AEFI reporting system, like misclassification of vaccine batch, had a profound impact on the quality of data analysis and the subsequent decision-making processes for policymakers. The introduction of the DHIS2 platform aims to rationalize complete data reporting to improve data quality. By ensuring timely and accurate information, the DHIS2 platform enhances visibility into service delivery and outcomes, supports evidence-based decision-making, and strengthens trust among stakeholders, which contributes to more effective healthcare delivery. This assessment of data completeness for AEFI reporting at six study sites in Bangladesh highlights critical inconsistencies between the paper-based AEFI reporting form and the DHIS2 Tracker system at the early implementation stage.

Our findings indicate that the overall data completeness of the paper-based forms was greater than that of the DHIS2 AEFI system. In this study, only 75% of the AEFI paper-based form variables were filled, whereas 68% of the variable entries were found in the DHIS2 AEFI tracker application, and the difference was statistically significant (CI:5.07, 9.28; p < 0.001). A previous study in Nigeria revealed that aggregated routine immunization data completeness rates through DHIS2 increased intensely from 2014 to 2019 [17]. This paper presents a different type of data reporting approach from ours, but it explains trend variations, as aggregate systems capture overall service performance, while individual case-based surveillance provides more detailed insights. However, the AEFI online reporting system was implemented in 2021; the data analyzed in this study represent the early phase of system adoption, and the system adjustments during this period may have contributed to underreporting and discrepancies in AEFI patterns. These early-implementation effects should be considered when interpreting the relatively low reporting rates. We found that vaccine-related information was mostly missing from the DHIS2-based AEFI system, as vaccine details were not mandatory in the system. Our recommendation from the study findings is that vaccine-related information, like the name of the vaccine, date of vaccination, time of vaccination, dose, batch/lot number, and expiry date, should be assigned as a mandatory field in the digital system to achieve and maintain data completeness. For efficient operation of these, the implementation of mandatory dropdowns for vaccine name (cannot move to next field), barcode scanning for batch numbers, and auto-population of facility metadata to reduce manual entry. Routine DQAs, targeted trainings, and built-in system validation rules are also recommended to ensure overall data reliability and integrity. Achieving completeness together with timeliness and accuracy ensures that health data are not only comprehensive but also reliable and actionable for evidence-based decision-making. In Ghana, the deployment of the DHIS2 app significantly improved the data completeness of AEFI reporting and enhanced the surveillance infrastructure due to the high knowledge of the system among the health workers [18].

Several key demographic fields, such as the mother’s name, phone number, house number, village, ward number, and union, in the ‘patient details’ section, were underreported, indicating inconsistencies or omissions during data transfer from paper-based forms to the digital system. These types of underreporting might decrease the capacity for effective follow-up and case linkage, and this is also suggested that health workers need better training on the importance of filling in demographic fields to ensure geospatial mapping. In the Bangladesh context, all AEFI participants were well known to the particular EPI staff, as specific healthcare workers were responsible for the specific area of EPI vaccination [19]. In contrast, certain variables, such as sex, father’s name, and administrative location fields (upazila, district, division), were more frequently populated in DHIS2 than in the original paper forms. This suggests potential overreporting, which may not reflect improved data capture but rather the possible use of defaults or retrospective additions during data entry.

Most of the variables in the “Reporter’s Details” section showed overreporting in the digital system relative to the paper forms. The reporter’s full name, designation, upazila, district, and division were overreported in the digital system, relatively to the paper forms. The health workers may skip them under time pressure during completion of the hard copy or assume that the information is already known to the data entry operator, which causes more data completion in the ‘Reporter’s Details’ section in the digital system. The variables in the “Vaccine Center Details” section showed underreporting in the digital system relative to the paper forms. Variables specific to the actual vaccination site, such as the name of the center, ward, union, and moholla, were underreported in DHIS2, although the geographic fields (upazila, district, division) were more likely to be completed in the DHIS2 system, as these three fields were auto-filled. This pattern indicates a gap in capturing data at the local service delivery level, where adverse events are most directly observed and recorded. The vaccine center name is essential for tracing, linking, and responding to AEFI or outbreak signals for programmatic decision-making. The AEFI surveillance system in Ghana revealed that the DHIS2 system was useful, but overall, data were underreported due to factors like fear of victimization, no feedback from caregivers, work overload, and lack of motivation [18].

None of the variables in the “Vaccine and Diluent Details” section was consistently recorded in DHIS2, and more than half of the vaccine names were missing in the DHIS2 system, whereas all paper-based forms included 100% of vaccine names. These vaccine and diluent data elements, particularly vaccine manufacturer, batch/lot number, and diluent details, serve as the foundation for effective vaccine safety surveillance and signal detection for quality defects. The “Adverse Event Types and Dates of AEFI” section was relatively more complete in DHIS2. Descriptions of signs and symptoms and the date of onset were generally well documented across both data sources. However, the time of onset was often missing in DHIS2, and variables such as “date of reporting” and “reporting source” were more frequently present in the digital system than in the paper form. This suggests potential efforts of the healthcare staff prioritize the medical fields to meet system requirements during digital entry, although the lack of original documentation may impact the validity of such data. Evidence from both Kebbi State, Nigeria, and Ethiopia shows that while AEFI reporting systems are functional, their effectiveness is constrained by data quality gaps. In Kebbi State, the challenge lies in underreporting and poor documentation [20], whereas in Ethiopia, high reporting rates through DHIS2 are offset by persistent issues of completeness and accuracy [21]. This suggests that system establishment alone is insufficient; improvements in frontline reporting compliance and harmonization of multiple data streams are necessary to ensure reliable AEFI surveillance.

We found site-wise variation in data completeness, and heterogeneity was found in high reporting sites. Differences in infrastructure and resources (internet, computers, power supply), human capacity (training, data-entry staff), system maturity (early vs. established implementation), burden of reporting, and the strength of supervision. Previous studies of DHIS2 in LMICs have documented these as key challenges for data completeness, which may be responsible for such heterogeneity [8, 15, 18].

Despite the uniqueness and strengths of this study, it has several limitations. We visited only six sites with ≥ 4 AEFI cases were included for this AEFI data completeness assessment due to budget restrictions, which may not fully represent national-level data completeness trends, and these sites were selected purposively based on accessibility, security, and AEFI reporting rate. The purposive nature of site selection could introduce selection bias, as sites with higher AEFI reporting may also have more experience and better data completeness. We did not include any sites that reported less than 4 AEFI cases in 2023; hence, low-reporting sites may have different completeness profiles. We failed to measure the data completeness of planned 97 AEFI cases as two hard copies were misplaced in two sites, which was a potential bias of this study, but this did not affect overall completeness estimation. We presented all variables in three groups: filled completely, partially filled, and missing, whereas missing is optional for not mandatory fields. On the other hand, multiple comparisons were conducted across 38 mandatory fields, and no formal correction for multiple testing was applied to prevent false interpretation of results. There is a lack of qualitative insight in this study, and it relies solely on quantitative comparisons between DHIS2 and paper forms. Future qualitative research should be incorporated to explore user experiences, reporting practices, and contextual factors influencing data quality. Future study can be planned for in-depth interviews with data entry staff to understand the reason behind missing vaccine names/batch numbers in the DHIS2 system. Such insights would complement quantitative comparisons and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the DHIS2 implementation. It did not explore health workers’ perspectives, motivations, or challenges through interviews, which are critical to understanding the behavior behind data entry practices, though the staff’s awareness of the audit may have led to temporarily improved reporting practices in the DHIS2. A previous study in Bangladesh revealed that the main barrier to using DHIS2 was that many healthcare workers did not fully appreciate its importance [22].

Periodic supportive supervision by health management teams and structured data quality audits comparing paper and digital records should be institutionalized. This helps to identify where specific data losses or inconsistencies occur in the reporting chain. Mandatory data entry protocols are linked to system validation checks in the DHIS AEFI tracker app, which could improve data consistency. Fields that are frequently underreported may need to be made mandatory or redesigned for clarity. Ongoing capacity-building efforts should target frontline health workers and data entry personnel. Emphasis should be placed not only on the technical use of DHIS2 but also on the importance of timely, complete, and accurate documentation for patient safety and public health monitoring.

Conclusion

Until full digital adoption of the AEFI reporting system, harmonizing paper and electronic workflows is essential to ensure consistent and complete data capturing. In conclusion, DHIS2 offers an important platform for centralized AEFI surveillance. The AEFI surveillance system in Bangladesh is well organized with standardized data collection tools. This surveillance system is useful for estimating morbidity due to AEFI. This study demonstrates that overall AEFI data completeness is moderate in the DHIS2 but lower in the critical vaccine fields, at this early implementation stage in Bangladesh, although several critical domains remain suboptimal. Enhancing the reliability of digital AEFI data requires not only technological improvements but also targeted policy and operational responses focused on the quality of the data entered at the point of care.
