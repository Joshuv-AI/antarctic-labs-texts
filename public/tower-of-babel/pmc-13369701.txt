Development and validation of a machine learning-based clinical decision support tool for stratifying intravenous medication risk in hospitalized patients with heart failure
Authors: Yang Yang, ZeJie Xu, Yu Peilin, Haidong Li, Hongmei Wang, Xuefeng Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHospitalized patients with heart failure (HF) frequently receive multiple high-risk intravenous (IV) medications, placing them at a substantial risk of clinically significant drug-related problems (DRPs). Timely severity-based stratification of IV medication-related risks remains challenging, particularly in the context of complex regimens and organ dysfunction.AimTo develop and externally validate a clinically applicable machine learning–based stratification tool for classifying IV medication-related risk severity in hospitalized patients with HF and to support pharmacist-led medication safety management through a web-based clinical decision support tool.MethodThis multicenter retrospective study included 1,884 adult patients hospitalized with HF from seven tertiary hospitals, with an independent external validation cohort of 100 patients. IV medication-related DRPs were identified and classified by senior clinical pharmacists using the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe (PCNE) DRP classification (version 9.1) and stratified by severity using the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCC MERP) index. Medication risk severity (levels C-F) was used as a multiclass stratification outcome. Candidate clinical and medication-related variables reflecting patient characteristics, organ function, and medication burden were selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and six machine learning algorithms for multiclass risk stratification were developed and compared.ResultsOverall, 1405 patients (74.6%) experienced at least one IV medication–related DRP, with treatment safety problems predominating. Eleven clinically interpretable predictors, including neutrophil percentage, fibrinogen, serum albumin, and creatinine clearance were retained in the final models. In internal validation, the random forest (RF) model achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.934), whereas in external validation, the artificial neural network (ANN) demonstrated the best performance (AUC = 0.921). Considering its consistent performance across datasets, ANN was selected as the final model, achieving AUC of 0.889 and 0.921 in the internal and external validation, respectively. Based on this model, a web-based clinical decision support tool was developed to provide individualized IV medication risk stratification at the point-of-care.ConclusionA machine learning-based clinical decision support tool that incorporates routinely available clinical and medication-related variables can accurately stratify IV medication-related risk severity in hospitalized patients with HF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11096-026-02147-7.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents an advanced stage of various cardiac diseases and remains a major global public health challenge [1]. According to the 2021 Global Burden of Disease Study, approximately 55.5 million individuals worldwide live with HF, including more than 8.9 million in China, where hospitalization rates for HF continue to increase [2, 3]. The rising prevalence of HF contributes substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures. Previous analyses have estimated that the global annual cost of HF exceeds USD 108 billion, largely driven by frequent hospitalizations and long-term pharmacotherapy, with costs expected to increase further in aging populations [4]. As pharmacotherapy remains central to HF management, medication safety has become an increasingly important component of quality inpatient care, particularly in hospitalized patients with complex treatment requirements.

Intravenous (IV) medication remains a cornerstone of treatment for patients hospitalized with HF, particularly during acute decompensation, when diuretics, positive inotropic agents, vasodilators, and IV antimicrobials are frequently required to relieve symptoms and to stabilize hemodynamics. However, the IV route bypasses first-pass metabolism and delivers drugs directly into the systemic circulation, increasing the likelihood of rapid onset and potentially serious adverse events (AEs). Data from the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) indicate that serious AEs occur more frequently with IV administration than with other routes [5]. Although the World Health Organization recommends IV administration only when clinically necessary [6], its extensive use in routine inpatient care continues to pose substantial challenges in terms of medication safety and monitoring.

Patients hospitalized with HF are often older, have multiple comorbidities, and have impaired renal or hepatic function, and commonly require polypharmacy, frequent dose adjustments, and complex combination therapy [7, 8]. These factors substantially increase the risk of medication-related injuries [9]. IV administration is associated with a high incidence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and drug-related problems (DRPs), which may affect multiple organ systems and result in death in severe cases. National pharmacovigilance data indicate that > 60% of reported ADRs are related to the IV route, with > 70% classified as serious [10]. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic alterations, reduced drug clearance, and diminished physiological reserve in advanced HF further increase susceptibility to DRPs [11, 12]. Given the intensity of IV drug exposure in this population, the early identification of patients at increased risk of medication is a key priority for clinical pharmacists and multidisciplinary care teams [13]. However, existing inpatient ADR/DRP studies have predominantly focused on binary event prediction, with limited attention to the severity-based stratification of IV medication–related risks in hospitalized patients with HF [14, 15].

Machine learning (ML) techniques have shown promise in medical risk prediction by capturing complex nonlinear relationships within multidimensional clinical data [16–18]. Several ML studies on HF have demonstrated promising performance in predicting outcomes, such as readmission, mortality, and hospitalization risk, across different clinical settings [19, 20]. However, these models have primarily focused on disease prognosis rather than predicting DRPs, particularly those associated with IV medication use. Recent studies have highlighted the growing application of ML in clinical pharmacy, including medication safety surveillance, pharmacovigilance, and clinical decision support systems [21, 22]. Nevertheless, the use of ML for severity-based stratification of IV medication–related risks remains limited [15]. Given that the severity of IV medication–related risk may vary according to patients’ clinical and sociodemographic characteristics, there is a clear need for clinically applicable ML–based tools to stratify hospitalized patients with HF according to IV medication-related risk severity and to support targeted pharmacist-led interventions.

Aim

This study aimed to develop and externally validate a clinically applicable machine learning–based tool for stratifying IV medication-related risk severity in hospitalized patients with HF and to support pharmacist-led medication safety management using a web-based clinical decision support tool.

Method

Study design and data source

This multicenter retrospective study utilized electronic medical records (EMRs) from seven tertiary hospitals in China between January 2020 and December 2024. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) hospitalized with a confirmed diagnosis of HF and complete intravenous medication records were eligible for inclusion. For patients with multiple admissions, only the first eligible hospitalization was included to avoid duplicate data. Patients with missing key clinical or medication data, unclear diagnoses, or incomplete information on intravenous medication were excluded.

Study population

Hospitalized patients with HF were identified using discharge diagnoses recorded in EMR. Patients were eligible if HF was documented as a discharge diagnosis in the EMR, regardless of the HF phenotype, in accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure (2024) [23]. All included patients received at least one intravenous medication during hospitalization, reflecting routine inpatient HF management.

Outcome definition: intravenous medication risk severity

The primary outcome was the severity-stratified IV medication risk, categorized according to the NCC MERP severity framework (categories C–F) based on pharmacist-assessed IV medication–related problems during hospitalization. Two senior clinical pharmacists independently identified and classified these problems using the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe (PCNE) classification system (version 9.1) [24]. The clinical severity of the identified DRPs was subsequently graded using the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCC MERP) index as an operational severity framework (categories C–F), consistent with previous DRP studies [25–27]. Disagreements in DRP identification or severity grading were resolved by consensus with a third senior clinical pharmacist. Although formal inter-rater agreement statistics were not recorded, DRP identification followed standardized PCNE criteria and was performed by experienced clinical pharmacists with consensus adjudication, which has been widely used in similar DRP studies and supports the reliability of pharmacist-based assessments. Outcome assessors were not blinded to the predictor information, as DRP adjudication inherently requires a review of medication orders, laboratory results, and relevant clinical records. Medication risk severity (categories C–F) was used as the multiclass outcome for the development of the risk stratification tool. Lower NCC MERP categories (A–B) and catastrophic outcomes (G–I) were not observed in this hospitalized cohort and were therefore not included in the stratification analysis.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was estimated using the formula N = EPV × n/[P1 × (1 + P2)] [28], where EPV represents the number of events per variable, n is the number of candidate predictors, P1 denotes the estimated probability of DRPs among hospitalized patients with HF, and P2 accounts for the anticipated data loss. In this study, the EPV was set at 10, n at 30 candidate predictors, P1 at 0.66, based on a previous study reporting the prevalence of DRPs among hospitalized patients with HF [29], and P2 at 0.20. Accordingly, a minimum sample size of 379 patients was required. The final cohort of 1,884 patients substantially exceeded this requirement, supporting robust model development and validation.

Data collection and candidate variables

Clinical data were extracted from EMR systems using standardized data collection procedures across all participating centers. Candidate variables were grouped into four domains: (1) demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, body mass index, allergy history, smoking, and alcohol use); (2) hospitalization-related variables (e.g., length of stay, discharge diagnosis); (3) laboratory parameters (e.g., hematologic indices, hepatic and renal function tests, metabolic markers, brain natriuretic peptide, and left ventricular ejection fraction); and (4) medication-related variables, including the total number of medications received during the entire hospital stay, the number of IV medications, and major therapeutic classes (e.g., diuretics, inotropes, antibiotics, and cardiotonic agents).

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Data preprocessing included the removal of duplicate records and implausible values, with missing data imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Potential outliers were reviewed in collaboration with clinical pharmacists to ensure their clinical plausibility. Categorical variables were numerically encoded, and continuous variables were standardized prior to model development.

Feature selection was performed using a two-stage method. Univariate analyses were performed to identify candidate variables associated with the severity of medication risk (P < 0.05). Second, these variables were entered into a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model with tenfold cross-validation to identify the most parsimonious variable set for constructing the risk-stratification model.

Model development and internal validation

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and an internal validation set (30%) using stratified sampling to preserve the distribution of medication risk severity across the study centers. Six ML models for multiclass risk stratification were developed and compared: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Naïve Bayes (NB), and artificial neural network (ANN). All models were trained using the same predictor set, with hyperparameters optimized using cross-validation, where applicable.

Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as the primary discrimination metric, along with the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), Youden index, and F1 score. Multiclass ROC analyses were conducted using micro-averaging.

External validation

External validation was performed using an independent cohort of hospitalized patients with HF from a separate tertiary hospital outside the seven-center derivation network between 2023 and 2024. Data preprocessing and variable definitions were identical to those used in the derivation cohort. All six models were evaluated on an external dataset using the same performance metrics to assess their generalizability. The model demonstrating the most consistent performance across internal and external validations was selected as the final risk stratification model.

Web-based clinical decision support tool

Based on the optimal model, a web-based clinical decision support tool was developed to facilitate pharmacist-assisted severity risk stratification of IV medication use. The system integrates HTML, JavaScript, and CSS to provide an interactive interface in which clinicians can input patient-specific variables and obtain real-time estimates of the severity of medication risk. The tool was designed to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment by assisting in the prioritization of patients for targeted medication reviews according to stratified risk severity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) and SPSS software (version 26.0). Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (P25, P75), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analyses were considered for feature selection in the training set. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. This study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI guidelines for prediction model studies [30].

Ethics approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (approval number: 2024-211-01; approval date: September 25, 2024).

Intravenous drug–related problems and risk severity

Based on the PCNE-DRP classification, 1405 of 1884 patients (74.6%) experienced at least one DRP, with a total of 6293 instances of DRPs. Potential ADRs (P2.1) were identified in 68.7% of patients. Predictor selection

In the univariate analyses, 42 variables were significantly associated with IV medication-related risk severity, spanning the demographic, clinical, laboratory, and medication-related domains. Subsequent LASSO regression with tenfold cross-validation identified the optimal penalty parameter (λ = 0.0426, λ + 1SE criterion). Eleven predictors with nonzero coefficients were retained, including allergy history, inflammatory markers, hepatic and renal function indices, glycemic parameters, and medication burden-related variables (Supplemental Table S4). Model development and internal validation

All six ML models were successfully developed. In internal validation, the RF model demonstrated the highest performance (AUC = 0.934, sensitivity = 0.825, specificity = 0.608, Youden index = 0.434), followed by XGBoost (AUC = 0.932) and DT (AUC = 0.917). External validation

The external validation cohort comprised 46 C-level, 39 D-level, and 15 E-level medication risk cases. Among the evaluated models, ANN demonstrated the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.921, sensitivity = 0.780, specificity = 0.640, Youden index = 0.419). The LR and RF models also showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.903 and 0.853, respectively), whereas the NB model demonstrated limited performance (AUC = 0.654). Except for the NB model, all algorithms had AUC values above 0.80, thus supporting acceptable external validity (Table 3 and Fig. 3B).

Web-based clinical decision support tool

The ANN model selected for clinical deployment was implemented as a web-based IV medication risk stratification system (version 1.0) accessible via desktop, tablet, and smartphone platforms (Supplemental Fig. 1). Clinicians can enter routinely available patient variables to obtain an individualized classification of medication risk severity in real time, with visual outputs supporting the interpretation of stratified risk levels. The tool is intended to support pharmacist-led prioritization of medication reviews and targeted interventions in routine clinical practice.

Discussion

This multicenter study, conducted across seven tertiary hospitals, provides real-world evidence of the burden and characteristics of IV medication–related DRPs among hospitalized patients with HF. The overall incidence of DRPs was high (74.6%), with a mean of 3.34 events per patient, which is consistent with previous reports indicating that 50–90% of hospitalized patients with HF experience at least one DRP [29, 31]. Treatment safety-related problems and inappropriate drug selection predominated, highlighting that intravenous medication use represents a major medication safety challenge in inpatient HF care. These findings reinforce the need for a systematic medication review and proactive involvement of clinical pharmacists, particularly in patients receiving complex IV regimens, to reduce preventable medication-related harm.

By applying the NCC MERP index to define IV medication-related risk severity, the risk of medication in this cohort ranged from levels C to F, indicating that all identified events had reached the patient during hospitalization. Lower categories (A and B), which represent circumstances without drug exposure or patient impact, were not applicable in this study. Similarly, the most severe categories (G–I), indicating permanent harm or death, were not observed, suggesting that although clinically significant medication errors were common, catastrophic outcomes were relatively rare in this cohort. Severity stratification of IV medication risk using the NCC MERP index provides a clinically interpretable framework that aligns with routine pharmacist-led medication safety monitoring and prioritization of preventive interventions in hospitalized patients with HF.

Further analysis demonstrated a progressive increase in treatment safety–related DRPs with increasing medication risk severity, indicating that safety-related problems are the principal drivers of escalating IV medication risk. Drug selection errors, particularly inappropriate drug combinations and therapeutic duplication, are the most frequent underlying causes of DDIs. Typical high-risk examples include concomitant aminophylline and doxofylline use or combined furosemide and digoxin therapy, which may predispose patients to electrolyte disturbance and digoxin toxicity. In addition, the contraindicated use of drugs, such as levosimendan, in patients with severe hepatic or renal impairment highlights the importance of continuous monitoring, multidisciplinary communication, and pharmacist–physician collaboration in complex HF pharmacotherapy.

Using LASSO regression, 11 clinically relevant predictors of IV medication risk were identified from the initial pool of 65 variables. These predictors reflect the multifactorial nature of medication-related risks in HF, including comorbidities, organ dysfunction, inflammatory status, and medication burden in patients with HF. Although relatively few studies have specifically addressed the risk of IV medication in hospitalized patients with HF, the variables identified in our stratification tool overlap with factors reported in prior studies of ADR and DRP risk assessment, including renal and hepatic function indicators, medication load, and exposure to high-risk drugs [32, 33]. This concordance supports the clinical plausibility of the model and enhances its interpretability in routine clinical pharmacy practices.

A key methodological strength of this study was the adoption of a four-level outcome classification based on medication risk severity rather than a binary outcome. Severity-based stratification enables a more granular differentiation between mild, moderate, and severe medication risk states, which is particularly relevant for prioritizing pharmacist interventions in resource-limited inpatient settings. By capturing risk gradations, the model supports tailored clinical actions, such as routine monitoring for lower-risk patients and intensified surveillance or immediate pharmacist intervention for higher risk cases. This approach aligns with previous evidence suggesting that multiclass classification offers greater clinical insight than binary models by reflecting the progressive stages of patient risk [34].

In external validation, the ANN demonstrated the most consistent discriminative performance (AUC = 0.921) across the different IV risk categories. Rather than emphasizing algorithmic superiority, this finding suggests that ANN better accommodates complex nonlinear relationships among routinely collected clinical and laboratory variables. Compared with other evaluated models, including NB, LR, RF, DT, and XGBoost, ANN provided stable performance across internal and external datasets, supporting its clinical deployment. From a clinical perspective, improved discrimination facilitates more precise severity-based stratification of patients with heightened medication risk and enables more targeted pharmacist-led interventions, contributing to safer and more individualized medication management in patients with HF [35–38].

To support practical implementation, we developed a web-based IV medication risk stratification tool compatible with both desktop and mobile platforms. The tool was designed to align with clinical pharmacy workflows, allowing pharmacists and clinicians to rapidly stratify hospitalized patients with HF according to IV medication-related risk severity using routinely available data. By enabling real-time classification of IV medication-related risk severity at the point of care, this system may support the prioritization of medication review, enhance monitoring of high-risk patients, and facilitate more efficient allocation of clinical pharmacy resources. These findings are consistent with recent international literature highlighting the potential of artificial intelligence–enabled approaches to support clinical pharmacy practice and medication safety activities [21], although a prospective evaluation of real-world implementation is warranted.

This study had several limitations. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, and certain potentially relevant confounders, particularly nursing-related administration factors and real-time monitoring data, were not available. In addition, the medication variables were not defined at a highly granular level, and some clinically important classes, such as anticoagulants, were not analyzed separately from other drugs. Second, although the stratification system has been implemented as a standalone web-based tool, it has not yet been integrated into hospital EMR systems and therefore requires manual data entry. Future studies should focus on system integration and automation to enable seamless data extraction and real-time risk alerts in clinical workflows. Prospective validation and impact studies are warranted to determine whether the implementation of this tool translates to measurable reductions in medication-related harm. Furthermore, the calibration performance was not formally evaluated, and future studies should include a calibration assessment to better characterize the clinical reliability of the model.

Conclusion

This multicenter study developed and externally validated an ML–based clinical decision support tool for stratifying IV medication-related risk severity in hospitalized patients with HF. The artificial neural network showed stable and consistent performance in the internal and external validation cohorts, supporting its clinical applicability. By integrating routinely available clinical and medication-related variables, the resulting web-based stratification tool may help clinical pharmacists classify patients according to medication risk severity, prioritize medication reviews, and enhance proactive medication safety management in clinical practice.
