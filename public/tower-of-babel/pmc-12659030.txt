Association between multimarkers of metabolic malnutrition and inflammation and risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and lung function: a prospective cohort study of UK biobank
Authors: Chengyan Jin, Peiyan Hua, Chunguang Wang, Bin Wang, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) pathogenesis involves the cross-links between inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation leading to irreversible airflow limitation. The metabolic vulnerability index (MVX) integrates inflammation and metabolic malnutrition related metabolic biomarkers that may reflect these underlying mechanisms. This study aimed to assess the associations of MVX score with risk of incident COPD and lung function in the UK Biobank.MethodsThe sex-specific MVI score was developed as a composite biomarker, which integrates six nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based plasma biomarkers, including glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA), small high-density lipoprotein particles (sHDL), citrate, and the branched-chain amino acids (isoleucine, leucine, and valine). Multivariable Cox or linear regression model was used to assess the association of MVX score with risk of COPD and lung function level, respectively.ResultsA total of 240,873 participants were included. The results showed that per 1-SD increase in MVX score was associated with a 19% increased risk of COPD (HR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.16, 1.21). Furthermore, compared to low MVX score group (Q1), high MVX score group (Q4) was associated with 55% increased risk of COPD (HR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.45, 1.66). Furthermore, per 1-SD increase in MVX score was associated with decreased lung function level, including FVC (β: -67.55, 95% CI: -71.36, -63.74) and FEV1 (β: -52.24, 95% CI: -55.34, -49.14). Compared to low MVX score group (Q1), high MVX score group (Q4) was also associated with lower FVC (β: -174.78, 95% CI: -185.47, -164.10) and FEV1 (β: -135.72, 95% CI: -144.42, -127.02) levels. Furthermore, we observed the significant dose-response relationship between MVX score and COPD risk and lung function level.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that higher MVX score, reflecting inflammation and metabolic malnutrition, is significantly associated with increased COPD risk and impaired lung function. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent and typically irreversible airflow limitation. COPD represents a major global health challenge, ranking as the third leading cause of death worldwide and caused more than 4 million deaths in 2019 [1, 2]. Beyond its mortality impact, COPD accounted for 74.4 million global DALYs due to its progressive nature and frequent comorbidities [3]. Epidemiological evidence reveals that while smoking remains the predominant risk factor, a substantial proportion of COPD cases worldwide (up to 50% in some populations) are attributable to non-tobacco-related risk factors, such as air pollution, occupational exposures, and poorly controlled asthma [4]. Potential mechanisms underlying COPD pathogenesis in never-smokers include many cross-links between inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation [4–7]. Recent advances in metabolomics have provided new insights into COPD pathogenesis, suggesting that alterations in specific metabolic pathways may contribute to disease development and progression.

Circulating metabolites, shaped by both endogenous and exogenous factors, have emerged as promising biomarkers for various diseases and health conditions. Although observational studies have identified associations between specific metabolites and COPD risk and lung function levels [8–10], these individual metabolites alone often fail to reflect the systemic metabolic landscape. The recently proposed metabolic vulnerability index (MVX) integrates six plasma metabolic biomarkers including glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA), small high-density lipoprotein particles (sHDL), citrate, and the branched-chain amino acids (isoleucine, leucine, and valine) that effectively reflects systemic inflammation and metabolic malnutrition [11, 12]. Previous prospective cohort studies have shown that MVX is strongly associated with mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease, even after adjusting for established clinical risk factors [11–13]. Additionally, the cross-sectional studies revealed that higher MVX and inflammatory vulnerability index (IVX) were associated with both disability and neurodegeneration in patients with multiple sclerosis [14], as well as with liver transplantation and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [15].

This study aims to investigate the association between the MVX and risk of incident COPD using extensive longitudinal cohort data from the UK Biobank, and to assess the cross-sectional association between MVX and lung function levels. Given the role of metabolic dysregulation in the pathogenesis of COPD and pulmonary dysfunction, identifying reliable metabolic biomarkers may contribute to improved early detection, risk stratification, and personalized management across diverse disease etiologies.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large-scale prospective cohort study including over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years at enrollment between 2006 and 2010 across the United Kingdom. This resource provides extensive phenotypic and genotypic data, including detailed questionnaires, physical measurements, biological samples, and linkages to electronic health records (e.g., hospital admissions, primary care, and mortality data). The study design and data access procedures of the UK Biobank have been published elsewhere [16]. Ethical approval was obtained from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. For this study, we accessed approved data under Application Number 430,579, adhering to the data usage protocols of UK Biobank.

The analytical sample was derived from the UK Biobank cohort comprising 502,132 participants at baseline. For the analysis examining the association between MVX and risk of incident COPD, we sequentially excluded: (1) participants lacking plasma metabolomic biomarkers (n = 228,153); (2) those with prevalent COPD at baseline (n = 5,265); and (3) individuals with missing data on key covariates (n = 27,841). A total of 240,873 participants were included. For analyses assessing association between MVX and lung function, we applied additional exclusion criteria to the COPD-free population with plasma metabolomic biomarkers (n = 268,714), excluding: (1) participants with incomplete covariates data (n = 27,094); and (2) those lacking baseline spirometry measurements (n = 66,671), resulting in a final sample of 174,949 participants. Assessment of metabolic vulnerability index

The UK Biobank performed high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomic profiling on baseline EDTA plasma samples from a subset of participants using a commercially available platform (Nightingale Health, Finland). This platform quantifies 249 metabolic measures, including 168 directly measured biomarkers (e.g., lipoprotein sub-classes, fatty acids, and glycolysis-related metabolites) and 81 derived ratios and composites. The sex-specific MVX was developed as a composite biomarker that effectively reflects overall metabolic malnutrition and inflammation, which integrates six NMR-based plasma biomarkers, including GlycA (mmol/L), sHDL (mmol/L), citrate (mmol/L), and the branched-chain amino acids (isoleucine [mmol/L], leucine [mmol/L], and valine[mmol/L]). Detailed of MVX calculation process has been published elsewhere [11]. The MVX ranged from 1 to 100, with a higher score indicating greater metabolic vulnerability.

Outcome ascertainment

In the UK Biobank, incident cases of COPD were ascertained through multiple linked data sources to ensure comprehensive case capture. Primary identification relied on hospital inpatient records (ICD-10 codes J44) from national health registries, supplemented with primary care data, mortality records, and self-reported physician diagnoses [17]. Participants were followed up from baseline assessment until the date of diagnosis of COPD, date of loss to follow-up, date of death, or last date of follow-up (8 December 2022), whichever came first.

The spirometry measurements including forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1, L) and forced vital capacity (FVC, L) were collected at baseline assessment centers using Vitalograph Pneumotrac 6800 devices (Vitalograph Ltd, UK) by trained technicians. For each participant, at least two blows were recorded, with a third blow administered if the differences between either FEV1 or FVC values from the first two blows exceeded 5%. The highest values of FEV1 and FVC from technically satisfactory maneuvers were selected for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study population were described using mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (proportion) for categorical variables. We used the χ² statistic and t test to compare baseline characteristics of participants with and without COPD. Multivariable Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between MVX and risk of COPD. Multivariable-adjusted model was adjusted age, sex, townsend deprivation index, ethnicity, educational level, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol drinking, family history of chronic lung disease, NO2, NOX, PM2.5, PM2.5−10, and PM10. We selected these confounders on the basis of their associations with the outcomes of interest or a change in effect estimate of more than 10% [18]. Furthermore, multivariable linear regression models were used to estimate β coefficients and 95% CI for the associations between MVX and lung function level. The multivariable linear regression models did not include ethnicity adjustment because the spirometry analysis was restricted exclusively to White participants. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to assess the dose-response associations between MVX and lung function level and risk of COPD. We performed subgroup analyses to assess possible effect modifications by sex. Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses within strata defined by all confounding variables. A sensitivity analysis was performed on the outcome of COPD risk excluding the first 2 or 5 years of follow-up for each participants. In addition, we performed a discrimination analysis using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to identify the most influential components within the MVI. The non-zero coefficients (β) identify the variables retained by the model for each outcome. We also evaluated the incremental predictive value of the MVX score for COPD risk by incorporating it into a conventional risk factor model. The conventional model was adjusted for age, sex, townsend deprivation index, ethnicity, educational level, BMI, smoking status, alcohol drinking, family history of chronic lung disease, and exposure to air pollutants (NO₂, NOₓ, PM₂.₅, PM₂.₅-₁₀, and PM₁₀). The discriminatory power of the models with and without the MVX score was assessed using Harrell’s c-statistic. The likelihood ratio test was employed to examine whether the extended model provided a significantly better fit. Additionally, both Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were computed to measure the improvement in predictive performance. All analyses were performed in R version 3.5.1, and a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

The COPD group also had lower educational attainment and higher prevalence of obesity. Air pollution exposure was consistently higher among COPD cases across all measured pollutants. A significant positive dose-response relationship was observed between the MVX and risk of incident COPD (Fig. 2). The multivariate adjustment model showed that per 1-SD increase in MVX score was associated with a 19% higher risk of COPD (HR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.16, 1.21; P < 0.001). Furthermore, compared to low MVX score group (Q1), high MVX score group (Q4) was associated with 55% increased risk of COPD (HR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.45, 1.66; P < 0.001). Similar findings were also found in crude and age, sex-adjusted model (Table 2). In the subgroup analysis, the significant positive association between MVX score and risk of COPD existed across all subgroups of age, sex, townsend deprivation i+ndex, ethnicity, educational level, BMI, smoking status, alcohol drinking, family history of chronic lung disease, and air pollutants (Supplementary Table S2). A sensitivity analysis excluding the first 2 or 5 years of follow-up showed that the results did not change substantially (Supplementary Table S3). The LASSO analysis identified sHDL as the strongest protective factor, which was inversely associated with COPD risk and positively associated with both FVC and FEV₁. In contrast, GlycA and isoleucine were identified as risk factors, demonstrating positive associations with COPD risk and negative associations with lung function. Additionally, citrate emerged as a positive predictor of lung function volumes (Supplementary Table S4).

The results of incremental predictive value of the MVX score for COPD risk showed that the addition of MVX to the conventional risk factors-based model modest improved the prediction precision although the increase was not statistically significant (Harrell’s c-statistic: 0.828 versus 0.826, P = 0.472) (Supplementary Figure S1). However, the significant overall continuous NRI (0.134, 95% CI: 0.113, 0.162) and IDI (0.001, 95% CI: 0.001, 0.002) demonstrated that MVX meaningfully refines risk stratification.

Discussion

The present study is the first, to our knowledge, to investigate the associations between MVX, a composite biomarker reflecting systemic metabolic malnutrition and inflammation, and risk of COPD and lung function. Participants with higher MVX scores showed a 55% increased long-term risk of incident COPD relative to those with low MVX, suggesting the potential role of metabolic dysregulation in COPD pathogenesis, independent of traditional risk factors such as smoking and socioeconomic status. Furthermore, the inverse association between MVX score and lung function levels (FEV₁ and FVC) suggests metabolic malnutrition and inflammation may contribute to airway obstruction and impaired lung function. We also observed a significant dose-response relationship between MVX score and COPD risk and lung function. Importantly, these associations remained stable across diverse demographic and lifestyle subgroups, including stratification by age, sex, townsend deprivation index, ethnicity, educational level, BMI, smoking status, alcohol drinking, family history of chronic lung disease, and air pollutants.

Identifying and validating metabolites related to lung function and COPD risk can help to better understand mechanisms of lung health and facilitate biomarker development. A recent case-control study included 88 matched COPD patients and healthy controls, and identifying 137 metabolic biomarkers were associated with risk of COPD [9]. Similarly, another cross-sectional analysis of serum metabolomic in 4,742 participants identified 95 metabolites associated with FEV₁, 100 with FVC (including branched-chain amino acids), and 17 linked to COPD risk [19]. Additionally, sphingomyelins and unsaturated fatty acid-containing lipids have been found to effectively differentiate COPD patients from controls [20]. In two independent adult cohorts, 34 metabolites were found and validated to be significantly associated with FEV1 [10]. Taken together, existing studies have focused on individual metabolic biomarkers in relation to COPD risk and lung function, whereas epidemiological evidence on composite metabolic indices for lung health remains scarce.

COPD is a progressive respiratory disease characterized by chronic inflammation and structural airway remodeling. This remodeling process is driven by impaired epithelial repair and the accumulation of fibroblasts, which contribute to excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. Notably, epithelial cells undergoing abnormal repair have been identified as a potential source of fibroblasts [21]. In addition, inhalational exposures—such as cigarette smoke and viral pathogens—are well-established triggers that activate the innate immune and inflammatory systems [22–24]. This activation prompts a robust inflammatory cell infiltration within the mucosal, submucosal, and glandular compartments of the airway, culminating in hyperplastic changes in the epithelium, mucus hypersecretion, and thickening of the small airway walls [25]. These pathological changes, including airway fibrosis and luminal narrowing, form a continuum that progressively impairs respiratory function. This process occurs not only in individuals with early, subclinical lung function impairment but also in those who eventually develop clinically overt COPD. While the cross-sectional nature of our study cannot establish the causal inferences regarding MVX and lung function decline, the strong inverse association we observed provides a crucial foundation for the hypothesis that the MVX score may capture the downstream molecular consequences of the mechanical and fibrotic narrowing of the airways, which is a central driver of fixed airflow obstruction in COPD. This proposition is strongly supported by our longitudinal analysis, where a higher baseline MVX score significantly predicted an increased risk of future COPD diagnosis.

The MVX score effectively integrates multiple pathophysiological pathways implicated in COPD development—particularly oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction—which collectively contribute to disease progression. As a novel inflammatory component of the MVX score, GlycA reflects both acute and low-grade chronic inflammation and has been significantly associated with the risk of various systemic conditions, including diabetes [26], rheumatoid arthritis [27], depression [28], cardiovascular disease [29, 30], and all-cause and cancer mortality [31]. Notably, prior study also found that MVX score was significantly associated with increased COPD risk and decreased lung function [32]. The Transcriptome-Wide Association Studies (TWAS) findings further revealed that these associations may be mediated through common biological mechanisms in various tissues and cells, including ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, immune system modulation, Wnt/β-catenin signaling transduction, and the control of cellular proliferation and differentiation [32]. In addition, altered BCAA (isoleucine, leucine, valine) metabolism, a hallmark of metabolic malnutrition, is frequently observed in COPD patients, who typically exhibit decreased circulating BCAA levels [33, 34]. While chronic systemic inflammation in COPD may drive BCAA depletion through activation of muscle branched-chain keto-acid dehydrogenase and enhanced hepatic amino acid uptake [35, 36], our findings suggest potential reverse causality, indicating BCAAs may influence long-term COPD risk and lung function impairment. The sHDL, traditionally recognized for its antiatherogenic and anti-inflammatory properties, may play a significant role in the pathogenesis not only of cardiovascular disease, but also of COPD. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that HDL cholesterol level was significantly associated with decreased pulmonary function, including FVC and FEV1. Interestingly, HDL stimulates type II pneumocytes to produce pulmonary surfactant - a phospholipid, cholesterol and protein mixture essential for maintaining alveolar surface tension and proper lung function [7, 37].

Our findings provide novel evidence that MVX score may help stratify COPD risk and impaired lung function in diverse populations. These results highlight the critical role of systemic biomarkers in chronic respiratory disease management. Following validation in external cohorts, the MVX score may enable earlier identification of high-risk individuals for targeted interventions, including intensive smoking cessation programs, pulmonary rehabilitation, or enhanced surveillance for early disease detection. Notably, the components of MVX score, which include routinely measurable NMR metabolic markers, enhance its feasibility for implementation in various healthcare settings [11]. Since the MVX score is derived from routinely available NMR metabolic markers, it offers practical advantages for implementation in real-world clinical practice. Future studies should explore whether dynamic changes in MVX scores over time correlate with COPD progression or response to treatment, which could further enhance personalized care strategies.

The present study demonstrates that the MVX score offers only a modest improvement in predictive performance when added to a model based on conventional risk factors. In the clinical diagnosis and management of COPD, pulmonary function testing remains the established gold standard, owing to its capacity to deliver objective, reproducible, and quantitative physiological assessment of airway obstruction [38]. Given the authoritative role of pulmonary function testing in diagnostic accuracy, it is unlikely that any emerging biomarker—including the MVX score—could supplant its central position in definitive diagnosis. Instead, the primary aim of our study was to investigate, from an etiological perspective, the association between the MVX score, lung function impairment, and the risk of developing COPD. The value of MVX may reside in its ability to reflect systemic biological processes underlying COPD, which are not fully captured by spirometry alone. Thus, the MVX score could potentially serve as a complementary screening tool, particularly in high-risk populations, where it may help identify individuals with early pathological changes before significant functional decline becomes detectable.

Our study has several advantages. Firstly, the prospective cohort design minimizes recall bias and reverse causation, providing clearer evidence of the temporal association of MVX score with risk of COPD. Secondly, the UK Biobank-based design, featuring a large, well-characterized population cohort with extended follow-up, provides the sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful associations and enhances the external validity of our findings. Thirdly, the MVX score was constructed using a data-driven approach, incorporating multiple metabolic malnutrition and inflammation biomarkers, which provides a more comprehensive and objective measure than single-biomarker or self-reported exposure assessments. Finally, the comprehensive data of UK Biobank allowed us to extensively adjust for potential confounders in our analysis. In addition, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity and subgroup analyses according to confounding factors, and found the the main findings were generally consistent with the overall study population.

An important limitation of our study lies in the cross-sectional nature of the analysis, which precludes definitive causal inference regarding the observed inverse association between the MVX score and lung function levels (FEV₁ and FVC). While we posit that a higher metabolic vulnerability may contribute to impaired lung physiology, we cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality. It is also plausible that subclinical airway pathology precedes and directly or indirectly drives changes in the MVX, for instance, through systemic inflammation, reduced physical activity, or other mechanisms related to early-stage lung disease. Prospective studies with repeated measurements of both metabolic and pulmonary function are needed to elucidate the temporal sequence and disentangle the direction of this relationship. Furthermore, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Firstly, the UK Biobank did not provide a trajectory for MVX score changes during follow-up; therefore, we could not assess the impact of cumulative changes in MVX score on risk of COPD. Secondly, the observational nature of present study precludes inference of causality, because even though we attempted to adjust for multiple potential confounders, it is not possible to exclude residual confounding. Finally, the UK Biobank study was conducted exclusively in the United Kingdom, and the participants were predominantly of White ethnicity. As a result, the generalizability of the findings, including the clinical utility of the MVX score for risk stratification in other ethnic groups, remains uncertain. Further validation in independent and diverse cohorts is required.

Conclusion

In summary, findings from the UK Biobank suggest that MVX, a novel risk score integrating NMR-quantified metabolic biomarkers of inflammation and metabolic malnutrition, was positively associated with COPD risk and inversely associated with lung function levels independently of traditional risk factors. These results highlight the importance of metabolic dysregulation in the pathogenesis of COPD and lung function decline, while indicating the potential of MVX components for early risk identification. Future study should validate these findings in external cohorts.
