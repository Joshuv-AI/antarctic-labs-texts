Excellence without borders: an interview with Rachael Dangarembizi on conducting globally competitive research in resource-constrained settings
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Dr. Rachael Dangarembizi is a Senior Lecturer in the Department of Human Biology and a neuroscientist in the Neuroscience Institute at the University of Cape Town in South Africa. She has established the first laboratory in Africa that studies the mechanisms of brain injury caused by fungal neuroinfections. Her research bridges basic science with clinical insights, aiming to improve diagnosis, treatment, and patient outcomes by understanding fungal-host interactions, neuroimmune responses, and brain injury. Dr. Dangarembizi has earned multiple awards for her work and leads several organizations dedicated to advancing neuroscience capacity across Africa with the aim of growing local expertise and infrastructure to drive impactful research. In this Q&A, she tells us about the challenges and opportunities of conducting globally competitive research with limited resources.

Could you tell us about your background and research experience?

I received a PhD in Neuroscience from the University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg where I studied the immune-to-brain communication and inflammatory signaling during infections.

As an early career neuroscientist, my research focuses on understanding the complex neuroimmune processes underlying the brain’s responses to infections and how dysregulations in these host defense processes lead to neurological damage and dysfunction. I established a neuroinfections laboratory and I currently lead a 12-member research group, The CryptoLab, focused on studying brain injury in cryptococcal meningitis. We chose to focus on cryptococcal meningitis because it has been neglected in research and policy, yet it is highly prevalent, difficult to treat, and a leading cause of HIV-related deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa.

What inspired you to become a scientist?

There is an element of truth to the famous statement “Every child is a scientist”… I think that child never grew old in me…I remained curious, inquisitive, open-minded and I still enjoy making small (and sometimes not so small) hypotheses about the world around me. I was very strong in STEM subjects throughout my school days and because our curriculum automatically put the strongest students in the science track, this set the path for me…I never left that path, quite fortunately for me it was a perfect match.

What challenges did you face when you started your own lab?

I started the CryptoLab just over a year after getting my PhD. I had to start raising funds for my own research without much postdoctoral experience and outputs. As an early career researcher, you have the “burden of proof” pressure – the need to provide evidence (pilot data, previous outputs and a track record) to funders to prove that your proposed independent work is achievable. For me, this was made worse by the fact that I started my lab a month before we went into a hard lockdown during the COVID-19 pandemic, and everything ground to a halt just as I was ready to take off. We had no laboratory access for almost a year, pilot data collection was slow and I didn’t have any funding. It was also a time when funders were reconsidering their priorities, so my very first international grant was rescinded, it could not be disbursed after award because of policy shifts.

What is your favorite memory from your scientific career so far?

There have been several great moments, but I think my favorite memory is when I received the award letter for the African Research Leaders grant. It was an affirming moment for me as a young African female scientist that my ideas were valid and that the UKRI Medical Research Council had decided to put a significant investment towards them.

What are the current challenges and opportunities of neuroscience research in Africa?

Challenges—The scientific research landscape in Africa is a dry and rugged terrain especially for the early career researcher. The greatest challenge is that there is very limited funding/investment in scientific research by governments and industry. Currently, African governments invest between 0.2–0.75% of their GDP to research and development. This means that in most countries, there is no local funding for research and in the few countries where funding is available, it is usually inadequate and highly competitive. Even when one manages to secure funding, they are still faced with a lack of suppliers for basic consumables, equipment and limited research support. We rely on the Global North for consumables and that drives costs to 2-3 times as high as they normally are; reagents sometimes take months before they reach us. This slows down science. Compounding all this is the lack of infrastructure, lack of expertise and skills, lack of mentorship and support, lack of career options for young scientists and a myriad of other problems in policies surrounding research.

Opportunities—I would like to say that one positive aspect of the fact that we have a huge disease burden is the great opportunity to carry out meaningful, impactful research that can address local and global challenges. The high disease incidence also means that we have developed the clinical experience and expertise to diagnose and treat these diseases and therefore we can use this experience to tackle global health challenges.

How do you see the African neuroscience research landscape evolving in the future?

Neuroscience is growing significantly in Africa, we have scientific institutions and African centers of excellence that are focused on brain research, and they are doing groundbreaking research at a globally competitive level. Several collaborative research networks have been established across the continent and globally to connect researchers and share resources. With more research being conducted locally, African countries are also developing policies and ethical frameworks to protect African data, manage data privacy and to regulate research. The research culture is also significantly changing and becoming more inclusive, and we have more and more young African women training at doctoral and postdoctoral levels in neuroscience. We have grown local, regional and international neuroscience societies that bring together African neuroscientists and give them a platform to network and share their findings and expertise. The coming years are likely to see a more self-sustaining, African-led research landscape where efforts are directed at both addressing local priorities and contributing to the global brain wellness agenda.

Advice to young, underrepresented students who want to pursue a scientific career?

It’s very possible to pursue a successful, impactful and rewarding scientific career in Africa. It is critical to seek mentorship and to build a support network of local and international collaborators with whom you can share resources, skills and expertise.
