Characteristics of plantar pressure during walking in stroke patients with hemiplegia of different brunnstrom stages: a study based on five-region smart insoles
Authors: Jingsong Mu, Xue Liu, Yan Yuan, Songran Wang, Xingyu Dou, Shujie Hao, Chaobing Zhang, Wenming Fei, Baohua Hu, Yong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlantar pressure measurement is frequently employed to assess the gait characteristics of stroke patients. This study developed a gait assessment system for stroke patients utilizing a self-developed five-region smart insole, with the goal of objectively evaluating their gait characteristics. A total of 75 stroke patients (14 at Brunnstrom stage III, 18 at stage IV, 27 at stage V, and 16 at stage VI) and 24 healthy subjects (designated “Health”) were recruited. A smart insole was used to collect the subjects’ gait parameters and plot plantar pressure curves. The results showed the following: (1) There were statistically significant differences among the five groups in the durations of the gait cycle, the double support phase, the bilateral single support phases, and the swing phase, as well as in the ratio of peak pressure to body weight for the bilateral toe bone regions, medial metatarsal regions, lateral metatarsal regions, and heel regions. No statistically significant differences were found in the bilateral arch peak pressure-to-body weight ratio among the five groups. (2) Each of the five subject groups exhibited a unique plantar pressure curve profile. The smart insole used in this study can provide objective gait assessment for stroke patients and effectively differentiate the gait characteristics of patients at different Brunnstrom stages.Keywords: Stroke, Hemiplegia, Brunnstrom Stages, Plantar Pressure, Gait

Introduction

Stroke is among the leading causes of disability worldwide [1], with hemiplegia being its most common functional impairment. Studies have shown that the prevalence of unilateral lower limb paralysis can be as high as 50–70% among stroke survivors even 2–4 years after the initial event [2]. Walking ability forms the foundation for patients to achieve independence in daily living and to reintegrate into the community; restoring walking function is therefore a core goal of rehabilitation therapy. However, hemiplegic patients often manifest a circumduction gait, which not only leads to inefficient walking and increased energy expenditure but also increases the risk of falls, severely impacting patients’ quality of life [3–5]. Consequently, precise assessment of stroke-induced hemiplegic gait is a critical prerequisite for developing personalized, effective rehabilitation plans.

In the assessment of motor function in hemiplegia, the Brunnstrom staging system is a classic and widely used tool. It categorizes the recovery process of the paralyzed limb into six stages based on the emergence and regression of synergistic movements and associated reactions, effectively reflecting the patient’s neurological recovery journey from flaccidity to spasticity and eventually to the appearance of dissociated movements [6, 7]. Moreover, plantar pressure serves as a crucial window into the biomechanical characteristics of the lower limbs. The distribution characteristics of plantar pressure during walking objectively and quantitatively reveal the interaction between the stance-phase foot and the ground, directly reflecting the patient’s weight-bearing capacity, balance control, and gait symmetry [8]. Integrating Brunnstrom staging with plantar pressure analysis holds promise for providing deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms of hemiplegic gait in the dimensions of both neurological recovery and biomechanical performance.

Numerous studies have employed plantar pressure testing to investigate gait characteristics in patients with stroke-induced hemiplegia. These studies have consistently reported reduced peak pressure on the affected side, center of pressure (COP), and asymmetric pressure distribution between affected and unaffected sides in affected individuals compared with those in healthy individuals [9, 10]. However, the existing research has certain limitations. First, most studies treat hemiplegic patients as a homogeneous group without conducting detailed subanalyses based on Brunnstrom stages [9]. Patients at different recovery stages exhibit variations in muscle tone, movement patterns, and walking ability, and current analytical approaches may obscure unique pressure characteristics specific to each stage. Second, while traditional plantar pressure plates offer high precision, they are confined primarily to laboratory-based walking assessments, making it difficult to capture continuous walking characteristics in natural settings.

To overcome these limitations, this study introduces a five-region intelligent insole-based plantar pressure measurement system. This system uses a five-region insole to investigate the plantar pressure distribution and its dynamic changes during gait in poststroke hemiplegic patients across different Brunnstrom stages. This approach not only provides new theoretical insights into the evolution of hemiplegic gait but also offers precise, quantitative assessment metrics for clinical rehabilitation practice. These metrics can guide therapists in the development of more targeted rehabilitation strategies for patients at different recovery stages, ultimately promoting maximal restoration of walking function.

Methods

Study participants

A total of 75 stroke patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected. The following inclusion criteria were applied: ① diagnosis of stroke meeting the criteria adopted at the 4th National Academic Conference on Cerebrovascular Diseases, confirmed by cranial CT or MRI as intracerebral hemorrhage or cerebral infarction; ② unilateral lower limb paralysis with Brunnstrom stage III˜VI; ③ ability to walk at least 10 m independently or with supervision; ④ signed informed consent. The following exclusion criteria were applied: ① impaired consciousness, cognitive dysfunction, or psychiatric disorders unable to follow instructions; ② lower limb fractures, joint pain, or other conditions preventing completion of the examination; ③ other conditions potentially affecting balance function, such as Parkinson’s disease or cerebellar lesions; ④ congenital foot deformities, such as clubfoot or hallux valgus; and ⑤ ocular conditions such as severe refractive errors, hemianopia, or cataracts. Clinical data such as age, sex, hemiplegic side, Brunnstrom stage, height, weight, and shoe size were collected. The study included 14 patients in Brunnstrom stage III, 18 patients in Brunnstrom stage IV, 27 patients in Brunnstrom stage V, and 16 patients in Brunnstrom stage VI. Concurrently, 24 healthy subjects were recruited. For the healthy control group, the nondominant side was defined as the ‘analogous affected side’ for comparison purposes, and the dominant side was defined as the ‘analogous unaffected side’. Instruments and equipment

The five-region smart insole (Fig. 1) developed by our research team comprises an airbagbased smart insole divided into five plantar regions (namely, the toe bone region, medial metatarsal region, lateral metatarsal region, arch region, and heel region). The airbag was fabricated from SHORE A30 silicone rubber (Dragon Skin 30, Smooth-On, Inc., USA). Each region was connected to a pressure sensor (model RSCM17100KP201; accuracy: ±1–2% of span; nonlinearity: ±0.3% of full scale (FS); output: 0.5-−4.5 V; resolution: 1 mV). An STM32 microcontroller unit (MCU) served as the core for acquiring plantar pressure data during movement. The input analog signals are conditioned and filtered through a preprocessing circuit before being converted into digital signals by the built-in analog-to-digital converter (ADC) module of the MCU. The digitized data are then transmitted to a computer terminal via a Bluetooth module (HC-05). Subsequent data processing is performed using MATLAB to obtain evaluation metrics. Preliminary experiments have demonstrated that the device exhibits good test–retest reliability [11–13].

Experimental method

The study was conducted in a corridor (Fig. 2). The participants wore comfortable thin cotton socks and shoes fitted with smart insoles and walked as naturally as possible to simulate real-life conditions. Before testing, the subjects were instructed to perform one 10-meter free walk to familiarize themselves with the entire process. Formal testing commenced once the participants were comfortable with the procedure. Each subject completed three tests, with a 2minute rest period between each test. Following each test, one stable gait cycle duration was selected for analysis. MATLAB software was used to extract characteristic values from the plantar pressure and posture sensor data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0). Age and BMI is presented as the mean ± standard deviation, while other baseline characteristics are described using counts. Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that none of the gait parameters followed a normal distribution. Therefore, comparisons of gait parameters across different Brunnstrom stages were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis (K–W) test). The results of these comparisons are reported as M (P25, P75). To visualize the dynamic changes in plantar pressure, a pressure curve was plotted. The gait cycle duration (%) served as the abscissa, while the mean value of the peak pressure-tobody weight ratio (N/N) at the corresponding percentage time points was used as the ordinate. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Comparison of Gait Parameters Among the Five Groups of Participants

Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed a progressive normalization of spatiotemporal parameters with advancing Brunnstrom stages. Specifically, the gait cycle duration in Brunnstrom III (median 1.765 s) was significantly longer than that in the control group, “Health” (median 1.145 s, p<0.001). This duration progressively decreased from Brunnstrom III to Brunnstrom VI (1.230 s), with Brunnstrom VI showing no significant difference from Health. A similar pattern of gradual improvement towards Health was observed for the double support phase duration, single support phase duration, and swing phase duration on both sides.

Discussion

The dynamic variation characteristics of plantar pressure during walking are a core aspect of gait analysis and are crucial for assessing and diagnosing diseases [14] and designing orthoses [15]. In healthy individuals, the dynamic variation in plantar pressure during walking is vividly described as a “foot roll-over” process, manifesting as a “double-peak and single-valley” phenomenon on the plantar pressure curve (Fig. 3a, b). However, poststroke hemiplegic gait patterns exhibit extensive heterogeneity [16] (Fig. 3a-l). Previous research has shown that lower limb muscle strength, spasticity, and the recovery status of motor function influence the gait of stroke patients [17]. Nevertheless, the gait patterns across different Brunnstrom stages and their underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

Patterns and mechanisms of dynamic plantar pressure changes during walking in patients across brunnstrom stages

Characteristics and mechanisms of dynamic plantar pressure changes on the affected side

Heel strike patterns can serve as an important basis for disease diagnosis [18]. In Stage III patients, during walking, the originally expected high-pressure regions at the heel and forefoot disappear because of alterations in the foot strike pattern and disruption of the arch structure. The entire plantar surface bears relatively uniform pressure from back to front, manifesting as a smooth, continuous “single-peak” or “plateau-like” curve on the pressure distribution graph (Fig. 3a).

① Owing to ankle plantar flexor spasticity leading to a plantar flexion strike pattern at initial contact, the heel experiences minimal force during the initial contact phase (corresponding to the initial small serrations on the Stage III curve in Fig. 3c). During the pre-swing phase, abnormal neural impulses and weakness in the plantar flexor muscles [19, 20] necessitate compensatory push-off using whole-limb extensor synergy, resulting in a pathological heel strike gait that manifests as elevated peak pressure at the heel (the highest peak on the Stage III curve in Fig. 3c);

② Weakness of the tibialis anterior, tibialis posterior, peroneus longus, and brevis muscles, combined with spasticity of the triceps surae, leads to lateral arch loading and arch collapse, resulting in a plateau-like peak in pressure under the arch during weight bearing (Fig. 3e);

③ Owing to the dominance of the extensor synergy pattern, with the foot in plantar flexion and inversion, the forefoot-particularly the lateral metatarsal region-experiences peak pressure prematurely during mid-stance, before the body’s center of gravity has adequately advanced over the foot. Furthermore, effective propulsive force is lacking during push-off. Consequently, the lateral metatarsal region exhibits a smooth, elongated curve during this phase (Fig. 3g).

④ Owing to foot inversion, loading increases in the lateral metatarsal region, whereas the medial metatarsal region fails to make effective, stable contact with the ground. Therefore, its pressure curve remains relatively flat during the initial contact and loading response. Additionally, the presence of spasticity causes the entire foot to function as a rigid, inverted unit, resulting in the loss of the normal roll-over and pressure transfer mechanism. This prevents the medial metatarsal region and hallux from cooperating to generate propulsive force during push-off, resulting in the absence of a typical medial metatarsal peak pressure and a low, flattened curve morphology (Fig. 3i).

⑤ The toe-off mode is also important for disease diagnosis [18]. The peak pressure in the toe region primarily originates from the terminal stance phase (push-off). In Stage III patients, owing to muscle weakness on the affected side combined with weak knee extension and ankle plantar flexion during terminal stance, the speed of plantar flexion is slow during toe-off, leading to insufficient push-off force during stepping [21] (Fig. 3k). Instead, patients complete the gait cycle duration by tilting the trunk toward the unaffected side, hiking the hip, and externally rotating and abducting the lower limb (circumduction gait) to lift the affected foot off the ground. As the motor function of the affected lower limb improves, muscle strength on that side increases, weight-bearing capacity is enhanced, and triceps surae spasticity decreases. Consequently, the peak pressure timing in the affected heel region gradually advances to the initial contact phase, and its magnitude increases (Fig. 3c). The morphology of the affected foot arch progressively recovers, improving its capacity to transfer load between the heel and forefoot (Fig. 3e). Loading on the affected lateral metatarsal region gradually decreases, and its peak pressure gradually shifts to the push-off phase. Furthermore, as the function improves, the propulsive force increases, leading to a gradual increase in the peak pressure during push-off (Fig. 3g). The plantar roll-over mechanism on the affected side gradually recovers, with the medial metatarsal and toe regions gradually generating forward propulsive force during terminal stance. As the function improves, the push-off force gradually increases, leading to a progressive increase in the peak pressure (Fig. 3i and k).

⑥ The increased peak pressure in the lateral metatarsal region of the affected side in Stage VI indicates that the center of gravity remains biased laterally during weight bearing on that limb and that the recovery of neurological function is incomplete. It also reveals underlying issues such as insufficient distal fine motor control, proximal compensatory strategies, and sensorimotor integration deficits beneath a seemingly normal gait. If this abnormal pressure distribution persists in the long term, it can not only affect gait efficiency but also potentially lead to falls, foot pain, callus formation, or even stress fractures. Therefore, during rehabilitation, targeted training for ankle proprioception, coordination, and gait retraining remains crucial.

Characteristics and mechanisms of dynamic plantar pressure changes on the unaffected side

The fundamental cause of abnormal plantar pressure on the unaffected side in Stage III patients is compensation for the dysfunction of the affected limb. ① Owing to instability of stance on the affected limb, patients shorten the step length on the unaffected side for safety, allowing quicker ground contact. During single-limb support on the unaffected side (while the affected limb is in mid-swing), spasticity in the affected limb slows its swing, prolonging its swing phase duration [22]. This, in turn, forces a prolonged stance phase on the unaffected side [23] (Fig. 3d). Concurrent leaning of the trunk toward the unaffected side increases loading on that limb, making push-off more effortful and resulting in a delayed and broadened heel peak. ② During single-limb support on the unaffected side, the stepping characteristics of the affected limb (circumduction gait) cause the body to lean toward the unaffected side, thereby increasing loading under the arch region and lateral metatarsal region of that foot (Fig. 3f and h) [24]. At initial contact of the affected limb, the unaffected limb rapidly transfers pressure through the medial arch to the medial metatarsal region, utilizing the push-off force from this region to propel the body’s center of gravity forward (Fig. 3j). ③ Throughout the stance phase on the unaffected side, the toes remain in a flexed, gripping posture to maintain body stability until push-off is completed (Fig. 3l: the unaffected toe region exhibits a broad peak pressure).

As the motor function of the affected lower limb improves, with reduced spasticity, the emergence of isolated and coordinated movements, improved muscle strength, and enhanced weight-bearing capacity, the asymmetry of plantar pressure between the two feet decreases [25]. The pressure curve morphology and peak timing in the various regions of the unaffected foot also gradually approach those observed in healthy individuals.

Clinical significance and limitations

Our findings have direct clinical implications. First, the distinct plantar pressure curve profiles for each Brunnstrom stage can serve as objective, quantitative benchmarks for clinicians to assess gait recovery. Second, the persistent lateral metatarsal loading in Stage VI patients suggests that conventional gait training should be supplemented with targeted interventions, such as ankle proprioceptive training and lateral arch support, even in later stages of recovery.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size in each Brunnstrom subgroup was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. As shown in Table 1, there were significant differences in the distributions of sex and hemiplegic side among the subjects, which may have affected the results to some extent. In the future, we will increase the number of subjects to further verify the effectiveness of the designed system. Second, the study is cross-sectional; a longitudinal follow-up of the same patients across different stages would provide stronger evidence for the recovery trajectory. Third, while our smart insole provides valuable plantar pressure data, we did not simultaneously collect full kinematic (e.g., joint angles) or electromyographic data, which could further elucidate the neuromuscular mechanisms underlying the observed pressure patterns. Future studies should integrate these modalities.

Conclusion

Using our self-developed plantar pressure insole, this study revealed that abnormal gait characteristics on both the affected and unaffected sides were most pronounced in Brunnstrom Stage III. With increasing Brunnstrom stages, the plantar pressure characteristics progressively approximated those of healthy individuals. However, Stage VI patients still exhibited abnormal plantar pressure, including phenomena such as instability in the center of gravity, indicating a continued need for targeted rehabilitation training. This work explored the patterns of dynamic plantar pressure changes during walking in poststroke patients across different Brunnstrom stages, providing an objective basis for gait research in this population.
