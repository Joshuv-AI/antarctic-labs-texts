Transcriptomic Profiling of SLC and ABC Transporters in the Human Term Placenta
Authors: Elijah Marsh Jung, Nicholas Ieronimakis, Bothaina Mousa, Emily D Sheikh, Sarah E Ligon, Joshua W Monson, Jennifer R Damicis, Hillary J Kinsman, Lydia E Bettridge, Samuel LM Arnold, Edward J Kelly, Jashvant D Unadkat, Joanne Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolute carriers (SLC) and ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporters are essential for placental solute exchange and fetal protection, yet their transcriptomic profiles in the human placenta remain poorly characterized. Although fetal sex influences placental development and function, its impact on transporter expression is unclear. Using RNA sequencing, we profiled SLC and ABC transporter expression in two anatomical regions of term human placentas (N = 10) with balanced fetal sex distribution and controlled clinical features. In the intervillous region, 276 SLCs were detected, with 60% expressed at low levels (CPM < 25) and 110 (40%) expressed at higher levels (CPM 25–1650). Four transporters—SLC2A1 (GLUT1), SLC44A2 (CTL2), SLC38A2 (SNAT2), and SLC38A1 (SNAT1)—showed the highest expression (CPM > 500). Functional annotation of 110 SLCs revealed transporters for amino acids, metals, and inorganic ions each accounted for ~9%. Other well‐represented SLCs included xenobiotic and vitamin transporters. Mitochondrial, lysosomal, and endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi transporters together accounted for ~37%, while 11% were orphan transporters. Thirty‐seven ABC transporter transcripts were detected, primarily associated with xenobiotic and lipid transport. Compared with the intervillous region, 1676 genes—including 32 SLC and 2 ABC transporters—were differentially expressed in the decidual region. Although 117 genes in the intervillous region and 79 genes in the decidual region exhibited sexually dimorphic expression, none were SLCs. Notably, male placentas showed higher expression of ABCB1 (P‐gp), confirmed by qPCR and western blot. Our findings provide a transcriptomic map of placental transporters and highlight a potential sex‐specific difference on ABCB1 expression and xenobiotic protection.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Placental transporters are key determinants of fetal exposure to drugs and dietary supplements. Although a limited number of transporters have been well characterized in the human placentas, the transcriptome of SLC and ABC transporters in the human placentas remains poorly characterized.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

What is the transcriptomic landscape of SLC and ABC transporters in the term human placentas? Does the expression profile of transporter genes show regional differences between the intervillous and decidual regions? Is transporter gene expression in human term placenta influenced by fetal sex?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Transcriptomic profiling of SLC and ABC transporter genes in term human placenta highlighted their importance in nutrient transport, xenobiotic protection, and homeostasis maintenance at the maternal–fetal interface. There is a significant regional difference in transporter gene expression, and P‐gp expression appears to be impacted by fetal sex.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

Identification of major SLCs and ABC transporters in term human placentas provides a foundation for defining the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying fetal exposure to xenobiotics and nutrients. Understanding the factors affecting placental transporter expression has important implications for model‐based prediction of fetal exposure to nutrients and drugs.

About 90% of women take at least one medication during pregnancy, yet for many drugs there is limited information about fetal exposure and safety. 1 Predictive tools such as mechanistic physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic modeling can support assessment of fetal exposure to xenobiotics 2 ; however, these approaches require a comprehensive understanding of the molecular pathways involved in xenobiotic and nutrient transport in placenta cells. The human placenta has three main types of epithelial trophoblasts: cytotrophoblasts (CTBs), the syncytiotrophoblast (STB), and extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs). CTBs are progenitor cells that fuse to the multinucleated STBs or differentiate into EVTs (Figure S1 ). STBs lining the chorionic villi form the placental barrier and are in direct contact with the maternal blood. Numerous SLC and ABC transporters are expressed in STBs to facilitate solute exchange and fetal protection. EVTs, on the contrary, invade the decidua and remodel uterine spiral arteries. These cells exhibit tumor‐like behavior and play a key role in implantation and placentation. 3 , 4 Although microarray and RNA‐sequencing studies have examined placental gene expression, 5 , 6 , 7 comprehensive analyses of the intricated network of SLC and ABC transporters and their regional differences in the human placenta remain scarce.

Emerging evidence suggests fetal sex influences placental development and function with male and female placentas showing distinct morphological features, growth trajectories, and stress responses. 7 , 8 , 9 These sex‐specific differences may contribute to differential pregnancy outcomes and perinatal complications. For instance, male fetuses have been previously reported to be at elevated risk for preterm birth, term preeclampsia, placental inflammation, and placental abruption while female fetuses are at higher risk for intrauterine growth restriction and preterm preeclampsia. 8 Previous studies have reported sexual dimorphism in placental gene expression, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 but data on sex‐specific expression of placental transporters remain scarce and are often confounded by other factors, such as maternal conditions, gestational age, delivery methods, medication use, and environmental exposures.

The primary goal of this study was to define the transcriptomic profiles of SLC and ABC transporters in a cohort of healthy human placentas controlled for several clinical features. The secondary goal was to determine whether transporter expression differs between intervillous and decidual regions and between male and female fetuses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human placenta samples

This study was approved by the Madigan Army Medical Center (Tacoma, WA, USA) IRB, protocol number 220019. Placentas from uncomplicated, unlabored pregnancies were collected by cesarean delivery at the Madigan Army Medical Center at Joint Base Lewis–McChord. Clinical characteristics are provided in Table S1 . Placentas were received and processed within 10 minutes of delivery. Intervillous and decidua biopsies (~0.5 × 2 cm) were dissected to avoid including other sections of tissue and triturated in ice‐cold saline to remove blood. Tissue sectioning was verified by H&E staining and staining with a trophoblast marker (Figure S2 ). For RNA sequencing, biopsies were placed in 4 mL ice‐cold RNAlater (Ambion; Thermo Fisher) and kept at 4°C for up to 7 days before being transferred to dry tubes for long‐term storage at −80°C. For western blot, biopsies were placed in dry tubes, flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

RNA extraction

At the time of RNA isolation, biopsies were weighed and homogenized in TRIzol Reagent (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) using a bead‐beater apparatus (Precellys, Bertin Corp., Rockville, MD, USA). Total RNA was isolated using a phenol‐chloroform extraction method and then purified with the RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was measured and the integrity was evaluated by formaldehyde‐agarose gel electrophoresis and confirmed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc, Santa Clara, California). Placenta RNA samples with RNA integrity (RIN) values above 8.0 were used for RNA sequencing.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

RNA sequencing of matched decidua and intervillous samples was performed at Novogene (Sacramento, CA) on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The cDNA library was constructed using the poly‐A tail selection strategy. The RNA fragmentation, first‐ and second‐strand cDNA syntheses, end repair, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. The cDNA libraries were then validated for quantity and integrity using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA) before sequencing. Reads were sequenced using 150 bp paired‐end sequencing per the Illumina manufacturer's protocol. The libraries were sequenced to a depth of 40–50 million reads per sample.

FASTQ files were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p14—release date: 02/03/2022) and counts were generated using STAR v2.7.11b 14 following the pipeline from ENCODE (https://www.encodeproject.org/microrna/microrna‐seq/). Transcript abundance was derived using the integrated Quantmode (STAR v2.7.11b) with scaling to the average transcript length. Low‐abundance transcripts were removed using filterByExpr in EdgeR v4.4.1 with default parameters. Expression data were normalized using trimmed‐mean of m‐values and expressed as counts per million (CPM). 15 Differential expression analysis was performed using EdgeR v4.4.1 16 between tissue types and sex. P values were computed in EdgeR by modeling count‐level variability and applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini–Hochberg method) across all tested features. The dataset generated in this study has been deposited at the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), with the accession number GSE325186.

Real‐time PCR

RNA samples were processed as aforementioned and reverse transcribed into cDNA by High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit from Applied Biosystems (Foster City, CA). Twenty nanogram of cDNA were used for qRT‐PCR according to the manufacturer's protocol with the following TaqMan assays: Hs00939627_m1 (GUSB), Hs00184500_m1 (ABCB1), and Hs01053790_m1 (ABCG2). The relative expression was analyzed using the delta–delta Ct method by normalizing to GUSB expression. 17

Western blot

Proteins were extracted by homogenizing samples overnight at 4°C with gentle agitation in RIPA buffer supplemented with Halt protease inhibitor. Samples were then centrifuged and aqueous layers were aliquoted into protein‐low‐bind tubes for storage at −80°C. Protein concentrations were evaluated by BCA assay (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) following the manufacturers' instructions. Samples were diluted and run on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis‐Tris gels. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with non‐fat dry milk, and then probed overnight with a rabbit monoclonal antibody against P‐gp (no.: E1Y7S; Cell Signaling) at a 1:1000 dilution. The next day a secondary anti‐rabbit (no.: 7074S; Cell Signaling) conjugated to HRP was applied for 1 h at 1:1000 and then visualized using the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA). Anti β‐actin (no.: D6A8; Cell Signaling) was subsequently applied for 1 h at 1:1000 and the relative protein concentrations were analyzed using ImageJ.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical information

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table S1 . The cohort consisted of healthy pregnancies with maternal BMI ranging from 22 to 29 kg/m2. The majority of participants were of Caucasian ethnicity, with no reported history of tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use during pregnancy. None of the participants had diabetes, and all had taken prenatal supplements as part of standard prenatal care. All deliveries were via cesarean section at 39 weeks' gestation (±3 days), resulting in healthy live births. The sex distribution of the newborns was balanced (five males and five females).

Major SLC genes expressed in the intervillous region of human term placenta

RNA sequencing of intervillous biopsies dissected from term placentas detected 276 SLC transcripts in this region. These SLCs were grouped into categories based on their average expression levels (Figure 1 a ). Among the 276 SLCs expressed in term placenta, 115 (42%) and 51 (18%) showed very low (CPM < 10) and low (CPM 10–25) expression, respectively. Forty‐three (16%) and 33 (12%) exhibited moderate (CPM 25–50) and intermediate (CPM 50–100) expression, respectively. Thirty‐four (12%) were highly expressed (CPM > 100), including 20 SLCs with CPM values of 100–250 and 10 with CPM values of 250–500. Four SLC transporters—SLC2A1, SLC44A2, SLC38A2, and SLC38A1—showed the highest expression with CPM values exceeding 500. These genes encode a glucose transporter (SLC2A1/GLUT1), a choline transporter (SLC44A2/CTL2), and two System A amino acid transporters (SLC38A1/SNAT1 and SLC38A2/SNAT2).

We next functionally annotated the top 110 SLC transporters (CPM ≥ 25) based on published information. The average CPM values of these transporters along with their substrate and placental localization information are shown in Table 1 . The top 20 highly expressed SLC genes include mainly nutrient transporters for glucose (SLC2A1), choline (SLC44A2), amino acids (SLC38A1/A2, SLC7A2/A5/A8, SLC3A2, SLC38A10), B and C vitamins (SLC5A6, SLC23A2, SLC19A3), as well as dietary minerals, such as Fe (SLC40A1), Zn (SLC39A8), and phosphate (SLC20A1) (Table 1 ). Other highly expressed SLC genes include a Na+/H+ exchanger (SLC9A1), a Cl−/HCO3 − exchanger (SLC4A2), and a K+‐Cl− cotransporter (SLC12A4) (Table 1 ). A xenobiotic transporter (SLCO2A1) and an orphan transporter (SLC16A4) are also among the highly expressed SLC genes.

We then categorized the 110 annotated SLCs according to their known substrates and subcellular localization (Figure 1 b ). Transporters without an identified substrate were classified as orphan transporters, regardless of their cellular localization. Approximately 52% SLC genes are localized, or expected to be localized, to the plasma membrane and also have known substrates (Table 1 ). In particular, transporters for amino acids, metals (e.g., Fe, Zn, Mg, Cu, Ca, and Mn), and inorganic ions (e.g. Na+, H+, Cl−, HCO3 −, sulfate, and phosphate) were the most highly represented classes, with each respectively accounting for 9% of the 110 annotated SLC genes (Figure 1 b ). Other well‐represented plasma membrane SLCs included xenobiotic transporters (6%) and transporters for vitamins (4.5%), sugar (2.7%), choline (2.7%), neurotransmitters (1.8%), nucleosides (1.8%), and mono‐ and dicarboxylates (1.8%).

About 37% of the SLCs have known substrates but are, or predicted to be, intracellular transporters residing in the ER/Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, or lysosomes (Table 1 ), and respectively account for 15%, 14%, and 8% of the 110 annotated SLC genes (Figure 1 b ).

The remaining 11% are orphan transporters without a known substrate. Among them, 3 were predicted to be intracellular, 1 localized to the plasma membrane, and 8 had unknown subcellular localization (Table 1 ).

Major ABC transporter genes expressed in the intervillous region of human term placenta

Transcripts of 36 ABC transporters were detected in the intervillous region of term placenta. The majority (59%) exhibited very low (CPM < 10) or low (CPM 10–25) expression (Figure 1 c ). Six ABC transporters showed moderate (CPM 25–50) and eight displayed intermediate (CPM 50–100) expression. ABCG2 (BCRP) demonstrated the highest expression, with a CPM value of 124. We further annotated the 23 ABC genes with CPM > 5 (Table 2 ) and categorized them based on known substrates and/or subcellular localization (Figure 1 d ). Among these transporters, 43% are known or predicted to be expressed on the plasma membrane, with 26% involved in the transport of xenobiotics and 17% in the transport of lipids (e.g., phospholipids, cholesterol, and long‐chain fatty acids). The majority (52%) are known or predicted to localize intracellularly (mitochondria, peroxisomes, lysosomes, and the ER) and are associated with the transport of substrates, such as heme precursors, small peptides, and fatty acids. One transporter is classified as an orphan.

Differential gene expression in placental decidua and intervillous regions

RNA sequencing was also performed in matched decidual tissues and compared with intervillous region. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed an apparent separation of the intervillous and decidual samples (Figure 2 a ). Differential expression analysis revealed 1676 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and among them, 1254 were upregulated and 422 were downregulated in the decidua using a P value of 0.05. Volcano plot showed asymmetric enrichment of DEGs in the decidua samples (Figure 2 b ). Among the DEGs, MMP12, ITGAD, CNR1, and KRT6A exhibited 29–70‐fold higher expression in the decidua than in the intervillous region (Figure S3 ). On the contrary, MAGEA4 and LEP were found to be highly enriched in the intervillous regions.

Differentially expressed SLC and ABC genes between decidua and intervillous regions

Next, we compared transporter expression in the decidua and intervillous regions. In the decidual region, 276 SLC and 36 ABC transcripts were detected. Among them, 32 SLC and 2 ABC genes were found to be differentially expressed between the decidual and intervillous regions (Figure 3 a ). Interestingly, many of the differentially expressed transporters had higher expression in the decidual region, with SLC2A5, SLC12A8, and SLC43A3 exhibiting greatest differences (Log2 fold change >2) between the two regions (Figure 3 b ). Of note, the expression of SLC12A8 is approximately 12‐fold higher in the decidua than in the intervillous region, indicating a specific role of this transporter in the decidua. In contrast, only a few SLC genes are enriched in the intervillous region. These include SLC6A4 that encodes the serotonin transporter (SERT), an important regulator of monoamine neurotransmitters in the placenta (Figure 3 b ).

Sexual dimorphic gene expression in term human placenta

Given that the placenta is a fetal‐derived tissue, we next examined whether gene expression in the intervillous and decidual regions is influenced by fetal sex. Fetal sex was first affirmed by correlating with X‐ and Y‐linked gene markers previously validated for sex determination. 18 The results showed that the expression patterns of sex‐linked genes in all samples were consistent with the reported sex of the newborns (Figure S4 ). Differential expression analysis identified 117 genes that were significantly different (adjusted P < 0.05) between males and females in the intervillous region, including 6 X‐linked and 17 Y‐linked genes (Figure S5 ). In the decidual region, 79 genes exhibited sex‐dimorphic expression, including 4 X‐linked and 14 Y‐linked genes (Figure S5 ). Interestingly, ABCB1 (P‐gp) was among the genes differentially expressed between sexes, whereas SLC and other ABC transporters did not display such differences.

GO network analysis revealed the DEGs congregate into two distinct functional clusters in both the intervillous region (Figure 4 a ) and the decidua (Figure 4 b ). The upper clusters in both Figure 4 a,b feature mainly sex‐chromosome genes (e.g., KDM5D, DDX3Y, UTY, ZFY, and XIST) and are associated with nuclear activity, transcription regulation, and macromolecule metabolic processes. The lower clusters consist of genes associated with extracellular vesicle transport and exosome signaling.

DISCUSSION

In this study, bulk RNA‐sequencing was employed to characterize the transcriptomic landscape of SLC and ABC transporters in two anatomical regions of term human placentas obtained at elective cesarean section from uncomplicated pregnancies at a single clinical site. None of the donors had an obese BMI or a history of tobacco, alcohol, or other substance use, and all cases resulted in live births of healthy newborns.

The human genome contains over 450 SLC genes in more than 65 families. In the intervillous region, 276 transcripts were detected. The top 20 highly expressed SLC genes (Table 1 ) include nutrient transporters for glucose, amino acids, vitamins, and dietary minerals as well as transporters for acid–base balance and ion homeostasis. Among the 110 significantly expressed SLCs (CPM > 25), about one third of them (~34%) are involved in transporting amino acids, metals, inorganic ions, vitamins, and sugars (Figure 1 ). The prominent expression of these SLC genes—characterized by both the high transcript abundance of individual genes and the diversity of genes within a specific substrate category—underscores the vital role of SLCs in supplying nutrients and minerals for fetal development. The expression patterns also highlight an important contribution of placental SLCs to maintaining acid–base balance and ion homeostasis at the maternal–fetal interface.

One striking finding of our analysis is that intracellular SLCs—including ER/Golgi, mitochondrial, and lysosomal transporters—collectively accounted for 37% of the 110 significantly expressed SLC genes (Figure 1 ). The mitochondrial transporters are predominantly members of the SLC25 mitochondrial carrier family known to facilitate the transport of a variety of metabolites, nucleotides, and cofactors across the inner mitochondrial membrane 19 (Table 1 ). Similarly, more than half of the ER/Golgi transporters are from the SLC35 family known to deliver essential substrates (e.g., nucleotide sugar) into these organelles for catalytic reactions, such as glycosylation. 20 Given their intracellular localization, these SLCs may not directly facilitate maternal–fetal solute exchange. However, they may coordinate the intracellular trafficking of metabolites and precursors essential for specialized cellular processes in placental cells, such as mitochondrial ATP synthesis and protein glycosylation within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus. The abundance and diversity of these intracellular transporters may suggest active cellular activities, elevated energy production, and robust protein processing processes in placenta cells.

Of the 48 known human ABC transporters, 37 were detected in the intervillous region of the term placenta. The expression level of ABC transporters was considerably lower than SLCs, and the majority of plasma membrane ABC transporters are involved in xenobiotic or lipid (including phospholipids, cholesterol, and long‐chain fatty acids) transport. The most highly expressed ABC transporter genes include the multi‐specific drug transporters BCRP (ABCG2) and P‐gp (ABCB1), alongside ABCA1, which facilitates the transport of cholesterol and phospholipids. These findings suggest that placental ABC transporters are primarily dedicated to xenobiotic protection and lipid transport across the placenta barrier.

The STBs forming the placenta barrier are terminally differentiated cells derived from the fusion of CTB progenitors. In the decidua, which anchors the placenta to the uterine wall, the same progenitors assume a different fate, differentiating into EVTs. The EVTs exhibit invasive, “cancer‐like” behavior as they infiltrate the maternal lining, remodel the spiral arteries, and promote immune tolerance. 3 , 4 Given their shared cellular origin but divergent physiological roles, we hypothesize that a comparison of the transcriptomes between these regions will reveal region‐specific gene expression profiles. Indeed, our results revealed distinct clustering and asymmetrical enrichment of genes in the decidual samples (Figure 2 a,b ). Consistent with their expected function, pathways reflecting STB characteristics (e.g., solute transport, cellular senescence) are enriched in the intervillous samples (Figure 2 c,d ). On the contrary, pathways, such as protein digestion and absorption, neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, and host immune responses (Amoebiasis) are enriched in the decidual tissues (Figure 2 c,d ). Many of the highly enriched decidual genes appear to be involved in placentation and invasion of the endometrium (Figure S3 ). For instance, MMP12 has been found to be involved in the breakdown of extracellular matrix in embryonic development, tissue remodeling, and invasion of the maternal tissue. 21 , 22 CNR1, one of two cannabinoid receptors, has previously been found to be enriched in invasive CTBs and facilitate cell invasion and deep placentation. 23 , 24

Limited research has differentiated transporter gene expressions in the two regions. Single‐cell transcriptomics indicates major cell population groups different between the intervillous region and the decidua; however, transporter expression has rarely been characterized. 21 , 25 Interestingly, 32 SLC and 2 ABC transporter genes were differentially expressed between the decidual and intervillous regions (Figure 3 a ). Notably, SLC12A8, which encodes a Na+‐dependent nicotinamide mononucleotide transporter, exhibited ~12‐fold higher expression in the decidua than in the intervillous region (Figure 3 b ). Although the role of SLC12A8 in the placenta has not been investigated, recent studies have shown that it is highly upregulated in bladder, lung, and colorectal cancers, where it promotes cancer cell migration and invasion. 26 The enriched expression of SLC12A8 in the decidua may therefore suggest a potential role in facilitating EVT invasion and infiltration into the maternal endometrium.

The human placenta exhibits sex‐specific differences in function, morphology, and responses to environmental exposures. 12 , 27 , 28 Consistent with previous reports, 7 , 9 , 29 our transcriptomic analysis identified 117 and 79 DEGs showing sex‐dependent expression in intervillous and decidual regions (Figure S5 ). GO network plots revealed similar patterns consisting of two major gene clusters in the intervillous and decidual regions (Figure 4 ). Unsurprisingly, the upper clusters in both regions consisted of many sex chromosome–linked genes as expected. Interestingly, the lower clusters consisted of autosomal genes associated with extracellular vesicles and exosomes. Placenta‐derived extracellular vesicles are thought to facilitate fetal‐maternal communication and modulate maternal tolerogenic immune responses to prevent fetal rejection. 30 Our data suggest these pathways may differ between male and female fetuses.

Interestingly, ABCB1 (P‐gp) showed higher expression in placentas of male fetuses in both RNA and western blot analyses (Figure 5 ). To our knowledge, this is the first time such an observation has been reported for P‐gp in the human placenta. Our observation corroborates with recent exploratory studies suggesting sex‐dependent P‐gp expression in human peripheral organs, such as the intestines and liver. 31 , 32 For instance, Mai et al. reported significantly higher RNA and protein expression of intestinal P‐gp in male donors. A meta‐analysis of proteomic data collated from multiple independent studies also revealed a 1.2‐fold higher hepatic expression of P‐gp in males in Caucasians. 31 Nevertheless, a previous study reported no sex difference of ABCB1 in first trimester placenta. 33 Given the small sample size of our study, the impact of sex on placenta P‐gp expression warrants further investigation.

The STBs form a large multinucleated syncytium through the fusion of thousands of CTBs. Consequently, single‐cell RNA sequencing often underrepresents the STB population. 34 Hence, we employed bulk RNA sequencing in this study. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, mRNA expression does not necessarily reflect protein abundance or functional activity. Second, bulk RNA sequencing lacks cellular resolution. Although trophoblasts constitute the predominant cell type in term placenta, other cell populations—including stromal, vascular, and immune cells—are also present. As a result, transporters with low overall expression may be enriched in less abundant cell types and can still play important cell‐specific roles. Recently, single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) has been successfully applied to the study of placenta and may provide a valuable approach for investigating cell‐specific transporter expression in future studies. 18 , 21 , 34 Lastly, the anatomical proximity of the decidua basalis and the intervillous space makes absolute tissue demarcation challenging, and the decidual samples may inadvertently incorporate cells or extracellular material originating from the intervillous compartment, and vice versa. Although steps were taken to minimize this risk, the possibility of residual contamination cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies employing spatial transcriptomics or laser capture microdissection would help circumvent this limitation by enabling precise, anatomy‐guided sampling of each compartment.

In summary, we defined the transcriptomic landscape of SLC and ABC transporters in term human placenta. Our findings highlight the multifaceted roles of placental transporters in nutrient transport, xenobiotic protection, ion homeostasis and intracellular metabolism at the maternal–fetal interface. Our data demonstrate regional differences in transporter gene expression and SLC genes enriched in decidua may contribute to the placentation process. We also provided evidence of sex‐dependent expression of P‐gp in term human placenta. Understanding the specific expression pattern of SLC and ABC transporters within the human placentas is essential toward understanding their roles in healthy and complicated pregnancies. Our work also provides a valuable resource for the presence and relative abundance of transporters in the human placenta. These data can be a starting point for proteomic validation and physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic modeling (PBPK) to predict placental transport of nutrients and xenobiotics. In addition, although our characterization of sex differences should be replicated in larger studies, it may suggest the inclusion of fetal sex as a covariate for PBPK modeling.
