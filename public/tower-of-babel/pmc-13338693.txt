Late-Onset Sepsis in Preterm Infants Caused by Clostridium ramosum: A Case Series and Literature Review
Authors: Qi Zhang, Ke Cheng, Jingxin Zhao, Yufeng Jin, Jun Deng, Suling Wei, Fengjuan Ji, Xiaolu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClostridium ramosum is an opportunistic anaerobic gut commensal associated with invasive infections in immunocompromised adults. However, sepsis caused by C. ramosum in newborn infants, especially in preterm infants, is extremely scarce.Materials and MethodsA retrospective case series of ten preterm infants with C. ramosum sepsis who were admitted to a neonatal intensive care unit since birth between 2022 and 2024. Clinical data including epidemiologic and demographic characteristics, management, and outcomes were collected. A literature review was also performed on published cases from 2010 to 2025.ResultsAll infants were preterm with multiple comorbidities, the median gestational age was 32+3 weeks. No significant temporal clustering was observed. Common presentations were feeding intolerance and bloody stool, with 80% of cases having necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). Laboratory findings showed elevated inflammatory markers. All infants received meropenem-based therapy, and 40% of cases required surgical intervention. No acute infection-related mortality occurred. Literature review of adult cases (n=30) showed a direct mortality of 36.7%, often associated with unresolved infection sources and severe sepsis.ConclusionC. ramosum can cause severe sepsis in preterm infants, often associated with NEC. Early recognition, appropriate antimicrobial therapy based on susceptibility testing (e.g., a carbapenem), and prompt surgical intervention when indicated are crucial for favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Clostridium ramosum is a Gram-positive, obligately anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium of the genus Clostridium. It was recently proposed to be reclassified as Erysipelatoclostridium ramosum due to revised genome-based bacterial taxonomy [12]. It is a member of the so-called "RIC group" (C. ramosum, C. innocuum, C. clostridioforme), which is often misidentified due to Gram-stain variability and atypical morphology [13]. C. ramosum is a gut commensal, but recently it has been increasingly recognized as an opportunistic agent capable of causing invasive disease including bacteremia, intra-abdominal abscesses, and soft tissue infections in immunocompromised adults [45678]. Comorbidities such as malignancy, diabetes mellitus, liver cirrhosis, and recent abdominal surgery increase the risk of infection [4567]. Despite its presence in normal gut flora, isolation of C. ramosum from sterile sites should not be dismissed as a contaminant. Most of the reported cases were distributed in elderly or immunocompromised individuals while data on infections in preterm infants are extremely scarce [9]. This study presents a case series of C. ramosum sepsis in preterm infants, reviews pertinent literature, and aims to enhance the understanding about clinical features, management, and outcomes of this uncommon infection in this vulnerable population.

Materials and Methods

1. Patients

This is a retrospective case series study conducted in a tertiary women’s hospital in Hangzhou, China, with an annual delivery volume of more than 25,000. Preterm infants who admitted to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) since birth and diagnosed with C. ramosum caused sepsis between January 2022 and June 2024 were enrolled. Electronic medical records were reviewed, and clinical data including epidemiologic and demographic characteristics, management, and outcomes were collected and described.

2. Ethical statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Women’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval No. IRB-20240272-R). The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

3. Definitions

Sepsis: Patients present clinical signs of infection with positive blood culture [10].

Late-onset sepsis (LOS): Sepsis occurred 72h after birth or admitted to NICU [11].

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC): Diagnosed and staged according to modified Bell's criteria, based on the presence of clinical signs (feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, bloody stools) and characteristic radiographic findings (pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, pneumoperitoneum) or surgical findings [12].

Acute infection-related mortality: Death occurring during the active infection episode, directly attributable to septic shock or multi-organ failure resulting from the C. ramosum infection.

4. Isolate identification

A BACTEC™ FX40 blood-culture bottle (Becton, Dickinson & CO., Sparks, MD, USA) inoculated with the infant’s venous blood signalled positive after continuous incubation. Gram staining of the positive broth revealed large, straight to slightly curved Gram-positive rods with sub-terminal spores. Subculture onto 5% sheep-blood, chocolate and Sabouraud-dextrose agars yielded pinpoint, grey, non-haemolytic colonies following 24 h incubation at 35°C in 5% CO2. MALDI-TOF MS using the VITEK® MS system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France) unambiguously identified the isolate as C. ramosum (score ≥2.0); no additional organisms were recovered.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed utilising two methodologies concurrently. The E-test (bioMérieux SA, Marcy-l'Étoile, FrancebioMérieux) was conducted on Brucella blood agar following 48 hours of anaerobic incubation, with results interpreted in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M11, 9th edition) to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Concurrently, the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method was performed using antibiotic disks on Brucella blood agar under the same incubation conditions, and the diameters of inhibition zones were measured. All results were interpreted as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) in accordance with CLSI breakpoints.

5. Literature review

A literature review was performed in Springer Nature Link, PubMed and Embase. We use the search terms “Clostridium ramosum,” “Erysipelatoclostridium ramosum,” and “sepsis,” “infection,” and “bacteremia,” respectively between 2010 and 2025. Only English language articles were included. The case definition for infection in the literature review was based on a positive culture of C. ramosum identified from blood and positive symptoms/signs of sepsis. The identified articles were reviewed and data were extracted, including demographic data, potential risk factors, source of infection, infection management and final outcome.

2. Demographic data

The median gestational age and birth weight were 32+3 weeks (range 30+4–34+0 weeks) and 1,740 g (range 1,220–2,210g), respectively. Six infants (60.0%) were male. Five infants (50.0%) were from multiple-gestation pregnancies; in all instances, the co-twin(s) did not develop C. ramosum sepsis. All infants had comorbidities, most commonly respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) in 5 (50.0%). Prior to sepsis onset, all infants had received broad-spectrum antibiotics, primarily penicillin G and cefotaxime (duration 2–6 days). Two infants received azithromycin for Ureaplasma urealyticum. Enteral feeding was initiated on the first or second day of life in all but one infant. Only one infant was exclusively breastfed. The median duration of parenteral nutrition (PN) before sepsis onset was 8 days (range 7–16 days). Detailed characteristics are in Table 1.

3. Clinical presentation and laboratory findings

The clinical presentation of C. ramosum infection usually presents with gastrointestinal symptoms including feeding intolerance (7/10, 70.0%), bloody stool (7/10, 70.0%), abdominal distension (5/10, 50.0%), and abdominal mass (2/10, 20.0%), which aligned with the high incidence of NEC (Bell stage II–III, 80.0%, 8/10). Other symptoms were non-specific, overlapping with symptoms of neonatal LOS, including tachycardia (3/10, 30.0%), irritability/excessive crying (3/10, 30.0%), apnea (2/10, 20.0%), and cyanosis (2/10, 20.0%). Hypothermia was observed in only one infant.

Laboratory investigations revealed a consistent inflammatory response. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were elevated in all patients, with a median value of 41.5 mg/L (range: 13.7 mg/L to 201 mg/L). Thrombocytopenia was observed in five infants, with the lowest recorded platelet count being 95 × 109/L. Hyperlactatemia was also noted in five cases, with a median lactate level of 3.9 mmol/L. Leukopenia, an indicator of severe infection often associated with bone marrow suppression, was present in a notable proportion of infants (4/10, 40.0%), with white blood cell counts as low as 2.0 × 109/L. These findings underscore the severity of the infection and its potential systemic impact. Fortunately, all infants received lumbar puncture with a normal cerebrospinal fluid analysis.

4. Management and therapeutic strategies

All ten C. ramosum isolates were susceptible to meropenem (MIC 0.125 µg/mL) (Table 2). And all infants received meropenem (60 mg/kg/day, divided every 8 hours) as the backbone of therapy, consistent with the AST results. Vancomycin was administered concomitantly to 70.0% of the infants (n=7) as part of the initial empirical regimen to cover potential co-infecting Gram-positive organisms (e.g., coagulase-negative staphylococci), not because of anticipated resistance of C. ramosum, for which CLSI interpretive breakpoints are not available. The duration of administration ranged from 3 to 14 days, with the specific duration varying based on clinical factors, including the presence of co-infections. In one infant who developed a postoperative methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci co-infection, vancomycin was continued for 14 days. The duration of meropenem therapy ranged from 7 to 14 days, tailored based on clinical response, source control, and underlying comorbidities. Surgical intervention was required in 4 infants (40.0%) for abdominal mass (n=2), NEC progression (n=1), and intestinal perforation (n=1). Surgical exploration confirmed the masses to be localized abscesses with necrotic bowel loops. Intestinal necrosis was found in all four, with resection lengths ranging from 10 to 40 cm. Three required an ileostomy and reversal.

5. Outcomes

In this case series, all infants achieved clearance of C. ramosum from the bloodstream, with no reported recurrent infections. The acute infection-related short-term mortality rate was 0.0%. Long-term outcomes remain to be determined.

6. Literature review

18 previous articles were identified that described a total of 30 cases, derived from 14 case reports and four case series published between 2012 and 2025 (Fig. 3). The demographic data, comorbidities, source of infection, infection management and final outcome were listed in Table 3 [45671314151617181920212223242526]. Their ages ranged from 26 to 91 years (median 73 years). Notably, no pediatric cases (<18 years) were identified. There were 15 males (50.0%) and 15 females (50.0%), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1:1. The vast majority of patients (29/30, 96.7%) had at least one underlying comorbidity, most commonly cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and metoblic syndrome (12/30, 40.0%), various cancer or malignancy (10/30, 33.3%), and chronic organ failure or major organ dysfunction (e.g., chronic kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, mental disorder) (9/30, 30.0%). Bacteremia was the most common clinical presentation (27/30, 90.0%), often secondary to intra-abdominal infections or skin and soft tissue infections. The source of infection was most commonly isolated from blood (25/30, 83.3%), followed by bone samples (1/30, 3.3%), joint fluid (1/30, 3.3%), aortic tissue (1/30, 3.3%), fibroid tissue (1/30, 3.3%), and peritoneal fluid (1/30, 3.3%), indicating that the source may not be from a single location. Initial empirical therapy commonly utilized broad-spectrum regimens with anaerobic coverage, such as piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenems, often in combination. Treatment was frequently adjusted or de-escalated in accordance with the clinical response. A total of 18 patients (18/30, 60.0%) showed clinical improvement or recovery with appropriate therapy, while the rest 12 patients (12/30, 40.0%) experienced a deterioration. The vast majority of them died of unresolved infection sources and severe sepsis.

Discussion

This case series is the first to describe C. ramosum as a cause of LOS in a series of preterm infants. Our findings expands the recognized host spectrum of this pathogen to include this vulnerable population. The literature review confirms its rarity in children, underscoring the novelty of this report. The cases predominantly affected moderate preterm infants with risk factors for intestinal barrier compromise, such as broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure and non-exclusive breastfeeding [2728].

The pathogenesis of C. ramosum in neonates is linked to its production of IgA proteases, which can degrade secretory immunoglobulin A, potentially facilitating translocation across a compromised mucosal barrier [29]. In our series, the universal association with NEC (80.0%) strongly suggests that intestinal injury is a key predisposing factor. It remains unclear whether C. ramosum is a primary pathogen causing NEC or a commensal that translocates secondarily to NEC-related necrosis. Given its ability to produce IgA protease and cause invasive disease in other contexts, we hypothesize that it may act as an opportunistic pathogen that exacerbates intestinal injury in a susceptible host, contributing to the severity of the NEC episode. This is supported by the severe inflammatory response and the need for surgery in 40.0% of cases.

In contrast to adult cases with diverse clinical presentations (bacteremia, abscesses, osteomyelitis, endocarditis), the clinical presentation of our neonatal series was dominated by gastrointestinal symptoms, mirroring the high rate of NEC. The laboratory findings (elevated CRP, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia) underscore the systemic inflammatory impact of this infection.

Management of C. ramosum infections hinges on timely administration of effective antimicrobials and, when necessary, source control. In our series, all isolates were susceptible to carbapenems. This supports the use of carbapenems as effective first-line therapy in this setting, as was done in our series. The use of vancomycin in combination initially was driven by the need for broad empirical coverage in neonatal LOS, where Gram-positive cocci are common [11]. Notably, surgical intervention was required in 40.0% of cases for complications such as intestinal perforation or abdominal mass, emphasizing the critical role of source control in managing invasive infections. These therapeutic strategies align with recommendations from adult literature, which advocate for antimicrobials such as metronidazole, piperacillin-tazobactam, or carbapenems, often combined with surgical debridement or drainage for localized infections.

The absence of acute infection-related mortality in the present series is in stark contrast to the 36.7% mortality observed in the adult literature that has been reviewed. This discrepancy is likely to be multifactorial in nature. Firstly, while the infants were premature, they did not possess the severe, irreversible end-organ comorbidities (e.g., metastatic cancer, end-stage cirrhosis) that were prevalent in the adult cohort. Secondly, the infection source in the infants (NEC-affected bowel) was frequently amenable to definitive source control via surgical resection, which was achieved in 40.0% of cases. In adults, the source (e.g., intra-abdominal abscess in a frail patient) may be more difficult to control. Thirdly, the controlled environment of the NICU allows for prompt recognition of clinical deterioration and aggressive supportive care.

Although this study is the first report about C. ramosum infection in preterm infants with a favorable short-term outcome, it is a single-center, retrospective study with a small sample size. Long-term neurodevelopmental and growth outcomes of these infants, particulary those who required surgery, remain unknown and warrant prospective follow-up. The observational design limits our ability to make causal inferences about risk factors.

In summary, C. ramosum is an opportunistic pathogen capable of causing severe invasive infections in susceptible hosts, including preterm infants. Clinicians should consider this organism in cases of neonatal sepsis, particularly with abdominal pathology or in the setting of risk factors such as prematurity, early exposure to antibiotics and non-exclusive breastfeeding. Early recognition, appropriate antimicrobial therapy (preferably with a carbapenem), and prompt surgical intervention when indicated are key to successful outcomes. Further multicenter studies are required in order to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology, optimal management, and long-term consequences of this infection in neonates.
