Identifying Memorization of Diffusion Models through p p -Laplace Analysis
Jonathan Brokman, Amit Giloni, Omer Hofman, Roman Vainshtein, Hisashi Kojima, Guy Gilboa

Abstract
Diffusion models, today’s leading image generative models, estimate the score function, i.e. the gradient of the log probability of (perturbed) data samples, without direct access to the underlying probability distribution. This work investigates whether the estimated score function can be leveraged to compute higher-order differentials, namely p-Laplace operators. We show here these operators can be employed to identify memorized training data. We propose a numerical p-Laplace approximation based on the learned score functions, showing its effectiveness in identifying key features of the probability landscape. We analyze the structured case of Gaussian mixture models, and demonstrate the results carry-over to image generative models, where memorization identification based on the p-Laplace operator is performed for the first time.

Introduction

Memorization in generative models occurs when the model produces outputs that closely replicate samples from its training data, rather than generating novel content. This issue is notably present even in contemporary popular diffusion models. This highlights important questions about generalization, and also raises privacy concerns in generative AI, as it can lead to the unintended leakage of sensitive training data
[
22
]
,
[
32
]
.

A key aspect of memorization lies in its relationship with the probability density of data points. Research findings often link memorization to “bumps” or “delta regions” in the learned data distribution. These bumps may arise in sparsely populated areas of the training dataset due to limited data size. They also occur in inherently low-probability regions (of valid data samples), or result from unintended amplification of probability mass around replicated samples within the training set
[
17
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
23
]
.

The
p
-Laplace is a fundamental differential operator which can be used to quantify degrees of smoothness (also in high dimensions, as desired for our case). We outline below some of its essential properties. Recall the discrete Hilbert space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with standard discrete Euclidean inner product and norm definitions, i.e.
\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{i}u_{i}\cdot v_{i}
and
\norm{u}^{2}=\langle u,u\rangle
, respectively.
The
p
-Dirichlet energy for a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined by,

\begin{split}J_{p}(u):=&\frac{1}{p}\langle\absolutevalue{\nabla u}^{p},1\rangle=\frac{1}{p}\langle\absolutevalue{\nabla u}^{p-2}\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle=\frac{1}{p}\langle-\textrm{div}\left(\absolutevalue{\nabla u}^{p-2}\nabla u\right),u\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{p}\langle-\Delta_{p}u,u\rangle,\end{split}

(1)

where
\Delta_{p}u
is the
p
-Laplace operator defined as

\Delta_{p}u:=\nabla\cdot\left(\absolutevalue{\nabla u}^{p-2}\nabla u\right),

(2)

\nabla\cdot
and
\nabla
are the discrete divergence and gradient operators respectively, and
\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}=\left[\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x(1)}\right)^{2}+\cdots+\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x(d)}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{p-2}{2}}
. We note that
-\Delta_{p}
is a positive semi-definite operator and that
J_{p}(u)\geq 0,\,\forall u
.
The
p
-Laplace is
p-1
homogeneous i.e.

\Delta_{p}(a\cdot u)=a\absolutevalue{a}^{p-2}\cdot\Delta_{p}(u),\quad\forall a\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

We focus on function analysis via the
p
-Laplace and its ability to reliably characterize important phenomena.

We investigate the ability to use the
p
-Laplace to characterize memorized samples in diffusion models. In this setting, the probability distribution is that of natural image data, which is unknown. To address this challenge we propose to capture the “probability bump” around memorized samples using
p
-Laplace approximations, a technique not yet explored. This leads us to several fundamental questions:

•

How can we estimate the
p
-Laplace operator in the settings of diffusion models?

•

What estimation techniques and values of
p
are best suited for practical applications?

•

Can the
p
-Laplace operator effectively characterize points of memorization?

Our findings outline both recommended and less desired approaches for this task, thereby advancing the research on the geometry of the implicitly learned probability function in diffusion models. Furthermore, we provide a novel mathematical perspective that supports the concept of a ‘‘bumpy probability’’ around memorized samples.

3
Method

3.1
Hypothesis: Measuring Memorization with
p
-Laplace

Building upon prior observations that relate memorized samples to “bumps” in the training data distribution, we hypothesize that these memorized samples will manifest as local maxima in the learned (log) probability distribution. The
p
-Laplace, which quantifies the outward flux of
|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u
around a point (as elaborated below in Sec.
3.3
), serves as a tool for this analysis. Typically, around local maxima, the gradient vectors point inward, indicating a negative flux. Therefore, we expect that memorized points will exhibit lower values of the
p
-Laplace for their learned probabilities.

3.2
Diffusion Model Settings

Let us describe basic principals of diffusion models which we will use for our method. Given a dataset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
drawn from an unknown distribution, diffusion models learn to approximate the
score function

s(x):=\nabla\log p(x)
, where
x
is a perturbed data sample with probability density function
p(\cdot)
.

Score function approximation via noise prediction:

Diffusion models rely on the result that estimating
s(x)
is similar to predicting the noise, when
x
is a noisy data-sample
[
33
]
,
[
29
]
. This connection can be traced back to the theoretical foundations laid out by Miyasawa (1961)
[
28
]
, showing that the minimum mean square error (MMSE) estimator of
s
is obtained by denoising
x
under a suitable Gaussian perturbation. More concretely, consider a noising process governed by:

q(x_{t}\mid x_{0}):=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};x_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}I),

(4)

where
\sigma_{t}
increases with time
t
,
x_{t}
is a corresponding noised sample and
\mathcal{N}(a;b,c)
denotes the probability density function of a Gaussian RV
a
with mean and covariance
b,c
2
2

2

This
\mathcal{N}(a;b,c)
is standard notation in the diffusion models literature
. The MMSE predictor of the noise added at
t
aligns with
-s(x_{t})
. Thus, by training a neural network to predict this noise, we effectively learn the score function for varying degrees of noise.

Forward and backward diffusion processes:

The
forward
diffusion process gradually corrupts data samples
x_{0}
with increasing noise levels over discrete timesteps
t=0,\ldots,T
as follows:

x_{t}=\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}x_{0}+\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\epsilon,\,\epsilon\sim N(0,I),

(5)

i.e. we have
q(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}x_{0},\alpha_{t}I)
. The
backward
(reverse) diffusion process is constructed to invert this corruption, starting from pure Gaussian noise at
t=T
and iteratively denoising, aiming to recover a sample from the clean (un-perturbed) data distribution at
t=0
. To obtain this, as
t\to 0
, the diffusion model predictions of added noise become increasingly similar to the score function of an unperturbed sample
s(x_{0})
.

Langevin sampling and stochastic differential equation (SDE):

Once the score is known or approximated, fixing
\alpha_{t}\leftarrow\alpha
,
x\leftarrow\sqrt{1-\alpha}x_{0}+\sqrt{\alpha}\epsilon
, we can sample from
p(x)
via a stochastic process following Langevin dynamics:

dx=\frac{\alpha}{2}s(x)\,dt+\sqrt{\alpha}\,dW_{t},

where
W_{t}
is a Wiener process (Brownian motion), and
\alpha>0
controls step size or “temperature”. This stochastic differential equation, known as "Ito’s SDE"
[
21
]
, ensures that, under suitable conditions, samples drawn from its stationary distribution approximate samples from
p(x)
. When
\alpha
is not constant but scheduled to reduce with time, or "annealed", we obtain
annealed Langevin dynamics
, which further improves mixing and coverage by gradually lowering noise levels as the chain proceeds. Formally, if we have a decreasing schedule
\{\alpha_{t}\}
,
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,\forall t
, we write:

dx=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}s(x)\,dt+\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\,dW_{t},

thereby guiding samples from a broad noisy prior toward the target distribution as
t\to 0
.

Importantly, this entire process often takes place in latent space, as introduced by
[
30
]
. In their work, they proposed to run diffusion in a learned latent space rather than in pixel space. First, a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) maps data

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\longmapsto\quad z=E(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

where
E
is the encoder. The forward noising in latent space is then

z_{t}=\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\,z_{0}+\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\,\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(6)

and the model is trained to invert (
6
) via noise prediction. After denoising, the final latent sample
z_{0}
is decoded to pixel space:

\widehat{x}_{0}=D(z_{0}),

(7)

where
D
is the VAE decoder.

Working in latent space lowers compute costs and speeds up training, compared to direct diffusion on high-resolution images. Artifacts may appear due to imperfect VAE reconstruction, yet our
p
-Laplace analysis, introduced below, applies if we treat the learned latent distribution as
p(z)
and measure memorized “bumps” within that domain.

3.3
Method:
p
-Laplace Estimation

Consider
u(x)=\log p(x)
, and consequently
s(x)=\nabla u(x)
. Denote
\hat{s}(x)
as the approximation of
s
given by the diffusion model - i.e. the relationships are:

\nabla u(x)=s(x)\approx\hat{s}(x).

(8)

By Eq. (
8
), the
p
-Laplace
\Delta_{p}u
can be written as:

\Delta_{p}u(x)=\nabla\cdot\bigl(|s(x)|^{p-2}s(x)\bigr).

(9)

Volume and boundary integral formulations:

To approximate
\Delta_{p}u(x_{0})
locally, we consider a
d
-dimensional ball
B_{R}(x_{0})
of radius
R
. The average
p
-Laplace in the ball is:

\overline{\Delta_{p}u}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{|B_{R}(x_{0})|}\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\Delta_{p}u(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{|B_{R}(x_{0})|}\int_{B_{R}(x_{0})}\nabla\cdot\bigl(|s(x)|^{p-2}s(x)\bigr)\,dx,

where
|B_{R}(x_{0})|
is the volume of the ball. Alternatively, by the divergence theorem,

\overline{\Delta_{p}u}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{|B_{R}(x_{0})|}\int_{\partial B_{R}(x_{0})}|s(x)|^{p-2}s(x)\cdot n\,ds.

with
n
the outward unit normal on the boundary
\partial B_{R}(x_{0})
.

d-Dimensional ball and sphere measures:

The volume of a
d
-dimensional ball of radius
R
is:

|B_{R}(x_{0})|=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)}R^{d},

where
\Gamma
denotes the gamma function. The surface measure of the d-sphere
S^{d-1}
of radius
R
is:

|\partial B_{R}(x_{0})|=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}R^{d-1}.

Discrete Monte Carlo approximation:

Since we do not have closed-form integrals, we approximate these integrals numerically:

1.
Volume Integral Approximation:

Uniformly sample
N
points
\{x_{i}\}
inside
B_{R}(x_{0})
. Compute
\Delta_{p}u(x_{i})
at each sampled point:

\overline{\Delta_{p}u}(x_{0})\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla\cdot\bigl(|s(x)|^{p-2}s(x)),

(10)

since the factor
|B_{R}(x_{0})|/N
is the average volume element for uniform sampling. The normalization ensures that the final average approximates the integral.

2.
Boundary Integral Approximation:

Uniformly sample
N
points
\{y_{i}\}
on the sphere
\partial B_{R}(x_{0})
. Evaluate
|s(y_{i})|^{p-2}s(y_{i})\cdot n_{i}
and sum:

\overline{\Delta_{p}u}(x_{0})\approx\frac{|\partial B_{R}(x_{0})|}{|B_{R}(x_{0})|}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|s(y_{i})|^{p-2}s(y_{i})\cdot n_{i}\right),

(11)

where the
\frac{|\partial B_{R}(x_{0})|}{|B_{R}(x_{0})|}
factor was introduced, since without it the Monte Carlo converges to the mean over the sphere
\partial B_{R}(x_{0})
. This factor ensures that we do not normalize by the sphere’s surface, and correctly normalize by the volume.

Regularity for Applying the Divergence Theorem.

The perturbations in Eq. (
5
) induce the probability density

p_{\alpha}(x)\;=\;(\sqrt{1-\alpha}p_{0}\;*\;\mathcal{N}(x;\sqrt{1-\alpha}x_{0},\alpha I)\;=\;\sqrt{1-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{0}(y)\;G_{\alpha}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

(12)

where
p_{0}
is the underlying (unperturbed) and
\mathcal{N}
denotes the Gaussian probability density function similarly to Eq. (
4
). As is well known, convolving any locally integrable density (and assuming
p_{0}
is such) with a Gaussian kernel yields a
C^{\infty}
function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
- (see for instance "properties of mollifications" in
[
1
]
). Hence, the resulting
p_{\alpha}(x)
is smooth for all
\alpha>0
. Furthermore, since
p_{0}
is nonnegative everywhere, its convolution with a Gaussian
p_{\alpha}
is strictly positive everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an important property when taking
\log
- which is ill-defined at
0
. To conclude -
p_{\alpha}
is both
C^{\infty}
and strictly positive everywhere - implying
\log\,p_{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}
. Therefore, the log-likelihood inherits sufficient regularity for the divergence theorem to apply in our derivations.

Using score from diffusion model:

In practice,
s(x)
may be unknown. If we have a trained diffusion model that estimates the score function, we plug in its approximation
\hat{s}(x)
instead of
s(x)
. Note that this introduces an additional degree of approximation to Eqs. (
10
), (
11
).

By comparing
p
-Laplace estimates computed from the true score (if available) and the learned score, we assess the fidelity of the diffusion model
p
-Laplace approximation. We also compare between the volume-based and boundary-based formulations, and, finally, experiment with
p
-Laplace upon memorization of the diffusion model.

The final experiment extends these methods to a full-scale diffusion model, applying the insights gained from the GMM experiments. We demonstrate that our findings generalize effectively to the unknown, complex, high-dimensional probability landscape of natural images.

4.4
Experiment 3: Image Generative Model Application

Here we apply our approach to a large-scale, pre-trained image diffusion model trained on LAION-5B. We choose Stable Diffusion v1.4
[
30
]
as a testbed due to its popularity as well as well-studied memorization issues for certain prompts. We test a known memorized prompt of this model, “Mothers influence on her young hippo” (see e.g.
[
32
]
), and a nearly identical non-memorized prompt, “Mother’s influence on her young hippo,” generating 100 samples from each, see Fig.
3
. As recommended by our previous experiments, we employ the Boundary integral formulation with
64
samples and
p=1
-Laplace. We use
T=500
generation steps. We leave out the
\frac{|\partial B_{R}(x_{0})|}{|B_{R}(x_{0})|}
factor, since we are only interested in distinguishing memorized from non-memorized points, and this factor is uniform across samples (it only depends on the size of the neighborhood ball, which is fixed).

The memorized prompt yields 100% memorized generations, whereas the non-memorized prompt results in 96% varied outcomes (and 4% memorization still). By examining the approximated
p
-Laplace values at generated samples, we find that the approach can reliably identify and distinguish these memorized outputs.

5
Conclusion

We introduce a novel approach, using score-function analysis of diffusion models to approximate the
p
-Laplace operator, and use it to distinguish memorized from non-memorized generations. We proposed and tested two formulas to approximate the average of the
p
-Laplace over a neighborhood, approximating either a hyper-ball or a hyper-sphere integral. Our experiments indicate that the
1
-Laplace with the boundary integral formula is most effective. We demonstrated this approach’s efficacy on stable-diffusion 1.4, a practical and widely used image generative model. To summarize, this work advances the understanding of diffusion models by offering new insights into the approximation of the
p
-Laplace operator and demonstrating its utility in characterizing training data memorization in image generative models. This initiates a novel theoretical research direction for this timely task.
