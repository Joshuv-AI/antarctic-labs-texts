Brain neurovascular unit in response to intra- and extra-central nervous system cancers: implications, mechanisms, and challenges
Authors: Behnaz Mirzaahmadi, Fatemeh Salahpour-Anarjan, Akbar Mousakhani, Parinaz Haddadi, Fereshteh Vaziri Nezamdoust, Reza Rahbarghazi, Mohammad Karimipour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The neurovascular unit (NVU) plays an important role in maintaining central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis by the dynamic control of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). An increasing amount of evidence indicates that endogenous and exogenous brain tumors can disrupt NVU integrity and function, promoting tumor progression from the brain to the blood side and vice versa. Endogenous CNS tumors promote substantial BBB remodeling through enhanced angiogenesis, tight-junction rearrangements, matrix metalloproteinase activation, and inflammatory mechanisms. In contrast, exogenous brain tumors can hijack the endothelial adhesion molecules and chemokines crossing barriers to the BBB and create a functionally variable blood-tumor barrier (BTB). The resulting microenvironment that presents significant obstacles to delivery is characterized by increased permeability, abnormal vasculature, and retained efflux transporter functionalities. This review summarizes the current understanding of NVU responses to endogenous and exogenous brain tumors, highlighting molecular mechanisms, regional heterogeneity, and translational challenges. We emphasize emerging therapeutic techniques, from transcytotic targeting to nanocarrier systems, that are currently being developed, which circumvent or modify dysfunctional barriers. Insights into induced NVU remodeling over space and time also present new ways for advancing diagnosis, improving drug delivery, and ultimately, patient outcomes in neuro-oncology.

Introduction

Both endogenous and exogenous brain tumors are devastating pathological conditions in females and males with variable morbidity and mortality rates [1]. It has been indicated that endogenous tumors such as gliomas, medulloblastomas, and meningiomas are primarily generated in the brain parenchyma and associated structures [2]. Among these tumors, gliomas are highly aggressive and frequently neoplasms of the CNS [3]. Through the progression and development, tumor cells exhibit metastatic behavior and potentially invade the surrounding parenchyma. These features hinder tumor mass surgical manipulation and removal compared to other malignancies [4]. Migrating tumor cells can promote disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, allowing them to enter the circulation [5]. Unlike endogenous CNS tumors, exogenous tumors are induced in other tissues such as the pulmonary tract, mammary glands, cutaneous tissue, etc., and more commonly metastasize into the brain to form pre-metastatic foci [6]. Again, the distribution of exogenous tumor cells into the brain parenchyma does necessitate the reciprocal molecular interactions between circulating cells and with the BBB cell compartments. To this end, tumor cells adhere to the luminal endothelial cells (ECs), disrupting the physical connection (tight junctions) and allowing them to reach the deep layers of the brain. The changes in BBB integrity led to the emergence of a unique structure known as the brain-tumor barrier (BTB). To be specific, BTB exhibits a permeabilized structure with the loss of tight junctions and transport proteins via the release of several factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), etc [7, 8]. Along with these changes, the efflux capacity in the BTB is intact or enhanced due to some factors, such as P-gp structure, to prevent the accession into tumor cells within the CNS [9]. These features contribute to partial drug delivery and reduced retention time [10]. The alteration of BBB integrity and the existence of inflammation in this structure and metastatic foci can predispose to neurodegeneration and impaired brain function [11]. The severe reduction of adhesion molecules in BBB ECs via stimulated endocytosis has been documented in response to the secretion of different proangiogenesis factors by tumor cells, resulting in the loss of BBB integrity [12]. Despite these changes, efflux mechanisms are still active, which can lessen the efficiency of chemotherapeutics in the CNS [13]. Upon homing in the brain parenchyma, tumor cells stimulate de novo blood vessels, and BTB formation and concurrent ECM modification lead to the development of metastatic foci. Compared to the normal BBB, BTB has distinct physicochemical properties and ultrastructural features [14].

Due to the NVU includes endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, neurons, and the extracellular matrix, we should mention the role of astrocytes. Astrocytes play a dynamic role in tumor progression through their tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), especially those containing non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) and paracrine signals, which are key mediators of this process [15]. Using their endfeet, these cells induce endothelial expression of tight junction proteins, such as occludin, and enhance barrier resistance via TGF-1 [16].

Of course, it should not be forgotten that BBB response and interaction with endogenous and exogenous CNS tumors are associated with tumor origin, type, stage, microenvironment, and involved molecular mechanisms [17]. Both the angiogenic behavior of tumor cells and the recruitment of immune cells can loosen the integrity of the BBB and facilitate the cancer progression into and out of the brain parenchyma [18]. It has been suggested that the behavior of BBB cells against endogenous and exogenous CNS tumors is intricate, provoking complex molecular and cellular interactions [19]. In line with these descriptions, and due to the critical role of NVU structure in controlling the influx and efflux of different substances and cell trafficking, we aimed to add recent data about the activity of the NVU in the inhibition/promotion of endogenous and exogenous tumor cell transport between the blood and brain parenchyma. It is believed that understanding the response of NVU hemodynamics to several pathological conditions can help us to develop and use suitable therapeutic interventions in CNS-derived tumor cells and prevent the further distribution of non-CNS tumors into the brain parenchyma.

BBB structure and physiology

Under physiological conditions, the BBB is a neurovascular unit (Fig. 1) with a specialized and selective biological barrier between the blood components and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to maintain the homeostasis of neuronal cells. Due to the precise activity of cells within the BBB structure, the trafficking of various substances is tightly controlled [20]. Within the BBB structure, ECs are closely attached to tight junctions and wrapped by a basement membrane, astrocyte feet, pericytes, and occasionally microglia in the abluminal surface [21]. In some CNS areas, such as the choroid plexus (CP) and the circumventricular organs (CVO) BBB is not present, resulting in the distribution of substances into brain tissue via fenestrated vascular units [22]. It is important to distinguish these fenestrated vascular units from the BBB and BTB. The fenestrated capillaries of the choroid plexus and certain circumventricular organs form the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB), which is anatomically and functionally distinct from the BBB. Unlike the continuous, non-fenestrated endothelium of the BBB that relies on tight junctions between brain microvascular endothelial cells, the BCSFB comprises fenestrated capillaries and a specialized epithelial cell layer joined by apical tight junctions. Consequently, the permeability profiles and transport mechanisms differ between these interfaces [7]. The BBB primarily controls exchange between blood and brain parenchyma, the BCSFB governs exchange between blood and cerebrospinal fluid, and the BTB which arises in and around tumors, displays heterogeneous permeability properties that reflect tumor angiogenesis and ECM remodeling [23]. Explicitly distinguishing these barriers is essential when discussing permeability, drug delivery approaches, and mechanisms of metastatic entry.

Molecular and ultrastructural studies have revealed that tight junctions are more prominently located at the luminal side with anastomosing strands between the ECs to keep the adjacent membrane very tightly [24]. The type and distribution of tight junctions in the BBB can contribute to barrier cell polarization and the development of asymmetrical transport systems between the luminal, abluminal, apical, and basolateral surfaces [25, 26]. Further molecular analyses have revealed the existence of integral membrane proteins belonging to tight junctions with different intracellular partners [27]. For instance, intracellular compartments include scaffold proteins ZO-1, −2, and − 3, and cingulin, which are closely attached to the F-actin cytoskeleton [28]. Tight junction-integrated proteins encompass tricellulin, occludin, JAMs (junction-associated molecules), and claudins [29]. The exchanges of distinct inorganic ions, gases, non-charged solutes, and water can easily pass the BBB [30]. Besides, other polar molecules such as glucose, amino acids, and nucleosides can cross the BBB compartment. Besides, the existence of specific transporters such as the insulin-independent glucose transporter (GLUT) can facilitate the glucose influx [31].

Small-sized substrates commonly use simple diffusion in the form of paracellular or transcellular routes for entry into the brain parenchyma, while large-sized molecules are transferred through the BBB using carrier-mediated transport systems, facilitated diffusion, or active transport, to reach the CSF [33]. Transcytosis is the main pathway for the entry of peptides and proteins into the brain parenchyma. In this scenario, mechanisms such as adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT) are activated in response to macromolecules and nano-sized particles crossing the BBB, while receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) can control the transport of drugs [34]. Of note, the transport of certain drugs and molecules is orchestrated by multidrug transporters and Pgp-like proteins to restrict uncontrolled influx and prevent the neurotoxic effects [35].

Effects of inflammation on BBB integrity and function

It has been confirmed that BBB integrity can be partially or completely disrupted following inflammation, injuries, tumors, and neurological disease [36]. Under severe inflammatory conditions, the release of various cytokines and factors such as interleukins (IL-1β and IL-6), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and interferon-gamma (INF-γ) can alter the BBB integrity [37]. Brain microvascular endothelial cells (ECs) form the fundamental cellular basis of the BBB. They are not only interconnected by tight junctions but are also the primary producers of tight junction proteins such as claudin-5, occludin, and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1). These proteins are essential for establishing and maintaining the restrictive paracellular barrier that limits the passage of solutes, immune cells, and pathogens between the blood and the CNS. The proper development and regulation of tight junctions by ECs are therefore critical for BBB integrity, and their dysfunction represents an early event in inflammation-induced barrier breakdown [38]. By the progression of inflammation into the brain parenchyma, neurons, microglia, and astrocytes produce and release TNF-α [39]. The exposure of microglia to TNF-α leads to the disruption of BBB EC connectivity and induction of necroptosis. These features can increase the possibility of toxic substances, pathogens, and tumor cells in the CNS [40]. The attachment of TNF-α to cell membrane receptors reduces claudin-5 via the activation of the NF-kB signaling pathway, and alteration of p38MAPK and MEK1/2-ERK1/2 signaling cascade [41]. The local increase of TNF-α can stimulate the production of IL-1β and IL-6 cytokines by ECs and juxtaposed astrocytes, which predispose the cognitive problems [37]. Tumor cells are fundamental components contributing to the leakage of the BBB directly and indirectly. These cells can produce a large number of cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, etc. with a direct effect on immune cells and BBB ECs [42]. This response is concurrent with the increase of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, resulting in enhanced recruitment of T lymphocytes and macrophages into the pre-metastatic foci or tumor site, and peritumoral edema [43]. In addition, systemic or non-CNS tumors can release inflammatory mediators that indirectly compromise BBB integrity. Among these, complement component C5a is a key effector of the innate immune response that promotes endothelial dysfunction and permeability changes [44]. Activation of C5a-C5aR1 signaling on brain microvascular endothelial cells induces cytoskeletal remodeling, tight-junction disassembly, and increased expression of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 [45]. These effects enhance leukocyte and circulating tumor cell transmigration across the BBB. Preclinical evidence indicates that blockade of C5a-C5aR1 signaling attenuates BBB breakdown and reduces brain metastasis formation, underscoring the role of systemic inflammatory pathways in CNS involvement by non-CNS malignancies [46]. It is important to note that adhesion molecules can play dual roles depending on their expression state. Elevated levels of endothelial ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and CD44 promote tumor cell attachment and transendothelial migration across the BBB and BTB, supporting metastatic colonization. In contrast, the downregulation or proteolytic cleavage of junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), claudins, and occludins weakens endothelial cell-cell cohesion, increasing paracellular permeability and facilitating passive tumor cell entry [47]. This differential regulation of adhesion molecule expression represents a coordinated mechanism by which both inflammatory and tumor-derived factors promote BBB/BTB disruption during metastasis formation [48]. The regulation of adhesion molecule expression at the BBB is tightly controlled by the interplay between tumor cells, inflammatory mediators, and BBB component cells. Tumor-derived cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β activate NF-κB and MAPK signaling in endothelial cells, leading to transcriptional upregulation of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, which facilitate tumor cell adhesion and diapedesis [49]. In parallel, astrocytes and microglia exposed to these cytokines release additional mediators such as IL-6, TGF-β, and VEGF that further enhance endothelial activation and vascular permeability. Conversely, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2, MMP-9) secreted by cancer cells and reactive astrocytes degrade tight-junction proteins (claudin-5, occludin) and junctional adhesion molecules (JAM-A/B), thereby complementing cytokine-driven adhesion changes [50]. This dynamic crosstalk between cancer cells, inflammatory molecules, and BBB-resident cells collectively shifts the BBB from a restrictive barrier to a permissive interface for metastatic cell transmigration.

On the other hand, persistent and chronic inflammatory responses due to anaplastic changes, such as glioblastoma multiforme, contribute to the loss of BBB integrity and the loss of therapeutic transfer into the target sites [51]. Previous works have revealed that CNS tumors can increase the levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) such as MMP-9 to degrade the ECM components and loosen the EC-to-EC tight junctions via the lysis of claudin-5 and occludin [52]. Of note, the EC-to-EC connection is susceptible to the modification of the underlying basal membrane, and tumor cell-derived VEGF can distort the production and supporting role of the basal membrane [53]. Along with these changes, tumor cells can activate local pericytes and astrocytes to acquire an inflammatory status with the potential to influence the BBB function.

Reactive astrocytes play a pivotal role in BBB regulation under inflammatory and tumor-associated conditions. Upon activation by cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, astrocytes undergo morphological and transcriptional changes that lead to the release of vascular modulators, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9). These factors weaken tight-junction integrity in adjacent endothelial cells by downregulating claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1, thereby enhancing paracellular permeability [54]. Additionally, reactive astrocytes secrete chemokines such as CCL2 and CXCL10, promoting immune-cell recruitment and amplifying neuroinflammation. Conversely, astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factors (e.g., GDNF, angiopoietin-1) can transiently stabilize the barrier under controlled conditions, highlighting their dual, context-dependent role in BBB dysfunction and repair [55].

It is thought that the increase of cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS) can promote the injury of ECs [56]. In the presence of higher ROS contents, MMPs are in an active state with more digestive properties [57]. The release of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 by inflamed astrocytes is believed to protect the neuronal cells from death and injury [53]. On the other hand, the sustained release of these cytokines can make the astrocytes produce miRNA-19a-enriched exosomes with the potential to inhibit the PTEN and stimulate the tumor cell dynamic growth [53–59]. In line with the astrocyte activity, microglia release angiogenesis-related cytokines such as TGF-β, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, VEGF, and EGF, MMPs, which can pre-determine tumor expansion, enlargement, angiogenesis behavior, and metastasis [60, 61]. It is thought that microglia can provoke distinct effectors belonging to the Wnt signaling pathway [62, 63]. The pathological consequences can lead to the abnormal activity of desmin+ in metastatic regions with the increase of BTB leakage [62, 63]. Besides the loss of BBB integrity to facilitate the tumor cell crossing, the expression of specific membrane-bound factors, such as CD44, in response to breast cancer cells expedites trans-endothelial migration. Other pro-angiogenic factors, such as angiopoietin-2, help loosen the endothelial barrier by altering ZO-1 and claudin-5 function [64]. Of course, it should not be forgotten that the local density of chemokines such as CXCL12 and its receptor CXCR4 reported on breast tumor cells is integral in metastasis into brain parenchyma, in which the direct inhibition of CXCR4-related signaling pathway reduces the formation of breast cancer metastatic foci inside the brain [65]. Based on the obtained data, these effects were more evident in differentiated GBM cells when compared to the tumor cells with the stemness feature (Fig. 2) [66]. In this model, the permeability assay employed dextran-70 kDa, a large-molecular-weight tracer that does not traverse an intact BBB. Its passage, therefore, reflects marked barrier disruption induced by IL-6 rather than subtle tight-junction alterations. As reported in the original study, the authors combined dextran diffusion with measurements of ABC transporter and tight-junction protein expression to validate BBB compromise. This distinction is important because using high-molecular-weight dextran alone could otherwise be misinterpreted as indicating physiological permeability.

BBB response to exogenous and endogenous CNS tumors

Endogenous CNS tumors

Glioblastoma

To treat glioma, drugs must be able to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a semipermeable defense barrier that shields the brain from toxins. The BBB consists of tightly adherent capillary endothelial cells lining brain capillaries, a basement membrane, astrocyte end-feet, and pericytes. Tight junctions function as a selective barrier, limiting paracellular transit and inhibiting drug penetration in brain tissue. Moreover, endo-thelia transport mechanisms regulate the entry and release of molecules, further restricting BBB access. Successful glioma management requires knowledge of and overcoming structural barriers. In malignant cerebral neoplasms, contrast-enhancement reflects the partial breakdown of the BBB (BBBD), but clinical evidence discloses that the majority of the tumor retains an intact BBB, restricting uniform drug delivery. 1 Some anticancer drugs employed in chemotherapy are too bulky to pass through the blood-brain barrier, and the rise in concentration results in tolerance and systemic toxicity [67]. The most aggressive brain tumor, glioblastoma, is characterized by hypercellularity, pleomorphism, mitoses, central necrosis, and hypervascularity. The extensive literature on the morphology of tumor blood vessels [68]. Focuses on fenestration formation, changes in tight junctions, caveolae, density of mitochondria, subendothelial basal lamina thickness, perivascular space widening, and pericytes. The terms “astrocytomas,” “oligodendrogliomas,” and “ependymomas” are employed to characterize the normal stromal cells of the brain, or microglial cells, based on their morphology and molecular characteristics [69]. Gliomas are extremely heterogeneous, infiltrative, and diffused with varying degrees of invasiveness [70]. They may invade the entire organ with invasive cells spreading beyond the initial tumor. However, gliomas rarely spread beyond the primary tumor, remaining within the cranium with minimal systemic spread [69]. The formation of new vasculature also accompanies the development of glioblastoma, and the product of such an endeavor has the dire clinical consequence of creating vasogenic edema within the brain, raising intracranial pressure (ICP) by BBB disruption [71]. But a defective vessel formation results in abnormal form and function with different diameters, permeability, and uneven distribution because of it [72]. Regulation of aquaporins (AQP) expression by the BBB (astrocytic end feet) has been linked with glioma pathogenesis as well as VEGF overexpression. AQPs are integral membrane transport proteins that enable water efflux through cell membranes at high osmotic gradients [73]. AQP8 expression in human astrocytomas correlates with aggressiveness and increased pathological grade [74]. Aquaporins regulate BBB integrity, and the AQP4 pattern contributed by the membrane in gliomas compensates for perivascular space enlargement and foot loss [71]. Disruption of the BBB has no impact on the early development of glioma, and normal brain vessels sustain the tumor mass. Normal vessel endothelial cells detach from the vessel structure during tumor growth and become new angiogenic sites at the tumor site. They displace normal vessels and migrate [68]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can detect BBB disruptions through the use of a contrast medium [75]. Gadolinium is the most widely used contrast medium (CM) for GBM diagnosis since it would not typically be able to cross the intact BBB. In GBM, the blood-brain barrier is disrupted, and therefore, gadolinium leaks into the tissue and creates enhancing lesions. In the central nervous system (CNS), tight junctions between endothelial cells form the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which limits Inflammation is also accompanied by compromised BBB function with cerebral edema. Studies recognized that immunofluorescence stainings for TJ molecules in an animal model of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) and in human tissue with glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) demonstrate correlations of BBB leakiness with molecular alterations at BBB TJs. In addition, the altered cerebral microvessels of human GBM showed selective loss of claudin-3 immunostaining. Additional evidence supports that claudin-3 is key to determining BBB TJ integrity in vivo because it is selectively deleted from BBB TJs in pathologic disease states like EAE or GBM when BBB integrity is lost [76]. Human glioblastoma was found to exhibit increased ECM thickness despite agrin loss and AQP4 redistribution over the glioma cell surface [77, 78]. Glioma cells upregulate MMPs, a family of degrading enzymes involved in tumor cell invasion and transmigration through blood vessels [79]. MMP-2, MMP-9, and MMP-12 are involved in collagen degradation.

Proteases can contribute to BBB disruption in the following three ways: (a) release of growth factors trapped in the vessel ECM, which is involved in angiogenic processes; (b) remodeling of the vessel ECM through stimulation of integrin receptors by binding to the RGD binding motives; (c) integrin was reported to be upregulated in glioma endothelial cells [80], whose binding to such domains can induce cell spreading, migration, and angiogenesis; and degradation of the basal lamina, which disrupts some of the functions of ECM molecules, such as agrin, that are postulated to play a role in BBB integrity [81]. Glioblastoma invasion disrupts the BBB by several molecular mechanisms. Downregulation of tight junction proteins, disrupted endothelial cell interaction, cytokine release, extracellular matrix remodeling, and increased angiogenesis increase permeability and tumor growth.

Due to extravasation secondary to BBB disruption, glioblastoma-related brain edema is linked to vasogenic edema. Edema is alleviated by DEX via the promotion of tight junction gene expression, controlling the endothelial permeability of the BBB [82]. Experiments showed that glioblastoma tumors circumvented antiangiogenic therapy by (a) reactivating angiogenesis through up-regulation of other proangiogenic factors and (b) invading normal brain tissue, which was associated with up-regulation of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)−2, MMP-9, and MMP-12; secreted protein, acidic, cysteine-rich; and tissue inhibitor 1, respectively. In vitro, tumor invasiveness was enhanced by anti-VEGF treatment, demonstrating that autocrine VEGF signaling regulates glioblastoma invasion in addition to paracrine effects on endothelial cells [83]. Different transcription factors with different cytokines cooperate in GBM formation. STAT transcription factors, and more specifically STAT3, depend on neural stem cells and astrocytes during development. Constitutively active STAT3 is involved in mediating the IFN and IL-6 cytokine families and is correlated with tumor grade. Cytokine receptors are phosphorylated by JAK, which recruits STAT1 and STAT3, dimerizes, and binds to nuclear promoters [82]. Yet another transcription factor modified in GBM is nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). NF-κB activates inflammatory genes like IL-6 and IL-8 upon stimulation by different cytokines like VEGF, TNF-α, and IL-1. NF-κB is activated by TNF-α via its receptor TNFR1 to result in anti-apoptotic and NF-κB pathways. TMZ resistance has also been associated with low expression of the negative regulator A20 in GBM. Tumor growth, invasion, and angiogenesis are all inhibited by blocking the activity of NF-κB. Cytokine activation of transcription factors plays an important part in GBM due to the amplification of signals. Tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, immune cell infiltration, migration, and invasion are all influenced [84, 85]. The poor therapeutic outcomes in glioblastoma are largely attributable to the restrictive nature of the BBB and BTB, which limit the penetration and retention of conventional chemotherapeutics, monoclonal antibodies, and gene-based therapies [86, 87]. Advanced strategies such as nanoparticles, receptor-mediated transcytosis, and cell-based carriers are being explored to overcome these barriers and improve drug delivery and efficacy in GBM [88].

Medulloblastoma

Cancer cells can disrupt brain-EC interactions. This blockage disrupts the BBB, resulting in a modified CNS structure called the blood-brain tumor barrier (BTB) [89]. Understanding the sort of BTB a tumor creates is crucial for effective therapy due to its structure, quality, and function. WNT medulloblastoma has a leaky BBB due to abnormal EC cell WNT signaling, resulting in hemorrhagic vasculature and fenestration. This dysfunctional BBB contributes to the excellent prognosis of this molecular subtype [90, 91]. However, a study of BBB and BTB features in preclinical models of medulloblastoma revealed varying permeability [89]. In genetically engineered models of SHH medulloblastoma, SOX2+ cells significantly contribute to BTB integrity by expanding capillary lining processes [92].

Drug transcytosis and treatment effectiveness are impacted by BBB breakdown, which is also variable among the four histological/molecular subtypes of medulloblastoma, where endothelial cells exhibit varying degrees of fenestration. The WNT subtype exhibits many fenestrations in blood vessels as a result of the down-regulation of certain genes that are typically expressed in the CNS endothelium due to the activation of the WNT/β-catenin pathway (such as claudin5 and the solute carrier family 2 member 1, also known as SLC2A1). A decrease in the Notch3 pathway, which interacts with the WNT pathway in CNS vasculature development, may partially explain the branching vasculature in WNT-medulloblastomas [91]. WNT medulloblastomas have a morphologically thick-walled, hemorrhagic vasculature that also contains many tiny capillary and venous structures that do not have an intact BBB [91, 93]. Work from other systems has linked β-catenin to the direct control of endothelial cell-cell adhesions and sustaining vascular barrier function during embryonic and postnatal development, despite the need for mechanistic research in WNT medulloblastomas [94, 95]. It has been demonstrated that in CTN1B−/−mice, endothelial cell-cell junctions are impacted by loss of β-catenin expression and activity in endothelial cells, leading to increased permeability and fragility throughout vascular development [96]. These findings suggest that vascular and BBB integrity should be impacted similarly by the deregulation of WNT signaling in medulloblastoma [97]. It should be noted that individuals with medulloblastoma who have hereditary mutations in WNT/β-catenin signaling may benefit more from the findings mentioned above.

Meningiomas

Brain tumors known as meningiomas arise in the meninges covering the brain and spinal cord [98]. They are the second most frequent occurrence after gliomas and are one of the most common primary cerebral CNS tumors. Benign meningiomas can be managed by surgery and radiation, but malignant ones are invasive, recurrent, and metastasizing, and have few treatment options [99]. Since most meningiomas are located outside the BBB, they are more susceptible to inflammation and systemic immunology compared to BBB structures [100]. TNF-α, which causes inflammatory damage, initiates inflammatory reactions and stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory proteins, including IL-6, was reported to be increased in meningioma patients in retrospective studies [101]. Aside from another, there is a correlation between meningiomas and individuals with increased inflammatory cytokine levels, such as TNF-β and CXCL1. By disrupting endothelial cell junctions, these molecules could potentially inhibit the BBB [102]. Most meningiomas are beyond the BBB and can be invaded by immune cells, making them more sensitive to systemic immunity and inflammation. Systemic inflammatory disorders are linked to several meningioma variations [103]. In all types of meningiomas, whether malignant or not, MCs with different morphologies were present predominantly in the perivascular region and occasionally in connective tissue lobules, irrespective of growth rate, grade, or degree of calcification [104]. Prestored mediators like corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), neurotensin (NT), substance P (SP), tryptase, chymase, vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), TNF, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and various chemokines and cytokines, some of which have been proved to compromise the integrity of the BBB, are heavily secreted by MCs with a large granule number. The key functions of MCs in inflammation [105] and disruption of the BBB [106, 107] determine major areas to investigate novel therapeutic strategies. Moreover, stress is inevitable in cancer patients and can provoke metastatic brain tumors by the action of brain MCs and disruption of the BBB via the CRH pathway [108, 109]. Increased permeability of the BBB promotes peripherally localized primary tumor cells to pass into the parenchyma of the brain and metastasize [110]. Physical destruction and swelling (edema) result from meningioma invasion into brain tissue, disrupting the BBB. Breakdown of the tightly adherent endothelial cells of the BBB by tumor invasion disables its protective action and causes structural damage. Edema generated from this injury elevates pressure and degrades the barrier, making the brain more permeable to toxic chemicals [111].

Exogenous CNS tumors

Lung

Brain metastasis (BM) is a global public health issue, present in 20% of cancer patients and responsible for cancer-related mortality. Lung cancer (LC) is the most common etiology of BM, with 25%−50% of LC patients developing BM [112, 113]. BMs are the cause of neurological symptoms and cognitive impairment, reducing the quality of life of the patients. The BBB is considered to be one of the mediating mechanisms in the complex pathophysiology of BM. LC cells move along the blood circulation to the brain vascular system, bind to microvascular endothelial cells, penetrate the parenchyma, cause angiogenesis, grow upon growth factor stimulation, and ultimately invade the blood-brain barrier to establish intracerebral metastases [114]. BBB consists of endothelial cells, basement membrane, astrocytic foot, and pericytes [115, 116]. Integrity is essential to prevent the entry of toxic chemicals into peripheral circulation and to inhibit most tumor cells [46]. Despite that, certain cytokines, e.g., cyclooxygenase2 (COX2 or PTGS2), heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HB-EGF), ST6GALNAC5, PLEKHA5, placental growth factor (PLGF), and proteolysis of adhesion molecules (like JAM-B, junctional adhesion molecule B) [117], and leukocyte mimicry [117], can facilitate metastatic cells to move through the various layers of cells that make up the blood-brain barrier [118, 119]. Cell transfer across the BBB leads to disruption of the BBB and increased permeability. Thus, BBB permeability testing can be useful in the prognostication of BM. The permeability of the BBB is significant in BM, but hard to quantify human BBB permeability. Measurement of BBB permeability by cerebrospinal fluid to serum albumin ratio is one of the well-standardized techniques. Its validity remains questionable due to its invasive nature and sensitivity to cerebrospinal fluid flow [120]. The research revealed that BBB permeability may rise before BM or micro-metastasis and correlates with the primary LC stage. Researchers expected higher LC stages to cause more BBB breakdown and risk of BM development [121]. BBB integrity is required to prevent invasion of tumor cells into the brain. BBB permeability increases in brain metastases in lung cancer [122]. However, the mechanisms behind these alterations remain unclear.

Breasts

Metastatic breast cancer metastasizes into the brain in 10–30% of women with HER2-positive or triple-negative tumor subtypes [123]. Neither tumor phenotype nor size correlates with BBB modification, but it is triggered in glial tumors by endothelial cell growth and vascular leakage after overstimulation with VEGF, impacting BBB integrity [124, 125]. Increased loose associations between tumor brain capillaries endow them with a distinct composition. Pathologic overexpression of pro-angiogenic factors within tumors promotes endothelial cell membrane adhesion molecule endocytosis, which increases BBB drug permeability [12]. Remarkably, efflux transporters remain active in metastases, contributing to anticancer drugs’ low CNS penetrance and effectiveness [13]. Novel brain vasculature tumor cells start neo-angiogenesis and microenvironment reconstruction upon first metastatic colonization, increasing tumor invasion and growth. Development of a blood-tumor barrier (BTB) creates a unique neurovascular-tumor unit with certain physiological characteristics distinct from those of the intact BBB [14]. The BTB is permeable because there are no tight junctions and astrocytic-endothelial cell contacts, creating a fenestra and discontinuous endothelium, which increases permeability [126]. Metastatic lesions outgrow the oxygen supply, causing angiogenesis that is VEGF-mediated [127]. Since the dynamic angiogenesis that takes place during brain metastasis development is highly variable among lesions, this is considered to be one of the primary factors responsible for the heterogeneity 5. Astrocytes, pericytes, and microglia enhance BTB cellular and molecular features. Astrocytes protect neuronal cells from damage and death by secreting inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 [53]. The continuous release of these cytokines supports tumor proliferation and survival. Astrocytes secrete exosomes containing miRNA-19a, which causes PTEN loss and increases tumor cell proliferation and invasion in the brain [53, 58]. Brain tumor microglia secrete growth factors and cytokines, including TGFβ, TNFα, IL1, IL6, VEGF, EGF, and metalloproteinases, that increase tumor proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis [60, 61]. Microglia cell populations promote metastatic colonization through the Wnt pathway. Furthermore, the large numbers of desmin-positive pericytes within metastases to the brain correlate with enhanced BTB permeability [62, 63]. The BBB endothelial cell junctions are especially susceptible to changes in disease states, such as BM formation. Key molecules playing a role in the mechanism have been identified. VEGF can make BM contribution through the stimulation of tumor cell migration via impaired endothelial integrity [64]. Moreover, CD44 plays a critical role as a breast cancer cell transendothelial migration regulator. Angiopoietin-2 enhances the brain vascular permeability and rearranges Zonula occludens ZO-1 and claudin-5 TJ protein architecture, which leads to disruption of the BBB and TNBC cell colonization [64]. The chemokine CXCL12 and its receptor CXCR4 on breast tumor cells play a vital role in the migration of tumor cells in the brain. Inhibition of the CXCR4-dependent intracellular pathway can restrict this migration [65].

Skin (Melanoma)

Melanoma is the most dangerous type of skin cancer that originates from melanocytes, cells that produce melanin [128]. Older patients with advanced melanomas typically get distant metastases within the liver, lung, and brain. Brain metastasis progression is associated with a poorer prognosis [129, 130]. Immunotherapy and targeted therapies are successful in melanoma for some patients, but are less successful at controlling established brain metastases [131, 132]. Metastatic tumor cells that can survive the harshness of the circulation and arrest at the capillary level in the distant site are given the chance to extravasate and invade secondary organs. In the parenchyma of the organ, micrometastases and, ultimately, macrometastases can form [133, 134]. In the brain, the formation of melanoma metastases is dependent on the successful penetration of the BBB [135, 136]. Melanoma metastases result from a multi-step process that begins with local primary tumor invasion and intravasation into blood or lymphatic vessels. BBB is a highly tight endothelium with low paracellular permeability that limits extravasation of cellular components and even the diffusion of ions from blood to the CNS parenchyma [137]. Adherens and tight junctions are significant molecular complexes of the endothelial cell-cell junctions. VE-Cadherin is the most abundant member of the endothelial adherens junctions, while junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), claudins, and TJ-associated MARVEL (MAL and related proteins for vesicle trafficking and membrane link) proteins (TAMPs) [138] together form the endothelial tight junctions. Endothelial junctions also include other proteins such as CD99 and platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (PECAM-1) [139]. Its remarkable barrier properties are due to the robust expression and intricate organization of TAMPs and claudins in the BBB, hindering extravasation of cells from circulation [140, 141]. Melanoma cell integrin very late antigen (VLA)−4 on melanoma cells has an adhesive interaction with the immunoglobulin (Ig)-like cell adhesion molecule VCAM-1 on endothelial cells to enable melanoma cell extravasation, as was established for the blood-brain barrier [142] and other endothelial layers of other types [143–145]. Melanoma cells penetrate between the endothelial cell layer of the BBB, which triggers the subsequent diapedesis through the endothelial layer. This can be achieved through the paracellular pathway, which passes between adjacent endothelial cells, or the transcellular pathway, which passes through a pore within an endothelial cell’s cytoplasm, as depicted for immune cells [144, 145] and metastatic cancer cells [146]. Changes in the molecular structure of BBB endothelial cell junctions or inflammatory stimuli can influence their barrier function [147, 148]. PECAM-1 deficiency [149] or IL-1 stimulation, in contrast to TNF-stimulation, has been found to compromise the barrier function of a tight in vitro blood-brain barrier model of primary mouse brain microvascular endothelial cells (pMBMECs) by experiments [145]. Melanotransferrin is a melanoma cell surface molecule. Melanotransferrin expression was linked with augmented transmigration of melanoma cells through the BBB, whereas down-regulation of its expression significantly decreased transmigration. Mediation of melanotransferrin action can involve the plasmin-metalloproteinase system [150]. More recent research identifies that MMP-2 is instrumental in the development of brain metastases of breast cancer, melanoma, and leukemia cells [151, 152]. MMP-3 and MMP-9 are involved in breast cancer brain metastases [153].

Challenges in drug delivery across the BBB & BTB in CNS cancers

The BBB and the BTB represent formidable obstacles in the effective treatment of CNS tumors, particularly glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and other malignant brain neoplasms. The BBB and BTB are both vascular barriers formed by brain endothelial cells, but they exhibit critical differences in structure and function (Fig. 3). These barriers severely limit the penetration of most chemotherapeutic agents, biological drugs, and gene therapies, contributing to the poor prognosis associated with these malignancies [154, 155]. BBB with multicellular layers restricts the easy access of several compounds to the brain context, and only small, non-charged lipophilic substrates cross the BBB via passive diffusion; meanwhile, most chemotherapeutics (e.g., doxorubicin and paclitaxel) possess large sizes with hydrophilic or charged molecular backbone [156]. Unfortunately, the overexpression of P-gp/BCRP in BBB& BTB cells can hinder the entry of the chemodrugs. Of note, the permeabilized BBB structure allows only sporadic chemotherapeutic transfer from blood to the brain side; however, BTB leads to failure in compound distribution into the central hypoxic zone inside the tumor masses because of very concentrated supporting ECM and concomitant high interstitial pressure [157]. In general, the phenomenon of drug transfer through the BBB is done via engaging passive and/or active mechanisms (Fig. 4). In the passive form, the chemical compounds can cross the BBB due to unique physicochemical properties (small with lipophilic structure), while a large number of modern therapeutics rely on antibodies and genes that need active mechanisms to increase the delivery rate. For instance, activation of RMT and delivery using nanoparticles helps, in part but not completely, to circumvent the limitations associated with the size and charge of administered compounds. Taking advantage of BBB permeability with fenestrated vascular units and simultaneous inhibition of efflux mechanisms are very helpful in the delivery of target molecules into the brain parenchyma [158]. It is important to note that permeability-based assays, such as those using fluorescent tracers or dextran diffusion, primarily assess the functional passage of molecules across the BBB but do not directly measure the structural integrity of endothelial tight junctions. Therefore, these assays should ideally be complemented by measurements of transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER), which quantitatively reflects the barrier’s tightness and helps distinguish between functional permeability changes and structural tight-junction disruption [159]. Combining permeability and TEER assessments before and after experimental interventions provides a more accurate validation of BBB integrity and the extent of barrier modulation.

Up to the present time, the application of numerous drugs has been approved in the CNS. Unfortunately, most of these compounds lack suitable tumoricidal properties due to because of restricted delivery into the brain parenchyma. For instance, the existence of activated efflux mechanisms like P-gp and BCRP can reduce the retention of penetrated drugs inside the CNS that crossed the BBB via passive diffusion [161]. As aforementioned, features such as physicochemical properties and size of target drugs can be determinants in the transfer through the BBB & BTB. It is thought that in small-sized molecules, features such as polar surface area (PSA), molecular weight, partition coefficient (Log P), hydrogen bond donor (HBD), and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) can directly affect the BBB & BTB crossing potential [162, 163]. According to these descriptions, small lipophilic compounds with less PSA use passive diffusion from the EC membrane. For optimization of drug delivery through passive diffusion, compounds with PSA ≤ 60–70 Å², Log P = 1–3, and molecular weight ≤ 400 Da are selected [164]. For large-sized compounds with high molecular weights, such as bevacizumab and pembrolizumab (150 kDa ≤ X), the passive diffusion mechanism is not effective in BBB & BTB crossing [165]. On the other hand, drugs with higher PSA (i.e., methotrexate) efflux transport systems such as MRP1 (ABCC1), BCRP (ABCG2), and P-gp (ABCB1) restrict the CNS bioavailability [165, 166]. Vincristine and Etoposide are commonly detected by efflux transporter systems such as P-gp due to higher molecular weights, PSA, and certain HBA features with limited availability in the brain parenchyma [167, 168]. In addition to the limited brain availability, other features, such as side effects, can also restrict the application of modern drugs in human medicine. For instance, NUV-422, a Lomustine analog and CDK2/4/6 inhibitor, has been suggested for malignant glioma patients [169]. However, phase I/II clinical trials (NCT04541225) revealed uveitis, leading to the lack of clinical use [170]. Other sophisticated strategies have also been developed to hinder uncontrolled tumor cell growth. For example, proteolysis-targeting chimeric molecules (PROTACs) are fabricated using various linkers, leading to variations in molecular weight (ranging between 700 and 1100 Da) [37]. These molecules are in the preclinical phase, targeting the oncogenic effectors inside the glioma tumor cells [171]. As expected, the higher molecular weights restrict PROTACs entry through the BBB via passive diffusion, and thus active mechanisms like CMT, and RMT should be provoked for obtaining suitable tumoricidal properties [172–174]. In general, limitations associated with the transfer of drugs through BBB, and BTB are low passive entry and less retention time which in turn necessitate the application of sophisticated delivery mechanisms in individuals with CNS tumors.

Strategies to overcome the BTB/BBB barrier in CNS tumors

The disruption of blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity is a key strategy for enhancing drug delivery to brain tumors [9]. Conventional systemic administration often fails to achieve sufficient therapeutic concentrations due to the BBB’s selective permeability to chemotherapeutic agents [10]. To address this limitation, various methods have been explored to transiently increase BBB permeability. However, critical considerations include the degree of permeabilization, potential neurotoxicity, optimal drug delivery timing, precise targeting, and appropriate dosing. Excessive or prolonged BBB disruption may lead to chronic inflammation and significant barrier damage [175]. While preclinical and translational studies have demonstrated the efficacy of many strategies, their clinical application remains challenging [157]. Over the years, approaches to overcome these barriers have evolved and can be broadly classified into pharmacological and physical disruption methods. This report evaluates these strategies, examines recent advancements and clinical trials, and compares the efficacy of pharmacological versus physical methods in CNS tumor therapy. Before implementing such strategies, however, the safety implications of BBB and BTB modulation must be carefully considered. Strategies designed to transiently increase the permeability of the BBB or BTB must carefully balance improved drug delivery with potential safety risks. While enhanced permeability can facilitate higher intratumoral drug concentrations, it also carries the risk of neurotoxicity and off-target damage due to uncontrolled drug exposure [176]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that excessive or improperly controlled BBB disruption can cause local tissue injury, including microhemorrhage, sterile inflammatory responses, and neuronal stress. These effects have been reported with physical modulation techniques such as focused ultrasound (FUS) combined with microbubbles [177]. Moreover, increasing CNS penetration may exacerbate the systemic toxicity of certain chemotherapeutics, such as doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity [178]. Also, clinical experience further underscores the importance of careful control. Osmotic BBB opening using agents like mannitol carries well-documented risks of cerebral edema and rebound intracranial hypertension if not meticulously managed [179]. Conversely, clinical studies using magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) indicate that image-guided, low-intensity BBB opening is feasible and generally well tolerated in small cohorts, though longer follow-up and standardized safety assessments are required [180].

Pharmacological disruption methods

Pharmacological strategies to overcome the BBB/BTB focus on modulating the barrier’s properties or exploiting endogenous transport mechanisms to enhance drug delivery. These approaches include the use of nanoparticles, adenosine A2 receptor A2AR agonists, and bradykinin analogs.

Application of NPS to overcome BBB/BTB

Nanoparticles (NPs) with multifunctional properties, including organic (polymeric, lipid-based, albumin-based) and inorganic (gold, iron oxide, silicon) systems (Fig. 5), enhance targeted drug delivery by prolonging circulation time and enabling co-delivery of therapeutic and imaging agents [161, 181]. Nanoparticles offer a multifaceted platform for overcoming the BBB/BTB through size, surface modification, and functionalization with targeting ligands [181]. These designs exploit CMT, RMT, AMT mechanisms or tight junction modulation to improve penetration across these restrictive barriers [175]. Thus, a thorough understanding of NP-BBB/BTB interactions, cellular uptake mechanisms, and biodistribution is essential for engineering more effective NPs with enhanced tumoricidal efficacy in brain tumor therapy [182, 183]. The size and shape of NPs critically influence their ability to cross the BBB. Smaller NPs (< 12 nm) may utilize paracellular transport via transient tight junction disruptions (e.g., in pathological conditions), whereas NPs between 10 and 100 nm predominantly rely on active transport mechanisms like RMT [184]. For instance, Sarin et al. demonstrated that a minimal increase (~ 2 nm) in the size of Gd-labeled polyamidoamine dendrimers significantly reduced their penetration through the BTB in orthotopic RG2 glioma-bearing rats [184]. Beyond size, NP geometry and surface properties influence barrier interactions and biodistribution. Spherical NPs exhibit higher cellular uptake compared to rod- or disk-shaped counterparts [161]. While passive diffusion is generally restricted to small, lipophilic molecules under physiological conditions, it plays a negligible role in NP translocation across the intact BBB [182, 185]. However, exceptions exist; for example, 10 nm gold-based NPs have demonstrated passive diffusion across the normal BBB [181].

Of note, the distribution of large-sized NPs is limited because of the spatial molecular structure and endothelial glycocalyx [184, 186]. In the BBB and BTB, the surface fibrous glycocalyx, rich in negatively charged components such as sialic acids and hyaluronan, serves as the primary physical and electrostatic obstacle restricting nanoparticle extravasation [166]. As demonstrated by Chan and colleagues, enzymatic removal of glycocalyx components (e.g., via neuraminidase or hyaluronidase) or tuning nanoparticle surface charge (e.g., cationic coatings) can significantly enhance cellular binding and uptake, suggesting strategic modifications to overcome this barrier for improved drug delivery [165, 166]. For instance, neuraminidase treatment reduced sialic acid levels by 60%, increasing nanoparticle binding by 80% in THP-1 cells, while cationic nanoparticles showed 3–9× higher uptake than neutral/anionic variants. In vivo, charge-dependent clearance kinetics (e.g., rapid cationic nanoparticle removal) further highlight the glycocalyx’s role in biodistribution [166].

Cell-mediated transport (CMT) represents a promising strategy for enhancing BBB penetration by exploiting the natural migratory capabilities of specific cell types to deliver nanoparticle (NP) payloads into the brain. Engineered cellular carriers can be utilized to transport NPs across both the BBB and BTB, capitalizing on their intrinsic homing properties to achieve targeted therapeutic delivery. This approach combines the biological specificity of cellular trafficking with the versatility of nanocarriers, offering a potential solution to the challenge of CNS drug delivery [181]. This approach exploits the capacity of immune cells (e.g., macrophages, neutrophils) and stem cells [e.g., mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)] to adhere to the BBB, extravasate in response to inflammatory or tumor-derived signals, and infiltrate the brain parenchyma (Fig. 4G). By encapsulating NPs within these cells, drug-loaded cargo can be efficiently shuttled to target sites while minimizing off-target effects [175]. For example, TRAIL-expressing MSCs loaded with paclitaxel-loaded NPs demonstrated enhanced on-target delivery and antitumor efficacy in glioma models [175, 187]. Such cell-based carriers not only improve NP biodistribution but also actively respond to disease microenvironments, offering a dynamic and biologically responsive delivery system. A recent study [188] demonstrated a novel nanosystem combining mouse bone marrow-derived macrophage membranes with a targeted polymer and low-frequency ultrasound (LFU) to enhance drug delivery to glioma (Fig. 6). This system achieved a high drug loading efficiency (44.2%) and significantly improved temozolomide (TMZ) delivery by evading immune clearance and facilitating BBB opening via LFU. The macrophage membrane coating enabled selective targeting of glioma cells through the Angiopep-2 peptide, which binds to LRP-1 receptors highly expressed on glioma cells. This resulted in a 5- to 15-fold increase in uptake by glioma cell lines (U87 and U251) and led to significant tumor inhibition and improved survival in glioma-bearing mice [188].

In recent years, cell-based delivery systems have represented a highly promising strategy for glioma therapy, offering prolonged circulation, active targeting, controlled drug release, and superior biocompatibility. By protecting therapeutic agents from degradation and facilitating transport across the BBB, a major challenge in glioma treatment, these systems significantly enhance drug accumulation in tumors [88]. Approaches such as macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles and engineered neural stem cells (NSCs) exploit tumor-specific receptors and natural homing properties to precisely target glioma cells, while minimizing systemic toxicity through localized, controlled release within the tumor microenvironment. Preclinical and early clinical studies demonstrate their potential to improve tumor inhibition, prolong survival, and synergize with adjunct therapies like ultrasound for enhanced delivery [189]. Furthermore, recent advances combining cell-membrane coatings with targeting peptides and physical BBB disruption techniques highlight the versatility and efficacy of these systems. Collectively, cell-based platforms address critical limitations in glioma therapy, showing great potential to transform treatment outcomes through targeted, efficient, and adaptable drug delivery [190].

Adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT) is a key mechanism enabling cationic nanocarriers to cross the BBB (Fig. 4F). This process relies on electrostatic interactions between positively charged nanoparticles and the negatively charged glycocalyx on the luminal surface of brain endothelial cells (BCECs), triggering endocytosis via clathrin-coated pits or caveolae, followed by vesicular transport into the brain parenchyma [34, 160, 182]. Unlike RMT, AMT is less specific but can be exploited by designing cationic nanoparticles to bind the BBB surface. However, indiscriminate interactions with anionic membranes may lead to off-target effects, driving recent advances in enzyme-activatable nanoprobes and biocompatible cationic polymers to improve specificity and reduce toxicity [191]. Cationic nano-polymers such as polyethyleneimine, chitosan, dendrimers, and lipid-based NPs exploit this mechanism for brain delivery. Polysaccharides like chitosan and maltodextrin further enhance AMT by strengthening electrostatic adhesion to the BBB, boosting nanoparticle uptake [191]. Notably, cationic nanoparticles decorated with cell-penetrating peptides (e.g., TAT) or albumin coatings promote vesicle generation and transcytosis, enabling targeted delivery of therapeutics (e.g., TRAIL plasmid DNA) to brain tumors [192, 193]. While polyethyleneimine and dendrimers rely on their strong cationic charge, cationic liposomes exploit transcytosis mechanisms [193]. Recent preclinical studies highlight the potential of these strategies, particularly in glioma models, where engineered AMT-based systems show improved parenchymal accumulation and therapeutic efficacy. A recent study [194] developed a neutrally charged nanoprobe decorated with γ-glutamyl moieties that are cleaved by γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), an enzyme overexpressed on brain capillaries. Upon cleavage, positively charged primary amines are exposed, activating AMT to cross the BBB effectively while reducing systemic cationic toxicity (Fig. 7). This nanoprobe achieved about 15% BBB penetration in mice, significantly higher than traditional methods, and delivered therapeutic agents that induced strong anti-glioma immune responses and prolonged survival [194].

Carrier-mediated transport (CMT), a physiological mechanism by which small nutrients (e.g., glucose, amino acids) are shuttled across the BBB via transmembrane carrier proteins, offers a selective pathway for NPs delivery to the brain parenchyma (Fig. 4C). This biomimetic approach exploits the BBB’s inherent nutrient transport machinery, combining molecular specificity with nanoscale precision to overcome traditional delivery limitations. Recent studies have exploited key carrier proteins, including glucose transporters (GLUT1), large neutral amino acid transporters (LAT1), and monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1), by functionalizing NPs with their cognate ligands to enable selective uptake and transcytosis, particularly for glioma targeting [181, 195]. Strategic functionalization of NPs with substrates like amino acids or glucose activates these CMT systems [196]. For instance, LAT1-decorated NPs efficiently deliver L-DOPA derivatives across the BBB, a critical advancement for Parkinson’s disease treatment, as demonstrated by enhanced CNS bioavailability in preclinical studies [192, 193]. Beyond small molecules, liposome-based NPs conjugated to CMT ligands have achieved up to 12-fold increases in drug delivery efficiency to gliomas compared to free drugs, underscoring the translational potential of this approach. CMT complements other BBB-penetrating strategies (e.g., receptor- or adsorptive-mediated transcytosis) by offering higher specificity and safety [195, 197]. Recent advances in biomimetic NPs, such as those mimicking endogenous lipids or glucose, further enhance tumor accumulation while minimizing off-target effects, positioning CMT as a cornerstone for next-generation brain tumor therapies [197].

Receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) is another possible way that NPs can use for BBB crossing, with the potential to circumvent all limitations related to AMT and CMT [160]. Nanotechnology-based strategies exploiting RMT are currently under extensive investigation as a promising approach for targeted drug delivery to the brain parenchyma in neurological disorders. In RMT-based delivery, NPs with suitable physicochemical properties are coated with certain ligands (Table 1) to physically attach to cognate receptors on the BBB EC surface [198]. Upon the attachment of ligand-coated NPs with receptors, endocytosis is activated, followed by the transfer of cargo (siRNA and monoclonal antibodies such as bevacizumab, etc.) from the luminal side to an abluminal surface (Fig. 4E). Such strategies increase the transfer of paclitaxel using angiopep-2@NPs via the activation of LRP1-mediated transport in brain tissue bearing glioblastoma [193, 199]. For example, liposomes have the potential to harbor both hydrophilic and lipophilic chemotherapeutics, siRNA, expressing plasmids, and other therapeutics [34, 182]. The encapsulation of these cargoes inside liposomes protects them from direct biodegradation, while simultaneously enabling sustained and controlled release in the tumor microenvironment, like GBM. Recent advances highlight the potential of multifunctional liposomes, such as stepwise-targeting and hypoxia-responsive liposomes (AMVY@NPs), which co-deliver siYAP (targeting the Hippo/YAP pathway) and verteporfin (VP) to glioblastoma (GBM) [200]. Qi et al. showed these liposomes exhibit enhanced BBB penetration via angiopep-2 modification, hypoxia-triggered drug release, and synergistic suppression of GBM growth and stemness in vitro and in vivo [200]. Such strategies align with clinical trials demonstrating the efficacy of liposomal systems in improving drug delivery and therapeutic outcomes by circumventing the BBB in GBM patients. RMT-based nanocarriers demonstrate significant potential to overcome BBB limitations for gene-editing therapies. Zhang et al. [201] developed angiopep-2-decorated polymeric nanoparticles functionalized with guanidinium and fluorine domains to stabilize Cas9/gRNA RNPs and facilitate BBB penetration via LRP1 (low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1) binding. This system achieved 32% gene knockout efficiency of PLK1 in GBM cells, suppressed tumor growth, and extended median survival in orthotopic mouse models by 40 days without off-target effects. As illustrated in Fig. 8, this approach combines targeted delivery with robust gene-editing efficacy, offering a promising strategy for treating glioblastoma.

A2AR agonists

Using pharmacological treatment, the transient disruption of the BBB/BTB can be achieved via the changes in function and levels of endothelial tight junctions or receptor signaling [214]. It has been confirmed that the activation of A2AR increases BBB permeability in CNS tumors. In this regard, Adenosine A2 receptors A2AR agonists such as Regadenoson (Lexiscan®), CVT-3146, NECA, CGS 21,680 can alter the BBB permeability via the remodeling of actin cytoskeleton, reduction of ZO-1, and Claudin-5 expression, inhibition of P-gp function [214]. To increase delivery efficiency, it is also possible that A2AR agonists are used alone or in combination with other platforms such as certain nanoparticles or physical disruption methods, to increase BBB opening time [214]. Data have shown that BBB permeabilization using A2AR agonists for 30 to 50 min can help the CNS delivery of temozolomide in mice [214]. However, finding optimized doses and the long-term side effects of A2AR agonists on BBB function should be carefully monitored [214, 215]. Recent attempts have been directed at finding optimum doses. In a clinical trial study (registered code: NCT03971734), different doses of Regadenoson were used to find the optimum dose for permeabilizing the BBB along with A2AR agonist and high-grade glioma candidates [215]. The simultaneous application of nanotechnology-based modalities can help researchers and clinicians find standard protocols for enhanced drug delivery and less toxic effects on CNS and non-CNS tissues [156, 182]. In an experiment, Lexiscan-loaded nanoparticles can increase the BBB opening up to 2 h when compared to a free A2AR agonist, leading to increased delivery of doxorubicin in glioblastoma models [210, 214]. Compared to the A2AR agonists alone group, the simultaneous application of NPs enhanced selectivity and reduced systemic side effects. NPs not only enhance drug accumulation in tumors but also reduce systemic side effects by improving selectivity for the brain microvasculature. Building on this, a novel autocatalytic nanoparticle system (p28-LBM NPs) was recently developed to address dual challenges in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) treatment for efficient BBB penetration and potent antitumor [216]. These NPs combine p28 peptide for targeted brain tumor delivery, Lexiscan (LEX) to enhance NP penetration via transient BBB disruption, and Bardoxolone methyl (BM) as a self-assembling phytochemical nanomaterial with intrinsic antitumor activity. In vivo studies demonstrated significant tumor growth inhibition and extended survival in GBM-bearing mice, with minimal off-target accumulation in major organs (Fig. 9). LEX not only provided temporal control over BBB opening but also amplifying delivery efficiency by 3.5-fold in murine U87 GBM models [216].

Osmotic substances

Osmotic substrates such as mannitol can alter BBB permeability via simultaneous osmotic shrinkage of BBB ECs [217]. To this end, it is essential to infuse hyperosmolar solutions like mannitol into the systemic circulation to reduce EC intracellular water content and thereby open tight junctions [218]. This strategy is helpful in the passage of large molecules through the BBB. It is believed that BBB opening can be controlled for 15 min to 4 h, related to the therapeutic molecular size [217]. It is indicated that the BBB reclosure time also depends on the therapeutic molecular sizes, in which high-weight molecules introduced into the permeabilized BBB cause relatively fast reclosure [217]. Osmotic alteration is done via intra-arterial injection to heighten the drug concentrations in tumor masses. Likewise, controlled intracerebral administration of drugs can increase the on-target properties and reduce neurotoxicity and systemic side effects. In this regard, approaches such as real-time imaging techniques (MRI and digital subtraction angiography) and guided catheter placement can optimize drug delivery [219]. Despite these advantages, it is thought that this method can lead to transient cerebral edema and increased intracranial pressure in neurological disorders. The permeabilization of the BBB via osmotic pressure allows the simultaneous entry of non-specific substances such as albumin into the CNS, resulting in the exacerbation of neurological symptoms [218, 220].

Bradykinin receptor activators

Bradykinin increases BBB permeability via the activation of transcellular signaling pathways [221]. This substance is a vasoactive peptide with the potential to selectively blunt the BBB integrity juxtaposed to tumor masses [221, 222]. The close physical attachment to endothelial B2 receptors causes the elevation of intracellular calcium and NO, which in turn stimulates vasodilation and BBB permeability [222]. RMP-7, a synthetic analog of bradykinin with higher affinity, life span, and specificity, is administered via a systemic route for the modulation of BBB integrity [223]. In this regard, tumoricidal agents such as alkylglycerols and non-ionic amphiphilic compounds can integrate into EC membranes and last for BBB opening 3–15 min [24]. Despite the efficiency of bradykinin use in BBB disruption, the therapeutic window is relatively narrow in higher doses, showing various side effects [221].

Physical disruption methods

Physical methods to overcome the BBB/BTB involve the application of external energy or mechanical forces to transiently increase barrier permeability. These techniques include focused ultrasound (FUS), convection-enhanced delivery (CED), laser-guided interstitial thermal therapy (LITT), and magnetic field-based methods.

Focused ultrasound (FUS) for transient BBB/BTB disruption

FUS has emerged as a promising non-invasive technique for transiently opening the BBB and BTB, enabling targeted drug delivery to brain tumors [224, 225]. When combined with intravenously administered microbubbles, FUS generates acoustic cavitation, producing mechanical stress on ECs that reversibly disrupts tight junctions and enhances permeability (Fig. 10) [168]. This approach facilitates the entry of therapeutics, including chemotherapeutics (e.g., doxorubicin, temozolomide), nanoparticles, and immunomodulators (e.g., IL-12, PD-1 antibodies), while minimizing systemic toxicity [226]. The efficacy of FUS-mediated BBB opening depends on microbubble dynamics and ultrasound parameters. Microbubbles (~ 2 μm in diameter) exhibit superior oscillation and harmonic emissions compared to smaller counterparts (~ 1 μm), leading to more pronounced BBB permeabilization [227]. The resulting shear stress on ECs not only disrupts tight junctions but also activates transcellular transport mechanisms, further enhancing drug delivery [228]. Two primary FUS modalities are employed: High-intensity FUS (HIFU), which delivers strong ultrasound waves, achieving BBB opening for 4–6 h, albeit with higher risks of hemorrhage or inflammation. While low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPU) uses milder waves, permitting repeated applications with improved safety profiles, though with slightly reduced delivery efficiency [226, 227]. Preclinical studies in glioblastoma models have validated the safety and efficacy of FUS, with clinical trials confirming its feasibility in humans [224, 225]. For example, MRI-guided FUS (MRgFUS) enables real-time monitoring of BBB disruption, ensuring precise spatial control and minimizing off-target effects [168]. Recent applications in glioblastoma and astrocytoma patients demonstrated enhanced delivery of paclitaxel, carboplatin, and liposomal doxorubicin, with synergistic effects observed when combined with NP-based carriers or checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1 antibodies) [227]. Systematic reviews highlight the importance of optimizing FUS parameters (e.g., frequency, pressure, microbubble dosage) for safe and efficient BBB opening [226]. The integration of FUS with advanced drug carriers (e.g., NPs) and immunotherapies represents a transformative strategy for brain tumor therapy, offering improved tumor targeting and reduced systemic exposure [224].

Magnetic Resonance-Mediated BBB disruption

MR-based disruption of the BBB applies magnetic NPs (MNPs) and alternating magnetic fields (AMFs) to stimulate local hyperthermia and blunt the BBB integrity [193, 229]. The exposure of MNPs, such as iron oxide NPs, to AMFs contributes to local heat production [230]. It is thought that mild hyperthermia between 38 and 39 °C can permeabilize the BBB via reversible disruption of tight junctions [193]. Notably, the BBB structure is thermo-sensitive, which in turn helps the transfer of enhanced NPs and drugs into the tumor parenchyma inside the brain. The presence of MNPs increases the imaging contrast, especially in MR imaging [229]. Despite the efficiency of MR-based techniques in the induction of BBB permeabilization, standard protocols are mandatory to reduce side effects for clinical purposes. In this regard, the intensity and frequency of AMF and exposure time are critical issues in the safety of MR-based BBB opening. To be specific, the physicochemical properties such as size, surface coating, and magnetic NP concentration are influencing factors in distribution, internalization into the ECs, and cytotoxicity [229]. It is thought that refining parameters related to MR-based BBB opening can reduce the possible detrimental effects [230].

Magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) is an emerging, non-invasive technique that transiently disrupts the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in targeted brain regions, enabling enhanced delivery of therapeutic agents to glioblastoma (GBM) tumors [231]. MRgFUS uses low-intensity ultrasound waves focused precisely on the tumor site, combined with intravenously injected microbubbles. The ultrasound causes microbubble oscillations that mechanically disrupt tight junctions between endothelial cells, increasing BBB permeability temporarily and reversibly [231]. MRI guides and monitors the ultrasound application in real time with submillimeter accuracy, ensuring spatial precision and safety. Unlike high-intensity ultrasound that causes thermal ablation and CNS damage, low-intensity MRgFUS induces mainly mechanical effects (cavitation, acoustic streaming) with minimal thermal injury, allowing repeated treatments with fewer side effects (Fig. 11). First-in-human clinical trial, Mainprize et al. demonstrated MRgFUS-mediated BBB opening in brain tumor patients, showing a 7.7-fold increase in doxorubicin and temozolomide levels in sonicated tumor regions compared to non-sonicated areas. The procedure was well tolerated, with BBB permeability returning to baseline within 24 h [232]. Preclinical studies in glioblastoma mouse models have shown that MRgFUS can safely deliver large therapeutic nanoparticles such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) carrying siRNA and mRNA, achieving up to a 6.7- to 12-fold increase in delivery efficiency and significant tumor uptake [233]. In recent years, advanced imaging techniques using superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) combined with MRI have improved visualization of glioblastoma vasculature and BBB disruption, aiding treatment planning and assessment [234].

Convection-Enhanced delivery (CED)

CED is the direct administration or infusion of therapeutics such as NPs or drugs into the brain parenchyma via implanted catheters [67]. Using pressure gradients induced by a pump, chemotherapeutics are injected into the ECM, resulting in massive bulk flow [235]. CED provides homogenous distribution of up to 4000-fold chemotherapeutics with defined concentrations using catheters while minimizing direct brain injury in the healthy regions [236]. There are some reports associated with the application of CED in the clinics for GBM candidates with direct infusion into the target sites after tumor resection or directly administered into the tumor parenchyma [237]. For example, radiosensitizer AGuIX® (gadolinium-based NPs) and liposomal irinotecan (CPT-11) have been tested in clinical trials for GBM patients using CED [238, 239]. Despite the existence of several advantages for CED, issues such as invasive entities and neurological interventions limit its application under tumor conditions. Besides, infection and direct tissue damage are inevitable during the application of catheters, and situations become more problematic when long-term administration is mandatory [240].

Exploiting endogenous inflammatory pathways

The application of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines for transient disruption of the BBB has been at the center of attention [241]. For example, administration of TNF-α or IL-1β stimulates BBB opening via receptors. Of note, the use of various receptor antagonists or agonists enables us to regulate their effects [242]. As aforementioned, these cytokines abrogate the tight junction function via the activation of downstream effectors such as NF-κB [154, 241]. In preclinical experiments, systemic administration of TNF-α at low doses led to enhanced doxorubicin BBB bypass in GBM-bearing models [241]. Like TNF-α, IL-6 is a putative cytokine to induce BBB integrity loss (VE-cadherin↓ and ZO-1↓) via the expression of VEGF, and concurrent recruitment of immune cells into the brain parenchyma [243, 244]. More importantly, excessive cytokine use contributes to edematous changes, neuronal toxicity, and CNS pressure with seizure [154]. In this regard, liposome and/or NP-loaded cytokines (TNF-α or IL-1β) with affinity to endothelial receptors (ICAM-1, etc.) can increase on-target properties [245]. It seems that cytokine-based modulation of BBB permeabilization using nanomedicine techniques is a relatively safe approach to delivering the therapeutics into the brain; however, attention should be taken to minimize the concurrent neuronal cell injuries.

Conclusions and perspectives

To control the tumor cell transfer via the BBB, several issues should be considered [226]. Parameters such as inherent BBB heterogeneity in different parts of the brain should not be neglected; meanwhile, certain pathological conditions can induce various patterns in BBB function and activity [7]. Some available strategies can be applied to the control of BBB integrity and function. The control of degrading enzymes, MMPs, can prevent the permeabilization of the BBB. In this regard, selective chemicals can be used against MMPs such as Marimastat with the potential to reduce the expansion of tumor cells into the brain via preserving the basal membrane in several preclinical models [246]. It is also possible that the expression of tight junctions is stimulated using certain compounds such as resveratrol. It was suggested that resveratrol increases the expression of Claudin-5 and occludin [247]. The inhibition of the VEGF signaling pathway using monoclonal antibodies is also possible [7]. However, excessive anti-VEGF antibody doses can predispose to hypoxia and invasion of tumor cells [248]. Activation of ATP-sensitive potassium channel inhibitors can blunt the efflux mechanism [249]. The inhibition of these channels by sulfonylurea increases vasoconstriction via the activation of calcium channels [250]. NPs loaded with certain substances, such as curcumin or other anti-inflammatories, can strengthen BBB integrity via the reduction of permeabilization [251].
