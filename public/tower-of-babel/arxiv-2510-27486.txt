FedAdamW: A Communication-Efficient Optimizer with Convergence and Generalization Guarantees for Federated Large Models
Junkang Liu, Fanhua Shang, Kewen Zhu, Hongying Liu, Yuanyuan Liu, Jin Liu

Abstract
AdamW has become one of the most effective optimizers for training large-scale models. We have also observed its effectiveness in the context of federated learning (FL). However, directly applying AdamW in federated learning settings poses significant challenges: (1) due to data heterogeneity, AdamW often yields high variance in the second-moment estimate 𝒗 \boldsymbol{v} ; (2) the local overfitting of AdamW may cause client drift; and (3) Reinitializing moment estimates ( 𝒗 \boldsymbol{v} , 𝒎 \boldsymbol{m} ) at each round slows down convergence. To address these challenges, we propose the first Fed erated AdamW algorithm, called FedAdamW , for training and fine-tuning various large models. FedAdamW aligns local updates with the global update using both a local correction mechanism and decoupled weight decay to mitigate local overfitting. FedAdamW efficiently aggregates the mean of the second-moment estimates to reduce their variance and reinitialize them. Theoretically, we prove that FedAdamW achieves a linear speedup convergence rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( L ​ Δ ​ σ l 2 ) / ( S ​ K ​ R ​ ϵ 2 ) + ( L ​ Δ ) / R ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{(L\Delta\sigma_{l}^{2})/(SKR\epsilon^{2})}+(L\Delta)/R) without heterogeneity assumption , where S S is the number of participating clients per round, K K is the number of local iterations, and R R is the total number of communication rounds. We also employ PAC-Bayesian generalization analysis to explain the effectiveness of decoupled weight decay in local training. Empirically, we validate the effectiveness of FedAdamW on language and vision Transformer models. Compared to several baselines, FedAdamW significantly reduces communication rounds and improves test accuracy. The code is available in https://github.com/junkangLiu0/FedAdamW.



Introduction

With the rapid growth of data and rising concerns over user privacy, traditional centralized training paradigms have become inadequate.
Federated Learning (FL)

(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
offers a scalable and privacy-preserving framework that enables collaborative model training across decentralized clients without sharing raw data
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025b
;
Liu et al. 2025a
)
. As data becomes increasingly siloed, FL is a practical solution for large-scale distributed deep learning.

However, recent trends in model design-particularly the rise of large-scale architectures such as GPT
(
Radford et al. 2018
)
, RoBERTa
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
, and Vision Transformers (ViT)
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
—pose new challenges for existing FL algorithms. Specifically, the widely-used
FedAvg
algorithm, which relies on stochastic gradient descent (
SGD
)
(
Bottou 2010
)
in local, struggles to efficiently train Transformer models.
This is due to the slow convergence and poor adaptivity of SGD in Transformer models
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Liu et al. 2025a
)
, which have more complex architectures compared to CNNs. For example, components such as query, key, and value often require different learning rates to be trained effectively
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.
In contrast,
AdamW

(
Loshchilov, Hutter et al. 2017
)
, an adaptive optimizer with decoupled weight decay, has demonstrated superior performance in centralized training of large models based on Transformer
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2019
)
, offering faster convergence and improved generalization, compared to
Adam

(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
and SGD.

Empirically, we also observe this advantage in FL: as shown in
Figure

, local training with AdamW (
Local AdamW
) converges significantly faster than
Local SGD

(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
for training various Transformer models .
However, naively applying AdamW in FL leads to the following new challenges:

•

Challenge 1: High variance in second-moment estimate (
\boldsymbol{v}
).
Due to non-i.i.d. data across clients, gradient noise leads to high variance in second-moment estimate.

•

Challenge 2: Local overfitting and client drift.
While AdamW accelerates local training, it intensifies local overfitting. Under non-i.i.d. data,
this manifests as client drift, severely hindering the global model’s performance.

•

Challenge 3: Moment estimate reinitialization.
reinitializing the first- and second-moment estimates from scratch in every round hinders the convergence rate.

Related Work

\bullet
Heterogeneity Issues in Federated Learning.
Data heterogeneity across clients is a fundamental challenge in FL. A range of algorithms have been proposed to mitigate the adverse effects of non-i.i.d. data distributions. For example, FedProx
(
Li et al. 2020
)
introduces a proximal term to restrict local updates; SCAFFOLD
(
Karimireddy et al. 2020
)
applies control variates to correct client drift; and FedCM
(
Xu et al. 2021
)
leverages client momentum to stabilize updates. These methods are predominantly built upon the SGD optimizer, and thus inherit its limitations when optimizing large-scale or deep Transformer-based models.

\bullet
Adaptive Optimization in Centralized Settings.

Adaptive gradient methods have demonstrated superior empirical performance over SGD in centralized settings, particularly for deep neural networks. Pioneering works include Adagrad
(
Duchi, Hazan, and Singer 2011
)
, Adadelta
(
Zeiler 2012
)
, Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
, AMSGrad
(
Reddi, Kale, and Kumar 2019
)
, and AdamW
(
Loshchilov, Hutter et al. 2017
)
. AdamW, in particular, decouples weight decay from gradient updates, offering improved generalization and training stability—attributes especially critical for Transformer models
(
Liu et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.

\bullet
Adaptive Optimization in Federated Learning.
Recent efforts have explored integrating adaptive methods into FL. FedOpt
(
Reddi et al. 2020
)
incorporates server-side adaptivity using Adam and Yogi. FAFED
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
aggregates both the first- and second-moment estimates of Adam across clients to stabilize training. FedAMS
(
Chen, Li, and Li 2020
)
shows that averaging the second-moment estimate of Adam is crucial to prevent divergence. More recently,
Sun et al. 2023
proposed to only aggregate the second-moment estimate to reduce communication overhead. However, these works only conducted experiments on CNN models. These studies are all based on
Adam
, which performs poorly with large weight decay.

\bullet
Our Contribution for Large Models.
We propose
FedAdamW
, the first federated learning algorithm based on
AdamW
tailored for Transformer models. Our method integrates a global-local update alignment strategy and a communication-efficient aggregation of second-moment estimates. Furthermore, we provide theoretical convergence and PAC-Bayesian generalization analysis, offering new insights into adaptive optimization in FL.

Challenges of AdamW in FL

Despite the widespread use of AdamW
(
Loshchilov, Hutter et al. 2017
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
in centralized deep learning, its adaptation to federated settings remains largely unexplored. In this section, we analyze three fundamental challenges that hinder its effectiveness in FL settings.

Challenge 1: High Variance in Second-Moment Estimates (
\boldsymbol{v}
).
AdamW maintains a second-moment estimate (
\boldsymbol{v}
) to scale gradients adaptively, updated as:

\boldsymbol{v}^{r,k}_{i}=\beta_{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{r,k-1}_{i}+(1-\beta_{2})\boldsymbol{g}^{r,k}_{i}\odot\boldsymbol{g}^{r,k}_{i},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{v}^{r,k}_{i}
denotes the second-moment estimate maintained by client
i
at local step
k
of round
r
,
\boldsymbol{g}^{r,k}_{i}
is the stochastic gradient,
\beta_{2}=0.999
is the exponential decay rate for the second moment, and
\odot
represents the element-wise (Hadamard) product in
Algorithm
1
.
In FL, data heterogeneity leads to gradient heterogeneity. The squared stochastic gradients
\boldsymbol{g}^{r,k}_{i}\odot\boldsymbol{g}^{r,k}_{i}
in Eq. (
2
) amplify the variance of
\boldsymbol{v}
across clients in
Figure
2
(a)
. This can cause instability and inefficient aggregation, especially when using non-i.i.d. data
(
Chen, Li, and Li 2020
)
.

Challenge 2: Local Overfitting and Client Drift.
While AdamW accelerates convergence through its adaptivity, it may exacerbate local overfitting. In FL, where each client minimizes its own local objective
f_{i}(\cdot)
, creating a natural gap between the local and global optima. Adaptive optimizers such as AdamW, with stronger update magnitudes, can drive clients further toward their local optima—diverging from the global direction. This leads to client drift as illustrated in
Figure
2
(b)
, which manifests as inconsistencies in local models that degrade the global aggregation performance.

Challenge 3: Reinitialization Overhead.
In FL, AdamW optimizer states are reinitialized from zero each round:

\boldsymbol{m}^{r,0}_{i}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{v}^{r,0}_{i}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{0}.

(3)

Reinitializing moment estimates across rounds erases temporal memory, hindering the accumulation of adaptive statistics and slowing convergence, particularly in deep or large-scale models.

(Q2)

How to overcome overfitting in Local AdamW?

To address local overfitting (i.e.,
Challenge 2
), we adopt a stronger weight decay. Unlike Adam, AdamW employs decoupled weight decay, which improves generalization, particularly in federated settings (see
Table
6
below).
To further mitigate client drift under non-i.i.d. data, we incorporate a global update estimate into the local update rule:

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r,k+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r,k}-\eta\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}^{r,k}_{i}\odot\vartheta_{i}^{r,k}-\lambda\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r,k}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{G}^{r}\right),

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{G}^{r}\!=\!\frac{-1}{SK\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{S}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r,K}\!-\!\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r,0})
is the estimated global update. As shown in
Figure
5

(Q3)

How to initialize second-moment estimates?

We find that initializing the second-moment estimate
\boldsymbol{v}
with its aggregated
mean

\boldsymbol{v}
significantly accelerates convergence (see
Table
7
below). In contrast, we reinitialize the first-moment estimate
\boldsymbol{m}
to zero at each round. This is because
\boldsymbol{m}
adapts quickly to recent gradients and does not require long-term accumulation to remain effective.

Theoretical Analysis

Convergence Analysis

In this part, we give the convergence theoretical analysis of our proposed
FedAdamW
algorithm. Firstly we state some standard assumptions for the non-convex function
f
.

Assumption 1

(Smoothness)
.

(Smoothness) The non-convex
f_{i}
is a
L
-smooth function for all
i\in[m]
, i.e.,
\|\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y})\|\leq L\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|
, for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 2

(Bounded Stochastic Gradient)
.

\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{r}=\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r},\xi_{i}^{r})
computed by using a sampled mini-batch data
\xi_{i}^{r}
in the local client
i
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f_{i}
with bounded variance, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}^{r}}[\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{r}]=\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r})
and
\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}^{r}}\|g_{i}^{r}-\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r})\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{l}^{2}
, for all
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 3

(Bounded Stochastic Gradient II)
.

Each element of stochastic gradient
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{r}
is bounded, i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{r}\|_{\infty}=\|f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r},\xi_{i}^{r})\|_{\infty}\leq G_{g}
, for all
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any sampled mini-batch data
\xi_{i}^{r}
.

Assumption 4

(Bounded Heterogeneity)
.

The dissimilarity between local clients is bounded on the gradients, i.e.,
\|\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{g}^{2}
, for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

These assumptions are standard in federated adaptive optimization literature

Sun et al. 2023
)

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a novel federated optimization algorithm (
FedAdamW
) for training large-scale Transformer models.
FedAdamW
tackles the key challenges of applying AdamW in federated settings, including high variance in second-moment estimates, local overfitting under non-i.i.d. data, and inefficiencies from frequent reinitialization. It integrates second-moment aggregation, global update correction, and decoupled weight decay. We provided convergence analysis under non-convex and use the PAC Bayesian theory to support its generalization benefits. Extensive experiments on vision and language tasks verified that
FedAdamW
consistently outperforms strong FL baselines, especially on Transformer architectures, demonstrating its practical and theoretical strengths. We believe
FedAdamW
opens a new direction for adapting modern optimizers to FL such as LAMB
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
or Lion
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
.

Main Lemmas

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\left\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{\tau}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be random variables that are potentially dependent. If their marginal means and variances satisfy
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]=\mu_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\|X_{i}-\right.

\left.\mu_{i}\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}
, then it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}X_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_{i}\right\|^{2}+\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}.

If they are correlated in the Markov way such that
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\mid X_{i-1},\cdots X_{1}\right]=\mu_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{i}-\mu_{i}\right\|^{2}\mid\right.

\left.\mu_{i}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}
, i.e., the variables
\left\{X_{i}-\mu_{i}\right\}
form a martingale. Then the following tighter bound holds:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}X_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]+2\tau\sigma^{2}.

Lemma 2

Given vectors

\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
uniformly randomly such that
|\mathcal{S}|=S

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}v_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]=\|\bar{v}\|^{2}+\frac{N-S}{S(N-1)}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v_{i}-\bar{v}\right\|^{2}.

Letting

, we prove this lemma by direct calculation as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}v_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\mathbb{I}\{i\in\mathcal{S}\}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i}\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}\mathbb{I}\{i\in\mathcal{S}\}+2\sum_{i<j}v_{i}^{\top}v_{j}\mathbb{I}\{i,j\in\mathcal{S}\}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{SN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{S^{2}}\frac{S(S-1)}{N(N-1)}2\sum_{i<j}v_{i}^{\top}v_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{SN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{S^{2}}\frac{S(S-1)}{N(N-1)}\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\right\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N-S}{S(N-1)}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}+\frac{N(S-1)}{S(N-1)}\|\bar{v}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N-S}{S(N-1)}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|v_{i}-\bar{v}\right\|^{2}+\|\bar{v}\|^{2}.

∎

Combine this inequality with Lemma
3
and we get

\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)-f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\right)\right]\leq-\frac{1}{7}\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]+\frac{39}{56\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{-1}+\frac{78}{7}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{4}}(e\alpha\eta KL)^{2}G_{0}R+\frac{39\alpha\sigma_{l}^{2}}{14SK\epsilon^{2}}R.

Finally, noticing that
g^{0}=0
implies
\mathcal{E}_{-1}\leq 2L\left(f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\right)-f^{*}\right)=2L\Delta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{L\Delta}{\gamma LR}+\frac{\mathcal{E}_{-1}}{\alpha R}+(\alpha\eta KL)^{2}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{4}}G_{0}+\frac{\alpha\sigma_{l}^{2}}{SK\epsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{L\Delta}{R}+\frac{L\Delta}{\alpha R}+\frac{\alpha\sigma_{l}^{2}}{SK\epsilon^{2}}+(\alpha\eta KL)^{2}G_{0}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{L\Delta}{R}+\sqrt{\frac{L\Delta\sigma_{l}^{2}}{SKR\epsilon^{2}}}.

∎

Appendix B
Appendix B: Generalization Analysis

Analysis on hypothesis posterior.
According to the classical PAC-Bayesian theory
(
McAllester 1998
)
, the generalization error bound is closely related to the hypothesis posterior learned by an algorithm. We therefore begin by analyzing the hypothesis posterior induced by
FedAdamW
, followed by its impact on generalization. Following prior work
(
Xie et al. 2021
;
Mandt, Hoffman, and Blei 2016
;
Chaudhari and Soatto 2018
;
Jastrzbski et al. 2017
;
Zhu et al. 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
, we adopt the stochastic differential equation (SDE) view of optimization, as it captures similar convergence behavior to that of the actual algorithm.
We focus on a simplified version of the second-moment estimate used in AdaGrad
(
Duchi, Hazan, and Singer 2011
)
, which is given by:
\mathrm{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{n}_{r+1}\right)
, which is commonly used in practice. The observed fisher information matrix,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)^{\top}
serves as a reliable approximation of the local Hessian matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}

(
Pawitan 2001
;
Jastrzbski et al. 2017
;
Zhu et al. 2018
)
. Based on these insights, we follow previous works and treat
\boldsymbol{n}_{r}
as a proxy for the Hessian matrix in our analysis.

\boldsymbol{n}_{r}\approx\boldsymbol{H}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}.

Accordingly, the updating rule of
FedAdamW
can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{r+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{r}-\eta\frac{1}{SK\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r}

(9)

\displaystyle=\!\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\!-\!\eta\frac{1}{SK\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\!-\eta\Big(\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}^{r,k}_{i}\odot\vartheta_{i}^{r,k}\!-\!w\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{r,k}\!+\!\alpha\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{G}\Big)

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{x}^{r}-\eta\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)-\eta\lambda\boldsymbol{x}^{r}+\eta\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\boldsymbol{u}^{r}.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{r}=\nabla F(\boldsymbol{x}^{r})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla F_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{r})
denote the heterogeneity gradient noise, and define
\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}=\boldsymbol{n}^{-1/2}
for simplicity. In the
FedAdamW
formulation, we omit the small constant
\epsilon
for clarity, as it does not affect subsequent results. Following
(
Mandt, Hoffman, and Blei 2017
;
Smith and Le 2017
;
Chaudhari and Soatto 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2018
)
, we assume the noise
\boldsymbol{u}^{r}
follows a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}})
by the Central Limit Theorem. Under this assumption, the SDE of
FedAdamW
can be expressed as:

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{r}=-\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)\mathrm{d}r-\lambda\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\mathrm{~d}r+\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\left(2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{~d}\xi_{r},

where
\mathrm{d}\xi_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}\mathrm{~d}r)
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}=\frac{\eta}{2}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}
is defined in the above literatures as

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}\!\!=\!\!\frac{1}{S}\!\left[\!\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)\!\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)^{\top}\!\!-\!\!\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)\!\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)^{\top}\!\!\right],

where
N
is the number of all clients, and
S
is participate clients. Since we analyze the local convergence around an optimum, this means
\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)\approx 0
and the variance of the gradient noise would dominate
(
Mandt, Hoffman, and Blei 2017
;
Smith and Le 2017
;
Chaudhari and Soatto 2018
)
. So following these works, we approximate
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}
as

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}\approx\frac{1}{S}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)\nabla f\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)^{\top}\right]\approx\frac{1}{S}\boldsymbol{H}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{r}}.

Based on these approximations, the hypothesis posterior distribution induced by
FedAdamW
is characterized in Lemma
7
.

Lemma 7
.

Assume the objective loss can be approximated by a second-order Taylor approximation, namely,
F(\boldsymbol{x})=F\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}_{*}\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\right)
. Then the solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{r}
of
FedAdamW
obeys a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{*},\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{FedAdamW }}\right)
where the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{FedAdamW }}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]
is defined

\boldsymbol{M}_{AdamW}=\frac{\eta}{2B}\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top},

where
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
is the SVD of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
in which
\boldsymbol{S}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\right)
with singular values
\left\{\sigma_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{d}
.

Lemma
7
shows that
FedAdamW
converges to a solution concentrated around the minimum
\boldsymbol{x}_{*}
, confirming its favorable convergence from the SDE perspective. We further examine the effect of the decoupled weight decay parameter
\lambda
. As
\lambda
increases, the singular values of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\texttt{FedAdamW}}
decrease, indicating improved stability. This suggests that decoupled weight decay not only enhances convergence to
\boldsymbol{x}_{*}
but also leads to better generalization performance.

Building on the posterior analysis, we adopt the PAC-Bayesian framework
(
McAllester 1998
)
to study the generalization performance of
FedAdamW
. Given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and a training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}
with samples
\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{D}
, the learned posterior hypothesis follows a distribution
\mathcal{P}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{*},\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{{FedAdamW}}})
as shown in Lemma
7
. We define the expected risk as
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]
and the empirical risk as
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]
. In practice, the prior is often chosen as a Gaussian
\mathcal{P}_{\text{pre}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\rho\boldsymbol{I})

(
Szegedy et al. 2015
;
He et al. 2016
;
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
, due to the lack of prior knowledge about the posterior. Based on Lemma
7
, we establish the generalization bound in Theorem
4
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume the prior hypothesis
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}_{\text{pre }}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\rho\boldsymbol{I})
. Then the expected risk for the posterior hypothesis
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}
of
FedAdamW
learned on training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{tr}\sim\mathcal{D}
with
n
samples holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{D}_{tr},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]\leq

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{8}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{2\rho b\left(\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\right)}{\eta}\!+\!\frac{\eta}{2\rho b}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda}\!+\!c_{0}\!\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

with at least probability
1-\tau
, where
\tau\in(0,1)
and
c_{0}=\frac{1}{2\rho}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\right\|^{2}-\frac{d}{2}+2\ln\left(\frac{2n}{\tau}\right)
.

Theorem
2
shows that the generalization error of
FedAdamW
can be upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
(up to constant factors), consistent with classical results from PAC theory, stability, and uniform convergence
(
Vapnik 2006
)
.
We further analyze the impact of the decoupled weight decay parameter
\lambda
on this bound. As
\lambda
increases, the first term
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log 2\rho b\left(\sigma_{i}^{1/2}+\lambda\right)\eta^{-1}
increases, while the second term
\frac{\eta}{2\rho b}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{i}^{1/2}+\lambda\right)^{-1}
decreases. Although choosing the optimal
\lambda
is challenging in practice, this trade-off suggests that tuning
\lambda
appropriately can lead to a smaller generalization error.
Since the empirical risk
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]
is typically small for modern over-parameterized models, adjusting
\lambda
helps reduce the expected risk. This explains why
FedAdamW
often outperforms Local Adam (which corresponds to
\lambda=0
). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first theoretical result that explicitly demonstrates how weight decay improves the generalization in FL.

To begin with, we first give one useful lemma to prove our generalization error bound.

Lemma 8

(PAC-Bayesian generalization bound)
.

(McAllester, 1999) For any
\tau\in(0,1)
, the expected risk for the posterior hypothesis of an algorithm over a training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{tr}\sim\mathcal{D}
with
n
samples holds with at least probability
1-\tau
:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{D}_{tr},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\left(KL\left(\mathcal{P}\|\mathcal{P}_{pre}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{2n}{\tau}\right)\right)},

where
KL\left(\mathcal{P}\|\mathcal{P}_{\text{pre }}\right)
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence from prior
\mathcal{P}_{\text{pre }}
to posterior
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof of Lemma
7

Proof.

Based on the assumptions in Lemma
7
and the inequality (9), we can write the SDE equations as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x^{r}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\nabla F\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right)\mathrm{d}r-\lambda\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\mathrm{~d}r+\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\left(2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{~d}\zeta_{r}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\boldsymbol{H}_{*}\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\mathrm{~d}r-\lambda\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\mathrm{~d}r+\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}\left(2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{~d}\zeta_{r}

where
\mathrm{d}\zeta_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}\mathrm{~d}r),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r}\approx\frac{\eta}{2B}\boldsymbol{H}_{*};\boldsymbol{Q}_{r}=\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Then for this Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, we can compute its closed form solution as follows:

\boldsymbol{x}^{r}=\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)r\right)\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{b}}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\zeta_{r^{\prime}}.

Let
\boldsymbol{M}=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\boldsymbol{x}^{r}\right]
. In this way, considering
\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)=\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{\top}
, we follow (Mandt et al., 2017) (see their Appendix b) and can further compute the algebraic relation for the stationary covariance of the multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as follows:

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{b}\int_{-\infty}^{r}\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta}{b}\int_{-\infty}^{r}\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{b}\int_{-\infty}^{r}\frac{\mathrm{~d}}{\mathrm{~d}r^{\prime}}\left(\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\exp\left(-\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\left(r-r^{\prime}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{b}\boldsymbol{I},

where we use the lower limits of the integral vanishes by the positivity of the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}+

\lambda\boldsymbol{I}
. Next, let
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
is the SVD of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
, where
\boldsymbol{S}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\right)
. Then we have

\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}=\frac{\eta}{b}\boldsymbol{I}.

Then multiplying
\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
from left side and also
\boldsymbol{U}
from right side gives

\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{U}+\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)=\frac{\eta}{b}\boldsymbol{I}.

Therefore, we know

\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathrm{FedAdamW}}=\frac{\eta}{2B}\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}.

We complete the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

According to the assumption in Theorem 5 and Lemma 4, we know that for AdamW, its prior and posterior distributions are both Gaussian distribution, namely
\mathcal{P}_{\text{pre }}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho\boldsymbol{I})
and
\mathcal{P}\sim

\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{*},\boldsymbol{M}_{\text{FedAdamW }}\right)

Here,
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
is the SVD of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
in which
\boldsymbol{S}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{d}\right)
.
On the other hand, for KL between two Gaussian distributions
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\sim\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\sim\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\right)
, we can follow (Pardo, 2018) and compute it as follows:

\operatorname{KL}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\|\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\frac{\operatorname{det}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\right)}{\operatorname{det}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\right)}+\operatorname{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\right)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right)-\frac{d}{2}.

Accordingly, for
FedAdamW
, we can compute

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathcal{P}\|\mathcal{P}_{\text{pre }}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\frac{\rho^{d}}{\left(\frac{\eta}{2b}\right)^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda}}+\frac{\eta}{2\rho b}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\right\|^{2}-\frac{d}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{2\rho b\left(\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\right)}{\eta}+\frac{\eta}{2\rho b}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{*}\right\|^{2}-\frac{d}{2}\right].

Then by using Lemma 8, it further yields the generalization bound of
FedAdamW
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{rr}},\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\frac{2\rho b\left(\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda\right)}{\eta}+\frac{\eta}{2\rho b}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda}+c_{0}\right)},

D.1 Motivation and Background

The motivation for using Hessian-based partitioning stems from the empirical observation that the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f(x)
of modern deep neural networks is often approximately block-diagonal, particularly in Transformer-like architectures. Each block corresponds to a structural or functional module (e.g., attention heads, feed-forward layers) which share similar curvature characteristics and learning dynamics.

Instead of transmitting the entire second-moment vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we propose to group parameters into
B
blocks
\{v^{(1)},\ldots,v^{(B)}\}
based on the underlying Hessian block structure, and communicate only the block-wise mean:

\bar{v}^{(b)}=\text{mean}\left(v^{(b)}\right),\quad b=1,\ldots,B

This strategy dramatically reduces communication overhead and improves robustness under data heterogeneity.

From subfigures (a)–(h), we observe that the Hessian matrices of different parameter groups exhibit
a near block-diagonal structure, where each large block contains several smaller dense sub-blocks.
Following
Principle 1
, we assign one distinct learning rate to each smallest dense sub-block
in the Hessian. Specifically:

•

Query / Key:
The Hessian shows a clear block-diagonal structure, with each block corresponding to an attention head. We therefore partition these parameters
by head
(e.g.,
4
heads
\rightarrow

4
blocks).

•

Value:
The structure is less clear than for Query/Key, but hints at approximately as many diagonal blocks as the number of output neurons. By default, we partition
by output neurons
(e.g.,
16

16
blocks), though in some special cases it may be better to treat the entire value as a single block.

•

Attention projection (
attn.proj
) and MLP layers:
The Hessian exhibits a block structure aligned with the output neurons. We thus partition
by output neurons
(e.g.,
16
or
32
blocks depending on the layer).

•

Embedding and Output layers:
The Hessian shows a near block-diagonal structure by token. We partition these parameters
by token
(e.g.,
8
tokens
\rightarrow

8
blocks).

This Hessian-based partitioning ensures that each dense Hessian sub-block is assigned at least one
distinct learning rate, avoiding the overly coarse grouping of the PyTorch default partition.

D.2 Practical Partitioning Strategy

•

Class 1:
query
and
key
.
The Hessian of
query
and
key
have near-block-diagonal structures. The number of blocks equals the number of heads.

•

Class 2:
attn.proj
and MLPs.
The Hessian of
attn.proj
and MLPs have block-diagonal structures. The number of blocks equals the number of output neurons.

•

Class 3:
value
.
For
value
, the structure of the Hessian seems less clear. It appears to have 16 diagonal blocks (matching the number of output neurons), but the pattern is less obvious. This structure is significantly different from
query
and
key
, despite all using four heads. Additionally, the Hessian entries of
value
are about
10^{6}
larger than those of
query
and
key
1
1

1

This might be one source of the heterogeneity of Hessian eigenvalues as reported by
Zhang et al. 2024a
.
One possible reason is that
value
is positioned outside the softmax operator in the self-attention design, unlike
query
and
key
.

•

Class 4:
embed
and
output
.
For these two layers, the Hessian sub-block has a near-block-diagonal structure, and the number of blocks equals the number of tokens.

We adopt a structural partitioning strategy aligned with model architecture. Concretely:

•

Transformer layers:
Group by layer-wise modules. For instance, all weights in the self-attention block (e.g.,
W_{Q}
,
W_{K}
,
W_{V}
), feed-forward networks (
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
), and layer norms are treated as separate blocks.

•

CNNs (e.g., ResNet):
Group by convolutional layers or residual blocks.

•

Vision Transformers (ViT):
Group patches and MLP heads independently.

Each block
v^{(b)}
is a vector corresponding to a functional module. For stability, we use the following heuristic:

B=\min\left(\texttt{max\_blocks},\texttt{floor}(d/\texttt{min\_block\_size})\right)

where
min_block_size
is a hyperparameter (e.g., 512), and
d
is the total parameter count.
