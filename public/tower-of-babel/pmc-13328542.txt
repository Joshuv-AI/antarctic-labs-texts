NS2 of respiratory syncytial virus causes G2/M accumulation in HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells
Authors: Xiaoli Tao, Tatsuki Takahashi, Sodbayasgalan Amarbayasgalan, Yoshiro Sugiura, Kenta Shimizu, Enkhjin Dorjsuren, Yonggang Li, Wataru Kamitani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cell cycle is a fundamental mechanism that regulates eukaryotic cell proliferation. It comprises the G1, S, G2, and M phases and is tightly controlled by cyclin/cyclin-dependent kinase complexes. Many viruses manipulate the host cell cycle to create a favorable environment for replication. In particular, RNA viruses such as Hepatitis C virus and human immunodeficiency virus interfere with cell cycle progression. Human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), an RNA virus that infects the respiratory tract, suppresses the cell cycle. However, previous studies did not clearly distinguish between infected and uninfected cells, consequently limiting the interpretability of their results. Therefore, we utilized recombinant RSV expressing a fluorescent reporter in combination with HeLa-Fucci cells to enable the real-time visualization of cell cycle progression. This system enabled clear distinction between infected and uninfected cells. In this study, RSV infection induced cell cycle arrest at the G2 phase. This arrest was attributed to the viral nonstructural protein NS2. The fully infectious RSV system developed in this study provides novel and physiologically relevant insights into the mechanisms underlying RSV modulation of the host cell cycle to facilitate viral replication and pathogenesis. Introduction

Human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), a common respiratory virus in infants and young children, is prevalent worldwide1–4. RSV is a non-segmented negative-sense single-stranded enveloped RNA virus belonging to the genus Orthopneumovirus of the family Pneumoviridae5. Its RNA genome encodes 11 viral proteins (N, P, M, SH, G, F, M2-1, M2-2, L, NS1, and NS2)6,7. This virus mainly infects lung epithelial cells, and patients present with symptoms of respiratory tract infection, which can induce bronchitis, bronchiolitis, and pneumonia8,9. Moreover, severe cases can lead to respiratory failure and even death. Infants, elderly people, and immunocompromised individuals are at high risk of RSV infection10–12. However, no effective antiviral therapy has been developed for this disease. Thus, prevention is the most effective strategy for managing severe RSV infections. Therefore, RSV inhibitors must be identified to facilitate the development of viable treatments.

Viruses typically rely on various host susceptibility factors within their target cells to replicate their genome and produce new progeny virus13. Several viruses typically regulate the host cell cycle to create a favorable cellular environment for replication14. Many RNA viruses can induce G1, S, or G2 arrest in infected cells to facilitate viral replication. For example, mouse hepatitis virus, influenza A virus A/WSN/33 (H1N1), and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus induce cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase15–19. Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus M and Hepatitis C virus (HCV) NS2 proteins block the progression of the S phase in mammalian cells20–22. Contrastingly, the non-structural protein δ1s of reovirus causes cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase23. In addition, infectious bronchitis virus and transmissible gastroenteritis virus induce S and G2/M phase arrest to promote viral replication24–26. Furthermore, RSV regulates the cell cycle of primary human epithelial cells through TGF-β- and p53-dependent pathways to enhance their replication27. However, further research is required to investigate the effects of RSV infection on the cell cycle of HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells and the significance of RSV replication in cell cycle regulation.

The HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cell line is commonly used for cell cycle visualization. The proteins involved in the cell cycle are labeled with different fluorescent proteins. Different stages of the cell cycle are then visualized through fluorescence color changes, allowing direct observation of dynamic changes in the cell cycle using a fluorescence microscope The HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cell line is based on the Fluorescent Ubiquitination-based Cell Cycle Indicator (FUCCI) system originally established by Dr. Atsushi Miyawaki et al.28. A fragment of Cdt1 (amino acids 1–100) is fused with the fluorescent protein mcherry to monitor the G1 phase, resulting in red fluorescence. To monitor the S/G2/M phases, a fragment of Geminin (amino acids 1–110) is fused with the fluorescent protein mVenus to produce green fluorescence. FUCCI monitors cell cycle progression in real time by leveraging the ubiquitin-proteasome system. It uses two fluorescently tagged proteins, Cdt1 (red) and Geminin (green), which are reciprocally degraded during specific phases of the cell cycle. Consequently, cells emit red fluorescence during G1 and switch to green during S/G2/M, allowing clear visual tracking in living cells.

In this study, we investigated the effects of RSV infection on HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cell growth and cell cycle progression. Our findings elucidate the pathogenesis of RSV and provide potential targets for its treatment and prevention.

Results

Rescue of RSV-eBFP2 virus

Because HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells present green and red fluorescence, recombinant RSV expressing blue fluorescent protein 2 (RSV-eBFP2) was designed to distinguish between infected and uninfected cells. BHK/T7-9 cells seeded in 6-well plates were transfected with pBAC-T7-RSV-eBFP2 and four helper plasmids; pBAC-T7-RSV-ZsGreen was used as a positive control (Fig. 1A). The expression of BFP2 and ZsGreen in the transfected cells was observed at 4–5 d.p.t. The transfected BHK/T7-9 cells were then harvested and their growth kinetics evaluated. Viral titers in VeroE6 cells were determined using the TCID50 assay at 0, 1, 2, and 3 days post-infection (d.p.i.). As shown in Fig. 1C the growth curve of RSV-eBFP2 was similar to that of RSV-ZsGreen. In addition, the BFP2 of RSV-eBFP2 was effectively expressed in transfected BHK/T7-9 cells (Fig. 1B) and infected HEp-2 cells (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the viral titers of RSV-eBFP2 and RSV-ZsGreen both reached 106 TCID50/mL. This indicated that RSV-eBFP2 was successfully rescued.

Infection of RSV-eBFP2 caused an increase in the G2/M phase

HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells seeded in 6-well plates were infected with RSV-eBFP2 at an MOI (Multiplicity of infection) of 0.1 to investigate the effect of RSV on the cell cycle (Fig. 2A). At 2 d.p.i., we determined the number of BFP2-positive HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells infected with RSV-eBFP2 using fluorescence microscopy. The HeLa Fucci system identifies cells in the G2/M phase through the overlap of red and green signals, which appears as a yellow signal as shown in Fig. 2A. In Fig. 2B, we presented only the individual red, green, and blue channels to maintain visual clarity, as the merged yellow signal from overlapping channels can be difficult to discern in microscopy images. Therefore, the cells in the G2/M phase (double-positive for red and green) were further validated using flow cytometric analysis. Images of HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells were captured using red, green, and blue filters (Fig. 2B); a separate yellow filter was not used, as the yellow signal represents a composite of red and green fluorescence. In contrast, Fig. 2C presents the results of a four-quadrant analysis based on these signals.

The samples were harvested and subjected to cell cycle detection using a flow cytometer (FCM, SH800; SONY) at 1, 2, and 3 d.p.i. The number of cells in the G2/M phase significantly increased in the infected group compared to that in the uninfected group. In addition, the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase continued to increase with an increase in viral infection time (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that infection with RSV-eBFP2 caused an obvious increase in the occurrence of the G2/M phase in HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells.

Next, we confirmed G2/M arrest in cells infected with RSV-eBFP2 using a DNA content assay. To avoid spectral overlap during flow cytometry, FxCycle Far Red was used instead of DAPI, as eBFP2 served as the reporter gene. Furthermore, HEp-2 cells were used for this assay to prevent interference with the mCherry reporter protein present in the HeLa FUCCI cells. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the G2/M (4 N) population increased significantly in RSV-eBFP2–infected cells.

Detection of cell cycle arrest

Thymidine for S phase and olomoucine were used to determine the effect of cell cycle arrest on RSV-eBFP2 replication. Thymidine arrests the cell cycle in the S phase by blocking the production of DNA building blocks. Olomoucine arrests the cell cycle at the G1/S or G2/M phases by inhibiting the enzymes (CDKs) that regulate cell cycle progression. HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells seeded in 6-well plates were treated with thymidine and olomoucine for 24 h. Next, the HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cell cycle was analyzed using FCM. As shown in Fig. 3A, the proportion of cells in the S phase significantly increased after treatment with 0.85 mM thymidine. Similarly, the number of G1-phase cells visibly increased following treatment with 50 µM olomoucine. Changes in the cell cycle after drug administration were observed using fluorescence microscopy. Most cells treated with thymidine and olomoucine presented with green and red colors, respectively (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that both drugs had an excellent causative effect on cell cycle arrest.

Assessment of key viral proteins associated with cell cycle arrest

To identify the key viral proteins that cause cell cycle arrest, HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells were transfected with the following plasmids: pCAG-RSV-SH-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-NS1-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-NS2-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-M-FLAG, or pCAG-RSV-M2-2-FLAG. The cell cycle was detected using FCM at 48 h.p.i. As shown in Fig. 4A, the number of G1-phase HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells transfected with pCAG-RSV-NS1-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-NS2-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-M-FLAG, and pCAG-RSV-M2-2-FLAG significantly increased compared to that of cells transfected with empty vectors. Contrastingly, pCAG-RSV-SH-FLAG transfection had no significant effect. Transfection with pCAG-RSV-NS1-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-NS2-FLAG, or pCAG-RSV-M-FLAG significantly decreased the number of cells in the G2/M phase, whereas the changes caused by other plasmid transfections were not significant. We further validated the expression of the target protein in HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells transfected with these plasmids using western blotting (Fig. 4B).

Rescue of gene-deleted viruses

We designed and successfully rescued four gene-deleted viruses to elucidate the mechanism by which viruses cause cell cycle arrest in HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells: RSVΔNS1-eBFP2, RSVΔNS2-eBFP2, RSVΔSH-eBFP2, and RSVΔM2-2-eBFP2 (Fig. 5A). Sequencing verification confirmed the accuracy of all mutant sequences. We amplified RSVΔSH-eBFP2 and RSVΔM2-2-eBFP2 using HEp-2 cells, whereas RSVΔNS1-eBFP2 and rRSVΔNS2-eBFP2 were amplified using VeroE6 cells to obtain high concentrations of the virus.

As shown in Fig. 5B, the infected cells displayed high BFP2 expression, indicating that the virus replicated well in the corresponding cells. Finally, we evaluated the growth kinetics of deletion mutants. When RSVΔNS1-eBFP2, RSVΔNS2-eBFP2, and RSV-eBFP2 were inoculated into VeroE6 cells at an MOI of 0.1, RSVΔNS2-eBFP2 exhibited growth kinetics comparable to those of RSV-eBFP2. Although RSVΔNS1-eBFP2 showed slower growth than RSV-eBFP2, it still grew from Day 0 to Day 2, followed by a decreasing trend similar to that of RSV-eBFP2. The growth curves of RSVΔM2-2 and RSVΔSH-eBFP2 in HEp-2 cells presented almost the same growth trend as RSV-eBFP2 (Fig. 5C), although the growth of RSVΔM2-2 was slow.

Compared with uninfected cells, HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells infected with RSV-eBFP2, RSVΔSH-eBFP2, RSVΔM2-2-eBFP2, and RSVΔNS1-eBFP2 showed a similar trend in cell cycle changes, with a significant increase in the number of G2/M phase cells (Fig. 6G–L). Although the cell cycle distribution in Fig. 6H differed from that in Fig. 2C, a significant increase in the G2/M phase was observed in both figures following infection with RSV-eBFP2. However, no significant changes were observed in the number of G2/M phase HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells infected with RSVΔNS2-eBFP2 (Fig. 6J).

Next, we examined whether differences in infectivity influenced the cell cycle distribution between RSV-eBFP2 and RSVΔNS2-eBFP2. We calculated the ratio of uninfected to infected cells for the Mock, RSV-eBFP2, and RSVΔNS2-eBFP2 groups based on the data in Fig. 6G and H, and 6J. As shown in Fig. 6M, the infectivity levels were comparable between the two viruses. Therefore, differences in cell cycle distribution were not attributable to variations in viral infection rates.

This indicated that the loss of NS2 expression limits cell cycle arrest in the G2 phase, suggesting that NS2 is a key viral protein that causes G2 phase arrest in RSV-infected HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells.

Discussion

In this study, RSV infection caused G2 phase arrest during cell cycle development in HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells. Furthermore, infection with NS2-knockout viruses demonstrated the key role of NS2 in G2/M phase arrest.

Viruses employ diverse mechanisms to manipulate the host cell cycle, creating an environment optimized for their replication and survival. One primary strategy involves direct targeting of CDKs and cyclins. For instance, human cytomegalovirus induces G2/M phase arrest by degrading cyclins A and B29, while the HIV-1 Vpr protein triggers a similar G2/M arrest by inhibiting the CDK p34^cdc2-cyclin B complex and activating ATR-mediated DNA damage responses30. Additionally, viruses can leverage checkpoint pathways, such as the HCV core protein inducing p21 expression to cause G1 arrest in hepatocytes31. Conversely, some viral proteins drive cells toward proliferation: the EBV LMP1 protein induces cyclin D2 to promote the G1/S transition32, and the adenovirus E1A protein disrupts retinoblastoma protein (Rb) control to push quiescent cells into the S phase33. A notable example of this dysregulation occurs in human papillomavirus (HPV), where the E6 and E7 oncoproteins interfere with the tumor suppressors p53 and Rb, respectively34. By degrading p53 and releasing E2F transcription factors from Rb, these proteins prevent apoptosis and drive cells into the S phase, facilitating uncontrolled proliferation34. Furthermore, some viruses target structural components such as centrosomes—as seen with the HTLV-1 Tax protein inducing mitotic abnormalities35—or trigger cellular senescence, a state of permanent arrest, through proteins such as KSHV vFLIP. However, the mechanisms by which RSV infection causes G2/M phase arrest remain unclear.

The present study shows that the NS2-deleted RSV virus did not induce the G2/M phase. This suggests that the NS2 protein may be the key viral protein responsible for inducing G2/M arrest. The induction of G2/M phase arrest by NS2 may be functionally integrated with its established role in immune evasion. RSV NS2 is well known to antagonize host antiviral responses by promoting the proteasomal degradation of STAT1, thereby inhibiting interferon singling36,37. STAT1 is not only a key immune mediator but also a regulator of the cell cycle, as it inhibits cell proliferation and promotes pro-apoptotic pathways in response to cellular stress38,39. It is reasonable to suppose that, by degrading STAT1, NS2 interferes with the host’s ability to maintain normal cell cycle checkpoints or undergo apoptosis, which are typically triggered by viral infection. NS2 serves as a master regulator that simultaneously neutralizes host immune defenses and orchestrates a favorable cellular landscape for the RSV life cycle.

Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 6G–L, a shift in cell cycle distribution was also observed in uninfected cells within the same well. This finding suggests a paracrine effect, likely mediated by the secretion of type I interferons and other proinflammatory cytokines from neighboring infected cells. Thus, the observed cell cycle changes in uninfected cells represent a coordinated host cellular response to infection signals.

Previous studies using plasmid expression systems have reported that the RSV M protein induces G1 arrest via a p53-dependent pathway in A549 cells and in human bronchial epithelial cells27. In our experiments, the effect of the M protein on G1 arrest was smaller than previously reported. However, our experiments using the HeLa/Fucci(CA)₂ system confirmed that the M protein expressed from the plasmid induces G1 arrest.

Furthermore, although NS2 is essential for G2/M arrest during RSV infection, the NS2 plasmid alone was unable to replicate this effect in HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells. This finding suggests that NS2-mediated modulation of the cell cycle is not an autonomous process but rather a context-dependent event. During authentic infection, NS2 likely functions in concert with other viral components or cellular stress responses triggered by viral replication. The absence of these synergistic factors in a plasmid-based system may explain why NS2 alone is insufficient to induce arrest. Consequently, RSV appears to orchestrate the hijacking of the host cell cycle to support its own replication by strategically coordinating multiple factors.

Although advantageous to the virus, this mechanism contributes to the pathology of RSV infections and represents a promising target for therapeutic intervention. However, further research is required to elucidate the precise molecular events underlying this arrest and to develop targeted treatments that can disrupt viral replication without harming host cells.

Methods

Cells, viruses, and plasmids

BHK/T7-9 cells (hamster kidney cell clones stably expressing T7RNAP; RCB4942; RIKEN BRC Cell Bank, Ibaraki, Japan)40 were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 5% inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biosera, Cholet, France) and 10% tryptase phosphate broth (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). HEp-2 (human epithelial carcinoma contaminated with HeLa cells) and VeroE6 (African green monkey kidney) cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS. Furthermore, HeLa/Fucci (CA)2 cells were obtained from the RIKEN BRC Cell Bank (RCB4676) and maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS.

RSV A2 strain, isolated from the lower respiratory tract of an infant in Australia in 196116 was used for this study. RSV RSV-NLuc and RSV-ZsGreen were rescued using a reverse genetics system41. The titers of all viruses were measured using VeroE6 cells as previously described42.

pBAC-T7-RSV-ZsGreen, a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) DNA clone, was used as the original genome for the generation of pBAC-T7-RSV-eBFP2, pBAC-T7-RSVΔNS1-eBFP2, pBAC-T7-RSVΔNS2-eBFP2, pBAC-T7-RSVΔSH-eBFP2, and pBAC-T7-RSVΔM2-2-eBFP2 vectors. Each gene was inserted in the opposite direction to abolish the expression of the respective proteins. Plasmids were constructed as previously described41. Each gene was fused with a C-terminal FLAG tag and cloned into the CAG vector to generate the RSV-M, SH-, NS2-, NS1-, and M2-2-protein–expressing plasmids (pCAG-RSV-M-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-SH-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-NS2-FLAG, pCAG-RSV-NS1-FLAG, and pCAG-RSV-M2-2-FLAG, respectively). The previously constructed plasmids pCAG-RSV-N, pCAG-RSV-P, pCAG-RSV-M2-1, and pCAG-RSV-L were used as helper plasmids for the reverse genetics system41. All constructed plasmids were sequenced to verify their sequence accuracy.

Recovery of recombinant viruses and four gene-deleted viruses

BHK/T7-9 cells seeded in 6-well plates (VIOLAMO) were transfected with pBAC-T7-RSV-eBFP2 (3600 ng/well) and four helper plasmids—pCAG-RSV‐N (1200 ng/well), pCAG‐RSV‐P (400 ng/well), pCAG‐RSV‐M2‐1 (400 ng/well), and pCAG‐RSV‐L (1200 ng/well)—using X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, Switzerland); pBAC-T7-RSV-ZsGreen was used as a positive control. The culture medium was discarded after 3–4 days of cultivation, and 500 µl/well of 25% sucrose was added to the 6-well plate. Next, the cell pellets were frozen, thawed twice, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min to collect the supernatant as the P0 viruses of RSV-eBFP2. P0 viruses were then passaged twice in HEp-2 cells, and the resultant P2 viruses were used for subsequent experiments.

Gene-deleted viruses (RSVΔNS1-eBFP2, RSVΔNS2-eBFP2, RSVΔSH-eBFP2, and RSVΔM2-2-eBFP2), were rescued using the same method as described above, with slight modification. VeroE6 cells rather than HEp-2 cells were adopted for passaging of RSVΔNS1-eBFP2 and RSVΔNS2-eBFP2 viruses to facilitate viral replication. To produce high concentrations of these viruses, the viruses were precipitated to a final concentration of 10% using polyethylene glycol 6000 (Nacalai Tesque) through incubation for 2 h at 4 °C. Next, the pellets were collected via centrifugation at 4,300 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in 25% sucrose (diluted in phosphate-buffered saline).

Detection of BFP protein expression

HEp-2 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and infected with RSV-eBFP2 at an MOI of 0.1 for 2 h. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% PS. Infected HEp-2 cells were incubated at 37 ℃ for 48 h. The expression of eBFP2 in infected HEp-2 cells was then observed using an EVOS M7000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with the DAPI 2.0 light cube (excitation, 357/44 nm; emission, 447/60 nm).

Cell cycle imaging using FUCCI reporters

HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells were infected with RSV-eBFP2 at an MOI of 0.1 and incubated at 37 ℃ for 48 h. Cell cycle reporter signals and viral infection were then observed using an EVOS M7000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). eBFP2 fluorescence was detected using the DAPI 2.0 light cube. The FUCCI reporters were detected as follows: mVenus using the GFP 2.0 light cube (excitation, 482/25 nm; emission, 524/24 nm) and mCherry using the Texas Red 2.0 light cube (excitation, 585/29 nm; emission, 628/32 nm).

Growth kinetics of viruses

HEp-2 cells for RSV-eBFP2, RSVΔSH-eBFP2, and RSVΔM2-2-eBFP2, as well as VeroE6 cells for RSVΔNS1-eBFP2 and RSVΔNS2-eBFP2, were seeded in 24-well plates. The following day, cells were infected with the virus at an MOI of 0.1. The medium was replaced with fresh DMEM, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C at 2 h.p.i. Cells were then frozen and thawed twice with 25% sucrose at 0, 1, 2, and 3 d.p.i. The titration of these samples was performed using TCID50 on VeroE6 and HEp-2 cells. TCID50 was calculated as reported by Reed and Muench42.

Flow cytometric detection of the cell cycle

HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells seeded in 6-well plates were infected with RSV-eBFP2 at an MOI of 0.1. Infected cells were cultured at 37 °C for 1–3 days, and cell pellets were collected using 0.25% trypsin. After centrifugation (1,000 rpm for 5 min), the cell pellets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Nacalai Tesque) for 15 min at room temperature. The cell pellets were subsequently washed twice with PBS to remove residual PFA. Finally, the cell cycle of these samples was analyzed using FCM (SH800; SONY). eBFP2 was excited with a 405 nm laser and detected using a 450/50 band-pass filter (FL1). mVenus was excited with a 488 nm laser and detected using a 525/50 band-pass filter (FL2). mCherry was excited with a 561 nm laser and detected using a 600/60 band-pass filter (FL3). The gating strategy for flow cytometric analysis is shown in Figure S1.

Cell cycle arrest using reagents

HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and treated with 0.85 mM thymidine and 50 µM olomoucine to evaluate the effect of cell cycle arrest on RSV replication. The cells were then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, infected with RSV RSV-NLuc at an MOI of 0.1, and cultured at 37 °C. NanoLuc luciferase activity was detected using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) with GloMax (Promega) at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h.p.i.

Western blotting

HeLa/Fucci(CA)2 cells cultured in 6-well plates were separately transfected with plasmids expressing RSV proteins using Lipofectamine® 2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After inoculation for 48 h at 37 °C, transfected cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Nacalai Tesque) on ice for 10 min. Next, the lysed cells were centrifuged at 16,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and 2× sample buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 0.004% bromophenol blue, and 10% 2‐mercaptoethanol) was added. After denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, the samples were loaded into SDS-PAGE using ePAGEL HR (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan), and the electrophoresed proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using Q Blot kit M (ATTO). Anti-Flag (012-22384; Fujifilm) and anti‐β‐actin (A5441; Sigma‐Aldrich) antibodies were used as primary antibodies. Goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (SAB3701066; Sigma-Aldrich) and anti-mouse IgG–HRP (SAB3700878; Sigma-Aldrich) were used as secondary antibodies. Finally, the ChemiLumi One Ultra (Nacalai Tesque) and LuminoGraph (ATTO) were used for visualization.

DNA content assay

HEp-2 cells were seeded in six-well plates and inoculated with RSV-eBFP2 at an MOI of 0.1. After incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin and collected by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 5 min. The cell pellets were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Subsequently, FxCycle™ Far Red stain (Invitrogen) with RNase A (100 µg/mL) was added. Cell cycle profiles were analyzed by flow cytometry (SH800). eBFP2 was excited with a 405 nm laser and detected using a 450/50 band-pass filter (FL1), whereas far-red fluorescence was excited with a 638 nm laser and detected using a 665/30 band-pass filter (FL4).

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used to determine statistical significance on GraphPad Prism Ver.10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All assays were performed in triplicate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
