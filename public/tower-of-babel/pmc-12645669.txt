Smoking and vaping in prevocational schools: qualitative insights to support the development of effective prevention strategies
Authors: Anne Zijp, Eline Meijer, Lisanne M. Wezenaar, Anke Versluis, Jessica C. Kiefte-de Jong, Jiska J. Aardoom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEducational disparities in smoking emerge early, with adolescent smoking strongly linked to educational level. This study aims to explore the current situation, and students’ and teachers’ attitudes towards smoking and vaping, prevention, and experiences and needs for effective smoking prevention programs in prevocational schools.MethodsSemi-structured focus groups with 40 (non)smoking and vaping students and individual interviews with six teachers were conducted at two Dutch prevocational schools in 2024, one with and one without prior experience in a smoking and/or vaping prevention program. Interactive tasks (e.g., mind maps, opinion statements) were used in the focus groups. Qualitative data were analysed using the Framework Approach.ResultsStudents perceived e-cigarettes as healthier than traditional cigarettes, viewed (e-)cigarette use as a personal choice and denied addiction despite regular use. E-cigarettes were widely available in and around both schools through students, while traditional cigarettes were limited to outdoor areas. The availability of e-cigarettes inside schools seemed to increase their appeal. Students purchased e-cigarettes via social media and dealers, bypassing age and flavour bans. Students preferred non-judgmental, open discussions in prevention programs, without parental involvement. Students with parents who use cigarettes found parental correction ineffective, while others made no comments on this. Teachers reported defensive reactions of students to traditional smoking education (e.g., fear appeals) and expressed a need for information on e-cigarettes and an unified prevention approach in schools.ConclusionBoth students and teachers report that cigarette use around school and e-cigarette use within the school are common. Offering information on e-cigarettes, fostering open dialogues with students, and implementing a unified school-wide approach emerged as key strategies for prevention programs.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25302-7.

Introduction

Educational disparities in smoking behaviour emerge early in life. Previous research has shown that educational level is strongly associated with adolescent smoking behaviour [1, 2]. Recent data reveal that this disparity in smoking behaviour across educational levels persisted among Dutch high school students as of 2023. For instance, 21.0% of students in the lowest secondary education level (in Dutch: voortgezet middelbaar beroepsonderwijs basis [vmbo-b]) reported ever smoking, and 4.3% reported daily smoking, compared to 10.5% and 1.0% respectively in the highest education level (in Dutch: voortgezet wetenschappelijk onderwijs [VWO]) [3]. In the Netherlands, secondary education typically serves students aged 12–16 at different levels: vmbo prepares them for vocational training, while VWO is an academically oriented track that prepares for research university entrance. Vmbo schools, also known as prevocational schools, are the focus of this study. As early initiation of smoking predicts continued tobacco use in adulthood, the observed educational disparities in tobacco use are likely to contribute to socioeconomic differences in smoking later in life [4].

Beside conventional cigarette use, data suggest a rapid increase in the prevalence of e-cigarette use among adolescents, with over 40% of youths in high-income countries reporting ever having tried e-cigarettes [5]. In the Netherlands, as of 2023, 24.6% of adolescents aged 12–16 reported ever using e-cigarettes [6]. 31.2% of students in the lowest secondary educational level reported ever using an e-cigarette, and 7.6% reported daily use, compared to 15.9% and 1.2% respectively in the highest educational level [6]. The emergence of e-cigarettes has rendered (e-)cigarette use more appealing to young individuals, partly due to enticing flavours, peer influence, portrayal of vaping on social media platforms, and marketing efforts by the industry [7]. Consequently, many perceive it as advantageous that e-cigarettes contain fewer harmful substances compared to conventional cigarettes, albeit with a limited understanding of their long-term effects [8–10]. This is not without risk, as e-cigarettes may contain substantial doses of nicotine, reaching up to 50 mg, equivalent to a pack of cigarettes [11]. The highly addictive nicotine can impede healthy brain development and cause addiction [12], with over half of adolescent e-cigarette users experiencing symptoms of nicotine dependence [13].

Regardless of smoking bans, e-cigarette use is prevalent within school premises due to the discreet nature of e-cigarettes, which are easy to use and store without detection due to their size and lack of noticeable odor compared to traditional cigarettes [14]. A school environment where e-cigarette use is common may encourage individuals who have never used e-cigarettes to start using them [15]. Indeed, common use of e-cigarette at school is associated with intention to use e-cigarettes in students who have never used e-cigarettes. In addition, the popularity of e-cigarettes in schools may normalize tobacco use in general, hence increasing the use of conventional tobacco products alongside the fact that e-cigarette use in itself may lead to cigarette smoking [16–18]. Given these risks, combined the substantial amount of time adolescents spend in school environments, school settings serve as critical arenas for both preventing young people from start using (e-)cigarettes and assisting those who want to quit.

Despite the extensive development of school-based programs targeting conventional cigarette smoking prevention, they often do not reach the anticipated level of success [19]. Moreover, these interventions tend to be less effective among students with a lower socioeconomic position (SEP), exacerbating existing socioeconomic inequalities [20]. Inequalities may also exist in the implementation of school-based prevention programs. An implementation study of a smoking prevention program in primary schools, for instance, found that the intervention was implemented to a lesser extent among children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds [21]. Furthermore, few prevention programs have yet been designed and implemented for e-cigarettes. Exploring the current situation in secondary prevocational schools and students’ needs regarding a (e-)cigarette use prevention program is an essential step in developing an appropriate and effective smoking prevention program in such schools. At the same time, examining teachers’ perspectives of the current situation, and their opinions and needs for a future prevention program, will add valuable insights into what is needed for an effective and well-implementable intervention. This study aims to explore the current situation regarding (e-)cigarette use and prevention in Dutch prevocational secondary schools by gaining insight into students’ and teachers’ attitudes, experiences, and needs.

Methods

Design and participants

A qualitative approach, using semi-structured interviews and focus groups, was chosen for its suitability in allowing in-depth exploration examining different facets of the subject in a nuanced manner. Data collection took place in December 2023 until April 2024. Data collection and analysis were primarily done by two researchers (LW and AZ).

Two groups of participants participated in the study; teachers and their respective students in their mentoring class. Both teachers and students were eligible for inclusion in the study if they were teaching or respectively attending prevocational education at the time of data collection, and were able to speak, read, and understand the Dutch language. There were no exclusion or inclusion criteria based on the use of (e-)cigarettes.

Participants were recruited through self-selection with all individuals expressing interest being given the opportunity to participate, regardless of their experience with (e-)cigarettes. For the recruitment of participants, ten prevocational schools throughout the Netherlands were approached by e-mail. Two schools expressed interest in participating in the study and were sent information letters for the teachers. When teachers were included, an individual interview was scheduled. They were also asked to invite their mentor students to participate in the research. For this purpose, they provided the students with an information letter and a brief videographic invitation. Upon agreement to participate, students younger than 16 years of age needed a written parent or guardian consent. Teachers were subsequently instructed to form groups with six to ten students per group, considering any relevant factors such as bullying or complicated relationships that could affect the atmosphere and confidentiality of the focus groups). We aimed to conduct three to six focus groups with students until data saturation was reached, as research has shown that this is sufficient to discover 90% of the themes [22]. Since secondary vocational education consists of four different grades, we attempted to hold at least one focus group in each grade. Additionally, we strived to interview as many teachers as there were focus groups with students. In the Netherlands, secondary school teachers teach multiple year groups, so in addition to their mentor class, they teach specific subjects to students from various grades.

Focus groups with students

Semi-structured focus groups with students were hosted at a pre-arranged place at school and lasted approximately two hours. Focus groups were always conducted in a private room to ensure the privacy of the group discussion. Focus groups were chosen instead of individual interviews to create a safe peer environment for adolescents, as they may feel safer with familiar peer group members [23]. Therefore, each focus group consisted of students from the same class. The semi-structured focus group guide can be found in Appendix 1.

Each focus group started with a short introduction followed by a brief introduction round. Students were offered something to eat and drink at this point to make them feel more comfortable. Subsequently, questions concerning their general view of traditional cigarettes and e-cigarettes were discussed (e.g., What comes to mind when you think of cigarettes/e-cigarettes?). Besides (e-)cigarettes, other nicotine-containing products were explored. If it turned out that this was a significant factor, it was also covered in the rest of the interview.

The group was then asked to split into two smaller groups and create and present a mind map concerning their associations with either ‘smoking at school’, ‘vaping at school’, or ‘other nicotine-containing substances at school’. Which group worked on which topic was decided in consultation with the students, based on which substance was most commonly used or observed at school. This method was used to foster engagement and help students organize and express their ideas visually. While creating the mind map, accompanying questions were asked by the interviewer (e.g., ‘In which places at school do you use (e-)cigarettes or see people use (e-)cigarettes?‘). To motivate students to create and explain the mind map as thoroughly and clearly as possible, a small incentive was provided for the group that made the most comprehensive mind map (e.g., a small gadget).

After a short break, students were presented with several statements to discuss their opinions on cigarettes, e-cigarettes, and other nicotine-containing substances, and to assess the need for support from the school and how this support could be structured. Depending on the composition of the group regarding (e-)cigarette use, as discussed earlier in the sessions, the interviewer selected statements tailored for (e-)cigarette users or non-users. The statements for (e-)cigarette users pertained more to the cessation of smoking, vaping, and/or other nicotine-containing products, while for non-users, statements focused more on the initiation of these behaviours. After the focus group was conducted each participant received a gift card of 10 euro.

Interviews with teachers

Semi-structured interviews with teachers took place at the respective school site (n = 2) or online (n = 4) and lasted for 30 to 45 min. The interview started with a short introduction and general questions about their opinions and views on (e-)cigarette use, as well as their (e-)cigarette use. Then, the situation regarding the use of cigarettes, e-cigarettes and possibly other nicotine-containing substances at school by both teachers and students was discussed. In addition, it was discussed whether other nicotine-containing substances are used at school and to what extent. Also, measures against smoking and vaping at school in the past or present, as well as perceived possibilities for a smoking and vaping prevention program, were explored. Interviews were recorded with the permission of the teachers. After the interview was conducted, the teachers received a gift card with a value of 25 euros. The semi-structured interviewguide can be found in Appendix 1.

Data analysis

The interviews and focus groups were manually transcribed verbatim, after which the audio recordings were deleted. Qualitative data were analysed according to the principles of the Framework Approach and cross-case analysis [24]. After familiarization with the transcripts, important passages of the transcripts were coded using a combination of inductive and deductive coding, with coding trees developed during the process. Codes were primarily developed based on pre-defined focus group and interview topics outlined above but the code tree was expanded to other codes as they emerged during the analysis. Within the expanded topics, codes were divided into subcategories. To enhance the reliability of the analysis, one interview and two focus groups were independently coded. Subsequently, the coded transcripts, labels, and code trees were discussed, and discrepancies were resolved. Upon completing the coding of the last transcripts and finalizing the analytical framework, LW and AZ relevant quotes were selected and translated. The quotes were shortened for brevity and clarity.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team consisted of six female Dutch-speaking researchers, residing in the Netherlands, with varying levels of expertise in public health, psychology, health behaviour and qualitative research. Several members have a strong research focus on smoking behaviour and health promotion among vulnerable population groups. Others specialise in e-health, mental health and behaviour change in primary care. In line with these backgrounds, the team members consider prevention of smoking and vaping, particularly in teenagers, essential. The team consisted of both junior and senior researchers, which contributed to a mix of perspectives during the design of the research, data collection and analysis. All focus groups and interviews were conducted by the youngest team members, whose proximity in age and professional experience to the participating students contributed to a more informal and open atmosphere. The characteristics of the research team, particularly the differences with the participants (e.g. age), may have influenced both the participants’ responses and the interpretation of the data. To overcome this, the team held regular reflective discussions about assumptions, roles and possible influences on data collection and analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of Good Research Practice from the Leiden University Medical Center and the Dutch General Data Protection Regulation. The Medical Ethical Review Board of Leiden University Medical Center mandated the review of research not falling under the Dutch Medical Research with Human Subjects Law (nWMO) to individual nWMO committees at Leiden University Medical Center. THe nWMO committee reviewed the proposal and provided a declaration of no objection (WSC-2023-40/PvN). This study has been further approved by the scientific committee of the department of Public Health and Primary Care at Leiden University Medical Center. Informed consent was obtained from both teachers and students prior to participation; for students under the age of 16, additional parental consent was required. Recordings of interviews and focus groups were processed confidentially and digitally stored on a secured server at the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC). Only the main researchers had access to this server.

Perceptions of e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes

In general, students tend to have more positive perceptions of vaping compared to smoking. Students’ perceptions of smoking were both positive and negative. Positive perceptions of smoking included notions of curiosity and expected relief from stress and problems. Regarding the negative perceptions, almost all participants highlighted the addictive nature of smoking. Other negative perceptions associated with smoking were nearly unanimous among participants, notably the pervasive odor of cigarettes and the associated health risks. The health risks of smoking were described as lung damage and lung cancer, as well as dry throat, coughing, and the development of poor skin and yellow teeth. Notably, the term “black lungs”, which refers to the darkening of the lungs caused by smoking, was frequently used as a risk associated with cigarette use. Additionally, students frequently indicated that they perceived smoking as a personal choice. Additionally, it was emphasized that parents who used (e-)cigarettes themselves had less authority in prohibiting (e-)cigarette use among their children compared to non-using parents.

In contrast, students’ perceptions of vaping were more positive than their conventional smoking perceptions. Negative perceptions included the belief that vaping is addictive, detrimental to one’s physical endurance, unwise or unconventional, and prohibited by their parents. Nonetheless, positive perceptions prevailed with vaping. Students emphasize that vaping smells better, is cheaper, lasts longer, is more accessible, is easier to use, and can also be used discreetly compared to conventional cigarettes. One of the students said: ‘You can hide it. For example in your purse or your jeans pocket. And then if you say “I’m going to the toilet”, you can just go vape there. ’ – focus group 5. Additionally, vaping was perceived as healthier than smoking, with many stating that it does not result in “black lungs”. Remarkably, students demonstrated a profound knowledge of vape characteristics. More specifically, in each focus group, numerous colours, flavours, brands, and puff counts of e-cigarettes were spontaneously mentioned. Several female students suggested that the bright colours increased their appeal, commenting ‘Well if it’s coloured, that does make it more attractive than if it’s white or boring’- focus group 1. However, most male students stated that appearance did not affect their usage, explaining that is ‘more about the flavour’.

Regarding the image of ‘individuals who use (e-)cigarettes’, students identified peers starting from the age of eleven as possible (e-)cigarette users. Students added that first-grade students were more likely to be shy or withdrawn and, in their opinion, therefore less likely to start using (e-)cigarettes. “It was stated several times that the majority of students began using (e-)cigarettes by third grade, which corresponds to the age of 14–15 years in the Netherlands. Three fourth-grade students also recognized this to be their own experience, stating that: ‘Yes, [we mostly started] in the third grade. In the first grade you are still insecure, you know. You are still more quiet. And then from the second grade onwards, you go outside the school grounds a lot more. – focus group 3’ This experience was echoed by one of the teachers, who reported that first-graders were more prone to rule-following, while third graders were less likely to care about school policy.

Teachers’ perceptions of smoking are generally negative. Many teachers express predominantly negative attitudes towards smoking and deem it ‘unnecessary for enjoying a fulfilling youth’. However, two teachers mentioned that smoking is a part of adolescence and experimenting. Similar to students, teachers also mention health risks of smoking such as poor dental health, skin issues, and addiction associated with smoking. Additionally, the term ‘unhealthy’ is often mentioned in a general sense, with only a few teachers referencing serious chronic diseases or cancer. Despite acknowledging that vaping is unhealthy and addictive, some teachers perceived vaping as less harmful than smoking conventional cigarettes. Conversely, some teachers mention that they view vaping the same as smoking, though they find the smell of e-cigarettes less unpleasant than conventional cigarettes. In addition, many teachers mention the gateway effect of vaping to conventional smoking. Notably, nearly all teachers admit being largely uninformed about vaping and lacking knowledge about both the health risks of vaping and the prevalence of vaping among students in school. One teacher mentioned: ‘I think that in terms of risks, it isn’t less than smoking […] but I don’t know if that’s really the case’ - teacher 5.

Students’ cigarette and e-cigarette usage and social environment

Numerous students reported to have ever used a cigarette and/or e-cigarette. Some students additionally reported regular vaping, but none reported regular smoking. The students indicate that many peers of their age also use e-cigarettes, generally more than they use conventional cigarettes. The primary reason cited by students for vaping or smoking is generally to alleviate stress in challenging home or school environments, with some commenting that ‘I think that if you start [vaping or smoking], that something happens at home or something happened, that makes you start’ – focus group 3. The sentiment of underlying problems serving as motivation to initiate vaping was also noted by a teacher, who mentioned that ‘A while ago three students with some mental issues were suspended because of vaping […] So this seems to have become some sort of trend. Maybe some sort of coping mechanism, or a call for attention’ – teacher 3. Habitual e-cigarette users also expressed that vaping calmed them down and that they enjoyed the sensation of the nicotine kick. Additionally, the social aspect of vaping and group pressure were frequently mentioned, with a slight majority of students expressing fear of exclusion if they did not use e-cigarettes. However, this was contradicted by other students, who reported not feeling pressured by their peers who use e-cigarettes, with one student stating ‘It is not that they like force you or something, like that you have to participate’ – focus group 3.

In addition, extracurricular situations play a significant role for students, with a majority noting that friends and family members outside of school are also vaping or smoking. While one student mentioned having a parent who uses e-cigarettes, parents who use conventional cigarettes were more prevalent. Some students expressed disapproval of their parents’ smoking, primarily due to the associated (‘disgusting’) smell, which deterred them from smoking themselves. However, some students reiterated their sentiment of ‘it is their own decision’, stating that their parents’ behaviour was their own responsibility. Two students described smoking together with their parents, which they considered to have normalized smoking for them, with one student commenting: ‘I think it really depends on whom you live with at home, my parents smoke so that is actually why I started smoking’ – focus group 3. Additionally, an example was provided of a student who secretly used the e-cigarette of a friend’s parent. Furthermore, all participants with smoking or vaping parents found that parents lost their authority to have an opinion on the smoking or vaping behaviour of their children.

Students who indicated that they use e-cigarettes occasionally seemed to downplay their level of addiction to the e-cigarettes. Students who use e-cigarettes emphasize that they are not addicted because they believe they can stop at any moment if they choose to do so, stating: ‘I am not addicted, I do it because I enjoy it. – focus group 6 ' One reason to quit using e-cigarettes included students’ personal experiences of negative health effects, such as a decrease in physical endurance during sports or frequent coughing. One participant emphasized that the latter repercussion further motivated her to quit as it raised suspicion from her parents, stating: ‘I am coughing on the couch at home frequently, and then my dad asks me whether I smoke. And then I must lie and say that I have a sore throat’ – focus group 3.

Use of cigarettes and e-cigarettes in and around schools

Both smoking and vaping are reported to often occur just outside the school grounds, due to the smoking ban on school premises, often in groups at locations where benches are available. Smoking mainly occurs during school breaks. One student commented: ‘half of the school stands outside during the breaks to smoke or vape’ – focus group 3. Unlike smoking, vaping also occurs indoors at school. Reasons provided for this include the diminished odour emitted by e-cigarettes compared to conventional cigarettes, and the ease of concealing them quickly when a teacher approaches. The most frequently mentioned indoor location for vaping within the school premises is the restrooms, as they are typically devoid of teacher supervision. Non-vaping students report observing a significant amount of vaping in the restrooms, with one student commenting: ‘It smells like a chemical fruit bowl in here’- FG5. At one school equipped with gender-neutral restrooms, this was identified as the primary vaping location, as both boys and girls have access without teacher oversight.

Teachers emphasized that they feel uninformed about student activities in and around school. Some teachers admit to being aware of e-cigarette use on school premises but are uncertain about the most effective approach to address it.

When asked how underage students obtained their e-cigarettes, they described this as effortless, identifying several routes such as purchasing from dealers, acquaintances, or family members, as well as accessing specific stores known for selling e-cigarettes to underage individuals. Moreover, social media played an important role in the online distribution of e-cigarettes, with several students describing TikTok videos marketing ‘Snapchat-dealers’, whom students could contact to acquire e-cigarettes. While the Dutch government issued a ban on flavoured e-cigtarettes starting in 2024, students who participated in the focus groups in 2024 reported not noticing a difference.

School policy and experiences with prevention programs

According to both students and teachers, experience with school policies regarding the use of cigarettes and e-cigarettes vary greatly. Some students report that the school takes no action to prevent the use of (e-)cigarettes, while others state that the penalties for smoking and vaping at school include suspension or parental notification. The experience with prevention in schools primarily consists of informational workshops, conducted either by a teacher at one school or by an expert with firsthand experience at another. According to students, these informational sessions are informative and influence their perceptions of smoking in general, but do not affect their intentions regarding smoking initiation or cessation. Teachers have varying opinions on these informational sessions. One teacher noted that students who use (e-)cigarettes tend to exhibit provocative behaviour during and after attending such sessions, while non-smoking students appear to be more affected and concerned.

Among teachers, there is prevalent uncertainty regarding the exact regulations and policies as well as their enforcement. Multiple interviewees specifically highlighted instances where other teachers observed students using e-cigarettes on school grounds and deliberately ignored it, or were advised by superiors themselves not to enforce the rules to maintain their reputation as ‘nice teachers.’ Teachers also experience this dilemma themselves, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a strong and confidential relationship with their students, especially their mentees. One teacher mentioned having a bond with certain students that allows for addressing their (e-)cigarette use, while this bond is absent with others. Outside of school premises, teachers similarly refrain from intervening, a fact of which students are aware. One teacher noted that a policy was implemented to patrol areas outside the school grounds to prevent disturbances in the surrounding neighbourhood caused by students who use (e-)cigarettes. However, in practice, few teachers volunteered for this duty. The reason provided is that, besides students from their own school, there are sometimes students from other schools present, which makes teachers feel uncomfortable enforcing the rules. Additionally, teachers described missing an ‘escalation ladder’ to enable them to apply consequences for repeated transgressions.

Teachers also emphasized the lack of a school-based prevention program or guidance for cessation, especially missing the opportunity to easily refer students who want to quit vaping. Talking about this issue, a teacher said: ‘I think that, that would be the best thing. If you like as a school, have an external organization ready or on call. So that if there is a request for help from parents, or if you have concerns yourself. That you can do something at least or have someone guide you with that’– teacher 3. One teacher expressed that in her school a committee had been established to develop a program focused on both vaping and smoking but had not yet been able to follow through effectively due to lack of time, resources, and sense of urgency. She stated that: ‘Actually very little is known and done. So there needs to be some kind of eye-opener for everyone, that this is indeed something that is happening […], and we really need to act on it now’- teacher 3.

Future school-based intervention needs

Opinions among students varied greatly on whether a potential intervention should be school based. Some students expressed their wish for the existence of either a school-based prevention or cessation program, while most of the students did not find this useful or appropriate. Students who were positive about a school-based intervention cited reasons such as the need for more information concerning vaping and the high visibility of vaping and smoking in the school environment as indicative of the magnitude of the problem. One first-grade student stated that: ‘So many kids here at school are vaping or smoking during the breaks, that school has to do something about it’ – focus group 3. Students who were negative towards a school-based intervention often cited the earlier mentioned theme of whether to use e-cigarettes being their own choice, arguing that school has no place in that decision. However, when this topic was discussed for a longer period, increasingly more students became receptive to it in multiple focus groups. This was mainly because they realized that the researchers were open to their ideas for a different approach. Teachers stated that the school was an appropriate place due to its educational responsibility, and referring to the amount of time that students spend in the school environment. Perceived barriers for them were the previously mentioned lack of time and money, the fear that parents might not agree with the school policy, and possible increased risk behaviours due to the education.

Several possible formats for a school-based intervention were discussed, most often suggested as a combined program for both smoking and vaping. Both teachers and students felt that a prevention program should start with classroom sessions that inform students about the negative health impacts of vaping. Among teachers, this preference was supported by the fact that they themselves want to acquire more knowledge about the use of e-cigarettes. Nonetheless, the majority of participants were sceptical about whether this would influence their behaviour. It was argued that receiving the information from a professional or experienced individual might increase effectiveness, and a few participants felt that stories with notable outcomes from previous users or family members potentially might leave a larger impression on them. This was illustrated during one of the focus groups, as one of the students associated the use of cigarettes and e-cigarettes with the consequence of a tracheotomy (described as: ‘a hole in your throat’), which was then repeated by two other students as their biggest takeaway from the focus group session. Another specific wish from students was to not only have static informational sessions; instead, they prefer a more interactive program.

A common view amongst both teachers and students was that one-on-one or group conversations concerning smoking and their reasons behind (e-)cigarette use might be beneficial. This came from the notion that problems at school or in their personal lives were often a trigger for their (e-)cigarette use. The interest in having such conversations was also supported by their evaluation of these focus groups, in which several students expressed that this was the first time they had ever talked so openly about this subject. This allowed them to reflect on their behaviour without the fear that their parents or other teachers would find out, with students stating: ‘I think this is the only moment to truly talk about it. […] If I talk about this at home, then I’ll only get told that it’s bad for me and that I must quit and then they will immediately become angry’ – focus group 3. Students did comment that a specific concern for such conversations would be the prerequisite for confidentiality, in which participants mentioned that it was important that conversations about this subject would not have direct negative consequences. As group influences and social pressure were also identified as potential motivators for initiating vaping, students were asked to consider the effectiveness of interventions addressing these factors, for example, workshops to teach students to learn to stand their ground or resist peer pressure. Some participants felt that this might be useful for students who struggle to voice their boundaries, while the majority were sceptical about the effect. For example, one student stated that: ‘I don’t think that you can teach someone to be less insecure’ – focus group 3. When asked if there was a difference in students who would benefit more from a school-based program, participants mentioned that it might be most useful for first graders, citing that they might be more sensitive to group pressure and might not have started vaping yet. This view was echoed by several teachers, who preferred starting as early as possible. However, several other first-graders stated that such sessions did not feel relevant to them because they were not as confronted with smoking as older students yet.

Finally, several teachers emphasized the importance of making the rules regarding smoking and vaping in and around the school visually prominent with signs and other indicators. This aligns with the specific desire of the teachers for the non-smoking culture to be embraced by the entire school community, stressing that it must be a collective effort: ‘We must do it together’.

Discussion

(E-)cigarette use is more prevalent among students in prevocational education compared to their peers in higher educational tracks [25]. Despite this, existing prevention programs often fail to adequately address the specific needs of this group. This study aims to explore the current situation regarding (e-)cigarette use and prevention in Dutch prevocational secondary schools by gaining insight into students’ and teachers’ attitudes, experiences, and needs. The key qualitative findings of this study will be discussed below and compared with existing literature.

The majority of students and teachers mistakenly perceived e-cigarettes as healthier than traditional cigarettes. In fact, research indicates that e-cigarette use also carries health risks, including those associated with nicotine and an increased risk of cancer, with the long-term effects still being studied [23, 24]. Students view smoking and vaping as a personal choice and claim not to be addicted to (e-)cigarettes, despite regular use. This attitude towards (e-)cigarette use is consistent with findings from other research, where young adults (< 24 years) overestimate their ability to quit compared to older adults (>24 years) [26, 27]. The current study also showed that e-cigarettes are widely available in and around prevocational schools, whereas traditional cigarettes are mostly restricted to outdoor areas outside the school premises. The fact that e-cigarettes are also used inside the school, albeit secretly, further seemed to enhance their appeal. Students reported to obtain e-cigarettes from dealers through social media platforms, disregarding the minimum age requirement and the flavour ban, which was instigated in the Netherlands in 2024, during data collection [28]. The illegal purchase of e-cigarettes could increase appeal of using e-cigarettes in certain adolescents, as it is risky behaviour which especially can increase during adolescence [29].

For a future prevention program, students express a need for non-judgmental, open discussions without parental involvement. They explained that adults often speak to them in an angry tone or make them feel as though they are doing something wrong, which leads them to avoid future conversations. This aligns with findings from a recent systematic review by our group on school-based prevention programs, which showed that programs that focus on delivering information without explicitly telling adolescents that they should not use e-cigarettes, or dictating what conclusions they should draw from the information, appear to be more effective [30]. The same systematic review also showed that interventions with parental involvement as a component have generally shown limited effectiveness [30]. Parents play a crucial role in shaping their children’s substance use, with their involvement potentially having both beneficial and detrimental effects [31, 32]. Students with parents who use (e-)cigarettes specifically reported that parental correction was neither desirable nor effective, while students whose parents do not use (e-)cigarettes did not make any statements regarding this. Parents who use (e-)cigarettes are not only a significant predictor but also a risk factor for e-cigarette use in adolescence [31, 32]. In contrast, parents who do not use (e-)cigarettes may act as a protective factor, possibly by setting a good example and providing a sense of fair parental correction [31]. However, the higher percentage of parents who use (e-)cigarettes among prevocational students suggests that parental (e-)cigarette use may pose an even greater risk within this group [31]. Moreover, other research on adolescents’ perceptions of health education programs highlights that young people value prevention programs that give them a sense of autonomy, which may be reduced when parents are involved in such interventions [33].

In addition, findings showed that students prefer an interactive program where they are actively engaged rather than passively receive information. Students in the focus groups reported struggling to concentrate when required to sit still and absorb information, expressing a preference for learning in a more active and interactive manner. This contributes to the need for tailored intervention programs for various educational levels. Students also expressed a strong desire to decide for themselves whether to use (e-)cigarettes. This aligns with Self-Determination Theory, which highlights the importance of autonomy for motivation [34]. Adolescents typically have a strong need for autonomy, and this suggests that prevention efforts should support autonomous motivation, such as linking messages to students’ identity or interests, rather than relying on external pressure or control [34]. Therefore, it is crucial that future interventions are developed based on the needs and experiences of the target group, focusing on empowering adolescents to feel they are making the right choice themselves in an autonomy supportive style. This aligns with findings from a recent systematic review specifically on digital e-cigarette interventions for youth, which addresses the importance of designing, developing, and evaluating prevention and cessation strategies in collaboration with youth to ensure that interventions are relevant, engaging, and effective [35].

Some teachers were negative about traditional smoking education, such as making use of fear appeal, as they have experienced it can provoke provocative behaviour or defensive reactions among students. This finding is also reflected in literature, where the use of fear appeals in interventions does not work as intended and can even have the opposite effect, mainly because adolescents feel attacked, leading to defensive behaviour toward interventions [36]. In this study, teachers collectively expressed a need for information on e-cigarettes, especially regarding their differences from cigarettes, as well as a standardized approach to smoking prevention for all teachers within schools. Providing information about e-cigarettes could help teachers increase their motivation to contribute to prevention programs [37]. In addition, teachers indicated that they have limited time to implement current or new prevention programs, despite recognizing their value. Their perceived role in future prevention programs also influenced their willingness to engage in such programs. While some teachers felt responsible for educating students about (e-)cigarettes use, others were more hesitant, fearing it might strain their relationship with students. They associated this relationship with maintaining a safe environment where students feel comfortable discussing personal or sensitive issues. The highly variable factors influencing overall teacher satisfaction with school-based smoking prevention programs have also been identified in previous research, underscoring the importance of involving teachers in the development of such interventions [38].

Implications

The preferences for a future program differ between students and teachers, but there are also similarities. The biggest difference in preferences for a future prevention program between students and teachers is that students want to be informed but do not want restrictions imposed on them, whereas teachers prefer to enforce stricter rules in and around the school. One key similarity is that both students and teachers need information about e-cigarettes, specifically regarding the differences between using e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes. For teachers, this need is supported by the fact that they do not use e-cigarettes themselves and (mistakenly) see them as something only young people use and as a safer option than conventional cigarettes. Therefore, it is useful to provide teachers with this information, but also to inform them about the situation at school, how students purchase these e-cigarettes, and why they use them. For students, it is important to understand that using e-cigarettes is not without risks. It was notable that students were initially reluctant to engage with such an intervention, but as the discussion progressed, they became more open to it. For practice, this finding emphasizes the importance of involving the target group in the development of a smoking and vaping prevention program.

A key reason for the resistance to a prevention program was students’ perception that (e-)cigarette use is a personal choice and their belief that they were not addicted. In practice, this views can be addressed by highlighting the contrast between what students believe and the reality of addiction. For example, students could be encouraged to reflect on their (e-)cigarette use and compare it with objective indicators of nicotine dependence, such as difficulty of quitting. This may help them recognize that their sense of autonomy may already be undermined by addiction. A similar approach is used in the effective vaping prevention program, “Catch My Breath,” in which students compare their expectations about e-cigarette use among peers with actual prevalence data. This allows them to discover (or reinforce) the realization that e-cigarette use is not normative behaviour for high school students [39]. Furthermore, adolescents indicated a need for open communication about smoking and vaping, without fear judgment from teachers or parents. Currently, this fear prevents students from discussing smoking and vaping with teachers or mentors, despite their specific desire for such conversations expressed during the focus groups. In practice, it may be difficult for students to keep their parents unaware of their e-cigarette use. Instead, parents could be informed about why students are hesitant to talk about smoking and vaping. This could help make the topic easier to discuss. Additionally, teachers or prevention workers could support students before or during these conversations and explain the next steps in the quitting process.

Teachers also expressed their needs for the implementation of a future smoking and vaping prevention program. According to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), several factors may influence the successful implementation of interventions, such as prevention programs in prevocational schools [40]. From the intervention characteristics perspective, our findings suggest that the program should clearly differ from traditional fear appeal approaches to prevent defensive reactions or resistance from students. If the program can minimize the likelihood of unintentional encouraging of risk behaviour, its chances of successful implementation will be higher. Teachers are more likely to support the program if they see it as a positive and practical approach that fits their classroom experience. Within the inner setting, a school’s culture and rules play a key role in whether teachers feel supported in addressing smoking and vaping. If the school environment lacks a standardized approach, teachers may feel uncertain about how to respond to student behaviour. Providing clear guidelines and institutional support can help integrate vaping prevention into their existing responsibilities. On an individual level, teachers vary in their knowledge, attitudes, and confidence in discussing smoking and vaping with students. Some feel incapable due to limited training, while others might hesitate to engage in discussions for fear of resistance from students. Addressing these concerns through professional development and informational resources can strengthen their ability to intervene effectively. The outer setting also plays a crucial role in implementation. Local policies, public health guidelines, and community support can enhance the program’s feasibility, while external funding and national campaigns may provide additional resources. Aligning with broader public health initiatives can strengthen implementation. Finally, involving teachers and students in the co-creation of such interventions may support their successful implementation. Active teacher involvement will not only enhance program feasibility but also increase long-term sustainability within school. As a next step in the development process of such program, co-creation sessions with both students and teachers will be organized to collaboratively design this initiative.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study that explores the situation, attitudes, and experiences and needs in prevention programs for smoking and vaping, from the perspectives of both prevocational students and teachers. The study has yielded valuable insights that can be applied to enhance the development of a targeted smoking- and vaping prevention program. The strengths of this study include its interactive and participatory methods, which were well-suited to the study population. In addition, prevalence rates of (e-)cigarette use among participating students are comparable to national data, which supports the representativeness of the sample [3, 6]. However, a few limitations need to be discussed. First, potential drawbacks of group interviews include students feeling unable to answer freely due to peer pressure or the desire to be liked by classmates. We attempted to mitigate this by asking the students’ mentors to identify instances where certain students would be better interviewed separately and by emphasizing that everything discussed in the focus group would remain confidential. However, neither the mentors nor the researcher observed any such issues in any of the focus groups. Second, teachers and students were invited to participate in the study on a voluntary basis. As a result, the participants may have been more motivated to prevent smoking, potentially biasing the results in terms of a more negative attitude towards smoking and more positive experiences with prevention programs. However, the invitation was intentionally broad, focusing on discussing smoking and vaping at school rather than specifically on prevention, and was open to all students, regardless of whether they used (e-)cigarettes, to minimize selection bias. Future studies could address this by using more systematic sampling methods, such as stratified or random sampling, to reduce the risk of self-selection bias.

Conclusion

This qualitative study contributes to new insights into the context and specific needs of students in prevocational education and their teachers, a group that is often underrepresented in research on smoking prevention. The study highlights the need for a tailored, school-based prevention program that addresses both smoking and vaping separately in prevocational education. Smoking around school premises and the widespread use and availability of e-cigarettes within schools contribute to the normalization of nicotine use. While students and teachers differ in their preferences—students emphasize autonomy and open, non-judgmental discussions, whereas teachers prefer clearer enforcement—both groups express a need for accurate information about e-cigarettes. A future prevention program should be interactive, free of fear-based messaging, and developed in close collaboration with students and teachers to ensure it fits their needs and the reality of the school environment. Acting on these findings is essential to create interventions that resonate with the population and effectively stop the normalization of (e-)cigarette use in school environments.
