Abstract
. Evaluating UX in the context of AI’s complexity, unpredictability, and generative nature presents unique challenges. HCI scholars lack sufficient tool support to build knowledge around diverse evaluation metrics and develop comprehensive UX evaluation plans. In this paper, we introduce EvAlignUX , an innovative system grounded in scientific literature and powered by large language models (LLMs), designed to help HCI scholars explore evaluation metrics and their relationship to potential research outcomes. A user study involving 19 HCI scholars revealed that EvAlignUX significantly improved the perceived clarity, specificity, feasibility, and overall quality of their evaluation proposals. The use of EvAlignUX enhanced participants’ thought processes, resulting in the creation of a Question Bank that can be used to guide UX Evaluation Development. Additionally, the influence of researchers’ backgrounds on their perceived inspiration and concerns about over-reliance on AI highlights future research directions for AI’s role in fostering critical thinking.

1.
Introduction

The rapid integration of AI into interactive systems presents a growing challenge for user experience (UX) evaluation
(
Stige et al. 2023
;
Chaudhry 2024
)
. Traditional usability evaluation guided by standard frameworks such as the System Usability Scale (SUS)
(
Bangor et al. 2008
)
, User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ)
(
Schrepp et al. 2017
)
, NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX)
(
Hart 2006
)
, fall short in capturing the complex, dynamic interaction between humans and AI
(
Kuang et al. 2024
)
. As AI systems become more context-aware and capable of generating responses in real-time, UX researchers are confronted with new questions about trust, emotional engagement, and ethical considerations
(
Liao et al. 2020a
;
Ahmad et al. 2023a
;
Shneiderman 2020
;
Ntoa et al. 2021
)
.
Addressing these new considerations is especially challenging for systems research, where a range of evaluation methods, often borrowing and combining techniques from software engineering, design, and usability evaluation, is triangulated to evaluate the UX of interactive computing systems
(
Marquardt et al. 2017
)
. Current UX evaluation methods, e.g., Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire (PSSUQ)
(
Lewis 1992
)
, Subjective Mental Effort Questionnaire (SMEQ)
(
Hyland et al. 1989
)
, while robust for traditional systems, are inadequate for AI-powered interfaces. Researchers are striving to develop new frameworks, which take into account ethical considerations, trust dynamics, intimacy, and multimodal behaviors, to adequately guide scholars and practitioners in developing their UX evaluation plans for human-AI interaction research, e.g.,
(
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Liao et al. 2024
)
.
Given the plethora of well documented evaluation methods, heuristics and metrics (e.g.,
(
Lazar et al. 2017
;
Nielsen 1992
;
Vermeeren et al. 2010
)
), much less is known about
How researchers select appropriate metrics to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of a system
.

Also, despite the availability of various UX evaluation methods
(
Pettersson et al. 2018
;
Vermeeren et al. 2010
;
Rivero and Conte 2017
;
Darin et al. 2019
;
Kieffer et al. 2019
)
, there is a lack of practical tools to guide researchers in developing comprehensive evaluation plans. Research has shown that incorporating evaluation metrics as part of the design materials in early stages can improve the clarity of designing human-AI interaction research
(
Zheng and Huang 2023
)
. UX is frequently regarded as a rigid set of methods, leading to calls for research and education that encourage framing UX as “
more of a mindset than a method
” within the community
(
Gray 2016
)
.
However, with the rapid advancement of AI models, the literature on AI-mediated multi-stakeholder interactions has expanded exponentially. This growth presents challenges for novice researchers in building up knowledge of relevant evaluation metrics, while seasoned researchers also face increasing pressure to stay current with the latest developments.

To bridge this gap, we designed, implemented, and evaluated
EvAlignUX
, an interactive system that supports researchers in developing UX evaluation plans for their research projects.
EvAlignUX
is powered by the generative capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) to facilitate the exploration of UX evaluation ideas, as LLMs have demonstrated their potential for ideation of scientific research by processing a vast amount of information, generating context-aware insights, retrieving literature, and brainstorming
(
Doshi and Hauser 2024
;
Glickman and Zhang 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
.
Specifically, as shown in Fig.
1
,
EvAlignUX
comprises three primary panels: the
Project Ideation
Panel, the
Metric Explorer
Panel, and the
Outcomes and Risks
Panel. These panels allow users to explore UX metrics, potential outcomes, and potential risks associated with human-AI interaction research. In an evaluation with 19 HCI scholars,
EvAlignUX
led to significant improvements in the clarity, concreteness, and confidence in their UX proposals. The results demonstrated that
EvAlignUX
significantly improved the clarity, concreteness, and confidence of researchers in their proposed UX evaluation plans. Participants were able to identify and integrate detailed UX metrics, self-critique their plans, and raise awareness of potential risks using the system features.

This work makes several significant contributions to the HCI community.
First
,
EvAlignUX
is a novel system, designed to support the ideation process of developing UX evaluation plans. Powered by LLMs,
EvAlignUX
enables users to explore UX metrics and their relationships with potential research outcomes. The unique visual interface of the metrics graph supports users in examining the connections between different UX metrics. To the best of our knowledge,
EvAlignUX
fills the gap in tool support for researchers, providing insights into the research literature of human-AI evaluation metrics and facilitating better decision-making regarding UX evaluation.
Second
, our user studies demonstrate the effectiveness of
EvAlignUX
in helping users gain significantly more clarity and feasibility in their proposed UX evaluation plans. Our findings revealed that researchers’ background impacted their perceived levels of inspiration and their concerns about over-reliance on AI. They also integrate the system features into their thought processes differently, focusing on varied utilities such as comprehensiveness of self-critique and efficiency of implementing the ideas.

Third
, our analysis of participants’ think-aloud and perceived value of different features produced a comprehensive
Question Bank
, highlighting the specific questions researchers asked at different steps of UX evaluation development and identifying how AI provide valuable support such as envisioning the research impact, assessing the risks involved in the research, and justifying metric selection.

Lastly
, our findings prompt a timely and profound discussion on shifting the focus from method to mindset in future research on UX evaluation.

2.
Related Work

In this section, we review the literature on (1) current UX evaluation approaches, and (2) the potential of leveraging LLMs to provide tool support.

2.1.
UX Evaluation of HCI System Research

Developing a UX evaluation plan can be challenging, as it’s often unclear what constitutes "evaluation" and which metrics are appropriate
(
Ledo et al. 2018
;
Hillman et al. 2023
)
. Hillman et al.
(
Hillman et al. 2023
)
elaborated on several challenges involved in developing a solid evaluation plan, such as selecting metrics that accurately reflect UX and research goals, choosing data collection methods, establishing collection protocols, and ensuring consistency across products and organizations. Additionally, long-term effects and social or cultural barriers further complicate the process
(
Hillman et al. 2023
;
Darin et al. 2019
;
Vermeeren et al. 2010
)
. In the past, HCI scholars often relied on established surveys like System Usability Scale (SUS), User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ), Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire (PSSUQ), NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX), Subjective Mental Effort Questionnaire (SMEQ) and Customer Satisfaction (CSAT) to measure users’ perceptions
(
Bangor et al. 2008
;
Schrepp et al. 2017
;
Lewis 1992
;
Hart 2006
;
Reichheld 2003
)
. PULSE metrics (Page views, Uptime, Latency, etc.) are also commonly used to track user behavior
(
Rodden et al. 2010
)
. However, these metrics are often “low-level or indirect measures of user experience”
(
Rodden et al. 2010
)
. Several evaluation frameworks relating to user-centered metrics have been introduced to the HCI and UX domains, such as the Software Development Productivity Framework
(
Sadowski et al. 2019
)
, and Google’s HEART framework
(
Rodden et al. 2010
)
. For instance, the HEART framework, which includes Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task success, was introduced for measuring web-based applications at scale
(
Rodden et al. 2010
)
.

With the rise of AI, recent studies on UX research lay out several new trends and challenges. A school of HCI researchers aims to identify and propose new evaluation metrics for both usability and user-centered evaluation in the age of AI. For example, Zheng et al.’s literature review of human-AI interaction research in HCI, which included user evaluations, reported a suite of metrics using a survey, system logs, and interviews to collect
(
Zheng et al. 2022
)
and argued the importance of including more user-centered metrics (e.g., sociability, socio-emotional, and AI anthropomorphism metrics) with AI’s evolving role in human social interactions. Likewise, Faruk et al.’s works also proposed new subjective scales for measuring the UX of voice agents
(
Faruk et al. 2024a
)
and conversational agents
(
Faruk et al. 2024b
)
. In other domains, like healthcare, Abbasian et al. also summarized some foundation metrics for evaluating the effectiveness of healthcare conversations powered by generative AI
(
Abbasian et al. 2024
)
.

However, there is a lack of tools to help researchers understand the evaluation metrics literature and design a comprehensive evaluation plan for human-AI interaction research. As Yang et al.
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
systematically reported how AI system’s uncertainty and complexity can make unpredictable errors, which easily damage UX and lead to undesired societal impact. Gray’s finding of
"More of a Mindset Than a Method"
also highlighted that UX practitioners often follow a codified method rather than adopting a "mindset" that aligns with their design process, practice context, and the specific design problem
(
Gray 2016
)
. All these works suggest that new tools and approaches are needed to support researchers and practitioners in developing their UX evaluation plans.

2.2.
The Potential of LLMs to Support Ideation

Large Language Models (LLMs) can process vast amounts of multi-modal information and generate context-aware, coherent text, making them ideal tools for evaluation-related tasks in various domains. A group of researchers developed novel interaction prototypes using LLMs for evaluating generative outputs
(
Desmond et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Shankar et al. 2024
)
. For example, Kim et al. developed EvalLM
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
, an interactive system for refining prompts by evaluating multiple outputs on user-defined criteria. This helps developers compare their prompt outputs to diagnose weaknesses in their prompts.
Regarding LLMs’ support for scientific tasks, recent studies have showcased LLM-powered methods for retrieving and recommending relevant literature
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
, reviewing and critiquing research drafts, writing research manuscripts, and augmenting research ideation and brainstorming for scientific writing based on scientific evidence and database
(
Nigam et al. 2024
)
, utilizing large scientific dataset. Recent advancements in HCI and AI research also attempt to support UX practitioners with AI-enabled tools to conduct usability testing
(
Fan et al. 2022
;
Kuang et al. 2024
)
, e.g., LLMs are employed to help human evaluators to identify more usability issues in usability test video recordings.
All these research highlights the significant potential of LLMs to support research ideation and provide valuable feedback for task evaluation. However, little is known about how researchers with varying levels of experiences can benefit from using LLM-supported tools to develop their UX evaluation plans.

3.
EvAlignUX

To address the research gap,
EvAlignUX
was designed as a tool to aid researchers explore evaluation metrics for developing UX plans of Human-AI research. The system was developed through an iterative process, informed by both the literature review
(
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Inan Nur et al. 2021
;
Vermeeren et al. 2010
;
Rivero and Conte 2017
;
Darin et al. 2019
)
and input from a focus group. Leveraging the authors’ expertise in HCI research and practices, we began with an initial process of developing UX evaluation plan, e.g., proposing of a research topic, identifying relevant UX metrics for evaluation, and then framing the expected research contribution. The design was refined over two months through multiple weekly feedback sessions with an HCI research group, consisting of nine graduate students and faculty members from the HCI, Informatics, and Computer Science departments (n=9). All of the participants have worked on at least one HCI project where they designed or developed systems. Their applications covered diverse contexts: vision-based question-answering, ideation and writing, accessibility, immersive technologies, and intelligent assistants. Based on the UX evaluation challenges reported from prior literature
(
Ledo et al. 2018
;
Hillman et al. 2023
)
, and after piloting the initial pipeline with this group, we established three design goals (DG), then we iteratively searched for prior literature and consulted for feedback through this weekly focus group meetings.

•

DG1:
Recommend UX metrics relevant for a project;

•

DG2:
Explain how the metrics are used in existing literature; and

•

DG3:
Inform researchers with potential risks associated with their design and evaluation plans.

This panel recommends relevant evaluation metrics and related literature while enabling users to explore additional metrics through both list-view and graph-view interactions, helping them formulate a comprehensive evaluation plan.
Prior literature has highlighted that users are often excluded from the process of defining evaluation criteria for a system, with most participants consulted only at the end of the design process to assess the system’s effects, rather than being actively involved in setting these criteria. This exclusion contradicts the goal of democratizing the design process
(
Bossen et al. 2016

Zheng and Huang 2023

. Inspired by this challenge, we propose a metric recommendation feature that introduces UX evaluation metrics into design materials as a design or evaluation input.
Given that graph-based interaction is a commonly used and effective approach for recommendation systems
(

, we propose incorporating a graph-view alongside the traditional list-view. This graph-view will allow users to visualize UX metrics and their correlating metrics, fostering inspiration and enabling more informed filtering and selection of metrics. (

. To enhance user understanding, we will provide context and examples of how these metrics have been derived and applied.

Assists users in framing their research contributions based on previous studies and identifies potential pitfalls by referencing databases of prior AI risks and incidents.
Technologies have positively transformed society, but they
have also led to undesirable consequences not anticipated at the time of design or development. Previous HCI research explored using insights into past undesirable consequences in the world to help researchers and practitioners gain awareness anticipate potential adverse effects and reflect on their own experiences with technology
(
Pang et al. 2024
)
. Inspired by this, we propose a panel for generating related research outcomes and risks based on existing research and real-world cases. We sourced case studies from a database indexing real AI incidents documented by journalists — the AI, Algorithmic, and Automation Incident and Controversy (AIAAIC) repository that previous papers have used
(

. This approach aims to strengthen UX evaluation metrics by incorporating research outcomes based on literature, and relevant risks grounded in real-world incidents.

As Sarah adds and removes metrics from her shopping cart, she notices that the results in the Outcome feature update dynamically. She selects outcomes from prior studies that catch her interest. 3.2.1.
Data Preparation

In this section, we present how the scientific database was prepared.
Based on several UX evaluation survey papers, e.g.,
(
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Inan Nur et al. 2021
;
Vermeeren et al. 2010
)
, we established an
initial repository of evaluation metrics
. Two authors expanded this repository by incorporating additional metrics identified in the reviewed papers. Definitions for all metrics, including the newly added ones, were either directly sourced from these surveys or developed collaboratively by the authors to ensure completeness and coherence.

After building the repository of evaluation metrics, we created a
paper database
from the same survey papers and utilized GPT-4o
(
OpenAI et al. 2024
)
to build up more fine-grained information associated with the evaluation metrics. We prompted GPT with each full text of the papers and asked GPT to provide a narrative of the system developed in the papers, along with the challenges or the research gaps that the papers addressed. We provided index definitions and asked GPT to generate a list of ten descriptive indices based on the content of the papers. These descriptive indices include: interaction paradigms, application domain, modality, system features, design novelty, design methods, human-AI relationship types, stakeholders involved, social scale and theoretical frameworks used. The definitions for these indices can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

Between the metrics and the papers in the database, we also created the usage information of each metric in relevant paper. For example, a metric usage follows the following format:
"This paper uses the [X] metric to evaluate users’ [Y] towards [Z technology]. It was found that [S findings related to Y]."

[X]
is the metric identified in 1,
[Y]
is the aspect of user interaction being evaluated,
[Z]
is the technology or system being evaluated, and
[S]
is the findings related to the user interaction being evaluated.

We then crawled metadata and appended metadata to each paper. The metadata includes the uid of the paper, the title, authors, the abstract, the date of publication, uids of papers that cited this paper, keywords, the publication venue, the publisher and the affiliation of authors. Besides, we also leverage the existing AI Incident Database as a repository for risks
(
McGregor 2020
)
and extracted relevant incidents and risks.

EvAlignUX
’s backend recommends UX metrics based on an initial
System Description
. After a series of user interactions, it generates a new Evaluation Plan and UX Outcome using LLM and data from research papers via a Knowledge Graph, representing complex entity relations derived from source documents.

3.2.2.
Index Generation and derivation of Knowledge Graph

A list of indexes is generated by
EvAlignUX
using the initial system description provided by the user. The indexes are intended to standardize the system description into fixed categories. Indexes are generated by providing the System Description, examples of other generated indexes, and corresponding definitions of each index as input to the LLM. Subsequently, the user is recommended three additional index values corresponding to each category. These indexes and the initial system description are used to retrieve a list of metrics from our Knowledge Graph.

The Knowledge Graph serves as the foundation for recommending metrics, built using the Neo4j Graph Database. We structured our data as a graph to capture the intricate relationships within our dataset
(
Verma et al. 2023
;
Kejriwal 2022
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
, enabling efficient information extraction and retrieval of global contextual information
(
Edge et al. 2024
)
. The backing dataset of research papers is organized into distinct clusters of communities
(
Fortunato 2010
)
based on their index similarity, citations, and common metrics using the Louvain Community detection algorithm
(
De Meo et al. 2011
)
. The graph consists of three different types of nodes, each characterized by distinct properties. Table
5
in the Appendix provides a detailed overview of the distinct nodes and their associated properties. The three unique nodes are as follows:

•

Paper Node:
This node contains details about each unique research paper.

•

Metric Node:
The node includes attributes specific to a UX metric. This node is attached to the Paper and the Outcome node via several edges. The node is shared between two paper nodes if they measure the metric.

•

Outcome Node:
This node is attached to the Metric node via an edge and contains the outcome and other information corresponding to each metric used in a paper. Since the metric node could be shared between multiple paper nodes, this node stores the unique information of a metric corresponding to a research paper.

All nodes are unique and do not contain duplication. Furthermore, weighted edges exist between the nodes. For instance, there exist three edges amongst each pair of paper nodes, namely,

[SHARED_METRIC], [CITES], and [CITED_BY]. The

[SHARED_METRIC] edge exists between two paper nodes if they share at least one common metric. Similarly, if any paper A cites another paper B, there exists a [CITES] edge from paper node A to paper node B, and a [CITED_BY] edge from paper node B to paper node A. Edges of type [CITES] and [CITED_BY] are assigned relatively higher weight, compared to [SHARED_METRIC] edge, by multiplying the edge weight with a constant value, due to stronger relation amongst the papers because of the citation relation, based on an increase in the
modularity score
as discussed in
(
Blondel et al. 2008
)
. The weight assigned to these edges is influenced by the relative importance of each index category in evaluating the similarity between research papers. Index categories that indicate greater similarity receive higher weights, which are then multiplied by the similarity score between index values of the categories for each paper, contributing to the overall edge weight. Additionally, each Paper node is connected to a Metric node via a non-weighted edge of type [HAS_METRIC], which is then connected to an Outcome node via another non-weighted edge of type [HAS_OUTCOME].

3.2.3.
Metrics Recommendation

The provided system description and its corresponding generated indexes extract metrics from the Knowledge Graph. The system description and indexes are vectorized, and the most similar paper is determined using
cosine similarity
in the existing Knowledge Graph. The community for the most similar paper is determined from the above Knowledge Graph and consequently, all metrics linked to the paper nodes belonging to the above community are extracted. These metrics, along with the user system description and generated indexes are used to further filter metrics, by prompting a LLM. Additionally, each metric is further classified into three metric collection methods, based on their usage in previous research papers, using the Knowledge Graph. These metrics are displayed in a list view and a graphical view to the user.

3.2.4.
Potential risks and metric outcomes

Each metric in the Knowledge Graph is associated with outcomes corresponding to the research paper. Subsequently, these results are retrieved from the Knowledge Graph based on the final metrics list, and displayed to the user to filter, based on their relevance to the user’s system description. Each outcome is specific to a research paper and is mapped to all the metrics associated with the research paper. Consequently, these outcomes are further considered while generating the Potential Evaluation Plan and the Potential UX Outcome.

In addition to the recommended metric outcomes, our system generates potential risks associated with the user system, using the AIAAIC repository. We extract the top three incidents that have a
Euclidean distance
of less than 0.5 by performing a vector-based similarity search, using
FAISS
, between the user system description, and the AI system description present in the AI Incident dataset. Subsequently, we extract risks associated with these AI systems, and provide them along with the user system description to a LLM, to filter the most relevant risks for the system. The risks identified are contextualized based on the initial system description, with corresponding URLs for related incidents also available to the user.

Once the metrics have been finalized by the user, along with the corresponding metric outcomes, a Potential Evaluation Plan and Potential UX Outcome is recommended to the user. The Potential Evaluation Plan includes detailed instructions, regarding the usage of each metric and the methods for measuring each metric, based on the user’s System Description and initial evaluation plan. The Potential Evaluation Plan is generated by providing the system description, a list of selected metrics, the specified metric outcome, and methods used to previously evaluate the UX Metrics, to a LLM, and subsequently, prompting the LLM to generate the Potential Evaluation Plan. The Potential UX Outcomes are generated by considering the list of selected metrics, desired results from the metric outcomes, initial system description, and also the risks associated with the system.

3.3.
Technical Evaluation

We conducted a small-scale evaluation to compare
EvAlignUX
’s understanding of research metrics with human understanding and assess the quality of its metric suggestions based on a given narration.

3.3.1.
Evaluating LLM’s Ability to Extract the Metrics

To assess GPT’s capability in identifying evaluation metrics in research papers from a predefined list of candidate metrics, we adopted a similar strategy as
(
Pang et al. 2024
)
and randomly selected 15 papers from our initial pool of research articles. The evaluation metrics used in these papers were extracted using our GPT prompt. One author reviewed each identified metric and rated it from three aspects: whether the metric was actually measured, whether the metric was in the candidate list and whether the reasoning or citation provided makes sense. A second author verified these assessments. A metric was considered accurate if it fulfils requirement one and two. Common sources of inaccuracy included:

The metric identified was not actually measured in the evaluation part of the paper (i.e. hallucination
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
). There were three cases for this: a) GPT inferred metrics based on descriptions from the paper, but the metrics were not explicitly measured in the evaluation process. b) GPT synthesized metrics from the results, but the metrics were not explicitly measured in the evaluation process and c) GPT making recommendations for a metric that should have been used but was not used, based on the context of the paper. The case for (a) and (b) was often that GPT picked out a statement or a conclusion from a qualitative result described in the paper. However, such qualitative results were synthesized from interviews or surveys, and therefore the identified metric was not explicitly measured in the paper. For example, GPT identified "attitude towards the agent" as an evaluation metric from
(
Wambsganss et al. 2020
)
, quoting
"students made positive comments about the interaction with CAs related to the perceived level of enjoyment and interaction."
While attitude towards the agent was not explicitly measured in the paper, it was described in the findings and was thus misidentified. For (c), an example is GPT identifying "privacy concern" as a metric from
(
Lee et al. 2021
)
, quoting
"because chatbot use can result in users’ self-disclosure of sensitive topics"
from the discussion section. Although it makes sense to measure "privacy concern" based on the context of potential self-disclosure of sensitive information, the said metric was not explicitly measure in the paper so it was marked as inaccurate.
The metric identified was outside the list of candidate metrics. There were two cases for this: a) GPT reworded the metric in the candidate list to express the same meaning, and b) GPT made up a metric that was not included in the candidate list. For (a), an example was GPT identifying "utterance length" from
(
Thomas et al. 2020
)
, while the actual metric in the candidate list is worded as "length of utterance". Regarding (b), "transparency" was identified as a metric from
(
Pecune et al. 2019
)
, while "transparency" is not part of the candidate list.

We found that out of the 102 metrics identified in the 15 papers, 94% (96/102) were indeed measured, 92% (94/102) were in the candidate list and 88% were accurate (90/102), as there were two overlapping metrics across requirement one and two. 100% (102/102) of the reasoning and citations made sense. Overall, the results suggest that with our prompt, GPT’s ability to identify metrics is largely reliable.

4.1.
Participant

Our study was approved by the university’s IRB. We recruited 19 participants from social media. All participants reported they had extensive experience with HCI/UX research, 11 participants (57.9%) have 3-4 years experience, 7 participants (36.8%) have 1-2 years experience, only one participant has less than one year UX experience. Regarding participant’s AI usage in research study, the majority of participants used AI to help with simple tasks, such as coding & writing (17, 89.5%), literature review (7, 36.8%), and data analysis (11, 57.9%),. Notably, we found that few participants used AI for tasks that require rigor and creativity, including research ideaiton (7, 36.8%), design prototyping (7, 36.8%), research evaluation plan development (3, 15.8%), and usability testing (1, 5.3%). The background information of participants is shown in Appendix
6
. Each user study lasted 1.5 to 2 hours. Participants received 20 USD per hour as compensation in the user study.

4.2.
Procedure

Figure
6
demonstrates the study procedure. ① The study began with the background collection, including their UX experience levels. Researchers then introduced the study context and explained the tasks. Then, they used
EvAlignUX
’s landing page to spend about 10 minutes entering their project description, evaluation plans, and expected outcomes. After that, they completed an initial survey that capture their perceptions of the proposed project plans (survey 1, initial plan). Then, participants used the Zoom Whiteboard to draw their thought processes for developing the UX evaluation plan and think-aloud to ensure their ideas were communicated clearly.

In
EvAlignUX
, participants were guided through the system features and asked to refine their project plans using all available features. After experimenting with the features and achieving satisfactory results, participants used the "Export Data" button to review, regenerate, and re-edit their project plans. Upon finalizing their plans, they completed exit surveys (survey 2, revised plan; survey 3,
EvAlignUX
) to evaluate their perceptions of the generated plans and their experience with the system. During the
EvAlignUX
process, the think-aloud method was employed to observe and record participants’ interactions and thoughts. Next, participants used Zoom Whiteboard to revise their thought process for developing the UX evaluation plan if they felt there were any changes. Finally, participants were interviewed to delve deeper into their overall experiences and gather additional insights. This step provided a comprehensive understanding of user perceptions and allowed for collecting feedback on both the proposed plans and the system features.

4.3.
Measures

We recorded the following data to assess participants’ performance and perceptions during the study: task logs, thought process diagrams, think-aloud data, Likert-scale questionnaire responses, and interview transcripts.

4.3.1.
System Logs

For system log data, we firstly collected user’s input to the system, including project description (i.e.
What are the main objectives of your project?
), UX evaluation plans (i.e.
How do you plan to evaluate the UX of your project?
), and expected outcomes (i.e.
What outcomes do you expect from this evaluation?
) as well as their updated ones. Additionally, we logged their usage behavior data, including (1) the number of times a user regenerate indexes, (2) the total number of metrics selected, (3) the number of metrics added through graph, (4) the number of outcomes clicked including both selected and deselected, and (5) the number of risks clicked. We also logged their revised system description, UX evaluation plans, and expected outcomes.

4.3.2.
Thought Process Diagrams

We collected user’s thought process visualization for developing a UX evaluation plan using Zoom Whiteboard before and after using the
EvAlignUX
to see how AI-based influenced their thinking.

4.3.3.
Questionnaire

The questionnaire is divided into three main parts:

•

Background information: Users answered questions related their past UX experiences, AI literacy, and AI usage for research purposes.

•

Perceptions of initial and revised UX study plan (survey 1 and 2): We collected user perceptions before and after they explored
EvAlignUX
to determine if there were significant differences between their perceptions of their UX research plans.
The survey items are adapted from main schemes for analyzing UX evaluation methods
(
Vermeeren et al. 2010
;
Venable et al. 2012
;
Dean et al. 2006
)
, including novelty, relevance, efficiency, completeness, clarity, concreteness, quality, feasibility, rigorous, risks considerations, and confidence.

•

System feature perceptions: For each metric, we asked participants to rate their perceptions of each feature across 8 Likert-scale items (survey 3): enjoyment of use
(
Davidson et al. 2023
)
, intention to use it in the future
(
Everard and Galletta 2005
)
, perceived usefulness
(
Davis 1989
)
, trust in the suggestions and decisions provided
(
Everard and Galletta 2005
)
, perceived cognitive load in understanding the information
(
Paas et al. 2008
)
, inspiration for exploring and developing UX research
(
Chu et al. 2010
)
,
the system’s contributions to the UX evaluation plan, and the system’s contributions to the UX outcome.

4.3.4.
Think Aloud

We collected their think-aloud while using the system features as well as their thought process diagrams. For example, we asked to provide us with reasoning behind their decision making process.

4.3.5.
Interview

Finally, we conducted a short follow-up semi-structured interview. We asked questions in three main categories with follow-up questions based on the interviewee’s responses.

•

Perceptions of features and their role in decision making for proposing a UX plan:
Which feature is the most impressive? Which feature is the least impressive? How do these features support your decision making?

•

Overall user experience and potential improvements:
After using the system, how useful do you feel about the system? Were there any features or information that you felt were lacking? How do you view your original and revised plans?

•

Follow-up questions about their research mental model:
What differences do you notice between your previous research process and the process of interacting with
EvAlignUX
? How do you perceive the strengths and weaknesses of these strategies?

4.4.
Data Analysis

We analyze each participant’s ① thought process diagrams, ② quantitative data from surveys and logs, and ③ user decision making reasoning through think-aloud, interview transcripts, and UX research plans.

4.4.1.
Thought Process Diagrams Analysis

We standardized thought process diagrams drawn by participants by coding each illustrated step. Modifications, such as envisioning AI assistance or adding new steps, were identified. These changes were further analyzed by examining their diagrams with the think-aloud data. We summarized the benefits participants identified where AI could assist in proposing a UX research plan.

4.4.2.
Quantitative Data Analysis

We analyzed participants’ responses to the two post-task surveys. For all these measures, we conducted a Shapiro-Wilk test
(
González-Estrada and Cosmes 2019
)
and found that all data were not normally distributed.
Given the nature of our data, we applied Kendall’s tau correlation to explore relationships between user experience levels, system usage, and perceptions of changes in plans and system features
(
Bolboaca and Jäntschi 2006
;
Kendall 1938
)
. We also reported effect sizes using standardized methodologies
(
Dixon and Massey Jr 1951
)
. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.12.

4.4.3.
Behavioral Analysis through Think-Aloud and Interviews

A thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2012
)
was performed on data from think-aloud sessions and interviews. Two researchers collaboratively identified common themes through an iterative process. This analysis helped map how
EvAlignUX
features address identified challenges from the thought processes and outline the pros and cons of these features.

6.
Discussion

In this section, we explore methods for promoting a mindset-focused UX evaluation in HCI education and practice, examine the potential risks of using large language models for evaluation, and discuss potential design implications yielded from our findings.

6.1.
From
Method-Centric
to
Mindset-Centric
: a Paradigm Shift of Creating UX Evaluation Plans

In this study, we found that highlighting UX metrics as a center of UX evaluation empowers UX researchers holistically, enhancing both their perceived quality of their proposed plans and their confidence in their UX evaluation plans (RQ1). Furthermore, the combined use of different
EvAlignUX
components for refining their UX plans allowed participants to self-validate, critique, and rationalize their evaluation choices (RQ2), with their perceived inspirations from the system varying according to their UX experience (RQ3). Meanwhile, we observed their thought processes evolving from asking general questions to using AI to support iterative reasoning for their chosen metrics, considering various types of information, including previously not considered aspects, such as outcomes and risks.

UX evaluation plays a vital role in ensuring the quality of HCI system research, and it is a growing field with diverse approaches
(
Pettersson et al. 2018
)
. Although several UX evaluation methods have been outlined in previous literature reviews
(
Pettersson et al. 2018
;
Vermeeren et al. 2010
;
Rivero and Conte 2017
;
Darin et al. 2019
;
Kieffer et al. 2019
)
, few interaction designs have been proposed to support UX researchers in developing comprehensive UX evaluation plans. Gray’s previous work
(
Gray 2016
)
found that many UX designers and practitioners view UX as a set of rigid methods rather than a
"mindset,"
suggesting that HCI education should move beyond teaching predefined methods and instead focus on fostering the adaptability and improvisation skills necessary for UX designers to address the unique challenges they face. A mindset approach to UX evaluation, as emphasized in our findings, aligns with their argument.

Several scholars emphasize adaptability and context-sensitive practices in UX research. Vermeeren et al.
(
Vermeeren et al. 2010
)
note that no single UX evaluation method suits all projects, urging researchers to adapt methods to specific contexts. Rivero et al.
(
Rivero and Conte 2017
)
advocate for iterative frameworks that incorporate feedback, while Darin et al.
(
Darin et al. 2019
)
stress the importance of critical reflection throughout the evaluation process. Building on these insights, our study highlights the role of metrics in establishing a mindset-oriented approach to UX evaluation. This shift is essential for advancing the field and ensuring UX evaluations meaningfully contribute to the success of HCI systems research.

6.2.
Design Implications: Using LLM for UX for Interdisciplinary and Crossed-Studies Research

Our findings reveal that
EvAlignUX
provides five key benefits for developing UX research plans. However, we observed varying usage patterns among participants (RQ1, RQ2): some enriched system-generated plans with detailed context, others focused on applying specific metrics, and some simply copied the plan without critical evaluation. We found that these patterns were, to some extent, linked to participants’ UX experience (RQ3).

While some participants viewed
EvAlignUX
as a potential “teammate” in developing UX plans, the challenge remains in ensuring that LLM usage does not lead to the
deskilling
of UX practitioners’ research expertise. Previous literature has highlighted the ethical concerns and potential harms of over-reliance on LLMs in various domains. For instance, Ahmad
(
Ahmad et al. 2023b
)
found that excessive dependence on AI in education led to students becoming passive, diminishing their decision-making abilities and negatively impacting cognitive skills.
Similarly, in human-AI co-creative writing, AI outputs are heavily influenced by the distribution of their training datasets
(
Roselli et al. 2019
;
Argyle et al. 2023
)
. Without considering users’ unique needs, over-reliance on these outputs can result in research homogeneity and a lack of creativity
(
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Rezwana and Maher 2023
)
. Additionally, issues such as hallucination, lack of transparency, and algorithmic bias have been identified as major barriers to effective AI collaboration
(
Zhai et al. 2024
)
.
Given the limitations of LLMs, future studies need to carefully consider and address the
ethical concerns
raised to support the critical thinking of UX researchers. Here are some potential research directions:

6.2.1.
Mitigating Potential Over-Reliance and Triggering Deeper Thought Processes

One potential direction is to use AI systems that provide hints, or to use
Question Bank
(see Fig.
8
), this approach may aim to augment researchers’ reasoning by prompting reflection and encouraging deeper engagement with their work.
Essential features include controllability, and explainability, alongside clear disclaimers that warn users about the risks of over-reliance on generative AI
(
Liao et al. 2020b
)
. Previous research highlights that transparent systems with clear AI outputs foster better human-AI collaboration by encouraging users to engage more critically with the tool
(
Wang et al. 2020
;
Glinka and Müller-Birn 2023
)
. Furthermore, studies indicate that embedding mechanisms that encourage active thinking—such as presenting alternative viewpoints or highlighting uncertainties—can help mitigate the risk of over-dependence on AI-generated solutions and foster deeper cognitive engagement
(
Bansal et al. 2021
)
.

6.2.2.
Using AI to Support Self-Critique

Our findings suggest that AI may assist in addressing challenges through self-critique mechanisms. For example,
EvAlignUX
uses its literature base to provide users with relevant information that encourages self-critique. However, unlike human experts, AI systems often fail to identify nuanced flaws in research plans that arise from researchers’ misunderstandings. Previous studies have shown that AI’s capacity for self-critique is limited, particularly when dealing with complex, contextual errors that require domain-specific knowledge or critical thinking
(
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. AI’s ability to critique existing research, while promising, lacks the contextual depth found in human-to-human collaboration
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Xu and Mulligan 2022
)
. Further research is needed to explore how AI can more closely mimic human critical evaluation in the research process.

6.2.3.
Using AI to Mitigate UX Risks

A key design implication often overlooked is the concept of “impact visioning." While only one participant in our study considered the potential risks during the initial development of their UX plans, previous studies have shown that negative consequences of new technologies can often be predicted in early design stages
(
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Pang et al. 2024
)
.
AI systems, by drawing on cross-disciplinary insights, may also help researchers anticipate and mitigate potential risks in UX evaluation, raising awareness of emerging concerns in technology use and design. Prior studies in AI ethics and risk management suggest that AI can be instrumental in identifying unintended consequences and risks in various domains, including UX design, by leveraging data across disciplines to foresee issues that might not be immediately apparent
(
Vinuesa et al. 2020
;
Floridi and Chiriatti 2021
)
.

6.3.
Limitations and Future Work

We acknowledge several limitations that may affect both
EvAlignUX
’s performance and the findings of our research. First, as an exploratory system,
EvAlignUX
incorporates a limited selection of papers, primarily focused on human-AI interaction. Participants proposing studies in interdisciplinary fields may find the system’s suggestions less relevant or accurate.
Second, although we observed differences in participants’ perceptions and usage behaviors, the majority of participants were doctoral students, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.
Third, for the
EvAlignUX
backend, the recommendation is confined to metrics that have been predefined in our database. Future studies should explore the continued expansion of the database to include a broader spectrum of metrics and online publication sources.

7.
Conclusion

User experience is vital to any Human-Computer Interaction research, yet many UX designers and practitioners often rely on rigid evaluation methods rather than adopting a holistic approach. In this paper, we introduced
EvAlignUX
, an LLM-based tool designed to support UX evaluation development. Through an evaluation with 19 HCI researchers, we found that centering UX metrics in the evaluation process fostered a mindset that balanced holistic UX impact with contextual factors, evaluation practices, and research value. This approach significantly improved participants’ perceived plan quality and confidence, while also prompting their thought processes to consider risks when evaluating systems. For practical implications, we offer a
Question Bank
to guide future UX evaluation efforts; and suggest that future researchers can enhance
EvAlignUX
by incorporating domain-specific literature to further refine their evaluation planning. In a world where experience defines impact, a UX-driven mindset serves as the key to meaningful and lasting designs.
