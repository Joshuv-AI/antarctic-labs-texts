Lipopolysaccharide‐Specific Aptasensor: A Spectroscopic Approach for Identifying Gram‐Negative Bacteria
Authors: Hamidreza Majidifard, Rahman Emamzadeh, Donald Keith Roper, Mahboobeh Nazari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDifferentiating bacteria into Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive categories is a standard method of identification in biological science and is essential for understanding bacterial characteristics. This study presents a novel aptasensor that integrates an anti‐lipopolysaccharide (LPS) aptamer with a G‐quadruplex‐based DNAzyme. This aptasensor enables spectroscopy‐based detection of LPS‐containing Gram‐negative bacteria. With a detection limit of approximately 2.7 × 105 cells/mL for Escherichia coli, this aptasensor offers an efficient and straightforward solution for laboratory detection of Gram‐negative bacteria.

Introduction

Gram staining, an essential technique in bacteriology, has evolved significantly since its introduction by Hans Christian Joachim Gram in 1884 [1]. Its speed and simplicity have made it a crucial diagnostic tool for microbiologists and clinicians [2]. However, despite its extensive application, the reliability of Gram staining results has been questioned due to inconsistencies in operators' techniques, insufficient quality control protocols and the absence of standardisation [3]. Traditional culture‐based methods are labour‐intensive and time‐consuming, and they often fail to differentiate target bacteria from non‐target microorganisms or detect viable but non‐culturable cells [4]. Molecular techniques such as PCR, although sensitive, require specialised equipment and trained personnel, limiting their use for rapid routine analysis [5]. Antibody‐based detection also has drawbacks, including batch‐to‐batch variation, temperature sensitivity and high production costs [6]. Aptamers are short single‐stranded DNA or RNA oligonucleotides. They can bind effectively and selectively to diverse targets, including ions, macromolecules and whole cells. Because of their small size, low immunogenicity, low synthesis cost and high target affinity, aptamers have gained attention in biosensing. Compared to antibodies, aptamers offer greater stability, simpler production and reversible folding capabilities [7, 8]. The adaptability of aptamers has been demonstrated in various detection platforms, including lateral flow immunoassays combining aptamers with antibodies for rapid and sensitive target detection [9]. Specific aptamers for lipopolysaccharide (LPS) have been successfully developed using the Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential enrichment (SELEX) technique [10]. One of the key features of the Gram‐negative cell membrane is the presence of LPS, which is absent in Gram‐positive bacteria. The use of LPS aptamers in combination with poly(diacetylene) (PDA) vesicles for the detection of Escherichia coli has been reported [11]. Despite the potential of this approach for visual detection, the aptamer‐PDA conjugation step limits its practical application.

Several aptamer‐based detection systems have been explored for Gram‐negative pathogens, including a microfluidic assay designed for Acinetobacter baumannii detection using two aptamers [12]. DNA aptamers have been selected against Pseudomonas aeruginosa using cell SELEX for fluorescence‐based identification [13]. Colourimetric aptasensors have also been designed for Campylobacter jejuni detection in food samples using nanoparticle‐based signal amplification [14]. However, many of these approaches require labelling, complex sample preparation or multiple incubation steps.

Here, we present the design and development of a spectroscopy‐based, label‐free aptasensor. This sensor integrates an anti‐LPS aptamer directly with a G‐quadruplex DNAzyme sequence into a single construct. To our knowledge, this is the first time an anti‐LPS aptamer has been combined with a G‐quadruplex DNAzyme in a single construct for label‐free detection of whole Gram‐negative bacterial cells. This design differs from previous approaches that require separate recognition and signal transduction elements or that rely on labelling or amplification steps. The assay is simple, rapid and provides results in a shorter time compared to many existing methods. The aptasensor enables colourimetric detection of Gram‐negative bacteria, which is crucial for rapid diagnostics in clinical and environmental analysis. The anti‐LPS aptasensor effectively identified Gram‐negative bacteria, demonstrating its efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Material

Utilising a technology made by Zolalan (Iran), deionised water with a conductivity of 18.2 MΩ cm was obtained. Sodium chloride (NaCl), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), tris[hydroxymethyl]aminomethane (Tris) and 1,2‐bis(dimethylamino)ethane (TEMED) were purchased from BioBasic (Canada). Haemin was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA). 2,2′‐Azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulphonic acid) (ABTS) was procured from Molekula (UK). Acetic acid was acquired from Merck (Germany). All sequences were synthesised by GENEray Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Design, Synthesis and Preparation of LPS Aptasensor

The aptamer sequence was extracted from the study reported by Bruno et al. [10]. The G‐quadruplex sequence was obtained from research conducted by Chen et al. [15]. These sequences were linked together using a 3‐nucleotide linker (Table 1). The sequences were prepared and stored at −20°C before use using a protocol provided by Zhou et al. [16].

Briefly, to prepare active DNAs, including aptasensor, aptamer and G‐quadruplex solutions at a concentration of 20 µM in TE buffer, they were thermally denatured at 88°C for 10 min to ease the unfolding of DNA strands. The solutions were then gradually cooled to room temperature to allow for the progressive reformation of DNA structures. Subsequently, the DNA solutions were treated with haemin (20 µM) diluted in HEPES buffer (25 mM HEPES, 200 mM NaCl, 20 mM KCl, 0.05% (w/v) Triton X‐100, 1% (v/v) DMSO and pH 7.4). Then, to ensure optimal binding of haemin to the G‐quadruplex structure, the mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 1 h.

Cultivation and Preparation of Bacteria

E. coli ATCC 1943, E. coli pCOLD‐EGFP and P. aeruginosa were selected as representative Gram‐negative bacteria based on their different phylogenetic backgrounds and clinical relevance. Limosilactobacillus reuteri, Levilactobacillus brevis and Lactobacillus helveticus were included as Gram‐positive controls to confirm the selectivity of the aptasensor for LPS‐containing bacteria. The bacterial strains used in this study were obtained from the University of Isfahan's microbial bank.

E. coli ATCC 1943 and P. aeruginosa were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth containing 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract and 10 g/L sodium chloride. The medium was prepared by dissolving the components in distilled water, adjusting the pH to 7.0 with 1 N NaOH, and autoclaving at 121°C for 15 min. For solid medium, 15 g/L agar was added before autoclaving, and the medium was poured into Petri dishes.

Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions

LB broth supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin was used for culturing E. coli pCOLD‐EGFP. The medium was autoclaved, inoculated and incubated overnight at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm.

L. reuteri, L. brevis and L. helveticus were cultured in de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions (52.2 g/L in distilled water, autoclaved at 121°C for 15 min). MRS agar was prepared similarly with 68.2 g/L of the powder and poured into Petri dishes.

For each experiment, bacterial strains were first streaked from glycerol stocks stored at −80°C onto LB agar plates (E. coli and P. aeruginosa) or MRS agar plates (L. reuteri, L. brevis and L. helveticus) and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. A single colony was then picked from each plate and inoculated into fresh broth medium. E. coli and P. aeruginosa were grown in LB broth at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 18–24 h. L. reuteri, L. brevis and L. helveticus were grown in MRS broth at 37°C under anaerobic conditions for 24 h. The cultures were grown to mid‐log phase, which corresponded to an optical density at 600 nm of approximately 0.6–0.8. Cell density was determined by measuring OD600 and confirmed by serial dilution and plate counting. Planktonic cells were used in all experiments. Cell viability was checked before each experiment by the same plating method.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay for Detecting Aptasensor–Bacteria Interactions

Gram‐negative bacteria (P. aeruginosa, E. coli pCOLD‐EGFP and E. coli ATCC 1943) and Gram‐positive species (L. reuteri) were obtained from the University of Isfahan's microbial bank. To analyse the binding interactions, 100 µL of cultured bacteria (containing 4 × 105 cells) was incubated with aptasensors (0.5–8 µM) and aptamers (0.5–8 µM). Subsequently, the binding reactions were assessed using 12% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), following the protocol established by Hellman and Fried [17]. Briefly, the binding reactions were loaded onto a 12% native polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed in 0.5× TBE buffer at 100 V for approximately 1.5 h at 4°C. Band shifts were analysed with ImageJ software [18]. Each experiment was conducted with at least three repetitions.

Spectroscopy‐Based Detection of Gram‐Negative Bacteria

To confirm that the G‐quadruplex domain retained its catalytic activity after integration with the aptamer sequence, a separate control experiment was performed using the ABTS–H2O2 substrate system in the absence of bacteria. The enzymatic activity of the G‐quadruplex‐based DNAzyme and aptasensor was assessed by their ability to oxidise the substrate ABTS, with absorbance changes measured at 420 nm using a multi‐mode reader (Synergy HTX Biotek, USA) after adding various concentrations of ABTS–H2O2 ranging from 0 to 8 mM. In a separate experiment, 100 µL of diluted Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacterial solutions (containing 4 × 105 cells) were incubated with a blocking solution of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% Tween 20, washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST) and then treated with the aptasensor (20 µM) and ABTS–H2O2 (8 mM), with absorbance changes also recorded at 420 nm. The limit of detection (LOD) for the aptasensor binding to E. coli was determined through serial dilutions of cells (ranging from 2.5 × 105 to 4 × 106 cells) treated with the aptasensor (20 µM) and ABTS–H2O2 (8 mM), calculated using the equation 3.3 × SD (blank)/slope in the linear region of the plot.

Statistical Analysis

Experiments were conducted at least three times to ensure data reliability, with results presented as the mean ± standard deviation and analysed using one‐way ANOVA and t‐tests, considering p < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Structure and the Detection Principle of the Designed LPS‐Aptasensor

In this study, an aptamer targeting LPS [10] was combined with a G‐quadruplex sequence [15] to create a novel biosensor for detecting Gram‐negative bacteria. Structural analysis performed using the Mfold server [19] demonstrated that the secondary structures of these components can fold independently without interfering with each other (Figure 1a).

(a) The secondary structures of the G‐quadruplex motif (green), the aptamer (brown) and the aptasensor, which integrates both domains linked by a TTA linker sequence (blue). (b) The aptasensor binds to Gram‐negative bacteria with LPS, and upon washing and adding an ABTS–H2O2 substrate, its DNAzyme domain catalyses a colour change to green, with intensity at 420 nm correlating to the bacteria's concentration.

The detection method used in this study is illustrated in Figure 1b. The aptasensor binds specifically to LPS present on Gram‐negative bacteria, and then a fresh buffer containing the ABTS–H2O2 substrate is added. The DNAzyme domain of the LPS aptasensor catalyses the conversion of the substrate into a chromogenic product that intensifies for 1–3 min. The intensity of this measurement, recorded at a wavelength of 420 nm, correlates with the concentration of Gram‐negative bacteria.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay for Detecting Aptasensor–LPS Interaction

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was used to evaluate the binding of DNA to Gram‐negative bacteria (Figure 2). The EMSA results demonstrated significant binding between the aptamer and the aptasensor with Gram‐negative bacteria, with no interaction observed with Gram‐positive bacteria. In addition, the negative control DNA showed no shift with either type of bacteria. These findings suggest that the aptasensor specifically binds to Gram‐negative bacteria via its aptamer domain.

Catalytic Assay of G‐Quadruplex‐Based DNAzyme

The catalytic activity of the designed aptasensor was evaluated using the ABTS–H2O2 system (Figure 3a). The aptasensor generated a concentration‐dependent colourimetric response over the investigated substrate range, confirming that the integrated G‐quadruplex domain retained its catalytic function. The findings revealed that the G‐quadruplex‐based DNAzyme significantly increased absorbance (p ≤ 0.01) compared with the negative control, and the aptasensor also exhibited considerable DNAzyme activity (p ≤ 0.05). The G‐quadruplex domain provides the catalytic activity of the aptasensor, whereas the aptamer confers selective recognition of LPS on Gram‐negative bacteria. Consequently, the analytical signal does not arise from activation of the DNAzyme following target binding. Instead, after the washing step, only aptasensors bound to Gram‐negative bacteria remain in the reaction mixture and catalyse ABTS oxidation, while unbound molecules are removed. Thus, the colourimetric response reflects the selective retention of the catalytically active aptasensor through aptamer target recognition.

(a) The catalytic activity of the DNAzyme in response to varying concentrations of ABTS–H2O2 (0–6 µM). (b) The optical density as a function of different concentrations of Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria (0–4 million cells/mL) after treatment with the aptasensor and subsequent absorbance measurement at 420 nm.

Analytical Performance of LPS‐Aptasensor

The specificity of the aptasensor in identifying Gram‐negative bacteria was evaluated using freshly prepared aptasensors. Various concentrations of both Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria were assessed (Figure 3b). The results showed that absorbance at 420 nm increased with the number of Gram‐negative bacteria, with a significant signal observed at 1 × 106 cells and saturation occurring at over 4 × 106 cells (p ≤ 0.05). The aptasensor demonstrated a linear response within the range of 0 to 1 × 106 cells (R 2 = 0.88). In addition, no significant signal was detected for Gram‐positive bacteria, indicating that the observed signals resulted from specific interactions between the aptasensor and Gram‐negative bacteria. Finally, the LOD was 266,934 cells/mL for E. coli and 118,785 cells/mL for P. aeruginosa. The LOD of approximately 105 cells/mL is moderate but reaches the traditional clinical threshold for significant bacteriuria (≥ 105 CFU/mL), suggesting potential utility in some diagnostic settings. However, this sensitivity is not sufficient for food safety monitoring or early clinical diagnosis. Future studies will address this limitation through signal amplification strategies. The successful detection of both E. coli and P. aeruginosa suggests that the aptasensor recognises a conserved Gram‐negative surface component rather than a species‐specific marker. Because LPS is a characteristic constituent of the outer membrane of Gram‐negative bacteria, these findings support the broader applicability of the proposed platform for Gram‐negative bacterial screening.

Previous reports on using aptamers to detect LPS were limited by slow kinetics [20, 21], the complex modifications required for development [20, 21, 22], lack of labelling [23] or not being applied for bacterial detection [20, 21, 22, 24] (Table S1). The combination of G‐quadruplex and anti‐LPS aptamer presents a promising strategy for developing an LPS aptasensor for the spectroscopy‐based detection of Gram‐negative bacteria. Aptamers have attracted considerable interest as recognition elements because of their broad target range, low molecular weight, ease of synthesis and low immunogenicity, and are often described as chemical antibodies [25]. In the present study, the aptamer retained its recognition capability after integration with the G‐quadruplex sequence, while the G‐quadruplex domain remained catalytically active. Importantly, the analytical signal does not result from activation of the DNAzyme following aptamer–LPS binding. Rather, aptamer recognition enables selective retention of the catalytically active aptasensor after the washing step, whereas unbound molecules are removed. Similar aptamer–DNAzyme architectures have also been explored in other analytical applications, including cancer biomarker and circulating tumour cell detection, demonstrating the versatility of this design strategy [26, 27]. Common spectrophotometric methods minimise operator‐induced errors. The aptasensor is also affordable, as it does not require complex modifications or labelling. Despite the advantages of this new LPS aptasensor, certain limitations must also be considered in its application. The biosensor is restricted to the detection of Gram‐negative bacteria and does not identify Gram‐positive bacteria. Significantly, an aptamer that binds to peptidoglycan has been reported [28]. Attaching the aptamer to the G‐quadruplex sequence, which complements the LPS aptasensor, may address some of the challenges associated with existing aptasensors. However, the sensor demonstrated selectivity towards Gram‐negative bacteria, only a limited number of strains were tested. Potential interference from mixed microbial populations, free LPS or complex sample matrices such as serum or food extracts requires further investigation. Nonetheless, aptamer‐based detection remains an effective method for laboratory analysis of labelled Gram‐negative bacteria using spectroscopy.

Conclusions

A spectroscopy‐based, label‐free aptasensor was developed by integrating an anti‐LPS aptamer with a G‐quadruplex DNAzyme and evaluating its performance through binding, catalytic and bacterial detection assays. The results demonstrated that the aptasensor retained both target‐recognition and catalytic functions, enabling selective detection of Gram‐negative bacteria with a suitable detection limit and a linear relationship between signal intensity and bacterial concentration.

The aptasensor combines structural simplicity, low cost, straightforward preparation and ease of operation within a single construct. Compared with many reported LPS detection approaches, the proposed platform does not require labelling or extensive modification steps and therefore provides a simple strategy for Gram‐negative bacterial detection.

The successful detection of both E. coli and P. aeruginosa suggests that the approach may be applicable to a broader range of Gram‐negative bacteria. Although the current study focused on spectrophotometric measurements, the visible colour change observed during the assay indicates that future studies may explore visual or portable readout formats for field and point‐of‐use applications.

Furthermore, the modular design of the aptasensor provides opportunities for incorporating alternative recognition sequences and extending the approach to other analytical targets. Similar aptamer–DNAzyme systems have already been investigated for applications beyond bacterial detection, including cancer biomarkers and circulating tumour cells [26, 27]. Therefore, the present work may provide a foundation for the future development of practical biosensing platforms and may complement existing endotoxin detection approaches. The present study was conducted as a proof of concept for the aptasensor. Further validation through additional controls and complementary assays will be addressed in future work.
