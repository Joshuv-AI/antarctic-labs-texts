Big Algebra in Type 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} for the Coordinate Ring of the Matrix Space
Nhok Tkhai Shon NGO

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the big algebra recently introduced by Hausel for the GL n \mathrm{GL}_{n} -action on the coordinate ring of the matrix space Mat ⁡ ( n , r ) \mathrm{Mat}(n,r) . In particular, we obtain explicit formulas for the big algebra generators in terms of differential operators with polynomial coefficients. We show that big algebras in type A A are commutative and relate them to the Bethe subalgebra in the Yangian Y ⁡ ( 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 n ) \operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}) . We apply these results to big algebras of symmetric powers of the standard representation of GL n \mathrm{GL}_{n} .

Introduction

Recently, Hausel introduced in
[
11
]
the notion of the
big algebra
associated to an irreducible representation
V(\lambda)
of a complex semi-simple Lie group
G
. In fact, the corresponding big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)
can be seen as a certain maximal commutative subalgebra of the algebra of all
G
-equivariant polynomial maps from
\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{Lie}(G)
to
\operatorname{End}V(\lambda)
.

From the geometric point of view, big algebras were motivated by the study of the geometry of affine Schubert varieties. Namely, big algebras are isomorphic to the equivariant intersection cohomology of affine Schubert varieties. In particular, this fact can be used to define a ring structure on equivariant intersection cohomology for such varieties, which is not possible in general.
From the algebraic side, big algebras resemble objects that are well-studied in mathematical physics and representation theory. For example, Gaudin spin chain models and Mischenko–Fomenko integrable systems, see
[
33
]
. Moreover, big algebras give a novel approach to representations of complex semi-simple Lie groups. For instance, Hausel in
[
11
]
suggests how one can read off from the spectrum of the big algebra a lot of representation-theoretic data, such as the weight diagram, the crystal structure and Lusztig’s
q
-weight multiplicity polynomial.

In this paper, we work with big algebras from a purely algebraic point of view. We study the big algebras associated to polynomial representations of the general linear group
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
.
We obtain certain explicit formulas for the generators of the big algebra in this case. Using these, we give an independent proof of the commutativity of big algebras by relating them to
Bethe subalgebras
of the
Yangian

\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
. As an application of our results, we use them to study the big algebras associated to symmetric powers of the vector representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. Before we state our results more precisely, we first review the definition of big algebras.

1.1
Big algebras

Let
G
be a connected complex reductive group with Lie algebra
\mathrm{Lie}(G)=\mathfrak{g}
(note that in
[
11
]

\mathfrak{g}
is semi-simple, but most constructions can be extended to the reductive case as well). Fix a triangular decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}
, where
\mathfrak{h}
is a Cartan subalgebra and
\mathfrak{n}_{\pm}
are nilpotent subalgebras spanned by positive and negative root spaces. In particular, we fix a choice of a set of positive roots of
\mathfrak{g}
and this defines the set of dominant weights.
For any dominant integral weight
\lambda
, let
V(\lambda)
be the irreducible representation of the highest weight
\lambda
and denote by
{\pi_{\lambda}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{End}V(\lambda)}
be the corresponding Lie algebra homomorphism.

Although in
[
11
]
big algebras are defined only for irreducible representations, it will be useful for us to define them in a more general setting.
Let
\pi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{End}V
be a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
which is isomorphic to a direct sum of finite-dimensional representations. Denote by
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
the symmetric algebra of
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, which we identify with the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]
of polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{g}
. Then, the
Kirillov algebra

[
21
]
of the representation
(\pi,V)
is defined

Note that Kirillov in
[
21
]
studied the algebra
(S(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\operatorname{End}V)^{G}
which can be identified with
\mathscr{C}(V)
since
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{*}
as
\mathfrak{g}
-modules. We decided to use
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
instead of
S(\mathfrak{g})
because the former is more natural for geometric applications. For instance, for the
G

H_{G}(\mathrm{pt})\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}\simeq S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{G}
.
as
\mathscr{C}(V)=(S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V)^{G}
(here we use the adjoint action of

on

). Alternatively, we can view the elements of the Kirillov algebras as polynomial maps
F\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{End}V
which satisfy the following equivariance condition:

F(\operatorname{Ad}(g)(X))=\pi(g)F(X)\pi(g)^{-1},\qquad X\in\mathfrak{g},\ g\in G.

Note that
\mathscr{C}(V)
is an
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{G}
-algebra since
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{G}
embeds into
\mathscr{C}(V)
as the subalgebra of scalar operators, i.e., operators of the form
P\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{V}
with
P\in S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{G}
.

In general, Kirillov algebras need not be commutative.
However, one can construct a large commutative subalgebra inside
\mathscr{C}(V)
and this is the
big algebra

\mathscr{B}(V)
which we define next. The subalgebra
\mathscr{B}(V)
can be defined by means of a certain “differential-like” operator
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{V}
acting on
\mathscr{C}(V)
which we call the
Kirillov–Wei operator

[
21
, Section 1.2]
and
[
36
, equations (42) and (57)]
.

Fix a non-degenerate invariant symmetric bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
(e.g., the Killing form in the case of simple
\mathfrak{g}
) and let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\dim\mathfrak{g}}
and
\{X^{i}\}_{i=1}^{\dim\mathfrak{g}}
be two bases of
\mathfrak{g}
dual with respect to this form. Then, for any
F\in\mathscr{C}(V)
define

(\mathbf{D}F)(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathfrak{g}}\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{i}}(X)\cdot\pi\bigl(X^{i}\bigr),

where
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{i}}
denotes the directional derivative of
F\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{End}V
along
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}
. It turns out that in types
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
and
G
there exist homogeneous generators
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
of the ring
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{G}
such that the subalgebra generated by elements
\mathbf{D}^{p}(c_{q})
, is commutative (see
[
11
, Section 2.1]
and Definition
3.7
for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
).
The resulting commutative subalgebra of
\mathscr{C}(V)
is called the
big algebra
of
V
and is denoted by
\mathscr{B}(V)
. Namely, we set

\mathscr{B}(V)=\langle\mathbf{D}^{p}(c_{q})\mid 1\leq q\leq r,\,0\leq p\leq\deg(c_{q})\rangle\subset\mathscr{C}(V).

For any dominant integral weight
\lambda
, we define
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mathscr{B}(V(\lambda))
and
\mathscr{C}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mathscr{C}(V(\lambda))
.

More generally, big algebras can be defined for any simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
in a different way,
using the notion of the
Feigin–Frenkel center
(see
[
11
, Section 2]
and also
[
33
, Section 4]
). Let
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}
be the affine Kac–Moody algebra associated to
\mathfrak{g}
. As a Lie algebra,
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}
is a central extension of the loop algebra
\mathfrak{g}\bigl[t,t^{-1}\bigr]
. Then, the Feigin–Frenkel center
\mathfrak{z}(\hat{\mathfrak{g}})
can be regarded as a certain commutative subalgebra of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{-})
, where
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{-}=t^{-1}\mathfrak{g}\bigl[t^{-1}\bigr]
is the negative part of
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}
. The big algebra
\mathscr{B}(V)
now can be defined as the image of
\mathfrak{z}(\hat{\mathfrak{g}})
under certain homomorphism
\varrho_{z}\colon U(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{-})\to S(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\operatorname{End}V\simeq S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V
which depends on a non-zero complex parameter
z
. The homomorphism
\varrho_{z}
acts on
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{-}
as follows:

\varrho_{z}(x\otimes t^{-k})=z^{-k}\cdot 1\otimes\pi(x)+\delta_{k,1}\cdot x\otimes 1,\qquad x\in\mathfrak{g},\ k=1,2,\dots\,.

One can show that the image
\varrho_{z}(\mathfrak{z}(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}))
does not depend on the choice of
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, see
[
33
, Section 4, Corollary 3]
.
This approach, however, does not give a simple way to compute big algebras since the formulas for the generators of
\mathfrak{z}(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{-})
are rather complicated (see
[
27
, Chapters 7 and 8]
). Besides that, the commutativity of big algebras then relies on deep results of Feigin and Frenkel
[
5
]
on
\mathfrak{z}(\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{-})
. One of the motivations for our work was to obtain a more elementary proof of the commutativity of big algebras (in type
A
) using the definition involving the Kirillov–Wei operator.

1.2
Main results

In this paper, we consider big algebras for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
. Note that Hausel considered big algebras only for semi-simple Lie algebras, but the construction can be applied in our case as well. We fix a positive integer
r
and consider the coordinate ring
\mathcal{P}(n,r)=\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]
of the affine space
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
of complex
n\times r
matrices.
The natural action of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}\times\mathrm{GL}_{r}
on
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
endows
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
with the structure of
(\mathrm{GL}_{n}\times\mathrm{GL}_{r})
-module.
Moreover, one can explicitly describe the decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
into the direct sum of irreducible
(\mathrm{GL}_{n}\times\mathrm{GL}_{r})
-representations. Namely, by the
Howe duality

[
15
, Section 2.1.2]
, we have

\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]=\mathcal{P}(n,r)\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda}V_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}(\lambda)\otimes V_{\mathrm{GL}_{r}}(\lambda),

where
\lambda
runs over all partitions with length
\ell(\lambda)\leq\min\{r,n\}
, where
\lambda
is regarded as a dominant weight for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and
\mathrm{GL}_{r}
, respectively. Note that if
r=n
, then
\mathcal{P}(n,r)=\mathcal{P}(n,n)
contains all polynomial irreducible representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
.

In particular, we can regard
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
as a
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-module and can consider the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
. It is not difficult to verify that the elements of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
can be viewed as certain polynomial differential operators on
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
.

We find explicit formulas for the generators of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
using direct calculations and the Kirillov–Wei operator
\mathbf{D}
(Theorem
3.12
and Corollary
3.14
). Using these formulas and the Capelli identities, we identify the generators of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
with homomorphic images of some elements of the Bethe subalgebra of the Yangian
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
. As a consequence, we obtain that the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
is commutative (Theorem
7.1
). It follows from the big algebra construction that for any
V(\lambda)
appearing in the decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
there is a surjective algebra homomorphism
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))\twoheadrightarrow\mathscr{B}(\lambda)
. Therefore, the discussion above implies the following fact.

Theorem 1.1

(Corollary
7.2
)
.

The big algebra of any polynomial finite-dimensional irreducible representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
is commutative.

The approach outlined above has an advantage of considering big algebras of all polynomial representations of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
simultaneously. Besides that, our proof of commutativity relies only on direct calculations and thus, avoids the use of more complicated constructions such as the Feigin–Frenkel center.

Using the formulas for generators of big algebra, we can give a more explicit description of the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
for any positive integer

(Proposition
8.8
and Theorem

\varpi_{1}
is the first fundamental weight of

is isomorphic to the

1.3
Related works and generalizations

The algebras of the form
\mathscr{C}(V)=(S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V)^{G}
and
\mathscr{Q}(V)=(U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\operatorname{End}V)^{G}
were called
classical and quantum family algebras
by Kirillov in
[
21
,
22
]
. However, these objects were also considered before in earlier works, in particular, by Kostant
[
23
,
24
]
, Bracken and Green
[
2
,
8
]
, Gould
[
6
]
and others. For instance, Kostant in
[
24
]
uses the name
strongly commuting ring
for algebras
\mathscr{Q}(V)
. Several papers including
[
2
,
6
,
8
]
are devoted to the study of
characteristic identities
for special elements in
\mathscr{Q}(V)
. In view of this, a more recent work by Hausel
[
12
]
adopts the term
Kostant algebra
for
\mathscr{Q}(V)
.

We believe that our approach to proving the commutativity of big algebras in type
A
can be extended to other classical types. In types
B
,
C
and
D
, one also have many polynomial representations and one might use the properties of the evaluation homomorphism and Bethe subalgebras for twisted Yangians (see
[
26
]
and
[
29
]
).

Using the work of
[
33
]
, one can construct in a similar way a large commutative subalgebra
\mathscr{G}(V)
in
\mathscr{Q}(V)=(U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\operatorname{End}V)^{G}
(see
[
11
, Section 2]
). A more explicit construction of this algebra involves a variant of the Kirillov–Wei operator
\mathbf{D}
on
\mathscr{Q}(V)
. We speculate that a direct proof of the commutativity of
\mathscr{G}(V)
in this case would require some generalizations of Capelli identities, e.g., from
[
28
]
. Note that a part of the algebra
\mathscr{G}(V)
can be described very explicitly, see
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
. Namely, the algebra
\mathscr{G}(V)
contains the center of
\mathscr{Q}(V)
and in
[
12
]
the author obtains a description of the center of
\mathscr{Q}(V(\lambda))
as a certain quotient of
Z(\mathfrak{g})\otimes Z(\mathfrak{g})
, where
Z(\mathfrak{g})
is the center of
U(\mathfrak{g})
. Similarly to
\mathscr{B}(V)
, the algebra
\mathscr{G}(V)
also has a geometric interpretation via the affine Schubert varieties (see
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Finally, let us mention that certain analogues of algebras
\mathscr{C}(V)
and
\mathscr{Q}(V)
and characteristic identities for their elements were also studied for quantum groups
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
(e.g., in
[
7
,
9
,
40
]
). Besides that, there exists a version of Capelli identities for
U_{q}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
where the algebra of differential operators is replaced by the so-called
reflection equation algebra
(see
[
10
,
19
,
39
]
for more details). It seems plausible that some of our constructions can be generalized to these settings as well.

Notation and preliminaries

Most of the proofs in this paper involve many direct calculations. To simplify the formulas, we introduce the following notation.

2.1
Operations with tuples

For every positive integer
m
, we denote
[m]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,m\}
and let
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
be the symmetric group of
[m]
. For any integer
k
such that
0\leq k\leq m
, we define
\binom{[m]}{k}
to be the set of all
k
-element subsets of
[m]
and
[m]^{\underline{k}}
to be the set of all
k
-tuples which consist of
k
distinct elements of
m
. Clearly,

\#\binom{[m]}{k}=\binom{m}{k},\qquad\#[m]^{\underline{k}}=m^{\underline{k}},

where
m^{\underline{k}}
is the so-called
falling factorial
,

m^{\underline{k}}=m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)=k!\cdot\binom{m}{k}.

We also denote by
[m]^{k}=[m]^{[k]}
the set of all
k
-tuples with entries in
[m]
. It is occasionally convenient to view a
k
-tuple
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
as a function on the set
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
, namely, we set
I(l)=i_{l}
for
l\in[k]
.

2.1.1
Action of the symmetric group

For any
k
-tuple
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and any permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, define

\pi I=\bigl(i_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,i_{\pi^{-1}(k)}\bigr).

(2.1)

Regarding
I
as a function on
[k]
, we can alternatively write
\pi I=I\circ\pi^{-1}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Using this group action, we can identify
\binom{[m]}{k}
with quotient
\mathfrak{S}_{k}\backslash[m]^{\underline{k}}
. In particular, we will often regard a
k
-element subset as a
k
-tuple with arbitrarily chosen ordering (and in these situations, the choice of ordering will be irrelevant).

2.1.2
Sign functions for tuples

For any
I,J\in[n]^{\underline{k}}
, define the generalized sign function as follows:

\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I}{J}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)&\text{if as sets}~I=J,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where in the first case
\tau
is the unique permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
that maps the
k
-tuple
J
to
I
. In other words,
\tau
satisfies
I=\tau\cdot J
.

Remark 2.2
.

Clearly, this agrees with the usual sign of permutation in the case when both
I
and
J
are permutations of
(1,2,\dots,n)
. For instance, if
I=(1,\dots,n)
and
J=(\tau(1),\dots,\tau(n))
for
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, then

\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I}{J}=\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\cdots&n\\
\tau(1)&\tau(2)&\cdots&\tau(n)\end{pmatrix}=\operatorname{sgn}(\tau).

Now assume that we have tuples
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
and
J_{1},\dots,J_{k}
such that
I_{l},J_{l}\in[n]^{\underline{p_{l}}}
for each
l=1,\dots,k
and some positive integers
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
. We assume additionally that
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
are disjoint as sets and similarly for
J_{1},\dots,J_{k}
. Denote also
p=p_{1}+\dots+p_{k}
. Then, we define

\operatorname{sgn}\begin{pmatrix}I_{1}&\cdots&I_{k}\\
J_{1}&\cdots&J_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I}{J},

where
I
and
J
are
p
-tuples obtained by concatenating
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
and
J_{1},\dots,J_{k}
, respectively. For example, if
k=2
, then

\displaystyle I(s)=\begin{cases}I_{1}(s),&s\in\{1,\dots,p_{1}\},\\
I_{2}(s-p_{1}),&s\in\{p_{1}+1,\dots,p_{1}+p_{2}\},\end{cases}

\displaystyle J(s)=\begin{cases}J_{1}(s),&s\in\{1,\dots,p_{1}\},\\
J_{2}(s-p_{1}),&s\in\{p_{1}+1,\dots,p_{1}+p_{2}\}.\end{cases}

In our calculations we also use another variant of the signature function. For any tuples
I_{1},J_{1}\in[m]^{\underline{p}}
and
I_{2},J_{2}\in[m]^{\underline{q}}
with
p\geq q
, define

\varepsilon(I_{1},J_{1},I_{2},J_{2})=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{1}\tau_{2})&\text{if as sets}~I_{1}\setminus I_{2}=J_{1}\setminus J_{2}\in\binom{[m]}{p-q},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here, in the first case,
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are elements of
\mathfrak{S}_{p}
that satisfy the following equalities:

\tau_{1}I_{1}|_{[q]}=I_{2},\qquad\tau_{2}J_{1}|_{[q]}=J_{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau_{1}I_{1}|_{\{p+1,\dots,q\}}=\tau_{2}J_{1}|_{\{p+1,\dots,q\}}.

Note that a pair
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
is not defined uniquely in general. However, for any other such pair
(\tau_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{2}^{\prime})
, there exists an element
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{p}
which fixes each element of
[q]
and such that
(\tau_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{2}^{\prime})=(\sigma\tau_{1},\sigma\tau_{2})
. In particular,
\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{1}\tau_{2})=\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{1}^{\prime}\tau_{2}^{\prime})
and, consequently,
\varepsilon(I_{1},J_{1},I_{2},J_{2})
is well defined.

Observe that both generalized sign functions are skew-symmetric in the sense of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any tuples
I,J\in[m]^{\underline{p}}
and any permutations
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{p}
, one has

\operatorname{sgn}\binom{\sigma I}{\tau J}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I}{J}.

Similarly, for any
I_{1},J_{1}\in[m]^{\underline{p}}
,
I_{2},J_{2}\in[m]^{\underline{q}}
with
p\geq q
and any permutations
\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{p}
,
\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{q}
, one has

\varepsilon(\sigma_{1}I_{1},\tau_{1}J_{1},\sigma_{2}I_{2},\tau_{2}J_{2})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{1}\tau_{1})\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{2}\tau_{2})\cdot\varepsilon(I_{1},J_{1},I_{2},J_{2}).

2.2
Non-commutative matrices

We often work with matrices whose entries are elements of non-commutative (associative) algebras. In particular, many computations involve non-commutative versions of determinants.

Let
A=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{N}
be an
N\times N
matrix with entries in a certain non-commutative algebra. We define for matrix
A
its

•

row determinant
:

\operatorname{rdet}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)a_{1,\sigma(1)}a_{2,\sigma(2)}\cdots a_{N,\sigma(N)},

•

column determinant
:

\operatorname{cdet}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1),1}a_{\sigma(2),2}\cdots a_{\sigma(N),N},

•

symmetrized determinant
:

\operatorname{symdet}(A)=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)a_{\sigma(1),\tau(1)}a_{\sigma(2),\tau(2)}\cdots a_{\sigma(N),\tau(N)}.

Observe that if the entries of
A
do commute, then the row and the column determinants coincide with the usual one while for the symmetrized version one has
\operatorname{symdet}(A)=N!\cdot\det(A)
. Finally, note that the row (column) determinant is skew-symmetric with respect to columns (rows) while the symmetrized determinant is skew-symmetric with respect to both rows and columns.

For any
N\times N
matrix
M
and any tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in[N]^{k}
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{l})\in[N]^{l}
, we denote by
M_{IJ}
the following
k\times l
matrix:

M_{IJ}=[M_{i_{\alpha},j_{\beta}}]_{\alpha\in[k],\beta\in[l]}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{i_{1}j_{1}}&\cdots&M_{i_{1}j_{l}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{i_{k}j_{1}}&\cdots&M_{i_{k}j_{l}}\end{bmatrix}.

In the case, when the entries of
I
and
J
are strictly increasing,
M_{IJ}
is a submatrix of
M
.

2.3
The Lie algebra
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
be the general linear Lie algebra of complex
n\times n
matrices. We denote by
\{E_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
the standard basis of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
consisting of matrix units. They satisfy the following commutation relations:

[E_{ij},E_{kl}]=\delta_{jk}E_{il}-\delta_{li}E_{kj}.

Denote also by
\{y_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
the corresponding coordinates on
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
.
In particular, we can view the algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{gl}_{n}]=S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})
as the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[y_{ij},1\leq i,j\leq n]
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}=\operatorname{span}\{E_{ii}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
be the Cartan subalgebra consisting of diagonal matrices. Let
\mathfrak{n}_{+}=\operatorname{span}\{E_{ij}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
and
\mathfrak{n}_{-}=\operatorname{span}\{E_{ij}\}_{1\leq j<i\leq n}
be the nilpotent subalgebras consisting of upper-triangular and lower-triangular matrices, respectively. Thus, we obtain a triangular decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}
.

2.4
The coordinate ring of the matrix space

Consider the coordinate ring
\mathcal{P}(n,r)=\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]
of the space
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
of complex
n\times r
matrices. Denote by
\{x_{ij}\mid 1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq j\leq r\}
the standard coordinates on
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
. Then,
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
is the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{ij},\,1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq j\leq r]
in
rn
variables. We denote by
\partial_{ij}
the partial derivative with respect to the variable
x_{ij}
.

2.4.1
The action of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}

The matrix space
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
possesses the following
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-action:

(g,A)\mapsto\bigl(g^{-1}\bigr)^{t}\cdot A,\qquad g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n},\ A\in\operatorname{Mat}(n,r).

This action induces a
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-action on the coordinate ring
\mathcal{P}(n,r)=\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]
.
Denote by
\widetilde{L}(g)
the corresponding linear operator in
\operatorname{End}\mathcal{P}(n,r)
.
Differentiating this action along one-parameter subgroups in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
yields the infinitesimal
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-action on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
, which we denote by
L
. A direct calculation shows that

L(E_{ij})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}x_{i\alpha}\partial_{j\alpha},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq n.

(2.2)

2.4.2
The algebra
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{PD}(n,r)}
of differential operators on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}(n,r)}

Since
L
is a representation of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
it also gives rise to a representation of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
. In particular, the formula for
L(E_{ij})
above implies that the elements of
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
act on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
as differential operators with polynomial coefficients.

Let
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
be the (non-commutative) algebra of differential operators on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
with polynomial coefficients. In other words,
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
is the Weyl algebra generated by
\{x_{i\alpha},\partial_{i\alpha}\mid 1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq\alpha\leq r\}
and relations of the form

[\partial_{i\alpha},x_{j\beta}]=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq n,\ 1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq r.

We will need the following well-known fact in Section
5
.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
r=n
, then the map
L\colon U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\to\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
is injective.

Remark 2.5
.

In fact, this follows from the observation that
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
is isomorphic to the algebra of all left-invariant differential operators on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. In general, the image of
L
coincides with the subalgebra of all differential operators in
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
which commute with the
\mathrm{GL}_{r}
-action on
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]
. See also
[
16
, Section 1]
.

Denote by
E
,
X
and
D
the matrices, whose
(i,j)
-th entry equals
E_{ij}
,
x_{ij}
and
\partial_{ij}
, respectively.
In particular, we have a formal identity
L(E)=X\cdot D^{t}
.

3
Kirillov algebra, big algebra and medium algebra on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}(n,r)}

3.1
Invariant polynomials on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}

Define the elements
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
as the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
Y
:

\det(Y-z\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}c_{k}(Y)\cdot z^{n-k}.

Denote by
y_{ij}
the coordinates on
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
which correspond to standard matrix units
E_{ij}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
. Then,
Y=[y_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
and

c_{k}(Y)=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\det Y_{II}.

(3.1)

It is well known that the elements
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
are free generators of the ring
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-invariants of
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})
.

3.2
Construction of the Kirillov algebra

Let
\widetilde{\pi}\colon\mathrm{GL}_{n}\to V
be a finite-dimensional representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and let
\pi\colon\mathfrak{gl}_{n}\to\operatorname{End}V
be the associated representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
Kirillov algebra
4
4

4

Also known as the
classical family algebra
, see Kirillov’s original papers
[
21
,
22
]
.
of
V

is defined as the algebra

\mathscr{C}(V)=(S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V)^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}.

In other words, the Kirillov algebra is the algebra of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-equivariant polynomial maps from
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
to
\operatorname{End}V
, i.e., any
F\in\mathscr{C}(V)
satisfies the following equivariance condition:

F(\operatorname{Ad}(g)(Y))=\widetilde{\pi}(g)F(Y)\widetilde{\pi}(g)^{-1},\qquad g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n},\ Y\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n},

(3.2)

Note that
\mathscr{C}(V)
is an algebra over the ring
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
. The elements of
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
are realized inside
\mathscr{C}(V)
as scalar operators.

Remark 3.2
.

If
V=\bigoplus_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}
is an infinite direct sum of finite-dimensional representations
V_{\alpha}
, then we replace the algebra
\operatorname{End}V
in the definition
\mathscr{C}(V)
with the direct sum
\bigoplus_{\alpha,\beta}V_{\alpha}^{*}\otimes V_{\beta}
.

Define the
Kirillov–Wei operator

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{V}
which acts on
\mathscr{C}(V)
as follows: for any
F\in\mathscr{C}(V)
, set

(\mathbf{D}F)(Y)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{ji}}(Y)\cdot\pi(E_{ij}).

(3.3)

It follows from the definition that for any positive integer
p
, we have

(\mathbf{D}^{p}F)(Y)=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}=1}^{n}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{p}=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{p}F}{\partial y_{j_{1}i_{1}}\cdots\partial y_{j_{p}i_{p}}}(Y)\cdot\pi(E_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots E_{i_{p}j_{p}}).

In
[
21
, Lemma 1]
and
[
22
, Section 1.4]
, Kirillov hinted at the following fact.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
36
, Proposition 5.2]
)
.

The operator
\mathbf{D}
maps
\mathscr{C}(V)
to itself.

Proof.

This is shown via a direct verification of (
3.2
) for
F=\mathbf{D}G
,
G\in\mathscr{C}(V)
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

One can generalize the construction of the Kirillov algebra, the operator
\mathbf{D}
and the results of this subsection for any semi-simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. The corresponding operator
\mathbf{D}
can be described using a non-degenerate invariant symmetric bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
(e.g., the Killing form in the case of simple
\mathfrak{g}
).

The explicit formulas for specific elements of
\mathscr{C}(V)
are often quite complicated. However, these formulas can be simplified if one restricts elements of Kirillov algebra to Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
. Moreover, these restrictions uniquely determine the elements of
\mathscr{C}(V)
.

Proposition 3.5

(
[
22
, Section 1.2]
and
[
31
, Section 2]
)
.

The restriction map
\Phi=\Phi_{V}\colon F\mapsto F|_{\mathfrak{h}}
,
F\in\mathscr{C}(V)
is an injective homomorphism of algebras over
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}\simeq S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}
. Moreover, the image of
\Phi
is contained inside the algebra

(S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}V)^{W}=\biggl(\bigoplus_{\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(V)}S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V_{\mu}\biggr)^{W}.

Here
W\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denotes the Weyl group of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
,
\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}V
is the space of all
\mathfrak{h}
-equivariant linear operators on
V
, and
\operatorname{wt}(V)
is the set of weights of
V
.

Proof.

We use the argument outlined in
[
21
, Theorem 2]
. It follows from the equality (
3.2
) that the restriction
F|_{\mathfrak{h}}
completely determines the map
F\colon\mathfrak{gl}_{n}\to\operatorname{End}V
on the set of all regular semi-simple elements of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
(those are conjugate to elements of
\mathfrak{h}
). Since the latter set is Zariski dense in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, the injectivity of
\Phi
follows.

The second statement follows from the fact that the Weyl group
W
is the normalizer of
\mathfrak{h}
in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and the equivariance condition (
3.2
) applied for
g\in W
.
∎

The following statement shows that in order to study the Kirillov algebra of
V
one can study the Kirillov algebra of a “larger” representation.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
V=\bigoplus_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}
be a direct sum of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-modules. Then, the natural algebra homomorphism
\prod_{\alpha}\mathscr{C}(V_{\alpha})\to\mathscr{C}(V)
induced by the embeddings
\iota_{\alpha}\colon\operatorname{End}(V_{\alpha})\hookrightarrow\operatorname{End}(V)
is injective.

Proof.

The map in question sends a family of maps
F_{\alpha}\colon\mathfrak{gl}_{n}\to\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha}
to the map
\sum_{\alpha}\iota_{\alpha}\circ F_{\alpha}\colon\mathfrak{gl}_{n}\to\mathscr{C}(V)
. Note that this expression is well defined since for every
Y\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
the summand
(\iota_{\alpha}\circ F_{\alpha})(Y)
belongs to
\iota_{\alpha}(\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha})
. In particular, this map is indeed an algebra homomorphism. The injectivity now follows from the injectivity of the maps
\iota_{\alpha}
.
∎

3.3
Big and medium algebras

First we recall the definitions of the big and the medium algebras in type
A
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
V
be a direct sum of finite-dimensional
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-modules. The
big algebra

\mathscr{B}(V)
is the subalgebra of
\mathscr{C}(V)
generated by the elements

M_{p,q}=\mathbf{D}^{q}(c_{p+q}),\qquad 0\leq p,q\leq n,\ p+q\leq n.

(3.4)

The
medium algebra

\mathscr{M}(V)
of
V
is a subalgebra of
\mathscr{B}(V)
defined as follows:

\mathscr{M}(V)=\bigl\langle F,\mathbf{D}(F)\mid F\in S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}\bigr\rangle\subset\mathscr{B}(V).

(3.5)

Remark 3.8
.

For a direct sum
V=\bigoplus_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}
of finite-dimensional representation the elements of
\mathscr{B}(V)
and
\mathscr{M}(V)
belong to
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\otimes\bigl(\bigoplus_{\alpha}\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha}\bigr)
(this follows from the definition of
\mathbf{D}
, see (
3.3
)). Therefore, the algebra structure on
\mathscr{B}(V)
and
\mathscr{M}(V)
is well defined even if
V
is infinite-dimensional.

Remark 3.9
.

A similar approach for defining big algebras can be used for complex semi-simple Lie groups of types
B
,
C
,
D
and
G
, see
[
11
, Sections 1 and 2]
.

Now let us give a more explicit formula for the generators of
\mathscr{B}(V)
. Recall that
\pi\colon\mathfrak{gl}_{n}\to\operatorname{End}V
is the representation of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
which comes from the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-action on
V
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For any
Y\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, we have

M_{p,q}(Y)=\mathbf{D}^{q}(c_{p+q})(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},J_{1}\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\
I_{2},J_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{q}\\
I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}=J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I_{1}~I_{2}}{J_{1}~J_{2}}\det Y_{I_{1}J_{1}}\cdot\operatorname{symdet}\pi(E)_{J_{2}I_{2}}.

Here
\pi(E)
stands for the
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry equals
\pi(E_{ij})
.

Proof.

This follows by a direct calculation using (
3.3
) and (
3.1
).
∎

The next proposition relates the big (resp. medium) algebras of direct sums with big (resp. medium) algebras of summands.

Proposition 3.11
.

(i)

The image of the algebra homomorphism
\prod_{\alpha}\mathscr{C}(V_{\alpha})\to\mathscr{C}(V)
from Proposition
3.6
contains the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(V)
.

(ii)

For each
\alpha
, there exist surjective homomorphisms
\mathscr{B}(V)\to\mathscr{B}(V_{\alpha})
and
\mathscr{M}(V)\to\mathscr{M}(V_{\alpha})
. These homomorphisms are induced by the natural algebra homomorphisms
\prod_{\alpha^{\prime}}\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha^{\prime}}\to\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The definition of the Kirillov–Wei operator implies the subalgebra
\prod_{\alpha}\mathscr{C}(V_{\alpha})
of
\mathscr{C}(V)
is invariant under the action of
\mathbf{D}_{V}
. Moreover,
\mathbf{D}_{V}
acts on
\prod_{\alpha}\mathscr{C}(V_{\alpha})
in the same way as the operator
\prod_{\alpha}\mathbf{D}_{V_{\alpha}}
. The first part now follows from the fact that
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
are realized inside
\mathscr{C}(V)
as scalar operators and the identity
\mathbf{D}^{p}_{V}(c_{k}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{V})=\prod_{\alpha}\mathbf{D}^{p}_{V_{\alpha}}(c_{k}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{V_{\alpha}})
for all
p\geq 1
. The required homomorphisms
\mathscr{B}(V)\to\mathscr{B}(V_{\alpha})
and
\mathscr{M}(V)\to\mathscr{M}(V_{\alpha})
are induced by the projection homomorphisms
\prod_{\alpha^{\prime}}\mathscr{C}(V_{\alpha^{\prime}})\to\mathscr{C}(V_{\alpha})
.
∎

3.4
Kirillov algebra for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}(n,r)}

There are natural actions of the groups
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and
\mathrm{GL}_{r}
on the matrix space
\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
that induce actions of these groups on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
.
Namely, for any
A\in\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)
,
g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and
h\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}
, we have

\widetilde{L}(g)(P)(A)=P\bigl(g^{t}\cdot A\bigr),\qquad\widetilde{R}(h)(P)(A)=P(A\cdot h).

Note that these two actions commute and give rise to an action of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}\times\mathrm{GL}_{r}
on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
.
Then,
Howe duality
(see
[
15
, Section 2.1.2]
) implies that the
(\mathrm{GL}_{n}\times\mathrm{GL}_{r})
-module
\mathcal{P}(n,r)=\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]
decomposes as follows:

\mathcal{P}(n,r)\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\colon\ell(\lambda)\leq r}V_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}(\lambda)\otimes V_{\mathrm{GL}_{r}}(\lambda),

(3.6)

where the summation is over all partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
of length at most
r
(i.e., with
{\lambda_{i}=0}
for
i>r
). Here
V_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}(\lambda)
and
V_{\mathrm{GL}_{r}}(\lambda)
denote the irreducible representations of highest weight
\lambda
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
and
\mathrm{GL}_{r}
, respectively.
In particular, we conclude that
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
contains as subrepresentations all irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
that have highest weight
\lambda
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq r
.

3.5
Generators of the big algebra of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}(n,r)}

From now on, we consider the case
V=\mathcal{P}(n,r)
and the corresponding Kirillov and big algebras. Proposition
3.11
implies that many properties of big algebras of irreducible representations can be read off from
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
.

Observe that the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
is contained in
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
. In other words, the elements of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
are certain differential operators on
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
whose coefficients are polynomials in variables
x_{i\alpha}
and
y_{jk}
. One of the main results of this paper is an explicit formula (the so-called
normal form
) for the operators
M_{p,q}
.

Theorem 3.12
.

The normal form of the operator
M_{p,q}(Y)
is as follows:

\displaystyle M_{p,q}(Y)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{q}(-1)^{q-\ell}(q-\ell)!\,\ell!\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{q}{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},J_{1}\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\
I_{2},J_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{q}\\
I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}=J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I_{1}~I_{2}}{J_{1}~J_{2}}\det Y_{I_{1}J_{1}}

\displaystyle{}{\times}\,\sum_{R\in\binom{[r]}{\ell}}\sum_{V,W\in\binom{[q]}{\ell}}\varepsilon(J_{2},I_{2},J_{2}(V),I_{2}(W))\det\bigl(X_{J_{2}(V),R}\bigr)\det\bigl(D_{I_{2}(W),R}\bigr).

(3.7)

Here, for tuples
I
and
J
we denote by
X_{IJ}
,
Y_{IJ}
and
D_{IJ}
the corresponding submatrices of
X=[x_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n,r}
,
D=[\partial_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n,r}
and
Y=[y_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n,n}
, respectively.

Remark 3.13
.

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}
stands for the
Stirling number of the second kind
, i.e., the number of ways to split a
k
-element set into
\ell
non-empty subsets.

Corollary 3.14
.

The big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
is generated by the operators
\{F_{p,q}\mid p,q\geq 0,\allowbreak{p+q\leq n}\}
, where

F_{p,q}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},J_{1}\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\
I_{2},J_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{q}\\
I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}=J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I_{1}~I_{2}}{J_{1}~J_{2}}\det Y_{I_{1}J_{1}}\sum_{R\in\binom{[r]}{q}}\det(X_{J_{2},R})\det(D_{I_{2},R}).

(3.8)

In particular, the operators
\{M_{p,q}\mid p,q\geq 0,\,p+q\leq n\}
and
\{F_{p,q}\mid p,q\geq 0,\,p+q\leq n\}
are related to each other in the following way:

M_{p,q}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{q}(-1)^{q-\ell}(q-\ell)!\,\ell!\,\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{q}{\ell}\binom{n-p-\ell}{q-\ell}F_{p,\ell}.

(3.9)

We prove these formulas for
M_{p,q}
and
F_{p,q}
in the next section.
The following fact is an immediate consequence of the explicit formulas from Corollary
3.14
.

Corollary 3.15
.

The big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
is generated by operators
\{F_{p,q}\mid 1\leq p\leq n,\allowbreak{0\leq q\leq r},\,{p+q\leq n}\}
.

Proof.

Indeed, it follows from (
3.8
) that all operators
F_{p,q}
with
q>r
are zero.
∎

3.6
Restriction to the Cartan subalgebra

The formulas (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) simplify a lot if we restrict
Y\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
to Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
, i.e., to diagonal matrices.
We expect that, at least for small values of
r
, these formulas can be used for finding common eigenfunctions in
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,r)]
of operators
\{F_{p,q}\}
and the corresponding eigenvalues (for
r=1
this is done in Section
8
). This would be helpful for obtaining presentations of
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)
in terms of generators and relations.

Proposition 3.16
.

For
Y=\operatorname{diag}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}
, we have

F_{p,q}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\
J\in\binom{[n]}{q}\\
I\cap J=\varnothing\end{subarray}}\biggl(\prod_{i\in I}z_{i}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{R\in\binom{[r]}{q}}\det(X_{JR})\det(D_{JR})\biggr).

Proof.

This is a consequence of Corollary
3.14
and the observation that for
Y\in\mathfrak{h}
the determinant
\det Y_{I_{1}J_{1}}
vanishes unless
I_{1}=J_{1}
.
∎

We use this formula in the proof of commutativity of the big algebra (see Theorem
7.1
). More precisely, we match these normal form expressions with images of certain elements of the Bethe subalgebra using the Capelli identities (see Section
7
).

4
Proofs of Theorem
3.12
and Corollary
3.14

In this section, we prove Theorem
3.12
and Corollary
3.14
, i.e., formulas (
3.7
) and (
3.8
). The proof is purely computational and reduces to some identities in the Weyl algebra.

4.1
Computation of the symmetrized determinants

In view of Proposition
3.10
, to prove Theorem
3.12
, we first need to obtain a formula for
\operatorname{symdet}L(E)_{IJ}
. Recall that for
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
in
[n]^{\underline{k}}
the symmetrized determinant
\operatorname{symdet}L(E_{IJ})
is defined as (see (
2.2
))

\displaystyle\operatorname{symdet}L(E_{IJ})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)L\bigl(E_{i_{\sigma(1)}i_{\tau(1)}}\bigr)\cdots L\bigl(E_{i_{\sigma(k)}i_{\tau(k)}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot x_{i_{\sigma(1)}a_{1}}\partial_{j_{\tau(1)}a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\sigma(k)}a_{k}}\partial_{j_{\tau(k)}a_{k}}.

Our aim is to obtain a reduced expression (the normal form) for
\operatorname{symdet}L(E_{IJ})
in the algebra
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
of differential operators on
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
with polynomial coefficients.
The computation is divided into several steps.

We start with the following auxiliary identity.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
N
be a positive integer. For any permutations
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
and any
s\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, define

\delta_{s}(\sigma,\tau)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ \sigma(s)\leq\tau(s),\\
1&\text{if}\ \sigma(s)>\tau(s).\end{cases}

Then, for any
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{C}
the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot(\alpha_{1}+\delta_{1}(\sigma,\tau))\cdots(\alpha_{N}+\delta_{N}(\sigma,\tau))

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(-1)^{N-1}(N-1)!(\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{N}).

(4.1)

Proof.

The identity clearly holds for
N=1
, so let us assume that
N\geq 2
.
Observe that the left-hand side can be expressed as

\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}C_{\ell}\cdot e_{\ell}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}),

where
e_{\ell}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N})
is the
\ell
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}
and
C_{0},\dots,C_{\ell}
are certain real numbers. Therefore, to check the identity (
4.1
) it suffices to calculate the coefficients
C_{\ell}
for every
0\leq\ell\leq N
. Note that
C_{\ell}
is equal to the coefficient of the term
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{\ell}

on the left-hand side. Then, it is not difficult to see that

C_{\ell}=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{s=\ell+1}^{N}\delta_{s}(\sigma,\tau).

In other words,
C_{\ell}
is the sum of
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)
, where
(\sigma,\tau)
runs over all pairs of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
such that

\sigma(s)>\tau(s)\qquad\text{for all}~\ell+1\leq s\leq N.

Let
\Gamma_{\ell}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{N}\times\mathfrak{S}_{N}
be the set of all such pairs.
Now let us consider several cases:

•

\ell=0
. In this case, we have

C_{0}=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot\delta_{1}(\sigma,\tau)\cdots\delta_{N}(\sigma,\tau)=0

because for any
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
at least one of
\delta_{s}(\sigma,\tau)
is zero
(
for example, one can take
s=\sigma^{-1}(1)
)
.

•

\ell=1
. We claim that in this case the set
\Gamma_{\ell}=\Gamma_{1}
contains exactly
(N-1)!
elements. Indeed, by definition a pair
(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}\times\mathfrak{S}_{N}
belongs to
\Gamma_{1}
if
\sigma(s)>\tau(s)
for every
s\in\{2,\dots,N\}
. One checks that this holds if and only if these permutations satisfy
\sigma(1)=1
,
\tau(1)=N
and
\sigma(s)=\tau(s)+1
for all
s=2,\dots,N
. In particular,
|\Gamma_{1}|=(N-1)!
and for any
(\sigma,\tau)\in\Gamma_{1}
the permutation
\sigma\tau^{-1}
is the cycle
(1~2~\dots~N)
. Hence,

C_{1}=\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in\Gamma_{1}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)=|\Gamma_{1}|\cdot(-1)^{N-1}=(-1)^{N-1}\cdot(N-1)!.

•

2\leq\ell\leq N
. Note that for any permutations
\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
which fix each of
\ell+1,\dots,N
the pair
(\sigma,\tau)
belongs to
\Gamma_{\ell}
if and only if
(\sigma\sigma^{\prime},\tau\tau^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{\ell}
. This and the equality

\sum_{\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{\prime}\tau^{\prime})=\biggl(\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma^{\prime}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}}\operatorname{sgn}\tau^{\prime}\biggr)=0

imply that
C_{\ell}=0
for all
2\leq\ell\leq N
.

Combining everything, we obtain

C_{\ell}=\begin{cases}0,&~\ell\neq 1,\\
(-1)^{N-1}(N-1)!,&~\ell=1,\end{cases}

which is equivalent to (
4.1
).
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
N
be a positive integer and let
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. Then, in the notation of Lemma
4.1
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(\alpha_{j}+\delta_{j}(\sigma,\tau))

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\begin{cases}0,&\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,N-2\},\\
(-1)^{N-1}(N-1)!,&\ell=N-1,\\
(-1)^{N-1}(N-1)!\cdot(\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{N}),&\ell=N.\end{cases}

Proof.

This follows from (
4.1
) by differentiating both sides with respect to
\alpha_{\ell+1},\dots,\alpha_{N}
.
∎

Next, we prove the following identity in the Weyl algebra which essentially computes the symmetrized determinant
\operatorname{symdet}L(E)_{IJ}
in the simplest case
r=1
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider the Weyl algebra generated by variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
and the corresponding partial derivatives
\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}
. Let
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
be two
k
-tuples in
[n]^{\underline{k}}
. Assume that
I
and
J
have
\ell
common elements,
0\leq\ell\leq k
. Denote

\Psi(I,J)=\operatorname{symdet}\bigl(\bigl[u_{i_{\alpha}}\partial_{j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}\bigr)=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)u_{i_{\sigma(1)}}\partial_{j_{\tau(1)}}\cdots u_{i_{\sigma(k)}}\partial_{j_{\tau(k)}}.

Then,
\Psi(I,J)
, as an element of the Weyl algebra, can be simplified as follows:

•

if
\ell=k
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
is such that
i_{l}=j_{\sigma(l)}
for all
l\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, then

\Psi(I,J)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\sum_{i\in I}u_{i}\partial_{i},

•

if
\ell=k-1
and
\pi,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
are such that
i_{\pi(l)}=j_{\sigma(l)}
for all
l\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
and
i_{\pi(k)}\neq j_{\sigma(k)}
, then

\Psi(I,J)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(\pi\sigma)u_{i_{\pi(k)}}\partial_{j_{\sigma(k)}},

•

if
\ell\leq k-2
, then
\Psi(I,J)=0
.

Moreover, viewing
I
and
J
as functions on
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
, one can express
\Psi(I,J)
as follows:

\Psi(I,J)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{s\}}=\pi^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{s\}}\bigr)\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)u_{i_{s}}\partial_{j_{\pi(s)}}.

Here, for an assertion
A
we put
\mathds{1}(A)=1
, if
A
holds, and
\mathds{1}(A)=0
otherwise.

Proof.

We start with the following observation: for any permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, we have

\Psi(\pi I,\sigma J)=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi\sigma)\cdot\Psi(I,J),

where
\pi I=\bigl(i_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots,i_{\pi^{-1}(k)}\bigr)
and
\sigma J=\bigl(j_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,j_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}\bigr)
. Therefore, it suffices to prove the statement in the case when
i_{1}=j_{1}
, …,
i_{\ell}=j_{\ell}
and

\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\cap\{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\}.

Denote
K=(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})=(j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell})
. To simplify the notation, let us also assume that
K=(1,\dots,\ell)
. Now note that elements
u_{i_{\ell+1}},\dots,u_{i_{k}}
and
\partial_{j_{\ell+1}},\dots,\partial_{j_{k}}
commute with each other and also with
u_{i}
and
\partial_{i}
for
i\in K
. Therefore, we can rewrite
\Psi(I,J)
as

\Psi(I,J)=u_{i_{\ell+1}}\cdots u_{i_{k}}\partial_{j_{\ell+1}}\cdots\partial_{j_{k}}\cdot\Phi(\ell;K),

(4.2)

where
\Phi(\ell;K)
is a certain element in the Weyl algebra generated by
u_{i}
,
\partial_{i}
with
i\in K
. Namely,
\Phi(\ell;K)
is obtained from the expression for
\Phi(I,J)
by removing all
u_{i}
and
\partial_{i}
with
i\notin K
. One finds that the action of
\Phi(\ell;K)
on a monomial
u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}
, where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is given by

\displaystyle\Phi(\ell;K)\bigl(u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\biggl(\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\bigl(\alpha_{1}+\delta_{1}\bigl(\sigma^{-1},\tau^{-1}\bigr)\bigr)\cdots\bigl(\alpha_{\ell}+\delta_{\ell}\bigl(\sigma^{-1},\tau^{-1}\bigr)\bigr)\biggr)\cdot u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}},

where the
\delta_{i}
’s are defined as in Lemma
4.1
. Indeed, this follows from the identities

\displaystyle(u_{i}\partial_{i})\bigl(u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\bigr)=\alpha_{i}\cdot u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}},

\displaystyle(\partial_{i}u_{i})\bigl(u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\bigr)=(\alpha_{i}+1)\cdot u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq\ell.

Now observe that

\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\bigl(\alpha_{j}+\delta_{j}\bigl(\sigma^{-1},\tau^{-1}\bigr)\bigr)=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(\alpha_{j}+\delta_{j}(\sigma,\tau)),

and hence Corollary
4.2
implies that

•

if
0\leq\ell\leq k-2
, then
\Phi(\ell;K)\bigl(u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\bigr)=0
;

•

if
\ell=k-1
, then
\Phi(\ell;K)\bigl(u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\bigr)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\cdot u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}
;

•

if
\ell=k
, then
\Phi(\ell;K)\bigl(u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\bigr)=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\cdot(\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k})u_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots u_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}
.

Since the elements of Weyl algebra are uniquely defined by their action on the corresponding polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[u_{1},\dots,u_{n}]
, we obtain

\Phi(\ell;K)=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq\ell\leq k-2,\\
(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!,&\ell=k-1,\\
(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\sum_{i\in K}u_{i}\partial_{i},&\ell=k.\end{cases}

Combining this with (
4.2
) concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
. For any two
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
of distinct elements of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, denote

\Delta(I,J)=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot x_{i_{\sigma(1)}a_{1}}\partial_{j_{\tau(1)}a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\sigma(k)}a_{k}}\partial_{j_{\tau(k)}a_{k}}.

Then, we have the following identity:

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)=(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!\sum_{V,W\in\binom{[k]}{\ell}}\varepsilon(I,J,I(V),J(W))\det\bigl(X_{I(V),R}\bigr)\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J(W),R}\bigr),

(4.3)

where
R=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
and
\ell=|R|
.

Remark 4.5
.

Here
I(V)
and
J(W)
are the subtuples of
I
and
J
that correspond to
V
and
W
, respectively. The
k
-element subsets

and

are viewed as

). Note that the summand does not depend on a choice of the ordering of

and

.

Remark 4.6
.

Observe that the formula for
\Psi(I,J)
from Lemma

is a particular case of (

) when

. Indeed, for any

where in

the elements

and

are viewed as

Remark 4.7
.

In the notation of Section

,

.
When

\displaystyle\Delta(I,I)=(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!\sum_{V\in\binom{[k]}{\ell}}\det\bigl(X_{I(V),R}\bigr)\cdot\det\bigl(D_{I(V),R}\bigr).

Thus, we obtain an expression from the Cauchy–Binet type identity, see Proposition

m_{l}

).

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot x_{i_{\sigma(1)}a_{1}}\partial_{j_{\tau(1)}a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\sigma(k)}a_{k}}\partial_{j_{\tau(k)}a_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{r}\Theta_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},\tau^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{[\sigma],[\tau]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\biggl(\sum_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}(K)}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{1}\pi_{2})\prod_{l=1}^{r}\Theta_{l}\bigl((\sigma\pi_{1})^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},(\tau\pi_{2})^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)\biggr).

Note that for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
the tuples
\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}}
and
\sigma^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}
depend only on how
\sigma
acts on
K_{l}
. Recall also that
\mathfrak{S}(K)=\mathfrak{S}(K_{1})\times\dots\times\mathfrak{S}(K_{r})
. Using this for any
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}(K)}\prod_{l=1}^{r}\bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{1}\pi_{2}|_{K_{l}})\Theta_{l}\bigl((\sigma\pi_{1})^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},(\tau\pi_{2})^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\prod_{l=1}^{r}\operatorname{symdet}\bigl(\bigl[x_{i_{\sigma(s)}l}\partial_{j_{\tau(t)}l}\bigr]_{s,t\in K_{l}}\bigr)=\prod_{l=1}^{r}\Psi_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},\tau^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr).

Therefore, we get the following expression for
\Delta(I,J)
:

\Delta(I,J)=\sum_{[\sigma],[\tau]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{l=1}^{r}\Psi_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},\tau^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr).

Remark 4.8
.

The summation here runs over all the left cosets of
\mathfrak{S}(K)
in
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
. Note that the expression
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{l=1}^{r}\Psi_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},\tau^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)
does not depend on the choice of representatives of
[\sigma],[\tau]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)
.

Now we apply Lemma
4.3
in order to compute for every
l\in R
the symmetrized determinant
\Psi_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},\tau^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)
. Firstly, observe that
\Psi_{l}(I_{l}(\sigma),J_{l}(\tau))=0
unless there exist a permutation
\pi_{l}\in\mathfrak{S}(K_{l})
and an element
s_{l}\in K_{l}
such that
j_{\tau(\pi(s))}=i_{\sigma(s)}
for all
s\in K_{l}\setminus\{s_{l}\}
. Moreover, in this case we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},\tau^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)=(-1)^{m_{l}-1}(m_{l}-1)!

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{\pi_{l}\in\mathfrak{S}(K_{l})}\sum_{s_{l}\in K_{l}}\mathds{1}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}\setminus\{s_{l}\}}=(\tau\pi_{l})^{-1}J|_{K_{l}\setminus\{s_{l}\}}\bigr)\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{l})x_{(\sigma^{-1}I)(s_{l}),l}\partial_{(\tau^{-1}J)(\pi_{l}(s_{l})),l}.

Combining everything, we get

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)=\sum_{[\sigma],[\tau]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_{l=1}^{r}\Psi_{l}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}},(\tau\pi_{l})^{-1}J|_{K_{l}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\prod_{l\in R}(-1)^{m_{l}-1}(m_{l}-1)!\biggr)\sum_{[\sigma],[\tau]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)

\displaystyle\quad{}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{l}\in K_{l}\\
\pi_{l}\in\mathfrak{S}(K_{l})\\
\text{for each}~l\in R\end{subarray}}\prod_{l\in R}\bigl(\mathds{1}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{K_{l}\setminus\{s_{l}\}}=(\tau\pi_{l})^{-1}J|_{K_{l}\setminus\{s_{l}\}}\bigr)\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{l})x_{(\sigma^{-1}I)(s_{l}),l)}\partial_{(\tau^{-1}J)(\pi_{l}(s_{l})),l}\bigr).

Now observe that
\mathfrak{S}(K)=\prod_{l\in R}\mathfrak{S}(K_{l})
. Hence, when
\tau
runs over
\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)
and
\pi_{l}
runs over
\mathfrak{S}(K_{l})
for each
l\in R
, the product
\tau\cdot\prod_{l\in R}\pi_{l}
runs over
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
. Therefore, we can rewrite the formula above using the summation over
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)=\biggl(\prod_{l\in R}(-1)^{m_{l}-1}(m_{l}-1)!\biggr)\sum_{[\sigma]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{l}\in K_{l}\\
\text{for each}~l\in R\end{subarray}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\mathds{1}\bigl(\sigma^{-1}I|_{[k]\setminus\{s_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{s_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\prod_{l\in R}\bigl(x_{I(\sigma(s_{l})),l}\partial_{J(\tau(s_{l})),l}\bigr).

Replacing
\tau
with
\tau\sigma
and introducing the summation over
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
instead of the summation over
[\sigma]\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}/\mathfrak{S}(K)
yields

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)=(-1)^{k-\ell}\biggl(\prod_{l\in R}m_{l}^{-1}\!\biggr)\!\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{l}\in K_{l}\\
\text{for each}~l\in R\end{subarray}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\!\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{\sigma(s_{l})\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{\sigma(s_{l})\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Delta(I,J)=(-1)^{k-\ell}\biggl(\prod_{l\in R}m_l^{-1}\biggr)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_l\in K_l\\
\text{for each}~l\in R\end{subarray}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_k}}\times\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\prod_{l\in R}\bigl(x_{I(\sigma(s_{l})),l}\partial_{J(\tau(\sigma(s_{l}))),l}\bigr).

For a given
\ell
-tuple
\{s_{l}\}_{l\in R}
, as
\sigma
runs over
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
the tuple
\{\sigma(s_{l})\}_{l\in R}
runs over all
\ell
-tuples in
[k]^{\underline{\ell}}
and each of them occurs exactly
(k-\ell)!
times. Since there are precisely
\prod_{l\in R}m_{l}
tuples
\{s_{l}\}_{l\in R}
in
\prod_{l\in R}R_{l}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)=(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!\sum_{U\in[k]^{\underline{R}}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Delta(I,J)=(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!\sum_{U\in[k]^{\lx@text@underline{R}}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_k}}{}\times\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\prod_{l\in R}\bigl(x_{I(u_{l}),l}\partial_{J(\tau(u_{l})),l}\bigr).

Here by
[k]^{\underline{R}}
we denote the set of all
\ell
-tuples
U=\{u_{l}\}_{l\in R}
of distinct elements in
[k]
. The next step is to replace the summation over
\ell
-tuples
U\in[k]^{\underline{R}}
by the summation over
\ell
-element subsets of
R
. Namely, we collect the terms which correspond to the same
\ell
-element subset of
R
and after some algebraic manipulations obtain the determinants in
x_{ij}
and
\partial_{ij}
. Let
\mathfrak{S}(U)
be the permutation group of the
\ell
-element set
\{u_{l}\mid l\in R\}
. Then, we can rewrite the last sum as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)=\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{U\in[k]^{\underline{R}}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}(U)}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Delta(I,J)=\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{U\in[k]^{\lx@text@underline{R}}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}(U)}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_k}}{}\times\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\prod_{l\in R}\bigl(x_{I(\pi(u_{l})),l}\partial_{J(\tau(\pi(u_{l}))),l}\bigr).

Clearly, for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}(U)
, we have

\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)=\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=(\tau\pi)^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr).

(4.4)

Therefore, substituting
\tau\mapsto\tau\pi^{-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)={}

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{U\in[k]^{\underline{R}}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}(U)}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{U\in[k]^{\lx@text@underline{R}}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}(U)}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_k}}{}\times\operatorname{sgn}(\tau\pi)\prod_{l\in R}x_{I(\pi(u_{l})),l}\partial_{J(\tau(u_{l})),l}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{U\in[k]^{\underline{R}}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}=\tau^{-1}J|_{[k]\setminus\{u_{l}\colon l\in R\}}\bigr)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{U\in[k]^{\lx@text@underline{R}}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_k}}{}\times\det\bigl(\bigl[x_{I(u_{\alpha}),\beta}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta\in R}\bigr)\prod_{l\in R}\partial_{J(\tau(u_{l})),l}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{U\in[k]^{\underline{R}}}\det\bigl(\bigl[x_{I(u_{\alpha}),\beta}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta\in R}\bigr)\prod_{l\in R}\partial_{J(\tau(u_{l})),l}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{V\in\binom{[k]}{\ell}}\det\bigl(\bigl[x_{I(v),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr)\cdot\det\bigl(\bigl[\partial_{J(\tau(v)),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr).

Plugging this into the previous formula and taking into account (
4.4
) gives

\displaystyle\Delta(I,J)={}

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!}{\ell!}\sum_{V\in\binom{[k]}{\ell}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus V}=J\circ\tau|_{[k]\setminus V}\bigr)

\displaystyle{}{\times}\,\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\det\bigl(\bigl[x_{I(v),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr)\cdot\det\bigl(\bigl[\partial_{J(\tau(v)),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr).

(4.5)

Now observe that for any given subset
V\subset[k]
with
|V|=\ell
there exist either
\ell!
permutations
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
such that

I|_{[k]\setminus V}=J\circ\tau|_{[k]\setminus V},

(4.6)

or none. Indeed, suppose such a permutation
\tau_{0}
exists. Then, any other permutation
\tau
that satisfies (

) is of the form
\tau=\tau_{0}\pi
, where
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}(V)
. In particular,

\begin{split}\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\det\bigl(\bigl[\partial_{J(\tau(v)),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr)&=\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{0}\pi)\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\det\bigl(\bigl[\partial_{J(\tau_{0}(v)),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr)\\
&=\operatorname{sgn}(\tau_{0})\det\bigl(\bigl[\partial_{J(\tau_{0}(v)),l}\bigr]_{v\in V,l\in R}\bigr).\end{split}

Hence, the summation over
\tau
in (
4.5
) in fact contains
\ell!
equal summands.
Note that
\tau
satisfying (
4.6
) exists if and only if all elements of
I|_{[k]\setminus V}
are contained in
J
. This allows us to write the final formula for
\Delta(I,J)
:

\Delta(I,J)=(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!\sum_{V,W\in\binom{[k]}{\ell}}\varepsilon(I,J,I(V),J(W))\det(X_{I(V),R})\cdot\det(D_{J(W),R}).

(4.7)

Indeed, to get (
4.7
) from (
4.5
), we just note that
\mathds{1}\bigl(I|_{[k]\setminus V}=J\circ\tau|_{[k]\setminus V}\bigr)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)
is equal to
\varepsilon(I,J,I(V),J(W))
if
W=\tau(V)
and
\tau
satisfies (
4.6
), and is zero otherwise.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

For any
I,J\in[n]^{\underline{k}}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{symdet}L(E)_{IJ}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell)!\ell!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\!\sum_{R\in\binom{[r]}{\ell}}\sum_{V,W\in\binom{[k]}{\ell}}\varepsilon(I,J,I(V),J(W))\det\bigl(X_{I(V),R}\bigr)\det\bigl(D_{J(W),R}\bigr).

Here by
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}
we denote the Stirling number of the second kind, i.e., the number of ways to split a
k
-element set into
\ell
non-empty subsets.

Proof.

We start with the identity

\operatorname{symdet}(L(E))_{IJ}=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot x_{i_{\sigma(1)}a_{1}}\partial_{j_{\tau(1)}a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{\sigma(k)}a_{k}}\partial_{j_{\tau(k)}a_{k}}.

For each choice of
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
we rewrite the products using Lemma
4.4
. It remains to notice that for any given
R\in\binom{r}{\ell}
the number of sequences
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in[r]^{k}
such that
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}=R
equals
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\cdot\ell!

∎

4.2
Proof of the main formulas

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

and Corollary

.

Proof of Theorem
3.12
.

By Proposition
3.10
, we have

M_{p,q}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},J_{1}\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\
I_{2},J_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{q}\\
I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}=J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I_{1}~I_{2}}{J_{1}~J_{2}}\det Y_{I_{1}J_{1}}\cdot\operatorname{symdet}L(E)_{J_{2}I_{2}}.

Applying Lemma

to

).
∎

Proof of Corollary

.

Indeed, the identity (

) is a direct consequence of (

\operatorname{sgn}\binom{I_{1}~I_{2}}{J_{1}~J_{2}}\varepsilon(J_{2},I_{2},J_{2}(V),I_{2}(W))

.

Then, it follows from (

is a linear combination of

with

. Therefore, the sets
\{M_{p,q}\}_{p+q\leq n}

5
Capelli identities and their variations

In this section, we give an account of Capelli identities and their variants which we use in the proof of Theorem
7.1
. Since some of these generalizations are not widely known, we also include the proofs for the sake of completeness.

5.1
Classical Capelli identity

Assume that
r=n
, i.e., that
\mathcal{P}(n,n)
is a polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[\operatorname{Mat}(n,n)]
in
n^{2}
variables
x_{ij}
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Recall that
\mathcal{PD}(n,n)
is the algebra of differential operators on
\mathcal{P}(n,n)
with polynomial coefficients. Define an element
\Pi\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
as

\Pi=\operatorname{rdet}(E_{ij}+(i-1)\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}=\operatorname{rdet}\begin{bmatrix}E_{11}+0&E_{12}&\cdots&E_{1k}\\
E_{21}&E_{22}+1&\cdots&E_{2k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
E_{k1}&E_{k2}&\cdots&E_{kk}+k-1\end{bmatrix}.

The next statement was observed by Alfredo Capelli
[
3
]
and was used by Hermann Weyl in his treatment of invariant theory for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}

[
37
, Section II.4]
.

Proposition 5.1

(Capelli identity,
[
3
]
)
.

The image of
\Pi
in
\mathcal{PD}(n,n)
equals

L(\Pi)=\det(X)\det(D),

where
L
is defined as in (
2.2
).
In particular, the expression on the left is a
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-invariant differential operator.

5.2
Capelli identities for rectangular matrices

Now let
r
be an arbitrary positive integer.
It turns out that one can generalize the Capelli identity for all minors of the matrix
E
(see
[
4
,
16
,
35
]
and also
[
14
,
30
]
).

Firstly, for arbitrary
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
in
[n]^{k}
, introduce the following element
\Pi_{IJ}\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
:

\Pi_{IJ}=\operatorname{rdet}[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(\alpha-1)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}.

For example, if
I=J=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in[n]^{\underline{k}}
, then

\Pi_{II}=\operatorname{rdet}\begin{bmatrix}E_{i_{1}i_{1}}+0&E_{i_{1}i_{2}}&\cdots&E_{i_{1}i_{k}}\\
E_{i_{2}i_{1}}&E_{i_{2}i_{2}}+1&\cdots&E_{i_{2}i_{k}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
E_{i_{k}i_{1}}&E_{i_{k}i_{2}}&\cdots&E_{i_{k}i_{k}}+k-1\end{bmatrix},

which coincides with
\Pi
above when
I=(1,2,\dots,n)
.

Proposition 5.2

(
[
35
, Proposition 4 and Theorem 5]
)
.

The following identity holds in
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})[z]
:

\operatorname{rdet}(E_{ij}+(i-1-z)\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}z^{\underline{k}}\cdot C_{n-k},

(5.1)

where
z^{\underline{k}}=z(z-1)\cdots(z-k+1)
and

C_{k}=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\Pi_{II}
.
Moreover, the elements
C_{k}
belong to the center
Z(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
and their images in
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
are as follows:

L(C_{k})=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sum_{K\in\binom{[r]}{k}}\det(X_{IK})\det(D_{IK})

(5.2)

In particular, if
r=n
, then plugging in
z=0
yields the classical Capelli identity
(
Proposition
5.1
)
.

Remark 5.3
.

In formula (
5.2
), we regard
K\in\binom{[r]}{k}
as an element of
\mathfrak{S}_{k}\backslash[r]^{\underline{k}}
, i.e., as
k
-tuple with arbitrarily chosen ordering of elements. Note that the term
\det(X_{IK})\det(D_{IK})
does not depend on this choice, so the expression on the right is well defined.

Remark 5.4
.

The elements
C_{k}
are often called
Capelli generators
. Moreover,
Z(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
is a free polynomial ring generated by
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
. The classical counterparts of the Capelli generators are the coefficients
c_{k}
(see (
3.1
)) of the characteristic polynomial which are the generators of
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
.

Instead of proving Proposition
5.2
directly, we prove more general versions of equalities (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), see Propositions
5.11
and
5.5
below. These generalizations are then verified by a straightforward inductive argument.

5.3
Cauchy–Binet type identity

We start with a formula for
L(\Pi_{IJ})
for an arbitrary minor
\Pi_{IJ}
. It generalizes the classical Capelli identity and can be viewed as a non-commutative analogue of the Cauchy–Binet identity from linear algebra (see also
[
4
, Corollary 1.3 (b)]
).

Proposition 5.5

(Cauchy–Binet type identity,
[
35
, Theorem 2]
)
.

For any
I,J\in[n]^{k}
, the image of
\Pi_{IJ}
in
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
under the map
L
equals

L(\Pi_{IJ})=\sum_{K\in\binom{[r]}{k}}\det(X_{IK})\det(D_{JK}).

(5.3)

Remark 5.6
.

Note that the right-hand side of (
5.3
) is skew-symmetric in the entries of
I
and
J
and hence the same holds for
L(\Pi_{IJ})
. From the definition of
\Pi_{IJ}
, it is clear that
\Pi_{IJ}
is skew-symmetric in
J
(as row determinant), but the skew-symmetry in
I
is not immediate from this definition. Formula (
5.3
) in particular implies that
L(\Pi_{IJ})
is zero whenever
I
or
J
contains equal elements.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

Induct on
k
. For
k=1
, the identity follows immediately from the definition of
L(E_{ij})
, see (
2.2
). Now assume that
k>1
. Consider arbitrary
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
. Denote
I^{\prime}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
and
J^{(l)}=\bigl(j_{1},\dots,\widehat{j_{l}},\dots,j_{k}\bigr)
for every
l\in[k]
. Expanding
\Pi_{IJ}
along the
k
-th row yields

\Pi_{IJ}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-l}\Pi_{I^{\prime}J^{(l)}}\cdot(E_{i_{k}j_{l}}+(k-1)\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}),

and hence by the inductive hypothesis

\displaystyle L(\Pi_{IJ})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-l}L\big(\Pi_{I^{\prime}J^{(l)}}\big)\cdot\biggl(\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}x_{i_{k}\alpha}\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}+(k-1)\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-l}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\cdot\biggl(\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}x_{i_{k}\alpha}\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}+(k-1)\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}\biggr).

Using the identity
\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)x_{i_{k}\alpha}=x_{i_{k}\alpha}\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)+\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]
, we can rewrite the last sum as follows:

\displaystyle L(\Pi_{IJ})={}

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})x_{i_{k}\alpha}\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}

\displaystyle{}{+}\,(k-1)\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}

\displaystyle{}{+}\,\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}.

(5.4)

Denote the three summands on the right-hand side of (
5.4
) by
S^{(1)}_{IJ}
,
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
and
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
, respectively.
Observe that
S^{(1)}_{IJ}
equals

\displaystyle S^{(1)}_{IJ}

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})x_{i_{k}\alpha}\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})x_{i_{k}\alpha}\cdot\det\bigl(D_{JK^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}}\bigr)=\sum_{K\in\binom{[r]}{k}}\det(X_{IK})\cdot\det(D_{JK}).

Here by
K^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}
we denote a
k
-tuple in
[n]^{\underline{k}}
whose first
k-1
entries coincide with those of
K^{\prime}
and whose
k
-th entry equals
\alpha
. (Recall that
K^{\prime}
can be viewed as a
(k-1)
-tuple.) The last equality follows from the cofactor expansion of
\det(X_{IK})
along the last row. Indeed, for
K=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})
we have
\det(X_{IK})=\sum_{l=1}^{l}(-1)^{k-l}\det\bigl(X_{I^{\prime}K^{(l)}}\bigr)x_{i_{k}\beta_{l}}
, where
K^{(l)}=\bigl(\beta_{1},\dots,\widehat{\beta_{l}},\dots,\beta_{k}\bigr)\in[r]^{\underline{k-1}}
. If we denote
\widetilde{K}_{(l)}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l-1},\beta_{l+1},\dots,\beta_{k},\beta_{l})\in[r]^{\underline{k}}
for
l=1,\dots,k
, then we have the equality
\det(D_{JK})=(-1)^{k-l}\det\bigl(D_{J\widetilde{K}_{(l)}}\bigr)
and the formula for
S^{(1)}_{IJ}
above follows.

Thus, it remains to show that the sum
S^{(2)}_{IJ}+S^{(3)}_{IJ}
is zero. We consider three cases depending on the number
q
of occurrences of
i_{k}
in the
k
-tuple
J
.

Case 1:

q=0
. Then, for any
l\in[k]
and
\alpha\in[r]
, we have
\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}=0
and
{\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]\!=\!0}
. Hence, both
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
and
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
are zero which concludes the proof.

In the remaining two cases, we use the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

For any
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
in
[n]^{k}
and any
i,j\in[n]
, we have

\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}[\det(D_{IJ}),x_{i\alpha}]\partial_{j\alpha}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}~I\notin[n]^{\underline{k}}~\text{or}~i\notin I,\\
\det(D_{KJ})&\text{if}~i_{p}=i~\text{for}~p\in[k],\end{cases}

where in the second case we put
K=(i_{1},\dots,i_{p-1},j,i_{p+1},\dots,i_{k})
.

Proof.

If
I
contains equal entries or
i\notin I
, then it clear that
[\det(D_{IJ}),x_{i\alpha}]=0
for all
\alpha\in[r]
. Otherwise, if
p\in[k]
is such that
i_{p}=i
, then
[\det(D_{IJ}),x_{i\alpha}]
is zero if
\alpha\notin J
and equals to the cofactor of the element
\partial_{j\alpha}
of the matrix
D_{KJ}
otherwise. Thus,
\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}[\det(D_{IJ}),x_{i\alpha}]\partial_{j\alpha}=\det(D_{KJ})
, as claimed.
∎

Case 2:

q=1
. Let
p\in[k]
be such that
i_{k}=j_{p}
. Observe that both sides of (
5.3
) are skew-symmetric with respect to
J
(see also Remark
5.6
). Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
p=k
. In this case, we can rewrite
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}S^{(2)}_{IJ}&=(k-1)\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}\\
&=(k-1)\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k)}K^{\prime}}\bigr).\end{split}

Applying Lemma
5.7
gives the following expression for
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
:

\begin{split}S^{(3)}_{IJ}&=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}\\
&=\sum_{l\in[k-1]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot(-1)^{k-l-1}\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\\
&=-(k-1)\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k)}K^{\prime}}\bigr).\end{split}

Therefore,
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
and
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
add up to zero, as claimed.

Case 3:

q>1
. Let us show that in this case both
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
and
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
vanish. If
q\geq 3
, then each
(k-1)
-tuple
J^{(l)}
,
l=1,\dots,k
, contains at least two entries that are equal to
i_{k}
. Hence, all terms in sums
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
and
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
are zero since
\det(D_{J^{l}K^{\prime}})=0
.

Now assume that
q=2
. Similar to the second case, we may assume that
j_{k-1}=j_{k}=i_{k}
and
j_{l}\neq i_{k}
for
l<k-1
. Then, for
S^{(2)}_{IJ}
, we have

\begin{split}S^{(2)}_{IJ}&=(k-1)\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\delta_{i_{k}j_{l}}\\
&=(k-1)\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}\bigl(-\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k-1)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)+\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)\bigr)=0,\end{split}

because
\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr)=0
for
l<k-1
while
J^{(k-1)}=J^{(k)}
.
Lemma
5.7
implies that
S^{(3)}_{IJ}
equals

\displaystyle S^{(3)}_{IJ}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}(-1)^{k-l}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})\cdot\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(l)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]\partial_{j_{l}\alpha}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{K^{\prime}\in\binom{[r]}{k-1}}\det(X_{I^{\prime}K^{\prime}})

\displaystyle{}{\times}\,\sum_{\alpha\in[r]}\bigl(-\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k-1)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]\partial_{j_{k-1}\alpha}+\bigl[\det\bigl(D_{J^{(k)}K^{\prime}}\bigr),x_{i_{k}\alpha}\bigr]\partial_{j_{k}\alpha}\bigr)=0,

since
J^{(k-1)}=J^{(k)}
and
j_{k-1}=j_{k}=i_{k}
.

Therefore, if
q>1
, we have
S^{(2)}_{IJ}=S^{(3)}_{IJ}=0
which concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

For any
I,J\in[n]^{k}
and any
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, we have
\Pi_{\sigma I,\tau J}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot\Pi_{IJ}
.

Proof.

By the previous proposition, we have the identity
L(\Pi_{\sigma I,\tau J})=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\cdot L(\Pi_{IJ})
. By Proposition
2.4
, for
r=n
the map
L
faithfully maps
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
to
\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
, hence the result follows.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

For any
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
in
[n]^{k}
, we have

\Pi_{IJ}=\operatorname{rdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(\alpha-1)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}=\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(k-\beta)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}.

Proof.

We prove that the images of
\Pi_{IJ}
and
\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(k-\beta)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}
under the map
L
coincide. This is sufficient since by Proposition
2.4
, for
r=n
the map
L\colon U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\to\mathcal{PD}(n,r)
is injective.

In view of formula (
5.3
), the equality
L(\Pi_{IJ})=\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[L\bigl(E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr)+(k-\beta)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}
is equivalent to

\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[L\bigl(E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr)+(k-\beta)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}=\sum_{K\in\binom{[r]}{k}}\det(X_{IK})\det(D_{JK}).

This identity can be proved in essentially the same way as Proposition
5.5
.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

One can prove this identity directly in
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
using the ternary relations and the Yang–Baxter equation (see Lemma
6.10
in the next section).

5.4
Characteristic polynomial of the Capelli matrix

Now we state a generalization of the formula (
5.1
) for
k\times k
minors.
Define (see also
[
16
, equation (11.1.11)]
)

C_{k}(z)=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{i_{\alpha}i_{\beta}}+(\alpha-1-z)\delta_{ij}\bigr)_{\alpha,\beta\in[k]}.

Observe that
C_{k}(0)=C_{k}
and
C_{n}(z)
is the left-hand side of (
5.1
).

Proposition 5.11

(
[
16
, equation (11.1.13)]
and
[
35
, Section 2]
)
.

For any
0\leq k\leq n
, we have

C_{k}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}(-1)^{m}\binom{n-k+m}{m}z^{\underline{m}}\cdot C_{k-m}.

(5.5)

In particular, for
k=n
this identity is equivalent to (
5.1
).

Proof.

We follow the proof from
[
16
, Section 11.1]
.
Induct on
k
. The cases
k=0
and
k=1
are clear. Denote the right hand-side of (
5.5
) by
B_{k}(z)
. Let
\Delta
be the difference operator defined as
{(\Delta f)(z)=f(z+1)-f(z)}
. Since both
B_{k}(z)
and
C_{k}(z)
are
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-valued polynomials in
z
and
{B_{k}(0)=C_{k}(0)=C_{k}}
, it suffices to check that
(\Delta B_{k})(z)=(\Delta C_{k})(z)
. We have

\displaystyle(\Delta B_{k})(z)

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}(-1)^{m}\binom{n-k+m}{m}\Delta(z^{\underline{m}})\cdot C_{k-m}

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}(-1)^{m}m\binom{n-k+m}{m}z^{\underline{m-1}}\cdot C_{k-m}.

Note that
m\binom{n-k+m}{m}=(n-k+1)\binom{n-k+m}{m-1}
, and hence we obtain

\displaystyle(\Delta B_{k})(z)

\displaystyle{}=(n-k+1)\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{m+1}\binom{n-k+m+1}{m}z^{\underline{m}}\cdot C_{k-1-m}

\displaystyle{}=-(n-k+1)B_{k-1}(z).

Now let us compute
(\Delta C_{k})(z)
. For any
k
-element subset
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
of
[n]
, where
i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}
, we set

\displaystyle E_{II}^{\natural}=\begin{bmatrix}E_{i_{1}i_{1}}+0&E_{i_{1}i_{2}}&\cdots&E_{i_{1}i_{k}}\\
E_{i_{2}i_{1}}&E_{i_{2}i_{2}}+1&\cdots&E_{i_{2}i_{k}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
E_{i_{k}i_{1}}&E_{i_{k}i_{2}}&\cdots&E_{i_{k}i_{k}}+k-1\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi_{II}(z)=\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{II}^{\natural}-z\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k}\bigr).

Then,

\begin{split}(\Delta\Pi_{II})(z)&=\Pi_{II}(z+1)-\Pi_{II}(z)\\
&=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\bigl(\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{II}^{\natural}-(z+1)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k}+\Lambda_{p-1}\bigr)-\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{II}^{\natural}-(z+1)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k}+\Lambda_{p}\bigr)\bigr),\end{split}

where
\Lambda_{p}
is a diagonal matrix with the first
p
diagonal entries equal to 1 and the last
k-p
entries equal to 0. Recall that the row determinant is an additive function of a fixed row. Applying this to the
p
-th row of an
n\times n
matrix
E^{\natural}-(z+1)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k}+\Lambda_{p-1}
gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{II}^{\natural}-(z+1)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k}+\Lambda_{p-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{II}^{\natural}-(z+1)\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k}+\Lambda_{p}\bigr)-\operatorname{rdet}\bigl(E_{I^{(p)}I^{(p)}}^{\natural}-z\cdot\operatorname{Id}_{k-1}\bigr),

where
I^{(p)}=I\setminus\{i_{p}\}
. Therefore,

(\Delta C_{k})(z)=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}(\Delta\Pi_{II})(z)=-\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\Pi_{I^{(p)}I^{(p)}}(z)=-(n-k+1)C_{k-1}(z).

By the inductive hypothesis,
B_{k-1}(z)=C_{k-1}(z)
. Therefore,
(\Delta B_{k})(z)=(\Delta C_{k})(z)
which concludes the inductive step due to the equality
B_{k}(0)=C_{k}(0)=C_{k}
.
∎

6
The Yangian
\boldsymbol{\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})}
and its Bethe subalgebras

Here we recall the definitions and basic properties of the Yangian
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
and the Bethe subalgebras. Our exposition follows the monograph by Molev (see
[
26
, Section 1]
for more details).

6.1
Notation

Let
\{e_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
be the standard matrix units of
\operatorname{Mat}(n,n)
and let
\{E_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
be the corresponding generators of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
. The matrix units
\{e_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
act on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
spanned by
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
in the usual way,

e_{ij}e_{k}=\delta_{jk}e_{i}
.

We often work with algebras of the form
\mathcal{A}\otimes(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an associative algebra. For any
C=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\otimes e_{ij}\in\mathcal{A}\otimes\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n}
and any
D=\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{n}d_{ijkl}e_{ij}\otimes e_{kl}\in(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}
(these operators might depend on some parameters), we define

\begin{split}&C_{a}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\otimes 1^{\otimes(a-1)}\otimes e_{ij}\otimes 1^{\otimes(m-a)}\in\mathcal{A}\otimes(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m},\qquad\text{and}\\
&D_{ab}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}d_{ijkl}\cdot 1^{\otimes(a-1)}\otimes e_{ij}\otimes 1^{\otimes(b-a-1)}\otimes e_{kl}\otimes 1^{\otimes(m-b)}\in(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}.\end{split}

Usually, we identify
(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
with the subalgebra
1\otimes(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
inside
\mathcal{A}\otimes(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
.

Each element
\sigma
of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
defines an element of
(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
whose action on
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
corresponds to permuting the tensors via
\sigma
. Namely,
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
corresponds to

\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=1}^{n}e_{i_{1}i_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{m}i_{\sigma(m)}}\in(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}.

Clearly, this gives rise to an embedding of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{m}]
into
(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
. For any distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, denote by
P_{ij}
the image of the transposition
(i~j)\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{m}]
in
(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
.
Let
A_{m}
be the antisymmetrization operator, i.e.,

A_{m}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=1}^{n}e_{i_{1}i_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{m}i_{\sigma(m)}}\in(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}.

It is not difficult to see that
A_{m}^{2}=m!\cdot A_{m}
and
A_{m}P_{ij}=P_{ij}A_{m}=-A_{m}
for any distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

6.2
\boldsymbol{R}
-matrices

Define the
Yang
R
-matrix
as an element
R(u)\in\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\bigl[\hskip-0.85358pt\bigl[u^{-1}\bigr]\hskip-0.85358pt\bigr]\otimes\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}
given by

R(u)=1-u^{-1}P,\qquad\text{where}\ P=P_{12}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{ij}\otimes e_{ji}.

The element
R(u)
satisfies the
Yang–Baxter equation
.

Proposition 6.1

(
[
26
, Proposition 1.2.1]
)
.

For any commuting indeterminates
u
,
v
and
w
,

R_{12}(u-v)R_{13}(u-w)R_{23}(v-w)=R_{23}(v-w)R_{13}(u-w)R_{12}(u-v).

We also define for any
m\geq 2
commuting indeterminates
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
the following rational function:

R(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m})=(R_{m-1,m})(R_{m-2,m}R_{m-2,m-1})\cdots(R_{1m}\cdots R_{12}),

where we use shorthand notation
R_{ij}=R_{ij}(u_{i}-u_{j})
. Note that for
m=1
, this is just the Yang
R
-matrix,
R(u_{1},u_{2})=R_{12}(u_{1}-u_{2})
.

Using the Yang–Baxter equation, one can give an alternative definition of
R(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m})
.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
26
, Section 1.6]
)
.

In the notation above, the following identity holds:

R(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m})=(R_{12}\cdots R_{1m})\cdots(R_{m-2,m-1}R_{m-2,m})(R_{m-1,m}).

In fact, the operator
A_{m}
is a certain specialization of
R(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})
.

Proposition 6.3

(
[
26
, Proposition 1.6.2]
)
.

If
u_{i}-u_{i+1}=1
for all
i=1,\dots,m-1
, then

R(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m})=A_{m}.

6.3
Yangian
\boldsymbol{\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})}

The Yangian
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
is an associative algebra generated by elements
t_{ij}^{(r)}
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
,
r\geq 1
, which satisfy certain relations. These relations can be succinctly written using the so-called
ternary
(
RTT
)
relation
. To state it, we need to introduce more notation.

For each
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
consider a formal Laurent series

t_{ij}(u)=\delta_{ij}+\sum_{r\geq 1}t_{ij}^{(r)}u^{-r}\in\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\bigl[\hskip-0.85358pt\bigl[u^{-1}\bigr]\hskip-0.85358pt\bigr].

Let
T(u)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}t_{ij}(u)\otimes e_{ij}\in\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\bigl[\hskip-0.85358pt\bigl[u^{-1}\bigr]\hskip-0.85358pt\bigr]\otimes\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry equals
t_{ij}(u)
.

Now we can state the defining relations of
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
(see
[
26
, Proposition 1.2.2]
):

R(u-v)T_{1}(u)T_{2}(v)=T_{2}(v)T_{1}(u)R(u-v).

(6.1)

Here both sides are viewed as elements of
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}\otimes\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\bigl[\hskip-0.85358pt\bigl[u^{-1},v^{-1}\bigr]\hskip-0.85358pt\bigr]
. Expanding them in
u^{-1}
and
v^{-1}
gives infinitely many relations between generators
t_{ij}^{(r)}
.

We need the following generalization of the ternary relation (
6.1
).

Proposition 6.4
.

For any
m\geq 2
commuting indeterminates
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
,

R(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})T_{1}(u_{1})\cdots T_{m}(u_{m})=T_{m}(u_{m})\cdots T_{1}(u_{1})R(u_{1},\dots,u_{m}).

6.4
The evaluation homomorphism

The relation between
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
and
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
is given by the so-called
evaluation homomorphism

\mathrm{ev}\colon\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\to U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
. The map
\mathrm{ev}
is defined by the formula
\mathrm{ev}(t_{ij}(u))=\delta_{ij}+u^{-1}E_{ij}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{ev}\bigl(t_{ij}^{(r)}\bigr)=\delta_{1r}E_{ij}
. One can verify that

is a surjective algebra homomorphism (see also

). Note that
\mathrm{ev}
maps the

to

.

6.5
Bethe subalgebras

Define the
trace
on
(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
as the linear map
\operatorname{tr}_{m}
defined by

\operatorname{tr}_{m}\,(e_{i_{1}j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{m}j_{m}})=\delta_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{m}j_{m}}.

Observe that if we view

as an element of
\operatorname{End}\,(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
, then
\operatorname{tr}_{m}(A)
is indeed the trace of the operator

acting on

We extend

to a map
\operatorname{tr}_{m}\colon\mathcal{A}\otimes(\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}\to\mathcal{A}
where
\mathcal{A}
is an arbitrary associative algebra,

\operatorname{tr}_{m}\,(a\otimes e_{i_{1}j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{m}j_{m}})=\delta_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{m}j_{m}}a,~a\in\mathcal{A}.

Consider the elements
\tau_{k}(u)
in
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\bigl[\hskip-0.85358pt\bigl[u^{-1}\bigr]\hskip-0.85358pt\bigr]
defined as follows:

\tau_{k}(u)=\frac{1}{k!}\operatorname{tr}_{k}A_{k}T_{1}(u)\cdots T_{k}(u-k+1).

Proposition 6.5

(
[
26
, Proposition 1.14.1]
)
.

The coefficients of the series

\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
.

Observe that for any complex
n\times n
matrix
C

also satisfies the ternary relation (

). Indeed, this is a consequence of the fact that the element

commutes with the image of

in
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes m}
. Thus, one can generalize Proposition

to construct commutative subalgebras of the Yangian which depend on a parameter in
\operatorname{End}\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Corollary 6.6

(
[
26
, Proposition 1.14.2]
)
.

For any complex

generate a commutative subalgebra of
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
.

Corollary 6.7

(
[
26
, Proposition 1.14.3]
)
.

For any complex

generate a commutative subalgebra of
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
.

.

The commutative subalgebras

In the notation of Corollaries

6.6
Application to Capelli identities

Let us show how the
R
-matrix formalism can be used in order to prove various identities related to the Capelli identity.

Lemma 6.10
.

For any
k
-tuples
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
in
[n]^{k}
, we have the following identity in
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})[z]
:

\operatorname{rdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(z+\alpha-1)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}=\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(z+k-\beta)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}.

In particular, these expressions are skew-symmetric in
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
and
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
, respectively.

Proof.

We start with the ternary relation for
m=k
(see Propositions
6.3
and
6.4
):

A_{k}T_{1}(u)\cdots T_{k}(u-k+1)=T_{k}(u-k+1)\cdots T_{1}(u)A_{k}.

Now let us apply both sides as operators on
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}
to the vector
e_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{j_{k}}
.
The left-hand side gives

\displaystyle(A_{k}T_{1}(u)\cdots T_{k}(u-k+1))(e_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{l_{1},\dots,l_{k}=1}^{n}t_{l_{1}j_{1}}(u)\cdots t_{l_{k}j_{k}}(u-k+1)\cdot A_{k}(e_{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{l_{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{l_{1},\dots,l_{k}=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)t_{l_{1}j_{1}}(u)\cdots t_{l_{k}j_{k}}(u-k+1)\cdot e_{l_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{l_{\sigma(k)}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{l_{1},\dots,l_{k}=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)t_{l_{\sigma(1)}j_{1}}(u)\cdots t_{l_{\sigma(k)}j_{k}}(u-k+1)\cdot e_{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{l_{k}},

while the right-hand side gives

\displaystyle(T_{k}(u-k+1)\cdots T_{1}(u)A_{k})(e_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(T_{k}(u-k+1)\cdots T_{1}(u))\bigl(e_{j_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{j_{\sigma(k)}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\sum_{l_{1},\dots,l_{k}=1}^{n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)t_{l_{1}j_{\sigma(1)}}(u-k+1)\cdots t_{l_{k}j_{\sigma(k)}}(u)\cdot e_{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{l_{k}}.

Comparing the coefficients of
e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{k}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)t_{i_{\sigma(1)}j_{1}}(u)\cdots t_{i_{\sigma(k)}j_{k}}(u-k+1)\\
&\qquad{}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)t_{i_{1}j_{\sigma(1)}}(u-k+1)\cdots t_{i_{k}j_{\sigma(k)}}(u).\end{aligned}

(6.2)

Then, we apply the evaluation map to both sides and note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{ev}(\text{LHS of \eqref{t_minor_skew_sym}})=\bigl(u^{\underline{k}}\bigr)^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{p=1,\dots,k}\bigl(E_{i_{\sigma(p)}j_{p}}+(u-p+1)\delta_{i_{\sigma(p)}j_{p}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{ev}(\text{LHS of \eqref{t_minor_skew_sym}})}{}=\bigl(u^{\underline{k}}\bigr)^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(u-\beta+1)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k},

\displaystyle\mathrm{ev}(\text{RHS of \eqref{t_minor_skew_sym}})=\bigl(u^{\underline{k}}\bigr)^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{p=1,\dots,k}\bigl(E_{i_{p}j_{\sigma(p)}}+(u-k+p)\delta_{i_{p}j_{\sigma(p)}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathrm{ev}(\text{RHS of \eqref{t_minor_skew_sym}})}{}=\bigl(u^{\underline{k}}\bigr)^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{rdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(u-k+\alpha)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}.

Combining everything, we obtain

\operatorname{rdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(u-k+\alpha)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}=\operatorname{cdet}\bigl[E_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}+(u-\beta+1)\delta_{i_{\alpha}j_{\beta}}\bigr]_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{k}.

Substituting
u=z+k-1
finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 6.11

In particular, by plugging

7
Commutativity of big algebras

The primary aim of this section is to prove the following crucial result.

Theorem 7.1
.

The algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
is commutative.

Before we turn to the proof of the theorem, let us discuss its consequences for the big algebras of irreducible representations.

7.1
Big algebras of irreducible representations

For any dominant integral weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, we set
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)\coloneqq\mathscr{B}(V(\lambda))
, i.e., the big algebra associated to the finite-dimensional irreducible
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-module
V(\lambda)
of highest weight
\lambda
.

Recall from Proposition
3.11
that for any direct sum
V=\bigoplus_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-modules the big algebras
\mathscr{B}(V_{\alpha})
are homomorphic images of the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(V)
. Moreover, we have the inclusion
\mathscr{B}(V)\subset S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\bigl(\prod_{\alpha}\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha}\bigr)
and the map
\mathscr{B}(V)\to\mathscr{B}(V_{\alpha})
is induced by the projection
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\bigl(\prod_{\alpha}\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha}\bigr)\to S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V_{\alpha}
. In particular, the image of an element
F\in\mathscr{B}(V)
in
\mathscr{B}(V_{\alpha})
can be regarded as the “restriction of
F
to
V_{\alpha}
”.
More precisely, for any
Y\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
the linear operator
F(Y)\in\operatorname{End}V
preserves each subspace
V_{\alpha}
and its restriction to
V_{\alpha}
is the image of
F
in
\mathscr{B}(V_{\alpha})
evaluated at
Y
.

Now let us apply these observations to the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-module decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(n,r)
, i.e., to
\mathcal{P}(n,r)\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\colon\ell(\lambda)\leq r}V(\lambda)^{\oplus d_{\lambda}}
, where
V(\lambda)=V_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}(\lambda)
and
d_{\lambda}=\dim V_{\mathrm{GL}_{r}}(\lambda)
(see (
3.6
) for more details). Then, Proposition
3.11
implies that
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)
is a quotient of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
for any
\lambda
of the form
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r},0,\dots,0)
. In particular, the big algebra of any finite-dimensional irreducible polynomial
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-representation is a quotient of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,n))
. The discussion above together with Theorem
7.1
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 7.2
.

The big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)
of any polynomial finite-dimensional irreducible
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-representation
V(\lambda)
is commutative.

Remark 7.3
.

The commutativity of big algebras defined by means of the Kirillov–Wei operator was first shown by Hausel and Zveryk, see
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
. However, their proof involves quite non-trivial constructions such as the
Feigin–Frenkel center
and
Segal–Sugawara vectors
. Namely, they use explicit formulas for the Segal–Sugawara vectors for the Feigin–Frenkel center that were obtained by Yakimova in
[
38
]
.
Our proof of the commutativity of big algebras in type
A
is different and essentially elementary. It relies on direct calculations and known facts about the Bethe subalgebras in Yangians and representation theory of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1

Proof.

In view of Corollary
3.14
, it suffices to check that the operators
F_{p,q}

) and (

),

F_{p,q}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},J_{1}\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\

Note that in view of Proposition
3.5
, we only need to check the commutativity for

, i.e., for diagonal matrices. According to Proposition
3.16
, for

, we have

F_{p,q}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\

To show that the operators

commute, we will show that (see also (

) below)

L(\mathrm{ev}(\sigma_{n-p}(v,Y)))=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-p}(v(v-1)\cdots(v-\ell+1))^{-1}\cdot F_{p,\ell}(Y),\qquad Y\in\mathfrak{h}

and then use the commutativity of the Bethe subalgebra. (Here we use the notation from Corollary

Indeed, applying Lemma

\displaystyle n!\cdot\sigma_{k}(u,Y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{n}A_{n}T_{k}(u-k+1)\cdots T_{1}(u)Y_{k+1}\cdots Y_{n}

7.3
Big algebras and Bethe subalgebras

In fact, the images of elements of the Bethe subalgebra under the evaluation homomorphism can be regarded as elements of Kirillov algebra in the sense of the lemma below. This observation together with the formula (

) implies that, roughly speaking, big algebras are homomorphic images of Bethe subalgebras of the Yangian
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
.
The connection with Bethe subalgebras becomes more transparent if we recall that big algebras

are homomorhic images of the Feigin–Frenkel center
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g}_{-})

[
34
]
has shown how one can obtain explicit formulas for generators of
\mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g}_{-})
in type
A
from the description of Bethe subalgebra of the Yangian. To avoid the reference to the Feigin–Frenkel center, one may regard the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(V)
as a family of commutative subalgebras in
\operatorname{End}V
that depend on a parameter
\chi\in\operatorname{Spec}I(\mathfrak{g})=\mathfrak{g}\sslash G
. For a fixed
\chi
, the corresponding algebra is the homomorphic image of the
quantum shift of argument subalgebra

\mathcal{A}_{\chi}\subset U(\mathfrak{g})
(see
[
33
, Section 4]
). The work of Ilin and Rybnikov
[
18
, Theorem B]
implies
5
5

5

that the latter can be obtained as a certain limit of Bethe subalgebras, namely as
\mathcal{A}_{\chi}=U(\mathfrak{g})\cap\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}B(\exp(\varepsilon\chi))
.
Our formulas establish a more direct link between big algebras and Bethe subalgebras of
\operatorname{Y}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Consider the Laurent expansion

Denote

. Then we have to verify the condition (

) for the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-representation
\widetilde{\pi}=\widetilde{L}
, i.e., that for any

The goal of this section is to give a more explicit description of the big algebra for the case of symmetric powers

8.1
Kirillov algebras in the weight multiplicity free case

Note that Kirillov algebra
\mathscr{C}(V)

[

[

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
V

and any

, the operator

is diagonal in the weight basis of

. In particular, the Kirillov algebra
\mathscr{C}(V)

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition
3.5
.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

Note that since
V
is weight multiplicity free, the elements of the weight basis of

are determined uniquely up to multiplication by a non-zero scalar.

As was discussed in Section

, we can treat the elements of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,r))
as elements of the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\lambda)
by restricting the corresponding linear operators to a subrepresentation
V

\mathcal{P}(n,r)
which is isomorphic to

. For such a subrepresentation
V

in

(or simply by
F

Corollary 8.3
.

The big algebra
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))

\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.15
applied to

, both algebras
\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))
and
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))
are generated by the operators
\{F_{p,0}\}_{1\leq p\leq n}\cup\{F_{p,1}\}_{0\leq p\leq n-1}
. Hence,
\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))
.
∎

Recall that the medium algebra
\mathscr{M}(V)
is the subalgebra of
\mathscr{C}(V)
generated by

and

(

, Theorem 1.2]
an analogous statement is shown for the center of the algebra
(U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\operatorname{End}V(\lambda))^{G}
. The only consequence of Conjecture
8.4
that is actually relevant for us in this section is that the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
coincides with the medium algebra
\mathscr{M}(m\varpi_{1})=Z(\mathscr{C}(m\varpi_{1}))
. However, the latter fact holds as a consequence of Corollary
8.3
.

8.2
Description of the algebra
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}(S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))}

Now we apply our results to symmetric powers of the vector representation of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
.
From now on, we only consider
\mathcal{P}(n,1)
, i.e., we set
r=1
.
Denote for brevity
x_{j}=x_{j1}
and
\partial_{j}=\partial_{j1}
,
1\leq j\leq n
.
Then,
\mathcal{P}(n,r)=\mathcal{P}(n,1)=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is isomorphic to the symmetric algebra
{S(\mathbb{C}^{n})=\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}
. Note that the latter decomposition is also compatible with the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
-action. Namely, the
m
-th summand
{S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\simeq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{m}}
is isomorphic to
V(m\varpi_{1})
. In particular, each irreducible representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
occurs in
\mathcal{P}(n,1)
with the multiplicity at most
1
.

Denote by
\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}\}
the basis of the weight lattice in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
that is dual to
\{E_{11},\dots,E_{nn}\}
in
\mathfrak{h}
. The fundamental weights
\varpi_{1},\dots,\varpi_{n}
are given by
\varpi_{k}=\varepsilon_{1}+\dots+\varepsilon_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq n
.
Recall that the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
consists of diagonal matrices with
n
entries. We identify
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})
with a polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
using the natural coordinates on
\mathfrak{h}
.
Then, by the Chevalley restriction theorem
[
17
, Chapter VI, §23.1]
,
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl_{n}}}\simeq S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}=\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
.

Proposition 8.7
.

For any positive integer
m
, the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
is generated by the operators
\{F_{p,1}\}_{0\leq p\leq n-1}
restricted to
V(m\varpi_{1})
over
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{P}(n,1)\simeq S(\mathbb{C}^{n})
decomposes into the direct sum
\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}V(m\varpi_{1})
as a
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-module. Thus, the second part of the proposition now follows from the proof of Corollary
8.3
.
∎

The following proposition gives a more explicit description of the big algebra for symmetric powers of the vector representation.

Proposition 8.8
.

Assume
\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
is the algebra of
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})
-valued functions on
\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
. There exists an injective
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}
-algebra homomorphism

\mathfrak{i}_{m}\colon\ \mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})\to\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})).

Namely, the map
\mathfrak{i}_{m}
sends an element
F\in\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
to a function
\hat{F}\in\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
such that the operator
F(Y)
acts as multiplication by
\bigl(\hat{F}(\mu)\bigr)(Y)
on
V_{\mu}(m\varpi_{1})
for all
\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
and
Y\in\mathfrak{h}
.

The image
\mathfrak{i}_{m}(\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1}))\simeq\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
is the subalgebra of
\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
generated by the following two subalgebras:

•

the subalgebra
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{0}
of constant maps
\mu\mapsto f
for
f\in S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}\simeq\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
, and

•

the subalgebra
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{1}
of maps
\mu\mapsto f(\underbrace{t_{1},\dots,t_{1}}_{\mu_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{t_{n},\dots,t_{n}}_{\mu_{n}})
for
f\in\Lambda_{m}
.

Here
\Lambda_{m}
stands for the ring of symmetric polynomials in
m
variables.

Remark 8.9
.

Using the language of symmetric functions, one can interpret the expression

f(\underbrace{t_{1},\dots,t_{1}}_{\mu_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{t_{n},\dots,t_{n}}_{\mu_{n}})

as the
plethystic substitution

f[\mu_{1}t_{1}+\dots+\mu_{n}t_{n}]
, where
f
is a symmetric function (see
[
25
, Section I.8]
for definitions).

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.5
, we can view
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
as a subalgebra of
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
According to Proposition
3.16
, for
Y=\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}
, we have

\begin{split}&F_{p,0}(Y)=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{p}}\prod_{i\in I}t_{i}=e_{p}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n}),\qquad\text{and}\\
&F_{p,1}(Y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\binom{[n]}{p}\\
j\in[n]\setminus I\end{subarray}}\biggl(\prod_{i\in I}t_{i}\biggr)\cdot x_{j}\partial_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{p}\bigl(t_{1},\dots,\widehat{t}_{j},\dots,t_{n}\bigr)\cdot x_{j}\partial_{j}.\end{split}

Now we restrict these operators to the subrepresentation
S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\subset\mathcal{P}(n,1)\simeq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
Consider the monomial basis
\{x^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})}
of
S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\simeq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{m}
. Here we denote
x^{\mu}\coloneqq x_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\dots x_{n}^{\mu_{n}}
and
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n})
runs over
\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
, that is, over all
n
-tuples of non-negative integers that add up to
m
. Clearly, the
\mu
-eigenspace of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{m}
is spanned by
x^{\mu}
for all
\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
.

The operator
x_{j}\partial_{j}
acts on
\{x^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})}
as
x_{j}\partial_{j}(x^{\mu})=\mu_{j}x^{\mu}
. Thus, the operators
F_{p,0}
and
F_{p,1}
are diagonal in the basis
\{x^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})}
and the corresponding matrices are as follows:

\begin{split}&F_{p,0}(Y)\leftrightarrow(e_{p}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\mid\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})),\\
&F_{p,1}(Y)\leftrightarrow\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}e_{p}\bigl(t_{1},\dots,\widehat{t}_{j},\dots,t_{n}\bigr)\mid\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})\Biggr).\end{split}

Let
\hat{F}_{p,q}
(here
q=0,1
) be the element of
\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
that corresponds to
F_{p,q}
. In other words, we set
\hat{F}_{p,0}(\mu)=e_{p}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
and
\hat{F}_{p,1}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}e_{p}\bigl(t_{1},\dots,\widehat{t}_{j},\dots,t_{n}\bigr)
.
Using the fact that the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
is generated by
\{F_{p,0}\}_{1\leq p\leq n}
and
\{F_{p,1}\}_{0\leq p\leq n-1}
, we can identify
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
with the subalgebra
\mathcal{A}
of the algebra
\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
generated by the elements
\bigl\{\hat{F}_{p,0}\bigr\}_{1\leq p\leq n}
and
\bigl\{\hat{F}_{p,1}\bigr\}_{0\leq p\leq n-1}
. Our goal is to prove that
\mathcal{A}
is generated by
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{1}
.

The elements
\bigl\{\hat{F}_{p,0}\bigr\}_{1\leq p\leq n}
are constant functions on
\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
and they generate the subalgebra
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{0}\subset\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
. Thus,
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}
.
Next, observe that

\hat{F}_{p,1}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}e_{p}\bigl(t_{1},\dots,\widehat{t}_{j},\dots,t_{n}\bigr)=\biggl(\mu_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}+\dots+\mu_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{n}}\biggr)(e_{p+1}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})).

Recall that the elementary symmetric polynomials
e_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
generate the ring
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
. Then, the Leibniz rule implies that
\mathcal{A}
contains all functions of the form

\mu\mapsto\biggl(\mu_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}+\dots+\mu_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{n}}\biggr)(f)\qquad\text{for}~f\in\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}.

In particular, by setting
f=p_{r}
, where
p_{r}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=t_{1}^{r}+\dots+t_{n}^{r}
is the
r
-th power sum, we obtain the function

\displaystyle\mu\mapsto\biggl(\mu_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}+\dots+\mu_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{n}}\biggr)(p_{r}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n}))

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}rt_{j}^{r-1}

\displaystyle{}=rp_{r-1}(\underbrace{t_{1},\dots,t_{1}}_{\mu_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{t_{n},\dots,t_{n}}_{\mu_{n}}).

(8.1)

Since the power sums also generate the ring of symmetric functions, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{1}\subset\mathcal{A}
.

It remains to show that the elements
\bigl\{\hat{F}_{p,0}\bigr\}{}_{1\leq p\leq n}
and
\bigl\{\hat{F}_{p,1}\bigr\}{}_{0\leq p\leq n-1}
also belong to the subalgebra of
\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
generated by
\mathcal{F}^{\smash{(m)}}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\smash{(m)}}_{1}
. Indeed, this is clear for
\hat{F}_{p,0}
as it belongs to
\mathcal{F}^{\smash{(m)}}_{0}
. As for the
\hat{F}_{p,1}
, note first that the function
\mu\mapsto\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}\bigr)(p_{r}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n}))
belongs to
\mathcal{F}^{\smash{(m)}}_{1}
due to the formula (
8.1
). Since the power sums
p_{r}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
generate the ring
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
, the Leibniz rule implies that the function
\mu\mapsto\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}\bigr)(e_{p+1}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n}))
belongs to the subalgebra generated by
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(m)}_{1}
.
∎

Remark 8.10
.

In fact, the proof of the proposition above can be easily modified to obtain a similar description of the medium algebra
\mathscr{M}(\lambda)=\mathscr{M}(V(\lambda))
for any
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
.

As an application of Proposition
8.8
, one can explicitly describe the big algebra of the vector representation of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
. Since
\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})=\mathscr{C}(\varpi_{1})
, this description recovers a special case of a result due to Panyushev
[
31
]
. For later use in the proof of Theorem
8.14
, we state it in the following form.

Corollary 8.11

(
[
31
, Theorem 2.6 for
\lambda=\varpi_{1}
]
)
.

The
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-algebra
\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})
is isomorphic to the
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}
-algebra
S(\mathfrak{h})^{W(\varpi_{1})}
, where
W(\varpi_{1})
denotes the stabilizer of
\varpi_{1}\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
in the Weyl group
W\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. More explicitly, we have isomorphisms

\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})\simeq\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}}\simeq\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}[T]/\bigl(T^{n}-c_{1}T^{n-1}+\dots+(-1)^{n}c_{n}\bigr).

Here,
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}
are defined as in Section
3.1
and
T
can be identified with
t_{1}
.

Proof.

For
m=1
, the set
\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})=\operatorname{wt}(\varpi_{1})
consists of
n
weights which lie in the
W
-orbit of
\varpi_{1}
. Then, it is not difficult to see that the subalgebra
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{1}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[t_{1}]
in this case (namely,
F\mapsto F(\varpi_{1})
is an isomorphism). Therefore,
\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})
is isomorphic to the subalgebra of
{\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]}
generated by
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
and
\mathbb{C}[t_{1}]
, i.e., to
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}}
.

The last assertion of the corollary follows from the fact that
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}}
is a free
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
-module generated by
1,t_{1},\dots,t_{1}^{n-1}
and the identity
t_{1}^{n}-c_{1}t_{1}^{n-1}+\dots+(-1)^{n}c_{n}=0
in
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
.
∎

Remark 8.12
.

The element
T
can be described in terms of the isomorphism from Proposition
8.8
. Namely, it corresponds to the function on
\operatorname{wt}(\varpi_{1})=\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}\}
that sends
\varepsilon_{i}
to
t_{i}
for
{i=1,\dots,n}
.

8.3
An isomorphism between
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})}
and
\boldsymbol{S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))}

For each positive integer
\alpha
, define an element
P_{\alpha}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))
by the formula

P_{\alpha}(Y)=L(Y^{\alpha}),

(8.2)

where
Y\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
is viewed as an
n\times n
matrix. One checks using (
3.2
) that
P_{\alpha}
is indeed an element of the Kirillov algebra
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{P}(n,1))
.

Proposition 8.13
.

The algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
is generated over the ring
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
by the elements
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
restricted to
V(m\varpi_{1})
.

Proof.

Note that for
Y=\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}
, we have
P_{\alpha}(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{\alpha}\cdot x_{i}\partial_{i}
.
It now follows from Proposition
3.5
and the proof of Proposition
8.8
that the restriction of
P_{\alpha}
to
V(m\varpi_{1})
coincides with the preimage of the function
\mu\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}t_{i}^{\alpha}
under the map
\mathfrak{i}_{m}
. In that proof we also showed that these functions generate the algebra
\mathfrak{i}_{m}(\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1}))
over
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}
.
Therefore, the elements
P_{\alpha}|_{V(m\varpi_{1})}
generate the algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
over
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
.
∎

Using the obtained facts, we can relate the big algebras of
\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})
and
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
.

Theorem 8.14
.

The
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
is isomorphic to the subalgebra
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
of
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
-invariant elements of the tensor product
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-algebra
\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})^{\otimes m}
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, all tensor products are considered over the ring
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}\simeq S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}
.
By Proposition
8.8
, for any positive integer
k
, we can view
\mathscr{B}(k\varpi_{1})
as a subalgebra
\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(k\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
. We use this identification for
k=1
and
k=m
.

Consider the map
\Upsilon\colon\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))^{\otimes m}\to\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
defined by the following formula:

\displaystyle\Upsilon(f_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes f_{m})(\mu)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\prod_{l=1}^{\mu_{j}}f_{\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{j-1}+l}(\varepsilon_{j})

for
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\operatorname{Func}(\operatorname{wt}(\varpi_{1}),S(\mathfrak{h}^{*}))
,
\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
.
It is clear that
\Upsilon
defined in this way is an
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-algebra homomorphism.

Now let us show that
\Upsilon
maps
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
to
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
under the identification above. Using the notation of Corollary
8.11
, any
f\in\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})
can be uniquely expressed as
a_{0}+a_{1}T+\dots+a_{n-1}T^{n-1}
with
a_{i}\in S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
.
Define the following elements:

X_{i}=1\otimes\dots\otimes\underset{i}{T}\otimes\dots\otimes 1
,
{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}
.

Claim
.

The subalgebra
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
is spanned by symmetric polynomials in
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
as a
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-module.

Proof.

As an
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-module, the algebra
\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})^{\otimes m}
is a free module spanned by
X_{1}^{i_{1}}\!\cdots\!X_{m}^{i_{m}}
with
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
. Since the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
permutes
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
, the
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-module
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1})^{\otimes m})
is free with generators
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}X_{1}^{\smash{i_{\sigma(1)}}\vphantom{{}_{0}}}\cdots X_{m}^{\smash{i_{\sigma(m)}}\vphantom{{}_{0}}}
, where
(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
runs over all
m
-tuples of integers such that
0\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{m}\leq n-1
.
(
In particular, the rank of this module equals
\binom{n+m-1}{m}
.
)
It is clear that these expressions are symmetric polynomials in
{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}}
and generate all of them since
e_{k}(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
are among the generators.
∎

Now let us prove that the image of the restriction of
\Upsilon
to
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
coincides with
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
. To start with, observe that for any
\mu\in\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
, we have

(\Upsilon(X_{1})(\mu),\Upsilon(X_{2})(\mu),\dots,\Upsilon(X_{m})(\mu))=(\underbrace{t_{1},\dots,t_{1}}_{\mu_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{t_{n},\dots,t_{n}}_{\mu_{n}}).

Therefore, for any polynomial
f
in
m
variables with coefficients in
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
, we have

\Upsilon(f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m}))(\mu)=f(\underbrace{t_{1},\dots,t_{1}}_{\mu_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{t_{n},\dots,t_{n}}_{\mu_{n}}).

(8.3)

In particular, if
f
is symmetric, then
\Upsilon(f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m}))\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{(m)}\subset\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
. Hence,
\Upsilon
maps elements of
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
onto
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
(see also Proposition
8.8
).

Finally, let us show that
\Upsilon
is injective on
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
. Suppose that
f
is a symmetric polynomial in
m
variables with coefficients in
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
such that
\Upsilon(f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m}))\equiv 0
on
\operatorname{wt}(m\varpi_{1})
. Since
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
is a free
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-module generated by
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}X_{1}^{i_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots X_{m}^{i_{\sigma(m)}}
with
0\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{m}\leq n-1
,
we may assume that
f
has degree at most
n-1
in each of the variables. The symmetry and (
8.3
) now imply that
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
vanishes for any
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}^{m}
. Now the following lemma, proved in Appendix
A
, implies that
f\equiv 0
.

Lemma 8.15
.

Let
f
be a polynomial in
m
variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
with coefficients in
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
which has degree at most
n-1
in each of the variables. If
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
vanishes for all
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}^{m}
, then
f\equiv 0
.

Thus, the map
\Upsilon\colon S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))\!\to\!\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
is indeed an isomorphism of
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
-algebras.∎

Remark 8.16
.

Let us explain why the isomorphism described in the proof of Theorem
8.14
is in fact “natural”.
Indeed,
S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))
and
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
can be regarded as certain subalgebras of
{S^{m}(S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V(\varpi_{1}))}
and
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})\otimes\operatorname{End}V(m\varpi_{1})
, respectively. If we evaluate these algebras at an element
Y=\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathfrak{h}
, we will obtain subalgebras of
S^{m}(\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}V(\varpi_{1}))
and
\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}V(m\varpi_{1})
, respectively, that depend on parameters
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
. The formula for the isomorphism
\Upsilon\colon S^{m}(\mathscr{B}(\varpi_{1}))\to\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
essentially comes from identifications
S^{m}(\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}V(\varpi_{1}))\simeq\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}S^{m}(V(\varpi_{1}))\simeq\operatorname{End}_{\mathfrak{h}}V(m\varpi_{1})
.

8.4
Generators and relations

In this subsection, we give a presentation of the big algebra
\mathscr{B}(m\varpi_{1})
in terms of generators and relations.
Recall that we identify
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*})^{\mathfrak{gl}_{n}}
with
S(\mathfrak{h}^{*})^{W}=\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
.

Theorem 8.17

Lemma 8.19
.

In the notation of the proof of Theorem

, we have

(i)

, where

(ii)

.

Proof.

(i) It is clear from (

) that
J\subset\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\rangle\cap R[u_{1},\dots,u_{m}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{m}}
. Take any element

that

permutes

, i.e.,

\sigma(j)=i\end{subarray}}(\sigma\cdot a_{j})=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\bigl(\tau^{-1}\cdot a_{\tau(i)}\bigr).

It is not difficult to see that for each

belongs to

, where

in
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
. Indeed, it follows from a more general observation that

.

Note that

. Moreover, the elements

The discussion above implies that

for all

(ii) Observe that for each

as polynomials in variables

with coefficients in

J\subset\bigl\{f(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in R[u_{1},\dots,u_{m}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\mid f|_{\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}^{m}}\equiv 0\bigr\}.

To show the other inclusion, take any element

that vanishes on

which has degree at most

and such that

in

vanishes on

as well and Lemma

implies that

. As

.

To compute
D(f,1)
, note that

D(f,1)=\biggl(y_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}+\dots+y_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\biggr)(f[x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}]),

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
f=p_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{\alpha_{l}}
for
\alpha_{i}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, i.e., that
f[x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}]=p_{\alpha_{1},0}\cdots p_{\alpha_{l},0}
. Then,

D(f,1)=\biggl(y_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}+\dots+y_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\biggr)(p_{\alpha_{1},0}\cdots p_{\alpha_{l},0})=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}p_{\alpha_{k}-1,1}\prod_{s\neq k}p_{\alpha_{s},0}\in DI_{n}^{1}.

Similarly, to check that
D(1,g)\in DI_{n}^{1}
, we may assume that
g=p_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{\alpha_{l}}
for some
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, i.e., that
g[x_{1}y_{1}+\dots+x_{n}y_{n}]=p_{\alpha_{1},1}\cdots p_{\alpha_{l},1}
. We obtain

\displaystyle D(1,g)={}

\displaystyle\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Biggr)(p_{\alpha_{1},1}\cdots p_{\alpha_{l},1})

\displaystyle{}{+}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{y_{j}(y_{i}+1)}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\Biggl(\prod_{k=1}^{l}\bigl(p_{\alpha_{k},1}+\bigl(x_{i}^{\alpha_{k}}-x_{j}^{\alpha_{k}}\bigr)\bigr)-\prod_{k=1}^{l}p_{\alpha_{k},1}\Biggr)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{\alpha_{k}-1}\Biggr)\prod_{s\neq k}p_{\alpha_{s},1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{y_{j}(y_{i}+1)}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[l]\\
I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\prod_{s\in I}\bigl(x_{i}^{\alpha_{s}}-x_{j}^{\alpha_{s}}\bigr)\prod_{s\notin I}p_{\alpha_{s},1}.

Observe that for any non-empty subset
I
of
[l]=\{1,\dots,l\}
the expression
\frac{1}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\prod_{s\in I}(x_{i}^{\alpha_{s}}-x_{j}^{\alpha_{s}})
is in fact a polynomial in
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
. Moreover, if
I
is not a singleton, then this expression vanishes when
x_{i}=x_{j}
. Thus, we can rewrite the formula above as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_{x,y}(p_{\alpha_{1},1}\dots p_{\alpha_{l},1})

\displaystyle\quad{}=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}x_{i}^{\alpha_{k}-1}\Biggr)\prod_{s\neq k}p_{\alpha_{s},1}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}y_{j}(y_{i}+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[l]\\
I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\biggl(\frac{\prod_{s\in I}\bigl(x_{i}^{\alpha_{s}}-x_{j}^{\alpha_{s}}\bigr)}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\biggr)\prod_{s\notin I}p_{\alpha_{s},1}

\displaystyle\quad{}=-\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}p_{\alpha_{k}-1,1}\prod_{s\neq k}p_{\alpha_{s},1}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}y_{j}(y_{i}+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[l]\\
I\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}Q_{I}(x_{i},x_{j})\prod_{s\notin I}p_{\alpha_{s},1},

where
Q_{I}(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{1}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\prod_{s\in I}(x_{i}^{\alpha_{s}}-x_{j}^{\alpha_{s}})
is a certain polynomial in
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
whose coefficients depend on
I
. Finally, note that for any polynomial
Q(u,v)=\sum_{a,b}Q_{a,b}u^{a}v^{b}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}y_{j}(y_{i}+1)Q(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{a,b}Q_{a,b}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}+1)x_{i}^{a}\Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}x_{j}^{b}\Biggr)

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{a,b}Q_{a,b}(p_{a,0}+p_{a,1})p_{b,1}\in DI_{n}^{1}.

This completes the proof of the fact that
D(1,g)\in DI_{n}^{1}
. Thus,
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_{x,y}
is an operator on
DI_{n}^{1}
satisfying (
8.15
).
∎

Remark 8.38
.

Note that a priori it is not clear that
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_{x,y}
descends to a well defined map on
\mathscr{B}(S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))
. It is only a consequence of the computations from Proposition
8.32
.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
8.15

In this appendix, we prove Lemma
8.15
. In fact, it is a direct consequence of Corollary
A.3
. The latter fact is one of the variants of the celebrated combinatorial Nullstellensatz by Alon
[
1
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]
(see also
[
20
, Theorem 4]
). For the sake of completeness, we present here a short proof of this result following
[
20
]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A
any finite subset of a field
\mathbb{F}
. Then, for any
k\in\{0,1,\dots,|A|-1\}
, we have

\sum_{a\in A}\frac{a^{k}}{\prod_{b\in A\setminus\{a\}}(b-a)}=\begin{cases}0,&k\in\{0,1,\dots,|A|-2\},\\
1,&k=|A|-1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Applying the Lagrange interpolation formula to the polynomial
x^{k}
and the elements of
A
(note that
k<|A|
) gives the identity

\sum_{a\in A}a^{k}\cdot\frac{\prod_{b\in A\setminus\{a\}}(x-a)}{\prod_{b\in A\setminus\{a\}}(b-a)}=x^{k}.

Equating the coefficients of
x^{|A|-1}
on both sides, we obtain the required equality.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\dots,X_{m}]
be a polynomial in
m
variables over a field
\mathbb{F}
of total degree
d
. Let
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}
be non-negative integers such that
d_{1}+\dots+d_{m}=d
. Then, for any subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
of
\mathbb{F}
such that
|A_{i}|=d_{i}+1
for all
i
, the
X_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots X_{m}^{d_{m}}
term of
g
equals

[g]_{X_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots X_{m}^{d_{m}}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\\
A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}\end{subarray}}g(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\prod_{a\in A_{i}\setminus\{a_{i}\}}(a_{i}-a)}.

Proof.

Because of linearity, it suffices to verify the identity in the case when
g=X_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots X_{m}^{i_{m}}
is a monomial such that
i_{1}+\dots+i_{m}\leq d
. Then, either
(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
, or
i_{j}<d_{j}
for some
j
. Applying Lemma
A.1

m
times now yields the required identity.
∎

Corollary A.3
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\dots,X_{m}]
be a non-zero polynomial in
m
variables over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Let
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}
be non-negative integers such that for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
the degree of
g
in the variable
x_{i}
is less than
d_{i}
. Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
be subsets of
\mathbb{F}
such that
|A_{i}|=d_{i}+1
for all
i
. Then,
g
cannot vanish on
A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}
.

Proof.

The corollary is a direct consequence of the proposition above. Indeed, assume that
g
vanishes on
A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}
and choose a monomial
X_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots X_{m}^{i_{m}}
of the largest degree which occurs in
g
. Then, by the assumption, we have
0\leq i_{j}\leq d_{j}-1
for all
j
. Choose arbitrary subsets
B_{j}\subset A_{j}
such that
|B_{j}|=i_{j}+1
. Applying Proposition
A.2
to
g
, the subsets
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
and the monomial
X_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots X_{m}^{i_{m}}
yields a contradiction.
∎

Now Lemma
8.15
follows from Corollary
A.3
applied to the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathrm{Frac}(S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*}))
.
