Comparative Efficacy and Safety of Bruton Tyrosine Kinase and Spleen Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors in Immune Thrombocytopenia: A Network Meta‐Analysis of Clinical Trials
Authors: Ayah Abulehia, Raneen Bilal Alshareef, Shorouq khateeb, Usra Ghanem, Amir Abadi, Nora Zatar, Peter Bael, Raghad Sweity, Hamid Ghanem, Hazem Ayesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Primary immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an acquired autoimmune bleeding disorder marked by low platelet counts due to immune‐mediated platelet destruction. The annual incidence ranges from 2 to 5 cases per 100,000 persons, with a higher incidence in females [1], and the global prevalence is estimated at 10 to 23 per 100,000 individuals [2]. While many patients are asymptomatic, ITP can cause significant bleeding, ranging from minor mucocutaneous bleeding such as bruising, purpura, and epistaxis to major life‐threatening bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage [3, 4]. ITP is classified as primary or secondary depending on the presence of underlying causes and further categorized by disease duration into newly diagnosed, persistent, and chronic forms [5].

The American Society of Hematology (ASH) guidelines 2019 recommend that treatment be guided by bleeding severity, medical history, and platelet count [6]. First‐line therapies include corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, and anti‐D immunoglobulin; however, durable remission is uncommon, necessitating second‐line treatments in many patients [7]. These include thrombopoietin receptor agonists, rituximab, splenectomy, and other immunosuppressants, yet many patients continue to have inadequate responses [7]. Despite its efficacy, splenectomy carries surgical risks and long‐term infection concerns, and some patients remain refractory [8].

These limitations have led to the development of targeted oral kinase inhibitors that disrupt key immune pathways related to platelet destruction. Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, including rilzabrutinib, have shown promising platelet responses in clinical trials and relatively safe side‐effect profiles [9]. Fostamatinib, a spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) inhibitor, has also been effective in increasing platelet counts in adults with refractory ITP. However, adverse effects such as diarrhea, hypertension, and elevated liver enzymes have been reported [10]. Despite these advances, evidence is largely limited to individual trials with varying designs. Direct comparisons between BTK and SYK inhibitors are lacking, leaving the optimal targeted therapy uncertain.

Network meta‐analysis (NMA) enables the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions by combining direct and indirect evidence through a common comparator. This approach allows estimation of the relative efficacy and safety of competing treatments and provides a hierarchy of treatment rankings. Accordingly, this systematic review and NMA aims to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of BTK inhibitors and SYK inhibitors against placebo in patients with primary ITP. The primary outcome was the number of patients with platelet response (≥ 50 × 109/L), while secondary outcomes included safety. Where feasible, an NMA was conducted; otherwise, findings were synthesized narratively to inform clinical decision‐making and future research.

Methods

Study Design and Registration

This NMA followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) and PRISMA for Network Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA‐NMA) guidelines, as detailed in Supporting Information S10. The study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) registry (https://osf.io/h6m7k/overview) [11].

Eligibility Criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the efficacy and safety of BTK inhibitors, specifically rilzabrutinib, and SYK inhibitors, HMPL‐523 (sovleplenib), and fostamatinib, in adult patients (aged > 18 years), diagnosed with primary (idiopathic) ITP, who met the baseline thrombocytopenia thresholds (platelet count < 30 × 109/L or < 50 × 109/L) defined by the primary trials. ITP was defined according to the ASH 2019 guidelines and international consensus reports [12]. Studies had to report at least one of the following outcomes: the number of patients with platelet response (≥ 50 × 109/L) versus placebo, and secondary outcomes included adverse event profiles, number of rescue therapies needed, and treatment discontinuation due to adverse events. We excluded studies that were nonrandomized or observational, conference abstracts or abstract‐only publications, studies that included participants without a formal diagnosis of ITP, were diagnosed with secondary ITP, investigated interventions other than the specified treatments, or lacked complete or accessible outcome data.

Search Strategy

A detailed database‐specific search strategy, including full search strings and MeSH terms, is provided in Supporting Information S1.

Study Selection

Of 1053 records identified (Scopus 421, Embase 402, Cochrane 87, PubMed 143), 416 remained after duplicates were removed. After excluding 402 irrelevant records (including non‐randomized, observational, or non‐interventional study designs) during the title and abstract screening, 14 full texts were assessed; nine were excluded, leaving five trials (two published in one paper) for inclusion. See PRISMA diagram in Figure 1.

The PRISMA flowchart for study selection.

Data Extraction

Four reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form. Collected variables included study characteristics, population criteria, follow‐up duration, sample size, and patient demographics. Interventions (rilzabrutinib, sovleplenib, and fostamatinib) were compared with placebo. Outcomes included platelet response, durable response, need for rescue therapy, adverse events, and treatment discontinuation. Rescue therapy was defined according to the criteria specified in the original trials, which generally included the administration of additional ITP‐directed treatment because of inadequate platelet response or clinically significant bleeding.

The risk of bias for each included study was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB2) tool [13], rating each domain as low (1), moderate (2), or high (3) risk, see Supporting Information S9.1–S9.4. In addition, we used the Confidence in NMA (CINeMA) framework to evaluate confidence in this network [14]. The certainty of evidence for each outcome using CINeMA is presented in Supporting Information S5.1–S5.14.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

We conducted a frequentist random‐effects NMA, using risk ratios (RRs) for binary outcomes and mean difference (MD) for continuous outcomes, with placebo as the reference. The lowest evaluated dose was selected for the primary analysis, as several trials used starting‐dose or dose‐escalation designs. This approach ensured consistency across studies and better reflected real‐world clinical practice. Treatment rankings were estimated using p‐scores (ranging from 0 to 1). The higher p‐score value, which is closer to 1, indicates the most effective treatment among all other remedies.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic, Tau2, and Cochran's Q test [15]. Global inconsistency was examined using design‐by‐treatment interaction models. To validate the NMA, we performed meta‐regression analyses to evaluate how differences in baseline characteristics (age, sample size, sex, duration of ITP, baseline platelet count, history of previous splenectomy, and/or number of unique therapies) might affect treatment outcomes. In addition, planned sensitivity analyses included excluding studies with a high risk of bias and conducting leave‐one‐out analyses to assess the impact of individual studies on the overall network estimates.

A frequentist framework was employed using the netmeta 2.9.0, R 4.4.1 package to synthesize both direct and indirect evidence from RCTs evaluating the efficacy and safety of included treatments; see Supporting Information S8.1–S8.14 and Supporting Information S6.1–S6.14.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

A total of five RCTs from four published studies involving 660 participants were included [16, 17, 18, 19]. All studies investigated the efficacy and tolerability of rilzabrutinib, sovleplenib, and fostamatinib in ITP patients. Studies evaluating orelabrutinib, ibrutinib, and zanubrutinib were identified but excluded, as they were single‐arm trials and did not meet the inclusion criteria. We identified three double‐arm trials investigating fostamatinib with a total of 123 participants (18.6%), one trial for rilzabrutinib with 133 participants (20.15%), and one trial for sovleplenib with 126 participants (19.09%). Of the included participants, 124 (18.78%) had previously undergone splenectomy but experienced subsequent ITP relapse. All the patients had prior steroid treatment. Baseline characteristics of the patients are given in the characteristics of studies and patients’ baseline features in Supporting Information S2 and S3.

Efficacy Outcome:

Number of Patients Achieving Platelet Response ≥ 50 × 109/L, Weeks 14–24

In this NMA, four studies were included; all active therapies demonstrated significantly higher response rates compared with placebo under a random‐effects model. Sovleplenib 300 mg once daily showed the greatest effect (RR = 30.51, 95% CI 4.33–214.90; p < 0.001), followed by rilzabrutinib 400 mg twice daily (RR = 16.60, 95% CI 2.32–118.92; p < 0.001) and fostamatinib 100 mg twice daily (RR = 6.51, 95% CI 1.62–26.08; p = 0.001). See Supporting Information S7.1. The estimated treatment ranking based on P‐scores indicated that sovleplenib had the highest probability of being the most effective therapy with a p‐score of 0.85, followed by rilzabrutinib (0.70) and fostamatinib (0.44).

No statistical heterogeneity or inconsistency was detected across the network (τ 2 = 0.00; I 2 = 0.0%; Q = 0.17, p = 0.68). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. Leave‐one‐out analyses demonstrated that any single study did not drive the overall conclusions; treatment effects for rilzabrutinib and sovleplenib remained consistently significant, while fostamatinib showed stable estimates with overlapping confidence intervals. Overall, heterogeneity and inconsistency were negligible, supporting the stability and reliability of the NMA results.

In univariable meta‐regression analyses, none of the examined baseline covariates significantly changes the treatment effect. Certainty of evidence according to the CINeMA framework was rated as low due to concerns of within‐study bias and imprecision.

Platelet Count Changes

Reporting the mean change in platelet count could not be pooled due to limited number of data points. For studies reporting medians, means, and standard deviations were estimated using established statistical conversion methods, assuming approximate normal distribution [20, 21]. Both studies showed increased platelet counts in the intervention groups compared with the placebo.

First study reported a mean platelet count of 78.04 ± 74.53 with rilzabrutinib 400 mg versus 30.06 ± 8.12 in the placebo group (MD = 47.98, SE = 25.02) [19]. The second study reported 84.88 ± 47.50 with fostamatinib 100 mg versus 27.76 ± 9.47 in the placebo group (MD = 57, SE = 24.4) [18]. Numerically, fostamatinib showed a greater platelet increase; however, this should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority due to study heterogeneity, lack of direct head‐to‐head comparison, and the absence of concurrent time‐to‐response kinetics data. Descriptive analysis of end‐of‐treatment platelet counts showed increases across active treatments; detailed results are presented in Supporting Information S11.

Safety Outcomes

Rescue Treatment

A total of four RCTs were included in this network to assess the number of patients requiring rescue therapy. See Supporting Information S7.2. Using a random‐effects model, all active treatments were associated with a lower likelihood of requiring rescue therapy compared with placebo. Fostamatinib 100 mg significantly reduced the need of rescue therapy (RR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.37–0.85), as did rilzabrutinib 400 mg (RR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.42–78). Sovleplenib 300 mg also demonstrated a reduction (RR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.39–1.00), showing a trend toward reduced need for rescue therapy; however, this did not reach statistical significance. Based on p‐scores, fostamatinib ranked highest (0.71), closely followed by rilzabrutinib (0.71), with sovleplenib ranked lowest.

No heterogeneity or inconsistency was observed across the network (τ 2 = 0.00; I 2 = 0.0%; Q = 0.25, p = 0.616). Certainty of evidence according to the CINeMA framework was rated as low due to concerns of within‐study and reporting bias.

Treatment Discontinuation due to Adverse Events

Five studies were included for this outcome. NMA results which presented in Supporting Information S7.3 showed that fostamatinib 100 mg demonstrated the lowest relative discontinuation risk (RR = 1.10, [0.29; 4.27]; p = 0.58), followed by sovleplenib 300 mg with a moderate discontinuation risk (RR = 1.97, [0.19; 20.32]; p = 0.55), while rilzabrutinib 400 mg showed the highest relative discontinuation rate due to adverse events (RR = 4.67, [0.51; 42.94]; p = 0.13), see Supporting Information S7.3. However, none of the treatments demonstrated a statistically significant difference in treatment discontinuation due to adverse events compared with placebo.

Based on p‐scores, fostamatinib 100 mg ranked as the most favorable treatment regarding tolerability (0.66), sovleplenib 300 mg (0.44), while rilzabrutinib 400 mg (0.17) showed the highest risk of treatment discontinuation due to adverse events.

Heterogeneity was low across studies (τ 2 = 0.19; I 2 = 13.4%), and there was no evidence of significant heterogeneity or inconsistency (Q = 2.31, df = 2, p = 0.32).

Sensitivity analyses generally confirmed the robustness of the results. Excluding high‐risk‐of‐bias studies and small‐sample‐size studies produced consistent, non‐significant effects versus placebo. However, moderate heterogeneity was observed in the leave‐one‐out analysis (I 2 = 47.5%), indicating some between‐study variability, while overall network tests showed no significant heterogeneity or inconsistency, and results remained stable across sensitivity analyses. Certainty of evidence according to the CINeMA framework was rated as low due to concerns of within‐study and reporting bias.

Adverse Events

Four studies were included for this outcome, and none of the active treatments demonstrated a statistically significant increase in overall adverse events, as presented in Supporting Information S7.4. Fostamatinib 100 mg showed an RR of 2.57 (95% CI: 0.33–19.93; p = 0.37), rilzabrutinib 400 mg an RR of 2.94 (95% CI: 0.20–43.27; p = 0.46), and sovleplenib an RR of 1.16 (95% CI: 0.08–16.20; p = 0.76).

Based on p‐scores, sovleplenib has the highest probability of being the most tolerable among the three, with a p‐score of 0.61, while fostamatinib 100 mg (0.34) and rilzabrutinib 400 mg (0.33) ranked lowest. However, given the wide confidence intervals and high heterogeneity, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Univariable meta‐regression analyses indicate that none of the examined covariates significantly change the treatment effect. Certainty of evidence according to the CINeMA framework was rated as low due to concerns of within‐study and reporting bias.

Detailed safety data for all secondary clinical outcomes are provided in the Supporting Information S12.

Discussion

This NMA provides the first quantitative indirect comparison of fostamatinib, sovleplenib (SYK inhibitors), and rilzabrutinib (BTK inhibitor) for primary ITP. It provides a structured framework to comparatively evaluate the relative efficacy and safety of these agents.

Our results show that all evaluated targeted therapies significantly improved platelet response (≥ 50 × 109/L) compared with placebo. sovleplenib 300 mg demonstrated the highest relative effect (RR 30.51, 95% CI 4.33–214.90), followed by rilzabrutinib 400 mg (RR 16.60, 95% CI 2.32–118.92), and fostamatinib 100 mg (RR 6.51, 95% CI 1.62–26.08). While the wide confidence intervals reflect the inherent imprecision of current trial data, our findings represent the best available evidence for comparing these novel agents. The consistent direction of effect favoring active therapy comparator, coupled with a clinically significant magnitude of platelet response, underscores the therapeutic potential of these inhibitors in refractory ITP despite the current lack of head‐to‐head data.

Safety analyses showed no statistically significant increase in overall adverse events compared with placebo. While fostamatinib and rilzabrutinib were associated with lower risks of rescue therapy use, suggesting adequate disease control, event‐specific safety profiles varied across treatments. The interpretation of the relative safety profiles must be done with adequate caution, taking into consideration that the current network is associated with some degree of statistical imprecision.

The therapeutic effects observed are consistent with the immunopathophysiology of ITP. SYK inhibition primarily disrupts Fcγ receptor‐mediated macrophage phagocytosis of antibody‐coated platelets, thereby reducing peripheral platelet destruction [22]. In contrast, BTK inhibition interferes with B‐cell receptor signaling, decreasing autoantibody production and downstream immune activation [23]. Importantly, it should be noted that the higher ranking of sovleplenib does not necessarily mean that SYK is better than BTK inhibition, as sovleplenib and fostamatinib, in particular, both target the SYK pathway. Both drugs target SYK, but differences in their kinase profile likely lead to differences in their effect estimates, as fostamatinib, a prodrug of R406, targets various kinases, including VEGFR2, which explains its adverse effects, such as hypertension [24, 25]. In contrast, sovleplenib was designed with greater SYK selectivity, potentially allowing more targeted and potent inhibition of Fcγ receptor‐mediated signaling in splenic macrophages [26]. Consequently, the observed differences in our analysis more likely reflect compound‐specific pharmacologic properties, dosing strategies, trial populations, or study design variations rather than the mechanistic dominance of one pathway. Thus, current data do not establish class‐level superiority between BTK and SYK inhibition.

Clinically, our findings provide solid evidence for prioritizing targeted kinase inhibitors in patients with refractory or persistent ITP who have not responded to multiple previous treatments, such as thrombopoietin receptor agonists or rituximab. The strong effect seen with sovleplenib shows the potential of highly selective SYK inhibition for quickly stabilizing platelet counts. On the other hand, the broader immune modulation linked to BTK inhibition by rilzabrutinib might be especially important for patients where B‐cell‐driven autoantibody production is a major factor in the disease's development. In addition, awareness of specific safety concerns, like the risk of hypertension associated with fostamatinib compared to the potentially safer profile of sovleplenib, supports a more personalized treatment plan.

Our NMA extends this literature by providing indirect quantitative comparisons and treatment ranking across efficacy and safety outcomes [27]. In addition, individual phase III trials have demonstrated the efficacy of fostamatinib in chronic ITP, while clinical studies of rilzabrutinib have reported rapid and durable platelet responses in previously treated patients. Similarly, Phase III data for sovleplenib have shown promising durable response rates at 24 weeks. Our network estimates align with these findings while offering comparative context that was previously unavailable due to the absence of direct head‐to‐head trials.

Targeted kinase inhibitors represent rational therapeutic options for patients with persistent or chronic ITP who are refractory to first‐line treatments such as corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, or anti‐D immunoglobulin [9]. The substantial relative increases in platelet response, particularly with sovleplenib and rilzabrutinib, suggest clinically meaningful disease control that may reduce bleeding risk and the need for rescue therapy. However, treatment selection should remain individualized. Consideration of bleeding phenotype, comorbid conditions, prior treatment exposure, safety profiles, route of administration, and patient preference is essential.

This meta‐analysis has several key strengths. We performed a comprehensive systematic search and included only RCTs, enhancing methodological rigor. By applying an NMA framework, we synthesized both direct and indirect evidence, enabling the first indirect comparison between SYK and BTK inhibitors in ITP, where no head‐to‐head trials exist. We formally assessed heterogeneity, consistency assumptions, and found that the findings were stable across sensitivity analyses. In addition, the use of the CINeMA framework strengthened the transparency and credibility of the certainty‐of‐evidence assessment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The overall sample size was modest for an NMA, resulting in statistical imprecision and wide confidence intervals for some comparisons. In addition, clinical heterogeneity existed across trials, including differences in patient populations, prior treatment exposure, and outcome definitions, particularly for platelet response, as most studies emphasized dichotomous thresholds (e.g., ≥ 50 × 109/L) rather than the kinetics of response or long‐term durability and bleeding outcomes, which are critical for characterizing the stability of these kinase inhibitors over time.

Furthermore, evidence for some interventions was derived from a single trial, particularly rilzabrutinib and sovleplenib, which may limit the robustness of the corresponding treatment effect estimates and rankings and increase susceptibility to study‐specific characteristics and statistical imprecision.

While the results were consistent, many treatment comparisons were based on indirect data rather than head‐to‐head trials. This makes the findings less certain. In addition, a lack of long‐term data and detailed reporting on side effects made it difficult to fully compare safety across treatments. The possibility of publication bias cannot be excluded, given the small number of available trials.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered RCTs with standardized outcome definitions including bleeding endpoints, comprehensive safety reporting including quality‐of‐life outcomes, and longer follow‐up durations. Direct head‐to‐head comparisons between SYK and BTK inhibitors are critically needed to validate the indirect comparative signals identified in this analysis and to inform optimal sequencing strategies in persistent and refractory ITP.

Conclusions

This NMA has shown the efficacy of targeted kinase inhibition through SYK or BTK inhibition in the management of platelet response in primary ITP. Sovleplenib has the best efficacy in the management of platelet response, followed by rilzabrutinib and fostamatinib. However, these results are drug‐specific rather than class‐specific. Safety was generally acceptable but varied by specific adverse events. These results support the growing role of immune signaling modulation in ITP and underscore the need for well‐powered, head‐to‐head trials with standardized outcomes to guide treatment.
