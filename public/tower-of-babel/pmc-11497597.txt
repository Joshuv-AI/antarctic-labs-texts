Severe Hypercalcemia in an Infant With Transient Disaccharidase Deficiency
Authors: Carly Barron, Shira Novack, Erealda Prendaj, Molly O Regelmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA previously healthy 11-month-old, full-term female developed growth failure, feeding intolerance, and irritability starting at 10 months of age. She was found to have a serum calcium level of 3.75 mmol/L (15 mg/dL) (reference range, 2.13-2.62 mmol/L; 8.5-10.5 mg/dL), consistent with severe hypercalcemia. She had bilateral nephrocalcinosis. Hypercalcemia improved with intravenous fluid hydration, but calcium rose again once intravenous fluids were discontinued and while consuming a low-calcium diet. Laboratory tests for common causes of hypercalcemia were negative. Stool-reducing substances were positive on 2 occasions. Endoscopic biopsy confirmed low disaccharidase levels. Her diet was changed to disaccharide-free formula, and hypercalcemia resolved. Genetic testing for congenital disaccharidase deficiency was negative. After repeat endoscopy demonstrating improved disaccharidase levels, disaccharides were incrementally reintroduced to the diet and calcium levels remained normal. This case demonstrates that disaccharidase deficiency, a rare inborn error of metabolism associated with hypercalcemia, may be a transient cause of hypercalcemia. The mechanism of hypercalcemia remains unknown.Keywords: hypercalcemia, disaccharidase deficiency, growth failure

Introduction

Disaccharidases are brush border enzymes located in the small intestine responsible for the breakdown of disaccharides into monosaccharides for absorption [1]. Congenital disaccharidase deficiencies, including congenital lactase deficiency and sucrase isomaltase deficiency, are associated with osmotic diarrhea and, in congenital forms, early-onset growth failure and malnutrition. Limited case reports have described congenital disaccharidase deficiencies associated with hypercalcemia, and the mechanism for hypercalcemia remains unknown [2]. The patient presented is an older infant with transient disaccharidase deficiency causing severe hypercalcemia.

Case Presentation

The patient was born at full term to nonconsanguineous parents of Pakistani descent after a reportedly uncomplicated pregnancy. Birth weight was reported to be appropriate. Family history was unremarkable with no known history of hypercalcemia, bone disease, or unusual/heritable malignancies. She had normal growth in weight and length through her 9-month healthcare maintenance visit. Her diet included appropriately mixed Similac® formula, oatmeal, and pureed vegetables and fruits. She was not taking additional vitamins or medications.

At 10 months of age, she developed intermittent nonbloody, nonbilious emesis; irritability; and less frequent stools attributed to decreased oral intake. She was only able to tolerate Similac® formula and refused previously tolerated foods. At 11 months of age, a 283 gram weight loss was noted over 3 weeks, with length maintained at the 66th percentile. Screening laboratory tests were significant for a serum calcium >3.75 mmol/L (>15 mg/dL) (reference range, 2.13-2.62 mmol/L; 8.5-10.5 mg/dL). The patient was urgently referred to the pediatric emergency department for further evaluation.

Diagnostic Assessment

At presentation, the patient was fussy but easily consoled. She was tachycardic and otherwise had age-appropriate vital signs. She did not have notable dysmorphology, including normally set ears and appropriate head circumference for age. The abdominal examination was normal, with no palpable masses. She had 3 normal-appearing lower incisors, and the anterior fontanel was described as open, flat, and appropriate in size for age. No signs of metabolic bone disease, tenderness to limb palpation, or muscle cramping were noted. The skin examination revealed no subcutaneous fat necrosis or neurocutaneous lesions. After laboratory tests were collected, she received a 20 mL/kg normal saline bolus, followed by intravenous (IV) hydration with 5% dextrose and normal saline at twice the maintenance rate. She was admitted for further evaluation and management of hypercalcemia.

Initial hospital laboratory tests confirmed a serum calcium of >3.75 mmol/L (>15 mg/dL) (reference range, 2.13-2.62 mmol/L; 8.5-10.5 mg/dL) and elevated ionized calcium of 1.71 mmol/L (6.84 mg/dL) (reference range, 1.10-1.35 mmol/L; 4.4-5.4 mg/dL). The intact PTH was suppressed at <0.52 pmol/L (<5.0 pg/mL) (reference range 1.59-6.89 pmol/L; 15.0-65.0 pg/mL). The other initial laboratory tests were unremarkable, including phosphate, magnesium, alkaline phosphatase, 25-hydroxy vitamin D, and serum creatinine (Table 1). The urine calcium to creatinine ratio was elevated at 0.86, consistent with an appropriate renal response to hypercalcemia. Thyroid function tests and liver function tests were normal, and the complete blood count had a mild elevation in white blood cell count (18.8 × 109 L, 18.8 k/µL) (reference range 6.0-17.5 × 109 L; 6.0-17.5 k/μL) and platelets (574 × 109 L, 574 k/µL) (reference range 150-400 × 109 L; 150-400 k/μL). Vitamin A level and inflammatory markers were also normal. Her electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm with normal corrected QT interval. Bilateral medullary calcinosis was noted on renal ultrasound (Fig. 1). A chest x-ray, uric acid, lactate dehydrogenase, and PTH-related peptide were normal, excluding a paraneoplastic process.

Laboratory tests ruled out hyperparathyroidism given the appropriately suppressed PTH and normal serum phosphate. Hyperthyroidism, vitamin D toxicity, and vitamin A toxicity were also excluded following review of laboratory tests. Adrenal insufficiency was determined to be unlikely based on physical examination findings, including lack of hyperpigmentation, as well as normal serum sodium and potassium. The suppressed 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D, negative QuantiFERON®-TB Gold, and normal chest x-ray ruled out the possibility of a granulomatous disease. The normal serum phosphate and low-normal alkaline phosphatase ruled out hypophosphatemia and metabolic bone disease. Idiopathic infantile hypercalcemia (due to CYP24A1 mutation/24 hydroxylase deficiency or NaP-Cotransporter 2A deficiency) was ruled out by the suppressed 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D. The elevated urine calcium to creatinine ratio, low PTH, nephrocalcinosis, and history of normal parental calcium levels excluded benign familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia. Although suspicion was low, William-Beuren syndrome was ruled-out by microarray; the microarray noted a 166 kb deletion of 19p12 that was of uncertain clinical significance (included genes ZNF728 and ZNF730).

Treatment

With IV normal saline and the elimination of calcium from the diet, the serum calcium normalized within 2 days. The patient was subsequently started on Calcilo XD® formula (2.9 mg calcium/100 calories for Calcilo XD® vs 78 mg calcium/100 calories for Similac®) and within a day was noted to have loose, watery stools. She remained on the Calcilo XD® formula. The infant was discharged home, and laboratory tests were monitored every 2 to 7 days in the outpatient setting. The infant was readmitted on 2 occasions for IV fluid hydration in the setting of recurrent hypercalcemia, which was defined as serum calcium trending above 2.75 mmol/L (11 mg/dL).

Given the diarrhea and rise in calcium on a very low calcium diet, an atypical presentation of a disaccharidase deficiency leading to increased intestinal absorption of calcium was considered. Multiple stool samples resulted in positive stool-reducing substances and low pH. In collaboration with the pediatric gastroenterology service, an endoscopic biopsy, the gold standard for diagnosis of disaccharidase deficiency [3], was performed, notable for normal histologic appearance of the mucosa. Two biopsy samples from distal duodenum were sent to Mayo Clinic Laboratories™ for disaccharidase analysis through spectrophotometry, and the results were consistent with generalized disaccharidase deficiency (lactase 5.2 Units, abnormal <15.0 Units; sucrase 15.5 Units, abnormal <25.0 Units; maltase 62.0 Units, abnormal <100.0 Units; palatinase quantity not sufficient). The gene analyses for sucrase isomaltase deficiency (SI gene) and congenital lactase deficiency (LCT gene) were also sent during the admission, and both had normal sequencing.

Thirty-two days after initial presentation, the patient was then admitted for initiation of a disaccharide-free diet and IV hydration for recurrent hypercalcemia. RCF® formula (carbohydrate-free soy formula) was initiated, with added dextrose (a monosaccharide); Calcilo XD® was discontinued. The RCF® formula had approximately 36 times more calcium than Calcilo XD® (105 mg calcium/100 calories for RCF® vs 2.9 mg calcium/100 calories for Calcilo XD®). The patient remained on IV fluids. Three and 4 days after exclusively being on RCF® formula, the patient received a trial of calcitonin (3 Units/kg/dose every 8 hours) without improvement in calcium. Seven days after being on exclusive RCF® formula, the calcium levels normalized. Serum calcium levels remained within normal limits on the exclusive RCF®/dextrose formula diet, and she was discharged home. Figure 2 highlights the trends for serum calcium with the interventions.

Outcome and Follow-up

In the outpatient setting, the pateint’s serum calcium levels remained normal (2.5-2.73 mmol/L, 10.0-10.9 mg/dL), PTH normalized, and her alkaline phosphatase level rose from 3.2 µkat/L (197 U/L) to 4.05 µkat/L (243 U/L) (normal < 8.35 µkat/L, <500 U/L). A second endoscopy, after normalization of serum calcium and while on a disaccharide-free diet for 2 weeks, revealed restoration of normal disaccharidase levels. Disaccharides were incrementally reintroduced into her diet over the course of several months, and her calcium levels remained normal. Eight months after disaccharides had been fully reintroduced to the diet, the serum calcium was 2.43 mmol/L (9.7 mg/dL) and stool-reducing substances remained negative.

Discussion

The differential diagnosis of hypercalcemia in infancy is broad and includes hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, vitamin A and D toxicity, adrenal insufficiency, paraneoplastic process, and benign hypocalciuric hypercalcemia [4, 5]. This patient underwent an extensive and unremarkable evaluation for these known etiologies. Given the low suspicion for adrenal insufficiency based on physical exam and normal serum electrolytes, as well as the lack of predictable diurnal secretion during infancy, cortisol and ACTH levels were not measured. This case report emphasizes the importance of considering disaccharidase deficiency in the evaluation of children and infants with severe hypercalcemia of unknown etiology. Disaccharidase deficiency is rare, and case reports in the literature are limited. Saarela et al described a case series of infants with congenital lactase deficiency; 7 of 10 infants had hypercalcemia at presentation and hypercalcemia resolved with lactose-free diet [6]. The more recent case report by Belmont et al discussed hypercalcemia in an infant with sucrase-isomaltase deficiency that was also not transient in nature [2].

The mechanism of increased calcium absorption associated with disaccharidase deficiency remains unclear. One proposed mechanism for the hypercalcemia was metabolic acidosis from diarrhea and dehydration leading to the release of calcium from bone [6]. The infant presented is particularly unusual given that the disaccharidase deficiency was transient and not congenital in nature. Transient disaccharidase deficiency may occur through damage to the intestinal mucosa associated with various disorders, including but not limited to celiac disease, Crohn's disease, and acute gastroenteritis. Lactose intolerance is the most common secondary disaccharidase deficiency and may be transient [7, 8]. The lack of response to calcitonin, which acts by inhibiting osteoclast activity and renal tubular resorption of calcium, further supports that the hypercalcemia was secondary to increased absorption at the level of the intestines [4]. Based on the mechanism of hypercalcemia and inadequate response to calcitonin, it was determined the patient would be unlikely to benefit from a trial of bisphosphonate therapy. After 7 days of the RCF® formula, calcium was normalized without further need for IV fluids. Therefore, in the absence of known genetic etiology for the disaccharidase deficiency, after stabilization of calcium levels through fluid hydration and removal of disaccharides from the diet, it may be reasonable to consider reintroduction of disaccharides to the diet with careful monitoring of calcium and stool-reducing substances.
