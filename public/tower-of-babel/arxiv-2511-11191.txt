Integrating Aggregated Electric Vehicle Flexibilities in Unit Commitment Models using Submodular Optimization
Hélène Arvis, Olivier Beaude, Nicolas Gast, Stéphane Gaubert, Bruno Gaujal

Abstract
The UC ( UC ) problem consists in controlling a large fleet of heterogeneous electricity production units in order to minimize the total production cost while satisfying consumer demand. EV are used as a source of flexibility and are often aggregated for problem tractability. We develop a new approach to integrate EV flexibilities in the UC problem and exploit the generalized polymatroid structure of aggregated flexibilities of a large population of users to develop an exact optimization algorithm, combining a cutting-plane approach and submodular optimization. We show in particular that the UC can be solved exactly in a time which scales linearly, up to a logarithmic factor, in the number of EV users when each production unit is subject to convex constraints. We illustrate our approach by solving a real instance of a long-term UC problem, combining open-source data of the European grid (European Resource Adequacy Assessment project) and data originating from a survey of user behavior of the French EV fleet.



I
INTRODUCTION

I-A

Context

As the installed capacity for renewable energy sources increases around the world, so does the need for flexibility of the electric power system to absorb their fluctuations. In particular, these flexibilities, including hydropower or
EV
(
EV
). must be correctly modeled in long-term optimization problems to evaluate their contribution to the system.
UC
(
UC
) problems are a focus of study in this context and consist in dispatching electricity to balance demand and production while respecting the constraints of large number of heteregeneous productions units,
generally including nuclear power plants (subject to specific dynamic constraints), thermal plants, hydroelectric plants, and other renewable enery sources.

The
UC
problem has been studied since the 1940s and progressively enriched by resolution techniques, as well as by the appearance of new production means, as demonstrated by the literature review of Abdou and Tkiouat
[
1
]
. We focus on the deterministic version of the
UC
, though it can also be studied in the context of uncertainties
[
31
]
.

Typically, the
UC
is a very large
MILP
(
MILP
), modeling dispatch decisions over hundreds of time steps with thousands of power plants in a country. A common approach to solving these problems is through aggregation, i.e., replacing a group of similar units by a class representative in order to reduce problem size and compute solutions in reasonable time.
The clustering of similar units has been explored by Meus et al.
[
20
]
, who demonstrate the benefits of aggregation in terms of tractability. However, aggregation generally leads to approximate solutions.

In this paper, we consider the specific case of
EV
charging profiles, in particular the dispatch of power through smart charging by a centralized operator. The correct integration of
EV
in long-term, thus high-level,
UC
problems has an impact on realism of solutions. In France in 2035, there will be an estimated 15.6 million light-duty
EV

[
29
]
; if all vehicles charge simultaneously at 6kW, this amounts to a national demand of 93.6 GW. In comparison, the French installed production capacity is estimated to reach around 210GW (with 90GW of controllable means) in 2035
[
30
]
, thus would be heavily impacted by uncoordinated charging.
Hence, our goal is to integrate an accurate and tractable model of
EV
flexibilities in global
UC
formulations.

Aggregating a large number of flexibilities boils down to computing, or approximating, the Minkowski sum of a large number of polytopes, where each summand represents the flexibility set of an individual user
or group of similar users.
This Minkowski sum generally has exponentially many inequalities; moreover, calculating the Minkowski sum of a family of polytopes defined by their facets is generally NP-hard
[
32
]
.
Therefore, techniques must be explored to avoid an explicit computation of this Minkowski sum. The traditional, “direct”, aggregation method consists in summing the characteristics of individual users so as to have a ”super-user” that represents the whole population. This generally leads to an aggregation error.
Special cases in which an exact and tractable aggregation is possible have been
identified, by Beaude et al.
[
3
]
where each local constraint set
has the structure of a (bipartite) network flow polytope, and recently
by Mukhi et al.
[
24
]
, where each local constraint set is shown to have a generalized
polymatroid structure.

I-B

Contribution

We study a general
UC

[

In particular, the algorithm scales well relative to the number of iterations and computed cutting planes.

Mukhi et al.

Independently (before the appearance of

Finally, Angeli et al.
[

]
undertook the aggregation of

flexibilities in the

II
THE
UC
PROBLEM AND
EV
FLEXIBILITIES

We consider a
UC
problem on a discrete time horizon
\mathcal{T}=\{1,...,T\}
with time steps of duration
\tau
. In practice, the horizon is often one week, while the time step is equal to an hour.

II-A

Modeling the
UC
problem

Consider a set
\mathcal{M}
of production units. Each element
m\in\mathcal{M}
represents a single unit, which may be a nuclear or thermal (gas, coal, etc.) power plant, as well as a hydroelectric plant.
The production of unit
m
is represented by a vector
\boldsymbol{z}^{m}=(z^{m}_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
, where
z^{m}_{t}
represents the production at time
t
.

We require the vector
\boldsymbol{z}^{m}
to belong to a
constraint set

\mathcal{K}^{m}
, which can differ according to the type of production unit.
This constraint set generally includes upper and lower bounds on production at each instant, i.e.,
\underline{k}_{t}^{m}\leqslant z_{t}^{m}\leqslant\overline{k}_{t}^{m}
for given bounds
(\underline{k}^{m}_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}},(\overline{k}^{m}_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
.
For instance, nuclear units are subject to ramping constraints, as well as to minimal on/off duration constraints. The latter lead to
MILP
formulations, rendering the corresponding set
\mathcal{K}^{m}
non-convex.

Variable (uncontrollable) renewable energy sources (solar, wind) are integrated via a residual demand
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
, obtained by subtracting the renewable production to the the power consumption, at every instant
t
.
Finally, we consider an aggregate of
EV
flexibilities with constraint set
\mathcal{P}
,
which aggregates the behavior of a large heterogeneous population of users.
We will elaborate on this set in the next section.

The
UC
problem is of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle\ \sum_{m\in\mathcal{M},t\in\mathcal{T}}c_{t}^{m}z_{t}^{m}

(1a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{m}z_{t}^{m}

\displaystyle=D_{t}+p_{t},\quad

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathcal{T}

(1b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{m}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{K}^{m},

\displaystyle\forall m\in\mathcal{M}

(1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{P}\kern 5.0pt.

(1d)

Here,
c^{m}_{t}
denotes the unitary production cost
at time
t
for the production plant
m
so that the total production cost is linear in the dispatching production variables
\boldsymbol{z}^{m}
. The variable
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
represents the charging power (positive or negative, in the case of
V2G
) of the fleet of
EV
. Finally, the relation (
1b
) expresses the balance between production and demand at every time step.

II-B

Modeling
EV
flexibilities

We consider
EV
constraints over the whole time horizon. For an individual, this requires the knowledge of charging availability slots, maximal charging power, and energy need at the end of each slot.
Consider an
EV

n\in\mathcal{N}
and its charging profile
\boldsymbol{p}^{n}
. At each time step
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

\underline{p}^{n}_{t}\leqslant p^{n}_{t}\leqslant\overline{p}^{n}_{t}\kern 5.0pt.

(2)

If the
EV
is plugged in at time
t
, then
\overline{p}^{n}_{t}
is equal to the maximal charging power available, typically based on charger limits, and
\underline{p}^{n}_{t}
is equal to the maximal discharging power (negative). We include
V2G
mechanisms, allowing vehicles to inject power back into the grid. In the absence of
V2G
technology,
\underline{p}^{n}_{t}\equiv 0
. If the
EV
is not plugged in at
t
, either because it is on the road or does not have access to a charging point, we have
\overline{p}^{n}_{t}=\underline{p}^{n}_{t}=0
.

At each instant, the
EV
also has a
SoC
(
SoC
)
\text{SoC}^{n}_{t}
, bounded by the battery capacity
\overline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}
and the minimal energy required at time
t
,
\underline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}
, giving that

\underline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}\leqslant\text{SoC}^{n}_{t}\leqslant\overline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}\kern 5.0pt.

(3)

The minimal energy required at time
t
is typically the energy required by the user to complete his or her next trip. Define
\gamma^{n}_{t}\geqslant 0
the energy consumed by driving during time step
t
.

Finally, we can link
\boldsymbol{\text{SoC}}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{p}^{n}
by

\text{SoC}^{n}_{t}=\text{SoC}^{n}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{p}^{n}_{t}-\gamma^{n}_{t},\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}

(4)

where
\text{SoC}_{0}^{n}
is the initial state-of-charge of the given user.

We can see that (
3
) and (
4
) can be combined into a constraint on the cumulative injected power:

\underline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}\leqslant\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}p_{t^{\prime}}^{n}+\text{SoC}_{0}^{n}-\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\gamma^{n}_{t^{\prime}}\leqslant\overline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t},\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}\ .

(5)

To simplify notations, let
\underline{s}^{n}_{t}=\underline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}-\text{SoC}_{0}^{n}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\gamma^{n}_{t^{\prime}}
and
\overline{s}^{n}_{t}=\overline{\text{SoC}}^{n}_{t}-\text{SoC}_{0}^{n}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\gamma^{n}_{t^{\prime}}
such that (
5
) can be rewritten as follows:

\underline{s}^{n}_{t}\leqslant\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}p_{t^{\prime}}^{n}\leqslant\overline{s}^{n}_{t}\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}\kern 5.0pt.

(6)

In this case, the individual
EV
constraint set is defined by

\mathcal{P}^{n}=\left\{\boldsymbol{p}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}\middle|\boldsymbol{p}^{n}\text{ respects \eqref{ev_power_bounds} and \eqref{ev_cum_bounds}}\right\}.

(7)

II-C

Aggregating
EV
constraints

The power profile set
\mathcal{P}
of constraint (
1d
) arising from the flexible
EV
is in fact the Minkowski sum of the individual power profile sets of the
EV
:
\mathcal{P}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
. In general, this set has a very rich face description whose complexity can grow quickly with
N
. This makes the
UC
(
1
) hard to solve.

To cope with the complexity of
\mathcal{P}
, a classical method is to aggregate the set constraints by using what we call a
naive
aggregation. This naive aggregation set
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is defined by summing the individual constraints:

\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}\middle|\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\underline{p}^{n}_{t}\leqslant p_{t}\leqslant\sum\limits_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\overline{p}^{n}_{t},&\forall t\in\mathcal{T}\\
\sum\limits_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\underline{s}^{n}_{t}\leqslant\sum\limits_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}p_{t^{\prime}}\leqslant\sum\limits_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\overline{s}^{n}_{t},&\forall t\in\mathcal{T}\end{array}\right\}

(8)

Note that the aggregation method defines at most
4T
constraints, thus
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
has at most
4T
facets.

It is immediate that
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is an outer approximation of
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
The inverse is not true, as taking the Minkowski sum creates “mixed” facets,
not obtained by a naive summation.
However, we shall see that one can leverage the special character of the defining constraints (
2
),(
6
) to obtain an exact solution of the
UC
problem.

GENERALIZED POLYMATROIDS AND SUBMODULAR OPTIMIZATION

In this section, we recall basic results on generalized polymatroids, based on Frank and Tardos
[
11
]
and Danilov and Koshevoy
[
8
]
. We also recall their application to
EV

[
24
]
.
The considered ground set is
\mathcal{T}=\{1,\cdots,T\}
where
T
is the planning horizon.

In what follows, when considering a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
, we use the notation
x(A)=\sum_{a\in A}x_{a}
.

III-A

Basics of generalized polymatroids

First, we define special types of set-functions.

Definition 1
.

A function
f:2^{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

is

submodular
if

f(A)+f(B)\geqslant f(A\cap B)+f(A\cup B),\quad\forall A,B\subseteq\mathcal{T}\kern 5.0pt.

(9)

A function
g:2^{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is
supermodular
if
-g
is submodular.

The function
f
is submodular if, and only if,

f(A\cup\{a\})-f(A)\geqslant f(B\cup\{a\})-f(B),\ \forall A\subset B\subseteq\mathcal{T}\kern 4.62497pt.

(10)

This can be interpreted as a decreasing marginal utility, i.e., adding an individual element to a smaller set
A\subset B
has more value than adding it to the larger set
B
. Submodularity may be thought of as an analog, for set-functions, of the notion of concavity.

Definition 2
.

Consider a submodular function
f
and a supermodular function
g
such that
g(\emptyset)=f(\emptyset)=0
. The pair
(g,f)
is said
paramodular
if the following cross-inequality holds:

f(A)-g(B)\geqslant f(A\setminus B)-g(B\setminus A),\quad\forall A,B\subseteq\mathcal{T}\kern 5.0pt.

(11)

We can note that paramodularity ensures that the space between the two functions is not empty, i.e.,

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}\

\displaystyle|x(A)\leqslant f(A),\ \forall A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\}\ \cap

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}\ |\ g(A)\leqslant x(A),\ \forall A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\}\neq\emptyset\kern 5.0pt.

Polymatroids, introduced by Edmonds
[
9
]
, are polyhedra defined by a submodular function as
\{\boldsymbol{x}\ |x(A)\leqslant f(A),\ \forall A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\}
.
This is inspired by the theory of matroids in which
f
is the rank function
[
33
]
.
Frank and Tardos
[
11
]
extended this notion to polyhedra defined by a paramodular pair, leading to the notion of
generalized
polymatroids.

Definition 3
.

Consider a paramodular pair of functions
(g,f)
. The polyhedron

Q(g,f)\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}\ |\ g(A)\leqslant x(A)\leqslant f(A),\ \forall A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\}

(12)

is a
generalized polymatroid
, or a
g-polymatroid
.
The pair
(g,f)
is called the
border pair
, with
g
the
lower border function
and
f
the
upper border function
.

Generalized polymatroids have also appeared in work by Danilov and Koshevoy in the broader setting of discrete convexity, deadling with polyhedra whose edges are parallel to a unimodular collection of vectors.
In particular, generalized polymatroids correspond to the special unimodular family
consisting of the vectors
e_{i}-e_{j}
and
e_{i}
for
i,j\in\mathcal{T}
originating from the root system

A_{n}
; we refer the reader to
[
8
]
for more information.

According to
[
11
, Prop. 2.5]
, the border pair of a given g-polymatroid
Q\neq\emptyset
is unique. In particular, we have
f(A)=\max_{x\in Q}x(A)
and
g(A)=\min_{x\in Q}x(A)
for every subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{T}
.

One of the main advantages of these polytopes is the following property,
which is established in
[
12
, Thm. 14.2.15]
,
and also follows from
[
8
, Sec. 7]
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider paramodular pairs
(g^{i},f^{i}),\ i\in[k]
. Then

\sum_{i}Q(g^{i},f^{i})=Q(\sum_{i}g^{i},\sum_{i}f^{i})\kern 5.0pt.

(13)

In particular, g-polymatroids are stable by Minkowski sum.

The following key observation ensures that EV-flexibilities
are modeled by generalized polymatroids.

Theorem 2

(
[
18
,
24
]
)
.

For each
EV

n\in\mathcal{N}
, the set
\mathcal{P}^{n}
is a g-polymatroid.

As pointed out to us by
[
18
]
, this follows from the laminar character of the family of constraints.
Recall that a collection of sets
\mathcal{F}
is
laminar
if

\forall A,B\in\mathcal{F},\ A\subseteq B\text{ or }B\subseteq A\text{ or }A\cap B=\emptyset\kern 5.0pt,

(14)

i.e., any two sets are either contained in or contain each other or are non-intersecting.
It is straightfoward to see that the family of subsets
\mathcal{F}=\{\{t\}|t\in\mathcal{T}\}\cup\{\{1,\cdots,t\}|t\in\mathcal{T}\}
on which the initial constraints
2
,
6
are defined is a laminar set family.
For an arbitrary set function
f
(not necessarily submodular), the polyhedron

\{x|x(A)\leqslant f(A),\forall A\in\mathcal{F}\}

is in fact a generalized polymatroid (see
[
5
, Sec. 3]
,
[
8
, Sec. 6]
).

Without loss of generality, we assume that the initial collection of constraints (
2
) and (
6
) is tight. By this, we mean that, considering the border pair

(g^{n},f^{n})
such that
\mathcal{P}^{n}=Q(f^{n},g^{n})
, we have
f^{n}(\{t\})=\overline{p}^{n}_{t}
and
g^{n}(\{t\})=\underline{p}^{n}_{t}
, as well as
f^{n}(\{1,\cdots,t\})=\overline{s}^{n}_{t}
and
g^{n}(\{1,\cdots,t\})=\underline{s}^{n}_{t}
for each

. We denote by
(g,f)=(\sum_{n}g^{n},\sum_{n}f^{n})
the border function of the Minkowski sum
\sum_{n}\mathcal{P}^{n}
,
which is a g-polymatroid, by

and

2
.

.

(see
[

). This setting is described in

A

g(A)\coloneq f(\tilde{\mathcal{T}})-f(\tilde{\mathcal{T}}\setminus A)

[
12
]

.

(See
[

)
Every g-polymatroid

Therefore,

.
These polyhedra can easily be described as, given a paramodular pair

, the submodular function defining the base polyhedron is given by

f^{*}(A)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(A)&\text{ if }A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\\

SOLVING THE
UC
PROBLEM WITH
EV

We next show that the
UC

EV

In
Section

, we provide a theoretical complexity result.
Furthermore, we give in

IV-A

Polynomial-time solvability of the convex
UC
problem

In this subsection, we suppose that the constraint sets
\mathcal{K}^{m}

are polyhedra given by collections of linear inequalities,
so that the production
z^{m}
of unit
m
must belong
to the set

\mathcal{K}^{m}=\{z^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}\mid F^{m}z^{m}\leqslant b^{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
,
where
F^{m}
is a
r_{m}\times T
matrix and
b^{m}
is a column vector of dimension
r_{m}
,
where
r_{m}
is the number of inequalities.
This allows both for bound constraints and dynamic linear constraints on the production
but excludes minimal on/off duration constraints for nuclear plants (which
lead to nonconvex – in fact
MILP
– formulation). We refer to
this problem as the
convex

UC
problem.
The convexity assumption is useful to get a theoretical complexity result; it will
be relaxed in the practical method of
Section

IV-B
.

Our approach relies on the analysis of linear programs defined implicitly by separation oracles, which goes back to the work of Gröetschel, Lovász and Schrijver
[
14
]
. To obtain a tighter explicit bound, we use a recent
improvement of their approach
[
7
]
relying on
the cutting-plane method of
[
17
]
.

A key notion is that of a
polyhedral separation oracle

[
7
]
. Given a polyhedron
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by a finite collection of inequalities
(G_{i}x\leqslant h_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
,
with
G_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
,
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
a
polyhedral separation oracle
is an algorithm
which, given a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, asserts that
\boldsymbol{x}\in P
or returns
a violated inequality, i.e., an index
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
G_{i}\boldsymbol{x}>h_{i}
.
Note that this notion is more demanding than the classical notion
of strong separation oracle, used in
[
14
]
.
The number of inequalities
|\mathcal{I}|
is allowed to be exponential in
d
.

We work in the Turing model of computation, so we assume that
F^{m}
is a rational matrix and
b^{m}
is a rational vector, for all
m\in\mathcal{M}
.
Without loss of generality, after multiplying by common denominators, we will even assume that the entries of
F^{m}
and
b^{m}
are integers. We also assume that the bounds

\underline{s}^{n}_{t}
,
\overline{s}^{n}_{t}
,
\underline{p}^{n}_{t}
and
\overline{p}^{n}_{t}
arising in the description of the
EV
are rational numbers, and even integers, which is a matter of scaling
(choice of units of the charging vector
p^{n}
).
Then, we denote by
B
the maximal absolute value of all the integer coefficients appearing in the problem input, i.e., of the entries of the matrix
F^{m}
, of the vector
b^{m}
, and of the bounds

\underline{s}^{n}_{t}
,
\overline{s}^{n}_{t}
,
\underline{p}^{n}_{t}
and
\overline{p}^{n}_{t}
.
The vector-valued variable of the

UC
problem is the vector
((z^{m})_{m\in\mathcal{M}},p)\in(\prod_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}})\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
.
So the
dimension

(total number of scalar variables) is
d=(|\mathcal{M}|+1)T
.
In this context, given a vector
x=((z^{m})_{m\in\mathcal{M}},p)
, a polyhedral separation oracle either asserts that
x
belongs to the feasible set of the
UC
problem or returns either an inequality of the system
F^{m}z^{m}\leqslant b^{m}
violated by
z^{m}
, a generalized-polymatroid type inequality
p(A)\leqslant f(A)
or
p(A)\geqslant g(A)
violated by
p
for some subset
A\subset\mathcal{T}
, or a violated global demand inequality

\sum_{m}z_{t}^{m}\,\scalebox{0.8}{$\lessgtr$}\,D_{t}+p_{t}
for some
t\in\mathcal{T}
(when considering the demand
equality as two opposite inequalities).

Theorem 4
.

The convex
UC
problem integrating a collection of
N

flexible
EV
can be solved in a
number of polyhedral separation oracle queries bounded by
O(d^{3}\log(dBN)))
, with
d
the dimension and
B
the maximal absolute value of integer coefficients of the problem input.

Remark
.

This bound is independent of the bit-size of of the cost vector
\boldsymbol{c}^{m}
.

Before proving this theorem, we explain how such an oracle can be implemented
efficiently. Given a vector
x=((z^{m})_{m\in\mathcal{M}},p)
,
checking the validity of the inequalities
F^{m}z^{m}\leqslant b^{m}
or of the global demand
constraint is immediate (there are
\sum_{m}r_{m}+T
scalar constraints that can be checked
one by one). The hard part of the separation oracle consists in deciding
whether the vector
p
belongs to the Minkowski sum
\sum_{n}\mathcal{P}^{n}
, which
is decribed by
2^{T}
g-polymatroid type inequalities.
We show that this can be done in polynomial time.

Lemma 5
.

Let
f^{*}
be the submodular function defined as per (
16
), where

(g,f)
is the border pair of the generalized polymatroid

\sum_{n}\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
Then, a polyhedral separation oracle for the Minkowski sum
\sum_{n}\mathcal{P}^{n}

can be implemented in
O((T^{4}E(f^{*})+T^{5})\log(TW))

arithmetic operations, where
E(f^{*})
is the number of arithmetic operations needed
to evaluate
f^{*}
, and
W=\max_{A\subseteq\mathcal{T}}|f^{*}(A)|
the maximal absolute value of
f^{*}
. A strongly polynomial version can be implemented in
O(T^{5}E(f^{*})+T^{6})
arithmetic operations.

Proof.

The polyhedral separation oracle will be implemented by a reduction to
SFM
(
SFM
). In fact, since
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
is a g-polymatroid, define
(g,f)
the corresponding border pair. We can then define
f^{*}
as in (
16
) and
\mathcal{B}(f^{*})
the corresponding 0-base polyhedron. For a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
, define the extended vector
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{T},-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}x_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}+1}
.
In this case,

\boldsymbol{x}\in\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}\iff\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(f^{*})\kern 5.0pt,

(17)

meaning that the membership problem of
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
is equivalent to that of
\mathcal{B}(f^{*})
, and the separation problem for
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
can be deduced from that for
\mathcal{B}(f^{*})
.

Given a vector
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
, the separation problem is given by minimizing the submodular function
f^{*}-\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
. In fact, if for a minimal subset
A
,
(f^{*}-\boldsymbol{p}^{*})(A)\geqslant 0
, then
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(f^{*})
and
\boldsymbol{p}\in\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
, thus the solution is an exact aggregation. If
(f^{*}-\boldsymbol{p}^{*})(A)<0
, then the constraint
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}(A)\geqslant f^{*}(A)
is violated and yields a cutting plane. The corresponding cut for
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{P}^{n}
can be deduced from (
16
).

Therefore, the oracle consists in calling a
SFM
algorithm. Such an algorithm with a weakly polynomial complexity is given by
Iwata and Orlin
[
15
]
, while the strongly polynomial
algorithm is given by Orlin
[
26
]
.
This leads to the complexity bounds stated in the lemma.
∎

We now prove
Theorem

4
.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

Dadush et al.
[
7
, Thm. 1.1]
showed that if
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

is a polyhedron, and if
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, then
a vector in
P
minimizing the linear form
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
can
be obtained in a number of calls to a polyhedral separation oracle in
O(d^{2}\log(d/\delta))
, where
\delta
is a geometric condition number.

Owing to the aggregation of
N
charging constraint sets, we have that the maximal absolute value
\bar{B}
of the integers arising in the problem input satisfies
\bar{B}\leqslant BN
.
Moreover, it follows from
[
7
, p. 2703, para. 2, line 4]
that
\delta\geqslant 1/({\bar{B}}^{2d}d^{d+1})
.
This gives us the bound of
O(d^{3}\log(dBN))
for the number
of calls to the separation oracle.
∎

Corollary 6
.

The convex
UC
problem integrating
N
flexible
EV
can be solved
in
N\log N\operatorname{poly}(T,d,\log B,r)
arithmetic operations,
where
r=\sum_{m}r_{m}
is the total number of constraints satisfied by production units, and
\operatorname{poly}(\cdot)

denotes a function polynomially bounded in its arguments.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4
, the convex
UC
can be solved in
O(d^{3}\log(dBN))
separation oracle calls. By
Lemma

5
and the preceding remarks, the separation oracle can be implemented in
O(T^{5}E(f^{*})+T^{6}+r)
arithmetic operations.

We have
f^{*}=\sum_{n}f^{*,n}
, where
f^{*,n}
denotes
the submodular function defining the base polyhedron of
\mathcal{P}^{n}
,
replacing
f,g
by
f^{n},g^{n}
in (
16
). Therefore,
evaluating
f^{*}(A)
reduces to evaluating
f^{*,n}(A)

for each
n\in\mathcal{N}
, i.e.,

f^{*}(A)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}f^{*,n}(A)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{B}(f^{*}_{n})}x(A)\kern 5.0pt.

(18)

In practice, this involves solving
N

LP

with
T
variables
and
O(T)
constraints, of type (

in dimension

with a number of inequalities

can be solved by interior point methods in time
O(m^{1.5}n^{2}(L+|\log\epsilon|))

where
L
is the bit-length of the input, and
\epsilon
is the desired
accuracy, see
[
28
,
4
]
.
Moreover, the bit-size of
\epsilon
needed to recover
the exact solution is in
O(L)
, see
[
34
, Thm. 2]
.

In our case,
m=4T
,
n=T
, and
L=O(T\log B)
,
yielding a complexity of the evaluation oracle of
O(NT^{4.5}\log B)
.

By combining these elements, we obtain a final complexity of
O(d^{3}\log(dBN)(NT^{9.5}\log B+r))
for the resolution of the convex
UC
problem, with the announced dependence
of
N\log N
in the number of flexible
EV
.
∎

V-A

The European Grid Instance

Following the EU Electricity Regulation in 2019, the

In particular, the

In this paper, we exploit the data of the 2023.2 edition. In particular, this dataset gives a projected demand scenario on each node, installed generation capacities aggregated per type of production unit, capacity factors for concerned renewable energy sources, and interconnection capacities between market nodes for selected target years (2025, 2028, 2030, and 2033) and climatic scenarios (corresponding to weather conditions of historical years ranging from 1982 to 2016).

Using this data, we were able to implement a seven-country

VI
CONCLUDING REMARKS

We studied the integration of flexible
EV

in a unit commitment problem.
We showed that this problem can be solved exactly and
in a scalable way for instances
with a large number
N
of categories of flexible
EV
users,
with a theoretical complexity scaling in
N\log N
.
We also developed a practical cutting-plane algorithm,
which, on a real large-scale instance, converged in few iterations.
This work can be extended in several ways. First, improvements of the practical algorithm can be considered, such as exploring other strategies to add cutting planes. Next, it would be interesting to
obtain tight bounds on the true number of facets of the polytopes representing

EV
flexibilities
(experiments in small dimension
indicate that for typical instances, this number is far below the theoretical

O(2^{T+1})
bound); parcimonious approximations of these polytopes may also be
investigated.
Finally, it may be interesting to cover other types of flexibilities, such as hydropower.
