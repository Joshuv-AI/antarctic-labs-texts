Novel fixative solutions with superior preservation of structure and antigenicity are suitable for optical clearing, tissue staining, and flow cytometry
Authors: Kumiko Fujieda, Kazuhiro Furuhashi, Kenshi Watanabe, Yuriko Sawa, Akihito Tanaka, Tomohiro Kawazoe, Akiko Owaki, Keita Hattori, Chikao Onogi, Eri Koshi-Ito, Yu Watanabe, Shoichi Maruyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFixation of tissues and cells is an essential step in histological analysis; however, structural deformation and fluorescence signal attenuation caused by fixatives often pose challenges. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of a newly developed fixative, the FM Fix, by comparing it with conventional fixation methods in tissue staining, optical clearing, and flow cytometry analysis. FM Fix demonstrated staining properties comparable to those of standard fixatives and showed superiority with respect to tissue structure retention. Optical clearing allowed the visualization of fluorescent signals at greater depths. In flow cytometry using green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing cells, FM Fix minimized the loss of GFP fluorescence, preserved cell membrane integrity, and maintained cell size. Furthermore, the staining quality of human renal biopsy samples was equivalent to that of 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF). These findings suggest that FM Fix has excellent structural preservation, fluorescence retention, and antigen preservation properties, making it broadly applicable for histological evaluation and quantitative cellular analysis.

INTRODUCTION

Tissue fixation is a technique used to preserve the dynamic state of biological tissues in a static form, allowing ex vivo observations.1 Once biological tissues are removed from the body, they begin to lose the information inherent to their in vivo state. However, appropriate fixation enables the long-term preservation of biological information such as cellular morphology, tissue structure, and protein expression levels and distribution.1 During the preparation of tissue specimens, various procedures such as treatment with reagents, temperature fluctuations, and pH changes can cause tissue degeneration or deformation. Appropriate fixation before these steps minimizes these alterations.1 Therefore, the development of fixatives has enabled detailed tissue observation under a microscope, significantly advancing the fields of histology and pathology.

The most widely used fixative to date has been formaldehyde, commonly used in the form of 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) or 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA).2-4 NBF (10%) is highly stable under light and offers practical convenience; however, it generates formic acid upon oxidation, leading to a decrease in pH and a subsequent reduction in fixation strength, as well as the polymerization of formaldehyde. Therefore, methanol was added as a stabilizer. In contrast, 4% PFA is a fixative prepared from polymerized formaldehyde that does not contain stabilizers; however, it is prone to degradation by ultraviolet light and oxygen.2

In recent years, with the rapid development of imaging technologies such as advanced microscopy and fluorescent proteins, several limitations of conventional fixatives, including 10% NBF and 4% PFA, have become apparent. Fixatives act on the functional groups of proteins, forming intra- and inter-molecular crosslinks that alter their three-dimensional (3D) structures. Consequently, the higher-order structures of the epitopes (antibody-binding sites) may be modified, hindering antibody binding. Similarly, fluorescent proteins can also be denatured, resulting in weakened fluorescence signals. It has been reported that formalin fixation induces protein cross-linking, leading to altered protein folding and physical tissue shrinkage.5-9 Therefore, the information obtained after fixation does not necessarily reflect the true in vivo state, and the observed structures or signals are heavily influenced by the type and condition of the fixative used.

These issues are particularly significant in the emerging field of optical clearing. Traditionally, 3D tissue structures have been analyzed by reconstructing two-dimensional images from serial sections. In contrast, optical clearing techniques reduce light absorption and scattering by rendering fixed tissues transparent, allowing deep, 3D visualization of tissues and organs without morphological distortion when combined with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins.10,11 Transparent brain and nervous system tissues have become widely used in neuroscience research,12,13 and the application of optical clearing has expanded beyond neural tissues to include various organs and whole-body clearing in mice.14,15

In optical clearing, higher tissue transparency enables deeper imaging; however, it is equally critical to preserve the fluorescent protein signals, epitope antigenicity, and fine tissue architecture. To meet these requirements, various protocols tailored to the specific properties of the tissues being studied.15-17 Thus, tissue fixation is no longer merely a preparatory step for histological specimen preparation; it is a key factor that directly influences the quality of the scientific findings. As a result, ongoing efforts are being made to select the most suitable fixative for specific experimental systems and develop novel fixatives.

This study aimed to investigate the applicability of a novel formaldehyde-modified fixative, the FM Fix, in imaging technologies. The focus was primarily on the kidney, which has a complex 3D nephron structure, and conducted comparative evaluations of FM Fix and conventional fixatives in tissue staining for pathological diagnosis, optical clearing, and flow cytometry analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study approval

All animal protocols were approved by Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (approval number, 20351). All in vivo experiments were performed in compliance with Nagoya University Animal Facility regulations. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for Human Research and approved by Ethics Committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (approval number, 2010-1135). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for participation in Nagoya Kidney Disease Registry (N-KDR).

Animals

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River Inc (Yokohama, Japan). CX3CR-1green fluorescent protein (GFP) knock-in mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All mice were housed at a constant temperature and humidity with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had unrestricted access to water and food. Isoflurane (2–3%) was used for anesthesia during surgery and nephrectomy. After nephrectomy, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Fixative solution

The kidneys and blood were fixed in 10% NBF (Muto Pure Chemicals Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) and 4% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and FM Fix, respectively. FM Fix (s) (Code, 295-97201) and FM Fix (L) (Code, 299-97101) were provided by FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Human renal biopsy specimens were fixed using FM Fix (s), whereas FM Fix (L) was used for other analyses.

Histological analysis

For immunohistochemistry, kidneys extracted from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were incubated in the respective fixative solutions at 4 °C for 48 hours and paraffin-embedded. Human kidney biopsy samples were incubated in the respective fixative solutions at 4 °C for 24 hours and paraffin-embedded. Fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized using a Ventana Discovery Ultra system (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA) and stained using conventional procedures.

For immunofluorescent staining, kidneys extracted from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were incubated in the respective fixative solutions at 4 °C for 48 hours and paraffin-embedded. Paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized using a Ventana Discovery Ultra system. After incubation with the primary antibody, an anti-GFP antibody (1:200; cat. no. GFP-1010, AvesLab, Davis, CA, USA), at 4 °C overnight. The samples were then washed with PBS and incubated with a secondary antibody, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) goat anti-chicken IgY (1:200; cat. no. 410802; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 30 minutes at room temperature. Finally, the sections were mounted using the VECTASHIELD Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA).

Frozen tissue samples of GFP-positive cells in CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were used. Kidneys extracted from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were incubated in the respective fixative solutions at 4 °C for 48 hours, followed by incubation in 30% sucrose solution for 6 hours. Fixed kidneys were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and frozen. Thin sections were mounted using the VECTASHIELD Mounting Medium with DAPI.

Frozen tissue samples were immersed in an A1RMP confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and processed using the Imaris software (v8.4.2; Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland). Paraffin-embedded tissue samples were imaged using an inverse immunofluorescence microscope (Axio Imager M2; Carl Zeiss).

For human renal biopsy samples, the tissues were fixed with 10% NBF or FM Fix (s) before being embedded in paraffin. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Masson’s trichrome (MT), periodic acid-methenamine-silver (PAM), and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were performed.

To evaluate the antigenic retention of each fixative solution, renal biopsy specimens from patients with systemic lupus erythematosus, known to exhibit immune complex deposition of IgG, IgA, IgM, C1q, C3, and C4, were used. Frozen tissue samples were sectioned using a cryostat and subjected to post-fixation with acetone, FM Fix (s), or 10% NBF. Immunofluorescent staining was performed using the following antibodies: FITC-conjugated polyclonal rabbit anti-human IgG (γ-chain) antibody (1:100; cat. no. F0202; DAKO, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), anti-IgA (1:100; cat. no. F0204; DAKO), anti-IgM (1:100; cat. no. F0203; DAKO), anti-C1q (1:50; cat. no. F0254; DAKO), anti-C3 (1:100; cat. no. F0201; DAKO), and anti-C4 antibodies (1:100; cat. no. F0169; DAKO).

Multiphoton intravital microscopy of kidneys

Multiphoton intravital microscopy was performed as previously described.18 During anesthesia, the mice were placed on a heating pad to maintain their body temperature. To observe blood vessels, FITC-labeled 70 kDa dextran (cat. no. D1823, Invitrogen, UK) was administered intravenously at 0.5 mg per mouse. The kidney was immobilized in a custom-made holder after removing fat and connective tissue from the kidney surface. The vessels and second-harmonic generation in the kidneys were imaged using a multiphoton microscope (Nikon A1RMP) equipped with a 25× water immersion objective (Olympus) under a two-photon laser at 810 nm excitation. After image acquisition, volume rendering was performed using the Imaris software (Bitplane).

Optical clearing of fixed kidney samples

Optical clearing was performed as previously described.18 Anti-CD31 antibody Alexa Fluor 647 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA) and isolection GS-IB4-Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen-Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) were injected intravenously into the CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice. Sixty minutes after injection, the mouse kidneys were extracted. The kidneys were incubated overnight in the respective fixative solutions at 4 °C, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C in 30% sucrose in PBS. Fixed kidneys were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and frozen. Frozen sections were thawed in Tris-buffered saline (TBS). In the control group, acetone fixation was performed for 10 minutes before optical clearing. Samples were incubated in Permeabilization solution (0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in TBS) at 4 °C for 12 to 16 hours. Samples were then incubated in solution for Step 1 (33.3% (v/v) Omnipaque 350, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in TBS) and solution for Step 2 (50% (v/v) Omnipaque 350, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in TBS) at 4 °C for 6–10 hours each. Next, the samples were incubated in a solution for Step 3 (0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in Omnipaque 350) at 4 °C for at least 12 hours. Images were acquired using an A1RMP confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and processed using the Imaris software (Bitplane).

Flow cytometry analyses

We collected 300 μL of blood from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice, mixed it with 10 mL of lysis buffer (BD Biosciences Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA), incubated it on ice for 5 minutes, and then added 40 mL of PBS. The solution was centrifuged at 500 g for 8 minutes and washed twice with PBS.

Assessment of GFP-positive cells. The mixture was incubated with the following antibodies on ice for 30 minutes: anti-Ly6C-APC/Cyanine7 (cat. no. 128026; BioLegend), anti-CX3CR1-biotin (cat. no. 149018; BioLegend), anti-CD11b-PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (cat. no. 101228; BioLegend), anti-CD45-Pacific Blue (cat. no. 103126; BioLegend), anti-Ly6G-PE/Dazzle (cat. no. 127648; BioLegend), Zombie Aqua (cat. no. 423101; BioLegend), and anti-CD16/32 (cat. no. 101302; BioLegend). After washing twice with PBS, the mixture was incubated with streptavidin-Alexa Fluor 647 (cat. no. 405237; BioLegend) for 15 minutes on ice. After washing twice with PBS, 200 μL of the respective fixative solution was added and reacted overnight at 4 °C.

Assessment of live cells with intact membrane. The mixture was incubated with the following antibodies on ice for 30 minutes: anti-Ly6C-APC/Cyanine7, anti-CX3CR1-biotin, anti-CD11b-PerCP/Cyanine5.5, anti-CD45-Pacific Blue, anti-Ly6G-PE/Dazzle, and anti-CD16/32. After washing twice with PBS, the mixture was incubated with streptavidin-Alexa Fluor 647 for 15 minutes on ice. After washing twice with PBS, 200 µL of the respective fixative solution was added and reacted for 2 hours at 4 °C. After washing twice with PBS, Zombie Aqua was added to the mixture and allowed to react on ice for 15 minutes.

Flow cytometry was performed using an LSRFortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences), followed by data analysis using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc, Ashland, OR, USA).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.R-project.org/). Means and standard deviations (SDs) were calculated for all the parameters determined in this study. Statistical significance between experimental groups was assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at P value <0.05.

RESULTS

FM Fix demonstrated comparable staining properties to conventional fixatives in both frozen and paraffin-embedded tissues

First, we assessed the staining properties of tissues fixed with FM Fix and embedded them using either cryoembedding or paraffin embedding. Kidneys from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were fixed with FM Fix, 10% NBF, or 4% PFA, followed by cryoembedding and assessment of GFP fluorescence intensity (Figure 1). Tissues fixed with FM Fix retained a GFP fluorescence intensity comparable to those fixed with 10% NBF or 4% PFA.

To evaluate the staining performance in paraffin-embedded tissues, kidneys from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were fixed with FM Fix, 10% NBF, 4% PFA, or ethanol, followed by paraffin embedding, and subjected to HE and PAS staining. Under intravital microscopy, the kidney vasculature was distributed around the renal tubules, with peritubular spaces visible between adjacent tubules (Figure 2A). It is widely known that most tissues undergo a certain degree of shrinkage when immersed in formalin fixative solution.19 Consistent with this, in tissues fixed with 10% NBF and 4% PFA, the peritubular spaces were lost due to tissue shrinkage, whereas those fixed with FM Fix retained them, demonstrating superior structural preservation. In contrast, tissues fixed with FM Fix preserved the peritubular space, indicating superior structural preservation. The PAS and HE staining quality was comparable among the FM Fix, 10% NBF, and 4% PFA fixes (Figure 2B). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry using an anti-GFP antibody on deparaffinized sections showed that antigen-antibody reactivity was preserved with FM Fix, and the GFP signal intensity was equivalent to that observed with conventional fixatives (Figure 2B).

FM Fix provides higher tissue transparency than conventional fixatives during optical clearing

To assess the staining compatibility of the FM Fix in optical clearing, the kidneys were harvested from CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice after labeling vascular endothelial cells with CD31 and isolectin B4. Tissues were fixed with FM Fix, 10% NBF, or 4% PFA, followed by optical clearing (Figure 3A). Kidneys fixed with FM Fix exhibited higher tissue transparency than those fixed with conventional fixatives, allowing significantly deeper visualization of GFP and endothelial markers (Figure 3B–D).

FM Fix preserves fluorescence intensity, cellular morphology, and membrane integrity better than conventional fixatives in flow cytometry

Next, the effects of FM Fix were investigated using flow cytometry. Leukocytes from the CX3CR-1GFP knock-in mice were isolated and fixed in FM Fix, 10% NBF, or 4% PFA. The GFP fluorescence intensity was analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 4A). Fixation with 10% NBF or 4% PFA significantly reduced the GFP signal, whereas FM Fix maintained a fluorescence intensity comparable to that of unfixed cells.

To assess the membrane integrity, Zombie Aqua staining was used to detect phosphatidylserine exposure. The cells fixed with 10% NBF or 4% PFA exhibited substantial membrane damage, whereas those fixed with FM Fix showed only minor damage (Figure 4B).

Moreover, the effects of fixation on cell size were analyzed by measuring the forward scatter (FSC) in monocytes (Figure 4C). Compared to 10% NBF and 4% PFA, FM Fix caused only a slight reduction in FSC, indicating that cell size was better preserved.

FM Fix preserves tissue architecture in human kidney biopsy samples better than 10% NBF

To evaluate the applicability of FM Fix to clinical specimens, human kidney biopsy tissues were obtained using a 16G needle and then fixed for 24 hours with either FM Fix or 10% NBF. The samples were embedded in paraffin and stained with HE, MT, PAM, and PAS (Figure 5). FM Fix preserved peritubular vascular spaces and prevents shrinkage, demonstrating superior structural integrity. The staining performance was comparable to that of 10% NBF.

FM Fix retains antigenicity in human kidney biopsy tissues

To assess antigen preservation, renal biopsy samples from a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus known to be positive for IgG, IgA, IgM, C1q, C3, and C4 were analyzed. Freshly frozen sections were cut using a cryostat and post-fixed using acetone, FM Fix, or 10% NBF. Immunostaining for IgG, IgA, IgM, C1q, C3, and C4 was performed (Figure 6). All antigens were detectable in FM Fix–treated samples, and the staining clarity was comparable to or superior to that of acetone- and 10% NBF-fixed tissues. Notably, FM Fix also preserved complement staining, which was diminished in tissues fixed with 10% NBF, thereby demonstrating its excellent antigen preservation.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the performance of a novel fixative, FM Fix, in comparison with conventional fixatives, including 10% NBF and 4% PFA, across various experimental applications, such as tissue staining, optical clearing, and flow cytometry.

In two-dimensional thin sections, the FM Fix showed staining properties and preservation of tissue architecture in HE, PAS, and immunofluorescence staining comparable to those of conventional fixatives, with a tendency to maintain tissue structure. A particularly noteworthy finding is that FM Fix the preserved tissue architecture and fluorescence signals during optical clearing, enabling deeper tissue imaging. This suggests that the FM Fix is highly compatible with optical clearing techniques aimed at visualizing 3D tissue networks and is expected to contribute to advances in intercellular network analysis.

In flow cytometry, FM Fix preserved GFP fluorescence intensity was significantly better than that of 10% NBF or 4% PFA, maintaining fluorescence at levels comparable to those of unfixed samples. Interestingly, FM Fix preserved the integrity of the plasma membrane in a near-normal state, even after fixation. This was evaluated using Zombie Aqua staining, which detects intracellular phosphatidylserine when membrane damage or structural fragility allows the dye to penetrate the cells. The proportion of Zombie Aqua–positive cells was lower in FM-Fixed-treated samples, indicating that membrane integrity was well preserved. Supporting this finding, FSC analysis, which reflects cell size, showed only a slight decrease in FSC values with FM Fix compared with 10% NBF and 4% PFA, indicating that cell size was preserved. This suggests that FM Fix has less of an impact on the plasma membrane and cytoskeleton than conventional fixatives. This observation is supported by previous studies, such as that of Pereira et al, who reported fixation-induced artifacts in the actin cytoskeletal structure using single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) when inappropriate fixation methods were used.20 Although fixation outcomes may vary depending on factors such as temperature and duration, our data indicate that FM Fix exerts minimal structural disruption on proteins and preserves the native conformation of both cytoskeletal components and fluorescent proteins, such as GFP.

Furthermore, fixatives play a crucial role in ensuring biological safety. It has been demonstrated that various viruses can be completely inactivated by fixation with agents such as formalin,21-23 and its importance has become even more evident since the COVID-19 pandemic. Studies involving human specimens or infected tissues often require fixed samples to ensure biosafety. The FM Fix is considered useful for translational research and clinical applications, as it preserves the performance of tissue staining and cell analysis while also ensuring safety.

This study evaluated the staining properties of FM Fix in human renal biopsy specimens and found that, compared to 10% NBF, there was no difference in staining quality and preserved tissue architecture. These findings suggest that FM Fix possesses sufficient performance for practical use in diagnostic pathology and holds promise for application in definitive diagnoses using human clinical specimens.

In summary, the FM Fix outperformed conventional fixatives in preserving tissue architecture and fluorescence signals across a range of experimental techniques. Its particularly high compatibility with optical-clearing techniques suggests that it may contribute to advancements in histological methods, particularly those involving 3D imaging. While this study did not address the effects of FM Fix on non-protein molecules such as nucleic acids or lipids, further investigation is necessary to explore its broader applicability and utility in biomedical research.
