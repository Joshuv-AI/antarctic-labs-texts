Yajieshaba extract improves alcohol‑induced liver injury by regulating hepatic lipid metabolism and gut microbiota
Authors: Yang Liping, Yu Xingzhi, Tao Jie, Duan Xiaohua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) refers to a wide spectrum of disorders including steatosis, hepatitis, fibrosis, liver cirrhosis and liver cancer, which is a disease with a high mortality rate [1]. Although research on ALD mechanisms is expanding, there is still a critical need for effective clinical strategies and safe medications for ALD prevention and treatment.

Numerous experimental and clinical studies indicate that gut microbiota significantly influences the progression of ALD [2, 3]. Alcohol consumption disrupts microbiota and compromises barrier integrity, facilitating the entry of gram-negative bacteria into the bloodstream and the accumulation of endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [4–7]. Endotoxins move from the gut to the liver through the portal venous bloodstream, where they interact with toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4) on hepatocytes, triggering signaling pathways that lead to dyslipidemia, inflammation and hepatocyte necrosis [8]. Research indicates that microbiota intervention can prevent ethanol-induced liver damage [9, 10]. Oral supplementation with probiotics, bacteria, and some immunomodulatory molecules can mitigate alcohol-induced liver damage [11, 12]. The gut microbiota is a potential therapeutic target for various liver diseases, and altering its composition with drugs may offer a promising approach for treating ALD.

Studies have consistently shown that Chinese herbal extracts are effective in treating and preventing ALD [13]. Dai medicine, one of China’s four major ethnic medical systems, is crucial for healthcare, particularly in Mekong countries. “Yajie” is known as Dai antidotes, and have been widely used to detoxify toxins obtained from food, drug and others. Yajieshaba, a well-known Dai antidote in capsule form, was created by Xishuangbanna Dai Hospital in Yunnan, China, and approved by the Yunnan Provincial Food and Drug Administration in 2008 (approval number Yunnan Pharmaceutical Production (Z) 20082252 K). The prescription includes eight drugs, each with specific quality standards and specifications to ensure ingredient stability (Supplementary Table 1) [14]. Yajieshaba is clinically utilized to treat hepatitis and gastritis caused by alcohol, medications, food, or toxic substances [15, 16]. The formula has demonstrated promising efficacy in clinical settings. A preliminary study involving 50 patients with alcoholic hepatitis reported that Yajieshaba treatment resulted in symptomatic improvement in all patients, corresponding to a 100% response rate within the context of that study [17]. Experimental research reveals that YJSB significantly reduces serum levels of ALT and AST in CCl₄-induced liver injury rats model [18]. Furthermore, Yajieshaba alleviates CCl4-induced liver injury and fibrosis through modulation of the Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway [19]. Additionally, Yajieshaba effectively prevents acute alcohol-induced liver damage by preserving intestinal barrier integrity [14]. However, the underlying mechanism of Yajieshaba on gut microbiota and its relationship with hepatoprotective effects have not been explored so far and needs further investigations.

This study utilized the Lieber-DeCarli liquid method to create a mouse model of alcoholic liver injury, aiming to assess the hepatoprotective effect of Yajieshaba. The liver lipid composition identified through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and the gut microbiota diversity assayed via 16s rRNA analysis. The findings provide valuable insights into the mechanism by which Yajieshaba prevents ALD.

Materials and methods

Extraction of Yajieshaba extract

Yajieshaba was sourced from Xishuangbanna Hospital of Dai Medicine and authenticated by Feng Dengqiang, a pharmacist at the Yunnan Key Laboratory of Dai and Yi Medicines. For extraction, the crushed Yajieshaba powder was immersed in three volumes of distilled water for 12 h at room temperature. The mixture was then heated to boiling and refluxed for 30 min. This decoction process was repeated two times. The extracts from each decoction were combined and filtered. The combined filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator with the water bath temperature set at 60 ± 5 °C. The concentration was terminated when a paste-like extract was obtained (no visible water evaporation), and the extract was stored at -20 °C until use. The final extraction yield was approximately 18%, calculated as (weight of dry extract / weight of crude herbs) × 100%. The resulting aqueous extract was reconstituted in distilled water to a concentration of 0.351 g raw drug/mL for animal administration.

Animals and experimental design

Male C57BL/6 mice (aged 8 weeks, weighing 20–22 g) were obtained from the Institute of Laboratory Animal Sciences (Sichuan, China). Mice were individually housed under specific pathogen-free conditions: 23 ± 2 °C, 45% humidity and a 12-hour light-dark cycle, with free access to food and water. The experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Yunnan University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. R-062021LH-130). After a 7-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 6 per group): control, model, low-dose Yajieshaba (YJSB-L), and high-dose Yajieshaba (YJSB-H). Following the reference approach [20], a pair-feeding protocol was implemented to eliminate caloric bias: the control group mice received a Lieber-DeCarli diet with 35% fat-derived calories, 18% protein, and 47% carbohydrate equivalents. The remaining mice groups were given a liquid diet with 5% ethanol, consisting of 35% fat, 18% protein, 11% carbohydrate, and 36% alcohol. Yajieshaba was administered daily via oral gavage to the treatment groups at two distinct doses: 1.17 g crude drug/kg (3×clinical equivalent) and 3.51 g crude drug/kg (9×clinical equivalent), sustained for 8 consecutive weeks, while the other groups received an equal volume of the solvent vehicle. The conversion formula used was: Animal Dose = Human Dose × (Km-human / Km-animal), where the Km factors are 37 for humans and 3 for mice. This dosing regimen was selected with reference to previously published studies, which have adopted the same doses [14]. After the final administration, mice were fasted for 12 h. Body weights were then recorded, and anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% pentobarbital sodium (0.2 mL/10 g body weight). Euthanasia was performed by exsanguination through abdominal aorta puncture, followed by blood collection and serum separation.

Serum biochemical analysis

Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT, cat. no. P2711S), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, cat. no. P2715S), total cholesterol (TC, cat. no. S0211S), total triglyceride (TG, cat. no. S0219S) and LPS (cat. no. C0276S) in mice were assessed using Beyotime test kits (Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Analysis of inflammatory parameters

Liver tissue was homogenized with PBS (1:9, w/v); after centrifugation, the supernatant was collected. Interleukin-1β (IL-1β, cat. no. SEKM-0002) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α, cat. no. SEKM-0034) levels were measured via Solarbio ELISA kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Assay validation confirmed intra-/inter-assay coefficients of variation < 10% for both cytokines, meeting standard quality control criteria.

Histological examination

Tissues from the liver or ileum were fixed by immersion in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h. For histological evaluation, samples were dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned into 4 mm slices, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). Lipid accumulation was evaluated by slicing frozen liver samples embedded in OCT and staining them with Oil Red O solution. A fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe the stained tissue sections above.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Frozen ileum slices underwent immunofluorescence analysis with anti-occludin or anti-ZO-1 antibodies (1:100, ProteinTech, Chicago, USA) at 4 °C overnight, followed by a 60-minute incubation with FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100, ProteinTech, Chicago, USA) at 37 °C in the dark. The images were then examined with a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Liver untargeted lipidomic investigation

Briefly, samples were first mixed with 200 µL methanol using a vortex mixer. Subsequently, 20 µL internal lipid standards were added into the mixture, followed by phase separation via addition of 800 µL methyl tertiary butyl ether. The mixture was sonicated for 20 min at 4℃, followed by 200 µL of MS-grade water and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. The organic solvent layer was isolated and dried under nitrogen. The analysis was conducted using a UHPLC system (Nexera LC-30 A, Shimadzu) linked to a QTRAP MS (6500+, Sciex). The analytes were separated using HILIC (Phenomenex, Luna NH2, 2.0 mm × 100 mm, 3 μm) and C18 column (Phenomenex, Kinetex C18, 2.1 × 100 mm, 2.6 μm). For RPLC separation, the column was maintained at 45℃. Mobile phase A consisted of 70% acetonitrile, 30% water, and 5mM ammonium acetate, while mobile phase B was an IPA solution. A gradient was applied starting with 20% B at 0 min, increasing to 60% B at 5 min, reaching 100% B at 13 min, and returning to 20% B from 13.1 to 17 min. The sample remained at 10 ℃ for the entire duration of the analysis. For HILIC separation, the column was kept at 40 ℃. Mobile phase A included 2mM ammonium acetate, 50% methanol, and 50% acetonitrile, whereas mobile phase B contained 2mM ammonium acetate, 50% acetonitrile and 50% water. A gradient was started with 3% B at 0 to 3 min, increased to 100% B between 3 and 13 min, maintained at 100% B from 13 to 17 min, and returned to 3% B from 17.1 to 22 min, all at a flow rate of 400 µL/min. The 6500 + QTRAP (AB SCIEX) operated in both positive and negative switch modes. The conditions for the ESI positive source were as follows: Source temperature at 400℃; Ion Source Gas1 (GS1) set to 50; Ion Source Gas2 (GS2) set to 55; Curtain Gas (CUR) at 35; IonSpray Voltage(IS)at + 5500 V. The ESI negative source conditions were as follows: Source temperature set to 400℃; Ion Source Gas1 (GS1) at 50; Ion Source Gas2 (GS2) at 55; Curtain gas (CUR) at 35; IonSpray Voltage༈IS༉at -4500 V. MRM method was employed for acquiring quantitative data via mass spectrometry. Polled quality control (QC) samples were set in the sample queue to evaluate the stability and repeatability of the system; detailed QC metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. MultiQuant was used for quantitative data processing, with QC samples processed alongside biological samples. For lipidomic differential comparisons, a false-discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method) was applied for multiple-testing correction, and significant differential lipids were screened using the criteria of VIP > 1 (from OPLS-DA models) and p-value < 0.05 (after FDR correction).

Gut microbiota analysis

Genes for 16 S rRNA were amplified from cecal contents after total genomic DNA extraction. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, incorporating index codes. The constructed library quality was assessed by Agilent Bioanalyzer and Promega QuantiFluor. Qualified libraries underwent double-terminal sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (San Diego, CA, USA). The data were presented as mean ± SE. A one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s test was used to evaluate significant differences between the groups. The relationship between intestinal microbiota and liver lipids was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Yajieshaba treatment significantly alleviated alcoholic liver injury

To assess the hepatoprotective effects of Yajieshaba against alcohol-induced injury, serum biomarkers and hepatic histopathology were systematically evaluated. Chronic ethanol exposure induced pronounced liver dysfunction, as evidenced by elevated serum ALT (Fig. 1A), AST (Fig. 1B), TC (Fig. 1C), and TG (Fig. 1D) levels compared to control animals. Notably, Yajieshaba administration dose-dependently attenuated these pathological increases, with the high-dose regimen showing the most pronounced protective effects in this mouse model. Furthermore, hepatic proinflammatory cytokines including TNF-α (Fig. 1E) and IL-1β (Fig. 1F) were substantially suppressed following Yajieshaba intervention, concomitant with reduced systemic LPS concentrations (Fig. 1G). Histopathological corroboration revealed extensive macrovesicular steatosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and disrupted hepatic cord architecture in ethanol-fed mice (Fig. 2), whereas Yajieshaba treatment markedly ameliorated these structural aberrations.

Effect of Yajieshaba on the liver lipid metabolomics in ALD mice

We conducted an untargeted lipidomic analysis on three groups of liver samples to assess impact of Yajieshaba on lipid metabolism. As can be seen from the principal component analysis (PCA) plot (Fig. 3A), the model group and the control group could be separated, and the Yajieshaba group can also be separated from the model group. The orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score map highlighted notable lipid differences among the three groups (Fig. 3B and C). The validity of the OPLS-DA model is confirmed through a permutation test (Fig. 3D and E). Lipid differences were analyzed using variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and p value < 0.05 as criteria for identifying significantly different lipid molecules, which were then visualized using bubble charts (Fig. 3F and G). As shown in Supplementary Tables 2 and Table 3, there are 97 distinct lipid molecules when comparing the control group to the model group, and 35 significantly different lipid molecules when comparing the model group to the Yajieshaba group. Heat maps were used to compare the relative abundance of differential lipids between the model group and the Yajieshaba group (Fig. 3H). We found that alcohol mainly changed the levels of phosphatidic acid (PA), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), phosphatidylcholine (PC), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylinositol (PI), cholinesterase (ChE), diacylglycerol (DG) and TG, while Yajieshaba mainly affected PA, PC, PE, PG, PI and TG. These outcomes indicated that Yajieshaba treatment can particularly affect the lipids in ALD mice.

Yajieshaba minimizes intestinal injury induced by alcohol intake

As shown in the Fig. 4, HE staining showed that mice in the model group experienced cellular breakdown, disorganized intestinal glands, a sparse and atrophic mucosal layer around villi, and villi structural damage. Contrastingly, Yajieshaba treatment decreased these morphological and pathological alterations. We measured the expression level of occludin and ZO-1 to assess the impact of Yajieshaba on the intestinal barrier. In the model group, occludin and ZO-1 protein expression were downregulated compared to the control group. However, Yajieshaba intervention effectively restored their expression levels.

Yajieshaba treatment changed the distribution of gut microbiota

The α-diversity can reflect the abundance and uniformity of microbial communities, is evaluated using the Shannon, Simpson, Chao 1 and observed-species. The Shannon and Simpson indices measure community diversity, while the Chao 1 and observed-species reflect species abundance. Our findings indicate no significant group difference (Fig. 5A). The Bray-Curtis-based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plot revealed distinct microbiota compositions among the three groups (Fig. 5B). Weighted UniFrac PCoA analysis revealed a notable difference in microbiota composition between the model and control groups. Additionally, the gut microbiota of the Yajieshaba group closely resembled that of the control group (Fig. 5C). The Venn diagram analysis identified a total of 64 shared operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among the control, model, and Yajieshaba groups. The model group demonstrated the greatest diversity with 4357 unique OTUs, surpassing 1718 in the control group and 1380 in the Yajieshaba group (Fig. 5D).

Yajieshaba modulated the composition of gut microbiota

Microbial profiling at the phylum level demonstrated predominant abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes across cohorts (Fig. 6A). Ethanol-exposed mice exhibited characteristic dysbiosis, including depletion of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia alongside enrichment of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria, these trends reversed through Yajieshaba intervention. This finding is consistent with previous research reporting similar outcomes [21]. Family-level taxonomic dissection identified marked reductions in beneficial taxa (Erysipelotrichaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae) following chronic alcohol exposure, with Yajieshaba administration effectively reversed these changes (Fig. 6B). Notably, genus-resolved analysis revealed Yajieshaba-mediated enrichment of mucin-associated Akkermansia and butyrate-producing Allobaculum (Fig. 6C), concurrent with suppression of alcohol-induced Desulfovibrio overgrowth.

Discussion

This study explored the mechanism of Yajieshaba on ALD mice, emphasizing the lipid metabolism-gut microbiome axis for the first time. Our findings indicate that Yajieshaba intervention ameliorates alcohol-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis in mice, ameliorated abnormal lipid metabolism, enhanced the intestinal mucosal barrier, reduced endotoxin-induced intestinal inflammation, and ultimately alleviated alcoholic liver injury (Fig. 8).

Graphical abstract. Yajieshaba ameliorates chronic ethanol-induced liver injury by modulating the gut-liver axis. Mechanistically, Yajieshaba can improve the structure of the dysfunctional gut flora and regulate the hepatic lipid metabolism, and at the same time, Yajieshaba can reduce the entry of LPS into the enterohepatic circulation by restoring the intestinal barrier function, thereby inhibiting the occurrence of liver inflammatory response

Serum ALT and AST levels serve as key biochemical markers for assessing hepatocellular injury severity [22]. Our results show that Yajieshaba significantly reduces alcohol-induced elevations in serum ALT and AST. HE staining results confirmed the hepatoprotective effect of Yajieshaba, which could alleviate the liver lobule damage and hepatocyte volume increase following alcohol exposure. In addition, Yajieshaba intervention could significantly inhibit the release of inflammatory cytokines in liver. These data confirmed the successful establishment of ALD mouse model and showed that Yajieshaba effectively mitigated ethanol-induced liver injury.

In general, hepatic steatosis is a direct consequence of alcohol metabolism, and the metabolic process of alcohol in the body requires the consumption of large amounts of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, resulting in inhibition of fatty acid β-oxidation, which ultimately leads to hepatic steatosis [23]. Our findings indicate that alcohol intake significantly elevated serum TG and TC levels, while Yajieshaba administration markedly reduced these concentrations. Aligned with histological observation of Oil Red O-stained liver sections, Yajieshaba treatment markedly reduced liver lipid accumulation.

Notably, gut-derived LPS is a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis of ALD [24, 25]. Excessive alcohol consumption disrupts the tight junctions of the intestinal epithelium and the protective barrier of the mucosa, resulting in increased intestinal permeability [26, 27]. The dysfunction of the gastrointestinal epithelial barrier allows LPS to translocate from the gut to the liver and systemic circulation, serving as a critical link between the gut microbiota imbalance and the development of ALD [28]. Alcohol-induced alterations in tight junction proteins further contribute to increased intestinal permeability and LPS accumulation [29]. In this study, Yajieshaba treatment significantly improved intestinal barrier function and reduced serum LPS level. These findings are consistent with the gut microbiota analysis results. Yajieshaba treatment reduced the abundance of Desulfovibrio, an endotoxin-producing pathobiont [30], while increasing the abundance of Akkermansia, which strengthens the intestinal barrier by promoting mucus production and maintaining the mucosal layer [31].

Long-term alcohol consumption significantly alters the abundance and diversity of the gut microbiota, and the resulting gut microbiota imbalance is an important risk factor for the development of ALD [32]. Studies have shown that alcohol consumption can cause an decrease in the abundance of Firmicutes and a increase in the abundance of Bacteroidetes, leading to gut microbiota dysbiosis [33]. Proteobacteria is also an important marker of gut microbiota imbalance and is considered a high-risk factor for the occurrence of gut microbiota dysbiosis and gastrointestinal diseases [34]. Similarly, in this study, we found that chronic ethanol exposure led to a decrease in the abundance of Firmicutes and an increase in the abundance of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria. However, treatment with Yajieshaba significantly reversed this trend. At the family level, Yajieshaba administration increased the abundance of the butyrate-producing families Lachnospiraceacee, Ruminococccaceae and Erysipelotrichaceae [35, 36]. Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) produced by the gut microbiome, has been shown to offer various health benefits. It reduces the synthesis of cholesterol and fatty acids in the liver while enhancing hepatic lipid oxidation [37]. Additionally, butyrate inhibits the migration and proliferation of immune cells and suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory factors [38]. Sodium butyrate has been shown to prevent the progression of simple steatosis to steatohepatitis through multiple mechanisms, as well as to mitigate the adverse effects of alcohol on intestinal junction proteins and hepatic inflammation [39, 40]. Studies have found that diammonium glycyrrhizinate can ameliorate liver steatosis in high-fat diet-fed mice by increasing the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae [41]. Additionally, berberine has been demonstrated to reduce blood lipid levels primarily by promoting the production of butyrate [42]. Correlation analysis revealed a strong association between differential lipids and Blautia. It is known that this genus is in the family Lachnospiraceae, one of the important intestinal floras that produces butyrate [43].

KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of microbial functions related to amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, cofactor and vitamin metabolism. These highly abundant pathways are suggested a potential mechanistic link between the altered gut microbiota and the observed amelioration of hepatic steatosis and inflammation. Specifically, the enrichment in carbohydrate metabolism implies enhanced microbial fermentation, likely leading to increased production of SCFAs. The significant enrichment in amino acid metabolism may indicate altered microbial processing of dietary proteins, potentially reducing the generation of harmful nitrogenous metabolites that contribute to oxidative stress and hepatocyte injury. The predicted enhancement in cofactor and vitamin metabolism suggests that the remodeled microbial community may improve the host’s micronutrient status. In summary, these functional alterations indicate that Yajieshaba may alleviate hepatic steatosis and inflammation, at least in part, by modulating microbial metabolic pathways that influence host lipid metabolism and immune responses.

Our study also has several limitations. First, the sample size for microbiota and lipidomics could be expanded in future work to strengthen the findings. Second, the lack of a positive control group precludes a direct efficacy comparison with known drugs. Future studies are planned to include such controls to rigorously evaluate Yajieshaba’s relative performance.

Conclusions

Yajieshaba may enhance the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria, and subsequently regulate lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, forming a virtuous cycle that improves gut-liver axis function. These effects work synergistically, collectively constituting a multi-target mechanism underlying the alleviation of alcohol-induced liver injury by Yajieshaba.
