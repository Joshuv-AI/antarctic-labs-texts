A comparative analysis of monascin and ankaflavin biosynthesis in Monascus purpureus and its albino variant
Authors: Tzu-Hsing Lin, Yi-hao Cheng, Chih-Hui Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRed mold rice (RMR), a functional food ingredient, is produced via Monascus spp. fermentation, a process that yields a diverse range of bioactive compounds, most notably the Monascus yellow pigments monascin and ankaflavin. This study provides the first report of spontaneous albinism in Monascus species. Although the specific mechanisms and conditions triggering the transition to albino strains remain to be definitively elucidated, our analyses reveal that the albino strain of Monascus purpureus exhibits a faster growth rate and significantly lower yellow pigment production compared to the wild-type strain. This suggests that the emergence of albino strains poses a considerable risk to the stability of industrial M. purpureus cultures. Furthermore, comparative analysis of gene expression associated with monascin and ankaflavin biosynthesis between M. purpureus wild-type and albino strains under various culture conditions indicates that the malate-citrate shuttle, specifically the mitochondrial dicarboxylate carrier (DIC), may play a critical role in yellow pigment biosynthesis. The comparative analysis also revealed significant correlations among glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), DIC, mrpigH expression, and overall yellow pigment production, suggesting a potential metabolic approach for enhancing M. purpureus pigment biosynthesis. Ultimately, given the profound differences in pigment yield and fermentation characteristics, these findings demonstrate that the occurrence of spontaneous albinism represents not only a critical, previously overlooked risk factor for industrial M. purpureus production, but also a possible approach for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of M. purpureus secondary metabolism.Key points• This study provides the first report of spontaneous albinism in Monascus species.• Expression of GAPDH, DIC, and mrpigH, as well as MS/AK production were significantly correlated.• Albino strain represents an overlooked risk factor for industrial M. purpureus production.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13883-1.

Introduction

Red mold rice (RMR), a well-known functional food ingredient, is produced through Monascus spp. fermentation. A variety of bioactive compounds have been identified from RMR, among which the Monascus yellow pigments—particularly monascin and ankaflavin—have attracted considerable attention in recent functional food research (Lin et al. 2017). Numerous studies have systematically investigated fermentation conditions to optimize the production of Monascus spp.-derived bioactive secondary metabolites (Huang et al. 2017; Li et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2017; Shi et al 2021). However, the extensive diversity of secondary metabolites and the complexity of metabolic network regulation in Monascus spp. present significant challenges to targeted metabolite production. Consequently, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms underlying Monascus spp. secondary metabolism has become a major research focus. Although advances in Monascus spp. genomics and the development of genetic engineering tools have gradually revealed the biosynthetic pathways involved in secondary metabolite production (Lakrod et al. 2003; Chen et al. 2017; Duan et al. 2022; Balakrishnan et al. 2017), further exploratory tools are needed to fully elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms. Mutant strains have long been fundamental tools in genetic research, offering valuable insights into various biological processes. This research investigates the phenotypic characteristics of M. purpureus albino strain and its regulatory mechanisms for monascin and ankaflavin biosynthesis. Differential pigment production and gene expression profiles were analyzed between M. purpureus wild-type and albino strains across various cultivation conditions to identify possible key biosynthetic drivers.

Material and methods

Monascus spp. strains, culture conditions, and PCR sequencing of ITS and β-tubulin gene

Monascus purpureus strain SA, GHA, HJT, LA, and TA were isolated from a Chinese herb medicine red mold rice in Taiwan. Monascus lunisporas BCRC 33640 was purchased from Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC, Hsinchu, Taiwan). Monascus spp. strains were maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plate at 28 °C and subcultured every 3 weeks. Albino M. purpureus strains SAa, GHAa, HJTa, LAa, and TAa were isolated from their counterpart wild-type strains during continuous subculturing. Albino strains of Monascus spp. emerging as a part of the colony (Fig. 1) were isolated and confirmed by PCR-ITS sequencing. Monascus spp. mycelium powder (50 mg) was used for DNA extraction using a QIAamp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Germany). DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit™ dsDNA BR assay kit (Life Technologies Corporation, USA) with a Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies). Partial internal transcribed spacer (ITS)- and β-tubulin genes were amplified and sequenced for phylogenetic analysis. The primer sets used in this study are listed in Table 1, and PCR amplifications were performed using EXSEL high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Bersing Ltd., Taiwan). Monascin and ankaflavin production level assay

M. purpureus spores were harvested from cultures grown in sporulation medium (each liter of medium contains 100.0 g sucrose, 0.5 g MgSO4·7 H2O, 2.0 g NaNO3, 1.0 g KH2PO4, 0.5 g KCl, 0.01 g FeSO4·7 H2O, 3.0 g yeast extract, and 5.0 g casamino acid) (Lakrod et al. 2003) at 28 °C and 150 rpm for 2 days. The resulting spore suspension was used for inoculation, establishing an initial inoculum density of 2500 spores/mL of pigment production medium (each liter of medium contains 50.0 g glucose, 5.0 g (NH4)2SO4, 4.0 g KH2PO4, 0.5 g KCl, 0.01 g FeSO4·7 H2O, 0.5 g MgSO4·7 H2O, and 0.01 g ZnSO4·7 H2O) (Huang et al. 2017). HPLC analysis samples were prepared by 95% ethanol extraction of eight-day-old dry mycelium/pellet at 50 ℃ for 1 h and subsequently filtrated through 0.45-μm PVDF filter. The HPLC analysis method was adopted from a previous report (Lee et al. 2007). Isocratic elution (water: acetonitrile: trifluoroacetate = 38:62:0.05 at 1 mL/min.) was carried out on a Hitachi D-2000 Elite HPLC system (Hitachi, Japan) with a LUNA C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size, Phenomenex, USA), fluorescence detector (L-2485 FL, Hitachi), and a photodiode array detector (L2455 DAD detector, Hitachi). The 385-nm absorbance chromatogram was used for yellow pigment quantification. Pure monascin and ankaflavin were provided by SunWay Biotech Co. Ltd. (Taipei, Taiwan). Experiments were carried out in three independent replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Monascin and ankaflavin biosynthesis related gene expression level assay

MrpigB plays a central role in the early stages of pigment biosynthesis. MrpigH and MrpigF catalyze the final steps in the production of yellow and orange pigments, respectively, from a common precursor (Chen et al. 2017). Previous studies have indicated that acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA are major precursors for pigment biosynthesis, and increasing cellular malonyl-CoA supply through genetic engineering has been demonstrated to enhance pigment production (Duan et al. 2022). Cellular acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA primarily originate from citrate produced by the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle within the mitochondria (Wang et al. 2021). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), a key enzyme in cellular energy metabolism, exhibits a close connection to the TCA cycle. Therefore, we hypothesized that GAPDH is involved in the regulation of M. purpureus pigment biosynthesis. The malate-citrate shuttle system represents a major route for citrate transport between the mitochondria and the cytosol (Fig. 2), with the DIC playing a critical role in malate transport across the mitochondrial membrane (Poirier et al. 2002). In this study, we analyzed the expression levels of genes involved in yellow pigment biosynthesis (mrpigB, mrpigH, and mrpigF) and those related to cytosolic malonyl-CoA supply (GAPDH and DIC) (Fig. 2). The expression patterns in wild-type and albino M. purpureus GHAa strains under various culture conditions were investigated to elucidate the mechanisms underlying pigment biosynthesis regulation. The initial culture conditions used for expression analysis were 5% glucose, an inoculum density of 2500 spores/mL at pH 5; subsequent conditions included 10% glucose, 15% glucose, 10% glycerol, 1 mM dihydrocoumarin (DHC) supplementation, 0.25 mM valproic acid supplementation, and 5% glucose at pH 3.Fig. 2Monascin and ankaflavin biosynthesis related genes for expression level analysis. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy® Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. (2019). DIC gene primers were designed using Geneious Prime software (Table 1). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using KAPA SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Sigma-Aldrich Co, Louis, MO, USA) at a primer concentration of 200 nM in a total reaction volume of 20 µL. Real-time qPCR reaction was performed in triplicate, with actin serving as the housekeeping gene (Li et al. 2019). ΔCt values were used to represent differences in target gene expression levels, and relative gene expression was calculated as 2−ΔΔCt. Experiments were carried out in three independent replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Albino variants Monascus spp. strains arising from prolonged subculturing or low temperature storage

During extended, repeated subculturing of approximately 60 Monascus spp. strains in our lab, albino variant strains were occasionally observed on PDA agar plate. Initially, the paler mycelia were suspected to be a contamination. However, subsequent isolation and purification confirmed that these pale phenotypes represented albino variant of the wild-type Monascus spp. strains. To investigate this phenomenon further, we tracked eight Monascus spp. strains through seven subcultures over approximately 5 months, documenting a total of eight albino emergence events, predominantly within M. purpureus. A consistent characteristic of these albino Monascus spp. strains was a visibly lighter mycelial color and notably larger colony size (Fig. 1a). Transfer of the albino strains to liquid culture, followed by re-streaking onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates, demonstrated that liquid culture conditions did not revert the albino phenotype (Fig. 1b). Analysis of pigment production in liquid cultures revealed a significantly reduced level of monascin in the albino strains compared to the wild-type (Fig. 1c). The albino mycelia typically emerged from the colony center and exhibited a distinctive fan-like outward growth pattern (Fig. 1d). The frequency of albino emergence was correlated with extended subculture intervals and low-temperature storage conditions.

The production of monascin and ankaflavin in M. purpureus GHAa was significantly lower than that of the wild-type strain

We examined the production of yellow pigments in the albino strain M. purpureus GHAa by comparing it with its wild-type counterpart, M. purpureus GHA, under varying glucose and glycerol concentrations. Results demonstrated that glycerol, used as the sole carbon source, significantly increased yellow pigment production compared to glucose in M. purpureus GHAa (Fig. 3a). Varying glucose concentrations (1%, 5%, 15%, 35%, and 45%) resulted in a peak in yellow pigment production at 5%, followed by 15%, with lower and higher concentrations resulting in decreased yields (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). Previous research has established that dihydrocoumarin, a food additive exhibiting HDAC inhibitor activity, can significantly enhance yellow pigment production in Monascus species (Hu et al. 2017). Therefore, this study further investigated the effects of dihydrocoumarin and valproic acid, another HDAC inhibitor (Göttlicher et al. 2001), on monascin and ankaflavin production in M. purpureus GHA and GHAa. Results demonstrated that even low concentrations (0.25 mM) of valproic acid drastically inhibited growth and monascin/ankaflavin production in M. purpureus GHA. Conversely, valproic acid elicited a slight increase in monascin production in the albino strain M. purpureus GHAa (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, supplementation of the culture medium with 0.25–1 mM dihydrocoumarin significantly increased monascin/ankaflavin production in the wild-type strain GHA; however, higher concentrations (4 mM) resulted in a significant reduction in pigment production. Regarding carbon source and initial medium pH, both the wild-type and albino strains displayed comparable responses, with maximal monascin/ankaflavin production observed at an initial culture pH of 5 and a glucose concentration of 5–10%. Discussion

During our investigations, we occasionally observed albino variants of Monascus spp. strains arising during prolonged subculturing, characterized by a significant reduction in pigment production and a concomitant increase in growth rate. Screening a range of Monascus spp. strains including Monascus pilosus/ruber and M. purpureus, revealed that albino variants were exclusively observed in M. purpureus and M. lunisporas, while closely related M. pilosus/ruber strains remained unaffected. The consistent phenotypic alterations observed across different albino isolates, coupled with their differential responses to various cultivation conditions compared to wild-type strains (Figs. 1 and 4), suggest a shared origin likely involving a recombination event (such as the insertion of a transposable element) or epigenetic modification, rather than arising from random mutations. Furthermore, our results indicate that beyond its stable phenotype (Fig. 1c), the albino strain demonstrates highly consistent gene expression profiles under diverse cultivation conditions (Fig. S1). Through personal communications with commercial Monascus producers, we have confirmed the routine emergence of albino strains. Although we attempted to identify potential triggering factors and evaluate the reversibility of this albinism, there is currently no conclusive evidence—aside from observations related to prolonged cold storage—to deduce the precise mechanisms governing albino strain generation. In a previous investigation of cleistothecia formation across 26 Monascus spp. strains sourced from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC), we found that only Monascus ruber BCRC 33329 failed to produce ascospores. This observation indicates that Monascus spp. strains are predominantly teleomorphic and lack self-incompatibility. While continuous sexual reproduction renders phenotypic drift inevitable in Monascus spp. cultures, the occurrence of such dramatic and directionally consistent phenotypic alterations cannot be adequately explained by phenotypic drift alone. Given that ascospores exhibit higher survival rates under environmental stress compared to conidia, we also tried to purify ascospores and apply heat treatments to induce germination (O'Gorman et al. 2009). The objective was to determine whether the phenotypic transition stems from mycelia derived from germinating ascospores; however, effective culture conditions for inducing ascospore germination remain elusive. Although whole-genome methylation profiling or comparative transcriptomic analyses could further elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving the physiological disparities between albino and wild-type strains, the absence of anamorphic strains continues to present a challenge in uncovering the underlying mechanisms of albinism generation. Given the importance of strain stability in industrial microbiology and the utility of mutant strains as genetic research tools, we propose that these albino M. purpureus strains offer a valuable opportunity to investigate the genetic regulatory mechanisms and genomic stability.

Monascin and ankaflavin, yellow pigments produced by Monascus spp., have garnered significant attention due to their diverse health benefits, improved safety profile compared to monacolin K, and abundance (Lin et al. 2017). While the biosynthetic pathways for these pigments are relatively well-characterized (Chen et al. 2017), the metabolic versatility of M. purpureus, the diversity of its metabolites, and the complex regulatory networks governing pigment biosynthesis have hindered a comprehensive understanding of the underlying regulatory mechanisms. Previous research has extensively investigated the biosynthetic pathways and regulation of pigments in Monascus species. Duan et al. (2022) employed systematic metabolic engineering of M. purpureus HJ11 to enhance azaphilone production, demonstrating a significant positive correlation between azaphilone biosynthesis and intracellular concentrations of malonyl-CoA, acetyl-CoA, pyruvate, and fructose-1,6-biphosphate. This finding underscores the importance of these metabolites, closely linked to glycolysis and the TCA cycle, as crucial upstream precursors for pigment biosynthesis. The study also showed that disrupting metabolic pathways, such as fatty acid synthesis, which consume malonyl-CoA, can increase pigment production. Given that citrate from the mitochondria serves as a major source of intracellular malonyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA (Poirier et al. 2002), and the malate-citrate shuttle plays a vital role in citrate transport between the mitochondria and the cytosol, representing a key anaplerotic pathway (Guo et al. 2023), we investigated the expression of a highly conserved DIC in M. purpureus. Results showed that DIC expression was markedly reduced in the albino strain compared to the wild-type strain, except when cultivated with 10% glycerol as the sole carbon source. (Figs. 6 and 7). This suggests that the DIC gene has not undergone a disruptive mutation in the albino strain, but rather a substantial alteration has occurred upstream of its regulation. The differential response of DIC expression under different cultivation conditions represented the most significant difference in gene expression analysis between wild-type and albino strains. Notably, DIC expression in the albino strain was exclusively elevated under 10% glycerol cultivation. This upregulation coincided with enhanced yellow pigment production (Fig. 4), indicating that malate transport is integral to pigment biosynthesis in M. purpureus.

Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) is a well-characterized core enzyme and considered a central hub of cellular energy metabolism with relatively constant expression. However, recent research has revealed that GAPDH possesses multiple cellular physiological roles, including involvement in the regulation of gene expression related to cell cycle control (Zheng et al. 2003). For instance, overexpression of a second GAPDH isozyme (MA-RGAPDH2) in filamentous fungi Mortierella alpine has been shown to enhance intracellular NADPH supply and increase lipid production (Wang et al. 2020). Furthermore, the production of specific secondary metabolites in Trichoderma virens has been demonstrated to be influenced by a dedicated GAPDH isozyme (vGPD), which not only participates in energy metabolism but also regulates the expression of biosynthetic genes (Bansal et al. 2021). Our comparative gene expression analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between GAPDH expression and that of mrpigH and DIC (Fig. 8a, b), with mrpigH and GAPDH expression also significantly correlating with the production of monascin (Fig. 8c and d). The strong positive correlation between mrpigH and GAPDH expression suggests that GAPDH may regulate mrpigH expression. While a previous study indicated that knocking out the mrpigH gene in M. ruber M7 significantly elevated yellow pigment yield (Li et al. 2021), our results align with the earlier deduction that MrpigH catalyzes an essential step in monascin and ankaflavin biosynthesis (Chen et al 2017). This discrepancy can likely be attributed to variations in the specific Monascus species, cultivation methodologies, and measurement targets utilized. Although the M. purpureus genome contains only one GAPDH gene, our gene expression analysis indicates that the M. purpureus GAPDH protein fulfills dual roles in cellular energy metabolism and regulation of biosynthetic gene expression. DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNAs—key components of epigenetic regulation—play critical roles in regulating secondary metabolism in fungi (Zhang et al. 2024). Histone deacetylases (HDACs) as key regulators of histone acetylation, have been strongly implicated in pigment biosynthesis in Monascus spp. Previous studies have demonstrated that supplementing the growth medium with 5 mM dihydrocoumarin significantly enhances pigment biosynthesis in M. ruber, and also affects biomass, citrinin production, and hyphal morphology (Hu et al. 2017). Conversely, treatment with the HDAC inhibitor vorinostat has been shown to reduce biomass, pigment production, and citrinin production in M. purpureus, while simultaneously upregulating the expression of genes involved in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid metabolism, cell membrane anchor-protein related genes, and biosynthetic pathways for ergosterol and chitin synthesis (Li et al. 2025). In this study, we investigated the effects of the HDAC inhibitors dihydrocoumarin and valproic acid on yellow pigment production and gene expression in wild-type and albino M. purpureus strains. Valproic acid exhibited a potent inhibitory effect on M. purpureus growth and pigment production, effectively suppressing mrpigB expression in both strain types, while concurrently increasing the expression of GAPDH, mrpacC, and mrpigH. This suggests a pivotal role for mrpigB in the M. purpureus pigment biosynthetic pathway and indicates that the mechanism of action of valproic acid may resemble that of vorinostat (Fig. 7). At low concentrations, dihydrocoumarin stimulated yellow pigment production in the wild-type M. purpureus strain, consistent with previous findings (Hu et al. 2017); however, dihydrocoumarin inhibited pigment production in the albino strain. Regarding gene expression, the responses of the wild-type and albino M. purpureus strains to dihydrocoumarin were largely similar, with the exception of DIC (Fig. 7c). These results underscore the substantial influence of histone modification on M. purpureus physiology and metabolism, revealing a significant difference in histone modification response between the wild-type and albino strains. Although overexpression of the hos2 gene in M. pilosus increases monacolin K and pigment production (Li et al. 2024), the complex interplay of histone modification and secondary metabolite regulation in M. purpureus highlights the need to identify the key histone modification response governing pigment biosynthesis. Albino M. purpureus strains represent a valuable opportunity to elucidate the mechanisms of histone modification and secondary metabolite regulation in Monascus spp.

Previous research has identified mrpacC as a pH-responsive transcription factor involved in the Pal/Rim pathway; under acidic conditions, its expression is typically suppressed, and this is associated with increased orange pigment production in M. ruber (Li et al. 2019). In the wild-type strain, mrpacC expression was suppressed by low initial pH, whereas exposure to high concentrations of glucose, 1 mM DHC, 0.25 mM valproic acid, and 10% glycerol induced increased expression; no other gene expression changes demonstrated statistical significance (Fig. 6a). Conversely, neither mrpacC expression nor yellow pigment production in the albino strain was affected by a low initial medium pH (Fig. 6b). In the wild-type strain, however, a low initial pH led to the suppression of mrpacC expression, accompanied by a decrease in yellow pigment production (Fig. 4e, f). Since previous study (Li et al. 2019) utilized constant pH control within bioreactors, whereas the present study only controlled the initial pH in shake-flask cultures, our results demonstrate that initial pH also significantly impacts mrpacC expression in the wild-type strain; however, the resulting yellow pigment yield differs from the outcomes obtained under constant pH cultivation.

In the M. purpureus pigment biosynthesis pathway, mrpigB participates in an early reaction, mrpigF mediates the terminal synthesis of orange pigments, and mrpigH is responsible for the terminal synthesis of yellow pigments (Chen et al. 2017). Prior research has demonstrated that increasing mrpigB expression via promoter replacement enhances pigment production (Duan et al. 2022), while disruption of mrpigF significantly reduces orange pigment production (Balakrishnan et al. 2017). In this study, irrespective of the transcriptional status of GAPDH and mrpigH, valproic acid almost completely inhibited mrpigB expression and pigment production in both strains. Furthermore, in the wild-type strain, elevated mrpigB expression under 10–15% glucose and 10% glycerol conditions did not correlate with a concomitant increase in yellow pigment production (Figs. 4, 6, and 7). These findings reaffirm the essential role of mrpigB in M. purpureus pigment biosynthesis; however, they suggest that its involvement at an early stage of the biosynthetic pathway restricts its direct influence on downstream yellow pigment accumulation. While mrpigF expression responses differed between wild-type and albino strains, the lack of a consistent pattern suggests independent regulation. A strong correlation was observed between mrpigH expression and yellow pigment production (Fig. 6c); however, factors known to significantly influence yellow pigment production, such as glucose concentration, did not correlate with mrpigF expression. Furthermore, mrpigF expression did not significantly affect yellow pigment production. These results support independent regulation of orange and yellow pigment biosynthesis (Duan et al. 2022) and suggest that the reaction catalyzed by mrpigH may represent a rate-limiting step in M. purpureus yellow pigment biosynthesis.

Previous studies have indicated that in M. pilosus, the addition of 16% glycerol when using 6% sucrose as a carbon source can significantly enhance pigment production (Shi et al. 2021). Cultivation of the albino M. purpureus strain with 10% glycerol elicited a markedly different response than observed with the wild-type strain (Fig. 4f), characterized by decreased mrpacC expression and significantly increased expression of GAPDH, mrpigH, and DIC. This resulted in a unique condition wherein the albino strain exhibited substantially higher expression levels compared to the wild-type. Glycerol metabolism in Aspergillus nidulans is thought to transform glycerol into dihydroxyacetone phosphate and subsequently enters the glycolysis pathway (Fillinger et al. 2001). The glycerol metabolic pathway in Monascus spp. remains unclear; our preliminary tests revealed that M. pilosus cannot be cultured using glycerol as the sole carbon source, while 10% glycerol cultivation of M. purpureus induces significant alterations in pellet morphology and gene expression, indicating differences in glycerol metabolism capabilities across Monascus species. Compared to glucose, glycerol at the same concentration creates an approximately two-fold higher osmotic pressure; however, the trend in yellow pigment production in the albino strain under high glucose conditions is clearly different from that observed with glycerol as the carbon source (Fig. 4e, f), suggesting that differences caused by glycerol and glucose cultivation cannot be solely attributed to osmotic pressure. The stark differences in response to glycerol between the albino and wild-type strains highlight fundamental differences in the overall metabolic regulation between these strains.

Conclusion

Strain stability is a critical factor in the industrial application of microorganisms. In this study, we identified the emergence of albino strains during the prolonged subculturing of Monascus spp. Compared to the wild-type, these variants exhibited accelerated growth and significantly diminished pigment production, posing a potential risk to the quality and consistency of industrial fermentation. The regulation of pigment biosynthetic genes and the physiological responses to varying culture conditions differed substantially between the albino and wild-type strains, suggesting a global metabolic shift rather than a localized mutation. Our findings revealed a strong positive correlation between the expression levels of GAPDH, mrpigH, and DIC, and the production of yellow pigments. This implies that GAPDH may regulate mrpigH and DIC expression, acting as a key determinant in M. purpureus yellow pigment biosynthesis. Furthermore, these results underscore the importance of the malate-citrate shuttle in supplying acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA for cellular metabolism. Although our comparative analysis of albino strains has illuminated novel research directions regarding the regulation of M. purpureus pigment biosynthesis, this process remains a highly complex metabolic pathway involving numerous genes. Consequently, the specific functions and regulatory mechanisms of many genes implicated in this biosynthetic pathway have yet to be fully elucidated. Ultimately, while albino M. purpureus strains represent a risk factor to industrial strain stability, they may concurrently serve as invaluable model systems for elucidating these complex metabolic networks.
