Association between albumin-corrected anion gap and kidney function in individuals with hypertension – NHANES 2009–2016 cycle
Authors: Hui Jiang, Xiaofeng Lan, Linmeng Zhou, Xiang Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLong-term uncontrolled hypertension increases the risk of kidney decompensation. This study aimed to explore the connection between albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG) and kidney function in hypertensive patients.MethodsThis study utilized data from 1988 participants diagnosed with hypertension sourced from the NHANES database. Binary logistic regression analysis and subgroup analysis were utilized to investigate the relationship between ACAG and kidney function. The study employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) to assess the non-linear associations between ACAG and eGFR, as well as ACAG and ACR. Furthermore, mediation and moderation effect analyses were carried out, with blood pressure serving as the mediator and moderator, ACAG as the independent variable, and eGFR and ACR as the dependent variables. Finally, the study developed ACAG-based models for predicting kidney function decline.ResultsHigher ACAG is identified as an independent risk factor for eGFR < 60 mL/minute/1.73 m2 and ACR ≥ 30 mg/g. Results from RCS indicate a non-linear relationship between ACAG and eGFR, as well as between ACAG and ACR. The mediation effect analysis revealed that DBP mediated the relationship between ACAG and eGFR. Analysis on moderation effect demonstrated that SBP played a significant role in moderating the interaction between ACAG and ACR. Moreover, the models based on ACAG showed strong performance.ConclusionsThe levels of ACAG are found to be independently associated with both eGFR and ACR. Additionally, ACAG shows promise as a new and dependable biomarker for predicting the decline in kidney function in hypertensive patients.

Introduction

Long-term hypertension can result in glomerular hyperperfusion, exacerbating kidney function damage. Hypertension ranks as the second leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) following diabetes [1]. CKD is identified by a decline in kidney function, as shown by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) lower than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a high urinary albumin (UAlb)/creatinine ratio (ACR) surpassing 30 mg/g persisting for at least three months [2,3]. EGFR is a crucial indicator for assessing kidney function by measuring blood perfusion and kidney filtration levels. Meanwhile, ACR is a sensitive marker for evaluating glomerular filtration membrane charge and mechanical barrier damage, and its clinical significance is increasingly acknowledged [4]. Risk factors like proteinuria, poorly managed hypertension, and diabetes can contribute to the progression of CKD to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [5]. Identifying other risk factors promptly and intervening early can potentially mitigate the advancement of CKD patients to ESKD.

Acid-base disorders, particularly metabolic acidosis, have been linked to an increased risk of CKD [6]. The anion gap (AG) is a measure that indicates the balance between positive and negative ions [7] and is utilized in the identification and assessment of metabolic acidosis. However, due to the fact that the albumin molecule is negatively charged, fluctuations in its levels can impact the AG findings [8]. Hence, the concept of albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG) was introduced to prevent misleading negative outcomes. The ACAG holds substantial clinical significance. Elevated ACAG levels were predictive of intensive care unit mortality [9]. Furthermore, ACAG can serve as a predictive tool for mortality among patients with sepsis-associated acute kidney injury [10]. Despite these findings, there is currently a lack of research on the association between ACAG levels and decreased kidney function in hypertensive patients.

This study conducted a cross-sectional study on the US population using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from the 2009–2016 cycle. The objective of this study was to explore the relationship between ACAG and kidney function in individuals with hypertension. Furthermore, prediction models based on ACAG for kidney function decline were developed and their performance was evaluated.

Materials and methods

Data source

NHANES is a well-known survey in the United States that gathers data on the health and nutritional status of a representative population through a complex multi-stage random sampling approach [11]. This study utilized data from four consecutive survey cycles (2009–2016) of the NHANES database, involving 40431 participants. Following exclusions of 13538 participants under 18 years of age, 518 participants who were pregnant or did not specify their pregnancy status, 21576 participants with missing Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), demographic information and comorbidities, 2584 non-hypertensive participants, 119 participants with missing ACAG, eGFR and ACR data, 108 participants who had taken diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARB, insulin, or SGLT2 inhibitors within the past month, the final analysis included 1988 eligible participants (Figure 1). The criteria for hypertensive patients in this research are as follows [12]: 1. SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. 2. Medical history of elevated blood pressure confirmed by a healthcare professional. 3. Current use of anti-hypertensive medication. The NHANES protocol was approved by the Ethical Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Flowchart of participant selection.

Exposure and outcome variables

The formula for calculating AG is as follows: AG (mmol/L) = [Sodium (mmol/L) + Potassium (mmol/L)] - [Chloride (mmol/L) + Bicarbonate (mmol/L)] [13]. The formula for exposure variable ACAG is as follows: ACAG (mmol/L) = [4.4 - Albumin (g/dl)] * 2.5 + AG. The main outcome of the study was eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. EGFR was estimated using a specific formula: 142 × min(Cr/k, 1)a × max(Cr/k, 1)−1.200 × 0.9938age × 1.012 (if the participant is female) (k = 0.9 for males and k = 0.7 for females, while a = −0.302 for males and a = −0.241 for females) [14]. The secondary outcome was ACR ≥ 30 mg/g.

Covariates

The study collected demographic information, laboratory parameters, and comorbidities from the participants. Demographic information included age, gender, race, marital status, education attainment, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol intake status [15–17]. Smoking status was determined by asking participants, ‘Do you currently smoke?’ Alcohol intake was categorized as light, moderate, and heavy. Heavy drinking was defined as more than 3 drinks per day for women and more than 4 drinks per day for men. Moderate drinking is defined as 2–3 drinks per day for women and 3–4 drinks per day for men. Any other level of drinking is considered light drinking [18]. Possible confounding variables in laboratory tests include white blood cell count (WBC), absolute neutrophil count (ANC), lymphocyte count (LYM), monocyte count (MONO), hemoglobin (Hb), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBil), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) and Ca+. Additionally, comorbidity data encompassed diabetes. Definition of Diabetes [19]: 1. Fasting blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥6.5%. 2. Prior diabetes diagnosis. 3. Currently treated with insulin.

Grouping

This study classified the exposure variables into four groups based on their quartiles of ACAG levels: Q1 (n = 497, ACAG ≤ 13.66 mmol/L), Q2 (n = 501, 13.66 mmol/L < ACAG ≤ 14.90 mmol/L), Q3 (n = 495, 14.90 mmol/L < ACAG ≤ 16.22 mmol/L), Q4 (n = 495, ACAG > 16.22 mmol/L). The study participants were categorized into two distinct groups according to their primary outcome event, with one group having an eGFR of less than 60 mL/minute/m2, and the other group having an eGFR greater than or equal to 60 mL/minute/m2. Likewise, the participants were also divided into two groups based on secondary outcome event, with one group having an ACR of greater than or equal to 30 mg/g, and the other group having an ACR of less than 30 mg/g. The delineation of participants into these separate groups based on their primary and secondary outcome events allowed for a more detailed analysis of the study data.

Statistical analysis

The analysis included 1988 eligible participants from different periods in the NHANES database. Due to the uneven distribution, weights of the combined sample varied significantly. Covariates such as age, gender, and race, which were used to calculate the weights, were also included in the regression model. Therefore, this study will conduct an unweighted analysis [20]. Continuous variables were represented using [M(Q1, Q3)], while categorical variables were represented by [n(%)]. The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were applied to continuous variables, and the χ2 test was used for categorical variables. Missing data was handled using the multiple imputation method, and discrete data was processed using the one-hot encoding method. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using the Q1 group as the reference group [21–23]. The regression models included eGFR < 60 mL/minute/m2 and ACR ≥ 30 mg/g as endpoint events. Model 1 was unadjusted model. Model 2 (minimum-adjusted model) was adjusted for age, gender, race, marital status, education attainment, BMI, current smoking status, alcohol intake, and diabetes. Model 3 (Fully adjusted model) was further adjusted for WBC, ANC, LYM, MONO, as well as factors such as Hb, ALT, AST, TBil, Ca+, LDL-C, and TC. A dummy variable was set at the median of ACAG for trend testing. Smooth curves were fitted using RCS for analysis, with adjustments equivalent to the fully adjusted regression model. Interaction tests were subsequently performed in subgroup analyses. Mediation and moderation effect analyses were performed to assess the impact of SBP and DBP on the relationship between ACAG and kidney function. In order to assess the clinical value of ACAG, this study employed Random Forest (RF) [24] and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [25] algorithms to prioritize features identified through univariate analysis. Important features were then selected to build two Light Gradient Boosting Machine (lightGBM) models for predicting kidney function decline. The models’ performance was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, and their clinical significance was analyzed using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). A significance level of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically meaningful.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study analyzed a total of 1988 hypertensive patients with an average age of 56.93 years, consisting of 1248 (62.78%) males and 740 (37.22%) females. The results of the study indicated that the average age, proportion of women, proportion of non-Hispanic blacks, proportion of non-Hispanic White, LDL-C, ACAG, and diabetes prevalence were higher in the eGFR < 60 mL/minute/m2 group compared to the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/minute/m2 group. Conversely, LYM, Hb, ALT, AST, TBil, and Ca+ levels in the eGFR < 60 mL/minute/m2 group were lower than those in the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/minute/m2 group. Furthermore, the average age, BMI, WBC, ANC, MONO, LDL-C, ACAG, and diabetes prevalence in the ACR ≥ 30 mg/g group were higher than in the ACR < 30 mg/g group, while Hb levels were lower in the ACR ≥ 30 mg/g group compared to the ACR < 30 mg/g group. RCS analysis revealed a non-linear association between ACAG and both the decrease in eGFR (p = 0.003) and the increase in ACR (p = 0.005) (Figure 2). Further analysis indicated that, when considering eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 as the outcome event, the risk of kidney function decline escalated with rising levels of ACAG. Using ACR ≥ 30 mg/g as the outcome event, we observed that when ACAG levels were less than or equal to 14.6 mmol/L, there was an increased risk of kidney function decline with higher ACAG levels. However, for ACAG levels between 14.6 mmol/L and 16.0 mmol/L, the risk of kidney function decline decreased as ACAG levels increased. Conversely, when ACAG levels exceeded 16.0 mmol/L, the risk of kidney function decline increased with higher ACAG levels.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, gender, BMI, race, education attainment, marital status, current smoking status, alcohol intake and diabetes. The relationship between ACAG and both eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Supplementary Table S3) and ACR ≥ 30 mg/g (Supplementary Table S4) remained consistent across all subgroups. Additionally, no significant interactions were observed between ACAG and the stratification variables (P for interaction > 0.05).

Mediation effect and moderation effect of blood pressure

Correlation analysis revealed significant relationships between DBP and both eGFR and ACAG. Specifically, DBP exhibited a positive correlation with eGFR (r = 0.205, p < 0.001) and a negative correlation with ACAG (r = −0.075, p < 0.001). Conversely, no significant correlation was observed between DBP and ACR (p > 0.05). Additionally, SBP demonstrated a negative correlation with eGFR (r = −0.099, p < 0.001) and a positive correlation with ACR (r = 0.118, p < 0.001), while no significant correlation was found between SBP and ACAG (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S1(A)).

Subsequently, this study investigated the mediation effect of DBP on the relationship between ACAG and eGFR. The analysis revealed that ACAG negatively predicts eGFR (β = −1.634, p < 0.001). Even after incorporating the mediation variable DBP, ACAG continued to negatively predict eGFR (β = −1.479, p < 0.001). Additionally, the mediation effect of DBP between ACAG and eGFR was found to be significant, with an indirect effect value of −0.155 (95% CI: −0.025, −0.005), where the confidence interval did not include 0. The mediation effect accounted for 9.48% of the total effect. These findings suggest that ACAG exerts both a direct influence on eGFR and an indirect influence through the mediation effect of DBP. The relevant parameters for mediation effect analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

The relevant parameters for moderation effect analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S6. In examining the moderation effect of SBP on the relationship between ACAG and eGFR, we found that the interaction term between ACAG and SBP was significant (β = 0.027, p = 0.016). This indicates that SBP serves as a moderator in the predictive relationship between ACAG and eGFR. Specifically, when SBP levels are low (M - SD), the correlation between ACAG and eGFR is stronger (β = −2.162, p < 0.001). Conversely, when SBP levels are high (M + SD), the correlation between ACAG and eGFR is weaker (β = −1.102, p = 0.001) (Supplementary Table S7). In analyzing the moderation effect of DBP on the relationship between ACAG and ACR, we found that the interaction term between ACAG and DBP was not significant (p > 0.05), indicating that DBP does not moderate the relationship between ACAG and ACR. Furthermore, when assessing the moderation effect of SBP on the relationship between ACAG and ACR, we found that the interaction term was also not significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that SBP does not play a moderation role in these context. Supplementary Figure S1(B–D) illustrates the conceptual diagrams depicting both the mediation effect and moderation effect.

Important features screening

Through univariate analysis, this study identified 12 clinically important factors significantly associated with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and 9 with ACR ≥ 30 mg/g. Following this, two algorithms, RF and XGBoost, were employed to rank the importance of features for further variable screening (Supplementary Figure S2(A–D)). The top 5 variables in terms of importance for each algorithm were then analyzed using a Veen diagram (Supplementary Figure S2(E,F)). The findings revealed that age, ACAG, Hb, and race were key features for eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, while age, ACAG, Hb, and diabetes were key features for ACR ≥ 30 mg/g.

ACAG-based prediction models

To evaluate the independent diagnostic value of ACAG, we compared its effectiveness to that of AG in the diagnosis of kidney function decline. The AUC for ACAG in predicting eGFR < 60 mL/minute/1.73 m2 was 0.64 (95% CI: 0.60–0.68), while the AUC for AG in predicting the same was 0.59 (95% CI: 0.55–0.63) (Figure 3(A)). Similarly, the AUC for ACAG predicting ACR ≥ 30 mg/g was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.59–0.66), compared to an AUC of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.56–0.62) for AG (Figure 3(B)). These results indicate that ACAG has the potential to independently predict kidney dysfunction, demonstrating a superior value compared to AG. Two lightGBM models were developed based on ACAG to predict kidney function decline. The lightGBM model predicting eGFR < 60 mL/minute/1.73 m2 achieved an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.89) in the training set. In the test set, this model achieved an the AUC is 0.81 (95% CI: 0.77–0.86) (Figure 3(C)). The lightGBM model predicting ACR ≥ 30 mg/g had an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.86–0.90) in the training set. In the test set, this model achieved an AUC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.71–0.84) (Figure 3(F)). The results of all ROC analyzes are presented in Supplementary Table S8. Through decision curves analysis (DCA) for both models, we have verified their superior clinical value (Figure 3(D,G)). In addition, the impact of ACAG on the output results of the two models was explored using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm [26]. It was observed that patients with higher ACAG levels were more susceptible to developing kidney insufficiency (Figure 3(E,H)).

Discussion

This study utilized binary logistic regression models to demonstrate a significant association between high ACAG and decreased kidney function in hypertensive patients (p < 0.05). Model 1 did not account for any confounding factors, while model 2 adjusted for baseline population characteristics and comorbidities. Model 3, building upon model 2, also incorporated laboratory data corrections. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the highest ACAG was identified as an independent risk factor for decreased eGFR [OR = 2.12, 95% CI (1.35, 3.32)] and also as an independent risk factor for increased ACR [OR = 1.88, 95% CI (1.30, 2.74)]. The RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between ACAG in hypertensive patients and decreased eGFR as well as increased ACR (p < 0.05). Subgroup analyses followed the adjustment strategy of the fully adjusted model (Model 3), yielding consistent and robust results.

Both RCS and binary logistic regression models indicated that the risk of increased ACR initially rises, then declines, and subsequently rises again as ACAG increases. In contrast, the risk of reduced eGFR consistently increases with rising ACAG. This intricate fluctuation in ACR may be associated with the body’s compensatory mechanisms. Elevated ACAG suggests a certain degree of disturbance in the body’s acid-base balance, which may impact kidney perfusion, glomerular filtration, and tubular reabsorption and secretion. In the early stages, kidney function is only slightly affected, leading to increased urinary excretion of proteins such as albumin, which in turn raises the ACR [27]. As ACAG continues to escalate, the body may initiate a series of compensatory responses to this abnormal condition. The kidneys may adjust their metabolism and transport functions to enhance albumin reabsorption and reduce its urinary excretion [28]. However, if ACAG continues to rise or surpasses the body’s compensatory capacity, these mechanisms may gradually fail. Consequently, kidney function may deteriorate further, leading to increased permeability of the glomerular filtration membrane and significant albumin leakage, resulting in a subsequent rise in ACR. Unlike ACR, eGFR primarily reflects the overall filtration function of the glomerulus [29]. In cases of kidney damage caused by elevated ACAG, the body may lack effective compensatory mechanisms to sustain eGFR.

Hypertension is a chronic cardiovascular disease, with an incidence rate of approximately 25% in the general population [30]. If the blood pressure of hypertensive patients is not controlled, it can lead to a decrease in kidney function and eventually progress to ESKD [31]. The traditional belief is that the decline in kidney function in patients with hypertension is attributed to nephrosclerosis, capillary hyalinizations, and increased activity of the renin-angiotensin system. However, with advancements in molecular biology research, it has been discovered that hypertension triggers endothelial damage and hypoxia, leading to inflammation, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. This cascade of events results in extensive damage to kidney tissue and ultimately kidney failure [32–35]. Research indicates that the dysregulation of acid-base balance may exacerbate kidney damage and elevate mortality rates in patients with kidney impairment [9,36]. The regulation of acid-base balance involves multiple organ systems including the brain, lungs, kidneys, and liver. Failure to correct acid-base imbalances promptly can result in damage to various organs [37]. When the kidneys face acid challenges, the body may exacerbate kidney damage through adaptive responses to enhance acid excretion [38]. This process involves the release of hormones such as angiotensin II, aldosterone, and endothelin, which, when elevated, can negatively impact kidney function [39,40]. However, there is currently a lack of evidence confirming a direct link between the dysregulation of acid-base balance and kidney function in hypertensive patients. AG is a common indicator used in clinical assessment of acid-base balance, calculated from serum concentrations of ions like sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate. However, since albumin in serum carries a negative charge, errors may arise when using AG to assess acid-base balance [41,42]. To mitigate these errors, some researchers have suggested using ACAG for assessing the body’s acid-base balance [8,43]. ACAG demonstrates strong practicality, particularly in complex diseases related to acidosis where its predictive ability is notably superior [44]. This study employed various methods to investigate the correlation between ACAG levels and kidney function in patients with hypertension. The findings indicate that elevated ACAG levels are significantly associated with a decline in kidney function.

A multicenter study discovered that having systolic ambulatory blood pressure above the target level increases the risk of kidney disease progression, regardless of the ambulatory blood pressure [45]. Our research suggests that increased levels of ACAG may play a role in the decline of kidney function in individuals with hypertension. To explore the inherent connection among blood pressure, ACAG, and kidney function, this study utilized mediation effect analysis and moderation effect analysis. The mediation effect analysis revealed that DBP mediated the relationship between ACAG and eGFR, with an indirect effect value of −0.155 (95% CI: −0.025, −0.005). In the analysis of moderation effects, it was found that SBP did have a significant moderation effect between ACAG and eGFR (β = 0.027, p = 0.016). However, SBP and DBP did not show a moderation effect on the interaction between ACAG and ACR (p > 0.05).

Numerous studies have highlighted the clinical significance of ACAG. Zhao et al. demonstrated that heightened serum ACAG levels were associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular mortality [46]. Wang et al. analyzed data from 12,246 individuals diagnosed with sepsis and found a significant association between higher preoperative levels of serum ACAG and increased rates of in-hospital mortality among sepsis patients [47]. Furthermore, Zhong et al. discovered that critically ill patients with acute kidney injury who had serum ACAG levels above 20 mmol/l when starting continuous kidney replacement therapy had a markedly higher risk of mortality in the intensive care unit [9]. To further investigate the clinical application value of ACAG in kidney dysfunction among patients with hypertension. This study utilized ROC curve analysis to demonstrate the potential of ACAG as an independent predictor of kidney dysfunction. Furthermore, employing the RF and XGBoost algorithms, the study identifies crucial features linked to kidney function decline, integrating them into the lightGBM risk prediction model. The ROC curves demonstrate the performance of both models, while the DCA show clinical value of both models. These models can assist clinicians in promptly identifying high-risk groups for kidney function decline in hypertensive patients, enabling timely interventions that can positively impact the prognosis of these patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the research based on the NHANES database is a cross-sectional study, meaning that the results obtained can only indicate correlation, not causation. Second, ambulatory blood pressure values were not utilized as research variables, and it is possible that some patients were on antihypertensive medications that could have influenced their blood pressure. Third, serum ACAG levels were not measured repeatedly. Fourth, the sample distribution between the study group and the control group in this study was uneven. Lastly, the AUC for ACAG is lower when used independently to diagnose kidney function decline, indicating that ACAG has relatively limited accuracy when employed alone for this purpose. Although this study reveals an association between ACAG and the decline in kidney function, it cannot be solely relied upon for an accurate diagnosis of kidney function decline; rather, it should be used in conjunction with other indicators.

Conclusion

This study discovered a significant correlation between ACAG and kidney function in hypertensive patients. Blood pressure could potentially act as a mediator and moderator between ACAG and kidney function. To prevent kidney function deterioration in hypertensive patients, it is recommended that they correct their acid-base balance. Furthermore, ACAG may serve as a promising biomarker for predicting decline in kidney function.
