Decoding the human gut bacterial plasmids in colorectal cancer
Authors: Shuwen Han, Zheng Wu, Yinhang Wu, Zefeng Wang, Peng Qian, Jian Chu, Jinyou Li, Jing Zhuang, Xi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gut plasmids show heightened sensitivity to gut microenvironmental changes compared to their bacterial hosts. To explore their significance in colorectal cancer (CRC), we analyzed metagenomic data from 863 participants (312 CRC, 387 high-risk, 164 low-risk). Plasmid and bacterial profiles were characterized, along with trace elements and metabolites. Differential analysis, functional gene assessment (ARG, MGE, MRG, VFGB), random forest modeling, and structural equation modeling (SEM) were applied. In terms of overall abundance, plasmids in both the high-risk and CRC groups exhibited a decreasing trend. Gut plasmids significantly influenced the functional genes (ARG, MGE, MRG, VFGB) of their bacterial hosts. Six key bacterial hosts (Enterobacterales, Bucrkholderiales, Hyphomicrobiales, Lactobacillales, Bacteroidales, Campylobacterales) and 12 plasmid markers were identified. The plasmid-based model effectively predicted CRC risk. SEM revealed that trace elements (e.g., Ni), metabolites (e.g., 5-Hydroxytryptophol), and host bacteria (e.g., Campylobacterales, Enterobacterales) predominantly exerted negative effects on most plasmids, whereas Ni exhibited a positive influence on plasmids NZ_CP013564.1, NZ_CP024312.1, and NZ_CP48284.1. We characterized the composition of gut plasmids and their bacterial hosts, explored the impacts of gut plasmids on bacterial functionality, and mapped multi-omics interaction networks linking plasmids, hosts, and metabolic features.

Introduction

The human gut microbiome is a complex ecosystem comprising trillions of microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses, and other microbes1. The gut microenvironment is a highly dynamic and interdependent ecosystem in which changes in any single component can trigger chain reactions, ultimately affecting the structure and function of the entire microbial community2. The occurrence and development of colorectal cancer (CRC) involve intricate interactions between the human body and gut environment3,4, with gut microbes being pivotal in shaping this environment5. From a genomic perspective, the gut microbiome is often referred to as the “second genome” of the human body, as it contains 100 to 150 times more genes than the human genome itself does6. The genetic diversity of the gut microbiota reflects the variations and richness of microbial species at the genetic level, which are critical for determining the functional capabilities and stability of microbial communities. These diverse genomes encode genes involved in various metabolic pathways and biological functions, influencing host nutrient absorption, immune regulation, and metabolic health, and play a significant role in the development of CRC7,8. Certain pathogenic bacteria, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Escherichia coli, contribute to carcinogenesis by synthesizing or activating carcinogenic compounds, including adhesins9, colibactin, and other toxins10,11. Conversely, beneficial bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, can produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) by fermenting dietary fibre, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects12,13. For instance, the bacterial metabolite butyrate inhibits histone deacetylase activity, thereby modulating gene expression, promoting cell differentiation, and suppressing tumour cell growth14. Each gut bacterium has a unique genome encoding numerous genes involved in diverse biological processes, such as metabolism, immune modulation, and gene expression regulation, all of which collectively contribute to the initiation and progression of CRC.

The genomes of gut bacteria exhibit remarkable plasticity, enabling rapid adaptation to environmental changes through mechanisms such as gene recombination and mutation. Gene exchange is particularly prevalent among gut bacteria because of the dense biofilm environment of bacteria and their frequent exposure to antibiotics. Plasmids, which are circular double-stranded DNA molecules, exist independently of bacterial chromosomes and can self-replicate15. These mobile genetic elements serve as key carriers of genetic material exchange within the gut microbial community16,17. Plasmids can harbour various genes, including those conferring antibiotic resistance, heavy metal resistance, virulence factors, or metabolic functions18–21. Through bacterial conjugation, plasmids facilitate horizontal gene transfer (HGT) between hosts18, enabling the dissemination of genes across different strains or even species. This dynamic process underscores the adaptability and evolutionary potential of gut bacteria under selective pressures.

In the human gut microbiome, the abundance of plasmids is closely linked to the abundance of their bacterial hosts22. During gut inflammation, the richness of the gut microbiota decreases, with a concurrent decline in plasmid diversity23. Compared with their bacterial hosts, plasmids are more sensitive to environmental changes and can serve as markers for the rapid adaptation of gut bacteria to such changes22. In the context of inflammatory bowel diseases, mobilizable plasmids expedite HGT23,24, facilitating the exchange of proinflammatory functional genes between bacterial species. The successful extraction and significant amplification of CRC-associated gut bacterial plasmids provide valuable data for identifying plasmid variations between healthy individuals and CRC patients, as well as for investigating CRC-related plasmids and elucidating the molecular mechanisms of specific plasmids in colorectal carcinogenesis25. Identifying which plasmids are associated with CRC development and the functions they mobilize can provide insight into the genetic interactions and ecological roles that plasmids play in individual gut microbial species and microbial communities. This line of inquiry broadens the scope of research into the structure and function of gut microbial communities, as well as the dynamic shifts within these communities during the onset and progression of CRC.

In this study, we focused on the gut microecology of three groups: healthy individuals, those at risk of CRC, and patients diagnosed with CRC. We investigated characteristic plasmids present under CRC conditions and analyzed how trace elements and metabolites within the gut environment influence these plasmids. Utilizing predictive models, we assessed the potential of gut plasmids to differentiate between healthy individuals and CRC patients. Furthermore, we explored the distribution and localization of specific plasmid pairs within host functional genes. To delve deeper into these relationships, we constructed a structural equation model aimed at uncovering the causal connections between trace elements and metabolites in the gut environment and the role of plasmid-mediated gene delivery in host bacteria. Our findings highlight the significance of plasmids, which act as bacterial gene exchange vectors, in the context of CRC. This research opened avenues for the development of new intervention strategies for the prevention and treatment of CRC.

Results

Characterization of bacterial plasmid diversity and host distribution in the human gut microbiota

A total of 312 CRC patients, 164 low-risk patients, and 387 high-risk patients were included in the study. Fecal samples were collected and subjected to metagenomic sequencing. Following data annotation, differences in plasmid abundance and bacterial abundance were analyzed (Fig. 1A). The host distribution of plasmids was found to be relatively broad. The top 10 most abundant plasmid hosts were selected, and a chord diagram was generated using the R-package. At the order level, no significant differences were observed in the host distribution of plasmids among the three groups: low-risk, high-risk, and CRC (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of plasmid abundance differences across groups revealed that 122 plasmids were more abundant in the high-risk group compared to the CRC group, while 265 plasmids were less abundant in the high-risk group than in the CRC group. When comparing the high-risk and low-risk groups, 235 plasmids were found to be more abundant in the low-risk group, whereas 1,148 plasmids were less abundant in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group. Additionally, 344 plasmids showed higher abundance in the low-risk group compared to the CRC group, while 3,890 plasmids were less abundant in the low-risk group than in the CRC group. In terms of overall abundance, plasmids in both the high-risk and CRC groups exhibited a decreasing trend. Determination of bacterial plasmid effects on host Functionality and screening of plasmid markers

The analysis revealed the presence of Enterobacterales, Burkholderiales, Hyphomicrobiales, Lactobacillales, Bacteroidales, and Campylobacterales in CRC samples. Significant differences in their abundance levels were observed among the high-risk, low-risk, and CRC groups (Fig. 2A). Using the median abundance level of differential plasmids associated with host bacteria as a classification threshold, the samples were categorized into high plasmid abundance and low plasmid abundance groups. Notably, functional genes (ARG, MGE, MRG, VFGB) of the host bacteria exhibited significant variations between these groups (Fig. 2B). From the six plasmid-host pairs (Enterobacterales, Burkholderiales, Hyphomicrobiales, Lactobacillales, Bacteroidales, and Campylobacterales), plasmids and their corresponding host bacteria were analyzed based on correlation coefficients. The top three plasmids with correlation coefficients greater than 0.5 were selected as potential markers for each group. Plasmid markers in CRC risk prediction and their interactions with gut metabolites and trace elements

On the basis of the previously screened plasmid markers, a CRC risk prediction model was constructed using the random forest algorithm and validated through tenfold cross-validation. The performance of the new CRC prediction model was superior, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.78 in internal cross-validation and 0.70 in the external dataset (Fig. 3A, B and Figure S2A), demonstrating its enhanced efficacy for CRC classification compared with that of the other methods. Decision and calibration curves, generated based on model performance in the test set and external validation set, revealed minimal deviation between predicted and observed outcomes, reflecting satisfactory model reliability (Figure S2C, D). For comparison, a model incorporating sex, age, BMI, and smoking history was also constructed. To explore potential correlational structures among trace elements, metabolites, and plasmid-associated host bacteria in CRC, we performed trace element and metabolome profiling on 126 samples. Random forest analysis identified nickel (Ni) and titanium (Ti) as the most important trace element features, and D-xylonic acid and 5-hydroxytryptophol as the top metabolite features (Fig. 3C). These variables, together with the previously identified plasmid–host pairs, were integrated into a structural equation model (SEM) to exploratorily assess multivariate associations among plasmids, bacterial hosts, trace elements, and metabolites (Fig. 3D). Under this exploratory framework, the model showed that NZ_CP017026.1, NZ_CP8186.1, and NZ_CP076506.1 were negatively associated with Ni (coefficient = –2.31), 5-hydroxytryptophol (coefficient = –0.488), and the host bacterium Campylobacterales (coefficient = –0.351). Similarly, NZ_CP38858.1 and NZ_CP48383.1 showed negative coefficients with Ni (coefficient = –0.088), 5-hydroxytryptophol (coefficient = –0.03), and the host Enterobacterales (coefficient = –0.731). In contrast, NZ_CP013564.1, NZ_CP024312.1, and NZ_CP48284.1 were positively associated with Ni (coefficient = 0.741) under the assumed model structure, while remaining negatively associated with 5-hydroxytryptophol (coefficient = –0.656) and the host Hyphomicrobiales (coefficient = –0.148). These findings illustrate modeled correlation patterns among plasmids, bacterial hosts, and environmental factors in CRC.

Taking into consideration the potential confounding effects of age and other demographic factors, we further validated the robustness of our findings by adjusting for sex, age, and BMI as covariates. As shown in Supplementary data 1, these factors did not significantly influence the differential abundance of the 12 identified plasmid markers, supporting the independence of their association with CRC status. To complement this analysis, we also performed an age-stratified subsampling using the quartile method, selecting samples from the first to the third quartile of the age distribution to minimize age-related bias. From this subset, we extracted 144 Normal samples and 144 CRC samples. The overall results show that, in the selected samples, the majority of the six target bacteria exhibited significant differences between the Normal and CRC groups (Figure S3A). Additionally, the correlations between the 12 marker plasmids and their corresponding hosts were significant (Figure S3B). The previously established model demonstrated good classification performance in these samples (AUC = 0.67), which is generally consistent with the results from the original sample set (Figure S3C). These findings collectively demonstrate the robustness of the plasmid markers and their predictive model, independent of age, sex, or BMI.

Discussion

The gut microenvironment is a dynamic ecosystem in which genetic elements such as plasmids can drive functional shifts in the microbial community. Our analysis of gut bacteria and plasmids across 312 CRC patients and 164 low-risk and 387 high-risk individuals revealed that plasmids are associated with marked alterations in the key functional genes (ARG, MGE, MRG, and VFGB) of their bacterial hosts. Our study establishes a tangible link between plasmids and functional dynamics in the CRC gut microbiome. This connection enabled the development of a plasmid marker-based risk prediction model with strong discriminatory power. By identifying differential host bacteria and their associated plasmid markers, we constructed a CRC risk prediction model that demonstrated robust performance upon validation. Furthermore, we constructed an interaction network integrating plasmids, host bacteria, trace elements, and metabolites, offering new insights into the complex, multifactorial interplay involved in CRC development.

We described the bacterial and plasmid composition of the human gut across different CRC states. Initial analyses of metagenomic datasets from various human gut microbiome studies largely overlooked plasmid-derived sequences. The gut microbiome harbours a vast array of plasmids, which play pivotal roles in facilitating HGT within the gut ecosystem26. Plasmids equip host bacteria with diverse gene expression mechanisms and evolutionary pathways, enhancing their adaptability and survival in dynamic environments23. For example, the genetic determinants facilitating lactose and casein catabolism in Lactobacillus lactis are plasmid encoded rather than chromosomally located27. In this study, we performed metagenomic sequencing of faecal samples and analyzed the differences in gut bacterial and plasmid abundance among low-risk, high-risk, and CRC groups through comprehensive data annotation. Our findings revealed no significant differences in plasmid hosts at the taxonomic level across the three groups, whereas notable variations were observed in the plasmid composition. Specifically, compared with those in the low-risk group, the overall abundance of plasmids tended to decrease in both the high-risk group and the CRC group. Notably, certain plasmids were significantly more abundant in the low-risk group. A subset of these plasmids demonstrated a broad host range, which allows their long-term persistence in the human gut. These plasmids are widely distributed across human populations and various environmental niches globally, suggesting their potential influence on human health17.

We also identified six bacterial hosts and twelve plasmid markers that effectively differentiated the three groups while simultaneously investigating the impact of gut plasmids on bacterial functionality. Plasmids are essential components of bacterial cells and play a crucial role in regulating bacterial gene expression. Plasmids can carry and transmit genes that confer benefits or risks to host bacteria, including genes related to antibiotic resistance, heavy metal resistance, virulence factors, or metabolic functions, and may serve as powerful microbial markers for identifying human diseases21. In this study, we analyzed bacterial hosts associated with different plasmids and focused on 6 major bacterial orders: Enterobacterales, Burkholderiales, Hyphomicrobiales, Lactobacillales, Bacteroidales, and Campylobacterales. By grouping samples on the basis of the median abundance levels of plasmids corresponding to these host bacteria, we observed significant differences in the functional genes (ARG, MGE, MRG, and VFGB) of host bacteria with varying levels of plasmid abundance, confirming the vital role of plasmids in equipping host bacteria with the ability to survive under specific environmental conditions.

Currently, CRC risk prediction models focus primarily on mutations in tumour suppressor genes and other human genes28–30, changes in RNA and protein expression levels31,32, and alterations in gut microorganisms and metabolites32,33. However, the role of characteristics of bacterial plasmids is often overlooked. Different plasmids in gut bacteria have been demonstrated to have the potential to be combined with bacterial biomarkers to improve the accuracy of CRC diagnosis34. Building on this foundation, our work further focuses on investigating the relationship between plasmids and host bacterial functions and integrates multiomics data, including gut metabolites and trace elements, to elucidate the associations among host bacteria, trace elements, and metabolites mediated by plasmids within the CRC gut environment.

In addition, we delineated elaborate networks that encapsulated the interactions between gut plasmids, bacterial hosts, trace elements, and metabolites. The gut microenvironment engages in complex interactions with gut bacteria and plasmids, where changes in this microenvironment directly impact the abundance, distribution, and function of both bacteria and plasmids. Within this ecosystem, metabolites and trace elements play crucial roles35,36. In our study, we revealed that during the onset and progression of CRC, specific trace elements such as nickel (Ni) and titanium (Ti), along with metabolites such as D-xylonic acid and 5-hydroxytryptophol, are key characteristics of the gut microenvironment. Ni is known to exert a regulatory influence on gut bacteria37,38, whereas the synthesis of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) is modulated by gut bacteria and their metabolic byproducts, impacting gut immune responses39. The intricate network of interactions among gut plasmid markers, bacterial hosts, trace elements, and metabolites highlights the multifaceted interplay of various factors within the gut microenvironment, which collectively shape the dynamics of gut plasmids and significantly impact the initiation and progression of CRC. Although the SEM analysis provided valuable insights into the relationships among variables, the overall model fit indices were not entirely satisfactory. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further validation using larger cohorts or alternative modeling approaches is warranted.

However, this study is not without its limitations. First, owing to the constraints of current research technologies, most gut bacteria remain uncultivable in vitro, and studies focusing on individual bacterial species fail to replicate the complex human gut microenvironment. Future advancements should aim to develop animal or in vitro models that emulate the human gut as an integrated ecosystem, allowing a more comprehensive analysis of the intricate interaction mechanisms between plasmids and their bacterial hosts. Second, we acknowledge that targeted experimental validation (e.g., PCR amplification or conjugation assays) was not performed in this study. While the recovery of circularized plasmid contigs from PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing provides direct structural evidence for extrachromosomal status, experimental confirmation would further strengthen confidence in plasmid identification. Future mechanistic studies should prioritize experimental validation of key plasmid markers to confirm their biological roles and host associations. Third, while our analyses identified a robust set of twelve plasmid markers associated with CRC, we acknowledge the need for rigorous control of potential confounding factors. Although our differential analysis of these markers incorporated age, sex, and BMI as covariates, and confirmed that these factors did not significantly influence marker abundance, we recognize that this does not replace a fully adjusted multivariable framework. The inclusion of these covariates in our predictive model paradoxically reduced its performance, suggesting that the microbial, metabolomic, and plasmidomic features already captured the relevant biological signal. Nevertheless, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of residual confounding from age-driven shifts in the gut microbiome, metabolome, or plasmidome. Future studies employing larger, more diverse cohorts should integrate comprehensive statistical adjustments for demographic and clinical covariates to further disentangle disease-specific effects from background physiological variations. Fourth, the lack of in-depth molecular mechanism research is another important drawback of this study. Although we utilized metagenomic sequencing and other technologies to explore the potential associations among these factors, laying a foundation for future molecular mechanism research, more detailed investigations are needed. In future large-scale, multicentre clinical studies, recruiting additional samples could further validate the findings of this research. Moreover, conducting continuous follow-ups on both high-risk and low-risk patients, alongside regular monitoring of changes in the gut microbiota and plasmids as low-risk patients progress to high-risk status and as high-risk patients develop CRC, would be highly valuable. This longitudinal approach provides deeper insights into the dynamics of microbiota and plasmid alterations during disease progression, potentially leading to more effective strategies for early detection and intervention.

Conclusion

We observed that specific plasmid types and their associated bacterial hosts were differentially enriched across groups, and that plasmid composition was correlated with distinct functional gene repertoires in their bacterial hosts, including ARGs, MGEs, MRGs, and VFGBs. Six major plasmid-associated bacterial hosts and twelve plasmid markers were identified. Based on these associative features, we constructed a CRC risk prediction model and further described multi-omics networks linking plasmids, bacterial hosts, trace elements, and metabolites. Taken together, this study provides a systematic, descriptive account of gut plasmid-host ecology across the CRC continuum. These findings offer a hypothesis-generating resource and a potential framework for integrating plasmid profiling into CRC risk assessment. Future studies incorporating confounder-adjusted models, functional validation, and longitudinal sampling are needed to determine whether the observed plasmid-host signatures are drivers or bystanders in CRC pathogenesis.

Methods

The study flowchart is shown in Figure S1.

Research object

This study recruited participants from Huzhou Central Hospital in Zhejiang Province and its eight affiliated community hospitals. A random sampling method was employed to select patient samples from these hospitals between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023. All sampled individuals who met the study criteria were invited to participate, and all 863 ultimately included subjects agreed to take part. Thus, the response rate for the final study sample was 100%. The subjects were 551 healthy people and 312 CRC patients. The high-risk cohort (n = 387) was defined by age ≥45 years with first-degree relative CRC history. Low-risk individuals (n = 164) lacked aforementioned criteria. All CRC patients were pathologically diagnosed and clinically staged in accordance with the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging guidelines. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) presence of other malignant tumours; (2) use of antibiotics or probiotics within the first 3 months of study participation; (3) diagnosis of severe cardiovascular or pulmonary diseases; and (4) history of other intestinal diseases, such as Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis. All eligible volunteers provided written informed consent.

All subjects signed informed consent in accordance with the guidelines approved by Huzhou Central Hospital Ethics Committee (202202005-02). Metagenomic sequencing

Stool sample

Stool samples (approximately 5–10 grams) were collected from fasting subjects without the use of laxatives or lubricants. After the samples were collected, they were immediately placed on ice. The time from sample collection to transfer to the -80 °C freezer was rigorously controlled and did not exceed 30 minutes. This short duration at near -0°C (on ice) minimized any significant metabolic activity or degradation. All the samples were transported from the collection site to the laboratory using cold chain transportation on dry ice. This continuous cold chain was maintained until the samples were placed in the long-term storage freezer. The samples were stored in a -80 °C freezer for less than one month before analysis.

DNA extraction

Microbial DNA was extracted from the faecal samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). Briefly, 0.2–1 g of stool sample was mixed with 1 mL SLX-Mlus Buffer and 500 mg magnetic beads, vortexed for 3–5 min, then incubated with 100 µL DS Buffer at 70 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was mixed with 270 µL P2 Buffer, incubated at ‑20 °C for 5 min, and centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was mixed with equal volume XP5 Buffer and 40 µL magnetic beads, inverted for 8 min, and washed sequentially with XP5 Buffer, PHB, and twice with SPW Wash Buffer. DNA was eluted in 100 µL Elution Buffer. High-quality DNA samples (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0) were used for library construction. The remaining supernatant was subjected to lysis, and viral DNA was extracted using a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit without carrier RNA (Qiagen).

NGS library construction and sequencing

Metagenomic shotgun sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced at Shanghai Biozeron Biological Technology Co. Ltd. In brief, for each sample, Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation kit from Illumina were used to construct sequencing libraries and the concentration of all libraries were measured by High Sensitivity Double Stranded DNA kit on a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All samples were sequenced in the Illumina Novoseq instrument with pair-end 150 bp (PE150) mode.

PacBio-HiFi library construction and sequencing

Metagenomic DNA extraction was performed using a QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, cat. no. 51604) for each sample and the genomic DNA was mixed for metagenomic sequencing. We made some minor protocol modifications to extract longer DNA fragments to meet the requirements of long-read sequencing technology. Briefly, in the second step, we followed the major instructions for the ‘Isolation of DNA from stool for pathogen detection’ and added 1 ml of Inhibit EX Buffer. A sterile 1 ml pipette tip was used to grind the sediment, and 0.5 mm sterile glass beads were added to help homogenize the sample. To reduce short DNA fragments, 0.4× AMPure XP beads were used. The quality and quantity of the obtained DNA were evaluated by running it in a 0.5% agarose gel and using the QubitTM dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Finally, DNA samples with a high molecular weight (modal size >4 kbp) and sufficient quantity (>10 µg) were used for sequencing.

For PacBio sequencing, 5 μg of DNA was used to prepare a SMRTbell library with the PacBio SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA, Part Number: 102-182-700). Damaged double-stranded DNA in the initial DNA sample was repaired using the New England BioLabs PreCR® Repair Mix Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions before library preparation. Damaged double-stranded DNA was repaired using the New England BioLabs PreCR® Repair Mix Kit in a 50 µL reaction at 37°C for 20 min. Then, the repaired DNA was size-selected by using the BluePippin system (Sage Science, MA, USA) to obtain molecules larger than 4 kb. The SMRTbell library was sequenced with v3 chemistry on a PacBio Sequel IIe instrument (Pacific Biosciences, USA) using SMRT 8 M cells (Part Number: 101-389-001). HiFi reads were generated with the ‘ccs’ module (parameters: --min-length 500 --min-passes 3 --min-rq 0.99) within the SMRT Link v10.0 package (Pacific Biosciences, USA).

Metagenome assembly

For the NGS short-read data, the raw sequence reads underwent quality trimming using Trimmomatic (V0.33; parameters: -phred33 ILLUMINACLIP:$Bin/Trimmomatic-0.33/adapters/merge.fa:2:30:10 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:75; http://www.usadellab.org/cms/uploads/supplementary/Trimmomatic) to remove adaptor contaminants and low-quality reads. Reads that passed quality control were then mapped against the human genome (NCBI) by using the BWA mem algorithm (v0.7.17; parameters: -M -k 32 -t 16; http://biobwa.sourceforge.net/bwa.shtml). After removing host-genome contamination and low-quality data, the reads were assembled by MegaHit with “--min-contig-len 500” parameters (v1.2.9; https://github.com/voutcn/megahit). For the PacBio HiFi long-read data, HiFiasm-meta (https://github.com/lh3/hifiasm-meta, version 0.2-r053, default parameters) was used to assemble the HiFi reads.

Reads-based phylogenetic annotation

Plasmid identification and abundance calculation

PLASMe is an end-to-end automated plasmid identification tool. This study referenced the PLASMe tool from the research by Tang et al., relying on PLASMe’s probability to identify plasmids rather than chromosomal contamination or other scenarios42. The assembled contigs that larger than 2 kb were used to detect plasmid. PLASMe (https://github.com/HubertTang/PLASMe) was introduced in this study to identify plasmid contigs and the filtered parameters were (1) > 90% sequence similarity, (2) Transformer probability > 0.5, and (3) PLASMe score > 0.9. Meanwhile the plasmid taxonomic information was predicted by PLASMe. Gene models within plasmid contigs were predicted by Prodigal with the default parameter. The corresponding protein sequences were annotated in KEGG website (https://www.kegg.jp/blastkoala/). The clean reads from each sample were aligned against the gene catalogs using BWA-MEM (v.0.7.17), and abundance profiles of genes (alignment length ≥ 50 bp and sequence identity >95%) were calculated in transcripts per million (TPM), with corrections for variations in gene length and mapped reads per sample. Differential analysis was performed using the R package MaAsLin2, with thresholds set at |log2FC | > 2 and FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Metabolomics detection

For metabolomics detection, 50 mg of solid sample was processed in a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube with extraction solution. After mixing and low-temperature ultrasonic extraction, the sample was centrifuged, and the supernatant was dried and redissolved. Both solvent blanks (extraction solvent) and process blanks (undergoing the entire preparation procedure without biological sample) were inserted throughout the sample preparation process to monitor background contamination and interference introduced during preparation. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of metabolite extracts. During instrumental analysis, a QC sample was analyzed every ten samples to ensure process repeatability. The regular insertion of QC samples throughout the analytical sequence served to monitor instrumental stability, data quality, and overall reproducibility.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis was conducted under specific chromatographic and mass spectrometry conditions. Sample separation was performed on a BEH C18 column using a gradient mobile phase of water and acetonitrile/isopropanol, both containing 0.1% formic acid. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive and negative ion scanning mode, with a scan range of m/z-50-1000.

Raw LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software for baseline filtering, peak detection, integration, and alignment, generating a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity. Missing values were handled using the 80% rule, and the data were sum-normalized. Variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) exceeding 30% in QC samples were excluded. The remaining data were log-transformed to improve normality, yielding the final data matrix for downstream analysis.

Metabolite identification was performed by matching acquired MS and MS/MS spectra against public databases HMDB and Metlin. Chromatographic peaks were integrated, with peak areas reflecting the relative abundance of each metabolite. Background ions detected in blank samples were subtracted during quantification. The raw quantitative data were normalized using total peak area, and only metabolites exhibiting a coefficient of variation (CV) < 30% in QC samples were retained. Finally, metabolite identification and relative quantification results were obtained.

Trace element detection

After the samples were freeze-dried, a certain amount of sample was weighed in a graphite digestion tube. First, 15 mL of nitric acid was added and the sample was heated to 80 °C for 20 min. Next, 3 mL of perchloric acid was added and the sample was digested at 80 °C for 10 min. The sample was then heated to 130 °C for 15 min, after which the temperature was increased and maintained at 180 °C for 120 min until the digestion solution was colourless and clear. The perchloric acid was evaporated to near dry, and the digestion was complete. The digestion solution was cooled to room temperature, transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask and adjusted to the scale line with distilled water to be tested. Nitroric acid (GR) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Shanghai 10014508, perchloric acid was purchased from Tianjin Zhengcheng Chemical Products Co., Ltd. Model Construction

As previously described, plasmid identification was successfully conducted on 75 CRC and 53 normal samples from the downloaded PRJEB10878 dataset. It was utilized as the external validation set.

A random forest algorithm was employed to construct a classification model using the R package “randomForest.” A binary classifier was built using plasmid abundance as input features based on the screened plasmid markers. Model evaluation was performed via ten-fold cross-validation: the dataset was evenly divided into ten subsets, with nine subsets used as the training set and the remaining one as the test set in each iteration, resulting in a training-to-test ratio of approximately 9:1. In each iteration, the model was trained on the training set and predictions were made on the test set. The R package “pROC” was used to perform ROC analysis on both the training and test sets for each cross-validation round, calculating the AUC values and plotting ROC curves. The average AUC value from the ten cross-validation rounds was used as an objective measure of model performance. The parameters of the random forest model are: mtry = 4, ntree = 500, and nodesize = 1.

Multi-Omics Association Analysis

To investigate the multi-omics associations among plasmids, bacteria, ions, and metabolites, this study utilized structural equation modeling (SEM)43. The specific procedures were as follows. Trace elements analysis: The R package “randomForest” was first used to filter the top 10 most important ions based on the random forest algorithm. Using the SGE module from the Python library “semopy,” we constructed a structural equation model and selected the top two most influential ions for in-depth analysis. Metabolomic analysis: Similarly, the R package “randomForest” was used to screen the top 10 most important metabolites, followed by the construction of a structural equation model using the “semopy” SGE module. The top two metabolites with the greatest contributions were selected for further interpretation. Through these methods, potential interactions and causal pathways among bacteria, ions, and metabolites were systematically explored.

Statistics and Reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0 and Python 3.9. For differential abundance analysis of plasmids, bacteria, metabolites, and trace elements, the MaAsLin2 package was used with the following thresholds: |log2(fold change)| > 2 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg method). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test unless otherwise specified. For multiple group comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied.

The random forest classification model was implemented using the ‘randomForest’ R package with parameters: mtry = 4, ntree = 500, nodesize = 1. Model performance was evaluated using ten‑fold cross‑validation; the dataset was randomly partitioned into ten subsets, with nine used for training and one for testing in each iteration. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated using the ‘pROC’ package, and the average AUC from ten folds is reported.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using the ‘semopy’ Python library. The SGE (structural equation model with genetic algorithm) module was used to automatically search for model structures. Model fit was assessed using chi‑square test, comparative fit index (CFI), goodness‑of‑fit index (GFI), normed fit index (NFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA).

To ensure reproducibility, the random forest cross‑validation was repeated with ten different random seeds, yielding consistent AUC values (standard deviation < 0.02). The SEM analyses were run with multiple starting values to confirm convergence to the same solution.
