Electronic nicotine delivery systems versus nicotine replacement therapies and risk of smoking relapse: evidence from a US nationwide prospective cohort (Cancer Prevention Study-3)
Authors: Johann L Westmaas, Mariah Landry, Tyler Nighbor, Zheng Xue, Ryan W Diver, Alpa Patel, Karli K Kondo, Samuel Asare, Adriana Lori, Priti Bandi, Nigar Nargis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo compare use of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) with nicotine replacement therapies (NRTs) on risk of cigarette smoking relapse by people who had already quit cigarettes.DesignProspective cohort study.SettingThe American Cancer Society Cancer Prevention Study-3, a nationwide US cohort with follow-up every 3 years beginning in 2015.ParticipantsAdults who in 2015 had already quit smoking (n=3112) or were smoking (n=1018) and who in 2018 reported past or current exclusive use of ENDS or NRT and provided smoking status.Outcome measuresRelapse to cigarette smoking in 2018 among people who were already quit in 2015, and abstinence from cigarettes in 2018 among people who were smoking in 2015.ResultsAmong respondents who had already quit in 2015, the unadjusted risk of relapse in 2018 was approximately three times greater for those who reported past exclusive ENDS versus past exclusive NRT use (11.2% vs 3.9%; relative risk (RR)=2.90, 95% CI 2.12 to 3.98). This association remained significant in a multivariable-adjusted model (RR=2.09, 95% CI 1.49 to 2.92). Among those smoking in 2015, the unadjusted likelihood of abstinence in 2018 was higher for those who reported current ENDS versus NRT use (RR=1.35, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.80), but the multivariable model adjusted for smoking frequency indicated no difference in abstinence (RR=1.38; 95% CI 0.93 to 2.05).ConclusionsENDS use was associated with greater relapse risk than NRT among people who had already quit. Although ENDS may support shorter-term cessation, further long-term observational research is needed to clarify relapse risks associated with ENDS relative to NRT.Keywords: Tobacco Use, Smoking Reduction, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Cigarette smoking causes an estimated 29% of all cancer deaths in the USA1 and quitting smoking can substantially reduce cancer risk.2 Whether electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) should be recommended over nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) for smoking cessation is currently highly contentious in the tobacco control community.3 Although many ENDS products are currently legally available for purchase as consumer products,4 no ENDS have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) as a safe and effective cessation aid.5,8 In contrast, NRT and other medications such as varenicline and bupropion are FDA-approved and considered mainstays of standard tobacco cessation treatment, especially when combined with behavioural counselling.9 Concerns about ENDS include their role in initiating nicotine addiction among youth who may be enticed by flavoured options (as suggested by the JUUL epidemic among young people),10,12 its potential as a ‘gateway’ to using combustible cigarettes,13,16 the presence of carcinogenic chemicals in ENDS vapour,17 and as yet unknown but possibly harmful, long-term effects on health.8 18 The increase in popularity of ENDS has also prompted concern about whether it may contribute to a decline in use or sales of pharmacological treatments for smoking cessation (eg, NRT) though evidence suggests that product prices, advertising, source of NRT (eg, prescription vs over-the-counter) and type of NRT are important considerations.19,21

Proponents of ENDS for cessation or as a harm reduction strategy assert that for people who smoke cigarettes, ENDS may be a more appealing and effective alternative to NRT. Indeed, a 2025 Cochrane review that pooled data from nine randomized control trials (RCTs) with follow-ups of at least 6 months concluded that ENDS were associated with greater cessation compared with NRT (relative risk (RR)=1.55, 95% CI 1.28 to 1.88). The longer-term risk of relapse associated with ENDS or NRT use, however, would be more evident in observational studies that track smoking status (abstinence and relapse) over several years. It is currently unknown whether ENDS use compared with NRT use is associated with a higher or lower risk of relapsing back to combustible cigarettes among people who had already quit smoking. Instead, observational studies have compared, among people who formerly smoked, rates of relapse between current or past use of ENDS with never or non-use of ENDS.22,25 For example, in an observational study of 2025 people who had already quit, ENDS use, compared with non-use, was associated with an increased risk of relapse at a 2-year follow-up.26 Moreover, a recent systematic review of 10 observational studies and one RCT concluded that inconsistency in findings and methodological flaws preclude generalisations about the risk of relapse from ENDS by those who had already quit.25 No observational studies, to our knowledge, have directly compared ENDs with NRT on the longer-term risk of relapse among people who had already quit.

To address the gap in research specifically comparing ENDS with NRT on risk of relapsing, the objective of the current study was to estimate, among people who were already quit in 2015, their likelihood of relapse (in 2018) if they reported past (or current) use of ENDS or NRT exclusively. We hypothesised that risk of relapse among people who had already quit will be higher for those who used ENDS (vs NRT) because of greater similarity between ENDS and cigarettes in tactile, visual or physical characteristics (eg, throat hit, vapour production) and nicotine levels.

Because risk of relapse should be considered along with cessation efficacy in evaluating risks of ENDS versus NRT in clinical decision-making for cessation, we also estimated, among people who were smoking in 2015, the rate of abstinence in 2018 associated with past or current use of ENDS or NRT exclusively. We were also interested in identifying covariates (sociodemographic factors and health and smoking behaviour-related variables) that may be important (or not) for observing any associations obtained.

Methods

Study design and participants

Participants were drawn from a large prospective cohort study designed to investigate the association between lifestyle and other factors and cancer incidence and mortality (American Cancer Society Cancer Prevention Study-3). The study recruited participants aged 30 and older with no history of cancer (N=303 682). Baseline enrolment was conducted between 2006 and 2013 at fundraising events (eg, annual Relay For Life) and community locations (eg, churches, workplaces and other community-oriented institutions),27 with follow-up surveys sent triennially beginning in 2015. Recruitment and IRB approval details are reported elsewhere.27 Inclusion criteria for the analytic sample included completion of study questions on the 2015 and 2018 surveys (to limit analyses to a 3-year period), and because some covariates were not assessed at later waves. At baseline, included participants had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime (to capture established smoking behaviour). After exclusions, among 42 431 individuals identified in 2015 (T1) as having formerly smoked, 3112 reported in 2018 (T2) using ENDS or NRT currently or in the past exclusively (figure 1). Among 3531 individuals smoking at T1, 1018 reported at T2 using ENDS or NRT currently or in the past exclusively.

Exclusions and final analytic sample. ENDS, electronic nicotine delivery systems; NRT, nicotine replacement therapy.

Measures

Smoking status

For all surveys, current cigarette smoking was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in one’s lifetime (assessed at baseline) and answering ‘Yes’ to ‘Do you currently smoke cigarettes (not including electronic cigarettes)?’ Also assessed were frequency of smoking (everyday/somedays), and average number of cigarettes smoked per day (cpd) on days smoked. Cigarettes per day for those smoking some days was calculated by multiplying number of days smoked in the last 30 days by cigarettes smoked on those days and dividing the result by 30.

Former smoking at T1 (ie, ‘already quit’ at T1) was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in one’s lifetime but indicating not smoking at T1. Smoking status at T2 was used to determine if people who had already quit cigarettes at T1 were smoking at T2 (ie, had relapsed), and if people who were smoking at T1 were abstinent (not smoking) at T2 (figure 2).

Smoking status at T2 (2018) among participants who were already quit or were smoking at T1 (2015).

Exposure variables

Respondents were asked at T2, “Have you ever used e-cigarettes or other electronic vapor products at least once a month for at least 3 months? (Do not include products such as iQOS or Glo that heat tobacco leaf)” and “Have you ever used nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) including as a patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, or nasal spray for 1 year or more? (Do not include e-cigarette or NRT use lasting less than 1 year)?” Possible responses were ‘No’, ‘Yes, currently’ or ‘Yes, but only in the past’. Duration (years) of use was asked of past or current users of ENDS or NRT. ENDS users provided average number of days per month used and number of times per day on days used.

The combination of responses to the ENDS and NRT questions identified four mutually exclusive groups reporting use of only ENDS or only NRT, either only currently or only in the past. Analyses compared (1) past exclusive ENDS users with past exclusive NRT users and (2) current exclusive ENDS users with current exclusive NRT users.

Outcome variables

A dichotomous variable was created to indicate subsequent relapse (at T2) among those already quit at T1 (figure 2). If they indicated at T2 that they currently smoked cigarettes they were coded as having relapsed (1), and if not currently smoking, as not having relapsed (0).

A second dichotomous variable indicated subsequent abstinence (at T2) among those currently smoking at T1 (figure 2). If they indicated at T2 that they were not currently smoking they were coded as abstinent (1) (ie, had quit), and if currently smoking as not abstinent (0).

Covariates

Multivariable and sensitivity analyses included covariates selected for their associations in previous research with smoking cessation28,33 and to minimise potential confounding. Covariates assessed at baseline included age, sex, education, race, medical insurance status, number of quit smoking attempts, years smoked, age when quit (among those already quit at T1) and ever use of prescription cessation medications.

Covariates assessed at T1 were marital status, income, quitting intentions, current use of ENDS, mental and physical health34 (5-point Likert scales), history of anxiety and/or depression, perceived stress,35 and number of hours spent on typical weekdays and weekend days engaging in ‘moderate’ and ‘strenuous’ physical activity. For those already quit at T1, years abstinent at T1 was estimated using baseline age, age when quit (asked at baseline), and age when the T1 survey was completed. Current use of other tobacco products and self-reported cancer diagnosis were assessed at both T1 and T2. The duration of time between completion of T1 and T2 surveys was calculated.

Statistical analyses

For all measures, frequencies and proportions were calculated for respondents who at T1 had already quit or were smoking and who reported at T2 using ENDS or NRT exclusively. For those already quit at T1, two unadjusted log-binomial regressions compared ENDS versus NRT use (past and current) on RR of relapse at T2. Similarly, for those who were smoking at T1, two unadjusted log-binomial regressions compared ENDS versus NRT use (past and current) on likelihood of abstinence (RR) at T2. Multivariable (adjusted) log-binomial regressions were also conducted and included age, sex, race, and income, with retention of covariates in model fitting and final models based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) criterion to avoid overfitting.36 Also included as a covariate in the multivariable models among respondents who were already quit at T1 was number of quit smoking attempts (as an indication of prior level of dependence).37 38 In multivariable models for respondents who were smoking at T1 we included T1 smoking frequency (everyday/somedays smoking).39 Sensitivity analyses to check robustness of ENDS versus NRT associations with relapse and abstinence were conducted, ie, covariates not retained in multivariable models, along with other covariates assessed, were tested individually in log-binomial regressions (ie, only one covariate and the exposure variable).

Results

Sample characteristics

Already quit (T1)

Approximately half of respondents who had already quit at T1 were younger than 55 years (52%; n=1602; online supplemental table 1). 76% (n=2379) were female, 78% (n=2411) reported household income of US$50 000 or higher, and 89% (n=2766) had received post-secondary education. 90% (n=2811) were White, and 94% (n=2938) were medically insured. Almost half (45%; n=1400) had smoked for more than 20 years.

Currently smoking (T1)

Approximately two-thirds of respondents who smoked at T1 were younger than 55 years (63%; n=639; online supplemental table 1), 80% (n=810) were female, 63% (n=636) reported household income of US$50 000 or higher, and 83% (n=844) had received post-secondary education. 88% (n=898) were White, and 89% (n=902) were medically insured. The majority (81%; n=827) smoked daily. The median number of cigarettes smoked per day was 10.0 (IQR=5.0–15.0), and 86% (n=878) had been smoking for more than 20 years. Two-thirds (68%; n=687) had made between 1 to 5 quit attempts. 40% (n=404) indicated wanting to quit within 6 months.

Smoking (relapse) at T2

Past use of ENDS versus NRT

Among respondents who had already quit cigarettes at T1, the unadjusted risk of relapse at T2 was higher for past exclusive ENDS versus past exclusive NRT users (RR=2.90; 95% CI 2.12 to 3.98; p<0.001; table 1); specifically, 11.2% (71/634) of past ENDS users had relapsed compared with 3.9% (73/1893) of past NRT users (figure 3). The multivariable model, which retained age, sex and income, indicated two times greater risk of relapse for past exclusive ENDS versus past exclusive NRT users (RR=2.09; 95% CI 1.49 to 2.92; p<0.001; table 1). Sensitivity analyses that adjusted for remaining covariates individually indicated that risk of relapse remained significantly higher for past exclusive ENDS users versus past exclusive NRT users (RRs=1.96–3.07, all p<0.005) (online supplemental table 2).

Current use of ENDS versus NRT

Among respondents who had already quit cigarettes at T1, the unadjusted risk of relapse at T2 was not significantly different between current exclusive ENDS versus current exclusive NRT users (RR=1.04; 95% CI 0.59 to 1.84; p=0.89; table 1); specifically, 8.2% (30/368) of current ENDS users had relapsed compared with 7.8% (17/217) of current NRT users (figure 3). In the multivariable analysis none of the included covariates were retained; sensitivity analyses that adjusted for covariates individually also indicated nonsignificance between current exclusive ENDS versus current exclusive NRT users (all p>0.57) (online supplemental table 3).

Smoking abstinence at T2

Past use of ENDS versus NRT

Among respondents who were smoking cigarettes at T1, the unadjusted likelihood of abstinence at T2 was higher for past exclusive ENDS versus past exclusive NRT users (RR=1.56; 95% CI 1.18 to 2.06; p=0.002; table 1); specifically, 26% (101/384) of past ENDS users were abstinent compared with 17% (64/380) of past NRT users (figure 4). The multivariable model, which retained age, sex and smoking frequency, indicated a similar likelihood of abstinence (RR=1.56; 95% CI 1.13 to 2.16; p=0.007; table 1). Sensitivity analyses that adjusted for remaining covariates individually, however, found no difference in abstinence between past exclusive ENDS users versus past exclusive NRT users after adjustment for quitting intentions (RR=1.30, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.76; p=0.09), but a higher likelihood of abstinence for past exclusive ENDS versus past exclusive NRT users after individual adjustment for remaining covariates (RRs=1.30–1.78, all p<0.03) (online supplemental table 4).

Rate of abstinence (unadjusted) at T2 by current or past use of ENDS or NRT among respondents who were smoking at T1 (number quit appears above bars). ENDS, electronic nicotine delivery systems; NRT, nicotine replacement therapy.

Current use of ENDS versus NRT

Among respondents who were smoking at T1, the unadjusted likelihood of abstinence at T2 was higher for current exclusive ENDS versus current exclusive NRT users (RR=1.35; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.80; p=0.04; table 1); specifically, 58% (105/180) of current ENDs users were abstinent at T2 compared with 43% (32/74) of current NRT users (figure 4). In contrast, the multivariable model, which retained only smoking frequency, indicated no significant difference in the likelihood of abstinence for current exclusive ENDS versus current exclusive NRT users (RR=1.38; 95% CI 0.93 to 2.05; p=0.11; table 1). Sensitivity analyses that adjusted for remaining covariates individually indicated non-significance after adjustment for age (RR=1.27; 95% CI 0.95 to 1.70, p=0.11), number of previous quit smoking attempts (RR=1.40; 95% CI 0.94 to 2.09, p=0.10), years smoked (RR=1.32; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.75; p=0.06), quitting intentions (RR=1.26; 95% CI 0.92 to 1.72, p=0.14), or hours of weekly physical activity (RR=1.35; 95% CI 0.87 to 2.09, p=0.18) (online supplemental table 5). Individual adjustment for other covariates, however, indicated a higher likelihood of abstinence for current exclusive ENDS versus current exclusive NRT users (RRs=1.34–1.39, all p<0.05).

Discussion

Whether ENDS should be recommended over NRT for smoking cessation is currently controversial in the tobacco control community.3 Important to this debate is whether ENDS use by people who had already quit cigarettes increases their risk of relapsing to combustible cigarettes compared with use of NRT. We observed that among people who were already quit at T1, reporting past exclusive use of ENDS at T2 was associated, in unadjusted analyses, with approximately three times the risk of a return to smoking at T2 compared with past exclusive use of NRT. In the multivariable analysis that adjusted for age, sex and income, past use of ENDS was associated with more than double the risk of relapse by those already quit compared with past use of NRT. This is the first observational study, to our knowledge, to document an increased risk of smoking relapse associated with past exclusive use of ENDS compared with past exclusive use of NRT.

Our measures of exclusive ENDS and exclusive NRT use captured repeated but not necessarily daily use. Among respondents who were already quit at T1, past exclusive NRT use reported at T2 (ie, ever use for at least 1 year) likely captured use of NRT to quit initially (when daily use is recommended), but possibly also occasionally after quitting to avoid lapsing during tempting situations (eg, when around other people who smoke or during stressful circumstances). Our broad measure of ENDS use (at least once per month for at least 3 months) among those already quit at T1 may have also captured similar patterns of past use.

Although among respondents smoking at T1 current exclusive use of ENDS at T2 was associated with a higher unadjusted likelihood of abstinence compared with use of NRT, the multivariable analysis (which adjusted for smoking frequency) indicated no significant difference in likelihood of abstinence. Sensitivity analyses that adjusted for remaining covariates individually found either no difference in likelihood of abstinence (ie, when adjusted for age, number of previous quit smoking attempts, years smoked, quitting intentions or physical activity), or an increased likelihood (eg, after individual adjustment for sex, income, race, cigarettes per day). This pattern of results points out the potential importance for future observational studies to assess and control for a number of sociodemographic and health and smoking behaviour-related covariates in estimating the degree to which ENDS (vs NRT) is (or is not) associated with subsequent abstinence by people who smoke.

Whether a smoking behaviour-related variable is an important covariate, however, may depend on whether relapse or abstinence is the outcome under investigation. We found that smoking frequency (someday vs everyday smoking) was not a significant predictor of relapse (and therefore not retained in final relapse models) but was significant in predicting abstinence. This is theoretically plausible because participants contributing to relapse analyses were people who had already quit for some years before T1, making their historical smoking frequency less relevant to whether they relapsed at T2 using ENDS versus NRT; this contrasts with participants who were smoking at T1 and for whom heaviness or frequency of smoking would be more predictive of abstinence in the period between T1 and T2.40 For people who were already quit at T1, similarities between ENDS (vs NRT) and cigarettes, rather than frequency of smoking in the past, may be more significant drivers of relapse. In addition to smoking frequency, future research should consider whether variables which while separately do not appear to be confounders (ie, are of minor interest) could, with other covariates, cumulatively contribute to confounding bias.

Although we did not have sufficient power to test for interactions, future observational studies that do so may illuminate for whom ENDS (vs NRT) may or may not be more efficacious for short- or longer-term smoking cessation. A recent cross-sectional study of 11 703 individuals in the UK highlighted the potential importance of testing for interactions, particularly for age. In that study the use of electronic cigarettes (vs non-use) was associated with greater odds of quitting among younger and middle-aged adults, but results were inconclusive for older adults (>65 years).41

A 2025 Cochrane review that pooled data from nine RCTs with follow-ups of at least 6 months concluded that ENDS were associated with greater cessation compared with NRT42; however, methodological limitations of RCTs included in the review have been noted,43 for example, possible non-equivalency between levels of nicotine consumed in ENDS versus NRT arms.43 Future research that assesses dosages of ENDS and NRT consumed, whether use of ENDS continues after smoking cessation, and that include longer follow-up periods, will further address whether ENDS (vs NRT) delay smoking cessation by increasing the risk of relapse.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the study included its direct comparison between ENDS and NRT, the 3-year (mean) follow-up assessment of smoking relapse by those who were already quit at T1, and the large number of covariates assessed that allowed sensitivity analyses for testing robustness of results.

Our sample of people who had already quit or were smoking (at T1) is not representative of the US population because it included a higher proportion of individuals who were older, female, had higher income and education, and Non-Hispanic White.

Because our measures of NRT captured repeated (longer-term) use, our results cannot generalise to individuals using NRT for shorter periods of time. Additionally, our sample of people smoking at T1 were arguably highly tobacco dependent. The majority smoked every day and had smoked for more than 20 years. Their generally higher rates of quitting compared with other samples44 may possibly have been due to a strong motivation to reduce their health risks.

We were not able to determine whether past exclusive use of ENDS or NRT among people who had already quit amounted to similar levels of nicotine intake in the past. Accomplishing this is challenging because ENDS users, especially users of disposable ENDS, may be unaware of nicotine concentrations,45 particularly as nicotine content in some ENDS may have increased over time46 and devices continually evolve to be more efficient in delivering nicotine.47 Future observational studies should more closely monitor the nicotine dosages of NRT, strength of nicotine in e-liquids used in ENDS, and how often and for how long they are used in comparing risk of relapse from ENDS versus NRT.

We also could not determine if past ENDS use among people who had already quit at T1 occurred before or after T1, nor their reasons for using ENDS. If use had occurred before T1, it may have been to quit smoking, a goal which could be seen as successful because they were not smoking at T1. It is also plausible, however, that use may have occurred after T1. This may be more likely because ENDS’ popularity in the USA increased after our baseline assessment (2006–2013)48 when most formerly smoking respondents had already quit. Reasons for starting ENDS use after established abstinence could include the appeal of flavours or other aspects of ENDS and/or a perception of reduced risk from ENDS compared with combustible cigarettes. Similarities in the tactile, visual and/or physical characteristics of combustible cigarettes and ENDS (eg, throat hit, vapour production) may have also played a role. These factors might have precipitated ENDS use that was followed by relapse back to combustible cigarettes.

Conclusions

Compared with NRT, using ENDS may increase smoking cessation in the shorter term but it may also increase the risk of relapsing back to combustible cigarettes compared with if NRT had been used. Assessing and controlling for sociodemographic and smoking behaviour-related variables may be important for determining the likelihood of abstinence and risks of relapse when comparing ENDS with NRT.
