From Molecular Crystals to Electronic Devices: Investigating Structure–Property Relationships of Iron(III) and Cobalt(II) Coordination Complexes
Authors: Subhajit Saha, Samit Pramanik, Sudipta Pathak, Riya Sadhukhan, Arnab Ghosh, Dipak K. Goswami, Hon Man Lee, Joaquín Ortega-Castro, Rosa M. Gomila, Antonio Frontera, Subrata Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal–organic semiconductors based on earth-abundant 3d transition metals offer great potential for optoelectronic devices, yet establishing direct structure–property relationships between specific supramolecular networks, electronic structure, and charge transport remains a major challenge. In this work, we address this gap through a combined experimental and theoretical investigation of two new coordination complexes, [Fe­(N 3 L 1 )­(HN 3 L 1 )]­Cl4.4­(H2O) (complex 1) and [Co­(HN 3 L 1 )­(H2O)2Cl]­Cl2.3­(H2O) (complex 2), derived from the previously unexplored 4-imidazole-2,6-di­(pyrazinyl)­pyridine ligand. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction reveals extensive supramolecular architectures stabilized by π···π stacking and hydrogen bonding involving lattice water molecules and chloride counterions. Hirshfeld surface analysis and 2D fingerprint plots quantify the dominant intermolecular contacts, while molecular electrostatic potential, Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules, and NCI plot calculations confirm significant anion···π and lone-pair···π interactions in the solid state. Optical absorption studies yield band gaps of 2.02 and 2.16 eV for complexes 1 and 2, respectively, positioning them for visible-light applications. Fabricated Schottky barrier diodes (ITO/sample/Au) demonstrate rectifying behavior, with device 1 exhibiting a rectification ratio of approximately 66 and a conductivity of 9.2 × 10–5 S m–1, outperforming device 2 (rectification ratio ∼17, conductivity 1.14 × 10–5 S m–1). Periodic DFT calculations reveal that the superior electrical performance of complex 1 stems from localized intra-gap states associated with the deprotonated imidazolyl moiety, which facilitate charge transport through hopping mechanisms. These findings elucidate the crucial role of molecular packing and ligand deprotonation in tuning charge transport in functional metal–organic materials.

Introduction

The design and synthesis of metal–organic complexes for optoelectronic applications have emerged as a rapidly expanding field of research due to their distinctive optical and electronic properties originating from metal–ligand interactions. − Precise control over the chemical structure at the molecular level enables effective tuning of these properties. , When processed as thin films, such materials support the development of low-cost electronic and optoelectronic devices, including LEDs, solar cells, photodetectors, transistors, and chemical or biological sensors. ,− Related operational stability principles and defect-passivation strategies have also proven vital in temperature-immune organic transistor architectures and dielectric energy-storage ceramics. This overview highlights recent advances in molecular structure control and their extension to solid-state materials for energy conversion and information technology applications. ,

Researchers have widely employed terpyridines and other polypyridines as chelating ligands for d-block metal ions because of their strong electron-withdrawing properties and notable coordination chemistry. − Terpyridines and polypyridines have emerged as foundational chelating ligands for d-block metal ions, primarily due to their robust electron affinity. − By utilizing oligo-pyridine motifs, researchers can synthesize exceptionally stable metal complexes that serve as the backbone for various chemical innovations. Furthermore, recent developments in transition-metal-catalyzed multicomponent reactions, such as single-atom copper platforms or dual Au/Ag systems, demonstrate the growing utility of tailored metal–nitrogen coordination environments in assembling complex functional architectures. Consequently, terpyridine-based complexes have become a focal point in supramolecular chemistry, with their utility increasingly extending into materials science. ,, These complexes exhibit a diverse array of magnetic and optoelectronic properties, making them indispensable for applications such as electrocatalysis and hydrogen evolution. Beyond these functions, they are also strategically employed as active layers in the fabrication of resistive switching memory devices, as sensitive fluorescent probes for pyrophosphate (PPi) detection, and in the development of advanced photo-functional nanomaterials. ,

Aromatic ligands (such as bipyridine, terpyridine, phenanthroline, porphyrins, or other conjugated systems) are involved in π···π stacking between neighboring molecules that leads to closer packing and better orbital overlap. − This enhanced overlap allows partial delocalization of π-electrons across adjacent moieties, facilitating charge transport through the solid. As a result, metal complexes exhibiting strong π···π interactions often show increased electrical conductivity compared to complexes lacking such interactions. , The stacked arrangement creates pathways for electron hopping or band-like charge movement, thereby lowering resistance. Hydrogen bonding similarly promotes close packing and ordered arrangement of neighboring metal complexes, leading to the formation of extended supramolecular networks. This structural organization facilitates charge transport by providing continuous pathways for the movement of charge carriers. Hydrogen bonding also stabilizes charge-separated and mixed-valence states in metal complexes, which further promotes metal–ligand charge transfer and improves conductivity. In addition, hydrogen-bonding interactions also enhance the electrical conductivity of metal complexes by improving molecular organization, stabilizing charge-transfer states, and enabling efficient electron or proton transport pathways.

Iron­(III) and cobalt­(II) metal complexes have emerged as pivotal materials in the design of next-generation optoelectronic devices due to their earth abundance and tunable electronic properties. , Unlike traditional noble-metal-based luminophores, iron­(III) complexes utilize ligand-to-metal charge-transfer (LMCT) states to achieve photoactive excited states suitable for light-harvesting applications. Recent advances have extended the luminescence lifetimes of iron­(III) carbene complexes to the 100 ns range, making them viable candidates for photon upconversion and high-efficiency organic light-emitting diodes. Simultaneously, cobalt­(II) complexes are extensively researched for their role in dye-sensitized solar cells, where they function as efficient redox mediators that minimize voltage losses. The structural versatility of cobalt­(II) coordination environments allows for the precise modulation of carrier transport and interfacial polarization in flexible electronics. Furthermore, these 3d transition-metal complexes are being integrated into hybrid electro-spun nanofibers to create intelligent photodetectors with high specific surface. The ability to engineer the energy levels of iron­(III) complexes through ligand modification has facilitated their use in photo-redox catalysis, bridging the gap between solar energy conversion and chemical synthesis. In the realm of stretchable optoelectronics, ionic-conduction-based cobalt systems provide the necessary stability under high operating voltages while mitigating electrochemical degradation. Collectively, these developments demonstrate that the shift from scarce 4d/5d metals to iron- and cobalt-based systems is essential for the sustainable commercialization of third-generation photovoltaics. By optimizing doublet-triplet energy transfer in iron­(III) systems and enhancing the redox kinetics in cobalt­(II) platforms, researchers are achieving performance levels previously reserved for iridium and ruthenium analogues. −

Despite these advances, a fundamental research gap remains regarding how noncovalent network assemblies (such as π···π stacking, hydrogen-bonding, and anion/lone-pair···π interactions) coupled with ligand protonation states directly dictate solid-state intra-gap electronic states and charge transport mechanisms in 3d transition-metal complexes. In this work, we combine experimental and theoretical investigations of two new iron­(III) and cobalt­(II) coordination complexes derived from 4-imidazole-2,6-di­(pyrazinyl)­pyridine which has not been explored to date. We provide a detailed analysis of their supramolecular architectures, which are stabilized by extensive networks of π···π and hydrogen-bonding interactions for the title complexes 1 and 2. The Hirshfeld surface (HS) analysis, together with the corresponding two-dimensional fingerprint plots, provides a comparative insight into the intermolecular interactions, highlighting both their common features and their distinct contributions to the overall supramolecular architectures. To complement the experimental findings, a theoretical investigation was performed for complex 2, owing to its more accessible molecular size and electronic configuration, using molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surface analysis, Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM), and NCI plot to quantify the nature of the supramolecular assembly and the impact of anion···π and lone-pair···π interactions. Furthermore, the potential of these materials in electronic applications was evaluated through the fabrication of ITO/sample/Au Schottky devices, supported by periodic DFT calculations, to elucidate the charge transport mechanisms and the role of localized intra-gap states in enhancing electrical conductivity. Therefore, this work provides new insights into the development of previously unexplored Fe­(III) and Co­(II) coordination complexes of 4-imidazole-2,6-di­(pyrazinyl)­pyridine and the establishment of a direct structure–property relationship between supramolecular interactions, ligand-centered intra-gap states, and charge transport behavior through combined experimental device fabrication and periodic DFT analysis.

Experimental Section

Materials and Apparatus

All chemical reagents of analytical grade and solvents of spectroscopic grade were purchased from commercial suppliers and employed without further purification. Doubly distilled water prepared freshly was utilized throughout the synthetic process, and all reactions were conducted under aerobic conditions. Elemental analyses (carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen) were conducted using a PerkinElmer 2400 Series-II CHN analyzer, USA. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were captured using a Perkin Elmer LX-1 FT-IR spectrophotometer over the range of 4000 to 400 cm–1, utilizing a contemporary diamond attenuated total reflectance accessory. Keithley 2450 source-measure units were used for the electrical characterizations of the Schottky devices at room temperature (300 K) under dark conditions. Avaspec-3648 UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer was engaged to record the ground-state absorption spectra of the concerned complexes (spin-coated thin films on ITO/glass substrates).

Results and Discussion

FT-IR Analysis

The characteristic IR stretching frequencies for O–H, N–H, and C–H vibrations are observed in the ranges of 3300–3600 cm–1 (broad), 3100–3500 cm–1 (broad), and 2700–3100 cm–1, respectively. In the IR spectra of complexes 1 and 2, a broad absorption band accompanied by several shoulder peaks is observed in the 3000–3600 cm–1 region. These bands are attributed to the overlapping O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl, coordinated and noncoordinated water molecules, and/or imino group in the complexes. In infrared (IR) spectroscopy, the CC stretching vibrations of aromatic rings and the CN stretching vibrations of heteroaromatic or azomethine groups are generally observed in the 1650–1600 cm–1 region. Owing to the higher bond strength and force constant of the CN bond compared with the CC bond, the CN stretching vibration typically appears at a slightly higher wavenumber. In the IR spectrum of complex 1, the absorption bands at 1644 and 1601 cm–1 are assigned to the CN and aromatic CC stretching vibrations, respectively. Similarly, the corresponding bands appear at 1648 and 1610 cm–1 in the IR spectrum of complex 2, confirming the presence of CN and aromatic CC functionalities in the coordinated ligands. IR peaks in the 1500–1400 cm–1 region are generally attributed to the skeletal stretching vibrations of aromatic and heteroaromatic compounds (mixed CC/azomethine (CN) skeletal modes). This region may also contain contributions from C–H in-plane bending, CH2 scissoring, and CH3 asymmetric and symmetric bending (umbrella deformation) vibrations, depending on the ligand structures of the complexes. Accordingly, several absorption bands are observed for both complexes in the 1500–1400 cm–1 region, which are primarily assigned to the aromatic ring skeletal (CC and CN) vibrations, with possible contributions from C–H bending. These functional groups and their corresponding absorption bands are also present in the free ligand; however, slight shifts in their positions are observed upon complex formation. These shifts indicate the coordination of the ligand to the metal center, resulting in changes in the electronic environment of the corresponding functional groups.

The variation in the number of coordinated tridentate ligands (N 3 L 1 ) with different metal ions in complexes 1 and 2 not only leads to distinct structural architectures but also significantly influences the extent of their supramolecular behavior. The coordination mode around the metal center can be best described as the meridional geometry of the distorted octahedron for complex 1. For complex 1, the two pyrazinyl nitrogen atoms (N2 and N5) and the two pyridyl nitrogen atoms (N4 and N6) of our title ligand (N 3 L 1 ) generate the basal plane, while the axial positions are occupied by another two pyrazinyl nitrogen atoms (N1 and N3). The average Fe–Neq bond distance is 1.935 Å [Fe1–N2 = 1.888(2) Å, Fe1–N4 = 1.977(2) Å, Fe1–N5 = 1.886(2) Å, and Fe1–N6 = 1.990(2) Å]. The axial nitrogen atoms are positioned somewhere at a longer distance [Fe1–N1 = 1.998(3) Å and Fe1–N3 = 1.977(2) Å] than the equatorial bond distances [Fe1–Neq = 1.935 Å]. For complex 2, the basal plane is formed by the two oxygen atoms (O1 and O2) of two water molecules and the one pyrazinyl nitrogen atom (N5) of the title ligand (N 3 L 1 ) and one chloride ion (Cl1). The trans-axial positions are occupied by the two pyrazinyl nitrogen atoms (N1 and N3) of the title ligand (N 3 L 1 ). In the basal plane, the Co1–N5 bond length is 2.075(2) Å, Co1–Cl1 is 2.3455(10) Å, whereas the axial Co–O bond lengths [2.100(2) Å and 2.120(2) Å] are shorter compared to the equatorial bond. Co–N (nitrogen atoms come from pyrazine moiety) bond distances are 2.190 Å and 2.183 Å. As per data available from the crystallographic database, the Co–N (pyridine) and average Co–N (pyrazine) bond distances are 2.075 Å and 2.1865 Å. From the data, it is evident that pyridine (presence of one nitrogen atom) can form a comparatively stronger bond compared to pyrazine (presence of two nitrogen atoms). Hence, Co–N (pyridine) bond distances are shorter than Co–N (pyrazine). The average metal-centric bond angles ∠N–Ni–N are 105.82° for complex 1 and 105.72° for complex 2. In complex 1, the two ligands (N 3 L 1 ) coordinate to the Fe­(III) center through six nitrogen donor atoms, generating four distinct five-membered chelate rings: [Fe1/N1/C5/C6/N2], [Fe1/N2/C10/C11/N3], [Fe1/N4/C25/C26/N5] and [Fe1/N5/C30/C31/N6]. These chelate rings exhibit bite angles of 80.59(10)° for ∠N1–Fe1–N2, 80.55(10)° for ∠N2–Fe1–N3, 80.51(10)° for ∠N4–Fe1–N5, and 80.69(10)° for ∠N5–Fe1–N6, indicating a nearly uniform coordination geometry (FeN6 chromophore) around the Fe­(III) ion. However, in complex 2, the N 3 L 1 ligand is able to bind with the Co­(II) ion to form two five-membered chelate rings, [Co1/N1/C4/C9/N5] and [Co1/N5/C13/C8/N3]. The corresponding bite angles, ∠N1–Co1–N5 = 75.89(9)° and ∠N3–Co1–N5 = 75.51(9)°, are noticeably smaller than those observed for complex 1, reflecting the different coordination environment (CoN3O2Cl chromophore) and geometric preferences of the Co­(II) center. In complex 1, the Fe­(III) ion is deviated by a distance of 0.023 Å toward the axial nitrogen atom (N34) from the basal plane (N15N7N9N25), whereas in complex 2, the same is 0.014 Å toward the axial O1 atom from the basal plane (N35N30N12N19). The overall electrical charge of [Fe­(HN 3 L 1 )­(N 3 L 1 )] fragment is +4 (+3 charge of Fe­(III) and +1 charge from protonated N atom of imidazole unit of one N 3 L 1 ligand), which is taken care by four noncoordinated chloride (Cl–) for complex 1. In the case of complex 2, the overall charge of [Co­(HN 3 L 1 )­Cl­(H2O)2] is +2 (+2 charge of Co­(II) and +1 charge from the protonated N atom of imidazole unit of one N3L 1 ligand and −1 charge from Cl–), which is taken care by two noncoordinated Cl–. Here, in the present work, the title ligand (N 3 L 1 ) utilizes two pyrazine and one pyridine nitrogen in the coordination game. The solid-state structures of both the complexes are stabilized through the combination of π···π stacking and various types of hydrogen-bonding interactions (Tables S4 and S5).

From a supramolecular point of view, the observed noncovalent interactions play a crucial role in directing the self-assembly of the coordination complexes into stable higher-dimensional architectures for both the complexes 1 and 2. The cooperative combination of π···π stacking and hydrogen-bonding interactions generates well-organized 1D chains, supramolecular tapes, and 2D layered networks in the solid state. Chloride ions and lattice water molecules act as important supramolecular linkers and bridges, leading to the formation of robust hydrogen-bonded architecture. Overall, these weak interactions collectively govern crystal packing, stabilize the supramolecular assembly, and control the long-range organization of the crystal structure.

The crystal structures of the synthesized complexes 1 and 2 reveal that the coordinated ligand (N 3 L 1 ) acts as a tridentate chelating ligand, forming chelate rings around the metal centers. In both complexes, coordination of the heterocyclic ligand generates five-membered metallacyclic rings, which establish a well-defined coordination environment and significantly influence the overall geometry of the metal centers. Here, the tridentate ligand coordinates to the metal center twice in complex 1, whereas it coordinates only once in complex 2, while the remaining coordination sites of complex 2 are occupied by other coordinating ligands. Consequently, complex 1 contains four five-membered metallacyclic chelate rings formed by the tridentate ligand, while complex 2 possesses only two such chelate rings. This difference in the chelation pattern significantly influences the coordination environment around the metal centers, contributing to variations in their molecular geometry, stability, and supramolecular assembly. The formation of these chelate rings also enhances the thermodynamic stability of the complexes through the chelate effect (largely driven by the entropy). Moreover, the rigid coordination imposed by the tridentate ligand governs the spatial orientation of the coordinated molecules, thereby influencing the supramolecular assembly and the resulting intermolecular interactions within the crystal lattice. Thus, these rings reduce the likelihood of ligand dissociation and enhance the structural integrity of the complex.

Hirshfeld Surface Analysis

To investigate the intermolecular interactions governing crystal packing, HS analysis − and the corresponding two-dimensional (2D) fingerprint plots − were generated using Crystal Explorer. Standard neutron-derived hydrogen-atom bond lengths were employed throughout the calculations. Each point on the HS is described by two geometrical parameters: the external distance (d e ), corresponding to the distance from the surface to the nearest neighboring atom outside the molecule, and the internal distance (d i ), representing the distance to the nearest atom within the molecule. These parameters are used to calculate the normalized contact distance (d or), which serves as an effective descriptor for identifying significant intermolecular interactions and is given by dnorm=(di−rivdw)rivdw+(de−revdw)revdw where r i vdw and r e vdw represent the van der Waals radii of the internal and external atoms, respectively. The value of d norm reflects the nature of intermolecular contacts, with negative values indicating distances shorter than the sum of the van der Waals radii and positive values corresponding to longer separations. For visualization, the HS is mapped using a red–white–blue color scheme, where red identifies regions of close intermolecular contacts, white denotes contacts close to the van der Waals limit, and blue represents areas with comparatively longer intermolecular distances. Owing to its dependence on the crystallographic geometry, the HS generated from each CIF is unique for a given crystal structure.

Hirshfeld surfaces of complexes 1 and 2 were generated and mapped with several surface properties, including d norm, d i, d e, shape index, curvedness, and fragment patches (Figures and ). Each of these surface mappings provides complementary information regarding the intermolecular interactions governing the crystal packing. The shape index surface is particularly useful for identifying donor–acceptor contacts and recognizing complementary surface features associated with specific intermolecular interactions, whereas the curvedness surface illustrates the degree of local surface curvature, thereby partitioning the molecular surface into distinct regions. Fragment-patch mapping highlights the spatial relationship between neighboring molecules by assigning different color patches according to their proximity to the reference molecule. To facilitate visualization of the molecular framework enclosed by the HS, all surfaces were rendered with partial transparency while maintaining a common molecular orientation for both structures. The overall pattern of intermolecular contacts was further examined using two-dimensional (2D) fingerprint plots, which combine the internal (d i) and external (d e) distance parameters to provide a quantitative representation of all close contacts present within the crystal (Figures and ). Characteristic intermolecular interactions appear as well-defined features or spikes in these plots, allowing their nature and relative significance to be readily distinguished. In addition, the fingerprint plots were decomposed into individual atom-pair contributions, enabling the identification and quantitative assessment of specific interaction types that overlap in the complete fingerprint map. The HS analysis, together with the decomposed fingerprint plots, confirms the occurrence of the intermolecular interactions discussed above and demonstrates their collective role in stabilizing the crystal packing and promoting the three-dimensional supramolecular architecture of the complexes.

Among the heteroatom-involving interactions, Cl···H/H···Cl contacts represent the second most significant contribution in both complexes. These interactions appear as two well-defined spikes in the fingerprint plots and contribute 18.2% to the HS of complex 1, whereas their contribution increases markedly to 26.3% in complex 2. This notable enhancement suggests that chloride-mediated hydrogen-bonding interactions play a more dominant role in stabilizing the crystal lattice of complex 2. The O···H/H···O contacts are also characterized by a pair of sharp spikes in the fingerprint plots, reflecting the presence of strong hydrogen-bonding interactions involving oxygen atoms. These contacts account for 8.6% of the HS in complex 1 but increase to 12.9% in complex 2, demonstrating a greater participation of oxygen-containing hydrogen bonds in the supramolecular assembly of the latter. In contrast, the N···H/H···N interactions contribute more significantly to the crystal packing of complex 1 (11.9%) than complex 2 (7.8%). The reduced contribution in complex 2 indicates that nitrogen-centered hydrogen-bonding interactions are comparatively less important, with the packing instead being dominated by Cl···H/H···Cl and O···H/H···O contacts. The C···C contacts, which are commonly associated with aromatic π···π stacking interactions, make only a minor contribution in both structures. Nevertheless, the contribution is slightly higher for complex 2 (1.8%) than for complex 1 (1.1%), implying a somewhat greater extent of π···π interactions in the former.

Overall, the fingerprint plot analysis reveals that complex 2 exhibits substantially stronger Cl···H/H···Cl and O···H/H···O interactions, together with a modest increase in C···C contacts, whereas complex 1 is characterized by a relatively larger contribution from N···H/H···N interactions. These differences indicate that, despite sharing similar types of intermolecular interactions, the relative importance of individual contact types varies between the two complexes, resulting in distinct supramolecular packing environments and stabilization mechanisms.

Theoretical Analysis for Noncovalent Interactions

In this section, we provide a detailed theoretical investigation of the noncovalent interactions that stabilize the solid-state structure of complex 2. We have employed MEP surface analysis on a neutral fragment of complex 2 to identify the nucleophilic and electrophilic regions of the molecule. To gain deeper insights into the nature and strength of the supramolecular contacts, QTAIM was utilized to analyze the hydrogen-bonding (H-bonding) networks within several representative assemblies of complex 2 with the lattice water molecules. Furthermore, we have examined the occurrence of anion···π and lone-pair···π interactions involving the chloride anions and water molecules, respectively. The latter interactions were further characterized using Noncovalent interaction (NCI plot) analysis to visualize the stabilizing regions between the interacting moieties.

The theoretical study focuses exclusively on complex 2 because of several practical and computational considerations. Primarily, complex 2 presents a more compact system, where the impact of extensive H-bonding and auxiliary interactions can be more clearly isolated and evaluated. In contrast, complex 1 poses significant computational challenges; its larger molecular size and the requirement to model assemblies involving multiple units would result in prohibitively high computational costs. Additionally, the presence of Fe­(III) centers in complex 1 introduces a high number of unpaired electrons, which significantly complicates the electronic convergence and reliability of calculations at the PBE0-D4/def2-TZVP level of theory employed in this work. Therefore, complex 2 serves as a more accessible and representative model for understanding the supramolecular forces at play in these types of imidazole-pyrazinyl pyridine systems.

The MEP surface of complex 2 was analyzed using a neutral system where two chloride anions are located in the molecular plane and hydrogen-bonded to the imidazolium NH groups (see Figure ). The calculated MEP reveals that the global minima are located at the noncoordinated Cl atoms with values of −55.8 kcal/mol, followed by the nitrogen atoms of the pyrazine rings at −23.1 kcal/mol and the coordinated Cl atom at −22.5 kcal/mol. The MEP maximum is located at the H atoms of the coordinated water molecules at 82.8 kcal/mol, which point toward the central pyridine ring. Other H atoms on these water molecules show a value of 74.4 kcal/mol. These large positive values align with the network of hydrogen bonds involving these water molecules. Finally, the π–surface of the organic ligand is positive throughout, ranging from 30.1 kcal/mol over the imidazolium ring to 38.9 kcal/mol over the pyridine. The value over the pyrazine rings is 34.5 kcal/mol. This consistent positive potential across the surface of the ligand explains its ability to participate in anion···π and lone-pair···π interactions.

While QTAIM provides quantification, the NCI plot is particularly well-suited for visualizing the extended nature of π-interactions in real space, offering complementary information regarding the spatial extent and relative strength of the contact. The crystal structure shows that a chloride anion and a lattice water molecule are positioned directly above and below the central pyridine ring, respectively. These interactions occur in concert with strong H bonds, the quantification of which is established in Figure . Both the anion···π interaction involving the chloride and the lone-pair···π (lp···π) interaction involving the water molecule are distinctly characterized by a BCP and a bond path connecting the Cl or O atoms to one of the carbon atoms of the pyridine ring.

The extended visual nature of these contacts is more clearly delineated by the reduced density gradient (RDG) isosurfaces generated by the NCI plot analysis. For the anion···π interaction, a significant green RDG surface is observed, which partially embraces the adjacent five-membered Co-chelate ring, highlighting the extensive stabilizing region. In contrast, the RDG isosurface corresponding to the lone-pair···π interaction is significantly smaller and more localized, suggesting a comparatively less-influential interaction.

To evaluate the strength of this multi-interaction assembly, we used a neutral fragment, defined as [Co­(HN 3 L 1 )­(H2O)2Cl]­Cl2, to minimize the overestimation of binding energy that would arise from pure coulombic attraction if fully charged species were used as monomers. Despite this, the formation energy for this assembly is very large at −59.2 kcal/mol. This high value reflects the robust overall stabilization provided by the strong electrostatic attraction between the anionic Cl– species and the protonated ligand, further augmented by the coordination of the Co­(II) cation, which significantly increases the π-acidity of the aromatic ligand framework.

These transitions are characterized by higher oscillator strengths and primarily involve π → π* transitions within the aromatic ligand framework, with varying degrees of metal involvement (see Figure ).

In complex 1, the most intense transition (S0 → S37) appears at 305.2 nm (4.06 eV) with a substantial oscillator strength (f = 0.7540), reflecting strong absorption in this spectral region. This state arises from a complex superposition of LLCT transitions, predominantly involving HOMO–3 → LUMO (25.4%) (beta), HOMO–4 → LUMO (alpha, 28.8%), and HOMO–7 → LUMO (beta, 13.6%) excitations. This assignment confirms that the intense absorption at ∼305 nm arises mainly from π → π* transitions localized on the conjugated heterocyclic ligand. In complex 2, the most intense transition (S0 → S17) is located at 301.8 nm (4.11 eV) with an oscillator strength (f = 0.1892) that is lower than that in complex 1. This excitation is predominantly composed of LLCT transitions, with the primary contribution from HOMO–2 → LUMO (beta, 34.3%) and a secondary contribution from HOMO–1 → LUMO (alpha, 10.6%). The NTO analysis (Figure c) demonstrates that electron density is initially localized on the ligand π-framework (particularly the coordinated pyridine and pyrazine rings) and is transferred to unoccupied π*-orbitals of the conjugated system upon excitation, with the participation of the metal center in the hole orbitals, thus suggesting some MLCT character of this transition. The lower oscillator strength compared to complex 1 suggests that the absorption intensity is reduced, which correlates with the experimental observation that complex 2 exhibits weaker absorption in this spectral region compared to complex 1. The difference in f-values between the two complexes likely reflects the different coordination environments: the octahedral Fe­(III) complex with two coordinated ligands (complex 1) provides a more symmetric and extended conjugation pathway compared to the more distorted coordination geometry in complex 2, resulting in more efficient electronic coupling and stronger oscillator strength for the π → π* transitions.

This decomposition allows the identification of the specific regions of the electronic structure associated with each component, enabling a clear assignment of the states forming the valence band, conduction band, and possible intra-gap levels. As a result, the spatial and chemical localization of the electronic states can be directly analyzed in terms of their atomic origins. The projected density of states (PDOS) shown in Figure for the Fe-based complex 1 provides detailed insights into the orbital contributions governing its electronic structure. The valence band is mainly dominated by ligand-based p orbitals, particularly from the organic ligand, with minor hybridization from Fe d states. In contrast, the conduction band exhibits a stronger contribution from Fe d orbitals, indicating a partial metal-centered character of the unoccupied states.

A key feature of the electronic structure is the presence of intermediate states within the band gap. The PDOS clearly shows that these intra-gap states arise predominantly from the deprotonated imidazolyl fragment, with a smaller contribution from chloride ions and negligible involvement of Fe centers and coordinated water molecules.

The lack of significant Fe d character in these intermediate levels confirms their ligand-centered nature rather than metal-induced defect states. This localization highlights the important role of imidazolyl deprotonation in modulating the electronic structure by introducing accessible energy levels within the gap. As a result, these states can act as stepping stones for charge transport via hopping mechanisms, effectively reducing the transport gap and enhancing the electrical conductivity of the Fe-based systems.

In the Co-based system, both the chloride ions and water molecules play a dual role within the crystal structure. In addition to acting as coordinating ligands to the metal center, they are also present as counterions or crystallization species depending on their specific environment in the lattice.

In crystal 2, the valence band is mainly dominated by p orbital contributions from the organic ligand, which define the occupied states near the Fermi level. Additional contributions from coordinated and crystallization water molecules appear in the deeper valence region, reflecting their localized character, while chloride contributes through p states slightly below the valence-band maximum (Figure ).

The Co center contributes primarily via its d orbitals, which are distributed across the upper valence region and the onset of the conduction band. Importantly, the PDOS does not show any significant density of states within the band gap region, indicating a clean separation between the valence and conduction bands. This suggests that the coordination environment composed of the organic ligand, chloride, and water molecules does not induce localized intra-gap states, in contrast to the behavior observed in analogous Fe-based systems. As a result, the electronic structure of the Co complex is characteristic of a conventional semiconductor, where charge transport is expected to occur via direct band-to-band excitation rather than through hopping mechanisms mediated by localized states.

The dielectric response of the Fe-based system (complex 1) exhibits a structured profile in both the real and imaginary components of the dielectric function (Figure S9, Supporting Information). In the low-energy region (<3 eV), the imaginary part shows multiple pronounced peaks, indicating the presence of several allowed electronic excitations, which can be associated with the intra-gap and near-edge states identified in the band structure and PDOS. These features are more intense and numerous than in the Co-based system, suggesting a richer distribution of accessible transitions at low energies. In the intermediate energy range (3–6 eV), the dielectric function of 1 displays broader and less intense features, consistent with transitions involving more delocalized states. At higher energies, both systems tend to converge toward similar dielectric behavior, indicating that the main differences are confined to the low-energy region. The optical conductivity spectra (Figure S9, Supporting Information) show that the Fe-based system (complex 1) exhibits a slightly higher and more structured response in the low-energy region (<3 eV), consistent with the presence of additional electronic states facilitating low-energy transitions. In contrast, the Co-based system (2) displays a smoother onset, characteristic of a more conventional semiconductor. At higher energies, both systems follow similar trends, although 1 retains a more structured profile, reflecting its more complex electronic structure.

In summary, both systems exhibit semiconducting behavior with comparable band gap values and well-defined spin-resolved α and β channels. However, a key distinction arises in the Fe-based system, where intermediate states are present within the α channel, mainly associated with the deprotonated imidazole group. These intra-gap states, characterized by relatively flat bands, indicate localized electronic levels that may facilitate charge transport by providing additional pathways, such as thermally activated hopping or tunneling, effectively lowering the transport barrier. In contrast, the Co-based system lacks such intermediate states, and charge transport is therefore expected to occur via direct valence-to-conduction band excitation, resulting in a more conventional semiconducting behavior. It should be noted, however, that electrical conductivity in these materials may arise from multiple competing mechanisms, and thus, this interpretation should be considered with caution.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully synthesized, structurally characterized, and evaluated the optoelectronic properties of two new iron­(III) and cobalt­(II) coordination complexes derived from the previously unexplored 4-imidazole-2,6-di­(pyrazinyl)­pyridine ligand. X-ray crystallography coupled with HS analysis demonstrates that both molecular lattices are stabilized by extensive 2D supramolecular networks governed by π···π stacking, classical/nonclassical hydrogen-bonding, and anion/lone-pair···π interactions. Theoretical modeling via MEP, QTAIM, and NCI plots confirms the critical role of these noncovalent contacts in maintaining crystal packing and structural integrity. Both complexes demonstrate suitable optical band gaps (2.02 eV for 1 and 2.16 eV for 2) falling well within the visible regime. Fabricated ITO/sample/Au SBDs reveal clear rectifying behavior and space–charge-limited current (SCLC) conduction. Device 1 (FeIII) exhibits superior electrical performance, boasting a rectification ratio of ∼66 and an electrical conductivity nearly eight times higher than Device 2 (CoII). Periodic DFT calculations resolve the origin of this disparity, demonstrating that deprotonation of the imidazolyl fragment in complex 1 induces localized intra-gap states that serve as efficient intermediate pathways for charge hopping.

Looking forward, this work underscores the power of ligand modification and protonation-state control in tuning charge transport in 3d transition-metal complexes. A key future research direction will be expanding this structural design strategy to 4d and 5d transition metals. The broader spatial radial extension of 4d/5d orbitals is expected to increase metal–ligand electronic communication and intermolecular overlap, while stronger spin–orbit coupling could further modulate photophysical relaxation pathways and carrier lifetimes. Such comparisons between 3d and heavier 4d/5d analogues will provide deeper fundamental insights into designing cost-effective, high-performance molecular semiconductors for next-generation optoelectronic and energy-harvesting technologies.
