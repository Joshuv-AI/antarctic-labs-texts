On-site experiment on the rainfall erosion process of soil slopes and its hydraulic mechanism
Authors: Cong Li, Shipeng Hu, Xinxing Zou, Yongcan Chen, Gang Lai, Mingfang Pu, Yingqi Xiang, Qi Chen, Meiru Li, Haoran Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Loess Plateau in China is facing serious ecological problems, such as ecological degradation and slope instability, as well as the threat of geological hazards. In this study, by selecting typical slope surfaces, an on-site rainfall simulation experiment was conducted, through which the erosion process, morphology, and dynamic characteristics under different rainfall intensity, slope gradient, and vegetation coverage were probed. Major findings include the following: (1) The time to first runoff varied under different conditions, ranging from 56 to 100 s. As the three factors of rainfall intensity, slope gradient and vegetation cover varied, surface infiltration, runoff yield and sediment yield all underwent significant changes. (2) Following rainfall, the morphological parameters of the rills underwent significant changes. DEM data further revealed that all rills exhibited the characteristic of being narrow and shallow at the top and wide and deep at the bottom, with rill development being most pronounced under a rainfall intensity of 90 mm/h. (3) Flow velocity within the rills increased with increasing rainfall intensity and slope, but decreased with increasing vegetation cover. Calculations of hydraulic parameters indicated that surface runoff exhibited turbulent flow. (4) A clear linear correlation existed between the average sediment yield rate and the three indicators. Based on a comparison of these three indicators, it was concluded that unit runoff power is more suitable for describing the sediment yield process in the study area.Keywords: Silty soil slopes, Erosion, On-site rainfall simulation, Rill morphology, Hydraulic mechanismSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

As a common natural geological phenomenon, soil erosion refers to the process in which soil and its parent material are destroyed, eroded, transported, and deposited due to exogenic forcing such as moving water, wind, freeze–thaw and gravitational processes. Soil erosion can lead to the differentiation of physical parameters of small strain and hence reduce the peak intensity of soil1,2, which exacerbates environmental devastation3 and significantly hinders the sustainable development of social economy. At present, the mitigation or control of soil loss has been extensively discussed among scholars around the globe4,5.

During the past decades, numerous studies on soil erosion have been conducted, which enormously enhanced our understanding of the erosion mechanism. At the beginning of rainfall, raindrop splash is suggested to play the most dominant role in slope surface erosion6,7. As the rain continues, the splash erosion is gradually inhibited and then taken over by runoff erosion8. When the runoff erosional competency exceeds the anti-erosion ability of soil, rill erosion becomes the major cause of surface erosion9–11. In the investigations of rill erosion, the shear strength of topsoil is often used to reflect soil erodibility12. When rills are generated, surface runoff becomes multiple faster and deeper streams that possess stronger scouring and sediment-carrying abilities, and meanwhile the erosion-induced sediment yield increases drastically13. The main factors affecting surface soil erosion have also been explored by several studies. Chang et al.14 and Zhu et al.15 propose that the erosion degree increases with the increase of rainfall intensity, and slope surfaces with vegetation are less eroded than bare ones. The vertical stratification of vegetation plays a significant role in runoff and sediment yields on a slope surface16,17. In addition, a series of parameters (e.g. runoff shear force, runoff power, unit runoff power, runoff energy) has been proposed to reflect the dynamic changing process of slope surface erosion18–21. Specifically, by investigating the erosion process of a red soil slope surface, Lin et al.22 report that Reynolds number is most closely related to erosion-induced sediment yield. Through multiple modeling tests, the model based on unit runoff power has been proposed to be most suitable for describing the hydraulic process of sediment concentration23.

Although previous studies have extensively examined the various factors influencing soil slope erosion, relatively limited attention has been paid to the morphological evolution of slope surfaces before and after erosion, as well as to the hydraulic mechanisms under the combined effects of multiple factors (e.g., rainfall intensity, slope gradient, and vegetation cover). To address these deficiencies, this study selected several representative silty soil slopes in the Loess Plateau and conducted in situ simulated rainfall experiments. The runoff and sediment transport, erosion morphology, and dynamic characteristics under different rainfall intensities, slope gradients, and vegetation cover conditions were systematically analyzed. Furthermore, three-dimensional laser scanning technology was employed to quantitatively characterize the development of rills following surface erosion. The results provide important scientific support for regional soil and water conservation planning.

Characteristics of slopes in the study area

Overview of the study area

The study area is located in the Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture in Qinghai province (Fig. 1). The selected slope, situated at the western Longwu hill in the middle section of the Longwu river, has experienced serious rill erosion. The elevation is from 2475 to 2755 m, with a drop height of 280 m. Topographically, the region is high in the west and low in the east. The dark yellow or tawny-colored soil is mainly distributed in the top and the entire surface layer of the slope, with small grains and a loose and soft texture. The slope is steep at the top and bottom, and gentle in the middle, with a slope gradient ranging from 25° to 50°. Some local scarps are developed, and most of these regions are exposed. In geomorphology, the slope area belongs to a low-elevation hilly area that has undergone frequent erosion. Additionally, the area is a typical plateau temperate zone with an arid/semi-arid climate. Such a dry and cold climate, combined with abundant sunshine, often leads to a large diurnal temperature variation. According to regional meteorological data, the precipitation in the study area is scarce and concentrated, and the average annual evaporation (1470.6 mm) is greater than the average annual rainfall (426.3 mm). The daily average maximum rainfall reaches 29.05 mm, and the hourly one reaches 13.17mm. The average annual temperature is 5.2 °C, with the maximum and minimum values mainly recorded in July and August (10.1–39.7°C) and January (– 6.3 to 13.3 ℃), respectively.

Hydro geomorphology units of the study area

The zones with a slope gradient less than 30° accounts for 60% of the study area, primarily concentrated in the central and southwestern areas. These regions are favourable zones for vegetation growth. Those with a slope gradient of 35°–50° cover approximately 20% land of the study area, which is equivalent to those with a gradient greater than 50°. Based on hydrology analysis, the catchment boundary that corresponds to the rills can be obtained. This boundary can indicate the origin of material inside the rills and the potential water energy distribution, which is beneficial for determining the risk of debris flow and rill erosion. By tracking the water flow direction from the rill outlet, one can calculate the distance between the water flow and any points in space, and this index can be utilized to estimate the influence range of rill erosion. In addition, rill morphology is also an important index in implementing erosion-treatment measures spatially. Specifically for a loess region, the extraction of rill edge polygons is the key for accurate identification of rill morphology. In this study, based on the rill distribution data as well as the concave relief and profile curvature indexes, the rill edge polygons in the western Longwu hill were extracted (Fig. 2).

Surface water energy is the main influencing factor for the water and soil loss in the western Longwu hill. Assuming that the hydrology condition of the soil in the study area is uniform, surface water power can be described as the product of cumulative catchment area and slope gradient. Such an index is an essential hydraulic parameter reflecting the potential energy of the flowing water at a given altitude. In this study, the average water flow power inside the rills is approximately 10 times that of the average value of the entire study area. Experiment design

Rainfall amount and pattern design

On the basis of the instantaneous heavy rainfall criteria of erosional rains in the loess plateau24,25 and the on-site experimental condition, the rainfall duration was limited within 30 min. However, the actual rainfall data in the study area show that most of the rainfall durations are greater than 2 h. To compensate this influence, we simulated three short-duration erosional rainfall events with higher than normal rainfall intensities, that is, 60 mm/h, 90 mm/h, and 120 mm/h. According to a recent investigation on the slope erosion under different rainfall patterns26, when the total rainfall amounts are similar, short-duration and high-intensity rainfalls exert more significant impact on slope surface erosion compared with long-duration and low-intensity ones. In this study, the former type of rainfall was selected to decipher the erosion characteristics of silty soil slopes under heavy rains. With the consideration of early rainfall events and total rainfall, we enhanced the rainfall intensity to achieve the effect of actual rainfall erosion in the study area.

The rainfall simulator in our experiment is the standard and wide-angle cone-shape Full Jet nozzles produced by Spraying Systems Company from USA. The nozzle combination and distribution were set according to the nozzle parameters and the area of the experiment site, and rainfall intensity was altered by changing the pressure. The measurements from Sun et al.27 show that the median diameter of raindrops produced by the nozzle is about 80% of that of natural raindrops, meeting the requirement of artificial rainfall simulation. Some studies on raindrop fall speed suggest that the terminal velocity of natural raindrops is 2–2.9 m/s. For cone-down nozzles that spray raindrops with an initial velocity, a 2–3 m height can produce this required terminal velocity28. Therefore, in this study the nozzle height was set to 3 m, and the uniformity of raindrops can reach over 82%.

Site selection and grouping of the experiment

Based on the on-site topographic conditions, three groups of bare slopes with a gradient of 30°, 34°, and 38°were selected at first. Then, by referring to the experiment design of Wu et al.29 and our on-site survey results, we altered the vegetation coverage to 30% and 60% and conducted the rainfall simulation experiments under rainfall intensities (i.e. 60 mm/h,90 mm/h; 120 mm/h), making a total of 15 sets of experiments (Table 2). The length and width of the experiment site were set to 3 m and 1 m, respectively. To prevent the runoff from flowing outside the experiment site and leading to errors, we selected ~ 50 cm plastic boards to reconstruct the base and two sides of the site. The boards were embedded into the soil about 5 cm, and some thin steel bars were implanted on the left and right sides to fix it. After the plastic boards were installed, the gap between the boards and the experiment site was filled with soil, which was then slightly compacted to reduce the influence of site boundary. The base was connected with a PVC water collection hole used for collecting runoff and sediment samples. In addition, we selected natural slopes with mainly indigenous plants such as Elymus dahuricus and seabuckthorn. The average proportion of the vegetation within the site was measured multiple times by the sampling method to obtain the vegetation coverage.

Experiment process

One day before the formal artificial rainfall experiment, a “prerainfall” step was carried out until the slope surface is about to produce runoff. Such a process can reduce the spatial heterogeneity of the underlying surface, ensuring the same soil moisture content of each slope. After that, the slopes were covered by plastic films in order to reduce the evaporation capacity.

Prior to the experiment, the moisture content of the slope surface was measured to be 27.69–29.27%, and the rainfall intensity was calibrated to be consistent with the designed value. In each time of experiment, the rainfall duration was set to 30min. The time was counted after the raindrops sprayed out steadily. During the experiment, the runoff generation time and morphology change of each slope were recorded. The time of runoff needed to pass a fixed length was measured using the purple potassium permanganate solution, so that the water flow velocity in each time period was obtained. In addition, the runoff and sediment samples were collected from the water outlet of the test site by a bucket. Such a step was performed every 1 min in the 3 min period before runoff generation and every 2 min thereafter.

Each time before and after the simulated rainfall, all the surface slopes were scanned using a three-dimensional laser scanner. Furthermore, each time after the rainfall, we counted and recorded the rill number, depth, and width, and measured the total weight of the generated runoff and sediment. Then, the runoff and sediment samples were kept standing for 12 h to remove the supernatant, and were dried at the temperature of 105–110 °C in the oven to weigh the solid content. Finally, the runoff and sediment yield amount produced by each simulated rainfall events were obtained.

With the increase of rainfall intensity, the runoff generation time on different slopes was gradually advanced by 4–18s, demonstrating that the “prerainfall” process in the early stage increased the soil moisture content. When the rainfall intensity changed from 60 mm/h to 90 mm/h, the slope infiltration amount increased by 31.08–125.58%, the runoff yield amount increased by 23.42–46.86%, and the sediment yield amount increased by 23.57–38.50%. When the rainfall intensity further increased to 120 mm/h, the three values reached 85.39–200.07%, 70.19–94.29%, and 57.54–81.53%, respectively. Thus, it can be concluded that the increase of rainfall intensity can promote water infiltration into slope surfaces and simultaneously increase the runoff volume and energy. As a result, the erosion on slope soil is increased, which promotes the runoff and sediment generation.

As slope gradient increased, the runoff generation on different slopes was gradually advanced by 3–9 s. Specifically when the slope gradient increased from 30° to 34°, the infiltration amount of the slope surface decreased by 16.21–28.54%, the runoff yield amount increased by 0.27–1.84%, and the sediment yield amount increased by 14.33–27.67%. When the slope gradient was altered to 38°, the infiltration amount decreased by 30.97–88.01% compared with that at 30°, the runoff yield amount increased by 2.03–8.66%, and the sediment yield amount increased by 29.47–49.19%. Such changes can be attributed to the fact that the rise of slope gradient increases the gravitational potential energy, which further increases the flow velocity of runoff. The erosion on slope surface is weakened, and the infiltration amount decreases, resulting in the increase in runoff and sediment yield amounts.

With the increase of vegetation coverage, the runoff generation time on different slopes was delayed by 2–23 s. In the experiment, when the vegetation coverage increased to 30%, the infiltration amount generally increased, reaching as high as 19.34%. Meanwhile, the runoff and sediment yield amounts both decreased, with a proportion of 0.15–8.78% and 19.96–27.84%, respectively. When the vegetation coverage increased to 60%, the slope infiltration further increased, which was 18.78–35.51% higher than that of the bare slope. The runoff yield amount decreased by 7.95–17.62%. Furthermore, the inhibition on sediment yield was particularly marked, with a decrease of 34.02–41.14%. Such results are due to the fact that the branches and leaves of vegetation can partially prevent the water from flowing downward, which greatly mitigates the runoff erosion and thereby suppresses the runoff and sediment yield processes.

As shown in Fig. 5, when rainfall intensity is 60mm/h, the runoff yield rate of all slopes increases rapidly. The runoff yield rate does not change significantly as the slope gradient changes, but decreases as the vegetation coverage increases. When rainfall intensity is 90mm/h, the variation trend of runoff yield rate is similar to that under 60mm/h rainfall intensity, but with some fluctuations. When rainfall intensity reaches 120mm/h, the runoff yield rate enormously increases at first. Then, the value remains stable and fluctuates within a narrow range at last. It is clear that the changing pattern of runoff yield rate under different rainfall intensities varies obviously. This is because the increase of rainfall intensity promotes rill development. The subsequent interrill connection and stream piracy lead to the fluctuation of runoff yield rate.

Variation of runoff yield rate.

Notably, the runoff yield rate of different slopes all shows some fluctuations, which become more prominent as rainfall intensity, slope gradient, and vegetation coverage increase. The fitted curves reveal that the relationship between the runoff yield rate and rainfall duration follows a power function, which is consistent with previous results31,32.

(2) Sediment yield process

(2) Sediment yield process

The variations of sediment yield rate under different rainfall intensities are similar and are displayed in Fig. 6. In the initial stage of the simulated rainfall (0–6min), the sediment yield rate on the bare slope (i.e. slope with no vegetation coverage) increases rapidly. By contrast, the rate on slopes with vegetation coverage first increase quickly and then increase slowly. Such a phenomenon can be interpreted as follows: The loose soil material is abundant at first; when being hit by raindrops, the structure of soil particles is easily destroyed and the thin soil layer may be washed away. As the rainfall continues, the increased runoff velocity makes soil structure more easily destroyed, leading to the maximum erosion rate during the time period of 3–10min. When the sediment yield rate reaches its maximum, the splash erosion induced by raindrop hitting promotes the formation of surface layer crust, thus resulting in the drop of sediment yield rate. Finally in the period of 18-20min, the soil erosion rate becomes stable.

Variation of sediment yield rate.

During the entire rainfall process, the sediment yield rate continuously increased with the increase of rainfall intensity. For example, the average sediment yield rate of the 30° bare slope under the rainfall intensity of 60 mm/h was 61.214 g/m2 min, while that under 90 mm/h and 120 mm/h rainfall intensities increased by 40.2% and 82.5%, respectively. The average sediment yield rate of the slope with 30% vegetation coverage was 47.187 g/m2 min under 60 mm/h rain intensity; however, for the same slope, the average values under 90 mm/h and 120 mm/h rainfall intensities increased by 29.1% and 63.4%, respectively. This is because as the rainfall intensity increases, the raindrop hitting energy on the slope increases, making the surface soil more easily to be destroyed. At the same time, the increase in rainfall intensity enhances the runoff amount and the ability of surface water to move soil particles, thereby increasing the sediment yield rate. Under the same rainfall intensity and vegetation coverage, the increase in slope gradient is conducive to erosion and sediment production. This is because as the slope gradient increases, the kinetic energy of the water flow on the slope surface increases correspondingly, and thus the runoff scouring and sediment-carrying capabilities both increase. Meanwhile, the infiltration amount decreases and the runoff amount increases, leading to more severe erosion. Under the same rainfall intensity and slope gradient, slopes with less vegetation coverage have higher sediment yield rates. Due to the increase of vegetation coverage, the roughness of the slope surface is increased, thus reducing the scouring ability of water flow. Additionally, the raindrop hitting effect on the slope surface is reduced, which mitigates the splash erosion.

Morphology characteristics of rill erosion

The major contributors of slope surface erosion include interrill erosion, rill erosion and gully erosion33. During the runoff generation process, the water flows gradually converge due to topographic reliefs and the increase of runoff amount, forming local streams that are responsible for the subsequent rill erosion. In order to probe the variation characteristics of rills under different rainfall conditions, we counted the rill numbers and measured their widths and depths. Moreover, we scanned the slope before and after the experiment using a 3-D laser scanner to obtain the DEM data of each slope, and finally investigated the morphological characteristics of rill erosion.

The morphological characteristics of rills are shown in Fig. 7. It can be seen that the average density of rills basically reaches the minimum under 60 mm/h rainfall intensity on all slopes except for that of the slope with 60% vegetation coverage under 90 mm/h rainfall intensity. On the bare slopes, the average rill densities under 90 mm/h and 120 mm/h rainfall intensities are similar. By contrast, on the vegetation-covered slopes, the densities on the slopes with 30% vegetation coverage reaches a maximum of 414 cm/m2, which is significantly higher than those on the slopes with 60% vegetation coverage. Under 90 mm/h and 120 mm/h rainfall intensities, the average width of the rills on each bare slope is within 2.5–2.7 cm, and the minimum rill width is seen under 60 mm/h rainfall intensity. It is notable that under the same rainfall intensity, the rill width generally increases with the increase of slope gradient. On slopes covered by vegetation, the average rill width decreases significantly as the coverage increases. In terms of rill depth, those on the bare slopes show an increasing trend with the increase of rainfall intensity and slope gradient. Under the rainfall intensity of 120 mm/h, the average erosion depth decreases with the increase of vegetation coverage, while under the rainfall intensity of 60 mm/h and 90 mm/h, it increases first and then decreases with the increase of vegetation coverage34–38.

Variation characteristics of (a) rill average density, (b) average width, and (c) average depth with the change of slope gradient and vegetation coverage under different rainfall intensities.

As the rainfall intensity and slope gradient increase, the kinetic energy and potential energy of runoff both increase correspondingly, which promotes the erosion of slope surfaces. Therefore, the average rill density, width, and depth will all increase theoretically. However, the experiment in this study found that although the increased kinetic and potential energy has facilitated the development of rills as the rainfall continued, the simultaneously caused stream piracy and rill connection reduced the average rill density. Such a reduction became more obvious when the rainfall intensity and slope gradient were lager.

In this study, we extracted the rills using the hydrological analysis tool (ArcGIS) and calculated their volumes using the surface volume tool. By taking the average elevation values of pre-rainfall rills as the reference plane, we obtained the volume between post-rainfall rills and the reference plane, and finally calculated the rill erosion amount through conversion. The proportion of rill erosion was then employed to reflect the development of rills. As shown in Fig. 4, the rill erosion amount increases with the increase of rainfall intensity and slope gradient, which is similar to the variation of the total erosion amount. For bare slopes, the proportion of rill erosion reaches the maximum under 90mm/h rainfall intensity, and reaches the minimum under 60mm/h rainfall intensity. For slopes with vegetation coverage, the proportion of rill erosion increases with the increase of rainfall intensity, but is still far lower than that of bare slopes. Such results indicate that vegetation coverage can effectively suppress rill formation and evolution.

To more effectively compare the morphological variation of slope surfaces before and after rainfall, we acquired high-resolution pre- and post-rainfall DEM models using the Surfer software (Fig. 8). The morphology changes of bare slopes are the most remarkable, while those of vegetation-covered slopes are less obvious. In addition, it should be noted that more vegetation coverage may weaken the laser scanning effect and limit the rill extraction.

By comparing the rill erosion characteristics of each bare slope, we found that there was no obvious difference in rill morphology with the change of rainfall intensity and slope gradient. Such a phenomenon may be due to the fact that the on-site soil is naturally deposited and highly compacted. Thus, under short-duration rainfall (i.e. 30 min), rills are not completely developed. However, the results showed that the rills under 90 mm/h rainfall intensity were the most highly developed, while those under 60 mm/h were the least, consistent with the results of rill erosion proportion. We suggest that under rainfall with a moderate intensity (i.e. 90 mm/h), rill heads will experience more intense headward erosion, producing more rills. When the rainfall intensity is small (i.e. 60 mm/h), the resulting less runoff shear force will limit the headward movement and thus slow down the rill development process. When the rainfall intensity is large (120 mm/h), rills will develop more rapidly and result in connections and stream piracy, causing larger rill widths and depths. The evolution of rill morphology is not a unidirectional process, but a process associated with the combined effect of multiple forces within the surface erosion system. Moreover, rill erosion is also affected by regional microrelief, soil compaction, and vegetation, etc., leading to the varying erosion network.

Hydraulic mechanism of runoff erosion

Velocity variation of surface runoff

Slope soil erosion is mainly caused by the drag force generated by surface runoff, and the velocity of surface runoff is the dominant factor determining the magnitude of drag force31. The studies on the distribution and variation characteristics of runoff flow velocity are beneficial for deeply understanding the flow rule along the slope surface and further revealing the dynamic mechanism of runoff erosion. The intra-rill runoff velocity variation measured during the experiment is shown in Fig. 9.

The variation of intra-rill runoff velocity on each slope.

When the rainfall intensity is 60 mm/h, the runoff velocity inside the rills on the bare slopes gradually increases and remains stable after reaching its maximum (Fig. 9). Specifically for the 34° slope, the velocity starts to be stable at 18 min. For the 30° and 38° slopes, there are some fluctuations in the velocity change. The average runoff velocities of the three bare slopes are 19.14 cm/s, 20.38 cm/s, and 21.05 cm/s, respectively. In contrast, the runoff velocities of the vegetation-covered slopes (30% and 60%) fluctuate more drastically and start to be stable at 22 min. The corresponding average runoff velocities are 17.21 cm/s and 16.20 cm/s, respectively. When the rainfall intensity reaches 90 mm/h, the runoff velocities of all slopes become larger. For the 38° slope, the velocity reaches the maximum at approximately 13 min, while the time needed for the 34° slope is 25 min. The average velocities for the three bare slopes are 20.60 cm/s, 21.07 cm/s, and 22.76 cm/s, respectively. For the vegetation-covered slopes, the runoff velocities are prominently lower than those on bare slopes, and the velocities decrease as the vegetation coverage increases. Under 30% and 60% vegetation coverage, the average runoff velocities are 17.50 cm/s and 16.48 cm/s, respectively. When the rainfall intensity is 120 mm/h, the runoff velocities of each slope fluctuate intensely. For the bare slopes, the maximum runoff velocity under 120 mm/h rainfall intensity is almost the same as that under 90 mm/h rainfall velocity. For the vegetation-covered slopes, however, the velocities all increase obviously. The average runoff velocities of the three bare slopes are 21.43 cm/s, 22.86 cm/s, and 23.17 cm/s, respectively, while those of the two vegetation-covered slopes are 20.37 cm/s and 18.89 cm/s, respectively.

Figure 10 shows the variation of average runoff velocity under the combined effect of multiple influencing factors. Under the same rainfall intensity, the intra-rill water flow velocity increases with the increase of slope gradient, and decreases with the increase of vegetation coverage. Under the same slope gradient, the water flow velocity increases with the increase of rainfall intensity. This is because as rainfall intensity and slope gradient increase, rills are more developed and tend to be more parallel with the slope surface. Thus, the force exerted by rill sides on the intra-rill water flow decreases correspondingly, leading to the gradually increasing average runoff velocity. When slopes are covered by vegetation, rill formation is suppressed and the water catchment area is limited. In addition, the vegetation roots may slow down the runoff movement. The more the vegetation coverage, the larger the resistance. Thus, the average runoff velocity on vegetation-covered slopes is prominently lower than that of bare slopes.

Hydraulic parameters of surface runoff

To investigate the relationships among different hydraulic factors and the corresponding manifestations in the process of slope surface erosion, we considered the rills as open channels based on the open channel flow theory. The cross-section of the water flow was approximated as a rectangle. Then, the measured rectangle width and the average runoff velocity were utilized to calculate the relevant parameters such as Reynolds number, Froude number and flow resistance coefficient, thereby obtaining the flow pattern of runoffs.

As the rainfall intensity increases, runoff amount and depth all increase, resulting in the increase in hydraulic radius. By contrast, the increase of vegetation coverage suppresses the runoff formation and development; thus, hydraulic radius decreases with the increase of vegetation coverage. In addition, the Reynolds number increases remarkably as rainfall intensity and slope gradient increase, but decreases significantly as vegetation coverage increases. In the experiment, the Reynolds number of all slopes are greater than 500, indicating that all the slope runoffs are turbulent flows. Meanwhile, the Froude number of all slopes are less than 1, which means all the slope runoffs are slow/tranquil flows. The Froude number varies only slightly with the change of rainfall intensity and slope gradient, but decreases with the increase of vegetation coverage, possibly associated with the strong compaction of slope surface soil and the short rainfall duration. As rainfall intensity and slope gradient increase, flow resistance coefficient does not change significantly. This is because with the increase of the two influencing factors, the corresponding slope surface will alter to the mode with the least energy consumed. Meanwhile, it should be noted that rill development is a complex dynamic process in which rill connection, rill side collapse, and stream piracy can all influence the value of flow resistance coefficient.

Conclusions

By conducting a series of on-site rainfall simulation experiments, we explored the runoff and sediment yield, erosional morphology, and dynamics characteristics of typical silty soil slopes in the western Longwu hill under different rainfall intensity, slope gradient, and vegetation coverage. Major conclusions of this study include the following,

The runoff generation time varies under different conditions, with a range of 56-100s. Likewise, different rainfall and surface conditions lead to the difference in infiltration amount, runoff yield amount, and sediment yield amount. In general, runoff and sediment yield amount is positively correlated with rainfall intensity and slope gradient, and is negatively correlated with vegetation coverage. On the other hand, the infiltration amount has a positive correlation with rainfall intensity and vegetation coverage, and has a negative correlation with slope gradient. The relationship between runoff yield rate and rainfall duration can be described by a power function; as the rainfall intensity increases, the runoff yield rate becomes more stable. The sediment yield rate under different conditions also changes similarly, all showing a rapid increase followed by a gradual decline, with some fluctuations.
