Filamin C Genetic Cardiomyopathy Presenting as Acute Myocardial Injury
Authors: Francisco B Alexandrino, Brett Curtis, Christopher Nguyen, Deborah Kwon, Jay Ramchand, WH Wilson Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myocardial injury with a nonischemic imaging pattern presents a diagnostic challenge.Case SummaryA 46-year-old previously healthy man presented with intermittent chest pain and markedly elevated high-sensitivity troponin. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed extensive ring-like subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement involving 10 left ventricular segments but no myocardial edema. Genetic testing subsequently identified a pathogenic variant in the FLNC gene. Follow-up imaging 4 months later showed persistent fibrosis with preserved ventricular function and no arrhythmias.DiscussionRing-like late gadolinium enhancement is an uncommon but increasingly recognized imaging phenotype associated with genetic cardiomyopathies, particularly involving desmosomal and nondesmosomal genes such as FLNC. History of Presentation

A 46-year-old otherwise healthy man presented to the emergency department of an outside hospital with intermittent chest pain at rest for the past 24 hours. The initial episode began around 2:00 am after a day of working in the backyard. The patient awoke with substernal chest pressure radiating to the back, which the patient rated as 6-7 of 10 and was exacerbated by respiration. There were no clear relieving factors, but symptoms spontaneously resolved after approximately 3 hours, allowing him to return to sleep. He remained at home the following day out of caution, noting dyspnea on exertion. A second episode of chest pain with similar characteristics occurred later that evening at 10:00 pm, prompting his presentation to the emergency department. He reported occasional alcohol consumption (once weekly) and no regular use of medications or supplements. He denied associated symptoms including nausea, headache, cough, gastrointestinal disturbances, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, rash, arthralgia, or recent viral syndromes. On initial evaluation, the patient was hemodynamically stable and saturating 100% on room air. Electrocardiogram revealed normal sinus rhythm at 75 beats/min with left-axis deviation (See Figure 1A). Chest computed tomography angiography findings were unremarkable, aside from a trace pericardial effusion. Initial laboratory testing showed elevated high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (TnI) at 14,000 ng/L, peaking at 17,000 ng/L. Findings from a comprehensive metabolic panel, complete blood count, respiratory viral panel, and toxicology screen were all unremarkable. The patient was treated as having acute coronary syndrome with aspirin loading, intravenous heparin, metoprolol tartrate 25 mg, and atorvastatin 20 mg. Left heart catheterization demonstrated no obstructive coronary artery disease. Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 55% without structural abnormalities. A provisional diagnosis of myopericarditis was made, colchicine was initiated, and the patient was transferred to our facility for further management. Upon arrival, 1 day after initial presentation, the patient remained stable and completely asymptomatic.

Past Medical History

His past medical history included no prior medical conditions, no regular intake of medications or supplements, and occasional alcohol use (weekly). Family history was notable for coronary artery disease in maternal grandparents.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for the patient included acute coronary syndrome, myopericarditis, pulmonary embolism, and nonischemic myocardial injury including genetic cardiomyopathy (CMP).

Investigations

Laboratory markers were notable for a C-reactive protein level of 5.4 mg/L, an erythrocyte sedimentary rate of 15 mm/h, D-dimer 2,070 ng/mL, and creatine kinase 180 U/L. The repeated high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (TnT) level was 1,316 ng/L, and aminoterminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was 2,900 pg/mL. Remaining workup was negative. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) on day 3 of admission demonstrated LVEF of 56% with epicardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in a ring-like distribution involving 10 segments and a T2-weighted imaging without edema shown in Figures 2A and 2B, which did not fulfill the updated 2018 Lake Louise criteria. Telemetry monitoring showed no malignant arrhythmia, with only infrequent premature ventricular contractions and borderline low-voltage QRS. He remained free from chest pain throughout the entire hospitalization.

Management

Patient was discharged on aspirin and with 14-day Holter monitoring.

Outcomes and Follow-up

One month later, genetic testing revealed a heterozygous pathogenic gene variant of filamin C (FLNC, c.970-4A>G, intronic). Fourteen-day Holter monitoring was negative for any malignant arrhythmias. Repeat laboratory testing showed complete normalization of cardiac biomarkers (Figure 1B). CMR 4 months later showed slight improvement but persistent LGE in the same 10 segments (Figures 2C and 2D). Cardiac diffusion tensor imaging demonstrated that, in the anterior-septal region, mean diffusivity and helix angle were normal, and fractional anisotropy was reduced (0.265, normal >0.3), signifying microstructural disarray (Figure 2E).

Discussion

This case illustrates a diagnostic challenge involving a young, previously healthy man who initially presented with features consistent with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction but was ultimately found to have a nondilated left ventricular CMP (NDLVC) with preserved systolic function and extensive myocardial scarring on CMR. The presence of myocardial injury with imaging evidence of ring-like fibrosis, but no edema in the absence of ventricular dilation, systolic dysfunction, and arrhythmia and the fact that he remained asymptomatic, except on the day before presentation, presents a distinct diagnostic and management challenge.

The ring-like LGE offers valuable insights into the underlying cause. Ring-like enhancement is defined as subepicardial or midwall LGE involving at least 3 contiguous left ventricular segments within the same short-axis slice.1 Although initially described in association with genetic CMP, particularly those involving desmoplakin (DSP) and filamin C mutations,2 this imaging pattern has since been reported across a broader spectrum of conditions.1

In a single-center observational study of consecutive patients who underwent LGE CMR between 2002 and 2024 (N = 14,091), ring-like LGE was identified in 1.1% of cases (n = 152), with a median of 10 segments involved. Genetic testing was available in 65% of patients; among those tested, 58% had pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, involving both desmosomal genes (11.9%) and nondesmosomal genes (35.6%). This study also applied the novel morphofunctional phenotypic classification of CMP recently proposed by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) in 2023. In their cohort, 37.5% were classified as dilated CMP (DCM), 1.3% as arrhythmogenic right ventricular CMP, 0.7% as hypertrophic CMP, 36.8% as NDLVC, and 7.9% remained unclassified. Interestingly, the extent of LGE, reflected by the number of segments involved, was associated with adverse outcomes, highlighting the prognostic importance of LGE burden.3

Among the various CMP phenotypes, DCM is the most extensively studied. A 2023 single-center study from Spain including 600 patients with DCM identified a causative genetic variant in 38% of cases, and LGE was present in 25% of the overall cohort. Subepicardial LGE was most commonly associated with DMD, DSP, and FLNC mutations. Importantly, LGE patterns differed significantly by genotype (P <0.001), suggesting meaningful genotype-phenotype correlations. It is of interest that the patient presented with a NDLVC phenotype that is defined by the ESC in their latest CMP guideline as the presence of nonischemic left ventricular fibrosis, as evidenced by LGE on CMR, regardless of whether there are regional or global wall motion abnormalities.4 This phenotype captures patients with early-stage or atypical presentations, where structural changes may not yet be present like in our patient.

In our patient, the absence of T2-based edema meant that the updated Lake Louise criteria in 2018 were not fulfilled,5 highlighting that, despite a clinical presentation consistent with acute myocarditis, the imaging findings did not fully support the diagnosis. The elevated biomarkers likely reflect a transient inflammatory response that unmasked an underlying genetic CMP, manifesting with fibrosis rather than edema on imaging. The rapid decline in biomarker levels, together with the absence of persistent inflammatory changes on CMR, suggests swift resolution of the acute inflammatory component (myocarditis).

Interestingly, our patient was transferred from an outside hospital that uses a different troponin assay than our institution, allowing comparison of TnI and TnT responses during the same episode of myocardial injury. TnI is generally more specific for myocardial necrosis and rises more rapidly, often reaching peak concentrations significantly higher than TnT. In contrast, TnT remains more tightly bound to tropomyosin within the myofilament, and its degradation occurs primarily through local phagocytic processes, leading to a slower release profile and lower circulating levels. TnI, on the other hand, undergoes faster proteolytic degradation, contributing to its earlier and more pronounced elevation in the bloodstream.6

This pattern of transient myocardial injury without edema is increasingly recognized in genetic cardiomyopathies, particularly those involving desmosomal genes. In DSP carriers, subepicardial LGE is observed in approximately 40% of individuals, many of whom maintain preserved left ventricular systolic function. Notably, about 15% experience episodes of acute myocardial injury, characterized by chest pain, elevated troponin levels, and nonobstructive coronary arteries, mirroring the presentation seen in our patient.7 Furthermore, among patients presenting with acute myocardial injury, the presence of a ring-like LGE pattern on CMR is one of the strongest predictors of an underlying genetic etiology, occurring far more frequently in those with desmosomal gene variants (67.5% vs 29.1%; P <0.0001).8

Of note, primary prevention with implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation was not pursued following shared decision-making with the patient. He had preserved LVEF, no documented arrhythmias, no heart failure symptoms, and normalization of NT-proBNP during follow-up, suggesting no ongoing myocardial injury. Although the 2023 ESC CMP guidelines provide a class IIa recommendation for ICD consideration in patients with high-risk genotypes such as FLNC truncating variants, risk stratification remains individualized.4 Extensive LGE, as seen in our patient, has been associated with increased arrhythmic risk and sudden cardiac death (SCD) in a cohort of 167 patients with FLNC truncating variants.9 However, in the absence of additional clinical risk markers, ICD implantation was deferred by patient choice, acknowledging a potentially increased arrhythmic risk and recommending close follow-up with biomarker and rhythm monitoring.

Our case further reinforces the emerging concept of “imagenetics,” wherein specific imaging signatures, such as ring-like LGE in a myocarditis-like presentation, should prompt consideration of genetic testing, underscoring the need for heightened awareness among cardiologists.

However, nondesmosomal genes such as FLNC (encoding filamin C) are also strongly associated with various CMP, including arrhythmogenic, hypertrophic, restrictive, and myofibrillar myopathy.10 In the heart, filamin C is highly expressed near the Z-discs, plasma membrane, and intercalated discs. Following myotubule injury, filamin C is rapidly recruited to the site of damage, where it plays an essential role in repair by acting as a scaffold for heat shock proteins. Studies have linked FLNC truncations to impaired proteostasis, in which misfolded proteins accumulate and exert proteotoxic effects, thereby increasing the risk of arrhythmias when defective filamin C fails to mediate their clearance.11 A study of patients with FLNC truncating variants (n = 308) reported a median age of 45 years, with 52% male, 36% presenting as probands, and two-thirds maintaining a normal ejection fraction, features that closely mirror the demographics of our patient. Overall, 19% experienced SCD or ventricular arrhythmias. From this cohort, a 5-variable model was developed to estimate individualized arrhythmic risk, incorporating nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, syncope, male sex, age, and LVEF. In our case, the only applicable risk factor was male sex, given the absence of syncope, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, or reduced LVEF (<45%).12 Previous reports have described FLNC-related disease presenting with myocarditis-like episodes and CMR findings similar to those in our patient, including a ring-like pattern of LGE on T1-weighted imaging, consistent with nonischemic fibrosis and scarring.13 However, most cases were associated with significant LV dysfunction, ventricular arrhythmias, or SCD.14 By contrast, our case represents one of the few with a relatively benign short-term course.

Finally, management decisions required careful consideration. Although reduced fractional anisotropy may increase arrhythmic risk, the absence of electrocardiographic abnormalities or active myocardial inflammation made the benefit of primary prevention uncertain. Genotype is a major determinant of SCD risk, yet given this patient's benign clinical presentation, preserved ventricular function, and lack of arrhythmias, device implantation was not pursued. Regarding medical therapy, although titin-truncating variant-related DCM often responds to guideline-directed medical therapy, it remains unclear how genetic etiology influences treatment response. With normal NT-proBNP levels and preserved LVEF 4 months after presentation, indicating no neurohormonal activation, we did not initiate guideline-directed medical therapy because its benefit in this context is likely limited.4

We propose that an acute inflammatory event may have triggered myocardial injury that could not be adequately repaired because of the underlying FLNC truncation, highlighting a possible 2-hit mechanism.

Conclusions

This case and brief review support the hypothesis that acute myocarditis may have acted as an environmental trigger, unmasking a latent cardiomyopathic phenotype in a previously asymptomatic, genotype-positive individual. The inflammatory response was transient, with a relatively benign short-term course, as suggested by the rapid resolution of symptoms and decline in cardiac biomarkers within 5 days of hospitalization. Notably, the patient did not meet the updated Lake Louise criteria, underscoring the diagnostic challenges when myocarditis manifests without typical edema on CMR.
