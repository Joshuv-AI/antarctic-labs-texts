Fusion strategy after odontoid fracture and three-column atlantoaxial displacement in a patient with prior C2-pelvis fusion: a case report
Authors: Paarth Patel, Michael McGinity, Cristian Gragnaniello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical intervention for scoliosis often involves extensive spinal fusions, at times spanning from the cervical spine to the pelvis. Proximal junctional failure is a known complication of C2-pelvis fusions, frequently presenting as fractures of the odontoid process. Managing these fractures is complex due to anatomical constraints and the need for structural stability while minimizing risks. This report presents a nuanced solution for a C2 fracture involving all three columns and C1 displacement in a patient with previous anterior C3–C6 and posterior C2-pelvis fusions.Case DescriptionA 66-year-old male with extensive spinal fusion history presented with bilateral hand paresthesia, global extremity weakness, and neck pain following a fall. Imaging revealed a comminuted type II odontoid fracture with C2 ventral body and right lateral mass involvement, along with significant spondylolisthesis of C2 secondary to ligamentous injury. Surgery was indicated due to spinal instability. Intraoperative reduction was followed by a new occiput-C7 posterior arthrodesis, including pedicle screws at C6 and C7 with a quad rod construct. C6 and C7 were not instrumented previously, or their instrumentation had been removed at some point during one of the several revisions that the patient underwent. The new rods were purposefully kept longer to connect the occipital plate to the C6 and C7 pedicle screws and then to the original C2-pelvis fusion at upper thoracic levels using side-to-side connectors. This conferred more stability to the new construct.ConclusionsThis case highlights a surgical strategy for stabilizing complex cervical fractures in patients with previous extensive fusions and limited options for fixation while allowing minimal disruption of previous fusion masses and preexisting hardware. By extending the fusion to the occiput, using cervical pedicle screws, and integrating existing hardware, we achieved sound structural stability. This approach offers a potential fusion strategy for similar cases where preserving fusion integrity and spinal stability is critical amidst challenging anatomical constraints.Keywords: Atlantoaxial displacement, case report, C2 to pelvis fusion, proximal junction odontoid fracture, three column displacement

Highlight box

Key findings

• This case illustrates a successful occipitocervical extension for a C2 odontoid fracture with three-column C1 displacement in a patient with prior anterior and posterior fusions, including a C2-pelvis construct. The fusion was extended from the occiput to C7 using uninstrumented C6 and C7 pedicles and side-to-side connectors, avoiding disruption of the original long fusion and resulting in a robust quad-rod construct.

What is known and what is new?

• Odontoid fractures are a recognized complication after long-segment cervical-to-pelvis fusions, often representing proximal junctional failure (PJF). Traditional fusion strategies typically involve extending constructs using available uninstrumented levels or revising prior instrumentation, which can be technically demanding and increase morbidity.

• This report presents a practical variation for managing such fractures by leveraging preserved lower cervical pedicles and multi-rod linkage to create a stable occipitocervical construct without revising the existing long-segment hardware. It highlights the technical feasibility of extending fixation proximally in complex revision settings.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings emphasize the importance of individualized surgical planning when extending long fusions and advocate for preserving lower cervical pedicles at the index surgery. This approach maintains revision options should PJF occur.

Introduction

Severe spinal deformities often require instrumented fusions spanning the cervical spine down to the pelvis to achieve corrective alignment and durability (1,2). Despite their benefits, these constructs have high complication rates, especially proximal junctional failure (PJF), which may present as fractures or adjacent segment degeneration (2,3). Long fusions can also accelerate adjacent segment degeneration in the cervical spine, further increasing the risk of junctional mechanical failure (4).

Odontoid fractures have recently been recognized as a form of PJF above long posterior constructs, with series reporting such injuries in multiple patients (5,6). In the setting of a C2-pelvis fusion, the entire subaxial spine is immobilized, turning the atlantoaxial junction into the final mobile segment. Biomechanical analyses and clinical studies demonstrate that long posterior constructs concentrate mechanical stress at the proximal junction, leading to increased flexion loading and shear stress at the adjacent mobile segment and predisposing to junctional failure (7). Cadaveric experiments demonstrate that type II odontoid fractures markedly reduce craniovertebral stability, highlighting the dynamic instability of these injuries (8). This mechanical concentration explains the occurrence of complex injuries, such as odontoid fractures accompanied by C1 displacement, which differ substantially from isolated odontoid trauma (9,10).

These scenarios pose formidable surgical challenges. Prior instrumentation constrains options for fixation, while the upper cervical spine’s limited bone stock and the nearby vertebral artery (VA) amplify the risks of pedicle or lateral mass screw placement (11-14). Although occipitocervical fusion may be required for stabilization, surgeons must navigate hardware density, vascular anatomy, and fixation viability (15). Case reports have described both nonoperative interventions and fusion extension techniques adjacent to prior cervical instrumentation, but formal guidance remains scarce for scenarios involving odontoid fractures plus C1 disruption in the setting of C2-pelvis fusions (9,10,16).

Here, we report a rare case of comminuted type II odontoid fracture with three-column C1 displacement atop prior anterior and posterior long spinal fusions. We describe a construct strategy that stabilized the craniocervical junction, preserved existing hardware, and achieved neurological recovery. We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-135/rc).

Case presentation

This is a case of a 66-year-old male with a prior anterior C3 to C6 fusion and a posterior C2 to pelvis fusion performed for scoliosis. There had also been multiple revisions of the posterior fusion due to complications from an infection, for which the patient is now on chronic antibiotics. Medical history is significant for hypothyroidism, hypertension (HTN), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), stage 3 chronic kidney disease (CKD), asthma, and ambulatory dysfunction. He presented with bilateral paresthesia in hands, weakness in all extremities, and neck pain after suffering a fall and loss of consciousness. His neurological exam confirmed mild weakness in all muscle groups, with more pronounced new weakness observed in the left lower extremity. Computed tomography (CT) identified a comminuted type II odontoid fracture with adjacent involvement of the C2 ventral vertebral body and the right sided pars (Figure 1). An atlantoaxial ligamentous injury is also evident, resulting in significant spondylolisthesis of C2, involving all three columns. This was seen on CT and X-ray imaging (Figure 2). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed spinal cord compression and signal changes in the cord resulting from the displacement (Figure 3).

Preoperative computed tomography imaging of the cervical spine in sagittal and coronal planes. Sagittal (A) and coronal (B) computed tomography scans demonstrate a comminuted type II odontoid fracture (red arrow) involving the pars interarticularis of C2.

Preoperative imaging showing atlantoaxial displacement and available levels for instrumentation. Sagittal computed tomography (A) and lateral radiograph (B) of the cervical spine reveal significant anterior displacement between C1 and C2 (red star), with no existing instrumentation at C6 or C7.

Sagittal T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates spinal cord compression and intramedullary signal changes (red star) at the level of the C2 fracture.

Surgery was recommended because of the ligamentous injury and the resulting spinal instability. The fracture was reduced intraoperatively. Because of significant C1 displacement and instability, the fusion was extended to the occiput. To navigate around the existing C2 to pelvis fusion, a new fusion was performed from the occiput to C7 (Figure 4). The rods were left longer inferiorly to allow connection to the existing fusion at upper thoracic levels using side-to-side connectors so that the previous construct could be left undisturbed (Figure 5). After surgery, the patient wore a cervical-thoracolumbar orthosis (CTLO) brace, and his sensation and weakness both improved at his 3-month follow-up. Postoperative imaging demonstrates stable hardware alignment with no evidence of instrument failure or reinjury (Figure 6). The latest follow-up at 15 months showed no complications and baseline neurological function with resolution of his presenting symptoms. No complications or adverse events have occurred to date.

Preoperative computed tomography evaluation of C6 and C7 for instrumentation. Sagittal (A,C) and transverse (B,D) computed tomography scans confirm the absence of prior instrumentation at C6 (A,B) and C7 (C,D), allowing for potential pedicle screw placement.

Postoperative computed tomography imaging at 3-month follow-up. Sagittal (A) and transverse (B) computed tomography scans demonstrate successful placement of pedicle screws at C6 and C7 as part of the occiput-to-C7 construct.

Postoperative radiographic imaging demonstrating the final construct. Coronal (A) and sagittal (B) radiographs at 3-month follow-up show the occiput-to-C7 instrumentation connected to the existing C2-to-pelvis construct using bilateral side-to-side connectors, resulting in a four-rod configuration.

Discussion

Several reports describe C2 fractures adjacent to long cervical fusions. Ladd et al. reported nine such patients, all sustaining type II odontoid fractures and five had concomitant C1 involvement. Seven underwent surgical extension to C1, while two treated non-operatively developed nonunion (5). Lim et al. described six cases following C2-pelvis fusions: three type IIA fractures (one with pars involvement) and three comminuted type III fractures with pars fractures. Four patients were initially managed non-operatively, with failure in 3 (75%), particularly those with pars involvement. One patient with an intact pars remained stable without revision (6). Chen et al. presented a single patient with a comminuted C2 fracture and C1–2 subluxation above a prior C2-sacrum fusion. This case was treated with occiput-C3 fusion using a C3 pedicle screw; however, the authors emphasized the increased risk of VA injury associated with cervical pedicle screw placement (10). Our case reflects these broader findings that odontoid fractures after long fusions are unstable injuries best managed surgically, especially with significant atlantoaxial displacement.

Although proximal extension is a recognized strategy, to our knowledge there are no documented cases of odontoid fractures in patients with prior C2-pelvis fusions managed with distal anchorage as low as C7 and linkage into the upper thoracic fusion mass. By reporting our construct design, this report contributes a potential strategy to the spectrum of revision options for these challenging scenarios. The choice of proximal fixation depends on anatomy and instability. C2 pedicle screws can be used when adequate pedicle width and safe VA placement is available (11,12,17,18). C1 lateral mass screws are suitable when the atlas is intact and occipital fixation is not required (15,19). Occipital plate fixation is reserved for cases with irreducible atlantoaxial instability, comminuted odontoid fractures, or when C1–C2 screw placement is not feasible (15,19).

In our patient, severe three-column C1 displacement and existing C2 hardware made occipital fixation the most stable option. The patient already had instrumentation across C2 and the lateral masses of C3–C5, but C6 and C7 were uninstrumented. This preserved anatomy created an opportunity to strengthen the construct without revising the existing fusion mass. C6 and C7 are particularly favorable for pedicle screw placement because their pedicles are larger, and the VA typically enters the transverse foramen at C6 (14). Therefore, we placed bilateral pedicle screws at C6 and C7 to provide robust distal anchors. To further reinforce stability, we left the new rods intentionally long and linked them to the existing thoracic construct using two side-to-side connectors on each side. This created a four-rod configuration with anchorage at the occiput, lower cervical spine, and thoracic spine fusion mass. The construct provided strong fixation, preserved the integrity of the original instrumentation, and resulted in both neurological recovery and radiographic stability.

Limitations of this approach include reliance on the availability of uninstrumented C6 and C7 pedicles, which may not be present in every patient. This consideration underscores a broader implication of this case and others reported—the importance of considering proximal junctional fractures when planning long-segment constructs. Deliberately preserving lower cervical pedicles, such as at C6 or C7, could provide critical options for future revision should proximal failure occur. Further investigation on this subject is warranted.

Conclusions

We report a case of a comminuted type II odontoid fracture with three-column C1 displacement in the setting of prior anterior C3–C6 and posterior C2-pelvis fusions. Proximal extension to the occiput is a well-recognized strategy for managing instability above long constructs, but this case illustrates how existing hardware and available anatomy can shape the specific construct design. Here, uninstrumented C6 and C7 pedicles provided robust distal fixation, and intentionally long rods allowed side-to-side linkage into the thoracic fusion mass. This represents a practical variation within established occipitocervical extension techniques. More broadly, the case underscores the value of preserving select lower cervical pedicles during index fusions to maintain flexibility for revision should PJF occur.
