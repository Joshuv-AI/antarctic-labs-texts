Longitudinal alterations in cerebral perfusion following a season of adolescent contact sport participation compared to non-contact athletes
Authors: Benjamin L Brett, Alex D Cohen, Michael A McCrea, Yang Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Seasonal differences in rCBF, arterial transit time (ATT) and cognition between adolescent contact and non-contact sport athletes examined.•Brain perfusion was evaluated using an advanced 3D pseudo-continuous ASL sequence with Hadamard encoded multiple post-labeling delays.•Differential trajectories of perfusion changes observed following a season of contact and non-contact athletes who did not sustain a concussion.•Pre- to post- season differences in verbal memory and visual motor speed were also observed between contact and non-contact athletes.•Alterations in rCBF and cognitive variability, but not neurovasculature changes (ATT), observed following a season of contact sports.Keywords: Adolescent athletes, Arterial spin labeling, Arterial transit time, Cerebral blood flow, MRI, Repetitive head impacts

Introduction

History of sport-related concussion (SRC) and cumulative exposure to repetitive head impacts (RHI) have been associated with a range of adverse long-term neurobehavioral/neurologic outcomes in older former athletes (Brett et al., 2022, Lehman et al., 2012, Ueda et al., 2023, Russell et al., 2022). While SRC can occur as a result of head impacts sustained during contact sport participation, the adverse influence of cumulative exposure to RHI, even in the absence of diagnosed concussion, may confer risk for these adverse long-term outcomes. Some have suggested that RHI and SRC may instigate distinct pathological processes as two different mechanisms of insult, representing unique risk factors for adverse long-term outcomes (McCann et al., 2022, Montenigro et al., 2017, Tagge et al., 2018, Brett et al., 2022). For example, in a study of active collegiate football players, smaller hippocampal volume was independently associated with history of concussion and years of participation (proxy for RHI) (Singh et al., 2014). As such, investigations into the risk of long-term difficulties associated with cumulative head impacts should seek to isolate RHI from the effects of SRC.

Preliminary evidence suggests that the effects of cumulative RHI on brain structure and function via contact sport participation may be present in active, younger athletes (Singh et al., 2014, Brett et al., 2020, Brett et al., 2022, Mayer et al., 2015, Karimpoor et al., n.d.). These preliminary studies are limited, in that most involve retrospective designs that assess differences based on recall of exposure (e.g., number of contact sport participation years). Among select prospective studies investigating pre- to post-season changes in brain structure and function, greater changes in functional connectivity (Abbas et al., 2015, DeSimone et al., 2021), white matter integrity (Mayinger et al., 2018, McAllister et al., 2014), and structural volume/thickness (Parivash et al., 2019, Mills et al., 2020), have been reported in contact sport athletes, primarily at the collegiate level. Investigating changes during adolescence is also important, as select studies have reported greater associations of long-term outcomes in former athletes with exposure to contact sport earlier in development, though not all studies have observed these associations (Montenigro et al., 2017, Alosco et al., 2017, Alosco et al., 2023, Lempke et al., 2023). These changes provide initial evidence that subtle biological changes associated with a single season of contact sport participation may occur, but further investigation into other aspects of brain function (e.g., cerebral perfusion) and changes at lower levels of play (i.e., middle and high school athletes) are needed to more comprehensively understand the potential effects of RHI.

Alterations in cerebral blood flow (CBF) have been shown to be a critical biological marker of acute SRC effects and recovery (i.e., decreased CBF during the acute to subacute stages) (Wang et al., 2019, Wang et al., 2016, Battista et al., 2018, Churchill et al., 2017). Additionally, preliminary evidence indicates a greater history of concussion is associated with changes in CBF in active and former athletes (Hamer et al., 2020, Amen et al., 2016). Within non-athlete populations, decrease in CBF is a risk factor for a number of pathological aging outcomes, such as an increased risk for Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias and future cognitive impairment (Moonen et al., 2021, Duan et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2022, Camargo and Wang, 2021). In one preliminary study, significant decreases in CBF were observed among contact sport athletes who sustained higher levels of head impact exposure over the course of a single season. Elevations in cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption at mid-season in the high impact exposure group were purported to partially influence the decreases in CBF (Champagne et al., 2020). Prior studies examining prospective changes in CBF over the course of a contact sport season have primarily included collegiate athletes and no investigations to date have included adolescent athletes. Taken together, alterations in CBF following a single season of contact sport participation may represent a promising mechanism by which RHI may increase the risk for adverse long-term outcomes.

Recent advances in arterial spin labeling (ASL) have led to innovative techniques for measuring aspects of CBF, including a three-dimensional (3D) pseudo-continuous ASL (pCASL) sequence with Hadamard-encoded multiple post-labeling delays (PLDs) (Cohen et al., 2020). This unique method allows for the simultaneous calculation of CBF and arterial transit time (ATT), which reflects the duration of time from labeling to the region of CBF measurement (Cohen et al., 2020). It also represents an interesting metric by which to assess the neurobiological effects of RHI, as elongated ATT has been associated with greater disease severity in clinical populations such as multiple sclerosis (Paling et al., 2014). This approach has shown a superior noise-error factor and higher reproducibility of ATT compared to conventionally computed CBF (Cohen et al., 2020). Thus, there is the potential to detect subtle biological alterations in CBF and ATT in contact sport athletes over the course of a single season.

Neuropsychological changes associated with RHI following a single season of contact sport participation are limited and generally inconsistent. Most studies have shown little to no clinically significant associations between pre- to post-season changes in performance on cognitive and other clinical assessments following a single season of contact sport/RHI exposure (Belanger et al., 2016, Rose et al., 2021, Tarnutzer et al., 2017). However, McAllister et al. (2012) reported that contact sport athletes performed worse in the area of verbal memory compared to regression-based expected scores at follow-up (post-season), which were derived from pre- to post-season scores of non-contact athletes.

Limited studies have utilized both cognitive tests and neuroimaging, particularly CBF, to examine the effects of a single season of contact sport exposure on brain structure, function, and clinical changes in maturing adolescent athletes. As such, the objective of the current study was to examine the difference in pre-to post-season CBF, ATT, and neurocognitive changes between contact and well-match non-contact sport middle and high school athletes. We hypothesize that there will be a significant decreased in relative CBF (rCBF) from pre-to post-season among contact sport athletes compared to non-contact sport athletes. We also hypothesize that there will be less developmental improvement in verbal memory pre- to post-season in contact athletes compared to non-contact athletes, though prior studies to inform these hypotheses are limited (Karimpoor et al., n.d., DeSimone et al., 2021, McAllister et al., 2014, Champagne et al., 2020). We would also anticipate these changes in CBF and neurocognition observed for group by time interactions will be positively correlated (i.e., decreases in CBF significantly associated with less improvement in pre- to post-season cognition).

Materials & methods

Participants and procedures

Participants were recruited from local middle and high school athletic programs in the Southeast Wisconsin area. Inclusion criteria included being enrolled in middle school or high school level athletic programs in either contact or non-contact sport. For participants included in the current analysis, contact sports included football, ice hockey, and soccer. Non-contact athletes participated in either basketball, baseball, cross-country, swimming, tennis, or track and field. Participants were enrolled in a single sport throughout the pre- to post-season period. Exclusion criteria included history or clinical suspicion of significant neurologic or developmental disorder (e.g., epilepsy, stroke, brain tumor), history of moderate or severe TBI, or contraindication to study procedures. This study was approved by the Medical College of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board. All participants or legal guardians gave written informed consent and assent was obtained for minor participants. All participants underwent MRI scans before and after the season of play.

As part of pre- and post-season assessment, participants completed a structured questionnaire of sociodemographic, medical history (including height and weight for BMI calculation and history of prior concussion), and patient reported outcome measures. To aid in greater accuracy of concussion history, a standard definition of concussion was presented to athletes, which stated: a concussion is a blow to the head followed by a variety of symptoms that may include any of the following: headache, dizziness, loss of balance, blurred vision, seeing stars, feeling in a fog or slowed down, memory problems, poor concentration, nausea, or throwing-up. Getting knocked out or being unconscious does NOT always occur with a concussion (McCrea et al., 2004). Socioeconomic status was estimated using Hollingshead four factor index. Athletes also completed a targeted battery of neurocognitive measures assessing cognitive domains reflecting verbal memory, visual memory, processing speed, reaction time, psychomotor speed, and select aspects of executive function.

Study activities were initiated just prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and associated limitations of research activities occurred with shutdowns. Study activities continued, but aspects of the protocol were adversely impacted by imposed restrictions, such as limited access to non-essential personnel and schedule availability with the scanner for imaging. As a result, there was a notable difference in pre- to post-season scan times between contact and non-contact athletes. Specifically, average time from pre- to post-season scan for contact and non-contact groups were 100.3 days (SD = 22.8, range 70 – 155 days) and 246.5 days (SD = 108.7, range 112 – 464 days) respectively. In order to account for this, sensitivity analyses that included time between visits/scan as a covariate were performed and reported within the Results section and Supplemental material. The data utilized as part of this study that support the reported findings are available from the corresponding author, upon reasonable request.

Neurocognitive assessment

The clinical battery included the Immediate Post-Concussion Assessment of Cognition Test (ImPACT) and the Trailmaking Test (TmT) subtests from the Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (DKEFS). ImPACT is computer-based assessment comprised of four indices reflecting different domains of cognition that include verbal memory, visual memory, visual motor speed, and reaction time. The DKEFS TmT includes five separate indices that measure complimentary components of processing speed and executive function, including visual scanning, simple psychomotor processing speed (two separate trials), ability to maintain a complex set, and pure motor speed. Neurocognitive outcomes of interests for the current study included the four primary indices from ImPACT and five primary indices from DKEFS TmT.

MRI acquisition

T1-weighted structural images were obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: FOV = 240 mm, acquisition matrix = 240x240, slice thickness = 1 mm, 188 slices, TR/TE/TI = 2280/2.84/900 ms, flip angle = 8°. Next, an ASL image was acquired using an “enhanced” Hadamard-encoded 3D pCASL (eASL) sequence was utilized with seven PLDs (1.0, 1.4, 1.7, 2.1, 2.6, 3.1, 3.7 s) (Cohen et al., 2020). A long (3.5 s) labeling block was divided into seven sub-boluses and eight images were acquired with control and label sub-boluses corresponding to a Hadamard matrix. This was followed by a segmented stack of spirals readout with 4 arms of 640 points. A raw magnetization (M0) image was collected on the last repetition. Additional imaging parameters included: TR/TE = 7480/61 ms, slice thickness = 3 mm, 48 slices, FOV = 220 mm, and FA = 110°. Images for each of the seven delays were extracted using a linear combination of the eight images. Individual ATT was computed using the signal-weighted delay method (Eq. (1)) (Dai et al., 2012).

Imaging processing

Data preprocessing used a combination of AFNI (Cox, 1996, Cox, 2012) and FSL (Jenkinson et al., 2012). First, the anatomical image was ACPC aligned using aff2rigid in FSL. Then, a brain mask was created by first linearly registering the MPRAGE image to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using flirt in FSL with 12 degrees of freedom (Jenkinson et al., 2002). Then, fnirt in FSL was used to non-linearly refine the registration. A masked reference image in MNI space was inverse warped to native space using the transformations determined above. This was used as the brain mask for the MPRAGE image. Finally, the MPRAGE brain-only image was registered to MNI space, with a 2 mm isotropic voxel size, using flirt with 12 degrees of freedom followed by fnirt.

The M0 image was registered to the MPRAGE image using epi_reg in FSL (Jenkinson et al., 2012). The CBF and ATT maps were then registered to the MPRAGE image using the transformation matrix of the M0 to MPRAGE registration. CBF and ATT maps were normalized to MNI standard space using the MPRAGE to MNI transformation matrix computed above. Finally, the individual CBF maps were normalized by dividing each voxel by the mean of the gray matter to create relative CBF (rCBF) maps, to overcome individual variability and increase the sensitivity of detecting the subtle difference in CBF (Aslan and Lu, 2010).

Statistical analysis

Separate linear mixed-effect models (LMEs) were used to assess associations of contact sport status (contact vs non-contact), time (pre- to post- season), and time by contact sport status interactions. LMEs are ideal for longitudinal (pre-to post-) designs because they allow for testing of fixed (e.g., contact sport group) and random effects, which incorporate inter-individual differences and account for within-subject correlations of outcomes across time points (i.e., subject-specific trajectory) (Pinheiro, 2014). Age and concussion history were included as covariates for all LMEs. Statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS software (version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and statistical significance was assessed at the (alpha) 0.05 level. Those with only a single time point were included to contribute maximum information to LME models. LME models were fit using 3dLME in AFNI to test the effects of time (pre- to post-season difference), contact group (contact vs non-contact) and group-by-time interactions in rCBF and ATT. Covariates within models, including BMI (Willeumier et al., 2012, Aghjayan et al., 2021), history of prior concussion (Churchill et al., 2021, Brooks et al., 2019), and, age (Satterthwaite et al., 2014) were selected a priori based on previous studies demonstrating associations with CBF. Overall group, time, and group by time interaction effects were computed using chi-squared tests. Post-hoc comparisons were evaluated using general linear tests. Multiple comparisons were controlled for using a cluster size correction technique as follows: spatial autocorrelation function (ACF) parameters were estimated for the residuals of the 3dLME tests using 3dFWHMx in AFNI. Then, the ACF parameters for rCBF and ATT datasets were separately input into 3dClustSim in AFNI. LME results were thresholded at p < 0.05 and cluster size corrected with α < 0.05, meaning the probability of a cluster being a false positive was 5 %.

Results

Demographic information, sport, and medical history are provided in Table 1. The final sample consisted of 34 contact and 23 well-matched non-contact sport athletes. Athletes included in the analyses did not report sustaining a diagnosed concussion during the season of focus. Mean age of the sample was 14.7 (SD = 1.97; range = 10–18 years) and median time since most recent concussion was 48 months (interquartile range = 9.5–72.0). Contact and non-contact groups did not significantly differ in estimated socioeconomic status background, t(54) = -1.15, p = 0.257. Football was the most commonly enrolled sport in the contact group (n = 27; 79.4 %) and baseball was the most commonly enrolled non-contact sport (n = 9; 39.1 %). Average time from pre- to post-season scan was 150.2 days (SD = 99.5, range 70 – 464 days) for the whole group.

rCBF changes across contact and Non-contact group by time

A significant group by time interaction effect was observed for rCBF in a cluster centered at MNI coordinates (14,-16,54) that consisted of pre- and post-central gyri, superior parietal lobule, and inferior and middle frontal gyri regions (cluster size = 15147 voxels, maximum chi-squared score = 20.8; Fig. 1). Sensitivity analyses including only football players as the contact sport group yielded comparable results as the full sample, which was expected given the majority of the contact sport group is comprised of athletes participating in football (Supplemental Fig. 1). Sensitivity analyses with time between visits as a covariate yielded a significant interaction between group and time for rCBF in a cluster centered with lower expanded regional coverage (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Post-hoc analyses revealed that the interaction effect was driven by disparate trajectories of rCBF across the season for each group. Specifically, significant diffuse rCBF increases in contact athletes (centered at MNI coordinates [38,-58, 30]; cluster size = 3979 voxels, maximum z-score = 5.08) and diffuse rCBF decreases among non-contact athletes (centered at MNI coordinates [26,2, 54]; cluster size = 6695 voxels, maximum z-score = -5.59) with shared elements of overlapping regions were recorded pre- to post-season (Fig. 2). This pre-to post-season interaction was also reflected in the fact rCBF differences were not observed between contact and non-contact athletes at pre-season; however, significant higher rCBF was recorded for the contact group vs the non-contact group at the postseason scan in a cluster centered at MNI coordinates [38,–22,52] that consisted of the superior parietal lobule and pre- and post-central gyri (cluster size = 3702 voxels, maximum z-score = 4.51). When time between visits was included within the model, similar patterns of pre- to post-season changes in rCBF were observed in post-hoc tests for groups independently, with decreases in non-contact athletes and increases in contact sport athletes over time (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Neurocognitive function

Descriptive statistics for neurocognitive indices at pre- and post-season for contact and non-contact athletes are provided in Table 2. A significant group by time interaction effect was observed for ImPACT Verbal Memory performance, B = -14.85, SE = 5.23, p = 0.007 (Table 3). Specifically, while non-contact sport athletes improved over time from pre- to post-season (pre-season mean score = 81.55[SD = 14.74]; post-season mean score = 90.77[SD = 7.58]), contact sport athletes declined from pre-to post-season (pre-season mean score = 85.03[SD = 11.56]; post-season mean score = 80.48[SD = 19.19]). A significant group by time interaction was also observed for ImPACT Visual Motor Speed, B = -3.06, SE = 1.41, p = 0.037, with the contact sport group showing less improvement from pre-to post-season compared to the non-contact group. Group by time interactions were not observed for other neurocognitive indices (ps > 0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

The innovative 3D pCASL sequence with Hadamard-encoded multiple PLDs and use of ATT to administer the appropriate PLDs in the current study provides the opportunity to more precisely measure rCBF, which allows for the detection of even subtle biological alterations (Cohen et al., 2020, van der Thiel et al., 2018). Using this approach, diffuse alterations in rCBF, as well as parallel differences in verbal memory and visual motor speed, were observed following a single season of contact sport participation. Additionally, a single season of sport participation among the two groups was not differentially associated with neurovasculature (as measured by ATT) changes over time. Overall, findings from this study suggest that cerebral perfusion changes may represent one pathway by which exposure to cumulative RHI in the absence of concussion represents a potential risk factor for adverse long-term outcomes in contact athletes.

Most studies to date have reported on changes in cerebral perfusion following acute concussion but not related to RHI in contact sport exposure. While there has been some variability across reported results, most studies of sport-related concussion have generally observed lower levels in CBF during the acute stage of injury (24–72 h) compared to controls, which may be further exacerbated among those with a previous history of concussion (Churchill et al., 2021, Churchill et al., 2023). Further, studies have observed within-subject CBF decreases from the acute to subacute stage, with generally comparable levels following a typical window of recovery (approximately 30 days) (Wang et al., 2019, Wang et al., 2016, Meier et al., 2015, Churchill et al., 2017). An inverse association was observed in the current study, such that contact sport participation was associated with increased CBF from pre- to post-season and compared to non-contact sport athletes (i.e., interaction effect), who displayed decreases in CBF from pre- to post-season. Taken together, current results support the growing notion that cumulative RHI via contact sport exposure and concussion may induce distinct processes and represent independent risk factors for adverse long-term outcomes among contact sport athletes (McCann et al., 2022, Montenigro et al., 2017, Tagge et al., 2018, McKee et al., 2016; McKee et al., 2016).

Prospective investigations into the effects of contact sport exposure via pre- to post- season changes in CBF are extremely limited. In one previous study of collegiate football players using helmet-based accelerometers to quantify head impacts during play, Champange and colleagues observed decreased CBF from pre- to post-season among players with greater levels of head impact exposure over the course of the season (Champagne et al., 2020). Interestingly, although season-related CBF change was recorded in the reverse direction in the aforementioned study, the location of CBF alterations from pre- to post- season showed notable overlap with the regions observed for the current study (parietal lobule, and middle frontal gyrus). While this overlap shows that CBF regulation within these regions may be particularly vulnerable to the effects of RHI, the cause of disparate CBF alteration directions across the two studies is unclear.

Two potential sources for the discrepancy in CBF signal direction for the current study and results from Champagne et al. (2020) include developmental/age sample differences (i.e., collegiate athletes compared to adolescent athletes; see below) and method of cerebral perfusion acquisition and processing (3D pCASL sequence with Hadamard-encoded multiple post-labeling delays and rCBF compared to CBF). In implementing the Hadamard-encoding process, the currently applied ASL technique can correct CBF based on ATT. This is critical due to the fact that if cerebrovascular alterations are present and ATT extended, a decayed ASL signal and rCBF underestimation can result. In other words, with longer ATT in contact athletes, CBF uncorrected for ATT may be lower than the “true” CBF and caused by longer ATT, artificially lowering CBF measurements, though ATT differences were not observed in the current study.

The large majority of studies investigating the physiological effects of RHI via contact sport participation over the course of the season have involved collegiate (Parivash et al., 2019, Mills et al., 2020, Champagne et al., 2020, Churchill et al., 2021), and less often high-school athletes (Davenport et al., 2016, Yuan et al., 2018). The current study involved adolescent athletes, including those at the middle- and high- school levels. A large longitudinal study of cerebral perfusion in non-athlete adolescents from pre- to post-puberty reported a general linear trend of decreased CBF across multiple regions in males between the ages of 8-to 22-years old (Satterthwaite et al., 2014). Presumably, this developmental reduction in cerebral perfusion was reflected in the decreased rCBF from pre- to post-season among the non-contact athletes in the current study. Within the contact sport group, the alterations in CBF could reflect a disruption of this natural developmental decrease in perfusion with age.

The relationship between the observed alterations in CBF over the course of a single season of contact sport exposure and adverse long-term outcomes reported in older former contact sport athletes is unclear. Specifically, it is not known if these alterations remain stable (representing a static risk factor), gradually worsen over time, or recover and return to pre-season levels following a period (off-season) or complete discontinuation from contact sport. A growing number of studies examining physiological changes associated with RHI via neuroimaging across multiple seasons supports this third possibility. For example, two independent studies of active contact sport athletes have observed significant alterations in white matter microstructural integrity pre-to post-season, with return toward pre-season levels at 6–9 months following an off-season period (Mayinger et al., 2018, Yuan et al., 2018). Interestingly, in the current study, the regional coverage of the significant contact group by time interaction was notably smaller when time between scans was included as a covariate in the model, further supporting this possibility that season-related alterations in CBF may return closer to baseline with greater time since contact sport participation. These early findings require replication to understanding their significance and clinical meaningfulness. Future studies with additional follow-up are needed to better understand the time course of CBF alterations associated with a single season of contact participation, as well as factors that may exacerbate these alterations and exacerbate their role as a risk factor for adverse neurologic outcomes later in life (e.g., threshold for cumulative exposure, exposure closer in proximity such as consecutive contact sport seasons, medical comorbidities).

Within the current study, contact and non-contact athlete contrasts served as a proxy for high and low/no head impact exposure. Additional variability across these groups in pertinent factors, such as frequency of exercise and physical activity over the course of the season may have potentially contributed to the observed results as well. While it is well established that exercise at moderate intensity acutely increases metabolic demand and CBF with a return to baseline following a period of rest, it is not clear whether routine exercise over time increases CBF at rest (Barnes, 2015, Querido and Sheel, 2007). One study of multi-sport university athletes measuring CBF changes pre- to post- season and one month post-season observed decreases in CBF immediately following and one month post-season compared to pre-season, which would be antithetical to this idea (Churchill et al., 2021). Regardless, future studies should include wearable head impact telemetry or accelerometer sensors to more directly and precisely estimate the associations between RHI and rCBF observed across contact sport groups, reducing confounds such as exercise frequency.

To our knowledge, no studies to date have examined changes in ATT as a result of concussion or RHI exposure. Current results revealed that ATT was not significantly longer in contact athletes compared to non-contact athletes following a season of RHI exposure. In preclinical studies of single and repetitive TBI, prolonged ATT has been related to the decreased blood velocity near an affected site and possible increase in microvascular reactivity. These studies reflect sequelae reflective of concussive injury, and not RHI as was of interest in the current study (Østergaard et al., 2014, Adams et al., 2018). Taken together, the current findings suggest rCBF changes occur in the absence of pre- to post-season structural differences in neurovasculature.

Studies investigating neurobehavioral function over the course of a single contact sport season among non-concussed athletes have been variable, with most studies failing to observe significant changes pre-to post-season (Rose et al., 2021, Tarnutzer et al., 2017, Broglio et al., 2018). Our results are consistent with one previous investigation, which also showed differences in verbal memory pre- to post- season in contact athletes as compared to non-contact controls (McAllister et al., 2012). While the reduction of verbal memory scores from pre- to season among contact sport athletes did not exceed a threshold of reliable change and clinically meaningful difference within the current study (Brett et al., 2016), the rate of score decrease over the course of a single season exceeds what has commonly been observed across most studies and what would be expected outside the acute to subacute period of concussive injury. As such, it is unlikely that the pre- to post- season difference in verbal memory represents stable decreases with each season of contact exposure, but rather non-permanent processes, such as greater intra-individual variability across time (Bielak et al., 2010, Merritt et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2014).

Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be noted. The generalizability of the current findings to female athletes is limited given that sample was comprised of male athletes only. Additionally, although the proportion of individuals identifying as Black/or African American was comparable to the U.S. population (approximately 14 %), the current sample size does not allow for examination of within group associations and the limited representation of other races in the current study limits generalizability in that regard. It is important to note that the contact sport group was comprised primarily of football players. While a number of studies have observed pre- to post- season changes in neuroimaging metrics (e.g., functional connectivity on resting state fMRI, white matter microstructure integrity on diffusion MRI) across a number of contact sports (e.g., soccer, hockey), future larger studies with greater statistical power are needed in order to conduct sub-group analyses for different contact sports independently (Monroe et al., 2020, DiFabio et al., 2023, Koerte et al., 2023). Though age was included as a covariate within our statistical models, the current sample included a wide age range across adolescence/early-adulthood and it is not clear how changes over the course of the season may have interacted with age. Though no participants reported being diagnosed with a concussion over the course of the study period, it is possible that undiagnosed concussions may have occurred.

4.2 Conclusions

Differential trajectories of cerebral perfusion changes were observed over the course of a single season among contact and non-contact athletes who did not sustain a concussion during the study period. These differences in CBF may represent one pathway by which exposure to RHI, independent of concussion history, represents a potential risk factor for adverse long-term outcomes in contact athletes. Although contact sport athletes exhibit season-related effects in cerebral perfusion, the natural course of these changes as reversible (recovery or rebound in off-season or between years of participation), stable, or intensifying over time requires further investigation.
