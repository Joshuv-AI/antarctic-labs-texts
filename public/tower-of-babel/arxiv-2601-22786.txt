Toward IIT-Inspired Consciousness in LLMs: A Reward-Based Learning Framework
Hamid Reza Akbari, Mohammad Hossein Sameti, Amir M. Mansourian, Mohammad Hossein Rohban, Hossein Sameti

Abstract
The pursuit of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is a central goal in language model development, in which consciousness-like processing could serve as a key facilitator. While current language models are not conscious, they exhibit behaviors analogous to certain aspects of consciousness. This paper investigates the implementation of a leading theory of consciousness—Integrated Information Theory (IIT)—within language models via a reward-based learning paradigm. IIT provides a formal, axiom-based mathematical framework for quantifying consciousness. Drawing inspiration from its core principles, we formulate a novel reward function that quantifies a text’s causality, coherence and integration, characteristics associated with conscious processing. Empirically, it is found that optimizing for this IIT-inspired reward leads to more concise text generation. On out of domain tasks, careful tuning achieves up to a 31% reduction in output length while preserving accuracy levels comparable to the base model. In addition to primary task performance, the broader effects of this training methodology on the model’s confidence calibration and test-time computational scaling is analyzed. The proposed framework offers significant practical advantages: it is conceptually simple, computationally efficient, requires no external data or auxiliary models, and leverages a general, capability-driven signal rather than task-specific heuristics. 3.1
Integrated Information Theory

IIT aims to answer such questions—that is, to offer a scientific account of consciousness, of what it feels like to be you right now. The IIT method can be summarized as
“consciousness first”
. It starts from the fact that one’s experience exists, right now, and it uses introspection to characterize the properties of experience. The theory then asks how we might account for these properties of experience in physical terms—which means in terms of something we can observe and manipulate “out there.”
The starting point of IIT is the existence of experience. This truth is not the result of an inference; it is rather immediate and confirmed through introspection. The five axioms of IIT express the essential properties of experience, discovered through introspection and reasoning. The five axioms are essential properties of experience because they characterize what experience is—or the nature of experience—and because they are irrefutably true of every conceivable experience, as confirmed through introspection and reflection alone. The axioms can be most succinctly stated as follows :

Existence:
Experience exists; there is something.

Intrinsicality:
Experience is intrinsic; it exists for itself.

Information:
Experience is specific; it is this particular experience.

Integration:
Experience is unitary; it is irreducible to separate experiences.

Exclusion:
Experience is definite; it is this whole experience.

Composition:
Experience is structured; it consists of distinctions and relations forming a phenomenal structure.

According to IIT, A system is any subset of units that can be considered as a candidate substrate of consciousness. A unit is a constituent of a substrate, operationally defined by the grain at which it is observed and manipulated to evaluate its cause–effect power. A substrate of consciousness (also called a complex) is a set of units in a state that satisfies all postulates of IIT. A substrate graph is a way to visually represent the substrate under investigation as a network. It typically consists of the (labeled) units and their causal connections, as nodes and edges of a network respectively. The state of a unit in IIT is binary. A
T
ransition
P
robability
M
atrix (TPM) is a mathematical object that completely describes the dynamics of a substrate of units. More precisely, it is a matrix of numbers that can be used to find the probability that a substrate state will transition from one to another.

A central issue concerns how the level of consciousness of a given substrate can be determined. The procedure can be understood as six sequential steps, each checking whether a candidate substrate satisfies each of the postulates (0th plus five)

. A conscious system generates integrated information, measured as
\phi
.

3.2
Problem Setup

Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote an autoregressive LLM policy

\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{i}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i}),

(1)

where
x
is the input prompt and
y=y_{1},\dots,y_{|y|}
is the output sequence. For clarity, we will suppress the conditioning on x whenever it is clear from the context.

Let
r(y)
denote a reward function assigning a scalar score to an output sequence. RL seeks to maximize the expected reward

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[r(y)].

(2)

The model’s consciousness of the generated text is equivalent to the sum of the consciousness of each individual token so

r(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{|y|}r(y_{i}).

(3)

The following sections detail how the consciousness associated with each token is computed.

According to IIT, a system is composed of interconnected units with binary states. The transition probabilities between these states are defined by a TPM. A key question is how a LLM can be utilized to construct such a system—including its units and TPM—from generated texts.
For this purpose, the token embeddings of the LLM serve as system states. Initially, the output representation is adjusted according to the input representation via an attention mechanism. Due to the high-dimensional nature of LLM embeddings, the resulting state space becomes prohibitively large. Moreover, given the limited number of tokens, dimensionality reduction of the representations is unavoidable. To binarize the system states, the mean value across all representations is computed, with values above the mean thresholded to 1 and those below to 0. The number of system units corresponds to the embedding dimensionality. Since all layer inputs contribute to representation generation within the LLM, the associated units are assumed to be fully connected. This approach is adopted from
(
39
)
. The above steps are illustrated in Figure
2
(a).

To construct the TPM, each consecutive pair of tokens generated by the LLM is interpreted as a state transition from the first token to the second. Consequently, for a sequence of
n
tokens,
n-1
transitions are identified and incorporated into the TPM. By normalizing the frequency of these transitions, the resulting matrix is converted into a probabilistic form.

Intrinsic Information

According to the information postulate, a system’s cause–effect state is determined by computing intrinsic information (II) and selecting the cause and effect that maximize its value. II quantifies the system’s specific cause–effect power in its current state. Systems with high II are both

3.4
Why Intrinsic Inforamtion Reward?

This study aims to investigate which aspects of consciousness in a language model can be meaningfully quantified under IIT. Based on these quantifiable aspects, the model is trained to enhance its consciousness-related features. Importantly, the presence of such features does not imply that the language model itself is conscious.According to IIT, a conscious system is characterized by two fundamental features:

1.

It has intrinsic cause–effect power.

2.

The experience is irreducible to a set of separate or independent experiences.

A high
\Phi
value in a LLM indicates that it exhibits the two aforementioned features. One of the main challenges associated with computing
\Phi
is its high computational cost, which increases exponentially with the number of units, thereby rendering its calculation difficult and impractical for large-scale systems. The fundamental formulation proposed by IIT is computationally intractable and, in certain cases, lacks a well-defined specification
(
9
;
43
)

To address this challenge, the present study proposes a new metric, termed
intrinsic information
, which has lower computational complexity and focuses specifically on the first feature. The II is computed for each output token of the LLM according to the information postulate. In each computation, the target token is considered as the current state of the system. Consequently, by evaluating the II for each token, a quantitative measure of token-level consciousness is obtained. Subsequently, the corresponding cause state and effect state are identified by selecting those states that maximize the II associated with the current state. Formally, conditioned on the current state, two II measures are computed:
ii_{c}
, corresponding to the cause state, and
ii_{e}
, corresponding to the effect state. The II of the current state is then defined as the sum
ii=ii_{c}+ii_{e}
. Although, in accordance with
the Principle of Maximal Existence
in IIT, one may alternatively define the II of the current state as
\max\!\left(ii_{c},\,ii_{e}\right)
, this formulation was also evaluated experimentally. Nevertheless, empirical findings demonstrate that the additive formulation yields more favorable performance during the model training process. The computation process of II is illustrated in Figure
2
(b).

II
is the product of two factors,
informativeness
and
selectivity
, which are calculated through operations on the cause TPM and effect TPM as described below

ii_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}c}/{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}e}}=\underbrace{\left(\frac{P^{\prime}_{\text{constraint}}}{1}\right)}_{\text{selectivity}}\;\underbrace{\log\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{constraint}}}{P_{\text{unconstraint}}}\right)}_{\text{informativeness}}.

(4)

Conceptually, informativeness can be thought of as quantifying causal “raw power,” and selectivity as quantifying “control”. In this setting, due to the absence of a pinned state, the cause TPM and the effect TPM are both identical to the system’s original TPM.
Constrained probability
is the probability of a specific cause/effect state given that the system is set to its specific current state.
Unconstrained probability
is the average probability of that cause/effect state if the system is initialized in all possible states.
Selectivity
characterizes the degree to which a mechanism’s cause–effect power is concentrated over a particular state. On the effect side, selectivity is quantified by the constrained effect state, whereas on the cause side, it is quantified by the backward constrained cause state. For computational details, please refer to
(
5
)

Response length is reduced across all benchmarks.

The temperature parameter was set to 0.7 to control the stochasticity of the model’s output. Confidence calibration is evaluated using the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) metrics. For the purpose of this analysis, the data was discretized into 10 bin intervals. Table

By comparing the accuracy metric between Tables 1 and 2 across all benchmarks, an improvement in the model’s performance under the self-consistency setting can be observed. Although our models reduce the MCE and ECE metrics on certain benchmarks, they fail to achieve comparable performance on others.

Conclusion

We presented a framework to enhance feature-consciousness in LLMs via reinforcement learning with an intrinsic information reward based on integrated information theory information postulate. Benchmark evaluations show that models with improved cause-effect power produce more concise responses. While validated on both in-domain and out-of-domain data, extending this approach to more complex tasks—such as very hard mathematical reasoning and code generation—remains a valuable direction for future work.

Additionally, consciousness was quantified through pre- and post-state; given its abstract nature, alternative definitions could provide further insights.

Given the inherent trade-off between improving predictive accuracy and reducing output length, a systematic analysis of the interaction between these two objectives is required. This analysis should be grounded in training-process variables, such as the in intrinsic information, which can significantly influence optimization dynamics. From an information-theoretic perspective, examining how reward shaping affects this trade-off may yield insights into the emergence of compact yet high-accuracy representations during model training.

Hyperparameter selection poses challenges due to dynamic model behavior during training, such as varying response lengths affecting the data required for transition probability matrix construction. Exploring adaptive or hyperparameter-free strategies represents a promising avenue for further enhancing this framework.

Appendix A

A.1
Hyperparameters

In the process of determining the number of units and constructing the TPM, the following two hyperparameters are considered:

Reduced dimensionality:
This hyperparameter defines the size of the discretized state space used to represent the system. The dimensionality must be compatible with the batch size of the training data to ensure reliable estimation of transition probabilities. Notably, when computing rewards based on the
\Phi
measure, there is an inherent limitation on the number of dimensions due to combinatorial explosion; given our available computational resources,
\Phi
-based rewards cannot be reliably computed for dimensionalities greater than three. In contrast, rewards derived from II do not impose this dimensionality restriction.

Dataset size / TPM construction policy:
Different strategies can be adopted for TPM construction, reflecting the way trajectories or responses are aggregated:

•

Batch mode: All responses generated by the LLM within a single batch are aggregated to construct a single TPM, representing the collective state transitions of that batch.

•

Prompt mode: Since multiple responses may be generated for a single prompt during training, only responses corresponding to the same prompt are used to construct the TPM for that specific prompt. Consequently, one TPM is generated per prompt, allowing reward computation that is specific to individual prompts.

•

Trajectory mode: A separate TPM is constructed for each individual response, capturing the transition dynamics at the granularity of single trajectories.

State size:
As discussed in Section 4, the reward assigned to a response is computed as the sum of the rewards for each individual token in the response. In this approach, each state is initially defined as a single token. However, the state size can be extended beyond a single token to include a fixed-width sequence of multiple tokens. For instance, if the state size is set to four tokens, an output sequence of 200 tokens would correspond to 50 states. Increasing the state size allows the model to capture higher-level cause–effect relationships across token sequences, aligning with the principles of transition dynamics in both RL and IIT frameworks. Therefore, the state size is considered in two forms: token-based and chunk-based. In the chunk-based setting, the state size is treated as a hyperparameter.

A.4
Analysis of Experimental Results

Based on the experimental results, several observations merit discussion.

First,
the proposed II Reward consistently outperforms the Phi reward function across the evaluated settings, indicating its stronger capacity to capture informative structural properties of generated sequences.

Figure 4
:
Phi-based reward Training

Second,
within the GRPO framework, the final reward signal used for training the LLM is defined as the sum of the “accuracy reward” and the “II Reward”. While the accuracy reward explicitly incentivizes the model toward producing the correct final answer, the “II Reward” operates differently. Specifically, a high II Reward indicates that each generated token is highly constrained by both its preceding and succeeding tokens, thereby encouraging the emergence of stronger cause–effect dependencies within the generated text. This behavior provides a plausible explanation for the observed reduction in response length. Intuitively, when the model exhibits increased consciousness of its current state—characterized by an understanding of the most likely causal predecessor (cause state) and successor (effect state)—it tends to favor more compact generations with reduced redundancy.

Figure 5
:
II Reward Training Using Trajectory Mode

Third,
increased consciousness of preceding and succeeding states does not necessarily align with improvements in task accuracy. The absence of a substantial accuracy gain suggests that the level of structural consciousness induced by maximizing the II Reward alone may be insufficient to improve accuracy. In some configurations, over-optimization of this reward even leads to a degradation in accuracy, highlighting a potential trade-off between response length and task performance.

Figure 6
:
II Reward Training Using Prompt Mode

Fourth,
an additional noteworthy observation is the reduction in the entropy of the generated texts. This reduction is consistent with the learned strengthening of cause–effect structures, indicating a shift toward more predictable token transitions.

Figure 7
:
II Reward Training Using Batch Mode

Finally,
the TPM construction policy and the choice of reduced dimensionality are shown to have a significant impact on evaluation outcomes. While the Trajectory mode leads to a pronounced reduction in response length, the Batch mode preserves model accuracy more effectively. Moreover, increasing the dimensionality of the TPM substantially raises sample complexity requirements, underscoring the importance of selecting an appropriate dimensionality that is well matched to the amount of data generated in a single training step.
