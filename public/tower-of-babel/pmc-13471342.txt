Comparative effects of viable and gamma-irradiated Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis on immune modulation and resistance to furunculosis in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss)
Authors: Amirhossein Haghparast, Gholamreza Rafiee, Alireza Neissi, Ali Kalantari-Hesari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the effects of dietary supplementation with live (B-PRO) and gamma-irradiated (B-PAR) Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis on growth performance, innate immune-related parameters, antioxidant status, and resistance to Aeromonas salmonicida in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss).ResultsAfter 60 days of feeding, both B-PRO and B-PAR were associated with improved growth performance, feed utilization, and body composition compared with the control group, with generally higher values in B-PRO. Hematological parameters, including erythrocyte and leukocyte counts, were elevated in treated groups.Innate immune indicators (lysozyme activity, complement C3, and bactericidal activity) and antioxidant status (SOD, GSH-Px, and total antioxidant capacity) were significantly improved, accompanied by reduced malondialdehyde levels.Gene expression analysis showed tissue-dependent modulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8, indicating a non-uniform immunoregulatory response.Following A. salmonicida challenge, survival was ~95% in both treated groups versus ~15% in controls. Histopathological evaluation indicated reduced tissue damage in supplemented fish.ConclusionsDietary supplementation with both live and gamma-irradiated B. infantis is associated with improved growth, modulation of innate immune-related parameters, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and increased disease resistance in rainbow trout. The comparable effects of both preparations suggest that bacterial viability is not essential for several observed responses under the present conditions. Introduction

Furunculosis, caused by Aeromonas salmonicida, is a major infectious disease in salmonid aquaculture and is associated with high mortality and substantial economic losses, particularly under intensive farming conditions and elevated water temperatures. Conventional control strategies include vaccination, antibiotics, and functional feed additives such as probiotics and prebiotics. However, the extensive use of antibiotics has contributed to the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance in aquatic environments, raising concerns regarding ecosystem and food safety [1–3].

Probiotics have therefore gained considerable attention as sustainable alternatives to antibiotics in aquaculture. They are defined as live microorganisms which, when administered in adequate amounts, confer health benefits to the host [4, 5]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that probiotics improve growth performance, feed utilization, gut microbiota balance, and immune responses in fish; [6] Probiotics and immunity in rainbow trout). However, their application is limited by challenges such as poor environmental stability, host specificity, inconsistent colonization, and potential concerns related to horizontal gene transfer [7, 8].

To overcome these limitations, paraprobiotics defined as non-viable microbial cells or cell fragments that confer health benefits—have emerged as an alternative strategy [9, 10]. Unlike live probiotics, paraprobiotics do not require survival or colonization in the gastrointestinal tract, improving biosafety and storage stability while retaining immunomodulatory activity.

The beneficial effects of paraprobiotics are mainly attributed to microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), including peptidoglycans and lipoteichoic acids, which can interact with host pattern-recognition receptors and activate innate immune responses even in the absence of viable cells [11–13]. In aquaculture species, paraprobiotics have been reported to enhance immune functions such as lysozyme activity, phagocytosis, respiratory burst, and antioxidant defenses, contributing to increased disease resistance [14, 15].

Although most studies have focused on heat-killed bacterial preparations, alternative inactivation approaches such as gamma irradiation remain poorly investigated. Gamma irradiation can inactivate microorganisms while potentially preserving structural integrity of immunologically active cell components, making it a promising method for paraprobiotic preparation [16].

Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis is a well-known beneficial bacterium with documented probiotic and immunomodulatory properties in terrestrial animals [17]; however, its application in aquaculture remains limited. In particular, comparative studies between its viable and paraprobiotic (gamma-irradiated) forms in fish are still scarce.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of dietary supplementation with live and gamma-irradiated B. longum subsp. infantis on growth performance, immune response, oxidative status, and resistance to Aeromonas salmonicida infection in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss). This study addresses the current knowledge gap regarding the functional equivalence of viable and non-viable probiotic forms in salmonid aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Culture conditions and growth monitoring

Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis (ATCC 15697) was cultured in modified de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth supplemented with lactose and 0.25% L-cysteine under anaerobic conditions at 30 °C overnight. For growth kinetics, 1 mL of overnight culture was inoculated into 50 mL fresh medium and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 140 rpm. Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured at defined time intervals (0–46 h). Sterile MRS broth was used as blank control. Growth curves were generated to determine bacterial growth phases [18].

Microscopic verification of culture purity

Culture purity was confirmed by Gram staining using standard procedures. Smears from single colonies were heat-fixed and stained to verify Gram-positive morphology consistent with Bifidobacterium spp [19].

Gamma irradiation for bacterial inactivation

Gamma irradiation was used to inactivate B. infantis because ionizing radiation can effectively prevent bacterial replication while preserving structural cellular components that may contribute to immunomodulatory activity. Unlike thermal inactivation, gamma irradiation minimizes denaturation of surface proteins and cell wall-associated molecules [20].

Lyophilized biomass was exposed to gamma irradiation (1–15 kGy) using a Gammacell Nordion 220 irradiator (dose rate: 0.72 Gy/s). Post-irradiation viability was assessed by serial dilution and plating on MRS agar under anaerobic conditions at 30 °C for 48 h.

The D₁₀ value (dose required for 1-log reduction in viable cells) was estimated from survivor curves using linear regression and was approximately 11.6 kGy. Based on this, 12 kGy was selected as the operational inactivation dose. No CFU were detected at this dose in three independent plating assays, indicating loss of culturability below the detection limit of the method [16].

Preparation of B-PRO and B-PAR diets

Freeze-dried biomass of B. infantis was divided into two equal portions to prepare experimental diets. The first portion (B-PRO) consisted of non-irradiated freeze-dried biomass containing viable cells, standardized to approximately 10⁶–10⁷ CFU/g feed prior to incorporation into the basal diet.

The second portion (B-PAR) consisted of the same freeze-dried bacterial biomass, which was subjected to gamma irradiation at 12 kGy to eliminate culturability while preserving structural cellular components. This dose was selected based on survival curve analysis and confirmed to reduce viable counts below the detection limit of culture-based assays (no CFU detected in three independent plating experiments). Therefore, B-PAR is referred to as a non-culturable (paraprobiotic) formulation rather than a sterile preparation.

To ensure comparability between treatments, B-PRO and B-PAR were incorporated into the basal commercial diet at an identical inclusion level of 1% (w/w). Importantly, B-PAR was standardized based on equivalent dry biomass rather than CFU, ensuring equal initial bacterial input independent of viability.

For feed preparation, bacterial powders were uniformly sprayed onto commercial pellets and thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneous distribution. To improve adhesion and minimize nutrient leaching, a thin layer of vegetable oil was applied to the pellet surface. Prepared feeds were air-dried at room temperature and stored at 4 °C until use.

To confirm stability of the viable formulation, periodic plate counts were performed on B-PRO-supplemented feed during storage, ensuring maintenance of bacterial viability within the intended range throughout the feeding trial.

Fish were fed twice daily to apparent satiation, with feeding rates adjusted according to biomass and water temperature (approximately 1.5–6% body weight per day).

Experimental design and fish husbandry

After a 7-day acclimatization period, rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss, 81.12 ± 2.12 g) were acclimated for 2 weeks and randomly allocated into 300 L tanks. Each treatment included three replicate tanks (15–20 fish per tank). The tank was defined as the experimental unit. All fish-level measurements were averaged within each tank and tank mean values were used for statistical analysis across all parameters. Environmental conditions, aeration, and water quality were maintained uniformly across all tanks throughout the 60-day feeding trial. Appropriate aeration and environmental conditions were maintained throughout the experiment [21].

Animal experiments

Healthy rainbow trout (O. mykiss) were obtained from a licensed commercial aquaculture facility and acclimated under laboratory conditions for two weeks prior to the experiment. During acclimation, fish were maintained under identical environmental and feeding conditions to minimize handling and environmental stress before initiation of dietary treatments. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines for animal care and use (protocol: E52# 412 − 15).

Dietary treatments

The basal diet used in this study was a commercially formulated grower feed for rainbow trout manufactured according to standard aquaculture industry specifications. Experimental diets were prepared by supplementing the basal commercial feed with either viable (B-PRO) or gamma-irradiated (B-PAR) B. infantis. Therefore, all experimental diets were based on a practical commercial formulation rather than a purified laboratory-formulated diet, improving the applied relevance of the study for commercial aquaculture conditions.

The ingredient composition and proximate analysis of the experimental diets are presented in Table 1. The basal commercial diet contained approximately 40% crude protein and 12% crude lipid, which are within the recommended nutritional range for grower-stage rainbow trout. Commercial salmonid feeds typically contain 40–45% crude protein with sufficient lipid content to support optimal growth performance, feed utilization, and physiological function under intensive aquaculture conditions.

Ingredient composition and proximate analysis of experimental diets

*Gross energy (GE) of the diets was estimated based on standard physiological conversion factors for macronutrients and is within the typical range reported for commercial rainbow trout diets

The B-PRO diet was prepared by supplementing the basal commercial feed with 1% (w/w) freeze-dried viable B. infantis, standardized to approximately 10⁶–10⁷ CFU/g feed. The B-PAR diet contained the same inclusion level (1% w/w) of gamma-irradiated, non-culturable bacterial biomass to ensure equivalent bacterial biomass input between treatments. The bacterial preparations were incorporated after feed manufacture to minimize potential thermal damage and preserve structurally intact bacterial components.

Bacterial powders were evenly sprayed onto the pellets and thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneous distribution throughout the diets. A thin layer of vegetable oil was subsequently applied to improve adherence of bacterial biomass to the pellets, enhance palatability, and minimize leaching of additives into the water. Prepared diets were air-dried at room temperature and stored at 4 °C until use. Fish were fed twice daily for 60 days, and feeding rates were adjusted according to fish biomass and water temperature, ranging from approximately 1.5% to 6% of body weight per day, consistent with standard rainbow trout feeding protocols. To minimize variability among experimental units, feeding schedules, ration adjustments, and feed administration procedures were standardized across all tanks throughout the experimental period.

Growth performance

At the end of the 60-day feeding trial, all fish were weighed individually following a 24 h fasting period to minimize potential variation associated with gut content and assess growth performance. The following growth parameters were evaluated, final body weight (FBW), weight gain (WG), specific growth rate (SGR), and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Weight gain was calculated as the difference between final and initial body weight. SGR was calculated as the daily percentage increase in body weight during the experimental period. FCR was calculated by dividing total feed intake by total weight gain. To avoid pseudo-replication, tank mean values were used as the experimental unit for statistical analysis. These parameters provide comprehensive insight into the growth performance and feed efficiency of rainbow trout under different feeding treatments.

Hematological and biochemical analysis

To determine hematological and biochemical parameters, three fish per replicate were randomly selected at the end of the experimental period. After anesthesia with 50 mg/L clove oil, blood was withdrawn from the caudal peduncle using sterile syringes [22]. The whole blood samples were analyzed for red blood cell (RBC) count, white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin concentration, and hematocrit using standard hematological methods. White blood cells were differentiated by preparing blood smears and staining them with 5% Giemsa solution [23]. Serum samples were obtained by centrifuging blood at 4,500 rpm for 5 min and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Total protein content was measured using standard laboratory techniques. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activities were determined using commercially available Biorex kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions [24]. Glucose and cholesterol concentrations were measured using Biorex kits. Albumin and globulin concentrations were determined from total serum protein [25, 26]. All biochemical measurements were performed under identical analytical conditions for all experimental groups.

Oxidative stress biomarker assessment

To assess the oxidative status of rainbow trout under different dietary treatments, the activities of key antioxidant enzymes and the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation, were measured. Total antioxidant capacity (TAC), glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [27], and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were determined using standardized spectrophotometric methods [28]. MDA content was measured using the Thio barbituric acid assay (TBARS), which quantifies MDA as an indicator of lipid peroxidation [29]. These measurements provide information on the oxidative stress responses of the fish under different nutritional conditions. For each treatment, three fish per tank were randomly sampled (n = 3 tanks per group), and the tank was considered the experimental unit for statistical analysis to avoid pseudo-replication. All biochemical assays were performed in technical triplicate for each biological sample.

Transcriptomic analysis of immune markers

Kidney and spleen tissues from rainbow trout in the B-PRO, B-PAR, and control groups were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized using a chilled mortar and pestle. Approximately 20–40 mg of tissue was transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 750 µL of RL buffer (Parstous Total RNA Extraction Kit). Samples were homogenized on ice using a Precellys Tissue Homogenizer. After 5 min incubation at room temperature, 200 µL of chloroform was added, followed by vigorous shaking for 15 s and centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 12 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was collected, mixed with 70% ethanol, and processed through a spin column according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was eluted in 50 µL DEPC-treated water. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples were stored at − 70 °C until further use. For each experimental tank, three fish were randomly sampled (n = 3 tanks per treatment), and tissue samples were processed per individual fish. The tank was considered the biological replicate for all downstream statistical analyses.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis

cDNA was synthesized using the Easy™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Parstous). Each 20 µL reaction contained 0.5–1 µg total RNA, 10 µL 2× reaction buffer, 1 µL enzyme mix, and DEPC-treated water. The reaction was incubated at 25 °C for 10 min, followed by 55 °C for 30 min, and terminated at 85 °C for 5 min. cDNA was stored at − 20 °C. All cDNA samples were derived from individual fish per tank, and each tank was treated as one biological replicate.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Parstous, Iran). Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL forward and reverse primers (10 pmol/µL), 1–2 µL cDNA (10 fg–1 ng), and nuclease-free water. All reactions were performed using the MIC Real-Time PCR System (Bio Molecular Systems, Australia) only.

The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 50–60 °C for 30 s (based on primer melting temperatures; see Supplementary Table 1), and extension at 72 °C for 30 s and final extension was performed at 72 °C for 5 min. Melting curve analysis was performed from 65 °C to 95 °C at a ramp rate of 0.1 °C per second to evaluate product specificity. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in technical triplicate, and relative expression values were calculated using tank-level biological replication (n = 3 tanks per treatment).

Reference gene validation

The reference gene was selected based on its stable expression across kidney and spleen tissues and across all experimental groups (control, B-PRO, and B-PAR). Prior to relative quantification, reference gene stability was evaluated by comparing Ct values among all samples, and only genes showing minimal variation were used for normalization. This ensured reliable correction for sample-to-sample variation in RNA input and reverse transcription efficiency. Reference gene validation and normalization were performed at the individual fish level, and Ct values were subsequently aggregated at the tank level for statistical analysis.

Data analysis

Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates, and tank was considered the biological replicate for statistical analysis. Target genes included TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10. For each gene, ΔCt values were first calculated for individual fish, and then mean ΔCt values were averaged per tank prior to statistical comparison among treatments (n = 3 tanks per group). This procedure was used to avoid pseudo-replication and ensure independence of biological replicates.

Post-dietary challenge with A. salmonicida

After 60 days of feeding, rainbow trout from each dietary group (Control, B‑PRO, and B‑PAR) were subjected to a bacterial challenge with A. salmonicida to evaluate their immune responses and disease resistance. A total of 15 fish per treatment group (45 fish total) were selected from replicate tanks and acclimated prior to infection. Fish were sampled proportionally from each replicate tank (n = 3 tanks per treatment; 5 fish per tank), ensuring equal representation of all biological replicates.

The A. salmonicida strain was first cultured on blood agar to confirm purity and morphological consistency, and subsequently propagated in liquid medium under optimal growth conditions. Following incubation, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and washed twice with sterile 0.9% NaCl solution to remove residual culture media. The bacterial suspension was then standardized by serial dilution and CFU enumeration, and each fish was intraperitoneally injected with 200 µL of the final suspension, corresponding to approximately 2 × 10⁶ CFU per fish [30, 31].

Fish in the control (negative control) groups from each dietary treatment were injected with 200 µL of sterile physiological saline to account for handling and injection stress. After injection, fish were monitored daily for clinical signs of furunculosis, including lethargy, anorexia, skin lesions, hemorrhaging, and abnormal swimming behavior.

Mortality was recorded throughout the experimental period, and dead or moribund fish were immediately removed and subjected to aseptic necropsy. Kidney and spleen tissues were sampled and bacteriologically examined for the presence of A. salmonicida using standard culture-based isolation methods to confirm infection [31].

The challenge experiment was terminated when cumulative mortality in the positive control group exceeded 85% (at 3 days post-infection), consistent with a severe infection model. At termination, tissue samples (spleen, kidney, liver, gills, and intestine) were collected from all experimental groups (Control, B-PRO, and B-PAR) for histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining [32]. Histopathological evaluation was performed in a blinded manner, where the observer was unaware of the experimental group assignments to minimize observational bias. Tissue sections were examined under a light microscope and evaluated using a semi-quantitative lesion scoring system. Lesions were scored based on severity (0 = absent, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe) for key pathological features including hepatic necrosis, sinusoidal congestion, leukocyte infiltration, renal tubular degeneration, and tissue architecture disruption. The total histopathological score for each fish was calculated by summing individual lesion scores. For statistical analysis, tank was considered the experimental unit (n = 3 tanks per treatment), and mean lesion scores per tank were used to avoid pseudo-replication.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS version 2022. Before statistical comparison, percentage data were arcsine square-root transformed to meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances required for parametric testing of proportion data, as arcsine transformations are commonly recommended for proportion or percentage data to stabilize variances and approximate normality. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effects of the different dietary treatments on measured parameters, with the null hypothesis that all group means were equal. When the ANOVA indicated significant differences (P < 0.05), Duncan’s multiple range test was used as a post hoc comparison to identify specific differences among treatment means. Significance was set at P < 0.05 for all tests. Results are presented in tables and figures generated using Microsoft Excel version 2019. For all statistical analyses, the tank was defined as the experimental unit (n = 3 tanks per treatment). All datasets were first averaged at the tank level, and these tank means were used for ANOVA. Individual fish were treated as subsamples and were not considered independent replicates, thereby avoiding pseudo-replication.

Ethical approval

All experimental procedures involving fish were conducted in accordance with institutional, national, and international guidelines for the care and use of animals in research, and complied with the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting in vivo experiments. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Science, University of Tehran (Protocol No. E52#412-15). The study adhered to the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), and all efforts were made to minimize animal stress and suffering. Fish were monitored throughout the experimental period, and handling procedures were performed under standardized conditions to ensure welfare. The experimental design followed ARRIVE recommendations, including appropriate sample size determination and definition of the experimental unit (tank). No additional field collection permits were required, as all biological materials were obtained from established laboratory stocks under institutional authorization.

Results

Bacterial morphology, growth dynamics, and radiation inactivation

Microscopic examination confirmed that B. infantis cells were Gram-positive, non-motile, and non-sporulating, predominantly rod-shaped with occasional bifurcated forms (Fig. 1a). Cells exhibited smooth surfaces without visible flagella or pili, consistent with typical morphological characteristics of the species.

B. longum subsp. infantis characterization and response to gamma radiation. a) Micrograph displaying uniform, Gram-positive, rod-shaped to Y-shaped cells. b) Growth curve illustrating exponential growth and stationary phase after approximately 20 hours. c) Gamma radiation inactivation curve with a D₁₀ value of approximately 11.6 kGy

Growth curve analysis showed a typical bacterial growth pattern, with OD₆₀₀ increasing during the exponential phase and reaching a stationary phase after approximately 20 h (Fig. 1b). Gamma irradiation resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in culturability, and the D₁₀ value was estimated at approximately 11.6 kGy (Fig. 1c), indicating progressive loss of viability with increasing radiation dose.

Growth performance and body composition

Dietary supplementation with B-PRO and B-PAR significantly improved growth performance compared with the control group (Fig. 2a). Fish fed B-PRO showed the highest final weight, weight gain, SGR, and the lowest FCR (p < 0.05), while B-PAR also significantly improved all growth indices compared to control, but to a lesser extent than B-PRO.

Effects of dietary supplementation with B-PRO and B-PAR on growth performance, body composition, and hematological parameters in fish (n = 3 tanks; pooled fish samples analyzed per replicate). a) Growth metrics and proximate composition, including weight gain, specific growth rate (%SGR), feed conversion ratio (FCR), moisture, protein, fat, and ash content. b–e) Hematological indices: b) red blood cell (RBC) count, c) white blood cell (WBC) count, d) hematocrit (%), and (e) hemoglobin concentration. Body composition analysis showed slight but statistically significant increases in protein, lipid, and ash content, accompanied by reduced moisture content in both treated groups (p < 0.05), indicating modest improvements in nutrient deposition efficiency.

Hematological parameters

Both B-PRO and B-PAR significantly increased total erythrocyte and leukocyte counts compared to the control (Fig. 2b–e). The B-PRO group showed the highest erythrocyte count, while leukocyte elevation was observed in both treatments (p < 0.05). These changes suggest enhanced hematological status and immune responsiveness in supplemented fish.

Serum biochemistry and innate immunity

Dietary supplementation significantly affected hematological and immune parameters compared with the control group (Fig. 3). Neutrophil and monocyte percentages increased significantly in both B-PRO and B-PAR groups (p < 0.05), whereas lymphocyte percentages were lower than those observed in the control group. These findings indicate modulation of leukocyte profiles in response to dietary supplementation. Innate immune parameters, including serum lysozyme activity, serum complement C3 activity, and serum bactericidal activity, were significantly elevated in both treatment groups compared with the control (p < 0.05), indicating enhanced non-specific immune responses. Serum biochemical analysis showed significantly higher total protein, globulin, and glucose levels in the supplemented groups (p < 0.05), while serum cholesterol and bilirubin concentrations were reduced. Albumin levels did not differ significantly among groups, suggesting maintenance of overall protein homeostasis. Overall, B-PRO generally produced stronger hematological and biochemical responses than B-PAR under the experimental conditions tested.

Hepatic enzymes and antioxidant status

Serum ALT, AST, and ALP activities were significantly reduced in both treatment groups compared with the control (p < 0.05), indicating improved liver status (Fig. 4a–c). Oxidative stress biomarkers measured in tissue homogenates showed clear improvement. MDA levels decreased significantly, while total antioxidant capacity T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px activities increased in both B-PRO and B-PAR groups (Fig. 4d–g). B-PRO showed stronger effects on reduction of serum hepatic enzyme activities, whereas B-PAR slightly enhanced antioxidant capacity.

Cytokine gene expression analysis

In kidney tissue, the expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 were significantly downregulated in the B-PRO and B-PAR groups compared with the control group (p < 0.05), indicating reduced basal inflammatory signaling in this tissue under non-infected conditions. In contrast, spleen tissue showed reduced TNF-α and IL-6 expression but increased IL-8 expression, indicating a tissue-specific pattern of immune modulation rather than a uniform systemic anti-inflammatory response in both B-PRO and B-PAR groups. Since IL-8 functions as a chemokine involved in leukocyte recruitment and immune surveillance in teleost fish, its elevated splenic expression may reflect selective activation of immune cell trafficking and immunoregulatory processes (Fig. 5 a-e).

Protective efficacy and histopathological assessment following A. salmonicida challenge

Dietary supplementation with B-PRO and B-PAR significantly improved survival following A. salmonicida challenge compared with the control group. Survival in the control group was approximately 15%, whereas both supplemented groups showed survival rates close to 95% (p < 0.05), corresponding to a Relative Percent Survival (RPS) value of approximately 94% (Fig. 6A. a) These findings indicate a marked protective effect associated with both viable and gamma-irradiated B. infantis supplementation.

Fish supplemented with B-PRO and B-PAR showed significantly higher survival compared with the control group, corresponding to an approximate Relative Percent Survival (RPS) of 94%. Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3 tanks; pooled fish samples analyzed per replicate). Different letters above bars indicate statistically significant differences among groups (p < 0.05). Clinical signs in moribund control fish included darkened body coloration, lethargy, hemorrhagic lesions, and abdominal distension, whereas these signs were markedly reduced in B-PRO and B-PAR groups (Fig. 6A. b and c).

Histopathological evaluation revealed pronounced tissue alterations in control fish, characterized by severe hepatic necrosis, sinusoidal congestion, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration in the liver, together with marked tubular degeneration and structural disorganization in the kidney. In contrast, fish receiving B-PRO or B-PAR diets exhibited largely preserved tissue architecture, with only mild and focal lesions in hepatic and renal tissues. (Fig. 6 B and C).

Importantly, semi-quantitative lesion scoring analysis demonstrated significantly lower histopathological scores in both B-PRO and B-PAR groups compared with the control group (P < 0.05), confirming reduced tissue damage in supplemented fish. Overall, both treatments effectively mitigated infection-induced pathological alterations and significantly improved host resistance to A. salmonicida challenge.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with both live (B-PRO) and gamma-irradiated (B-PAR) B. infantis significantly improved growth performance, innate immune-related parameters, antioxidant capacity, and resistance to A. salmonicida in rainbow trout. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that probiotic supplementation enhances physiological performance and disease resistance in aquaculture species [11, 33].

Growth performance and physiological regulation

The improved growth performance and feed utilization observed in both experimental groups are in agreement with earlier studies reporting that probiotics enhance digestive efficiency, nutrient absorption, and intestinal health in fish [11, 33]. The similar growth responses induced by gamma-irradiated bacteria further suggest that microbial metabolic activity is not required for growth promotion. Instead, structural bacterial components may contribute to host physiological modulation through intestinal and immune–metabolic interactions, consistent with the emerging paraprobiotic concept [11].

Immune-related responses

Dietary supplementation significantly modulated innate immune parameters, including leukocyte counts, lysozyme activity, complement C3, and serum bactericidal activity. These indicators are widely accepted markers of non-specific immunity in teleost fish and are commonly enhanced by probiotic administration [11, 33].

Importantly, the absence of clear differences between B-PRO and B-PAR groups indicates that bacterial viability is not strictly required for the modulation of these immune-related parameters. This supports the hypothesis that structural components of bacterial cells, rather than metabolic activity, may play a central role in triggering host immune responses. Such findings are consistent with the emerging concept of paraprobiotics, where non-viable but structurally intact microbial cells retain biological functionality [9, 11].

Gene expression analysis revealed tissue-dependent regulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 in kidney and spleen. The non-uniform modulation of cytokines indicates that immune responses were not characterized by a simple pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory pattern, but rather reflect a more complex immunoregulatory adjustment associated with dietary intervention. However, since key signaling components such as Toll-like receptors and NF-κB were not directly assessed, mechanistic interpretation of immune signaling remains hypothetical and requires further validation.

Although microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) derived from probiotic and paraprobiotic bacteria are known to interact with host pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) and regulate innate immune signaling pathways in fish [11, 34]. The present study did not directly assess key signaling molecules such as Toll-like receptors or NF-κB. Therefore, any mechanistic interpretation involving MAMP–PRR signaling remains speculative and should be interpreted with caution.

Oxidative stress and antioxidant status

Both B-PRO and B-PAR improved physiological and biochemical status, as evidenced by increased total protein and globulin levels and reduced cholesterol and bilirubin concentrations. These changes indicate improved metabolic balance and reduced physiological stress in fish, consistent with previous probiotic studies in teleost [11, 35].

In addition, antioxidant defenses were significantly enhanced, as shown by increased T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px activities, along with reduced MDA levels. These results indicate improved redox homeostasis and reduced lipid peroxidation. The comparable antioxidant responses between live and gamma-irradiated bacteria further support the notion that microbial viability is not required for modulation of oxidative stress-related parameters [4, 16, 36].

Disease resistance and histopathological observations

The significantly higher survival rates observed following A. salmonicida challenge in both B-PRO and B-PAR groups (~95%) compared with the control group (~15%) resulted in a Relative Percent Survival (RPS) of approximately 94%, indicating strong protection under the present experimental conditions. Given the high virulence of A. salmonicida, these results are biologically and aquaculturally relevant [11, 34, 37]. Histopathological examination further supported these findings. Control fish exhibited severe hepatic necrosis, sinusoidal congestion, and renal tubular degeneration, which are consistent with systemic infection and secondary oxidative injury typically associated with furunculosis. In contrast, fish fed both B-PRO and B-PAR showed largely preserved liver and kidney architecture, with only mild and localized lesions, indicating a reduction in tissue-level damage.

The improved survival and histological protection observed in both treated groups likely result from enhanced host defensive capacity. However, this effect should be interpreted as multifactorial, potentially involving improved innate immune readiness, better physiological status, and increased antioxidant capacity rather than a single defined mechanism. Although probiotic and paraprobiotic supplementation has been associated with enhanced pathogen resistance in fish through modulation of innate immunity and mucosal barrier function and pathogen clearance [11, 34, 37]. The present study did not directly assess specific signaling pathways such as PRRs or downstream immune mediators.The comparable protective effects observed between B-PRO and B-PAR groups suggest that microbial viability is not essential for conferring resistance to infection under these experimental conditions. This supports the concept that structurally preserved bacterial components may contribute to host protection [4, 16, 36].

Mechanistic and applied implications

Overall, the present findings are consistent with a general model in which both probiotics and paraprobiotics may influence host physiology through interactions between microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) and host pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), leading to modulation of immune and metabolic responses. However, since key signaling components such as PRRs, Toll-like receptors, and downstream pathways were not directly measured in this study, this mechanistic interpretation remains hypothetical and should be validated in future investigations.

A key distinction between the two approaches is that, unlike live probiotics, paraprobiotics exert their effects without colonization or replication, relying instead on structurally preserved microbial components. This may explain the comparable biological effects observed between B-PRO and B-PAR under the present experimental conditions. From an applied perspective, this distinction is relevant for aquaculture practice. Paraprobiotics may offer advantages such as improved safety, greater storage stability, and reduced risk of environmental release or variability associated with live microbial cultures. These features make them poetical candidates for aquaculture applications where consistency and biosafety are important considerations [11].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with both live (B-PRO) and gamma-irradiated (B-PAR) B. longum subsp. infantis can improve growth performance, innate immune-related parameters, antioxidant status, and resistance to A. salmonicida in rainbow trout. The comparable effects observed between live and irradiated bacterial preparations suggest that bacterial viability is not essential for several of the measured physiological responses under the present experimental conditions. The observed changes in immune-related parameters, including humoral immune indices and cytokine expression profiles, indicate a complex immunoregulatory response rather than a uniform inflammatory activation pattern. However, as key signaling components such as pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), Toll-like receptors, and NF-κB were not directly assessed, mechanistic interpretations remain hypothetical. Overall, the results suggest that both probiotic and paraprobiotic forms of B. infantis may serve as functional feed additives with potential benefits for aquaculture health management. Nevertheless, further studies focusing on molecular signaling pathways are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms of action.
