Honey yield prediction and neonicotinoid risk assessment utilizing a machine learning framework in smart agriculture
Authors: Attia Ghafoor, Muhammad Majid, Hina Jahangir, Syed Ali Jafar Zaidi, Syed Rizwan Hassan, Waleed M. Ismael, Ateeq Ur Rehman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reduction in pollinator abundance (predominantly honeybees) stemming from environmental and chemical stressors notably neonicotinoid pesticides poses serious threats to biodiversity and agricultural productivity. This study presents a scalable machine learning framework to foresee honey yield and assess the impact of neonicotinoid exposure. Drawing upon a curated dataset, of 825 records encompassing 16 agro-environmental and chemical parameters including colony counts, yield metrics, and pesticide residue concentrations. Subsequently, we assessed 13 classification models, marked improvement was evident, as ensemble models consistently outperformed individual learners. Notably, our proposed voting classifier (AgriBuzzEnsemble), which synergistically fuses Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), surpassed all baseline models, showcasing robust accuracy of 98%, a Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) of 0.9751, a specificity of 0.9917, and an ROC AUC of 0.9985. Data preparation encompassed missing value imputation, outlier detection, feature scaling, and SMOTE based class balancing. For subsequent analysis, we employed Z-score filtering to detect and remove outliers, followed by a log1p transformation to mitigate skewness adhering to standard and well established preprocessing standards. Correlation analysis and statistical tests comprising McNemar’s Test, ANOVA, and Tukey’s HSD validated model reliability and confirmed the negative association between neonicotinoid burden and honey yield. Our proposed framework AgriBuzzEnsemble facilitates precision focused beekeeping by identifying yield risk zones and can be generalized to other regions facing pollinator stress, offering a robust and interpretable solution for sustainable agricultural planning.

Introduction

The global decline in honeybee populations has emerged as a critical ecological concern, with far reaching implications for biodiversity, agricultural sustainability, and global food security1. Approximately 87.5% of flowering plant species and nearly 35% of global food crops rely on insect mediated pollination2,3. Honey bees pollinate over two-thirds of 1,500 globally significant crops, underscoring their central role in food production and economic sustainability4. As essential pollinators, they contribute significantly to the reproduction of major crops and the maintenance of ecological balance5. However, a complex interplay of stressor including habitat loss, climate variability, disease, and pesticide exposure has jeopardized colony health and honey production across regions.

The economic significance of honey and pollination services extends far beyond the direct sale of honey, playing a pivotal role in agricultural productivity, rural livelihoods, and global food systems. Environmental risks and challenges in pollinator-safe honey yield prediction

The financial vulnerabilities faced by beekeepers and agricultural systems have been exacerbated by environmental stressors such as neonicotinoid pesticide exposure, which can reduce honey bee foraging efficiency and colony health. Studies show that even low levels of neonicotinoid exposure can impair pollen foraging at both individual and colony levels, with potential downstream effects on crop pollination services and honey yields9. Neonicotinoid residues in honey and bee products have also been detected in field studies, indicating that these compounds persist within agricultural ecosystems and may affect bee colony survival, foraging activity, and ultimately the economic returns from both pollination services and honey production10. The cumulative effect of elevated colony losses, whether from pesticides, parasites, or environmental change, showcases a significant economic risk to apiculture and crop production systems that rely on managed bees.

Among the most scrutinized contributors to pollinator decline are neonicotinoid pesticides11. These systemic agrochemicals, while effective in pest control, are associated with sublethal effects on honeybees, impairing foraging behavior, navigation, immunity, and ultimately reducing colony performance12–14. Understanding the extent to which these compounds affect honey yield remains a pressing scientific and regulatory priority. Conventional approaches for honey yield prediction predominantly employ statistical regression models and field-based observations, integrating historical yield records, climatic variables, and colony strength indicators. In parallel, neonicotinoid risk assessment has traditionally been performed using laboratory bioassays, residue analysis, and field toxicity studies, often supported by Hazard Quotient (HQ) and Risk Quotient (RQ) models that compare measured pesticide residues with established toxicity thresholds. More advanced classical frameworks further incorporate probabilistic risk assessment techniques to account for variability and uncertainty in honey and pollen exposure under realistic field conditions15–18.

While these conventional methods provide valuable insights, they are often limited in capturing the complex, nonlinear interactions among environmental, phenotypic, and chemical exposure variables that ultimately influence honey yield. This limitation motivates the exploration of more flexible, data-driven approaches, such as machine learning, to complement traditional assessments and enhance predictive accuracy.

Ensemble machine learning framework for honey yield forecasting

In parallel, the deployment of machine learning frameworks in agricultural forecasting has intensified19–21. However, its integration with environmental exposure data, particularly neonicotinoid pesticide concentrations for modeling honey yield, remains limited22. This study addresses this gap by combining neonicotinoid concentration data with phenotypic and regional yield features in a structured classification framework. While existing studies have investigated the effects of neonicotinoid residues on honeybee health and honey yield, few have systematically integrated pesticide concentration data with phenotypic and environmental features within a machine learning framework. This study builds on existing ecological and residue-based research by employing established machine learning methods on this integrated dataset, offering a structured and data-driven approach for honey yield forecasting under environmental stressors.

To mitigate the growing risks to pollinator health, we propose a robust ensemble machine learning framework23 to forecast honey yield and quantify the impact of neonicotinoid exposure. By leveraging advanced ensemble strategies such as stacking and voting24,25, the framework captures complex, nonlinear relationships among agro-environmental variables, enhancing predictive performance over conventional methods. Base learners such as GaussianNB and SVC were selected to balance high-dimensional chemical data with smaller phenotypic datasets, ensuring both stability and sensitivity across heterogeneous features. To enhance interpretability, the framework incorporates feature importance analysis, highlighting which neonicotinoids contribute most significantly to honey yield variation. Conceptually, the machine learning model functions as the core engine for risk assessment, transforming raw pesticide and environmental data into predictive insights for colony performance. For example, while developed using U.S. datasets, it can accommodate variables from other regions, such as European temperature fluctuations or tropical humidity, demonstrating scalability and adaptability to diverse ecological conditions. The framework thus provides actionable insights for policymakers and agricultural stakeholders while remaining flexible for global application.

Literature review

This section reviews literature relevant to honey yield forecasting and neonicotinoid risk assessment. Honey bee populations, essential for agricultural ecosystems, face threats from climate change, habitat loss, diseases, parasites, and pesticide exposure. Reliable prediction of honey yield and assessment of pesticide risk are critical for sustainable apiculture and food security. Prior studies have applied computational models, including Linear Regression, Random Forest, and neural networks, to predict honey yield and classify honey origin based on chemical or phenotypic traits23,28. While these studies demonstrate the potential of machine learning, they typically focus on single data sources and neglect integration of neonicotinoid exposure, colony health, and environmental variables, limiting practical applicability.

Precision apiculture systems (PAS) and multi-sensor monitoring have been shown to improve yield estimation and detect environmental stressors29. Factors such as floral diversity, colony traits, weather conditions, and management practices strongly influence honey yield30–33. Field studies also highlight the sublethal impacts of neonicotinoids, including reduced foraging efficiency, worker mortality, and impaired colony growth, with direct consequences for honey production34–36. In the broader agricultural context, ensemble and multi-layered models have enhanced crop yield forecasting, emphasizing the need for robust, multi-source integration37–39. Recent work by1 employed radar-based monitoring and machine learning classifiers to perform real-time bee counting, achieving 70% accuracy. While this study did not directly predict honey yield, hive entrance activity is a well established proxy for colony health and productivity, providing a valuable indirect indicator for yield estimation.

Sardar et al.15 analyzed 79 honey and 27 pollen samples from agricultural, mountain, and urban areas in South Korea to evaluate neonicotinoid exposure in pollinators. Using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry, all samples contained at least one target compound, with acetamiprid (honey: 82%, pollen: 96%), dinotefuran (honey: 58%, pollen: 96%), and flonicamid (honey: 52%) most frequently detected. Honey from agricultural areas exhibited the highest concentrations of flonicamid (0.33–190 ng/g), acetamiprid (0.04–152 ng/g), and imidacloprid (0.24–27.8 ng/g), while pollen showed varying patterns, with acetamiprid (0.2–260 ng/g) highest in mountainous areas and 5-hydroxy-imidacloprid (6.4–94.3 ng/g) highest in agricultural areas. Probabilistic risk assessment indicated minimal human risk except for potential exposure to thiacloprid in children, whereas risk quotients exceeded 1 for clothianidin and imidacloprid, indicating significant risks to bees. While demonstrating widespread contamination and spatial variability, the study primarily provides descriptive risk estimates and does not integrate these findings into predictive models for honey yield or pollinator health, highlighting the need for frameworks combining environmental exposure with productivity outcomes.

Yıldız et al.21 applied statistical and computational models, including Linear Regression, K-Nearest Neighbor, Random Forest, and Gaussian Naive Bayes, to predict Türkiye’s honey yield, with Linear Regression secured R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document} = 0.97. Meanwhile, these approaches lack an integrated framework combining yield prediction with neonicotinoid risk assessment and often omit key factors such as pesticide exposure, colony health, and economic indicators. The present study addresses these gaps by developing a comprehensive framework that incorporates environmental, biological, and management factors to elevate forecasting accuracy and support sustainable honey production.

Though the research40 was specifically focused on crop yield estimation, the incorporation of honeybee census data indicated a positive relationship between bee colony presence and enhanced yield with bees accounting for 52.34% of canola and 57.18% of soybean yield variation. Such a high pollination contribution suggests that desirable environmental conditions and bee activity not only favor crop productivity but most likely result in greater nectar availability and higher honey production. Therefore, by maximizing landscape conditions and minimizing pesticide risks to pollinators, honey production can be indirectly enhanced together with crop production.

A contemporary study41 analyzed the correlation between neonicotinoid honey residues and land use in the vicinity by testing multi floral honey samples from 30 Apis mellifera colonies in urban, agricultural, and semi-natural environments. By employing UHPLC-MS, the presence of imidacloprid, clothianidin, and thiacloprid residues was detected in 70% of the samples, of which imidacloprid was the most common (43%). Worthily, almost half of the samples had more than one neonicotinoid, and neonicotinoids were also prevalent in urban honey, suggesting that bee exposure is not restricted to agricultural fields. These observations underscore the necessity of considering non-agricultural pesticide application while evaluating pollinator risks and predicting honey yield.

A nationwide study42 in China utilized ic-ELISA and LC-MS/MS to screen neonicotinoid residues in 160 honey and 26 pollen samples and detected contamination in 59.4% of honey. Though human risk was low, Risk Quotients for clothianidin and imidacloprid were above safety levels for bees, which suggests acute toxicity. These results provide useful quantitative reference points for ecological modeling of neonicotinoid impacts, and support the focus of the present study to incorporate pesticide exposure into ensemble machine learning models for accurate honey yield prediction and pollinator risk assessment.

Another recent work33 explored the application of machine learning and thermal imaging to forecast honey yield (HY) and beeswax yield (BWY) in Apis mellifera. Their dataset included 13 colonies of six breeds in 10-frame Langstroth hives and contained numerical features extracted from thermal and color image processing like Tmin, Tmax, Tmean, red, green, saturation, brightness), colony power, and categorical features such as breed and queen’s birth year. They used XGBoost, CART, and Random Forest algorithms and evaluated model performance encompassed diverse parameters. Findings showcased that XGBoost and Classification and Regression Tree (CART) demonstrated highest prediction accuracy, with Colony Power (CP), thermal information, and color features emerging as primary predictors. The research also emphasized the promise of non-invasive, sensor-based methods in conjunction with ensemble learning to enable sustainable apiculture by predicting yields early.

Wu et al.16 analyzed 396 stamens and maternal flowers from cotton fields across China, revealing widespread neonicotinoid contamination, with acetamiprid, dinotefuran, and clothianidin most frequently detected. Hazard Quotient (HQ) assessment indicated high risk levels (median HQ > 1,000), although Risk Quotient (RQ) values remained below concern thresholds (median RQ < 0.4). While this study provides essential exposure data, it does not link neonicotinoid residues to honey yield or colony health. In contrast, the present study integrates environmental exposure data with honey yield prediction using a comprehensive machine learning framework, addressing this critical gap and enabling simultaneous assessment of productivity and pesticide risks in smart agriculture.

Honey is a strategic product for Turkey, yet natural honey yield has declined despite increasing hive numbers. Conventional forecasting incorporating the ARIMA model (1961–2019) projected an average annual yield increase of 0.82%, with higher gains possible if initiatives like the ’Honey Forest Action Plans’ (2018–2023) are fully implemented17. While impactful for trend analysis, ARIMA and other classical methods do not account for critical factors such as pesticide exposure, colony health, or environmental variability. This limitation underscores the need for integrated frameworks, motivating the present study to combine ecological, biological, and management variables for robust honey yield prediction and neonicotinoid risk assessment in smart agriculture.

Extensive novel research have explored non-invasive strategies for assessing honey quality, particularly sugar content, incorporating spectral analysis combined with statistical and machine learning techniques. For instance43, employed hyperspectral imaging, Savitzky–Golay filtering, and Principal Component Analysis with stacking models to predict 32 sugar concentration levels, classify honey into six sugar ranges, and distinguish 11 honey types alongside pure sugar. This highlights a gap in integrating non-invasive quality assessment with predictive modeling of honey production and agrochemical exposure, which the present study aims to address by combining machine learning frameworks for both yield prediction and neonicotinoid risk assessment in smart agriculture.

While effective in capturing management and socio-demographic influences, the study did not consider environmental stressors or pesticide exposure, highlighting the need for integrated frameworks that combine honey yield prediction with neonicotinoid risk assessment in smart agriculture.

Challenges remain in data generalization, biological variability, and pesticide impacts. Few studies provide an integrated framework for honey yield prediction coupled with neonicotinoid risk assessment. Research gap

While previous studies have explored honey yield prediction using classical machine learning approaches, they concentrate predominantly on binary classification or regression tasks, with scarce granularity in yield categorization as presented in Table 1. As well, extensive performance assessment incorporating class-wise metrics, specificity, MCC, ROC-AUC, and cross-validation are rarely addressed. Ensemble learning, particularly combinations of lightweight and interpretable models such as SVM and GaussianNB, has not been sufficiently explored or statistically validated using rigorous methods like t-tests, ANOVA, McNemar’s test, and Tukey’s HSD. Beyond this, essential probabilistic based parameters, encompassing Brier score and log-loss, which reflect model calibration, are often overlooked. Lastly, real-time deployment considerations, encompassing computational efficiency and model simplicity, are largely ignored. This study addresses these gaps by proposing a robust, interpretable, and computationally efficient multi-class categorizationn framework for honey yield prediction, supported by extensive evaluation and statistical validation.

Proposed methodology

Despite advancements in agricultural data analytics, challenges persist in accurately predicting honey yield due to the interplay of environmental, economic, and chemical factors. This study tackles these challenges by leveraging machine learning techniques trained on a comprehensive dataset that integrates agro-environmental attributes consisting of colony counts, yield per colony, pesticide residue levels as exemplified by (CLOTHIANIDIN, IMIDACLOPRID), production statistics, pricing, regional characteristics, and temporal data. The dataset features 825 entries with 16 features, including both categorical and numerical variables. A subset of pesticide related features contains missing values, which are handled through appropriate preprocessing strategies. The proposed AgriBuzzNet model is developed using this dataset after a thorough preprocessing phase comprising the treatment of missing values, outlier removal, duplicate elimination, categorical encoding, feature scaling, and class balancing. The dataset is then split into 80% training and 20% testing sets using stratified sampling to preserve class distribution. Phase 5: Feature encoding

Categorical features were encoded using multiple strategies to ensure compatibility and optimize performance across diverse machine learning models. Label Encoding was applied to the State and Region columns, assigning a unique integer to each category (e.g., State_Encoded_Label, Region_Encoded_Label), which is particularly suitable for tree-based algorithms such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting. Target Encoding was additionally employed for the State column, replacing each category with the mean target value (State_Encoded_Target) to capture category-target dependencies and enhance models sensitive to target correlations. To preserve categorical distinctions without implying ordinality, One-Hot Encoding was applied (e.g., State_AL, State_GA, Region_South), enabling compatibility with algorithms that require non-ordinal inputs, such as Logistic Regression, SVM, and Gaussian Naive Bayes. This multi-strategy encoding approach provides flexible, model-compatible representations while maintaining careful documentation to ensure clarity, reproducibility, and interpretability of results.

Phase 11: Proposed ensemble model AgriBuzzEnsemble

To enhance the predictive performance of honey yield classification, we developed AgriBuzzEnsemble, an ensemble machine learning model that combines the outputs of two distinct classifiers: Support Vector Classifier (SVC) and Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB). The model employs a soft voting strategy, where the final class prediction is derived by averaging the class probabilities predicted by each base learner. This hybrid design leverages the complementary characteristics of both models: GNB is fast, efficient, and performs well with smaller datasets, while SVC is effective in handling non-linear relationships and high-dimensional feature spaces.

Results

This section provides a detailed evaluation of machine learning models applied to the prediction of honey yield using a real-world dataset comprising production metrics, regional information, and pesticide usage. The models are compared based on key performance indicators such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Specificity, MCC and computational time, offering insights into their predictive effectiveness and generalization capabilities.

ML model performance comparison

The dataset comprised multiple environmental, production, and pesticide-related attributes, including numcol, yieldpercol, totalprod, stocks, priceperlb, prodvalue, and neonicotinoid residue variables. Table 7 summarize the performance of thirteen classifiers, encompassing conventional algorithms, tree based ensembles, and hybrid ensemble strategies. The models were evaluated by employing multiple metrics including training and test accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, specificity, MCC, computational time, ROC AUC curves, confusion matrices, and ten-fold stratified cross-validation with Supplementary findings into specificity, MCC, and computational time. The comparative analysis facilitate in-depth holistic comparison of the model’s predictive capabilities and robustness. All models demonstrated strong performance, with accuracy ranging from 96% to 98%. Quantifying neonicotinoid pesticide impact

While ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD were previously applied to evaluate model performance, here they were employed to examine the association between pesticide exposure levels and honey yield. To quantitatively assess the impact of neonicotinoid exposure, both individual and cumulative concentrations of five major compounds Clothianidin, Imidacloprid, Thiamethoxam, Acetamiprid, and Thiacloprid were analyzed. Correlation analysis indicated weak to moderately negative relationships between these chemicals and yield per colony, with Imidacloprid exhibiting the strongest inverse correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = -0.07$$\end{document}). To further assess group level differences, total neonicotinoid exposure (nAllNeonic) was categorized into three risk levels: low (<1 ppb), medium (1–5 ppb), and high (>5 ppb). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in mean yield across exposure groups (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F = 5.32$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.01$$\end{document}), indicating that higher pesticide burden is associated with reduced productivity. Tukey’s HSD test further showed that colonies in the high-exposure group exhibited significantly lower average yield compared with the low-exposure group (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document}). When incorporated into the machine learning framework, neonicotinoid-related variables contributed to predictive performance, as supported by correlation based analysis. These results suggest a consistent negative association between pesticide exposure and colony yield, supporting the inclusion of pesticide monitoring variables in quantitatively informed yield forecasting. Nonetheless, the findings should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal, given the observational nature of the dataset.

Discussion

Our results reveal clear performance gradients and practical trade offs among the thirteen evaluated classifiers. SVM, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), and Extra Trees (ET) emerged as the most effective single model baselines, consistently achieving near perfect classification of High Yield colonies, a pivotal requirement for decision support in precision apiculture. Their strong Matthews Correlation Coefficients (MCC \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document} 0.97) and specificities (> 0.99) confirm an exceptional ability to discriminate true positives from false positives, especially valuable for flagging Low Yield colonies that may be experiencing environmental stressors such as neonicotinoid exposure. Ensemble learning further enhanced predictive accuracy and robustness. Soft Voting and Stacking ensembles that fuse GNB and SVM delivered top tier metrics (accuracy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document} 98%, AUC = 1.00) while maintaining modest computational overhead, demonstrating that complementary inductive biases can be synergistically combined. Although Stacking (ET + CatBoost) achieved comparable accuracy, its substantially longer execution time > 5 seconds per inference renders it less suitable for real-time or edge deployments with latency and energy constraints.

From a broader implications perspective, the findings have several practical and scientific applications. The spatially explicit yield risk maps generated by AgriBuzzEnsemble can guide regulatory decisions by identifying regions where neonicotinoid exposure most significantly reduces honey yield, supporting evidence-based pesticide application policies. Although the dataset is U.S. centric, the model’s architecture is geographically agnostic and can be fine-tuned using region-specific data to accommodate variations in climate, floral composition, and pesticide use. Future extensions should incorporate additional biotic stressors, such as Varroa destructor and floral diversity metrics, to improve ecological validity. While the current approach provides annual forecasts, incorporating temporal models like recurrent or transformer-based ensembles could enable in-season predictions and early warning capabilities. By quantifying the marginal impact of individual neonicotinoids on yield, the model offers insights for policymakers and supports informed management strategies for beekeepers, including optimized hive placement and resource planning.

Limitations and future work

Despite promising predictive performance, this study has several limitations. The dataset is geographically limited to the United States and spans only a few seasons, restricting generalizability across diverse agro climatic conditions. The CNN-LSTM architecture achieved only 50% accuracy with multi-year sequences, suggesting that the model requires a larger and more diverse dataset to effectively capture temporal dependencies. Key ecological stressors, including Varroa destructor infestations, pathogen loads, diseases, and floral diversity metrics, were not included, which may contribute to unexplained yield variance. Additionally, reliance on laboratory-grade sensors may introduce variability in real-world deployments due to sensor noise and drift.

Future work will focus on expanding the dataset across regions and seasons, incorporating multi-modal data fusion of image, temporal, and ecological features. To enhance interpretability, explainable AI methods will be applied: LIME for local feature importance, Grad-CAM for image-based predictions, and Skater for global sensitivity analysis. Advanced feature selection and dimensionality reduction will also be explored to improve efficiency and robustness, supporting deployable, pollinator-safe precision apiculture strategies.

Conclusion

This study introduces AgriBuzzEnsemble, an innovative machine learning framework for precise honey yield prediction that synergistically integrates environmental, phenotypic, and chemical attributes, including neonicotinoid exposure. Among thirteen models evaluated, the ensemble based Voting Classifier (SVC + GaussianNB) demonstrated superior performance, achieving 98% accuracy, MCC of 0.9751, specificity of 0.9917, and a macro-averaged ROC AUC of 0.9985, underscoring its predictive robustness. Complementary analyses leveraging the Wrapper based (RFE) regression model and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) elucidated the most influential determinants of honey yield. Colony scale productivity metrics, totalprod and numcol, emerged as primary drivers, while economic variables (prodvalue, priceperlb) and neonicotinoid residues exerted secondary but notable effects.
