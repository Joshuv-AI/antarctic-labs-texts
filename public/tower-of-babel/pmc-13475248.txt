Ionic self-assembled supramolecular polymers: synthesis, properties and applications
Authors: Juanjuan Wang, Xin Li, Guoheng Xu, Hongdeng Qiu, Jia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ionic self-assembled supramolecular polymers (IS-SPs), emerging as a class of dynamically tunable functional materials, are generated via ionic self-assembly driven primarily by electrostatic interactions (primarily electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, metal coordination, etc.). Over the past two decades, IS-SPs have garnered substantial research interest owing to their exceptional properties and broad application prospects. This review provides a systematic and critical overview of the recent advances in IS-SPs, with a particular focus on the rational design of building blocks, from small organic molecules to polymers, and their profound impact on the resulting materials' properties. By adjusting the chemical structure of the monomers and reaction conditions, precise control over the morphology, size, and properties of the polymers can be achieved. Furthermore, we highlight their emerging applications in drug delivery and sensing. Finally, the development trends and key challenges of IS-SPs are discussed, including the design of smart materials and the development of new functional materials, to promote their wider application in future technologies.

Introduction

Supramolecular chemistry focuses on non-covalent interactions (electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals force) to study molecular recognition and form higher-order assemblies, playing an important role in the development of living systems, energy conversion, and functional materials.1–4 Over the past two decades, chemists have synthesized thousands of molecules that can be assembled into diverse supramolecular systems with specialized functions across structural levels, opening new avenues for creating novel substances and advanced materials.5–10 After years of rapid development, it has evolved into a broader supramolecular science through interdisciplinary integration with materials science, life science, information science, and nanotechnology.11–13 When applied to ionic polymers, it results in the formation of ionic supramolecular polymers (ISPs).14,15 Such materials utilize electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, metal coordination among ions, and other non-covalent interactions in a well-designed manner. It enables the ordered arrangement and self-assembly of polymer chain segments, ultimately forming unique supramolecular structures.

Ionic self-assembly (ISA) is an important branch of supramolecular chemistry. It primarily relies on electrostatic interactions to bring opposite charge building units together, forming an ionic self-assembled supramolecular polymer (IS-SP). Subsequently, additional non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic action, π–π stacking, further facilitate the assembly to ordered aggregates (Fig. 1).6,16 Specifically, Fig. 1A illustrates the ionic self-assembly of two oppositely charged small molecules—typically multi-carboxylic acids and multi-amine/imidazolium salts—driven by electrostatic recognition (often reinforced by charge-assisted hydrogen bonding), these systems commonly yield ionic networks with well-defined supramolecular topologies. Fig. 1B and C depict hybrid supramolecular systems in which one component is a macromolecule. In Fig. 1B, an anionic polymer (such as poly(acrylic acid) or poly(styrenesulfonate)) interacts with cationic small molecules, such as phosphonium or imidazolium compounds. In Fig. 1C, a cationic polymer (e.g., a dendrimer or poly(vinylbenzyltrimethylammonium)) complexes with anionic small molecules (dicarboxylates or sulfonated dyes), enabling hierarchical assemblies. Fig. 1D and E depict two classes of polymer–polymer assemblies: Fig. 1D shows the classical polyelectrolyte complex formed from linear anionic and linear cationic polymers, while Fig. 1E extends to higher-order architectures involving branched/dendritic polymers or networks featuring multiple ionic junctions. The synthesis strategies and structure–property relationships for each category are systematically discussed in the following sections, with representative examples provided throughout the design and synthesis of IS-SPs. Conventional supramolecular polymers are mainly governed by specific directional motifs (e.g., hydrogen bonding, host–guest recognition, metal–ligand coordination, or π–π interactions). In contrast, IS-SPs use electrostatic complexation as the primary assembly trigger and therefore exhibit pronounced ion-related tunability.

Their structures and properties can be readily regulated by charge density, counterion identity, ionic strength, and solvent polarity, providing a versatile handle to control association strength, morphology, and responsiveness.17 As an important method for preparing ISPs, ISA can select appropriate functional units according to specific application requirements, thereby yielding ionic complexes with desired properties. Moreover, this approach enables the integration of multiple functionalities, facilitating the design and synthesis of diverse multifunctional composite materials.18,19 Additionally, the self-assembly process is strongly affected by the geometric configuration, functional characteristics and cohesive energy distribution of self-assembled blocks.20–23 Consequently, ISA has emerged as an effective approach for developing nanostructured and composite functional materials.24

The properties of IS-SPs are governed not only by the assembly principles but also significantly influenced by the molecular structure (monomer designs, the length and architecture of polymer chains, the types and distribution of functional groups), environmental factors (temperature, pH, and solvent) and molecular weight and its polydispersity.25–31 The comprehensive understanding and regulation of these factors are crucial for comprehending and precisely controlling the properties of IS-SPs. Molecular design is the primary approach for tuning IS-SPs. By engineering monomer structures, introducing targeted functional groups, and fine-tuning molecular weight and its distribution, we can precisely modulate the self-assembly behavior and polymers' performance, thereby enabling broader applications.32–34

This article focuses on reviewing the latest research progress and developmental directions of IS-SPs, including their synthesis strategies, properties, applications, and future development and challenges, aiming to provide valuable insights and references for scientists and engineers working in related fields.

Design and synthesis of IS-SPs

In the construction of IS-SPs, the selection of appropriate functional building blocks is crucial for achieving successful assembly.35 Ionic bonds are typically formed through the interaction of organic acid anions with organic base cations, resulting in the formation of ionic polymers. Reported cation-forming groups commonly include imidazole, pyridine, organophosphorus compounds, and amines whereas anions typically consist of carboxylic acids and sulfonic acids. During self-assembly, electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged species drive the organization of these building blocks, thereby facilitating the formation of ordered supramolecular aggregates.36

For particular application scenarios of IS-SPs, monomers are carefully selected for the construction of IS-SPs. Structural characteristics of building blocks, such as chain length, substituent size, and molecular rigidity, strongly influence the resulting IS-SPs. For example, introducing a long-chain alkyl substituent into a cationic monomer increases its hydrophobicity. Upon polymerization, this enhanced hydrophobicity affects the solubility characteristics of the resulting polymer, reducing its solubility in polar solvents and further enhancing its solubility in non-polar solvents. Simultaneously, the long-chain alkyl groups introduce considerable steric hindrance, which disrupts the orderly arrangement of polymer chains, reduces crystallinity, and weakens electrostatic interactions. Consequently, this improvement enhances the material's flexibility and improves its tensile properties. In contrast, the rigidity of monomers also significantly influences on the ionic polymer performance. Rigid structural monomers, for instance, containing benzene rings or conjugated double bonds, impart stiffness to the polymer chains. This rigidity restricts the free rotation and movement of the polymer chains, increasing the glass transition temperature and mechanical strength of the polymer. Therefore, by rationally designing and selecting synthetic building blocks according to specific functional requirements, IS-SPs with tailored properties can be constructed.

Small molecules as building blocks

In equilibrium, well-defined structures are constructed from charged small organic molecules via ISA, and relying on mainly non-covalent bonds. The resulting assemblies possess specific functions and exhibit excellent mechanical properties and processability.37 The advantages of these small building blocks depend on their structural flexibility and ease of synthesis. By adjusting the structure, functional groups, and concentration, the properties of the final material can be precisely controlled. This method is commonly employed to synthesize supramolecular networks with targeted functionalities, such as photoelectric properties, catalytic activity, or gas adsorption behavior. Recently, our groups have developed a range of ionic organic crystals through the self-assembly of small molecules bearing multidentate cationic and anionic groups. The anionic building blocks employed include multidentate organic sulfonic acids, carboxylic acids, and phosphoric acids; the cationic components consist of multidentate organic pyridines, imidazoles, ammonium and phosphates.38–48 In this section, we will mainly discuss IS-SPs based on small molecules.

Carboxylic acids as anion building blocks

The carboxy group is an excellent hydrogen bond donor and acceptor, and has been widely utilized in the synthesis of multifunctional materials.49 Hosseini et al. constructed structures based on ion–pair interactions and hydrogen bonds using ISA (Fig. 2A). They employed a divalent anion derived from isophthalic acid or terephthalic acid as a building block. This anion was combined with a cycloamidinyl unit to construct a linear polymer. The polymer consists of alternating dicationic and dianionic pairs, stabilized by hydrogen bonds in the aqueous phase.50 The linear structure was characterized by X-ray diffraction, which confirmed a single crystal structure was obtained. The same group later reported dianionic and dicationic linear polymers prepared from cycloamidine and homophenyltetracarboxylic acid, as well as crystalline two-dimensional (2D) molecular networks obtained through the self-assembly.51

Synthetic route to synthesize IS-SPs based on (A) divalent anion and cycloamidinyl unit; (B) renewable monomers; (C) citric acid and dicationic ionic liquids.

In recent years, commercially available citric acid and primary or tertiary amines were utilized to self-assemble into protic IS-SPs (Fig. 2B).52 In a typical synthesis, acids and amines were mixed in equimolar amounts in methanol and stirred at room temperature, leading to rapid reactions. Upon completion, the solvent was removed and a series of ionic supramolecular products was obtained. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed that proton transfer occurred during the process. More importantly, this IS-SP displays remarkable conductivity and self-healing capabilities. To further probe the role of hydrogen bonding, a controlled experiment was performed. Glycerol (a triol) was reacted with a diamine molecule (specifically, 1,3-propylenediamine) in a 1 : 1 molar ratio, replacing citric acid. The reaction yielded a low-viscosity liquid, indicating that the effect of hydrogen bonding is relatively minor compared to ionic interactions.53 In contrast, a straightforward strategy was proposed through using commercially accessible di- or tri-functional amines and carboxylic acids to synthesize supramolecular ionic networks.54 In this paper, a bio-based fatty diamine molecule (priamine 1074) and naturally occurring carboxylic acids such as malonic acid, citric acid, tartaric acid, and 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid were employed as building blocks to construct novel supramolecular ionic networks through proton transfer reaction. A similar approach was also applied to 1-(2-aminoethyl)piperazine (AEPZ) and citric acid.55 It is the same reaction used to construct protic ionic liquids, also known as an acid-based complexation.56

In addition, the same synthetic strategy was extended to fabricate IS-SPs entirely from citric acid and geminal dicationic ionic liquids, such as di-imidazolium, di-pyridinium, or di-pyrrolidonium (Fig. 2C).57 In this case, the networks were synthesized through ionic bonding between multi-carboxylate and cationic molecules. This mechanism differs from the previously reported proton transfer approaches in several key aspects. The resulting ionic networks exist as soft solids at room temperature, but undergo a thermoreversible transition and transform into viscoelastic liquids upon heating. Notably, these materials also maintain the inherent chemical features of carboxylic acid building blocks. As a potential application, this synthetic strategy enables the development of self-healing materials using fully renewable chemicals, these materials also exhibit enhanced ionic conductivity properties.

The forming ionic bonding strategy has provided a wide range of supramolecular structures with rich potential applications. Among these materials, certain ionic networks are particularly fascinating because their properties and morphologies can be significantly different from those of the individual starting components.58 By 2008, Grinstaff and coworkers utilized phosphonium dication (P2+) as a multicationic component and ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA4−) as multianionic components to prepare IS-SP at 140 °C. This study demonstrated a unique, practical, and complementary method to form ionic networks from entirely non-coordinating ionic pairs, especially those derived from ionic liquids chemistry. Moreover, the authors employed another carboxylic acid para-tetracarboxy-5,10,15,20-tetraphenyl-21H,23H-porphine (H2TPP4−) instead of EDTA under the same conditions to evaluate the universality of this strategy (Fig. 3A). The results showed that porphyrin-based ionic network retained its optical properties, confirming the feasibility of incorporating functional molecules into the network (Fig. 3B).59 This demonstrates that acids with the potential to form anions can combine with higher viscosity, whereas H2TPP in the absence of carboxylic acids does not yield ionic materials. In 2009, a comment highlighted the practical significance of this ionic network construction strategy.60 Building on the above experimental results, the research group subsequently constructed a crosslinked ionic network based on reversible disulfide bonds and ionic bonds by utilizing a disulfide diphosphonium ionic liquid and an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. Interestingly, the crosslinked network structure could be disrupted by external stimuli, such as temperature changes or mild redox reactions, indicating reversible assembly of the ionic network.61

In contrast to the previously reported viscoelastic IS-SPs, solid IS-SPs constructed from two oppositely charged building blocks exhibit greater potential for practical applications. In 2020, Yu et al. reported a synthesis strategy using an anionic naphthalenediimide derivative appended with a rigid carboxylate (ANDI), which underwent ISA with cationic surfactants CnTAB to afford ionic materials with diverse morphologies (Fig. 3C).62 The morphology was tuned by changing the concentration of the building blocks and the chain length of the surfactants. The assemblies included sphere, rod, sheets, and helix. This work demonstrates the critical role of molecular architectures and environmental conditions in constructing diverse supramolecular assemblies. In this work, the ionic interactions represent only one of several interaction types in the naphthalenediimide-based system, indicating that electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking all play an essential role in the fabrication of supramolecular architectures.63 Similarly, another notable study presented a facile strategy for constructing large-area ultrathin 2D supramolecular nanosheets with nanometers-scale thickness.64 This approach employed deprotonated tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin (TCPP) and protonated bis(2-dimethylaminoethyl)ether (BDMAEE) to assemble via electrostatic interactions in aqueous solution (Fig. 4). In this 2D supramolecular nanosheet, one deprotonated TCPP molecule is connected to four protonated BDMAEE molecules via electrostatic interaction, giving rise to a well-defined 2D layered architecture. Moreover, these nanosheets exhibited large area and excellent dispersibility, offering promising candidates for catalytic and sensing applications.

Schematic illustration of the assembly process of 2D TCPP-BDMAEE via electrostatic interaction of deprotonated TCPP and protonated BDMAEE in water. A photo of 2D TCPP-BDMAEE dispersion in water (1 mg mL−1) is also shown.

The above-mentioned synthesis strategies serve as the foundation for synthesizing IS-SPs. In fact, the above supramolecular ionic nanomaterials formed from carboxylate building blocks are typically synthesized via a straightforward ISA in highly polar solvents such as water, alcohol, and acetone. This approach not only facilitates polymer formation but also enables the generation of a wide variety of porous materials. As a result, ionic materials derived from carboxylic acids constitute a promising class of porous materials with broad application potential. Simultaneously, their studies demonstrated that IS-SPs could be categorized into two distinct types: viscous liquids and amorphous solids. Furthermore, when highly rigid and regular dicarboxylates they were used as building blocks, the resulting materials exhibited semi-crystalline solid polymer characteristics.

Organosulfonic acids as anion building blocks

Organic sulfonic acids, as strong acids that fully dissociate in water to form sulfonate anions, have also been favored for the fabrication of ionic materials via ion exchange. In 2004, Shelnutt et al. reported the preparation of ionic nanotubes using porphyrin-based cations and anions via ISA.65 This study demonstrated that it was possible to construct a robust ionic nanotube of two oppositely charged ionic building blocks in solution.

These porphyrin nanotubes represent a new class of porphyrin-based nanostructures, in which tectons can be utilized to control both the structural and functional properties of the nanotubes. The obtained ionic porphyrin nanotubes exhibit uniform size and morphology, with well-defined hollow structures (Fig. 5A), as well as photocatalytic properties, mechanical responsiveness, and light adaptability. Their unique electronic and optical characteristics were suggested to be analogous to those of chlorosomal rods. In 2010, they prepared microscale four-leaf clover-shaped structures formed from anionic and cationic porphyrins. The electron-donating or electron-accepting behavior of porphyrin cores is determined by the nature of the metal ion complexed within their macrocycle (Zn or Sn) (Fig. 5B).66 In this research, the effects of salt concentration and temperature on the morphology of the assembled materials were systematically investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). At the highest salt concentration (20 mM), a distinct smooth four-pointed star-shaped morphology was observed. The dependence of morphology on salt concentration may potentially be attributed to variations in diffusion-limited growth rates. Temperature exerted a more pronounced influence than that of ionic strength, as different growth temperatures produced distinct morphologies. Additionally, varying the growth temperature is expected to alter the solubility of the ionic solid, thereby influencing the diffusion-limited growth rates. Microclovers with similar morphologies could be obtained by switching the metals ions in the two porphyrins structures. This finding highlight that porphyrin–porphyrin electrostatic interactions dominate the self-assembly process, while metal ions induced intermolecular forces play a secondary role. Additionally, 8-hydroxypyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid trisodium salt and viologen have been employed as host–guest pairs to construct supramolecular ionic polymer.67

Subsequently, Song et al. reported a novel synthetic approach for fabricating flower-like structures with unprecedented three-dimensional (3D) entangled chains of porphyrin dimers. This method integrated the ISA of FeIIImeso-tetra(N-methyl-4-pyridyl)porphyrin (FeIIINMePyP) with the negatively charged FeIIImeso-tetra(4-sulfonatophenyl)porphyrin (FeIIITPPS4) generated complex supramolecular architectures (Fig. 5C). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggested oppositely charged porphyrins alternatively assemble into J-aggregates, forming twisted molecular chains that further interweave with others chains to construct flower-like nanospheres with an average diameter of 338 ± 77 nm. Interestingly, after heat treatment and acid leaching, the overall flower-like morphology was well preserved with the average diameter shrinking to 301 ± 62 nm; the UV-visible spectra of FeIIINMePyP/FeIIITPPS4-RT, FeIIINMePyP, and FeIIITPPS4 compared with the individual building blocks exhibited a red shift in the Soret band, indicating strong intermolecular interactions and J-aggregation between FeIIINMePyP and FeIIITPPS4, Surprisingly, after pyrolysis at 775 °C and acid leaching, Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra show the characteristic peaks are still present. Moreover, the flower-like morphology of ionic self-assembled porphyrins is well maintained merely with some overall shrinkage.68

These results confirm that the obtained IS-SPs can well survive high temperature and acidic conditions effectively. The perylenediimide and a copper–phthalocyanine derivative were used building blocks for the construction of ionic supramolecular polymer (Fig. 5D). In this work, Coulomb coupling, as a crucial strong interaction, stabilizes the whole structure, and a combination of charge transfer interactions and discotic stacking promote the exclusive formation of one-dimensional polymeric chains. Meanwhile, it has high molecular weight and high mechanical stability. TEM data showed a regular, helical structure was preparation.24

To investigate water-stable assemblies with adaptive encapsulation, Yu and co-workers reported a new class of water-stable, adaptive, and electroactive supramolecular ionic materials (SIMs). These materials are primarily stabilized by electrostatic interactions and exhibit a hexagonal microstructure in aqueous solution. This material was constructed via ISA using imidazolium-based dication and dianionic dye (i.e., 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid), ABTS). The size of the SIM ranges from several to tens of micrometers with the thickness varying from hundreds of nanometers to several micrometers, suggesting the ionic material exhibits a nearly two-dimensional (2D) microstructure (Fig. 6A).69 Recently, our group have reported two supramolecular ionic nanomaterials (SINs) constructed through ISA of multidentate imidazolium organic salts and multidentate acid. These materials are formed by tetraphenylethylene (TPE)-based organic salts tetraphenylethylene tetrakis(3-methylimidazolium) tetrabromide (TPE-COS) and tetraphenylethylene tetrakis(1-hexadecyl-3-imidazolium) tetrabromide (TPE-C16OS) and 4,4′,4″,4‴-(ethene-1,1,2,2-tetrayl)-tetrabenzenesulfonic acid (TPE-SO3H) in MeOH, these SINs exhibited high thermal stability and are insoluble in solvents (Fig. 6B).70

A distinct, practical, and complementary strategy has been employed for the construction of ionic supramolecular polymer using two oppositely charged porphyrin derivatives with different metal centers. Coulomb coupling, as a crucial strong interaction, stabilizes the whole structure, while a synergistic combination of charge transfer interactions and discotic stacking promotes the exclusive formation of one-dimensional polymeric chains. Meanwhile, it possesses a high molecular weight and excellent mechanical stability. TEM data further revealed that a regular, helical structure was prepared.24 Additionally, low molecular chiral tectons can be transferred into supramolecular assemblies through ionic interactions in both solution and solid state, indicating that chiral building blocks have an effect on determining the structure and properties of supramolecular ionic materials.17,71

Small molecules and polymers as building blocks

As the term suggests, polymers and small molecules bearing opposite charges can interact through self-assembly to form IS-SPs with excellent functional properties. Many of these materials exhibit excellent physical and chemical properties, making them highly promising for various applications.72 Furthermore, it is often possible to adjust the properties of these combinations by selection of suitable small molecules, such as surfactants, lipids, or dyes, thereby enabling the design of specific structures and functions.

Polymers as anion building blocks

Polyelectrolyte-surfactant complexes, consisting of highly charged polymers, represent one of the earliest investigated classes of IS-SPs.73,74 These ionic complexes combine small molecules and polymers with hydrocarbon chains typically ranging from 8–18 carbons in length, as a result, they possess both polymeric properties, such as mechanical strength and viscoelasticity, and surfactants-like characteristics (formation of highly hierarchical mesophases).75 The incorporation of small molecules, such as lipids, peptides, dyes, or surfactants, into charged surfactants via ISA can further endow specific ionic polymers with superior functionality. This study was one of the pioneering works to investigate the interaction between polymers and small molecules, and it represents a common approach to forming IS-SPs.76 In these systems, the ionic interaction between ion pairs, as well as the hydrophobic interaction among the hydrocarbon tails of the surfactant, bring about thermodynamically stable, ordered phases or aggregates, the nature of which depends on the preparation strategy employed.77 In one pioneering study, Antonietti and coworkers reported the formation of an ionic complex using poly(styrenesulfonate) and alkyltrimethy-lammonium cations with different alkyl tails through self-assembly in water.78 Similarly, ordered structures were obtained by combining poly(alkylacrylamide) or poly(acrylic acid) and surfactants.79,80

Based on these results, Grinstaff and coworkers synthesized and characterized a new type of IS-SPs, and systematically investigated its rheological properties. The supramolecular polymeric assembly was formed between a dicationic phosphonium ionic liquid and the carboxylate residues of poly(acrylic acid) (Fig. 7A).81,82 When the dicationic phosphonium was replaced with a monocationic phosphonium or when the polyacrylate was replaced with poly(vinyl alcohol), the network was lost, this behavior is characteristic of a typical anionic polymer in which the negative charges are distributed along the side chains. In this case, small cationic molecules, particularly bisphosphonic cations, can effectively bridge two anions, leading to the formation of the ionic network (Fig. 7B). In addition, they prepared a new type of polyelectrolyte, which formed self-assembled supramolecular architectures using a polymerized phosphonium ionic liquid, and mixed this polymer mixed with a variety of carboxylic acid-containing surfactants to form ordered structures. A cationic polymer is used, in which the cationic groups are positioned along the side chains of the covalent polymer backbone and consist of single cations (Fig. 7C). The anions originate from monocarboxylic acids; as a result, the main chain of the copolymer maintains a covalent structure, whereas the side chains contain carboxylic acid functionalities formed through ionic bonding. Depending on the number of ionic interactions and the type of anionic surfactant or anionic crosslinker used, the resulting structures displayed dramatically different properties, ranging from brittle solids, to rubbery balls that bounce, to sticky fibers. Zwitterions, as unique small molecules with an equal number of opposite charges within the same structure, are ideal candidates for the preparation of ISA.83 Wu group selected five representative zwitterions (dimethylglycine, l-proline, sarcosine, and trimethylamine oxide (TMAO)) to synthesize a series of proton-conductive supramolecular zwitterionic reorganizable network with acrylic acid (AA) (Fig. 7D).84 The synthesized polymers exhibit highly stretchability and transparency. Especially, all the acid dissociation constants (pKa) of zwitterions are lower than those of polyacrylic acid (PAA) in their bulk aqueous solutions, indicating that zwitterions did not significantly promote deprotonation of the PAA carboxylic acid groups. Consequently, three main types of interactions exist in this system, including COOH dimeric H-bonds, polymer–zwitterion complex, and zwitterion–zwitterion complex.

In another study, Hvilsted introduced a facile route to create macroscopic ionic networks by employing dendritic macromolecules.85 The IS-SPs were synthesized through the simple mixing of stoichiometric quantities of carboxylic acid telechelic poly(ethylene glycol)s (DiCOOH-PEG) with varying molecular weights (Mn ∼250, Mn ∼600, and Mn ∼4800) and poly(propylene imine) (PPI) dendrimers or simpler amines, such as tris(2-aminoethyl)amine (TAEA) and hexamethylene diamine (HMDA) (Fig. 8A). The 1H NMR analysis results revealed that only the four primary amines of the poly(propylene imine) dendrimer participate in the formation of the ionic network. The class of IS-SPs can also be templated by cations, such as pyridinium cations on the side chains of covalent polymers or other heteroatom-containing polymers; these polymers form branched ionic networks through ionic interactions with small anionic organic acids, including sulfonic and carboxylic acids.86,87 Similar structures can be formed by cross-linking linear polyvinylmine (l-PEI) with sulfonic acid ions.88 Additionally, polystyrene sulfonate (PSS) and PAA are utilized to synthesize analogous ionic polymers containing quaternary ammonium salts.89,90

Up to now, polymers used as anion donor to synthesize IS-SPs mainly focus on carboxylic acid groups. Carboxylic acid groups (pKa ≈ 4–5) are capable of forming stable coordination bonds with cations through deprotonation under neutral or mildly alkaline conditions. However, systematic studies have been reported on sulfonic acid groups with stronger acidity (pKa ≈ 1–2). The absence may be attributed to several challenges:

(1) The strong acidity of the sulfonic acid makes it difficult to maintain a stable deprotonated state under conventional reaction conditions; (2) the three-dimensional spatial configuration of the sulfonic acid group may introduce the spatial steric hindrance effect at the intermolecular interaction sites; (3) the highly hydrophilic sulfonic acid groups may weaken the structural stability of the assemblies. Furthermore, comparative studies on the co-assembly behavior of carboxylic acid/sulfonic acid bifunctional polymers may provide new ideas for the development of multifunctional ionic materials.

Polymers as cation building blocks

Inspired by naturally occurring materials with exceptional properties, Gröhn et al. developed a straightforward strategy to prepare stable and functional IS-SPs through electrostatic self-assembly involving cationic dendrimers and oppositely charged small divalent organic ions. Using poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimer ions and the divalent 1,4- or 2,3-naphthalene dicarboxylic acid, they successfully synthesized nanoparticles with spherical, rod-like, and hollow spheres through ISA and aromatic molecule stacking in methanolic solution. Their sizes are in the range of several 10 to 100 nm; the stable ionic structures formed from 2,3-NDC and G5 are spherical aggregates with a diameter of 105 nm, whereas the 1,4-NDC-G5-aggregate exhibits a homogeneous cylindrical shape (Fig. 8B and C).91,92

Polymers and polymers as building blocks

Oppositely charged polymers can be employed to generate different stacking of assembled architectures (Fig. 9A).93 Miekka et al. reported the formation of an ultrathin, water-insoluble film composed of sodium poly(styrenesulfonate) and poly(vinylbenzyltrimethylammonium chloride) in dilute aqueous solution. As a product of the reaction, the film contained equivalent quantities of each polyelectrolyte.94 The formation of the IS-SPs depends on several factors, including polyion concentration, polymers molecular weight, pH, and the ionic strength. Depending on these factors, the complexes may remain water-soluble or precipitate as a result of charge neutralization. The pioneering work was fundamentally aimed at enhancing the miscibility of polymer blocks in blends via the transfer of protons from acidic to basic sites existing in the different polymers. As is well-known, block copolymers are of great importance for their ability to assemble into nanostructures with well-defined geometries and long-range order. In these structures, the constituent block length, chemical composition and ionic interactions collectively dictate the final morphology. The materials formed through such interactions are referred to as ionic copolymers.95,96 Based on the above facts, proton transfer has led to the development of a range of further supramolecular ionic copolymers. In these studies, polymers with COOH or SO3H end groups, as well as amine-functionalized polymers, were investigated.97–99 This approach to forming IS-SPs follows a typical Brønsted-type acid–base reaction.

Regarding ionic interactions, polymers bearing carboxylic end groups, as well as those functionalized with amine or poly(triethyl(4-vinylbenzyl)phosphonium chloride) end groups, were used as building blocks to prepare supramolecular copolymers.100–102 Polyelectrolyte complexes were prepared by reacting the sodium salt of PAA with poly(allylamine hydrochloride) (PAH) (Fig. 9B).103 Additionally, PAA and poly(triethyl(4-vinylbenzyl)phosphonium chloride) (P-Et-P) are used as anion building blocks to synthesize self-healing, ionically cross-linked networks. Three different ratios of PAA/P-Et-P were incorporated into the polymers by varying the addition orders of the components. The NaCl concentration was found to play a significant role influencing the preparation of ionically cross-linked networks. With all networks maintaining structural integrity in 0.1 M NaCl solution (Fig. 9C).104 Multifunctional ionic self-assembles with intelligent self-healing and shape memory capabilities were further demonstrated via the integrating of covalent and non-covalent cross-links (Fig. 9D).105 The functionalized polybutadiene acid (PB-COOH) and polybutadiene amine PB-NH2 were mixed in an organic solvent to prepare an IS-SP with ionic hydrogen bonding. Subsequently, a small amount of the covalent cross-linker, trifunctional thiol (TMPT) was added. The solution was subsequently poured into a Teflon mold, and UV irradiation initiated the thiol–ene reaction, yielding a hybrid supramolecular network with both non-covalent and covalent cross-linking interactions.

Compared to polymers with carboxylic end groups, those with sulfonic end groups exhibit higher acidity. When combined with sulfonic acid end-functionalized polymers, amine end groups-modified polymers can form ionically IS-SP.96,106–108 For this purpose, α,ω-sulfonated polystyrene (HO3S-PS-SO3H) and α,ω-amino-polyisobutylene (H2N-PIB-NH2) were investigated by Weiss and co-workers.109 Supramolecular multiblock polystyrene–polyisobutylene copolymers were prepared by pairing HO3S-PS-SO3H and H2N-PIB-NH2, where the protonated amine groups established ionic bonds with the sulfonate groups via proton transfer; these copolymers further enable the formation of free-standing flexible films with a modulus of 90 MPa (Fig. 9E). In addition, a series of block copolymers were successfully synthesized through ionic interaction by simply blending polymers functionalized with amine end groups and those with sulfonic end groups.110–113

Properties of IS-SPs

IS-SPs, as a unique class of supramolecular materials formed via ionic interaction, have garnered significant attention from researchers. These materials facilitate the design of predictable polymeric ionic structures and the development of desirable properties. The reversibility and low energy nature of ionic bonds endows these polymers unique properties, including mechanical strength, viscosity, flow behavior and self-healing capabilities. In this part, we will thoroughly examine the properties of IS-SPs by investigating their thermal properties, electrical characteristics, rheological behavior, and self-healing abilities in detail.

Electronic properties

IS-SPs exhibit excellent electrochemical properties due to their unique structural and charge transport characteristics.64,68,69,114 So, we will focus on the electrical properties of IS-SPs. For instance, phthalocyanine compounds, characterized by their macrocyclic structures, possess rich electronic configurations and superior electrochemical activities, enabling their outstanding performance in this field. When phthalocyanine is used as a synthetic building block, its intrinsic electrochemical properties can impart these characteristics to the resulting ionic polymer. Modifying the substituents on the building block can influence the nature of ionic interactions, thereby modulating the electrochemical properties of the polymer after aggregation and assembly.115 The ionic conductivity of IS-SPs is influenced by the structural variations of building blocks.

Specifically, as the chain length between cationic imidazole groups increases, the decrease in conductivity becomes more pronounced (Fig. 10A–C). Furthermore, polymers containing aromatic anions display electrical conductivity approximately three orders of magnitude higher than those containing aliphatic anions. To investigate the relationship between temperature and conductivity, crystalline iNet-1 and amorphous iNet-3 were selected as model systems. Below 70 °C, the iNet-1 exhibited a linear increase in conductivity from 1.3 × 10−10 to 4.4 × 10−9 S cm−1, as measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS).116 However, as the temperature approaches the melting point, the conductivity exhibits a sharp increase, reaching nearly three orders of magnitude higher. By contrast, iNet-3 maintains consistently high values (10−6 to 10−3 S cm−1) within the entire temperature range of 30–100 °C, showing an approximately linear positive correlation with temperature.117 Similarly, iNet-2 demonstrates high ionic conductivity (10−6–10−3 S cm−1) throughout 20–100 °C, with nearly linear temperature dependence at elevated temperatures and no observable conductivity hysteresis during heating–cooling cycles.118 Subsequently, 10% LiTFSI was incorporated into iNet-3, and the results demonstrated that this composite exhibited slightly enhanced conductivity at low temperatures relative to pure iNet, a finding consistent with the established characteristics of polymer electrolytes. However, at higher temperatures, decreased viscosity led to lower conductivity relative to the undoped sample, a trend property of ionic liquids. This behavior aligns with the typical properties of ionic liquids. Overall, the addition of lithium salt resulted in reduced conductivity. When comparing the conductivity of iNet-1, a significant enhancement of approximately 3 or 4 orders of magnitude compared to the neat sample was observed, achieving conductivity values close to 10−3 S cm−1. Additionally, a comparative analysis was conducted between the newly developed supramolecular network incorporating delocalized anions and the ionic polymer derived from the citrate-diamine system. The results demonstrated that the novel ionic polymer exhibits significantly higher electrical conductivity at elevated temperatures (100 °C). These findings highlight the potential of the IS-SPs for applications in electronic devices, through their limited conductivity at room temperature remains a key challenge for practical implementation.119

The electrical conductivity of IS-SPs is also sensitive to humidity. For example, Yu et al. investigated the electrical properties of supramolecular ionic materials (SIMs) using the sandwich-type device configuration (Fig. 10D and E).120 Under nitrogen conditions, three relative humidity (RH) levels were examined: dry, 11% RH, and 23% RH. In the absence of moisture, the SIM exhibited insulating properties. However, upon exposure to a humid condition, its conductivity increased markedly. This enhancement is attributed to the ionization of ions adsorbed by water molecules, which promoted ion exchange and facilitated charge transport within the materials. These findings indicate that both humidity and temperature are the key external factors that govern the electrical conductivity of the IS-SPs.

In addition, porphyrin molecules feature a distinctive 18π-conjugated macrocyclic structure and can undergo multiple redox transitions. This distinctive architecture enables efficient electron transfer and storage capabilities in electrochemical processes, thereby conferring electrochemical properties.66 A typical example is that after porphyrin-based ISA have been subjected to high-temperature treatment, they become highly promising ionic materials toward the application in the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR).

Thermal properties

The thermal properties of IS-SPs were investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Grinstaff and coworkers studied the thermal behavior of IS-SPs prepared via ISA between PAA and a series of phosphonium monocations.82 They systematically investigated the effect of the alkyl chains surrounding the phosphonium cation on the macroscopic properties of the ionic polymers, including thermal properties, and found that these properties are highly dependent on the alkyl chain length. The six phosphonium ionic liquids exhibit high thermal stability, with decomposition beginning only when the temperature reaches approximately 300 °C. Diphosphate ionic liquids exhibited significantly more solid-like compared with other monophosphate ionic liquids and ionic networks (Table 2). Later, Mecerreyes et al. evaluated the thermal properties of IS-SPs derived from employing citric acid and four different geminal dicationic ionic liquids.121 They found that the glass transition temperature (Tg) of those ionic polymers followed a trend similar to that of the equilibrium elasticity modulus (Ge), both being affected by the chemical nature of the dicationic molecule and the substituents on it. Furthermore, an increase in crosslinking density reduced the free volume of the ionic polymers, thereby elevating Tg. In essence, the density of ionic bonds was directly proportional to the equilibrium elastic modulus Ge which in turn determined Tg. Superior thermal properties were achieved with IS-SPs containing highly delocalized dianions, such as (trifluoromethane-sulfonyl)imide, (propylsulfonyl)methanide and (cyano-propylsulfonyl)imide groups, revealing a strong dependence of these properties on the nature of the substrates. DSC analysis revealed distinct melting points during the first heating cycle, the IS-SPs displayed melting points at 101, 94, 116 and 109 °C for iNet-1, iNet-2, iNet-4 and iNet-5, respectively (Table 2). In contrast, only glass transition temperatures (−1 °C to −22 °C) were observed during the second heating cycle, while solid iNet-3 displayed only a glass transition at −20 °C in both heating cycles. These results suggest that replacing aliphatic dianions with aromatic dianions leads to more amorphous characteristics.52 Furthermore, when the same dianions are used as building blocks, increasing the spacer length between two imidazolium cations also reduces crystal formation (Table 2). TGA results revealed decomposition onset temperatures in air ranging from 200 to 295 °C.118 IS-SPs formed by larger-molecular-weight amines and carboxylic acids exhibited a higher decomposition temperature.54 In addition, the DSC thermograms of several amine-carboxylic acid based IS-SPs exhibit two additional transitions, denoted as Tnl (the transition temperatures from the network to the liquid state) and Tln (the transition temperatures from the liquid to the network state) in Table 2, which correspond to the transition from an ordered phase to the isotropic liquid state and a preceding solid–solid or solid-mesophase transformation, respectively.54,116 As shown in Table 2, these values are highly dependent on the structure of the acid component, with the citric-priamine system showing a broader mesophase window (26 °C and 29.5 °C) compared to the malonic-priamine system (−10 °C and −10.5 °C). In terms of thermal stability, supramolecular ionic networks based on aromatic ionic liquids with bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)amide anions exhibited the highest thermal stability, significantly outperforming those with dicyanamide anions.

Rheological properties

The investigation of the viscosity of IS-SPs dates back to the 1980s, particularly in studies of polyelectrolyte-surfactant complexes.122 The viscosity and rheological behavior of these materials are strongly influenced by specific functional groups or building blocks, often imparting temperature-dependent properties. For instance, citric acid derivatives can form IS-SPs that undergo a rapid soft-solid to liquid transition.52,81,121 Grinstaff and co-workers have conducted extensive and insightful research on this subject.61,81 In their works, the ionic liquid P2+:2Cl− has a viscosity of 1000 Pa s. Whereas replacing Cl with the tetraanion EDTA4− (maintaining a 1 : 1 charge ratio) produced 2P2+:EDTA4− with a 12-fold increase in viscosity (∼12 000 Pa s), attributed to the formation of ionic polymers resulting from the interaction between the dication and tetraanion.59 In another study, replacing Cl− with PAA yielded new ionic polymers with a viscosity as high as 180 000 Pa s, exhibiting creep-recovery behavior.123 In addition, the alkyl chain length of diphosphonium cations was also found to significantly influence rheology. Ionic polymers prepared from diphosphonium cations with shorter alkyl chains displayed much higher viscosities. For instance, di-ButC10PAA exhibited the highest viscosity (∼107 Pa s). In contrast, di-HexC10PAA and di-OctC10PAA displayed lower values of 170 000 and 35 000 Pa s, respectively.82 Importantly, such polymers can reassemble rapidly after disassembly by heat or shear, retaining their mechanical properties. Creep-recovery experiments confirmed their viscoelastic nature. Rheological properties investigation further revealed that the creep recovery behavior and the thermal, as well as mechanical properties, were highly dependent on the alkyl chain length of the building blocks. Specifically, a longer alkyl chain introduces greater steric hindrance, which reduces electrostatic interactions and weakens the material's properties. This demonstrates that the properties of the IS-SPs are intricately linked to the design of its building block structure, thereby elucidating the relationship. Additionally, it is noteworthy that high-water-content mouldable hydrogels prepared simply by mixing clay, a dendritic molecular binder and sodium poly(acrylate) at room temperature, displayed remarkable mechanical properties.124 Therefore, supramolecular ionic network materials formed from aliphatic acids and amines demonstrate outstanding rheological properties. Mecerreyes et al. synthesized a series of ionic polymers through proton transfer, and systematically investigated their rheological properties. Thereby establishing the correlation between chemical structure and rheological properties.116,121 Rheological characterization was conducted using small-amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) tests and continuous flow viscosity measurements across range of frequencies and temperatures. The results revealed that a clear transition: at low temperatures and/or low frequencies, the materials behave as elastic network or gel (with frequency-dependent moduli G′ > G″, G′, storage modulus and G″, loss modulus), while at high temperatures/low frequencies, they transform into viscoelastic liquid (G″ > G′), reflecting the breakdown of the ionic network and suppression of flow.54,125

Their rheological results display two distinct characteristic time scales that govern the dynamics of IS-SPs, which are simpler than those observed in conventional polymer systems. The first corresponds to the formation/dissociation of supramolecular associations, and the second to chain relaxation via reptation.126 These dual relaxation mechanisms manifest as two plateaus: one associated with the classical entanglement network plateau and another arising from reversible ionic interactions. Similar “double plateau” behavior has been reported by Craig et al. in metallopincer-crosslinked poly(4-vinylpyridine).127 Consistent results were obtained for ionic polymers derived from di-/tricarboxylic acids and di-/trialkyl amines, further supporting this interpretation.53 In 2014, they extended this approach to bio-based IS-SPs via proton transfer reaction between an aliphatic diamine and carboxylic acids.54 These solid soft polymers exhibited thermoreversible transitions, transforming into viscoelastic liquids at high temperatures-a behavior also observed in biopolymer gels.121,125

Self-healing properties

One of the most remarkable characteristics of living systems is their inherent ability to self-repair. In polymer science and engineering, the inevitable degradation and unexpected damage limit material lifetime. To address this challenge, self-healing polymers, defined as ‘‘materials in which damage triggers an autonomous healing response”128,129 have emerged as a rapidly expanding research frontier. In recent years, ionic polymers have been recognized as self-healing smart materials.104,125 The self-healing capability originates from the reversible properties of IS-SPs, which enables network reassembly upon damage when triggered by external stimuli such as light, pressure, heat, or mechanical stress.130

The self-healing property plays a crucial role in the application of ionic materials. Some IS-SPs formed by carboxylic acids and organic bases exhibit remarkable self-healing capabilities.116

For example, Mecerreyes has explored the self-healing properties of ionic polymers formed by citrate and N-tetramethyl-1,3-propanediammonium. They discovered that after the film was cut at room temperature and subsequently heated to 50 °C, it exhibited rapid self-healing and returned to its original state. Furthermore, one cylinder of a bulk supramolecular ionic polymer was cut. After the cut pieces were kept in an N2 atmosphere for 2 hours, the polymer cylinders healed themselves without any external stimuli.53 Similar results were obtained for citric acid-priamine ionic polymers, which after healing, could again be stretched to twice its original length again without breaking from the cut site. By contrast, polymers based on tartaric acid and priamine showed no self-healing capability, likely due to their semicrystalline structure. In addition, excessive soft or rigid ionic materials (e.g. malonic-priamine and furan-priamine systems) also lack self-healing properties.54

Multifunctional supramolecular rubber materials provide another striking example. Functionalized polybutadiene (PB), prepared via thiol–ene chemistry to introduce both covalent and non-covalent cross-links, exhibited intelligent self-healing and shape-memory capabilities.105 The cut-and-heal method was employed to demonstrate that the material possesses this property (Fig. 12A). Fractured PB networks spontaneously healed at room temperature without external stimuli. Within 1 h, the repaired sample could be stretched to three times its original length, while optical microscope confirmed the near-complete disappearance of fracture lines. Specifically, increasing the density of covalent cross-links reduced both healing rate and efficiency, as irreversible covalent bonds hindered chain migration. By contrast, elevated temperature accelerated the process, underscoring the importance of dynamic ionic bonds in driving effective healing. Similar findings were reported by Heinrich et al., who converted allylic bromide groups into ionic imidazolium bromide groups in elastic materials. These materials also exhibited self-healing properties at room temperature,131 indicating that a certain degree of dynamic motion is already present between the polymer chains and ionic associates (Fig. 12B).

This healing mechanism is attributed to NaCl-induced disruption of interpolymer cross-links, which increases chain mobility and facilitates interdiffusion across the damaged interface.104 Overall, the ionic interactions present in ionic polymers offer a distinctive opportunity to develop self-healable commodity materials.

Mechanistic understanding of IS-SPs

Although ionic interactions can serve as dynamic physical crosslinks, self-healing is not guaranteed for all ionic supramolecular materials. Efficient self-healing of IS-SPs relies on two concurrent conditions: (i) reversible ionic association dynamics of ion pairs or ionic polymers must dissociate and reassociate on relevant experimental time scales and (ii) sufficient polymer chain mobility to facilitate interfacial wetting, chain interdiffusion, and network reconfiguration upon damage.134,135 Systems with tuned ionic binding energy and favorable segmental dynamics generally exhibit rapid, repeatable healing.136 In contrast, excessively strong ionic aggregation-forming kinetically trapped, vitrified, or microphase-separated crystalline domains-severely suppresses exchange dynamics and chain diffusion, resulting in solid-like behavior and poor self-healing efficiency despite high ionic content.136 Charge density and molecular architecture further govern linear and nonlinear viscoelastic responses by tuning the number, spacing, lifetime, and distribution of ionic stickers as well as aggregate morphology.137,138 Increasing charge density typically raises effective crosslink density and promotes self-assembly and bridging, leading to higher plateau modulus, zero-shear viscosity, and longer relaxation times.139,140 However, beyond a threshold, overly strong ionic correlations can kinetically arrest segmental motion, slowing relaxation and reducing healing.141 Meanwhile, architecture influences entanglement density, bridge-to-loop ratio, and microphase separation, thereby tuning yield stress, shear-thinning behavior, recovery rate, and relaxation-time spectrum breadth.142

These mechanistic insights, when viewed collectively, point toward a unified design framework governed by three coupled levers: (i) sticker spacing and local segmental dynamics, which can be tuned by spacer length/backbone flexibility and directly impact chain mobility and interfacial wetting; (ii) ion-pairing/cluster energetics and transport barriers, which are strongly influenced by anion chemistry (e.g., charge delocalisation) and dictate association lifetime and ion-hopping kinetics; and (iii) the resulting effective crosslink/bridge density and aggregate morphology, which together set the relaxation spectrum and the balance between mechanical robustness and dynamic adaptability. By rationally tailoring these parameters, one can navigate the performance space among ionic conductivity, mechanical modulus, and self-healing efficiency, enabling IS-SPs with properties matched to specific application requirements (e.g., energy storage, flexible electronics, or self-repairing coatings). This mechanistic framework provides the foundation for the applications discussed in the following section.

Applications of IS-SPs

The reversible ionic bonds impart ionic polymers with a high charge density, which in turn endows ionic polymers with distinctive features, such as remarkable stability, fluidity, and self-healing capabilities. These attributes establish IS-SPs as a versatile platform for exploring a wide range of applications.

Drug delivery

Ionic interactions play a key role in drug delivery systems by enhancing biocompatibility, drug loading efficiency, stability, the ability to penetrate biological barriers, targeting accuracy, and controlled release. IS-SPs are particularly attractive because they can form stable complexes with drug molecules.143–146 When the compound reaches the target site, IS-SPs can release the drug from the carrier under specific external stimuli, thereby enabling sustained and targeted drug delivery.72,147 Zhang et al. developed a photo-responsive supramolecular nanovesicle via ISA of an azobenzene-modified cationic surfactant (AzoC10) with PEG-PAA. Sodium fluorescein and sodium pyrene sulfonate were incorporated into the vesicles as fluorescent probes. Upon exposure to UV light, the vesicles disintegrated, resulting in the release of the fluorescent molecules into solution and causing fluorescence quenching. These supramolecular vesicles can serve as nanocarriers for the encapsulation of guest molecules, such as therapeutic drugs, and exhibit photo-controlled release capabilities.148 Kataoka et al. designed and synthesized a pH-responsive comb-like ionic polymer, poly(ethylene glycol)-poly[(N′-citraconyl-2-aminoethyl)aspartamide] (PEG-pAsp(EDACit)). The presence of citraconamide groups in the side chains introduces a high density of negative charges, thereby facilitating the formation of supramolecular nanomicelles with a particle size of 140 nm via ion self-assembly with cationic lysozyme at pH 7.4. However, when the solvent medium is transitioned to a weakly acidic environment (pH 5.5), the citraconamide groups undergo hydrolysis, converting the negative charges on the polymer into positive charges. This charge inversion generates electrostatic repulsion between lysozyme and polymer, leading to rapid cargo release, with a rate of ∼97% within 5 hours.149

In addition, the integration of ion self-assembly and host–guest interactions provides a versatile strategy to construct multifunctional supramolecular nanoparticles. Compounds such as cyclodextrins, cucurbiturils, and calixarenes, which contain recognition sites for guest molecules, serve as typical examples of nanocontainers. These compounds form cavities that are hydrophilic on the outside and hydrophobic on the inside, allowing for the recognition and encapsulation of various guest molecules via hydrophobic forces, thus facilitating the construction of supramolecular nanoparticles.150–153

Sensing

IS-SPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility, rendering them highly suitable for biosensing applications. This is because most biological processes occur in aqueous environments, where biomolecules such as proteins and enzymes remain highly active. Supramolecular ionic materials with stable and adaptive encapsulation properties in water are especially promising for biosensor development. Yu and co-workers successfully developed a novel water-stable, adaptive, and electroactive supramolecular ionic material (SIM) derived from ABTS and 1,10-bis(3-methylimidazolium-1-yl) decane (C10(mim)2) via ISA. The resulting SIMs exhibit excellent stability in both water and salt-rich environments, as well as adaptive encapsulation capabilities, thereby enabling their integration into sensing technologies. Methylene green (MG)-encapsulated SIM could not only be stably confined onto the electrode surface, but also maintain the electrochemical properties of MG, this feature enable efficient electrocatalytic oxidation of dihydronicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), highlighting their potential for NADH detection (Fig. 14A).69

Based on the leucine supramolecular ionic polymerization under intense blue light and the detection of CS2 in an entirely aqueous solution, polyampholytic polymers can achieve highly selective and sensitive “open-eye” sensing of CS2 vapor through colorimetric methods.155,156 The formed supramolecular ionic polymer not only exhibits excellent performance for detecting organic compounds but also demonstrates remarkable sensitivity to pH variations.157

Electrolytes/solid electrolytes

Ionic supramolecular hydrogels represent a promising electrolyte platform for energy-related ionic devices.158 In an advanced electrochromic device, double-network ionic gel electrolytes (SP-DN) were formed via the self-assembly of 1,3 : 2,4-dibenzylidene sorbitol synergistically integrated with a covalently cross-linked polymer network. SP-DN demonstrated superior mechanical strength and robust interfacial adhesion with high ionic conductivity and optical transparency (Fig. 15A). Electrochromic devices based on SP-DN exhibit rapid switching dynamics, high coloration efficiency, and robust stability over 2000 cycles.159 Moreover, electrochromic devices prepared on ITO-poly(ethylene terephthalate) substrates retain 90.6% of their initial optical contrast after 1000 bending cycles, highlighting the potential of the SP-DN ionic gel for flexible applications. Similarly, a triple-network supramolecular polymer hydrogel electrolyte (CD(β)/PAAm/CMCS) developed via UV-induced polymerization provides strong noncovalent (multiple hydrogen-bonding) interactions, broad environmental compatibility (−45 to 45 °C), high ionic conductivity (206 mS cm−1 at 25 °C), and enables flexible zinc–air batteries with high specific capacities of 555 mAh g−1 at −45 °C and 549 mAh g−1 at 45 °C (Fig. 15B).160 For flexible zinc–air batteries, flexible electrolytes with solid self-supporting properties are crucial in the fields of energy and electronics. Li and co-workers developed supramolecular ionic network electrolytes, in which polyoxometalate nanoclusters serve as supramolecular crosslinkers to solidify bola-type zwitterionic liquids.161 The resulting electrolytes are semi-solid, while maintaining a proton conductivity of 8.2 × 10−4 S cm−1 at low humidity (RH = 30%) (Fig. 15C and D).

Hydrogen-bonded supramolecular polymers often exhibit rapid self-healing and good reprocessability because hydrogen bonds are relatively weak and highly reversible. However, their mechanical robustness is frequently limited, especially under high humidity or elevated temperatures, where hydrogen bonding is progressively weakened, leading to reduced modulus/strength and compromised long-term stability.162

Metal–ligand coordination polymers offer stronger and more tunable interactions, leading to improved stiffness and toughness. Nevertheless, their performance can depend sensitively on the metal identity, ligand environment, and chemical stability, which can restrict long-term durability or scalability.163

Covalent adaptable networks and dynamic covalent polymers provide superior mechanical integrity and thermal resistance, as their network rearrangement is governed by covalent bond exchange reactions. These systems are particularly advantageous for structural applications requiring creep resistance. However, bond exchange typically requires elevated temperatures, catalysts, or specific stimuli, which may limit rapid healing under ambient conditions.164,165 In contrast, IS-SPs combine relatively strong electrostatic interactions with intrinsic reversibility under mild conditions.6 Ionic associations can significantly enhance modulus, toughness, and resistance to permanent deformation without introducing permanent crosslinks. Moreover, the dynamic nature of ionic clusters enables stress relaxation, shear thinning, and self-healing without external catalysts.

A distinctive feature of IS-SPs is the collective and long-range character of electrostatic interactions, which promotes ionic clustering and microphase separation. This structural hierarchy can provide mechanical reinforcement comparable to lightly crosslinked systems while maintaining processability.

However, IS-SPs also present intrinsic limitations. Their properties are highly sensitive to dielectric environment, humidity, counterion type, and ionic strength.166 Excessively strong ionic correlations may kinetically hinder segmental motion, slow relaxation, and reduce healing efficiency.167 Thus, careful control of ionic content and architecture is essential to balance mechanical strength and dynamic adaptability.

Overall, IS-SPs offer a unique combination of mechanical reinforcement, ambient-condition healing, and structural tunability, positioning them between weak supramolecular assemblies and covalent adaptive networks for advanced functional materials.

Summary and outlook

In this review, we summarized recent advances in IS-SPs, with particular emphasis on their synthesis, properties and applications. IS-SPs are constructed by using multi-organic acids, including multi-organic carboxylic acids and organic sulfonic acids, as well as organic bases or organic salts, through ISA. The conditions for the formation of ionic polymers via ISA are generally mild, and some of the reagents involved can be considered relatively green and environmentally friendly, depending on the solvent system and waste management. Importantly, in the synthesis of such block structures, the building blocks of such supramolecular structures can be either small molecules containing multiple acidic and basic groups or polymers with similar functionalities. Over the past two decades, materials formed through ISA have demonstrated exceptional performance. Depending on the individuality of the building blocks and the different synthesis conditions, the resulting IS-SPs exhibit differences in structure, including elastic bulk, solid bulk, linear or dendritic. For building blocks, organic carboxylic acids and organic sulfonic acids are the most utilized commonly substances. Among organic carboxylic acids, the majority are aliphatic acids, in contrast, organic sulfonic acids, mainly are porphyrin-based sulfonic acids. Especially when polymers and small molecules are used as building blocks. The development of other aromatic polymeric sulfonic acids has only emerged in recent years.

Key structure–property insights are as follows: (i) building block identity and environmental factors govern performance: the properties of IS-SPs are influenced by the structure of their building blocks, assembly conditions, and environment factors. Distinctive structural features such π-conjugated macrocyclic frameworks, the chain length between cationic imidazole groups, the type of cationic moieties (e.g. pyridinium or imidazolium), the density of ionic bonds, and molecular weight directly affect their performance. (ii) Environmental responsiveness: moreover, the environmental factors such as humidity and temperature significantly modulate conductivity and rheological behaviors, with temperature being the most critical parameter for viscoelastic properties. (iii) Self-healing requires a balance: self-healing capability originates from the reversible reassembly of ionic bonds upon damage, triggered by external stimuli such as light, pressure, heat, or mechanical stress, it has been observed that ionic materials that are either too soft or excessively rigid do not exhibit self-healing property. However, polymers that are excessively soft or rigid lack this ability. (iv) Validated applications in biocompatible systems: this distinctive structural and functional properties endow IS-SPs with great potential for advanced applications. Ionic interactions play a crucial role in drug delivery systems by improving drug biocompatibility, drug loading efficiency, stability, biological barrier penetration, targeting precision, and controlled release. Moreover, IS-SPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility, rendering them highly suitable for biosensing applications. The currently developed water-stable IS-SP have been successfully applied to the sensing of NADH.

Although various IS-SP have been developed over the past two decades, their progress still lags behind that of covalent bond-based materials, and the research of ionic polymers continue to faces several challenges. Firstly, the non-directional movement of ions presents a significant challenge in precisely controlling the polymerization process. Secondly, ions tend to dissociate into charged species in solution, particularly in aqueous environments, so enhancing their stability in such conditions remains critical. In water, ionic self-assembly is further complicated by the high dielectric constant and ionic screening (which compresses the Debye length and weakens long-range electrostatic attractions), as well as strong hydration and competitive hydrogen bonding, which can compete with designed intermolecular interactions and alter ion-pairing motifs. Counterion effects/ion specificity and pH-dependent ionization equilibria can further influence nucleation, morphology selection, and kinetic stability, especially under high-salt or physiological conditions. Third, the dense packing of ionic bonds often results in low porosity and small specific surface areas, limiting applications that require high surface activity. Finally, most IS-SPs mainly rely on materials with covalent bonds, therefore the construction of multifunctional ionic materials through ISA using multiple organic compounds with opposite charges remains underdeveloped and requires further exploration.

The precise regulation of IS-SP property is another challenge. Given the complex interplay among building block structure, crosslinking degree, types of ionic groups, and environment factors. Currently, there is still a lack of in-depth research into the relationship among these factors. Integrating theoretical calculations with experimental studies will be crucial for revealing the mechanisms of intermolecular interactions, predicting material properties, and guiding the rational design of new systems.

Although various IS-SPs with excellent properties have been developed, their applications have not been extensively explored, having been applied only in the areas of drug delivery and sensing, which also provides vast opportunities for further investigation, especially, soft ionotronic devices, supramolecular electrolytes, ionic conductive elastomers, flexible electronics, neuromorphic ionic materials, ion separation membranes, and bioelectronics.168–173 Their inherent electrical and thermal properties suggest opportunities in high-performance conductive materials, while the reversibility of ionic bonds enables self-healing coatings and long-lifetime devices. Furthermore, their responsiveness to external stimuli such as temperature, pH, light, and electric field also positions them as ideal candidates for environmental sensing and real-time monitoring. Additionally, due to its combined characteristics of ionic liquids and polymers.20,36,174 IS-SPs hold great potential for future applications in intelligent adhesive materials, catalysis and green chemistry, and energy and environmental technologies.175–177

Looking forward, we highlight three promising directions.

Precisely controlled IS-SPs systems: from statistical to programmable assembly: current ISA strategies largely rely on statistical assembly of charged building blocks, offering limited control over sequence, connectivity, and network topology. Achieving programmable assembly-where the spatial arrangement of ionic pairs, chain connectivity, and hierarchical order are predetermined-remains a formidable challenge. Key bottlenecks include the lack of sequence-controlled ionic building blocks and the difficulty in predicting assembly pathways. To overcome these, we envision two complementary strategies: (i) the design of sequence-defined ionic oligomers via solid-phase or iterative synthesis, enabling positional control of ionic groups; and (ii) the use of template-directed assembly or external fields (e.g., electric or magnetic fields) to guide orientation and long-range order. These approaches could transform IS-SPs from statistically averaged structures into precisely engineered materials with predictable properties.

Machine-learning-guided materials design: the vast parameter space of IS-SPs-including ionic pair combinations, spacer lengths, and assembly conditions-makes exhaustive experimental screening impractical. Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful tool to accelerate the discovery of structure–property relationships by linking ionic pairs, assembly morphology, and performance. However, progress is currently limited by the lack of standardized, high-quality datasets. We suggest that future efforts should focus on: (i) building open-access databases of IS-SP properties (conductivity, modulus, healing efficiency, etc.) with standardized characterization protocols; (ii) developing ML models that predict assembly morphology and key performance metrics from building block descriptors; and (iii) integrating ML with high-throughput experimentation to rapidly validate predictions and iteratively refine models. Such an approach would significantly accelerate the rational design of IS-SPs for targeted applications.

Multi-stimuli-responsive IS-SPs: while IS-SPs are inherently responsive to temperature and ionic strength, their sensitivity to a broader range of stimuli-including pH, light, electric fields, and magnetic fields remains underexplored. The design of multi-stimuli-responsive IS-SPs requires careful integration of responsive motifs that can operate independently or synergistically without disrupting the ionic network. Key challenges include: (i) avoiding antagonistic responses when multiple stimuli are applied simultaneously; (ii) achieving orthogonal (independent) responses for each stimulus; and (iii) maintaining mechanical integrity under dynamic conditions. Potential strategies include the incorporation of photo-responsive ionic groups (e.g., azobenzene-based cations), pH-sensitive linkers, or field-responsive additives that impart additional functionality while preserving the core ionic network. Future work should also explore the development of logic gate IS-SPs that respond only to specific combinations of stimuli, enabling advanced applications in drug delivery, soft robotics, and adaptive materials.

In summary, the future of IS-SPs lies in moving from empirical design to predictive, programmable, and intelligent material systems. By addressing the bottlenecks outlined above, we believe that IS-SPs will evolve from laboratory curiosities to practical materials that address pressing technological challenges in energy, healthcare, and sustainable technologies.

IS-SPs have attracted considerable attention due to their structural diversity, fascinating properties, and broad application potential. However, their synthesis, properties, and applications remain insufficiently explored. By designing novel building blocks, and deepening the understanding of structure–property relationships, IS-SPs research can advance toward the rational creation of multifunctional materials. This review aims to provide a comprehensive roadmap to inspire further developments in the synthesis, regulation, and application of IS-SPs.
