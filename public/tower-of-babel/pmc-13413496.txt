Taxonomic study of Tulasnella species focusing on their teleomorphs: Descriptions of a new species and two newly recorded species in Japan
Authors: Kosuke Nagamune, Nitaro Maekawa, Kenta Fujii, Naoki Endo, Akira Nakagiri, Kenji Suetsugu, Dai Nagamatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The genus Tulasnella is generally regarded as saprotrophic, but several species are known to form mycorrhizal associations. Basidioma morphology is a crucial taxonomic character in Tulasnella; however, recent studies have focused on DNA sequences of cultured mycelia isolated from orchid roots for species delimitation and description. In this study, we collected 16 Tulasnella basidioma specimens in western Japan and conducted a taxonomic study based on their morphology, culture properties, and molecular phylogenetic analyses. Phylogenetic analyses of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region resolved the specimens into six clades: Tulasnella sp. 1 (T. eichleriana), Tulasnella sp. 4 (a new species described here as T. nematospora), Tulasnella sp. 5 (T. albida), and three unidentified species (Tulasnella spp. 2, 3, and 6). Tulasnella nematospora is described here as a new species based on its thread-like basidiospores, monilioid hyphae forming chains of 2-7 cells on PDA, and multinucleate hyphae. This study provides the first records of T. eichleriana and T. albida from Japan. The close phylogenetic relationship between Tulasnella sp. 6 and an orchid mycorrhizal Tulasnella lineage suggests symbiotic potential in this species, whereas the remaining taxa are presumed to be saprotrophic.

1. Introduction

The genus Tulasnella J. Schröt. (order: Cantharellales Gäum., phylum: Basidiomycota R.T. Moore) includes more than 80 species (Species Fungorum; https://www.speciesfungorum.org; accessed on Mar 17 2026). The type species is T. lilacina J. Schröt. (Schröter, 1888). Stalpers et al. (2021) integrated Epulorhiza R.T. Moore (anamorph), Hormisciopsis Sumst. (anamorph), Hormomyces Bonord. (anamorph), and Prototremella Pat. (teleomorph) into the genus Tulasnella. Although Hormomyces is an older genus than Tulasnella, Stalpers et al. (2021) proposed maintaining Tulasnella as nomen conservandum because it is more widely used than the other genera. Synonyms of Tulasnella include Gloeotulasnella Höhn. & Litsch., Muciporus Juel, and Pachysterigma Johan-Olsen ex Bref. (Mack et al., 2021). The basidiomata of Tulasnella are often pinkish or purple and are produced on rotten wood, leaves, or litter. Micromorphological characters of Tulasnella include a hymenium composed of hyphae that usually lack clamp connections, often absent subhymenial structures, basidia exhibiting strongly swollen sterigmata and secondary separation of basal portions of the sterigmata, and basidiospore formation on the sharply pointed ends of these sterigmata. The basidiospore shape varies among species (globose, allantoid, sigmoid, or spiral), and secondary spores may develop on basidiospores (Mack et al., 2021; Oberwinkler et al., 2017).

Ecologically, Tulasnella species are usually saprotrophic (Carvalho et al., 2017; Roberts, 1994a), but some have been reported as parasites of the amoeba Thecamoeba terricola (Greeff, 1866) Lepsi, 1960 (syn. Amoeba terricola Greeff, 1866) (Drechsler, 1969) or as mycorrhizal partners of various plants, including species of Orchidaceae Juss., ectomycorrhizal trees, and members of the liverwort family Aneuraceae H.Klinggr. (Bidartondo et al., 2003; Preußing et al., 2010; Suárez et al., 2006; Tedersoo & Smith, 2013). Establishing a symbiotic relationship with a mycorrhizal fungus is essential for orchid growth, and Tulasnella is a key orchid mycorrhizal partner (Fuji et al., 2020). Tulasnella species have been frequently isolated from orchid mycorrhizal roots (Kaur et al., 2018; Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2012; Rammitsu et al., 2021, 2023), and some have been described as new species based only on their cultured mycelia (particularly monilioid hyphae). Their molecular phylogenetic positions have been inferred from their DNA sequences (Almeida et al., 2014; Arifin et al., 2021, 2022; Freitas et al., 2020; Fujimori et al., 2019; Linde et al., 2017; Rachanarin et al., 2018). Since the genus Tulasnella was established in 1888, more than 50 species have been described based on their basidiomata characteristics; however, typified DNA information remains lacking (Cruz et al., 2016). Therefore, describing new species without basidioma information may lead to further synonyms of known species. Polyphasic studies integrating data on basidioma morphology, molecular phylogeny, and cultural characteristics are required to gain a better understanding of the taxonomy and ecology of Tulasnella.

To date, no teleomorphs of Tulasnella have been reported from natural environments in Japan. Four Tulasnella species from Japan have been reported as orchid root-derived cultures (Fujimori et al., 2019; Uetake et al., 1999). Uetake et al. (1999) successfully induced T. deliquescens (Juel) Juel basidiomata from cultured mycelia isolated from roots of Platanthera japonica (Thunb.) Lindl. (Orchidaceae). Fujimori et al. (2019) later described three new Tulasnella species based on DNA sequences and culture properties of mycelia isolated from roots of Spiranthes australis (R.Br.) Lindl. (syn. Spiranthes sinensis (Pers.) Ames var. amoena (M.Bieb.) H. Hara (Orchidaceae)). Recent studies on orchid mycorrhizal fungi have suggested that diverse unknown Tulasnella species are potentially distributed across Japan (Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2012; Tsutsumi et al., 2017, 2022). Therefore, we hypothesized that undescribed Tulasnella species forming basidiomata exist on substrates such as deadwood. Consequently, since 2022, we have collected 16 putative Tulasnella specimens in western Japan. We investigated the taxonomic positions of these specimens based on their basidioma morphology, culture properties, and molecular phylogenetic analyses.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Collection and isolation of basidioma specimens

Sixteen specimens morphologically identified as Tulasnella species were collected between 2022 and 2024 in western Japan (Table 1). These specimens were used for morphological observations of basidiomata. Because the basidiomata of this genus are usually produced on decaying wood, we collected the substrate along with the specimens using a chisel and/or pruning scissors. Samples were packed in resealable plastic bags to prevent drying during transportation to the laboratory. The external morphology of the basidiomata was photographed using a digital camera (PowerShot SX70 HS, Canon Co., Tokyo, Japan) and a stereomicroscope (SMZ1500-1, Nikon Co., Tokyo, Japan). Polysporic isolates were obtained from fresh basidiomata to characterize the cultured mycelia and to extract high-purity DNA for subsequent sequencing and phylogenetic analyses. For isolation and subculturing, we used either malt extract agar (MA; 1.5% malt extract; Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan or Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan and 1.5% agar; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan or Ina Food Industry Co., Ltd., Nagano, Japan) or potato dextrose agar (PDA; Shimadzu Diagnostic Corp., Tokyo, Japan). After isolation, the fresh basidiomata were air-dried at 45 °C for 2 d. Dried specimens and living cultures were deposited in the Biological Resource Center, National Institute of Technology and Evaluation (NBRC), and the Fungus/Mushroom Resource and Research Center (FMRC) of Tottori University. Deposition and utilization of Tottori University Fungal Culture Collection (TUFC) strains were supported by FMRC through the National BioResource Project of the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT), Japan (http://nbrp.jp).

2.2. DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, and sequencing

We successfully extracted DNA from the FKF004 sample, which had been immersed in TE buffer prior to drying; however, extraction failed in the remaining 15 samples, likely due to their thin basidiomata and post-drying treatment. However, polysporic cultures were established from all of these 15 samples. Accordingly, DNA extraction was performed using a single basidioma and 15 polysporic cultures (Table 1). Genomic DNA was extracted from the fresh cultures or basidiomata using a previously described microwave-based method (Izumitsu et al., 2012). PCR targeting the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the nuclear ribosomal RNA gene (nrDNA) was performed using the primer pair ITS5/ITS4-Tul (Taylor & McCormick, 2008; White et al., 1990). DreamTaq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or KOD One PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) was used for PCR, each with a specific protocol. For PCR with DreamTaq DNA Polymerase, initial denaturation was conducted at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 51 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR with KOD One PCR Master Mix included 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 5 s, and 68 °C for 1 s. The PCR products were purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) or ExoSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and directly sequenced using the same primer pair. Sequencing was performed by Eurofins Genomics Japan (Tokyo, Japan) or Macrogen Japan (Tokyo, Japan), and the resulting sequence data were analyzed using BioEdit v. 7.2.5 (Hall, 1999). All sequences were deposited in the GenBank database under the accession numbers LC884470-LC884485 via the DNA Data Bank of Japan.

2.3. Phylogenetic analyses

ITS sequences obtained from 15 cultures and one basidioma were subjected to phylogenetic analyses. To construct the phylogenetic tree, we supplemented our 16 sequences with 54 published sequences from the GenBank database (Table 2). Two strains of Rhizoctonia cornigera (Bourdot) Zmitr. (syn. Ceratobasidium cereale D.I. Murray & Burpee and C. cornigerum (Bourdot) D.P. Rogers) were selected as outgroups (González et al., 2002). The dataset was aligned in MEGA v. 7.0.26 using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar, 2004; Kumar et al., 2016). DNA sequences of 992 bp, including gaps, were utilized for phylogenetic analyses. Phylogenetic trees were inferred by the maximum likelihood (ML) method in MEGA v. 7.0.26 and by Bayesian inference (BI) in MrBayes v. 3.2.7 (Ronquist et al., 2012). MEGA v. 7.0.26 and KAKUSAN4 (Tanabe, 2011) were used to estimate the optimal base substitution model. ML bootstrap (MLBS) support was assessed via nonparametric bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates. BI analysis involved two sets of four chains of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) composed of three heated chains and one cold chain, with the topology sampled every 100 generations. In total, 3,000,000 generations were analyzed until the average standard deviation of the split frequencies was < 0.01. After convergence of the MCMC runs, the first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in, and a 50% majority-rule consensus tree was constructed from the remaining trees. The tree was visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.4 software (https://mybiosoftware.com/figtree-1-3-1-produce-figures-phylogenetic-trees.html). The alignment dataset and resulting trees were deposited in TreeBase (https://treebase.org/; TB2:S32256). The alignment is provided in Supplementary alignment S1.

2.4. Microscopic observations of basidiomata

Micromorphological characteristics of the dried specimens were observed under an optical microscope (BX43-R-SET-1, Olympus Co., Tokyo, Japan), and the parts of each basidioma (basidiospores, basidia, sterigmata, and hyphae) were measured using PhotoRuler 1.1.3 (https://www.inocybe.info/_userdata/ruler/PhotoRuler.html) in 3% potassium hydroxide. For each specimen, minimum and maximum values were measured for 15 basidia and 15 sterigmata, and minimum, maximum, and average values were determined for 30 basidiospores. The amyloidity of each element was assessed using Melzer's reagent and compared with that in distilled water. Because several basidiomata were very small, these micromorphological characteristics could not be observed clearly.

2.5. Morphological observations of cultures

Cultures obtained from basidiospores of the Tulasnella specimens were used for morphological observations and colony-diam measurements. Eight strains were examined (NBRC 117302, NBRC 117303, NBRC 117304, NBRC 117306, NBRC 117312, NBRC 117313, NBRC 117315, and NBRC 117316). Among isolates from nearby locations, one vigorous strain was selected for culture study. Agar discs containing mycelium were punched from precultured plates using an autoclaved commercially available plastic straw (6 mm diam) and transferred to PDA or water agar (1.5% agar; Ina Food Industry Co., Ltd., Nagano, Japan). Plates were incubated at 20 °C for 15 d in an incubator (MIR-153, SANYO Electric Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Colony diameters were measured every 3 d. At the end of the incubation period, cultures were observed microscopically to record hyphal thickness (reported as ranges), shapes of monilioid hyphae, and presence of coiled hyphae and clamp connections. Culture observations were made in triplicate per strain, except for NBRC 117303, which was examined in quadruplicate because only one specimen was collected from this clade. For comparison, strains MAFF244716 and MAFF244717, obtained from the Genebank of the Research Center of Genetic Resources, National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO Genebank), were observed under the same culture conditions. These strains were identified as clade T. deliquescens A by Fujimori et al. (2019), which included the ex-neotype of Epulorhiza repens (N. Bernard) R.T. Moore (recognized as an anamorph of T. deliquescens; Moore, 1996). We were unable to access the T. deliquescens A strains previously identified by teleomorphic morphology (MAFF305801, MAFF305802, and MAFF305805) because they are not publicly available. To compare culture properties, NBRC 117308 was incubated at 25 °C for 8 d following the method of Freitas et al. (2020). Cultured mycelia were observed under fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse 50i; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with an ultraviolet wavelength excitation apparatus (Nikon C-SHG1, Tokyo, Japan). The number of nuclei per cell was determined after fixation in Carnoy solution, followed by staining with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). DAPI observations followed the method of Chen et al. (2021), with a slight modification (staining for 2 h).

3. Results

3.1. Phylogenetic analyses

Model selection identified the GTR + G model as optimal for both ML and BI analyses. The phylogenetic tree inferred from the ITS dataset is shown in Fig. 1. The 16 specimens were resolved into six clades, designated as Tulasnella spp. 1-6. Tulasnella spp. 1-4 were assigned to Group IV as defined by Cruz et al. (2014), whereas Tulasnella spp. 5-6 were assigned to Group III. These group assignments were strongly supported in both analyses (Group III: MLBS = 98%, Bayesian posterior probability (BPP) = 1.00; Group IV: MLBS = 99%, BPP = 1.00).

Fig. 1 - Maximum-likelihood (ML) tree of Tulasnella species inferred from ITS sequences using MEGA 7. Node supports are shown as BS support in ML (MLBS)/Bayesian posterior probability (BPP). Thick branches indicate MLBS ≥ 90% and BPP ≥ 0.95; values below MLBS 50% or BPP 0.70 are omitted. Sequences obtained in this study are shown in red; others are from NCBI. Letters “m”, “b”, “s”, and “u” indicate the source of the DNA sequences; “m” = orchid mycorrhiza, “b” = basidioma, “s” = sporodochium, “u” = unknown.

Ten specimens of Tulasnella sp. 1 formed a monophyletic clade with T. eichleriana Bres. (accession numbers AY373292 and KC152381), with strong support in the ML analysis (MLBS = 96%) and moderate support in the BI analysis (BPP = 0.82). ITS sequence divergence within the Tulasnella sp. 1 clade, estimated by p-distance, ranged from 0-3.2% in the nrITS-5.8S region and 0-0.6% in the 5.8S region (Supplementary Tables S1, S2). Tulasnella spp. 2-4 each formed independent clades, and no closely related taxa were identified. Tulasnella sp. 5 formed a monophyletic clade with T. albida Bourdot & Galzin and Tulasnella cf. albida (accession numbers AY373294, KC152378, and KC152379; Cruz et al., 2014), with strong support in both ML and BI analyses (MLBS = 99%, BPP = 1.00). ITS sequence divergence within the Tulasnella sp. 5 clade ranged from 0.5-7.2% in the nrITS-5.8S region and 0-0.6% in the 5.8S region (Supplementary Tables S3, S4). Tulasnella sp. 6 clustered with T. brigadeiroensis E.F.S. Freitas, Meira. Silva & M.C.M. Kasuya with high support (MLBS = 89%, BPP = 0.99). ITS sequence divergence between Tulasnella sp. 6 and two strains of T. brigadeiroensis ranged from 2.7-9.8% in the nrITS-5.8S region and was 0% in the 5.8S region (Supplementary Tables S3, S4), suggesting that they may be conspecific. BLAST results for the 16 sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

3.2. Morphological characteristics and phylogenetic relationships

Japanese specimens consisting of the Tulasnella sp. 1 clade (Figs. 2, 3; Table 3) produced resupinate, pale violaceous or whitish basidiomata, clampless-septate hyphae, basidia (7-15 × 5-8 μm) with four protuberant sterigmata (5.5-17.5 × 2.5-6.5 μm), and ellipsoid to elongate basidiospores (4.5-6.5 × 3-4.5 μm, 5.8 × 4.1 μm on average). The Tulasnella sp. 1 clade showed monophyly with T. eichleriana in our phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1), and the morphological characteristics were almost identical to those of T. eichleriana (Cruz et al., 2016). The Tulasnella sp. 2 clade consisted of one specimen that produced resupinate, whitish basidioma, clampless-septate hyphae, basidia (7-10.5 × 5.5-6 μm) with four protuberant sterigmata (8.5-13 × 3.5-5 μm), and suballantoid to cylindrical basidiospores (5-6.5 × 2.5-3 μm, 6.1 × 3 μm on average). The Tulasnella sp. 3 clade consisted of one specimen that produced resupinate, pale violaceous (whitish when dry) basidioma, clampless-septate hyphae, basidia (7.5-12.5 × 4.5-7 μm) with four protuberant sterigmata (6.5-19 × 3.5-4.5 μm), and suballantoid to subphaseoliform basidiospores (5.5-7 × 3-4 μm, 6.2 × 3.4 μm on average). The Tulasnella sp. 4 clade (Figs. 2, 4; Table 3) consisted of one specimen that produced resupinate, whitish basidioma, clampless-septate hyphae, basidia (10-16.5 × 8-11 μm) with four protuberant sterigmata (11-19.5 × 6.5-7.5 μm), and sigmoid to thread-like basidiospores (26.5-48.5 × 2-3.5 μm, 35.1 × 2.6 μm on average). Tulasnella sp. 4 resembled T. deliquescens, mentioned by Roberts (1994b). However, the DNA sequences (EU218888; DQ388041-DQ388044) derived from orchid mycorrhizal isolates identified as T. deliquescens based on teleomorphic morphology (Roberts 1994b) were placed in a different phylogenetic clade (Fig. 1). In addition, Tulasnella sp. 4 (NBRC 117303) differed in culture characteristics from T. deliquescens obtained from the NARO Genebank: hyphae of the T. deliquescens cultures were binucleate, forming monilioid hyphae composed of chains of six or fewer cells, whereas those of Tulasnella sp. 4 were multinucleate, frequently forming monilioid hyphae consisting of chains of more than six cells (see Taxonomy and Discussion sections; Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. S1). The Japanese specimens consisting of Tulasnella sp. 5 (Figs. 2, 6; Table 3) produced resupinate, whitish basidiomata, clampless-septate hyphae, basidia (7.5-16.5 × 6-8.5 μm) with four protuberant sterigmata (10-17.5 × 4.5-5.5 μm), and ellipsoid to elongate basidiospores (5.5-7.5 × 3.5-4.5 μm, 6.2 × 4.1 μm on average). These characteristics are almost identical to those of T. albida (Cruz et al., 2016). The Tulasnella sp. 6 clade consisted of one specimen that produced resupinate, whitish basidioma, clampless-septate hyphae, basidia (8.5-9.5 × 6.5 μm) with four protuberant sterigmata (6.5-10.5 × 4-5 μm), and broadly ellipsoid to ellipsoid basidiospores (4.5-5.5 × 3.5-4 μm, 5 × 3.8 μm on average). Tulasnella sp. 6 differed from T. brigadeiroensis in its faster hyphal growth on PDA medium, slightly thicker hyphae (Supplementary Table S6), and occasional formation of monilioid hyphae (Supplementary Fig. S2).

3.3. Taxonomy

Because no known species corresponds to Tulasnella sp. 4 in terms of either morphology or phylogeny, we conclude that Tulasnella sp. 4 represents a novel species. Based on combined phylogenetic and morphological analyses, Tulasnella sp. 1 and Tulasnella sp. 5 were identified as T. eichleriana and T. albida, respectively. The morphological characteristics of these three species are described below. Tulasnella spp. 2, 3, and 6 are not described here because additional specimens are required for reliable species identification (see Discussion section). The characteristics of Tulasnella spp. 2, 3, and 6 are summarized in Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. S3.

4. Discussion

This is the first taxonomic study of Japanese Tulasnella species based on basidioma morphology, culture properties, and phylogenetic analyses. We recognized six Tulasnella species from Japan: T. eichleriana and T. albida (newly recorded in Japan), T. nematospora (a novel species), and three putatively novel taxa requiring further examination (see Table 3; Taxonomy section).

Tulasnella eichleriana was originally described by Bresadola (1903) in Great Britain. Our ITS phylogenetic tree showed that Japanese T. eichleriana specimens formed a clade with the T. eichleriana K(M)143600 specimen (accession no. KC152381), which was collected in England and identified as T. eichleriana by Cruz et al. (2014, 2016). Although ITS sequence divergences within the T. eichleriana clade were 0-3.2 % in the nrITS-5.8S region and 0-0.6 % in the 5.8S region (Supplementary Tables S1, S2), these values are within the range of intraspecific variation for this species (0-4%; Cruz et al., 2014). To date, morphospecies of T. eichleriana have been reported primarily in Europe (Belgium, Germany, Norway, Portugal, and United Kingdom), but also in South America (Ecuador, and Venezuela) and Asia (Taiwan) (Cruz et al., 2016). However, this species comprises several cryptic species. Cruz et al. (2014) proposed the T. eichleriana sensu stricto clade and suggested that only European basidiomata specimens fall within this clade. This study confirms its distribution in Japan, potentially representing the first report of T. eichleriana sensu stricto outside Europe. In Japan, this species has been collected from both lucidophyllous and deciduous broad-leaved forests (Table 1). The mean Warmth Index (Kira, 1948) at sites where we collected T. eichleriana during 2020-2024 was approximately 81-125, corresponding to cool- to warm-temperate climatic zones. The substrate of this species was the decaying wood of both coniferous and broad-leaved trees, and it has a long fruiting period (May to Nov). These findings suggest that T. eichleriana has adapted to inhabit a wide range of environments. While T. eichleriana is probably widely distributed across both Japan and Europe (Cruz et al., 2016), no orchid mycorrhizal associations have been reported, even from metagenomic analyses.

Tulasnella albida was originally reported in France by Bourdot and Galzin (1928). Because molecular data derived from the type materials are not available, we used sequence data for specimens identified as T. albida or Tulasnella cf. albida (Cruz et al., 2014) in our phylogenetic analyses. Our ITS phylogenetic tree showed that our Japanese T. albida specimens formed a clade with the T. albida specimens reported by Cruz et al. (2014). Our study confirms the distribution of T. albida in Japan, as it was collected from a lucidophyllous forest in Tottori Pref. Its fruiting period appears to be shorter (Jan to Mar) than that of T. eichleriana. According to Oberwinkler et al. (2017), T. albida has not previously been recorded in Asia, making this the first report from the region.

We describe Tulasnella nematospora as a new species, despite its morphological similarity to T. deliquescens (see Taxonomy section and Table 3). Epulorhiza repens was formerly recognized as an anamorph of T. calospora (Warcup & Talbot, 1967), but it is currently regarded as an anamorph of T. deliquescens (Roberts, 1994b). Moore (1996) designated specimen No. 33549 (C), the dried specimen of CBS 573.83 strain, originally isolated by Warcup (isolates 062; Warcup & Talbot, 1967), as a neotype of E. repens. Taylor & McCormick (2008) subsequently determined the DNA sequence of this strain (accession no. EU218888). In the present study, Tulasnella nematospora held a different phylogenetic position within the clade that included the CBS573.83 strain (EU218888). Fujimori et al. (2019) reported that T. deliquescens is divided into two clades; however, T. nematospora formed an independent clade (Fig. 1). As noted above, these two species can be distinguished by characteristics of their cultured mycelia: hyphal cells of T. deliquescens are binucleate and form monilioid hyphae composed of six or fewer chained cells, whereas those of T. nematospora are multinucleate and frequently form monilioid hyphae with more than six chained cells (Supplementary Fig. S1). Although DNA sequences of T. deliquescens isolated by Warcup from Australian orchids are available, there are currently no available sequences of the T. deliquescens type specimen, including its synonymized species T. rosella Bourdot & Galzin, nor sequences from basidiomata collected near its type locality. Moreover, the number of nuclei per hyphal cell in the type specimen of this species has apparently not been determined (Roberts, 1999). However, T. nematospora is phylogenetically distantly related to both clades of orchid-derived T. deliquescens and, to date, only one multinucleate Tulasnella species has been described: T. multinucleata Arifin, T.W. May & Linde (Arifin et al., 2022). These facts strongly suggest that T. nematospora is a novel species distinct from T. deliquescens.

We also detected three potentially undescribed species, Tulasnella spp. 2, 3, and 6 (Supplementary Fig. S3; Table 3). Because each is currently represented by a single specimen, additional collections and taxonomic study are required prior to formal description. Tulasnella sp. 2 is morphologically similar to T. rubropallens Bourdot & Galzin in terms of basidiospore shape and size (Cruz et al., 2016); however, DNA sequence data are not currently available for T. rubropallens. Similarly, Tulasnella sp. 3 resembles T. rubropallens, particularly in the color of its basidiomata and shape of its basidiospores (Cruz et al., 2016). The ITS sequence of Tulasnella sp. 6 differs by 6.2% from that of the ex-holotype of T. brigadeiroensis, which is described as an orchid mycorrhizal fungus of Cattleya jongheana (Rchb.f.) Van den Berg (syn. Hadrolaelia jongheana (Rchb.f.) Chiron & V.P.Castro (Orchidaceae)). However, the Tulasnella sp. 6 specimen was poorly preserved, preventing detailed morphological examination. Tulasnella brigadeiroensis was described from cultured material, and its teleomorphic characters were not documented (Freitas et al., 2020). Therefore, future study will be required to determine whether Tulasnella sp. 6 is conspecific with T. brigadeiroensis. The discovery of teleomorphic specimens of T. brigadeiroensis or the development of reliable methods to induce teleomorph formation in culture will be essential. Moreover, Tulasnella sp. 6 differs from T. brigadeiroensis in colony diam, hyphal thickness, and monilioid hypha formation (Supplementary Table S6), suggesting that they may represent distinct species.

Tulasnella sp. 6 also showed high ITS sequence similarity (99.3%) with the mycorrhizal fungus of Phalaenopsis japonica (Linden et Rchb.f.) Kocyan et Schuit. (Orchidaceae) (accession no. LC746360) detected by Chamara et al. (2024). Chamara et al. (2024) collected the LC746360 sample from Kagoshima Pref., Japan, while Tulasnella sp. 6 was collected from Tottori Pref. Phalaenopsis japonica does not occur in Tottori Pref.; therefore, it remains uncertain whether Tulasnella sp. 6 is truly a mycobiont of P. japonica. Indeed, many Tulasnella species can also persist as saprotrophs; therefore, basidioma of Tulasnella, even those forming orchid mycorrhizae, may appear outside orchid habitats. Continued collection both within and beyond the host plant ranges may reveal additional basidiomata of Tulasnella, including the species previously known only from mycorrhizal studies.

In summary, we identified six Tulasnella clades based on collected basidiomata; five of these clades were not recognized in previous surveys of orchid mycorrhizal fungi, as they shared less than 95% ITS sequence similarity with known mycorrhizal species (Supplementary Table S5). All 16 basidiomata were collected from decayed wood, suggesting that these six species are saprotrophic on dead trees. Notably, several specimens were collected from environments apart from orchid habitats. Broader sampling across diverse environments, including forests, grasslands, wetlands, and drylands, may reveal a wider ecological range for Tulasnella. Coupled with those of previous studies (Tsutsumi et al., 2017, 2022), our findings highlight the potential diversity of Tulasnella species, including undescribed species, in Japan. In the present study, we collected many Tulasnella specimens in autumn and winter, suggesting that basidiomata may be produced after the temperature drops. Humidity is likely another important factor, as we collected basidiomata after rain or near mountain streams. Continued efforts to collect Tulasnella teleomorphs and to integrate taxonomic and mycorrhizal data will be essential for clarifying species diversity, life cycles, and orchid symbioses in this genus.
