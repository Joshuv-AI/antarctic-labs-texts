NIRS monitoring during steep trendelenburg in robotic prostatectomy
Authors: L Campiglia, B Matta, Y Santoro, F Becherucci, Iacopo Cappellini, V Pavoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobotic-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy (RALP) requires steep Trendelenburg positioning and CO₂ pneumoperitoneum, which may significantly influence cerebral hemodynamic homeostasis. While postoperative neurological complications are uncommon, there is concern regarding increased intracranial pressure and potential edema during prolonged procedures. Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) allows continuous monitoring of cerebral oxygenation, rSO2 reflects the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption, integrating changes of oxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHbO2) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHHb). This study aimed to evaluate phase-dependent changes in cerebral oxygenation (rSO₂, ΔO₂Hb, ΔHHb) and optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) during RALP, and to assess their temporal correlation. This prospective observational study enrolled forty adult patients (ASA I–III) undergoing RALP in steep Trendelenburg position (30 degrees) at Nuovo Ospedale di Prato, Italy, between November 2024 and May 2025. Cerebral oxygenation was continuously monitored using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS). ONSD was measured bilaterally using ultrasound at predefined time points: baseline (T0), 45 min after Trendelenburg positioning (T1), 120 min after Trendelenburg (T2), during vesicourethral anastomosis (T3), and before extubation in neutral position (T4). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the association between NIRS-derived parameters and ONSD changes. Compared with baseline, Trendelenburg positioning was associated with significant phase-dependent changes in cerebral oxygenation and hemoglobin-derived parameters. rSO₂ and ΔO₂Hb increased during early and prolonged Trendelenburg phases, peaking during vesicourethral anastomosis (T3), and partially decreased after return to supine position (T4). ΔHHb demonstrated a progressive increase during later intraoperative phases, reaching highest values at T3 and T4. ONSD increased progressively across phases on both sides (right: 3.79 ± 0.36 mm at T0 to 4.59 ± 0.47 mm at T3; left: 3.80 ± 0.42 mm to 4.73 ± 0.47 mm). Correlation analysis demonstrated a moderate-to-strong association between ΔHHb and ONSD (right: r = 0.63, p < 10⁻¹⁶; left: r = 0.63, p < 10⁻¹⁶), supporting temporal coupling between cerebral deoxygenated hemoglobin accumulation and optic nerve sheath expansion. This study demonstrates distinct phase-dependent cerebral oxygenation patterns during RALP. The late-phase increase in ΔHHb occurring alongside ONSD enlargement may reflect cerebral venous congestion, a mechanism potentially involved in cerebral edema development during prolonged steep Trendelenburg positioning.Keywords: Near-infrared spectroscopy, Cerebral oxygenation, Robotic prostatectomy, Trendelenburg position, Optic nerve sheath diameter, Intracranial pressure

Introduction

Robotic surgery has emerged as a transformative technology in modern surgical practice, offering surgeons enhanced precision, and visualization compared to traditional approaches [1, 2]. This technological advancement has revolutionized various surgical specialties, including urology, gynecology, general surgery, otorhinolaryngology surgery and thoracic surgery.

The integration of robotic systems, such as the Da Vinci Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, CA, USA) has facilitated complex procedures and led to improved patient outcomes through minimally invasive approaches, reduced hemorrhage risk, lower postoperative pain and a consequent earlier discharge [3)].

However, the success of robotic surgery relies not only on the technological sophistication of the systems but also on meticulous preoperative planning, intraoperative management, and postoperative care [4].

Robotic surgery requires extreme patient positioning and application of CO2 pneumoperitoneum to allow adequate surgical exposure [5, 6]. Pneumoperitoneum and the consequent increase of intra-abdominal pressure can lead to physiological changes including decreased venous return, impaired cardiac function, hypercapnia, decrease of splanchnic and renal perfusion, and decreased cerebral venous outflow with increased risk of cerebral edema [7, 8].

Robotic-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy (RALP) specifically requires Trendelenburg positioning at 30 degrees. While this position offers significant surgical and technical advantages, it has the potential to significantly influence cerebral hemodynamic homeostasis with an increased risk of elevated intracranial pressure and cerebral edema [9, 10].

The European Association for Endoscopic Surgery guidelines recommend [11] using the lowest intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) compatible with an adequate view of the surgical field visualization, with recommended values between 8 and 10 mmHg to ensure stability of intraoperative clinical parameters and avoid intra-abdominal hypertension and organ ischemia [12].

Therefore, patient parameters must be monitored to understand the pathophysiological changes associated with the positioning and pneumoperitoneum, and to ensure optimal tissue perfusion both peripherally and cerebrally [13].

The effects of intraabdominal pressure and Trendelenburg position on intracranial pressure cannot be monitored with invasive methods in this surgical setting.

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) allows continuous, noninvasive monitoring of cerebral oxygenation and has been employed in various clinical areas including neurology, neurosurgery, vascular surgery, and cardiac surgery [14, 15]. NIRS assesses cerebral tissue oxygenation by emitting near-infrared light that penetrates the scalp and skull and is partially reflected by cerebral tissue, providing information on regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) and hemoglobin-derived parameters including oxygenated hemoglobin (ΔO₂Hb) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHHb) [16, 17].

Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) measured by ultrasound represents a noninvasive surrogate of intracranial pressure and has demonstrated high reliability and reproducibility across a wide range of clinical settings [18, 19]. To date, no studies have specifically evaluated combined changes in NIRS parameters and ONSD during Trendelenburg positioning in RALP, despite the potential risk of cerebral edema and rare but catastrophic complications such as postoperative visual loss [20, 21].

The aim of this study was to evaluate phase-dependent changes in cerebral oxygenation (rSO₂, ΔO₂Hb, ΔHHb) and ONSD during RALP, and to assess the temporal correlation between NIRS-derived parameters and optic nerve sheath expansion.

Methods

Study design and ethics approval

This prospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Area Vasta Toscana Centro on July 2024 (registration number 25336). The study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Study population

Forty adult patients (ASA physical status I–III) undergoing RALP with the Da Vinci V Robot (Intuitive Surgical, CA, USA) in steep Trendelenburg position were enrolled between November 2024 and May 2025 at Nuovo Ospedale di Prato, Prato, Italy. Patients with a history of ophthalmic or neurological diseases were excluded from this study. All enrolled patients had hemoglobin levels between 12 and 14 g/dL.

Anesthetic management

No premedication was administered. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was applied including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, noninvasive automated arterial pressure, train-of-four (TOF) neuromuscular blockade monitoring, temperature monitoring via esophageal probe, SedLine sensor for depth of anesthesia monitoring with Patient State Index (PSI, Masimo, CA, USA), and cerebral NIRS sensors (Masimo, CA, USA).

Anesthesia was induced with propofol 1–2 mg/kg, fentanyl 1–2 µg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg, followed by tracheal intubation. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane 1–1.5.5 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) in 40% oxygen/air, titrated according to Density Spectral Array (DSA), Spectral Edge Frequency (SEF), raw electroencephalogram trace, and PSI. Analgesia was provided by continuous infusion of remifentanil 0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min, titrated according to nociception patterns on DSA (beta arousal, alpha dropout, delta arousal). Magnesium sulfate (2 g) and lidocaine (1 mg/kg) were administered according to multimodal analgesia principles.

Acetaminophen 1 g, ketorolac 30 mg, tramadol 1.0–1.5.0.5 mg/kg, and ondansetron 4 mg in 100 mL of 0.9% saline solution were administered intravenously 40 min before the end of surgery. Gastric protection was provided with pantoprazole 40 mg intravenously. Neuromuscular blockade was maintained by continuous infusion of rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg/hour according to TOF monitoring.

Mechanical ventilation was performed with a tidal volume of 8 mL/kg of ideal body weight (IBW) [24], and respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure between 4.0 and 4.7 kPa during surgery. Normothermia was maintained with a forced air-warming system. Intraoperative fluid therapy consisted of balanced solutions at 6–8 mL/kg in accordance with Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) guidelines [25]. During surgery, mean arterial pressure was maintained above 60 mmHg in all patients, if mean arterial pressure decreased below 65 mmHg, intravenous ephedrine (5 mg) was administered.

Surgical procedure

After adequate patient positioning, the abdomen was insufflated with CO₂ to a pressure of 8–10 mmHg, and the patient was placed in mild Trendelenburg position for trocar placement. Subsequently, patients were slowly positioned in steep Trendelenburg (30 degrees from horizontal). The abdominal cavity was insufflated with CO₂ using the AirSeal System (ConMed, NY, USA) to enable working at low and constant intra-abdominal pressures (8–10 mmHg). At the end of the procedure, the table position was normalized and pneumoperitoneum was released. After surgical completion, patients were awakened and transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for postoperative monitoring. Once the Aldrete Score exceeded 8, patients were transferred to the surgery department.

NIRS monitoring

Cerebral oxygenation was continuously monitored using NIRS sensors (Masimo, CA, USA) placed bilaterally on the forehead. NIRS values (rSO₂, ΔO₂Hb, ΔHHb) were recorded at predefined time points: before induction of anesthesia in neutral position (T0, baseline), 45 min after Trendelenburg positioning at 30 degrees (T1), 120 min after Trendelenburg positioning (T2), during vesicourethral anastomosis (T3), and before extubation in neutral position (T4). (Fig. 1).

NIRS and ONSD parameters measurement times

ONSD measurement

Optic nerve sheath diameter was measured bilaterally by a single investigator using a linear 7.5 MHz ultrasound probe at the following time points: before induction of anesthesia in neutral position (T0, baseline), 45 min after Trendelenburg positioning at 30 degrees (T1), during vesicourethral anastomosis (T3), and before extubation in neutral position (T4). (Fig. 1)

Statistical analysis

Correlation analyses were performed to assess the association between changes in NIRS-derived parameters and optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) over time. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated on an intraindividual basis using the variations (Δ values) observed between predefined time points for each patient. To account for interindividual variability and repeated measurements, patient-specific correlation coefficients were subsequently averaged, and the mean Pearson correlation coefficient was reported for each parameter. Correlations were computed separately for the right and left hemispheres. The analyzed NIRS-derived variables included regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂), oxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHbO₂), deoxygenated hemoglobin (ΔHHb), and total hemoglobin (ΔHbc, defined as ΔHbO₂ + ΔHHb). Correlation strength was classified as weak (r < 0.30), moderate (r = 0.30–0.59), or strong (r ≥ 0.60). All statistical analyses were conducted using two-tailed tests, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The relationship between changes in cerebral oxygenation parameters and optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) was explored using scatter plot analysis, with individual data points representing paired measurements of NIRS-derived variables and ONSD on the right and left sides. Linear associations were quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r), and statistical significance was assessed with two-tailed p values. Statistical analysis was computed by Python version 3.12.11.

Power analysis (Post-hoc)

While a formal a priori power calculation was not performed, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted based on the observed primary outcome (correlation between Delta HHb and ONSD). For an observed correlation of r = 0.63 with a sample size of N = 40, the statistical power was > 99% at a significance level of alpha = 0.05. However, we acknowledge that the sample size is limited for defining precise clinical thresholds or diagnostic cut-off values with high sensitivity and specificity, as noted in the Limitations section.”

Discussion

This study demonstrates distinct phase-dependent cerebral oxygenation patterns during robotic-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy performed in steep Trendelenburg position. The main findings are: [1] rSO₂ and ΔO₂Hb increased progressively during Trendelenburg positioning, peaking during vesicourethral anastomosis; [2] ΔHHb demonstrated progressive increases during later intraoperative phases, reaching highest values alongside maximal ONSD enlargement; [3] moderate-to-strong correlation between ΔHHb and ONSD supports temporal coupling between cerebral deoxygenated hemoglobin accumulation and optic nerve sheath expansion.

The application of linear mixed-effects models allowed for a nuanced characterization of the relationship between cerebral oxygenation and optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD). Our analysis reveals a distinct divergence in the predictive value of hemoglobin derivatives. Specifically, deoxygenated hemoglobin (Delta HHb) emerged as a robust and significant predictor of ONSD expansion on both hemispheres (Right: beta = 0.0127, p = 0.0011; Left: beta = 0.0134, p = 0.0021). In contrast, oxygenated hemoglobin (Delta O_2Hb) alone demonstrated no statistically significant association with ONSD changes (Right: p = 0.3017; Left: p = 0.3607), suggesting that increased oxygen delivery is not the primary driver of optic nerve sheath expansion in this setting.

These findings are physiologicaly significant. The fact that total hemoglobin (\Delta cHb) remains a significant predictor (Right: p = 0.0179; Left: p = 0.0235) despite the non-significance of the oxygenated component further reinforces the hypothesis that deoxygenated blood accumulation—indicative of impaired venous drainage—is the principal mechanism driving intracranial pressure markers during steep Trendelenburg positioning.

Furthermore, while regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO_2) showed a strong association on the right side (p = 0.0003), it only demonstrated a trend toward significance on the left (p = 0.0704). This hemispheric asymmetry, combined with the consistent performance of \Delta HHb across both sides, suggests that monitoring deoxygenated hemoglobin may provide a more reliable and sensitive indicator of cerebral venous congestion than global saturation values alone.

Cerebral oxygenation monitoring in surgical settings

Tissue oxygenation is fundamental to anesthetic monitoring. Near-infrared spectroscopy has been employed since the 1970 s as a noninvasive, continuous monitor of the balance between cerebral oxygen delivery and consumption [22, 23, 26]. NIRS assesses cerebral tissue oxygenation by emitting near-infrared light (650–1000 nm) that penetrates the scalp and is partially reflected by cerebral tissue, utilizing the Beer-Lambert law and spatial resolution to estimate cerebral hemoglobin oxygen saturation.

The tissue saturation measured by cerebral oximetry represents a combination of arterial, capillary, and venous blood [28]. The proportion of cerebral venous blood can vary widely (33–84%), depending on physiological and clinical factors including arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure, arterial oxygen partial pressure, blood pH, head positioning, and pharmacological agents. NIRS provides indirect information on cerebral blood flow through variations in oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin concentrations, although these measurements are influenced by cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen and cerebral blood volume [29].

In the intraoperative setting, decreases in regional cerebral oxygen saturation greater than 10% from baseline or prolonged exposure to rSO₂ values below 50% have been associated with postoperative complications including postoperative cognitive dysfunction and cerebral ischemia [30]. However, there is a notable lack of studies investigating the determinants and clinical significance of increases in cerebral oxygenation values. Our data suggest that during RALP, increases in NIRS values may represent impaired cerebral venous outflow, potentially indicating cerebral edema development and increased intracranial pressure.

Pathophysiology of cerebral changes during RALP

During laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery, the combination of pneumoperitoneum and steep Trendelenburg positioning induces profound pathophysiological alterations affecting cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and cerebral systems [31, 32]. Increases in intracranial pressure and potential cerebral edema development are of particular concern during prolonged procedures, especially in patients with reduced intracranial compliance or pre-existing impairments in cerebrovascular autoregulation [33, 34].

Under these conditions, cerebral edema is vasogenic [35]. Impaired cerebral venous outflow leads to increased capillary hydrostatic pressure, promoting fluid extravasation into the interstitial space. Early increases in rSO₂ and ΔO₂Hb observed in our study reflect increased cerebral blood volume and flow during initial head-down tilt. However, the progressive increase in ΔHHb during later intraoperative phases, occurring alongside optic nerve sheath expansion, suggests impaired cerebral venous outflow and venous congestion.

In robot-assisted surgery, patient positioning cannot be modified once the robotic system is docked; therefore, preventive intraoperative strategies are essential [36]. These include use of low intra-abdominal pressure, optimization of ventilatory settings to maintain normocapnia, adequate neuromuscular blockade, and avoidance of excessive fluid administration to mitigate cerebral venous congestion [35].

ONSD as surrogate marker of intracranial pressure

Among noninvasive methods for estimating intracranial pressure, ultrasound assessment of optic nerve sheath diameter is a simple bedside tool that has demonstrated high reliability and reproducibility across various clinical settings [37]. In our study, ONSD increased progressively across intraoperative phases bilaterally, with maximal values observed during vesicourethral anastomosis. The observed moderate-to-strong correlation between ΔHHb and ONSD (r = 0.63 bilaterally) is consistent with the multifactorial nature of intracranial dynamics and supports temporal association rather than direct causality. Notably, Delta HHb exhibited a stronger association with ONSD compared to rSO_2 or Delta O_2Hb, suggesting that cerebral venous congestion, rather than increased oxygen delivery, is the predominant mechanism driving the increase in intracranial pressure markers in this surgical setting.

Although postoperative neurological complications are uncommon following RALP [38], minor clinical symptoms such as headache and nausea may occur. More concerning is the potential risk of significant visual impairment due to optic nerve injury secondary to increased intracranial pressure and cerebral edema. Postoperative visual loss after RALP is rare (0.02–0.1%) but represents a catastrophic complication [39].

Clinical implications

Continuous NIRS monitoring, particularly ΔHHb trends, may provide complementary information to intermittent ONSD assessment for identifying patients at risk of cerebral congestion or edema during robotic surgery. The combination of multimodal neuromonitoring with NIRS and ultrasound-measured ONSD may enable early detection of cerebral venous congestion and guide intraoperative management strategies.

In our cohort, despite the observed phase-dependent increases in ONSD and the significant correlation with cerebral venous congestion (ΔHHb), no episodes of postoperative delirium were detected. All patients were screened daily using the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) during the first 24 h after surgery, and all scores remained negative [40]. This suggests that while the steep Trendelenburg position induces measurable changes in intracranial pressure markers and cerebral hemodynamics, these alterations may not reach the clinical threshold required to trigger immediate cognitive dysfunction in this specific population.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. The observational design does not allow causal inferences regarding the relationship between cerebral oxygenation changes and optic nerve sheath diameter. The observed association between phase-dependent increases in ΔHHb and ONSD should be interpreted as temporal coupling rather than evidence of direct mechanistic linkage.

The small sample size and single-center nature of the study may limit generalizability of findings. Although consistent trends were observed across intraoperative phases, larger cohorts are needed to confirm the robustness of these results and to account for interindividual variability in cerebral hemodynamic responses to prolonged Trendelenburg positioning.

Intracranial pressure was not directly measured. ONSD, while a validated noninvasive surrogate, remains an indirect estimate and may be influenced by anatomical and operator-dependent factors. The present study was not powered to identify clinically meaningful thresholds. Future research should aim to define specific ONSD cutoff values associated with significant increases in ΔHHb, which may serve as indicators of impaired cerebral venous outflow or early cerebral congestion.

A potential limitation is the use of sevoflurane during anesthesia, as it induces cerebral vasodilation and may contribute to increased NIRS values.

Future directions

Larger prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore whether sustained elevations in ΔHHb combined with ONSD expansion are associated with postoperative neurological or visual complications. Integration of multimodal monitoring, including NIRS, ONSD, and advanced cerebral hemodynamic parameters, may help clarify the clinical significance of increased cerebral oxygenation values and distinguish adaptive from potentially harmful cerebral responses. Establishing combined NIRS-ONSD thresholds could improve intraoperative risk stratification and facilitate development of clinically actionable neuromonitoring algorithms during robotic surgery.

Future studies are warranted to clarify whether increases in ΔHHb correlate with the onset of postoperative delirium, potentially reflecting cerebral venous congestion, and to determine whether optimization of ΔHHb dynamics may also help prevent visual disturbances related to optic nerve injury secondary to elevated intracranial pressure.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates distinct phase-dependent cerebral oxygenation patterns during robotic-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy. The progressive increase in deoxygenated hemoglobin occurring alongside optic nerve sheath diameter enlargement suggests impaired cerebral venous outflow and potential venous congestion during prolonged steep Trendelenburg positioning. Continuous NIRS monitoring combined with intermittent ONSD assessment may provide complementary neuromonitoring strategies for identifying patients at risk of cerebral congestion during robotic surgery.
