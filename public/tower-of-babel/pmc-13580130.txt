Toward Imbalanced Molecular Property Regression: A Benchmark Study and Interval-Aware Mixture of Experts
Authors: Yan Sun, Yu Shi, Alana Deng, Zihao Jing, Carson Leung, Pingzhao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular property prediction is a key task in AI-driven drug discovery, yet the prevalence and impact of label imbalances in molecular property regression remain poorly understood. Through a systematic benchmark of widely used molecular property data sets, we show that target values are often highly imbalanced and that prediction errors are consistently concentrated in sparsely represented regions of the label space. We further evaluate representative imbalance-learning approaches developed for general regression tasks and find that their effectiveness on molecular data sets is limited. To address this challenge, we propose interval-aware mixture-of-experts (IA-MoE), a plug-in framework that partitions the continuous target space into intervals and promotes expert specialization across different regions of the label distribution. IA-MoE can be seamlessly integrated with diverse molecular encoders without modifying their underlying architectures. Experiments on five molecular property benchmarks and four graph neural network backbones demonstrate that IA-MoE consistently improves the predictive performance in underrepresented regions while maintaining or improving overall accuracy. Our findings establish label imbalance as an important challenge in molecular property regression and highlight interval-aware expert learning as an effective strategy for addressing this challenge.

Introduction

Predicting molecular properties from chemical structures is a fundamental task in AI-driven drug discovery, supporting applications ranging from pharmacokinetic modeling and toxicity assessment to lead optimization and virtual screening. − Recent advances in graph neural networks (GNNs) and molecular language models have significantly improved predictive performance by learning expressive molecular representations from graph structures and SMILES sequences. − Despite these advances, most existing studies primarily focus on improving overall predictive accuracy, often overlooking how model performance varies across different regions of the target distribution.

In practice, molecular property data sets frequently exhibit highly imbalanced label distributions. A large proportion of molecules are concentrated in a narrow range of target values, while only a small fraction occupy sparsely populated regions of the label space. Figure illustrates this phenomenon on the Lipophilicity data set. The label density rapidly decreases as log P values increase, while prediction errors from a representative GNN model simultaneously increase in these underrepresented regions. Notably, compounds with Log P values greater than five are associated with elevated risks of poor absorption and permeability according to Lipinski’s Rule of Five. Consequently, prediction errors in these low-density regions may lead to inaccurate compound prioritization during virtual screening and lead to optimization. More broadly, this example suggests that current molecular property prediction models may exhibit systematic performance degradation in sparsely represented regions, despite achieving strong overall performance.

The green curve denotes the kernel density estimation (KDE) of the target distribution, and the orange curve shows the mean absolute error (MAE) of a DeeperGCN model. Prediction errors increase substantially in sparsely represented regions of the label space. In particular, compounds with Log P > 5 are both underrepresented and associated with larger prediction errors, highlighting label imbalance as a potential challenge in molecular property regression.

Although label imbalance has been extensively studied in classification tasks, its counterpart in regression settings remains relatively underexplored. Existing approaches for imbalanced regression can generally be categorized into three groups. Data-level methods, such as SMOTER and SMOGN, , and the recently proposed graph augmentation framework SPECTRA, generate synthetic samples to improve coverage in sparsely represented regions. Loss-level methods, including LDS, DenseWeight, and Balanced MSE, modify sample weighting or training objectives to alleviate distribution bias. Feature-level approaches, such as FDS and QuanRel, improve representation learning under imbalance through feature distribution smoothing or target-aware feature regularization. Despite these advances, their effectiveness in molecular property regression remains insufficiently understood. Most imbalance learning methods were originally developed and validated on general regression tasks rather than molecular prediction benchmarks. Molecular data sets exhibit unique characteristics, including structured graph representations, limited sample sizes, and strict chemical constraints, which may substantially affect the behavior of the existing imbalance-handling strategies. As a result, an evaluation of different imbalance learning approaches in molecular property regression is still lacking.

Motivated by these observations, we evaluated representative imbalance-learning approaches on molecular property prediction benchmarks to better understand the impact of label imbalance in this domain. At the same time, we investigate whether architectural solutions can provide an alternative perspective for addressing imbalances in molecular property regression.

To this end, we propose interval-aware mixture-of-experts (IA-MoE), a lightweight plug-in framework that explicitly incorporates target-space structure into the learning process. Unlike conventional Mixture-of-Experts architectures that rely on input-dependent routing mechanisms, − IA-MoE partitions the continuous target space into predefined intervals and encourages expert specialization across different regions of the label distribution. For each molecule, representations from the two nearest interval experts are softly fused to generate the final prediction, enabling smooth transitions between neighboring target regions while reducing interference between densely and sparsely represented regions. As a plug-in module, IA-MoE can be seamlessly integrated with diverse molecular encoders without modifying their underlying architectures.

Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate model performance using mean absolute error (MAE) and Pearson correlation coefficient. In addition to overall test performance, we assess model behavior under a target distribution imbalance.

Specifically, label density is estimated using kernel density estimation (KDE) on the training set. Samples belonging to the lowest 20% density regions are categorized as low-density samples, while the remaining samples are categorized as high-density samples. We report performance separately for these two groups to evaluate predictive reliability across different regions of the target distribution.

To ensure a comprehensive evaluation, all methods are benchmarked on four representative molecular encoders, namely, GAT, DeeperGCN, ChemBERTa, and GROVER, covering both graph-based and transformer-based molecular representations.

Benchmarked Imbalance-Learning Methods

We compare IA-MoE against representative imbalance-learning approaches that address target distribution imbalance at different levels. Specifically, we consider data-level augmentation methods represented by SPECTRA, which generates synthetic molecular graphs to improve coverage in underrepresented regions; loss-level methods including LDS and DenseWeight, which mitigate imbalance through label-density smoothing and density-aware sample reweighting, respectively; and feature-level methods including FDS, , which smooth feature statistics across neighboring label regions, and QuanRel, which introduces target-aware feature regularization to improve representation learning under imbalanced regression. Overview of IA-MoE

Preliminaries and Notation

Let {(x i , y i )} i = 1 N denote the training set, where x i denotes a molecular input and yi∈R is the corresponding continuous-valued molecular property. We partition the label space Y into B intervals {I 1,..., I B } based on quantiles, with boundaries [y b–1, y b ], where b∈B={1,...,B} indexes the intervals. We assign an expert to each interval, enabling expert specialization across different regions of the target space. Given an input molecule x, a molecular encoder f(x; θ) produces a latent representation z∈Rd , which is used for expert routing and prediction.

Target-Space Decomposition and Expert Specialization

Existing imbalance-learning strategies improve learning in underrepresented target regions through data augmentation, loss reweighting, or feature and label distribution calibration. Despite their mechanisms, the underlying predictors generally rely on a shared representation transformation across the target space. When the regression relationships vary across target regions, optimizing a single shared transformation requires the same parameters to accommodate the heterogeneous region-dependent patterns. IA-MoE relaxes this constraint by introducing target-dependent expert transformations before the shared prediction head, enabling region-specific representation learning while retaining a common output function.

To formalize this property, let H(·) denote the shared prediction head, and let I b contain N b training samples from target interval b. Equation provides a function-space motivation for target space decomposition. Allowing region-dependent transformations relaxes the constraint that heterogeneous target regions must be represented through the same transformation. The resulting function class contains the globally shared formulation as a special case and can additionally represent region-specific regression behavior when such a heterogeneity is present.

IA-MoE realizes this idea through the expert modules FFN b (·) and interval-aware routing. Rather than using completely independent transformations for different intervals, IA-MoE adopts soft Top-2 routing so that each sample can combine information from two relevant experts. This preserves localized expert capacity while allowing information sharing between related target regions. The following analysis further shows how this routing mechanism translates target-space decomposition into region-dependent expert optimization.

Gradient Analysis

The function-space analysis above characterizes the additional representation capacity introduced by target-space decomposition. We next examine how interval-aware routing induces expert specialization during optimization. Given the total training objective in eq , the gradient of the task loss with respect to the parameters w k of expert FFN k can be written as 13 ∂Ltask∂wk=∑i=1N∂Li∂zi′·gk(xi)·∂FFNk(zi)∂wk where g k (x i ) is the routing weight defined in eq .

Equation shows that the contribution of each training sample to an expert is explicitly modulated by its routing weight. Under interval-aware gating supervision, samples associated with interval I k and its neighboring regions are encouraged to assign larger routing weights to expert k, whereas samples from more distant target regions receive smaller weights. Consequently, the effective gradient received by each expert is concentrated in a localized portion of the target space.

When routing weights outside the relevant neighborhood of I k are small, eq can be approximated as 14 ∂Ltask∂wk≈∑j:yj∈neighbor(Ik)∂Lj∂zj′·gk(xj)·∂FFNk(zj)∂wk

The localized gradient in eq provides the optimization mechanism underlying expert specialization. Instead of receiving equally direct optimization signals from the entire target distribution, each expert is updated primarily by samples assigned to its corresponding target region and nearby intervals. This reduces cross-region interference in expert-specific parameters and allows different experts to capture region-dependent representations. At the same time, top-2 routing retains information sharing between related experts, avoiding a completely isolated optimization of individual target intervals. Together, the function-space decomposition and routing-dependent gradient modulation explain how IA-MoE introduces localized representation capacity while maintaining a shared prediction head.

Experimental Configuration

In our experiments, we fix the number of intervals (and thus the number of experts) to B = 8 across all data sets. For the interval-aware gating supervision defined in eq , the smoothness hyperparameter is set to σ = 0.3. All models are trained using the Adam optimizer, and model selection is performed based on the best validation performance. Following common practice in molecular property prediction, model performance is evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC). Analysis

Effect of Gating Supervision on Expert Specialization

We introduce interval-aware gating supervision to encourage each expert to focus on a specific interval. As illustrated in Figure a, with this supervision, experts specialize in distinct regions of the target space, forming a structured partition that is aligned with the predefined intervals. In contrast, without supervision (Figure b), the routing becomes disorganized, with experts either overlapping in narrow ranges or remaining underutilized.

Expert specialization across five data sets. Upper panels (a): Distribution of assigned sample values per expert with interval-aware gating supervision. Lower panels (b): Results without the supervision.

This collapse behavior is reflected in the degraded performance (Table ). Without gate supervision, the model fails to utilize multiple experts, leading to suboptimal specialization. In extreme cases such as Caco2, routing degenerates to a single expert, effectively reducing the model to the backbone and resulting in near-baseline performance. In contrast, gating supervision encourages experts to specialize in distinct intervals, yielding consistent improvements in both MAE and PCC across data sets. These results highlight its importance in preventing expert collapse and enabling effective expert diversification.

Sensitivity to Target-Space Partitioning

Figure illustrates the impact of expert count B on model performance across five benchmarks. Overall, IA-MoE shows limited sensitivity to the number of experts, with performance remaining stable across a wide range of B values. However, when B becomes excessively large (e.g., B = 50), noticeable degradation is observed across all data sets, suggesting that overly fine-grained partitioning leads to insufficient samples per expert. A similar but more pronounced effect appears on PPBR, where performance degrades when B > 10, likely due to its more extreme long-tailed distribution. Overall, the best performance is consistently achieved with a relatively small number of experts (B < 10), indicating that coarse partitioning is sufficient and the method remains robust within a reasonable range.

We further examined whether performance depends on how the target-space boundaries are constructed. In addition to the default quantile partitioning, we evaluated equal-width partitioning and an adaptive strategy based on one-dimensional K-means clustering of the target values. No single strategy consistently performs best across all data sets and metrics, while quantile partitioning provides relatively stable performance, particularly in low-density regions. Prediction Distribution and Alignment

To assess the ability of the models to capture the structure of molecular property distributions, we compare the predictive kernel density estimates across all data sets (Figure ). In imbalanced regression, models often produce overconcentrated, unimodal predictions centered on high-density regions, failing to reflect more complex structures in the target distribution. This effect is particularly evident in the Caco2 and solubility data sets. The baseline model produces a single dominant peak, whereas IA-MoE can capture more complex local structures in the distribution, reflecting additional density patterns that are present in the ground truth. For lipophilicity and PPBR, the overall shapes of the predicted distributions are similar across methods. However, IA-MoE produces peak locations that are more closely aligned with the ground truth distribution, indicating improved calibration in high-density regions. In contrast, for LD50, both methods exhibit similar distributional behavior, suggesting that the IA-MoE maintains comparable performance without introducing degradation. Overall, IA-MoE captures more complex distributional patterns when present, improves peak alignment in high-density regions, and maintains comparable performance across data sets.

Error Distribution on Low-Density Samples

To quantify the impact of improved distributional modeling in low-density regions, we analyze the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of absolute errors on rare samples (Figure ), which measures the proportion of samples with prediction error below a given threshold. IA-MoE consistently shifts the error distribution toward lower values compared to the GAT baseline across data sets, indicating that a larger fraction of samples achieve low prediction errors. This effect is particularly evident in Caco2 and solubility, where IA-MoE reduces the median error on rare samples (e.g., 0.142 in Caco2), reflecting improved reliability in sparse regions. In lipophilicity and PPBR, the improvements are more moderate but still observable across the error distribution. For data sets such as LD50, where the median error remains comparable to the baseline, IA-MoE preserves performance without degradation. These results are consistent with the distributional patterns observed in the previous distribution alignment analysis. By reducing overconcentration in high-density regions and improving coverage of low-density regions, IA-MoE reduces high-error predictions on rare samples, leading to a higher proportion of low-error outcomes and improved robustness across the target space.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the problem of target distribution imbalance in molecular property regression. We first conduct a benchmark study of representative imbalance-learning approaches spanning data-level, loss-level, and feature-level strategies. Our results show that, although these methods can improve performance in certain settings, their effectiveness is often inconsistent across molecular data sets and model architectures.

Motivated by these observations, we propose interval-aware mixture-of-experts (IA-MoE), a lightweight architectural framework that promotes expert specialization across different regions of the continuous target space. Extensive experiments on five molecular property prediction benchmarks demonstrate that IA-MoE consistently improves the predictive performance in low-density regions while maintaining competitive performance in high-density regions. Compared with existing imbalance learning methods, IA-MoE achieves more balanced and robust prediction behavior across the target distribution.

Overall, our findings suggest that architectural approaches provide a promising direction for addressing target imbalance in molecular property regression and may complement existing data-, loss-, and feature-level techniques. One limitation of the current framework is that interval partitioning is predefined and does not explicitly adapt to the underlying target distribution. Future work could explore adaptive or data-driven interval construction strategies as well as extensions of the proposed framework to other continuous prediction tasks beyond molecular property modeling.
