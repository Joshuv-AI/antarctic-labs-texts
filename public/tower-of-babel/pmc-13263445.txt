Rare 19q13.42 duplication encompassing PRKCG associated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities
Authors: Jiasun Su, Lin Yang, Huiping Li, Ping Dong, Sujuan Wang, Guoqiang Cheng, Bingbing Wu, Huijun Wang, Yulan Lu, Wenhao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuplications at the 19q13.42 region are rare, and the relationship between 19q13.42 duplications and neurodevelopmental phenotypes remains poorly characterized due to the paucity of cases with well-documented clinical features.MethodsCases with 19q13.42 duplications were reviewed from a cohort of over seventy-five thousand pediatric patients. These patients, suspected of having genetic diseases, underwent next-generation sequencing (NGS) at two collaborating medical centers between 2016 and December 2024. Cases were identified and subjected to further phenotypic and genomic analysis. The reported cases of 19q13.42 duplications were also searched in public resources.ResultsTwenty patients, including 10 cases from our study and 10 cases from public resources with 19q13.42 duplication were enrolled. The duplication breakpoints detected by NGS in internal cases were nonrecurrent and the duplication sizes ranged from 76.864 to 724.194 Kb, which were verified by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) were the primary clinical phenotype, present in 94.1% (16/17) of patients. All 16 cases harbored duplications involving PRKCG, of which 12 contained complete gene duplications and 4 involved partial duplications. Genotype-phenotype correlation analysis and the smallest region of overlap (SRO) mapping indicates that PRKCG is a candidate gene underlying the associated NDD phenotypes.ConclusionsWe observed recurrent neurodevelopmental features in a subset of reported 19q13.42 duplications and propose PRKCG as a candidate gene warranting follow-up functional studies. These findings provide a preliminary genotype–phenotype framework for this region, though additional cases with comprehensive clinical phenotyping are required to validate and refine these observations.Keywords: 19q13.42, duplication, PRKCG, pediatrics, neurodevelopmental delay

Highlight box

Key findings

• The majority of cases harboring the 19q13.42 duplication presented with a variable yet broadly consistent phenotypic spectrum, predominantly manifesting as neurodevelopmental abnormalities.

• Genotype-phenotype correlation analysis and smallest-region-of-overlap (SRO) mapping identified PRKCG as a plausible candidate gene potentially contributing to the observed neurodevelopmental phenotypes within the 19q13.42 duplication region.

What is known and what is new?

• Copy-number variants can cause human genetic diseases associated with neurodevelopmental disorders.

• This is the first study of detailed phenotypic features and molecular basis of the 19q13.42 duplication, with PRKCG proposed as a candidate gene requiring further investigation regarding its potential phenotypic association

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• We observed recurrent neurodevelopmental features in a subset of reported 19q13.42 duplications and propose PRKCG as a candidate for follow-up functional studies, with potential implications for enhancing genetic diagnosis and counseling in future clinical management of similar conditions.

Introduction

Copy-number variants (CNVs) have been strongly linked to human genetic diseases (1), particularly pediatric conditions including congenital structural anomalies (2) and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) such as intellectual disability/developmental delay (ID/DD) (3,4), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (5) and epilepsy (6). The pathogenic potential of CNVs depends on several factors, including genomic size, the number of genes, the inheritance patterns of covered genes, and the specific chromosomal region involved (7).

Chromosome 19 exhibits the highest gene density among human chromosomes, containing more than double the genome-wide average gene density (8). The elevated guanine-cytosine (GC) content and abundance of repetitive elements characteristic of chromosome 19 contribute to its increased susceptibility to chromosomal rearrangements (9). While several disease-associated microdeletion and microduplication syndromes affecting the short arm of chromosome 19 have been well-characterized (10-13), no established syndromic entities have been definitively associated with the long arm (19q) to date. However, partial or interstitial duplications of 19q, particularly within the 19q13 region, have garnered increasing attention due to their association with various developmental disorders and growth abnormalities (14-18).

The 19q13.42 region (chr19:53,600,001–56,300,000, 2.7 Mb, hg19) harbors 103 protein-coding genes. Thirteen genes are known to be disease-causing, three (PRKCG, CNOT3, and U2AF2) of them have been linked to autosomal dominant developmental disorders or spinocerebellar ataxia. PRKCG (OMIM #176980) encodes protein kinase C gamma, a member of the protein kinase C family that plays crucial roles in neuronal development and synaptic function (19). Pathogenic variants in PRKCG have been associated with spinocerebellar ataxia type 14 (SCA14, OMIM #605361), characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia and cognitive impairment (20). Similarly, de novo or inherited germline variants in CNOT3 (OMIM #604910) or U2AF2 (OMIM #191318) have recently been identified as causative factors in clinically variable developmental disorders (21-24). However, the dosage sensitivity of these three genes and the broader 19q13.42 genomic region remains unknown due to limited evidence curated by the ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity Curation working groups (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/). Currently, fewer than 10 cases involving pure 19q13.42 duplications without additional potential pathogenic CNVs are recorded in the DECIPHER database (https://www.deciphergenomics.org/). Furthermore, PRKCG-containing 19q13.42 duplications are absent in healthy individuals annotated by the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV), indicating that such duplications are quite rare.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has emerged as a powerful diagnostic tool in clinical genomics, enabling simultaneous detection of both single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and CNVs (25,26). Advanced bioinformatic algorithms have substantially enhanced CNV detection from NGS data, in some instances surpassing the accuracy of low-resolution chromosomal microarray analysis (27). Highly sensitive and specific CNV detection pipelines have been developed and validated for NGS-based diagnostics (25,26,28).

In this study, we present a retrospective case series of patients with 19q13.42 duplications. By integrating our findings with literature and public database records, we observed that the majority of patients with 19q13.42 duplications exhibited recurrent neurodevelopmental features. Smallest region of overlap (SRO) analysis supported PRKCG as a plausible candidate gene potentially contributing to the observed phenotypes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-1-0073/rc).

Methods

Subjects

Cases with 19q13.42 duplications were collected from a cohort of seventy-five thousand pediatric patients suspected of genetic diseases who underwent NGS at two collaborating medical centers, the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University and the Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center of Guangzhou Medical University, between 2016 and December 2024. The identified cases were enrolled for further analysis.

The clinical information was extracted from electronic medical records, supplemented with follow-up information obtained via telephone interviews conducted by clinical professionals. Neurodevelopmental status was assessed using standardized criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). DD is diagnosed in children under 5 years who failed to meet expected developmental milestones in two or more domains (motor, speech/language, cognitive, social/emotional, or activities of daily living) (29). ID is defined as intelligence quotient (IQ) <70 and limitations in adaptive functioning, with an onset in children over 5 years of age. Speech delay is defined as inability to demonstrate speech-language skills that is expected according to the age. Epilepsy was defined by the occurrence of two unprovoked (or reflex) epileptic seizures more than twenty-four hours apart (30). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (No. 2025-321A01) and the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (No. 2015-130), and informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians of all patients.

A comprehensive literature review was conducted using PubMed and public databases, including the DECIPHER (https://www.deciphergenomics.org/) and UNIQUE (https://rarechromo.org/registered-chromosome-disorders/) databases. Cases were included only if they harbored pure 19q13.42 duplications with no additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs or SNVs, and if the duplicated region did not extend significantly beyond the 19q13.42 locus.

NGS analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood lymphocytes and fragmented using sonication for sequencing, including clinical exome sequencing (CES) using the Agilent ClearSeq Inherited Disease Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA), which targets 3,203 genes associated with inherited diseases or whole exome sequencing (WES) using the Agilent SureSelect XT Human All Exon V5 kit. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2000/2500/Illumina NextSeq/Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform following the manufacturer’s protocols. The sequencing reads were aligned to the Genome Reference Consortium Human genome build 37 (GRCh37/hg19). The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) was used for variant calling, with the single nucleotide variants and insertion-deletions saved in variant call format (VCF) files. Detailed procedures have been described in the previous study (31). CNVs were detected using a validated in-house pipeline that has previously demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity from raw high-throughput sequencing data (28,31). The pathogenicity of the identified CNVs or SNVs was assessed according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines (32,33).

Data analysis and critical region mapping

Duplication coordinates were mapped to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome. Critical regions associated with specific phenotypic features were determined using the SRO method across all affected cases. quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) validation was performed in available cases by targeting PRKCG and other genes distributed across the NGS-detected duplication interval. Primers were designed for exons of genes within or flanking the duplicated region (Table S1).

Results

In total, 10 cases with NGS-detected duplications at 19q13.42 region were identified (Figure 1). The qPCR results verified the authenticity of all NGS-detected duplications. Further qPCR targeting PRKCG confirmed that the PRKCG-centered duplications in our cohort are reliable (Figures S1,S2). The phenotypic features were summarized in Table 1 and provided in the Supplementary material. The sex ratio was 9:1 (male: female), and the median age at evaluation was 5.8 years (range, 11 months–11 years). Except for case 3 with short stature, the other 9 cases (90%) presented with NDDs. DD was observed in 60% (6/10) of cases, others included: speech/language delay (40%, 4/10), ID (30%, 3/10), motor delay (20%, 2/10), seizures/epilepsy (20%, 2/10) or tic disorder (20%, 2/10) and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)/ASD (10%, 1/10). Four cases (1, 2, 4, 5) (4/8, 50%) showed brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities including agenesis of the corpus callosum, widened Virchow-Robin spaces, and hypoplasia of the right anterior cerebral artery. Additional systemic manifestations included cardiovascular anomalies in case 7 and limb anomalies in 10, respectively. Two cases (5 and 10) (2/10, 20%) presented with dysmorphic facial features, including a prominent forehead, large ears, exotropia and long face. Cases 8 and 9 were twin brothers, both presenting with language delay and both were born at 29 weeks of gestation with extremely low birth weight and experienced neonatal sepsis, which represent strong independent risk factors for language and DDs. Parental testing was available for case 2, which revealed paternal inheritance.

Discussion

Currently, no triplosensitive (TS) genes or genomic regions have been established within the 19q13.42 region, therefore, the pathogenic potential of 19q13.42 duplication varies considerably depending on genomic size and gene content. To expand our understanding of this genomic region, we systematically reviewed and summarized clinical data from cases with 19q13.42 duplications documented in the literature and public databases.

We observed that NDDs represent the predominant clinical manifestation, affecting 94.1% (16/17) of patients (Table 1). The most frequently observed features included DD (71.4%, 10/14), speech and language delays (53.8%, 7/13), ID (36.4%, 4/11) and ADHD/ASD (30.8%, 4/13). Additional NDDs manifestations and dysmorphic facial features were also documented, these phenotypes are non-specific and given the small number of cases reported herein and the lack of sufficient details (e.g., whether the duplication was de novo or inherited), there remains insufficient evidence to definitively conclude that 19q13.42 region as the TS region based on the ClinGen guidelines (32). While this small, retrospective case series cannot credibly establish 19q13.42 duplication as a novel genomic disorder, we observed recurrent neurodevelopmental features in a subset of reported duplications and propose PRKCG as a candidate gene warranting follow-up. Further case accumulation is required to clearly delineate the genotype-phenotype correlations.

Two genes (CNOT3 and U2AF2) have been firmly linked to autosomal dominant developmental disorders in 19q13.42 duplications, none of them have yet been established as TS genes, thus the pathogenicity of 19q13.42 duplication depends mainly on the duplicated size and gene number involved (32). In cases (26094 and 36864) recorded in Unique, around 2.7 Mb duplications encompassing over 50 protein-coding genes were detected (Figure 1), which including CNOT3, U2AF2, and PRKCG gene. Case 26094 presented with global DD, speech/language delay and learning disability, while case 36864 exhibited similar neurodevelopmental features with facial hypotonia. These two duplications were classified as likely pathogenic (3C score =0.9) for the number of protein-coding RefSeq genes involved according to ClinGen CNV Interpretation Scoring Rubric (https://cnvcalc.clinicalgenome.org/cnvcalc/cnv-gain). Seven cases (cases 8, 9, 10, DECIPHER patient 252224, 295794, 411985 and 272967) presented with varied neurodevelopmental phenotype associated with duplications encompassing PRKCG and CNOT3 but not including U2AF2 gene. These duplications received VUS classification due to total scores below 0.9. Similarly, nine cases (cases 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, DECIPHER patient 285739, 4629 and 332042) carrying duplications involving PRKCG but not CNOT3 and U2AF2 gene were also classified as VUS. Though it is unclear which gene or genes are likely responsible for the NDDs phenotype, we noticed that 16 cases (94.1%, 16/17) presented diverse NDDs sharing one gene PRKCG within the SRO, making it the most consistently shared gene and thus a prioritized candidate warranting further investigation (Figure 2, orange dotted box).

To the best of our knowledge, no 19q13.42 duplications involving solely PRKCG have been reported to date. Case 7 harbored the smallest 19q13.42 duplication (76.864 Kb) associated with NDDs so far. This duplication was further verified by qPCR, which could help refine the critical region for NDDs within 19q13.42 region. This duplication only involving three protein-coding genes (NLRP12, MYADM and PRKCG). NLRP12 (OMIM#611762) encodes a member of the CATERPILLER family of cytoplasmic proteins, which functions as an attenuating factor of inflammation by suppressing inflammatory responses in activated monocytes. Mutations in this gene cause familial cold autoinflammatory syndrome type 2 (35). With a very low predicted probability of triplosensitivity (pTriplo) score (0.03) in DECIPHER, it is considered less likely to be TS. MYADM (OMIM #609959) encodes a myeloid-associated differentiation marker that remains functionally uncharacterized and has not been linked to Mendelian diseases. Its low pTriplo score (0.16) also indicates minimal triplosensitivity potential. PRKCG is associated with SCA14, a rare autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cerebellar dysfunction, ataxia, myoclonus and dystonia (20). The clinical heterogeneity of PRKCG-related disorders has been reported, with clinical presentations ranging from pure cerebellar ataxia to complex syndromes, and the molecular pathogenesis involving complex gain- and loss-of-function mechanisms (20,36-38). PRKCG exhibits remarkable tissue specificity, with predominant expression in cerebellar Purkinje cells contributing to neurological phenotypes (39), while established triplosensitivity data for PRKCG remains limited on ClinGen dosage sensitivity map (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/), this gene demonstrates relatively high triplosensitivity potential with a pTriplo score of 0.78 (DECIPHER). Collectively, these observations raised the hypothesis that duplication of PRKCG may contribute to the neurodevelopmental phenotype. Consistent with this hypothesis, case 3, whose 19q13.42 microduplication did not encompass PRKCG, presented only with short stature and no neurodevelopmental features at five years of age. However, it is important to note that four cases presenting with NDDs harbored only partial PRKCG duplications. Because the partial duplications can result in gene disruption or altered splicing rather than simply increasing gene dosage, the triplosensitivity of PRKCG remains speculative in the absence of direct evidence for increased PRKCG expression or kinase activity.

Case 5 and 10 are of particular interest to refine a minimal region associated with the shared phenotypic features. Case 5, a nine-months old boy with 110.22 Kb duplication, presented with inability to roll over or sit with support, failure to grasp objects actively. He had a prominent forehead and large ears and agenesis of the corpus callosum, while case 10, a 4 years boy with 432.62 Kb duplication exhibited motor development delay and abnormal behaviors (biting clothes) and dysmorphic facial features including long face and prominent ears. Comparative analysis with other cases, a minimal overlapping region of approximately 15 Kb (chr19:54386416-54401365), encompassing part of the PRKCG was refined (Figure 2, orange dotted box). This region was present in 18 of 20 cases (excluding case 3 and DECIPHER patient 332042). Notably, all patients harboring duplications encompassing partial duplications of PRKCG exhibited NDDs. Neither these partial duplications nor any known non-coding or regulatory elements within the minimally overlapping region have been reported to contribute to the observed neurodevelopmental phenotypes to date.

It is interesting to note that cases 5 and 6, sharing similar breakpoints with case 7, exhibited distinct phenotypes. Case 6 manifested ADHD, ID and tic disorder, while case 7 also manifested atrioventricular block, hyperuricemia, streptococcal tonsillitis and tic disorder, in addition to NDDs. Similar findings were also observed in DECIPHER patients 295794 and 411985, these two patients were recorded with the same 19q13.42 duplication, patient 295794 presented with global DD and strabismus, whereas patient 411985 exhibited generalized hypotonia. These observations further highlight the considerable phenotypic variability associated with 19q13.42 duplications, even among patients with similar genomic breakpoints. Besides, the 19q13.42 duplication may occur de novo or be inherited from a phenotypically normal parent (Table 1), indicating the possibility of incomplete penetrance. However, the limited number of carriers and the absence of detailed clinical information preclude definitive conclusions at this time, more cases are required. Our findings also provide a valuable prognosis reference for prenatal diagnosis and genetic counseling for families identified in the future, as individuals carrying this duplication may exhibit only non-specific ultrasound features during the prenatal period (34).

Previous reports described 19q13.42 duplications with distinct breakpoints and gene content without signs of NDDs. Tadaki et al. reported a de novo 19q13.42 duplications encompassing NLRP family genes (NLRP2, NLRP9 and NLRP11), IL11 and HSPBP1 in a patient with systemic-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis (40), Abu-Amero et al. identified a 95 Kb de novo 19q13.42 duplication encompassing four killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptor (KIR) genes in a patient with Duane retraction syndrome type III (41). Zhang et al. reported two prenatal 19q13.42 duplication cases sharing a similar duplicated locus encompassing part of NLRP12 with no apparent abnormalities postnatally (42). These sporadic, non-overlapping duplications suggest that the 19q13.42 region may harbor multiple critical intervals responsible for diverse clinical presentations.

Indeed, the evidence presented above is plausible but hypothesis-generating, there are several important limitations to keep in mind when reviewing these findings. First, this was a retrospective study conducted in a clinically referred population, sampling or selection bias may exist, the prevalence and penetrance of 19q13.42 duplications cannot be determined from this design. Second, the developmental assessment criteria for cases obtained from public resources were likely inconsistent and non-standardized compared to our internal cohort, which may have resulted in the underestimation or mischaracterization of certain phenotypic features. Third, the presence of significant clinical confounders in certain individuals limits our phenotypic interpretations. For example, cases 8 and 9 experienced extreme prematurity and neonatal complications, which are strong independent risk factors for language and DDs. Long-term follow-up is necessary to disentangle the effects of the duplication from these early life events. Other potential confounders contributing to NDDs, including perinatal complications, family history and environmental factors, may have been missed due to limited information. Finally, while we confirmed the presence of the PRKCG-centered duplications using qPCR in our internal cohort, the precise breakpoints of the structural variants reported here remain approximations due to the inherent technical limitations of NGS-based CNV calling. Future functional studies and prospective case evaluations will require rigorous molecular validation to confirm these boundaries.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to define a more consistent genotype-phenotype correlation in 19q13.42 region, where few well-described cases have been reported previously. We observed that patients with 19q13.42 duplications may share a broadly consistent phenotypic spectrum predominantly featuring neurodevelopmental abnormalities, mild facial dysmorphism, and variable systemic features. PRKCG is proposed as a candidate gene potentially contributing to the associated NDD phenotypes, although functional validation and accumulation of additional cases are essential to confirm this hypothesis.
