Prevalence and associated factors of demoralization in stroke patients: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Hongyan Yang, Ting Yang, Hui Wei, Miaomiao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIrreversible neurological trauma, long-term rehabilitation, physical disability, psychological disorders, heavy medical and financial burdens, and difficulties in reintegration into the community may ultimately lead to demoralization in stroke survivors. However, the prevalence and associated factors of demoralization have yet to be determined in stroke patients.AimTo identify the prevalence and associated factors of demoralization in stroke patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study using the convenient sampling method was conducted between January 2025 to May 2025. The Mandarin Version of the Demoralization Scale (DS-MV) and the Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS) were used to assess demoralization and self-perceived burden, respectively. General information, including sociodemographic and stroke-related variables, was collected. The independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance was used for univariate analysis. Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the relationships between DS-MV scores and SPBS scores, and multiple linear regression was used to identify the associated factors of demoralization in stroke patients.ResultsThis study included 376 stroke patients with a mean DS-MV score of 34.32 ± 11.25. 51.9% of stroke patients had high demoralization. Education (β =−0.217, p< 0.001), comorbidity (β = 0.147, p< 0.001), number of stroke episodes (β = 0.131, p< 0.001), activity of daily living (β = 0.118, p = 0.002), and self-perceived burden (β = 0.601, p< 0.001) were associated with the severity of demoralization in stroke patients.ConclusionsThe prevalence of demoralization in stroke patients is high. Several factors were found to be associated with a higher risk of demoralization, which can potentially be alleviated by targeted interventions. Healthcare providers should integrate demoralization screening into standard stroke care pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04702-3.Keywords: Stroke, Demoralization, Prevalence, Associated factors

Introduction

Demoralization is a psychological distress state triggered by a series of life events, which refers to inability to adapt or subjective incompetence when confronted with pressure, and characterized by unpleasant feelings expressed in terms such as “it is too much,” “it is no use,” “I cannot take it anymore.” [1]. The individual attribute these feelings to deficiencies in the environment (helplessness) and/or to their own unchangeable failures (sense of failure) or inadequacies (disheartenment) [2]. In a state of helplessness, the individual still retain the ability to react, yet they will feel left out and lack adequate support (dysphoria) [2]. When disheartenment occurs, the individual will think that they are the only one responsible for the situation, and at the same time have the illusion that this state will last forever (meaninglessness) [2]. The term “demoralization” was first introduced by Frank in 1961 [3] and was included as a psychological symptom in the 11th edition of the International Classification of Diseases in 2019 [4]. To date, the concept of demoralization has gained widespread recognition among researchers and scholars worldwide. Demoralization can reduce an individual’s ability to cope with stressful events, which may lead to a series of adverse health outcomes, such as impaired physical symptoms, low adherence to rehabilitation treatment, and limited social functioning [5]. Furthermore, demoralization was correlated with poor quality of life and had a unique contribution to suicidal ideation in cancer patients [6].

Demoralized patients often accompanied by depression, and there is comorbidity between them [7]. The core symptomatic difference between them lies in that the former is characterized by loss of hope and meaning for the future, while the latter is manifested by loss of pleasure and interest in the present [8]. In addition, the main distress of demoralization lies in subjective incompetence and uncertainty about the direction of action, whereas depression is characterized by decreased motivation, even when the direction of action is clear [7]. Due to the fact that patients with demoralization often exhibit persistent depression and few symptoms of functioning-related impairment, they may be overlooked and considered a common response to stressful stimuli [9].

The survival rate of stroke has increased steadily in the past 20 years, however, longer survival is often accompanied by long-term effects of the sequelae [10]. Half of stroke survivors are left disabled, with a third relying on others to assist with activities of daily living [11]. Psychological issues, such as depression and anxiety, may persist even after the physical symptoms have been stabilized. The incidence of depressive symptoms within 2 years after stroke was 11% to 41% [12], and approximately one-third of stroke patients at any time up to five years after stroke are affected by depressive symptoms [13]. With the development of social medicine and health, mental health has attracted widespread attention in healthcare discussions. However, a large number of existing studies on mental health in stroke patients have focused on depression, anxiety, and apathy [14, 15], with relatively less attention to demoralization. Irreversible neurological trauma, long-term rehabilitation, physical disability, psychological disorders, heavy medical and financial burdens, and difficulties in reintegration into the community may ultimately lead to demoralization in stroke patients [16]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to further investigate demoralization in stroke patients to improve clinical nursing practices in stroke care.

Investigating the associated factors of demoralization contributes to improving disease management and provides evidence for the development of mental health intervention strategies. Studies on the prevalence and associated factors of demoralization among cancer patients have been relatively mature. Hong et al. [17] based on the bio-psycho-social model reported associated factors of demoralization among cancer patients in a systematic review, and the psychological factors showed the strongest association with demoralization. Tang et al. [18] published a meta-analysis about factors correlated with demoralization among cancer patients. They found demoralization showed either positive or negative associations with sociodemographic (income, education, and age), clinical (symptom burden, and quality of life), and psychological (social support, anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation) variables. Wang et al. [16] investigated the potential profiles of demoralization and its influencing factors in elderly stroke patients with disability. And the results showed that age, residential status, monthly income, smoking history, history of other chronic diseases, social support, and self-efficacy were associated with it [16].

Self-perceived burden refers to feelings such as reduced self-worth, shame, and blame, which are caused by the physical and psychological burden on the caregivers due to the limitation of physical activities [19]. The prevalence of self-perceived burden is quite high in stroke survivors, ranging from 65% to 70% [20, 21]. Self-perceived burden has been associated with expressions of a desire for death, self-reported suicidal ideation, and an interest in receiving physician-assisted suicide [22]. We already know that demoralization is related to suicidal ideation, however, it is unknown whether self-perceived burden could reinforce demoralization and lead to suicidal ideation. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the relationship between self-perceived burden and demoralization.

Based on these premises, our hypothesis posited that the associated factors of demoralization in stroke patients included sociodemographic factors, stroke-related factors, and psychological factors, and we developed a conceptual framework to summarise the potential associated factors of demoralization in the stroke patients. This conceptual framework was aiming to sort out the correlations between sociodemographic factors, stroke-related factors, and psychological factors and the state of demoralization based on a cross-sectional study design. The dashed arrows indicated that there was a statistical correlation between variables, which did not represent causal inference. The conceptual framework of the study is presented in Fig. 1. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the prevalence and associated factors of demoralization in stroke patients to provide a reference basis for improving stroke clinical nursing practices.

Methods

Design and participants

This cross-sectional survey used convenience sampling to enroll stroke inpatients from a general public hospital in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, where the Department of Neurology has 5 wards and 200 beds. The survey time was from January 2025 to May 2025. A questionnaire survey of stroke patients was conducted on the day before discharge by researchers. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years old; (2) Patients diagnosed with stroke by imaging, without limitation of stages of stroke; (3) Clear consciousness and able to clearly understand the questionnaire content; (4) Voluntary participation in this study and signing informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with other major diseases, such as advanced cancer or critical diseases of the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys; (2) suffering from serious mental illness.

The sample size was determined using the G-Power 3.1.9.7 linear multiple regression algorithm. With an effect size of 0.15, an α value of 0.05, and an anticipated power (1-β) of 0.95, this study included 21 variables: 13 sociodemographic and stroke-related variables, and 8 dimensions related to the scale. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 226 stroke patients, considering a 20% rate of incomplete response, a minimum of 283 stroke patients were needed to meet the minimum sample size requirement. A convenient sample of 376 were enrolled in the study.

Measures

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire collected sociodemographic and stroke characteristics data. It included the following 2 sections: (1) sociodemographic data on age, sex, education, marital status, place of residence, occupational status, and medical payment method; (2) stroke-related characteristics, including type of stroke, comorbidity, number of stroke episodes, dysphagia, activity of daily living (ADL), and length of stay at the hospital.

Mandarin Version of the Demoralization Scale (DS-MV)

The English version of the Demoralization Scale (DS) was developed by Kissane et al. [23], consists of 24 items covering five dimensions: meaninglessness (five items), dysphoria (five items), disheartenment (five items), helplessness (four items), and sense of failure (five items). The DS score uses a five-point Likert scale, from “strong disagreement” to “strong agreement”. The total score range of the DS is 0–96, and score above 30 was used as an indicator of clinically relevant high demoralization. The DS-MV, used in this study, was translated by Hong et al. and the Cronbach’s alpha of the total scale was 0.92 [24]. The same cut-off value in defining a demoralized population was used in this study. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the DS-MV was 0.748.

Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS)

The SPBS, developed by Cousineau et al. [25], consists of 10 items covering three dimensions: body burden (two items), economic burden (two items), and emotional burden (six items). The SPBS score uses a five-point Likert scale, from “never” (1 point) to “always” (5 points). The total score range of the SPBS is 10–50, and the SPBS score is classified into the following four levels, none, mild, moderate and severe, corresponding to SPBS score < 20, 20 ≤ SPBS score < 30, 30 ≤ SPBS score < 40, and SPBS score ≥ 40. The Chinese version of SPBS, was translated by Wu et al. and the Cronbach’s alpha of the total scale was 0.91 [26]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the SPBS was 0.782.

Barthel Index (BI)

The BI, developed by Mahoney et al. [27], consists of 10 items: feeding, grooming, bathing, dressing, bowel control, bladder control, toilet use, transfers, mobility, and stairs. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, and the BI score is classified into the following four levels, heavy dependence, moderate dependence, mild dependence and no dependence, corresponding to BI score ≤ 40, 41 ≤ BI score ≤ 60, 61 ≤ BI score ≤ 99, and BI score = 100. The Chinese version of BI, was translated by Li et al. and the Cronbach’s alpha of the total scale was 0.858 [28].

Data collection

All researchers received unified questionnaire training and assessment before conducting the formal survey to ensure consistency in the interpretation of the questionnaire. All questionnaires were distributed on-site and collected immediately after completion. The sociodemographic information (e.g., age, sex, education, marital status, etc.) and relevant scales were completed independently by researchers through one-on-one, item-by-item interviews and recorded accurately, whereas clinical characteristics of stroke (e.g., type of stroke, comorbidity, number of stroke incidences, etc.) were collected from clinical records by the researchers.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0. Significance was set at p < 0.05, employing two-tailed tests. Sociodemographic/stroke-related variables were presented as frequency/percentage and scale scores were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance was used to compare the differences of total DS-MV score in sociodemographic and stroke-related variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the relationships between DS-MV scores and SPBS scores. Multiple linear regression was used to identify determinants of demoralization in stroke patients. Furthermore, multicollinearity diagnostics was assessed by the variance inflation factor (VIF). VIF value < 10 indicated no multicollinearity between independent variables.

Ethical considerations

The study procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the corresponding author’s hospital (IRB No.20240095). Written consent was obtained from all study participants or their family members. The study adhered to the reporting guidelines set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [29].

Discussion

High prevalence of demoralization in stroke patients

Notably, the prevalence of demoralization among stroke patients was remarkably high. Considering a DS-MV score > 30 for detection, 51.9% of stroke patients had high demoralization, which was much higher than that of Parkinson’s patients (18.1%) [30], and lower than that of palliative care patients (64.8%) [31]. The age of the stroke population included in this study was 60.50 ± 12.76 years, which was slightly lower than the average age of onset of stroke patients in China (65 years) [32]. Male patients accounted for 69.9% and female patients accounted for 30.1%, which was consistent with the gender epidemiological pattern of stroke onset. Overall, the population included in this study has a certain degree of group representativeness.

Although stroke occurs as an acute event, patients often face long-term and multidimensional rehabilitation, which affects the quality of life of patients and increases the economic burden. In addition, patients often experience movement disorder, speech dysfunction, cognitive dysfunction, and psychological disorders, which greatly affect their social roles and functions [33]. So stroke patients are prone to demoralization. It is necessary to raise again that in a state of demoralization, there is a prominent inability to cope, which distinguishes this condition from depression to a certain extent. Treatment approaches also differ accordingly, therefore, it is particularly important to identify demoralization.

At present, the main tools for identifying demoralization are the DS and DS-II, with DS-II being a simplified version of DS developed by Robinson et al. [34]. The DS-MV was originally validated in cancer populations, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was acceptable but borderline, which should be taken into account when interpreting the findings related to demoralization. Future studies are recommended to conduct a comprehensive verification of the psychometric properties of the DS-MV in stroke patients.

The associated factors of demoralization in stroke patients

Our findings showed that stroke patients with lower education level were more likely to be demoralized, which is consistent with prior studies [35, 36]. Usually, a low level of education may mean lower income, making it difficult to afford expensive treatment costs, and thus more likely to experience a higher level of demoralization [37]. Results further showed comorbidity was a predictor of demoralization level in stroke patients. The overwhelming majority of stroke patients exhibit comorbidity in China. Zhao et al. [38] included 2539 acute ischemic stroke patients, with 90% exhibiting multimorbidity (≥ 2 comorbidities). The more comorbidities a patient has, the more complex their condition becomes, requiring more time and effort to balance disease management with daily life and work. Secondly, patients with excessive comorbidities need to participate in multiple treatment trajectories, which can increase their time burden, transportation burden, and economic burden during the treatment process, leading to an increased treatment burden for patients [39]. Furthermore, having a high number of comorbidities may mean that patients need to take multiple medications, leading to an increased drug burden [38].

Additionally, the more frequent the stroke attacks, the higher the demoralization level in stroke patients. The frequency of stroke attacks indicates the severity and control of the disease [40]. Patients with more episodes often have more severe illnesses, which limits their social interactions. As the frequency of attacks increases, the psychological burden on patients increases, and they gradually lose confidence in disease management [40]. Subjectively, they are unwilling to communicate with others, isolate themselves, and their sense of social alienation increases, which are not conducive to the recovery of role functions [41]. The long-term existence of this situation can increase despair and helplessness. The study also demonstrated that ADL was a significant predictor for demoralization. Poor ADL often means that stroke patients cannot fully take care of themselves, and largely depend on their caregivers in daily life, which leads them to feel extremely guilty and perceive burden [42].

Furthermore, self-perceived burden has a positive predictive effect on demoralization. Self‐perceived burden is a common psychological characteristic in patients with chronic diseases and is associated with physical conditions and negative psychology, including depression and suicidal ideation [43]. It has been found that self‐perceived burden may be higher among those with other disabling but nonfatal conditions, such as stroke [43]. Previous studies [2, 6] have shown a positive correlation between demoralization and suicidal ideation. The results of this study suggest that early identification and specific treatment for self‐perceived burden may prevent or reduce the sense of demoralization, thereby reducing suicidal ideation.

However, in clinical practice, demoralization often manifests as persistent low mood, which is easily masked by other symptoms, leading to diagnostic difficulties [9]. Therefore, healthcare providers could integrate demoralization screening into standard stroke care pathways. Mindfulness-integrated cognitive behavior has been proven to have positive therapeutic effects on demoralization, which includes a set of evidence-based techniques to increase self-awareness, self-control, and self-efficacy in various areas of life [44].

Limitations

Some limitations need to be addressed. First, the cross-sectional design was used in this study, the lack of qualitative and mixed methods of research hinders an in-depth understanding of the demoralization characteristics. Future qualitative studies are necessary to capture trends, specific experiences, and insights into demoralization. Second, considering that demoralization level is a dynamic indicator, longitudinal studies are also needed. Third, this study selected stroke patients only from one hospital with the convenience sampling method, and the sample may not fully represent the entire stroke population. Multi-center studies are needed to determine a widespread belief in results in the future. Fourth, the stroke patients included in this study consist of both acute-phase and non-acute-phase cases, which meant we could not know how stroke staging affected the demoralization. Fifth, self-reported measurement tools may lead to response biases. In the future, self-evaluation may be conducted in conjunction with peer evaluation to more accurately portray the characteristics of demoralization. Finally, several important confounders, including depression, cognitive impairment, and stroke severity, were not measured, which may limit the robustness of the research findings. Future studies are recommended to incorporate these confounders into the research design to further improve the validity of the results.

Conclusions

The prevalence of demoralization in stroke patients is quite high, which warrants clinical attention. Interventions for stroke patients with demoralization should prioritize those with lower educational levels, comorbidities, poor activities of daily living, disease recurrence, and high self-perceived burden.

Given the high prevalence and adverse consequences of demoralization, healthcare providers should conduct routine monitoring of demoralization in stroke patients post-discharge and strengthen targeted communication with them to effectively alleviate demoralization.
