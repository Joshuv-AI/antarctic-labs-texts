Research Advances on the Molecular Structural Basis and Mechanisms of Quercetin and Its Derivatives in Improving Insulin Resistance
Authors: Qichang Xing, Jia Chen, Renzhu Liu, Wencan Li, Keqian Chen, Xiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsulin resistance is a core pathological link in metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes, and its prevention and treatment are current research hotspots. Quercetin, a natural flavonoid, has significant potential to improve insulin resistance through its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolic regulatory activities. This review systematically examines literature published between 2020 and 2025 from the PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases, focusing on original mechanistic studies in cellular and animal models of insulin resistance. Our synthesis reveals two mechanistic clusters: quercetin directly activates insulin signaling via the insulin receptor substrate 1/Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase /Protein Kinase B pathway, enhances muscle glucose uptake through AMP-activated protein kinase-dependent glucose transporter 4 translocation. Indirect mechanisms include gut microbiome modulation with increased short-chain fatty acid production, suppresses hepatic inflammation and targeting of transcription factors. Critically, a clear efficacy hierarchy among derivatives was identified: glycosides show superior bioavailability compared to the aglycone, while methylated derivatives exhibit enhanced stability but divergent effects on peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ signaling. Major unresolved challenges include the low oral bioavailability of quercetin aglycone, the lack of systematic structure-activity relationship models, and the absence of rigorous human trials. The review concludes that chemical derivatization and advanced delivery systems offer promising strategies, but clinical translation requires an integrated framework combining systematic structure-activity profiling, mechanistic target selection, and validated human studies.Keywords: quercetin, quercetin derivatives, insulin resistance, bioavailability, signaling pathway, structure-activity relationship

Introduction

Insulin resistance (IR) refers to decreased sensitivity of insulin-target tissues (such as the liver, adipose tissue, and muscle) to insulin, leading to impaired glucose uptake and utilization. It is the key driving factor for the occurrence and development of metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver.1 Although many therapeutic drugs are available, many have side effects or limited efficacy. Finding safe and effective insulin sensitizers from natural products has become a research hotspot.2 Quercetin, a representative dietary flavonoid, has been shown to improve IR in numerous animal models and in some clinical studies.3,4

Recent advances in molecular biology, structural pharmacology, and systems biology have deepened understanding of the “structure-activity” relationship and the underlying molecular mechanisms by which quercetin improves IR. For instance, molecular dynamics simulation analysis has revealed that quercetin can mimic the insulin-sensitizing effect of carbohydrate response element-binding protein (ChREBP) knockdown in the livers of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease models by sequestering ChREBP in the cytoplasm and preventing its nuclear translocation.5 This reveals a new mechanism by which quercetin regulates metabolism through direct intervention in the subcellular localization of transcription factors. In addition, its natural derivatives, such as quercetin-3-glucoside, have been found to enhance insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle by activating the Sirtuin 1(SIRT1)/ AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/ Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) signaling pathway and promoting glucose transporter 4(GLUT4) translocation.6 These observations suggest that distinguishing between the aglycone and different glycosylation patterns in terms of binding affinity, cellular accumulation, and downstream signaling outcomes is critical for deciphering its pleiotropic actions—yet this aspect remains inadequately synthesized in the current literature.

This review, therefore, distinguishes itself from prior overviews by addressing two core questions: (1) which specific structural features of quercetin determine its selective modulation of different nodes within the insulin signaling network? and (2) in light of its inherent bioavailability limitations, which emerging delivery strategies or derivative modifications can effectively bridge the gap between in vitro potency and in vivo translational efficacy, while preserving or even enhancing its molecular activity? To this end, this review systematically examines literature published between 2020 and 2025 from the PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases, and use “quercetin,” “insulin resistance,” “signaling pathways,” “bioavailability” et al as the search term. It aims to systematically analyze the molecular structural basis underlying the intervention of quercetin and its derivatives in IR, covering direct regulation of insulin signaling, suppression of inflammation and oxidative stress, modulation of the gut microbiome and metabolites, and novel delivery strategies.

The Molecular Basis of Quercetin’s Regulation of the Insulin Core Signaling Pathway

Direct Activation and Regulation of the IRS-1/Akt/FOXO1 Pathway

One of the key mechanisms by which quercetin improves IR lies in its direct regulation of key nodes in the insulin signaling pathway. Tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) is the initial and rate-limiting step in the insulin signaling cascade, and its dysfunction is an important feature of IR. Studies have shown that quercetin treatment can effectively restore the impaired phosphorylation level of IRS-1, thereby restoring the normal transmission of insulin signaling in palmitic acid-induced IR in the HepG2 cell model and high-fat-high-fructose diet-fed mouse model.7 This finding provides direct molecular evidence that quercetin improves peripheral tissue insulin sensitivity. Protein kinase B (Akt)-mediated phosphorylation prompts forkhead box O1 (FOXO1) to remain in the cytoplasm and be degraded, thereby inhibiting its translocation into the nucleus and its transcriptional activity. This directly leads to the downregulation of the gene transcription of two key gluconeogenic enzymes-phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase), ultimately inhibiting excessive glucose output from the liver, which is a key step in reducing fasting blood glucose. The activation of IRS-1 in turn initiates the phosphorylation and activation of downstream Akt. As a key hub in the insulin signaling pathway, Akt activation is essential for promoting the translocation of GLUT4 to the cell membrane, thereby directly driving glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue. Animal experiments confirmed that quercetin treatment significantly upregulated GLUT4 mRNA and protein expression in adipose and muscle tissues, thereby explaining its effect on glucose utilization at the levels of gene transcription and protein translation. More importantly, quercetin mediated the phosphorylation of FOXO1 via Akt activation. FOXO1 is a central transcription factor that regulates hepatic gluconeogenesis. Akt-mediated phosphorylation promotes FOXO1 retention in the cytoplasm and its degradation, thereby inhibiting its entry into the nucleus for transcriptional activity. This directly leads to the downregulation of gene transcription of two key gluconeogenic enzymes, PEPCK and G6Pase, and ultimately inhibits excessive hepatic glucose output, a key link in reducing fasting blood glucose.

Natural Activator of the AMPK Signaling Pathway

In addition to its direct effects on the classical insulin signaling pathway, quercetin is also widely recognized as natural activators of AMPK.8,9 Once activated, AMPK comprehensively improves insulin sensitivity through multiple approaches: it promotes fatty acid oxidation to reduce lipotoxicity, inhibits the activity of key enzymes involved in fat synthesis to reduce lipid accumulation, and enhances glucose uptake in peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle through mechanisms such as promoting GLUT4 membrane translocation. AMPK is the core sensor of cellular energy status and plays a central role in maintaining the balance of energy metabolism. The specific chemical structure of quercetin enables it to effectively activate AMPK through direct allosteric regulation or indirect effects on the cellular AMP/ATP ratio.8 The activation of AMPK increases the activity of key rate-limiting enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, such as hexokinase and pyruvate kinase, and promotes glucose uptake, thereby shifting metabolic flux from gluconeogenesis to glycolysis and facilitating the breakdown and utilization of glucose. This process complements the aforementioned inhibition of gluconeogenesis via FOXO1, thereby reducing hepatic glucose output. In addition, AMPK activation plays another important role in improving IR: inhibiting the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. In states of overnutrition, the overactive mTOR pathway can inhibit the tyrosine phosphorylation of IRS-1 via negative feedback, thereby inducing or exacerbating IR. Quercetin can effectively inhibit mTOR activity by activating AMPK,10 breaking the negative feedback loop, and reducing mTOR-mediated inhibition of upstream insulin signaling, thereby forming a positive regulatory loop that improves insulin sensitivity. Therefore, as an activator of AMPK, quercetin not only directly regulates multiple downstream targets of glucose and lipid metabolism but also remodels systemic metabolic homeostasis by modulating crosstalk pathways such as mTOR, thereby constituting a multidimensional action network against IR.

The Anti-Inflammatory and Antioxidant Structural Properties of Quercetin in Improving IR

There is a close pathophysiological link between inflammation and IR, and chronic low-grade inflammation is considered a key driver of IR. In the state of obesity, a high-fat diet or energy excess leads adipose tissue to recruit and activate macrophages, particularly the conversion of M2 macrophages to proinflammatory M1 macrophages, and to release cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6).11 These inflammatory factors can interfere with insulin signaling by activating multiple intracellular signaling pathway. Experimental evidence has shown that anti-inflammatory interventions, such as tofacitinib and aspirin, can improve insulin sensitivity to some extent.12

Inhibition of the NF-κB and NLRP3 Inflammasome Pathways

Through the strong antioxidant capacity conferred by its phenolic hydroxyl structure, quercetin is effective in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), which is a key trigger for the activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and NLRP3 inflammasome.13 In the inflammatory microenvironment, quercetin exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and downregulating the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins.14 For example, in the TNF-α-induced inflammation model of human periodontal ligament stem cells, quercetin reversed TNF-α induced damage to osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting the NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, a finding validated in multiple cell and disease models. In the lipopolysaccharide and adenosine triphosphate induced pyroptosis model of THP-1 macrophages, quercetin inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by inhibiting the excessive production of ROS. In addition, quercetin inhibited the phosphorylation of p65 and its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, indicating a direct inhibitory effect on the NF-κB pathway. In the nervous system, quercetin can eliminate damaged mitochondria by promoting mitophagy and reducing the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS, thereby attenuating NLRP3 inflammasome activation in microglia and ultimately protecting neurons from neuroinflammatory toxic damage.15 In addition, quercetin protected intestinal barrier function by inhibiting activation of the upstream MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway and blocking assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequent pyroptosis in a chicken intestinal injury model induced by deoxynivalenol.16 Together, these studies confirm that quercetin inhibits the inflammatory signaling cascade at multiple levels by targeting the ROS/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis, a core molecular mechanism underlying quercetin’s improvement of the inflammatory state associated with IR.

Regulating Specific Cell Populations and Inflammatory Microenvironment in Adipose Tissue

The modulatory effect of quercetin on the inflammatory microenvironment of adipose tissue stems in part from its potential as a senolytic agent. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed that a population of pro-inflammatory cells with high expression of p21Cip1 (p21high) accumulates in obese adipose tissue, and these cells exacerbate local and systemic IR by activating the NF-κB pathway.17 Quercetin, a key component of senolytic combination therapies such as dasatinib plus quercetin, can effectively eliminate these deleterious cell populations. The mechanism of action is related to the inhibition of Piezo1 mechanosensitive ion channel-mediated inflammatory response. Quercetin was shown to significantly inhibit the calcium response and the mechanically activated current induced by the agonist Yoda1 or by mechanical stretch in HUVECs, indicating that it is a selective modulator of Piezo1 channels. Quercetin effectively reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines and inhibited the activity of the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway in endothelial cells stimulated by oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) or mechanical stretch. Notably, the anti-inflammatory effects of quercetin persisted even after Piezo1 gene knockdown, suggesting that they were not dependent on the genetic deletion of Piezo1. In the atherosclerosis animal model, quercetin significantly reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation, and this effect was consistent in Piezo1 endothelium-specific knockout mice, further confirming that quercetin improves atherosclerosis by inhibiting vascular endothelial cell inflammation through targeting Piezo1 channels. Specifically, quercetin inhibits inflammation by suppressing the Piezo1-mediated NF-κB/IL-1β and NLRP3/caspase1/IL-1β signaling pathways. These findings provide direct experimental evidence that quercetin regulates the inflammatory state of specific cell populations (such as senescent cells and endothelial cells) in adipose tissue and its vascular microenvironment, thereby improving systemic insulin sensitivity and metabolic health. Removing these harmful p21high cells or inhibiting endothelial inflammation can help break the vicious cycle of chronic low-level inflammation in adipose tissue, offering new avenues for treating metabolic diseases.

Mechanisms by Which Quercetin Improves IR Through “Gut-Axis” Regulation

Remodeling the Structure and Diversity of Intestinal Flora

Quercetin can reshape the structure and diversity of intestinal flora, which is one of the important mechanisms by which quercetin improves IR. Studies have shown that quercetin can significantly modulate the composition of the gut microbiota, particularly by increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria and inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria. In the metabolic disorder model, quercetin intervention significantly increased the relative abundance of Akkermansia.18 Akkermansia is a recognized beneficial bacterium, and its increased abundance is closely associated with improved intestinal barrier function, alleviation of metabolic endotoxemia, and enhanced insulin sensitivity.19 In addition, quercetin was effective in reducing the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio (F/B), which is often regarded as a marker of a healthier gut microbiota.20 In the db/db mouse model, quercetin intervention further showed the ability to regulate specific bacterial flora, reducing the abundance of Proteobacteria, Bacteroides, and Escherichia/Shigella, which are associated with intestinal inflammation and barrier damage.21 The reduction of these microbiota helps repair the imbalance in gut microecology and create a healthier gut environment for the host. The regulation of quercetin on gut microbiota is structurally dependent. For example, its glycoside derivative, isoquercetin, has also shown significant regulatory effects on beneficial bacterial genera such as Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus in a mouse model of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.22 This positive effect on gut microbiota composition and diversity underlies the systemic health benefits of quercetin through “gut-axis” pathways (eg, gut-brain axis, gut-lung axis).23 By remodeling the gut microbiota, quercetin not only directly improves the gut’s local environment but also lays a microbiological foundation for subsequent repair of intestinal barrier function and regulation of systemic inflammation and metabolism.

Restoring Intestinal Barrier Function and Regulating Intestinal Metabolites

Another key mechanism by which quercetin improves IR is its ability to repair intestinal barrier function and regulate intestinal metabolites. The integrity of intestinal barrier function is essential to prevent endotoxin translocation and systemic low-grade inflammation. It was confirmed that quercetin intervention significantly reduced serum levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and D-lactate (D-LA), classic markers of increased intestinal permeability and bacterial translocation.21 By reducing the influx of intestinal LPS into the blood, that is, reducing “metabolic endotoxemia”, quercetin effectively inhibits the systemic low-grade inflammatory response triggered by LPS, thereby indirectly improving the sensitivity of the insulin signaling pathway.24 In ulcerative colitis models, quercetin promotes the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as Clostridium XIVa and XIa by regulating the gut microbiota, thereby driving microbial metabolism to produce isovanillic acid, which in turn enhances intestinal barrier integrity.25 In addition, quercetin can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling pathway by affecting tryptophan metabolism and increasing levels of microbial metabolites such as indole lactate, which plays a key role in maintaining intestinal epithelial barrier function.26 Untargeted metabolomics analysis further revealed the profound effects of quercetin on the host-microbe co-metabolic network. In db/db mice, quercetin intervention altered the intestinal metabolic profile, significantly increasing S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) levels while reducing concentrations of amino acids such as L-aspartate and L-glutamic acid. As an important methyl donor, S-adenosylmethionine participates in a variety of biosynthetic and metabolic regulation processes. The increase in S-adenosylmethionine levels may be related to improved cellular energy metabolism and insulin signaling. Quercetin can also regulate microbial enzyme activity, influence metabolic pathways, including phenylalanine metabolism, and reduce the production of uremic toxins derived from gut bacteria (such as 3-phenyllactic acid and hippuric acid). These toxins are positively correlated with kidney damage and inflammatory markers, and their reduction helps to alleviate metabolic disorders.27 These metabolite changes collectively point to a comprehensive mode of action for quercetin in improving host metabolic homeostasis and IR by repairing the intestinal barrier and regulating microbial metabolites.

Research Progress of Quercetin Derivatives in the Treatment of IR

Structure–Activity Relationship Analysis of Quercetin and Its Derivatives in IR

The direct application of the quercetin aglycone is limited by its poor water solubility and low bioavailability. In contrast, its glycoside derivatives have shown activity in reducing IR and exhibit distinct pharmacokinetic properties. This article summarizes some experimental studies on the improvement of IR by quercetin derivatives, as shown in Table 1. Quercetin-3-O-rhamnoside (quercitrin) and quercetin-3-O-rutinoside (Rutin) are common glycoside forms.41 Studies have shown that these glycoside derivatives may be converted into active aglycosides by gut microbiota metabolism in vivo, thereby exerting biological effects.42 Structural modifications, such as methylation, similarly affect their properties. For example, isorhamnetin, a 3′-methylated derivative of quercetin, has been shown to promote glucose uptake in hepatocytes by activating the AMPK/Akt signaling pathway.43 These structural modifications not only affect the solubility and stability of the compounds but may also alter their tissue targeting, thus optimizing their efficacy in improving IR.44

Quercetin Derivatives for Treating Insulin Resistance and Related Diseases

A dedicated structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis is essential to guide the rational design of quercetin-based insulin sensitizers. The C3 hydroxyl group (C3-OH) emerges as a critical determinant for adipogenesis-regulating activity: its retention is necessary—though not sufficient—for lipid-lowering effects, whereas its methylation paradoxically converts the compound into a pro-adipogenic agent that upregulates PPARγ transcription by nearly 10-fold.40 By contrast, dual methylation at the A-ring (C5 and C7) combined with C3-OH retention, as seen in 5,7,3′,4′-EMQ, significantly enhances both metabolic stability and cellular uptake while potently suppressing PPARγ expression and triglyceride accumulation, outperforming the parent quercetin aglycone. Glycosylation at the C3 position also profoundly alters pharmacokinetic behavior: isoquercitrin (3-O-glucoside) and rutin (3-O-rutinoside) exhibit superior bioavailability compared to the aglycone, yet their activity is highly dependent on enzymatic deglycosylation by intestinal microflora or lactase phlorizin hydrolase, which releases the active aglycone in a site- and rate-specific manner.29 Methylated metabolites such as isorhamnetin (3′-O-methylquercetin) activate the AMPK/Akt pathway to promote hepatic glucose uptake, indicating that B-ring methylation shifts the target preference toward insulin signaling nodes.35 Moreover, the disaccharide conjugate QAG [quercetin-3-O-α-L-arabinopyranosyl-(1→2)-β-D-glucopyranoside] directly binds to the insulin receptor (docking energy −4.91 kcal/mol) and relieves palmitate-induced insulin resistance through the IRS-1/PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β cascade, demonstrating that specific sugar moieties can confer receptor-binding capacity absent in the aglycone.34 Collectively, these findings reveal a complex SAR landscape: (i) C3-OH retention is a prerequisite for anti-adipogenic activity; (ii) A-ring polymethylation enhances stability and uptake, whereas B-ring methylation modulates target selectivity; (iii) glycosylation profoundly affects bioavailability but requires metabolic activation; and (iv) the number, position, and type of substituents jointly determine whether a derivative acts as an agonist or antagonist of PPARγ and other metabolic regulators. This diversity underscores that derivatization is not merely a solubility-enhancing strategy but a means to reprogram the multi-target selectivity profile of quercetin, offering a rational basis for designing next-generation insulin sensitizers with improved efficacy and reduced off-target effects.

New Delivery System Enhances the Bioavailability of Quercetin

To improve the water solubility, stability, and bioavailability of quercetin, the development of novel delivery systems is essential. Encapsulating quercetin in liposomes is an effective strategy that can significantly improve its encapsulation efficiency, stability, and cellular uptake. Studies have shown that quercetin liposomes are more effective than free quercetin at inhibiting the generation of ROS and inflammatory pathways, such as NF-κB, in a hepatocyte IR model.45 This delivery system exhibits enhanced biological activity in cellular and animal models by protecting quercetin from degradation and promoting its efficient endocytosis by target cells. For example, in fracture healing models, low concentrations of quercetin promote osteogenic differentiation through specific signaling pathways, further demonstrated that a suitable delivery system is expected to maintain effective local concentrations and enhance efficacy.46 In addition, nanotechnology-based delivery platforms, such as biodegradable nanocrystals, enable targeted, controlled, and sustained drug release, increase drug accumulation at the lesion site, and reduce systemic toxicity, which is of great significance for the treatment of chronic metabolic diseases, including IR.47

In addition to liposomes, the development of novel oral dosage forms using advanced manufacturing techniques is also a research hotspot. For example, immediate-release oral dosage forms loaded with quercetin, such as those derived from bilberry leaf extract, can be developed using 3D printing technology with carriers such as polyethylene oxide.48 This technology offers new avenues for personalized drug delivery, allowing drug dosage forms to be tailored to individual patient needs (eg, dose, release profile), thus promising to significantly improve patient treatment adherence and efficacy. Personalized formulations can more precisely control the release and absorption of drugs in the gastrointestinal tract, potentially partially overcoming quercetin’s low bioavailability due to the first-pass effect and rapid metabolism. Quercetin Targets Key Metabolic Transcription Factors and Epigenetic Regulation

Regulating the Nuclear-Cytoplasmic Distribution of ChREBP

Carbohydrate response element-binding protein (ChREBP) is a key transcription factor that senses intracellular glucose levels and regulates glucose and lipid metabolism. Under the stimulation of high glucose or lipid, ChREBP will translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and activate the transcription of a series of downstream target genes related to glycolysis and de novo lipid synthesis, such as fatty acid synthase, thereby promoting hepatic lipogenesis, which is an important link in the development of IR and metabolic diseases.51 Studies have shown that the over-activation of ChREBP is closely related to the pathological processes of obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and type 2 diabetes.52 Therefore, targeted regulation of ChREBP activity, especially its nucleoplasmic distribution, becomes a potential strategy to improve IR. Molecular dynamics simulation studies have provided new insights into this end, revealing that the natural flavonoid quercetin can bind to the ChREBP protein and “sequester” it in the cytoplasm, thereby effectively preventing its translocation to the nucleus in response to nutrient excess stimulation.5 This finding reveals a novel mechanism by which quercetin directly acts on ChREBP protein and interferes with its transcriptional activation function. In the animal model, quercetin’s intervention effect mimicked the phenotype of liver-specific ChREBP knockdown. By inhibiting the nuclear translocation of ChREBP, quercetin could down-regulate the expression of its key target gene, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN). PTEN is an important negative regulator of the insulin signaling pathway. On the one hand, its expression reduction can directly increase the phosphorylation level of the Akt signaling pathway in the liver, thereby improving local insulin sensitivity. On the other hand, decreased hepatic PTEN level promotes fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) release. FGF21 is an important metabolic regulatory hormone that has systemic effects on insulin sensitivity, energy expenditure, and glucose and lipid metabolism.53 Thus, quercetin works together to improve IR through this dual mechanism -that is, direct enhancement of hepatic Akt signaling and indirect systemic effects mediated through FGF21. These findings not only clarify the central role of ChREBP in regulating systemic insulin sensitivity but also provide a solid theoretical basis and new drug-design ideas for developing intervention strategies.

Epigenetic Regulation Potential of Metabolism-Related Genes

In addition to directly regulating transcription factor activity, quercetin’s effect in improving IR may also involve epigenetic regulation of metabolism-related genes. Epigenetic modifications, such as histone modifications and DNA methylation, can affect the long-term expression patterns of genes in the context of constantly changing DNA sequences, which is of great significance for understanding the pathogenesis and intervention strategies of metabolic diseases. As a polyphenolic compound, quercetin has been shown to regulate epigenetic enzymes. For example, quercetin can affect histone acetylation by activating the deacetylase SIRT1, which in turn regulates the transcription of downstream genes.54 Activation of SIRT1 is not only associated with the amelioration of inflammation and oxidative stress but also affects the activity of various metabolic transcription factors, including ChREBP, through deacetylation, thereby regulating the gene network of glucose and lipid metabolism at a broader level.55 This epigenetic regulation may affect the long-term expression of a series of genes involved in insulin secretion, insulin signaling, and chronic low-grade inflammation, providing a mechanistic explanation for the long-lasting metabolic benefits of quercetin.56 In addition, evidence suggests that quercetin may affect intergenerational metabolic programming through epigenetic mechanisms. In early-life stress models, such as maternal malnutrition, nutritional intervention can affect the offspring’s metabolic phenotype. Although studies directly targeting the mechanism of action of quercetin in such models are still being explored, reviews have pointed out that flavonoids may reduce the risk of IR and metabolic syndrome in offspring by modulating epigenetic marks through their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.57 For example, in obesity and IR, these conditions are often accompanied by specific alterations in DNA methylation or histone modification patterns, and phytochemicals such as quercetin may reverse these aberrant epigenetic changes.58 However, in the specific context of human metabolic syndrome, it remains to be clarified through more in-depth studies in cell and animal models exactly how quercetin reshapes the expression profile of metabolism-related genes via specific epigenetic pathways. Future research needs to integrate epigenomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data to comprehensively elucidate the complex network by which quercetin improves IR through epigenetic mechanisms.

Conclusion

As a natural polyphenol, quercetin has shown strong potential to improve IR. From the perspective of experts, its value lies not only in the direct pharmacological effects of multi-target approaches, but also in the fact that it provides a comprehensive intervention paradigm from the molecule to the system, from short-term regulation to long-term reprogramming. Its core mechanism is to positively regulate insulin signaling hubs such as IRS-1/Akt/FOXO1 and AMPK, and to strongly inhibit inflammatory hubs such as NF-κB and NLRP3, thereby constituting a “dual engine” that drives improvements in insulin sensitivity. This suggests that targeting two key nodes - energy sensing and inflammation control - may have fundamental advantages over single-targeted interventions in the complex network of metabolic diseases.

Of particular interest is that quercetin acts beyond the traditional cellular and molecular levels. It can reduce metabolic endotoxemia at the source by remodeling the ecology of intestinal flora and repairing the physical and immune barriers of the intestinal mucosa, thereby providing a new dimension of the “gut-liver axis” or “gut-periphery axis” for understanding its systemic anti-inflammatory effects and improving IR. This finding balances the perspective of previous studies that mostly focused on peripheral target organs such as liver, fat, or muscle, and emphasizes the importance of the gut as the starting station of cross-regulation between metabolism and immunity. In addition, its interaction with small molecules (such as ChREBP) to regulate subcellular localization, as well as the potential epigenetic regulation mechanism, reveals that quercetin may have long-term benefits in inducing “metabolic memory” or “metabolic reprogramming”, which provides a theoretical possibility for its leap from dietary supplement to metabolic regulatory drug.

However, there are still three major bottlenecks in transitioning from laboratory discoveries to clinical applications. First one, the bioavailability of quercetin is low and most delivery strategies remain in the preclinical stage. Second, the fragmentation of structure-activity relationships of quercetin derivatives. The existing evidence only suggests that C3-OH retention is a necessary prerequisite for activity, and A-ring methylation can enhance efficiency. However, the selective regulation of signaling pathways by different derivatization modes has not yet been established. The last one, there is a serious lack of high-quality clinical research, and no derivatives designed based on clear structure-activity relationships have entered Phase II or above trials.

In the future, we need to use computational chemistry, artificial intelligence, and structural biology to analyze the precise interaction patterns of quercetin with key targets at the atomic level to rationally design derivatives with higher selectivity and greater efficacy. On the other hand, we must vigorously promote well-designed, large-sample, long-term randomized controlled clinical trials to confirm their efficacy, optimal dose and formulation, and synergistic strategies with other hypoglycemic drugs in patients with type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. Only through this “in-depth mechanism” and “solidifying clinical” dual-drive research path can quercetin be accelerated from a promising preclinical molecule to a clinical drug with a clear therapeutic position, ultimately benefiting a large number of patients with IR and related metabolic diseases.
