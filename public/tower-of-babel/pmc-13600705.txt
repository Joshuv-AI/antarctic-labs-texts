Before the split: Early intervention as the key to surviving the cracked tooth epidemic
Authors: Shishir Singh
Published: 2026

Cracked teeth and vertical root fractures (VRFs) are becoming one of the more troubling problems in modern dentistry, and their growing frequency deserves attention.[1] A range of factors can set the stage for a crack to form – uneven occlusal forces, repeated mechanical loading, and parafunctional habits such as clenching or bruxism all play a role. Once a crack develops, bacteria and biofilm can work their way in, and over time this ingress can reach the pulp and lead to pulpal necrosis. What makes this especially difficult clinically is that recent studies have found a surprisingly high rate of cracks in vital posterior teeth that produce no symptoms at all, which makes diagnosis genuinely hard to pin down.[2] Given this, clinicians can no longer treat cracked teeth and VRFs as a secondary possibility in a differential diagnosis of dental pain – they need to be considered routinely, as a matter of course rather than an afterthought.

One meaningful step forward has been the effort to standardize terminology in this area. In the past, definitions overlapped and were often vague, which made it harder for clinicians to diagnose consistently or communicate clearly with one another.[3] The 2020 revision of the Glossary of Endodontic Terms addressed this gap by laying out clearer, more precise categories:[45]

Diagnosing these conditions is still difficult, but it has become considerably more reliable with the use of a surgical operating microscope – cracks and fractures are often too subtle for the unaided eye to catch. Shining light through the tooth from different angles (transillumination) further improves the odds of spotting a problem, and dye staining can make a crack stand out more clearly.[5] The clinical tests generally recommended for evaluating a suspected cracked or fractured tooth include:[5]

Perhaps the most important shift in recent years has been in how these cases are talked about with patients – a cracked tooth is no longer treated as an automatic loss. When a crown is placed early, ideally before the pulp reaches irreversible pulpitis, survival rates can reach as high as 97%–98%.[6] The Iowa Staging Index gives clinicians a practical, evidence-based framework for predicting outcomes and having realistic conversations with patients. It sorts cracked teeth into four stages:[57]

Iowa Stage 1 – probing depth under 5 mm, a crack on the mesial marginal ridge, and no apical pathology; success and survival rates run around 93% and 97%, respectively[8]Iowa Stage 2 – probing depth under 5 mm, a crack on the distal marginal ridge, and no apical pathology; treatment success is roughly 84%Iowa Stage 3 – probing depth under 5 mm, a crack on the distal marginal ridge, with periapical disease present; treatment success drops to around 69%Iowa Stage 4 – probing depth over 5 mm with a crack on either marginal ridge; treatment success falls further, to roughly 41%.

For VRFs specifically, single-rooted teeth are often lost to extraction, but multi-rooted teeth have a better chance – hemisection or root amputation can frequently save them, with survival outcomes that can match or even beat single-tooth implants, at a lower cost and in fewer visits.[910]

The takeaway is straightforward: catch these cracks early, crown promptly, and hold on to the natural tooth whenever that’s realistically possible.
