From Chinese Postman to Salesman and Beyond I: Approximating Shortest Tours δ \delta -Covering All Points on All Edges
Fabian Frei, Ahmed Ghazy, Tim A. Hartmann, Florian Hörsch, Dániel Marx

Abstract
A well-studied continuous model of graphs, introduced by Dearing and Francis [Transportation Science, 1974], considers each edge as a continuous unit-length interval of points. For δ ≥ 0 \delta\geq 0 , we introduce the problem δ \delta - Tour , where the objective is to find the shortest tour that comes within a distance of δ \delta of every point on every edge. It can be observed that 0 0 - Tour is essentially equivalent to the Chinese Postman Problem, which is solvable in polynomial time. In contrast, 1 / 2 1/2 - Tour is essentially equivalent to the Graphic Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), which is NP-hard but admits a constant-factor approximation in polynomial time. We investigate δ \delta - Tour for other values of δ \delta , noting that the problem’s behavior and the insights required to understand it differ significantly across various δ \delta regimes. We design polynomial-time approximation algorithms summarized as follows: (1) For every fixed 0 < δ < 3 / 2 0<\delta<3/2 , the problem δ \delta - Tour admits a constant-factor approximation. (2) For every fixed δ ≥ 3 / 2 \delta\geq 3/2 , the problem admits an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) -approximation. (3) If δ \delta is considered to be part of the input, then the problem admits an O ⁡ ( log 3 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{3}{n}) -approximation. This is the first of two articles on the δ \delta - Tour problem. In the second one we complement the approximation algorithms presented here with inapproximability results and related to parameterized complexity.

Introduction

We consider a well-studied continuous model of graphs introduced by Dearing and Francis
[
5
]
.
Each edge is seen as a continuous unit interval of points with its vertices as endpoints.
For any given graph
G
, this yields a compact metric space
(P(G),\operatorname{{d}})
with a point set
P(G)
and a distance function
\operatorname{{d}}\colon P(G)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

A prototypical problem in this setting is
\delta
-
Covering
,
introduced by Shier
[
22
]
for any positive real
\delta
.
The task is to find in
G
a minimum set
S
of points that
\delta
-covers

the entire graph, in the sense that each point in
P(G)
has distance at most
\delta
to some point in
S
.
This problem, which is also often referred to as the continuous
p
-
Center
problem has been extensively studied;
we cite only a few examples:
[
15
,
2
,
18
]
.
Observe that the problem differs from typical discrete graph problems
in two ways:
the solution has to
\delta
-cover every point of every edge
(not just the vertices) and the solution may (and for optimality sometimes must) use points inside edges.
How does the complexity of this problem depend on the distance
\delta
?
First, the problem is polynomial-time solvable when
\delta
is a unit fraction, i.e., a rational with numerator
1

and NP-hard for all other rational and irrational

10
,
13
]

One can show that

and

is reducible to

-
Covering

Thus

and can express problems of different nature and complexity:
for example, while vertex cover is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT)
when parameterized by the solution size,
dominating set is W[2]-hard.
This is reflected also in the complexity of
\delta
-
Covering
:
at the threshold of
\delta=3/2
, the parameterized complexity of the problem, parameterized by the size of the solution,
jumps from FPT to W[2]-hard
[
13
]
.
Similarly,
\delta
-
Covering
allows a constant factor approximation
for
\delta<3/2
and becomes log-APX-hard for

[

]
.
The problem dual to
\delta
-
Covering
is
\delta
-
Dispersion
,
as studied for example by Shier and Tamir
[
22
,
23
]
.
The task is to place a maximum number of points in the input graph
such that they pairwise have distance at least
\delta
from each other.
For this problem,
\delta=2
marks the threshold where the parameterized complexity
for the solution size as the parameter
jumps from FPT to W[1]-hard
[
12
]
.
Furthermore, the problem is polynomial-time solvable when
\delta
is a rational with numerator 1 or 2,
and NP-hard for all other rational and irrational
\delta

[
9
,
12
]
.
With
\delta
-
Covering
being the continuous version of
VertexCover
and
DominatingSet
,
and
\delta
-
Dispersion
being a continuous version of
IndependentSet
,
we now turn to the natural continuous variant of another famous problem.

We study the Graphic Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP)
with a positive real covering range
\delta
in the continuous model,
which we call
{\delta\textup{-{Tour}}}
.
A
{\delta\textup{-tour}}

is a tour
that may make U-turns at arbitrary points of the graph, even inside edges,

General Definitions.

For a positive integer

All graphs in this article are undirected, unweighted and do not contain parallel edges or loops. Let

Because TSP in the continuous model of graphs is studied in this article for the first time,
we need to lay a substantial amount of groundwork.
Due to the continuous nature of the problem, it is not clear a priori how to check if a solution is a valid
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
,
or whether it is possible to compute a shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
by a brute force search over a finite set of plausible tours.
We clarify these issues in this section.
While some of the arguments are intuitively easy to accept,
the formal proofs (presented in
Section

4
) are delicate with many corner cases to consider;
the reader might want to skip these proofs at first reading.

Sometimes, a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
has to make U-turns inside edges to be shortest; see
Figure

2(a)
.
Indeed, it can be checked that an optimal
1
-tour
for the graph in
Figure

2(a)
must look exactly as depicted.
Except for a single case, it is unnecessary for a tour to make more than one U-turn inside an edge.
Indeed, the only case where a shortest tour is forced to make two U-turns
in an edge is when the tour remains entirely within a single edge; see
Figure

2(b)
for an example.
Note also that there are degenerate cases in which a shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
consists of a single point.

However, unless a tour is completely contained in a single edge,
we can see that there are only four reasonable ways for a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
to interact with
the interior of any given edge,
illustrated in
Figure

:

(a)

completely avoiding the interior,

(b)

peeking into the edge from one side,

(c)

fully traversing the edge exactly once from one vertex to the other, or

(d)

traversing the edge exactly twice.

We call a tour that restricts itself to this reasonable behavior a

Lemma 2

(Nice Tours)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Further, let a tour

in

Then, in polynomial time, we can compute a tour

in

, such that,

is either nice or has at most two stopping points, and if

is a

{\delta\textup{-tour}}
for some
\delta\geq 0
, then so is

.

Despite the continuous nature of
{\delta\textup{-{Tour}}}

we show that we can actually study the problem in a discrete setting instead.
More precisely, we prove that
there is a nice shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}

T
defined by points
whose edge positions

come from a small explicitly defined set.
To show this, the idea is that there are only three scenarios for the edge position of a non-vertex stopping point

of

1.

It has distance exactly
\delta
to a vertex
u

An example is that

Then

has an edge position

2.

It has distance exactly
\delta
to a half-integral point

.
An example is that

is a long

-path with a triangle

and

is a closest point of

to

.
Then

has an edge position

which is the fractional part of

.

It has distance exactly

An example is that

contains a long

,

is the closest point of

and

\blacktriangleright

Lemma 2

(Discretization Lemma)
.

For every
\delta\geq 0
and every connected graph
G
, there is a shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
that is either nice or contains at most two stopping points and such that each stopping point of the tour can be described as
p(u,v,\lambda)
with
\lambda\in S_{\delta}

where

S_{\delta}=\big\{0,\delta-\lfloor\delta\rfloor,{\tfrac{1}{2}}+\delta-\lfloor{\tfrac{1}{2}}+\delta\rfloor,2\delta-\lfloor 2\delta\rfloor\big\}.

As a consequence, we can find a shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
by a brute-force algorithm. Using some related arguments, we can check whether a given tour actually is a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
in polynomial time.

3.2
Approximation Algorithms

Here, we overview the approximation algorithms we design for different ranges of
\delta
.
Most of our algorithms follow a general paradigm; our approach is to design
a collection of
core
approximation algorithms for certain key values
of
\delta
and rely on one of the following two ideas to extrapolate
the approximation ratios we get to previous and subsequent intervals.
The first main idea uses the simple fact that a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
is also
{(\delta+x)\textup{-tour}}
for all
x>0
.
Having an approximation algorithm for
{\delta\textup{-{Tour}}}
,
if we are able to reasonably bound the ratio between the lengths of a shortest
\delta
-tour and a shortest
(\delta+x)
-tour,
we obtain an approximation algorithm for
{(\delta+x)\textup{-{Tour}}}

4.1
General Observations

We first give a well-known result of Euler, that will be used several times throughout the article.
A multigraph
G
is called

if
\deg_{G}(v)
is even for all
v\in V(G)
.
An
Euler tour
of
G
is a sequence
v_{0},\ldots,v_{z}
of vertices in
G

such that for each pair
u,v\in V(G)
, the number of indices

with
\{v_{i-1},v_{i}\}=\{u,v\}
is exactly the number of edges in
E(G)
linking
u
and
v
.

Proposition 3

(Euler’s Theorem)
.

A multigraph
G
admits an Euler tour if and only if it is connected and Eulerian. Moreover, if an Euler tour exists, it can be computed in polynomial time.

We begin with two simple results.
As a little warm-up, we start with a simple observation giving an upper bound on the maximum length of
\delta
-tours if
\delta
is not too small.

Proposition 4
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
\delta\geq 1/2
a constant. Then
G
admits a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
of length at most

and this tour can be found in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
U
be an arbitrary spanning tree of
G
and let
U^{\prime}
be obtained from
U
by doubling every edge. Observe that
\deg_{U^{\prime}}(v)=2\deg_{U}(v)
is even for all

, hence by

3
, there is an Euler tour
T
of

. Observe that
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)=\lvert E(U^{\prime})\rvert=2n-2
. Further, let
p=(u,e,\lambda)\in P(G)
for some

and

. As

stops at

and

\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,T)\leq\min\{\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,u),\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,v)\}=\min\{\lambda,1-\lambda\}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+(1-\lambda))=\frac{1}{2}\leq\delta
. Hence

is a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
in

. It follows from Proposition
3
that

can be computed in polynomial time.
∎

In this section, we give a collection of results allowing to decide whether a given tour is actually a

\displaystyle 1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})

\displaystyle\geq 1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle\geq 1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})-(\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},x_{2})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2}))

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=0.

We further have

\displaystyle 1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{1})-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 1+(\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},x_{2}))-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})

\displaystyle=1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=2.

Hence
p
is well-defined.

As
T
stops at no point on
x_{1}x_{2}
and by assumption, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,T)

\displaystyle=\min\{\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,x_{1})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},T),\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,x_{2})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},T)\}

\displaystyle=\min\{(1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1}))/2+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1}),

\displaystyle\qquad 1-((1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})-\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})))/2+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})\}

\displaystyle=(1+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2}))/2

\displaystyle=(1+(\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},v_{1})+1-\lambda_{1})+(\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},v_{2})+1-\lambda_{2}))/2

\displaystyle=(3+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},v_{1})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},v_{2})-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}))/2

\displaystyle>\delta,

a contradiction to
p
being covered by
T
. We hence obtain
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}<\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},v_{1})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},v_{2})+3-2\delta
and so
(i)
holds.

Finally suppose that
T
stops at some point on
x_{1}x_{2}
. Let
p_{1}=p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda_{1})
and
p_{2}=p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda_{2})
be stopping points of
T
on
x_{1}x_{2}
which are chosen so that
\lambda_{1}
is minimized and
\lambda_{2}
is maximized. As
T
stops at none of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
and
T
is a tour, we obtain that all stopping points of
T
are on
x_{1}x_{2}
. As
T
covers
x_{1}x_{2}
, this yields
\delta\geq\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},T)=\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{1},p_{1})=\lambda_{1}
. A similar argument shows that
\lambda_{2}\leq 1-\delta
. Hence
(ii)
holds.
∎

We next deal with the case that the tour stops at exactly one vertex of the edge.

Lemma 12
.

Let
G
be a connected graph,
\delta\geq 0
a real,
T
a tour in
G
and
x_{1}x_{2}\in E(G)
such that
T
stops at
x_{1}
but not at
x_{2}
. Then
T
covers
x_{1}x_{2}
if and only if one of the following holds:

[(i)]

1.

There are stopping points
p_{1}=p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda_{1})
and
p_{2}=(v,x_{2},\lambda_{2})
of
T
with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in[0,1)
and
v\in N_{G}(x_{2})
such that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\geq 2-2\delta
holds.

2.

T
stops at the point
p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in[1-\delta,1]
.

Proof.

First suppose that
(i)
holds. Consider
p=p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in[0,1]
. If
\lambda\leq\lambda_{1}
, then, as
T
does not stop at
x_{2}
, we have that
T
passes
p
. If
\lambda>\lambda_{1}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(T,p)

\displaystyle\leq\min\{\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,p_{1}),\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,x_{2})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,p_{1})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,x_{2})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}((\lambda-\lambda_{1})+(1-\lambda)+(1-\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(2-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq\delta.

Hence
x_{1}x_{2}
is covered by
T
.

Next suppose that
(ii)
holds. Consider
p=p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in[0,1]
. If
\lambda\leq\lambda_{1}
, then, as
T
does not stop at
x_{2}
, we have that
T
passes
p
. If
\lambda>\lambda_{1}
, we have
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,T)\leq\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,p_{1})=\lambda-\lambda_{1}\leq 1-\lambda\leq\delta
. Hence
T
covers
x_{1}x_{2}
.

Now suppose that
T
covers
x_{1}x_{2}
. Let
\lambda_{1}
be the greatest real such that
T
stops at
p(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda_{1})
. If
\lambda_{1}\geq 1-\delta
, then
(ii)
holds, so there is nothing to prove. We may hence suppose that
\lambda_{1}<1-\delta
, so in particular
\delta<1
. Now let
p_{2}=(v,u,\lambda_{2})
be a point stopped at by
T
with
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})=\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},T)
. If
x_{2}\notin\{u,v\}
, we obtain
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},T)=\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})\geq 1>\delta
, a contradiction. We may hence suppose that
u=x_{2}
. If
\lambda_{2}<1-\delta
, we have
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},T)=\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})=1-\lambda_{2}>\delta
, a contradiction to
T
covering
x_{1}x_{2}
. We may hence suppose that
\lambda_{2}>1-\delta
. Suppose for the sake of a contradiction that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}<2-2\delta
. We now consider
p=(x_{1},x_{2},1+\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}))
. Observe that
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\geq-\lambda_{2}\geq-1
and
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\leq(1-\delta)-(1-\delta)=0
, so
p
is well-defined and, by the choice of
\lambda_{1}
, we have that
T
does not pass
p
. Next, by assumption, we have
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,p_{1})=1+\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})-\lambda_{1}=1-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})>1-\frac{1}{2}(2-2\delta)=\delta
. Further, by assumption, we have
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,x_{2})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})=1-(1+\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}))+(1-\lambda_{2})=1-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})>1-\frac{1}{2}(2-2\delta)=\delta
. By construction and the choice of
p_{2}
, this yields
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,T)=\min\{\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,p_{1}),\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,x_{2})+\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(x_{2},p_{2})\}>\delta
. This contradicts
T
covering
x_{1}x_{2}
. We obtain that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\geq 2-2\delta
, so
(i)
holds.
∎

We finally handle the case that the tour stops at both vertices of the edge.

Lemma 13
.

Let
G
be a connected graph,
\delta\geq 0
a real,

[(i)]

If

Now suppose that

or

, then
(i)
or
(ii)

holds, so there is nothing to prove. We may hence suppose that

does not traverse

and

\delta<\frac{1}{2}
. If

does not stop at any point of the form

and

, for

, a contradiction. Hence, as

is nice and

. If

4.4
Discretization

We now show that we can restrict ourselves to tours whose stopping points come from a small set of points. In particular, we aim to prove

, which we restate
below for convenience.

See

Again, we distinguish two domains of values for

For the main proof, we need some polyhedral results related to the vertex cover polytope.
Given a graph
G
, potentially containing loops, we consider the vertex-cover LP of
G
:

\boxed{\begin{gathered}\text{Minimize }\sum_{v\in V}z_{v}\text{, subject to the following:}\\
z_{u}+z_{v}\geq 1\text{ for all $e=uv\in E(G)$},\\
z_{v}\in[0,1]\text{ for all $v\in V(G)$.}\end{gathered}}

(
\diamond
)

Observe that, if
e
is a loop at a vertex

of

, then the corresponding inequality is
2z_{v}\geq 1
.
We use that there is a half-integral solution to the vertex-cover LP.

Proposition 14

(
[
19
]
)
.

For every graph
G

(z_{v}\colon v\in V(G))
of
(\diamond)
with

for all

\boxed{\begin{gathered}\text{Minimize }\sum_{v\in V}z_{v}\text{, subject to the following:}\\

\diamond\diamond
)

We next show that for all reasonable values of

for which (

Proposition 15
.

For every graph
G
and every
\gamma\in(0,1]

(z_{v}\colon v\in V(G))
of

with

Let

for all

z_{v}^{2}=\frac{1}{\gamma}z_{v}^{1}\leq 1
for all

z_{v}^{2}=\frac{1}{\gamma}z_{v}^{1}\geq 0

(\diamond)
.

Next, by Proposition
14
, there is an optimal solution
(z_{v}^{3}\colon v\in V(G))
for
(\diamond)

for all

for all

(\diamond\diamond)

We further have

Proposition 16
.

For every graph
G
without isolated vertices and every
\gamma\in(1,2]

(z_{v}\colon v\in V(G))

.

\gamma=2

\gamma<2

Let
(z_{v}^{1}\colon v\in V(G))

(z_{v}^{2}\colon v\in V(G))

z_{v}^{1}\geq\gamma-z_{u}^{1}-1\geq\gamma-1

(\diamond)
.

Next, by Proposition
14

(z_{v}^{3}\colon v\in V(G))

(z_{v}^{4}\colon v\in V(G))

Let

\delta\geq 0

4.5
Immediate Consequences

In this section, we conclude

The following result is an immediate consequence of Lemmas

Corollary 27
.

Given a graph
G
, a constant
\delta
and a tour

in
G
, we can decide in polynomial time whether

is a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
.

We further obtain the following result.

Corollary 28
.

Given a graph
G
and a constant
\delta>0
, there is an algorithm that computes a shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
in
G
and runs in
f(n)
.

Proof.

By

3.1
, it suffices to consider tours all of whose stopping points are contained in
S_{G,\delta}
where
S_{G,\delta}=\{p(u,v,\lambda)\mid uv\in E(G),\lambda\in S_{\delta}\}
. As a nice tour traverses every edge at most twice and stops at every point at most once, we obtain that it stops at most
\alpha n^{2}
times for an absolute constant
\alpha
. We now enumerate all possible sequences of points in

of length at most
\alpha n^{2}

.
∎

5
Approximation Algorithms

In this section, we deal with finding approximation algorithms for shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
s. The behavior of this problem is strongly influenced by the range
\delta
comes from.

The simplest case is when
\delta=0
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. For a
0
-tour
T
of
G
, we have
\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,T)=0
for every

p\in P(G)
. That is,
P(T)=P(G)
. A Chinese Postman tour is an integral tour in
G
traversing every
edge at least once. The crucial insight for finding a shortest 0-tour in
G
is that this problem is essentially equivalent
to finding a shortest Chinese Postman tour in
G
. Since a shortest Chinese Postman tour can
be found in polynomial time (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
7
]
), the problem
{0\textup{-{Tour}}}
is
also polynomial-time solvable. Formally, we prove the following result.

Observation 29
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm solving
{0\textup{-{Tour}}}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a connected graph.
We compute and output a shortest Chinese Postman Tour
T_{\text{CP}}
of
G
.
This can be done in polynomial time; see for example
[
6
,
7
]
.

Let
\mathsf{OPT}_{\text{CP}}
be the length of
T_{\text{CP}}
,
and let
\mathsf{OPT}_{0\textup{-tour}}
be the length of a shortest
{0\textup{-tour}}
.
Now we observe that a Chinese Postman tour given by a sequence
of adjacent vertices that traverses every edge is a
0
-tour of the same
length, so
\mathsf{OPT}_{0\textup{-tour}}\leq\mathsf{OPT}_{\text{CP}}
.
Conversely, it follows directly from
Lemma

26
that there is a
shortest
{0\textup{-tour}}
that is a Chinese Postman tour, so
\mathsf{OPT}_{\text{CP}}\leq\mathsf{OPT}_{0\textup{-tour}}
.
∎

For any
\delta>0
, the problem
{\delta\textup{-{Tour}}}
unfortunately becomes
NP-hard and even APX-hard as we show in
[
8
]
.
Therefore, we resort to the design of approximation algorithms for each
\delta>0
.

We consider the ranges in increasing order. More concretely, in Sections
5.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
,
5.4
and
5.5
, we consider the cases
\delta\in(0,1/6]
,
\delta\in(1/6,1/2)
,
\delta=1/2
,
\delta\in(1/2,33/40)
, and
\delta\in[33/40,3/2)
, respectively. The remaining part of this section is dedicated to dealing with large
\delta
. In Section
5.6
, we give the construction of an auxiliary graph which will be helpful for this purpose. We exploit this in Section
5.6.1
, where we give an approximation result for fixed, large
\delta
and in Section
5.6.2
, where we deal with the case that
\delta
is part of the input.

5.1
Covering Range
\delta\in(0,1/6]

We start with the smallest covering range, that is,
\delta\in(0,1/6]
. It turns out that in this range,
a shortest Chinese Postman Tour is a good approximation of a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
.
That is, our algorithm consists of computing a shortest Chinese Postman tour.
Intuitively speaking, for this small
\delta
, there is a nice shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
that stops at every vertex and passes large parts of every edge. We can hence show that
a shortest Chinese Postman Tour is only larger by small factor than such a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
.
Observe that the approximation ratio of our algorithm approaches
1
when
\delta
goes to
0
. More precisely, we prove Theorem
3.2
, which we restate here for convenience.

See
3.2

Proof.

Let
G
be a connected graph. By Corollary
28
, we may suppose that
|V(G)|\geq 3
. We compute and output a shortest Chinese Postman Tour

T_{\text{CP}}
of
G
.
Let
\mathsf{OPT}_{\text{CP}}
be the length of
T_{\text{CP}}
,
and let
\mathsf{OPT}_{\delta\textup{-tour}}
be the length of a shortest
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
.

By definition,
T_{\text{CP}}
traverses every edge and hence is a
{\delta\textup{-tour}}
.
It remains to bound its length.
By
Lemma

26
and as
|V(G)|\geq 3
, there is a
minimum length nice
{\delta\textup{-tour}}

T=p_{0}p_{1}\dots p_{z}
which, for every edge
uv\in E(G)
,
either traverses
uv
or contains the segment
u^{\prime}p(u^{\prime},v,^{\prime}\lambda)u^{\prime}

for some
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
with
\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}=\{u,v\}
and some
\lambda\geq 1-2\delta
.
It follows that the extension

of

\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(\lceil T\rceil)\leq\frac{1}{1-2\delta}\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T_{\text{CP}})

Hence
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T_{\text{CP}})=\mathsf{OPT}_{\text{CP}}\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(\lceil T\rceil)\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)/({1-2\delta})={\mathsf{OPT}_{\delta\textup{-tour}}}/({1-2\delta})
.
∎

5.2
Covering Range
\delta\in(1/6,1/2)

We next consider the case that
\delta\in(1/6,1/2)
.
It turns out that if
\delta
is in this range, we can benefit from a close connection to a deeply studied related algorithmic problem, namely
MetricTSP
(where TSP stands for Traveling Salesman Problem).
Formally, given an edge weighted graph
(H,w)
and a tour
T=p_{0}\dots p_{z}
in
H
such that
p_{i}\in V(H)
for all
i\in[z]
,
we define the length of a tour as
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)=\sum_{i\in[z]}w(p_{i-1}p_{i})
.
The problem can now be defined as follows.

Algorithm 2

MetricTSP

Instance

A connected graph
H
with a weight function
w\colon E(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Solution

Any tour
T
in
H
stopping at all vertices of
V(H)
.

Goal

Minimize the tour length
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)
.

We acknowledge that
MetricTSP
is often defined in a slightly different form where
H
is restricted to be complete,
w
is required to be metric and a Hamiltonian cycle is sought rather than an arbitrary tour stopping at all vertices. However, both versions are easily seen to be equivalent and so we use the above version which is more convenient for our purposes.

We heavily rely on a well-known result of Christofides stating the existence of a
1.5
-approximation algorithm for metric TSP. We use the following more formal restatement of the result of Christofides.

Lemma 30

(
[
3
,
4
]
)
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a TSP tour
T
of a given
weighted connected graph
(H,w)
such that
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)\leq 1.5\cdot\mathsf{OPT}_{\textup{TSP}}
,
where
\mathsf{OPT}_{\textup{TSP}}
is the length of a shortest TSP tour of
(H,w)
.

We are now ready to describe our algorithm which is based on a reduction to an instance of
MetricTSP
and applying Lemma
30
. Some results from Section
4
will be convenient in the analysis of the quality of the algorithm. Formally, we prove Theorem
3.2
, which we restate here for convenience.

5.6

In this section, we consider the problem of finding approximation algorithms for
\delta

In

We consider two different settings:

We now give a construction which is the crucial ingredient for the proofs of Theorems

and

Namely, for a connected graph

and a constant
\delta
, we construct an auxiliary graph
\Gamma(G,\delta)

We then show that there is a close relationship among
\delta
-tours in

and dominating sets in

. In Section

-approximation algorithm for arbitrary fixed

\delta
being part of the input, in Section

-approximation for this setting by relying on previous results on a slightly different problem, called

.

We now detail the construction of

. We fix a connected graph

Let

Let

and the set of points

, we have
that
|V(\Gamma(G,\delta))|=O(n^{4})
and hence
|Y|\leq\alpha^{\prime}\log(n)|Y^{*}|
for some absolute constant
\alpha^{\prime}
.
We now obtain
Y^{\prime}
from
Y
by replacing every
v\in Y-P_{\delta}(G)
by some arbitrary
v^{\prime}\in N_{\Gamma(G,\delta)}(v)
, keeping only one copy of multiple elements in
Y^{\prime}
. As
\Gamma(G,\delta)[P_{\delta}(G)]

is a clique and
\Gamma(G,\delta)-P_{\delta}(G)
is an independent set by construction, we obtain that
Y^{\prime}
is a
dominating set of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
. Further, by Proposition
45
, in polynomial time, we can compute a
tour
T
in
G
whose length is at most
4\delta|Y^{\prime}|
and that stops at all points of
Y^{\prime}
. We now output
T
.
Observe that
T
can be computed in polynomial time. As
T
stops at all points of
Y^{\prime}
, we obtain by
Lemma
3.2
that
T
is a
\delta
-tour in
G
. In order to bound the length of
T
, let
T^{*}
be a
shortest
\delta
-tour in
G
. Let
P_{T^{*}}
be the set of stopping points of
T^{*}
. By
Lemma
20
, we may suppose that
P_{T^{*}}
is contained in
P_{G,\delta}
. It hence
follows by Lemma
3.2
that
P_{T^{*}}
is a dominating set of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
. We obtain that
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T^{*})\geq s_{\delta}|P_{T^{*}}|
by
Proposition

48
. This
yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta|Y^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta|Y|

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta\alpha^{\prime}\log(n)|Y^{*}|

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta\alpha^{\prime}\log(n)|P_{T^{*}}|

\displaystyle\leq 4\delta\frac{1}{s_{\delta}}\alpha^{\prime}\log(n)\ell(T^{*}).

This finishes the proof.
∎

5.6.2
Covering Range
\delta
as Part of the Input

In the setting where
\delta
is provided as part of the input, the algorithm from the previous section does not yield any non-trivial approximation guarantee. This is due to two reasons: first, we get an additional factor of roughly
\delta
when connecting the dominating set into a tour and second, our estimation for the length of a shortest tour gives another factor of
\frac{1}{s_{\delta}}
which may be large for certain values of
\delta
.

In an attempt to overcome this problem, we use a result for a slightly different problem that also deals with dominating sets, but, in addition, also takes into account the distance of the points in the dominating set that is sought for.

Formally, given a graph
H
, a
dominating
tree
U
of
H
is a subgraph of
H
that is a tree and such that
V(U)
is a dominating set of
H
. Further, given a weight function
w\colon E(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the weight
w(U)
of a subgraph
U
of
H
is defined to be
\sum_{e\in E(U)}w(e)
.

We use the following result due to Kutiel
[
17
]
.

Lemma 49

(
[
17
]
)
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that,
given a weighted graph
G
of order
n
,
computes a
(\log n)^{3}
-approximation of a minimum weight
dominating tree of
G
.

We now fix a connected graph
G
and
\delta>1
and define a weight function
w\colon E(\Gamma(G,\delta))\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. For all
p,p^{\prime}\in P_{\delta}(G)
, we set
w(pp^{\prime})=\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p,p^{\prime})
and for all
p\in P_{\delta}(G)
and
I\in\mathcal{I}_{G,\delta}
such that
px_{I}\in E(G(\Gamma,\delta))
, we set
w(px_{I})=n^{3}
.

We now give the following key lemmas relating
\delta
-tours in
G
and dominating trees of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
.

Lemma 50
.

Let
U
be a dominating tree of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
with
V(U)\subseteq P_{\delta}(G)
. Then, in polynomial time, we can compute a
\delta
-tour
T
of
G
with
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)\leq 2w(U)
.

Proof.

Let
U^{\prime}
be the multigraph obtained from
U
by replacing every edge by two copies of itself. By Proposition
3
, we obtain that
U^{\prime}
has an Euler tour
T_{0}=p_{0}\ldots p_{z}
. We now obtain
T
from
T_{0}
by replacing the segment
p_{i-1}p_{i}
by a shortest walk in
G
from
p_{i-1}
to
p_{i}
. Observe that
T
can be constructed in polynomial time. Further, we have that
T
stops at all points in
V(U)
. It hence follows from Lemma
3.2
that
T
is a
\delta
-tour in
G
. Further, by the definition of
w
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{\ell}}(T)=\sum_{i\in[z]}\operatorname{{d}}_{G}(p_{i-1},p_{i})=\sum_{i\in[z]}w(p_{i-1}p_{i})=2w(U)
.
∎

Lemma 51
.

Let
T
be a
\delta
-tour in
G
all of whose stopping points are in

. Then there is a dominating tree

in

with

Proof.

Let
V_{T}
be the set of stopping points of

.
It follows by Lemma
3.2

that

.
Now let

be the graph on

that contains an edge linking two points

whenever there is some

such that
\{p_{i-1},p_{i}\}=\{p,p^{\prime}\}
.
Observe that
U_{0}
is a subgraph of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
.
As
T
is a tour, we have that
U_{0}
is connected.
Now let
U
be an arbitrary spanning tree of

. As

is a dominating set of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
,
we obtain that
U
is a dominating tree of
\Gamma(G,\delta)
. Further, we have

.
∎
