Identification of a mitochondrial biomarker signature linking neuroinflammation to neuronal dysfunction in spinal cord injury
Authors: Haipeng Xu, Yaheng Jiang, Luojie Xiong, Ding Tang, Honggen Du, Yunxing Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the lack of reliable biomarkers for mitochondrial dysfunction that drives secondary injury in spinal cord injury (SCI), this study aimed to identify and validate a robust mitochondria-focused biomarker signature by integrating transcriptomic profiling with machine learning. Gene expression data from 113 mouse spinal cord samples were integrated and analyzed to identify a distinct SCI-associated mitochondrial biomarker signature. Dysregulated mitochondrial genes were screened from this dataset. Multiple machine learning algorithms were then applied to derive a high-performing molecular signature. A quantitative nomogram model was constructed based on this signature, and its accuracy in distinguishing pathological SCI states from controls was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). The identified gene upregulation was further validated in an independent SCI mouse model. Finally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to investigate the biological pathways associated with the gene signature. A high-performing molecular signature comprising five mitochondrial genes, Mcl1, Dhrs1, Tspo, Star, and Pmaip1, was identified. A nomogram model was constructed based on this signature to quantify the mitochondrial dysregulation severity following injury. This model demonstrated high accuracy in reflecting the pathological status, achieving an AUC of 0.931. This signature offers a molecular tool to assess the extent of secondary pathological changes, potentially serving as a biomarker for therapeutic monitoring. The upregulation of all five genes was confirmed in an independent mouse SCI model. GSEA revealed strong associations between the identified signature and the activation of innate immune pathways, alongside the suppression of neuronal function pathways. This study established a robust, descriptive mitochondria-associated gene signature that accurately reflects the transcriptomic alterations following SCI. This signature maps a key pathogenic axis linking mitochondrial dysregulation with neuroimmune pathology. Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating neurological trauma that places a profound burden on individuals and society owing to its high incidence and long-term disability rates1. Globally, the annual incidence of SCI ranges from 10.4 to 83 cases/million, with traumatic causes, including traffic accidents and falls, predominating2. Patients frequently experience lifelong paralysis, chronic pain, and bladder or bowel dysfunction, all of which substantially reduce quality of life and impose considerable socioeconomic costs3.

From a pathophysiological perspective, SCI progression is biphasic, comprising an initial primary mechanical insult, followed by a prolonged secondary injury cascade4. The secondary phase, which unfolds over minutes to months, involves a complex interplay of vascular disruption, excitotoxicity, inflammation, and oxidative stress, ultimately determining the extent of irreversible neural damage5–7. Accordingly, most therapeutic strategies focus on mitigating secondary injury to preserve neural tissue and promote functional recovery.

In this intricate network of secondary mechanisms, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central hub and critical driver of pathology8. As the principal source of cellular energy, mitochondria generate the vast majority of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation, a process vital for meeting the high metabolic demands of neurons9. Following SCI, ischemia, excitotoxicity, and inflammation converge on the mitochondria, causing profound structural and functional collapse9,10. This bioenergetic failure rapidly depletes ATP, impairs ion pump activity, and exacerbates excitotoxic stress11. Concurrently, massive calcium influx into neurons is sequestered by the mitochondria, leading to sustained calcium overload. This triggers mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening, resulting in mitochondrial membrane potential loss, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, and release of pro-apoptotic factors, including cytochrome c12. Damaged mitochondria are also potent sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS), perpetuating a vicious cycle of oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and programmed cell death that amplifies the initial injury7.

Although the physical diagnosis of SCI is clinically evident, quantifying the underlying molecular pathology, specifically the severity of mitochondrial dysfunction, remains a challenge. Standard tools, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and neurological assessments, such as the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale, primarily evaluate gross structural damage and functional deficits13. While indispensable, MRI often lacks sensitivity to cellular and molecular pathology, particularly in the acute phase, and clinical scales can be subjective or difficult to implement in patients with polytrauma14. Efforts to overcome these limitations have focused on fluid-based molecular biomarkers in blood or cerebrospinal fluid. Several candidates, including glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1(UCH-L1), and neurofilament light chain (NfL), have revealed correlations with injury severity and outcomes. However, these markers lack specificity, as they are also elevated in other central nervous system injuries, including traumatic brain injury15,16. Moreover, they typically reflect downstream structural damage rather than upstream mechanistic events, most notably the extent of mitochondrial dysfunction5.

This highlights a critical research gap: the absence of validated biomarkers that can serve as pharmacodynamic endpoints to guide and monitor neuroprotective therapies targeting mitochondria. Such markers are urgently required not only to enhance molecular assessment and prognostic accuracy but also to facilitate the preclinical development of metabolic interventions. The growing availability of high-throughput transcriptomic datasets, combined with the analytical power of advanced machine learning algorithms, presents a unique opportunity to address this gap17. Unlike traditional single-marker approaches, machine learning can interrogate complex, high-dimensional data to identify multi-gene signatures that faithfully capture the underlying biological processes18. This data-driven strategy enables the discovery of biomarker panels with superior predictive accuracy and mechanistic relevance.

In this study, we harnessed publicly available murine SCI transcriptomic datasets and implemented a multilayered machine learning approach to systematically identify and validate a mitochondria-related biomarker signature. We aimed to construct and rigorously validate a high-performance evaluation model based on a set of mitochondrial hub genesto provide a novel molecular tool for assessing mitochondrial status in preclinical SCI models and to illuminatepotential therapeutic targets in the context of mitochondrial dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Microarray data acquisition and preprocessing

Two transcriptomic datasets associated with SCI, GSE5296 and GSE47681, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE5296 dataset (platform: GPL1261, Affymetrix Mouse Genome 430 2.0 Array) comprised 54 SCI and 42 control samples. However, the GSE47681 dataset (platform: GPL6246) included 13 SCI and 4 control samples. For GSE47681, only samples from wild-type mice were selected for the analyses. Samples from trkB.T1 knockout mice were strictly excluded to prevent genotypic confounding effects. For data preprocessing, the average expression value was calculated for genes corresponding to multiple probes, and batch effects between the two datasets were mitigated using the ComBat algorithm implemented in the sva R package. The effectiveness of preprocessing and normalization was assessed using visual and statistical methods, including box plots and principal component analysis (PCA) performed using ggplot2 and FactoMineR R packages. It is important to note that by pooling transcriptomic samples across multiple post-SCI time points to identify a persistent core signature, this analytical framework inherently lacks temporal resolution and cannot distinguish the distinct molecular variations specific to the acute, subacute, or chronic phases.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

DEGs between SCI and control groups in the merged, normalized dataset were identified using the limma R package. Genes with adjusted P < 0.05 and |log₂fold change| (|log2FC|) > 0.58 were considered statistically significant DEGs.

Functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological roles and pathways of the identified DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed19,20. Gene symbols were converted to Entrez IDs using org.Mm.eg.db. GO (biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions) and KEGG analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler. Significant enrichment was defined as an adjusted P < 0.05. Data visualization was performed using the ggplot2 package.

Screening for mitochondria-related DEGs (MitoDEGs)

A comprehensive list of 1140 mouse mitochondrial-related genes (Mito-Genes) encoding proteins localized to the mitochondria was retrieved from the MitoCarta (version 3.0) database. An intersection analysis between Mito-genes and identified DEGs yielded MitoDEGs.

Identification of hub MitoDEGs via machine learning

To identify hub genes with high classification potential from the list of mitoDEGs, five machine learning algorithms were applied in parallel: Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression, random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), generalized linear model (GLM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB). The LASSO algorithm was implemented using the glmnet package, with the optimal regularization parameter λ determined using tenfold cross-validation. RF, SVM, GLM, and XGB models were implemented using the caret package with default parameters and evaluated using a five-fold cross-validation scheme21–24. Model performance metrics and feature importance rankings were interpreted using DALEX and pROC packages. The most robust models were compared using a Venn diagram generated using jvenn to identify common hub genes, which were prioritized for subsequent validation and analysis.

Construction and validation of the evaluation nomogram

A nomogram model was developed based on the expression of the final hub genes using the rms package. The predictive accuracy and robustness were internally validated using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). Calibration curves were used to assess the agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the net benefits.

Animal model and SCI procedure

All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (IACUC-20220117-11) and conformed to the relevant guidelines. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (weight, 18–22 g) were bought from Shanghai BK Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, ad libitum access to food and water) at the Animal Experimental Center of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. For SCI induction, the mice were anesthetized with 0.3% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.). A laminectomy at T10 exposed the spinal cord, followed by a moderate contusion injury using an Infinite Horizon Impactor (IH-0400, force: 50 kdyn). The control mice underwent only a laminectomy. Mice with Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scores > 2 post-injury were excluded. At the designated experimental endpoints, the mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose followed by cervical dislocation.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

To validate the temporal expression profile of the hub genes, mice were euthanized at 1, 3, and 7 days post-injury. The spinal cord tissues were immediately harvested. To ensure precise sampling of the injury site, an approximately 5 mm long spinal cord segment centered at the impact epicenter (T10) was dissected and used for RNA and protein extractions. For the controls, the equivalent 5 mm spinal cord segment at T10 was collected. Tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 ℃ until processing. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Solarbio, China). Reverse transcription was performed using the HiFiScript Reverse Transcription Kit (Cwbio, China). qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler 7500 using TB Green® Fast qPCR Mix (Takara, China). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as a control. Primer sequences are detailed in Table 1.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from spinal cord tissues using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, China). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto Polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against Mcl1 (16225-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Dhrs1 (16275-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Tspo (15444-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Star (12225-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Pmaip1 (A9801, 1:1000, Abclonal), and β-tubulin (66240-1-Ig, 1:2000, Proteintech). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (111-035-003, Jackson, 1:5000). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and quantified using the ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Following paraffin embedding, sections were cut at 4 µm thickness. For staining, sections were first heated at 95°C for 15 min, then treated with 3% H2O2 and 0.5% Triton X-100. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubation with 10% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies against Mcl1 (16225-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Dhrs1 (16275-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Tspo (15444-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Star (12225-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), Pmaip1 (A9801, 1:1000, Abclonal) were applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, sections were incubated with a secondary antibody, counterstained with hematoxylin, and developed with DAB. Immunoreactivity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Single-gene GSEA was used to explore the pathways associated with each hub gene. All genes were ranked by correlation with the target gene expression, and enrichment analysis was performed against Reactome, WikiPathways, and GO databases (version 2023.08). Significant enrichment was defined as P < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) q-value < 0.25, following the standard recommendations for exploratory GSEA to capture subtle but consistent pathway alterations25–27.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) software. For the experimental data, normality was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As data followed a normal distribution (P > 0.05), the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two independent groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were used. For comparisons among multiple groups (e.g., different time points), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Specific statistical thresholds for bioinformatic analyses are detailed in the corresponding sections.

Results

GEO dataset preprocessing eliminated batch effects

To construct a robust dataset for identifying reliable SCI biomarkers, transcriptomic profiles from two independent GEO cohorts, GSE5296 and GSE47681, were integrated, yielding 67 SCI samples and 46 controls. Initial examination of the raw combined data revealed a pronounced batch effect between cohorts, as evidenced by inconsistent median expression levels in the box plots (Supplementary Figure 1A) and clear segregation of samples by dataset in the PCA plot (Supplementary Figure 1C). To eliminate this non-biological variance, the Combat algorithm was applied, resulting in uniform expression distributions across all samples (Supplementary Figure 1B) and homogeneous clustering in the PCA space (Supplementary Figure 1D). This successful batch effect correction generated a reliable integrated dataset that ensured the validity of subsequent differential expression and machine learning analyses.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed a dominant immuno-inflammatory signature in SCI

Following normalization, differential expression analysis identified 591 genes significantly dysregulated in SCI relative to controls (|log2FC|> 0.58, adjusted P < 0.05; Fig. 1A). A heatmap of the top 100 DEGs demonstrated distinct expression patterns that separated SCI from control samples (Fig. 1B), reflecting a consistent and injury-specific transcriptomic profile. GO enrichment analysis indicated the predominant involvement of immune-related biological processes, including “leukocyte-mediated immunity,” “leukocyte migration,” “cytokine-mediated signaling pathway,” and “regulation of inflammatory response” (Fig. 1C). Concordantly, KEGG pathway analysis revealed28 significant enrichment in canonical immuno-inflammatory pathways, including “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “chemokine signaling pathway,” “TNF signaling pathway,” and “Toll-like receptor signaling pathway” (Fig. 1D). Notably, pathways intrinsically associated with mitochondrial integrity, including apoptosis and canonical inflammasome complex, were also enriched. This suggests that the observed inflammatory storm is likely intertwined with mitochondrial-dependent cell death and metabolic dysregulation, necessitating a targeted investigation of mitochondrial gene expression to reveal the upstream drivers of this pathology. These findings indicate that the acute phase of SCI triggers profound transcriptomic reprogramming that coordinates a potent local immune and inflammatory response.

Mitochondria-related genes were significantly dysregulated following SCI

Given the pivotal role of mitochondria in cellular stress regulation and immune signaling, we examined mitochondrial gene expression within our dataset. The intersection of the 591 DEGs with the 1140 mitochondrial genes defined in MitoCarta (version 3.0) identified nine significantly dysregulated MitoDEGs (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical clustering based on expression patterns effectively distinguished SCI from control samples (Fig. 2B). Among the nine identified MitoDEGs, eight were significantly upregulated in SCI (P < 0.001), whereas Cox8b was significantly downregulated (Fig. 2C). Correlation analysis revealed strong positive interrelationships among several genes, particularly Tspo, Dhrs1, and Star (Fig. 2D), suggesting a potential co-regulatory module of mitochondrial function in the post-injury environment.

Machine learning algorithms identified a robust five-mitochondrial-gene signature

To refine candidate biomarkers from the nine MitoDEGs, a multi-algorithm machine learning approach was used. LASSO regression reduced the candidate set to six genes with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ value (Fig. 3A, B). Subsequently, the GLM, RF, SVM, and XGB classifiers were trained using all nine MitoDEGs. Feature importance analysis across models consistently ranked Mcl1, Dhrs1, and Tspo as the top contributors to classification accuracy (Fig. 3C). Comparative five-fold cross-validation demonstrated that GLM achieved the highest classification performance (AUC = 0.931) in the internal validation, closely followed by SVM (AUC = 0.900) (Fig. 3E). To derive a high-confidence molecular signature, the top-ranked genes from LASSO, GLM, and SVM were intersected, yielding a core set of five mitochondrial hub genes: Mcl1, Dhrs1, Tspo, Star, and Pmaip1 (Fig. 4A). All five genes were significantly upregulated in SCI compared to controls (Fig. 4B), and correlation analysis revealed notable interrelationships, with Dhrs1 revealing the strongest positive correlation with Tspo (r = 0.89) and the most pronounced negative correlation with Pmaip1 (r = − 0.16) (Fig. 4C). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses confirmed the strong individual discriminative potential of each gene, with Mcl1 yielding the highest AUC (0.933), followed by Dhrs1 (0.850), Tspo (0.813), Pmaip1 (0.800), and Star (0.787) (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these analyses defined a robust five-gene mitochondrial signature. However, because this signature was derived from temporally pooled datasets and reflects generalized mitochondrial dysfunction, it represents a pan-temporal core distress signal rather than a strictly phase-specific or exclusively SCI-specific biomarker.

A molecular nomogram based on the five-gene signature demonstrated high predictive accuracy and translational potential

To translate the five-gene mitochondrial signature (Mcl1, Dhrs1, Tspo, Star, and Pmaip1) into a quantitative tool for injury assessment, we constructed a quantitative nomogram that integrated the individual gene contributions to generate an overall probability score for SCI (Fig. 5A). This scoring system enables a quantitative estimation of injury severity using transcriptomic data. The model performance was rigorously validated. Calibration analysis revealed a close alignment between the nomogram-predicted probabilities and observed SCI frequencies, with the calibration curve closely following the ideal diagonal line (Fig. 5B). DCA further indicated a substantial net benefit across several threshold probabilities compared to the default strategies of classifying all or none of the samples (Fig. 5C). Together, these evaluations confirm that the five-gene signature-based nomogram offers high discriminative accuracy and meaningful utility for the experimental assessment of SCI.

The expression of five hub genes at protein and mRNA levels after SCI

Furthermore, to validate these findings at both the mRNA and protein level and to elucidate both their temporal dynamics and spatial distribution, we performed qRT-PCR, Western blot and immunohistochemical analyses at 1, 3, and 7 days post-SCI. Both qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis confirmed that all five hub genes were significantly upregulated at the mRNA and protein levels throughout this acute phase, consistent with the transcriptional data (Fig. 6). Complementing this, immunohistochemistry confirmed that the expression of these mitochondrial proteins was markedly upregulated within the spinal cord tissue at the lesion site following injury (Fig. 7). This demonstrates that the expression of these mitochondrial-associated genes spatially overlaps with the injury site, reflecting injury-associated transcriptional changes rather than a direct measurement of mitochondrial health or function.

GSEA linked hub gene expression to immune activation and neuronal functional suppression

GSEA was conducted to elucidate the biological roles of the five hub genes in SCI pathology. All genes in the dataset were ranked following their correlation with the expression of each hub gene. Across all five genes, strong positive correlations were observed with gene sets involved in acute immune and inflammatory responses, including “neutrophil degranulation,” “microglia pathogen phagocytosis pathway,” “cytokine signaling in immune system,” and “extracellular matrix organization” (Supplementary Figure 2A, C, and E and 3A, C). Conversely, hub gene expression revealed pronounced negative correlations with pathways essential for neuronal activity and homeostasis, including the “neuronal system,” “transmission across chemical synapses,” “neurotransmitter receptors,” and “voltage-gated potassium channels” (Supplementary Figure 2B, D, and F and 3B, D). Mechanistically, this dual enrichment pattern suggests that dysregulation of these core mitochondrial genes triggers a bioenergetic crisis and the release of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns. Specifically, the imbalance of apoptotic regulators (Mcl1/Pmaip1) and upregulation of stress sensors (Tspo) facilitate mitochondrial membrane permeabilization. This event compromises the ATP supply required for neuronal synaptic homeostasis (suppressing neuronal function)9,29 and releases immunogenic factors (for example, mitochondrial DNA and ROS) that actively fuel the observed innate immune response30. Consequently, these genes likely act as a ‘molecular switch,’ converting mitochondrial stress into neuroinflammation and functional impairment.

Discussion

SCI triggers a complex secondary injury cascade, with mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation as central pathological drivers31. However, there remains a critical gap in the biomarker repertoire of tools that accurately reflect these upstream mechanisms. Current markers, including GFAP and NfL, predominantly report downstream structural damage and lack specificity for SCI16,32,33. To address this unmet need, we used an integrated bioinformatics and machine learning framework to identify and validate a robust five-gene molecular signature, comprising Mcl1, Dhrs1, Tspo, Star, and Pmaip1, all of which are intimately associated with mitochondrial function. This signature provides a molecular window into the mitochondrial dysregulation that fuels the deleterious secondary injury cascade, moving beyond the conventional markers of general cellular damage.

The biological plausibility of these genes is strong. Three members of the signature, Tspo, Star, and Dhrs1, were highlighted for their robust statistical performance across machine learning models and their strong co-expression pattern, suggesting a functional module. Biologically, they represent a critical nexus for regulating neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. The upregulation of Tspo, an established marker of activated microglia and macrophages, aligns with the pronounced neuroinflammatory state characteristic of acute SCI33. Importantly, Tspo marks glial activation and actively modulates mitochondrial bioenergetics and apoptosis34, placing it at a critical control point for glial responses. Similarly, Star, the rate-limiting enzyme in neurosteroid synthesis, suggests the recruitment of an endogenous anti-inflammatory mechanism, as neurosteroids exert potent neuroprotective effects35,36. Dhrs1, which plays a role in redox homeostasis, likely reflects a cellular response attempting to mitigate mitochondrial oxidative stress after SCI37,38. Together, these three genes reinforce the concept that mitochondria play a central role in the interplay between inflammatory signaling and cellular stress regulation.

The remaining two signature genes, Mcl1 and Pmaip1 (Noxa), are key regulators in the Bcl-2 family-controlled intrinsic apoptosis pathway39. Their simultaneous upregulation is particularly compelling, suggesting a molecular “tug-of-war” in injured spinal cord cells; this dual expression profile provides direct molecular evidence of apoptotic rheostat disruption in SCI, reflecting dynamic and contested cell fate decisions during the secondary injury phase40–42.

A notable contribution of this study is the construction of a translationally relevant quantitative nomogram.While single biomarkers frequently suffer from limited sensitivity and specificity for complex diseases, multi-gene signatures derived through machine learning can offer a more comprehensive portrayal of the underlying pathology43. Our five-gene nomogram achieved excellent discriminative performance (AUC = 0.931), with calibration analysis confirming reliable predictive accuracy and DCA demonstrating a substantial net benefit over default strategies44,45. Notably, this model offers significant potential for the molecular stratification of injury severity. Rather than diagnosing SCI, which is clinically obvious, these biomarkers provide a window into the invisible secondary injury cascade, allowing future translational studies to objectively monitor mitochondrial-related transcriptional changes in response to treatment.

Mechanistic insights from GSEA further highlight the significance of our findings. Across all five genes, a strong positive correlation was observed with innate immune activation pathways, including neutrophil degranulation, microglial pathogen phagocytosis, and cytokine signaling, and a pronounced negative correlation with neuronal function pathways, including synaptic transmission and voltage-gated ion channel regulation. This dual pattern underscores the role of the signature as a molecular nexus in SCI pathology, bridging mitochondrial distress, heightened neuroinflammation, and impaired neuronal activity through the dual mechanisms of bioenergetic deficit and immunogenic signaling. Such integration moves our results from a mere biomarker panel to a functional disease module central to neuroimmune dysregulation.

To further contextualize our findings in the evolving landscape of SCI transcriptomics, it is instructive to distinguish our results from concurrent studies using overlapping data sources. Recently, Lv et al. analyzed the GSE47681 and GSE5296 datasets and identified Atf3 and Piezo1 as key regulators of ferroptosis46. However, distinct from their focus on iron-dependent cell death, our study used a hypothesis-driven approach targeting the broader core mitochondrial bioenergetic crisis. This specific focus allowed us to capture distinct pathological dimensions, including intrinsic apoptosis (Mcl1 and Pmaip1), neurosteroidogenesis (Star and Tspo), and redox homeostasis (Dhrs1). Consequently, our machine learning pipeline identified a completely non-overlapping five-gene signature. Furthermore, a critical innovation of this study is the rigorous validation of these markers at both the transcriptional and protein levels (qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry) across multiple time points, ensuring their translational relevance beyond transcriptomic prediction. Together, these studies highlight that the same datasets can yield complementary molecular insights, one mapping the ferroptotic landscape and this study revealing the core mitochondrial immunometabolic crisis.

Despite the promise of this study, several limitations must be considered. First, regarding practical clinical feasibility, we acknowledge that obtaining spinal cord tissue for transcriptomic profiling is not a viable routine diagnostic procedure in living patients due to its invasive nature. Consequently, the primary immediate utility of our nomogram is as a precision research tool for stratifying injury severity in preclinical models. For translation to clinical practice, future research must validate whether the dysregulation of these five mitochondrial hub genes can be detected in accessible biofluids, such as cerebrospinal fluid or blood, where they would serve as minimally invasive liquid biopsy markers for monitoring mitochondrial health.

Second, although the nomogram demonstrated a high AUC of 0.931, this metric was derived from internal validation (bootstrap resampling). Although we confirmed the expression of these genes in an independent experimental mouse cohort, the model’s performance has not yet been tested on a large-scale, independent transcriptomic dataset or human clinical samples. Accordingly, the reported accuracy should be interpreted as a measure of internal validity, and external validation is required to establish generalizability. Third, to identify a stable and robust mitochondrial signature, we integrated transcriptomic data from multiple time points. While this approach, consistent with other recent SCI bioinformatics studies, successfully identifies core mitochondrial markers that are persistently dysregulated throughout the secondary injury cascade, we explicitly acknowledge that this pooling strategy constitutes a methodological limitation. Specifically, it limits the resolution of strictly time-dependent gene expression changes and obscures the vastly different molecular and cellular processes underlying the acute, subacute, and chronic injury phases. Furthermore, we must acknowledge the lack of strict disease specificity. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a universally recognized, albeit non-specific, phenomenon across various central nervous system disorders, including multiple sclerosis, stroke, and neurodegeneration. Indeed, recent studies employing similar bioinformatic frameworks have identified related mitochondrial-immune signatures in conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease47. Therefore, our identified five-gene signature likely represents a core indicator of pan-neuropathological mitochondrial distress rather than an exclusively SCI-specific diagnostic tool, sharing non-specific characteristics similar to existing neural injury biomarkers like GFAP or NfL. Nevertheless, the biological relevance and translatability of this core signature in the context of SCI were robustly confirmed through our in vivo validation across multiple acute post-injury time points. Fourth, we must emphasize that our current in vivo validations are strictly correlative. The observed upregulation of these genes demonstrates injury-associated transcriptional changes rather than a direct measurement of mitochondrial health or function. As highlighted by the diverse roles of these genes—such as Mcl1, which is fundamentally anti-apoptotic and neuroprotective—their altered expression likely reflects a complex, partially compensatory response to injury rather than a simple metric of severity. Therefore, while our machine learning approach successfully consolidates known SCI pathophysiology into a prioritized descriptive signature, this study does not establish novel mechanistic insights. The clinical and translational implications of this signature remain speculative. Future studies must prioritize mechanistic investigations (e.g., utilizing conditional knockout models or direct mitochondrial respirometry) to delineate the exact functional roles of these individual genes. Additionally, prospective clinical cohorts and direct correlation with behavioral outcomes (e.g., BMS scores) and lesion volume are required to determine whether this descriptive signature can ultimately be translated into a bona fide biomarker of injury severity or prognosis. Fifth, the use of bulk-tissue microarray data presents an inherent limitation regarding tissue heterogeneity. It remains difficult to distinguish whether the observed gene upregulation (for example, Tspo) arises from transcriptional induction within specific resident cells or from the infiltration of high-expressing peripheral immune cells (for example, macrophages) into the lesion site. Future studies using single-cell RNA sequencing are required to resolve these cell-type-specific contributions. Finally, for pathway analysis, we used an FDR threshold of < 0.25 for GSEA. Although this is a standard recommendation for exploratory discovery, we acknowledge that this relatively lenient threshold may increase the risk of including false-positive pathways compared to more stringent criteria.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identifies and validates a robust, descriptive five-gene mitochondrial signature (Mcl1, Dhrs1, Tspo, Star, and Pmaip1) associated with SCI. Beyond its classification accuracy, the signature maps directly onto a key pathogenic axis linking injury-associated transcriptional changes with neuroimmune activation and neuronal suppression. The identified hub genes and the developed nomogram model provide a valuable molecular framework for evaluating the secondary injury cascade. Ultimately, this study lays a prioritized foundation for future mechanistic investigations and prospective translational studies, advancing the exploration of mitochondrial-focused precision medicine in SCI.
