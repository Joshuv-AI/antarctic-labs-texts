Global Bifurcation of Spiral Wave Solutions to the Complex Ginzburg-Landau Equation
Carlos Garcia-Azpeitia, Ziad Ghanem, Wieslaw Krawcewicz

Abstract
. We use the 𝕋 2 {\mathbb{T}}^{2} -equivariant degree to establish the existence of unbounded branches of rotating spiral wave solutions with any number of arms for the complex Ginzburg Landau equation GLe on the planar unit disc, leveraging the spatial symmetries inherent to the problem and avoiding limiting constraints encountered in previous studies [ 5 ] that utilized the classical Leray-Schauder degree. Our results provide rigorous mathematical justification for the formation and persitence of these fundamental patterns, which are ubiquitous in physical, chemical, and biological systems but have, until now, eluded formal proof under general conditions.

Introduction

Originally introduced in the context of condensed-matter physics by the Russian physicists Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau in the 1950s, the Ginzburg-Landau equation (GLe) serves as a unifying

For a comprehensive review of the complex GLe

In one of the many theoretical frameworks synthesized by Aranson et al. in this review, the stability of two-dimensional spiral wave solutions to a GLe defined on a bounded domain is inferred from the stability of the one-dimensional planar waves they emit in an unbounded one-dimensional medium. Complementing this theoretical perspective, Guzmán-Velázquez et al. use finite element simulations in
[
9

the validity of these analytical predictions for isolated spiral solutions on a bounded circular domain, providing crucial evidence that stability in this setting differs qualitatively from the stability analytically suggested by the associated planar waves. For instance, they show that the
Eckhaus criterion, a cornerstone for predicting one-dimensional wave persistence, is not a valid predictor for the stability of two-dimensional spiral waves in a finite domain. Taken together, these works reveal a apparent mismatch between the theory for unbounded systems and the observed dynamics in finite domains, highlighting the need for the rigorous analytical results obtained in this paper.

The existence of branches of spiral wave solutions with fixed temporal frequency
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, positive orientation and any finite number of arms emerging from the trivial solution to a class of one-parameter family of rGLes of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}(1+i\eta)\Delta u=u-|u|^{2}u-ib|u|^{2}u-i\beta u,\quad b\in\mathbb{R},

at an infinite sequence of positive critical bifurcation parameter values

was proved by Dai in
[
5

using a combination of perturbation and shooting arguments. Moreover, in the sequel article
[
6
]
, Dai et al. are able to
(\rm i)
describe global bifurcation diagrams,
(\rm ii)
prove the persistence of branches under parameter perturbation and
(\rm iii)
explicitly construct the global attractor for the branch of spiral wave solutions emerging from any critical point
(\lambda_{k},0)
by assuming sufficiently small values of
\eta,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

The structural constraints on the nonlinearity and the requirement that
|b|,|\eta|\ll 1
arise from Dai et al.’s reliance on Rabinowitz-type arguments—based on the classical Leray-Schauder degree—which underpin all of global bifurcation theory. Essentially, the difficulty lies in the fact that the eigenspaces of the linearized rGLe operator relevant to spiral wave solutions are inherently complex. Standard topological degree arguments depend on detecting a change in the sign of an index (e.g. the Leray-Schauder degree) as bifurcation parameters cross critical points, typically guaranteed when eigenvalues cross zero with odd multiplicity. This guarantee invariably fails for eigenvalues with even-dimensional eigenspaces, complicating the global tracking of solution branches.
To circumvent this problem, Dai et al. consider a continuation from the real stationary Ginzburg-Landau equation (corresponding to the case
b=\eta=0
) where the classical Leray-Schauder degree is a more appropriate tool.

In contrast, this paper utilizes the equivariant degree, a topological tool specifically designed for symmetric bifurcation problems.
Our approach is particularly well-suited for studying (
2

(\rm i)
the problem possesses inherent
\mathbb{T}^{2}
symmetry;
(\rm ii)
the irreducible representations of the abelian group
\mathbb{T}^{2}
are one-dimensional complex spaces, simplifying aspects of the degree computation; and
(\rm iii)
the values of the
\mathbb{T}^{2}
-equivariant degree are classified by the isotropy subgroups of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with one-dimensional Weyl groups, which correspond directly to the symmetries of the spiral and target wave solutions sought. In this way, the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-equivariant degree is able to detect and track bifurcating branches of solutions with specific symmetries, even when the Leray-Schauder degree provides insufficient information. Our primary goal is to leverage this tool to establish the existence of unbounded branches of these spiral-like solutions under the general conditions

\operatorname{coeff}^{H}(\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{0}))\neq 0,

then there exists a branch
\mathcal{C}
of non-trivial solutions to (
10
) bifurcating from
(\lambda_{0},0)
with symmetries at least
(H)
.

3.2.
The
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-Isotypic Decomposition of
\mathscr{H}

In order to effectively make use of Theorem
3.1
to determine the existence of a branch of non-trivial solutions to (
10
) bifurcating from the zero solution, we must derive a more practical formula for the computation of the local bifurcation invariant (
20
). Our first step in this direction is to describe
the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-isotypic decomposition of
\mathscr{H}
, i.e. a decomposition of our functional space into the direct sum of irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representations.

As demonstrated in Appendix (
A
), every irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representation can be described in terms of the irreducible
S^{1}
-representations. In particular, if for each

, we denote by

the irreducible
S^{1}
-representation equipped with the

-folded
S^{1}
-action

(21)

\displaystyle e^{i\vartheta}w:=e^{im\vartheta}\cdot w,\quad e^{i\vartheta}\in S^{1},\;w\in\mathcal{U}_{m},

where ‘
\cdot
’ indicates the standard complex multiplication and by
\mathcal{U}_{0}\simeq\mathbb{R}
the irreducible
S^{1}
-representation on which
S^{1}
acts trivially, then
the list of irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representations consists of the trivial
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{0}}\simeq\mathbb{R}
and, for each
\bm{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}^{2}:=\{(k_{1},k_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\text{ if }k_{1}=0\text{ then }k_{2}\geq 0\}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, the irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}
given by

\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}:=\mathcal{U}_{k_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{k_{1}},\quad\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2}),

-action

(e^{i\vartheta},e^{i\varphi})u:=e^{ik_{1}\vartheta}e^{ik_{2}\varphi}\cdot u,\quad u\in\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}.

Remark 3.2
.

Although the irreducible
S^{1}
-representations
\mathcal{U}_{m}
and
\mathcal{U}_{-m}
are equivalent for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, notice that two irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representations
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}
with
\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}^{2}
are equivalent if and only if
\bm{k}=\bm{k}^{\prime}
.
For more details, we refer the reader to Appendix
A
.

Notice that each of the eigenspaces
\mathcal{E}_{m,n}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
is naturally equipped with the diagonal
S^{1}
-action

(22)

\displaystyle e^{i\vartheta}u:=(e^{im\vartheta}\cdot u_{1},e^{-im\vartheta}\cdot u_{2}),\quad u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{m,n},

and that
S^{1}
acts trivially on each
\mathcal{E}_{0,n}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}
such that one has the equivalences

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{m,n}\simeq\begin{cases}{\mathbb{C}}&\text{ if }m=0;\\
(\mathcal{U}_{m}\oplus\mathcal{U}_{-m})\otimes{\mathbb{C}}&\text{ if }m\neq 0.\end{cases}

It follows that
\mathscr{H}
admits the
S^{1}
-isotypic decomposition

(23)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}:=\overline{\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{m}},\quad\mathcal{H}_{m}:=\overline{\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{m,n}},

where the closure is taken in
\mathscr{H}
. In turn,
as an orthogonal
S^{1}
-representation equipped with the natural
1
-folded
S^{1}
-action (see the action of the second copy of
S^{1}
in
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-action (
11
)), the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
is equivalent to the first irreducible
S^{1}
-representation, i.e.

{\mathbb{C}}\simeq\mathcal{U}_{1}.

Let’s define the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-invariant subspaces

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{m,n}:=\{J_{|m|}(\sqrt{s_{|m|,n}}r)e^{im\theta}a:a\in{\mathbb{C}}\},\quad(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}},

and equip each
\mathscr{E}_{m,n}\subset\mathscr{H}
with the corresponding
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-action

\displaystyle(e^{i\vartheta},e^{i\varphi})u(r,\theta)=e^{i\varphi}u(r,\theta+m\vartheta),\quad u\in\mathscr{E}_{m,n},

such that one has
\mathscr{E}_{m,n}\simeq\mathcal{U}_{m}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{1}
for all
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
.
Consequently, the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-isotypic decomposition of
\mathscr{H}
can now be described in terms of the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-isotypic components
\mathscr{H}_{m}:=\overline{\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathscr{E}_{m,n}}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}=\overline{\bigoplus_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathscr{H}_{m}}.

To be clear, each
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-isotypic component
\mathscr{H}_{m}
is modeled on the corresponding irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{m,1}:=\mathcal{U}_{m}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{1}
. For easy identification of the relevant symmetry subgroups (cf. Appendix
A
), we adopt the notation

H_{m}:=\{(e^{im\varphi},e^{-i\varphi})\in S^{1}\times S^{1}:\varphi\in[0,2\pi]\}\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{2},\quad m\in{\mathbb{Z}},

such that the full isotropy lattice becomes

\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};\mathscr{H})=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};\mathscr{H}_{m}),\quad\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};\mathscr{H}_{m}):=\{(H_{m})\}.

Remark 3.3
.

Notice that each
(H_{m})\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};\mathscr{H})
is maximal in the sense that if
(H)\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};\mathscr{H})
is such that
(H)\geq(H_{m})
, then it must be the case that
H=H_{m}
. Therefore, any branch
\mathcal{C}
of non-trivial solutions with symmetries at least
(H_{m})\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};\mathscr{H})
consists only of solutions
u\in\mathscr{H}
satisfying
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}_{u}=H_{m}
. Notice also that the isotropy subgroup
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}_{u}\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
associated with a non-trivial function
u\in\mathscr{H}\setminus\{0\}
satisfies the relation
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}_{u}\geq H_{m}
if and only if for all
\varphi\in[0,2\pi]
, one has

\displaystyle e^{im\varphi}u(r,\theta-\varphi)=u(r,\theta).

In particular, setting
\varphi=\theta
, we find that
u
must satisfy the relation

u(r,\theta)=e^{im\theta}u(r,0).

In other words, each element of the fixed point space
\mathscr{S}^{H_{m}}
is a spiral wave with
|m|
arms admitting orientation corresponding to the value of
\text{\rm sign\,}m
if
m\neq 0
and a target wave, otherwise.

With the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-isotypic decomposition of
\mathscr{H}
at hand, we can begin to collect the spectral data related to the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-equivariant linear operator
\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta):\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
. For example, we are guaranteed, by Schur’s Lemma, that
\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta)
respects the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-isotypic decomposition (
23
) in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta)(\mathscr{E}_{m,n})\subset\mathscr{E}_{m,n},\quad(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}.

Therefore,
\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta)
admits the following block-matrix decomposition

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta)=\bigoplus_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta),\quad\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta):=\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta)|_{\mathscr{E}_{m,n}}:\mathscr{E}_{m,n}\rightarrow\mathscr{E}_{m,n},

such that the spectrum of
\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta)
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta))=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma(\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)).

In particular, we find by direct computation that the spectrum associated with each matrix

\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)
is comprised of the complex eigenvalue

(24)

\displaystyle\mu_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta):=\frac{(1+i\eta)s_{|m|,n}-\alpha-i\beta+i\omega m}{(1+i\eta)s_{|m|,n}}.

Remark 3.4
.

Recall that a trivial solution
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},0)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{H}
belongs to the critical set of (
10
) if and only if
0\in\sigma(\mathscr{A}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}))
. In other words,
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},0)\in\Lambda
if and only if
there exist
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}

for which

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\alpha_{0}=s_{|m|,n};\\
\beta_{0}=\omega m+\eta s_{|m|,n}.\end{cases}

Notice that each eigenvalue
\mu_{m,n}:{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
admits exactly one root since the zeros of
J^{\prime}_{|m|}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
form a strictly increasing sequence
s_{|m|,1}<s_{|m|,2}<\cdots<s_{|m|,n}<\cdots
. On the other hand, two eigenvalues
\mu_{m,n}
and
\mu_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
share the same root if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{cases}s_{|m|,n}=s_{|m^{\prime}|,n^{\prime}};\\
\omega m+\eta s_{|m|,n}=\omega m^{\prime}+\eta s_{|m^{\prime}|,n^{\prime}},\end{cases}

which holds if and only if
m=m^{\prime}
and
s_{|m|,n}=s_{|m|,n^{\prime}}
. Again, by strict monotonicity of the sequence
\{s_{|m|,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, notice that the latter condition implies
n=n^{\prime}
. Therefore, each critical point can be uniquely associated with an index pair
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
using the notation

(25)

\displaystyle\lambda_{m,n}:=\alpha_{m,n}+i\beta_{m,n},\quad(\alpha_{m,n},\beta_{m,n}):=\left(s_{|m|,n},\;\omega m+\eta s_{|m|,n}\right),

such that the critical set, in addition to being discrete, admits the following explicit description

\Lambda=\{(\alpha_{m,n},\beta_{m,n},0):m\in{\mathbb{Z}},\;n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

3.3.
Computation of the Local Bifurcation Invariant

As before, let
(\lambda_{0},0)\in\Lambda
be a critical point for (
10
) with a deleted
\varepsilon
-neighborhood (
14
) on which
\mathscr{A}(\lambda):\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
is an isomorphism and suppose that a number
\delta>0
is chosen such that

\mathscr{F}(\lambda,v)\neq 0,\text{ for all }(\lambda,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{H}\text{ with }|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|=\epsilon\text{ and }0<\|v\|_{\mathscr{H}}\leq\delta.

Then, for any auxiliary function
\Theta:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying conditions (
17
) on the isolating cylinder (
16
) (in particular, for the auxiliary function (
18
)), the complemented operator (
19
) is
\mathscr{O}
-admissibly
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-homotopic to the linear operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{F}_{\Theta}}(\lambda,u):=(\Theta(\lambda,u),\mathscr{A}(\lambda)u)=\left(\Theta(\lambda,u),\bigoplus_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\lambda)\right).

Adopting the notations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widetilde{\Theta}_{m,n}(\lambda,u):=\theta|_{{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathscr{E}_{m,n}}(\lambda,u);\\
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{m,n}(\lambda)u:=\left(\widetilde{\Theta}_{m,n}(\lambda,u),\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\lambda)\right);\\
\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n}:=\mathscr{O}\cap\left({\mathbb{C}}\times\mathscr{E}_{m,n}\right),\end{cases}

and combining the homotopy property of the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-equivariant degree (see the third degree axiom in Appendix
A
) with the Splitting Lemma (see Lemma (
A.2
), Appendix
A.1
), the local bifurcation invariant (
20
) at the isolated critical point
(\lambda_{0},0)
becomes

(26)

\displaystyle\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{2}\text{\rm-deg}\left(\bigoplus_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{m,n},\bigoplus_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\text{\rm-deg}(\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{m,n},\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n}).

Since
\id-\mathscr{A}(\alpha,\beta):\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
is compact for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, the eigenvalues
\mu_{m,n}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
are nonzero for almost all index pairs
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
such that, with only finitely many exceptions, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}\text{\rm-deg}(\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{m,n},\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n})=0.

Indeed, by Lemma
A.1
, the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-equivariant degree of each complemented operator
(\theta_{m,n},\mathscr{A}_{m,n})
on its corresponding isolating neighborhood

\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n}
is fully specified by the spectrum of
\mathscr{A}_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)
and the irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{m,1}
(resp.
\mathcal{V}_{0}
, in the case that
m=0
) according to the rule:

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}\text{\rm-deg}(\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{m,n},\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n})=\begin{cases}\rho_{m,n}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})(H_{m})\quad&\text{ if }\mu_{m,n}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})=0;\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\rho_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta):=\deg(\det\nolimits_{\mathbb{C}}\mathscr{A}_{m,n},B_{\varepsilon}(\lambda))
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Using the notation (
25
), the local bifurcation invariant at any critical point
(\lambda_{m,n},0)=(\alpha_{m,n},\beta_{m,n})\in\Lambda
is given by the rule

(27)

\displaystyle\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{m,n})=(H_{m}).

Proof.

Since each critical point is isotopically simple, i.e. since one has
\mu_{m,n}^{-1}(0)=\{(\alpha_{m,n},\beta_{m,n})\}
for all
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
(cf. Remark
3.4
), the computational formula (
26
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{m,n})=\mathbb{T}^{2}\text{\rm-deg}(\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}_{m,n},\widetilde{\mathscr{O}}_{m,n}).

At this point the result follows from the observation that, for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, the Jacobian matrix

(28)

\displaystyle D\mu_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{(1+i\eta)s_{|m|,n}}\begin{pmatrix}[r]-1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

is non-singular such that the local Brouwer degrees
\rho_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)
are always well-defined and can be computed as follows

(29)

\displaystyle\rho_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\deg(\det\nolimits_{\mathbb{C}}\mathscr{A}_{m,n},B_{\varepsilon}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\deg(\mu_{m,n},B_{\varepsilon}(\lambda))=\operatorname{sign}\left[\det D\mu_{m,n}(\alpha,\beta)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sign}\left[\det\begin{pmatrix}[r]-1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right]=1.

\square

We are now in a position to formulate our main local equivariant bifurcation result.

Theorem 3.2
.

For each fixed
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the trivial solution to (
2
) at each of the critical parameter values
(\alpha_{m,n},\beta_{m,n})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is a branching point for a branch of non-trivial solutions, consisting
|m|
-armed spiral waves with orientation determined by the value of
\text{\rm sign\,}m
if
m\neq 0
and target waves, otherwise.

Proof.

A direct consequence of the combination of Theorem
3.1
, Remark
3.3
, Lemma
3.1
and the existence property of the
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-equivariant degree (cf. Appendix
A
).

\square

3.4.
Resolution of The Rabinowitz Alternative

We can study the global behaviour of the branches of spiral wave solutions whose existence has been predicted by Theorem
3.2
using the following Rabinowitz-type argument, the proof of which can be found in
[
3
,
4
]
:

Theorem 3.3
.

(Rabinowitz’ Alternative)

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{H}
be any open bounded
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-invariant set with
\partial\mathcal{U}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
. If
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathscr{S}
is a branch of non-trivial solutions to (
10
) bifurcating from a critical point
(\lambda_{0},0)\in\mathcal{U}\cap\Lambda
, then one has the following alternative:

(a)

either
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathcal{U}\neq\emptyset
;

(b)

or there exists a finite set

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap\Lambda=\{(\lambda_{0},0),(\lambda_{1},0),\ldots,(\lambda_{k},0)\},

satisfying the following relation

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{i})=0.

Remark 3.5
.

If a branch of non-trivial solutions
\mathcal{C}\subset\overline{\mathscr{S}}
satisfies
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathcal{U}\neq\emptyset
for every open bounded
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
-invariant set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathscr{H}
with
\partial\mathcal{U}\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{C}
must be
unbounded
. Therefore, a sufficient condition for the unboundedness of a branch
\mathcal{C}\subset\overline{\mathscr{S}}
is the following:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{(\lambda_{i},0)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap\Lambda}\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{i})\neq 0.

We now have all the necessary components to prove our main global equivariant bifurcation result:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Notice that the coefficient standing next to
(H_{m})
in the local bifurcation invariant at any particular
(\lambda_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}},0)\in\Lambda
is determined by the rule

\displaystyle\operatorname{coeff}^{H_{m}}\left(\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}})\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }m^{\prime}=m;\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

We notice again (cf. Theorem
3.2
) that emerging from the trivial solution at each critical point is a branch of non-trivial solutions
\mathcal{C}
with corresponding spiral or target pattern symmetries. To show that these branches are unbounded, suppose for contradiction that a branch
\mathcal{C}
emerging from the critical point
(\lambda_{m,n},0)\in\Lambda
is bounded. Then
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap\Lambda
is a finite set of critical points, including
(\lambda_{m,n},0)
. By the Rabinowitz alternative, this would require

\displaystyle\operatorname{coeff}^{H_{m}}\left(\sum_{(\lambda_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}},0)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap\Lambda}\omega_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}(\lambda_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}})\right)=|(\lambda_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}},0)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap\Lambda:m^{\prime}=m\}|=0,

which is in contradiction with the assumption that
(\lambda_{m,n},0)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}}\cap\Lambda
.

\square

Appendix A
The
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-Equivariant Degree

Equivariant notation:

In what follows, we indicate by
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
the
n
-dimensional torus and, for any subgroup

, we denote by
(H)
its conjugacy class in
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
.
Notice that the set of all subgroup conjugacy classes
\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n}):=\{(H):H\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\}
admits the natural partial ordering:

(H)\leq(K)\iff\;\;H\leq K.

As is possible with any partially ordered set, we extend the natural order over
\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
to a total order, which we indicate by
\preccurlyeq
to differentiate the two relations. We also put
\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n}):=\{(H)\in\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n}):\dim{\mathbb{T}}^{n}/H=1\}
and
A_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n}):=\mathbb{Z}[\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})]
, noticing that every element of the of the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module

can be expressed as a formal sum over some finite number of generator elements

a=n_{1}(H_{1})+n_{2}(H_{2})+\cdots+n_{m}(H_{m}),\quad a\in A_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n}).

In particular, we can specify the integer coefficient standing next to the generator element
(H)\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
in any element
a\in A_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
using the notation

(30)

\displaystyle\operatorname{coeff}^{H}(a)=n_{H}.

Given a
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-space
X
with an element
x\in X
, we denote by

{\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x}:=\{g\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}:gx=x\}
the

of
x

and we call
({\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x})\in\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
the
orbit type
of
x\in X
. Moreover, we put
\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n},X):=\{(H)\in\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n}):(H)=({\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x})\;\text{for some $x\in X$}\}
and also
\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n},X):=\Phi({\mathbb{T}}^{n},X)\cap\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
. For a subgroup
H\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
, the subspace
X^{H}:=\{x\in X:{\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x}\geq H\}
is called the
H
-fixed-point subspace
of
X
. If
Y
is another
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-space, then a continuous map

is said to be
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-equivariant
if

for each

and

.

Classification of the Real Irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-Representations:
Since every complex irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional, each irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation
\mathcal{V}
can be identified with a continuous homomorphism
T:{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\rightarrow S^{1}
. Notice also that the set of homomorphisms from the torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
to the circle group
S^{1}
is related to the group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
via the isomorphism

(31)

\displaystyle(z,\bm{k})\rightarrow z_{1}^{k_{1}}z_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots z_{n}^{k_{n}},\quad z=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\;\bm{k}:=(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

In particular, we can identify every the element in
\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{T}}^{n},S^{1})
with a corresponding vector
\bm{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
using the notation
T_{\bm{k}}:{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\rightarrow S^{1}
to indicate the map (
31
).

While any two complex irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representations

and
T_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}
are equivalent if and only if
\bm{k}=\bm{k}^{\prime}
, the irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representations

and
T_{-\bm{k}}
are always equivalent as real representations. It follows that the set of all non-trivial, real irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representations is in one-to-one correspondence with the set

{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}^{n}:=\{(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:\text{ if }k_{1}=k_{2}=\ldots=k_{i}=0\text{ then }k_{i+1}\geq 0\}.

Having removed possible duplicate indices, we can identify the list of all irreducible real
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representations, using the notation
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{0}}\simeq\mathbb{R}
to indicate the trivial
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation and
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
to indicate the irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation corresponding to the homomorphism
T_{\bm{k}}
with
\bm{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}^{n}
(see (
31
)), i.e. the irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representations

\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}\simeq\mathcal{U}_{k_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{k_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{U}_{k_{n}},\quad\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}^{n},

the irreducible

-representation equipped with the
m

S^{1}
-action
e^{i\vartheta}w:=e^{im\vartheta}\cdot w

\mathcal{U}_{-m}\simeq\overline{\mathcal{U}_{m}}
its conjugated counterpart with the

\mathcal{U}_{0}\simeq\mathbb{R}

Classification of the set
\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
:

Given a real non-trivial irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
, consider the normal subgroup
\Gamma_{\bm{k}}\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
given by
\Gamma_{\bm{k}}:=\text{\rm Ker\,}{T_{\bm{k}}}
, i.e. the subgroup

\Gamma_{\bm{k}}=\{(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}:z_{1}^{k_{1}}z_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots z_{n}^{k_{n}}=1\}.

Since
T_{\bm{k}}:{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\rightarrow S^{1}
is surjective, one always has
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}/\Gamma_{\bm{k}}\simeq S^{1}
such that
(\Gamma_{\bm{k}})\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
. Moreover, for any non-zero element
x_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x_{0}}=\{(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}:z_{1}^{k_{1}}z_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots z_{n}^{k_{n}}x_{0}=x_{0}\},

that the associated isotropy subgroup
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x_{0}}\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
coincides with the group
\Gamma_{\bm{k}}
. Therefore,
\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n},\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}})
is always the singleton set
\{(\Gamma_{\bm{k}})\}
and the isotropy lattice
\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n},V)
in the more general case that

V
is any orthogonal
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation with the isotypic decomposition

V=\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}_{m}},

is such that

\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n},V)\supseteq\{(\Gamma_{\bm{k}_{1}}),(\Gamma_{\bm{k}_{2}}),\ldots,(\Gamma_{\bm{k}_{m}})\}.

The
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-Equivariant Degree.

Let
V
be an orthogonal
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation and consider the induced representation
\mathbb{R}\times V
where
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
acts trivially on
\mathbb{R}
. An open bounded
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-invariant set

{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-equivariant map

constitute an
admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-pair in
\mathbb{R}\times V
if
f(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\partial\Omega
, in which case the map

is said to be
\Omega
-admissible
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}(V)
the set of all admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-pairs in
\mathbb{R}\times V
and by
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}
the set of all admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-pairs defined by taking a union over all orthogonal
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representations, i.e.

\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}:=\bigcup\limits_{V}\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}(V).

The
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-equivariant degree provides an algebraic count of solution orbits, according to their symmetric properties, to equations of the form

f(x)=0,\;x\in\Omega,

where
(f,\Omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}
. In fact, using arguments analogous to those used in the definition of the
S^{1}
-equivariant degree (cf.
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
) we define the
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-equivariant degree
as the unique map associating to every admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-pair
(f,\Omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}
an element from the free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module
A_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
, satisfying the four
degree axioms
of existence, additivity, homotopy and normalization:

Theorem A.1
.

There exists a unique map
\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}\to A_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
, that assigns to every admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-pair
(f,\Omega)
the module element

(32)

\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega)=\sum_{(H)\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})}{n_{H}(H)},

satisfying the following properties:

(Existence)
If
n_{H}\neq 0
for some
(H)\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n})
in (
32
), then there
exists
x\in\Omega
such that
f(x)=0
and
({\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{x})\geq(H)
.

(Additivity)

For any two disjoint open
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-invariant subsets

\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
with

f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega\subset\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega)=\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega_{1})+\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega_{2}).

(Homotopy)
For any

\Omega
-admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-homotopy,
h:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\times V\to V
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}(h_{t},\Omega)=\mathrm{constant}.

(Normalization)
Let
(f,\Omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}
be such that
f
is regular normal in
\Omega
(meaning
(\rm i)

f
is smooth,
(\rm ii)

f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega
consists of orbits
({\mathbb{T}}^{n}(w_{i}))
such that
f
is transversal to
\{0\}
along each orbit, and
(\rm iii)
for every
H\leq{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
such that
(H)=({\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{w_{i}})
for some
i
,
0
is a regular value of
f^{H}|_{\Omega^{H}}
- cf.
[
3
]
for a formal definition of regular normality). Assume further that
f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega={\mathbb{T}}^{n}(w_{0})
consists of a single orbit for some
w_{0}\in\Omega
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega)=\begin{cases}\rho_{0}({\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{w_{0}})&\text{if }({\mathbb{T}}^{n}_{w_{0}})\in\Phi_{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{n});\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where

(33)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}:=\operatorname{sign}\det(Df(w_{0})|_{S_{w_{0}}}),

and
S_{w_{0}}
is the positively oriented slice—that is, the orthogonal subspace in
\mathbb{R}\times V

{\mathbb{T}}^{n}(w_{0})
at

A.1.
Two Computational Formulae for two-parameter
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-Equivariant Degree Calculations

Given a non-trivial irreducible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}
, we can always put
V_{\bm{k}}:=\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
and identify
\mathbb{R}\times V_{\bm{k}}
with the space
{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
. Let
\mu_{\bm{k}}:S^{1}\rightarrow GL^{G}(\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}})
be a continuous family of
G
-equivariant invertible linear operators and define the family of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-equivariant complemented linear operators

\mathcal{A}_{\bm{k}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}},\quad\mathcal{A}_{\bm{k}}(\lambda)v:=\left(1-|\lambda|,\mu_{\bm{k}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|}\right)\cdot v\right).

Since the action of
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
on
\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
is isometric, the open bounded set

\mathcal{D}_{\bm{k}}:=\{(\lambda,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}:\|v\|<1,\;\frac{1}{2}<|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|<2\},

is
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-invariant. Moreover, since the system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu_{\bm{k}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|}\right)\cdot v=0\\
1-|\lambda|=0,\end{cases}

admits no solutions on
\partial\mathcal{D}_{\bm{k}}
, the pair
(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{k}},\mathcal{D}_{\bm{k}})
constitutes an admissible
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-pair in
\mathbb{R}\times V_{\bm{k}}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}}
. In this way, we are able to employ the
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
-equivariant degree for solving two-parameter bifurcation problems.

The proofs of the following pair analytical formulae are based on identical arguments used to prove analagous results for the
S^{1}
degree in
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
, and for this reason they are omitted.

Lemma A.1
.

For any admissible pair
(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{k}},\mathcal{D}_{\bm{k}})\in\mathcal{M}^{{\mathbb{T}}^{n}}_{1}(\mathcal{V}_{\bm{k}})
constructed in the above manner, one has

.

(The Splitting Lemma):

For any two admissible
