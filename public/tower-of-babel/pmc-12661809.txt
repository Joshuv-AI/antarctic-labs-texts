Working toward a stepped-care model of postnatal mental healthcare to improve access: the Theory of Change approach
Authors: Heather Rowe, Jane Fisher, Elizabeth Meggetto, Helen Lees, Shelly Makleff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen who are pregnant or have recently given birth constitute a distinct life-stage-specific population with elevated risks for mental health problems and well understood risk and protective factors. Perinatal mental health remains a worldwide public health problem that requires local health service reform to improve access and meet need. The aim of this paper was to create a framework for a proposed model of postnatal mental healthcare and understand its application in existing health services in a rural area in Australia.MethodsThe Theory of Change (ToC) method was used in a three-step procedure: 1) document reviews 2) stakeholder consultations and 3) ToC workshops. A proposed model of postnatal mental healthcare was developed and its application in a local setting was investigated. The model was documented using a ToC framework.ResultsDrawing on evidence from policy documents, clinical practice guidelines, existing health systems, and practice expertise, a ToC for a proposed ‘stepped model of postnatal mental healthcare’ was developed. Stepped care is a complex intervention intended to deliver tailored care pathways according to severity of individual need. It links four interrelated components: primary prevention, secondary prevention, early intervention and treatment. In the ToC, evidence-based Rationales support each Pathway, and Assumptions describe external conditions that must be satisfied for successful implementation of each Pathway and the overall model.ConclusionsThe proposed stepped-care model is a valuable investigation of an integrated model of postnatal mental health service reform in a local service environment. The Assumptions generate testable hypotheses for future research to understand the conditions for successful implementation in this or other settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13629-1.Keywords: Stepped-care model, Perinatal mental health, Postnatal mental health, Health service reform, Complex intervention, Development and evaluation, Theory of Change

Background

Perinatal mental health burden

Compromised maternal mental health is the most common complication of childbearing and confers personal, social, economic and health system burden [1]. The birth of a baby is a momentous life event accompanied by large adjustments to new roles and responsibilities and a degree of emotional turbulence. Exposure to gender-based social, economic and personal adversity influences mental health across the life course, but the perinatal period confers life-stage specific risks for elevated vulnerability to the common mental disorders as well as the rarer but serious conditions [2]. We use the term ‘women’ rather than ‘parent’ throughout, because this is the language used in documentary sources, including the available research.

Postpartum depressive and anxiety symptoms are frequently pre-existing, but may not have been detected or treated before or during pregnancy [2]. A personal history of mental health problems, but also potentially modifiable factors including traumatic birth and exposure to interpersonal violence and coercive control, including from an intimate partner, increase a woman’s risk of perinatal mental health problems [1]. Disabling symptoms can include reduced social and economic participation and compromised infant care-giving associated with potentially lasting impairments to infant and child development that is compounded by social disadvantage [3].

Population register data indicates that first-time and recurrent episodes treated at psychiatric inpatient facilities appear to be rare during pregnancy and most common within the first postpartum month (occurring in 0.25 and 0.40 per 1000 births respectively). This is unlike the more common but less severe first episodes treated in outpatient clinics, which show little change in prevalence during pregnancy and the postpartum periods [4]. Estimates of so-called ‘common mental disorders’ vary, but recent reviews report 17% (95% CI: 12, 21) prevalence of postnatal depressive disorder assessed from clinical interviews among healthy women without a history of depression [5], and 9.9% (95%CI: 6.1, 13.1) for any postnatal anxiety disorder at 1–24 weeks postpartum [6].

A persistent public health problem

Perinatal mental health remains a persistent public health problem worldwide, and may even be increasing. The problem demands reforms to both health policy and service delivery [2]. However, there is little recent evidence about how to improve access to high quality mental health services for women who have recently given birth or on the effectiveness of different service delivery models [2]. The mental health of new parents is especially important in the perinatal period because of the crucial role of the family in the intergenerational transfer of mental health and mental health problems. Access to affordable and acceptable mental health and social services to support population mental health is vital, particularly at this phase of life when greater need coincides with opportunities created by parents’ close contact with health services [2].

Perinatal mental health services

In Australia, despite considerable investment over more than a decade in perinatal psychosocial risk and depression symptom screening, prevalence of mental health problems has not declined [7]. This suggests that personal and social risks have increased, and/or that prevention, early intervention and treatment services are unavailable, unacceptable, or insufficient to meet need. In the Australian state of Victoria, mental health services are described as fragmented and difficult to navigate, and there is generally limited understanding of specific services for families with babies and young children [8]. This is exacerbated by low uptake of care in the perinatal period, even where it is available, reflecting poor community understanding of normal adjustment to childbirth and parenting, widespread stigma, and potential fear of mother and child separation [7]. Together with social adversity and mistreatment of women, these factors help to explain the persistent personal, social, economic and health system burdens of perinatal mental health problems despite increased investment in recent years [2]. There is growing international research interest in development and evaluation of service delivery models to address urgently needed improvements in perinatal mental health [2]. In a recent systematic review of interventions to integrate perinatal mental health into routine services, 20 papers met inclusion criteria, most of which evaluated single psychosocial interventions, but none addressed sector-wide reform intended to integrate existing services in pathways to care [9].

Rationale

Australian health policies commonly emphasise treatment services for moderate to severe mental illness. However, international evidence suggests that perinatal depression, anxiety and adjustment disorders (the ‘common mental disorders’) constitute a larger component of treatment need: for example, compared to inpatient admissions, two to three times as many outpatient, and five times as many primary care treatments, are reported [2, 4]. Reform of the perinatal mental health sector would therefore involve primary care, and demands an adequately-resourced and well-coordinated health system with integrated services in clear pathways through available interventions [7, 10]. Such health system reform is described by the UK Medical Research Council as a ‘complex intervention’ because it involves multiple components, change processes, and levels at which change is required, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of current policy as well as available service delivery in its local context [11].

Aim

The aim was to investigate the potential for an innovative, collaborative, person-centred model of postnatal mental healthcare to improve access and outcomes in a local Australian setting.

Objectives

The objectives of the study were to:

• use existing mental health policies, postnatal and mental health clinical practice guidelines, and health service and published academic evidence, to develop the proposed model in a specific health system and map pathways to care.

• employ the theory-driven approach for program design and evaluation in healthcare known as Theory of Change in order to reveal how change could occur [12].

• identify evidence-based rationales and elucidate the underlying assumptions of the model to detect evidence gaps about necessary external factors required for successful implementation.

Methods

Study context

In Australia, health system design and implementation are state responsibilities and therefore locally specific. The study was undertaken in the State of Victoria, the second most populous of the eight Australian states and territories. The components of the Victorian health system relevant to perinatal mental health care are Maternal and Child Health nursing services, primary medical care (General Practice), early parenting centres, and secondary and specialist mental health services.

Study design

We developed an empirically supported model for perinatal mental health care in an Australian setting using a Theory of Change (ToC) approach. ToC is a theory-driven approach for program design and evaluation used to identify hypothesised pathways to program impact and improve understanding of how change takes place [13, 14].

Procedures

ToC development is collaborative, and draws on community, academic, practitioner and policy perspectives to improve understandings of complex health programs and health systems [15]. The process in this study brought together decades of perinatal health service research (HR, JF, EM, HL) with theoretical expertise and practical ToC experience in sexual and reproductive health service design and evaluation (SM). The final ToC is a visual representation of the components and proposed mechanisms of change of an intervention, as well as potential barriers to achieving successful outcomes [14]. This visual representation makes explicit the Pathways through which each facet of the model of perinatal mental health care might operate as part of a broader health system. Results

Document review

The main findings from the evidence review are summarised below, bringing together academic, policy and practice evidence used to inform the identification of the Pathways, Rationales, and Assumptions underpinning the Theory of Change.

Mental health policy and health systems

The policy and practice evidence in Table 3 confirms the pressing need for reform of mental health services, and outlies specific areas in which reform has begun or is required. The perinatal period is identified as conferring elevated vulnerability, and women with young babies and their families as having specific needs. The evidence for the need to reorient health services to include prevention and early intervention as well as treatment is presented (Table 3). Effective perinatal mental health interventions exist but are underutilised [7]. They are optimally delivered in a collaborative dynamic system in which services are matched to individual level of need and include interventions for universal prevention and early identification, plus higher intensity interventions to treat those with greater need [1]; an effective model involves access to primary care prevention programs, as well as to specialist services when required [23].

Stepped care

A ‘stepped model of mental healthcare’ [24] is a comprehensive, patient-centred, mental healthcare system intended to empower individuals with personalized care pathways. The stepped-care model of health described in Table 4 has four components: primary prevention, secondary prevention, early intervention, and treatment, each underpinned by a considerable body of evidence. The evidence suggests that the pre-conditions for a stepped-care model of mental healthcare are present in existing services in Victoria (Table 4). The stepped-care model represents a ‘complex intervention’ [11].

Stepped care models: document review findings

The ‘Stepped Care Model’ is an integrated system with interventions in a hierarchy from the least to the most intensive, including universal strategies for prevention, measures for early identification and referral of those at elevated risk or already symptomatic, and treatment for those with established mental health problems [25]. In stepped care, services should be person-centred, matched to individual level of need, and available at the right place and time. The model should be evidence-based, flexible, efficient, timely and coordinated [10]. The approach should avoid under- and over-servicing, be responsive to the needs and preferences of consumers and carers rather than provider-driven, and the available services, linkages, referral mechanisms, timelines and costs should be clearly understood by consumers [26]. A recent scoping review to identify models and key elements of ‘integrated’ perinatal mental health care included 45 articles describing a range of models including collaborative and stepped-care frameworks. Key elements included screening, assessment, and triage; integrated care delivery; patient-centred care; a biopsychosocial approach; perinatal mental health-trained clinicians; health promotion and illness prevention; and transition and discharge planning. The authors concluded that care integration improves the accessibility, continuity, and quality of perinatal mental health care [27].

Interventions in an optimal stepped care model range from universal preventive risk reduction strategies (psychoeducation, self-management), to prompt access to limited counselling sessions without referral for those with mild to moderate symptoms, to more frequent, structured, judicious intensity therapies for mild to moderate mental illness, through to high intensity services and multi-disciplinary support for severe mental illness, and intensive team-based specialist assessment and intervention for high risk, severe impairment and persistent, complex problems. Each of these levels can be considered a ‘step’ in the model. Broader support from social services, peers, family and friends should be accessible at each level [26].

Enabling access and facilitating pathways through the stepped care model is critical to its success and requires clearly documented and widely understood care pathways. The use of digital platforms by health services is a promising and rapidly expanding reform [28] that employs internet and mobile-based interventions (IMI) at each level of intensity of a stepped care model. Digital platforms employ phone, videoconferencing, interactive online and mobile phone applications, and sensor-based monitoring and recording devices. They are used for but not limited to supporting self-management and low intensity care. Evidence-based digital interventions can also be an adjunct to face-to-face clinical care for people with more severe forms of mental illness and higher needs [29]. Digital solutions are therefore suitable for each step in the stepped care model, including for facilitating engagement and pathways into treatment [28].

The four steps describe levels of health service intensity, increasing according to severity of individual need

Step 1: Primary prevention

The ‘lowest’ level in the stepped-care approach is action aimed at reducing modifiable social and psychological determinants of mental health problems that are salient to cause as well as recovery [23]. Primary prevention is a public health approach offered to all members of a specific population, such as those who are expecting, or have recently had, a baby. This is recommended because universal preventive strategies that reduce risks across a whole population have a greater benefit than treating only those with established illness, because ‘a large number of people exposed to a small risk commonly generate many more cases than a small number exposed to a high risk’ [30]. This approach demands population-wide objectives to reduce the average level of risk through intervention for all members, plus higher intensity intervention for others at higher level of risk [25]. Specific social, psychological and behavioural determinants of perinatal mental health problems are amenable to change [31], but there is limited recognition of primary prevention in the Australian Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs) for perinatal mental health [7].

Step 2: Secondary prevention - screening

Screening or case finding should take place in primary care; it is included in the Australian CPGs but has been unevenly implemented [32] and involves identification of personal and psychosocial risks or existing symptoms followed by referral to appropriate social support and professional care for those who score above a threshold on a screening instrument. Validated psychosocial and symptom screening instruments for postnatal mental health are available [2], and follow-up may include formal diagnosis [33]. Universal screening should be conducted by trained and supported clinicians and must be integrated into a health system with clear referral pathways to locally available services that are accessible, affordable, acceptable and provide effective treatments [34]. Some concerns about unintended consequences of implementation remain, including the difficulty in practice of ensuring that all criteria for a safe and effective screening protocol are met, and that potential harms from misidentification, false reassurance, or over-treatment and stigma are avoided [33, 34]. Mental health screening is included in best practice perinatal care recommendations in Australia and elsewhere [2, 34].

An effective screening program identifies the small proportion of the population of women for whom further monitoring or mental health assessment is warranted. Screening can reduce severity through early intervention and referral to available specialist services. Most women accept screening as part of standard care, however, many elect not to accept referral, or need further exploration of their predicament and active support to identify an appropriate health professional or social service [7]. Women’s reluctance appears to derive from insufficient knowledge about usual adjustment reactions after a birth, normalising their symptoms, understanding their difficulties as private concerns rather than mental health problems, unwillingness to adopt an illness label, fear of stigma, or perceptions that a professional will not be of assistance [35]. Above all, effective and ethical screening programs rely on acceptable support being available and affordable [33].

Steps 3 and 4: Early intervention and treatment

Treatment decisions for perinatal mental disorders differ from those at other times despite similar characteristics and risk factors across the life course. Pregnancy and breastfeeding constitute a unique social circumstance and biological milieu, and therefore require specific non-pharmaceutical and pharmaceutical treatment considerations. A wide range of psychosocial, psychological, and drug interventions with growing quality of safety and efficacy evidence are in use internationally and in Australia [36]. Some pharmacotherapies may be considered for first-line treatments for moderate to severe depression [7].

Victorian postnatal health services

In Victoria, postnatal health services include universal access to Maternal and Child Health nursing and General (medical) Practice, in-patient and out-patient early parenting services, secondary psychological and medical specialist care, and hospital services (Table 5). The available evidence suggests that the four components of a stepped-care model (primary prevention, secondary prevention, early intervention, treatment) are contained in existing Victorian health services but require specific care plans that link services together and facilitate access according to individual level of need. Despite the limitations identified, General Practice remains an important component of stepped care.

Postnatal health services in Victoria

Maternal and Child (MCH) Nursing Services [37]

• No-cost universal primary health service for families with children from birth to school age; first point of contact for parents of newborns

• Individual-and group level activities

• Three components:

1. Universal MCH program

2. Enhanced MCH program for families with additional needs

3. 24-hour MCH Line (phone)

• Funded and delivered by the State Government and local governments in partnership, and in some Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations.

• Key Ages & Stages (KAS) Practice Guidelines specify:

• Seven visits during the 12-month postnatal period [17]

• Staffed by MCH nurses

• Current registration as nurse and midwife plus accredited postgraduate qualification

• Annual Continuing Professional Development required [38]

• Reaches almost all families state-wide at a home visit soon after birth

• More than 90% remain engaged for the first 6 months of an infant’s life (somewhat lower for First Nations families) [39].

The MCH Service’s high population coverage affords significant opportunities for primary prevention (risk reduction) and secondary prevention (screening). Both are central to the MCH model of care; nurses work in a social model of health and recognise the psychosocial risks for postnatal mental health problems [40]. The Key Ages and Stages (KAS) Framework includes provision of evidence-based health information and advice designed to support discussion with parents and ensure consistent clinical practice (primary prevention) [17]. However, specific information or psychoeducation about adjustment to parenting or emotional wellbeing is not, but could be, included in the KAS and routinely provided [17].

The KAS includes a maternal health and wellbeing check with mental health screening (secondary prevention) using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [41] at 4 weeks postpartum [17]. Staff undergo training [38], and practice is supported by a Practice Resource Manual [42]. Women with clinically-significant symptoms are offered other services, but uptake is low without well-defined, accessible and acceptable care pathways within existing services [34]. This can be facilitated by an existing relationship with a General Practitioner [7].

Primary medical care (General Practice)

Australian primary care services include nursing, pharmacy, community health, optometrists and general practice (GP).

• GP practices are funded federally by the Australian Government using a Medicare taxation levy that pays private practices according to a schedule of fees for services.

• Consumers pay a fee for each service and receive rebate from Medicare equal to 85% of the scheduled fee; most practices charge more than the scheduled fee, and patients also pay the gap.

• Some GP practices ‘bulk bill’, charging only the Medicare component of fee at no cost to the patient [43].

• GPs provide the required referral to specialist services and mental health care plans for those in need but, unlike in some other high-income countries, an established relationship with a GP is not universal, some avoid visits because of out-of-pocket cost, and workforce shortages may limit access especially in rural areas [44].

• In high demand

• Specialist mother and baby units for high intensity residential care [18]

• Private sector EPCs

• Require medical referral

• Either self-funded or covered by private health insurance

Discussion

In this innovative study we propose a rigorously developed Theory of Change (ToC) framework that delineates a theoretical stepped-care model with potential to transform postnatal mental healthcare for women and their families in a rural setting in Victoria, Australia. The ToC elucidates how coordination of existing services in this setting could deliver accessible postnatal mental healthcare that is matched to level of individual need. In the model, prevention (Pathway 1) and screening (Pathway 2) are provided at a population level in universal primary care nursing services, and through access to social and parenting services. Access to early medical, psychological or parenting interventions (Pathway 3) are available through primary care medical services that also refer patients to higher intensity care (Pathway 4) according to need.

Theory of Change (ToC) provides the conceptual framework, or program theory, to guide the design and implementation of health service reform that could be used for investigating the promising stepped-care postnatal mental healthcare model in this and other settings [51].

Assumptions identified in the study warranting further investigation

The four Pathways in the model are underpinned by evidence-based Rationales that align with current policies, available services, appropriately trained staff, platforms of care, and clinical guidelines in Victoria. However, the success of each Pathway depends on satisfying certain Assumptions about external impediments or facilitators that are outside the control of the model itself (Table 7). The extent to which these are in place is not known and likely to change over time. The ToC Assumptions expose key uncertainties that warrant further investigation and may be used as testable hypotheses in future research necessary to strengthen the model of care.

First, maximising participation of the specified population (Assumption B, Table 7) is central to the success of universal primary prevention activities. Specifically, the use of evidence-based primary prevention that addresses modifiable risks to postnatal mental health by improving parents’ knowledge and skills is the first element of the stepped-care model of postnatal mental health. Parents today use mobile devices for most activities of life, which suggests a role for development and evaluation of digital platforms, using social media in particular [52] to enable parents to participate in structured psychoeducation programs on mobile devices and also preserve the benefits of social support afforded by interaction with peers at the same life-stage.

Second, stepped care involves efficient and appropriate referral to local services when necessary and rests on available local knowledge (Assumption D, Table 7). Recommendations of Victoria’s Royal Commission into mental health services include the aspiration for community perinatal mental health teams to support prospective and new parents, described as “a responsive and integrated service stream of treatment, care and support [to] support children from birth to 11 years and their families, focusing on the start of life and the formative years … . recognising the different preferences of consumers, families, carers and supporters” [8]. An “integrated stream” suggests as a first step the need to develop, maintain, monitor and evaluate an accessible repository of local knowledge of relevant programs and services for use by practitioners and to assist families at all levels of the stepped-care model. A repository would carry the added benefit of identifying gaps in the system where additional resources need to be applied, thereby addressing Assumption E, Table 7.

Last, low uptake of early intervention and treatment services where they are available is well documented [34] (Assumptions E and F, Table 7). Further investigation about specific barriers to uptake is required, but community education to increase understanding about perinatal mental health and address persistent stigma, using language consistent with women’s own understandings of their concerns, appears warranted.

Utility of ToC

ToC is a valuable tool for guiding health service reform [12]. The ToC reported here elucidates how change could occur in the state of Victoria to coordinate existing services in an adaptive system and according to local services and contextual factors [8]. By mapping existing services against a suggested stepped-care model, the ToC articulates proposed pathways to care. The ToC method has addressed core elements of Step 1 of the UK Medical Research Council framework, which includes considering context, engaging stakeholders, developing program theory, and identifying uncertainties [55] and is also a crucial means of defining key evaluation questions.

The body of policy, practice and health service evidence, and the resulting Victorian-specific TOC reported here, will be a valuable tool for use by policy-makers and stakeholders in the sector. It will inform advocacy for investment, and guide policy and perinatal health service reform in accordance with recommendations in the report of the Royal Commission into Victoria’s mental health system [8].

Limitations

The available evidence was limited to the needs of women in the postnatal period, but consideration needs to be given to services and workforce development to ensure that the needs of trans or non-binary parents who give birth are met. The ToC represents only the first essential stage in the development of this complex intervention but forms the basis for stage 2 of the MRC framework that involves assessing feasibility and acceptability of intervention and evaluation design in order to make decisions about progression to next stage of evaluation [55]. The stakeholder consultations that informed the ToC developed in this research were limited to identifying local services, their operations and how they might be streamlined. Aspects of workforce development and training, particularly in the Maternal and Child Health service, may have changed since the study was conducted. Work towards implementation of the proposed stepped-care model would require further consultation with the stakeholders involved at all steps in order to address resourcing, required practical action and further potential barriers.

Economic considerations of the proposed stepped-care reform are not addressed in detail in this research and any cost-utility analysis would require resourcing to address the complexity of federal/state funding and governance arrangements and source relevant data. However, the ToC sets out the framework within which an economic analysis of a stepped-care model could be conducted.

Conclusions

The proposed stepped postnatal mental healthcare model presented in this paper documents the evidence for the rationale of a proposed healthcare reform, the care pathways through which change is expected to take place, and the potential barriers that need to be addressed for successful implementation. It is based on analysis of the real-world context in the Australian state of Victoria, is an essential representation of program theory for evaluation purposes, and could be used to inform planning for introduction of a stepped model of care in this setting or adapted for use in other settings.
