Prevalence and clinical correlates of hypertension among patients with type 2 diabetes in Spanish primary care: a cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is common among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and represents a major contributor to cardiovascular and renal morbidity. Real-world data from primary care are essential to characterise associated clinical profiles and cardiometabolic multimorbidity patterns in routine clinical practice.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of hypertension among patients with T2DM managed in primary care, to characterise associated clinical profiles, and to explore sex-related differences using real-world data.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted including 680 adults with T2DM receiving routine care in primary care settings. Clinical, sociodemographic and laboratory data were obtained from electronic health records, direct clinical assessment, and structured patient interviews. Hypertension was defined as a previously recorded clinical diagnosis, current antihypertensive treatment, or blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg. Comparisons were performed according to sex. Factors independently associated with hypertension were analysed using bivariate analyses and multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, duration of T2DM, pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia, and established cardiovascular disease.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 69.8 ± 13.3 years and the mean duration of diabetes was 9.9 ± 4.6 years; 52.1% were men. Overall hypertension prevalence was 84.3%, with no significant difference between men (85.9%) and women (82.5%). Hypertension prevalence increased with age in both sexes. Women had lower educational and socioeconomic levels, higher abdominal obesity, and higher lipid concentrations, whereas men showed higher fasting glucose, serum creatinine, and markers of renal damage. In multivariable analysis, hypertension was independently associated with older age (OR 1.04 per year; 95% CI 1.02–1.06), pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia (OR 1.83; 95% CI 1.17–2.86), established cardiovascular disease (OR 2.03; 95% CI 1.16–3.55), and longer duration of T2DM (OR 1.06 per year; 95% CI 1.00–1.12). Sex was not independently associated with hypertension after adjustment.ConclusionsHypertension was common among patients with T2DM in primary care and was associated with older age, longer diabetes duration, and established cardiovascular disease. Sex-related differences in clinical profiles were observed but should be interpreted as descriptive. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases managed in primary care and represents a major public health challenge due to its strong association with cardiovascular and renal morbidity and mortality. Among the comorbid conditions accompanying T2DM, arterial hypertension plays a central role as a major determinant of cardiovascular risk and hypertension-mediated organ damage [1–4]. It is estimated that hypertension affects more than 70–80% of patients with T2DM, substantially increasing their overall cardiovascular risk.

The coexistence of T2DM and hypertension has important clinical implications, as both conditions contribute synergistically to the development of cardiovascular and renal complications, including coronary artery disease, stroke, and chronic kidney disease [2–4].

Although previous studies have described the coexistence of hypertension and T2DM, there is limited evidence regarding the prevalence and clinical correlates of hypertension among patients with T2DM in Spanish primary care settings [5, 6]. In addition, potential sex-related differences in cardiometabolic profiles remain insufficiently explored in primary care populations [7, 8].

In primary care settings, where most patients with T2DM are managed, the burden of cardiometabolic multimorbidity is particularly high and represents a major challenge for clinical management [9, 10]. This multimorbidity is characterised by the clustering of conditions such as dyslipidaemia, obesity, and chronic kidney disease, contributing to an increased overall risk burden.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of hypertension among patients with T2DM in primary care, to characterise associated clinical and cardiometabolic factors, and to explore potential sex-related differences using real-world data.

Methods

Study design and setting

A population-based cross-sectional study was conducted using baseline data from a previously established cohort of adult patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) receiving routine care in primary care centres in Cantabria, Spain. The original cohort study was designed to evaluate cardiometabolic risk factors among patients with T2DM managed in primary care settings. Data collection was conducted between January 2014 and December 2017.

The present analysis represents a secondary analysis of this cohort, focusing specifically on the prevalence of hypertension, associated clinical characteristics, and sex-related differences among patients with T2DM in routine clinical practice. The detailed methodology of the original cohort, including sampling procedures and data collection methods, has been described previously [11].

No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this study represents a secondary analysis of an existing cohort originally designed to characterise cardiometabolic risk factors in patients with T2DM.

Sampling and recruitment

The study population was obtained using a multistage random sampling strategy designed to ensure a representative sample of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in Cantabria, Spain. Of the 42 primary care centres registered in the Cantabrian Health Service, approximately one third (n = 13) were randomly selected using probability proportional to size.

Subsequently, a random subsample of patient lists from participating physicians within the selected centres was used to identify individuals diagnosed with T2DM who were receiving routine care in primary care settings. If a selected centre declined participation, it was replaced by another centre randomly selected within the same stratum. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with an established diagnosis of T2DM recorded in primary care clinical records. Patients with incomplete information for key study variables required for the analyses (e.g., blood pressure measurements, laboratory parameters, or relevant clinical data) were excluded to ensure data completeness and reliability of the statistical analyses. Acute clinical conditions, including acute kidney injury, were not systematically recorded in the dataset and therefore could not be explicitly excluded from the analysis.

Using this sampling procedure, patients with T2DM receiving care in primary care settings in Cantabria were identified. A total of 742 patients were initially assessed, of whom 62 were excluded due to missing data in key study variables (including blood pressure measurements, laboratory parameters, or incomplete clinical information). After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 680 patients were included in the final study population and were considered for the cross-sectional analysis (Fig. 1).

Data collection and variables

Clinical, demographic and laboratory data were obtained from multiple sources, including electronic health records, direct clinical examination and structured interviews conducted with patients during study visits. Data collection followed standardised clinical procedures routinely used in primary care practice.

Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, educational level, employment status, household income and area of residence. Clinical variables comprised duration of T2DM, smoking status, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), abdominal obesity and the presence of established cardiovascular disease. Information on relevant comorbidities was also collected, including hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetic retinopathy and chronic kidney disease.

Anthropometric measurements were obtained during clinical assessment following standardised procedures. Blood pressure measurements were obtained during routine primary care visits using validated automated devices, based on the mean of three measurements obtained across successive visits, in accordance with standard clinical practice.

Laboratory parameters were obtained from fasting blood samples collected as part of routine clinical care. These included fasting plasma glucose, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, triglycerides and serum creatinine. For these parameters, values correspond to the mean of three determinations performed during the study period, as previously described [11].

The urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was assessed using first-morning urine samples collected at three different time points, in accordance with standard clinical practice. For the purposes of this study, UACR values correspond to the mean of these determinations, as previously described [11]. This approach improves the reliability of albuminuria assessment and reduces the impact of transient elevations.

Renal function was assessed by calculating the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation.

Some clinically relevant variables, such as non-HDL cholesterol, haemoglobin levels, detailed pharmacological treatments, and certain comorbidities (e.g., atrial fibrillation or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), were not consistently available for all patients in the electronic health records. Therefore, these variables were not included in the analysis to ensure data completeness and avoid potential bias related to missing information. This limitation is inherent to the use of real-world electronic health record data.

Information on antihypertensive treatment was available in the original cohort, including the number of drugs prescribed and the main pharmacological classes. However, detailed information on specific drug combinations and treatment regimens was not systematically collected. Therefore, the analysis of antihypertensive treatment in the present study was limited to general treatment patterns rather than detailed combination strategies, due to the structure of the available dataset.

Definition of hypertension

Hypertension was defined as the presence of a previously recorded clinical diagnosis of hypertension in the patient’s medical history, current treatment with antihypertensive medication, or documented blood pressure values equal to or greater than 140/90 mmHg. Blood pressure was measured during routine primary care visits using validated automated blood pressure devices according to standard clinical practice. This definition reflects prevalent diagnosed or detected hypertension rather than uncontrolled hypertension.

Operational definitions

All operational definitions were based on those used in the original cohort study [11]. The following definitions were applied:

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was defined as a previous diagnosis recorded in primary care clinical records or the use of antidiabetic medication.

Dyslipidaemia was defined according to the criteria used in the original cohort study [11], including LDL cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, or current treatment with lipid-lowering therapy. This definition reflects the structure of the available dataset; however, current clinical guidelines increasingly adopt a risk-based approach to lipid management.

Obesity was defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m², and overweight as BMI between 25.0 and 29.9 kg/m². Abdominal obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men and ≥ 88 cm in women.

Chronic kidney disease was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m².

Smoking status was defined as current tobacco use at the time of data collection, and sedentary lifestyle as engaging in less than 150 min of moderate physical activity per week.

Established cardiovascular disease was defined as a previous diagnosis of coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, or peripheral arterial disease, as recorded in primary care clinical records.

Sex-based analysis

All analyses were stratified by sex to explore potential differences in clinical characteristics, cardiometabolic comorbidity, and hypertension prevalence between men and women. In addition, sex was included as an independent variable in multivariable models to assess its association with hypertension after adjustment for potential confounders.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to examine the associations between clinical, sociodemographic, and cardiometabolic variables and hypertension in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Bivariate analyses were used to identify variables associated with hypertension. Variables with p < 0.10, together with clinically relevant factors, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Age, sex, duration of diabetes, pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia, and established cardiovascular disease were included as predefined variables.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with hypertension. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5). Model stability was ensured by maintaining more than 10 events per variable.

Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Additional performance measures included Nagelkerke R² and the Brier score.

Sensitivity analyses including body mass index (BMI), HbA1c, and renal function parameters were performed.

Pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia was included as a proxy variable capturing both underlying lipid abnormalities and healthcare utilisation. As such, it may reflect a combination of biological risk and treatment-related factors, and its interpretation should consider potential confounding by access to care.

A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A complete-case analysis approach was used. Missing data were minimal (< 10%) and showed no systematic pattern.

Reporting guideline

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable national regulations on data protection.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Cantabria (CEIC–IDIVAL), approval code 2014.151 (Act 23/2014).

Due to the observational nature of the study and the use of anonymised routinely collected data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 680 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) were included in the analysis. The mean age was 69.8 ± 13.3 years, and the mean duration of diabetes was 9.9 ± 4.6 years. Men accounted for 52.1% of the study population (n = 354/680) and women for 47.9% (n = 326/680). Most participants were of White ethnicity (96.6%, n = 657/680) and resided in urban areas (68.2%, n = 464/680).

No statistically significant differences between men and women were observed for age, ethnicity, or place of residence. However, socioeconomic differences were identified. Women more frequently reported having no formal education (19.9% vs. 9.0%; p = 0.001) and a higher proportion had an annual household income below €18,000 (81.0% vs. 69.2%; p = 0.001). Smoking status also differed by sex, with a higher prevalence of current and former smoking among men, whereas women were more frequently never smokers.

The proportion of missing data for key variables was minimal, and analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach.

Anthropometric profile and cardiometabolic comorbidities according to sex

Sex-related differences were observed in anthropometric characteristics. Obesity defined by body mass index (BMI) was more frequent among men (36.4% vs. 24.0%), whereas abdominal obesity was more frequent in women (76.4% vs. 59.6%; p = 0.001).

Dyslipidaemia was highly prevalent in the overall population (85.3%, n = 580/680), with no statistically significant differences between sexes. Diabetic retinopathy was present in 29.3% of patients (n = 199/680), without relevant sex-related differences (Table 1).

Laboratory parameters and renal function according to sex

Several biochemical parameters differed between men and women (Table 2). Men presented higher fasting plasma glucose levels (141.9 ± 50.6 vs. 133.4 ± 41.5 mg/dL; p = 0.001) and higher serum creatinine concentrations (1.06 ± 0.68 vs. 0.85 ± 0.32 mg/dL; p = 0.001).

Markers of renal damage were more frequent among men, including microalbuminuria (26.0% vs. 21.5%) and proteinuria (9.3% vs. 5.5%). Women showed higher levels of total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol (all p = 0.001).

No statistically significant differences were observed in the prevalence of chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), which affected approximately 23% of the study population.

Prevalence of hypertension according to sex and age

The overall prevalence of hypertension among patients with T2DM was 84.3% (n = 573/680), with no statistically significant differences between men (85.9%, n = 304/354) and women (82.5%, n = 269/326).

Hypertension prevalence increased with age in both sexes (Table 3). Among men, prevalence was 62.5% in the 18–44 year age group, 77.8% in those aged 45–64 years, and 90.3% among patients aged ≥ 65 years. Among women, the corresponding prevalences were 27.3%, 75.0%, and 87.9%, respectively. Estimates in younger age groups should be interpreted cautiously due to smaller subgroup sizes.

Among hypertensive patients, 88.7% (n = 508/573) had a previous diagnosis and were receiving antihypertensive treatment, whereas 11.3% (n = 65/573) were newly identified during the study. Information on blood pressure control was not available.

Antihypertensive treatment patterns

The most frequently prescribed antihypertensive drug classes were angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (41.3%), angiotensin II receptor blockers (38.6%), diuretics (36.1%), and beta-blockers (33.7%).

Combination therapy (≥ 2 antihypertensive drugs) was observed in 67.5% of hypertensive patients (n = 387/573), with no relevant differences between sexes.

Detailed information on specific drug combinations was not systematically available; therefore, further analysis of treatment regimens was not performed.

Each additional year of age was associated with higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.04; 95% CI 1.02–1.06; p < 0.001). Pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia was associated with higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.83; 95% CI 1.17–2.86; p = 0.008), as was established cardiovascular disease (OR 2.03; 95% CI 1.16–3.55; p = 0.013). Despite descriptive sex-related differences in clinical and socioeconomic characteristics, sex was not independently associated with hypertension after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that the observed differences may be explained by underlying cardiometabolic profiles and social determinants rather than sex itself acting as an independent risk factor.

The multivariable model showed moderate discriminative ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.69 (Fig. 3). Model calibration was adequate (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.38), and overall model performance was acceptable (Nagelkerke R² = 0.15; Brier score = 0.16). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the model. The inclusion of additional variables such as body mass index, HbA1c, and renal function parameters did not substantially modify the magnitude or direction of the associations (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this cross-sectional study of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in primary care, hypertension was common and was associated with older age, longer duration of diabetes, pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia, and established cardiovascular disease. These findings are consistent with previous evidence highlighting the importance of hypertension in cardiovascular risk among patients with diabetes [1, 2].

Hypertension frequently coexisted with other cardiometabolic conditions, including dyslipidaemia, obesity, and renal impairment, reflecting the clustering of risk factors commonly observed in T2DM. This underscores the need for integrated cardiovascular risk management strategies.

The model showed moderate discriminative ability and good calibration, consistent with real-world clinical prediction models. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the identified associations, as results remained stable across alternative model specifications.

Hypertension and cardiometabolic multimorbidity

The high prevalence of hypertension observed in our study is consistent with previous reports in primary care populations [5, 6]. Hypertension is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in patients with diabetes, and its coexistence with other risk factors increases overall cardiovascular risk [3, 4].

Our findings confirm that hypertension rarely occurs in isolation and is frequently associated with other cardiometabolic conditions, contributing to increased clinical complexity. This is consistent with previous studies describing high levels of multimorbidity in patients with T2DM [9, 10].

The use of electronic health records allowed the characterisation of these patterns in routine clinical practice. Previous studies have highlighted the value of real-world data for understanding disease burden and multimorbidity in patients with diabetes [11, 12].

Sex-related differences

Although the prevalence of hypertension was similar in men and women, differences were observed in clinical and socioeconomic profiles. Women showed higher abdominal obesity and less favourable lipid profiles.

However, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive, as sex was not independently associated with hypertension in the multivariable model. Previous studies have reported sex-related differences in cardiovascular risk among patients with T2DM [7, 8], and socioeconomic factors may also influence these patterns [13].

Clinical implications

The association between hypertension, age, and duration of diabetes reflects the accumulation of cardiovascular risk over time. These findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that longer duration of T2DM is associated with increased cardiovascular risk burden [14].

Given the high prevalence of hypertension and its association with multimorbidity, primary care plays a key role in early detection and integrated management of cardiovascular risk. Pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia may reflect underlying cardiovascular risk or healthcare utilisation rather than a direct biological association, and should be interpreted cautiously.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the lack of information on blood pressure control limits the assessment of hypertension management.

Residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the exclusion of patients with missing data may have introduced selection bias, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations. In addition, the pragmatic definition of hypertension based on diagnosis, treatment, or recorded blood pressure values may have led to some degree of misclassification. Albuminuria assessment was based on available measurements and did not allow strict confirmation of persistent albuminuria according to guideline-recommended criteria.

Despite these limitations, the use of real-world primary care data enhances the applicability of the findings to routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

Hypertension was common among patients with T2DM in primary care and was associated with older age, longer diabetes duration, pharmacologically treated dyslipidaemia, and established cardiovascular disease. Although sex was not independently associated with hypertension, differences in clinical profiles between men and women were observed and should be interpreted as descriptive. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
