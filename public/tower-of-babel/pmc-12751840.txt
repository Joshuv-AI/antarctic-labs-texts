Construction and application of a quality evaluation indicator system for intravenous therapy nursing
Authors: Yuejiao Zhang, Ruiyi Zhao, Hongling Sun, Nan Jiang, Xiaoling Shao, Jiangliu Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectTo construct a quality evaluation indicator system for intravenous therapy nursing and identify deficiencies in clinical practice.MethodsBased on the structure-process-outcome framework, we developed through literature review, semi-structured interviews, and Delphi consultation (n = 22 experts). Introduction

Intravenous therapy nursing is one of the most clinically significant and frequently performed nursing interventions worldwide. In China, nurses routinely conduct these procedures as part of daily practice. Vascular Access Teams (VATs), staffed by specially trained nurses, manage comprehensive catheter care. This includes ultrasound-guided peripheral venous access, midline catheters, PICC insertion, and central venous device maintenance [1–3]. This model differs substantially from the Certified Registered Nurse Infusion (CRNI) approach in the United States. There, specialists conduct infusion therapy while driving quality improvement initiatives, leading clinical research, and serving in educational, managerial, and consultative roles [4, 5].

Over the past decade, China has developed specialized infusion nurse training programs. However, many trained practitioners continue performing routine duties without fully utilizing their advanced skills. The growing clinical use of PICCs, midlines, and Totally Implantable Venous Access Ports (TIVAPs) has created substantial demand for dedicated VATs in nursing practice [6–10]. Some tertiary hospitals have established such teams over the last 20 years, with responsibilities similar to the CRNI model. However, significant gaps persist [6, 11]. Most notably, no standardized quality evaluation system exists to assess VAT performance. Also, most non-tertiary hospitals lack VATs altogether.

This study addresses these gaps by establishing a validated quality indicator framework for VAT performance assessment. It also provides standardized implementation guidance for hospitals developing vascular access services. The research aims to create evaluation criteria that objectively measure team effectiveness and generate evidence-based protocols for organizational adoption.

Method

Quality indicator development phase

We conducted a systematic literature review to identify quality indicators for intravenous therapy nursing teams. We performed comprehensive searches were performed across 12 databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, CBM) and 8 guideline repositories (INS, NICE, AHRQ, RNAO, GIN, NGC, JBI, SIGN) from inception through December 2024. Search strategies incorporated controlled vocabulary and keywords, including “vascular access team,” “infusion nurs,” “PICC team,” “quality indicator,” and “quality measurement.” After screening 8,983 records, 13 articles met inclusion criteria (primary literature on Ⅳ team evaluation). Exclusion criteria were news, commentaries, inaccessible texts, or non-English/Chinese publications.

A multidisciplinary research team led the development process. The team comprised two nurse administrators, one quality control specialist, three certified infusion nurses, and one nursing graduate researcher. Qualitative data collection included semi-structured interviews with eight clinicians (two infusion nursing managers, three nursing unit managers, three dedicated IV nurses) and six stakeholders (three patients requiring PICC/midline insertion, two patients needing PICC/port maintenance, one family caregiver). We obtained ethics approval and secured written informed consent from all participants, including nurse managers, patients, and caregivers. The institutional ethics committee approved the interview protocol. We thematically analyzed interview transcripts to extract preliminary indicators. Two iterative expert meetings with ten specialists (nurse managers, IV therapy experts, researchers) refined indicator relevance and operational definitions.

We performed a two-round Delphi consultation with 22 experts from nine Chinese provinces (senior: sub-senior ratio = 5:17; mean experience = 11.7 ± 4.2 years). Participants rated indicators on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not important; 5 = extremely important). Consensus was defined as mean score ≥ 3.5, coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 0.25, and ≥ 20% agreement [12]. We calculated expert authority coefficients as Cr = (Cs + Ca)/2, where Cs denotes familiarity with items and Ca indicates judgment basis (Round 1 Cr = 0.903; Round 2 Cr = 0.898). Kendall’s W concordance coefficients confirmed consensus strength (W = 0.321–0.329, P < 0.001). We established indicator weights using the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) in yaahp 10.3. We selected standard AHP over fuzzy AHP due to high consistency in expert judgments (CR < 0.1) and low variability (CV ≤ 0.25). This made fuzzy extensions unnecessary for reducing bias. Pairwise comparison matrices underwent consistency validation (CR values: structure = 0.045, process = 0.052, outcome = 0.038; all < 0.1). We calculated global weights through eigenvector decomposition. The final system allocated 20% to structure, 40% to process, and 40% to outcome domains.

Empirical validation phase

We operationalized the derived quality indicator system into a 100-point rating scale (structure: 20 points; process: 40 points; outcome: 40 points) comprising 49 scored elements [13]. Validation occurred across 30 Level-III (tertiary) public hospitals in 15 Chinese provinces. We selected them via purposive and convenience sampling to ensure geographical diversity (East China: 43.3%, North China: 23.3%, Central China: 20.0%, South China: 13.4%; response rate: 90.91%). We did not employ random sampling; selection prioritized institutions with established VATs. Before data collection, we obtained ethics approval and secured written informed consent from nurse managers. All participants received confidentiality assurances and study protocol documentation. Trained evaluators conducted on-site audits between January and March 2025. Data collection used a two-part instrument. Part A assessed indicator compliance using standardized scoring rubrics (e.g., binary checklists for structural elements; frequency-based calculations for complication rates). Part B captured daily practice documentation. We analyzed quantitative data using SPSS 24.0. We reported categorical variables as frequencies (%) and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. We evaluated hospital performance through: (1) Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) to calculate proximity scores (Ci); (2) rank-sum ratio (RSR) for non-parametric ranking; and (3) fuzzy combination models integrating TOPSIS and RSR outputs, with composite scores computed as 0.5 × Ci + 0.5 × RSR.

Results

Literature review and expert consensus development

The systematic search strategy initially identified 8,983 records. After removing 3,049 duplicates and excluding 4,802 irrelevant records, 1,081 articles underwent full-text review. Of these, we excluded 38 due to methodological limitations, yielding 13 eligible publications for analysis. Through iterative synthesis of literature findings, semi-structured interviews, and expert group discussions, we established a preliminary framework. It comprised 3 first-level, 19 s-level, and 61 third-level quality indicators. This framework informed the development of the Delphi consultation instrument.

Delphi expert consultation

Twenty-two qualified experts from nine Chinese provinces participated (mean age 47.05 ± 5.83 years; mean IV therapy experience 11.73 ± 4.22 years). They included five senior-ranked and seventeen sub-senior professionals. The consultation demonstrated high expert authority (Cr = 0.903 in Round 1, 0.898 in Round 2). The first Delphi round (21/22 response rate) yielded 59 suggestions for refinement. This led to revisions of 19 third-level and 6 s-level indicators based on quantitative ratings (3.05-5.00) and qualitative feedback. The second round (100% response) generated 11 additional suggestions from four experts, resulting in three further modifications. Final consensus established a framework of 3 first-level, 15 s-level, and 49 third-level indicators. Strong consensus was evidenced by Kendall’s W coefficients (Round 1: W = 0.321, χ² = 557.523, P < 0.001; Round 2: W = 0.329, χ² = 465.408, P < 0.001) and low coefficient of variation values (Round 1: 0.042–0.228; Round 2: 0-0.2).

Establishment of the rating scale

We operationalized the finalized indicator system into a 100-point rating scale with weighted domain allocations: structure (20 points), process (40 points), and outcome (40 points). We standardized scoring protocols as follows. For qualitative indicators, we converted performance levels through equivalent coefficients: optimal performance (coefficient 1.0 → score 5), good performance (coefficient 0.75 → score 4), moderate performance (coefficient 0.5 → score 3), limited performance (coefficient 0.25 → score 2), non-compliance (coefficient 0 → score 1). Final score calculation: mean mark × equivalent coefficient. For quantitative indicators, scoring depended on indicator polarity: positive indicators (e.g., success rates): score = mean mark × observed incidence rate; negative indicators (e.g., complication rates): score = mean mark × (1 - observed incidence rate). Application results

We empirically validated the quality indicator system through a multicenter survey involving 33 tertiary hospitals across 15 Chinese cities. We obtained complete datasets from 30 institutions, yielding a 90.91% response rate. Geographical representation spanned East China (43.3%), North China (23.3%), Central China (20.0%), and South China (13.4%). We quantified hospital performance using scoring rates (actual score / standardized score × 100%) [14]. Performance analysis revealed a mean overall score of 84.09 ± 8.63 points across institutions (Table 2). Dimensional performance showed scores of 14.90 ± 2.73 for structure, 33.77 ± 4.39 for process, and 35.42 ± 2.95 for outcome domains (Table 2). Scoring rates ranged from 35.83% to 99.17% for structure, 45.83% to 100% for process, and 20.00% to 99.96% for outcome (Table 2). The distribution of total scores showed 10 hospitals achieving ≥ 90 points, 10 scoring 80–89, 9 scoring 70–79, and 1 scoring below 70. Collectively, 66.7% (20/30) of hospitals attained scores of 80 or higher. Complete performance metrics are presented in Table 2.

Indicator-level analysis revealed performance gaps across dimensions. Structural deficiencies (score rates < 80%) included catheter-related complication information systems (35.83%), venous access selection pathways (48.33%), and environmental monitoring protocols (Class II surface/hand hygiene: 54.17%; catheterization room standards: 76.67%). The process dimension showed shortcomings in pain management (45.83%), research activity organization (59.17%), adverse event discussions (65.83%), and discharge follow-up systems (75.00%). Outcome measures showed deficits in professional development activities (standards development participation: 20.00%; academic exchanges: 24.70%) and satisfaction metrics (clinical staff: 56.67%; patients/families: 62.50%). These findings show the non-additive interdependence among evaluation dimensions. Structural elements establish foundational capabilities. Processes determine operational effectiveness. Outcomes validate service quality. Together, they form a functional continuum where dimensions interact multiplicatively rather than summatively. The observed heterogeneity in dimensional performance profiles (Table 3) necessitates multidimensional assessment methods. We consequently employed the integrated TOPSIS-RSR approach to mitigate unidimensional evaluation bias and capture the complex interactions between structural capacity, procedural implementation, and clinical impact.

Rank-sum ratio (RSR) analysis

We sorted RSR values in ascending order and calculated downward cumulative frequencies to determine probability units (Probit) per standard conversion methods. We established a statistically significant regression model: RSR = -0.7704 + 0.2533 × Probit (R2 = 0.9449, F = 498.530, P < 0.001). This confirmed excellent model fit. We applied optimal classification criteria based on percentile cutoffs from the RSR regression model (poor: <15.866 percentile, RSR < 0.2430; average: 15.866–84.134 percentile, RSR 0.2430–0.7497; good: >84.134 percentile, RSR >0.7497) [15]. Hospitals were stratified into three distinct performance tiers (Table 5). The superior category (RSR >0.7497, n = 8, 26.67%) included H22, H26, H21, H8, H27, H14, H3, and H13. These institutions showed high-level balanced performance, with structure, process, and outcome dimensions all exceeding mean values. The medium tier (0.2430 ≤ RSR ≤ 0.7497, n = 17, 56.66%) encompassed H4, H6, H11, H5, H9, H15, H12, H28, H10, H23, H19, H24, H17, H25, H18, H2, and H29. It exhibited internal heterogeneity characterized by specialized strengths alongside dimensional deficiencies. The poor category (RSR < 0.2430, n = 5, 16.67%) consisted of H1, H20, H7, H16, and H30. It displayed uniformly substandard performance across all dimensions below average values, indicating needs for structural and procedural interventions (Table 6).

Discussion

This study establishes a validated evaluation system for intravenous therapy nursing teams through rigorous methodological development. The framework’s construction incorporated systematic literature review, expert consultation, and empirical validation. It demonstrates strong reliability with expert authority coefficients exceeding 0.85. Its discriminative validity is evidenced by a 33.69-point score range across 30 hospitals. Practical applicability is confirmed through operational definitions for all 49 indicators, including 13 quantitative metrics with clinically validated calculation methods and 36 qualitative measures with standardized scoring rubrics.

Dimensional analysis reveals distinct performance patterns across healthcare institutions. Structural indicators (mean 14.90 ± 2.73) reflect disparities in resource allocation, particularly in electronic complication tracking systems (35.83% compliance) and environmental monitoring protocols (54.17%). Process indicators (mean 33.77 ± 4.39) show operational excellence in technical standards (100% compliance) but reveal critical gaps in pain management (45.83%) and adverse event documentation (65.83%). Outcome metrics (mean 35.42 ± 2.95) show satisfactory clinical results yet deficiencies in scholarly productivity (standards development participation: 20.00%) and stakeholder satisfaction (clinical staff: 56.67%). These findings confirm the non-additive interdependence among evaluation dimensions. Structural foundations enable service delivery, procedural implementation determines care quality, and clinical outcomes validate effectiveness. This functional hierarchy necessitated an integrated TOPSIS-RSR method to prevent unidimensional assessment bias. Consistent top-ranking performance of H13, H3, and H27 across weighting combinations underscoring the robustness of institutions exhibiting balanced excellence.

Two practice gaps merit attention: the absence of electronic complication tracking systems in 64.17% of hospitals impedes quality improvement despite high technical proficiency (91.60% first-attempt venipuncture success); and only 54.17% compliance with Class II environment standards [16, 17] reveals infection control vulnerabilities. Implementing digital surveillance systems would enhance complication monitoring per National Health Commission guidelines. This would address the disconnect between procedural skill and system management [18, 19]. Future research should develop a streamlined core indicator set focusing on complication tracking, stakeholder satisfaction, and procedure compliance. This would enable efficient routine monitoring while maintaining the comprehensive system for periodic evaluations.

Study limitations include an exclusive focus on tertiary hospitals, which may limit generalizability to non-tertiary settings where VATs are often absent. This would lead to lower structural scores and different performance patterns. Another limitation is the absence of longitudinal assessment. Additional limitations include potential Delphi biases from geographic concentration (nine provinces) and nursing predominance, which may overlook multidisciplinary perspectives. Cultural/institutional factors (e.g., regional resource disparities) could also affect indicator application. Validation in secondary care settings and development of digital reporting platforms represent essential next steps. Nevertheless, this system provides institutions with a multidimensional tool to identify strengths and address critical gaps, particularly in complication management and environmental compliance. It represents an advancement toward standardizing excellence in vascular access services across China’s healthcare system.
