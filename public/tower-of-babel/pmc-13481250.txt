Endophytic Flavobacterium promotes root hair development and enhances drought tolerance via an ERF–CEP5 hormonal regulatory module
Authors: Arezoo Rahimi, Sofia Stiegert, Omid Karami, Vladyslav Lysenko, Hugo A Pantigoso, Guillermo Guerrero-Egido, Priyanka Chopra, Jefri Heyman, Aixia Huang, Guodong Wang, Connor Philippo, Tingting Zhu, Thijs Stegmann, Victoria Berdion Gabarain, Jingke Kang, Mitja Remus-Emsermann, Viola Willemsen, Nathaniel I Martin, Lieven De Veylder, Ive De Smet, Marcel Wiermer, Jos M Raaijmakers, José Manuel Estevez, Bernd Mueller-Roeber, Victor J Carrión, Salma Balazadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoot hairs (RHs) are cellular outgrowths of plant root epidermal cells that are important for water uptake, nutrient acquisition and rhizosphere dynamics. Key genes controlling RH development have been identified, but the regulatory mechanisms of RH growth during drought stress remain largely elusive. Here we show that the bacterial root endophyte Flavobacterium sp. 98 (Flavo98) modulates root system architecture with a pronounced promotion of RH formation and cell elongation that is maintained under low-water conditions. We also show that Flavo98 improves plant performance under drought in diverse plant species, including Arabidopsis and wheat, and mitigates drought-associated seed yield loss. Using cellular, molecular and genetic analyses in Arabidopsis, we identified root stele-expressed ERF transcription factors, ERF115 and ERF114, as key mediators of Flavo98-induced RH development and plant drought responses. We show that both ERFs operate, in part, by inducing the expression of the small signalling peptide C-TERMINALLY ENCODED PEPTIDE 5 (CEP5). In addition, we present evidence for enhanced ethylene biosynthesis and signalling in Flavo98-induced ERF115 and ERF114 expression, revealing a new pathway that integrates ethylene signalling into RH regulation under drought. Together, our findings uncover a pathway that regulates RH growth as part of a broader, microorganism-dependent root system architecture plasticity under low-water conditions. This study highlights the potential of uncovering plant–microorganism mechanisms to strengthen crop resilience in a changing climate.Subject terms: Plant molecular biology, Microbiology

Main

Drought (water deficit) is one of the most detrimental environmental factors threatening agricultural crop production and food security. Droughts are predicted to become considerably more frequent and severe, leading to nearly three times greater reductions in global average plant production by the last quarter of this century (2075–2099) compared with the period between 1850 and 19991. This will further intensify the global food crisis, especially in developing countries2,3. Therefore, finding ways to maintain (or raise) dryland crop productivity has become more urgent than ever.

Recent advances in molecular tools and functional genomics have allowed the identification of various genes and regulatory proteins, including transcription factors (TFs), affecting plant drought tolerance, but the development of drought-resilient crop varieties without compromising growth and yield performance has been limited. This is partly due to the complexity and multifactorial nature of drought tolerance as well as the inhibitory crosstalk between growth and stress adaptation traits4. In addition, the effectiveness of individual drought-tolerance traits depends on both environmental and genetic contexts, complicating efforts to develop crops that are resilient to stress in diverse agro-ecosystems. For instance, while deep roots are advantageous for water acquisition during drought, they may offer limited benefits when water is confined to the upper soil layers or when deeper soil layers lack nutrients5. In such scenarios, traits such as adaptable lateral root (LR) architecture or enhanced root hair (RH) growth may offer greater benefits.

RHs extend from specialized root epidermal cells, called trichoblasts. They facilitate nutrient and water uptake, enhance root-soil contact, improve soil anchorage and promote interaction with the rhizosphere microbiome6–10. RH development is influenced by developmental and environmental factors, including hormones, soil nutrients and water availability11,12, allowing plants to dynamically adjust RH growth in response to changing environments. The molecular mechanisms of RH development (from initiation to elongation) have been studied extensively in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, and several core regulators have been identified11,13,14. The regulatory network includes several basic helix–loop–helix TFs that play critical roles in initiating RH formation and elongation in cells positioned outside the space separating two cortical cells (the H-cell position). These TFs include ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE 6 (RHD6) and its closest homologue RHD6-LIKE 1 (RSL1), which in turn activate the expression of other basic helix–loop–helix factors (such as RSL2 and RSL4) to promote RH growth15. By contrast, in non-hair cells (N-cell position), a regulatory cascade involving HD-Zip TFs, particularly GLABRA2 (GL2), represses the expression of RHD6 and RSL1, thereby inhibiting RH formation16. One phytohormone important for RH development is ethylene17,18. An elegant study revealed a physical interaction between EIN3, a key TF in ethylene signalling, and RHD6, unveiling their parallel and synergistic effects on regulating RSL4 and genes involved in RH formation13.

Several other studies revealed that enhancing RH length and density improves drought tolerance in Arabidopsis and in crops such as barley, maize and rice19–23. In Arabidopsis, the root hairless line N23, which overexpresses the N-terminal domain of the plasma membrane Ca2+-binding protein-2 in RH cells, develops more and longer LR than the wild type (WT) yet shows reduced water uptake and increased drought sensitivity compared with Col-0, indicating the importance of RHs for water uptake and drought tolerance19. In addition, studies in barley and maize have discovered a correlation between genetic variation in RH length and density and improved water uptake from drying soil and/or drought adaptation6,21,22. Notably, RHs contributed to maintaining water homeostasis and yield stability in barley plants during drought conditions in the field22. Despite these findings, our current understanding of the regulatory mechanisms controlling RH growth under drought remains limited, and only a few regulators, such as WOX11 in rice, ZmTIP1 in maize and HvEXPB7 in barley, have been identified so far20,21,23.

Plants in natural environments or cultivated farmlands are intricately associated with microorganisms that colonize the root surface (rhizoplane), inhabit the surrounding soil (rhizosphere) or live inside the roots (endosphere). A growing body of evidence has shown a profound impact of root-colonizing microorganisms on plant growth, development and survival in adverse environments. In recent years, several growth-promoting bacteria that can enhance plant drought tolerance with minimal growth-related trade-offs have been identified24–29. However, the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects on plant drought tolerance are largely unknown. Recent evidence suggests that Pseudomonas argentinensis sp. SA190 improves drought tolerance by affecting the expression of aquaporin genes through increased histone H3K4 trimethylation in an abscisic acid-dependent manner30.

Here, we report that Flavobacterium sp. 98 (Flavo98 hereafter), a growth-promoting bacterium previously isolated from the endosphere of sugar beet roots31, enhances drought stress tolerance in several plant species, including Arabidopsis and wheat (Triticum aestivum). Flavo98 colonizes the internal root tissues, including the vascular region, of Arabidopsis and enhances LR and RH development during drought stress. While both traits probably contribute to Flavo98-mediated drought tolerance, we focused our investigation on RH formation, as its control by bacteria is less well understood so far. We demonstrate that Flavo98-dependent RH induction and growth require ethylene biosynthesis and signalling in plants. Our analyses identified two APETALA2/ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR (ERF) TFs, ERF115 and ERF114, as key components in mediating RH development by Flavo98 and as regulators of plant drought response. Both ERF115 and ERF114 are transcriptionally induced by Flavo98 in an ethylene signalling-dependent manner. ERF115 was originally identified as a factor driving quiescent centre cell division during root development32. Further studies highlighted the roles of ERF115 and ERF114 in wound response, tissue regeneration, mechanical stress-induced LR development and jasmonic acid-induced RH elongation33–36. However, the involvement of these TFs in RH formation, RH growth under drought conditions and drought tolerance has not been reported previously.

We further explored the mechanism of ERF115/ERF114-induced RH development and discovered their common target gene, C-TERMINALLY ENCODED PEPTIDE 5 (CEP5). CEP5 is a member of the CEP family of small, secreted peptides. These peptides share considerable sequence similarity within a C-terminal conserved domain and are produced through posttranslational modification and proteolytic processing of precursor polypeptides37,38. CEP5 is known to inhibit LR initiation39 and has been implicated in promoting drought tolerance40. Here, we reveal a function of CEP5 as a mediator of RH development, particularly under low-water conditions.

Collectively, our study identifies an ERF115/ERF114–CEP5 regulatory module that controls RH development as part of broader root system plasticity, with potential relevance under drought conditions.

Results

Flavo98 promotes drought tolerance in multiple plant species

Among the eight bacterial isolates from six genera tested in our study (Supplementary Fig. 1a), three strains enhanced drought tolerance in Arabidopsis. Of these, Flavobacterium sp. 98 (Flavo98), which also improved performance in wheat and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), was selected for further characterization. Independent experiments in Arabidopsis under controlled conditions in γ-irradiated sandy soil validated the effects of Flavo98. Plants were inoculated with Flavo98 or mock buffer at 17 days after germination. Subsequently, plants were subjected to drought. Mock-treated plants exhibited wilting and desiccation (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1b) and a significant reduction in rosette growth (Fig. 1b), whereas Flavo98-inoculated plants were significantly less affected, indicating a positive effect of the bacterium on plant drought tolerance. Rewatering resulted in the survival of the majority (nearly 85%) of the Flavo98-inoculated plants; by contrast, only a few mock-treated plants survived the treatment. Among the surviving plants, Flavo98-inoculated individuals produced significantly more seeds than the surviving mock-treated plants (Fig. 1c).

We next tested the effect of Flavo98 on agronomically important plants, including two Brassicaceae family members, that is, rapeseed (Brassica napus) and camelina (Camelina sativa), as well as tomato. Flavo98 improved drought tolerance in the tested species (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). It also improved drought tolerance in wheat, a globally important monocot cereal (Fig. 1d). Under well-watered conditions, wheat plants inoculated with Flavo98 exhibited enhanced growth compared with mock-inoculated plants. While a significant decline in plant size occurred in mock-treated plants after 5 days of water deprivation, wheat plants inoculated with Flavo98 showed improved growth (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, Flavo98 inoculation improved seed yield after rewatering of drought-treated wheat plants compared with mock-inoculated plants (Fig. 1f).

Flavo98 affects root architecture and triggers RH development

To investigate how Flavo98 interacts with roots, we first examined its colonization pattern using confocal laser scanning microscopy. GFP-labelled Flavo98 (Flavo98–GFP) effectively colonized the endosphere of Arabidopsis roots, particularly the vascular system (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Video 1). Given that root system architecture (RSA) is a key trait influencing plant responses to water limitation, we next tested whether Flavo98 modulates RSA. Arabidopsis (Col-0) seedlings were inoculated with Flavo98 or mock-treated and their RSA was examined. Inoculation with Flavo98 increased LR formation but reduced primary root growth compared with mock treatment (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 4a,b). Moreover, Flavo98 significantly enhanced RH density and length (Fig. 1i–k and Supplementary Fig. 5a). RHs developed considerably closer to the root tips of Flavo98-inoculated than mock-treated plants. This effect was absent in roots inoculated with heat-inactivated Flavo98, indicating that live bacteria are required for promoting RH development (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Notably, the induction of RH development by Flavo98 occurred irrespective of whether roots were illuminated by light or kept in darkness (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

To test whether the effect of Flavo98 on RH induction and growth is specific to mono-association or retained in the presence of another bacterial strain, we combined Flavo98 with Chitinophaga sp. 94, which, in combination, has previously been reported to confer disease protection31. RH development was still induced by the strain combination, while Chitinophaga sp. 94 by itself did not promote RH formation (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Next, we analysed the epidermal cell types that develop RHs on Flavo98 inoculation by quantifying the proportion of cells displaying hairs in H or N positions. Flavo98-inoculated roots showed a higher proportion of RH-producing cells in both positions compared with mock-treated roots, with a particularly pronounced increase in N cells. In mock-treated roots, approximately 35% of H cells and 8% of N cells developed RHs, while Flavo98-inoculated roots developed RHs in approximately 60% of H cells and in 30% of N cells (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Accordingly, the activity of the GL2 promoter (in pGL2::GFP transgenic plants)41, indicated by the number of GFP-positive cell files, was reduced in Flavo98-treated roots compared with mock-treated roots, suggesting a shift towards RH cell fate (Supplementary Fig. 5e,f). These results suggest that the hairy phenotype of Flavo98-inoculated roots is largely due to ectopic RH formation. Similar to Arabidopsis, wheat plants inoculated with Flavo98 exhibited shorter main roots and an increased number of LRs (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). The number of RHs was also significantly increased compared with the mock treatment (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Fig. 4d). Collectively, these results show that Flavo98 modifies RSA and stimulates RH formation and elongation across different plant species.

Flavo98 promotes RH development under drought stress

To assess the effect of Flavo98 on RH growth during drought stress, we first examined the response of plants to polyethylene glycol (PEG) treatment, an established method for simulating water-limitation stress30,36, in both the absence and presence of the bacterium. We transferred 7-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings to half-Murashige–Skoog (MS) medium with or without 5% PEG 8000 and inoculated their root tips with Flavo98 or treated them with a mock solution. Under PEG conditions, roots inoculated with Flavo98 showed higher bacterial colonization (measured as colony-forming units, CFUs) than roots in PEG-free medium (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Of note, after 7 days of PEG treatment, mock-treated Arabidopsis roots displayed inhibited RH growth (Fig. 1m,n), consistent with previous observations in Arabidopsis42 and maize21. Remarkably, inoculation with Flavo98 alleviated this inhibition, restoring RH development even under water-limiting conditions.

After approximately 21 days of PEG treatment, Flavo98-inoculated plants appeared to better survive at this stage; however, a visible halo also developed at their root–medium interface, potentially due to PEG degradation or other root–medium interactions. This limited a reliable assessment of drought responses at this stage (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Therefore, subsequent analyses were performed using a hard agar (HA) medium as an alternative low-water-potential assay. In this setup, Arabidopsis seedlings were grown on HA medium, in which increasing the agar and nutrient concentrations lowers the medium’s water potential. This method has been shown to elicit gene expression responses closely resembling those occurring during vermiculite drying43. Similar to PEG treatment, RH formation and growth were significantly reduced in mock-treated plants on HA under our experimental conditions; this reduction was reversed by Flavo98 inoculation (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). On prolonged exposure to low water potential, Flavo98-inoculated plants showed improved survival compared with mock-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. 8d,e).

To substantiate these findings, we also examined several phylogenetically related Flavobacterium strains, Flavo96, Flavo97 and Flavo5B, which originated from disease-suppressive soil. These strains share over 99% genomic similarity with Flavo9831. All closely related Flavobacterium strains similarly affected RSA, including RH development (Supplementary Fig. 9a,b). Flavo96 was selected for further analysis under low-water-potential conditions on HA, where it similarly enhanced RH growth and improved plant survival, as reflected by a smaller reduction in biomass (Supplementary Fig. 9c,d).

Collectively, these results show that Flavo98 and close relatives promote RH development under water-limited conditions, which may contribute to the improved plant performance observed in the presence of the bacterium.

Flavo98-responsive ERF115/114 TFs regulate RH development under control and drought conditions

To understand the molecular mechanisms of Flavo98-dependent RH development in Arabidopsis, we compared the transcriptomes of Flavo98- and mock-treated roots at several timepoints (12 h, 48 h, 72 h and 6 days) after inoculation. In total, 1,052 Arabidopsis genes were affected by Flavo98 in at least one timepoint compared with mock treatment (≥log2(1.55) fold change (Fch), both up- and downregulated; adjusted P value ≤0.05; Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1). Upregulated genes included transcripts encoding cell-wall organization and structural proteins (for example, EXT, XTH and EXPA), as well as peroxidases (Fig. 2b). Their expression peaked at 72 h after Flavo98 inoculation.

At earlier timepoints, we observed Flavo98-triggered upregulation of genes involved in ethylene biosynthesis and signalling, including ACS11 and EIL2, together with increased ACS7 expression. Several TFs were also responsive, including two closely related members of the ERF subfamily X (ERF114 and ERF115).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT–PCR) on independent biological replicates confirmed Flavo98-enhanced expression of ERF115/114 (Fig. 2c). Moreover, confocal imaging of transgenic pERF114: NLS-GFP/GUS36 and pERF115: NLS-GFP/GUS32 Arabidopsis lines revealed that Flavo98 inoculation induces ERF115 and ERF114 expression in stele cells (Fig. 2d). To examine whether ERF115 and ERF114 mediate the induction of RH development by Flavo98, we analysed Arabidopsis plants overexpressing a dominant negative version of ERF115 (ERF115-SRDX), or wild-type ERF115 (ERF115-OX) and ERF114 (ERF114-OX). As shown in Fig. 2e–g (Supplementary Fig. 10), primary roots of ERF115-SRDX seedlings displayed significantly fewer and shorter RHs, while ERF115-OX and ERF114-OX exhibited a significant increase in RH development compared with the WT. To analyse whether the observed RH phenotype is independent of agar-based systems, ERF115-OX plants were grown in a two-dimensional (2D) soil-based environment (rhizotron). The roots developing in the rhizotrons exhibited a visibly higher number of RHs, which supports ERF115’s role in RH development under natural conditions (Fig. 2h). Importantly, soil-grown plants overexpressing ERFs, despite flowering earlier, exhibited seed yield comparable to WT, suggesting that enhanced RH development did not compromise reproductive performance under well-watered growth conditions (Supplementary Fig. 11).

To assess the importance of ERFs for RH development during drought stress, we characterized the phenotypes of ERF transgenic lines in response to HA and PEG treatments. Under both conditions, ERF115-OX and ERF114-OX plants produced significantly more and longer RHs than WT, similarly to the response observed in Flavo98-inoculated plants (Figs. 2i and 3d,e and Supplementary Fig. 12).

Both qRT–PCR analysis of WT plants exposed to PEG and confocal imaging of pERF114:NLS-GFP/GUS and pERF115:NLS-GFP/GUS lines grown on PEG-containing medium demonstrated that neither of the two ERFs is induced by PEG in roots (Fig. 3f,g and Supplementary Fig. 13a). Similarly, ERF115/ERF114 in roots remained unaffected under various treatments aimed at reducing water potential, including HA treatment43. By contrast, Flavo98 induced the expression of ERF115/ERF114 (Fig. 3f,g), which underscores the importance of the interaction of Flavo98 with plant roots for enhancing the expression of ERFs, thereby counteracting the negative impact of drought on RH growth.

To determine whether Flavo98 requires ERF115/114 to enhance RH development, we examined RH number and length in WT, ERF115-SRDX, erf115 single and erf115 erf114 double mutants after Flavo98 inoculation (Fig. 3a,b). In addition, we tested the effect of Flavo98 on a quadruple mutant in which the closely related ERFs, ERF113 and ERF109, are also mutated (Supplementary Fig. 14a–c). The induction of RH formation and growth was reduced in the erf115 and erf115 erf114 mutants and further impaired in the quadruple mutant compared with WT. Flavo98 inoculation had no significant effect on the ERF115-SRDX seedlings (Fig. 3a,b and Supplementary Fig. 14a–c). Likewise, Flavo98-induced RH formation and growth were absent in ERF115-SRDX seedlings when subjected to drought (PEG or HA) treatments (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 15). In addition, Flavo98-inoculated ERF115-SRDX seedlings did not exhibit improved drought tolerance on HA compared with the mock-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. 15). These findings indicate that ERF115 and ERF114 participate in the Flavo98-induced RH development under control and drought conditions.

ERF115/114 contribute to drought tolerance

To test whether ERF114 and ERF115 are involved in mediating drought tolerance, we investigated the stress response of ERF transgenic Arabidopsis lines under various conditions. In response to PEG treatment, ERF115-OX and ERF114-OX plants showed enhanced tolerance to drought with larger and greener shoots than those of WT and ERF115-SRDX seedlings (Fig. 3d). In the absence of PEG treatment, ERF transgenic and WT seedlings exhibited similar growth, except for ERF115-SRDX plants, which remained smaller (Supplementary Fig. 14d). We also tested the response of soil-grown ERF115-OX and WT plants to PEG treatment using a rhizotron. ERF115-OX displayed improved growth, with the development of more RHs than WT (Supplementary Fig. 14e).

On withholding water, ERF115-SRDX and WT plants showed severe drought symptoms. By contrast, ERF115 and ERF114 overexpressor lines were less affected (Fig. 3h,i and Supplementary Fig. 14f). Leaves of ERF overexpressors showed a significantly higher water content under drought (Fig. 3i), suggesting that elevated expression of ERF114 and ERF115 alleviates the experienced level of drought stress. To examine the relevance of ERF overexpression in roots for drought tolerance, we grafted WT scions (shoots) onto ERF115-OX rootstocks (referred to as WT/ERF115-OX), using WT/WT grafts as controls. A total of 21 days after grafting, plants were subjected to drought. WT/ERF115-OX plants showed improved drought tolerance, evidenced by reduced wilting and a higher relative water content (RWC) in rosettes compared with WT/WT grafts (Supplementary Fig. 16). This result suggests that overexpression of ERF115 in roots contributes to drought tolerance.

Accordingly, 35S:EIN3-GFP/ein3eil1 plants showed elevated GFP fluorescence, indicating EIN3 stabilization46–48 upon Flavo98 inoculation (Fig. 4b). To investigate the role of ethylene biosynthesis in Flavo98-induced RH development, we tested the acs heptuple mutant50 and treated wild-type plants with the chemical ethylene biosynthesis inhibitor aminoethoxyvinylglycine (AVG)51. As shown in Fig. 4c–e, both the acs heptuple mutant and AVG-treated plants exhibited significantly reduced responses to Flavo98 inoculation, with the effect being more pronounced in the AVG-treated plants (Supplementary Fig. 19, after 7 days). To explore the effect of Flavo98 on ethylene signalling, we characterized the phenotype of EIN2 (a key component in ethylene signalling)-deficient plants and those treated with silver nitrate, which inhibits ethylene responses52. The ein2 mutant prevented Flavo98 from inducing RH formation and growth (Fig. 4c–e). Treatment with silver nitrate also inhibited Flavo98-induced RH development (Fig. 4f,g). These results show the importance of ethylene biosynthesis and signalling in enabling Flavo98 to promote RH formation and growth.

Next, we assessed the role of ethylene in Flavo98-induced ERF115/ERF114 expression. Treatment with 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), the immediate precursor in ethylene biosynthesis, induced both genes with ERF115 being activated at earlier timepoints (Supplementary Fig. 18b). Notably, the transcriptional upregulation of both ERFs by Flavo98 was abolished in the presence of AVG and in the ein2 mutant (Fig. 4h,i and Supplementary Fig. 18c), suggesting that ethylene is important for the induction of ERF115 and ERF114 by Flavo98.

Finally, we analysed the role of EIN3/EIL1, the primary TFs downstream of EIN253,54, in Flavo98-induced RH development. Previously, ein3eil1 mutants were shown to exhibit reduced RH density and growth13. We observed that the RH-promoting effect of Flavo98 was reduced in ein3eil1 compared with WT (Supplementary Fig. 18d,e). This reduction was less pronounced than in ein2, suggesting that additional TFs may be involved. One such candidate could be EIL2, which is transcriptionally induced by Flavo98 (Supplementary Fig. 18a and Supplementary Table 1).

Importantly, we also identified new ERF-activated genes (Supplementary Table 2), including CEP5, which encodes a secreted, 15-amino-acid post-translationally modified peptide recently described to promote drought and osmotic stress tolerance in plants40. The CEP5 promoter contains two ERF binding motifs within 0.9 kb upstream of the translation start site. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)–qPCR showed ERF115–GFP enrichment at two predicted CEP5 promoter regions in planta (Fig. 5b). This is in accordance with enhanced CEP5 expression in roots after Flavo98 inoculation, as revealed by qRT–PCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 22a). In addition, examining transgenic plants expressing the GFP-GUS reporter under control of the CEP5 promoter (pCEP5:NLS-GFP-GUS) showed that Flavo98 increases CEP5 expression in root stele cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 22b). CEP5 promoter-driven reporter activity was absent in the ERF115-SRDX background, further supporting the notion that the CEP5 promoter is regulated by ERFs (Supplementary Fig. 20b).

To understand the role of CEP5 in RH development, we analysed transgenic plants with altered CEP5 expression. Consistent with previous findings, CEP5 overexpression inhibited primary root growth and early LR initiation39 (Supplementary Fig. 23a). In addition, we found that overexpression of CEP5 (CEP5-OX plants) significantly increases RH development (Fig. 5d–f). These results led us to test the effect of the synthetic CEP5 peptide. Exogenous application of CEP5Hyp similarly altered root architecture. Importantly, CEP5Hyp also induced RH formation in both Arabidopsis and wheat seedlings (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 23b–g). The increased RH growth triggered by CEP5 was maintained during drought, resembling the effects of Flavo98 inoculation and of ERF115/ERF114 overexpression (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 23h–j). Accordingly, RH development was reduced in CEP5 knockout plants (cep5-c1)56 (Fig. 5d–f). In addition, Flavo98 treatment led to partial recovery in both the cep5-c1 mutant and a quadruple mutant affecting other CEP family members (the cep5/6/7/8-c), indicating that CEP5 acts downstream and is required for the response (Supplementary Fig. 24). Consistent with this, exogenous application of the CEP5 peptide partially restored the impaired RH development of AVG-treated WT seedlings (Supplementary Fig. 25) and alleviated the RH phenotype of ERF115-SRDX plants (Supplementary Fig. 26).

Discussion

Plant development is highly adaptive in response to environmental conditions. RH development is a prominent example of this plasticity in response to variations in nutrient and water availability and other environmental cues21,23,57,58. While the inhibition of RH growth during drought may be interpreted as an energy-saving strategy, emerging evidence highlights the importance of increased RH number and elongation for enhancing drought tolerance20,21,23,42. Thus, the energy cost of RH development may be offset by the benefit of improved water and nutrient uptake, making it a beneficial strategy for survival under drought. Identifying molecular mechanisms and genes regulating RH development under drought conditions represents an important step towards developing crops with improved performance in water-limiting environments.

Here, we found that the root endophyte Flavobacterium sp. 98 (Flavo98) enhances drought resilience in multiple plant species, including Arabidopsis and wheat. Flavo98 alters RSA, notably by promoting RH formation and growth under both non-stress and drought conditions. This effect appears to be bacterium-species specific, as two phylogenetically related Flavobacterium strains, isolated from the rhizosphere of Carex arenaria59, did not induce the same changes in the plant phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 27).

By elucidating its mechanism of action in Arabidopsis, we uncovered a previously uncharacterized pathway for RH development that operates under both well-watered and drought conditions. This pathway functions downstream of ethylene signalling and involves activation of the TFs ERF115 and ERF114, which induce expression of the CEP5 gene encoding a hormone peptide39,40.

We showed that ERF115 and ERF114 are required for Flavo98-mediated promotion of RH formation and growth and contribute to plant performance under drought conditions (Figs. 2 and 3 and Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). The functions of ERF114 and ERF115 are partially redundant with other members of the ERF family, including ERF109 and ERF113. Consistent with this, the ability of Flavo98 to promote RH development and improve plant performance under low water potential was strongly impaired by overexpressing ERF115-SRDX, indicating the importance of these ERFs in mediating the microbial effect (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15).

Importantly, ERF115 and ERF114 are not induced on PEG-simulated drought (Fig. 3f,g and Supplementary Fig. 13a) and they do not appear as drought-responsive genes in publicly available databases43 (Supplementary Fig. 13b). This observation highlights the central role of Flavo98 in triggering cellular mechanisms that are not intrinsically activated in the plant when exposed to water limitation in the absence of the bacterium. It also emphasizes the importance of studying microorganism–plant interactions to reveal otherwise hidden mechanisms of stress adaptation in plants.

Previous studies detected ERF115 and ERF114 expression in root stele cells35,36. We observed similar, albeit more pronounced, expression patterns following Flavo98 inoculation (Fig. 2d), supporting a non-cell-autonomous model for the regulation of RH formation and growth by the two ERFs. We further analysed the signalling pathway downstream of ERF115/ERF114 and identified CEP5, which is activated by both TFs, as a likely direct target gene. By a combined analysis of CEP5 transgenic lines and the application of chemically synthesized CEP5 peptide (CEP5Hyp), we discovered a function of CEP5 in stimulating RH development (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 23). Furthermore, we demonstrated the capacity of CEP5 to partially restore the impaired RH development of ERF115-SRDX plants, confirming its function downstream of the ERF TFs (Supplementary Fig. 26). Previously, CEP5 was shown to play a role in drought stress by counteracting auxin effects through stabilizing AUX/IAA transcriptional repressors40. This newly identified RH development role of CEP5 further contributes to enhanced drought tolerance. Inoculation with Flavo98 enhanced the expression of CEP5 in stele cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 22), raising the question of how Flavo98-induced CEP5 signalling is perceived by root epidermal cells. This could involve long-distance signals from the stele to the epidermis or the movement of CEP5, either as a transcript or a peptide. How this signalling is relayed to the epidermis remains an intriguing question for future studies.

Ethylene is an important plant hormone that regulates RH development via a synergistic physical interaction between EIN3/EIL1 and RHD613. Elevated ethylene concentration induces ectopic RH formation, an effect not observed with auxin60. We show that Flavo98 induces the formation of ectopic RHs (Supplementary Fig. 5d–f) and provide multiple lines of evidence that support the importance of enhanced ethylene biosynthesis and signalling for Flavo98’s transcriptional induction of ERF115 and ERF114 and enhanced RH development. Our observations thus reveal an additional mechanism through which ethylene contributes to RH development (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19).

Flavo98’s reliance on ethylene biosynthesis in plants distinguishes it from Enterobacter sp. SA187, which enhances plant stress tolerance by producing 2-keto-4-methylthiobutyric acid and subsequently induces ethylene signalling45.

On the basis of our data, we propose a working model suggesting a dual role of Flavo98 in promoting RH development and growth (Fig. 5i). Flavo98 elevates ethylene levels in roots and stabilizes EIN3 (Fig. 4a,b). Although Flavo98 does not trigger RHD6 expression or protein accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 28), its effect on EIN3 probably enhances EIN3–RHD6 complex formation and activity, thus promoting the intrinsic pathway for RH development. This is supported by the induction of several RH initiation genes regulated by EIN3/EIL1 and RHD6/RSL1, as well as those targeted by RSL4 in response to Flavo98 (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, Flavo98 activates ERF115 and ERF114 to induce CEP5 and possibly other targets for enhancing RH formation and growth under non-stress and sustaining RH growth under drought conditions.

The activation of ERF115 and ERF114 by Flavo98 is mediated by EIN2 signalling, involving EIN3/EIL1 and possibly other TFs downstream of EIN2. In addition, we observed elevated expression of ERF114 in ERF115-overexpressing plants, suggesting a regulatory connection between the two TFs (Supplementary Fig. 29).

Consistent with the well-established dependence of ethylene-induced RH formation on RHD6/RSL1 and RSL4/RSL213, neither the inoculation with Flavo98 nor the treatment with synthetic CEP5Hyp alleviated the hairless phenotype of rhd6rsl1 and rsl4rsl2 mutants61 (Supplementary Fig. 30). These findings highlight the critical interplay between the Flavo98–ERFs–CEP5 signalling cascade and the intrinsic RH developmental pathway in modulating RH formation and growth in response to environmental cues. The precise interaction between ERF115/ERF114–CEP5 and RHD6/RSL1–RSL4/RSL2 remains to be elucidated. A recent study indicated a direct interaction between ERF114 and RHD661. However, as Flavo98-induced ERF115/ERF114 expression is restricted to vascular tissues, and RHD6 is predominantly expressed in root epidermal cells62, a physical interaction between the ERFs and RHD6 seems unlikely. In addition, the overlap between the putative target genes of ERF115/ERF114 and the RH initiation genes regulated by EIN3/EIL1 and RHD6/RSL1 as well as the RSL4-dependent genes is limited to only a few targets13,63,64 (Supplementary Fig. 31a,b and Supplementary Table 4). There is also no reported evidence suggesting that the ERFs and CEP5 act downstream of the EIN3/EIL1, RHD6/RSL1 or RSL4/RSL2 regulatory pathways. Vice versa, neither RHD6 nor RSL4 is transcriptionally activated by CEP5, ERF115 or ERF114 (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figs. 31c and 32). This indicates that the interaction among these regulatory components does not involve common or mutual transcriptional regulation but rather a more complex interplay in their downstream networks.

Although additional factors probably contribute to Flavo98-mediated plant responses, RHs make a measurable contribution to plant performance under drought. In both agar- and soil-based drought assays, the positive effect of Flavo98 on plant growth and leaf water content was more pronounced in WT plants than in the RH-deficient rhd6 rsl1 mutant. Although modest, this difference was consistent across independent experiments, supporting a contribution of RHs to Flavo98-associated drought responses in conjunction with other Flavo98-regulated traits (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34). Flavo98 also triggers LR formation, which further enhances drought tolerance. This effect of Flavo98 on LR induction may be mediated through the ethylene response pathway, similar to microbiota-driven effects reported previously65, and may involve ERFs that promote LR formation39 but act independently of CEPs that inhibit LR initiation56,66. Future studies should investigate Flavo98-driven mechanisms controlling LR development, as well as other traits through which Flavo98 collectively confers drought tolerance.

In both natural and agricultural environments, microorganisms coexist with a range of other organisms, including microorganisms (for example, bacteria, fungi and algae), soil fauna (for example, nematodes and microarthropods) and neighbouring plants. A key direction for future research will be to analyse how Flavo98 influences plant growth within more complex communities and in varying soil types and environmental conditions. Addressing this question will require a comprehensive, multiscale experimental approach67,68.

Utilizing root-endophytic Flavo98, we uncovered a regulatory module involving ERF115/114 and CEP5 peptide that promotes RH development and contributes to enhanced plant performance during drought conditions. The nature of the signals produced by Flavo98 that control these processes is elusive at present. Uncovering the genes and biochemical mechanisms involved will require a comprehensive experimental approach, including genome-wide mutant screening, gene-specific mutagenesis, heterologous expression of identified genes and metabolomics.

Given Flavo98’s consistent impact on multiple plant species and the conservation of ERF and CEP across a wide range of plants, fine-tuning the ERF–CEP module could be a promising target for breeding drought-tolerant plants, with significant potential for improving global food security amid climate change.

Plant materials and growth conditions

Moneymaker) were used as WT material. Seeds of the genome-sequenced wheat variety ‘Chinese Spring’ were obtained from N. Stein, IPK Gatersleben, Germany. Seeds of rapeseed and camelina were obtained from Hofman Agr. Prod (http://hofmanap.nl). Tomato seeds were from Buzzy Seeds (https://buzzyseeds.com/). Seeds of Arabidopsis Col-0 (NASC N1092) and ein2-5 (NASC 8844) were obtained from the European Arabidopsis Stock Centre (http://arabidopsis.info/). Seeds of rsl4rsl2, rhd6rsl1 and ein3eil113, 35S:ERF115-SRDX (ERF115-SRDX), 35S:ERF114 (ERF114-OX), 35S:ERF115 (ERF115-OX), pERF115:NLS-GFP/GUS, pERF114:NLS-GFP/GUS, erf115, erf115erf114, erf115erf114erf11336,45,69, 35S:EIN3-GFP/ein3eil170, acs heptuple and pEBF2:GUS45, pCEP5:NLS-GFP-GUS, 35S:CEP5 (CEP5-OX), pDR5:GFP and pDR5:GUS71, pRHD6:RHD6-GFP and pRSL4:RSL4-GFP55 and cep5-c156 were produced previously. To generate ERF115-SRDX/pCEP5:NLS-GFP-GUS, ERF115-SRDX plants were used as the pollen donor to pollinate pCEP5:NLS-GFP-GUS recipient plants.

The 35S:ERF115-GFP translational fusion line was generated by GATEWAY cloning. To this end, the ERF115 genomic locus without a stop codon was inserted into vector pDONR221 (Invitrogen) via a Gateway BP recombination reaction and subsequently recombined into the pFAST-R05 vector72 via an LR reaction. The construct was transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain C58C1RifR, harbouring plasmid pMP90. Agrobacterial strains were used to generate stably transformed Arabidopsis lines using the floral dip method73. Transgenic lines were selected on kanamycin (40 mg l−1) or identified by red seed fluorescence using a binocular.

The cep5/6/7/8-c quadruple mutant was generated by CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing56,74,75. Two guide RNAs targeted the CEP7 and CEP8 genomic sequences 5′-CCCCATAAATAGCCATCTAGAG-3′ and 5′-CCGGCCAACAACTCCGGGAAAC-3′ (protospacer adjacent motif sites underlined). We subsequently knocked out CEP5 and CEP6 genes in the cep7 cep8 double mutant by targeting CEP5 sites 5′-TAGGGTTCAATTGTGTCCATGG-3′ and 5′-CTGGCCACAGCCCTGGCATTGG-3′ and CEP6 sites 5′-CCAAAGTCGGGTAATCTAGACC-3′ and 5′-GCCTAATGCTTAATTAGAAAGG-3′ (protospacer adjacent motif sites underlined). Primers used for generating CRISPR–Cas9 constructs are presented in Supplementary Table 5.

For the generation of the erf114/erf115/erf113/erf109 quadruple mutant, gRNA coding sequences were inserted into gRNA entry vectors under the control of the U6 promoter using BbsI-HF in a Golden Gate reaction. By means of a Golden Gate reaction using BsaI, the gRNAs were inserted into the RPS5A::BP-Cas9-BP destination vector76. Cloning reactions were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α. Colonies were verified by whole-plasmid sequencing (Eurofins Scientific). Sequence-verified vectors were transformed into A. tumefaciens C58C1 by electroporation and used for stable transformation of A. thaliana via floral dip. In the T2 generation, Cas9-free plants were genotyped by sequencing, and subsequent T3 seeds were used for phenotypic investigation. Primers used for generating CRISPR–Cas9 constructs are presented in Supplementary Table 5.

Arabidopsis seeds were surface sterilized in 40% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min and rinsed three times with sterile tap water. Subsequently, seeds were sown on solid half-strength Murashige–Skoog (MS) medium (1/2 MS)77 containing 1.2% Daishin agar (Duchefa) or AB212298 agar (https://abcr.com), 1% sucrose and 0.5 g MES per litre. Seedlings were grown vertically in square or round plates under controlled conditions. Unless otherwise indicated, plants were grown under long-day conditions at the Institute of Biology Leiden, Leiden University (16 h light at 70–100 µmol m−2 s−1–8 h dark; 21 °C day and 18 °C night temperature; and 65% relative humidity). Seedlings grown under a 12-h photoperiod were cultivated at the Freie Universität Berlin, Institute of Biology, Biochemistry of Plant-Microbe Interactions (12 h light at 150–170 µmol m−2 s−1–12 h dark; 21 °C day and 18 °C night temperature; and 80% relative humidity).

For Arabidopsis grown in soil, plants were cultivated in a mixture of 80% soil and 20% sand and grown in a controlled growth chamber under standard conditions, unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends. Standard growth chamber conditions were as follows: plants were exposed to either a 12-h photoperiod, short-day conditions (8 h light and 16 h dark) or long-day conditions (16 h light and 8 h dark), with light intensity set to 130–140 µmol m−2 s−1, a day/night temperature of 21 °C and 60–65% relative humidity.

For crops grown in sand, plants were cultured in a growth chamber under controlled conditions at the Institute of Biology Leiden under a 16-h-light (120 µmol m−2 s−1)–8-h-dark cycle at 24 °C with 60–65% relative humidity. Plants were watered with tap water, and the sand was fertilized weekly with Hoagland solution.

Bacterial inoculation

Flavo98 bacteria were plated on Luria Broth (LB) medium and grown overnight at 28 °C. Bacteria were then resuspended in 10 mM MgSO4 to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1. For Arabidopsis plants grown on 1/2 MS plates, root tips were inoculated once with 2 µl Flavo98 or dead Flavo98 (heated at 100 °C for 10 min) as a control. Treatment of root tips with a mock solution (10 mM MgSO4) was used as a control (mock). For Arabidopsis grown in γ-irradiated sandy soil, the soil was collected from an agricultural sugar beet field close to the town of Hoeven, the Netherlands (51° 35′ 10″ N, 4° 34″ 44′ E)79. At a 7-day interval, 17-day-old seedlings received two bacterial inoculations or mock solution treatments. Inoculation was performed by pipetting 1 ml bacterial suspension (OD600 of 1) or mock solution onto the soil surface, with the pipette tip positioned near the expected root zone.

For wheat grown on MS plates, root tips were inoculated once with 2 µl Flavo98 or dead Flavo98 (heated at 100 °C for 10 min) at an OD600 of 1 and grown vertically. Treatment of root tips with a mock solution (10 mM MgSO4) was used as a control (mock). For wheat, brassica and camelina grown in sand, 10-day-old seedlings were inoculated with 10 ml bacterial suspension at an OD600 of 1 or mock solution (10 mM MgSO4). The bacterial suspension and mock solution were carefully and slowly added to the sand surface surrounding the plant root. For tomato, cultivar ‘Moneymaker’, seeds were germinated on MS medium for 14 days and then transferred to sand. At the time of transplanting, plants were inoculated by dipping the roots into either a Flavo98 suspension (OD600 of 1) or mock solution.

Quantification of root colonization and confocal microscopy of Flavo98–GFP

We grew 7-day-old Col-0 seedlings vertically on 1/2 MS medium and transferred them to fresh 1/2 MS medium (with or without 5% PEG 8000); root tips were treated with a mock solution or inoculated with rifampicin-resistant (RifR) Flavo9831 at an OD600 of 1 and allowed to grow vertically for another 2 weeks. Inoculated roots were then harvested and washed gently to remove non-attached bacteria. Afterwards, samples were ground by mortar and pestle in 1 ml 10 mM MgSO4 and vortexed for 5 min. The collected root homogenate was diluted tenfold and plated on a rifampicin-containing LB medium. CFUs were counted after 2 days of incubation at 28 °C. The experiment was performed in three biological replicates, with eight roots per sample.

To visualize bacterial colonization of roots, Flavo98 labelled with GFP (Flavo98–GFP) was generated by transferring the PAS43 plasmid (carrying the GFP coding sequence)83 to Flavo98 by electroporation. Root tips of 7-day-old seedlings grown vertically on 1/2 MS agar (with 1% sugar) were inoculated with Flavo98–GFP at an OD600 of 1 or treated with mock solution (10 mM MgSO4). Then, 24 h later, colonization of Arabidopsis roots with Flavo98–GFP was visualized and imaged using confocal airy scan microscopy (Zeiss).

Root phenotyping

For all root morphology experiments, seeds were germinated on 1/2 MS medium containing 1.2% Daishin agar (Duchefa) or AB212298 agar (https://abcr.com), 1% sucrose and 0.5 g MES per litre. After the specified period, seedlings were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS medium with and without treatments or inoculations and grown vertically until phenotyping. Photographs of the plates were captured using a Nikon camera; all visible LRs were counted to determine the total LR number. To determine RH number and length, approximately 10 mm above the primary root tip was imaged using a Nikon stereomicroscope or ZEISS SteREO Discovery V8, and the RH number was quantified using ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/) within a 5–7-mm segment above the primary root tip. RH density was determined by counting the number of RHs within a defined root segment (~7 mm). The exact length of each segment was measured using ImageJ, and RH density was calculated as the number of RHs per millimetre of root length. To determine RH length, between 10 and 30 RHs per root were typically measured, unless a genotype or condition resulted in fewer RHs, in which case the information is provided in the figure legends. Note that only roots growing along the surface of the medium were included in the analysis. Roots that were detached from the surface (that is, growing into the medium or away from it) were excluded.

Chemical treatments

A total of 7 days after the initial germination of seeds on 1/2 MS medium, seedlings were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 1 µM AVG (Sigma-Aldrich, 32999), 1 µM AgNO3 (Sigma-Aldrich, 204390), 1 µM ACC (Sigma-Aldrich, A3903) or 10 µM CEP5Hyp.

Synthesis of CEP5 peptide

CEP5Hyp peptide was obtained by solid-phase peptide synthesis as detailed in the Supplementary Methods. Rink amide was used to produce the peptide as the C-terminal amide (CEP5-NH2). Following acid-mediated global deprotection and cleavage from the resin, the crude peptides were submitted to preparative reverse-phase HPLC to obtain pure compounds (>95% by HPLC) with the desired mass. Both versions of CEP5s (C-terminal amide and acid) resulted in enhanced RH formation, with the amide version resulting in a slightly stronger impact. Thus, all experiments were based on the amide version. The sequence of the synthetic variant of mature 15-amino-acid CEP5 with modified proline hydroxylation on P4, P7 and P11 (CEP5Hyp) is as follows: (DFR{HYP}TT{HYP}GHS{HYP}GIGH)40.

Drought assays

Drought treatment in soil or sand was applied by withholding water. Osmotic stress was induced by growing seedlings in the presence of PEG 8000 (Sigma-Aldrich, P2139). To this end, 800 ml of 1/2 MS medium was supplemented with 200 ml filter-sterilized PEG 8000 solution in water, resulting in a final volume of 1 l. We transferred 7-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings to medium with or without PEG 8000 and grew them vertically. Both PEG medium and control MS medium contained 15 g agar per litre.

The HA assay was performed using a slightly modified version of the protocol of Gonzalez et al.43. Arabidopsis seedlings were first cultivated for 7 days on square vertical plates (12 cm × 12 cm) containing 1/2 MS medium (2.2 g MS powder, 10 g sucrose, 12 g agar and 0.5 g MES per litre, adjusted to pH 5.8 with KOH). After this initial growth period, seedlings were transferred to either 1/2 MS medium (control) or HA medium composed of 5.8 g MS powder, 26.6 g sucrose, 26 g agar and 1.33 g MES per litre, pH 5.8. Each square plate was prepared with 75 ml of either 1/2 MS or 2.7× HA medium. On transfer, the root tips of 7-day-old seedlings were treated with either 10 mM MgSO4 or Flavo98.

Rhizotron assay

Substrate soil and yellow river sand were sieved using a 4-mm automatic siever (FleXiever). Sieved soil and sand were then mixed in a 1:1 ratio on the basis of weight. Rhizotrons were prepared as described by Kerstens et al.84. After filling, rhizotrons were sealed with parafilm on top to prevent drying of the soil and subsequently soaked in water overnight.

Seeds of Col-0 (WT) and ERF115-OX were stratified in water at 4 °C in the dark for at least 48 h. Once this was done, three seeds were sown directly on the soil and parafilm was placed over the seeds to create a humid environment for germination. Parafilm was sparsely perforated to allow air exchange. Rhizotrons with sown seeds were placed in a climate cell at 20 °C and 65% relative humidity, with a 16-h–8-h day–night cycle. After 5 days, parafilm was loosened to allow seedlings to acclimate to the climate cell conditions. A total of 7 days after sowing, parafilm was removed from around the seedlings but remained over the rest of the soil to prevent rapid soil drying. Excess seedlings were removed until only one seedling remained per rhizotron.

Rhizotron imaging

Rhizotrons were imaged using an Epson Expression 11000 XL document scanner. Rhizotrons were placed on the scanner and scanned at 1,200 dots per inch after focusing the scanning depth accordingly. Close-up images were taken with a Nikon D3100 camera using a Tokina Macro 100 f2.8D lens on a top-down stand for camera stability. Rhizotrons were scanned 14 days after sowing.

Grafting

We grafted 6-day-old seedlings by making a horizontal incision with a sterile razor blade at the hypocotyl. The scion was joined with the corresponding root stock, and plants were grown under long-day conditions (16 h light, 8 h dark). Every 3–4 days, adventitious roots were trimmed to ensure successful grafting. Then, 12 days after grafting, the plants were evaluated for successful grafting by inspecting the root–shoot junction and assessing the RH phenotype. Only successful grafts were selected for the subsequent drought experiments and transferred to soil and grown under short-day (8 h light, 16 h dark) conditions to promote vegetative growth. Plants were subjected to water deprivation after 9–10 days in soil.

Leaf RWC and chlorophyll measurement

For the measurement of RWC in each genotype, leaves were harvested and initially weighed to determine fresh weight (FW) and subsequently rehydrated by placing them in a Petri dish with water at room temperature for 4 h (in darkness). After rehydration, the leaves were reweighed to determine their turgid weight (TW). Samples were subjected to drying at 65 °C for 24 h, after which their dry weight (DW) was measured. RWC was calculated according to the following formula: RWC (%) = ((FW − DW)/(TW − DW)) × 100.

Leaf chlorophyll was extracted using 80% acetone overnight. The absorbance of the extract was measured with a spectrophotometer at 663 and 646 nm, and total chlorophyll was calculated as milligram chlorophyll per gram FW85.

RNA-seq and data analysis

Root samples were collected from WT (Col-0) plants treated with mock solution or inoculated with Flavo98. Samples were harvested at various timepoints after inoculation (12 h, 48 h, 72 h and 6 days) and used for RNA extraction and sequencing. In addition, root samples from 4-day-old ERF115-OX, ERF114-OX, WT and ERF115-SRDX plants were harvested for RNA extraction and sequencing. Each genotype and/or treatment was represented by three biological replicates for total RNA extraction. Each replicate consisted of independently harvested root tissue, with each sample representing a pool of roots from more than 50 individual plants. RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. High-quality RNA was used for library preparation, and the library was validated on the Agilent Technologies 2100 bioanalyzer. The RNA was sequenced on a DNBseq-G400 machine at BGI Genomics (http://www.bgi.com/).

Initial processing of RNA sequencing reads involved quality checks and adapter trimming using trimmomatic (v0.39)86 paired-end mode with the arguments HEADCROP:11, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36. Trimmed reads were aligned to the reference A. thaliana genome (GCF_000001735) from the RefSeq database81 using hisat2 (v2.2.1)87. Gene counts were extracted using featureCounts88 from subread (v2.0.1) with the following arguments: -t transcript -g gene_id. A raw gene counts table was imported into R programming language (v4.3.1; https://www.r-project.org/), and DEseq2 (v1.36.0)89 was used for normalization and statistical analysis via the ‘apeglm’ model.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis and gene expression analysis by qRT–PCR

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis and qRT–PCR were performed as described previously90. PCR reactions were run on a CFX-Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad), and amplification products were visualized using SYBR Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative PCR data were analysed using the 2−ΔΔCt method91. The transcript level of each gene was normalized to that of the ACTIN2 (AT3G18780) reference gene. Primers were designed by QuantPrime (www.quantprime.de). Primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table 5.

ChIP assay

Seedlings of 3-week-old 35S:ERF115-GFP plants were used for ChIP. The ChIP experiment was conducted as described92. The chromatin extracts were isolated, and anti-GFP antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec) were used to immunoprecipitate protein–DNA complexes. After reversion of the cross-linking, DNA was purified with QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) and analysed by qPCR. Col-0 (WT) plants served as negative controls. Primers used to amplify CEP5 promoter regions harbouring ERF115 binding sites are presented in Supplementary Table 5. Primers that annealed to the ACTIN7 (AT5G09810) gene, which lacks an ERF binding site, were used as a negative control.

Histochemical assays

5-Bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-beta-d-glucuronide (X-gluc) was obtained from Thermo Fischer Scientific (R0851). β-Glucuronidase (GUS) staining at 37 °C was performed as described93 for a specific duration: 10 min for pEBF2:GUS, 2 h for pDR5:GUS and 1 h for pCEP5: NLS-GFP/GUS, followed by rehydration steps by incubating in a graded 75% to 25% ethanol series. GUS-stained tissue was mounted on a microscope slide with 50% glycerol and photographed using a Leica MZ12 microscope equipped with a Leica DC500 camera.

PI staining

Vertically grown roots were stained in 10 μg ml−1 propidium iodide (PI) (Invitrogen, P1304MP) for up to 5 min and mounted on a slide with a drop of water.

Confocal microscopy

A Zeiss Airyscan 900 confocal microscope or a Nikon AX confocal microscope was used to capture GFP and PI fluorescence. The excitation and detection parameters were as follows: PI, excitation: 560 nm, detection: 600–660 nm; GFP, excitation: 488 nm, detection: 500–550 nm. The confocal images were subsequently analysed and processed using Open Microscopy Environment Remote Objects (OMERO; https://www.openmicroscopy.org/omero/). PI staining is shown in red or magenta in the figures. DR5 promoter-driven GFP fluorescence signal was determined as reported on GitHub (https://github.com/mfitzp/theolb/blob/master/imaging/measuring-cell-fluorescence-using-imagej.rst). ImageJ software was used to quantify GFP signal intensity in the epidermis of roots (elongation and differentiation zones) of the pRHD6:RHD6-GFP and pRSL4:RSL4-GFP lines. The integrated density of each nucleus from at least eight different roots was measured; this parameter measures the total amount of fluorescence coming from each nucleus. The mean of the integrated density ± s.d. of each nucleus is shown.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 11.0.0 (GraphPad Software). Differences between groups were assessed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by multiple comparisons tests (Tukey’s or Šídák’s), as indicated in the figure legend. Where indicated, pairwise comparisons were performed using Welch’s t-tests. Data are presented as scatter dot plots (mean ± s.d.) or box-and-whisker plots (minimum to maximum with all points). Box plots show the median (centre line) and the interquartile range (box limits represent the 25th and 75th percentiles). Whiskers extend to the minimum and maximum values, and all individual data points are shown.
