Underdiagnosed brain abscess and meningitis caused by Parvimonas micra in an immunocompetent host: an idiopathic case
Authors: Laura Andrés-Eslava, Violeta Artal-Muñoz, Remedios Guna-Serrano, Concepción Gimeno-Cardona
Published: 2026

Sir,

Parvimonas micra is an obligate anaerobic, gram-positive coccus, that resides as a commensal in the oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract [1]. While typically associated with polymicrobial oral infections, systemic dissemination is rare, especially in immunocompetent individuals. Although it has been identified in intra-abdominal and epidural abscesses, brain involvement is exceptional [2–4]. Most reported brain abscesses involve dental procedures or periodontitis [4–7]. We describe a rare, idiopathic case of P. micra brain abscess and meningitis in a healthy host in whom no primary focus was identified after periodontal and intra-abdominal evaluation.

A 36-year-old male, originally from the Congo, who has lived in Spain for 20 years, presented to the emergency department with a two-month history of persistent hemicranial headache radiating to the same hemicranium, associated with tearing and nausea. Treatment with corticosteroids showed no improvement, and previous use of triptans was ineffective. On the last visit to the emergency department, right palpebral drooping was noted, associated with binocular double vision, leading to his hospitalization in the neurology department.

A complete blood count, biochemistry, and coagulation study were performed without alterations, and no assessable findings of pathological significance were found on brain computed tomography (CT) (Figure 1–A). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was extracted and sent to the Microbiology department, where bacterial aerobic culture was negative. The patient presented a fever spike on the second day of admission, and four blood cultures (aerobic and anaerobic) were taken and incubated in BD BACTEC™ FX. After an average incubation period of 3 days, both anaerobic blood culture bottles turned positive, yielding growth of Gram-positive cocci identified as P. micra. This finding was supported by a blood profile for bacteraemia, showing leukocytosis (20 109/L, neutrophils 93.9%), procalcitonin 6.55 ng/mL, and C-reactive protein (CRP) 19.7mg/dL.

Sequential neuroimaging of brain abscess development

On images A and B, brain CT scans were executed on the day of admission and 10 days later, respectively. On image C, a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study performed 20 days after admission, showing hypointense areas in the right cavernous sinus on FLAIR imaging, consistent with a purulent collection approximately 13mm in size with restricted diffusion, suggestive of a brain abscess.

One week later, there was no eye opening or verbal response. A brain CT scan performed on the 10th day of admission suggested the presence of an abscess in the right cavernous sinus. Subsequent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of a brain abscess, approximately 13 mm in size, with an adjacent purulent collection in the suprasellar cistern.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit due to the worsening of his clinical situation. Bacterial meningitis was suspected, and CSF was extracted, showing a turbid appearance and the following biochemical profile: glucose 14 mg/dL, protein 194.9 mg/dL, leukocyte count 3680 cells/μL (93% polymorphonuclear cells). Neurosurgery was consulted, and surgical drainage was considered but deferred because there was no safely accessible encapsulated parenchymal abscess (predominant parasellar/cavernous sinus involvement); an external ventricular drain was placed for acute hydrocephalus. Empirical therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was started; ampicillin was added for Listeria coverage, and given the severity of the condition, liposomal amphotericin B and tuberculostatics were added due to suspected Mucorales and mycobacteria, respectively.

CSF bacterial aerobic and anaerobic cultures were carried out, and P. micra was identified in the anaerobic medium. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the E-test on Brucella™ agar, obtaining the following MIC (mg/L) values; interpretation was carried out according to CLSI criteria for anaerobic bacteria. The isolate was categorized as susceptible (S) to penicillin (0.002), amoxicillin-clavulanate (0.016), piperacillin-tazobactam (0.016), cefotaxime (0.016), cefoxitin (0.016), imipenem (0.003), clindamycin (0.38), moxifloxacin (0.125), metronidazole (0.064), and tetracycline (0.023). Antibiotic therapy was modified to meropenem, amikacin, and linezolid. Although meropenem provides adequate coverage for P. micra, amikacin and linezolid were temporarily added to broaden empirical coverage in the setting of critical illness and concern for concomitant or nosocomial pathogens, while awaiting repeat cultures and clinical stabilization. Despite adequate treatment, the patient suffered a clinical decline caused by the brain abscess, which triggered an infectious vasculitis associated with multiple strokes that led to the patient’s death one month after hospitalisation.

Primary care physicians play a crucial role in the early recognition of nonspecific symptoms, such as persistent headaches, which may be indicative of serious conditions like brain abscesses. In this case, a treatment-resistant headache was a key symptom that preceded the neurological deterioration, highlighting the importance of referring these patients for more detailed studies when symptoms persist despite initial management.

Brain abscesses have an annual incidence of 0.3–1.3 per 100,000 people. In immunocompetent patients, they usually arise from contiguous spread (sinusitis, otitis) or hematogenous dissemination (dental or pulmonary foci) [8–10].

However, in approximately 20% of cases, no source is found. In a systematic literature review, only 15 cases of brain abscess caused by P. micra have been reported, most of them in the context of odontogenic infection [3]. We present a rare case in which extensive evaluation—including detailed dental and intra-abdominal assessment—failed to identify a primary focus. As P. micra is a component of the normal oral and gastrointestinal microbiota, transient bacteraemia from an unrecognized mucosal breach is a plausible mechanism. Even without overt dental disease, minor periodontal inflammation or subclinical gastrointestinal mucosal disruption may permit hematogenous dissemination, potentially accounting for the parasellar involvement observed in this case.

In this case, diagnosis was delayed as insufficient attention was initially given to the persistent headache. The definitive diagnosis was further delayed due to the failure to request anaerobic cultures initially, and this could explain why P. micra was found in blood cultures but not in the first CSF sample obtained. This suggests that CSF cultures for anaerobes should be considered in cases of suspected primary meningitis without a source of infection, in particular when aerobic culture is negative.
