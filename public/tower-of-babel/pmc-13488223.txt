The role of biomarkers across the care continuum in acute heart failure
Authors: Arzu Kalayci, C. Michael Gibson, James L. Januzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute heart failure (AHF) remains a global health challenge, characterized by high morbidity, mortality, and frequent rehospitalizations. The heterogeneity of clinical presentations in AHF and its complex underlying pathophysiology complicates diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic decision-making. Biomarkers have reshaped the clinical landscape in AHF by providing objective, biologically-grounded insights that complement traditional clinical assessment. Natriuretic peptides are established as indispensable for diagnosis and prognosis, while troponins, soluble ST2, galectin-3, and even newer candidates such as adrenomedullin and CA-125 may capture complementary domains including myocardial injury, fibrosis, congestion, and systemic stress. Applied dynamically across the care continuum—from the emergency department to hospitalization, discharge, and follow-up—biomarkers may support earlier diagnosis, more refined risk assessment, monitoring of therapeutic response, and post-discharge surveillance. Evidence increasingly favors multimarker strategies, integrating diverse biological signals; these consistently outperform single-marker approaches. Risk scores that incorporate biomarkers further strengthen clinical decision-making by providing structured risk estimates at critical time points. Future studies must explore best means of combining multiple markers into panels and whether multimarker-guided strategies have benefit in improving outcomes beyond the natriuretic peptides and troponin. Future advances will depend on embedding biomarkers within precision-medicine frameworks. Phenotype-specific algorithms, integration into electronic health records with automated decision support, and the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning offer clear potential. Equally, innovations in point-of-care testing and remote monitoring may extend biomarker utility beyond hospital walls. Collectively, these developments could shift AHF management from reactive stabilization toward proactive, biomarker-guided care aimed at improving outcomes in this high-risk population.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) affects an estimated 64 million people worldwide and substantially contributes to global morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1–3]. AHF is a heterogeneous clinical syndrome requiring urgent evaluation and treatment, whether it occurs as a first presentation or as worsening of pre-existing HF. Recent efforts to standardize AHF definitions emphasize a unified framework for evaluation, reflecting the substantial overlap in management principles across these presentations [4–6]. In practice, the diagnosis of AHF remains challenging because symptoms and signs overlap with pulmonary, systemic, and renal conditions. In the emergency setting, dyspnea—the cardinal symptom—offers limited diagnostic specificity, and delays in establishing the diagnosis or initiating therapy are consistently associated with worse outcomes [7–9].

AHF is not a uniform disease, but a syndrome characterized by diverse clinical phenotypes and complex pathophysiological interactions [10]. This heterogeneity complicates diagnosis and risk assessment and impedes personalized treatment. Conventional tools such as clinical history, physical examination, and chest radiography, while fundamental, lack sensitivity and specificity when used in isolation and frequently fail to guide early, targeted management [11, 12]. In this evolving clinical landscape, circulating biomarkers have reshaped the approach to AHF. By capturing key biological processes—hemodynamic stress, myocardial injury, inflammation, neurohormonal activation, and fibrosis—biomarkers provide objective insights that complement traditional evaluation [13–15] (Fig. 1).

The value of biomarker measurement extends across the continuum of care in AHF and clinical practice guidelines in the United States and Europe provide generally similar recommendations for biomarker testing in those with AHF, but with modest differences in the scope of how biomarkers may fit into routine care (Table 1) [2, 8, 9]. In the acute presentation setting, biomarkers aid in distinguishing cardiac from non-cardiac dyspnea, particularly when the presentation is ambiguous [16–18]. Beyond diagnosis, biomarkers play an essential role in risk stratification, helping to determine appropriate levels of care and identify patients at high risk for deterioration. During hospitalization, biomarker testing supports further risk stratification and helps monitor treatment response. Pre-discharge biomarker concentrations inform readiness for transition of care, while post-discharge trajectories predict risk of rehospitalization and mortality [19–21].

Substantial advances have recently been made in the biomarker-based evaluation and management of AHF. This review synthesizes the current evidence on circulating biomarkers in AHF, examining their biological underpinnings, clinical applications across the care continuum, and the emerging role of multimarker strategies. It also highlights areas where knowledge remains incomplete and outlines directions for future research, with the ultimate aim of enabling more precise, biomarker-leveraged care for this high-risk population.

Principles for clinical interpretation

Diagnostic, prognostic, and actionable roles

The clinical value of a biomarker depends on the question it is intended to answer (Table 2). Diagnostic biomarkers refine the probability that a current clinical syndrome is present; prognostic biomarkers estimate the likelihood of subsequent clinical events; and actionable biomarkers identify, quantify, or track a biological process for which a defined clinical response is supported by evidence. Natriuretic peptides (NPs) occupy all three domains, although their strongest and most consistently validated roles in acute heart failure are diagnostic and prognostic. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin is actionable when it prompts evaluation for acute myocardial infarction or other causes of myocardial injury; however, it should not be interpreted as a diagnostic biomarker for AHF itself. In contrast, most emerging biomarkers in acute heart failure remain primarily prognostic, and their incremental discrimination is frequently attenuated after accounting for clinical variables, natriuretic peptides, and high-sensitivity troponin [22–24].

Importantly, prognostic association does not by itself establish clinical utility. For most biomarkers beyond NPs and cardiac troponin, the evidence base is derived largely from observational studies demonstrating associations with future events or modest incremental discrimination. Although selected randomized studies have evaluated biomarker-linked strategies for diagnosis, natriuresis-guided decongestion, congestion management, and predischarge assessment, relatively few trials have tested care pathways in which a prespecified biomarker result triggers a defined clinical action and improves patient-centered outcomes. Accordingly, the principal evidence gap is not further demonstration of prognostic association, but randomized evaluation of whether biomarker-informed management strategies improve clinical outcomes [25–27].

Biomarker-informed care should not be interpreted as a substitute for phenotype-based, guideline-directed therapy. In patients with HFrEF who are eligible for foundational medical therapy, initiation and optimization of the four major therapeutic classes should be pursued irrespective of biomarker concentrations, unless limited by contraindications or intolerance. Rather, biomarkers may refine the timing and intensity of clinical management by clarifying the diagnosis, identifying specific treatable contributors, assessing the adequacy of decongestion, prompting evaluation for myocardial injury or end-organ dysfunction, and informing discharge readiness and the need for earlier follow-up. Only when a biomarker is linked to a validated biological process and a prespecified clinical response—such as post-diuretic urine sodium guiding diuretic escalation or iron indices identifying iron deficiency—can it presently be considered directly actionable. For most emerging biomarkers, evidence does not support selecting or withholding HF therapies solely on the basis of biomarker concentrations.

The timing and trajectory of biomarker assessment are also central to interpretation. A value obtained before decongestion may address a different clinical question than the same biomarker measured at discharge. Serial biomarker assessment may offer important insight into the biology of acute decompensation, the adequacy of decongestive therapy, and residual risk at transition points of care. However, temporal changes in biomarker concentrations are inherently influenced by clinical context, including treatment effects, assay variability, renal clearance, hemodynamic perturbations, and regression to the mean. Accordingly, although biomarker trajectories may inform risk assessment and clinical reasoning, they should not be interpreted in isolation as evidence that biomarker-guided treatment strategies improve outcomes. Clinical utility is greatest when biomarker testing is linked to a prespecified decision point and interpreted alongside symptoms, physical examination, imaging findings, comorbid conditions, and the suspected trigger for acute decompensation.

Natriuretic peptides: biology, assays, and concentration as a continuum

Natriuretic peptides are central biomarkers in acute heart failure because they integrate myocardial stress, neurohormonal activation, and cardiorenal physiology. Increased ventricular wall stress stimulates synthesis and secretion of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and its inactive N-terminal fragment, NT-proBNP, whereas atrial stretch promotes release of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP). The biologically active natriuretic peptides exert counter-regulatory effects through natriuresis, diuresis, vasodilation, inhibition of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and sympathetic nervous systems, and modulation of myocardial and vascular remodeling [28–30].

Natriuretic peptides are synthesized as pro-peptides and are cleaved upon release into the circulation, yielding an inert N-terminal pro-peptide and a C-terminal biologically active form. The biologically active forms of natriuretic peptides act as compensatory neurohormones, promoting natriuresis, diuresis, and vasodilation among other effects; each biologically active natriuretic peptide (ANP, BNP, CNP) has a ring structure responsible for its biological effect.

Given generally shorter half-lives of the biologically active natriuretic peptides, assays directed to the N-terminal pro-peptides have been developed. This is particularly relevant for ANP, which has very limited in vitro stability and is therefore less suitable for routine direct measurement; accordingly, the stable mid-regional pro-ANP (MR-proANP) fragment serves as a practical surrogate for ANP-system activation [28].

Much has been learned about natriuretic peptide biology since the first descriptions of testing in AHF. Under physiological conditions, the intracellular precursor pro-peptide (proBNP1−108) is cleaved primarily by furin, with corin also contributing to its cleavage, yielding a 76-residue N-terminal pro-peptide (NT-proBNP) and a 32-residue C-terminal fragment (BNP). With advancing HF severity, however, a progressively greater proportion of circulating immunoreactive “NT-proBNP” and “BNP” consists of uncleaved proBNP₁₋₁₀₈. This may have to do with an increase in post-translational glycosylation of the N-terminal cleavage site of proBNP1−108, rendering it less vulnerable to being cleaved [31]. (Fig. 2). The clinical importance of proBNP1−108 is recognized; it is less able to stimulate the natriuretic peptide receptor, and thus unable to stimulate the downstream biological benefits that might be expected from “free” BNP [32]. Notably, since proBNP1−108 contains the epitopes recognized by both “BNP” and “NT-proBNP” immunoassays, the assays used clinically for the care of patients indeed identify a mixture of peptides, which may include the target analyte (e.g. free BNP, free NT-proBNP) along with a variable percentage of proBNP1−108. Beyond this issue, owing to degradation by circulating endopeptidases, immunoreactive BNP circulates in a mostly degraded manner; “BNP” methods are unable to differentiate degraded fragments of BNP from intact BNP1−32.

Despite these important analytical and clinical implications, the assays for detection of NT-proBNP and BNP retain their diagnostic and prognostic value. Much more data are needed about the reason(s) leading to failure of proBNP1−108 cleavage (leading to physiologic states deficient in BNP effect) and whether further refinement in natriuretic peptide assays is needed to filter out (or specifically measure) the uncleaved 108 amino acid precursor.

Clinically, natriuretic peptide concentrations should be interpreted as a continuum of probability and risk rather than as discrete biological thresholds. Rule-out cutoffs and age-stratified rule-in thresholds are useful decision aids, particularly in the emergency department evaluation of acute dyspnea, but values close to any threshold require careful integration with the clinical context. Pretest probability, time from symptom onset, atrial fibrillation, renal function, age, body composition, and treatment before sampling all influence post-test interpretation [16, 22, 33]. Thus, natriuretic peptides are most informative when interpreted not as isolated laboratory values, but as dynamic markers of hemodynamic stress and residual risk within the broader clinical phenotype.

Cardiac troponin: a marker of myocardial injury and risk in acute heart failure

Cardiac troponin I and T are structural components of the cardiomyocyte contractile apparatus and are released into the circulation in the setting of myocardial injury. In acute heart failure, detectable or elevated troponin concentrations may result from multiple, often overlapping mechanisms, including increased wall stress, subendocardial ischemia, coronary microvascular dysfunction, inflammation, tachyarrhythmia, neurohormonal activation, impaired renal clearance, and concomitant noncardiac illness [34–37]. Thus, troponin elevation in acute heart failure identifies myocardial injury, but does not by itself establish acute type 1 myocardial infarction or define acute heart failure as the primary diagnosis.

The introduction of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin assays has further refined this interpretation. Contemporary assays detect low-level myocardial injury in a large proportion of patients hospitalized with acute heart failure, such that concentrations exceeding the 99th percentile are common and substantially less specific for acute coronary thrombosis than elevations detected with older-generation assays. In this context, troponin should be viewed less as a binary abnormal-versus-normal test and more as a continuous marker of myocardial injury burden and clinical risk.

The ADHERE registry provided early evidence supporting the prognostic relevance of troponin elevation in AHF, demonstrating that patients with a positive conventional troponin test had substantially higher in-hospital mortality than those with a negative test: 8.0% versus 2.7%, corresponding to an absolute difference of 5.3% points and an adjusted odds ratio of 2.55 [38]. However, the thresholds used in that analysis—cardiac troponin I ≥ 1.0 µg/L or cardiac troponin T ≥ 0.1 µg/L—were substantially higher than contemporary high-sensitivity assay detection limits and should not be directly extrapolated to current practice. Contemporary hs-cTn studies generally demonstrate a graded relationship between higher or rising troponin concentrations and adverse outcomes, although the incremental prognostic contribution of hs-cTn has varied across cohorts and is influenced by renal function, assay platform, timing of sampling, prevalence of acute coronary syndrome, and the extent of adjustment for clinical covariates [23, 39, 40]. Accordingly, hs-cTn has an important role in the initial evaluation of patients with suspected AHF, particularly because myocardial injury and acute coronary syndrome are common diagnostic considerations in this setting. Serial testing should follow established acute coronary syndrome (ACS) pathways when symptoms, electrocardiographic findings, or the initial troponin concentration raise concern for ischemia. However, troponin should not be used to rule in AHF, and dynamic change alone does not reliably distinguish type 1 myocardial infarction from type 2 myocardial infarction or nonischemic myocardial injury. Its greatest clinical value in AHF lies in identifying myocardial injury, refining risk stratification, and prompting targeted evaluation for ischemic or nonischemic precipitants when supported by the broader clinical context.

Clinical modifiers of biomarker interpretation

Biomarker concentrations in AHF are strongly shaped by patient phenotype and comorbid disease. Older age and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate are associated with higher natriuretic peptide and hs-cTn concentrations, even in the absence of an acute change in cardiac status. Atrial fibrillation increases natriuretic peptide concentrations through atrial and ventricular stretch, and some diagnostic algorithms incorporate higher rule-in thresholds in this setting. Conversely, obesity is associated with lower natriuretic peptide concentrations, such that unexpectedly low values should not be used to exclude HF when the pretest probability remains high. This consideration is particularly relevant in HF with preserved ejection fraction, especially when accompanied by obesity, in which natriuretic peptide concentrations may be lower than in HF with reduced ejection fraction despite clinically meaningful congestion or functional limitation [24, 41].

Treatment effects and extracardiac illness further modify interpretation. BNP concentrations may rise transiently after initiation of sacubitril/valsartan because neprilysin participates in BNP degradation, whereas NT-proBNP is not a neprilysin substrate [42, 43]. Acute and chronic noncardiac conditions—including sepsis, pulmonary hypertension, acute kidney dysfunction, chronic lung disease, anemia, cancer, and systemic inflammation—may elevate several biomarkers of myocardial injury, hemodynamic stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and renal dysfunction, thereby reducing cardiac specificity [44, 45]. Because no single correction factor adequately accounts for these interacting modifiers, biomarker results should be interpreted as context-dependent signals rather than isolated thresholds. In practice, the most clinically informative approach integrates the absolute concentration, direction and magnitude of change, timing of measurement, and the patient’s broader clinical phenotype.

Complementary and emerging biological domains

Beyond natriuretic peptides and cardiac troponin, several biomarker domains have been investigated to capture biological processes that contribute to AHF heterogeneity, including inflammation, myocardial and extracellular matrix remodeling, vascular dysfunction, neurohormonal activation, congestion, and comorbidity burden. These biomarkers may provide complementary pathobiological information and, in some cases, improve risk stratification. However, their clinical implementation remains limited by incomplete disease specificity, assay heterogeneity, modest incremental discrimination beyond established clinical variables and natriuretic peptides, and the absence of randomized evidence demonstrating that biomarker-targeted treatment strategies improve AHF outcomes.

Systemic inflammation is common in AHF; mechanistically, inflammation in HF may be related to bacterial translocation, hypoperfusion injury in the gut, or neurohormonal activation [45]. Circulating cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, along with acute-phase reactants including hsCRP, are frequently elevated [45–47]. Growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), a stress-responsive cytokine induced by oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular senescence, has emerged as a particularly strong prognostic marker in HF. In a prospective multicenter study of 455 patients with chronic HF, higher GDF-15 concentrations demonstrated a striking graded association with mortality, with 48-month death rates of 10.0%, 9.4%, 33.4%, and 56.2% across increasing quartiles, and remained an independent predictor of all-cause mortality after adjustment for NT-proBNP, renal function, anemia, and uric acid (adjusted HR per log-unit increase 2.26; 95% CI 1.52–3.37). Importantly, GDF-15 provided prognostic information across clinically relevant HF subgroups and complemented natriuretic peptides by capturing distinct biological pathways. Their extracardiac production, however, limits diagnostic specificity in the acute dyspnea setting, particularly among older patients with multimorbidity, infection, kidney dysfunction, malignancy, or systemic inflammatory illness [48–50].

Similarly, soluble ST2, a marker of interleukin-33/ST2 pathway activation, and galectin-3, a marker of macrophage activation and profibrotic signaling, have been evaluated as complementary biomarkers in AHF, primarily for risk stratification rather than diagnosis [18, 20, 48–51]. In a head-to-head emergency department study of 251 patients presenting with dyspnea, including 137 with adjudicated acute HF, BNP demonstrated substantially higher diagnostic accuracy for acute HF than either galectin-3 or sST2, with an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.95) compared with 0.57 (95% CI: 0.51–0.64) for galectin-3 and 0.63 (95% CI: 0.56–0.69) for sST2. In contrast, among patients with acute HF, the prognostic performance for 1-year all-cause mortality was broadly similar across biomarkers: AUC 0.72 (95% CI: 0.63–0.79) for BNP, 0.70 (95% CI: 0.62–0.78) for galectin-3, and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.67–0.82) for sST2, with 41 deaths among 137 patients during follow-up [18]. These findings support the concept that galectin-3 and sST2 may provide prognostic information in AHF but have limited utility as stand-alone diagnostic tests compared with natriuretic peptides. Mechanistically, galectin-3 has been linked to macrophage-mediated fibroblast activation, collagen production, and ventricular dysfunction; in experimental models, early myocardial galectin-3 upregulation identified failure-prone hypertrophied hearts, and low-dose pericardial galectin-3 infusion induced left ventricular dysfunction with a 3-fold preferential increase in collagen I over collagen III [51]. However, clinical interpretation of galectin-3 is complicated by its stronger dependence on kidney function, whereas sST2 appears relatively less influenced by renal function. Collectively, these biomarkers may refine risk assessment in selected patients, but none currently has an established role as a stand-alone diagnostic test or as a target for biomarker-guided therapy in AHF [20, 52].

Markers of myocardial remodeling, fibrosis, and cellular senescence are also of increasing interest in HF, particularly in phenotypes characterized by aging, obesity, renal dysfunction, and diastolic abnormalities. Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (IGFBP7), a component of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, has been linked to impaired ventricular relaxation, left atrial remodeling, and adverse outcomes. In the prospective, multicenter ICON-RELOADED study of 1,449 patients presenting with dyspnea [53], 274 (18.9%) were diagnosed with acute HF. IGFBP7 concentrations were higher in patients with acute HF than in those without HF (median 146.1 vs. 86.1 ng/mL; P < 0.001), and addition of IGFBP7 to NT-proBNP improved diagnostic discrimination for AHF, increasing the AUC from 0.91 to 0.94 (P < 0.001) with significant reclassification beyond NT-proBNP alone (net reclassification index + 0.25). Among patients with acute HF, higher log2-transformed IGFBP7 concentrations predicted death or rehospitalization at 6 months (HR 1.84 per log2-SD increase; 95% CI: 1.30–2.61; P = 0.001). In a prespecified echocardiographic substudy of ICON-RELOADED including 271 patients with acute dyspnea [17], higher IGFBP7 concentrations correlated with greater left atrial volume index (r = 0.49), higher E/e′ ratio (r = 0.44), lower left ventricular ejection fraction (r = − 0.27), and lower right ventricular fractional area change (r = − 0.31; all P < 0.001). In HFpEF, IGFBP7 has similarly been associated with left atrial dilation and diastolic abnormalities; in a substudy of 228 patients randomized to sacubitril/valsartan versus valsartan, higher IGFBP7 was associated with greater left atrial size and left atrial volume index (both P < 0.001), while sacubitril/valsartan was associated with lower IGFBP7 concentrations over 36 weeks compared with valsartan (adjusted treatment effect − 7%; P < 0.001) [54]. Collectively, these data support IGFBP7 as a candidate biomarker that may complement natriuretic peptides by capturing senescence, remodeling, and filling-pressure biology, although broader assay availability, external validation, and prospective studies demonstrating incremental clinical utility are needed before routine implementation in AHF.

A wider remodeling and myocardial stress literature includes osteopontin, syndecan-4, myostatin, procollagen type I C-terminal propeptide, procollagen type III N-terminal propeptide, vascular endothelial growth factor, nitric-oxide-synthase–related signals, heart-type fatty acid–binding protein, and heat shock protein 60 [14, 15, 55, 56]. These biomarkers illuminate extracellular matrix turnover, vascular signaling, myocyte stress, and tissue repair pathways. However, heterogeneous assay platforms, variable biological specificity, and limited evidence for incremental or actionable clinical utility currently favor their use in focused research panels rather than routine AHF care.

Neurohormonal and vascular stress biomarkers provide additional prognostic information in AHF, but their clinical actionability remains limited. Copeptin, a stable surrogate of arginine vasopressin activation, reflects neurohormonal stress and has been associated with short-term mortality in acute dyspnea and AHF cohorts [57, 58]. In 287 emergency department patients with acute dyspnea, copeptin demonstrated good discrimination for 30-day mortality (AUC 0.83; 95% CI: 0.76–0.90), remained independently associated with mortality after multivariable adjustment (HR 3.88; 95% CI: 1.94–7.77), and improved risk reclassification when added to natriuretic peptide testing (NRI 0.37; P < 0.001) [59]. Similarly, in the BACH trial, patients with AHF in the highest copeptin quartile had markedly higher 90-day mortality, and addition of copeptin and sodium to NT-proBNP significantly improved mortality risk reclassification (NRI 32.7%; P < 0.001) [60]. MR-proADM, a stable fragment reflecting adrenomedullin pathway activation and vascular/endothelial stress, has also shown prognostic value [61, 62]; in BACH, MR-proADM predicted 90-day survival in AHF with an accuracy of 73% (95% CI: 70%–77%) and added prognostic information beyond natriuretic peptides [62]. Despite these associations, copeptin and MR-proADM have not entered routine AHF practice because they reflect systemic stress and extracardiac illness as much as HF biology, lack disease-specific actionable thresholds, and have not been linked to biomarker-guided treatment strategies that improve outcomes. More recent markers within this axis, including bioactive adrenomedullin, which may more directly reflect endothelial barrier dysfunction and tissue congestion, and CA-125, a marker of serosal inflammation and congestion, may be most relevant to quantifying congestion burden, decongestive response, and residual in-hospital risk rather than serving as stand-alone diagnostic tests [63, 64].

Blood lactate provides complementary information regarding systemic hypoperfusion and metabolic stress in AHF. Although traditionally regarded as a marker of anaerobic metabolism, lactate reflects the integrated balance between tissue oxygen delivery, adrenergic activation, and hepatic and renal clearance rather than hypoperfusion alone. In a prospective cohort of 237 patients hospitalized with AHF without overt clinical evidence of peripheral hypoperfusion, elevated admission lactate (≥ 2 mmol/L) was present in 43% of patients and independently predicted 1-year mortality after adjustment for established prognostic variables (adjusted HR 1.24 per mmol/L; 95% CI 1.08–1.41; P = 0.002) [65]. Elevated lactate was also associated with biomarkers of myocardial injury, hepatic injury, vasoconstriction, and worsening renal injury, suggesting that it reflects multisystem physiological stress rather than isolated circulatory failure. Accordingly, lactate should be viewed primarily as a marker of disease severity and occult systemic hypoperfusion that may complement established biomarkers in selected high-risk patients, although its role in biomarker-guided management of AHF remains to be established.

Iron indices represent a distinct biomarker category because they identify a treatable comorbidity rather than the syndrome of AHF itself. Ferritin and transferrin saturation are the clinically established tests for identifying iron deficiency and guiding guideline-based iron replacement strategies. Soluble transferrin receptor and hepcidin may provide additional mechanistic information, particularly when inflammation complicates interpretation of ferritin [2, 8, 9]. Thus, the principal role of iron biomarkers is not to diagnose AHF, but to identify a modifiable contributor to symptoms, functional limitation, and prognosis.

Biomarkers across the clinical timeline

The clinical utility of biomarkers in AHF is inherently dynamic. The meaning of a biomarker result depends on where the patient is along the care continuum, from risk identification before an acute episode to first medical contact, hospitalization, discharge, and postdischarge follow-up. A time-dependent framework is therefore essential, because the same biomarker may serve different purposes at different stages: identifying subclinical risk before presentation, refining the diagnosis of acute dyspnea, assessing decongestive response and residual risk during hospitalization, and informing transition-of-care planning after discharge (Fig. 3).

Before acute presentation: identifying patients at risk

Biomarker use may begin before an acute episode, particularly among individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors or established structural heart disease. In selected at-risk populations, elevated natriuretic peptide concentrations identify subclinical hemodynamic stress and a phenotype at increased risk for future ventricular dysfunction and incident HF events. Notably, recommendations diverge across major guidelines. Consistent with this concept, the 2022 ACC/AHA/HFSA guideline states that natriuretic peptide–based screening followed by team-based preventive care can be useful in selected at-risk populations, whereas European guidelines do not provide an equivalent recommendation for population-level screening [2, 8, 9].

Randomized studies such as STOP-HF and PONTIAC provide proof of concept that natriuretic peptide–guided identification of higher-risk individuals, followed by collaborative care or intensified preventive therapy, can improve surrogate or clinical HF-related outcomes in selected populations. In STOP-HF, BNP-based screening with collaborative care reduced the primary endpoint of asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction with or without newly diagnosed HF from 8.7% to 5.3% (OR 0.55; 95% CI 0.37–0.82; P = 0.003) and reduced emergency hospitalization for major cardiovascular events from 40.4 to 22.3 per 1,000 patient-years (incidence rate ratio 0.60; 95% CI 0.45–0.81; P = 0.002) [66]. In PONTIAC, among patients with type 2 diabetes and NT-proBNP > 125 pg/mL but no established cardiac disease, intensified up-titration of renin–angiotensin system antagonists and beta-blockers reduced the risk of cardiac hospitalization or death at 2 years (HR 0.35; 95% CI 0.13–0.98; P = 0.044) [67]. These data, however, do not support indiscriminate biomarker screening. The most defensible contemporary approach is targeted testing in patients with substantial cardiometabolic risk when the result is linked to a prespecified clinical response, such as echocardiography, optimization of blood pressure and diabetes management, initiation or intensification of guideline-directed preventive therapies, or closer longitudinal surveillance. For prediction of incident HF, natriuretic peptides have the most mature evidence base and remain the only biomarkers supported by randomized screening studies linked to preventive intervention. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin may provide complementary information by identifying low-grade myocardial injury and refining long-term cardiovascular risk. When evaluated together for prediction of incident HF, NPs and hs-cTn provide complementary rather than interchangeable information. In the ARIC cohort of 9,868 participants without prevalent HF, 970 developed HF over a mean follow-up of 10.4 years. Adding cTnT and NT-proBNP to the established ARIC HF clinical model improved discrimination, with AUC increases of 0.040 in women and 0.057 in men, and yielded continuous net reclassification improvements of 50.7% and 54.7%, respectively. These findings support the complementary value of biomarkers reflecting hemodynamic stress and myocardial injury for prediction of future HF, while NPs remain the more established biomarkers for HF screening and prevention [68].

Emergency department / pre-hospital setting

In the undifferentiated acute setting, the priorities are rapid diagnosis and early risk stratification to guide triage and initial management.

Diagnosis and triage

Natriuretic peptides (BNP/NT-proBNP) are indispensable for diagnosis and triage of individuals with suspected or proven AHF. The high negative predictive value of the natriuretic peptides allows clinicians to confidently exclude AHF in dyspneic patients, avoiding unnecessary admissions and redirecting attention to alternative diagnoses [16, 22]. A single natriuretic peptide measurement significantly improves diagnostic certainty over clinical judgment alone and is particularly useful in the setting of diagnostic uncertainty [69, 70], a common scenario in the fast-paced environment of the ED; to facilitate clinical decision making in such patients, a timely measurement of BNP or NT-proBNP should be performed.

Guideline-defined thresholds, such as an NT-proBNP < 300 ng/L, are particularly effective for exclusion of AHF [9, 22, 33]. Further, age-stratified NT-proBNP thresholds (< 450 ng/L for ages < 50 years, < 900 ng/L for ages 50–75 years, and < 1800 ng/L for ages > 75 years) improve diagnostic accuracy compared with a single cut-off approach. In the ICON-Reloaded study, a single NT-proBNP cut-off of 300 ng/L yielded an overall diagnostic accuracy of 81%, whereas application of age-stratified thresholds improved accuracy to 84%, with notable gains in specificity among older patients [16]. These thresholds should nevertheless be interpreted within the broader clinical context rather than as rigid diagnostic boundaries. Atrial fibrillation independently increases natriuretic peptide concentrations, and higher diagnostic thresholds are frequently applied in clinical studies and diagnostic algorithms; however, no single adjustment fully accounts for the effects of cardiac rhythm, age, kidney function, obesity, and pretest probability. Accordingly, markedly elevated BNP or NT-proBNP concentrations strongly favor an AHF diagnosis and identify individuals at higher in-hospital risk who may require escalation of care.

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin is generally obtained during the initial evaluation of patients with suspected AHF to identify myocardial injury and assess for acute coronary syndrome as a potential precipitant, particularly when supported by ischemic symptoms, electrocardiographic abnormalities, hemodynamic instability, or an abrupt clinical presentation. Its purpose in this setting is not to establish the diagnosis of AHF; an elevated or changing concentration must be interpreted alongside the clinical presentation and established acute coronary syndrome pathways [2, 8, 9]. The growing availability of point-of-care assays for NPs and troponins has shortened time to diagnosis and treatment in the ED environment and possibly in the pre-hospital setting.

Novel biomarkers including IGFBP7 may in the future help clarify diagnostic uncertainty in NP “gray zones” particularly if merged into panels with BNP or NT-proBNP [53, 71] For example, Ibrahim and colleagues demonstrated that concentrations of IGFBP7 provided additive diagnostic value to NT-proBNP when measured in dyspneic individuals in the ED setting. Notably, the added value from IGFBP7 was most obvious in those with HF and NT-proBNP concentrations below the diagnostic threshold [53].

Hospitalization phase

Once an individual is admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of AHF, the focus shifts from diagnosis to risk stratification, treatment monitoring, and adjustment of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF.

Risk Stratification during hospitalization:

Biomarker concentrations at first evaluation are not only useful for diagnostic evaluation but they may provide powerful information to refine early prognostication following admission for AHF. Among available tests, natriuretic peptides and troponins remain the most widely validated and clinically applied. Current guidelines endorse measuring BNP or NT-proBNP at admission to establish prognosis, with additional consideration for a predischarge value to inform trajectory and post-discharge risk [9].

Cardiac troponin—while central to the evaluation of myocardial injury and possible acute coronary syndrome—also provides independent prognostic information in the AHF setting [2, 8, 9]. As noted the prognostic significance of troponin elevation in AHF is well supported by large registry data; among 67,924 hospitalized patients with AHF and preserved renal function, an abnormal troponin test (cardiac troponin I ≥ 1.0 µg/L or troponin T ≥ 0.1 µg/L) defined a distinctly higher-risk subgroup. In-hospital mortality was nearly threefold higher among those with elevated troponin compared with troponin-negative patients (8.0% vs. 2.7%; p < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment for clinical and laboratory predictors, an elevated troponin remained a strong independent predictor of in-hospital death (adjusted OR 2.55; 95% CI 2.24–2.89; p < 0.001). Elevated troponin was also associated with greater resource utilization, including longer length of stay, more frequent ICU admission, and increased need for advanced therapies, supporting its value for early risk stratification in AHF [38]. Complementing these findings, the PROTECT study further demonstrated the prognostic relevance of troponin in hospitalized AHF. Baseline cTnT elevation was common (detectable in 60% and positive > 0.03 ng/mL in 34%) and independently associated with a higher risk of the composite endpoint of cardiovascular or renal rehospitalization or death at 60 days (adjusted HR 1.84; 95% CI 1.04–3.26; p = 0.036). Notably, after initially having a troponin in the normal range, 21% of patients developed an abnormal troponin during hospitalization, and these individuals experienced similarly adverse outcomes, underscoring the added prognostic value of serial troponin assessment in AHF [39].

In addition to the independent prognostic power of natriuretic peptides and troponin, combined biomarker assessment further refines risk stratification in AHF. In pooled analyses of nearly 1,500 patients with AHF, patients with both elevated NT-proBNP (≥ 4382 ng/L) and high-sensitivity troponin T (hs-TnT ≥ 55 ng/L) on admission had a substantially higher risk of in-hospital mortality—nearly 12-fold greater—than those without both elevations (RR 11.7; 95% CI 6.9–19.7), demonstrating that combined natriuretic peptide and troponin profiling enhances prognostic discrimination beyond either biomarker alone [23].

Taken together, admission natriuretic peptides and hs-cTn reliably identify patients at heightened near-term risk who may warrant closer monitoring and careful optimization of GDMT based on clinical eligibility and treatment tolerance. Beyond these cornerstone biomarkers, additional markers reflecting complementary biological pathways—such as sST2, GDF-15, and MR-proADM—have demonstrated prognostic associations in AHF and may offer incremental value as part of multimarker risk-stratification strategies [20, 47, 72]. Rather than relying on a single biomarker, simultaneous assessment of biomarkers reflecting myocardial stress and injury together with renal and hepatic dysfunction provides a more comprehensive characterization of the complex multisystem pathophysiology of AHF and improves patient phenotyping across the clinical timeline. Supporting this concept, patients with evidence of concurrent cardiac, renal, and hepatic dysfunction have substantially worse outcomes than those with isolated organ involvement; in one prospective cohort, the presence of dysfunction in all three organs was associated with a nearly sevenfold higher risk of 1-year mortality compared with no organ dysfunction (HR 6.75; 95% CI 2.52–18.13; P < 0.01). Accordingly, multimarker strategies integrating complementary biological domains may provide more granular risk stratification and identify patients who warrant closer monitoring, reassessment of congestion and end-organ function, review of treatment tolerance and adherence, and more intensive transition-of-care planning. However, escalation of decongestive therapy, referral for advanced HF therapies, or modification of disease-directed treatment should remain grounded in the overall clinical phenotype rather than biomarker risk alone [73, 74].

Monitoring congestion

Once hospitalized, effective decongestion and optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy are the central inpatient goals. Traditional tools—daily weights, fluid balance—are insensitive to volume status and prone to error. Direct assessment of natriuresis is gaining traction as an early functional measure of decongestive response. Early natriuresis provides a simple, readily available marker of the kidney’s response to loop diuretic therapy, complementing conventional measures of kidney function. Spot urine sodium (UNa) measured 1–2 h after loop diuretic administration has emerged as a practical index of loop diuretic effectiveness [2]. A low post-diuretic UNa, commonly < 50–70 mmol/L, indicates an inadequate natriuretic response or ineffective diuresis at the administered dose, rather than diuretic resistance per se, because adequate natriuresis may still be achieved with timely dose escalation or sequential nephron blockade [2, 75]. This approach is supported by randomized and pragmatic data. In PUSH-AHF, UNa-guided therapy improved natriuresis at 24 h without renal harm, supporting a physiology-based approach to diuretic titration [26]. Similarly, in the ENACT-HF trial of 401 patients hospitalized with AHF and volume overload, a standardized natriuresis-guided diuretic protocol increased natriuresis at 1 day compared with standard care (282 vs. 174 mmol; adjusted mean ratio 1.64; p < 0.001), with sustained improvement at 2 days (538 vs. 365 mmol; adjusted mean ratio 1.52; p < 0.001), greater diuresis at 2 days (5776 vs. 4381 mL; adjusted mean ratio 1.33; p < 0.001), and shorter length of stay (5.8 vs. 7.0 days; adjusted mean ratio 0.87; p = 0.036), without a significant difference in in-hospital mortality [25]. Together, these findings support early natriuresis-guided adjustment of decongestive therapy while avoiding premature labeling of low UNa as fixed diuretic resistance.

While current guidelines do not recommend biomarkers for routine monitoring during decongestion or GDMT optimization (Table 1), biomarkers may nonetheless provide complementary insights. In AHF, much of the natriuretic peptide signal reflects congestion, but high-quality data establishing a role to “guide” diuresis are lacking; other biomarkers may better capture extravascular volume status. A biologically active form of adrenomedullin (bio-ADM) [64] appears to roughly correlate with tissue edema and vascular leakage, making it an attractive candidate for judging presence and extent of vascular congestion. In an ED cohort, higher bio-ADM at presentation predicted 30-day adverse outcomes, independent of clinical covariates (adjusted OR per IQR change: 2.68; 95% CI: 1.60, 4.51) [76]. More data are definitely needed in this regard before this marker would be adopted as a marker of congestion, and labeling it as such at present is premature.

Carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA-125) [63], a high molecular weight glycoprotein often linked to malignancy involving coelomic mesothelium may have a role in identifying congestion; its concentrations may be elevated in volume overload states such as HF or cirrhosis. In a randomized trial of patients hospitalized with AHF and renal dysfunction, a CA-125–guided diuretic strategy improved early decongestion signals, including better 72-hour eGFR compared with usual care (37.5 vs. 34.8, p = 0.036) [77]. Furthermore, in the multicenter, cluster-randomized CHANCE-HF trial (n = 380), CA-125–guided management significantly reduced recurrent HF-related visits (incidence rate ratio: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.28–0.82; p = 0.008) [78]. The association between CA-125 and fluid overload extends beyond HF. In a cohort of 170 patients with cirrhosis, serum CA-125 concentrations were markedly elevated in 97.8% of those with ascites (mean 321 ± 283 U/mL) compared with only 7.3% of those without ascites (mean 13 ± 15 U/mL; p < 0.001). Notably, concentrations of CA-125 declined rapidly following large-volume paracentesis, underscoring their dynamic responsiveness to changes in volume status [79]. These findings underscore the promise of CA-125 as a biomarker of serosal congestion and highlight its potential utility for assessing and monitoring congestion in acute heart failure.

Interpreting kidney function:

A rise in serum creatinine during aggressive diuresis is common but is not necessarily indicative of structural kidney injury. When accompanied by effective decongestion and hemoconcentration, or when occurring after initiation of therapies that produce an expected hemodynamic decline in eGFR, such as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, a modest worsening of kidney filtration does not necessarily portend adverse outcomes. In some circumstances, it may instead reflect more effective decongestion or an anticipated treatment effect [80]. By contrast, deterioration in kidney function associated with persistent congestion or severe HF may identify a higher-risk cardiorenal phenotype and prompt reassessment of the adequacy of decongestion and overall management. Notably, the prognostic implications of a decline in kidney filtration during effective decongestion appear less adverse than those of congestion-associated worsening kidney function [81], underscoring that changes in creatinine and eGFR are highly context dependent. Biomarkers have therefore been investigated to distinguish functional or hemodynamic reductions in filtration from clinically meaningful kidney injury.

Investigators have evaluated whether the combination of a natriuretic peptide (e.g. NT-proBNP) and a kidney function measure (e.g. eGFR) might be useful to sort out different forms of worsening kidney function; in concept, marked elevation of NT-proBNP in the setting of falling eGFR might be more likely to reflect congestive causes, whereas lower NT-proBNP might be more likely to associate with WRF that is not as deleterious. As first shown by van Kimmenade and colleagues, among a group of individuals with AHF, the combination of an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 plus an NT-proBNP > 4,647 ng/L predicted 60-day mortality (OR 3.46; 95% CI 2.13 to 5.63; p < 0.001). Notably, those study participants with a lower NT-proBNP, 60-day prognosis was not influenced by either baseline kidney function or the development of kidney impairment during admission [82]. In another study of patients hospitalized with AHF, those with both WRF and elevated BNP had more than a five-fold increased risk of 1-year cardiovascular death or heart-failure readmission compared with patients without either risk factor (HR 5.17; 95% CI 2.07–14.30; p = 0.0003) [83].

Cystatin C may provide complementary assessment of kidney filtration in AHF, but its interpretation depends on the compartment in which it is measured. Cystatin C is a 13-kDa endogenous cysteine protease inhibitor produced at a relatively constant rate by nucleated cells; in plasma, it is freely filtered by the glomerulus, nearly completely reabsorbed and catabolized by proximal tubular epithelial cells. Thus, plasma cystatin C reflects glomerular filtration and may be less influenced than creatinine by muscle mass or non-steady-state physiology. Among 328 patients hospitalized with worsening chronic HF and normal to moderately impaired kidney function, 52 cardiac deaths occurred over a median follow-up of 915 days; admission cystatin C, but not modified MDRD-derived eGFR, independently predicted cardiac mortality (p < 0.0001) [84]. In addition, supportive data from high-risk hospitalized populations suggest that serum cystatin C may detect acute declines in filtration earlier than creatinine; in one prospective cohort of 85 patients at risk for acute renal failure, a ≥ 50% rise in serum cystatin C preceded creatinine-defined RIFLE risk criteria by 1.5 ± 0.6 days, with ROC AUCs of 0.82 and 0.97 on the two preceding days [85]. Urinary cystatin C, however, reflects impaired proximal tubular handling: because filtered cystatin C is normally reabsorbed and metabolized by proximal tubular epithelial cells, increased urinary cystatin C may indicate proximal tubular dysfunction or injury [86].

Contemporary biomarkers of tubular injury—including neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin (NGAL), N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase (NAG), and kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1)—have been evaluated to help distinguish true renal injury from functional declines in glomerular filtration. NGAL, in particular, is rapidly upregulated in the kidney in response to tubular epithelial damage and has been associated with adverse outcomes in AHF. Early studies offered encouragement: patients who developed WRF had higher admission NGAL levels than those without WRF [87], and one cohort reported that an NGAL threshold of > 130 ng/mL predicted in-hospital WRF with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83 and 92% sensitivity [88]. However, results across studies have been mixed and often confounded by hemodynamic effects. In the ROSE-AHF trial – where all patients received aggressive high-dose loop diuretics – about 21% of subjects experienced worsened kidney function, yet this was not accompanied by any increase in NGAL, NAG, or KIM-1 concentration [89]. In fact, NGAL concentrations slightly decreased on average during intensive diuresis (–8.7 ng/mg; p < 0.001). Worsening of kidney function in this setting showed no significant association with changes in NGAL (p = 0.21), NAG (p = 0.46), or KIM-1 (p = 0.22). Similarly, in the AKINESIS trial [90], a multicenter prospective cohort of 927 patients hospitalized with AHF requiring intravenous diuretics, WRF occurred in 72 patients (7.8%), but plasma NGAL did not improve prediction of WRF beyond the first creatinine measurement, with similar AUCs for peak NGAL, first NGAL, and first creatinine (0.656, 0.647, and 0.652, respectively). NGAL also performed similarly to creatinine for prediction of in-hospital adverse events, with AUCs of 0.691 for first NGAL and 0.686 for first creatinine. Collectively, these findings indicate that plasma NGAL lacks diagnostic or prognostic value beyond creatinine in AHF.

Further insights come from a multicenter study in the GREAT (Global Research on Acute Conditions Team) network [91], which compared the prognostic utility of admission natriuretic peptides, NGAL, and sST2 for worsened kidney function in AHF. In this prospective emergency department cohort, 26% of patients developed worsened kidney function during hospitalization; those who worsened their GFR tended to have higher baseline levels of natriuretic peptides whereas tubular injury markers alone were less discriminating. Notably, NGAL by itself showed only modest predictive power, and sST2 was not significantly associated with worsened kidney function in this study. When biomarkers were considered in combination, a clear pattern emerged: the joint presence of elevated NGAL alongside a high natriuretic peptide identified patients at significantly greater risk for worsening of their kidney function (OR NT-proBNP+NGAL = 2.8; OR BNP+NGAL = 3.1, p < 0.05); this observation was confirmed in a validation cohort. By contrast, no single biomarker remained a standalone predictor of worsening of kidney function after multivariable adjustment in that analysis. Taken together, these data highlight a persistent unmet need: clinically meaningful kidney injury remains difficult to distinguish from hemodynamic or “functional” reductions in GFR during effective decongestion, and currently available tubular injury biomarkers have not yet solved this problem.

Hepatic and hepatorenal dysfunction

Liver dysfunction in AHF may reflect both systemic venous congestion and impaired hepatic perfusion. A predominantly cholestatic pattern, characterized by elevations in bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and gamma-glutamyl transferase, is commonly associated with elevated right-sided filling pressures and hepatic congestion, whereas marked elevations in aminotransferases may indicate acute hepatocellular injury related to reduced cardiac output and hypoperfusion. Abnormal liver function tests are common in AHF and provide prognostic information, particularly when hepatic dysfunction occurs alongside kidney or myocardial injury [74, 92].

Composite hepatorenal scores may further integrate these interacting domains. The Model for End-Stage Liver Disease excluding the international normalized ratio (MELD-XI) combines serum bilirubin and creatinine and may be particularly suitable in HF because its calculation is unaffected by vitamin K antagonist therapy. In a cohort of 203 patients hospitalized with AHF, higher MELD-XI scores at admission and on hospital days 2–3 were independently associated with 1-year mortality after adjustment for established prognostic factors, with adjusted hazard ratios of 1.11 per point (95% CI: 1.05–1.20; P < 0.001) and 1.14 per point (95% CI: 1.09–1.20; P < 0.001), respectively. Moreover, an increase in MELD-XI during hospitalization, observed in approximately one-third of patients, was associated with nearly a twofold higher risk of death at 1 year (adjusted HR: 1.97; 95% CI: 1.20–3.20; P < 0.005). Nevertheless, MELD-XI was developed outside the HF setting, remains primarily a prognostic rather than diagnostic tool, and has not been validated as a treatment target. Accordingly, it may complement—but should not replace—clinical, hemodynamic, and biomarker-based assessment of congestion, perfusion, and end-organ dysfunction [93].

Discharge planning

The pre-discharge period is a critical window for final risk assessment before transition to outpatient care. Biomarkers have substantial promise for assessing early post-discharge events, and therefore may play a role in this important moment.

Predicting post-discharge events:

Many studies have indicated that biomarker concentrations at discharge are more prognostic than admission values; this finding opens the possibility of clinical value. Persistently elevated NP values, or a failure to achieve at least a 30% decline from baseline, are among the strongest predictors of early rehospitalization and mortality [24, 94–96]. The use of a pre-discharge measurement of BNP or NT-proBNP is now embedded in HF clinical practice guidelines in the United States [9] (Table 1).

Marked pre-discharge BNP or NT-proBNP concentrations or failure to reduce after perceived adequate care might prompt clinicians to reconsider discharge readiness, intensify pharmacologic therapy, or arrange for early follow-up. However, its role is best understood as risk stratification rather than evidence-based biomarker-guided intensification to improve post-discharge outcomes. In PRIMA II, 405 patients hospitalized with acute decompensated HF and NT-proBNP > 1,700 ng/L were randomized after achieving clinical stability to NT-proBNP-guided therapy targeting a > 30% reduction from admission to discharge versus conventional therapy. Although the guided-therapy group more frequently achieved > 30% NT-proBNP reduction at discharge (80% vs. 64%; p = 0.001), this strategy did not improve 180-day all-cause mortality or HF readmission (HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.72–1.37; p = 0.99), days alive out of the hospital at 180 days (178 vs. 179 days; p = 0.39), or secondary clinical endpoints [27]. Thus, pre-discharge NP measurement provides important prognostic information and a useful baseline for early follow-up, but outcomes evidence does not support intensifying in-hospital therapy solely to achieve a predefined NT-proBNP reduction target after clinical stabilization.

With growing popularity for a “hospital at home” management strategy for less unstable AHF care, biomarkers also may serve as a gatekeeper for adjudicating safety for such an approach of care. The availability of a pre-discharge value also provides a useful baseline to connect with those measured at early follow up visits. Elevated predischarge sST2 or bio-ADM similarly identify patients at high risk [97, 98]. Multimarker panels, combining NPs, high-sensitivity troponins, and sST2, may therefore provide the most comprehensive assessment of residual risk [99, 100].

Post-discharge follow-up

The early post-discharge period after AHF hospitalization is characterized by heightened vulnerability, with recurrent congestion, incomplete GDMT optimization, renal dysfunction, and poor adherence contributing to early rehospitalization. Biomarkers measured during follow-up may help contextualize residual risk beyond the pre-discharge value and identify patients with recurrent hemodynamic stress or systemic myocardial vulnerability before overt clinical deterioration. Serial NPs remain the most established markers in this setting, providing a bridge between discharge risk assessment and early outpatient surveillance [24, 96, 97, 101]. However, other biomarkers may add complementary information. In the TRIUMPH cohort, which enrolled 496 patients with acute HF and obtained repeated blood samples during 1-year follow-up, the composite of all-cause mortality or HF rehospitalization occurred in 188 patients (40%) over a median follow-up of 325 days. Baseline sST2 was associated with the primary endpoint after adjustment for clinical factors and NT-proBNP (HR per 1-SD increase on the log2 scale, 1.30; 95% CI 1.08–1.56; p = 0.005). When serial measurements were incorporated, the prognostic association strengthened, with an adjusted HR of 1.85 per 1-SD increase in repeated sST2 levels (95% CI 1.02–3.33; p = 0.044), independent of repeated NT-proBNP measurements; sST2 concentrations also appeared to rise several weeks before the endpoint [101]. These findings support the concept that serial biomarker trajectories, particularly when integrating NPs with markers of myocardial stress, inflammation, or fibrosis such as sST2, may refine post-discharge risk assessment.

Importantly, outpatient biomarker surveillance should be interpreted as a risk-informed adjunct rather than a biomarker-only treatment strategy. Practical responses to persistently elevated or rising biomarker concentrations may include earlier clinical reassessment, medication and adherence review, focused evaluation for recurrent congestion, laboratory surveillance after GDMT changes, and rapid-access pathways for worsening symptoms. At present, however, evidence is insufficient to recommend routine serial measurement of sST2, galectin-3, IL-6, or other emerging biomarkers in all patients after discharge, and randomized data are needed to determine whether biomarker-guided outpatient management improves clinical outcomes.

Integration of biomarkers into clinical risk scores

A wide range of prognostic risk scores have been developed to support outcome prediction in AHF. Earlier models relied primarily on clinical variables, whereas contemporary scores increasingly integrate biomarkers to enhance accuracy and discrimination. These tools aim to provide objective estimates of short- and intermediate-term risk—ranging from in-hospital mortality to 7- and 30-day outcomes—thereby guiding triage, disposition, and early follow-up planning (Table 3).

The ADHERE classification tree [102], derived from a 65,000-patient registry, stratifies in-hospital mortality using a small set of admission clinical variables, achieving modest discrimination (c-statistic 0.69 in derivation, 0.67 in validation). Incorporation of biomarkers substantially enhances prognostic performance. In 48,629 hospitalizations with BNP measurements, mortality increased in a near-linear fashion across BNP quartiles, from 1.9% in the lowest to 6.0% in the highest quartile (adjusted OR for Q4 vs. Q1 = 2.23; 95% CI 1.91–2.62; p < 0.0001) [103]. A multimarker strategy further refined risk: among 42,636 admissions, combined elevation of BNP (> 840 ng/L) and troponin identified a subgroup with markedly higher in-hospital mortality (10.2% vs. 2.2%; adjusted OR = 3.00; 95% CI 2.47–3.66; p < 0.0001) [104]. Another subsequent risk score designed to predict in-hospital mortality, the GWTG-HF score, demonstrated good baseline discrimination in both derivation and validation cohorts (c-index 0.75). This performance was confirmed in external validation (c-statistic 0.763) and further improved with the addition of admission BNP (c-statistic 0.818), underscoring the value of biomarker integration in clinical risk stratification [105, 106].

Beyond in-hospital outcomes, several tools target early risk stratification in the ED. The EHMRG score predicts 7-day mortality in ED patients using 10 readily available clinical and laboratory variables [107]. In the derivation cohort, EHMRG demonstrated strong discrimination (AUC 0.805), with 7-day mortality ranging from 0.21% in the lowest-risk to 4.27% in the highest-risk strata among discharged patients. In external validation, removal of troponin reduced discrimination (c-statistic 0.747 to 0.740; p = 0.025), whereas incorporation of NPs improved performance (c-statistic 0.747 to 0.760; p = 0.007) [108]. Among ED-based models, MEESSI-AHF is one of the most robustly validated tools for early risk stratification [109]. Derived from 4,867 patients and externally validated in 3,229, it predicts 30-day all-cause mortality using 13 clinical and laboratory variables, including NPs and troponin. The model demonstrates excellent discrimination (c-statistic 0.836 in derivation, 0.828 in validation), with mortality ranging from 0.7% in the lowest-risk quintile to approximately 45% in the highest-risk decile.

In contrast to admission-based tools, the ELAN-HF score was specifically designed for use at hospital discharge to predict 180-day mortality and rehospitalization [110]. It integrates predischarge NT-proBNP concentrations and their relative change from admission with key clinical variables, reflecting both baseline disease severity and in-hospital treatment response. Incorporation of NT-proBNP improved the c-statistic from 0.71 to 0.78 (p < 0.001) and achieved a net reclassification improvement of 62%, underscoring its utility for post-discharge risk stratification and care planning.

Conclusions

AHF is a time-sensitive, biologically heterogeneous syndrome in which early diagnostic uncertainty and rapidly evolving risk demand objective tools beyond clinical assessment alone. Across the care continuum—from first medical contact through hospitalization, discharge, and early follow-up—circulating biomarkers provide a mechanistic readout of key pathophysiologic processes, thereby strengthening diagnostic precision and enabling more granular prognostication. Within this framework, natriuretic peptides remain the cornerstone biomarker across the AHF care continuum, from initial diagnosis and triage to in-hospital risk stratification and predischarge assessment. Multimarker strategies and integrated risk models further refine prognostication and may help identify patients who require closer clinical reassessment, intensified monitoring, or more structured transition-of-care planning; however, they should not be used to select or withhold GDMT without supporting randomized evidence.

Among emerging candidates, several biomarkers appear most likely to complement, rather than replace, natriuretic peptide and troponin testing. IGFBP7 is of particular interest because it may add diagnostic and prognostic information beyond NT-proBNP while capturing senescence, remodeling, and filling-pressure biology, especially in patients with diagnostic uncertainty or HFpEF-related phenotypes. Biomarkers of congestion biology, including CA-125 and bioactive adrenomedullin, may help quantify serosal or tissue congestion, decongestive response, and residual in-hospital risk. Markers of fibrosis, inflammation, and systemic stress, including sST2, galectin-3, IL-6, and GDF-15, may further refine risk stratification and biological phenotyping, although their extracardiac expression and limited disease specificity currently constrain routine use. Therefore, the most promising path forward is not a single replacement biomarker, but clinically integrated multimarker strategies that combine hemodynamic stress, myocardial injury, congestion, inflammation, fibrosis, and senescence signals at defined decision points.

As clinical practice evolves, contemporary guidelines now endorse routine natriuretic peptide testing in suspected AHF and recognize the prognostic value of predischarge measurements. However, the optimal clinical integration of emerging biomarkers remains an active area of investigation. Future efforts should prioritize prospective validation of biomarker-guided strategies, define actionable thresholds, and integrate multimarker signatures into pragmatic decision tools. By advancing a precision-medicine framework for AHF, such approaches have the potential to accelerate diagnosis, personalize care, and ultimately improve outcomes in this high-risk population.
