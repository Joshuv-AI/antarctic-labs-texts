Time-adaptive HénonNets for separable Hamiltonian systems
\firstname Konrad \lastname Janik, \firstname Peter \lastname Benner

Abstract
Measurement data is often sampled irregularly, i.e., not on equidistant time grids. This is also true for Hamiltonian systems. However, existing machine learning methods, which learn symplectic integrators, such as SympNets [ 1 ] and HénonNets [ 2 ] still require training data generated by fixed step sizes. To learn time-adaptive symplectic integrators, an extension to SympNets called TSympNets is introduced in [ 3 ] . The aim of this work is to do a similar extension for HénonNets. We propose a novel neural network architecture called T-HénonNets, which is symplectic by design and can handle adaptive time steps. We also extend the T-HénonNet architecture to non-autonomous Hamiltonian systems. Additionally, we provide universal approximation theorems for both new architectures for separable Hamiltonian systems and discuss why it is difficult to handle non-separable Hamiltonian systems with the proposed methods. To investigate these theoretical approximation capabilities, we perform different numerical experiments.

1.2
Hamiltonian systems

The problems we want to consider in this work are from the class of Hamiltonian systems.
Hamiltonian systems are given by

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=J^{-1}\nabla H(x(t),t),\qquad x(t_{0})=x_{0}

(1)

with
x=(p^{T},q^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and

\displaystyle J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}.

A Hamiltonian system (
1

\partial_{t}H(x,t)=0
. Otherwise, it is called non-autonomous.

It is well known that the flow of a Hamiltonian system is symplectic
[
5
]
. Therefore, many modern machine learning methods that try to learn the flow of Hamiltonian systems aim to enforce symplecticity in one way or another
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
10
,
11
,
18
,
12
]
. HénonNets
[
2
]
and SympNets
[
1
]
provide a neural Network architecture that is symplectic by design. We aim to extend the HénonNet architecture to be time-adaptive and suitable for non-autonomous Hamiltonian systems, i.e., systems, where the Hamiltonian
H(x,t)
explicitly depends on time, like SympNets
[
1
]
were extended in
[
3
]
.

2
HénonNets

HénonNets are an intrinsically symplectic neural network architecture, which were introduced in
[
2
]
. In this section, we first introduce the original HénonNets from
[
2
]
. Then we extend them to be time-adaptive, and lastly, we propose a new architecture for non-autonomous Hamiltonian systems.

2.1
Original HénonNets

HénonNets are compositions of mappings similar to Hénon-like maps. The layers are given by a composition of four mappings like

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla V_{i}(p)-q\\
p+\eta_{i}\end{pmatrix},

with trainable neural networks
V_{i}
and trainable parameters
\eta_{i}
. For the purpose of this work, we use fully-connected neural networks with one hidden layer,
V_{i}\in\Sigma_{d}(\sigma)
and activation function
\sigma
, but other architectures are possible as well. This way we can analytically calculate the gradient, which saves time in the forward pass.
The potential
V_{i}
and shift
\eta_{i}
are the same throughout one layer, but can be different from layer to layer.

Definition 2.1

(HénonNets
[
2
]
)

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla V_{i}(p)-q\\
p+\eta_{i}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

with a fully-connected neural network with one hidden layer
V_{i}\in\Sigma_{d}(\sigma)
and activation function
\sigma,
and a trainable parameter
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
Hénonlayer
is defined by applying the same Hénon-like map (
2
) four times

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}=\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}.

We define the set of
HénonNets
as finite compositions of Hénonlayers

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{H}}:=\left\{\psi=\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}}\,\middle|\,m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

We can easily check that the maps defined in (
2
) are symplectic. Using the fact that symplectic mappings form a group with composition as their group operation, we observe that all HénonNets are symplectic by design.

Furthermore, HénonNets can approximate any symplectic map arbitrarily well, since every symplectic map can be approximated by a composition of
4m,\penalty\ m\in\mathbb{N}
, maps defined in (
2
)
[
20
, Theorem 1]
.

2.2
Time-adaptive HénonNets

Following the time-adaptive extension of SympNets
[
3
]
, the analogous idea would be to replace (
2
) by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V,\eta}\left(h,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}h\nabla V&-I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Unfortunately, this comes with some problems. We will show in
Section

3.2
that these T-HénonNets can only approximate Hamiltonian systems where the Hamiltonian is given by
H(p,q)=V(-p)+V(q)
. Even though it is not obvious, the solution to this problem is a rather simple change in the architecture given by the following definition.

Definition 2.2

(T-HénonNets)

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\left(h,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}h\nabla V_{i}&-I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{i}^{(p)}\\
\eta_{i}^{(q)}\end{pmatrix}

(4)

with a fully-connected neural network with one hidden layer
V_{i}\in\Sigma_{d}(\sigma)
and activation function
\sigma,
and a trainable parameter
\eta_{i}=((\eta_{i}^{(p)})^{T},(\eta_{i}^{(q)})^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. A
T-Hénonlayer
is defined by applying the same Hénon-like map (
4
) four times

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}(h,x)=(\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}(h,\cdot)\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}(h,\cdot)\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}(h,\cdot)\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}(h,\cdot))(x).

We define the set of
time-adaptive HénonNets (T-HénonNets)
as finite compositions of T-Hénonlayers

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{TH}}:=\left\{\psi(h,x)=(\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}(h,\cdot)\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}}(h,\cdot))(x)\,\middle|\,m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Note that the only difference to the T-HénonNets based on (
3
) is that we add an additional bias
\eta^{(p)}
. Obviously, this change does not impact the symplecticity of the T-HénonNets. We will show in
Section

3.1
that the T-HénonNets from
Definition

2.2
are universal approximators for symplectic maps.

2.3
Non-autonomous time-adaptive HénonNets

Now we want to focus on non-autonomous Hamiltonian systems, i.e.,
H(p,q,t)
. As discussed in
[
3
]
, it is possible to transform a non-autonomous Hamiltonian system into one that is autonomous. However, this phase space extension generally does not preserve the separability of the Hamiltonian system. Current explicit time-adaptive symplectic neural network architectures like TSympNets
[
3
]
and our T-HénonNets are not able to deal with non-separable Hamiltonian systems. Hence, we have to deal with the non-autonomous case instead of transforming the system into an autonomous, but non-separable Hamiltonian system.

Just like the non-autonomous extension of TSympNets in
[
3
]
, the idea for non-autonomous T-HénonNets is to allow the potentials
V_{i}
to explicitly depend on time.

Definition 2.3

(Non-autonomous T-HénonNets)

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\left(h,t,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}h\nabla_{x}V_{i}(t,\cdot)&-I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{i}^{(p)}\\
\eta_{i}^{(q)}\end{pmatrix}

with a fully-connected neural network with one hidden layer
V_{i}\in\Sigma_{d+1}(\sigma)
and activation function
\sigma,
and a trainable parameter
\eta_{i}=((\eta_{i}^{(p)})^{T},(\eta_{i}^{(q)})^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Additionally, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}^{(m)}\left(h,t,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}t+\frac{h}{4m}\\
\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}\left(h,t,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)\end{pmatrix}

(5)

A
NAT-Hénonlayer
is defined by applying the same Hénon-like map (
5
) four times

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}(h,t,x)=(\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}^{(m)}(h,\cdot)\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}^{(m)}(h,\cdot)\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}^{(m)}(h,\cdot)\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}^{(m)}(h,\cdot))(t,x).

We define the set of
non-autonomous, time-adaptive HénonNets (NAT-HénonNets)
as finite compositions of NAT-Hénonlayers

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{NATH}}:=\left\{\psi(h,t,x)=(\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}(h,\cdot)\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}}(h,\cdot))(t,x)\,\middle|\,m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

3
Theory of T-HénonNets

3.2
Limitations

In this subsection, we discuss the limitations of T-HénonNets. As promised in
Section

2.2
, we start by showing that the naive T-HénonNets based on (
3
) are limited to systems with Hamiltonians of the form
H(p,q)=V(-p)+V(q)
. After that, we show that T-HénonNets cannot deal with non-separable Hamiltonian systems.

To get both of these results, we observe that in general for the flow
\Phi
it holds

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi(0,x)=\dot{x}=J^{-1}\nabla H(x).

So we can use
\partial_{h}\psi(0,x)
to determine which Hamiltonian systems a T-HénonNet can approximate. Therefore, it is useful to observe

\displaystyle\partial_{h}\psi(0,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}}{\partial x}(0,(\mathbf{H}_{V_{m-1},\eta_{m-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}})(0,x))\frac{d}{dh}(\mathbf{H}_{V_{m-1},\eta_{m-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}})(0,x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}}{\partial h}(0,(\mathbf{H}_{V_{m-1},\eta_{m-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}})(0,x))

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\frac{d}{dh}(\mathbf{H}_{V_{m-1},\eta_{m-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{V_{1},\eta_{1}})(0,x)+\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}}{\partial h}(0,x),

which holds because
\mathbf{H}_{V_{m},\eta_{m}}(0,x)=x
. By repeating this argument, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{h}\psi(0,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}_{V_{i},\eta_{i}}}{\partial h}(0,x).

(10)

With this preparation, we are ready to take a closer look at why the limitations of the naive T-HénonNets.

3.2.1
Naive T-HénonNets

We recall (
3
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V,\eta}\left(h,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}h\nabla V&-I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}

and observe

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}_{V,\eta}}{\partial h}\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla V(-q)+\nabla V(q-\eta)\\
-\nabla V(p)+\nabla V(-p-\eta)\end{pmatrix}.

Using (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{h}\psi\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla V_{i}(-q)+\nabla V_{i}(q-\eta_{i})\\
-\nabla V_{i}(p)+\nabla V_{i}(-p-\eta_{i})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla V(q)\\
-\nabla V(-p)\end{pmatrix}=J^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla V(-p)\\
\nabla V(q)\end{pmatrix},

with
V(y):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}-V_{i}(-y)-V_{i}(y-\eta_{i})
. So the associated Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
is given by
\tilde{H}(p,q)=V(-p)+V(q)
.

3.2.2
Non-separable Hamiltonian systems

In this subsection, we show that T-HénonNets cannot deal with non-seprable Hamiltonian systems.
To show that these limitations were not introduced by
Definition

2.2
, we generalize their definition. We allow
\eta=\eta(t)
and
V=V(h,p)
to be time-dependent as well as different potentials
V
and shifts
\eta
in each layer, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{V_{4i},\eta_{4i}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{4(i-1)+3},\eta_{4(i-1)+3}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{4(i-1)+2},\eta_{4(i-1)+2}}\circ\mathcal{H}_{V_{4(i-1)+1},\eta_{4(i-1)+1}}

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{m}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{H}_{1}.

Since we want to strongly enforce
\psi(0,x)=x
, we implement
\mathbf{H}_{i}(0,x)=x
by assuming
\nabla V_{i}(0,x)\equiv 0
for
i=1,\ldots,4m
and
\eta_{4k+1}(0)=\eta_{4k+3}(0),\penalty\ \eta_{4k+2}(0)=\eta_{4k+4}(0)
for
k=0,\ldots,m-1.
Under these assumptions, we observe

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}_{i}}{\partial h}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{h}\nabla V_{4i}(0,q-\eta_{4(i-1)+2}^{(q)}(0))-\partial_{h}\nabla V_{4(i-1)+2}(0,-q+\eta_{4(i-1)+1}^{(p)}(0))\\
\partial_{h}\nabla V_{4(i-1)+3}(0,-p-\eta_{4(i-1)+1}^{(q)}(0)+\eta_{4(i-1)+2}^{(p)}(0))-\partial_{h}\nabla V_{4(i-1)+1}(0,p)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\eta}_{4(i-1)+1}^{(q)}(0)-\dot{\eta}^{(q)}_{4(i-1)+3}(0)-\dot{\eta}_{4(i-1)+2}^{(p)}(0)+\dot{\eta}_{4i}^{(p)}(0)\\
-\dot{\eta}^{(q)}_{4(i-1)+2}(0)+\dot{\eta}^{(q)}_{4i}(0)-\dot{\eta}^{(p)}_{4(i-1)+1}(0)+\dot{\eta}_{4(i-1)+3}^{(p)}(0)\end{pmatrix}.

Using
eq.

10
again, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{h}\psi(0,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial t}(0,x)=\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla V(q)\\
\nabla K(p)\end{pmatrix},

(11)

with

\displaystyle V(q)

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\partial_{h}V_{4i}(0,q-\eta_{4(i-1)+2}^{(q)}(0))+\partial_{h}V_{4(i-1)+2}(0,-q+\eta_{4(i-1)+1}^{(p)}(0))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(\dot{\eta}_{4(i-1)+1}^{(q)}(0)-\dot{\eta}^{(q)}_{4(i-1)+3}(0)-\dot{\eta}_{4(i-1)+2}^{(p)}(0)+\dot{\eta}_{4i}^{(p)}(0))^{T}q,

\displaystyle K(p)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}-\partial_{h}V_{4(i-1)+3}(0,-p-\eta_{4(i-1)+1}^{(q)}(0)+\eta^{(p)}_{4(i-1)+2}(0))-\partial_{h}V_{4(i-1)+1}(0,p)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(-\dot{\eta}^{(q)}_{4(i-1)+2}(0)+\dot{\eta}^{(q)}_{4i}(0)-\dot{\eta}^{(p)}_{4(i-1)+1}(0)+\dot{\eta}_{4(i-1)+3}^{(p)}(0))^{T}p.

So even in this very general formulation, we have
\tilde{H}=K(p)+V(q)
and therefore, observe that T-HénonNets are not able to represent the flow of non-separable Hamiltonian systems.

4.3

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we developed a new time-adaptive symplectic neural network architecture, which we coined T-HénonNets and established a universal approximation theorem for them limited to separable Hamiltonian systems. This limitation is not an artifact of our proofs, but a direct result of the used architecture shown in
Section

3.2
theoretically and
Section

4.2
experimentally. Additionally, we proposed a novel intrinsically symplectic, time-adaptive neural network architecture to handle non-autonomous Hamiltonian systems in NAT-HénonNets. We were able to show that they are universal approximators for the flows of separable non-autonomous Hamiltonian systems. Lastly, we verified our theoretical findings on three numerical experiments.

The obvious open research question is how to adapt T-HénonNets such that they will be able to handle non-separable Hamiltonian systems as well.
