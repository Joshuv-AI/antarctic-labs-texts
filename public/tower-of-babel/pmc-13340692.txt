Introduction

Acute intestinal obstruction is a common surgical emergency. The main causes are postoperative adhesions, strangulated external hernias, and tumors. Internal hernias are rare, accounting for just 0.6–5.8% of cases, but they carry a high risk of strangulation and ischemia of the digestive tract, which can quickly become life-threatening[1–3].

A transmesocolic internal hernia occurs when small bowel loops pass through a breach in the mesocolon, which can be either congenital or acquired (following surgery or trauma)[4,5]. It is a rare type of internal hernia, with paraduodenal hernias being more common[1,6]. Preoperative diagnosis is difficult due to nonspecific clinical symptoms. The gold standard for identifying suggestive signs, such as abnormal grouping of small bowel loops, abnormal positioning, mesenteric mass effect, or signs of parietal distress, is abdominal CT with contrast injection[2,3].

We present a case of digestive obstruction due to an internal transmesocolic hernia in a young patient. We also propose a review of the literature to discuss the epidemiological, diagnostic, and therapeutic aspects of this rare condition.

This work has been reported following the SCARE Guidelines 2025[7].

No AI tools were used in the research and development of the manuscript.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old male patient, a smoker with a history of epigastric gurgling, was admitted for an occlusive syndrome that had been developing for 4 days.

Clinically, the patient was conscious and stable, with a slightly distended abdomen and peri-umbilical tenderness.

Discussion

Internal hernias are a rare cause of intestinal obstruction, accounting for between 0.6% and 5.8% of cases, depending on the study[1,3]. Transmesocolic hernias are much less common than paraduodenal hernias, the latter of which are the most frequent[1,2,4–6]. They result from small bowel loops passing through a congenital or acquired breach in the mesocolon, exposing the patient to a high risk of vascular strangulation.

The clinical presentation is often nonspecific, alternating between intermittent abdominal pain and subocclusive episodes until acute, complicated obstruction sets in. In the absence of a previous abdominal scar, as in our case, there should be suspicion of an unusual cause of small bowel obstruction, particularly an internal hernia[2].

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scanning is the key diagnostic test. It can identify abnormal bowel looping, displacement of the mesenteric vascular pedicles, a transmesocolic mass effect, and signs of parietal distress such as wall thickening, lack of enhancement, or intraperitoneal effusion[3,8]. In our experience, the presence of early CT signs of digestive distress has prompted urgent surgical intervention.

Complicated internal hernias are exclusively treated surgically. This involves reducing the herniated loops, rigorously assessing their viability, and closing the mesocolic defect to prevent recurrence[1,2]. If there are signs of irreversible ischemia or necrosis, intestinal resection is required. In our case, progression to ileal distress necessitated ileocecal resection, a procedure that was justified by the involvement of the ileocecal junction and the risk of perforation or secondary peritonitis.

Although digestive resection is more debilitating, it ensures the complete removal of nonviable segments and limits postoperative septic complications. The prognosis mainly depends on early diagnosis and rapid intervention. Morbidity and mortality are directly correlated with delays in treatment and the extent of intestinal necrosis[8].

Therefore, internal transmesocolic hernias should be considered in any case of small bowel obstruction in patients with no significant surgical history, although they are rare. The prognosis can be determined by the early use of imaging and rapid surgical exploration, which can avoid extensive intestinal resections[9].
