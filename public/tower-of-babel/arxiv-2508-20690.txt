Homogenized phase transition model for perforated ferromagnetic media
Catherine Choquet 1, Mohamed Ouhadan 1,2 and Mouhcine Tilioua 2

Abstract
This work presents a rigorous prediction of the effective equations governing the paramagnetic-ferromagnetic phase transition in a perforated three-dimensional body. Assuming a periodic distribution of perforations, we investigate the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the equations describing the thermodynamic and electromagnetic properties of the material as the period of the microstructure tends to zero. The microscopic model is a phase-field model within the Ginzburg-Landau framework for second-order phase transitions, where the phase-field is directly related to the magnetization vector. This model couples a nonlinear equation for the magnetization with the quasi-static Maxwell system and another nonlinear equation for the temperature. The primary mathematical challenge lies in homogenizing these equations, which exhibit a complex doubly non-linear structure. Additionally, the extension operators used within the homogenization framework precludes the application of standard Aubin–Lions compactness arguments. Our analysis employs two-scale convergence in conjunction with a two-scale decomposition based on an appropriate dilation operator. The nonlinearities are primarily addressed by means of a variant of compensated compactness and a Vitali compactness argument. From the perspective of practical applications, this work enables the explicit calculation of a Curie temperature tensor, capturing at the macroscopic scale the coupled effect of the material’s geometric structure and its magnetic permeability tensor.

1
Introduction

The theory of periodic homogenization focuses on partial differential equations whose coefficients exhibit periodic oscillations with a small period, denoted by
\epsilon
. The main objective of this theory is to derive a homogenized equation that describes the global behavior of the system as the oscillation period tends to zero. In other words, it seeks to establish an equivalent partial differential equation, whose solutions represent the weak limits of the original equation’s solutions as
\epsilon
decreases. For a comprehensive introduction to this theory, as well as general surveys, the works
[
7
,
15
,
23
,
25
,
38
]
provide essential references.
A major milestone in this theory was the introduction of the concept of two-scale convergence by
[
32
]
, which paved the way for the analysis of more complex systems. In its initial version, this convergence, explored in
[
32
,
1
]
, is referred to as “weak two-scale convergence". However, it became crucial to define a more robust form of convergence, known as “strong two-scale convergence", as presented in the works
[
40
,
27
]
. This strong convergence is particularly valuable for dealing with fully nonlinear problems, such as nonsmooth elastoplasticity, discussed in
[
31
,
41
,
36
,
20
,
21
]
, and also allows for more efficient numerical approximation of such problems, as mentioned in
[
26
]
.

The present work applies the concepts of two-scale convergence to a phenomenon of nonlinear phase transitions, which play a significant role in condensed matter physics. This area is rich with examples of such transitions, including magnetic, ferroelectric, superfluid, and superconducting transitions. We are interested in phase transitions in ferromagnetism, which refers to the ability of certain materials to become magnetized in the presence of an external magnetic field. In this state, the magnetic moments align in the same direction as the field, and even after the field is removed, part of this alignment is retained. However, when the temperature exceeds a specific threshold, known as the Curie temperature
\theta_{c}
, the residual alignment disappears, and the material reverts to the paramagnetic phase. The transition from the paramagnetic to the ferromagnetic state is modelled as a second-order phase transition (see
[
11
]
and
[
24
]
).
For the mathematical model, our starting point is the work of Berti et al. in
[
8
]
where a system of partial differential equations describing the coupled behavior of magnetization, temperature and magnetic field is provided.

We are interested in ferromagnetic materials with periodic perforated structure.
The characteristic size of the holes is assumed to be small, denoted by
\epsilon
. As emphasized in the seminal paper
[
16
]
, the homogenization process in this context involves letting
\epsilon
tend to zero. This presents two key challenges: (1) handling the rapid oscillations within the material due to the presence of the holes, and (2) accounting for the changes in the domain structure as the number of holes increases. In this work we shall make use of the two-scale convergence method as described in
[
1
,
33
]
. We also adopt the formalism introduced by Visintin in
[
40
]
which may be viewed as the use of an appropriate dilation operator as in
[
5
,
13
]
.

The paper is organized as follows:
The perforated geometry and the model for paramagnetic-ferromagnetic phase transitions are described in Section
2
.
We also introduce various notations.

Since we essentially use properties of two-scale convergence, the basics of this concept are recalled in Section
3
, together with other auxiliary tools.

The main result is stated in Section
4
.
It is the effective model describing the phase transition, justified by the analysis of the asymptotic behavior of the solutions of the micromodel in a periodically perforated domain.
The rest of the paper is devoted to the proof of the main result.
In Section
5
, the necessary uniform estimates are established.
Section
6
is finally dedicated to the homogenization process,
i.e.
, the passage to the limit in the different equations.

2.2
Microscopic model

Let us now describe the model equations. We assume that a ferromagnetic material occupies the domain
\Omega^{\epsilon}
.
At the microscopic scale, we adapt the model derived in the paper
[
8
]
of Berti
et al.
(see also Belov
[
6
]
).
It combines phenomenological constitutive equations for the magnetization
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}
, the magnetic field
\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}
and the absolute temperature
\theta^{\epsilon}
.
We enrich somehow the model proposed in
[
8
]
by introducing some anisotropy in the problem (about anisotropy, see also Remark
2.2
below).
More precisely we consider two heterogeneity scales in the ferromagnetic material.
First, using
\boldsymbol{A}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
defined before, we replace scalar coefficients by matrices.
Then, most importantly, microscopic heterogeneity is modelled by oscillations, here assumed of period of order
\epsilon
, thus the introduction of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{\epsilon}
,
\boldsymbol{K}^{\epsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\epsilon}
.

On the one hand, the temperature
\theta^{\epsilon}
is ruled by the following equation

\partial_{t}(c(\theta^{\epsilon}))-\theta^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}-\mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{K}^{\epsilon}(x)k(\theta^{\epsilon})\nabla\theta^{\epsilon})=r,

(1)

where the functions
c(\theta^{\epsilon})
and
k(\theta^{\epsilon})
are respectively the thermal conductivity and the specific temperature of the material and
\boldsymbol{K}^{\epsilon}
is a thermal diffusion matrix that depends on the characteristics and geometry of the material.

Studies on phase transitions consider various laws for heat conductivity and specific heat (see, for example,
[
9
,
11
]
). Here, we focus on the case studied by Berti
et al.

[
8
]
, where heat conductivity and specific heat depend on the absolute temperature according to polynomial laws
, as follows:

c(\theta^{\epsilon})=c_{1}\theta^{\epsilon}+c_{2}(\theta^{\epsilon})^{2}/2,\qquad k(\theta^{\epsilon})=k_{0}+k_{1}\theta^{\epsilon},

(2)

where
c_{1},c_{2},k_{0},k_{1}>0
.
Like
[
8
]
also, we develop the derivatives in (
1
) and neglect the quadratic terms in
\partial_{i}\theta^{\epsilon}
.
Dividing by
\theta^{\epsilon}
, we get

c_{1}\partial_{t}\ln(\theta^{\epsilon})+c_{2}\partial_{t}\theta^{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}-\mathrm{div}(\boldsymbol{K}^{\epsilon}\nabla(k_{0}\ln(\theta^{\epsilon})+k_{1}\theta^{\epsilon}))=\hat{r}

(3)

with
\displaystyle\hat{r}=r/\theta^{\epsilon}
.
For simplicity, assume
\hat{r}\equiv 0
.

On the other hand, the evolution of the ferromagnetic material is characterized by the magnetization
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}
which depends on the temperature
\theta^{\epsilon}
as follows:

\gamma\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}={\rm div}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\epsilon}\nabla\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})-\theta_{c}(|\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}|^{2}-1)\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}-\theta^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}~~~~\mbox{in}~\Omega^{\epsilon}_{T}.

(4)

The gyromagnetic ratio

\gamma
is a given positive real number and
\theta_{c}
is the Curie temperature.
The magnetic field
\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}
is the stray field that appears in the Maxwell’s equations. The magnetization
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}
links the magnetic field
\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}
and the magnetic
induction
\mathbf{B}^{\epsilon}
by the relation

\mathbf{B}^{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}+\chi^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon},

where

The magnetic field
\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}
satisfies

{\rm div}\mathbf{B}^{\epsilon}=0
.
Hence the magnetization

induces a magnetic field

which is given by

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm curl\ }(\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon})=0,\quad\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{h}^{\epsilon}+\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\right)=0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\

The magnetic field

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm div}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\epsilon}\nabla\varphi^{\epsilon}+\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})=0~~~~\mbox{in}~\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\epsilon}\nabla\varphi^{\epsilon}+\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})\cdot\nu^{\epsilon}=0~~\mbox{ on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega^{\epsilon}}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 2.1
.

The assumption that the initial magnetic field satisfies (
7
) is introduced to ensure the physical compatibility of the initial data with the magnetostatic constraint, and to formulate the coupled evolution problem from the outset. Mathematically, this assumption guarantees that the magnetic potential
\varphi^{\epsilon}_{0}
is well-defined, so that the total energy
\mathcal{E}^{\epsilon}
is finite at the initial time

, provided that
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})

Indeed, following Amrouche et al.
[
4
]
, we introduce the weighted Sobolev space

, adapted to our geometry, and referred to as the Beppo-Levi space, defined by

\displaystyle W_{bp}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon})=\Big\{\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon})\mbox{ such that }\nabla\omega\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon})\mbox{ and}~\frac{\omega(x)}{\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon})\Big\},

and equipped with scalar product

\displaystyle(\omega,\psi)_{W_{bp}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\epsilon}}\nabla\omega\cdot\nabla\psi\ {\rm d}x
.
Using Lax-Milgram theorem (see, for example,
[
35
]
), one proves that, for any
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}_{0}
given in
L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})
, Problem (

.

Moreover, in order for
\mathcal{E}^{\epsilon}
to be finite at time

, it is also necessary that
\nabla\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})
and
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}_{0}\in L^{4}(\Omega^{\epsilon})
. Since the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon})
embeds compactly into

, it is natural to assume that
\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon})
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let us provide some details regarding how we account for anisotropy in the model.
According to Berti et al.
[
8
]
, (
6a
) should be replaced by

\gamma\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}={\rm div}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\epsilon}\nabla\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})-\theta_{c}f_{1}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})-\theta^{\epsilon}f_{2}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})+\nabla\varphi^{\epsilon}

with
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
in the form

f_{1}(\boldsymbol{m})=\frac{1}{4}(\mathbb{F}\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m}-f_{2}(\boldsymbol{m}),\quad f_{2}(\boldsymbol{m})=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}\cdot(\boldsymbol{m}\otimes\boldsymbol{m})

where
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathcal{F}
are fourth-order and second-order positive definite tensors, respectively.
The isotropic assumption consists in choosing for
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathcal{F}
the identity tensor, thus recovering Eq. (
6a
), and in replacing
\boldsymbol{A}
by a scalar function.
In the present work, we use
\boldsymbol{A}
for taking into account the anisotropy but we keep the isotropic form of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.
This latter choice is for the sake of clarity in the computations. We emphasize that the characteristic nonlinearities are represented by this choice.
The only important assumption in the general setting with
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
would be that the existence result in Theorem
2.1
remains true.

2.3
Reformulation of the homogenization problem

We now aim to study the asymptotic behavior of Problem (
6
) as
\epsilon
tends to 0 in order to rigorously derive the corresponding homogenized model.
Classically the difficulty lies in the nonlinear terms.
Here the double nonlinearity in (
6b
) makes the passage to the limit significantly complex. It is worth noting that this equation can also be written in the following form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{1}(\theta^{\epsilon})-\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}={\rm div}F_{2}\left(x,\frac{x}{\epsilon},\theta^{\epsilon},\nabla\theta^{\epsilon}\right),

with
F_{1}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
and
F_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by

F_{1}(s)=c_{1}\ln(s)+c_{2}s,\quad\text{and}\quad F_{2}(x,y,s,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{K}(x,y)\left(\frac{k_{0}}{s}+k_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{\xi}.

In general, the homogenization of this type of equations is based on certain conditions imposed on
F_{i},\ i=1,2
. We cite, for example,
[
22
]
for the case of homogenization of parabolic equations in fixed domains. We also mention
[
29
]
and
[
30
]
for the case of perforated domains with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, respectively.
In particular, common assumptions imposed on
F_{1}
are: (i) Function
F_{1}
is continuous and non-decreasing, with
F_{1}(0)=0
; (ii) There exists a constant
\delta>0
such that, for every
r
and
S
satisfying
0<r<S
, there exists a constant
C(r,S)>0
such that

|F_{1}(s_{1})-F_{1}(s_{2})|\geq C(r,S)|s_{1}-s_{2}|^{\delta}

for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in[-S,S]
with
r<|s_{1}|<R
.
Assumptions (i) and (ii) play a crucial role in characterizing the homogenized model because they allow for strong convergence of the solution. For instance (i) is essential for proving the strong convergence of
F_{1}
valued in the zero extension of the solution (the nature of this extension varies depending on the context: in
[
29
]
and
[
30
]
, it refers to the extension by zero inside the perforated regions, whereas in
[
22
]
, it concerns the extension outside a set defined in the proof of Lemma 1.9 in
[
3
]
).
In our case,
F_{1}
do not satisfy (i) nor (ii), of course due to the logarithmic function.

In brief, our concern is the possibly very singular behaviour of the logarithmic function that characterizes the time dynamics, instead of the nonlinearity or the possible degenerescence of the time derivative.
Therefore, we adopt a different strategy to pass to the limit.

The first step is a reformulation of the problem.
Let
F
be the function defined in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
by

F(x)=c_{1}\ln(x)+c_{2}x.

(9)

Auxiliary tools

The aim of the paper is now to pass to the limit

This section compiles the principal convergence analysis tools that will be utilized subsequently.
We begin by recalling in Subsection

[

The strength of this method lies in its natural ability to capture the microscopic scale during the limiting process
However, passing to the limit in the nonlinear terms of Problem (

We make use of a dilation operator, as proposed in
[

The unfolding method transforms the problem into a fixed domain. It is thus renowned for allowing the use of standard notions of weak and strong convergence. However, we will primarily use it to concisely describe the limiting processes in the nonlinear terms. The essential compactness argument will first be obtained directly within the framework of two-scale convergence, using a compensated compactness argument between different types of solution extensions. 3.1

Definition 3.1

), is bounded by parameters depending on the model chosen for the heat conductivity and the specific heat (see (
2
) where
c_{1},c_{2},k_{0},k_{1}>0
):

0<\pi_{0}=\min\Big(\frac{k_{0}}{c_{1}},\frac{k_{1}}{c_{2}}\Big)\leq g(x)\leq\max\Big(\frac{k_{0}}{c_{1}},\frac{k_{1}}{c_{2}}\Big)=\pi_{1},\ \forall x>0.

(27)

Using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})v^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x\leq\|\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{3/2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}\leq\frac{C(t)}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v^{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{3}(\Omega^{\epsilon})},

(28)

where
t\mapsto C(t)
is uniformly bounded in
L^{1}(0,T)
since
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))
(see Lemma
5.1
).
Next, the interpolation inequality and once again the Young inequality leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}}\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}\leq\left(\frac{C^{2}}{8\pi_{0}\alpha_{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\pi_{0}\alpha_{2}}{2}}\right)\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}^{2}+\frac{\pi_{0}\alpha_{2}}{2}\|\nabla v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}^{2}.

Inserting the latter result into (
28
) and bearing in mind the coercivity of
\boldsymbol{K}^{\epsilon}
, we deduce from (
26
), after integrating over time, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}|v^{\epsilon}|^{2}+\frac{\pi_{0}\alpha_{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}|\nabla v^{\epsilon}|^{2}\leq\frac{C(t)}{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\left(\frac{C^{2}}{8\pi_{0}\alpha_{2}}+\frac{\pi_{0}\alpha_{2}}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}|v^{\epsilon}|^{2}

for any
\tau\in(0,T)
. According to Gr
\ddot{\mbox{o}}
nwall’s lemma, the following uniform estimates follow:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))}\leq C,\quad\|v^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))}\leq C.\end{array}

For the estimate of
\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))
, it is sufficient to show that there exists a constant
C
such that,

\Bigr|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}\Phi{\rm d}x{\rm d}t\Bigr|\leq C\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))},\ \forall\Phi\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon})).

(29)

Multiplying (
12b
) by
\Phi
and integrating over
\Omega^{\epsilon}_{T}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}_{T}}\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}\Phi{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\epsilon}_{T}}\boldsymbol{K}^{\epsilon}(x)g(\theta^{\epsilon})\nabla v^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\Phi{\rm d}x{\rm d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}^{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})\Phi{\rm d}x{\rm d}t.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon}_{T})}+C\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))}.

Using once again an interpolation inequality together with Poincaré’s inequality, we get
\displaystyle\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))}\leq C\biggr(\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\epsilon})}\biggr)^{1/2}\leq C\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\epsilon}))}
.
Lemma
5.2
is proved.
∎

6.2
Strong two-scale convergence results

Weak convergence results (
31
)-(
33
) are not sufficient for passing to the limit in the nonlinear terms of Problem (
12
).
A classical tool for obtaining compactness results when dealing with evolution problems, is the Aubin-Lions argument.
It does not apply here because an estimate for a term in the form
\partial_{t}f^{\epsilon}
does not provide information about
\partial_{t}(P^{\epsilon}f^{\epsilon})
.

To address this problem, we propose a kind of compensated compactness argument for passing to the limit in the product of the two kind of extensions used in the paper (extension by zero and extension by operator
P^{\epsilon}
). The following lemma, obtained using Proposition
3.2
, is the first step toward a strong two-scale convergence result.

Lemma 6.2
.

The following convergence results hold true:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{T}}(P^{\epsilon}v^{\epsilon})\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\phi{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\bar{\chi}v^{2}\phi{\rm d}x{\rm d}t,\ \forall\phi\in\mathcal{C}((0,T)\times\overline{\Omega}),

(35)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{T}}(P^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\boldsymbol{\psi}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\bar{\chi}\boldsymbol{m}^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t,\ \forall\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathcal{C}((0,T)\times\overline{\Omega}).

(36)

Proof.

In Prop.
3.2
, set
n=3
,
q=2
,
p=2
. Consequently,
\alpha=2<np/(n-p)
.
Set
a_{\epsilon}=P^{\epsilon}v^{\epsilon}
and
b_{\epsilon}=\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
.
It follows from
\eqref{ES1}_{4,5}
that
a_{\epsilon}
,
b_{\epsilon}
and
\partial_{t}b_{\epsilon}
are respectively bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
,
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, and
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.
Using the convergences
\eqref{conv}_{2}
and (
33
) as well as the previous proposition, one obtains (
35
).
To obtain (
36
), it is sufficient to consider
a_{\epsilon}=P^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and
b_{\epsilon}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, with
\partial_{t}b_{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
∎

Next, a strong two-scale convergence result for
\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
follows from Lemma
6.2
.

Lemma 6.3
.

The sequences
\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}
strongly two-scale converge in

to
\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v
and
\chi_{Y^{\ast}}\boldsymbol{m}

3.1

Proof.

Let
f^{\epsilon}
be a sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T};L_{\sharp}^{2}(Y))
such that
f^{\epsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}f
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)
.
The aim is to pass to the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}f^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}vf^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}v)f^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t

(37)

where

\displaystyle\Bigr|\int_{\Omega_{T}}(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}v)f^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t\Bigr|\leq C\|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}v^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t-2\int_{\Omega_{T}}\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}v{\rm d}x{\rm d}t.

Using

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\displaystyle\|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}-\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}=0.

Therefore, the limit in (

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}f^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\chi_{Y^{\ast}}vf{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

The proof of the convergence result for

follows the same line.
∎

Passing to the limit in the nonlinear terms of Problem (

Here we use the dilation operator introduced in Subsection

In particular, it allows to interpret the former two-scale convergence results as follows.
Notice that, for using the transform

Lemma 6.4
.

Up to a subsequence
, the following convergence results hold true

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\to\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v\ \mbox{ in }L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)\\
\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\to\chi_{Y^{\ast}}\boldsymbol{m}\ \mbox{ in }L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y),\\
\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup\chi_{Y^{\ast}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}\ \mbox{weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y).\end{array}

(38)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\nabla P^{\epsilon}v^{\epsilon}\circ S^{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla_{x}v+\nabla_{y}v_{1}\ \mbox{weakly in }\ L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y),\\
\displaystyle\nabla P^{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}\circ S^{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{m}+\nabla_{y}\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\ \mbox{weakly in }\ L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y),\\
\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{\varphi^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla_{x}\varphi+\nabla_{y}\varphi_{1}\ \mbox{weakly in }\ L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times Y).\end{array}

(39)

Proof.

The first convergence in (

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(S^{\epsilon}(t,x,y))-\chi_{Y^{\ast}}(y)v(t,x)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}=0.

From (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y}|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(S^{\epsilon}(t,x,y))|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(t,x)|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t,

where, according to Lemma

,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(t,x)|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{Y}|\chi_{Y^{\ast}}(y)v(t,x)|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

One infers from the two latter relations that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(S^{\epsilon}(t,x,y))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}=\|\chi_{Y^{\ast}}(y)v(t,x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}.

(41)

With (
41
) in hand, (

) follows from the weak convergence in

of

to
\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v

∎

We now present a result for handling nonlinear terms, particularly those involving a function composed with a Lipschitz but unbounded function which prevents from using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.
An example in Problem (

The following result of course exploits the two-scale compactness established for

Proposition 6.3
.

For every Lipschitz-continuous function

, we have, up to a subsequence:

h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}})\overset{2}{\to}h(\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v)\ \text{ strongly two-scale in}\ L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y).

(42)

Proof.

To prove (
42
), it is sufficient to show that

Since

such that

\displaystyle\|h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\circ S_{\epsilon})-h(\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}\leq C_{h}\displaystyle\|\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\circ S_{\epsilon}-\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}.

Now, using Lemma
6.4
, one gets

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\|h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\circ S_{\epsilon})-h(\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}=0,

that is (

Corollary 6.1
.

If
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz continuous function, then,
up to a subsequence:

\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}})\overset{2}{\to}\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(v)\text{ strongly two-scale in}\ L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y).

(43)

Proof.

First, notice that (see Corollary 1.2. of
[

]

\displaystyle\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}(x)=\chi\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right)=\chi\left(\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right)\right).

(44)

Hence proving (

) is equivalent to proving that

\chi^{\ast}(y)h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}(S_{\epsilon}(t,x,y))\to\chi_{Y^{\ast}}(y)h(v(t,x,y))\ \text{ in}\ L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y).

The latter results from using Proposition
6.3
in the following decomposition

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\circ S_{\epsilon})-\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}\leq\|\chi_{Y^{\ast}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Y)}\displaystyle\|h(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}}\circ S_{\epsilon})-h(\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\|\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v)-\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T}\times Y)},

where
\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(\chi_{Y^{\ast}}v)=\chi_{Y^{\ast}}h(v)
.
∎

6.3

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\gamma\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}={\rm div}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\ast}\nabla\boldsymbol{m})-\theta_{c}(|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}-1)\boldsymbol{m}-G(v)\boldsymbol{m}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\nabla\varphi+\mathbf{H}_{2}\boldsymbol{m}&\mbox{in}~\Omega_{T}\\

\end{array}\right.

(45)

with

one gets

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}(\gamma\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}+\theta_{c}(|\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}|^{2}-1)\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}+G(v^{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}-\nabla\varphi^{\epsilon})\cdot\boldsymbol{p}^{\epsilon}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t

\qquad\quad\displaystyle\cdot\Bigl[\nabla_{x}\Psi\Bigl(t,x,\frac{x}{\epsilon}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\nabla_{y}\Psi\Bigl(t,x,\frac{x}{\epsilon}\Bigr)\Bigr]{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=0.\end{array}

The demonstration now consists in three main steps:

Step 1.
The first stage is devoted to the convergence analysis of the nonlinear terms, based on the transformation
S^{\epsilon}
.

Limit of the term
\theta_{c}(|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}|^{2}-1)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}

Our estimates are not sufficient for relying on Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, as Berti did in

We thus pave the way for using Vitali’s convergence theorem.
Using the transformation

and the properties (

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega_{T}}\theta_{c}(|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}|^{2}-1)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\Bigl(t,x,\frac{x}{\epsilon}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!=\theta_{c}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}(|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}|^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}(S^{\epsilon},y)-\theta_{c}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}(S^{\epsilon},y).

and (

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\theta_{c}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\left(S^{\epsilon},y\right)=\theta_{c}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\chi_{Y^{\ast}}(y)\boldsymbol{m}(t,x)\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\left(t,x,y\right).

(50)

Additional arguments are required to calculate the asymptotic behavior of the second term in the right-hand side of (
49
).
First, the strong convergence result stated in Lemma
6.4
implies that,
up to a subsequence,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}
also converges almost everywhere in

to
\chi_{Y^{\ast}}\boldsymbol{m}
.
Then,
up to the same subsequence,

|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}|^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}
converges almost everywhere in
\Omega_{T}\times Y
to
\chi_{Y^{\ast}}|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}\boldsymbol{m}

Next,
|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}|^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}
is uniformly bounded in

for some

(see
(

5.1
:
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}

). It follows that the sequence of functions
(|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}|^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon})

Vitali’s convergence theorem applies:

|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}|^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\to\chi_{Y^{\ast}}|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}\boldsymbol{m}\mbox{ in }L^{1}(\Omega_{T}\times Y).

The latter result is sufficient for proving that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\bigl(|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}|^{2}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\circ S^{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}(S^{\epsilon},y)\bigr)=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\chi_{Y^{\ast}}|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}(t,x,y).

Inserting the latter result in (
50

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\theta_{c}\bigl(|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}|^{2}-1\bigr)\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}\Bigl(t,x,\frac{x}{\epsilon}\Bigr)=\int_{\Omega_{T}}\int_{Y}\chi_{Y^{\ast}}\theta_{c}\left(|\boldsymbol{m}|^{2}-1\right)\,\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}(t,x,y).

(51)

Convergence of the term
\chi_{\Omega^{\epsilon}}G(\widetilde{v^{\epsilon}})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}^{\epsilon}}
:

The computation relies on Proposition

and Corollary

.

7
Conclusion

The Curie temperature of ferromagnetic materials is known to depend on several factors, including the precise material composition, the presence of impurities and the manufacturing process. Porosity, including perforations, is another such factor. In this article, we derive an effective model that enables the accurate calculation of the parameters governing the ferromagnetic-paramagnetic transition, explicitly accounting for the microscopic geometry and the magnetic permeability tensor.
It is worth noticing that this explicit calculation, which can be done with standard numerical tools, is valid for any type of material and can avoid or be compared to the vast literature giving experimental results on the Curie temperature in nano-porous materials (we only quote
[
37
]
which is focused on the influence of geometric characteristics).
This is all the more important as we have demonstrated in this article that, in all generality, the concept of Curie temperature in a perforated material has to be replaced by a Curie temperature tensor which essentially averages the coupling of macro- and micro-scales (even if we assume a micro-model with a scalar Curie temperature contrary to a more proper definition as in
[
17
]
). While the assumption of periodicity might appear restrictive, studies of homogenization in other areas (
e.g.
, porous media flows) have demonstrated that our results can be extended to more general configurations, such as stochastic distributions (see
[
10
]
). Furthermore, the accuracy of the effective model can be assessed as a function of the parameter
\epsilon
, which characterizes the inclusion size (see,
e.g.
, work on correctors). Future work will address similar analyses with other inclusion morphologies.
