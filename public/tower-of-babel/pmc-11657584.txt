Leveraging machine learning models for anemia severity detection among pregnant women following ANC: Ethiopian context
Authors: Bekan Kitaw, Chera Asefa, Firew Legese
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnemia during pregnancy is a significant public health concern, particularly in resource-limited settings. Machine learning (ML) offers promising avenues for improved anemia detection and management. This study investigates the potential of ML models in predicting anemia severity among pregnant women attending Antenatal Care (ANC) visits in Ethiopia.MethodsData from the Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey, specialized hospitals, and public hospitals were utilized. The dataset included individuals diagnosed with severe (65.12%), moderate (15.63%), mild (16.65%) anemia, and non-anemic (2.61%) cases. Feature selection employed filter methods based on mutual information, and F-score was used to assess anemia severity prediction across four classes. Six ML models (MLP-NN, XGBoost, GNB, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and KNN) were evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.ResultsThe Random Forest classifier achieved the best overall performance across all categories, with an accuracy of 97%, precision of 93%, recall of 93%, and F1-score of 93%. This indicates high true positive rates and low false positive rates. While other models like XGBoost, MLP-NN, and Decision Tree showed good performance, they weren’t quite as strong as Random Forest. Classifiers like KNN and GNB had lower overall accuracy and a tendency to misclassify some cases.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the promising potential of Random Forest in predicting anemia severity among pregnant women in Ethiopia. The findings contribute to a more holistic understanding of anemia risk factors and pave the way for improved early detection and targeted interventions.Keywords: Anemia detection, Prediction model, Ethiopia, Pregnant women, Antenatal care

Introduction

Pregnancy is a physiological process experienced by a woman. However, many women face challenges related to anemia. Anemia is a condition characterized by a deficiency of healthy red blood cells that has a significant impact on expectant mothers and their unborn infants [1]. According to a report in [2], the global prevalence of anemia across all ages in 2021 was 24.3% (95% uncertainty interval [UI] 23.9 – 24.7%), equating to approximately 1.92 billion cases (1.89 – 1.95%). Women of reproductive age, children under five, and pregnant women are at increased risk for this condition. Anemia affects approximately 41.8% of all pregnancies worldwide, with Africa having the highest prevalence at 61.3% [2–4]. The impact of anemia extends beyond individual health, with potential implications for social and economic development.

Ethiopia, as a sub-Saharan African country with limited healthcare resources, faces considerable challenges in combating anemia. Factors such as poor dietary quality, low iron intake, susceptibility to infectious diseases, and demographic characteristics exacerbate the situation. In particular, pregnant women and children under five are especially vulnerable in this region [5, 6], and during pregnancy, anemia can lead to severe adverse outcomes for both mother and fetus [7, 25]. To effectively address these challenges, leveraging machine learning (ML) presents significant opportunities. ML algorithms can analyse complex datasets to identify patterns that may not be evident through traditional methods, enhancing predictive accuracy. This approach allows for personalized healthcare solutions, optimizing resource allocation in low-resource settings, and empowering healthcare providers with data-driven insights. Furthermore, ML models can be scaled and adapted for use in various contexts, potentially benefiting a wider range of vulnerable populations.

Despite previous research identifying risk factors and causes of anemia, a significant gap remains in understanding the application of machine learning (ML) for anemia severity detection in low-resource settings like Ethiopia. Existing studies often have limited geographical scope and do not utilize advanced analytical techniques. Hence, in this study, we aim to explore the potential of machine learning models in predicting the likelihood and severity of anemia among pregnant women attending Antenatal Care (ANC) clinics in Ethiopia. By leveraging a diverse dataset from multiple sources, including the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) database, specialized hospitals, and public hospitals, we will train and evaluate various machine learning models. This research aims to improve the detection and management of anemia, ultimately enhancing maternal and fetal health outcomes.

Related work

In recent times, computerized algorithms such as machine learning have become increasingly common in various sectors and industries for different purposes. The healthcare sector is one of the sectors that has embraced these technologies, particularly in predictive analysis and detection. Anemia, a disease that can have significant implications for both pregnant mothers and their fetuses, requires frequent attention. As a result, many scholars have made contributions in this area. Some focus on maternal determinants, while others consider environmental factors, socio-cultural factors, or adopt an image-based approach. Additionally, some researchers explore the combined effects of two or more factors.

The paper [8, 9] employs a cross-sectional study design to investigate the occurrence and risk factors of anemia. The study aims to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of pregnant women regarding iron deficiency anemia. The research [8] was conducted in Al-Madinah Al-Munawwarah, involving three public hospitals and two private hospitals. Additionally, the studies [10, 11] utilize a case-control study to analyse the potential factors and prevalence of Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA) among children under the age of 5. The findings indicate that the prevalence of IDA among children under the age of 5 is moderate, particularly in developing countries. However, both cross-sectional and case-control studies (which are retrospective in nature) have limitations due to temporal ambiguity. This refers to the fact that the assessment of exposure and outcome occurs simultaneously, making it difficult to establish a clear temporal relationship.

Moreover [12], proposes a new artificial intelligence approach using Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) to predict and analyze the diagnosis of anemia. It uses 190 datasets from patients in Indonesia diagnosed with Beta Thalassemia Trait, Iron Deficiency Anemia, Hemoglobin E, and combinations. The ELM model achieved 99.21% accuracy, 99.30% precision, 98.44% sensitivity, and 98.84% F1 score in identifying the different types of anemia. The research evaluates the ELM performance and compares it to other machine learning methods. It finds the ELM approach can efficiently and cost-effectively supplement tools used by healthcare professionals to diagnose anemia. The study in [13] investigated a multi-class anemia (considering three classes) classification problem as mild, moderate, and severe classes. The researchers try to base their categorization (mild, moderate, severe) to be based on the Hemoglobin (HGB) values of sample in the Complete Blood Count (CBC). The study focuses on India and majority voting is used as a feature selection criterion. Besides, they utilized SMOTE analysis to balance the data set.

The use of ensembles was proposed by [14] in which they try to combine both the supervised and unsupervised classification approach for disease (gestational diabetes) prediction and diagnosis. They utilized clustering and classification techniques on 1021 instances each having seven potential attributes. With this small dataset, they have obtained an accuracy score of 92%. Another approach [15] utilizing an ensemble model for maternal health risk analysis and feature selection during pregnancy is studied. This study combines the combined sum of a bi-LSTM, decision trees, and a temporal CNN using a small instance of 1218 samples. With their feature engineering, they reveal that the potential indicators of health risk during pregnancy are heart rate, diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure, and age of pregnant women. In paper [16] anemia diagnosis among children is studied using a very small instance of 402 samples. They performed a bi-classification (anemic or non-anemic) regardless of severity level. The synergistic approach utilizing decision tree, cat boost, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting to develop a predictive model for anemia is also studied [17]. The source of their data is the DHS of Ethiopia but with limited attributes excluding potential factors.

In [18], a research study analyzed 29,312 samples (which is the very largest in contrast with the other studies) to investigate the presence of anemia among children aged 6–59 months. They used the CARET method for classification and regression training. However, their predictions are limited to binary classification and do not account for the severity level of anemia. The power of deep learning technology in anemia prediction is also studied in [19] using visual information (facial images) of the patient. They extracted their data highlighting faces from videos of 316 patients. Performance measures such as accuracy and AUC (area under curve) were used to assess the performance of their approach. They compared the classification results of CNNs, such as ResNet50, MobileNet, InceptionV3, EfficientNetB0, and DenseNet121 but finally found that InceptionV3 as the best model. Studies such as [20, 21] also employed the use of deep learning techniques in detecting hemoglobin concentration from analysis of conjunctiva. But this type of studies needs a balanced annotated data else their accuracy level would be very low and their generalizable depends on the quality of the images.

Moreover, previous studies have primarily focused on data mining and machine learning techniques, but they have been constrained by a limited number of datasets and the analysis of only a few specific variables that are suggested by area experts as a potential factor. However, there is a lack of research focusing on the application of machine learning models for anemia severity detection among pregnant women in low resource setting like Ethiopian context. To overcome these limitations, this study introduces a machine learning approach that leverages both medical and non-medical variables [22] for anemia severity level assessment among expectant mothers following ANC visit, in the Ethiopia context. The performance of these models will be evaluated using various evaluation metrics, such as sensitivity, accuracy, specificity, precision and f1-score. The findings of this research will contribute to the development of an effective and efficient tool for anemia severity detection among pregnant women in Ethiopia, which can aid healthcare providers in making timely interventions and improving maternal and fetal health outcomes. The main research question of our research is therefore “How machine learning models can be used to assess the severity level of anemia among pregnant women following antenatal care clinic (ANC) in Ethiopia”.

Materials and methods

Data collection

In this study, the primary source of data was the Demographic Health Survey (DHS) database, which contains the maternal determinants of pregnant women who participated in antenatal care (ANC) follow-up up to the year 2020. The DHS is widely recognized as a reliable source of health and demographic data [23]. Besides, we have also included instances by visiting nearby hospitals, conducting interviews with pregnant women who had undergone ANC using questionnaires and surveys, observations through medical history cross-sections, and online document reviews. In total, a total of 11,174 observations were collected, each containing various features such as ‘Age’, ‘Region’, ‘Residence’, ‘ANC visit’, ‘Source of water’, ‘Toilet Type’, ‘Religion’, ‘Household Member’, ‘Frequency of Reading newspaper’, ‘Frequency of listening to TV’, ‘Duration of pregnancy’, ‘Birth History’, ‘Current pregnancy Wanted’, ‘Termination History’, ‘BMI’, ‘Mosquito Net’, ‘Marital status’, ‘H_education’, ‘H_occupation’, ‘Anemia’.

Building, training, and testing models

In this study, we employed various machine learning models to predict the severity levels of anemia in which the selection of machine learning models was made based on their unique strengths and appropriateness for our research context. We allocated 80% of the dataset for training and 20% for testing, ensuring a robust evaluation of model performance. However, we recognize the importance of validating model robustness and performance on unseen data. To address this, we incorporated cross-validation techniques in our model training process. This included k-fold cross-validation, which allows for a more reliable estimate of model performance by dividing the dataset into multiple subsets, ensuring that each instance is used for both training and validation across different iterations. This approach enhances the generalizability of our findings and assures that our models perform well when applied to unseen data. The distinct characteristics of each model played a crucial role in our analysis. For instance, the Multi-Layer Perceptron Neural Network (MLP-NN) is adept at capturing complex nonlinear relationships within the data [26], while the probabilistic nature of Gaussian Naive Bayes [27] offers a different perspective. The KNN classifier employs a distance-based approach [28], and the Decision Tree follows a rule-based methodology [29]. Additionally, we incorporated ensemble learning through Random Forest and the gradient boosting technique of XGBoost [30]. This diversity allowed us to compare the models from different perspectives in our study, and we categorized these distinct models into the following specific classes.

Gradient boosting machines (GBM)

GBM, a machine learning ensemble method, utilizes the predictions of multiple models, such as decision trees, with the goal of enhancing overall predictive performance. It achieves this by iteratively optimizing the model’s weights based on the errors from previous iterations, gradually reducing prediction errors and improving the model’s accuracy [30]. GBM algorithms, such as XGBoost, LightGBM, or CatBoost, are powerful and often provide high predictive accuracy. Neural networks

Deep learning models, such as neural networks, have the capability to capture complex patterns in the data and are effective when there is a huge dataset [26]. With our dataset we have employed a Multi-layer Perceptron based neural network (MLP-NN) classifier model with two hidden layer and each containing 100 neurons and the random state value of 0 is used to ensure reproducibility. Besides, we have also utilized a K-Nearest neighbour (KNN) classifier. KNN classifier is called a lazy learner and a non-parametric algorithm, since it does not perform any training when the training data is provided, and stores the data during the training provided. While the Naive Bayes method known as Gaussian Naive Bayes, or GNB for short, operates on the Gaussian normal distribution. It facilitates processing of continuous data and is advised when the size of the input variables is quite large [26, 27].

For instance, when considering the severe anemia class, the Random Forest Classifier (RFC) achieved the highest True Positive (TP) rate of 93%, demonstrating its ability to accurately identify severe cases. It also exhibited a high True Negative (TN) rate of 99%, suggesting a low incidence of false positives. However, the model displayed a relatively low False Negative (FN) rate of 7%, indicating a potential for missing some severe cases. In contrast, the K-Nearest Neighbour and GNB Classifier demonstrated slightly lower TP rates of 84% and 75% respectively, along with higher FN rates, suggesting a propensity to misclassify severe cases or a higher likelihood of categorizing non-severe cases as severe. The XGBoost and MLP-NN Classifiers performed similarly, achieving TP rates of 88% for severe anemia, but with a higher FN rate of 12%. The Decision Tree Classifiers exhibited the highest TP rates of 96%, and relatively high False Positive (FP) rates of 4%, indicating a very low incidence of false positives.

In the case of moderate anemia, both MLP-NN and Random Forest Classifier demonstrated a True Positive (TP) rate of 90%, signifying their capability to accurately identify moderate cases. They also exhibited a high True Negative (TN) rate of 99%. Conversely, the KNN and GNB Classifier displayed slightly lower TP rates of 76% and 83% respectively, along with higher False Negative (FN) rates of 24% and 17% respectively, indicating a greater likelihood of misclassifying moderate cases. The Decision Tree Classifier achieved a TP rate of 89%, while the XGBoost Classifier showed an even higher TP rate of 92% for moderate anemia. Additionally, the GNB Classifier displayed relatively high False Positive (FP) rates, suggesting a tendency to classify non-moderate cases as moderate.

Furthermore, in the mild and non-anemic anemia categories, the RFC, Decision Tree, and XGBoost Classifiers demonstrated relatively high TP rates, followed by the MLP-NN classifier, indicating their proficiency in accurately identifying mild and non-anemic cases. However, the GNB and KNN Classifier exhibited lower TP rates for both categories, implying a greater likelihood of misclassifying mild and non-anemic cases. Additionally, they both have shown a higher FP rates, suggesting an increased tendency to categorize non-mild and non-anemic cases as mild or non-anemic.

Results and discussion

All the experiments were executed on a google coolab, a cloud service based on jupyter notebook. Throughout the study period, among the pregnant women attending ANC visits, 7276 were diagnosed with severe anemia, 1746 were diagnosed with moderate anemia, 1860 were diagnosed with mild anemia, and 292 were diagnosed as non-anemic, collectively comprising our dataset sample of 11,174. This corresponds to prevalence rates of 65.12% for severe anemia, 15.63% for moderate anemia, 16.65% for mild anemia, and 2.61% for non-anemic cases. We utilized filter methods to select relevant features based on statistical measures (specifically mutual information) and ultimately relied on F-Score to assess the prediction of anemia severity across four major classes, as shown in Fig. 2. Key predictor variables included body mass index, wealth status, termination history, use of mosquito nets, and frequency of reading news. The performance of various machine learning models (MLP-NN, XGBoost, GNB, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and KNN) was evaluated, with performance metrics summarized visually in Fig. 8.

Comparison of machine learning models in anemia severity prediction

Among the models evaluated, the Random Forest classifier demonstrated the best performance as in Table 5, achieving an accuracy of 97%, precision of 93%, recall of 93%, and an F1-score of 93% across all anemia classes. This effectiveness stems from its ensemble nature, which reduces overfitting and captures complex interactions among features. Specifically, the Random Forest employs multiple decision trees and utilizes a bagging approach, enhancing both stability and robustness. The optimal configuration consisted of 10 estimators, with a default maximum tree depth and entropy as the splitting criterion. Our experiments indicated that this setup provided the best results, highlighting the model’s ability to generalize well to unseen data. The MLP-NN classifier, while performing reasonably well, encountered convergence issues and was sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, leading to overfitting. In contrast, XGBoost yielded commendable outcomes due to its gradient-boosting framework, effectively handling missing data and regularization, though it required careful tuning for optimal performance.

The Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) classifier, despite its computational efficiency, faced challenges due to its assumption of feature independence, resulting in suboptimal performance given the correlations among predictors like body mass index and socio-economic status. Meanwhile, the Decision Tree classifier, although interpretable, struggled with overfitting and had reduced generalizability in high-dimensional spaces. The KNN classifier, despite its intuitive appeal, was adversely affected by the curse of dimensionality, leading to misclassification when irrelevant features were present. A comparison with prior research reveals notable differences in datasets, methodologies, and feature selection approaches. For instance, a study [33] achieved 99.00% accuracy using a smaller dataset of 8,544 records focused on binary classification, which may have limited its complexity and generalizability.

Another research [34] utilizing a Random Forest model on 700 records attained 97.21% accuracy, relying on CBC test values, while a different approach [35] suggested by a study on 402 samples reported a lower accuracy of 86.4%. These studies often used limited feature sets, which may have excluded critical predictors relevant to anemia severity. The variations in methodologies, such as the choice of features and the modelling techniques employed, are significant. Previous studies [12–16] primarily focused on a narrow selection of variables, potentially overlooking important factors that could enhance predictive accuracy. In contrast, our study integrates a comprehensive array of maternal determinants: social, biological, and medical - providing a more holistic view of the factors influencing anemia severity.

Additionally, numerous research efforts have examined anemia through the lens of social determinants and biological factors, but the differences in target populations, data diversity, and modelling approaches underscore the need for further research to fully understand the complexities of predictive factors associated with anemia. By incorporating these multifaceted determinants, this study aims to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of anemia prediction, highlighting previously unexplored relationships and offering insights into the dynamics of anemia across diverse populations.

Limitations, future research, and conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the severity levels of anemia among pregnant women in Ethiopia through various multiclass classification techniques. While the Random Forest classifier demonstrated superior performance achieving a sensitivity of 93%, accuracy of 97%, specificity of 97%, and an F1-score of 93%, several limitations must be acknowledged.

Geographic Limitations: The study is primarily focused on a specific geographic context (Ethiopia). Variations in population demographics, lifestyle, healthcare services, and socio-economic determinants may impact the model’s performance when applied to other regions. Future research should aim to validate findings in different countries or regions, particularly within diverse African contexts.
