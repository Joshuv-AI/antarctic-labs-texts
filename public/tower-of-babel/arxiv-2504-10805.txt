The internal logic and finite colimits
William Troiani

Abstract
We describe how finite colimits can be described using the internal lanuage, also known as the Mitchell-Benabou language, of a topos, provided the topos admits countably infinite colimits. This description is based on the set theoretic definitions of colimits and coequalisers, however the translation is not direct due to the differences between set theory and the internal language, these differences are described as internal versus external . Solutions to the hurdles which thus arise are given.



keywords
Topos, finite colimits, internal language, type theory, logic.

Introduction

Type theories were originally suggested by Russell and Whitehead
[
Russell, 1903
]
as a foundation for mathematics, for a historical view, see
[
Lambek, Scott, 1986
, p.124]
. For an introduction to type theories, see
[
Sorensen, Urzyczyn, 2006
, §3.3]
. The idea of approaching logic from the perspective of category theory is originally due to Lawvere. In the modern form, associated to every elementary topos
\mathcal{E}
are several type theories. We will consider a fixed such type theory and refer to it as the
internal logic
. This type theory can be used to describe constructions of objects and morphisms in
\mathcal{E}
, and also to prove things about them. For example, if
f,g
are morphisms in
\mathcal{E}
both with domain
A
and both with codomain
B
, then if the following is derivable in the internal logic:

\vdash\forall a:A,\text{ }fa=ga

(1)

then the morphisms
f,g
are equal. More generally, the internal logic is
sound
in that any formula which is provable inside the internal logic represents some true statement about the topos in question (see
[
Johnstone, 2002
, §D1.3.2, D4.2.3, D4.3.3, D4.4.6]
for a discussion on soundness). In this paper we show how the type theory can be used to describe finite coproducts
5.2
and coequalisers
5.3
, which then leads to a description of finite colimits
5.4
. We remark that one must also describe an initial object, but this is easy and is done in Section
5.1
.

The internal logic offers many syntactical similarities with ZF set theory, in fact, due to the existence of the internal logic, elementary topoi are often referred to as “generalised theories of sets”
[
Meordijk, Mac Lane, 1994
, §Prologue, page 1]
. Hence, our approach will be to use the standard set-theoretic descriptions of finite coproducts and of coequalisers to form terms in the interal logic whose interpretations (ie, associated objects/morphisms) will be finite coproducts and coequalisers respectively
of the original topos
. Here, we must observe a subtlety: recall the standard construction of the coequaliser
\operatorname{Coeq}(f,g)
of two functions of sets
f,g:A\longrightarrow B
: first we define for each
n>0
a relation
R_{n}
on
B
as follows, a pair
(b,b^{\prime})
of elements in
B
is in
R_{n}
if and only if there exists a sequence
(a_{0},...,a_{n})
of elements in
A
such that for each
i=0,...,n-1
we either have
f(a_{i})=g(a_{i+1})
or
f(a_{i+1})=g(a_{i})
. Then, the Coequaliser
\operatorname{Coeq}(f,g)
is given by the quotient
B/\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{n}
. Our method for mimicking this description using the internal logic will require for each object
C
in our topos
\mathcal{E}
that the category
\operatorname{Sub}(C)
of subobjects of
C
admits countably infinite coproducts. A circumstance where this holds is when
\mathcal{E}
admits countably infinite coproducts, hence we assume this is true of the topoi that we work with.

Despite what has been said, to describe finite colimits using the internal logic of a topos, it is not as simple as directly translating the standard constructions in ZF, as there is a subtle but significant difference between the interal logic and ZF set theory. In ZF set theory, the set constructor union is
extensional
and in the internal logic it is
intensional
. We now explain what we mean by this point along with our solution.

Let
A,B
be sets. Recall that the disjoint union
A\sqcup B
can be defined by first constructing sets
A^{\ast},B^{\ast}
which are defined by the following rules.

(a,1)\in A^{\ast}\text{ iff }a\in A,\qquad(b,2)\in B^{\ast}\text{ iff }b\in B

(2)

Then, the disjoint union is given by taking the union
A^{\ast}\cup B^{\ast}
of these sets. Translating this into the internal logic, we run into trouble because we can only take the union of two subobjects
of the same object
. That the union of
any
pair of sets can be constructed in ZF is what we mean by the union being
extensional
. That the union of two subobjects via the internal logic can only be taken once they have each been embedded into a common object is what we mean by the union being
intensional
.

Our solution is given precisely by Corollary
19
, but we give the general idea here. Continuing with the same sets
A,B
, we consider the sets, each subsets of the set
\mathcal{P}A\times\mathcal{P}B
.

\displaystyle\{A\}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(\{a\},\varnothing)\in\mathcal{P}A\times\mathcal{P}B\mid a\in A\}

(3)

\displaystyle\{B\big\}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(\varnothing,\{b\})\in\mathcal{P}A\times\mathcal{P}B\mid b\in B\big\}

(4)

We notice that
A\cong\{A\}
and
B\cong\{B\}
. A coproduct of
A,B
can then be taken to be the set

A\coprod B:=\{A\}\cup\{B\}

(5)

along with the maps
A\longrightarrow A\coprod B,\text{ }a\longmapsto(\{a\},\varnothing)
and
B\longrightarrow A\coprod B,\text{ }b\longmapsto(\varnothing,\{b\})
.

We have already mentioned the standard construction of the coequaliser of two functions, we note here that this construction does
not
make use of union at any point, hence the translation of this construction into the internal logic is direct, and will not use Corollary
19
. Indeed, the main difficulty in describing finite coproduct using the interal language lies in describing finite colimits, whereas the description of coequalisers is straight forward.

3
Type theory

Type theories were originally suggested as a foundation for mathematics. In this paper, a type theory will be a convenient language of subobjects and morphisms inside a topos. We begin with the definition of a type theory and then specialise to those arising from topoi. In particular, Definition
1
will make no mention of any topoi.

Definition 1
.

A

•

A class of

\Omega,\mathbbm{1}

\tau

of type
\tau

•

A class of

is a formal symbol,
\tau
and
\sigma
are types.

•

A class of

\tau

•

A class of

\tau

•

A class of

•

Definition 8

Let

for some obejct

PA
with the type

. The function symbols are all

, where

is a morphism in
\mathcal{E}
, the relation symbols are all
r
where

in
\mathcal{E}

Remark 9
.

It is important in Definition
8
that the relation symbols are subobjects and
not
chosen representatives of subobjects. The reason is due to the Substitution Lemma (
16
below). We show an example of what can go wrong if particular representatives are taken. Let
r
be a formula with free variable
x
of type
X
, and
t_{1}:X,t_{2}:Y
terms, for simplicity we assume that
t_{1}
has a single free variable
y:Y
and
t_{2}
is closed. We assume also that

does not appear inside

. Since the terms
(r[x:=t_{1}])[y:=t_{2}]
and

are equal to each other, in order for the substitution Lemma to be well defined, we need the following to hold.

\llbracket.(r[x:=t_{1}])[y:=t_{2}]\rrbracket=\llbracket.r[x:=t_{1}[y:=t_{2}]]\rrbracket

(11)

Now,
\llbracket.(r[x:=t_{1}])[y:=t_{2}]\rrbracket
is given by the following pair of pullback squares.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket.(r[x:=t_{1}])[y:=t_{2}]\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket y:Y.r[x:=t_{1}]\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket x:X.r\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbbm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket.t_{2}\rrbracket}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket y:Y.t_{1}\rrbracket}

(12)

On the other hand,

is such that the following is a pullback diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket.r[x:=t_{1}[y:=t_{2}]]\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket x:X.r\rrbracket}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbbm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket t_{1}[y:=t_{2}]\rrbracket}

(13)

Hence, if we chose a subobject representative for each subobject, equality (
11

12

\delta:\pi_{B}^{-1}(\operatorname{im}f)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Equal}(f\pi_{A},\pi_{B})
.

It remains to show that
\gamma\delta=\operatorname{id}
and
\delta\gamma=\operatorname{id}
. This follows from the uniqueness of the induced morphisms coming from the universal properties of
\operatorname{Equal}(f\pi_{A},\pi_{B})
and
\pi_{B}^{-1}(\operatorname{im}f)
.
∎

.

Let

be a variable. Then there is a unique isomorphism

such that the following Diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega^{U}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket u:U.\{u\}\rrbracket}

(38)

Proof.

The interpretation of

is some morphism

4.2
Crucial terms

As mentioned in the Introduction, the union in the internal logic is an
intensional
operator, in that only the union of subobjects of a common parent object can be taken. This is in contrast to ZF set theory where the union of two arbitrary sets may be taken. We now show how to describe the union of two subobjects of the same object via the internal logic. The terms defined here will be used extensively in Section
5
.

Definition 20
.

We define:

•

Given two variables

both of type

\displaystyle:=\{u:U\mid u\in Z_{1}\vee u\in Z_{2}\}:\mathcal{P}U

\displaystyle\operatorname{FV}(Z_{1}\cup_{U}Z_{2})

\displaystyle=\{Z_{1}:\mathcal{P}U,Z_{2}:\mathcal{P}U\}

(41)

•

Given a function symbol

and a variable

\displaystyle f(Z)

\displaystyle:=\{b:B\mid\exists a:A,a\in Z\wedge b=fa\}:\mathcal{P}B

\displaystyle\operatorname{FV}(f(Z))

\displaystyle=\{Z:\mathcal{P}A\}

(43)

What is the interpretation of these terms inside a topos? We build this piece by piece. Recall that the morphism

5
Finite colimits in an arbitrary topos

We lift the ideas of Section

to the setting of an arbitrary topos admitting countably infinite colimits.

We begin with the description of an initial object. Then we show how finite coproducts and coequalisers are described. This is then put together in Section

where we show how to describe finite colimits in an arbitrary topos

5.1
Initial object

In the topos
\underline{\operatorname{Sets}}
an initial object is given by the empty set. To translate that this to the internal logic of a topos we consider the formula
\bot
and then interpret this with respect to the context
\ast:\mathbbm{1}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbbm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket\ast:\mathbbm{1}.\bot\rrbracket}

(47)

Now, given an arbitrary object

we claim there exists a unique morphism

. We notice that since

is interpreted as a

\mathcal{E}

for arbitrary

. However, there is a canonical morphism

which is given by
\llbracket.\varnothing_{U}\rrbracket
. Combining this with the fact that
U
can be exhibited as a subobject of

via the term
\big\{z:\mathcal{P}U\mid z=\{u\}\big\}
(Corollary

.

Consider the following Diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbbm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega^{U}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket\ast:\mathbbm{1}.\bot\rrbracket}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\llbracket.\varnothing_{U}\rrbracket}

(48)

To show that there exists a morphism
\llbracket\ast:\mathbbm{1}.\bot\rrbracket\longrightarrow\llbracket z:\mathcal{P}U.\exists u:U,z=\{u\}\rrbracket
it suffices by Lemma

to derive the following sequent in the internal logic.

\bot\vdash\varnothing_{U}\in\{z:\mathcal{P}U.\exists u:U,z=\{u\}\}

This is immediate though via

of Definition

.

In more detail, Lemma

implies that (

) but with the left most vertical arrow replaced by
\llbracket\ast:\mathbbm{1}.\{z:\mathcal{P}U.\exists u:U,z=\{u\}\}\rrbracket
is a pullback square. Then, since (

factors through
\llbracket\ast:\mathbbm{1}.\{z:\mathcal{P}U.\exists u:U,z=\{u\}\}\rrbracket

Proposition 23
.

The domain of any representative of the subobject
\llbracket\ast:\mathbbm{1}.\bot\rrbracket
is an initial object of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

We have already shown that for arbitrary

there exists a morphism

, so it remains to show uniqueness of this morphism.

To prove this, it suffices to show that

is a monomorphism. However this is immediate from the fact that the domain of this morphism is

\mathcal{E}
.
∎

Remark 25
.

The top row of Diagram (
59
) is exactly the top row of the Diagram in the middle of page 27 of
[
Mikkelson, 1976
]
. We note however that we were not aware of this paper when the Diagram (
59
) was first constructed.

The above is
not
sufficient in order to prove that an elementary topos admits finite coproducts, the reason why is because the formula (
50
) makes use of the conjunction
\wedge
which according to Definition
11
is interpreted as the disjoint union in a subobject category. To ensure that this exists, we use the fact a priori that an elementary topos admits finite colimits.

Indeed, the point is to come up with a

of these finite coproducts using the internal language. Hence, we cannot simply use the fact that we have a description of
binary
coproducts in order to obtain for free a descritpion of
arbitrary finite
coproducts.

The next definition gives an appropriate description, the fact that this is correct follows by making suitable changes to the case of binary coproducts.

Definition 26
.

Let
A_{1},...,A_{n}
be objects of a topos
\mathcal{E}
and consider them as types in the pure type theory (
1
). Consider the following formula which has a free variable

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big(\ldots\big(A_{1}\coprod A_{2}\big)&\coprod\ldots\coprod A_{n}\big)(z)\\
&:=\big(\exists a_{1}:A_{1},z=\langle\ldots\langle\{a\},\varnothing_{a_{2}:A_{2}}\rangle,\ldots\rangle,\varnothing_{a_{n}:A_{n}}\rangle\big)\\
&\vee\ldots\\
&\vee\big(\exists a_{n}:A_{n},z=\langle\ldots\langle\varnothing_{a_{1}:A_{1}},\varnothing_{a_{2}:A_{2}}\rangle,\ldots\rangle,\{a_{n}\}\rangle\big)\end{split}

(65)

Also, for each

, we let
\iota_{a_{i}}
denote the following term.

\iota_{a_{i}}:=\langle\ldots\langle\varnothing_{a_{1}:A_{1}},\ldots\rangle,\{a_{i}\}\rangle,\ldots\rangle,\varnothing_{a_{n}:A_{n}}\rangle

(66)

Proposition 27
.

The tuple
\big(\llbracket z:\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}A_{i}.(A_{1}\coprod A_{2})\coprod\ldots\coprod A_{n}\rrbracket,\{\iota_{a_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}\big)
is a coproduct of

5.3

In the topos

the coequaliser of functions

is given by

. We emulate this by taking the subobject of

\displaystyle\Big\{

\displaystyle z:B\times B\mid\exists b_{1},b_{2}:B\text{, }z=\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle\wedge\Big(b_{1}=b_{2}

\displaystyle\vee\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\big(\exists a_{1},...,a_{n},(b_{1}=t_{\alpha_{0}}[a:=a_{1}]

\displaystyle\wedge(t_{\alpha_{n}+1}[a:=a_{n}]=b_{2})\big)\Big)\Big\}

Appendix A
Topos theory and logic

What is a logical connective? One interpretation of connectives is offered by ZF set theory. The axiom of specification allows for the manipulation of sets via the manipulation of predicates. For instance, if
\varphi(a),\psi(a)
are formulas with free variable
a
, then the set
\{a\in A\mid\varphi(a)\wedge\psi(a)\}
is given by the intersection of the sets
\{a\in A\mid\varphi(a)\}\cap\{a\in A\mid\psi(a)\}
.

In fact, this turns out to be a special case of a more general theory. The category
\underline{\operatorname{Sets}}
is an elementary topos, and indeed any elementary topos
\mathcal{E}
offers a perspective on connectives. We show in this Section how the logical structure of the connectives
\forall,\exists,\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow
is captured by the categorical structure of
\mathcal{E}
.

Definition 37
.

A
Heyting algebra
is a set with a partial order, which as a category admits the following.

•

Initial and terminal objects.

•

Binary products, binary coproducts.

•

All exponentials.
