Dynamic functional connectivity and transcriptomic signatures reveal stage-dependent brain network dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease spectrum
Authors: Yan Shi, Yuanhao Li, Renpuchi Ci, Su Yan, Tian Tian, Ning Zheng, Wenzhen Zhu, Yuanyuan Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlzheimer’s Disease Spectrum (ADS) progresses from preclinical stages to dementia, with dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) changes emerging early. This study aimed to investigate the dynamic changes in brain networks across different stages of ADS and their underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 239 participants: 69 Healthy Controls (HC), 83 with Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD), 56 with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), and 31 with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). All participants underwent neuropsychological testing and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA), a data-driven method that captures time-resolved whole-brain dFC, was applied to identify transient brain states and calculated their occupancy rate, dwell time, and transition probabilities. Group differences in these dynamic metrics were assessed using a General Linear Model (GLM), and their correlations with cognitive performance were examined. To explore the molecular basis of significant dFC alterations, we performed gene-category enrichment analysis. This analysis integrated the spatial maps of altered brain states with regional gene expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA), using spin permutations to ensure statistical robustness.ResultsWe identified ten recurring brain states and characterized how their transition patterns, stability, and frequency differed as a function of disease severity. Specifically, early disruptions appeared as altered transition probabilities between states, while later stages showed pronounced changes in the dwell time and occurrence rates of specific states, closely associated with cognitive decline. Notably, one brain state marked by synchronized activity in attention, salience, and default mode networks emerged as a critical hub linked to both cognitive deterioration and excitatory-inhibitory imbalance. Genes associated with this state were enriched in glycine-mediated synaptic pathways and expressed in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, showing spatial and temporal patterns that extended from early development into late disease stages.ConclusionsOur study uncovered the stage-dependent dFC changes and their molecular underpinnings of brain network dysfunction across the ADS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-025-01898-1.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease spectrum, Dynamic functional connectivity, Transcriptomic, Excitatory-inhibitory imbalance

Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), a leading cause of dementia in the elderly, is pathologically characterized by the deposition of beta-amyloid (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) formed by tau protein [1]. The progression of this disease follows a continuum from a preclinical stage to severe dementia phase, referred to as the Alzheimer’s Disease Spectrum (ADS). Within this spectrum, the earliest preclinical stage is Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD), which may gradually progress to Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) and eventually develop into typical Alzheimer’s dementia [2].

The human brain is regarded as a dynamic functional system supported by relatively stable structural networks, with temporal fluctuations in functional networks believed to underlie complex cognitive functions and the emergence of consciousness [3]. Within this framework, dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) analysis based on resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) time series has shown great potential in revealing brain connectivity patterns in various neurological disorders, including schizophrenia [4], major depressive disorder [5], and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) [6]. Among dFC approaches, the Sliding Window Correlation (SWC) method has been widely adopted in ADS. For instance, Xue et al. [7] reported reduced dFC variability in the default mode network (DMN) of SCD patients, while Wang et al. [8] also observed weakened connectivity between the cognitive control network (Cont) and sensory-motor network (SMN) in the same population. However, Penalba-Sánchez et al. [9] revealed increased dFC variability between the central executive network (CEN) and dorsal attention network (DAN) in MCI patients. Jing et al. [10] indicated that brain dynamic states could reveal the different progression of MCI and may help to recognize high-risk MCI subjects early. All these studies suggest that dFC abnormalities emerge early in the disease course and may evolve alongside clinical progression. However, the SWC method is constrained by arbitrary selection of window length and limited sensitivity to transient or subtle fluctuations [11], which is particularly problematic in preclinical stages like SCD. This is especially important as brain functional changes in the SCD stage are typically subtle [12].

In contrast, a novel analysis method called Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA) has been developed. LEiDA captures the instantaneous phase-locking (PL) patterns of blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals at each time point without requiring sliding windows, making it particularly well-suited for detecting short-lived or weakly expressed network states [13]. Importantly, LEiDA has demonstrated high sensitivity in detecting subtle longitudinal changes in pain [14] and depression recurrence [15], as well as treatment-related alterations [16, 17]. These studies highlight the potential of LEiDA for tracking disease progression in ADS. Preliminary applications of LEiDA in AD and MCI have uncovered distinct alterations in dwell time and occupancy rate, suggesting the presence of aberrant dynamic brain states. However, studies applying LEiDA to SCD populations remain scarce, and the trajectory of dFC dysfunction across ADS stages has not been systemically characterized.

Recently, advances in transcriptomic and neuroimaging genetics have enabled the exploration of molecular mechanisms underlying macroscopic brain dysfunction. Integrating spatial gene expression data with neuroimaging phenotypes holds great promise for bridging micro- and macro-level pathology in AD [18, 19]. For instance, research on iron metabolism dysregulation in AD have revealed that genes linked to pathological iron deposition are enriched in protein phosphorylation and metal ion homeostasis pathways, and are primarily expressed in microglia and glutamatergic neurons [20].Additionally, Yu et al. reported that structural changes in the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe of AD patients are closely linked to the expression of genes involved in regulating chemical synaptic transmission [21]. Using Juelich cytoarchitectonic atlas, Rubido et al. further revealed that genetic variations, particularly those linked to cholinergic pathway, are associated with dFC changes across the ADS [22]. As the disease advances, accumulating evidence points to a gradual decline in global brain instability among individuals with ADS [6, 23]. Nevertheless, the transcriptional underpinnings of dFC changes and how these alterations evolve across the disease continuum remain poorly understood.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the stage-dependent alterations in dFC across ADS using LEiDA, and to further elucidate their genetic and neurobiological basis. We hypothesize that brain network dynamics deteriorate progressively across the ADS in parallel with cognition decline, and that these brain network dysfunctions are spatially associated with gene expression patterns related to AD pathology. By integrating brain imaging with transcriptomic data, our approach seeks to: (1) characterize distinct dynamic brain states across SCD, MCI, and AD stages; (2) identify dFC patterns linked to cognitive decline; (3) uncover transcriptomic signatures underlying functional brain instability. Our findings are expected to provide mechanistic insights into early brain network dysfunction in ADS and support the development of genetically informed neuroimaging biomarkers for early detection and disease monitoring.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study collected demographic data including age, sex, education, hypertension and diabetes status, and neurological disease history. All subjects underwent standardized neuropsychological evaluations by memory specialists, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clock Drawing Test (CDT), and both Part A (TMT-A) and Part B (TMT-B) of the Trail Making Test.

Participants were categorized into four groups: HC (n = 69), SCD (n = 83), MCI (n = 56), and AD (n = 31). HC group had no cognitive impairment or clinical abnormalities. SCD subjects met the SCD-plus clinical framework proposed by Jessen et al.(2014)(2); MCI cases were diagnosed per Petersen’s 2004 criteria [24]; AD was defined according to the “probable AD” standards outlined in the 2011 National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) guidelines [25]. Exclusion criteria included: (1) other neurodegenerative dementias (e.g., vascular, Lewy body, and frontotemporal dementia); (2) cognitive impairment due to medication or psychiatric disorder; (3) severe mental disorders per Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) (e.g. major depressive, schizophrenia). According to the Declaration of Helsinki, the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJ-IRB202401097). All participants provided informed consent.

MR imaging acquisition

MRI scanning were acquired using a 3 T GE Discovery 750 MRI system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The subjects were positioned in the standard supine position, with the scanning range covering from the vertex to the cranial base.

Rs-fMRI were acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence. Key parameters included: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 2000/30 ms, matrix size 64 × 64, field of view (FOV) 220 × 220 mm, slice thickness 3 mm, flip angle 90°, with a total of 36 continuous axial slices, and 185 time points acquired. Structural MRI used 3D-T1 BRAin Volume (BRAVO) sequence with parameters: TR/TE = 7.1/2.7 ms, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, number of excitations (NEX) = 1, slice thickness = 1 mm.

Post-processing of MR data

The data processing in this study is shown in Fig. 1. The primary processing steps include fMRI preprocessing, LEiDA and its correlation with clinical scores, as well as enrichment analyses linking transient brain states showing group differences to gene expression and associated biological processes.

fMRI preprocessing

The preprocessing of fMRI data was conducted using the CONN toolbox (version 22). The initial 5 time points were discarded to allow for scanner signal stabilization, resulting in 180 time points retained for analysis. Head motion estimation and correction were applied by adjusting the motion parameters for each time point, followed by slice timing correction. Specifically, 12 motion parameters were employed for head motion regression, including three translational and three rotational parameters, along with their first-order derivatives. To further mitigate the impact of motion-related artifacts, a scrubbing procedure was implemented to flag time points exhibiting excessive motion. The motion exclusion criteria were defined as follows: a framewise displacement (FD) threshold of 0.9 mm and a global BOLD signal change threshold of 5 standard deviations [26]. The functional data were then registered to the corresponding 3D-T1 BRAVO structural images, and all images were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space, with tissue segmentation of gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) performed. Finally, spatial smoothing using a Gaussian kernel with a full-width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm. After preprocessing, denoising is performed in the CONN toolbox by regressing out the first five principal components of white matter and CSF signals. To extract the BOLD time series for each brain region, the Schaefer100-Yeo7 atlas [27] was used for parcellation.

LEiDA and brain state features

For the analysis of dynamic connectivity, we used LEiDA codes developed by Cabral et al. [28] (https://github.com/PSYMARKER/leida-python), which we adapted to our data, with an additional 5 time points removed during the automated processing stage. The BOLD time-series were bandpass-filtered (0.02–0.10 Hz) using a second-order Butterworth filter to mitigate physiological noise [13]. Instantaneous phase signals were then derived for each ROI via the Hilbert transform, enabling phase-based connectivity analysis. We subsequently calculated the phase difference between each pair of brain regions at every TR using cosine distance, resulting in a dynamic PL matrix that quantifies the phase alignment between regions. Then the leading eigenvector of this matrix was calculated to display the main orientation of BOLD phases over all regions. These eigenvectors were clustered into recurring brain states using K-means [28]. The cluster number K was explored within a range of 5–10, encompassing the typical number of functional networks reported in rs-fMRI literature [13]. The optimal K (final K = 10) was selected by minimizing the Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI) [29] (Fig. S1).

Brain state dynamics were characterized using three key metrics: (1) occupancy rate, quantifying the total proportion of time spent in each state; (2) dwell time, measuring the average duration of continuous visits to a state. (3) state transition probabilities, reflecting the likelihood of switching between states. Between-group differences in these dFC metrics were assessed using a General Linear Model (GLM), controlling for age, sex, and years of education, with statistical significance adjusted using the Bonferroni (BFN) correction [30, 31]. For the comparisons of occupancy rate and dwell time among 10 states, the significance threshold was set at p < 0.005 (0.05/10). For the analysis of transition probabilities, which involved 100 possible directed transitions, the threshold was set at p < 5 × 10− 4(0.05/100). The p-values reported in the Results section are the Bonferroni-corrected value, obtained by multiplying the original p-values by the number of tests conducted.

Brain state features - cognition correlations

To investigate the relationship between brain functional state metrics and cognitive performance, we employed a GLM across four participant groups: HC, SCD, MCI, and AD. Adjusting for covariates of age, sex, and years of education, we examined the correlations between dwell time and occupancy rate of each of the defined brain states and cognitive function assessment indicators, including the MMSE, CDT, TMT-A, and TMT-B.

Gene-category enrichment analysis

For states exhibiting significant intergroup differences, we performed gene-category enrichment analysis (GCEA) to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms. First, regional microarray gene expression data from the AHBA were mapped onto the Schaefer100-Yeo7 brain regions using the abagen toolbox (v. 0.1.3) [32, 33]. We then used the ABAnnotate toolbox (https://github.com/LeonDLotter/ABAnnotate) to associate spatial brain states with transcriptomic features derived from AHBA data. This toolbox performs enrichment analyses across several gene transcription categories: (1) Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis focusing on biological process categories, provided by the DAVID database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/); (2) Developmental brain region gene expression profiling, using RNA-seq data from the BrainSpan database (https://www.brainspan.org/rnaseq/search/index.html) to characterize gene expression in developmental brain regions; (3) Neuron cell type association analysis, based on neuron cell type markers from the PsychENCODE database (http://resource.psychencode.org/).

To mitigate spatial autocorrelation and avoid biases from shared cortical embedding, we computed within-category co-expression patterns rather than direct gene-level associations(34). Specifically, spearman correlations between phenotype effects and gene expression were calculated. These correlations were then aggregated into phenotype category scores (cScorePheno) by taking the weighted mean of z-transformed correlations, scaled by category-specific expression levels [34]. Significance was assessed via 10,000 spin permutations to generate null distributions, followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons [34–36].

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were analyzed using SPSS 12.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test, and variance homogeneity was evaluated with Levene’s test. Since the continuous variables (age, education, MMSE, CDT, TMT-A, TMT-B) did not meet the normality assumption, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for all continuous variables, while categorical variables (sex, hypertension, diabetes) were compared using the chi-square test.

Differences in transient brain states across the ADS

Using K-means clustering analysis within the LEiDA framework, this study identified ten distinct whole-brain transient functional states (Fig. S2). One state, characterized by widespread phase consistency across the entire brain, is termed the global brain synchronization state (State 10, Global). The remaining nine states exhibit significant regional-specific synchronization patterns: (1) Coordinated activation of the Control Network (Cont) and the DMN across bilateral hemispheres (State 1, Cont-DMN); (2) High synchronization between the Dorsal Attention Network (DAN) and the Somatomotor Network (SMN) across bilateral hemispheres (State 2, DAN-SMN); (3)High synchronization between the Visual Network (Vis) and the SMN in bilateral hemispheres (State 3, Vis-SMN); (4) Strong synchronization between the Vis and the Cont in both hemispheres (State 4, Vis-Cont); (5) High synchronization of the Salience/Ventral Attention Networks (SVAN) with the Cont and the DMN in the bilateral hemispheres (State 5, SVAN-Cont-DMN); (6) High synchronization between the DAN and the Limbic Network (LIM) in both hemispheres (State 6, DAN-LIM); (7) Strong synchronization between the Vis and the DMN across the bilateral hemispheres (State 7, Vis-DMN); (8) High synchronization among the SMN, DAN, and DMN in the bilateral hemispheres (State 8, SMN-DAN-DMN); (9) High synchronization of the LIM in the left hemisphere with the DMN (State 9, LIM-DMN).

Compared to HC, patients with MCI exhibited a significant lower occupancy rate in State 7 (Vis-DMN, Pbfn*10 = 0.030, β = 0.03) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, patients with AD showed significant reductions in both dwell time (State4, Vis-Cont: Pbfn*10 = 0.025, β = 1.12; State5, SVAN-Cont-DMN: Pbfn*10 = 0.013, β = 1.08; State6, DAN-LIM: Pbfn*10 = 0.013, β = 1.16) and occupancy rate (State4, Vis-Cont: Pbfn*10 = 0.006, β = 0.04; State5, SVAN-Cont-DMN: Pbfn*10 = 0.026, β = 0.04; State6, DAN-LIM: Pbfn*10 = 0.013, β = 0.03) in State 4 (Vis-Cont), 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) and 6 (DAN-LIM). Additionally, AD patients showed a significant increase in both dwell time (Pbfn*10 = 0.001, β=−3.18) and occupancy rate (Pbfn*10 < 0.001, β=−0.13) in State 10(Global). AD patients also exhibited a significant higher occupancy rate in State 2 (DAN-SMN, Pbfn*10 = 0.028, β=−0.05) and a significantly lower dwell time (Pbfn*10 = 0.010, β = 1.05) in State 3 (Vis-SMN) (Fig. 2B). Notably, no significant differences were observed in either dwell time or occupancy rate for any brain state between the HC and SCD groups.

In terms of brain state transitions, compared to HC, patients with SCD showed a significantly higher probability of transitioning from State 8 (SMN-DAN-DMN) to State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.010, β=−0.033). However, the likelihood of SCD patients transitioning from State 4 (Vis-Cont) to State 3 (Vis-SMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.025, β = 0.024), State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) to State 1 (Cont-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.022, β = 0.038), and State 7 (Vis-DMN) to State 1 (Cont-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.049, β = 0.035) was significantly lower (Fig. 2C).

Compared to HC, MCI patients showed an increased probability of transitioning from State 10 (Global) to State 9 (LIM-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.023, β=−0.033), and from State 7 (Vis-DMN) to State2 (DAN-SMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.048, β=−0.037). However, the likelihood of MCI patients transitioning from State 2 (DAN-SMN) to State 3 (Vis-SMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.033, β = 0.057), State 6 (DAN-LIM) to State 9 (LIM-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.036, β = 0.035), and State 10 (Global) to brain state 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.007, β = 0.064) was lower (Fig. 2D).

In patients with AD, compared to HC, there were significant differences in multiple State transitions. The probability of AD patients transitioning from State 1 (Cont-DMN) to State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.018, β=−0.030), State 2 (DAN-SMN) to State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.037, β=−0.047), State 3 (Vis-SMN) to State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.002, β=−0.104), State 4 (Vis-Cont) to State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.003, β=−0.089), State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) to State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.009, β=−0.090), and staying in State 10 (Global) (Pbfn*100 = 0.014, β=−0.150) was significantly higher. Additionally, AD patients had a higher likelihood of transitioning from State 3 (Vis-SMN) to State 2 (DAN-SMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.019, β=−0.044). However, AD patients showed a lower probability of transitioning from several other States, particularly remaining in State 3 (Vis-SMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.003, β = 0.123), state 4 (Vis-Cont) (Pbfn*100 = 0.012, β = 0.113), State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.020, β = 0.115), and State 6 (DAN-LIM) (Pbfn*100 = 0.008, β = 0.130), as well as transitions from State 1 (Cont-DMN) to State 4 (Vis-Cont) (Pbfn*100 = 0.047, β = 0.039), State 2 (DAN-SMN) to State 3 (Vis-SMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.027, β = 0.079), State 6 (DAN-LIM) to State 1 (Cont-DMN) (Pbfn*100 = 0.047, β = 0.023), and State 9 (LIM-DMN) to State 4 (Vis-Cont) (Pbfn*100 = 0.045, β = 0.026) (Fig. 2E). All brain state transition probabilities, including dwelling state sequences, are described in detail in Fig. S3.

To verify the stability of the results, we conducted a between-group comparison of the dwell time and occupancy rate from K5 to K9, and also observed similar results between groups as K = 10 (Fig. S4).

Brain state features associated with neuropsychological assessments

The correlation analysis between 10 brain states and neuropsychological assessment results showed a significant positive correlation between the dwell time of brain State 3 (Vis-SMN) (p = 0.006, β = 0.182), State5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) (p = 0.011, β = 0.167) and the MMSE scores, whereas the dwell time of brain State 10 (Global) was significantly negatively correlated with MMSE scores (p = 0.002, β= −0.202). Additionally, the dwell time of State 3 (Vis-SMN) and 6 (DAN-LIM) showed a significant negative correlation with the TMT-A scores (State 3, Vis-SMN: p = 0.015, β = −0.188; State 6, DAN-LIM: p = 0.002, β = −0.234), while the dwell time of State 10 (Global) was significantly positively correlated with TMT-A scores (p < 0.001, β = 0.339) (Fig. 3A).

Brainspan developmental gene expression linked with transient brain states

The spatiotemporal expression patterns of genes related to 10 brain states were explored. It was found that the spatiotemporal expression profiles across developmental stages revealed a statistically significant enrichment of genes related to State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) in the cortical regions of the brain during infancy, adolescence, and adulthood (Fig. 4A). Specifically, during infancy, the genes were notably enriched in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC; PFDR = 0.048), medial prefrontal cortex (MFC; PFDR = 0.048), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DFC; PFDR = 0.048), primary somatosensory cortex (S1C; PFDR = 0.048), and inferior parietal cortex (IPC; PFDR = 0.043) (Fig. 4B). In adolescence and adulthood, these genes were specifically enriched in the OFC (adolescence: PFDR = 0.032; adulthood: PFDR = 0.048), MFC (adolescence: PFDR = 0.048; adulthood: PFDR = 0.032), and the inferior temporal gyrus (ITG) (adolescence: PFDR = 0.048; adulthood: PFDR = 0.032) (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the genes associated with the remaining 9 states did not exhibit statistically significant spatial clustering patterns in the brain.

Neuronal cell types linked with transient brain states

We performed cell type enrichment analysis on the gene sets associated with 10 brain states. The results showed that the gene set associated with State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) was significantly enriched in excitatory neuron subtypes, including Ex 4 (PFDR = 0.007), Ex 5 (PFDR = 0.007), and Ex 7 (PFDR = 0.026). It also showed specific expression signals in the inhibitory neuron subtype In 1 (PFDR = 0.047) (Fig. 5). Anatomically, Ex4, Ex5, and Ex7 represent cortical projection neurons with distinct laminar distributions, with Ex4 predominantly in layer 5 and Ex5/Ex7 mainly in layer 6. In1 is a GABAergic inhibitory interneuron, specially a parvalbumin-positive (PV⁺), fast-spiking subtype originating from the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) [37]. In contrast, the gene sets associated with the other 9 states did not show statistically significant enrichment in either excitatory or inhibitory neuron subtypes.

Gene ontology analyses linked with transient brain states

GO analysis of the 10 brain states revealed that only State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN), characterized by high synchronization of the SVAN with the Cont and the DMN in both hemispheres, exhibited specific gene enrichment in molecular functions related to ion channel activity. The genes most strongly enriched in this state include GLRA2, GLRB, and GLRA3, with the primary GO terms being “extracellular glycine-gated ion channel activity” (GO:0016933; PFDR = 0.0006) and its subclass “extracellular glycine-gated chloride ion channel activity” (GO:0016934; PFDR = 0.0006), both of which are involved in regulating inhibitory neurotransmitter transmission (Fig. 6). Notably, no significant enrichment was observed in the cellular component or biological process categories (PFDR >0.05). Furthermore, no statistically significant signals across any GO functional categories (molecular function, cellular component, or biological process) for the remaining 9 states.

Discussion

This study integrated dFC, clinical neuropsychological assessments, and whole-brain transcriptomic data to investigate the progressive evolution of brain network dysfunction and its underlying molecular mechanisms in the ADS. The findings revealed a clear, stage-dependent of dFC abnormalities in ADS. In the SCD stage, disruptions primarily manifested as altered transition probabilities between brain network states. As the disease progressed to MCI and AD stages, dFC abnormalities evolved into marked changes in dwell time and occurrence rates of specific brain states, which were strongly associated with cognitive decline, particularly as reflected in MMSE and TMT-A performance. Most critically, this study identified a specific dFC aberration—State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN), characterized by heightened synchrony involving the SVAN, Cont, and DMN—that was linked to gene expression profiles involving in glycine-mediated synaptic transmission pathways and specific neuronal subtypes, especially excitatory neurons (Ex4, Ex5, Ex7) and inhibitory interneurons (In1). Furthermore, these genes showed spatiotemporal expression patterns that extend across the entire lifespan, from infancy through the advanced stages of ADS pathology.

The core findings of this study revealed a progressive pattern of dysfunction in brain network dynamics within the ADS, closely aligned with the clinical stages of the disease. At the SCD stage, no significant alterations in dwell time or occupancy rate of brain states were observed. However, subtle yet meaningful abnormalities emerged in the transition probabilities between network states. Specifically, SCD patients exhibited a significantly increased transition rate from a complex state involving the coordinated activity of multiple higher-order cognitive networks -State 8 (SMN-DAN-DMN) to the globally synchronized state (State 10, Global). Meanwhile, their flexibility in transitioning between other higher-order cognitive network states (e.g. from Vis-Cont to Vis-SMN, from SVAN-Cont-DMN and Vis-DMNto Cont-DMN) was significantly reduced. These findings suggested that early dFC disruptions may reflect a shift toward less flexible, globally synchronized brain dynamics. Given that dFC impairments are already present at the SCD stage and progressively worsen with disease progression, our findings provide crucial candidate metrics for future research on sensitive biomarkers for the early identification and monitoring of ADS.

As the disease progressed to the stages of MCI and AD, brain network dysfunction underwent a fundamental transformation. Abnormalities extended beyond disrupted transitions to a marked collapse in the intrinsic stability of key cognitive network states. Specifically, in MCI patients, the occupancy rate of State 7 (Vis-DMN) decreased. This aligns with findings from Stampacchia et al. [38], who attributed reduced stability at the MCI stage to the reorganization and adaptation of functional connectivity, resulting in fewer stable connections across individuals. In AD patients, this instability became more widespread and severe, with significant decreases observed in the dwell time and occupancy rate of State 4 (Vis-Cont), State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN), and State 6 (DAN-LIM). Meanwhile, the dwell time and occurrence rate of State 10 (Global), a low-complexity state representing global synchronization, increased sharply, along with an increased probability of transitioning from States 1–5 (Cont-DMN, DAN-SMN, Vis-SMN, Vis-Cont, SVAN-Cont-DMN) to State 10 (Global), in align with Wang et al.’s study [39]. Importantly, these changes in dFC metrics were significantly associated with cognitive assessment scores, including the MMSE and TMT-A, underscoring their clinical relevance. This finding highlighted that dysfunction in network dynamics constitute a core neural mechanism driving the progressive cognitive decline in individuals with ADS [40].

Further research revealed that genes associated with State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) exhibited pronounced spatiotemporal specificity, suggesting their involvement in both early neurodevelopment and later pathological progression in AD. During infancy, these genes were enriched in brain regions such as the OFC, MFC, DFC, S1C, and IPC, suggesting that AD risk genes may influence early brain development [41]. Supporting this, Chen et al. [42] observed synaptic dysfunction in the early life stages of 5×FAD transgenic mice, indicating that gene-mediated neurocircuit developmental defects may already be latent in infancy. As neurodevelopment progressed into adolescence and adulthood, gene expression became more region-specific, concentrating in the OFC, MFC, and ITG. This aligns with the findings by Preti et al. [43], who revealed that as the brain’s neural development matures, the specificity of its regions significantly increases. Notably, these regions of sustained gene enrichment in later stages, significantly overlapped with areas affected by hallmarks of AD pathologies, including Aβ and NFTs [44]. These pathological aggregates disrupted the homeostasis and can trigger a cascade of molecular events, including synaptic integrity loss, mitochondrial dysfunction and ultimately neuronal apoptosis [45, 46]. Together, these findings suggest that AD risk genes exert a lifelong influence on brain structure and function, with effects that begin in early development and extent into the later stages of disease progression.

Furthermore, this study found that genes associated State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) also exhibited marked cell-type specificity, with expression spanning both excitatory (Ex4, Ex5, Ex7) and inhibitory (In1) neuronal subtypes. As widely reported, AD pathology involves a cascade imbalance in the excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) network, characterized by loss of glutamatergic (Glu) neurons and GABAergic inhibitory interneurons, leading to pathological hyperexcitability [47, 48]. In this study, GO enrichment analysis further revealed that genes were significantly enriched in the glycinergic synaptic transmission pathway. Glycine receptors, widely distributed on the postsynaptic membrane, mediated inhibitory neurotransmission and were essential for maintaining E/I balance in the brain [49]. In AD, this balance was often disrupted, leading to an increased risk of epileptiform activity [50], a phenomenon observed in up to 42% of AD patients, which could accelerate cognitive decline [51]. Research by Bi et al. further demonstrated that impaired inhibitory receptors function contributed neuronal hyperexcitability, which exacerbated Aβ deposition and worsen cognitive dysfunction [47]. Therefore, our findings highlight that abnormalities in E/I neurons, along with dysfunction in glycine receptor-mediated inhibitory signaling, are critical contributors to E/I imbalance in brain, collectively contributing to pathological neuronal hyperexcitability and the progression of AD.

Limitations and perspectives

The main strength of this study lies in its comprehensive inclusion of multiple clinical stages of ADS and the adoption of a novel dFC analysis method known as LEiDA. Additionally, the integrative framework that bridges macroscale neuroimaging with microscale transcriptomic data, enables a multilevel understanding of disease progression. However, there are still several limitations. First, the AHBA gene expression data is derived from healthy individuals, while individuals with ADS may undergo pathological remodeling of gene regulation in their brains. Therefore, the identified gene-function state associations may underestimate the impact of AD-specific molecular mechanisms on brain network dynamics. Second, reliable dFC detection typically requires more than 10 min of scanning. The relatively short acquisition time may bias LEiDA toward high-frequency dominant states, potentially reducing sensitivity to rarer or more transient states. Finally, the Schaefer100-Yeo7 atlas used in this study includes only cortical regions and excludes subcortical structures. Future studies should extend rs-fMRI scanning durations and integrate subcortical structures to comprehensively elucidate brain state dynamics mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a clear, stage-dependent of dFC abnormalities throughout the ADS. In the SCD stage, abnormalities primarily manifested as disruptions in the transition probabilities between brain network states. As the disease progressed to the MCI and AD stages, dFC abnormalities evolved into significant alterations in the dwell time and occurrence rates of specific brain states, with these changes closely correlating with declines in cognitive function, as assessed by the MMSE and TMT-A. Furthermore, the study revealed that the dynamic alterations of State 5 (SVAN-Cont-DMN) are not only associated with E/I imbalance in the brain, but also that its related genes exert a sustained influence on the brain that spans the entire lifespan. These findings uncovered the molecular underpinnings of early network dysfunction in the ADS.
