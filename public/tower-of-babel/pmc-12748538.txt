A new single input common ground seven level triple boost switched capacitor based inverter
Authors: Abolfazl Hosseinzadeh, Hasan Alipour, Jaber Pouladi, Leila Mohammadian, Taher Abedinzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a new 7-L switched capacitor-based inverter suitable for use in photovoltaic applications. Most conventional inverters not only can’t enhance the input voltage level with a single input source, but also require an isolated input source due to the problems of leakage current of solar panels. Therefore, the presented converter is employed to increase the voltage level of the input with switched capacitor cells; consequently, the seven-level inverter can be achieved with a single input source. Additionally, the proposed topology, due to the common ground between the input source and the load, reduces the differential mode and common mode voltages, thereby decreasing the leakage current of solar panels. Otherwise, this current increases the harmonic content of the output waveform, and necessitates a larger output filter size. To verify the accuracy of the claims, a 300 W laboratory prototype of the proposed inverter is tested, and simulation results carried out in PSCAD/EMTDC are provided as well. The overall theoretical efficiency is about 97.44%. Finally, the proposed inverter is compared with other states of the art.

Introduction

Global energy consumption is experiencing a steady increase on a daily basis. In response to the rising demand for energy, large-scale renewable sources, particularly solar photovoltaic systems, are being integrated into traditional power generation methods. However, generating substantial amounts of photovoltaic energy at a single site and transmitting this power over long distances can diminish system efficiency1. To address this challenge, distributed generation has been implemented, enhancing overall system efficiency. Notably, the contribution of photovoltaic generation from solar rooftops has surged, now accounting for over 20% of the total photovoltaic generation capacity2.

Solar photovoltaic (PV) systems are typically favored for operation in a grid-connected configuration to eliminate the need for large and expensive battery storage. The solar rooftop installations are integrated into a low-voltage distribution network operating at 380 V (three-phase) and 230 V (rms) for single-phase applications. To enhance the solar panels’ low DC voltage and convert to the AC grid voltage, high-gain DC-DC converters3 are employed at the input stage of the DC-AC converters.

Transformer-less inverters are increasingly significant in renewable energy systems, particularly in photovoltaic (PV) applications, where they contribute to numerous distributed generation (DG) systems in both commercial and residential sectors. These systems offer several advantages over traditional transformer-based configurations, including enhanced efficiency, suitable power density, and reduced costs. Furthermore, the elimination of the transformer from the inverter’s output aids in fulfilling various grid code requirements associated with many DG systems4,5.

A wide range of multilevel inverter (MLI) topologies have been proposed for DC–AC conversion in the literature. Among these, the diode-clamped, flying-capacitor (FC), and cascaded H-bridge MLIs6 are the most prominent configurations. The diode-clamped and FC MLIs inherently face capacitor voltage imbalance challenges; however, several modulation and control strategies such as redundant switching state selection and optimized space vector modulation have been proposed to mitigate this issue without requiring additional hardware7,8. In contrast, the cascaded H-bridge MLI offers a modular structure but requires multiple isolated DC sources9, which is not a case in the photovoltaic applications.

The use of common ground type inverters can significantly enhance system performance by addressing issues related to leakage current10. In this configuration, the negative terminal of the photovoltaic (PV) panel is directly linked to the grid’s neutral point, effectively allowing for the appropriate bypassing of the overall common mode voltage (CMV). In light of this11, presented the virtual DC link concept that uses five active power switches.

Recently, several reduced switch nine-level inverter structures have been introduced in references12,13. These innovative structures utilize fewer power semiconductors while achieving an equivalent number of output levels compared to conventional multilevel inverter (MLI) topologies. However, it is essential to note that the performance of these topologies may be constrained, rendering them potentially unsuitable for applications that necessitate a boost in input voltage. In references13,14, it is noted that certain topologies necessitate a greater number of isolated DC sources, with the number of sources correlating positively with an increase in voltage levels. References15–18 present several recently proposed topologies that employ the switched-capacitor (SC) technique. In these configurations, each capacitor is alternatively connected in parallel and in series with the DC source during each cycle of output voltage. This arrangement facilitates the charging of capacitors in parallel and their subsequent discharge to the load in series. In reference15, the structure yields a five-level output with a gain of two. Reference16 describes topologies that provide a seven-level output voltage through a triple boosting mechanism. Reference17 further discusses topologies that generate a nine-level output voltage; however, it should be noted that despite achieving this nine-level output, their voltage-boosting ability remains relatively low, with a gain of only two.

In reference19, a generalized boost multilevel inverter is introduced, utilizing the switched-capacitor technique. This design incorporates a series-parallel arrangement of switches, diodes, and capacitors to generate output voltage levels, alongside an H-bridge for polarity generation. The inverter described in reference20 incorporates a quasi-resonant unit that utilizes the switched-capacitor technique for voltage level generation. This system employs a single inductor within the resonance circuit coupled with an H-bridge designated for polarity generation. The inverter discussed in21 features two T-type modules at both ends of a series of interconnected switched capacitor (SC) units. Each of these cross-connected units consists of six switches and two capacitors. As more SC units are incorporated, the number of voltage levels and gain of the modular multilevel inverter (MLI) is enhanced.

Reference22 presents a nine-level switched capacitor-based multi-level inverter with quadruple boost capabilities, specifically designed for photovoltaic (PV) and is suitable for transformer-less grid integration applications. In23, a hybrid T-type nine-level inverter with a boosting gain of three is presented for PV applications, featuring four capacitors for voltage enhancement and a five-level T-type module at the output. Reference24 details a reduced switch quadruple boost nine-level inverter for EV and drive applications. In25, a five-level switched capacitor-based inverter has been expanded to a generalized topology characterized by high gain. This configuration features a repeating unit composed of four switches and a capacitor. Subsequently, as detailed in26, a generalized high gain inverter has been developed utilizing a repeating switched capacitor (SC) unit, which incorporates two switches, one diode, a capacitor, and an H-bridge. Also, in27, Reduced component high voltage-boost single-source 13-L switched capacitor inverter is presented, in which two different series-parallel switched-capacitor units interconnected. The topology features self-voltage balancing and the blocking voltage across the switches is less than the output voltage.

In28, a reduced-component switched-capacitor boost multilevel inverter topology is presented. To generate a nine-level stepped-up voltage across the load, the converter needs only eight power switches and two capacitors. To get the appropriate nine-level switching pattern, phase disposition pulse width modulation has been used.

In order to overcome the aforementioned challenges and applications in PV systems, this paper proposes a novel common ground seven-level switched capacitor-based inverter with the following merits:

Through these capacitors and switching states, the 7-level inverter can be produced.

Operating modes

In this part, the operating modes of the seven-level inverter are presented.

Voltage level (0Vin):

Zero voltage level (see Fig. 2d and e) can be achieved either by switches S6 and S7 for positive current flow or switch S8 and diode D2 for negative current flow. Capacitor C2 charged to Vin through switch S4 and diode D1 in the positive state and the capacitor C3 charged to 3Vin through switches S1 and S5 in the negative state. Also, capacitors C1 and C2 discharged in this operating mode.

Voltage level (+ Vin):

Capacitor C2 is charged in this state (see Fig. 2a) to Vin through Switch S4 and diode D1, and the output voltage level is provided by the main input voltage source, switches S5 and S7 and diode D1.

Voltage level (+ 2Vin):

Capacitor C1 is charged in this state (see Fig. 2b) through switches S2 and S3. The output voltage level is produced by switches S3, S5 and S7. Capacitor C2 discharges in this operating mode.

Fig. 2Operating modes of the proposed seven-level inverter. (a) + 1Vin, (b) + 2Vin, (c) + 3Vin, (d) zero state (positive flow), (e) zero state (negative flow), (f) -1Vin, (g) -2Vin, (h) -3Vin state.

Operating modes of the proposed seven-level inverter. (a) + 1Vin, (b) + 2Vin, (c) + 3Vin, (d) zero state (positive flow), (e) zero state (negative flow), (f) -1Vin, (g) -2Vin, (h) -3Vin state.

Voltage level (+ 3Vin):

The output voltage is + 3Vin (see Fig. 2c) and is produced through switch S7 and diode D2. Capacitor C3 is charged through switches S1 and S5 and diode D2 and discharged in this state. Capacitors C1 and C2 discharge, too.

Voltage level (-Vin):

As can be seen from Fig. 2f, capacitors C1 to C3 supply the output load through S2, S5 and S8. Also, capacitor C1 charges to Vin in this state through switches S2 and S3.

Voltage level (-2Vin):

Capacitors C3 and C2 provide the output voltage (see Fig. 2g) through switches S4, S5 and S8. Capacitor C2 is charged to Vin through switch S4 and diode D1 in this operating mode, too.

Voltage level (-3Vin):

The output voltage − 3Vin is provided by capacitor C3 and switches S6 and S8 (see Fig. 2h). Meanwhile, capacitor C1 is charged to Vin through switches S2 and S3.

Conclusion

In this study, a new seven-level single-input common-ground triple boost switched-capacitor-based inverter is proposed. Due to the common ground between the input and the output, the proposed topology can be used in photovoltaic applications. Because of the reduction of differential mode and common mode voltages, the panel leakage current to the grid is reduced, and harmonics are prevented. Also, the proposed 7-level inverter reduces harmonics to the grid and the size of the grid filter inductor as well. The number of switches in the topology is 8, and the lower number of switches beside the two switches that do not need an anti-body diode reduces the overall efficiency. The studied inverter can reach 3Vin with a single input source, and the overall cost factor is reasonable in comparison to the other topologies. The simulation results and experimental 300 W results confirm the theoretical calculations provided in the literature, and the overall theoretical efficiency of the proposed topology is 97.44%. For further investigations, the grid connection of the proposed topology and studying different controlling methods, like MPC, is suggested to the researchers.
