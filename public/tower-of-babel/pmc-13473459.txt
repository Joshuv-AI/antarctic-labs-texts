Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning via calibrated deep surrogates for rare-earth electrolysis energy efficiency
Authors: Yu Liu, Jun Peng, Fan Yang, Haitao Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Improving energy efficiency in rare-earth molten salt electrolysis is challenging due to strong process nonlinearity, limited evaluation budgets, and strict operational constraints. Existing data-driven parameter tuning methods often rely on deterministic surrogate models and pointwise optimization, which ignore predictive uncertainty and may result in aggressive decisions and increased risk of constraint violations. To address these issues, this paper proposes a risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning framework based on calibrated deep surrogate modeling and uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization. A deep ensemble surrogate is employed to predict energy-efficiency indicators and quantify uncertainty, while a calibration procedure is introduced to improve the statistical reliability of predictive uncertainty. Based on the calibrated surrogate, parameter tuning is formulated as a risk-sensitive constrained optimization problem and solved efficiently using an uncertainty-aware acquisition strategy. Numerical experiments show that the proposed framework achieves faster convergence, higher energy-efficiency improvement, and lower constraint violation probability than uncertainty-agnostic alternatives, demonstrating its effectiveness for safe and efficient electrolysis operation.

Introduction

Rare-earth molten salt electrolysis is a core industrial route for producing high-purity rare-earth metals and alloys, where energy consumption and current efficiency jointly determine production cost, carbon footprint, and product consistency1–3. In practice, plant operators adjust multiple operating parameters, such as current, voltage, temperature, and feed-related variables, to improve energy efficiency while maintaining stable electrochemical and thermal states4,5. However, the electrolysis process exhibits strong nonlinearity, thermal inertia, and regime-dependent behavior, while feasible operating windows are often narrow because of equipment limitations, safety margins, and product-quality requirements6,7. Consequently, improving energy efficiency in rare-earth electrolysis is essentially a constrained decision-making problem that requires sample-efficient parameter tuning with explicit consideration of operational risk.

Recent studies on rare-earth molten salt electrolysis have mainly focused on electrochemical mechanisms, energy-consumption analysis, electrolyte/electrode design, and environmental impacts. Vahidi and Zhao1 assessed the environmental footprint of rare-earth metal and alloy production via molten salt electrolysis, showing that electrical energy consumption is a dominant contributor to production cost and environmental burden. Liao et al.2 reviewed the electrolytic preparation of rare-earth metals and alloys and summarized the influence of electrolyte composition, current density, temperature, and cell configuration on production stability. Yin et al.3 discussed the recovery and separation of rare-earth elements through molten salt electrolysis, emphasizing the role of electrolyte chemistry and reaction conditions. Holcombe et al.4 investigated sustainable and energy-efficient rare-earth metal production through chloride-based molten salt electrolysis, highlighting the importance of electrolyte design and cell-operation conditions for improving energy performance. These studies provide valuable physical and chemical insights into rare-earth electrolysis, but most of them mainly target material preparation, cell design, electrolyte chemistry, or environmental assessment rather than closed-loop multi-parameter tuning under predictive uncertainty.

From the perspective of operational specificity, existing studies generally focus on one of several process links, such as electrolyte preparation, electrolysis cell operation, electrode material design, or product recovery. Studies on electrolyte and electrode design can improve long-term cell stability and reduce undesired reactions, while process-level analyses can reveal the relationship between operating conditions and energy consumption. However, these methods often require detailed physicochemical knowledge, accurate parameter calibration, or controlled experimental conditions. Their direct extension to industrial parameter tuning is limited when the process is affected by measurement noise, raw-material fluctuation, operating-regime transitions, and limited evaluation budgets.

From the perspective of performance implications, previous studies have reported potential benefits such as reduced specific energy consumption, improved current efficiency, enhanced product consistency, and lower environmental impact. Nevertheless, these improvements are often evaluated under fixed or manually selected operating conditions. In practical industrial operation, current, voltage, temperature, feeding rate, and oxide concentration are strongly coupled. Optimizing one parameter independently may lead to unstable operation or degraded performance in other indicators. Therefore, energy-efficiency improvement should be formulated as a multi-parameter constrained optimization problem rather than a single-factor adjustment problem.

Existing optimization practices for electrolysis tuning can be broadly categorized into rule-based heuristic adjustment, mechanism-driven modeling and optimization, and data-driven surrogate-assisted search8,9. Heuristic tuning is simple to deploy but heavily relies on expert experience and often converges to conservative operating points, resulting in limited and non-repeatable performance gains. Mechanism-based optimization leverages electrochemical and thermal models to guide parameter selection; nevertheless, accurate mechanism-model construction and calibration remain difficult in industrial environments because of unmodeled disturbances, measurement noise, raw-material fluctuation, and evolving operating regimes3,5,10. Data-driven surrogate modeling offers an attractive alternative by learning the mapping from operating conditions to energy-efficiency indicators directly from historical production data or high-fidelity simulations, and has shown promising performance in sample-efficient optimization for complex chemical and materials processes11–14.

From the perspective of methodological limitations, mechanism-based models are interpretable but difficult to calibrate accurately in industrial environments, while conventional data-driven models can learn nonlinear input–output mappings but usually provide only deterministic point predictions. Such deterministic predictions are insufficient for safe decision-making because prediction errors and data sparsity may result in overly aggressive parameter recommendations. Although Bayesian optimization and active learning have been applied to chemical and materials process optimization9,11,13,14, many existing methods do not explicitly calibrate predictive uncertainty or incorporate probabilistic feasibility constraints. Despite these advances, two critical limitations prevent existing surrogate-based tuning methods from being reliably deployed in rare-earth electrolysis. First, most surrogate models are trained deterministically and provide only point predictions, whereas the underlying process inevitably involves uncertainty arising from measurement noise, unobserved disturbances, and regime transitions10,15. When predictive uncertainty is ignored, optimization may become overly aggressive, leading to unstable decisions, oscillatory tuning trajectories, and an increased probability of violating operational constraints16–18. Second, even when uncertainty estimates are incorporated into deep surrogate models, they are often poorly calibrated, producing systematic under-coverage or over-conservative prediction intervals19,20. Such unreliable uncertainty directly degrades risk-aware optimization: under-estimated uncertainty encourages unsafe exploration, whereas over-estimated uncertainty suppresses performance improvement and wastes limited evaluation budgets. Therefore, a practical parameter tuning framework should provide not only accurate predictions but also statistically reliable uncertainty estimates and a principled mechanism to explicitly incorporate feasibility into the optimization process.

The proposed framework is related to recent data-driven chance-constrained learning methods, such as chance-constrained optimal scheduling using partial input convex neural networks21. However, the present work differs in both problem setting and methodological integration. Existing chance-constrained scheduling studies usually focus on power-system operation and exploit structural properties of the scheduling problem to approximate feasible decision regions. In contrast, rare-earth molten salt electrolysis involves nonlinear electrochemical and thermal coupling, limited evaluation budgets, and uncertain energy-efficiency responses. Therefore, our focus is not only on representing chance constraints, but also on calibrating predictive uncertainty and embedding it into a sequential Bayesian optimization loop. Specifically, the proposed method combines deep ensemble uncertainty quantification, prediction-interval calibration, risk-sensitive utility construction, probabilistic feasibility evaluation, and constrained acquisition optimization. This integration enables the framework to recommend operating parameters while explicitly balancing energy-efficiency improvement and constraint-violation risk. We do not claim that chance-constrained optimization itself is newly proposed in this work; rather, the contribution lies in adapting and integrating calibrated uncertainty-aware chance-constrained decision-making into rare-earth electrolysis parameter tuning, where predictive reliability and operational safety are both critical.

To address the above challenges, this paper proposes a risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning framework for rare-earth electrolysis energy-efficiency optimization based on calibrated deep surrogate modeling and uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization. First, a deep ensemble surrogate is constructed to model key energy-efficiency indicators while capturing predictive uncertainty22, and a dedicated calibration stage is applied to align predictive intervals with empirical coverage without distorting predictive means. Second, parameter tuning is formulated as a risk-sensitive constrained optimization problem, where uncertainty exposure is explicitly penalized and operational requirements are enforced through probabilistic feasibility constraints. Third, an uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization strategy is developed to efficiently select candidate operating conditions under limited evaluation budgets by jointly considering risk-sensitive improvement and feasibility probability23–25. The resulting closed-loop workflow enables multi-parameter joint tuning and provides transparent control over the trade-off between performance improvement and operational risk.

To further clarify the logical connection among the main methodological sections, the overall workflow of the proposed framework is summarized as follows. Section "Problem statement and preliminaries" first establishes the decision-making foundation by defining the controllable operating parameters, energy-efficiency indicators, and safety feasibility constraints for rare-earth molten salt electrolysis. Based on this formulation, Section "Calibrated deep surrogate modeling with uncertainty quantification" constructs the probabilistic modeling layer, where a calibrated deep ensemble surrogate maps operating conditions to predictive distributions of current efficiency and specific energy consumption. The calibrated predictive means and uncertainties are then used in Section "Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning" to build a risk-sensitive utility function and probabilistic feasibility measure. Finally, the constrained Bayesian optimization strategy sequentially selects candidate operating conditions by jointly considering expected improvement, predictive uncertainty, and feasibility probability. In this way, the manuscript follows a closed-loop logic from process-variable definition, to uncertainty-aware performance prediction, and finally to risk-sensitive parameter optimization.

Extensive numerical experiments validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The calibrated deep ensemble surrogate achieves strong predictive accuracy while substantially improving uncertainty consistency, as evidenced by improved prediction interval coverage on held-out test data. In optimization experiments with a fixed evaluation budget, the proposed method converges faster, attains higher best-so-far energy-efficiency utility, and significantly reduces empirical constraint violation probability compared with uncertainty-agnostic alternatives. Moreover, varying the feasibility tolerance yields a smooth performance–safety frontier, demonstrating that the framework enables explicit and continuous control over the trade-off between efficiency gain and operational risk.

Problem statement and preliminaries

This section formalizes the energy-efficiency optimization problem for industrial rare-earth molten salt electrolysis from a data-driven decision-making perspective. Specifically, we identify the controllable operating parameters, define physically meaningful energy-efficiency metrics, and characterize the operational constraints that govern stable and safe electrolysis. These elements jointly establish a well-posed optimization problem under uncertainty and provide the necessary preliminaries for the probabilistic surrogate modeling and risk-sensitive parameter tuning framework developed in subsequent sections.

Rather than relying on detailed first-principles electrochemical models, which are often impractical under realistic industrial conditions, the formulation adopted here emphasizes measurable quantities and data-accessible variables. This design choice ensures that the resulting learning and optimization framework is directly applicable to real-world electrolysis systems, while remaining sufficiently general to accommodate uncertainty, nonlinearity, and limited data availability.

Rather than considering a large number of auxiliary variables, this study focuses on a compact yet representative set of controllable parameters that are routinely available in industrial practice and have dominant influence on system performance.

Energy-efficiency metrics and learning targets

Given the controllable operating condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}\in \Omega$$\end{document} (Section "Decision Variables and Controllable Parameter Space"), energy-efficiency optimization requires performance metrics that are (i) physically meaningful, (ii) computable from routinely logged industrial signals, and (iii) suitable as learning targets for data-driven surrogates. Optimizing either metric alone may be misleading, since η and e are coupled through the underlying electrochemical and thermal dynamics; therefore, we model them jointly.

Safety and feasibility constraints for stable operation

In rare-earth molten salt electrolysis, energy-efficiency optimization cannot be pursued over the entire bounded decision space Ω without considering operational safety. Beyond hard equipment limits, stable electrolysis requires that critical process variables remain within empirically validated safety margins in order to avoid abnormal cell conditions such as anode effects, electrolyte instability, and excessive energy loss. Consequently, the optimization problem must explicitly incorporate safety and feasibility constraints.

We first formalize basic operational requirements using deterministic constraint functions. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_U$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_T$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_C$$\end{document} denote the voltage, temperature, and concentration components of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}$$\end{document}, respectively. Under this setting, the basic safety constraints can be written as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&T_{\min } \le \, x_T \,\le T_{\max }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&C_{\min } \le \, x_C \,\le C_{\max }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&x_U \le \, U_{\max }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\min },T_{\max },C_{\min },C_{\max }$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{\max }$$\end{document} are predefined safety thresholds. In this study, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_T$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_C$$\end{document} represent interval-averaged controllable or measurable operating variables. Thus, no additional explicit functional relationship such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} is required for imposing these basic feasibility bounds. For other industrial settings where temperature or concentration is not directly controlled but is instead a process response, the same formulation can be extended by replacing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_T$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_C$$\end{document} with surrogate-predicted constrained variables. Although the bounds in (12) are simple in form, the induced feasible region in the decision space is generally nonconvex due to strong nonlinear coupling among decision variables.

In practical operation, deterministic satisfaction of (12) cannot be guaranteed. Sensor noise, unmodeled disturbances, and intrinsic process variability introduce uncertainty into both measured states and surrogate predictions. As a result, a parameter configuration that appears feasible under point estimates may still violate safety limits with non-negligible probability. To explicitly account for this uncertainty, we adopt a probabilistic feasibility formulation based on chance constraints.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {F}$$\end{document} denote the event that all operational constraints are satisfied. Instead of enforcing hard feasibility, we require16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}\big ( \mathscr {F} \mid \textbf{x} \big ) \ge 1 - \delta \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta \in (0,1)$$\end{document} is a user-specified risk tolerance parameter. Smaller values of δ correspond to more conservative operation, while larger values allow controlled exploration closer to safety boundaries.

Under the probabilistic surrogate modeling framework introduced in Section "Calibrated deep surrogate modeling with uncertainty quantification", the probability in (16) can be evaluated from predictive distributions of the constrained variables.

Although \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_T$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_C$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_U$$\end{document} denote the nominal or interval-averaged operating values used for decision-making, the actually realized process variables may deviate from these nominal values because of measurement noise, control error, thermal inertia, and unmodeled disturbances. Therefore, the probabilistic constraint is applied to the realized constrained variables. For example, let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{T}(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote the realized interval-level electrolyte temperature under a candidate operating condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}$$\end{document}. Its predictive distribution can be approximated as17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widetilde{T}(\textbf{x}) \sim \mathscr {N}\!\left( \mu _T(\textbf{x}),\,\sigma _T^2(\textbf{x})\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} represent the predicted mean and standard deviation of the realized temperature. When temperature is directly controlled and well measured, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} can be close to the nominal component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_T$$\end{document}, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} characterizes measurement and process variability. When temperature is treated as a response variable, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _T(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} can be estimated by the probabilistic surrogate. Then the probability of satisfying the temperature constraint (13) is18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}\!\left( T_{\min } \le \widetilde{T} \le T_{\max }\right) = \Phi \!\left( \frac{T_{\max }-\mu _T(\textbf{x})}{\sigma _T(\textbf{x})}\right) - \Phi \!\left( \frac{T_{\min }-\mu _T(\textbf{x})}{\sigma _T(\textbf{x})}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Phi (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. Analogous expressions can be obtained for other safety constraints.

For optimization, it is convenient to aggregate individual constraint satisfaction probabilities into a single feasibility measure. Assuming conditional independence among constraint violations, the overall feasibility probability can be approximated as19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}\big ( \mathscr {F} \mid \textbf{x} \big ) \approx \prod _{j=1}^{J} \mathbb {P}\!\left( g_j(\textbf{x}) \le 0\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This approximation enables efficient computation and provides a smooth, differentiable surrogate for constraint enforcement.

The feasibility probability in (19) acts as a soft constraint that can be seamlessly integrated into the optimization process. In regions where predictive uncertainty is small, it approaches a binary feasibility indicator, recovering deterministic constraint enforcement. Conversely, in regions with high epistemic uncertainty, the feasibility probability naturally penalizes risky parameter configurations, thereby preventing overly aggressive updates that may compromise operational stability. This probabilistic constraint formulation establishes a consistent interface between empirical safety rules and data-driven optimization, and will be directly incorporated into the constrained Bayesian optimization framework developed in Section Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning.

Risk-sensitive optimization objective

The preceding subsections have established a probabilistic description of energy-efficiency performance (Section "Energy-efficiency metrics and learning targets") and operational feasibility under uncertainty (Section "Safety and feasibility constraints for stable operation"). Based on these formulations, this subsection defines a risk-sensitive optimization objective that explicitly incorporates predictive uncertainty, rather than relying solely on point estimates of performance metrics.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _\eta (\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _\eta (\textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote the predictive mean and standard deviation of the current efficiency, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _e(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _e(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote those of the specific energy consumption, as inferred from the calibrated probabilistic surrogate. To balance expected performance and uncertainty exposure, we define the following risk-sensitive utility function:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} J(\textbf{x}) \triangleq w_\eta \Big ( \mu _\eta (\textbf{x}) - \lambda _\eta \sigma _\eta (\textbf{x}) \Big ) - w_e \Big ( \mu _e(\textbf{x}) + \lambda _e \sigma _e(\textbf{x}) \Big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_\eta ,w_e>0$$\end{document} are weighting coefficients reflecting the relative importance of efficiency improvement and energy reduction, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\eta ,\lambda _e\ge 0$$\end{document} are risk-aversion parameters that control the penalty imposed on predictive uncertainty.

The structure of (20) reveals that uncertainty is treated asymmetrically for the two metrics. For current efficiency, uncertainty is penalized by subtracting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\eta \sigma _\eta (\textbf{x})$$\end{document}, thereby discouraging overly optimistic estimates. For energy consumption, uncertainty is penalized by adding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _e\sigma _e(\textbf{x})$$\end{document}, reflecting a conservative preference against underestimating energy cost. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\eta =\lambda _e=0$$\end{document}, the objective reduces to a conventional mean-based formulation, recovering standard data-driven optimization as a special case.

The proposed objective admits a clear interpretation from a probabilistic robustness perspective. Assuming that each predictive distribution is approximately Gaussian,21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y(\textbf{x}) \sim \mathscr {N}\!\big (\mu (\textbf{x}),\,\sigma ^2(\textbf{x})\big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}the quantity22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mu (\textbf{x}) \pm \lambda \sigma (\textbf{x}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}corresponds to a confidence bound on the predicted outcome. Accordingly, maximizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} can be interpreted as maximizing a lower confidence bound of current efficiency while minimizing an upper confidence bound of energy consumption. This interpretation clarifies that the objective explicitly guards against overly optimistic predictions that may arise in data-sparse or highly nonlinear regions of the decision space.

Compared with worst-case or distributionally robust formulations, which typically require explicit uncertainty sets or adversarial sampling, the objective in (20) provides a computationally efficient alternative. By directly leveraging predictive uncertainty learned from data, it achieves a favorable trade-off between robustness and scalability, making it suitable for industrial-scale deployment.

It is important to note that the risk-sensitive utility alone does not ensure safe operation, as parameter configurations with high utility may still violate feasibility constraints with non-negligible probability. Therefore, the overall optimization problem is formulated by jointly considering the utility function (20) and the chance constraints introduced in Section "Safety and feasibility constraints for stable operation":23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \max _{\textbf{x}\in \Omega } \quad&J(\textbf{x}) \\ \text {s.t.} \quad&\mathbb {P}\big (\mathscr {F}\mid \textbf{x}\big ) \ge 1-\delta \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta \in (0,1)$$\end{document} specifies the allowable probability of constraint violation.

The formulation in (23) yields a clear separation of roles: the risk-sensitive utility governs the trade-off between expected performance and uncertainty, while the chance constraint enforces a minimum level of operational reliability. Together, they define a well-posed optimization problem that balances energy-efficiency improvement and safety considerations. This problem formulation serves as the foundation for the uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization strategy developed in Section "Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning".

Calibrated deep surrogate modeling with uncertainty quantification

The objective of this section is to construct a calibrated deep surrogate model that provides a reliable probabilistic mapping from controllable operating parameters to energy-efficiency performance metrics. Specifically, the surrogate is designed to approximate the conditional distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(\textbf{y}\mid \textbf{x})$$\end{document}, thereby enabling both accurate prediction and principled uncertainty quantification. This probabilistic representation serves as the key information source for the risk-sensitive optimization framework developed in Section Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning.

Data representation and learning problem formulation

Let the available electrolysis dataset be denoted as24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {D} = \left\{ \left( \textbf{x}_i, \textbf{y}_i \right) \right\} _{i=1}^{N} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_i \in \Omega \subset \mathbb {R}^d$$\end{document} represents the controllable operating condition defined in Section "Decision variables and controllable parameter space", and25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{y}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \eta _i\\ e_i \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb {R}^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}collectively represents the current efficiency and specific energy consumption introduced in Section "Energy-efficiency metrics and learning targets". Each data sample corresponds to an aggregated production interval, over which the operating parameters are assumed to remain approximately constant. This assumption is consistent with industrial practice, where parameter adjustments occur at a slower timescale than electrochemical transients and sensor-level sampling.

It should be clarified that the input–output pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\textbf{x}_i,\textbf{y}_i)$$\end{document} in this study is defined at the aggregated production-interval level. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_i$$\end{document} denotes the nominal or interval-averaged operating condition within the i-th production interval, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_i$$\end{document} denotes the corresponding interval-level energy-efficiency response. Therefore, the surrogate model developed in this work aims to learn the nonlinear probabilistic mapping from interval-level operating conditions to energy-efficiency indicators, rather than explicitly modeling the full transient electrochemical and thermal dynamics within each interval.

Unlike many benchmark regression datasets, industrial electrolysis data exhibit strong temporal correlation due to process inertia, thermal accumulation, and long-term operational strategies. Randomly shuffling samples would therefore violate temporal causality and may lead to overly optimistic performance estimates. To preserve the chronological structure of the data, the dataset is partitioned in time order as26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {D} = \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{train}} \;\cup \; \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}} \;\cup \; \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{test}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{train}}$$\end{document} is used for model training, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}}$$\end{document} is reserved for uncertainty calibration, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{test}}$$\end{document} is used exclusively for out-of-sample evaluation. This time-ordered partitioning protocol is consistently adopted in all experiments reported in Section "Numerical experiments and performance evaluation".

The use of chronological partitioning does not imply that the present surrogate is a dynamic state-space model. Instead, it is adopted to avoid information leakage and to provide a more realistic evaluation under temporally correlated industrial data. In other words, process dynamics such as thermal inertia and delayed electrochemical response are reflected indirectly through interval-level measurements and time-ordered evaluation, but historical operating trajectories are not explicitly used as model inputs in the current formulation.

Conventional data-driven optimization approaches typically assume a deterministic input–output relationship, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y} = f(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and rely on point predictions to guide parameter tuning. However, as discussed in Sections "Energy-efficiency metrics and learning targets" and "Safety and feasibility constraints for stable operation", electrolysis performance is affected by measurement noise, unmodeled disturbances, and data sparsity in certain regions of the decision space. Under these conditions, deterministic predictions may underestimate uncertainty and expose the optimization process to excessive operational risk.

To explicitly account for these effects, surrogate modeling is formulated as a probabilistic learning problem. Specifically, the objective is to approximate the conditional distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(\textbf{y}\mid \textbf{x}),$$\end{document} from which both predictive expectation and uncertainty can be inferred.

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(\textbf{y}\mid \textbf{x})$$\end{document} should be interpreted as an interval-level conditional distribution. It characterizes the uncertainty of current efficiency and specific energy consumption under a given aggregated operating condition, including the effects of measurement noise, unobserved disturbances, and residual variability caused by unresolved process dynamics.

Accordingly, for each performance metric, the surrogate model produces27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \big (\mu _\eta (\textbf{x}),\,\sigma _\eta (\textbf{x})\big ), \qquad \big (\mu _e(\textbf{x}),\,\sigma _e(\textbf{x})\big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the predictive mean and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} quantifies the associated predictive uncertainty.

The two learning targets, current efficiency η and specific energy consumption e, are coupled through shared electrochemical and thermal mechanisms. Modeling them independently would ignore cross-task information and may lead to inflated variance, particularly under limited or noisy data. Therefore, this work adopts a multi-output learning formulation in which a shared latent representation is learned from the input parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}$$\end{document}, while task-specific heads capture the distinct statistical characteristics of each metric. This design improves data efficiency and yields a coherent probabilistic description required by the risk-sensitive optimization objective introduced in Section "Risk-sensitive optimization objective".

Although this formulation is suitable for batch-level or interval-level parameter tuning, it does not explicitly capture transient process evolution in the form of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_t=f(\textbf{x}_{1:t},\textbf{y}_{1:t-1})$$\end{document} or a latent dynamic state transition. Future extensions may augment the input with historical operating windows or introduce recurrent neural networks, temporal convolutional networks, or neural state-space surrogates to explicitly model dynamic electrochemical evolution.

The probabilistic learning formulation established in this subsection lays the foundation for the deep ensemble surrogate architecture and uncertainty modeling strategy presented in the following subsection.

Deep ensemble surrogate architecture and uncertainty modeling

Building on the probabilistic learning formulation introduced in Section "Data representation and learning problem formulation", this subsection presents the deep ensemble surrogate architecture used to model energy-efficiency performance and its associated predictive uncertainty. The primary objective is to obtain reliable estimates of both the predictive mean and uncertainty, which are subsequently leveraged by the risk-sensitive optimization framework.

Each base surrogate is implemented as a multi-output neural network that maps the controllable operating condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}\in \Omega$$\end{document} to distributional predictions of current efficiency and specific energy consumption.

Specifically, each ensemble member is constructed as a fully connected multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for heteroscedastic probabilistic regression. The network consists of several shared hidden layers with nonlinear activation functions, followed by task-specific output heads for current efficiency η and specific energy consumption e. For each output metric, the corresponding head predicts both the conditional mean and the logarithm of the predictive variance. This structure enables the base surrogate to learn not only the expected energy-efficiency response but also the data-dependent uncertainty associated with the prediction.

For the m-th ensemble member, the shared feature representation can be expressed as28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{h}^{(m)}(\textbf{x}) = \phi ^{(m)}_{\textrm{sh}}\left( \textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta }^{(m)}_{\textrm{sh}}\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi ^{(m)}_{\textrm{sh}}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the shared MLP backbone parameterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\theta }^{(m)}_{\textrm{sh}}$$\end{document}. Based on this shared representation, separate output heads are used to predict the distributional parameters of η and e.

For the m-th ensemble member, the network output is defined as29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_m(\textbf{x}) = \Big ( \mu _{\eta }^{(m)}(\textbf{x}),\; \log \sigma _{\eta }^{2(m)}(\textbf{x}),\; \mu _{e}^{(m)}(\textbf{x}),\; \log \sigma _{e}^{2(m)}(\textbf{x}) \Big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu ^{(m)}(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ^{2(m)}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denote the predictive mean and variance, respectively. The logarithm of the variance is predicted to ensure numerical stability and to enforce the non-negativity of uncertainty estimates. Implicitly, each base model assumes a Gaussian predictive distribution for each output metric,30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y_r(\textbf{x}) \mid f_m \sim \mathscr {N}\!\big (\mu _r^{(m)}(\textbf{x}),\,\sigma _r^{2(m)}(\textbf{x})\big ), \qquad r\in \{\eta ,e\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where r denotes the output type. This formulation treats current efficiency and specific energy consumption as two correlated learning targets that share a common feature extractor while retaining output-specific uncertainty estimates.

Each base surrogate is trained by minimizing the Gaussian negative log-likelihood loss over the training dataset:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}^{(m)} = \sum _{i=1}^{N} \sum _{r\in \{\eta ,e\}} \left[ \frac{ \left( y_{i,r}-\mu _r^{(m)}(\textbf{x}_i)\right) ^2 }{ 2\sigma _r^{2(m)}(\textbf{x}_i) } + \frac{1}{2}\log \sigma _r^{2(m)}(\textbf{x}_i) \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The first term penalizes prediction errors normalized by the predicted variance, while the second term prevents the model from trivially inflating uncertainty. Therefore, the loss jointly encourages accurate mean prediction and reasonable variance estimation.

To capture epistemic uncertainty arising from limited or uneven data coverage, an ensemble of M independently trained surrogate models is constructed. All ensemble members share the same network architecture but differ in random initialization and training data resampling. This diversity induces variability across model predictions, which can be exploited to quantify model uncertainty. Given the ensemble outputs, the aggregated predictive mean is computed as32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mu _r(\textbf{x}) = \frac{1}{M} \sum _{m=1}^{M} \mu _r^{(m)}(\textbf{x}), \qquad r\in \{\eta ,e\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}while the total predictive variance can be decomposed as33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _r^2(\textbf{x}) = \underbrace{ \frac{1}{M} \sum _{m=1}^{M} \Big ( \mu _r^{(m)}(\textbf{x}) - \mu _r(\textbf{x}) \Big )^2 }_{\text {epistemic uncertainty}} + \underbrace{ \frac{1}{M} \sum _{m=1}^{M} \sigma _r^{2(m)}(\textbf{x}) }_{\text {aleatoric uncertainty}}, \qquad r\in \{\eta ,e\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The first term reflects uncertainty due to limited knowledge of the underlying input–output relationship, while the second term captures irreducible variability inherent in the electrolysis process.

This uncertainty decomposition is particularly relevant for optimization. Epistemic uncertainty can, in principle, be reduced through additional data acquisition guided by the optimization process, whereas aleatoric uncertainty represents intrinsic process fluctuations that cannot be eliminated by improved modeling. Distinguishing these two sources of uncertainty enables more informed and risk-aware decision-making in subsequent optimization stages.

The deep ensemble approach is adopted due to its favorable balance between uncertainty quality, numerical robustness, and implementation simplicity. Compared with fully Bayesian neural networks, ensemble-based uncertainty estimation avoids complex posterior inference and exhibits reduced sensitivity to modeling assumptions, making it well suited for industrial applications characterized by noisy measurements and limited data availability.

In addition, because the present work focuses on aggregated interval-level parameter tuning, the MLP-based surrogate provides a compact and efficient mapping from operating parameters to performance distributions. It does not explicitly include recurrent or state-space transitions, and dynamic extensions using historical operating windows are discussed as future work.

For each operating condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}\in \Omega$$\end{document}, the resulting surrogate provides aggregated predictions34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \big (\mu _\eta (\textbf{x}),\,\sigma _\eta (\textbf{x})\big ), \qquad \big (\mu _e(\textbf{x}),\,\sigma _e(\textbf{x})\big ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which form the direct inputs to the risk-sensitive utility function and probabilistic feasibility constraints introduced in Section "Risk-sensitive optimization objective". In the following subsection, a calibration procedure is introduced to further improve the reliability of these uncertainty estimates before they are used for collaborative parameter tuning.

Predictive calibration and reliability enhancement

While the deep ensemble surrogate introduced in Section "Deep ensemble surrogate architecture and uncertainty modeling" provides a principled estimate of predictive uncertainty through ensemble diversity, such uncertainty estimates are not guaranteed to be statistically calibrated. In practice, data-driven models often exhibit miscalibration, manifesting as either overconfident predictions in data-sparse regions or overly conservative uncertainty estimates in well-explored regimes. If left uncorrected, such miscalibration can significantly impair downstream risk-sensitive optimization by distorting feasibility assessment and exploration behavior.

To enhance the reliability of uncertainty quantification, a predictive calibration step is incorporated prior to optimization. The objective of calibration is not to improve point prediction accuracy, but to enforce statistical consistency between predicted uncertainty and empirical prediction errors. This is achieved by adjusting the scale of predictive uncertainty using a held-out calibration dataset.

Let the raw predictive distribution produced by the ensemble surrogate be approximated as35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} p(\textbf{y}\mid \textbf{x}) \approx \mathscr {N}\!\big (\boldsymbol{\mu }(\textbf{x}),\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma }(\textbf{x})\big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\mu }(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote the ensemble-aggregated predictive mean and covariance, respectively. For notational simplicity, calibration is applied independently to each output dimension. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote the marginal predictive standard deviation of a given performance metric.

Because the two output variables, namely current efficiency η and specific energy consumption e, may exhibit different error scales and uncertainty characteristics, calibration is performed separately for each output dimension. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r\in \{\eta ,e\}$$\end{document} denote a generic output metric. The marginal predictive standard deviation of this metric is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _r(\textbf{x})$$\end{document}.

A simple yet effective calibration model is adopted by rescaling the predictive uncertainty as36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{r,\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x}) = \alpha _r \, \sigma _r(\textbf{x}), \qquad r\in \{\eta ,e\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _r>0$$\end{document} is the scalar calibration factor for the r-th output metric.

This formulation preserves the predictive mean while correcting systematic bias in uncertainty magnitude. The calibration factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _r$$\end{document} is determined using the calibration dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}}$$\end{document} defined in Eq. (26). Calibration quality is assessed through the prediction interval coverage probability (PICP). For a nominal confidence level 1-γ, the empirical coverage is defined as37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{PICP}_r(\alpha _r) = \frac{1}{|\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}}|} \sum _{(\textbf{x}_i,\textbf{y}_i)\in \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{cal}}} \mathbb {I} \Big [ \big | y_{i,r} - \mu _r(\textbf{x}_i) \big | \le z_{1-\gamma /2}\, \alpha _r\, \sigma _r(\textbf{x}_i) \Big ], \qquad r\in \{\eta ,e\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {I}[\cdot ]$$\end{document} is the indicator function and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_{1-\gamma /2}$$\end{document} denotes the (1-γ /2) quantile of the standard normal distribution. The calibration factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _r$$\end{document} is selected such that38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{PICP}_r(\alpha _r) \approx 1-\gamma , \qquad r\in \{\eta ,e\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}ensuring that the empirical coverage of predictive intervals matches the nominal confidence level.

After calibration, the surrogate defines a calibrated predictive distribution39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} p_{\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{y}\mid \textbf{x}) = \mathscr {N}\!\big (\boldsymbol{\mu }(\textbf{x}),\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x})\big ), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x}) = \textbf{A} \boldsymbol{\Sigma }(\textbf{x}) \textbf{A}, \quad \textbf{A}=\textrm{diag}(\alpha _\eta ,\alpha _e) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which provides statistically reliable uncertainty estimates for subsequent decision-making.

Compared with using a single shared scaling factor for all outputs, the output-wise calibration strategy is more suitable for the present multi-output regression problem because current efficiency and specific energy consumption have different physical units, numerical ranges, and error distributions.

This calibration step plays a critical role in the overall framework. In regions with high epistemic uncertainty, it prevents overconfident predictions from inducing unsafe or overly aggressive parameter updates. Conversely, in well-explored regions, it avoids excessive conservatism that may hinder optimization efficiency. By enforcing statistical consistency between predicted uncertainty and observed errors, the calibrated surrogate establishes a trustworthy foundation for the uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization strategy developed in Section "Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning".

Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning

Based on the calibrated probabilistic surrogate developed in Section "Calibrated deep surrogate modeling with uncertainty quantification", this section addresses the problem of energy-efficiency optimization under predictive uncertainty and operational risk. Rather than optimizing point estimates of performance, the proposed framework explicitly incorporates predictive uncertainty and probabilistic feasibility into the decision-making process. As a result, parameter tuning is formulated as a risk-sensitive constrained optimization problem, which is subsequently solved using an uncertainty-aware Bayesian optimization strategy under limited evaluation budgets.

Risk-sensitive optimization problem formulation

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}\in \Omega$$\end{document} denote a candidate operating condition in the admissible decision space defined in Section "Decision variables and controllable parameter space". Under the calibrated probabilistic surrogate model, the energy-efficiency performance associated with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}$$\end{document} is characterized by predictive distributions of current efficiency and specific energy consumption. Denote by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _\eta (\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{\eta ,\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} the calibrated predictive mean and standard deviation of current efficiency, and similarly by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _e(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{e,\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} those of specific energy consumption.

To explicitly account for predictive uncertainty, a risk-sensitive utility function is adopted:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} J(\textbf{x}) = w_\eta \big ( \mu _\eta (\textbf{x}) - \lambda _\eta \sigma _{\eta ,\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x}) \big ) - w_e \big ( \mu _e(\textbf{x}) + \lambda _e \sigma _{e,\textrm{cal}}(\textbf{x}) \big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_\eta , w_e > 0$$\end{document} are weighting coefficients, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\eta , \lambda _e \ge 0$$\end{document} are risk-aversion parameters. The uncertainty penalty terms discourage operating conditions whose predicted performance is highly uncertain, thereby reducing the risk of performance degradation due to model error or data sparsity.

In addition to performance considerations, safe and stable electrolysis operation requires that all operational constraints introduced in Section "Safety and feasibility constraints for stable operation" be satisfied with high probability. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {F}$$\end{document} denote the event that all safety and feasibility constraints are simultaneously satisfied. Using the calibrated predictive distributions, the feasibility of a candidate operating condition is quantified through the chance constraint41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {P}\big ( \mathscr {F} \mid \textbf{x} \big ) \ge 1 - \delta \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta \in (0,1)$$\end{document} specifies the allowable probability of constraint violation.

Combining the risk-sensitive utility and the probabilistic feasibility requirement, the collaborative parameter tuning problem is formulated as42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \max _{\textbf{x}\in \Omega } \quad&J(\textbf{x}) \\ \mathrm {s.t.} \quad&\mathbb {P}\big ( \mathscr {F} \mid \textbf{x} \big ) \ge 1 - \delta \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Problem (42) exhibits several characteristics that distinguish it from conventional deterministic optimization. First, both the objective function and the feasibility constraint are available only through surrogate-based probabilistic predictions, rendering analytical expressions intractable. Second, the resulting optimization landscape is generally nonconvex and may involve multiple competing local optima. Third, evaluating new operating conditions typically requires costly system-level experiments or high-fidelity simulations, which limits the number of feasible evaluations.

These challenges motivate the adoption of a data-efficient global optimization strategy capable of handling black-box objectives under probabilistic constraints. In the following subsection, an uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization framework is introduced to iteratively solve Problem (42) while explicitly balancing performance improvement, risk control, and evaluation cost.

Uncertainty-aware constrained bayesian optimization

The risk-sensitive optimization problem in (42) involves a black-box objective function and probabilistic feasibility constraints, both of which are accessible only through calibrated surrogate predictions. Moreover, evaluating a candidate operating condition typically requires expensive system-level experiments or high-fidelity simulations. These characteristics render gradient-based or exhaustive search methods impractical and motivate the adoption of Bayesian optimization as a data-efficient global optimization strategy.

In Bayesian optimization, candidate operating conditions are selected sequentially by maximizing an acquisition function that balances exploitation of high-performing regions and exploration of uncertain regions. In the present setting, this balance must additionally account for predictive uncertainty and probabilistic feasibility. Consequently, the acquisition function is designed to explicitly integrate risk-sensitive performance improvement with constraint satisfaction likelihood.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote the risk-sensitive utility defined in (40). Under the calibrated probabilistic surrogate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J(\textbf{x})$$\end{document} is a random variable whose distribution is induced by the predictive distributions of the underlying performance metrics. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_t^\star$$\end{document} denote the best feasible utility value observed up to iteration t. A risk-sensitive expected improvement (EI) criterion is defined as43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{EI}_{\textrm{risk}}(\textbf{x}) = \mathbb {E}\!\left[ \max \!\left( J(\textbf{x}) - J_t^\star ,\; 0 \right) \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the expectation is taken with respect to the calibrated predictive distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J(\textbf{x})$$\end{document}. Unlike conventional EI, this formulation implicitly penalizes high predictive uncertainty through the structure of the risk-sensitive utility function.

To incorporate operational safety, the expected improvement term is further weighted by the probability that all feasibility constraints are satisfied. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {P}(\mathscr {F}\mid \textbf{x})$$\end{document} denote the feasibility probability defined in (41). The resulting uncertainty-aware constrained acquisition function is given by44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} a(\textbf{x}) = \textrm{EI}_{\textrm{risk}}(\textbf{x}) \cdot \mathbb {P}\!\left( \mathscr {F}|\textbf{x}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The multiplicative structure in (44) yields a clear operational interpretation. The expected improvement term promotes candidate operating conditions with high potential for performance enhancement, while the feasibility probability smoothly suppresses candidates associated with elevated risk of constraint violation. Unlike hard constraint enforcement, this soft coupling allows controlled exploration near constraint boundaries, which is essential for discovering high-performance solutions in complex, tightly constrained operating regimes.

Predictive uncertainty plays a central role in shaping the acquisition landscape. In regions where epistemic uncertainty is high, the feasibility probability and the uncertainty-penalized utility jointly reduce the acquisition value, thereby discouraging overly aggressive exploration. Conversely, in well-sampled regions with reliable predictions, the acquisition function naturally emphasizes exploitation. This behavior critically depends on the calibration procedure described in Section "Predictive calibration and reliability enhancement", underscoring the importance of reliable uncertainty quantification for safe and efficient optimization.

At each iteration, the acquisition function (44) is maximized over the bounded domain Ω to select the next operating condition for evaluation. The resulting observation is then incorporated into the dataset to update the surrogate model, and the procedure repeats until a stopping criterion is met. Algorithmic implementation and collaborative tuning workflow

This subsection presents the algorithmic realization of the proposed uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization framework and details the collaborative parameter tuning workflow for practical deployment. The core idea is to iteratively refine operating conditions by coupling a calibrated probabilistic surrogate with a risk-sensitive acquisition strategy under a finite evaluation budget.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, this subsection corresponds to the final decision-making stage of the proposed framework, namely the “uncertainty-aware constrained bayesian optimization” module and the bottom closed-loop update process. The preceding sections provide the required inputs for this stage: Section "Problem statement and preliminaries" defines the admissible operating space Ω, energy-efficiency targets, and feasibility constraints; Section "Calibrated deep surrogate modeling with uncertainty quantification" provides calibrated predictive distributions of current efficiency and specific energy consumption; and Section "Risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning" formulates the risk-sensitive utility function and probabilistic feasibility measure. Based on these components, the present subsection describes how candidate operating conditions are sequentially selected, evaluated, and fed back to update the surrogate model.

The proposed workflow can be understood as a closed-loop decision process. First, historical or simulated electrolysis data are used to train the deep ensemble surrogate and calibrate predictive uncertainty. Second, for each candidate operating condition, the calibrated surrogate evaluates the predictive means, uncertainties, and feasibility probability. Third, the risk-sensitive utility and feasibility probability are combined into the constrained acquisition function. Fourth, the candidate with the highest acquisition value is selected for system-level evaluation or high-fidelity simulation. Finally, the newly obtained observation is incorporated into the dataset, after which the surrogate model and uncertainty calibration are updated. This loop continues until the evaluation budget is exhausted or convergence is reached.

At each iteration, the framework maintains a probabilistic surrogate model that jointly characterizes energy-efficiency performance and operational feasibility. Given the current dataset, candidate operating conditions are selected by maximizing the constrained acquisition function defined in (44), which integrates risk-sensitive expected improvement and feasibility probability. The selected candidate is then evaluated through system-level experiments or high-fidelity simulations, and the resulting observation is incorporated into the dataset to update the surrogate. This closed-loop process continues until a predefined stopping criterion, such as an evaluation budget or convergence threshold, is met.

The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1. The algorithm takes as input an initial dataset, risk-aversion parameters, a feasibility tolerance, and a finite evaluation budget, and returns the best feasible operating condition identified during the optimization process.

Here, the initial dataset may be obtained from historical production records or a validated high-fidelity simulation platform. During the optimization process, each newly evaluated sample is used to augment the available dataset, thereby enabling the surrogate and calibration factor to be refined progressively.

Beyond the algorithmic loop, the proposed framework embodies a collaborative tuning mechanism in two complementary aspects. First, multiple controllable operating parameters are optimized jointly through a shared surrogate model, enabling the capture of nonlinear interactions that are difficult to address using sequential or one-factor-at-a-time tuning strategies. Second, the surrogate model, acquisition function, and system evaluation form a tightly coupled feedback loop, in which information gained from each new evaluation is immediately propagated to guide subsequent decisions.

This collaborative mechanism is also reflected in the overall framework shown in Fig. 1. The problem formulation module supplies the decision space and feasibility constraints, the surrogate modeling and calibration modules provide reliable predictive distributions, and the optimization module converts these predictions into sequential parameter recommendations. Therefore, collaboration in this work refers not only to the joint tuning of multiple operating variables, but also to the closed-loop interaction among data acquisition, surrogate updating, uncertainty recalibration, and risk-sensitive decision-making.

From a deployment perspective, the proposed algorithm supports both offline and online tuning modes. Offline tuning is suitable when historical datasets or simulation platforms are available, while online tuning enables adaptive refinement during operation under strict safety constraints. Moreover, the modular structure of the framework facilitates seamless integration with existing monitoring and control systems, making it suitable for practical industrial electrolysis applications.

In offline mode, the workflow can be used to screen promising operating conditions before plant implementation by relying on historical records or high-fidelity simulation data. In online mode, the same framework can be embedded into a supervisory optimization layer, where recommended candidate parameters are evaluated only when the predicted feasibility probability satisfies the prescribed safety tolerance. Thus, the proposed algorithm provides a practical mechanism for linking uncertainty-aware learning with risk-controlled electrolysis operation.

Conclusion

This paper presents a risk-sensitive collaborative parameter tuning framework for energy-efficiency optimization in rare-earth molten salt electrolysis systems. By integrating calibrated deep surrogate modeling with uncertainty-aware constrained Bayesian optimization, the proposed approach enables efficient and reliable decision-making under limited evaluation budgets and strict operational constraints. Numerical experiments indicate that the proposed framework achieves improved energy-efficiency utility, faster convergence, and lower empirical constraint violation probability compared with uncertainty-agnostic strategies under the considered experimental settings. Moreover, the explicit incorporation of predictive uncertainty and probabilistic feasibility allows transparent control of the performance–safety trade-off, providing practical flexibility for risk-aware parameter tuning. These results highlight the importance of uncertainty modeling and calibration in data-driven electrolysis parameter optimization. Rather than relying only on point predictions, the proposed framework provides a feasible route for incorporating predictive reliability and operational risk into surrogate-assisted parameter tuning.

Limitations and future work

Although the proposed framework demonstrates promising performance, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current study is formulated at the aggregated production-interval level. Each input operating condition represents nominal or interval-averaged process parameters, and the corresponding output represents interval-level energy-efficiency indicators. Therefore, the proposed surrogate captures the nonlinear probabilistic relationship between operating parameters and energy-efficiency responses, but it does not explicitly model transient electrochemical dynamics, thermal accumulation processes, or latent state transitions within each production interval. Future work may incorporate historical operating windows and dynamic surrogate models, such as recurrent neural networks, temporal convolutional networks, or neural state-space models, to better characterize process evolution.

Second, the present validation is mainly based on numerical experiments and interval-level data. Although the experimental protocol is designed to reflect practical electrolysis parameter tuning, larger-scale industrial datasets collected under diverse production regimes are still required to further evaluate the robustness, transferability, and long-term reliability of the proposed method. In particular, future studies should examine the influence of raw-material fluctuation, equipment aging, sensor drift, and regime transitions on predictive calibration and optimization performance.

Third, the probabilistic feasibility measure currently aggregates individual constraint satisfaction probabilities under a conditional independence approximation. This treatment enables efficient optimization and provides a smooth feasibility indicator, but it may not fully capture correlated constraint violations among temperature, concentration, voltage, and other coupled process variables. Future work may introduce joint probabilistic constraint modeling, copula-based dependence modeling, or multi-output calibrated uncertainty estimation to improve feasibility assessment under coupled industrial constraints.

Finally, closed-loop online validation in practical electrolysis control systems remains an important future direction. Before industrial deployment, the recommended operating conditions should be further assessed under supervisory control constraints, operator intervention rules, and plant-level safety protocols. Future research will therefore focus on integrating the proposed framework with real-time monitoring systems, online calibration mechanisms, and adaptive risk-control strategies to improve its practical applicability and long-term operational safety.
