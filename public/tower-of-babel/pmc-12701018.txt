Identification and functional characterization of splicing factors implicated in mantle cell lymphoma aggressiveness
Authors: Juthamas Yosudjai, Jirarat Poohadsuan, Parinya Samart, Napachai Rodboon, Surapol Issaragrisil, Sudjit Luanpitpong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a clinically aggressive and incurable form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma with very heterogeneous clinical and biological behaviors. Dysregulation of RNA splicing machinery is common in various types of cancer, including hematologic malignancies, and is associated with cancer progression. However, whether and how splicing factors, the spliceosome components responsible for pre-mRNA splicing, regulate MCL aggressive behaviors remain largely unknown. Bioinformatics analyses were employed to profile the mRNA expression of two key families of splicing factors, that are serine/arginine-rich (SR) and heterogenous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) families, in clinical specimens of MCL patients in comparison to normal B cells. Functional roles of splicing factors in MCL aggressive phenotypes defined as hallmarks of cancer were investigated in MCL cell lines. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed to determine the clinical significance of splicing factors according to their gene expression level. Depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1, which are highly expressed in MCL clinical specimens, by CRISPR/Cas9 system significantly inhibit cell growth and proliferation, motility, and angiogenesis of MCL cells. SRSF1, hnRNP F (for Z-138 cells), and PTBP1 were found to mediate BTZ sensitivity in MCL cells, in agreement with an increase in caspase-3 activation and the occurrence of splicing events that favor the expression of pro-apoptotic isoforms of apoptosis regulatory genes. We also found that depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 induces the expression of autophagy-related genes and the accumulation of autophagic vacuoles, which may act as one of the tumor-suppressive mechanisms. Survival analyses revealed that co-high expression of SRSF1, HNRNPF, or PTBP1 and oncogenic MYC predicts poor clinical outcomes in MCL patients. Our data describe the clinical significance of aberrant SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 in MCL and their tumor-promoting roles via the regulation of cancer hallmarks, which could be important in understanding MCL pathogenesis and therapeutic development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27512-w.Keywords: Mantle cell lymphoma, Splicing factors, SRSF1, HNRNPF, PTBP1, Alternative splicingSubject terms: Cancer, Haematological cancer, Oncogenes

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a relatively uncommon, clinically aggressive, and incurable form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) that arises from pregerminal center mantle zone B cells. Most, if not all, MCL cells carry the translocation t(11;14)(q13;q32) that leads to constitutive overexpression of cyclin D1, representing the initial oncogenic event. The observations that MCL has very heterogeneous clinical and biological behaviors suggest that other genetic events/mechanisms drive the evolution of the disease1. For over two decades, the hallmarks of cancer have provided a unified framework for dissecting malignant phenotypes and understanding tumorigenesis2. In light of the hallmarks of cancer, cyclin D1 dysregulation promotes cell cycle progression and initially causes sustained proliferative signaling. Secondary genetic alterations would subsequently reinforce the aberrant cell growth and promote the acquisition of other hallmarks relevant to MCL pathogenesis. Recently, altered expression and mutations of the components of RNA splicing machinery have been identified to be among the most recurrent genomic abnormalities in various clonal hematopoietic conditions, including MCL and other hematologic malignancies3,4. In the present study, we asked whether splicing factors, the spliceosome components, play a role in MCL aggressive behaviors linked to the hallmarks of cancer.

Pre-mRNA splicing is a fundamental step in the expression of most eukaryotic genes carried out by the spliceosome, which comprises up to five small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) and many different (> 200) protein factors depending on a specific stage5,6. In humans, more than 95% of all genes undergo alternative splicing, leading to the production of multiple protein isoforms7. Alternative splicing is finely regulated by trans-acting splicing factors, including heterogenous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) and serine/arginine-rich (SR) proteins, which generally inhibit and activate the splicing, respectively8,9. Interestingly, recent evidence has demonstrated that splicing factors have non-canonical functions that are relevant to cellular processes, such as cell proliferation, migration, cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and cell death10. Dysregulation of splicing factors has been linked to cancer progression in several types of cancer, including hematologic malignancies11–13. For example, upregulation of SRSF1 and PTBP1 is observed in the progression of multiple myeloma (MM) and contributes to poor prognosis in MM patients14–16. However, the functional roles of aberrant splicing factors in MCL cells are still largely unknown.

The SR protein family includes twelve RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) (SRSF1 to 12), while the hnRNPs represent a large family of RBPs and are named alphabetically from hnRNP A1 to hnRNP U17,18. Herein, we profiled the mRNA expression of these splicing factors available on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) bioinformatics database to identify their differential expression between normal B and MCL cells. CRISPR/Cas9 system was then used to repress the expression of the identified splicing factors to further investigate their functional roles in MCL cell aggressiveness. We speculated that splicing factors could be potential targets that are promising for anticancer therapy and that identifying their functional roles could expand our understanding of MCL progression.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Bortezomib (BTZ) was obtained from Janssen-Cilag (Beerse, Belgium). Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Dallas, MA, USA). Hoechst 33342 was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). PE Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I was obtained from BD Bioscience (San Jose, CA, USA). Antibodies against SRSF1, hnRNP F, PTBP1, and β-actin were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), while antibodies against LC3 A/B and p62, and hydroxychloroquine sulfate (HCQ) were from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).

Bioinformatics database analysis

mRNA expression of the SR and hnRNP splicing factors in MCL cells was analyzed in comparison to normal B cells obtained from cord blood using the dataset available in GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), accession number GSE235019. Expression profiling of MCL tumor cells from patients and peripheral blood B cells from healthy volunteers were obtained from GSE46846 dataset. The comparison of gene expression between the parental and BTZ-resistant (40-fold higher IC50) MCL cells was performed using GSE20915 dataset.

Cell culture

Human MCL-derived Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, MA, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Mycoplasma contamination was regularly checked using MycoAlert™ PLUS test kit (Lonza, Cologne, Germany) and any cell lines found positive were discarded.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated SRSF1, HNRNPF, and PTBP1 gene knockdown

All-in-one pLentiCRISPR v2 plasmids carrying S. pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), single guide RNA (sgRNA) against human SRSF1 (CTTCAGGTCGATGTCGCGGA), HNRNPF (GTTCGTGCAGTTTGCCTCGC), or PTBP1 (GGGCCACGCACCGCCTGGTT), and puromycin resistance were obtained from GenScript. Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293FT cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using pCMV.dR8.2 packaging and pCMV-VSV-G envelope vectors (Addgene #8454 and #8455). Cells were transduced with the lentiviral particles in the presence of 8 μg/mL Polybrene for 48 h. Transduced cells were then selected with 1 µg/mL puromycin, and assessed for knockdown efficiency by Western blotting.

RNA isolation and polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) following the standard protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System and oligo (dT) primers (Invitrogen). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using SYBR™ Select Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The fold changes in mRNA expression were calculated by the 2–ΔΔCt method. To discriminate the mRNA variants of splicing factor-guided alternative splicing, reverse transcription (RT)-PCR was performed using Platinum™ Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen) for amplification according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers for RT-PCR were designed specifically to amplify two or more isoforms of different sizes and GAPDH was used as a loading control.

Western blot analysis

Cells were incubated in commercial protein lysis buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Molecular Biochemicals, Indianapolis, IN, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min. Protein content was analyzed using BCA protein assay (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA) and 30–50 μg of protein was resolved under denaturing conditions by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk, incubated with appropriate primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and subsequently incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The immune complexes were analyzed by enhanced chemiluminescence detection system on an ImageQuant LAS digital imager (GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA, USA).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated by using MTT assay and cell counting. Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate in complete media. At the indicated time point, MTT reagent at the concentration of 5 mg/mL was added, and cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Formazan crystals were dissolved in 10% SDS/0.01 M HCl solution, and the absorbance was read at 592 nm with a microplate reader. For the direct cell counting, trypan blue (0.4% w/v) staining was used for viable cell counting under a light microscope with a hemocytometer.

3D spheroid assay

Cells were cultured in MethoCult H4100 medium (Stem Cell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) containing 1.2% methylcellulose (MC) and additional components of 20% FBS, 1% bovine serum albumin, 100 μM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 2 mM L-glutamine in an ultralow adherent, round bottom 96-well plate. After setup, cells were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min, cultured for 7 days, and visualized under an inverted microscope at a low (10X) magnification.

Cell motility assay

Cell migration was assessed in a 24-well plate Transwell system (Corning, Kennebunk, ME, USA) using a cell culture insert with a pore size of 5.0 μm, whereas cell invasion was assessed using an insert coated with 0.5 mg/mL Matrigel (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA). After starvation for 2 h, cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were loaded onto the insert chamber containing serum-free medium, and complete medium was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. The migrating/invading cells in the lower chamber were collected at 16–18 h, stained with Hoechst 33342, and visualized under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti-U, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Tube formation assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan) at 1.2 × 104 cells/well were resuspended in RPMI 1640 media with 10% FBS or conditional medium collected from CRISPR control or splicing factor-depleted cells. After 12 h, cells were visualized under an inverted microscope, and tube formation was analyzed using Angiogenesis Analyzer program for ImageJ software.

Apoptosis assay

After BTZ treatment for 24 h, cells were incubated with 10 µg/mL Hoechst 33342 for 30 min and analyzed for apoptosis by scoring the cells with condensed and/or fragmented nuclei under fluorescence microscopy (Eclipse Ti-U). The apoptotic index was calculated as the percentage of cells with apoptotic nuclei over the total number of cells. For annexin V/7-AAD assay, cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and stained using Annexin V-PE Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were analyzed by BD FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) at the 488-nm excitation beam and 575-nm and 670-nm band-pass filters using CellQuest software. Data were further analyzed by Flowjo (v10.4.1) software.

Autophagy assay

Autophagy was evaluated by using CYTO-ID Autophagy detection kit (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed with complete medium, and resuspended in 300 μL of 1X assay buffer containing Cyto-ID green fluorescent dye, Hoechst 33342 dye, and 5% FBS at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were then wash, resuspended in 1X assay buffer, and either visualized under fluorescence microscopy (Eclipse Ti-U) or analyzed by BD FACSCanto flow cytometer.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival plots of MCL patients were generated using Prism 8 software according to the levels of gene expression, which were available in GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), accession number GSE93291, with the cutoff value for high expression at Q3. Expression values below Q3 were defined as low expression.

Statistical analysis

Data represent means ± SD from three or more independent experiments as indicated. Statistical analysis was performed by two-sided, unpaired Student’s t-test at a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Aberrant expression of splicing factors in MCL patients

With the initial objective of deciphering the potential involvement of splicing factors in tumorigenesis and progression of MCL, we performed mRNA expression analysis of the SR and hnRNP splicing factors using GSE2350 dataset available in GEO19. Among the SR protein family members, upregulation of SRSF1, SRSF3, SRSF4, SRSF6, SRSF7, and SRSF9 was observed in MCL clinical samples when compared with B cells from cord blood samples (Fig. 1A). For the hnRNP family, aberrantly high expression of HNRNPF and PTBP1 was observed in MCL (Fig. 1B). Among them, SRSF1, HNRNPF, and PTBP1 are emerging as a pro-oncogenic factor in multiple human cancers20–23, hence these genes were selected for further functional analyses in MCL cells herein. Further analysis of RNA sequencing data, available from the GSE46846 dataset, revealed that SRSF1 and HNRNPF, but not PTBP1, were significantly upregulated in MCL samples compared with normal adult peripheral blood B cells (Suppl. Fig. S1), suggesting that SRSF1 and HNRNPF may serve as potential biomarkers in MCL, although further studies are warranted.

(A, B) Differential mRNA expression of SR (A) and hnRNP (B) protein families in MCL (n = 8) in comparison with normal cord blood B cells (n = 5) using GSE2350 dataset available on GEO. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 versus normal B cells; two-sided Student’s t-test.

Depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 suppresses MCL cell growth and proliferation

To investigate the biological functions of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 in human MCL cells, CRISPR/Cas9 system was used to repress the specific gene expression in MCL Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells. Western blot analyses showed that the knockdown efficiency of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 in both MCL cells were more than 80% (Fig. 2A), hereafter, the knockdown cells were designated as SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i cells, respectively. Uncontrolled cell proliferation is among the well-defined hallmarks of cancer. To determine whether SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 affect MCL growth characteristics, the proliferative rates of control (CTL) and knockdown cells were compared by MTT assay. Figure 2B shows a significant decrease in the cell proliferation in SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells when compared with CTL cells at day 4 of culture. Growth curves were also generated for each cell population by hemocytometer cell counting, the results of which confirmed the growth suppression in SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i cells (Fig. 2C).

Cancer stem cells (CSCs), also known as tumor initiating cells, contribute to tumor growth, progression, and relapse. We previously showed that 3D spheroids profoundly enriched MCL CSCs compared with the traditional 2D culture and that its size correlated well with proportion of MCL CSCs by ALDH activity24. Figure 2D shows that, compared with their CTL counterparts, only SRSF1i Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells and PTBP1i Jeko-1 cells formed characteristically smaller spheroids, suggesting that SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 affect MCL cell growth in part via CSC acquisition, which is unlikely predominant, and other mechanisms.

SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 mediate MCL cell motility and angiogenic activity

Migration and invasion are key steps in the trafficking and homing of MCL cells to the stroma within tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby involving in the progression of MCL25,26. Accordingly, cell migration and invasion assays were performed at 16 and 18 h, respectively, to minimize the confounding effect of cell proliferation, which was assessed from 24 h onward. Figure 3A shows that SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 tended to have minimal effect on MCL cell migration based on the observations that, among all tested knockdown MCL cells, only SRSF1i and hnRNPFi Z-138 cells, displayed reduced cell migration. By contrast, depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 had a profound effect on cell invasion in all tested knockdown MCL cells (Fig. 3B), suggesting that these splicing factors mediate MCL cell motility likely through the mechanisms that specifically involve cell invasion—adhesion to extracellular matrix (ECM), proteolysis of ECM components, and/or cell protrusion27.

Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels, is a critical process in MCL growth and dissemination28. We herein compared the angiogenic potential of SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i MCL cells to their counterpart CTL cells by performing the endothelial (HUVEC) tube formation assay. HUVECs were incubated with conditioned medium collected from SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i MCL cells on Matrigel-coated plate, and endothelial capillary tubes were analyzed at 12 h using an inverted microscope. Figure 3C shows that depletion of SRSF1 and PTPB1 in both MCL Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells caused a reduction in tube formation, as denoted by a decrease in the HUVEC mesh number, thereby indicating the role of SRSF1 and PTBP1 in angiogenesis of MCL cells. Notably, a reduction of HUVEC mesh number in response to hnRNPFi MCL-derived conditioned medium was noticeable, but statistically significant only in response to those from Jeko-1 cells.

Roles of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 in of bortezomib sensitivity in MCL

Apoptosis, a major form of programmed cell death, plays an essential role in the removal of mutated or transformed cells, and its disruption contributes to abnormal cell growth and uncontrolled tumor progression29. In the present study, we investigated whether depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 affected (i) basal apoptosis of MCL cells and (ii) drug sensitivity to bortezomib (BTZ), a first-line treatment for relapsed/refractory MCL30,31. Figure 4A shows that SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i Z-138 cells had a significantly higher basal apoptosis than CTL cells as evaluated by Hoechst 33342 assay. BTZ (6 nM) induced a relatively greater apoptosis in all tested knockdown Z-138 cells, particularly hnRNPFi and PTBP1i cells, at 24 h, indicating that depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 sensitized Z-138 cells to BTZ-induced apoptosis. In Jeko-1 cells, we found that only SRSF1i cells had a higher basal apoptosis than CTL cells, while an increase in BTZ sensitivity was observed in SRSF1i and, to a lesser extent, PTBP1i cells, but not in hnRNPFi, cells. Analyses of cell death by Annexin V/7-AAD assay and the expression of cleaved caspase-3, an indicator of caspase-3 activation, by Western blotting further validated the results obtained from Hoechst 33342 assay in Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells (Fig. 4B and C; see also Suppl. Figs. S2 and S3). Using GSE20915 dataset, we found that mRNA expression of SRSF1 and PTBP1, but not HNRNPF, was relatively higher in BTZ-resistant Jeko-1 cells than its parental cells (Suppl. Fig. S4), thus supporting the key role of SRSF1 and PTBP1 in BTZ sensitivity in Jeko-1 cells. Altogether, our results suggest the presence of cellular context‐dependent mechanisms in the regulation of BTZ‐induced apoptosis by splicing factors in MCL cells.

Depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 induces autophagy

Autophagy is a highly conserved intracellular degradation system that functions to remove unnecessary or dysfunctional proteins and organelles through the autophagosome–lysosome fusion32. In cancers, the role of autophagy is considered controversial—activation of autophagy appears to suppress tumorigenesis but contributes to tumor cell survival during cancer progression 33. To identify whether splicing factors might regulate autophagy in MCL cells, we first profiled the expression of autophagy-related genes, including LC3B, ATG5, ATG7, BECN1, and SQSTM1 (encoding p62)34,35, upon depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1. Figure 5A shows that most, if not all, of the tested genes were upregulated in SRSF1i, hnRNPFi and PTBP1i Z-138 cells, whereas a striking upregulation of LC3B was observed in SRSF1i, hnRNPFi and, to a lesser extent, PTBP1i Jeko-1 cells. We then determined the autophagic activity based on the detection of autophagic vacuoles using a specific Cyto-ID fluorescent dye36. Fluorescence micrographs revealed an increase in Cyto-ID green fluorescence in all tested knockdown Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells when compared with their control cells (Fig. 5B; see also Suppl. Figs. S5 and S6 for higher magnification), in agreement with the quantitative analyses by flow cytometry (Fig. 5C; see also Suppl. Figs. S7 and S8 for gating strategy), indicating that inhibition of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 activated autophagy in MCL cells.

Further validation by Western blotting revealed an increase in basal level of LC3-II, a marker of autophagic vacuoles, in SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i Z-138 and Jeko-1 cells (Fig. 6), which was consistent with the accumulation of autophagic vacuoles, confirming that SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 play a key role in autophagic regulation. To investigate how SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 mediate autophagy, the cells were treated with hydroxychloroquine (HCQ; 50 µM) for 4 h to interfere with the autophagosome–lysosome fusion. We observed that HCQ remarkably increased the accumulation of LC3-II level in all control and knockdown cells, indicating the enhance of autophagosome induced by autophagic flux. However, there was no significant difference between SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, or PTBP1i cells and CTL cells in the presence of HCQ, which was likely due to the limited accumulation of autophagosomes in the system. Altogether, our results suggest that depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 may be involved in an early stage of autophagosome formation in MCL cells.

To further investigate how autophagy may affect MCL aggressiveness, the cells were treated with HCQ (50 µM), the autophagy inhibitor, for 24 h, after which cell proliferation was evaluated by MTT assay on day 2 following HCQ removal. The results showed that HCQ significantly promoted cell proliferation in CTL Z-138 cells, but not in Jeko-1 cells, indicating that autophagy may exert a negative effect in MCL cells, depending on the cellular context. Addition of HCQ to SRSF1i and PTBP1i Z-138 cells reversed their inhibitory effects on cell proliferation, indicating that SRSF1 and PTBP1 regulate MCL Z-138 cell growth, in part, via autophagy (Suppl. Fig. S9).

SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 control alternative splicing of apoptosis genes

SRSF1, hnRNP F and PTBP1 are pivotal in regulating numerous alternative splicing events in cancers, contributing to the diversity of proteins from the same gene37. Considering that depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F and PTBP1 affected basal and BTZ-induced apoptosis in certain MCL cells and that aberrant apoptosis contributed significantly to tumorigenesis29, we herein evaluated the apoptosis genes that were shown to be targets of splicing factors and alternative splicing events, including BCL2L1, MCL1, and CASP938–41, using conventional RT-PCR. Figure 7A–C shows an increase in the ratio of gene isoforms encoding pro-apoptotic proteins, including Bcl-xS, Mcl-1S, and caspase-9a, in SRSF1i, hnRNPFi, and PTBP1i Z-138 cells when compared with control cells (see also Suppl. Fig. S10 for loading control). We also observed that different alternative splicing events may occur in Jeko-1 cells because alternative splicing has long been known to be highly context-dependent42–44—an increase in BCL-xS, MCL1S, and CASP9a spliced isoforms was found only upon depletion of SRSF1i, consistent with the apoptosis and caspase-3 results (Fig. 4). Our data support that SRSF1, hnRNP F and PTBP1 mediate MCL aggressiveness in part via their canonical splicing functions.

SRSF1, HNRNPF, and PTBP1 expression are associated with clinical outcomes of MCL patients

MYC is a well-described oncogene and an important factor in the transformation and aggressiveness of B-cell lymphoma45. A recent study demonstrated that MCL patients with high MYC tumors had poorer clinical outcomes and were associated with other high-risk factors, such as TP53/p53 + aberrations and high proliferation46. To substantiate our findings, we performed mRNA expression analysis of SRSF1, HNRNPF, and PTBP1 with or without MYC using the data available on GEO (accession number GSE93291) to examine the associations between their expression and survival of MCL patients. Using the expression value at the upper quartile (Q3) as the cutoff, MCL patients were divided into two groups, namely, high expression (expression values ≥ Q3) and low expression (expression values < Q3). Our survival analysis revealed that although the overall survival in patients with high SRSF1 versus low SRSF1 alone was not significantly different (hazard ratio (HR) = 1.074), patients with co-high expression of SRSF1 and MYC (expression values of both gene ≥ Q3) had shorter survival with the HR increasing tremendously from 3.596 for high MYC alone to 11.880 (Fig. 8A and B). Similarly, an enormous increase in the HR of death was found in patients with co-high expression of HNRNPF and MYC (HR = 129.300) as well as in patients with high PTBP1 and MYC (HR = 24.090) (Fig. 8C and D). Interestingly, high HNRNPF or PTBP1 expression alone could also predict poor clinical outcomes in MCL patients. These clinical data support the involvement of SRSF1, HNRNPF, and PTBP1 splicing factors in controlling aggressive MCL.

Discussion

The concept of hallmarks of cancer has been implicated in the understanding of tumorigenesis and cancer progression for decades as it helps to rationalize the vast complexity of malignant phenotypes into a common set of functional capabilities acquired during oncogenic processes47. Alterations in RNA splicing patterns have been suggested to be drivers of tumorigenesis48,49, attributable in part to the recurrent somatic mutations in genes encoding splicing factors. In the present study, we identified aberrant splicing factors in MCL clinical specimens and addressed their functional roles in the hallmarks of cancer in MCL cells to investigate the possible link between identified splicing factors and MCL aggressiveness. Using bioinformatics analyses, we found the mRNA upregulation of 6 out of the 12 SR protein family members and 3 hnRNPs in MCL tissues when compared with those in normal B cells. Among them, three well-known pro-oncogenic factors, SRSF1, HNRNPF, and PTBP1 were selected for further studies.

SRSF1 (formerly SF2/ASF) is a prototypical member of the SR protein family. Although originally classified as a splicing factor, SRSF1 has been found to possess several additional functions in mRNA transcription, nuclear export and stability, miRNA processing, translation, and protein posttranscriptional modification20,50,51. SRSF1 is upregulated and is identified as a proto-oncogene that regulates the splicing of important cancer-related genes to promote tumorigenesis in lung, breast, colon, thyroid, ovarian and bladder cancers21,52–57. Recently, the global splicing landscape has been evaluated in MM, in which a significant enrichment of SRSF1 binding motifs was found around alternative splicing events58. The reported spliced targets of SRSF1 include; (i) apoptosis regulators BCL2L1, BCL2L11 (also known as BIM), BIN1, and MCL1; (ii) cell growth regulators MKNK2, CASC4, and S6K1; and (iii) DNA damage regulators PTPMT1 and DBF4B20,49. Here we found that depletion of SRSF1 in MCL cells resulted in the repression of all tested hallmarks of cancer, including cell growth and proliferation, cell invasion, angiogenesis, and resistance to apoptosis (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Consistent with the previous studies, we subsequently found that depletion of SRSF1, particularly in Z-138 cells, induced the expression of pro-apoptotic isoform of BCL2L1, MCL1, and CASP9 genes (Fig. 7)38,39,57. Given that the oncogenic role of SRSF1 is better known in solid tumors and is related mostly to the alternative splicing events, our data provide, for the first time, the supporting evidence that SRSF1 acts as a potential proto-oncogene in MCL development and progression.

hnRNP F and PTBP1 (also called hnRNP I) belong to the hnRNPs, a typical RNA-binding protein family that is generally thought to repress spliceosomal assembly and antagonize the activity of SR proteins in alternative splicing59. However, their alternative splicing outcomes depend on the context and the intronic or exonic regions where RNA G-quadruplexes are located60. hnRNP F and PTBP1 have been demonstrated to play an oncogenic role, similar to SRSF1. Recent pan-cancer bioinformatics analyses revealed that HNRNPF expression is increased in multiple tumors in the TCGA database, such as breast, lung, liver, ovarian, bladder, prostate, and renal carcinoma, in a positive correlation with MYC activation61. In earlier studies, hnRNP F, which was likely a downstream target PI3K/AKT/FOXO1 signaling, was shown to promote cell proliferation by regulating TPX2 and induce experimental metastasis via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in bladder cancer62–64. Likewise, higher expression of PTBP1 was found in multiple tumor tissues versus normal tissues when performing a pan-cancer analysis using TCGA databases, such as in bladder, breast, esophageal, head and neck, colon, liver and lung carcinoma65. In vitro studies revealed that PTBP1 promoted the migration and invasion in hepatocellular carcinoma cells by regulating the alternative splicing of NUMB into its pro-oncogenic isoform66. Depletion of hnRNP F and PTBP1 generally suppressed cell growth and proliferation, cell migration, and angiogenesis in both tested MCL cells; however, apoptotic sensitization was observed only in response to PTBP1 inhibition (Fig. 4). In Jeko-1 cells, depletion of hnRNP F inhibited BTZ-induced apoptosis. Altogether, our data support that hnRNP F and PTBP1 are pro-oncogenic in MCL cells, although SRSF1 seems to be a stronger regulator. It is, however, interesting to know that high co-expression of SRSF1, HNRNPF, or PTBP1 and MYC cooperatively predicted the higher risk of death in MCL patients and that high expression of either HNRNPF or PTBP1 could act as a prognosis factor for poor clinical outcomes (Fig. 8).

The role of autophagy is considered controversial in cancers. While autophagy is listed as one of the tumor-suppressive mechanisms, several studies reported that inhibition of pro-survival autophagy triggered cancer cell apoptosis and proposed the use of autophagy inhibitors, i.e., HCQ and chloroquine (CQ), for cancer treatment67. Although the consequences of autophagy activation in MCL cells in this context are unclear, we observed that depletion of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 strongly induced autophagy and autophagy-related genes (Fig. 5). In an attempt to explain how autophagy could carry out its tumor-inhibitory role, which is in line with the results of the present study, it was reported that: (i) sustained cellular self-cannibalism would impair cancer cell proliferation; (ii) Beclin-1 loss impaired p53 stability, leading to tumorigenesis; and (iii) prolonged activation of autophagy could lead to autophagic cell death, which was in response to cell transformation with oncogenic HRAS or MYC68,69. Additionally, several anticancer agents, including resveratrol, lapatinib, and arsenic trioxide, induce autophagic cell death in hematologic malignancies70–72. Indeed, we found that autophagy inhibition by HCQ promoted MCL growth, depending on the cellular context, and that HCQ rescued the reduced cell proliferation upon depletion of SRSF1 and PTBP1 (in Z-138 cells) (Suppl. Fig. S9), thereby supporting the tumor-inhibitory role of autophagy.

Overall, the evidence presented here demonstrates the significance of splicing factors SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1, which are aberrantly expressed in MCL clinical specimens, in regulating the fundamental hallmarks of cancer in MCL cells. We also revealed the roles of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 in the regulation of alternative splicing events of apoptosis-related genes into the pro-apoptotic isoforms. Mechanistically, SRSF1 typically exerts splicing activation by directly binding to pre-mRNA targets and enhancing the recognition of splicing sites, thereby promoting exon inclusion during splicing. By contrast, hnRNP F is an RNA-binding protein that has been reported to modulate the alternative splicing of pre-mRNAs by binding to G-rich sequences, with G triplets serving as the core recognition motif; thus, it may antagonize the activity of SR proteins by interfering their binding. However, its regulatory outcome largely depends on the context and varies according to the intronic or exonic regions where RNA G-quadruplexes are located73,74. A notable example is the BCL2L1 gene (encoding Bcl-xS and Bcl-xL), which is one of our identified targets. A previous report revealed that hnRNPF did not compete with SRSF1 to regulate the RNA splicing of BCL2L1. While SRSF1 directly bound to pre-mRNA (exon 2) and promoted the usage of the proximal 5’ splice site, leading to Bcl-xL expression, hnRNPF was found to repress Bcl-xS through the inhibition of hnRNP F binding to G-rich regulatory elements downstream of the Bcl-xS splice site75. As SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 have multiple biological functions that may or may not be related to their canonical splicing functions, further studies are required to investigate the regulatory mechanisms of SRSF1, hnRNP F, and PTBP1 underlying the acquisition of hallmarks of cancer, particularly those related to cell motility and autophagy. Our findings provide significant basic knowledge that could be important in understanding MCL pathogenesis and may lay the groundwork for novel therapeutic strategies for MCL treatment.
