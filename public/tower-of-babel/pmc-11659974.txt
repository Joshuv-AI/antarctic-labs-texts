Volenrelaxin (LY3540378) increases renal plasma flow: a randomized Phase 1 trial
Authors: Lai San Tham, Hiddo J L Heerspink, Xiaojun Wang, Petra Verdino, Chadi G Saifan, Eric A Benson, Paul Goldsmith, Zhenzhong Wang, Jeffrey M Testani, Axel Haupt, Flora Sam, David Z I Cherney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVolenrelaxin is a half-life-extended recombinant human relaxin protein developed for improving kidney perfusion and cardiorenal function. This study assessed the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) of volenrelaxin following single- and multiple-ascending doses (SAD and MAD) administration.MethodsIn this Phase 1, four-part, randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled SAD and MAD study in healthy participants, SAD participants (n = 56) received an intravenous or subcutaneous dose of volenrelaxin or placebo in a dose-ascending manner. MAD participants (n = 77) received volenrelaxin or placebo subcutaneously once weekly for 5 weeks. Effective renal plasma flow (ERPF) and measured glomerular filtration rate (mGFR) were determined by para-aminohippurate and iohexol clearance, respectively.ResultsVolenrelaxin demonstrated an extended half-life and increased acute and chronic placebo-adjusted ERPF change from baseline by 50% and 44%, respectively (P < .0001). mGFR was unchanged, while filtration fraction and afferent/efferent renal arteriolar resistances were reduced. Systolic and diastolic blood pressures decreased, and pulse rate increased with increasing volenrelaxin exposures, demonstrating maximal model-derived placebo-adjusted changes (90% confidence interval) of –6.16 (–8.04, –4.28) mmHg, –6.10 (–7.61, –4.58) mmHg and +4.39 (+3.38, +5.39) bpm, respectively. Adverse events were mild, with no difference in orthostatic hypotension between volenrelaxin and placebo.ConclusionVolenrelaxin was well-tolerated, safe and suitable for weekly subcutaneous dosing. Volenrelaxin showed a sustained improvement in kidney perfusion upon repeated dosing, supporting further clinical development in chronic kidney disease and chronic heart failure.Clinical trial registrationNCT04768855.Keywords: effective renal plasma flow, healthy participants, long-acting relaxin, LY3540378, volenrelaxin

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease and heart failure (HF) commonly coexist in the same individual, leading to significant morbidity, mortality [1–3] and progression of disease, despite maximal medical therapy. Kidney dysfunction in patients with HF complicates clinical management since therapies such as diuretics used for symptomatic management of HF often worsen kidney perfusion, leading to ischemia. This leads to a vicious cycle of worsening kidney function, recurrent hospitalizations and high mortality [4]. It is therefore necessary to identify therapies that attenuate intrarenal vasoconstriction whilst maintaining glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and improving the ability of the kidney to excrete solutes and water.

Human relaxin-2, an endogenous hormone, plays a critical role in hemodynamic and renovascular adaptive physiological changes during normal pregnancy [5, 6]. In disease states, serelaxin, a recombinant relaxin-2, improved dyspnea and kidney function in acute HF clinical trials when administered intravenously (IV) for 48 h, with hypothesized benefits being derived based on improved glomerular perfusion [7]. Since short-term, continuous IV infusions of serelaxin demonstrated acute hemodynamic benefits in HF patients including improved kidney perfusion [8–10], chronic treatment with a long-acting relaxin may durably improve cardiorenal function. This may facilitate maintenance of glomerular perfusion and GFR, stabilization of kidney function and maintenance of euvolemia and diuretic responsiveness in the management of chronic HF, potentially improving patient outcomes.

Volenrelaxin, previously LY3540378, is a long-acting human relaxin-2 analog that demonstrates a prolonged systemic circulatory half-life while maintaining endogenous relaxin biology [11]. In both rat and monkey studies, volenrelaxin demonstrated an in vivo half-life of >40 h. Similar to native relaxin, volenrelaxin is a potent agonist of the relaxin family peptide receptor 1 (RXFP1) and maintains selectivity against RXFP2/3/4 [11]. In rodents, volenrelaxin elicited relaxin-mediated pharmacodynamic (PD) responses with increased renal blood flow [11]. In the present study, volenrelaxin was investigated in healthy participants using single- and multiple-ascending doses (SAD and MAD) to assess its safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic (PK) and PD profiles.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a Phase 1, four-part, multicenter, participant- and investigator-blinded, placebo-controlled, randomized, SAD (Part A) and MAD (Parts B, C and D) study of volenrelaxin (LY3540378) in healthy participants (NCT04768855; registered on 23 February 2021) (Fig. 1). The primary objective of this first-in-human dose trial was to investigate the safety and tolerability of volenrelaxin. The key secondary and exploratory objectives were to characterize its PK, renal hemodynamic and functional effects, such as effective renal plasma flow (ERPF), filtration fraction (FF), effective renal vascular resistance, measured GFR (mGFR) and blood pressure. Parts C and D were designed to include first-generation Japanese and Chinese participants, respectively, to evaluate safety, tolerability and PK in these specific populations.

Trial design. The trial was a Phase 1, four-part, multicenter, participant- and investigator-blinded, placebo-controlled, randomized, SAD (Part A) and MAD (Parts B, C and D) study of volenrelaxin in healthy participants. Parts C and D included first-generation Japanese and Chinese participants, respectively. CRU, clinical research unit.

Due to a global shortage of para-aminohippurate (PAH) during the COVID-19 pandemic, only certain SAD and MAD cohorts were preselected for ERPF measurement. ERPF was determined via PAH clearance. The preselected volenrelaxin doses were expected to display an exposure-related ERPF relationship that informs dose-selection and reflects the targeted relaxin pharmacology in the kidneys. ERPF measurements were conducted in placebo, two subcutaneous (SC) (3.5 mg and 45 mg) and two IV (0.2 mg and 10 mg) cohorts of Part A and in placebo and two cohorts in Part B (25 mg and 120 mg). mGFR, derived from IV iohexol clearance, was determined in all cohorts (in Parts A and B). Inclusion criteria were healthy participants (by medical history, physical examination, laboratory testing and electrocardiogram) aged 21–65 years, body mass index of 19–30 kg/m2, an estimated GFR between 60 and 120 mL/min, and a normal pulse rate and blood pressure. Since volenrelaxin pharmacology is well understood with a wide safety margin, sentinel dosing was not performed [10, 12].

In Parts A, B and C, eight participants were randomized to volenrelaxin (n = 6) or placebo (n = 2) in each cohort. In Part D, six participants were randomized to volenrelaxin (n = 4) or placebo (n = 2). Participants’ safety and blood pressure measurements were assessed in the research unit for up to 5 days after the first and last doses and for at least 24 h after all other doses. Plasma PK samples for SC cohorts (Part A) were taken pre-dose, at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h post-dosing; and on Days 8, 15, 22, 29, 36, 43 and 70. For IV cohorts, PK samples were taken pre-dose; at 15, 30 and 45 min; at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h post-dose; and on Days 8, 15, 22, 29, 36, 43 and 70.

In Parts B, C and D, PK samples were taken as per SAD SC PK schedule up to Day 22; pre-dose on Day 29, followed by 6, 12-, 24-, 48- and 72-h post-dosing on Day 29; and on Days 33, 36, 43, 57, 85 and 113. Safety and tolerability were determined by the incidence of adverse events, treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and serious adverse events. Physical examination, vital signs, electrocardiograms, safety laboratory assessments, injection-site and any hypersensitivity reactions assessments were performed. Safety was monitored by vital signs, including both supine and standing blood pressure, and pulse rate. During inpatient stays, supine and standing blood pressure were measured pre-dose and every 3 h immediately post-dose. Blood pressure was also measured at all weekly outpatient dosing visits.

Iohexol was administered as a 36-mg/kg bolus injection over 2 min, followed by a 15 mg/min continuous infusion. PK sampling of iohexol was conducted at 120, 150 and 180 min into the iohexol infusion [13]. PAH was initiated 30 min after the commencement of iohexol administration via an 8 mg/kg bolus, followed by a 12-mg/min infusion. PAH PK blood samples were drawn at 90, 120 and 150 min into the PAH infusion [12, 14]. The iohexol and PAH administrations were scheduled to allow their PK blood samples to be drawn together.

Plasma volenrelaxin samples were analyzed using a validated liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry method at PPD Laboratory Services in Richmond, VA, USA. Plasma iohexol and PAH samples were analyzed using validated liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry methods at Labcorp Bioanalytical Services LLC in Indianapolis, IN, USA, and at Q2 Solutions in Ithaca, NY, USA.

Compliance with ethics guidelines

Trial protocols and informed consent forms were approved by an investigational review board at each site. The trial was conducted in accordance with ethical principles of Declaration of Helsinki, Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) and applicable International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practices (ICH GCP) guidelines. The protocol was approved by National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (NHG DSRB) and Salus IRB. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient at trial entry before any trial procedures.

SAD cohort (Part A)

Figure 1 shows five cohorts received IV doses, and two cohorts received SC doses as follows: 0.2 mg (IV), 0.65 mg (IV), 3.5 mg (SC), 3.5 mg (IV), 10 mg (IV), 45 mg (SC) and 80 mg (IV). Volenrelaxin doses were informed by preclinical data [11].

To establish baseline ERPF and measured GFR (mGFR) in IV cohorts, PAH and iohexol were administered before volenrelaxin administration and again on Day 1, immediately after volenrelaxin IV injection, to obtain ERPF and mGFR data corresponding to maximum volenrelaxin concentrations (Cmax). Similarly, PAH and iohexol infusions were administered before dosing for baseline determination and then again at Day 3 following SC administration, to acquire ERPF and mGFR data at volenrelaxin Cmax. Day 5 ERPF measurement was removed from all subsequent cohorts after the 0.2 mg (IV) SAD cohort to conserve PAH utilization to the most informative doses and timepoints.

MAD cohorts (Parts B, C and D)

Multiple-ascending doses of volenrelaxin were administered once weekly (QW) by SC injections for 5 weeks in Parts B, C and D (Fig. 1). The dose levels of volenrelaxin (SC) QW in the MAD study were 3, 10, 25, 60 and 120 mg (SC) QW.

The doses investigated for Part C and D were 25, 60 and 120 mg (SC) QW, and 60 and 120 mg (SC) QW, respectively. Dose escalation was conducted only after all safety and available PK data up to the immediately preceding lower dose was reviewed.

Iohexol and PAH [25 and 120 mg (SC) QW cohorts in Part B] infusions were administered prior to volenrelaxin administration and then on Days 3 and 31. These selected timepoints should be close to the Cmax of the first and last SC QW volenrelaxin doses, respectively, enabling assessment of acute and chronic PD effects. Renal hemodynamic investigations were not conducted for cohorts C and D.

Vital signs and statistical analyses

Scatter plots of placebo-adjusted change from baseline (ΔΔ) vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressures, mean arterial pressure) data versus volenrelaxin concentrations and their 90% confidence intervals (CI) were presented for all study parts combined. Based on the correlation models, ΔΔ vital sign parameters were derived for each dose at the observed steady-state volenrelaxin Cmax to illustrate a worst-case scenario. The regression equation, 90% CI for the slope, and its associated p-value were calculated.

PK and statistical analyses

The sample size selected provided sufficient data for evaluating safety, and PK and PD effects, and was standard for studies conducted in Phase 1 settings, but not necessarily powered for statistical hypothesis testing. Volenrelaxin PK parameters were calculated using standard noncompartmental methods in Phoenix WinNonLin Versions 8.1.1 and 8.3.5. Dose-proportionality and PD data analysis were evaluated for Parts A, B and C using SAS Version 9.4. Iohexol and PAH clearances representing mGFR and ERPF, respectively, for each participant, were derived based on the mean of three clearance values obtained from the concentration measurements taken on the same study day. Fractional sodium excretion was calculated from the first-morning urine sample via correspondingly timed blood samples of serum sodium and creatinine concentrations.

Derivation of renal hemodynamic parameters, including FF, effective renal vascular resistance, afferent renal arteriolar resistance, efferent renal arteriolar resistance and glomerular hydrostatic pressure was based on the Gomez equations [15] and Bjornstad et al. [16] Details of renal hemodynamic equations can be found in the Supplementary Appendix. Comparison of the PD parameters between volenrelaxin and placebo was performed for Parts A and B using a repeated measures mixed-effects model, which included baseline value, treatment, timepoint and treatment-by-timepoint interaction as fixed effects, and participant as a random effect. The SC placebo cohorts from Parts A and B were combined for the purpose of statistical analysis. The key PD parameters of ERPF, mGFR, FF and effective renal vascular resistance were log-transformed before statistical analyses, and subsequently back-transformed to present the treatment comparison as an estimated ratio to baseline compared with placebo. For afferent and efferent renal arteriolar resistances, and glomerular hydrostatic pressure, the treatment difference compared with placebo was shown as absolute difference when compared with placebo after adjustment for baseline. The comparison of PD parameters with placebo at different time points is presented along with the P-value and 90% CI.

There were 56 participants in Part A, 40 in Part B, 24 in Part C and 13 in Part D cohorts. Figure 2 provides details of participant disposition. The weekly doses of LY3540378 were well tolerated by all participants in the study. The majority of TEAEs were mild, and dose-escalation proceeded as planned. The incidence of TEAEs of orthostatic hypotension was not dose-related, with asymptomatic events occurring at random time points, and in both volenrelaxin- and placebo-treated participants.

All vital signs in the study were combined for analysis. There was either no correlation or a slight but statistically significant negative correlation between volenrelaxin concentration and placebo-corrected change from baseline in supine and standing systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure and pulse rate (Supplementary data, Fig. S1A–H). Model-derived placebo-corrected change from baseline (90% CI) occurred in supine systolic blood pressure, standing diastolic blood pressure and supine mean arterial pressure, at –6.16 (–8.04, –4.28) mmHg, –6.10 (–7.61, –4.58) mmHg and –5.62 (–7.02, –4.21) mmHg, respectively (Supplementary data, Fig. S1A, D and F). While there was a general trend for positive pulse rate at baseline without volenrelaxin exposure, importantly increasing volenrelaxin concentrations showed a reduction in model-derived placebo-corrected change from baseline for both supine and standing pulse rates for all study parts combined. The regression analysis showed a positive y-intercept (baseline), with supine pulse rate of +4.39 (+3.38, +5.39) bpm corresponding to the volenrelaxin Cmax for 0.2 mg IV dose (Supplementary data, Fig. S1G), but this elevated pulse rate was reduced to +1.25 bpm (–0.37, +2.88) at higher volenrelaxin Cmax at 120 mg (SC) QW.

Pharmacokinetics

In the SAD, the volenrelaxin SC PK elimination slopes were comparable to that of IV doses. In the MAD, SC QW dosing of volenrelaxin demonstrated that the mean concentration–time profiles remained relatively flat around the Cmax and were comparable for the same dose across Parts B, C and D (Fig. 3). Volenrelaxin half-life was approximately 8–10 days in the MAD study populations, supporting QW dosing. Volenrelaxin Cmax and area under the plasma concentration–time curve increased in a dose-proportional to a supra-proportional manner following SC doses, and the accumulation ratio ranged from 2 to 3.

Pharmacodynamics

Volenrelaxin significantly increased the ERPF from baseline by 50% (90% CI for ratio vs placebo: 1.33, 1.69; P < .0001), immediately after a single 10 mg (IV) dose. This % change is both baseline- and placebo-adjusted, and the effect on ERPF persisted with single (Fig. 4A) and multiple (Fig. 4B) SC QW dosing. Two days after a single SC dose of 25 and 120 mg, ERPF increased by 15% (90% CI for ratio versus placebo: 1.04, 1.27; P = .027) and 29% (1.17, 1.42; P = .0001), respectively (Fig. 4B; Supplementary data, Table S1). This increase in ERPF was further augmented at Day 31, after 5 weeks of SC QW dosing, to 44% (90% CI for ratio versus placebo: 1.28, 1.62; P < .0001) and 36% (1.21, 1.53; P < .0001) following 25 and 120 mg (SC) QW doses, respectively (Fig. 4B; Supplementary data, Table S1). Measured GFR remained relatively unchanged in these participants (Fig. 5A and B). The increases in ERPF were accompanied by significant reductions in FF by 31% (90% CI for ratio vs placebo: 0.61, 0.77; P < .0001), 29% (0.64, 0.78; P < .0001) and 30% (0.64, 0.78; P < .0001) after a single 10 mg (IV) dose, chronic 25 and 120 mg (SC) QW doses, respectively (Supplementary data, Fig. S2A and B).

DISCUSSION

In this Phase 1 study, volenrelaxin (LY3540378) was well tolerated. Adverse events were mild, with no clinically relevant adverse events related to symptomatic hypotension or changes in vital signs, electrocardiogram and clinical laboratory values. Volenrelaxin's flat concentration–time profile over a weekly dosing interval supports SC QW dosing.

Significant increases in ERPF were demonstrated with volenrelaxin administration. More importantly, ERPF was increased following acute and chronic dosing, indicating that volenrelaxin not only displayed the expected acute renal vasodilatory pharmacological effect of endogenous relaxin [6], but also maintained a persistent effect following repeated SC QW dosing of volenrelaxin. The magnitude of the acute ERPF effect for volenrelaxin after a single IV dose in the SAD was comparable to that previously displayed by the shorter-acting serelaxin compound in healthy participants [12] and chronic HF patients [17]. These findings also confirm that volenrelaxin's effect on ERPF via afferent and efferent renal arteriolar dilations can be reliably demonstrated in healthy participants.

GFR loss in chronic kidney disease and/or HF patients is associated with increased mortality where mortality increases with worsening renal function [18, 19]. Additionally, many HF treatments, such as diuretics, either rely on adequate kidney function for their efficacy or their safe use is limited by kidney dysfunction, such as with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. It is possible that by minimizing cardiorenal dysfunction, medications that improve renal perfusion could facilitate HF management, mitigate hospitalization risk and/or improve patient outcomes. However, there are no known approved therapies that have been shown to directly improve renal function chronically in HF patients.

In this trial, volenrelaxin was safe and demonstrated renal vasodilation and improved perfusion after 5 weeks of weekly SC dosing, supporting the hypothesis that its chronic use in HF could potentially provide benefit. Prior serelaxin studies demonstrated that, in addition to improving dyspnea, treatment for 48 h in acute HF led to several measures of improved renal function, including lower serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen and uric acid levels [20]. Importantly, this kidney function improvement occurred despite reductions in blood pressure. It is known that a decline in blood pressure is one of the strongest predictors of worsening kidney function in patients with acute HF. As such, small improvements in kidney function induced by serelaxin in the setting of a reduction in blood pressure suggests that the direct renal effect of serelaxin was clinically meaningful. Thus, chronic administration of long-acting relaxin could potentially reset renal autoregulation, thereby maintaining GFR in the presence of diuretics and other agents that impact kidney perfusion. Despite inducing a small renal benefit in this previous work, a 48-h serelaxin infusion did not translate into longer term improvement in patient clinical outcomes. Conversely, a sustained improvement in kidney function with long-acting relaxin administration over an extended treatment period has the potential to translate into improved clinical outcomes.

Signaling cascades leading to renal vasodilation in response to relaxin remain incompletely understood. Relaxin binds to the RXFP1 receptor, which is predominantly expressed in the atria [21]. Through RXFP1 receptor activation, relaxin activates a signaling cascade involving second messengers that include cyclic adenosine monophosphate and nitric oxide [22], which could result in generalized vasodilatory effects in the renal microcirculation—including both pre- and post-glomerular arteriolar relaxation. In addition to these hemodynamic benefits leading to improved renal perfusion, preclinical evidence demonstrates that relaxin mitigates injury pathways in the cardiovascular system, including adverse cardiac remodeling, largely by being antifibrotic [23–26].

Despite many strengths, our study does have some limitations. Direct invasive renal hemodynamics were not performed. Instead, the Gomez equations were used to estimate these parameters [15]. Hence, static constant values were assumed for certain parameters, such as the gross filtration coefficient and hydrostatic pressure, in the Bowman's space. To mitigate inter-individual variability in a study with a small sample size per treatment, ERPF was evaluated as a placebo-adjusted fold-change from the baseline of each participant. Current renal hemodynamic findings are limited to clinical data collected from healthy participants. As such, a proof-of-concept Phase 2 trial investigating the effects of volenrelaxin in patients with chronic HF (NCT05592275) is underway to investigate if these improvements translate from healthy participants to patients with chronic, worsening HF.

Although often a common finding in Phase 1 studies, the limited number (or absence) of female participants in some dose cohorts does not permit meaningful comparison of data for any sex-related differences in PK, PD or safety. Future studies are warranted to understand these differences between male and female healthy participants.

CONCLUSIONS

In this Phase 1 trial, volenrelaxin was well-tolerated with an acceptable safety profile over a wide dose range. Furthermore, volenrelaxin demonstrated improved renal hemodynamics and PK suitability for QW SC dosing. These results support further clinical development of volenrelaxin in chronic kidney disease and chronic HF.
