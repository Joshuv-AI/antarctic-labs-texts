Constrained hydrodynamic flocking models in the limit of large attraction-repulsion interactions
Thierry Goudon, Antoine Mellet

Abstract
We study the collective dynamics of a population of particles/organisms subject to self-consistent attraction-repulsion interactions and an external velocity field. The starting point of our analysis is a mean-field kinetic model and we investigate the singular limit corresponding to strong interaction forces. For well-prepared initial data, we show that the population asymptotically concentrates within a domain Ω ⁡ ( t ) = Ω 0 + X ⁡ ( t ) \Omega(t)=\Omega_{0}+X(t) whose shape Ω 0 \Omega_{0} is determined by the minimization of the interaction energy while the evolution of the domain’s center of mass X ⁡ ( t ) X(t) is determined by the external force field. In addition, we show that the internal flow of organisms within this moving domain is described by a classical hydrodynamic model (the lake equation). The first part of our result relies only on the existence and uniqueness of minimizers for the interaction energy and holds for rather general interaction kernels. The second part is proved using a modulated energy method under more restrictive conditions on the nature of the interactions, and assuming that the limiting lake equation admits strong solutions.



Keywords.

Hydrodynamic limits of kinetic equations, Attractive-repulsive dynamics, Emergent behavior, Lake equation.

Introduction

The mesoscopic model.

The starting point of this paper is a mesoscopic description of a large number of organisms (or particles/agents) moving under the combined actions of an attractive-repulsive force describing the interactions of the organisms with each others and a drag force due to an external velocity field.
The population of organisms is described by its distribution
function in phase space
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\geq 0

where
x
denotes the position of the organisms and
v
their velocity.
The evolution of this function is described by the
following Vlasov type kinetic equation:

\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}=\lambda\nabla_{v}\cdot((v-u_{ext})f_{\varepsilon}).

(1)

In the right hand side of
(
1
)
,
\lambda>0
is a parameter (the strength of the drag force) and

(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\mapsto u_{ext}(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the given external velocity field.
Interactions between the organisms are described by the self-consistent potential
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
defined by

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=W*\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x),\qquad\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v,

(2)

where the kernel
W(x)
combines the antagonistic effects of long-range attraction and short-range repulsion.
Equation
(
1
)
is set in the entire space
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and is supplemented with an initial data

f_{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)=f_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{init}}(x,v)\geq 0,\qquad\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{init}}(x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v\,{\mathrm{d}}x=m\in(0,\infty).

(3)

The total mass
m>0
will be fixed throughout the paper.

Hydrodynamic regime.

The derivation of hydrodynamic models from
(
1
)
-
(
2
)
in the regime
\varepsilon\to 0
is inspired by the pioneering work of Brenier
[
13
]
for the Vlasov-Poisson equation on the torus (
x\in\mathbb{T}^{N}
,
-\Delta\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\rho_{\varepsilon}
):
imposing asymptotically a monokinetic particle distribution,
one derives the incompressible Euler system (see also
[
50
]

for a refined asymptotic analysis).
Motivated by applications in plasma physics, this question has been revisited
in
[
9
]

when the equation is set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with an external confining potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}
.
In that case, the hydrodynamic regime is determined by the opposite actions of this external potential and the repulsive Coulomb forces.
The effect of this competition is two-fold:
it constrains the particles to a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and it prescribes the limiting macroscopic density (supported in that domain).
The flow of particles within this bounded domain is then described by the anelastic equation (or lake equation), which is closely related to the Incompressible Euler system.
In the simplest case
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}
,
the domain
\Omega
is a ball, but it can be noticeably
more complicated in other cases.
For example when
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)=\Lambda x\cdot x
with

\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(1/\lambda_{1}^{2},...,1/\lambda_{N}^{2})\neq\mathbb{I}
,

\Omega
has a surprising shape: it is an ellipsoid (as one would expect), but it does not correspond to a level set of the external potential.
A related asymptotic analysis is carried out in
[
57
,
58
]
, but starting from a particle system, and combining the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
with the mean-field limit (number of particles goes to
\infty
).

The present paper can be seen as a generalization of
[
9
]
in two directions: First we work here with an attractive potential which is defined
self-consistently instead of being a given confining potential. Second, we consider
repulsive potentials that are not necessarily Coulombian.

This paper was also motivated by some recent work of the second author and his collaborators on
another type of model that combines both attractive and repulsive forces: aggregation-diffusion equations. These are popular models for collective
dynamics that are very different in nature from
(
1
)
: They also involve a long range attractive force but they include a local repulsive force taken into account via a nonlinear diffusion term (overcrowding results in a pressure-like effect that drives the organisms away from each other).
In a series of work
[
41
,
42
,
52
,
53
]
, it was shown that singular limits of these equations lead to collective dynamics with sharp interfaces.
However, in that framework, the support of the limiting density changes with time and its evolution is determined by a free boundary problem of fluid type (such as Hele-Shaw flows with surface tension or mean-curvature flows depending on the regime of interest).
In the present work, we show that the competition between nonlocal attraction and nonlocal repulsion leads to a very different behavior and that the support of the density still moves, but only by translation of a fixed shape.

Indeed, formally, the limit
f(t,x,v)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
satisfies

\nabla_{x}\Phi\cdot\nabla_{v}f=0
. Multiplying this equality by
v
and integrating with respect to
v
yields

\rho\nabla_{x}\Phi=0,\qquad\rho(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(t,x,v)\,{\mathrm{d}}v,\quad\Phi=W*\rho.

This is the equation that characterizes the critical points of the interaction energy
(
4
)
under mass constraint.
For well-prepared initial data, we will show that the limiting density must actually be a
global energy minimizer
of that energy. Under some assumptions on
W
, this prescribes the density profile
\rho(t,x)
for all time, up to a translation in space.

Minimizers of the interaction energy.

With the mass
m>0
fixed by the initial data
(
3
)
,
the following minimization problem thus plays a central role in our analysis (with
\mathscr{E}
given by
(
4
)
):

\mathscr{E}[\rho_{0}]=\min\left\{\mathscr{E}[\rho]\,;\,\rho\geq 0,\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}x=m\right\}.

(5)

The first part of our analysis will be carried out for general kernels
W
and only requires
(
5
)
to have a unique solution (up to translation).
The second part of the paper requires additional properties of the energy
\mathscr{E}
and its minimizers.
The full characterization of these minimizers is a delicate issue and for that reason, we restrict ourselves to kernels
W
that are small perturbations of the classical repulsive power kernels with quadratic attraction:

W(x)=-\alpha\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}+\beta\Lambda x\cdot x,\qquad\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(1/\lambda^{2}_{1},...,1/\lambda^{2}_{N}),

(6)

where
p\in(-N,-N+2]
(with the usual convention that
\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}
becomes
\ln|x|
when
p=0
) and
\alpha,\beta>0
.
While the isotropic case
\Lambda=I
is more classical, more general
\Lambda\neq I

are relevant for many applications involving non-isotropic interactions (for fishes in a shallow river, for example, the vertical direction plays a different role from the horizontal ones).

When
W
is given by
(
6
)
with
p\in(-N,2)
, it is well-known that there exists a unique measure
\rho_{0}(x)
, solution of
(
5
)
with center of mass at
0
and supported on a compact set
\overline{\Omega_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
With the further restriction
p\in(-N,-N+2]
, this minimizer is a bounded function (and continuous when
p\in(-N,-N+2)
) - see for example
[
16
,
35
]
.
We note that
\rho_{0}
is still absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure when
p<-N+4
, while it is supported on a lower dimensional set when
p>-N+4
- see Proposition
2.1
(it would be interesting to extend our analysis to this case, though the nature of the limiting equation changes significantly in that case).
We refer to
[
6
,
35
]
for further results on this issue.

The asymptotic model: informal statements.

We now give an informal description of the main results of this paper, which characterize the asymptotic behavior of the solution
f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
of
(
1
)
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We recall that
\rho_{0}(x)
denotes the unique minimizer
(
5
)
with center of mass at
0
, and we denote by
\overline{\Omega_{0}}
its (compact) support.
We will prove the followings:

a)

Firstly, the density
\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\,dv
converges to
\rho(t,x)=\rho_{0}(x-X(t))
where
the center of mass
t\mapsto X(t)

solves:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}}{\,{\mathrm{d}}t}X(t)=V(t),

(7a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}}{\,{\mathrm{d}}t}V(t)=\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho_{0}(x-X(t))(u_{ext}(t,x)-V(t))\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

(7b)

When endowed with initial conditions

X(0)=X^{\mathrm{init}},\qquad V(0)=V^{\mathrm{init}},

(8)

this system admits a unique solution
(X,V)
, see Lemma
3.1
.

The limit density
\rho(t,\cdot)
is thus supported on the moving set
\Omega(t)=\Omega_{0}+X(t)
.
This result establishes the swarming behavior (or phase separation) in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
: it shows the formation of a moving interface

separating the vacuum region from the region of positive density.
A more precise statement is given in Theorem
2.2
and will be proved using basic a priori energy estimates and assumptions on the initial data which ensure that the limit density
\rho(t,x)
minimizes the interaction energy

for all

.
This result is quite general and holds for very general kernel

.

b)

Our second result shows that the macroscopic flux

converges, when
\varepsilon\to 0
, to

where the velocity field

is solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho\mathscr{V})=0&\mbox{ in }\Omega(t),\\
\partial_{t}(\rho\mathscr{V})+\nabla_{x}(\rho\mathscr{V}\otimes\mathscr{V})+\rho\nabla_{x}P=\lambda\rho(u_{ext}-\mathscr{V})&\mbox{ in }\Omega(t),\\
\rho\mathscr{V}\cdot\nu=\rho V\cdot\nu&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega(t),\end{cases}

(9)

with
\nu
standing for the outward unit vector on
\partial\Omega(t)
(and
V(t)
is the velocity appearing in
(
7a
)
-
(
7b
)
).
The equation is supplemented with an initial condition

\mathscr{V}(0,x)=\mathscr{V}^{\mathrm{init}}(x)

(10)

where the functions

\rho^{\mathrm{init}}(x):=\rho_{0}(x-X^{\mathrm{init}})
and
\mathscr{V}^{\mathrm{init}}(x)
satisfy the compatibility condition

\begin{cases}\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{init}}=\frac{1}{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho^{\mathrm{init}}(x)\mathscr{V}^{\mathrm{init}}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho^{\mathrm{init}}[\mathscr{V}^{\mathrm{init}}-V^{\mathrm{init}}])=0\mbox{ in }\Omega^{\mathrm{init}},\qquad\rho^{\mathrm{init}}[\mathscr{V}^{\mathrm{init}}-V^{\mathrm{init}}]\cdot\nu=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{init}}.\end{cases}

In the context of collective motion, we note that our first result above identifies the limiting density, while this second result tracks the motion (or flow) of the organisms/particles inside

We refer the reader to

The proof relies on a modulated energy argument, in the spirit of

2.1
Notations and Main Assumptions

We recall that the external velocity field
u_{ext}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is given, and we assume that it satisfies

u_{ext}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})).

(12)

Next, we denote by

\mathcal{M}^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

Since the energy

with center of mass at

. In fact, our first result will be proved under the following general assumptions:

(

The interaction kernel

(

There exists a unique

Assumption (

is lower-semicontinuous with respect to weak convergence of measures (see for instance

The existence of a global minimizers then follows under mild assumptions on the behavior of

at

[

]

The uniqueness of this minimizer, however, is not guaranteed and has only been proved under further structural assumptions on

(see

For our second result, we restrict ourselves to simple

(

W(x)=-\alpha\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}+\beta\Lambda x\cdot x+w(x),\qquad\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(1/\lambda^{2}_{1},...,1/\lambda^{2}_{N}),

and is such that

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{w(x)}{|x|^{2}}=0.

(
H2b)

In addition, we assume that

p\in(-N,-N+2]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathscr{F}[w](\xi)\geq-\kappa\mathscr{F}\left[-\alpha\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}\right](\xi)\;\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mbox{ for some $\kappa\in(0,1)$}

To end this section, we provide a quick proof of a simple yet important fact already mentioned above: While it is clear that the equation

This is shown in

Lemma 3.4

Let
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
U:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
be continuous functions such that

•

\mathrm{supp}(\rho\,U)\subset\overline{\Omega}
with
\Omega
a star-shaped open set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

•

\nabla\cdot(\rho\,U)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

•

\lim_{\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\to 0}\frac{\rho(x)}{\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{1-s}}=\omega>0
for some
0<s<1
.

Then,
U\cdot\nu\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

Proof.

As mentioned above, we already know that
\rho\,U\cdot\nu\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.
To prove the stronger statement, we take
0<r<1
and note that since
\Omega
is star-shaped, we have
r\Omega\subset\Omega
. For any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
we can then write:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial(r\Omega)}\rho U\cdot\nu\varphi\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus r\Omega}\rho U\cdot\nabla\varphi\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Since
\rho U
is continuous and supported in
\Omega
, the right hand side can be dominated by

\begin{array}[]{lll}\left|\int_{\Omega\setminus r\Omega}\rho U\cdot\nabla\varphi\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right|&\leq&\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\rho U\|_{L^{\infty}}|\Omega\setminus r\Omega|\\
&\leq&\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\rho U\|_{L^{\infty}}|\Omega|(1-r^{N})\end{array}

Since
s\in(0,1)
, we deduce:

\begin{array}[]{lll}0&=&\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 1}\displaystyle\int_{r\partial\Omega}\displaystyle\frac{\rho(x)}{(1-r)^{1-s}}U(x)\cdot\nu(x)\varphi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x)\\[8.5359pt]
&=&\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 1}\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\displaystyle\frac{r^{N-1}\rho(ry)}{(1-r)^{1-s}}U(ry)\cdot\nu(ry)\varphi(ry)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma(y)=\omega\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}U(y)\cdot\nu(y)\varphi(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\sigma(y).\end{array}

Since this holds for any test function
\varphi
, it follows that
U\cdot\nu
vanishes on
\partial\Omega
.

4.1
Properties of the Minimizer
\rho_{0}

The fact that the repulsive part of the kernel
-\frac{|x|^{p}}{p}
can be interpreted as the fundamental solution of an integro-differential operator plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
More precisely, we set

s:=\frac{p+N}{2}.

and introduce

E_{s}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(N,s)}{|x|^{N-2s}},\qquad&s<\displaystyle\frac{N}{2},\\[8.5359pt]
-2\sigma(N,s)\log|x|,&s=\displaystyle\frac{N}{2},\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\sigma(N,s)=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N}{2}-s)}{4^{s}\Gamma(s)\pi^{N/2}}.

This function is the fundamental solution of the operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
, for
s\in(0,1]
:
it satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}E_{s}=\delta
and its Fourier transform is given by

\widehat{E}_{s}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|\xi|^{2s}},

see
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
.
We also introduce the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, for
s\in(0,1)
,
endowed with the norm

\|\Phi\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\displaystyle\frac{|\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y\,{\mathrm{d}}x,

which can be equivalently defined by means of the Fourier transform,
see
[
4
, Section 1.3, sp. Def. 1.31 & Prop. 1.37]

In particular, its dual is the functional space

\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})=\Big\{\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\ \displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\widehat{\Phi}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2s}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}\xi}{(2\pi)^{N}}<\infty\Big\}

.

With these notations, we rewrite the interaction kernel

in
(

(43)

We will denote the attractive part of the potential by

(44)

Note that when
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho(x)\,dx=m
and

When

and

, the existence-uniqueness statement in Proposition

and domain

When

Lemma 4.3

For any

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(\alpha E_{s}(x-y)+w(x-y)\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\alpha\widehat{E_{s}}(\xi)+\widehat{w}(\xi))|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}\xi}{(2\pi)^{N}}

6.3
Time Compactness of the Flux

Theorem
2.3
only states that
j_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
converges to
j(t,x)
in
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{+}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
, but it is possible, by procedding as in
[
9
]
, to
establish some time compactness property for
j_{\varepsilon}
. This
ensures in particular that the initial data for the limit equation is meaningful. We recall briefly this argument here:

We introduce the set

\mathscr{W}=\big\{\Theta_{0}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},\ \Theta_{0}\text{ of class $C^{1}$},\ \mathrm{supp}(\Theta_{0})\subset[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega_{0}},\ \nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{0}\Theta_{0})=0\big\}.

This is a closed subspace of the Banach space
C^{1}
, endowed with the sup norm for
the function and its first order derivatives. Given
\Theta_{0}\in\mathscr{W}
, we set

\Theta(t,x)=\Theta_{0}(t,x+X(t))

which is supported in
\overline{\Omega(t)}
.
We multiply
(
25
)
by
\Theta
and we get

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}}{\,{\mathrm{d}}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}j_{\varepsilon}\cdot\Theta\,{\mathrm{d}}x&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}j_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\Theta\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Theta\cdot(\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon})\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
&&+\lambda\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}u_{ext}-j_{\varepsilon})\cdot\Theta\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}x.\end{array}

(82)

The first three terms in the right hand side can be readily dominated by using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
and owing to
(
58
)
, they are all bounded, uniformly with respect to
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and

0\leq t\leq T
.
We thus look at the last term:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}x&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}-\rho)\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}-\Phi)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
&&+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
&&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi\,{\mathrm{d}}x.\end{array}

The last two terms vanish since
\Phi(t,\cdot)
is constant on
\mathrm{supp}(\rho(t,\cdot))\subset\overline{\Omega(t)}
,
and

\mathrm{supp}(\Theta(t,\cdot))\subset\overline{\Omega(t)}
.
The second term vanishes too since
\Theta_{0}\in\mathscr{W}
and integrating by parts makes
\nabla\cdot(\rho\Theta)
appear,
with
\nabla\cdot(\rho\Theta)(t,x)=\nabla\cdot(\rho_{0}\Theta_{0})(t,x+X(t))=0
.
It follows that

\left|\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right|=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\rho_{\varepsilon}-\rho)\Theta\cdot\nabla_{x}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}-\Phi)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right|\leq C_{\Theta}\|(\rho_{\varepsilon}-\rho)(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

Going back to
(
82
)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{d}}}{\,{\mathrm{d}}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}j_{\varepsilon}\cdot\Theta\,{\mathrm{d}}x\text{ is bounded in $L^{\infty}((0,T))$. }

Since
\mathscr{W}
is separable, we can make use of a diagonal
argument to justify that, possibly at the price of
extracting a subsequence,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}j_{\varepsilon}\cdot\Theta\,{\mathrm{d}}x

converges uniformly on
[0,T]
for any
\Theta\in\mathscr{W}
.

Appendix A
Fractional Laplacians and Ellipsoids

In this section, we recall some classical (and some not so classical) computations regarding the minimizers of the interaction energy
\mathscr{E}
when
s\in(0,1)
(non-Newtonian repulsion) and
\Lambda\neq I
(not isotropic attraction).
These computations can be found elsewhere, but are gathered here for the reader’s sake.
One of our goal with this section is to show that the assumption
(
14
)
is satisfied in some non-trivial cases.

A.1
Proof of Proposition
4.1

Proposition
4.1

states that the energy minimizer of
\mathscr{E}
when
s\in(0,1]
and
\Lambda\neq I
(and with
w=0
) is supported in an ellipsoid.
As we will see below, this is not a simple extension of the radial symmetric case since the relation between the anisotropic matrix
\Lambda
and the ellipsoidal support of the minimizer
\Omega_{0}
is far from trivial (and largely not explicit).
The case
s=1
was treated in details in
[
9
]
, so we focus here on the case
s\in(0,1)
and show that the equilibrium function has the form
c_{0}(1-Ax\cdot x)_{+}^{1-s}
for some diagonal positive matrix
A
.

Given a symmetric positive definite matrix
A
, we introduce the associated inner product and norm

\langle x,y\rangle_{A}=Ax\cdot y,\qquad|x|_{A}=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{A}}.

Then, we consider the function

x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\longmapsto u_{A}(x)=(1-|x|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{1-s}

(83)

which is thus supported in the ellipsoid

\mathcal{E}_{A}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,;\,|x|_{A}\leq 1\}.

Note that
the associated mass is

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(1-Ax\cdot x)_{+}^{1-s}\,{\mathrm{d}}x&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(1-|y|^{2})_{+}^{1-s}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,{\mathrm{d}}y=|\mathbb{S}^{N-1}|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(1-r^{2})^{1-s}r^{N-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}r\prod_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\\[11.38092pt]
&=&|\mathbb{S}^{N-1}|\displaystyle\frac{B(N/2,2-s)}{2}\ \displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i},\end{array}

with
B(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}
the beta function.
We also recall
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
that
u_{A}
solves

1<|x|_{A}\leq 1+\delta
and let
x_{0}
be the point on
\partial\mathcal{E}_{0}
closest to
x
. Then

\displaystyle|\partial_{\nu_{0}}\Phi_{0}(x)|\leq C\,{\mathrm{dist}}(x,\mathcal{E}_{A})^{s},

(95)

\displaystyle|\partial_{\tau_{0}}\Phi_{0}(x)|\leq C\,{\mathrm{dist}}(x,\mathcal{E}_{A})^{s+1},

(96)

where
\nu_{0}=\nu(x_{0})
and
\tau_{0}=\nu_{0}^{\perp}
.

Proof of Proposition
A.4
.

Since
\mathscr{V}
is compactly supported and
\Phi_{0}(x)-A_{0}>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{A}
(with
\Phi_{0}
continuous), we only need to show that there is a constant
C
such that

|\mathscr{V}(x)\cdot\nabla\Phi_{0}(x)|\leq C(\Phi_{0}(x)-A_{0})

(97)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{A}
in a small neighborhood of
\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
to get the result (the inequality clearly holds in
\mathcal{E}_{A}
since both sides of the inequality vanish there).
We thus fix
x
such that

1<|x|_{A}\leq 1+\delta

with
\delta
such that the results of Proposition
A.5
and Lemma
A.7
hold.
We denote by
x_{0}
the point on
\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
closest to
x
and
\nu_{0}=\nu(x_{0})
.
We have

\displaystyle|\mathscr{V}(x)\cdot\nabla\Phi_{0}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathscr{V}(x)\cdot\nu_{0}||\partial_{\nu_{0}}\Phi_{0}(x)|+|\mathscr{V}(x)||\partial_{\tau_{0}}\Phi_{0}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathscr{V}(x)-\mathscr{V}(x_{0})\cdot\nu_{0}||\partial_{\nu_{0}}\Phi_{0}(x)|+|\mathscr{V}||\partial_{\tau_{0}}\Phi_{0}(x)|

where we used the boundary condition
(
42
)
.
Lemma
A.7
and the Lipschitz regularity of
\mathscr{V}
thus imply

\displaystyle|\mathscr{V}(x)\cdot\nabla\Phi_{0}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq C|x-x_{0}|\,{\mathrm{dist}}(x,\mathcal{E}_{A})^{s}+C\,{\mathrm{dist}}(x,\mathcal{E}_{A})^{s+1}

\displaystyle\leq C\,{\mathrm{dist}}(x,\mathcal{E}_{A})^{s+1}.

Combining this inequality with
(
92
)
, we deduce

|\mathscr{V}(x)\cdot\nabla\Phi_{0}(x)|\leq C(\Phi_{0}(x)-A_{0})

whenever
1<|x|_{A}\leq 1+\delta
, and the Proposition follows.

Proof of Lemma
A.6
.

We denote
\alpha:=1-s
. For
|x|_{A}>1
, we write

\displaystyle-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}(1-|x|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{(1-|y|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}-(1-|x|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|x-y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{A}}\frac{(1-|y|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|x-y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y.

Next, we denote by
x_{0}
the projection of
x
onto
\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
and
d=|x-x_{0}|={\mathrm{dist}}(x,\partial\mathcal{E}_{A})
. We then write

\displaystyle-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}(1-|x|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}\int_{Ay\cdot y\leq 1}\frac{(1-|y|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|x-x_{0}+x_{0}-y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}d^{-2\alpha}\int_{A(x_{0}-dy)\cdot(x_{0}-dy)\leq 1}\frac{(1-|x_{0}-dy|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|\frac{x-x_{0}}{d}+y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}d^{-2\alpha}\int_{A(x_{0}-dy)\cdot(x_{0}-dy)\leq 1}\frac{(1-|x_{0}-dy|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|e+y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y

where we used the change of variable
\frac{x_{0}-y}{d}\mapsto y
and denoted
e:=\frac{x-x_{0}}{d}\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
.
Since
|x_{0}-dy|_{A}^{2}=1-2dAx_{0}\cdot y+d^{2}|y|_{A}^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}(1-|x|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}d^{-2\alpha}\int_{A(x_{0}-dy)\cdot(x_{0}-dy)\leq 1}\frac{(2dAx_{0}\cdot y-d^{2}|y|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|e+y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha,N}d^{-\alpha}\int_{2Ax_{0}\cdot y\geq d|y|^{2}_{A}}\frac{(2Ax_{0}\cdot y-d|y|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}}{|e+y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y.

We note that
e=\frac{Ax_{0}}{|Ax_{0}|}
(since
e
is orthogonal to
\partial\mathcal{E}_{A}
),
so passing to the limit
d=|x-x_{0}|\to 0
,
the integral in the equality above converges to

\eta_{0}:=\int_{Ax_{0}\cdot y>0}\frac{(2Ax_{0}\cdot y)_{+}^{\alpha}}{|e+y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y=\int_{e\cdot y>0}\frac{(2|Ax_{0}|e\cdot y)_{+}^{\alpha}}{|e+y|^{N+2\alpha}}\,{\mathrm{d}}y\in(0,+\infty).

Indeed, there are no singularity since
|e+y|\geq 1
when
e\cdot y>0
and the integrand is bounded by
\frac{C}{|y|^{N+\alpha}}
and thus integrable for large
|y|
.

For
d
small enough, we thus have

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}(1-|x|_{A}^{2})_{+}^{\alpha}\geq\eta d^{-\alpha}

with
\eta=c_{\alpha,N}\eta_{0}/2
.
