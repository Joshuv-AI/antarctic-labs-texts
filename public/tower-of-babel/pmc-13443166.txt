Artificial neural manifolds
Authors: Rui Wang, Guolei Liu, Saisai Wang, Tonglong Zeng, Xinru Yang, Jing Sun, Xiaohua Ma, Bowen Zhu, Min Qiu, Hong Wang, Yue Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe brain can rapidly perform perception, prediction, and decision-making tasks. The capabilities stem from the collective coordination of neurons. This collective activity forms a manifold structure that naturally supports information representation and prediction, yet most neuromorphic work ignores this structure. Here, we construct an artificial neural manifold based on Mott memristors, enabling accurate and robust prediction while reducing the number of required samples. By constructing an artificial neuron circuit, a bell-shaped tuning curve similar to that of biological neurons is obtained. The tuning curve converges the large-scale neuronal firing into a compact, low-dimensional manifold structure. This structure satisfies the delay embedding theorem to establish a spatiotemporal information (STI) equation, enabling rapid prediction of neural activity with small sample sizes. In addition, we introduce a memory factor to modify the STI equation, which improves prediction accuracy and robustness. We not only accurately perceive incomplete images but also predict epileptic seizures.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic devices

Introduction

The brain performs functions such as perception, decision-making, memory, planning, and navigation thanks to the coordinated activity of groups of neurons1,2. This coordinated activity of neural groups presents a low-dimensional continuous structure that can carry complex perceptual, motor, and cognitive task information in the form of trajectories, which is called a neural manifold3. For different cognitive tasks, neural activity trajectories are typically confined to specific low-dimensional subspaces, along which the system evolves to maintain stable information processing and predictive functions—allowing organisms to perceive and make decisions efficiently in complex environments4. This type of trajectory not only reflects the dynamic evolution of neural states over time, but also inherently encodes the ability to predict environmental conditions and future events, forming the basis for the brain’s information encoding, decision-making, and anticipatory perception5. Therefore, constructing a brain-inspired neural manifold computing architecture is a key pathway to achieving efficient and intelligent perception.

In recent years, researchers have been striving to emulate neural functions through hardware implementations. As a promising solution, artificial neuron devices—such as Mott-transition memristors—have attracted widespread attention due to their structural simplicity, fast spiking response, and low power consumption6–12. These devices also offer the potential to preserve complex neural dynamics and simplify circuit architectures. However, most existing studies focus on achieving static input-output mappings for tuning curves, reproducing spiking-like behaviors, and scaling up arrays for computational capacity13–18. Constructing spike-based neural manifolds from a device perspective to replicate brain-like perception remains largely unexplored. Although various methods, such as long short-term memory (LSTM) networks19 and reservoir computing (RC)20, have been deployed in neuromorphic hardware for prediction, they typically require large training datasets and exhibit limited generalization to unseen scenarios. Therefore, developing a hardware manifold architecture capable of achieving efficient, accurate, and robust prediction with minimal samples is desirable.

Building upon this perspective, a fundamental question concerns how the brain leverages neural manifolds to infer and predict future trajectories from partial and noisy sensory inputs. This further raises the question of whether future trajectories can be directly predicted solely from experimental data. The spatiotemporal information (STI) equation, is derived from randomly distribution embedding based on Takens’ theorem21–24, and can convert high-dimensional data in experiments into future trajectories, effectively expanding the sample size and alleviating the small sample size problem. Recently, driven by the ever-increasing training resources and the importance of the short-term characteristics of dynamic systems, STI has gained increasing attention for short-term prediction tasks, including applications in typhoon, traffic flow, earthquake, and wireless data25–27. However, existing studies primarily focus on emergency application predictions. It remains unclear how STI can be implemented in a physically grounded, energy-efficient neuromorphic hardware with manifold structures to emulate the brain’s short-term predictive capabilities.

In this study, we construct an artificial neural manifold based on NbOx Mott memristors in hardware, transforming traditional discrete spike perception into a group state trajectory evolution and achieving accurate prediction of future neural activity. By building an artificial neuron circuit based on Mott memristors (Fig. 1a), a bell-shaped tuning curve with a phase difference, similar to that of biological neurons, is obtained (Fig. 1b). Large-scale neuronal firing can be mapped into co-evolutionary trajectories in state space through its bell-shaped tuning curve, thereby confining high-dimensional spike signals to a low-dimensional manifold (Fig. 1c). This low-dimensional manifold structure reflects the dynamic evolution of neural population activity over time, mathematically satisfying the delay embedding theorem to deploy the STI equation on hardware. By solving the STI equation, neural manifold representations can be learned, enabling high-precision predictions of future neural activity while significantly reducing the number of required samples. In addition, we introduce a memory factor to modify the STI equation. Compared with the traditional STI equation, the modified STI equation improves the prediction accuracy and noise robustness. As a verification, we constructed an NbOx-based artificial manifold hardware to perform manifold constraints on artificial neuron spiking signals and achieve short-term prediction. Furthermore, we demonstrate that manifold computation can accurately perceive incomplete images and predict epileptic signals, achieving early detection and precise tracking from pre-seizure onset to seizure onset. Although there is no faithful mathematical model of the brain, the method can still accurately infer its neural firing trajectories based on a small amount of experimental data. This method initiates a paradigm shift from discrete spike responses to the evolution of group state trajectories, laying the theoretical and hardware foundation for the next generation of efficient intelligent perception systems.

Results

Spike firing characteristics of artificial neurons

Natural replication of the spike firing characteristics of neurons is the basis for building artificial neural manifolds. NbOx memristors, based on Mott transition, have advantages such as typical threshold switching (TS) behavior, rich device physical dynamics, high device stability and durability, and can be used to realize high-performance artificial neuron devices8. Figure 2a shows the optical microscope image of a NbOx neuron device. The inset presents a magnified view, where the electrode width is 1 µm. Figure 2b displays the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) results of the NbOx neuron device. The NbOx device (1 × 1 µm2) exhibits excellent TS characteristics, as shown by its current-voltage curve in Fig. 2c. The threshold voltage (Vth) is 1.39 V (marked in red), and the holding voltage (Vhold) is 1.12 V (marked in yellow), with a high-resistance state (HRS) of 33.2 kΩ and a low-resistance state (LRS) of 1.4 kΩ. This TS characteristic is attributed to the Mott transition of NbOx8. In addition, stable Mott transition characteristics are observed for greater than 108 AC pulse cycles, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a. Supplementary Fig. 1b–d shows the rapid response time in nanoseconds.

Furthermore, leveraging the volatile characteristics of the NbOx device, an oscillatory neuron circuit is constructed, as illustrated in the inset of Fig. 2c. In this circuit, Vin represents the input voltage, Rload is the series resistor, and the threshold switching device exhibits a parasitic capacitance C. Supplementary Fig. 2a–c shows the internal circuit structure of the device, illustrating the generation of parasitic capacitance. Due to the HRS (33.2 kΩ) and LRS (1.4 kΩ) of the NbOx device, when LRS <Rload < HRS and Vin is first applied, the parasitic capacitance of the device begins to charge, and then most of the voltage drops across the NbOx device. When the voltage across the device exceeds the Vth, the device transitions from HRS to LRS. Subsequently, the capacitor discharges, and the voltage across the device decreases. When the voltage across the device is less than the Vhold, it is insufficient to maintain the LRS, causing the device to spontaneously switch back to HRS. This process repeats, resulting in current oscillations in the circuit and emulating the spiking behavior of biological neurons. Supplementary Fig. 2d shows a schematic diagram of the oscillation mechanism of the NbOx device. The effect of different external parallel capacitors on pulse delivery characteristics is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The detailed explanation of the oscillation mechanism based on NbOx devices is presented in Supplementary Note 1. As Vin increases, the load line shifts to the right with the same slope. When the load line falls within the Vth region, the firing frequency increases. When Vin reaches or exceeds Vhold regions, the spike probability decreases. This is because when the device switches to LRS, the excessively high Vin prevents the device voltage from falling back to Vhold, thus preventing the device from recovering to HRS.

Subsequently, a series of resistors ranging from 2 to 9 kΩ are connected in series with the NbOx device, with Vin fixed at 2.10 V, to evaluate the oscillatory neuron behavior (Fig. 2d). As Rload increases, the number of neuron spikes first increases and then decreases. This is attributed to the decreasing slope of the load line, and once beyond the hold region, the spiking activity vanishes. In addition, Fig. 2e illustrates the neuron spiking characteristics with a fixed Rload of 2 kΩ under various Vin values ranging from 1.65 to 2.25 V. The stability of spiking behavior under typical environmental conditions is also confirmed (Supplementary Figs. 4–6 and Note 2). When Vin serves as a task variable, the artificial neuron is stimulated to generate a bell-shaped tuning curve (Fig. 2f). As Vin gradually increases from 1.6 V to 2.3 V, the number of spikes exhibits a near-Gaussian distribution, peaking at 1.9 V. This phenomenon indicates that the spiking behavior of the oscillatory neuron depends on the strength of the input stimulus and exhibits selectivity and tunability. This characteristic also exists in biological neurons, often described using bell-shaped tuning curves. In response to varying stimuli (e.g., direction, speed, brightness), the firing frequency of biological neurons presents specific modulation curves28. The NbOx-based neuron displays diverse spiking behaviors depending on different Rload and Vin values. Stable cycle-to-cycle, device-to-device, and wafer-to-wafer spike firing characteristics are also demonstrated (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8 and Note 3). By adjusting neural circuit parameters, it is possible to tailor neuron characteristics to suit specific task scenarios, thereby enabling discrimination and encoding of different tasks.

Artificial neural manifold formation

In the brain, the perception, prediction, and decision-making of motor behavior are not mediated solely by individual neurons, but rather by a large number of neurons at the population level. Figure 3a illustrates the spiking behavior of neurons under different Rload values as Vin increases from 0 to 5 V. When the continuous stimulus variable Vin is applied across multiple neurons, each neuron exhibits a selective response within a limited state space, forming a distinct bell-shaped tuning curve, as shown in Fig. 3b. In the brain, the perception, prediction, and decision-making of motor behavior are not regulated by the modulation of individual neurons alone but are performed at the population level through networks of interconnected neurons. Figure 3c shows the tuning curves for load resistances of 2 kΩ and 8 kΩ. Based on the tuning curves, a 2D neural manifold geometry representation is shown in Fig. 3d. When Rload is 2 kΩ and 8 kΩ, the neurons exhibit significant phase offsets, resulting in a response manifold with an elliptical shape. In contrast, for Rload values of 8 kΩ and 9 kΩ, the phase shift is smaller, leading to a narrower response manifold shape (Supplementary Fig. 9). Similarly, Fig. 3e shows the tuning curves of 4, 6, and 8 kΩ, forming a neural manifold with a 3D geometric shape (Fig. 3f). It is important to note that due to human visual limitations, we can only visualize up to 3D neural manifolds, while in reality, the manifold can exist in an N-dimensional space. The detailed descriptions are presented in Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. 10. Geometrically, this limited set of activity patterns can be imagined as a surface embedded in the neural population state space, where each axis corresponds to one neuron’s activity. Supplementary Fig. 11 shows that the tuning curves between neuron devices have low variability, which means that the devices have stable manifold mappings. Supplementary Figs. 12–16 respectively show the impulse oscillation behaviors under different Vin when the Rload is 2, 4, 6, 8, and 9 kΩ, corresponding to the neural manifold in Fig. 3d, f. The neural manifolds suggest that during behavioral tasks, the responses of different neurons within a population are often tightly correlated, indicating that only a limited set of neural activity patterns is expressed by the population29,30. Although neural manifolds offer a concise summary of neuronal population responses and encode underlying constraints, they serve primarily as a descriptive model31. Understanding the topology of neural manifolds through population dynamics could be significant for experimentally simulating the efficient information processing and predictive capabilities observed in biological systems.

Solving artificial neural manifolds

A large-scale neuronal network in the brain is able to predict movement and facilitate rapid decision-making32. We further explore the neural manifolds formed by high-dimensional neuronal populations. Figure 4a, b shows the neural manifold computation setup based on NbOx devices. The NbOx neurons are driven by input voltage pulses, and the output spike signals are fed into the signal conditioning circuit to produce clean and stable spiking outputs (Fig. 4c). These spike sequences are collected by the host computer and interact with the MCU, where manifold calculations are then performed to achieve prediction. The detailed hardware implementation process is presented in Supplementary Note 5. Through Gaussian Process Factor Analysis (GPFA)33, Fig. 4d reveals the neural manifold formed by large-scale neuronal populations, which share an evolutionary trajectory in a low-dimensional subspace. Although neurons in a population respond differently, they express only a limited set of activity patterns, consistent with the biological neural manifold5,34. The high-dimensional data is first spike-encoded, and the historical time series is extracted to form a neural manifold. This behavior of confining high-dimensional impulse signals to low-dimensional manifolds satisfies the delay embedding theorem and can be used to establish the STI equation. Supplementary Note 6 presents the theory of STI-based manifold prediction. Then, the evolutionary trajectory of the neural manifold is used to construct the STI equation. By solving the equation, the future discharge trajectory can be obtained. Supplementary Fig. 17a shows a flowchart of mapping raw data to neural inputs for neural manifold computation, which includes a combined software and hardware process. Supplementary Note 7 presents the detailed power consumption analysis.

Specifically, the bell-shaped tuning curves of artificial neurons are used to encode the original D-dimensional input variable at time t, denoted as Xt=x1t,x2t,…,xDt′, into a high-dimensional space of dimension D~, F(Xt)=F1(Xt),F2(Xt),…,FD~(Xt)′. Here, both the input Xt and the output Yt evolve over time. Next, a linear transformation matrix A maps the high-dimensional feature space into a lower-dimensional space, AF(Xt)=Yt. The STI equation is modify by the memory factor of γ to improve the prediction accuracy and robustness. The mathematical expression of this network is given by Eq. (1), where t = 1, 2,…, m.

where AB = I, A is an L × D~ matrix, B is a D~ × L matrix, and I denotes the L × L identity matrix and γ is memory factor (γ = 0.5). Matrices A and B are two mapping matrices determined from observed data, forming a conjugate spatiotemporal mapping equation. By solving this conjugate equation, we can obtain both the future neural activities ym+1,ym+2,…,ym+L−1 of the target variable and the unknown mappings A and B, thereby enabling brain-like anticipatory perception and rapid decision-making. Typically, the condition m ≥ 2 L or m ≥ 2L-1 is required (Supplementary Note 8). It should be noted that yt=xkt represents any single input variable from the full set of input variables Xt=x1t,…,xkt,…,xDt′. Supplementary Fig. 17b shows a flowchart for the prediction process with t of 1 to 4. The detailed algorithm flow is shown in Supplementary Fig. 18. In this setup, the nonlinear function F and matrix A encode spatial information into temporal information, while matrix B decodes the encoded temporal information back into the original spatial domain. Importantly, this process relies solely on solving the conjugate equation to perform prediction35,36. The solution process for the STI equation is shown in Supplementary Note 9.

Due to the extraction of shared neural activity across three latent dimensions via GPFA, we utilize six historical time points along these three dimensions to construct the neural manifold and predict neural activity over the next two future time steps. Specifically, GPFA is used to obtain the neural population activity matrix Xt=x1t,x2t,x3t′, where t = 1, 2,…, 2000, resulting in a 2000 × 3 neural activity matrix. Using the neural activity from six consecutive past time steps, i.e., x11~6,x21~6,x31~6, we predict the neural states at the next two time points, yk7,yk8, k = 1, 2, 3. Subsequently, by shifting the time window forward, we use the activity from x13~8,x23~8,x33~8 to predict the future states (yk9,yk10), thereby enabling continuous, stepwise forecasting of neural dynamics. Figure 4e presents the 3D visualization of the predicted results, where the red line indicates the prediction. Figure 4f shows the predicted future activity matrix. The mean of each row in this matrix corresponds to the final predicted value Yt. Since the time variable Yt=ykm,ykm+1,…,ykm+L−1′ is embedded in the STI equation, ykm is known and ykm+1,…ykm+L−1′ is unknown. This network framework effectively captures and resolves the neural manifold structure, and accurately predicts the future dynamics of neural populations, achieving the structural similarity index measure (SSIM) of just 0.737. The experimental spiking neural signals based on NbOx devices have three dimensions, and we only show the prediction results for the different γ in one dimension to more clearly illustrate the differences. When the memory factor is 0 (traditional STI), 0.5, and 0.8, the SSIM values are 0.746, 0.824, and 0.770, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Furthermore, we use Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) images37 for verification (Fig. 4g). Only the 1st to 4th columns of the image data are needed to predict the next 5 columns, and then the next 6 columns is predicted based on the 2nd to 5th columns, and the image prediction is completed in this cycle. Figure 4h shows the prediction results for numbers 1 to 4. As the data from earlier columns serve as predictors for later columns, a left shift occurs in the predicted outputs. Figure 4i shows the SSIM results. The SSIM values of the predicted numbers 1 to 4 are 0.868, 0.740, 0.681, and 0.901, respectively. When the memory factor is 0 (traditional STI), 0.5, and 0.8, the SSIM values for predicting numbers 3 are 0.664, 0.829, and 0.613, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 20). When the memory factor = 0.8, the predicted image lacks edge information. Supplementary Fig. 21a shows the results of the prediction performed on the MCU with the memory factor of 0.5, and the SSIM value is 0.786.

Predicting brain neural electrical signals

To further demonstrate the practical potential of neural manifolds in neuroengineering and neurological disease intervention, we utilize the Bonn epilepsy dataset to accurately predict epileptic seizures. Epileptic seizures are often sudden and unpredictable, posing significant risks to physical and mental health38. Therefore, developing a universal epileptic seizure prediction mechanism has important clinical and engineering value, potentially reducing patient risk and improving quality of life. This dataset provides high-temporal-resolution local field potential recordings representing three typical brain states: normal brain state, interictal period, and ictal period. These local field effect signals are mapped to the input voltage of an artificial neuronal circuit and subsequently converted into the firing probability of oscillatory neurons. By constructing population-level neural response traces and embedding them into the neural manifold, we capture their intrinsic dynamical behavior.

Figure 5a–c illustrates the predictive performance of our method across the three epileptic states, highlighting the clear separability of brain states within the neural manifold framework. Further, Fig. 5d–f compares the original neural activity with the predicted neuron firing frequencies, showing high-temporal consistency and validating the effectiveness of neural manifolds in modeling population neural dynamics. Notably, this method not only reconstructs current brain states but also enables anticipatory prediction of imminent seizures. Figure 5g clearly identifies a pre-seizure prediction window during the transition from a normal to ictal state, demonstrating successful capture of early warning signals. Quantitatively, the prediction error for normal brain activity is less than 1.5 kHz, with the SSIM of 0.894. For interictal and ictal states, the prediction errors are 167.6 and 522.7 Hz, respectively, with the SSIM of 0.997 and 0.981 (Fig. 5h). For a detailed description of the epilepsy signal prediction process, please refer to the Methods section. In addition, Supplementary Note 10 summarizes the process of establishing the STI equation for experimental spike signals, incomplete images, and epilepsy signals. It is worth noting that we introduce a memory factor into the STI equation to achieve accurate and robust prediction of epilepsy signals (Supplementary Note 11). Supplementary Fig. 21b–d shows the hardware-predicted results of normal epilepsy, interictal epilepsy, and ictal epilepsy signals based on the tuning curve. In-depth research into device-level tuning curves, such as whether their shape, width, preference, and phase can be extended, can provide greater computational power and application value for neural manifold computing. Supplementary Fig. 22 demonstrates that introducing a memory factor into the STI equation enables a more accurate and robust neural signal prediction framework. With the introduction of the memory factor, the accuracy of predicting the typical number 3 improves by ~24.9%; the accuracy of predicting experimental spiking neuron electrical signals improves by ~10.5%; and the accuracy of predicting normal epileptic nerve signals improves by ~29.1%. Supplementary Fig. 23 shows a comparison with the traditional STI equation. In the absence of noise, the prediction accuracy improves by ~29.1%; with 5% noise, the prediction accuracy improves by ~91.2%, and noise robustness improves by ~78.6%. Supplementary Fig. 24 shows that our method is more robust to device deviations. Supplementary Fig. 25 shows the prediction accuracy under different known lengths and predicted lengths. The longer the known discharge trajectory, the more future neural activity can be predicted. It is worth noting that accuracy and future neural activity need to be balanced according to different application scenarios. Although the prediction accuracy for normal states is relatively lower due to subtle fluctuations in neural activity, this does not hinder the precise detection and tracking of seizure-related transitions.

Unlike traditional classification models that rely on extensive labeled data for training, our approach leverages the intrinsic geometry of neural manifolds. It achieves unsupervised prediction of seizures purely through decoding and mapping of the spatiotemporal evolution of neural activity. This dynamical-system-based modeling strategy avoids issues such as overfitting and poor generalization, offering greater universality, adaptability, and cross-subject prediction capability. For a comparison with traditional methods, see Methods. Figure 5i provides a comparison with the current state-of-the-art brain-inspired systems that have predictive capabilities19,39–45. Discussion

We have successfully constructed an artificial neural manifold based on Mott memristors at the hardware level, transforming brain-like computing from a discrete spike response to a group state trajectory evolution paradigm, enabling accurate prediction of future neural activity. The large-scale neuronal firing can converge into a compact manifold through its bell-shaped tuning curve, which is manifested as a co-evolutionary structure in the state space. This makes the manifold state satisfy the delay embedding theorem of dynamical systems, thus establishing a STI equation. By modifying and solving the spatiotemporal equations, we analyzed the evolution of the neural manifold and achieved high-precision and robust predictions of future neural activity. Furthermore, we successfully achieved accurate reconstruction of incomplete signals and early detection and accurate tracking of epileptic signals from pre-onset to onset. The SSIM for epileptic seizure prediction reached 0.997 (interictal period) and 0.981 (ictal period). The system’s perception tasks can be expanded to include emergency scenarios such as earthquakes, tsunamis, traffic, and typhoons. Traditional methods that rely on large-scale data training are often not applicable in these emergency scenarios. This study initiates a paradigm shift from discrete spike responses to group state trajectory evolution, providing a solid hardware foundation and model support for the development of future brain-like intelligent systems.

Methods

Device fabrication

We fabricated Pt/Ti/NbOx/Ti/Pt memristors on 4-inch silicon wafers. A bottom electrode consisting of Ti (5 nm)/Pt (35 nm) was deposited using UV lithography and a lift-off process, followed by electron beam evaporation. An amorphous NbOx layer was deposited by magnetron sputtering and patterned using a lift-off technique at room temperature. During sputtering, a 35 nm NbOx layer was deposited using Ar gas, with a sputtering power of 50 W and a sputtering pressure of 0.5 Pa. Next, a top electrode of Ti (5 nm)/Pt (35 nm) was deposited by electron beam evaporation and patterned using UV lithography and a lift-off process to form the NbOx neuron device. The effective working area of the NbOx device is 1 µm × 1 µm.

Device characterization

The cross-section TEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (FEI Tecnai TF-20, UK). The DC measurement was measured by Agilent B1500 semiconductor parameter analyzer. A B1530A fast measurement unit module was used for generating the voltage pulse and measure the spiking responses. All electrical measurements were carried out using a summit 1100B-M Probe Station.

Compared with traditional methods

Although various methods, such as LSTM19 and RC20, have been widely applied to time series forecasting in many fields, one of the biggest drawbacks of these machine learning methods is that they must use a large number of samples to train the network to learn the dynamic characteristics of the system to avoid overfitting. While RC can avoid costly backpropagation and enable efficient hardware deployment, it cannot make predictions based on short-term data like manifold computation (Supplementary Fig. 26). Unlike these methods, the manifold is the inherent geometry of neuronal activity, not constructed by algorithms, but rather an inherent property of the firing of a group of neurons. This discovery means that the neuronal manifold itself is hardware capable of carrying computational functions, rather than merely a representation. More importantly, this input can be mapped to multiple low-dimensional manifolds simultaneously, enabling parallel prediction (Supplementary Fig. 27). The core of neural manifold computation lies in the geometry of the manifold itself, independent of the transient dynamics of the device. Furthermore, for a detailed explanation of the differences between neural manifolds and RC, see Supplementary Note 12 (e.g., population firing in neural manifolds and reservoir in RC). Supplementary Table 1 provides a clear comparison between neural manifolds and RC in terms of network structure, input type, operation mode, underlying theory, physical implementation, and network interpretability.
