Improving protein and protein interactions using pseudo-dimers derived from monomeric proteins
Authors: Hao Du, Xinzhe Zheng, Yuchen Ren, He Huang, Xinqi Gong, Wanli Ouyang, Yang Zhang, Yan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurately predicting protein-protein interactions (PPIs) in dimeric complexes remains a fundamental challenge in computational biology. Although existing PPIs prediction models, such as AlphaFold-Multimer (AF-Multimer) and AlphaFold3 (AF3), have achieved impressive performance, they still suffer from unsatisfactory accuracy due to the limited availability of protein dimer structures, whose collection is both expensive and labor-intensive. Here, we introduce a simple yet effective pre-training method, termed split and merge proxy (SMP), that leverages abundant monomeric proteins to simulate various PPIs tasks for the first time. Specifically, SMP constructs pseudo-dimers by splitting monomer data into two subunits, referred to as pseudo-receptors and pseudo-ligands, and trains models to merge them back by predicting their pseudo interactions (e.g., contact or docking). This proxy task enables large-scale pre-training without additional cost. Models pre-trained with SMP and subsequently fine-tuned on real protein dimer datasets demonstrate consistently improved accuracy and generalization across multiple benchmarks, surpassing strong baselines. Notably, SMP delivers more accurate structure predictions than both AF-Multimer and AF3 on several CASP15 dimer targets. Our findings highlight SMP as a scalable strategy for harnessing monomeric data to advance protein complex modeling, providing insights into the linkage between monomers and multimers.

Introduction

Identifying protein-protein interactions (PPIs) in dimeric complexes remains a significant and long-standing challenge in computational biology1–3. The notable success of AlphaFold2 (AF2)4 has opened a promising avenue in this area. Building on this advance, a number of deep learning-based methods5–8 have been proposed to predict and analyze PPIs. These approaches are essentially data-driven, depending on well-curated datasets to achieve accurate and generalizable predictions, thereby underscoring the importance of large-scale, high-quality protein complex data.

Given the central role of multimeric data, especially dimeric proteins, in training and evaluating PPI prediction models, substantial efforts have been devoted to curating high-quality datasets. High-performing multimer structure prediction models, such as AlphaFold-Multimer (AF-Multimer)5 and AlphaFold3 (AF3)6, are trained on carefully curated entries from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)9, the largest open-access repository of experimentally determined structures, which by 2024 contains more than 100,000 protein complexes. These models have shown strong performance across diverse applications10,11. To support rigorous evaluation, several benchmarks have been established as well3,12,13. A prominent example is the Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction (CASP)14,15, a globally recognized benchmark that evaluates protein structure prediction models against newly solved but unpublished experimental structures, thereby ensuring unbiased assessment. Collectively, these datasets and benchmarks not only enable systematic assessments but also drive methodological advances in computational biology.

Despite substantial efforts to assemble high-quality protein complex datasets, current PPIs prediction models still fall short of achieving consistently reliable performance16,17. One major bottleneck is the scarcity of diverse, high-quality dimeric structures. Notably, AF2, the most successful protein structure prediction model to date, is trained on approximately 132,000 experimentally resolved single-chain structures available in the PDB prior to 2018, spanning both monomeric and multimeric entries18. In contrast, only 19,166 high-quality, non-redundant protein dimers were available in the same period19, after stringent filtering steps such as removing chains that were too short or too long and eliminating sequences with > 30% similarity. This represents nearly an order-of-magnitude reduction relative to single-chain proteins. This stark imbalance highlights the difficulty in acquiring high-quality experimental dimers, thereby limiting the performance of deep learning models. To overcome this challenge, cost-effective strategies for generating additional training samples without relying on labor-intensive experimental procedures are urgently needed.

In this work, we propose Split and Merge Proxy (SMP), a simple yet effective pre-training strategy that leverages abundant monomeric proteins to enhance PPIs prediction at no additional cost. SMP constructs pseudo-dimer samples by splitting a monomer into two subunits, termed pseudo-receptors and pseudo-ligands, and training an interaction model to merge them back, effectively simulating the process of protein interactions. Although these pseudo-dimers inevitably introduce biological noise, they nevertheless provide rich information that complements existing protein dimer datasets. The pretrained model can be directly fine-tuned on real dimeric structures without any modifications to the model architecture. Crucially, the alignment between the pre-training and downstream objectives eliminates task discrepancies during fine-tuning, enabling more efficient adaptation and yielding higher prediction accuracy.

Although some existing approaches fragment full-length proteins into shorter segments for training4–6, with AF2’s cropping strategy being a representative example, the motivation, operation, and learning objective differ fundamentally from SMP. Specifically, (1) Motivation: the cropping strategy of AF2 is primarily introduced to mitigate computational constraints, notably the memory limitations of TPUv3 cores, as the memory usage scales cubically with sequence length4. In contrast, SMP is driven by the scarcity of high-quality dimeric training data. Our aim is to explicitly simulate inter-chain settings and generate additional supervisory data without additional experimental structures. (2) Operation: motivated by distinct goals, the two methods adopt different operational designs. AF2 cropping only shortens sequence length by randomly selecting a contiguous window (typically 256 or 384 residues) from a single-chain protein. In contrast, SMP splits a monomer into two artificial chains, a pseudo-receptor and a pseudo-ligand, to construct pseudo-dimers, making its operation intrinsically different from AF2. (3) Learning objective: the operational distinctions naturally lead to divergent training objectives. AF2 performs intra-chain structure prediction with the cropping input. SMP, however, supervises the model on inter-chain labels, such as contact maps and docking poses, which go beyond the scope of AF2.

To evaluate the effectiveness of SMP, we apply it across multiple protein interaction tasks, including protein-protein contact map prediction20, protein-protein docking21, and protein-protein interaction22. Across all tasks, SMP consistently outperforms strong baselines and achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) results, demonstrating its generality and robustness for both residue-level and sequence-level interaction prediction. We further assess SMP under data-scarce settings with only limited real dimer samples and observe substantial performance gains, highlighting the benefit of the proposed pre-training strategy. Finally, we test SMP on real-world applications using the CASP15 benchmark23 and find that SMP can produce more accurate interaction predictions for some targets compared with AF-Multimer and AF3, despite their significantly larger model capacities and computational budgets.

Results

The overview of SMP

SMP is a simple yet effective proxy strategy designed for pre-training protein interaction models. The overall framework consists of two stages: split and merge. In the split stage, each monomeric protein is partitioned into two subsequences with corresponding structural information, forming a pseudo-dimer referred to as the pseudo-receptor and pseudo-ligand (Fig. 1a). Although such pseudo-dimers inevitably introduce biological noise, they retain meaningful structural patterns for protein interactions. To quantify this effect, we employ Foldseek-Multimer24 to assess structural similarity between pseudo-dimers (generated via random splits) and real protein complexes. As shown in Fig. 1b, 2974 pseudo-dimers achieve a TM-score25,26 greater than 0.5 when matched to real complexes, accounting for approximately 13% of the pseudo-dimer dataset, indirectly indicating that monomer data can complement existing multimer data. Representative examples are illustrated in Fig. 1c, including PDB pairs 2BQI-3ZVQ and 1BGO-5I4H, with TM-scores of 0.95 and 0.87, respectively. In the merge stage, pairwise pseudo-dimers are embedded as the input for sequence-based or structure-based interaction models. The models are trained using labels derived from the split procedure, enabling task-specific pre-training for interaction prediction. Subsequently, the pre-trained models are fine-tuned on real protein dimer datasets without architectural modification, serving as strong initializations for downstream tasks (Fig. 1d). Comprehensive analysis of splitting strategies

We begin by investigating how monomeric proteins are split, as randomly splitting can increase the number of training samples but may disrupt secondary structures or functional regions, while a domain-guided splitting strategy preserves biologically meaningful fragments, though not all proteins contain multiple domains. To systematically examine these two approaches, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation spanning both residue-level tasks, such as contact map prediction and protein docking, and sequence-level tasks, including interaction classification. For the domain-aware strategy, we adopt a domain segmentation tool built upon the CATH v4.4 database27, yielding 15,602 multi-domain proteins released before 2018-04-30. For the random-split strategy, we use the same protein set but follow a center-based rule, in which each split point is selected near the midpoint of the monomeric protein chain to maintain reasonable fragment lengths. Further analysis of the random split positions is provided in Supplementary Section S1, with detailed results summarized in Supplementary Table S1. We can observe a decline in performance when the split point deviates from the chain center, primarily because the resulting excessively short fragments are considered trivial and are consequently excluded during data pre-processing.

The comparisons are illustrated in Fig. 2. Our results show that: (1) the domain-aware split strategy consistently improves the model’s overall performance across multiple downstream tasks. For example, in the contact map prediction task, the domain-aware split outperforms the baseline model DeepInter8, achieving improvements of 14.7% in P@L for homodimers and 5.1% for heterodimers. In the interaction classification task, it also surpasses the baseline PPITrans28 model, yielding gains of 8.2% in recall and 4.3% in F1-score. These results indicate that incorporating monomeric proteins indeed benefits PPIs prediction by providing complementary and informative structural and sequence cues; (2) While the domain-aware split provides better biological interpretability, the random split strategy consistently yields higher performance and robustness in contact map prediction and protein docking tasks, and achieves comparable results in the interaction classification task. Specifically, for the docking task, the random split improves the success rate by an average of 3.0% over the domain-aware split (33% vs. 30%). For contact map prediction, the random split also performs better in both homodimer and heterodimer scenarios (homodimer P@L: 0.706 vs. 0.690; heterodimer P@L: 0.373 vs. 0.353). Although this finding may appear counterintuitive, it reflects a common property of deep learning-based methods: their ability can benefit from a broader and more diverse set of data patterns during training. In contrast, a domain-guided splitting strategy may inadvertently have structurally or sequentially similar samples together with real protein dimers, thereby reducing the diversity of training examples and limiting the model’s generalization capability. By comparison, the random split strategy introduces greater heterogeneity into the training data, which can enhance the model’s robustness and generalization performance across diverse PPIs scenarios. Hence, we use the randomly split strategy with a center-based rule to construct our pseudo-dimer pre-training dataset. In addition, we analyze the pre-training dataset composed of single-domain and multi-domain proteins for the contact map prediction task, as detailed in Supplementary Section S2. The results (Supplementary Table S2) show that multi-domain proteins provide more effective supervision than single-domain proteins in both the homodimer and heterodimer settings. Moreover, a mixed pre-training dataset combining single-domain and multi-domain proteins yields the best overall performance, suggesting that single-domain proteins still capture biologically meaningful patterns that are transferable to real protein complexes. We further examined how different pseudo-dimer construction strategies affect the learned PPI representations and what interaction-related properties are captured through SMP pre-training, as detailed in Supplementary Section S7. Our qualitative analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3) indicates that SMP pre-training enables the model to learn meaningful contact and interface priors. Moreover, for certain proteins, the random-split strategy, by introducing more heterogeneous protein data, further enhances the learning of contact-specific features and improves the modeling of interfacial regions.

SMP improves structure accuracy during docking

Protein-protein docking predicts the bound 3D structure of a protein complex based on the known 3D structures of two individual proteins in their unbound states, which is a crucial and realistic problem in many biological scenarios like drug design32 and the study of disease mechanisms33. This study focuses on the rigid body docking task, a common phenomenon in many biological scenarios, where the conformations during the protein binding process are not considered, as discussed in recent works34,35. To demonstrate the benefit of our method, we incorporate SMP into the popular method EquiDock34 and analyze it on a challenging docking benchmark DIPS-Het36,37. Fig. 4a illustrates the extensive docking results, which show that SMP achieves better performance than current deep learning-based models in terms of Complex and Interface Root Mean Square Deviations (CRMSD and IRMSD), and DockQ metrics. Specifically, compared to the best docking model, EquiDock, the mean and standard deviation of CRMSD for SMP decrease from 12.00 to 10.77 and from 10.18 to 9.21, respectively. Meanwhile, the mean and standard deviation of IRMSD decrease from 10.43 to 9.46 and from 8.72 to 7.87, respectively. In contrast, the mean and standard deviation of DockQ increase from 0.18 to 0.20 and from 0.20 to 0.21, respectively, when compared to EquiDock. These evaluations highlight SMP’s SOTA performance across multiple metrics compared to the existing models, demonstrating that the pseudo-dimer data can provide further structural prior knowledge, and our pre-training strategy is effective for protein complex structure prediction. In addition, Fig. 4a presents a comparison of the success rate (DockQ > 0.23) between SMP and other models. SMP outperforms current methods, achieving the highest success rate at 35%, namely the 5, 13, and 13% improvements over the EquiDock, DiffDock-PP, and GeoDock. We further evaluated the robustness of SMP on the DIPS-Het docking benchmark by conducting five independent runs with different random seeds, as detailed in Supplementary Section S4. SMP learns discriminative protein representations

We assess the ability of SMP to perform the protein-protein interaction (PPI) task. This task intends to find out whether two proteins bind, which is another essential problem for discovering signal transduction pathways38 and new drug targets39. The SMP is integrated with the high-performing method PPITrans28 and evaluated on multiple PPI benchmarks with different features like HIPPIE40 (balanced dataset) and D-SCRIPT41 (unbalanced dataset) to show its advantages.

In detail, Fig. 5a illustrates the interaction prediction results between SMP and other popular methods in the D-SCRIPT test set, indicating that SMP outperforms four previous models and achieves SOTA performance. For example, SMP obtains the best recall of 0.62, the best F1 score of 0.72, and the best AUPR of 0.788, corresponding to an average improvement of 12.7% (p-value = 0.0026) in recall, 5.9% (p-value = 0.0076) in F1 score, and 3.0% (p-value = 0.0083) in AUPR compared with the current strong model, PPITrans. These results indicate that SMP can provide extra interaction prior to knowledge from pseudo-dimers and have good robustness in the unbalanced scenario. Furthermore, analogous results are also exhibited in the HIPPIE test set, as shown in Fig. 5b. Specifically, SMP performs better than the SOTA model PPITrans again, achieving an average improvement of 10.0% (p-value = 0.0138) in recall, 3.0% (p-value = 0.0080) in F1 score, and 1.8% (p-value = 0.0175) in AUPR, reaching the highest recall of 0.77, F1 score of 0.69, and AUPR of 0.728. Discussion

In this study, we have introduced the Split and Merge Proxy (SMP), a simple yet effective pre-training framework designed to enhance protein and protein interactions tasks by leveraging the abundant available data from monomeric proteins. The scarcity of protein complex structure data poses significant challenges for predicting protein and protein interactions. SMP addresses this limitation by utilizing rich monomer information to simulate protein interactions as the pre-training goal. In detail, SMP involves splitting the monomeric proteins into pairwise pseudo-dimers with sequence and structure information, and training the model to merge them back by separately predicting the pseudo-interactions (e.g., contact or docking) for different PPIs tasks. Then the pre-trained model serves as an initialization for the respective task and is subsequently fine-tuned on the real protein dimer data.

Because the pre-training and fine-tuning stages share a consistent goal, SMP achieves remarkable performance gains over other deep learning-based methods that do not exploit monomeric proteins in this manner. Our comprehensive experiments demonstrate that incorporating monomer-derived pseudo-dimers during pre-training substantially benefits performance across various protein interaction tasks. This improvement is evident not only in residue-level tasks, such as protein-protein contact map prediction and protein-protein docking, but also extends to sequence-level tasks, including protein-protein interaction prediction. In addition, SMP can perform better than AF-Multimer and AF3 on certain intertwined protein complexes that pose substantial challenges for existing structure prediction models, indicating the effectiveness of our pre-training strategy and data. SMP can also reduce reliance on real dimer data and adapt well to data-scarce scenarios, benefiting scenarios where real protein complex data is difficult to obtain. The SMP framework not only enhances predictive accuracy but also proves to be a versatile approach applicable to other multimer-related tasks.

Although SMP has achieved impressive performance in various PPIs tasks and scenarios, it still has some limitations and room for improvement: First, our SMP currently mainly focuses on the dimeric proteins, we do not extend it to the multimeric proteins (i.e., three or more proteins), which would limit its applications in real-world scenarios; Second, we now only use monomers from the PDB, which primarily consists of experimentally determined structures, however, the AlphaFoldDB database48, comprising predicted monomeric protein structures generated by AF2, has been released, this database is ten times larger than the PDB, and leveraging this large-scale dataset for pre-training could further enhance the model’s performance; Third, the current SMP paradigm relies on training separate interaction models with different interaction labels generated from pseudo-dimer data, which may not be the most efficient strategy. Developing a unified pre-training framework capable of supporting multiple downstream interaction tasks within a single model may offer a more scalable and efficient solution. Finally, a more systematic investigation of the learned PPIs representations and the specific properties captured through SMP pre-training, while scientifically meaningful and bringing a broad range of interest, remains beyond the primary scope of the present study and warrants deeper exploration.

Task definition

The PPIs prediction task aims to calculate the interaction relationship I between the two given proteins x1 and x2 based on the designed model f. Datasets

In this section, we provide a detailed description of the datasets used in SMP, including our self-constructed pre-training dataset, as well as the datasets used for training, validation, and testing in PPIs tasks.

Pre-training dataset

We use the monomeric proteins from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)9, released before 2018-04-30, to generate the pseudo-dimer data. There are 60,206 PDB files in total, each containing the sequence and structural information of a protein. Monomers that cannot be parsed by Biopython49 are filtered out, as they contain unknown or missing atoms and disordered chain numbers. For consistency, only the primary biological assembly (assembly-1) is retained from each protein structure file, while alternative conformations are excluded6. In addition, we set the split range between 1/3 and 2/3 of the entire chain length so that the cut position is close to the middle of the given sequence to get pseudo-dimers. Too short split proteins, whose length of any chain is less than 20, are dropped. Finally, there are 22,589 pseudo-dimers, each comprising a sequence and its corresponding structural information. Because monomeric and multimeric structures are deposited as distinct PDB entries, the constructed pseudo-dimer dataset has no exact PDB-level overlap with the real multimer test sets. We further examined potential leakage using structure- and sequence-level similarity analyses across all downstream PPI tasks, as detailed in Supplementary Section S3. Only a small fraction of test samples showed high similarity to the pseudo-dimer set, defined as TM-score > 0.5 for structure-based benchmarks or sequence similarity > 0.4 for sequence-based PPI benchmarks (Supplementary Fig. S1). After excluding these potentially similar samples, the evaluation results remained essentially unchanged (Supplementary Tables S3, S4, and S5), supporting that the observed performance gains are not driven by data leakage. These findings suggest that the improvements brought by our pre-training dataset are not driven by sequence or structural similarity, but rather by the additional information introduced through pseudo-dimers.

Given that different interaction tasks have various labels, the pseudo-dimer data should be processed to adapt to distinct PPIs tasks. For the contact map prediction task, we calculate the Euclidean distance between the heavy atoms of the residues on different pseudo-proteins and utilize a threshold of 8Å to cut off the distance to acquire the contact map, yielding 22,589 labels. The protein docking task aims to regress the bound protein complex structure from the two unbound proteins. Therefore, we randomly rotate and translate the coordinates of each pseudo protein to simulate the unbound state as input and regard their original pose as the ground truth pose, obtaining 22,589 targets. In addition, in the protein-protein interaction task, proteins from the same pseudo-dimer interact with each other and are considered positive samples, while those from different pseudo-dimers are regarded as negative samples. We construct two datasets with different positive-to-negative sample ratios (1:1 and 1:10) to accommodate both balanced and imbalanced protein-protein interaction datasets, resulting in 45,302 and 249,161 samples, respectively.

Datasets used in the PPIs tasks

Various PPIs tasks utilize different datasets to evaluate the capability of respective models. Here, we introduce the detailed datasets used in the PPIs tasks.

Datasets in the contact map prediction task

In this task, we conduct the experiments on both the homodimers and heterodimers, utilizing the same datasets as in the previous work, DeepInter8, for training and testing. Specifically, all the homodimers and heterodimers are downloaded from the PDB. MMseqs250,51 is used to cluster the homologous sequences to remove the redundant proteins, while Biopython49 is employed to filter out PDB entries that could not be parsed. This procedure yields a dataset comprising 3,470 homodimers for training, 294 for validation, and 297 for testing, with the test set referred to as Homodimer300. The heterodimers are filtered based on the following principles: (1) the protein complexes only contain two single chains; (2) the X-ray resolution of the experimental protein structure should be better than 3Å; (3) the total length of the complex cannot be too long, which need to less than 1,023 and the length of the single chain should be more than 50; (4) the sequence identity of the two chains needs to be less than the cutoff of 70%; and (5) the PDB entry can be successfully parsed by Biopython49. Then the heterologous sequences are clustered with a sequence identity threshold of 40% to improve protein diversity and reduce bias. The final heterodimers are divided into a training set containing 1881 heterodimers, a validation set including 96 heterodimers, and a test set consisting of 99 heterodimers, with the test set referred to as Heterodimer99.

Dataset in the protein docking task

DIPS-Het is a challenging benchmark for protein docking, derived from prior studies36,37 and curated as a subset of the DIPS dataset52, which includes experimental protein complexes sourced from the PDB. This dataset primarily focuses on heterodimeric proteins, which are prevalent in biological processes such as antibody-antigen interactions and enzyme-inhibitor complexes. In detail, the PDB entries in the DIPS-Het are included according to the following conditions: (1) the proteins should be collected by diffraction-based methods or electron microscopy; (2) the X-ray resolution of the experimental structure should be lower than 3.5Å; (3) the complex should contain less than 5 chains; (4) the complex only belongs to protein-protein type; and (5) the PDB entry can be successfully parsed by Biopython49. In addition, the proteins sharing the sequence similarity of 30% with the DB5.5 dataset53 are removed. Finally, the DIPS-Het dataset includes a training set of 9064 proteins, a validation set of 661 proteins, and a test set of 1050 proteins. Following previous works34,36, we randomly sample 100 protein complexes from the entire test set to ensure both computational feasibility and representativeness for evaluation.

Datasets in the protein-protein interaction task

Two datasets from the previous works40,41, HIPPIE and D-SCRIPT, are used to train and evaluate the protein-protein interaction task. The D-SCRIPT is an unbalanced dataset with 1:10 positive-to-negative samples. The interaction proteins, as the positive cases, are downloaded from the STRING dataset54,55. The length of each protein is between 50 and 800 amino acids. Moreover, highly redundant sequences with a sequence identity more than 40% are removed. In the end, the interaction proteins are split into a training set of 38,344 positives and a test set of 4794 positive pairs. The negative samples are generated from the positive set through the 1:10 ratio, leading to 383,448 negatives in the training set and 47,931 negative cases in the test set. The HIPPIE is a balanced dataset, with positive protein samples taken directly from HIPPIEv2.356 and negative samples randomly generated from the positive set. To prevent potential information leakage, the training, validation, and test sets are completely non-overlapping, and the sequence similarity between these three sets is as minimal as possible. The CD-HIT tool57,58 is applied to remove the redundant proteins using a cutoff of 40%. As a result, there are 35,096 positives and 33,562 negatives in the training set, 20,062 positives and 19,389 negatives in the validation set, and 17,898 positives and 17,598 negatives in the test set.

Proteins from the CASP15

To guarantee the fair assessment and avoid potential data leakage, we sample the protein complexes that are released after 2023-05-01 from the CASP15, and the length of any chain cannot exceed 1024 amino acids, resulting in 8 multimers: T1113, T1132, H1151, T1176, T1178, T1179, and H1185, T1173, corresponding to PDB entries 7UYX59, 8ECX60, 8D5V61, 8SMQ46, 8UFN62, 8TN863, 8OUY64, and 8ON465, respectively. For evaluation, we use the A and B chains of each PDB target to form dimers.

SMP details

Model details

The Split and Merge Proxy (SMP) is an effective pre-training task designed for interaction prediction tasks (e.g., contact or docking). The overall pipeline includes a split stage and a merge stage, as illustrated in Fig. 1a, d. In the split stage, each monomer is partitioned into two subunits to construct a pseudo-dimer, comprising a pseudo-receptor and a pseudo-ligand, that contains sequence and structural information. In the merge stage, the model is trained directly on the generated pseudo-dimer data to learn the target interaction prediction task, without any modification to the model architecture. Finally, the pre-trained model is fine-tuned on real dimer data.

Split stage We utilize monomers from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)9, which provides both amino acid sequences \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s\in {{\mathbb{P}}}^{L}$$\end{document}s∈PL and corresponding 3D coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{L\times 3}$$\end{document}c∈RL×3. The objective of the split stage is to construct pseudo-dimers that share the same data structure as real dimers, consisting of two sub-sequences (s1 and s2) along with their associated structural information (c1 and c2). Specifically, we first partition the amino acid sequence into two sub-sequences: 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{1}=s[:l],{s}_{2}=s[l:],$$\end{document}s1=s[:l],s2=s[l:], where l denotes the split index. This index can be determined either by a domain segmentation tool or by randomly sampling in a position range around the center of the monomer sequence. For the 3D structure, we apply an analogous split operation: 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{1}=c[:l],{c}_{2}=c[l:].$$\end{document}c1=c[:l],c2=c[l:].

Merge stage

This stage essentially mimics the standard learning paradigm of interaction prediction. We employ task-specific, high-performing models, keeping their original network architectures, and pre-train them individually on our generated pseudo-dimer data. The model architectures are described below.

Protein-protein contact map prediction task

We adopt the DeepInter architecture8 for the contact map prediction task. The model integrates multiple input representations, including evolutionary features derived from single and paired multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) generated by HHblits66 and CCMPred67, sequence embeddings extracted from the ESM-MSA-1b model68, and structural priors encoded as intra-protein distance maps, with stacked ResNet-Inception8 and triangle-aware transformer8 blocks to capture both short- and long-range information. Finally, the model is pre-trained as a binary classification task using the focal loss function8,69 to address class imbalance in contact map data.

Protein-protein docking task

We adopt the Equidock34 framework, the first deep learning-based model for the protein docking task. The model comprises a series of SE(3)-equivariant graph networks 70 to capture the rotation and translation of the unbound protein complex to determine the final structure. The whole framework is pre-trained using a mean squared error (MSE) loss34 to regress the predicted atomic coordinates to the ground-truth structures. In addition, a pocket loss34 based on optimal transport is incorporated to predict the binding pocket on the interaction interface, as well as an intersection loss34 to prevent atomic collisions.

Protein-protein interaction task

We adopt the PPITrans28 architecture, which contains extracted sequence features using ProtT571, multiple transformer blocks72, and a classifier with multiple multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) to predict the interaction between two proteins. The model is pre-trained through the standard cross-entropy loss function28.

Fine-tuning stage

After pre-training on the pseudo-dimer dataset, each task-specific model is initialized with the corresponding pre-trained weights. As the pre-training and fine-tuning stages share the same task formulation, no task gap is introduced. The models are then fine-tuned end-to-end on real dimer data.

Implementation details

All the models are trained using the PyTorch framework73. The experiments of protein-protein contact map prediction and protein-protein docking are performed on the A100 GPU with 80GB memory, and the experiments of protein-protein interaction are performed on the GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 24GB memory. The MSAs generated by HHblits in the contact map prediction task are based on the UniRef30_2021_0351 database74. The implementation details for these three downstream tasks are provided in Supplementary Section S6, with the corresponding hyper-parameters listed in Supplementary Tables S7, S8, and S9. For the AlphaFold3 and Chai-1 inference, we utilize their respective online servers: AlphaFold3 Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) and Chai-1 Server (https://lab.chaidiscovery.com). AlphaFold-Multimer inference is conducted locally on an NVIDIA A100-80GB GPU with the v2.3.0 model weights, using the default databases (BFD4, MGnify75 (v2022_05), PDB7076 (cutoff: 1 November 2021), PDB_mmCIF77 (cutoff: 1 November 2021), PDB_seqres77 (cutoff: 1 November 2021), UniRef3074 (v2022_02), UniProt78 (v2020_05), and UniRef9079 (v2020_01)) for MSA and template searches, and enabling AMBER relaxation for structure refinement. All structure prediction models are inferred five times, yielding five structure predictions for each sample. Subsequently, the top-ranked (rank-1) structures from AlphaFold3, Chai-1, and AlphaFold-Multimer are selected for further analysis.

Metrics used in protein-protein interaction task

This task is a binary classification problem. We follow the same evaluation criteria as prior studies28,41, including precision, recall, F1-score, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) metrics.
