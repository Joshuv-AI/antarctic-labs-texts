A community‑based study of platelet‑to‑white blood cell ratio and new‑onset type 2 diabetes in older adults
Authors: Huan Sheng, Junyang Xu, Zhaoxiang Wang, Ying Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe platelet-to-white blood cell ratio (PWR) is a potential hematological indicator of dynamic changes in chronic inflammation. We aimed to investigate the association between the PWR and the risk of new-onset type 2 diabetes (T2D) in older adults.MethodsThis retrospective community-based cohort study included 7571 community-dwelling older adults without diabetes from Kunshan, Jiangsu, China, who underwent health check-ups between January 2018 and December 2023. The PWR was calculated as the platelet count divided by the white blood cell count. The primary outcome was new-onset T2D. Associations were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models to compute hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsDuring follow-up, 773 incident T2D cases occurred, and higher baseline PWR was significantly associated with a lower risk of T2D. Participants in the highest PWR quartile had a 24.0% lower risk (HR 0.760, 95% CI 0.615–0.940, P = 0.011) compared to the lowest quartile. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed an approximately linear, inverse dose-response relationship (P overall < 0.001, P non-linear = 0.282). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of this association.ConclusionsA higher baseline PWR is associated with a reduced risk of new-onset T2D in older adults, suggesting its potential as a simple, cost-effective inflammatory biomarker for T2D risk assessment in this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02258-2.Keywords: Platelet-white cell ratio; Type 2 diabetes, Community elderly population; Inflammation; Cohort study

Introduction

As a major chronic non-communicable disease, diabetes mellitus (DM) substantially contributes to the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [1]. Diabetes prevalence in China is the highest worldwide [2]. Data from the International Diabetes Federation indicate that, in 2021, approximately 536.6 million individuals globally had diabetes, with China accounting for about 140.9 million of these cases [3]. The global number is projected to increase to 783.2 million by 2045, underscoring the continuously rising burden [3]. Consequently, the identification of risk factors is critical for developing preventive and targeted intervention strategies aimed at mitigating the diabetes burden.

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) involves chronic low-grade inflammation driven by adipose tissue dysfunction, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance, which collectively stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokine production [4]. This inflammatory environment impairs β-cell function and exacerbates insulin resistance, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that worsens metabolic dysregulation [5–8]. In recent years, the platelet-to-white blood cell ratio (PWR) has been proposed as a novel hematological indicator for assessing dynamic inflammatory changes [9–13]. Since platelets and leukocytes interact closely during inflammation and tissue repair, their count variations can mirror the resolution status of inflammation [9]. Typically, during recovery, leukocyte counts decline while platelet levels return to or exceed normal values [9]. PWR integrates these bidirectional changes into a single quantitative measure, enabling a more accurate representation of systemic inflammation. Notably, hematological profiles of T2D patients typically show altered white blood cell and platelet counts [14, 15]. Previous studies also demonstrate that the PWR serves as a significant biomarker for chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disorders, and mortality risk, outperforming traditional inflammatory indices [9, 11–13, 16–18].

Compared to younger individuals, elderly adults show a higher incidence of T2D and more difficulty in glycemic control, possibly due to coexisting CVDs, chronic renal impairment, and cognitive decline [19, 20]. Cross-sectional studies have indicated that higher PWR values are significantly associated with a lower risk of diabetes [21]. However, the longitudinal relationship between baseline PWR levels and incident diabetes remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate this association using large-scale retrospective cohort data from community-dwelling older adults in China.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

This retrospective population-based cohort study utilized data from electronic health records (EHR) pertaining to the community-dwelling elderly population in Kunshan, Jiangsu, China. Through the medical consortium’s healthcare information management system, residents’ health records, routine annual examination results, and chronic disease follow-up information were comprehensively gathered. Detailed information on the cohort design has been reported in earlier publications [22, 23]. Approval of the research protocol was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kunshan First People’s Hospital (Grant No: 2023-03-014-H01-K01). Written informed consent was collected from all participants to use their comprehensive EHR data.

The study included community-dwelling elderly residents who underwent health check-ups in Kunshan between January 2018 and December 2023. Exclusion criteria were applied to those who met any of the following: (i) aged less than 60 years at baseline, (ii) diagnosed with diabetes at baseline, (iii) lack of follow-up and covariates information, or (iv) insufficient PWR data at both baselines. This study ultimately comprised 7571 individuals without diabetes at baseline.

Calculation of PWR and outcome definition

PWR was calculated as the ratio of platelet counts (10^9/L) to white blood cell counts (10^9/L). In the absence of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and repeat glucose measurements in the community screening database, incident T2D was identified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes (E11-E14) or a fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. The time from baseline assessment to the date of diagnosis was defined as the onset time. Participants who remained free of T2D were followed from baseline to their last survey date. The median follow-up duration in our study population was 3.88 years.

Data collection

The dataset employed in this research were originated from annual health assessments of residents living in the community, which were systematically compiled through the EHR platform. The database included demographic characteristics such as age and sex, as well as lifestyle information, including smoking and alcohol use. Smoking status was classified as never (< 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), former (≥ 100 cigarettes but none in the past 30 days), or current (≥ 100 cigarettes and smoking within the past 30 days). Alcohol consumption was categorized as never, infrequently (< 20 g/day for women, < 40 g/day for men), regularly (20–<40 g/day for women, 40–<60 g/day for men), or daily (≥ 40 g/day for women, ≥ 60 g/day for men, or ≥ 1 monthly binge episode [≥ 60 g per occasion], or alcohol-related harm). The database also contained medical history information regarding chronic conditions, including diabetes, hypertension (HTN), and CVDs. Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were used to calculate the body mass index (BMI), using the formula: BMI=weight (kg) / height (m)². Participants were classified as normal weight (BMI < 24 kg/m²), overweight (24 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m²), or obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²). Biochemical parameters were measured from fasting venous blood samples collected after an 8-10-hour overnight fast. The tests included FPG, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), serum creatinine (Scr), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) or as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. The Kruskal‑Wallis test was used for continuous variable comparisons, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing assessed diabetes incidence among PWR quartiles. The association between PWR and diabetes risk was examined using Cox proportional hazards regression, yielding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Model 1 included no covariate adjustment; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, smoking, and drinking habits; Model 3 further accounted for HTN, CVDs, BMI, ALT, AST, TG, TC, Scr, and eGFR. The potential nonlinear association was modeled using restricted cubic spline Cox regression, with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the PWR distribution, using the median value as the reference. Subgroup analyses were also performed. To compare the predictive performance of PWR with platelet count and white blood cell (WBC) count for incident T2D over time, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. All statistical analyses were performed with R 4.2.2 software, adopting a two-sided significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this study, 7571 community-dwelling older adults without diabetes at baseline were included and categorized into four quartiles (Q1-Q4) according to baseline PWR levels (Table 1). The mean age of the overall population was 66.96 ± 4.48 years, and age decreased progressively across increasing PWR quartiles (P < 0.001). Pronounced differences in sex distribution were observed, with a higher proportion of men in the lower PWR groups (P < 0.001). Regarding lifestyle factors, higher PWR levels were associated with a greater proportion of never smokers and never drinkers, accompanied by a gradual decline in the proportions of current smokers and daily drinkers (P < 0.001). In terms of comorbidities, the prevalence of HTN and CVDs decreased with rising PWR (P < 0.01). For anthropometric and metabolic parameters, BMI, FPG and liver enzymes (ALT and AST) were lower in the highest PWR quartile (P < 0.001). With respect to lipid profiles, increasing PWR was associated with higher TC, LDL-c, and HDL-c levels, along with a reduction in TG (P < 0.001). Renal function indices indicated lower Scr and higher eGFR in individuals with higher PWR (P < 0.001). Consistently, during follow-up, the incidence of new-onset T2D declined steadily from 12.36% to 8.24% across ascending PWR quartiles (P < 0.001).

Baseline PWR as a predictor of incident T2D

Kaplan-Meier curves depict the cumulative incidence of new‑onset T2D across baseline PWR quartiles (Q1-Q4) (Fig. 1). Overall, the cumulative incidence of T2D increased progressively with longer follow‑up in all quartiles; participants in the higher PWR quartiles consistently exhibited a lower cumulative incidence compared with those in the lower quartiles (P for log‑rank < 0.001).

Cumulative incidence of new‑onset T2D across PWR quartiles. [Kaplan-Meier curves of new‑onset T2D by baseline PWR quartiles (Q1-Q4); log‑rank test P < 0.001]

Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to quantify the association between baseline PWR and incident T2D (Table 2). No significant violation of the proportional hazards’ assumption was detected (P = 0.596). When entered as a continuous variable, a 1‑standard deviation (SD) increment in PWR was linked to a significantly reduced T2D risk in the unadjusted analysis (Model 1: HR 0.845, 95% CI 0.776–0.920; P < 0.001). Adjustment for demographic and lifestyle factors (age, sex, smoking, alcohol use) produced similar results (Model 2: HR 0.829, 95% CI 0.759–0.904; P < 0.001). After further adjustment for BMI, HTN, CVDs, liver enzymes (ALT, AST), lipid parameters (TG, TC), and renal function indices (Scr, eGFR), the association weakened but remained statistically significant (Model 3: HR 0.907, 95% CI 0.831–0.991; P = 0.030). When PWR was categorized into quartiles, individuals in Q4 had a substantially lower risk of T2D compared with those in Q1 across all models (Model 1: HR 0.655, 95% CI 0.535–0.802; P < 0.001; Model 2: HR 0.624, 95% CI 0.507–0.769; P < 0.001; Model 3: HR 0.760, 95% CI 0.615–0.940; P = 0.011), and there was a statistically significant trend of decreasing risk across increasing quartiles (all P trend ≤ 0.01). Restricted cubic spline models based on Model 3 (Fig. 2) further illustrated a steadily declining hazard of T2D with rising PWR, with a strong overall effect (P overall < 0.001) and no indication of nonlinearity (P non-linear = 0.282). These findings support an approximately linear, inverse relationship between baseline PWR and subsequent T2D risk.

Subgroup analysis

We evaluated the robustness of the PWR-T2D association by conducting subgroup analyses according to key clinical characteristics, such as age (< 70/≥70 years), sex (female vs. male), smoking status (never, former, and current), alcohol intake (never, infrequently, regularly, and daily), BMI categories (< 24/24–28/≥28 kg/m2), baseline FPG (< 5.6/≥5.6 mmol/L), HTN (yes vs. no), CVDs (yes vs. no), and eGFR (< 60/≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2) (Fig. 3). In the multivariable-adjusted Cox models, a 1‑SD increase in PWR was generally associated with a lower incidence of T2D across the above subgroups, and no significant interaction was detected (P for interaction > 0.05).

Subgroup analyses for the association between PWR and risk of T2D. [Forest plot of hazard ratios (per 1‑SD higher PWR) with 95% CIs in predefined subgroups; P values for interaction are shown on the right]

Sensitivity and ROC analysis

Sensitivity analyses were also performed to evaluate the robustness of our findings. To assess unmeasured confounding, we calculated the E-value (1.967 for the observed HR of 0.760 and 1.324 for the upper confidence limit), indicating moderate robustness to unmeasured confounding. (1) Platelet counts within 150–400 10^9 and white cell counts within 4.0–10.0 10^9 were used to calculate the PWR, after which we examined the association between PWR and incident T2D risk (Supplementary Material: Tables S1). (2) For PWR, observations with an absolute z‑score greater than 3 (|z|>3) were defined as outliers and excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Material: Tables S2). The results of all these analyses were consistent with the primary outcomes. Based on the time-dependent ROC curve analysis, PWR demonstrated a relatively better discriminative ability than platelet count or WBC count alone across all time points (1, 3, 5 years), although the overall predictive performance of all three markers remained modest (Supplementary Material: Figure S1).

Discussion

In a large-scale cohort of community-dwelling elderly individuals, we examined the prospective association between baseline PWR levels and T2D incidence. Multivariable-adjusted analyses indicated a significant inverse relationship between baseline PWR and the risk of developing T2D. The restricted cubic spline approach demonstrated an approximately linear association. The robustness of this association persisted in stratified analyses across subgroups.

The PWR is calculated from parameters routinely obtained in a standard complete blood count, without incurring additional testing costs or requiring specialized laboratory equipment. Owing to this simplicity, PWR is suitable for widespread application across different levels of healthcare delivery, including resource-limited environments. As a hematologic biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and immune status, PWR has been shown to be significantly associated with inflammatory activity, disease severity, and clinical outcomes in a variety of conditions, such as CVDs, stroke, and malignancies [9, 13, 18]. Previous large-scale cross-sectional epidemiological studies have demonstrated an inverse association between PWR and diabetes susceptibility [21]. Our findings extend this evidence by providing longitudinal data from a community-based elderly cohort, thereby offering stronger support for causality compared to cross-sectional designs. While Liu et al. utilized national survey data to examine prevalent diabetes, our study prospectively assessed incident T2D with comprehensive adjustment for confounders, including metabolic and renal parameters [21]. From a pathophysiological perspective, platelets serve dual functions as essential components in hemostasis and significant participants in inflammatory responses [24, 25]. Experimental evidence indicates that platelets modulate inflammatory processes through the release of various mediators (including P-selectin, CD40 ligand, and platelet factor 4) that facilitate interactions with leukocytes [26–28]. In diabetic conditions, platelet functionality undergoes alterations characterized by enhanced activation and increased pro-inflammatory properties [29, 30]. On the other hand, leukocytes function as central effectors in inflammatory cascades. Multiple studies have documented significantly elevated peripheral white blood cell counts in diabetic patients, with worsening glycemic control correlating with progressively increasing leukocyte levels [31–33]. As a composite indicator reflecting platelet-leukocyte interactions, PWR possesses substantial clinical significance. Reduced PWR values indicate unfavorable inflammatory-immune homeostasis, while moderately elevated PWR reflects a more favorable systemic physiological state. PWR’s relevance as an inflammatory marker in metabolic disorders arises from platelet-leukocyte interactions in chronic inflammation. Platelets release mediators that promote leukocyte activation, indicating systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [27, 28]. Compared to established markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) or neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), PWR reflects the combined dynamics of platelets and leukocytes and may provide additional insights into systemic inflammatory status [13]. Previous studies have suggested that PWR may outperform other complete blood count-derived markers (e.g., NLR, PLR, and RDW) in predicting cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events [9]. However, as a preliminary exploratory analysis, our study did not include direct comparative analyses. Therefore, PWR should be interpreted as a complementary indicator rather than a superior alternative [34, 37]. Moreover, elderly populations commonly manifest “inflammaging” - a phenomenon characterized by chronic low-grade inflammatory states associated with aging [38–39]. Inflammaging is often accompanied by alterations in both platelet function and white blood cell counts, contributing to increased vulnerability to metabolic disorders, cardiovascular events, and other age-related diseases [40, 41]. In this context, PWR may serve as a convenient integrative marker to capture age-related changes in inflammatory and immune status.

Our findings should be interpreted with several limitations. Given the observational design of this study, the findings indicate an association rather than a causal relationship. Our study was conducted in a single city (Kunshan), which may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, the study is based on an ongoing community-based longitudinal cohort (KARE) with standardized follow-up, the participants mainly represent an urban population [22]. Additionally, as this study relied on retrospective EHR data, selection bias cannot be excluded; however, missingness largely arose from incomplete examination records and likely approximates a random mechanism, potentially mitigating bias. Subgroup analyses were conducted without adjustment for multiplicity, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory. PWR was assessed only at baseline and may not capture its temporal variability over the follow-up period. Inflammatory markers can also fluctuate due to infections, medication use (such as aspirin, statins, and antidiabetic drugs) and lifestyle factors (e.g., diet and physical activity), attenuating the true association between PWR and incident T2D.

Conclusion

The PWR represents a cost-effective and readily accessible biomarker for monitoring inflammatory dynamics, demonstrating unique clinical utility in diabetes risk assessment.
