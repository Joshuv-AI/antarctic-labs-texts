Impact of apparent temperature on specific cardiovascular disease admissions: a time-series study and association modification in Xiangyang, China
Authors: Changyu Ju, Yili Zhang, Chunrong Huang, Hongjia Liu, Juming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effects of temperature variations on different types of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and the moderating roles of sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, and residential area remain poorly understood.MethodUsing a retrospective time-series design, this study analyzed hospital admission records from 2018 to 2022, including 70,108 CVD patients in Xiangyang city, China. Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM) were applied to examine the association of apparent temperature (AT) with hospital admissions for specific CVD types. Gender, age, and residential location were considered as potential moderators, with adjustments for confounders like air pollution and seasonal variations.ResultsTemperature extremes significantly influence CVD admissions. Hot effect were associated with a 16.80% increase in ischemic heart disease (IHD) admissions, while other CVD types showed minimal change. Cold effect had more pronounced and sustained effects, with a 15.52% increase in overall CVD admissions and significant rises in hypertensive diseases (27.16%), pulmonary heart disease (177.45%), and IHD (16.77%). Gender, age, and rural residence significantly modified the temperature-CVD relationship, with women, the elderly, and rural residents at heightened risk during extreme temperatures.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a significant association between temperature extremes and CVD admissions, with high temperatures notably increasing the risk of IHD, particularly among women. Cold temperatures exert a more prolonged effect, leading to higher rates of IHD, hypertensive diseases, and pulmonary heart disease. Vulnerable groups, including the elderly, women, and rural residents, are most affected. These findings underscore the necessity for targeted public health strategies to mitigate the health impacts of extreme temperatures on these at-risk populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26018-4.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) ranks among the leading causes of mortality globally, with its burden steadily increasing. From 1990 to 2019, there was a substantial rise in the global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and years of life lost due to CVD, with DALYs doubling during this period. Ischemic heart disease (IHD), in particular, was responsible for 182 million DALYs and 9.14 million deaths in 2019, with the number of cases reaching 197 million [1]. In China, CVD accounted for 46.74% of all deaths in rural areas and 44.26% in urban areas in 2019, with two out of every five deaths attributed to CVDs [2]. Consequently, the high incidence and mortality rates associated with CVD underscore its significance as a critical focus in global public health.

Simultaneously, extreme temperatures driven by climate change have emerged as a critical global challenge, posing significant threats to public health. Recent studies indicate that exposure to non-optimal temperatures ranks among the top ten leading causes of mortality worldwide [3]. Both heat and cold stress are strongly associated with a range of adverse health outcomes, including heatstroke, respiratory illnesses, and mental health disorders [4, 5]. Of particular concern, the incidence and mortality rates of CVD are markedly elevated under extreme temperature conditions. Exposure to such temperature extremes can precipitate acute cardiovascular events, with effects that may persist for months or even years [6, 7]. Evidence suggests that excessive heat may induce dehydration and electrolyte imbalances, thereby increasing cardiac workload, while severe cold can provoke vasoconstriction and elevate blood pressure, collectively heightening the risk of cardiovascular episodes [8, 9]. However, the relationship between temperature and CVD is influenced by factors such as gender, age, and specific disease subtypes. For instance, a cohort study conducted in Australia demonstrated that the incidence of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) among males was significantly greater in cold environments relative to hot or moderate conditions, and a pronounced interaction between male sex and exposure to extreme cold was observed [10]. Similarly, a five-year analysis of cardiovascular hospital admissions in Beijing revealed that extreme heat was associated with a substantial increase in CVD admissions, particularly as temperatures escalated from moderate to severe levels. In contrast, cold extremes did not significantly affect CVD admissions, highlighting differential impacts of thermal extremes on cardiovascular health [11]. In addition, data from 567 cities across 27 countries indicate that both heatwaves and cold spells are linked to excess cardiovascular mortality, especially resulting from heart failure and IHD [12].

Despite increasing research into the associations between temperature and CVD, available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. As climate change accelerates the frequency and intensity of extreme temperatures, there is growing concern regarding exacerbated CVD risk and acute surges in hospitalizations, particularly within resource-constrained populations [13, 14]. To address these research gaps, we employed apparent temperature (AT) as the primary exposure metric. By integrating air temperature, humidity, and wind speed, AT provides a more comprehensive representation of perceived thermal stress compared to ambient temperature or conventional heat indices, making it suitable for evaluating the effects of heatwaves and cold spells on vulnerable populations [15]. We employed stratified analyses to elucidate the associations of AT with various CVD subtypes, and further investigated the modifying effects of gender, age, and residential area. Specifically, this study assessed differential risks of hypertensive diseases, IHD, pulmonary heart disease (PHD), arrhythmias, heart failure, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke across a range of temperature exposures. Potential confounders, including air pollution, were adjusted for in our models. We utilized a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) to characterize the lagged relationships between temperature and CVD hospitalization rates. Our findings may inform regions with similar climatic and sociodemographic contexts in China, providing an evidence base for strategies to mitigate the climate-related cardiovascular burden.

Method and materials

Study design

his study employed a time-series design to assess the relationship between ambient temperature exposure and daily hospital admissions for cardiovascular diseases in Xiangyang, China. We collected daily data on meteorological factors and CVD hospitalizations from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022.

Research area

Xiangyang is a central city in the Han River basin of China, located between 110°45′ and 113°06′ east longitude and 31°13′ to 32°37′ north latitude. It covers a total area of approximately 19,700 square kilometers and has a population exceeding 5 million [16]. The city experiences a north subtropical monsoon climate, characterized by distinct seasons with cold winters and hot summers. The annual average temperature ranges from 15.2 °C to 16.0 °C. The coldest month is January, with an average temperature of approximately 3 °C, while the hottest month is July, with an average temperature of about 27.4 °C. The significant temperature fluctuations and extreme weather events in this region may have a considerable impact on the incidence of CVD.

Sources of information

The hospitalization data for this study were obtained from Xiangyang Central Hospital, the largest public hospital in Xiangyang and a teaching hospital affiliated with Hubei University of Arts and Science, which provides over 2 million medical services annually. Using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, a total of 70,108 residents of Xiangyang with CVD were included in the study. These records included the following conditions: hypertensive disease (ICD-10 codes I10-I15), IHD (ICD-10 codes I20-I25), PHD (ICD-10 codes I26-I28), arrhythmias (ICD-10 codes I44-I49), heart failure (ICD-10 code I50), hemorrhagic stroke (ICD-10 codes I60-I61), and ischemic stroke (ICD-10 code I63) [16]. Patients were classified by their primary diagnosis at admission. CVD-related hospital admissions were recorded daily based on admission dates. In this study, no other identifiable personal information was retrieved or analyzed, apart from data on admission date, diagnosis codes, home address, gender, and age.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data were sourced from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (http://data.cma.cn), including daily variables such as maximum temperature (Tmax), minimum temperature (Tmin), average wind speed (WS), dry-bulb temperature (Ta), and relative humidity (RH). Missing values in the raw data were interpolated using a proximity-based interpolation method. We selected AT as the primary exposure variable of interest because it accounts for the effects of RH and vapor pressure (hPa) in addition to temperature, thereby better capturing the physiological “felt” exposure. The air pollution index (API) serves as a critical indicator of ambient air quality, derived from the concentrations of sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and particulate matter with a diameter of 10 micrometers or less (PM10). To explore the role of air pollution as a modifier in the AT-CVD relationship, we used the API as a proxy for air pollution [13]. The calculation of the API follows the guidelines established by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the European Centre for Environment and Health. API measurements are collected from various monitoring stations within each city’s designated territory, with a lower API value indicating better air quality [17]. The API data used in this study were obtained from the Ministry of Environmental Protection of the People’s Republic of China.

Statistical analyses

Previous studies have established the non-linear relationship between AT and CVD as well as the lagged associations of AT on CVD incidence. To assess the non-linear exposure-response relationship between AT and CVD, and its lagged effects, we employed DLNM. This approach is better suited than traditional linear models for capturing the complex, temporal associations between temperature and CVD hospitalizations, as it can account for both lag effects and the non-linear exposure-response relationship [18]. We used negative binomial regression within the DLNM framework because CVD hospitalization data typically exhibit overdispersion, where the variance exceeds that predicted by a Poisson distribution. The negative binomial regression model effectively addresses this issue and provides more robust estimates [19–21]. The following formula was used for the model:

The degrees of freedom (df) for this time spline were set to 7 per year for the 5-year study period [13]. APIt is the air pollution index on day t; Season represents the seasonal trend; Holidayt and DOWt are dummy variables representing the effect of public holidays and day of the week, respectively. We adopted CVD-specific optimum temperature as the reference value, which was determined by comparing the relative risks (RR) of temperatures at 3-day lag, and the temperature with the lowest RR was set as the optimum temperature. To determine the optimal degrees of freedom for the exposure-response and lag-response functions, we performed a systematic search over a grid of possible values. Specifically, we considered natural cubic spline functions with degrees of freedom ranging from 3 to 6 for both dimensions. The goal was to identify the combination of degrees of freedom that minimized the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), a measure of model fit that balances goodness-of-fit and model complexity. Ultimately, we explored various combinations of degrees of freedom and found that using 4 degrees of freedom for the exposure-response dimension and 3 degrees of freedom for the lag-response dimension provided the best model fit (Table S1). A lag period of 25 days was motivated by previous studies and is considered as the short-term effect of AT on CVD [22].

We utilized a lag-stratified natural cubic spline model to investigate the non-linear and delayed associations between temperature and hospitalization risk. Specifically, we calculated the percentage change in CVD hospital admissions corresponding to the 1st to 10th percentiles of daily mean AT (cold effect) and the 99th to 90th percentiles (hot effect). To identify subpopulations that are more vulnerable to the effects of cold and heat, we conducted stratified analyses based on gender, age group, and residential location.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was performed by adjusting the df for the long-term trend and the API. For the time trend, df was set at either 6 or 8 per year, while for the API, df was set at 2 or 4. These choices were made to balance model complexity with fit, ensuring that seasonal variations and long-term trends were accurately captured, while also preventing overfitting. For the API, degrees of freedom of 2 and 4 were selected to allow sufficient flexibility in modeling the impact of air pollution on cardiovascular diseases, while maintaining simplicity and interpretability. In addition, because quasi-Poisson regression models are commonly used in similar studies, we conducted a further sensitivity analysis to compare the two models. We chose a negative binomial model over a quasi-Poisson model, as the AIC more effectively distinguished between candidate models. The negative binomial model also provides a more flexible relationship between variance and mean, making it more suitable for our data.

Results

Population characteristics and meteorological data

The study analyzed hospitalization data from 2018 to 2022 for a total of 70,108 CVD cases. IHD was the most prevalent condition, accounting for 39.42% of the cohort, followed by ischemic stroke at 27.64%. Other significant conditions included hypertensive disease (9.42%) and heart rhythm disorders (9.83%). The majority of the patients were urban residents (58.87%) and male (58.80%). In terms of age distribution, a slight majority of the patients were over 65 years old (52.33%) (Table 1). The average daily admissions were 38.39 ± 13.52. Weather data during the study period recorded AT ranging from − 9.88 °C to 39.73 °C, with a mean of 16.33 °C. The average ambient temperature was 16.89 °C. Relative humidity averaged 66.09%, while wind speeds averaged 1.96 m/s. Rainfall was minimal, with an average of 2.51 mm (Table 2).

AT-related risks and lagged associations on CVD hospital admissions

The contour plot (Fig. 1) shows the relationship between mean AT and the risk of CVD hospital admissions across a 25-day lag period. Under high-AT conditions, the risk of total CVD admissions peaks on day 0 (lag 0) and gradually decreases over time. Both IHD and ischemic stroke also exhibit elevated admission risks during the initial days of high AT, with the risk decreasing thereafter. For low AT, the total CVD risk rises significantly under extremely cold conditions (below approximately 0 °C), particularly during the first 0 to 5 days, highlighting a high sensitivity to cold exposure. For IHD, the risk increases at AT around 10 °C, especially during the 0 to 10-day lag period, with peak risk occurring early in the lag. Arrhythmias and heart failure maintained relatively low-risk levels throughout the 25-day lag period. The responses of hemorrhagic stroke and ischemic stroke to cold AT differed: the risk of hemorrhagic stroke increased significantly within the first 0 to 5 days of lag at extremely low AT, whereas ischemic stroke risk rose under moderately low AT (around 10 °C) during the 5 to 15-day lag period.

Impact of cold and hot effects on CVD hospitalization risks

Under cold effect conditions, the cumulative RR for total CVD gradually increased with lag and peaked at lag 14 days (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.02–1.25). For hypertensive disease, a slight increase was observed, but the peak at lag 14 days (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.87–1.43) was not statistically significant. IHD showed a more pronounced association, with the RR increasing to 1.17 (95% CI: 1.02–1.34) at lag 14 days. By contrast, PHD, arrhythmias, heart failure, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke showed minimal or no significant changes in risk under cold exposure. Under heat exposure, the risk of total CVD remained relatively stable across lag days. At lag 3 days, the RR was 1.01 (95% CI: 0.96–1.06), indicating no significant association. Hypertensive disease risk decreased under high AT, with an RR of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.74–0.96) at lag 0–3 days (Table S2). For other cardiovascular outcomes, including PHD, arrhythmias, heart failure, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke, the associations with hot effect were minimal and non-significant (Fig. 2). Given the patterns observed in the curves, we further quantified the cold and hot effects. At the 1st percentile AT (–5.10 °C) compared to the 10th percentile (0.60 °C), the risk of total CVD hospitalization increased by 15.52% (95% CI: 9.11%–22.30%). For hot effect, comparing the 99th percentile (36.73 °C) to the 90th percentile (31.88 °C) suggested a 3.79% increase in risk, although this was not statistically significant (95% CI: − 6.63% to 15.38%). For IHD, both cold and hot effects showed significant associations. At the 1st versus 10th percentile AT, the risk of IHD hospitalization increased by 16.77% (95% CI: 8.28%–25.93%). At the 99th versus 90th percentile AT, the risk increased by 16.80% (95% CI: 0.94%–35.15%) (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

Cold effect had the most pronounced impact on total CVD among women, the elderly, and rural residents. Specifically, at a 14-day lag, the cumulative RR for women increased to 1.17 (95% CI: 1.03–1.33), for the elderly to 1.20 (95% CI: 1.06–1.35), and for rural residents to 1.21 (95% CI: 1.07–1.38). For IHD, the associations were also more significant in women and the elderly, with the cumulative RR at a 14-day lag reaching 1.40 (95% CI: 1.15–1.70) for women and 1.31 (95% CI: 1.10–1.56) for the elderly. Rural residents experienced an increased risk at a 21-day lag (RR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.03–1.67). While variations in risk were observed for arrhythmias, heart failure, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke across different subgroups, the confidence intervals included 1, indicating a lack of statistical significance(Fig. 3).

In contrast, Hot effect had less pronounced effects on CVD risk. The risk of IHD in women increased significantly at a 3-day lag (RR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.04-1.28), while the risk of heart failure among individuals aged 65 and younger slightly increased at a 21-day lag (RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.03-1.48). The impact of high AT on other cardiovascular conditions, including total CVD, arrhythmias, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke, was minimal, with variations in relative risk failing to reach statistical significance across most subgroups. This suggests that high AT have a moderate or non-significant association with these diseases(Figure 4, Table S3-S10).

Sensitivity analyses

In this study, we performed sensitivity analyses by varying the df for the long-term trend and the API. The results showed that whether df (time) was set at 6 or 8 per year and df (API) at 2 or 4, the associations between cold and heat exposure and CVD hospitalization risk remained robust and consistent (Table S11). Furthermore, analyses using a quasi-Poisson regression model confirmed that the estimated effects of cold and heat on CVD hospitalization risk were stable across model specifications (Table S12). These results lend additional support to the reliability of our findings.

Discussion

This study conducted an in-depth analysis of the impact of AT on CVD hospitalization rates. Using DLNM, our analysis identified elevated risks of hospitalization for specific CVDs associated with both cold and hot effect exposure. We further examined effect modification by gender, age, and living environment. These findings provide important evidence to inform public health interventions aimed at reducing the adverse effects of extreme apparent temperatures on cardiovascular health.

We observed a pronounced cold effect on overall CVD admissions. Specifically, as AT decreased from 0.60 °C to − 5.10 °C, the risk of total CVD hospitalizations increased by 15.52%. Subtype analyses indicated substantially elevated risks for hypertensive diseases (27.16%), IHD (16.77%), and PHD (177.45%), These findings suggest that these conditions may be particularly sensitive to extreme cold, potentially because their pathophysiology is directly influenced by systemic vasoconstriction, the primary physiological response to cold, which markedly increases peripheral vascular resistance and cardiac workload [23, 24]. In contrast, no significant associations were observed for arrhythmias, heart failure, hemorrhagic stroke, or ischemic stroke. This does not imply a lack of association with cold exposure, but rather indicates that the cold effect on these conditions may be more complex, indirect, or manifest after a longer lag period. For example, previous studies have reported that plasma viscosity peaks approximately 27 days following cold exposure, suggesting that hemorheological changes are cumulative and delayed [25]. In addition, arrhythmias may arise as secondary outcomes of myocardial ischemia, while heart failure exacerbations can result from uncontrolled hypertension or ischemic events [26]. Consequently, these conditions may act as downstream or secondary outcomes, leading to dispersed or delayed risk signals in the exposure–response analysis, a pattern that is consistent with the trends illustrated in our contour plots.

The hot effect exhibited acute but short-lived associations with CVD, IHD, and ischemic stroke. Notably, the risk of IHD hospitalization increased by 16.80% under extreme heat exposure, indicating that IHD patients are particularly sensitive to extreme high temperatures. These findings are consistent with prior studies conducted in Gansu and Fujian Provinces and in the Linxia Hui Autonomous Prefecture, China [27–29]. Heat-related effects are plausibly mediated through dehydration and electrolyte imbalances, which elevate cardiac workload [30, 31]. However, apart from the observed 16.80% increase in IHD hospitalization risk due to heat, no significant heat effects were detected for other CVDs. Similar findings have been reported in other studies. A possible explanation is that Xiangyang, located in the Han River basin, has relatively higher socio-economic development and widespread air conditioning use, which may enhance residents’ adaptation to hot weather. Moreover, the relatively short duration of extreme heat events in this region may attenuate measurable health impacts. Therefore, the potential effects of hot effect on other CVD outcomes merit further investigation. For instance, a study in Hefei, China, found that compared to the 75th percentile temperature (25.6 °C), the cumulative relative risk (RR) for total CVD at extremely high temperatures (99th percentile, 33.7 °C) over a 0–27 day lag was 1.078 (95% CI: 0.752–1.547), while the cumulative RR at moderately high temperatures (90th percentile, 29.0 °C) was 1.015 (95% CI: 0.988–1.043) [32]. Similarly, a study in Thailand investigating the impact of extreme temperatures on CVD hospitalization risk did not find significant cumulative heat effects on CVD admissions [33]. A study on residents living in Ontario, Canada, found that high temperatures (99th percentile) increased coronary heart disease (CHD) hospitalization rates by 6% (95% CI: 1% to 11%), but no significant associations were observed for AMI, stroke, or ischemic stroke [31]. These variations may be due to different physiological processes by which various CVDs respond to cold and heat. The findings underscore the importance of understanding the specificity of temperature-related health impacts, which can help better target healthcare resources and interventions. However, current evidence on the associations of temperature on specific CVDs remains limited, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

Gender was found to significantly modify AT–CVD associations. Compared with men, women exhibited greater susceptibility to cold-related CVD risks, particularly in IHD. Similar patterns were observed under heat exposure, where women again demonstrated elevated vulnerability. These findings are consistent with time-series analyses from western China [27] and other reports suggesting stronger heat-related CVD effects among women [34, 35]. Several factors may contribute to this gender disparity. The GBD 2019 report identified CVD as the leading cause of death in women, accounting for 35% of total female mortality [3]. Women, particularly younger women, often experience inequities in access to preventive and therapeutic cardiovascular care [36]. Moreover, blood pressure progression appears faster in women than in men [37, 38], and coronary syndromes such as STEMI and non-ST elevation myocardial infarction occur more frequently in women [39]. Additionally, inappropriate temperatures have been linked to increased healthcare visits for mental illnesses, suicides, and self-reported poor mental health [40]. Persistent mental stresses such as depression and anxiety, recognized as etiological and prognostic risk factors for CAD, similarly elevate the risk of CAD in both genders. However, the prevalence is notably higher in females, especially among younger women [41]. Furthermore, given that females generally have a lower socioeconomic status globally compared to males, their access to resources for preventing and treating CVD is consequently less. Therefore, it is reasonable that females, particularly in the context of IHD, face a heightened CVD risk when exposed to adverse AT conditions.

Age has been widely reported as a risk factor for CVD, and our observations indicate that individuals over the age of 65 are more susceptible to the risks of IHD due to cold exposure compared to younger populations under 65. Studies suggest that physiological responses to cold stress, such as diminished peripheral vasoconstriction and reduced cold-induced metabolic heat production, deteriorate with aging, making older individuals less tolerant to extreme temperatures [42]. This may be attributed to age-related declines in peripheral vasoconstriction, cold-induced thermogenesis, and endothelial function, which collectively reduce physiological resilience to cold stress [43]. In contrast, younger individuals may benefit from healthier vasculature and more responsive sympathetic nervous systems, offering short-term protection during initial cold exposure [44]. Interestingly, heat exposure exerted protective effects on individuals>65 years during early lag days, possibly due to vasodilation and blood pressure reduction. Prior research supports this mechanism, with ultraviolet sunlight exposure shown to lower systolic blood pressure via nitric oxide release [45], and higher indoor temperatures associated with reduced blood pressure [46].

A comparison of rural and urban living environments shows significant differences in the susceptibility to AT-related CVD health risks. Rural populations are generally more affected by cold and heat effects, increasing their risk of IHD. The greater susceptibility of rural areas to AT impacts can be attributed to factors such as lower insulation standards in housing, fewer heating opportunities, and potentially more outdoor labor, which may expose them to inappropriate AT more frequently and for longer durations than urban residents [29, 47], thus leading to a higher risk of IHD hospitalizations.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between AT and CVD admissions in the Han River basin of China. We used AT as an indicator of cold and heat effects to calculate the cumulative RR, which more accurately represents the respondents’ sensitivity to ambient temperatures. Unlike traditional studies that often focus on the overall impact on CVDs, this research delves into specific cardiovascular conditions such as IHD, hypertensive diseases, and arrhythmias, providing a more precise and detailed risk assessment. Through stratified analysis by gender, age, and residential area, this study thoroughly explores how these sociodemographic characteristics influence individuals’ sensitivity to extreme AT. This analysis not only enhances our understanding of the differential health associations of AT but also provides a basis for developing more personalized public health interventions.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the analysis is based on hospital data from a single region. Although the hospital services more than half of the residents in Xiangyang, the findings should be cautiously generalized. Secondly, only outdoor exposure data were used. Indoor exposures, such as the mitigating effect of air conditioning, were not accounted for. Previous studies have shown that residential AC reduces—but does not eliminate—the risks of heat-related adverse health outcomes [48]. Future research should integrate indoor exposure data and explore the modifying role of AC use. Finally, although we adjusted for several key confounders such as air pollution, seasonality, and long-term temporal trends, individual-level sociodemographic factors—such as education level and socioeconomic status—were not considered, Those factors may influence the AT–CVD relationship.

Conclusion

This study utilized a DLNM to comprehensively analyze the impact of AT variations on hospital admissions for specific CVD in Xiangyang. The results indicate that cold effect significantly increase the risk of hospital admissions for total CVD and IHD, while hot effect particularly elevate the admission risk for female IHD patients and significantly increase the risk of ischemic stroke admissions in rural areas. Through stratified analysis by gender, age, and residential area, this study revealed the sociodemographic moderating associations of AT on CVD impacts, providing targeted scientific evidence for public health strategies. It underscores the importance of implementing effective protective measures for high-risk groups under extreme AT conditions. These findings are crucial for optimizing prevention and treatment strategies for CVDs, especially in the context of climate change.
