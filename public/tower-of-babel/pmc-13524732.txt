Growth performance and fecal characteristics of growing-finishing pigs fed a DDGS–palm kernel meal-based diet in mash or crumble form
Authors: Hatem M Eltahan, In Ho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of partial replacement of soybean meal (SBM) with palm kernel meal (PKM) and distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS), offered in mash or crumble feed forms, on growth performance and fecal characteristics in growing-finishing pigs. A total of 210 growing pigs ([Landrace × Yorkshire] × Duroc), weighted 30 ± 3.5 kg, were placed into three dietary treatments with fourteen replicates and five animals in each pen and fed TRT1, a mash diet based on corn-SBM; TRT2, a mash diet based on corn-SBM-DDGS-PKM; and TRT3, a crumble diet based on corn-SBM-DDGS-PKM. Our results showed that during the growing phase, body weight, average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) did not differ significantly in pigs fed either a mash or crumble diet. Likewise, no significant treatment effects were observed on fecal score or fecal moisture in mash or crumble treatments. Similar results were obtained during the finishing phase, where growth performance parameters remained unaffected by treatment under mash or crumble form, and fecal traits also showed no significant differences. These results indicated that PKM and DDGS can partially replace SBM in growing-finishing pig diets without detrimental effects on growth performance or fecal consistency under the present conditions.Keywords: Growing-finishing pigs, Palm kernel meal, DDGS, Crumble, Mash, Fecal characteristics

Introduction

Feed formulation remains one of the major determinants of profitability and sustainability in pig production, as feed accounts for the largest share of total production costs in intensive systems (Papatsiros et al., 2025). Therefore, increasing attention has been directed toward reducing reliance on conventional protein sources through the inclusion of lower-cost agro-industrial co-products without impairing growth performance or carcass quality (Maher et al., 2025). Soybean meal (SBM) remains the standard plant protein source in pig diets because of its high crude protein content, favorable amino acid composition, and high digestibility. However, fluctuations in price and supply, together with the need for more sustainable feed formulation strategies, have encouraged the search for practical alternatives that can partially replace SBM in commercial pig diets (Liu et al., 2025; Pope et al., 2024; Papatsiros et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025).

Among the available alternatives, palm kernel meal (PKM) and distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS) have received considerable attention. PKM is a by-product of the palm oil industry and is considered attractive because of its low cost and wide availability. However, its relatively high fiber content may reduce nutrient utilization when included at high levels. Jang et al. (2020) reported that increasing the inclusion level of PKM in growing-finishing pig diets reduced growth performance and nutrient digestibility, which suggests that its use should be carefully controlled up to 12%. DDGS, a by-product of the ethanol industry, is another important feed ingredient because it provides protein, energy, fiber, and minerals at a competitive cost. Even so, its nutritional value can vary, and excessive inclusion may reduce feed efficiency if diets are not properly balanced. Previous studies have shown that partial replacement of SBM with plant protein mixtures containing PKM and DDGS can maintain growth performance, fecal traits, and carcass quality when inclusion levels are kept within suitable limits from 5% to <12% (Jang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025).

Feed processing is another factor that may influence the value of these alternative ingredients. The physical form of the diet can affect feed handling, intake pattern, nutrient availability, and feed efficiency. Earlier studies have shown that processed feed forms such as crumble may improve animal performance in some cases, but the results have not been consistent. Rojas and Stein (2017) reported that the response to feed processing depends largely on diet composition, especially when fibrous ingredients are used. Likewise, Sampath and Kim (2023) found only limited benefits of mash/crumble feeding on performance and digestibility in growing-finish pigs, while Kim et al. (2015) reported that neither mash nor crumble significantly changes the growth performance parameter of lactating sows or the offspring. In contrast, Khan et al. (2023) observed some improvement in performance with crumble feed, but without clear advantages in carcass traits. In addition, Overholt et al. (2016) showed that the response to DDGS in finishing pigs depended on diet form, suggesting that feed processing may influence how pigs respond to fibrous co-products. However, information is still limited on diets specifically based on a DDGS-PKM mixture offered in mash or crumble form during both the growing and finishing stages. Despite these findings, information remains limited on the use of a DDGS-PKM-based alternative diet in different physical forms during both the growing and finishing periods.

Most previous studies have focused either on the nutritional value of alternative ingredients or on feed form alone, whereas less attention has been given to whether presenting a DDGS-PKM-based diet as mash or crumble provides any additional practical benefit under growing-finishing conditions. Therefore, the current study evaluated whether partial replacement of conventional corn-SBM mash diet with a DDGS-PKM could influence the productive performance and fecal characteristics of growing-finishing pigs under mash or crumble as different feed forms.

Materials and methods

The ethics of animals and approvment

This study was performed and followed the protocol that was approved by the Committee of Animal Care at Dankook University in South Korea (Protocol No. DK-1-2504).

Experimental design and dietary preparation

A total of 210 ([Landrace × Yorkshire] × Duroc) growing pigs were placed based on body weight into three dietary treatments with fourteen replicates and five animals in each, and fed as follows: TRT1, mash corn-SBM-based diet; TRT2, mash corn-SBM-DDGS-PKM-based diet; and TRT3, crumble corn-SBM-DDGS-PKM-based diet. Pigs were fed the experimental diets during a growing phase (initial to week 5) followed by a finishing phase (week 5 to week 14). The diets were formulated to meet nutrient requirements for growing-finishing pigs, following standard swine feeding practice and the general approach used in comparable feed-form studies formulated according to NRC (2012) recommendations (Tables 1, 2). The nutrient composition presented in Table 1, Table 2 represents analysed values obtained from representative feed samples. The crumble diet was prepared by steam-conditioning the mash diet at approximately 55 °C and pelleting it through a die with 4.8 mm-diameter holes, followed by cooling and mechanical crumbling before feeding. All pigs were kept under an equal, environmentally controlled facility with plastic-slatted flooring. The ambient temperature was automatically maintained at approximately 24 °C. Each pen was fitted with an automatic feeder and a nipple drinker, allowing pigs to access free feed and water.

Sampling and analysis

The body weight and feed consumption for each pen were measured initially and every week at weeks 1, 3, and 5 during the growing phase, and at weeks 5, 8, 11, and 14 during the finishing phase. Growth performance indices, including ADG, ADFI, and FCR, were computed for each time interval and for the overall experimental phase. Moreover, fecal score and fecal moisture were recorded during both growing and finishing phases. Fecal score was assessed using a 5-point scoring system, where 1 indicated hard (dry pellets); 2 indicated firm (well-formed feces); 3 indicated soft (moist feces) that still retained their shape; 4 indicated soft (unformed feces) that conformed to the shape of the container; and 5 indicated watery feces. This scoring system is consistent with methods used in previous swine studies assessing fecal consistency (Larson et al., 1977; Zhang et al., 2020).

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed by SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Body weight, ADG, ADFI, FCR, fecal moisture, and fecal score were evaluated by one-way ANOVA. Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was used to compare TRT1 vs. TRT2 and TRT2 vs. TRT3 (SAS Institute Inc., 2013). Least squares means are presented with their standard errors (SEM), and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Growth parameter in the growing period

Initial body weight did not differ among treatments. Likewise, body weight measured at weeks 1, 3, and 5 showed no significant differences among the experimental groups. No significant treatment effects were also detected for ADG, ADFI, or FCR during the intervals from initial to week 1, week 1 to 3, and week 3 to 5 (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study showed that replacing the conventional corn–SBM mash diet with a DDGS–PKM-based mash diet did not affect BW, ADG, ADFI, or FCR during either the growing or finishing period, suggesting that the PKM- and DDGS-containing diets were nutritionally adequate to sustain normal growth. A similar pattern was observed when the DDGS–PKM-based diet was offered in crumble rather than mash form, as no significant changes were detected in growth performance.

The absence of adverse effects on growth performance in the present study suggests that the inclusion level used for the DDGS–PKM-based diet was within a tolerable range for growing-finishing pigs. These results agree with Zhao et al. (2025), who found that partial replacement of soybean meal with a mixed plant-protein source containing rapeseed meal, 6% PKM, and 6% DDGS did not significantly affect overall growth performance in growing-finishing pigs. However, the present results differ from those of Jang et al. (2020), who observed linear reductions in body weight and feed intake, as the dietary level of PKM increased >12%. This difference is most likely related to the level of PKM inclusion and diet structure. In the current study, PKM and DDGS were used as part of a practical alternative formulation, whereas Jang et al. (2020) examined increasing the PKM levels and showed that excessive inclusion )18%) may depress performance. Although the inclusion of PKM in the present study was 8% approximately 2 percentage units higher than in some previous reports 6%, the alternative diets were formulated to maintain calculated metabolizable energy and amino acid levels. The moderate inclusion level, amino acid correction, and phase-specific formulation likely helped prevent measurable reductions in growth performance. The absence of a performance response may reflect the moderate inclusion level of the co-products, maintenance of calculated metabolizable energy and amino acid targets, and amino acid correction in the alternative diets. Under these conditions, the fiber increase may not have been sufficient to alter feed intake, growth, or fecal consistency. However, performance alone cannot confirm unchanged nutrient digestibility.

The current study demonstrated that fecal score and fecal moisture were not significantly affected during either the growing or finishing period under the mash or crumble form. This result is in line with Zhao and Kim (2024), who found no significant change in fecal score when soybean meal was partially replaced with PKM or DDGS in growing pigs. It also agrees with Zhao et al. (2025), who reported no significant effect of partial soybean meal replacement with a mixed plant-protein product on fecal score in growing-finishing pigs. These findings indicate that the alternative diet used in the present study did not negatively influence fecal consistency under the tested conditions. The lack of response in fecal traits may be explained by the moderate inclusion level of the fibrous co-products (PKM) used in the present experiment. Rojas and Stein (2017) emphasized that responses to fibrous ingredients and feed processing in pigs are strongly influenced by ingredient characteristics, processing conditions, and the overall composition of the diet. Therefore, the absence of significant differences in fecal score and fecal moisture in the current study does not necessarily contradict the known effects of fiber-rich ingredients, but rather suggests that the inclusion rate and diet formulation used here were not severe enough to produce measurable changes.

With regard to feed form, changing the DDGS–PKM-based diet from mash to crumble did not improve growth performance or fecal characteristics. This finding is consistent with Khan and Kim (2023), who reported no significant differences between mash and crumble diets in growth performance, nutrient digestibility, backfat thickness, or carcass quality in growing-finishing pigs. It is also partly consistent with Sampath and Kim (2023), who found that a mixed mash/crumble feeding program improved feed efficacy at an early stage and tended to improve it at the end of the trial, but did not significantly alter BW, DG, DFI, nutrient digestibility, or backfat thickness overall. Together, these studies suggest that the benefits of crumble feeding are not always consistent and may be limited under practical feeding conditions.

In contrast, Nguyen et al. (2017) reported that crumbled diets improved growth performance and reduced market age in growing-finishing pigs. Moreover, Overholt et al. (2016) showed that the response to DDGS in finishing pigs depended on diet form, as pelleting improved feed efficiency in some cases. The difference between those studies and the present one is likely due to the type of processing and the composition of the diet. Overholt et al. (2016) compared meal and pellet diets, whereas the current study compared mash and crumble forms. In addition, the diets included a higher DDGS level and a different processing intensity, which may increase the likelihood of detecting a response. This interpretation is supported by Rojas and Stein (2017), who noted that the nutritional effects of feed processing in pigs vary according to both the processing method and the diet composition.

Overall, the present results are more consistent with studies showing that moderate inclusion of distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS) can be used in growing-finishing pig diets without impairing growth performance, nutrient digestibility, or carcass traits (Biswas & Kim, 2022). In contrast, the evidence for PKM appears to be more conditional, as acceptable outcomes are generally reported at moderate inclusion rates, whereas increasing PKM at levels 18% may depress body weight, feed intake, and nutrient digestibility (Jang et al., 2020). The current findings are also in agreement with reports indicating that crumble feed does not consistently improve growth performance, nutrient digestibility, backfat thickness, or carcass quality compared with mash feed in growing-finishing pigs (Khan & Kim, 2023). At the same time, earlier work suggests that responses to feed form may vary depending on diet composition, processing conditions, and production stage, which may explain why crumble feed does not always outperform mash feed under practical conditions (Yang et al., 2001). From a practical standpoint, these findings support the use of PKM and DDGS as alternative ingredients in growing-finishing pig diets. They also suggest that mash form may be adequate under the conditions tested, which may have implications for reducing feed manufacturing costs.

A limitation of the present study is that a conventional corn-SBM crumble diet was not included. Therefore, the effect of crumble processing cannot be fully separated from diet composition, and the absence of a crumble response should be interpreted only within the DDGS-PKM-based diet tested here. Future studies using a factorial arrangement of diet composition and feed form would help clarify these effects. Additionally, studies on nutrient digestibility coefficients, carcass measurements, and economic evaluation would further strengthen the practical conclusions, but the present data already demonstrated that the alternative diets were nutritionally acceptable and well tolerated.

Conclusion

The current study indicated that the DDGS–PKM-based diet can be considered an effective alternative to the conventional corn–SBM diet without negative effects on productive performance or fecal consistency in mash or crumble form.
