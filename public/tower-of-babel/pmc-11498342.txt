Diet and depression
Authors: Nicholas Fabiano, Melissa M Lane, Wolfgang Marx
Published: 2024

Diet is associated with depressive symptoms

A 2024 umbrella review — including nearly 10 million participants enrolled in observational studies — found that people who consumed more ultra-processed foods had a 22% higher risk of incident depression or depressive symptoms than people who consumed less ultra-processed foods.1 People who adhere to a nutrient-dense diet are 30% less likely to have features of depression than those who do not.2 Examples of such diets include the Mediterranean diet, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet, and dietary patterns with low inflammatory potential according to the Dietary Inflammatory Index (e.g., unprocessed foods).2

Dietary interventions may reduce depressive symptoms

Small randomized controlled trials involving people with depression have shown moderate-to-large improvements in depressive symptoms for participants receiving a Mediterranean diet compared with controls.3 Most studies have investigated the Mediterranean diet, but evidence supporting specific dietary approaches is insufficient; rather, any dietary pattern that emphasizes the consumption of nutrient-dense foods and is individualized to patients’ preferences may be efficacious.2

Dietary advice should be individualized for optimal adherence

An individual’s ethical and spiritual preferences, food intolerances, allergies, taste preferences, and socioeconomic status should be considered. In general, people should be encouraged to prioritize consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole-grain cereals, nuts, and lean meat, while limiting processed foods.2

Advice should focus on food swaps and not food restriction

Evidence-based strategies to improve diet quality include swaps, whereby people replace nutrient-poor food items with nutrient-dense alternatives, rather than restriction.4 Full adherence to a specific dietary pattern is not essential to improve depressive symptoms and is likely an unreasonable expectation.2

Where available, people should be referred to a trained dietitian

The clinical benefit and adherence are greatest for dietary interventions delivered by a trained dietitian.2
