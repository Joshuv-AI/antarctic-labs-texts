Strengthening undergraduate dental public health education in Iran: insights from national symposia
Authors: Shabnam Varmazyari, Mahsa Eshrati, Hossein Hessari, Mohammad Reza Khami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree national symposia used consultation and consensus-building to identify core challenges in Iran’s undergraduate Dental Public Health (DPH) education: Infrastructure, Workforce, Curriculum, Research, and Community engagement. Solutions called for systemic reforms prioritizing robust infrastructure, adequate workforce, and strong community links. This commentary presents insights and implications from these symposia.Keywords: Public health dentistry, Community dentistry, Dental specialties, Dental education, Curriculum

Introduction

Iran, an upper-middle-income country with over 85 million people, carries a significant oral health burden that leads to substantial economic impacts, productivity losses, and dental care expenditures [1, 2]. This situation reflects a treatment-focused approach that addresses disease outcomes rather than root causes [3].

Dental public health (DPH) is the branch of dentistry dedicated to preventing oral diseases and promoting oral health through population-level efforts. Rather than individual treatment, the discipline focuses on community oral health by integrating policy-making, epidemiologic assessment, and resource management [4, 5]. Consequently, quality undergraduate DPH education is essential for dentists.

However, DPH in Iran (also referred to as Community Oral Health) faces challenges that undermine graduate preparedness. These include an outdated, treatment-oriented curriculum with limited emphasis on population-based prevention, inconsistent national implementation, and insufficient infrastructure and funding [2, 6, 7]. In addition, evidence on Iran’s undergraduate DPH education is largely limited and outdated [8].

To address these gaps, three national symposia were organized to evaluate the current state of Iran’s undergraduate DPH education, identify core challenges, and propose actionable solutions. This commentary synthesizes insights from these symposia.

Symposia arrangements

Three national symposia titled “Evaluating the Educational Status of DPH in Iran” were held between 2019 and 2023 to assess and improve Iran’s undergraduate DPH education. They were initiated by the Secretariat of the Dental Education Council of the Ministry of Health and organized by the Iranian Association of Community Oral Health, university DPH departments, the Research Center for Caries Prevention, and the Department of DPH at Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Each symposium brought together 40–50 participants, including DPH specialists, department heads, PhD students, and dental school representatives. Invitations were extended via official letters to ensure representative involvement.

The first symposium was held in person at Tehran University of Medical Sciences on April 23–24, 2019. On the first day, following a presentation on the role of DPH departments in dental education, participants identified key challenges through multiple rounds of structured, facilitated group discussions. This was a participatory consultation and consensus-building process. Ideas were recorded in real time on shared whiteboards and in facilitator notes, then reviewed and consolidated by the organizing team through grouping similar inputs and removing duplicates. The main challenge areas were refined through further discussion and brainstorming until consensus was reached. Five challenge domains were identified: infrastructure, budget and service delivery; workforce development and management; curriculum design; community-centric research; and community engagement and outreach. Participants then formed domain-specific working groups to develop solutions through structured discussions. Proposed solutions were presented in plenary sessions, refined through collective feedback, and finalized on the second day in a two-hour session led by group representatives.

The second (virtual, October 2020) and third (in-person, July 2023) symposia revisited these insights, reviewed the domains, and discussed their importance for educational reform through iterative discussion. Consensus was achieved through facilitated discussion and collective agreement. Three domains, including infrastructure strengthening, workforce development, and community engagement, were deemed priorities based on their perceived impact, feasibility, and alignment with national needs. The third symposium also featured a training workshop for DPH educators. Participant databases and virtual groups were created at the symposia’s conclusion to sustain dialogue and share resources.

Key challenges and proposed solutions

Infrastructure, budget, and service

Within this domain, Iran’s DPH program faces curriculum resource limitations, including scarce fluoride varnish supplies, transportation to community course sites, and low patient referrals to dental schools. It also struggles with province-specific issues such as oral cancer in Sistan and Baluchestan and molar-incisor hypomineralization in Ardabil.

Suggested mitigation measures include establishing Memorandums of Understanding (MOUs) between the Ministry of Health and dental school departments to secure resources; creating referral pathways from schools to DPH departments with lower tariffs, securing insurance contracts, and maintaining patient databases by DPH PhD students to streamline referrals. In addition, to align with local needs, it was recommended to form MOUs between DPH departments and provincial Health deputies; launch tailored initiatives (such as oral cancer screening in Sistan and Baluchestan, hypo-mineralization information centers in Ardabil); use provincial needs assessments to guide undergraduate research; review and improve dental care packages at Comprehensive Healthcare Centers; and introduce Family Dentistry to give students community-based experience.

Workforce development and management

Under this category, Iran’s DPH workforce faces: (a) too few DPH departments nationwide; (b) low appeal among dental students; (c) a shortage of experts (below the goal of one DPH PhD student per school/province by 2025); (d) underuse of PhD students in teaching; and (e) limited cross-specialty collaboration.

To address department shortage, suggested measures include developing flowcharts and checklists for department establishment; ensuring dental schools provide adequate resources; and using the hub model where the 10 regional lead universities share faculty and resources with nearby schools.

Proposed solutions to tackle expert shortage include DPH departments providing short competency-building courses for other specialties; hub schools training non-hub ones; and offering dual-degrees with pediatrics, periodontics, and restorative specialties.

To improve DPH’s appeal among undergraduate dental students, suggested measures include enhancing DPH publicity via schools newsletters and social media; clarifying clinical relevance in curricula; offering funded PhD programs to top students; and expanding career paths through oral health committees and Ministry of Health service contracts.

To put DPH PhD students to use, it was proposed to utilize those past their Comprehensive Examination as teaching assistants, and recruit senior PhD students and graduates fulfilling mandatory service into teaching roles.

To strengthen cross-specialty collaboration, it was suggested to use the hub model to have professors teach shared subjects.

Curriculum design

In this category, Iran’s undergraduate DPH education faces challenges in content, teaching, assessment, and evaluation.

To alleviate lack of theoretical and practical training content for Special Health Care Needs (SHCN) patients, solutions included integrating SHCN into DPH and other specialties’ curricula; directly inviting SHCN patients to dental schools; and establishing SHCN oral health committees and fellowships involving DPH graduates and clinical specialists.

To improve teaching, proposed solutions include offering nationwide educator-training workshops; addressing limited hours and transportation to community sites by alerting dental school deputies, developing 3–4 standardized course models; and having DPH and dentistry boards identify practical courses needing the most improvement.

To alleviate assessment challenges, proposed measures include aligning assessments with course objectives; using higher-level exam questions; dedicating part of the final grade to practical application; and developing detailed checklists for practical courses. To further address these challenges, the Iranian DPH board was established in 2022.

To tackle evaluation challenges, feedback is to be systematically collected from stakeholders through dialogue, surveys, and specialized forms.

Community-centric research

In this domain, Iran’s undergraduate DPH struggles with misalignment between thesis topics and societal oral health issues and lack of community-centric research.

To better align thesis topics with societal needs, it was proposed to base postgraduate thesis studies on needs assessments of the Ministry of Health; then have DPH authorities negotiate with the Ministry for financial support.

To increase community-focused research among undergraduates, it was suggested to design studies that evaluate the methodology, effectiveness, and cost-benefit of the Ministry of Health’s interventions; evaluate cohort data in thesis projects; and translate findings into policy briefs.

Community engagement and outreach

Iran’s undergraduate DPH program lacks meaningful community engagement and outreach.

To address this issue, it was proposed to develop tailored educational materials for underserved and SHCN populations; design initiatives that place students in diverse community settings; have dental students deliver oral health education in schools; launch national oral health campaigns involving undergraduate students; establish community-led oral health committees for local initiatives and designate undergraduate students as leaders; and partner with welfare organizations to create community-based education opportunities.

Responsible bodies

To effectively address these challenges, the primary responsible organizations and bodies include: (a) Secretariat of the Dental Education Council of Iran’s Ministry of Health (national curriculum); (b) Strategic Planning in Committee Community Oral Health / Iran’s DPH Board / Council for Dental and Specialized Education (policy-making); (c) University Educational Deputies & Educational Development Offices (local implementation); (d) University leadership, chancellors, and deans (infrastructure and support); (e) Department of Community Oral Health faculty (workforce, research, outreach); (f) Iranian Association of Community Oral Health (networking and advocacy). Collaboration among these entities, provincial health deputies, and the Ministry of Health, is essential.

Discussion

The three national symposia were conducted in Iran between 2019 and 2023 to examine the state of undergraduate DPH education in Iran, identify key challenges, and propose solutions.

The identified educational challenges mirror broader international patterns. Similar shortages in dedicated DPH departments and trained faculty have been reported in Egypt, where limited postgraduate training, particularly at the PhD level, has left DPH teaching largely to pediatric dentistry faculty [5]. Comparable gaps have been noted in Europe, where only about two-thirds of dental schools have dedicated DPH departments and teaching is often delivered by mixed faculty, especially pediatrics [9]. Likewise, Colombia and Kenya have no dedicated DPH track, and Brazil offers only three programs [10].

Underutilized community outreach has also been noted in Egypt, and Europe [5, 9]. In Egypt, community-based training is underused and peripheral, used by only about a fourth of schools [5]. Across Europe, outreach accounted for only 11% of teaching, with schools citing it as an example of “good practice” rather than routine [9]. India, Colombia, Kenya, and Brazil do not have sufficiently-structured community outreach either [10].

The identified challenges likely reflect the broader position of prevention and public health within Iran’s dental system. Infrastructure gaps may partly stem from the rapid, largely unplanned expansion of dental schools [11], alongside a longstanding emphasis on clinical and specialty-based dentistry over prevention [2]. The same structural conditions likely contribute to workforce shortages, as dental schools expanded faster than the supply of trained staff [11, 12]. Weak postgraduate training pathways and DPH’s lower perceived status relative to clinical specialties may be reinforcing these shortages [2, 12]. Community engagement may likewise remain underdeveloped, reflecting both the treatment-oriented nature of Iran’s dental system [2], and the under-resourced community training sites [6].

Addressing the identified challenges requires coordinated action across Iran’s Dental Education Council, policy bodies, universities, and DPH departments. International solutions may also prove useful. These include faculty development through mentorship-based “Growing Our Own” programs [13], stronger oral health integration into primary care, or recruiting undergraduate dental students for telephone counseling, school-based promotion, and community education [14, 15]. Progress can then be tracked through indicators such as the number of newly-developed DPH departments, competency-based DPH modules, or community-based training opportunities. Periodic review by stakeholders can monitor these reforms.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these insights. As the insights emerged primarily from discussions among academic and institutional stakeholders, they reflect expert judgment rather than empirical evidence. Participation was invitation-based and may have introduced selection bias, while the influence of senior participants on group consensus cannot be excluded. The insights are also shaped by Iran’s educational and policy context and may not be fully generalizable. However, the multi-year nature of the symposia and participation from across Iran still provide a broad perspective.

Conclusions

The three national symposia on undergraduate DPH education in Iran identified five core challenge domains: infrastructure, workforce, curriculum, research, and community engagement, with infrastructure strengthening, workforce development, and community engagement prioritized as for reform. Addressing these challenges requires collaboration among the Ministry of Health’s Dental Education Council, DPH policy bodies, university leadership, and the Iranian Association of Community Oral Health. Key solutions include establishing and strengthening DPH departments, expanding faculty through hub-university collaboration and targeted training, and adopting structured community-based education. Progress can be tracked through DPH department establishment, community-based training expansion, and competency-based DPH module integration. Together, these measures can better equip Iranian dentists for addressing the country’s oral health needs.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The activities described in this commentary were conducted as professional consultation and consensus-building. The discussions generated population-level educational insights and recommendations; no individual-level data, personal identifiers, or attributable statements were collected or analyzed. Participants were informed that the synthesized outputs could contribute to educational reports and dissemination activities. Therefore, these activities did not constitute human subjects research requiring ethics approval.
