Association of dietary patterns with anxiety and depression symptoms in people living with HIV: a population-based cross-sectional study from China
Authors: Zizheng Nie, Huiling Tang, Yingying Liu, Ke Zhu, Chenyang Wu, Junxia Cheng, Mu Zhang, Fengying Wang, Shufen Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health issues are increasingly common among individuals living with HIV, and diet plays a crucial role in managing mental health. This study aims to explore the association of dietary patterns with anxiety and depression symptoms within this population.MethodsThe population-based cross-sectional study included 700 Chinese individuals living with HIV, who participated in a health survey during routine checkups at the local HIV specific hospitals. Food-related information was collected using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire. Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for anxiety and depressive symptoms based on quartiles of each dietary pattern score.ResultsFactor analysis identified four primary dietary patterns within this population: the egg-dairy pattern, the livestock-meat pattern, the whole grain pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern. After adjusting for potential confounders, participants in the highest quartile of the egg-dairy pattern score exhibited significantly lower odds of anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.99) compared to those in the lowest quartile. Similarly, individuals in the highest quartile of the vegetable-fruit pattern score displayed significantly lower odds of depression symptoms (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.61) relative to those in the lowest quartile. Nevertheless, no significant associations were found between the livestock-meat pattern and the whole grain pattern with the risk of anxiety and depressive symptoms, even after controlling for potential confounders.ConclusionThe findings indicate that adherence to the egg-dairy pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern is associated with a reduced risk of anxiety and depressive symptoms among individuals living with HIV. Diet is acknowledged as a modifiable risk factor for experiencing anxiety and depression symptoms, providing valuable insights for potential precision health interventions for individuals living with HIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25475-1.

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global public health challenge, characterised by its detrimental effects on the body’s immune system. According to the report by the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) and the World Health Organization (WHO), it is estimated that 39.9 million individuals were living with HIV worldwide, and approximately 630,000 people died from HIV-related illness in 2023 [1, 2]. As of June 2024, there are approximately 1.33 million individuals living with HIV/AIDS in China. The effectiveness of combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV/AIDS into a chronic disease that can be treatable but presently difficult to completely cure [3]. With the increase of life expectancy [4, 5], the interaction of the compromised immune systems, the side effects of ART, and the stigma associated with HIV has negatively impacted their overall health, thereby elevating the risk of comorbidities [6, 7]. Comorbidities have arisen as a pivotal determinant impacting the quality of life and the prognosis of participants infected with HIV.

Individuals living with HIV encounter a multitude of intricate challenges that significantly affect their physical, psychological, emotional and social well-being [8–10]. Anxiety and depression are the most common neuropsychiatric complication among HIV-infected participants worldwide, which significantly impair the quality of life, and then reduce medication adherence as well as treatment outcome for those living with HIV [11–13]. The average prevalence rates of anxiety and depression among individuals living with HIV are approximately 15.5% [14] and 22%−44% [13], respectively. The rates are two to four times higher than those observed in the general population. An integrated approach that effectively addresses both anxiety and depression is crucial for improving outcomes for individuals living with HIV, as these mental health conditions are known to worsen HIV-related health outcomes [15, 16]. Consequently, the issue of anxiety and depression among people living with HIV must not be overlooked.

Numerous factors contribute to the increased incidence of mental health issues among individuals living with HIV, including social stress, stigma, prejudice and discrimination [8, 9, 17]. Recent epidemiological studies and literature reviews highlight that diet plays a crucial role in the management of mental health, particularly in addressing anxiety and depression, by targeting inflammation, oxidative stress and brain plasticity [18–21]. In recent years, dietary pattern analysis has been recommended as a more effective and recognizable approach for addressing anxiety and depression symptoms, as this method accounts for the complexity of overall diet and facilitates more tailored nutritional recommendations [22–24]. Most studies indicated that healthy dietary patterns tend to reduce the risk of depression, whereas Western-style diets may increase the risk of developing depression symptoms [24, 25]. To our knowledge, there was limited data regarding the association of dietary patterns with anxiety and depression symptoms among people living with HIV. Additionally, considering that poor diet quality is very disadvantageous for disease progression and overall health status in individuals living with HIV [26–28], it is essential to investigate the correlation between diet and mental health in this population. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to explore the association between dietary patterns and symptoms of anxiety and depression among individuals living with HIV in China.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a population-based cross-sectional analysis using baseline data from the study, funded by the Key Scientific and Technological Program Projects of Jinhua City. This three-year epidemiological survey through accidental sampling aims to investigate the relationship between lifestyle factors, including dietary behaviors, and the mental health of people living with HIV, which will contribute to our understanding of the risk factors affecting the quality of life in this population. The design and implementation of this cross-sectional study were rigorously in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (Supplementary Table 1).

In China, treatment for HIV-infected participants is limited to specific hospitals, which upload patient information to the database of local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Our population-based cross-sectional study was conducted at the HIV outpatient clinic of several specific hospitals in Jinhua City, Zhejiang province between December 2023 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria for this study consisted of individuals aged 18 years and older who were HIV-positive and receiving antiretroviral therapy following their diagnosis at local HIV-specific hospitals. Exclusion criteria included HIV-infected pregnant or breastfeeding women, HIV-infected individuals with severe physical disabilities or cognitive impairments, those with a history of psychiatric illness including anxiety and depression disorders prior to their HIV diagnosis, and individuals with prior experience participating in stress protocols. A total of 762 HIV-positive individuals participated in this survey. The final study population consisted of 700 adults living with HIV, all of whom voluntarily consented to complete the questionnaire. The participants were recruited for in-person interviews by trained medical graduate students using validated written questionnaire. Individuals living with HIV who met the eligibility criteria were informed of the study objectives and benefits, as well as the principle of confidentiality and related requirements to protect their personal information. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. This study adhered to the Ethical Principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines for medical research involving human subjects, as well as all applicable regulations. The study received approval from the Institution Review Board of Hangzhou Normal University (approval no. 202300010) and the Ethics Committee of the Jinhua Center for Disease Prevention and Control (approval no. 2024-2). All methods were performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki and its future amendments.

Assessment of dietary intake

Dietary intake and food information were collected using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), which has been previously validated for the Chinese population [29]. For each item on the FFQ, participants first reported whether they had consumed the specified food item over the past month. Subsequently, they detailed the frequency of their consumption by answering one of the provided options: (1) never consumed; (2) the frequency of occurrences per day; (3) the frequency of occurrences per week; (4) the frequency of occurrences per month. The actual amounts of food consumed in each item were estimated as daily intake quantities and subsequently used in the further analysis. The Cronbach’s alpha value for this dietary survey was 0.64.

Identification of dietary pattern

To determine the primary dietary patterns based on the included thirteen food groups in this study, we conducted the principal component analysis method of exploratory factor analysis. Dietary patterns were identified based on factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, in conjunction with cumulative variance contributions. To simplify the structure and enhance interpretability, the factors were rotated with an orthogonal transformation known as varimax rotation for the calculation of factor loadings. Food groups with absolute factor loading of 0.3 or higher were deemed important contributors to this pattern and were included in the present study. The naming of each dietary pattern was based on the characteristics of the food groups within each factor, focusing on the two food groups with the highest factor loading [30]. The factor score for each pattern was calculated by weighting the intake of food groups according to their respective factor loading. Food groups with an absolute factor loading of 0.3 or higher were regarded as significant contributors to this dietary pattern. Higher dietary pattern scores indicated a stronger preference for a specific dietary pattern. Based on the quartiles of their factor scores, participants were categorized into four groups in descending order.

Assessment of anxiety and depression

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is used to assess participants’ anxiety and depression states [31]. The scale consists of 14 items evaluating symptom severity with a 4-point scale of 0 to 3. It includes subscales for anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D), with possible scores for each subscale ranging from 0 to 21. The subscales were designed to differentiate between the two sets of symptoms while minimizing interference from somatic or serious mental disorders [31]. Cut-off scores of 8 or higher for both subscales indicate an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity in identifying symptoms of anxiety and depression [32]. The Cronbach’s coefficients were 0.90 for the anxiety subscale and 0.89 for the depression subscale, respectively, indicating good internal consistency across subscales (average Cronbach’s alpha = 0.83 and 0.82 for HADS-A and HADS-D, respectively) [32].

Covariates

The general questionnaire included sociodemographic information (age, gender, region of residence, education, body mass index (BMI), personal monthly income), living habits (physical activity and sedentary behavior), infection states (routes, duration and complications), CD4+ T lymphocytes counts and side effects of medications. Information regarding infection states and CD4+ T lymphocytes counts was obtained from the databases of local HIV-specific hospitals and CDC. In China, HIV infected individuals progress through three CD4 stages before being classified as AIDS participants (CD4+ cell counts < 200/mm3): no immune deficiency (CD4+ cell counts ≥ 500/mm3), mild immune deficiency (counts ranging from 350/mm3 to 499/mm3), and moderate immune deficiency (counts between 200/mm3 to 349/mm3). BMI is determined by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of body height (m). According to the Chinese weight standards for adults [33], BMI categories were defined as follows: < 18.5 kg/m2 (underweight), 18.5–23.9 kg/m2 (normal weight), 24.0–27.9 kg/m2 (overweight), and ≥ 28 kg/m2 (obese). Information about physical activity were obtained using a short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). According to the classification standard [34], physical activity levels are categorized into three groups: low, moderate, and high based on responses from the IPAQ questionnaire.

Data analysis

Database establishment and statistical analysis were all completed by EpiData 3.1 software and R software (version 4.3.3, R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were described as numbers and percentages (%), and analysed using the chi-square test. Given the presence of non-normal distributions, continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR), and examined using the Mann-Whitney test. The association between dietary patterns and the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms in people living with HIV was summarized using odds ratios (OR) along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI), which were obtained through binary logistic regression analysis. Four logistic models were built for analysis: crude model without adjustments; model 1 adjusted for participants’ age, gender, BMI and personal monthly income; model 2 further adjusted on the basis of model 1 for duration of infection, complications, CD4 + T lymphocytes, side effects of medication; model 3 built upon model 2 by additional adjustment for the participants’ physical activity and sedentary behavior. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

The overall prevalence of anxiety symptoms among people living with HIV was 31.4%, with the rate being 30.3% in males and 37.5% in females. Additionally, the prevalence of depression symptoms was 28.6%, with a higher rate of 38.4% in females compared to 26.7% in males (p = 0.012). The general characteristics based on participants with and without anxiety or depression symptoms are presented in Table 1. Individuals living with HIV with and without anxiety symptoms exhibited significant differences in several covariates, including age, personal monthly income, sedentary behavior, duration of infection and side effects of medication. Similarly, significant differences were found in these covariates of gender, personal monthly income, physical activity and sedentary behavior between participants with and without depression symptoms.

Dietary intake characteristics

According to the revised Dietary Guidelines (2022) and the Food Guide Pagoda for Chinese residents, the average dietary consumption among individuals living with HIV was significantly below the recommended intake for several food groups (Supplementary Table 2): whole grain and mixed beans (24.0 g/day vs. the recommended 50–150 g/day), tubers (29.3 g/day vs. the recommended 50–100 g/day), vegetables (196.2 g/day vs. the recommended 300–500 g/day), fruits (133.6 g/day vs. the recommended 200–350 g/day), seafood (33.6 g/day vs. the recommended 40–75 g/day), dairy and dairy products (120.5 g/day vs. the recommended 300–500 g/day), soybeans and nuts (18.5 g/day vs. the recommended 25–35 g/day). As shown in Fig. 1, more than half of the participants failed to meet the dietary recommendations for whole grains and mixed beans (82.3%), tubers (78.7%), vegetables (81.0%), fruits (65.4%), seafood (75.4%), eggs (51.7%), dairy and dairy products (90.3%), as well as soybeans and nuts (76.1%).

Dietary patterns and their association with anxiety and depression symptoms

Both the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin index (KMO = 0.710) and Bartlett’s test (p < 0.001) indicated that the correlations among the variables were sufficiently strong to support the conduct of a factor analysis. Our study identified four primary dietary patterns based on their corresponding factor loadings. The first, termed the egg-dairy pattern, was characterized by a predominance of eggs, dairy and dairy products, and seafood, with relatively less emphasis on refined grains. The second dietary pattern, referred to as the livestock-meat pattern, was distinguished by a predominance of red meat and poultry. The third pattern, identified as the whole grain pattern, is characterized by a prevalence of whole grains and tubers. Lastly, the fourth pattern, known as the vegetable-fruit pattern, was marked by a predominance of fruits and vegetables. The characteristics of the study participants, categorized by quartile of dietary pattern scores, are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Younger participants and individuals with high personal monthly incomes are more inclined towards the egg-dairy pattern and the livestock-meat pattern. In contrast, older participants and those engaged in moderate to high levels of physical activities tend to favor the whole-grain pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern.

The odds ratios for anxiety and depression symptoms, categorized by quartiles of each dietary pattern score, are shown in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, participants in the highest quartile of the egg-dairy pattern exhibited lower odds of anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.99, p = 0.016) compared to those in the lowest quartile. Additionally, model 2 indicated that participants in the highest quartile of the vegetable-fruit pattern also demonstrated lower odds of anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.96, p = 0.027) relative to those in the lowest quartile, however, the association was no longer significant after controlling for physical activity level and sedentary behavior. Similarly, participants in the highest quartile of the vegetable-fruit dietary pattern had a significantly lower odds of depression symptoms (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.61, p < 0.001) compared to those in the lowest quartile. Besides, no significant associations were observed between other dietary patterns and the risk of depression symptoms.

Discussion

Comprehending and addressing the psychological aspects of people living with HIV is essential not only for bolstering mental well-being but also for enhancing overall health outcomes [35, 36]. Our study found that the prevalence of anxiety symptoms among individuals living with HIV was 31.4%, which was significantly higher than the average prevalence of 15.5% observed in non-HIV populations [14]. This finding was consistent with the reported prevalence of 30.5% among people living with HIV/AIDS in Liangshan Prefecture, Sichuan, China [37]. The overall prevalence of depression symptoms within this population was 28.6%, a figure that was consistent with reports from most others studies involving individuals living with HIV [13, 37].

In our study, the majority of individuals living with HIV exhibited poor diet quality and significant dietary imbalances, particularly marked by inadequate consumption of whole grains, tubers, vegetables, fruits, seafood, eggs, dairy and dairy products, as well as soybeans and nuts. The present study identified four major dietary patterns among individuals living with HIV: the egg-dairy pattern, the livestock-meat pattern, the whole grain pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern. Our findings indicated that both the egg-dairy pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern were linked to a decreased risk of anxiety symptoms; the vegetable-fruit pattern was associated with a reduced risk of depression symptoms among individuals living with HIV.

Diet and nutrition are instrumental in the management of anxiety and depression disorders [18–21], a fact that holds especially true for people living with HIV [38]. High-quality diets have increasingly been acknowledged as a promising candidate to address the growing prevalence of anxiety and depression. A cross-sectional study found that a high intake of legumes, vegetables, and dairy products had a protective effect on the risk of depression among people living with HIV/AIDS [38]. The present study revealed that adherence to both the egg-dairy pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern was associated with a decreased risk of anxiety symptoms, while the vegetable-fruit pattern was further linked to a reduced risk of depression symptoms among individuals living with HIV. Numerous studies have shown that adherence to the Mediterranean diet, the plant-based diet, and the EAT-Lancet reference diet, which is characterized by high intakes of fruits, vegetables, fish, and whole grains, along with a limitation of red meat, added sugar and saturated fat, was associated with a reduced likelihood of experiencing anxiety and depression symptoms [18, 39–43]. High-quality proteins, along with tryptophan and B vitamins rich in eggs and dairy products, are essential for the production of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. These neurotransmitters play significant roles in regulating mood and emotional responses [44]. Vegetables and fruits are abundant sources of essential vitamins, minerals, antioxidant compounds, and dietary fiber [45], which may exert beneficial protective effects against anxiety and depression symptoms through reducing neuronal damage induced by oxidative stress and inhibiting neuroinflammatory processes [46–48].

The Western dietary pattern, characterized by high consumption of red or processed meat, refined grains, high-fat gravies and sugary foods, has been associated with an elevated risk of experiencing anxiety and depression symptoms [42, 49]. However, our present study did not find a statistically significant association between the livestock-meat pattern and experiencing the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms in individuals living with HIV. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that the majority of individuals living with HIV are in a state of malnutrition and nutritional risk stemming from the direct effects of the virus, side effects related to ART and socioeconomic factors [50–52]. Although food insecurity, encompassing concerns related to food quality and unhealthy diets, may lead to an increased inflammatory reaction due to poor HIV viral control [53], the consumption of red and processed meat was not significantly associated with inflammatory markers in individuals living with HIV [54]. Additionally, existing studies examining the relationship between the livestock-meat pattern and the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms have produced inconsistent findings [25, 49, 55].

Our study found no protective effect of the whole grain pattern against the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms among individuals living with HIV. This finding contrasts with existing evidence indicating that diets rich in whole grains may positively affect mood and mental well-being due to their high fiber content and antioxidant properties [39, 56]. Furthermore, there is currently limited research on whole grain intake among individuals living with HIV. Notably, our study found that 82.3% of participants had insufficient intake of whole grains and mixed beans, consistent with previous report [57]. This inadequate consumption may negatively affect the overall health outcomes of individuals living with HIV [58]. In summary, a well-balanced diet, achieved through nutritional interventions, is essential for managing HIV-specific symptoms, improving metal health, strengthening immune function, enhancing the effectiveness of ART, and ultimately contributing to an overall better quality of life [59].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to focus on the correlation between dietary patterns and health outcomes in people living with HIV. Furthermore, our findings suggest that adherence to the egg-dairy pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern exhibited a lower risk of experiencing anxiety and depression symptoms in this population. This study highlights the potential of promoting healthy dietary patterns as a strategic approach to improving health outcomes for individuals living with HIV.

Several potential limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting our findings. First, the sample for this study was only collected from a single city, Jinhua, using an accidental sampling method. This approach may limit the representativeness of the sample; therefore, the generalisability of the results should be interpreted with caution. Second, although we have controlled for several known confounding factors for the reliability of our analysis, it is important to note that our study design is a population-based cross-sectional study. This design limits the ability to clarify the causality between dietary patterns and the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms among people living with HIV. Third, the assessment of anxiety and depression symptoms was conducted using the HADS rather than a clinician-administered structured diagnostic interview, which may lead to potential overestimation or underestimation of the results. Finally, studying dietary patterns in relation to disease outcomes has significant public health implications, as it can guide the development of targeted nutrition interventions and educational initiatives. However, factor analysis, a widely used statistical technique for dimensionality reduction, presents challenges due to its reliance on subjective decisions [60]. Thus, further examination is required to determine the applicability of our findings to people living with HIV in other cities.

Conclusions

In summary, the population-based research indicated that variations in dietary patterns were associated with the likelihood of experiencing anxiety and depression symptoms among individuals living with HIV. Specifically, adherence to both the egg-dairy pattern and the vegetable-fruit pattern was found to be associated with a diminished risk of experiencing anxiety symptoms, while following a vegetable-fruit pattern was correlated with a reduced risk of experiencing depression symptoms in this population. Additionally, individuals living with HIV exhibited poor diet quality and significant dietary imbalances. The study highlights that diet is a modifiable risk factor for experiencing anxiety and depression symptoms, and also provides new insights for potential precision health interventions for individuals living with HIV. Further longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether a true causal association exists.
