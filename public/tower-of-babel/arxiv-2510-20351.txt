Evaluating Latent Knowledge of Public Tabular Datasets in Large Language Models
Matteo Silvestri, Flavio Giorgi, Fabrizio Silvestri, Gabriele Tolomei

Abstract
. Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly evaluated on their ability to reason over structured data, yet such assessments often overlook a crucial confound: dataset contamination. In this work, we investigate whether LLMs exhibit prior knowledge of widely used tabular benchmarks such as Adult Income, Titanic, and others. Through a series of controlled probing experiments, we reveal that contamination effects emerge exclusively for datasets containing strong semantic cues—for instance, meaningful column names or interpretable value categories. In contrast, when such cues are removed or randomized, performance sharply declines to near-random levels. These findings suggest that LLMs’ apparent competence on tabular reasoning tasks may, in part, reflect memorization of publicly available datasets rather than genuine generalization. We discuss implications for evaluation protocols and propose strategies to disentangle semantic leakage from authentic reasoning ability in future LLM assessments.



1.
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on unprecedented volumes of text—spanning trillions of tokens scraped from books, code repositories, web pages, and academic corpora. This scale of data ingestion enables impressive generalization across tasks, but it also obscures an important question: what exactly do these models already know? As LLMs increasingly intersect with machine learning research—being used to explainability, classification or regression tasks—the boundary between reasoning and recollection becomes alarmingly blurred.

In this work, we investigate a subtle yet critical issue:
dataset contamination
in LLMs, with a focus on widely used tabular benchmarks such as Adult Income, Titanic, and others. These datasets, publicly available for decades and extensively featured in tutorials, Kaggle competitions, and academic papers, are almost certainly present—explicitly or implicitly—in the massive corpora used to pretrain modern LLMs. As a result, apparent demonstrations of “reasoning over data” may instead reflect memorized statistical associations or schema-level cues absorbed during training.

Our motivation stems from the growing reliance on LLMs for tasks traditionally reserved for data scientists—such as feature importance attribution, classification or regeression tasks. If these models have prior exposure to benchmark datasets, their performance evaluations risk being inflated, undermining both reproducibility and the validity of claims about emergent reasoning. Through systematic probing and controlled testing, we aim to disentangle genuine reasoning from latent recall, revealing that contamination effects primarily emerge in datasets containing strong semantic cues—those where variable names or value labels align closely with natural language semantics.

Ultimately, our analysis contributes to a deeper understanding of how large-scale pretraining shapes apparent “intelligence” in LLMs, urging the community to adopt contamination-aware evaluation protocols for future benchmarks.

2.
Related Work

The problem of data contamination—unintended overlap between model training corpora and evaluation benchmarks—has become a central concern in large language model (LLM) research. Early work by
Brown et al. 2020
, introducing GPT-3, acknowledged this risk and proposed n-gram overlap filtering (e.g., 13-gram exclusion) to reduce leakage between training and test data. Follow-up analyses by
Dodge et al. 2021
showed that large-scale web-crawl datasets such as C4
(
Raffel et al. 2023
)
contained verbatim text from standard NLP benchmarks, confirming that contamination is both pervasive and difficult to eliminate through surface-level heuristics.

Subsequent empirical studies quantified the extent and impact of such leakage.
Magar and Schwartz 2022
formally distinguished between memorisation and exploitation of contaminated data, showing that even limited test-set duplication can inflate benchmark accuracy.
Bordt et al. 2024
later extended this line, revealing that contamination effects diminish only under extremely large-scale training—far beyond most research settings—thus reaffirming the need for explicit decontamination practices. Complementary large-scale analyses, such as the work by
Choi et al. [n. d.]
introduced the Kernel Divergence Score to measure the degree of contamination without direct access to training data, demonstrating strong correlations between low divergence and known overlaps.

Recent work has shifted toward detection and mitigation.
Golchin and Surdeanu 2023
proposed an instruction-based probing method that queries LLMs directly for specific benchmark instances, successfully identifying contaminated datasets even in commercial models like GPT-4.
Dong et al. 2024
introduced CDD (Contamination Detection via output Distribution) and TED (Trustworthy Evaluation via Decontamination), showing that high model confidence often signals prior exposure to test items and that post-hoc score correction can mitigate inflated results. Surveys such as
(
Fu et al. 2025
)
and
(
Cheng et al. 2025
)
consolidate these findings, highlighting the limitations of both heuristic n-gram filters and membership inference methods when applied to modern LLMs.

Beyond text, similar phenomena have been documented in code generation and vision–language models.
Riddell et al. 2024
revealed that benchmark programming tasks are often duplicated in pretraining corpora, inflating model accuracy, while
Lu et al. 2024
showed that image–text models can benefit from prior exposure to test images, motivating the use of dynamically refreshed benchmarks. These findings establish contamination as a cross-modal risk intrinsic to large-scale pretraining.

However, tabular data remain largely overlooked in this discourse. Despite their centrality in classical machine learning, benchmarks such as Adult Income, or Titanic, are often publicly available in unchanged form for years, making them especially susceptible to leakage through web-scraped pretraining data. Recent discussions (e.g.,
Kothiya 2025
) have warned that pretrained tabular and forecasting models may inadvertently include these datasets, but systematic analyses are scarce. Our work addresses this gap by investigating contamination effects in tabular benchmarks, distinguishing between syntactic (exact record overlap) and semantic (schema- or feature-level) leakage, and assessing how such contamination biases LLM-based reasoning evaluations.

3.
Background and Problem Definition

3.1.
Data Contamination in LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on massive text corpora collected from heterogeneous web sources, research repositories, and community-contributed datasets. While this extensive pretraining enables impressive generalisation, it also raises a critical methodological concern: dataset contamination, i.e., the unintentional inclusion of benchmark data within the training corpus.

We define dataset contamination as the presence of examples, labels, or metadata from evaluation datasets in the pretraining data of a model. Contamination can manifest in several forms:

•

Syntactic contamination
refers to the verbatim or near-verbatim inclusion of dataset entries, tables, or documentation. For instance, if the “Adult Income” dataset appears in tutorials online, its rows may be directly memorised.

•

Semantic contamination
arises when the model has been exposed not to the exact data but to descriptions, summaries, or derived representations (e.g., “people earning over 50K in the Adult dataset are often men in tech jobs”). Such contamination transmits conceptual patterns or correlations even without textual overlap.

Furthermore, contamination can be partial, affecting only subsets or summaries of the dataset, or full, when the complete dataset or its labelled distribution is available during training.

These distinctions are crucial because syntactic contamination leads to memorisation, whereas semantic contamination induces spurious familiarity, both of which can inflate downstream performance in evaluation tasks.

3.2.
Why Tabular Data Are a Unique Case

Tabular datasets differ fundamentally from natural language corpora. They consist of structured, schema-driven data, organised into columns (features) and rows (instances), that lack the contextual richness of free text. Each attribute (e.g., age, education, occupation, fare) encodes discrete, interpretable information rather than narrative content.

This structure presents a paradox for LLMs. On one hand, tabular data are not inherently linguistic, suggesting limited pretraining exposure. On the other hand, many popular benchmark datasets (e.g., Titanic, Adult Income) are widely distributed online in tutorials, GitHub repositories, and teaching materials. These datasets also include semantic cues (e.g., feature names or categorical values with intuitive real-world meanings) that can allow an LLM to leverage prior knowledge even in the absence of memorised entries. Thus, tabular datasets provide a controlled and revealing testbed for probing contamination mechanisms.

3.3.
Threats to Validity in LLM Evaluation

Dataset contamination poses a severe threat to validity in the empirical evaluation of LLMs. When a model achieves high accuracy on a benchmark dataset that it has partially seen during pretraining, the result does not reflect reasoning or generalisation but rather the retrieval of known patterns. This undermines claims about interpretability, causal reasoning, and zero-shot learning, core metrics in current AI research.

For tabular reasoning tasks, contamination can artificially inflate performance on tasks such as feature imputation, classification, or counterfactual reasoning. In effect, contamination obscures the boundary between knowledge and reasoning, making it difficult to assess whether LLMs genuinely infer from structured data or merely reproduce prior exposures.

3.4.
Research Questions

To systematically investigate this issue, we frame our study around the following research questions:

•

RQ1
: To what extent do LLMs recall specific entries from benchmark tabular datasets?

•

RQ2
: Does contamination primarily occur through semantic cues (feature and label meanings) or exact memorisation of data entries?

4.
Methodology

We designed an experimental framework to systematically assess both syntactic and semantic contamination effects in Large Language Models (LLMs) when interacting with structured tabular data. Our primary goal was to disentangle cases where model performance arises from memorisation of known records or column, i.e., value patterns (syntactic contamination) versus reliance on learned associations tied to domain semantics or feature names (semantic contamination).

Contamination Assessment Tasks

We implemented two complementary tasks designed to probe potential contamination at different levels of data representation: a completion task and an existence task.

(i)

Completion Task
. For each record, we randomly masked 20% of the available attributes, ensuring a balanced mix of numerical and categorical features. Masked variables were selected among the top four features with the highest entropy (categorical) or variance (numerical). For each masked attribute, we provided the model with five candidate values: the true value and four distractors independently sampled from the marginal distribution of that feature. This task evaluates whether the model can correctly complete a known record, potentially reflecting memorized associations between feature combinations. We use it as indicator of syntactic contamination.

(ii)

Existence Task
. This complementary setting examines whether the model recognizes authenticity rather than completing missing data. Here, 20% of the features within each instance were randomly perturbed by replacing their values with independent samples from the corresponding marginal distributions. The model was then shown five versions of the same record (one genuine and four perturbed) and asked to identify the original entry. This setup tests whether the LLM can discriminate between real and synthetic samples, thereby revealing potential memorization of exact tuples or high-level structural familiarity.

Synthetic and Obfuscated Variants

To distinguish contamination from genuine reasoning, we further introduced two controlled modifications of each dataset:

(i)

Like (Synthetic Marginal) Dataset
.
A synthetic variant generated by sampling each feature independently from the empirical marginal distributions of the real data. This dataset preserves low-level statistical properties but eliminates any inter-feature dependencies, thereby neutralizing syntactic memorization while retaining superficial distributions.

(ii)

Obfuscated Dataset
. A semantically neutral version preserving all numeric values and statistical correlations but systematically relabeling categorical attributes with abstract symbols (e.g., c01, c02, c03). This design disrupts all domain-level semantics (e.g., “occupation,” “sex,” “fare”), preventing the model from leveraging prior contextual or linguistic knowledge about feature meanings. Performance drops in this condition indicate reliance on semantic contamination.

Results

As summarised in
Table

1
, our experiments reveal a clear divide between semantic and non-semantic datasets in terms of potential data contamination. Remarkably, evidence of contamination emerges exclusively in the completion task, and only for the three most widely known semantic datasets: Adult Income, Titanic, and Default of Credit Card Clients. In these cases, all evaluated models achieved accuracies substantially above the random-guess baseline (
\approx 20\%
), suggesting that the models likely possess semantic-level familiarity with these datasets. This behaviour plausibly stems from indirect exposure to publicly available resources, tutorials, or benchmark reports frequently referencing these datasets across academic and online platforms.

By contrast, the existence task, which directly tests for the memorisation of specific records, was consistently failed across all models and datasets. This systematic failure indicates that none of the examined LLMs (
llama_8B
,
mistral_7B
, and
qwen
up to 32B) exhibit clear syntactic contamination or verbatim memorisation of data instances. Instead, the observed contamination appears semantic in nature, emerging only when the task can be solved through contextual reasoning or prior conceptual associations rather than through direct recall. This interpretation is further supported by the models’ high completion accuracy even on “like” variants of the adult and credit datasets, where variable names and categorical labels were obfuscated. Such resilience suggests that LLMs internalised abstract relational structures or co-occurrence patterns from prior exposure, rather than memorising surface text.

For the remaining semantic but less prominent datasets (mushroom and blood) as well as for the non-semantic datasets (gamma and synthetic), model performance remained consistently at or near random levels in both tasks. These results imply that without widespread dissemination or explicit semantic anchoring, LLMs lack sufficient prior knowledge to derive meaningful inferences.

An intriguing exception arises in the credit dataset, where some models maintain non-trivial accuracy even in the obfuscated variant. We hypothesise that this effect reflects the models’ ability to exploit latent statistical regularities within the tabular structure, an instance of what recent literature terms emergent reasoning abilities
(
Berti et al. 2025
)
, rather than memorised contamination.

Overall, our findings provide clear answers to the two research questions.
Regarding RQ1, the systematic failure of all models in the existence task indicates that LLMs do not recall specific entries from benchmark tabular datasets. This suggests that, at least for models up to 32B parameters, there is no evidence of verbatim memorisation or syntactic contamination at the instance level.
Concerning RQ2, the strong and consistent performance observed in the completion task for canonical datasets such as adult, titanic, and credit demonstrates that contamination primarily occurs through semantic channels, that is, through learned associations grounded in the meaning of features and labels rather than direct memorisation. These models appear to leverage contextual and conceptual knowledge acquired during pretraining, enabling them to infer plausible values when dataset semantics are familiar.

In summary, the results highlight a critical distinction: LLMs (used in this work) do not remember data, but they remember meaning. This semantic familiarity, while not a form of direct leakage, can still artificially inflate performance on benchmark datasets that are widely discussed or replicated online. Therefore, future evaluations of LLMs on tabular reasoning tasks should include less disseminated or synthetic datasets to ensure that measured performance reflects genuine reasoning rather than latent semantic contamination.

6.
Limitations and Future Work

Our study provides an initial yet informative exploration of contamination effects in LLMs when interacting with structured tabular data. Nonetheless, several limitations constrain the generalizability of our conclusions.
First, our experiments were conducted on models up to 32B parameters. While this range includes strong contemporary architectures, larger-scale models, exceeding 70B or even 100B parameters, may exhibit distinct contamination dynamics due to their increased memorization capacity and broader pretraining corpora.
Second, the scope of evaluated datasets remains limited. Expanding both the number and diversity of benchmarks, particularly by incorporating more widely used and non-semantic datasets, would enable a clearer separation between genuine reasoning ability and dataset familiarity effects.

Future work will aim to extend these analyses along multiple dimensions. A primary direction involves evaluating larger LLMs to test whether contamination patterns scale with model size or data exposure. Moreover, we plan to categorize datasets according to their empirically observed contamination level. For example, distinguishing contaminated from non-contaminated benchmarks—and assess how this classification impacts LLM performance across various machine learning tasks. Such an approach would enable a deeper understanding of whether contamination merely affects recall or also biases reasoning and generalization. Ultimately, this line of work could contribute to establishing standardized contamination-aware benchmarks for tabular data evaluation in LLMs.

7.
Conclusions

Our study provides empirical evidence of data contamination in well-known benchmark datasets, such as Adult Income and Titanic, when evaluated with large language models up to 32B parameters. The contamination observed is predominantly semantic, indicating that models leverage prior knowledge about feature meanings and contextual associations rather than directly memorising tabular entries. This finding highlights the subtle yet significant ways in which pretraining exposure can influence performance on structured data tasks. Beyond the specific results, our work aims to raise awareness and provide a methodological foundation for more transparent and contamination-aware evaluation pipelines of LLMs in tabular reasoning and prediction tasks, promoting fairer and more reproducible future research.
