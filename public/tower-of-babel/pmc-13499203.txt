Increased respiratory pathogen positivity and diversity in hospitalized children following the COVID-19 pandemic in Malaysia
Authors: Naim Che-Kamaruddin, Siti Naqiah Hudari, Norziha Zainul Abidin, Jamuna Jairaman, Anis Siham Zainal Abidin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory tract infections are a major cause of pediatric hospitalization. Post-COVID-19 pandemic, atypical surges and disrupted respiratory pathogen circulation underscore the need for continuous surveillance.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed respiratory pathogen detection among hospitalized children aged 28 days to 18 years at the largest quaternary hospital in Malaysia from January 2018 to May 2023. The study period was classified into pre-pandemic (2018–2019), pandemic (2020–2021), and post-pandemic (2022–2023) phases. Respiratory pathogens were identified using the FilmArray BioFire® Respiratory Panel 2.1.ResultsAmong 20,556 hospitalized children, 7707 (37.5%) underwent BioFire® testing. Overall pathogen positivity remained similar during the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods (50.4% and 50.9%, respectively) and increased to 86.2% post-pandemic. Single-pathogen infections increased from 46.4% to 43.1% to 64.6%, while co-infections rose from 4.0% to 7.9% to 21.6%, respectively. Human rhinovirus/enterovirus and respiratory syncytial virus were consistently detected, whereas adenovirus and influenza viruses increased significantly post-pandemic. Influenza A and B re-emerged after minimal detection during the pandemic, returning to pre-pandemic detection rates. Monthly positivity varied substantially before and during the pandemic but remained consistently high post-pandemic, peaking at 94.4%. Despite increased pathogen detection, pediatric intensive care unit admissions remained low across all periods.ConclusionSubstantial post-pandemic increases in respiratory pathogen positivity, co-infections, and pathogen diversity were observed among hospitalized children despite stable pediatric intensive care unit admission rates. These findings likely reflect respiratory pathogen re-emergence following relaxation of non-pharmaceutical interventions, altered exposure patterns, and increased BioFire® testing after the COVID-19 pandemic.Keywords: Co-infection, COVID-19 pandemic, Multiplex PCR, Pediatric intensive care unit, Respiratory pathogen surveillance

Introduction

Respiratory tract infections are among the most common illnesses affecting children and contribute substantially to the global healthcare burden due to their high morbidity and mortality [1]. A wide range of pathogens can cause these infections, with viruses accounting for 60–70% of cases [2,3]. Common respiratory viruses, including influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human rhinovirus (hRV), and human metapneumovirus (hMPV) frequently co-circulate within communities. The emergence of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) profoundly altered human behavior during the pandemic, largely through the implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) aimed at limiting transmission [4]. Increased hygiene awareness and changes in social interactions [5] further reduced exposure to circulating pathogens, subsequently reshaping population immunity, and influencing the circulation dynamics of other respiratory viruses.

Respiratory viruses exhibit distinct seasonal circulation patterns that vary globally, influenced by environmental factors such as temperature and humidity, as well as population characteristics including density, indoor behaviors, and vaccination coverage. In temperate regions like Europe and North America, viruses such as RSV and influenza typically peak during winter, when low temperature and humidity favor aerosol persistence and surface stability [6]. Tropical regions, including parts of Southeast Asia, show less-defined seasonality, with peaks often aligning with rainy seasons when higher humidity facilitates contact transmission, though year-round circulation is common [7]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, widespread NPIs, including social distancing, masking, and travel restrictions, sharply disrupted these seasonal patterns. In Malaysia, national lockdowns such as the Movement Control Order (MCO) began on 18 March 2020, followed by Conditional MCO (CMCO) in May 2020 and Recovery MCO (RMCO) in June 2020, with restrictions gradually lifted by late 2021 and full normalization by mid-2022 as vaccination coverage increased [8,9]. As a major Southeast Asian business hub along the Malacca Strait, with strong travel links particularly to China, Malaysia remains at elevated risk of respiratory pathogen importation, underscoring the need for ongoing multi-pathogen surveillance post-NPI relaxation.

Stringent NPIs during the pandemic substantially reduced the circulation of endemic respiratory viruses. This reduction in population exposure may have increase the number of susceptible individuals after restrictions were lifted, a phenomenon commonly referred to as “immunity debt.” This temporary deficit in naturally acquired immunity have also been suggested to increase susceptibility to respiratory pathogens, particularly among infants and young children with immature or waning maternal antibodies, once restrictions were lifted [[10], [11], [12]]. Additional hypotheses, including transient immune alterations following SARS-CoV-2 infection (“immunity theft”), have also been proposed to explain post-pandemic changes in respiratory pathogen epidemiology [13,14]. In parallel, viral interference, whereby infection with one virus induces an interferon-mediated antiviral state (e.g., hRV inhibiting replication of other co-circulating viruses) may also contribute to shifts in viral ecology and disrupted seasonality during NPI periods [[15], [16], [17]]. However, these mechanisms remain under investigation and were not evaluated in the present study. Therefore, this study focuses on describing temporal changes in respiratory pathogen detection rather than investigating the underlying immunological mechanisms.

To examine whether similar epidemiological shifts occurred in Malaysian children, this study retrospectively analyzed multiplex nucleic acid test results from 20,556 hospitalized children with respiratory symptoms in an urban quaternary hospital in Kuala Lumpur. We aimed to characterize the temporal distribution of respiratory pathogens across the pre-, pandemic, and post-COVID-19 pandemic periods and to examine their association with clinical outcomes, including total length of hospital stay, intensive care unit admissions, and patterns of multiple viral infections over time.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

Ethical approval for the present study was obtained from the Sunway Medical Research Ethics Committee (SREC) Reference No. 040/2024/IND/ER. De-identified patient data extracted from hospital medical records were retrospectively analyzed. The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population and inclusion criteria

This retrospective observational study analyzed respiratory pathogen data from pediatric patients aged 28 days to 18 years who presented with respiratory symptoms to Sunway Medical Centre, Kuala Lumpur, over a six-year period from January 2018 to May 2023. The study timeframe was categorized into three phases: pre-COVID-19 pandemic (2018-2019), COVID-19 pandemic (2020-2021), and post-COVID-19 pandemic (2022-2023).

Sunway Medical Centre is the largest quaternary hospital in Malaysia, with 146 pediatric beds, a specialized Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), and a dedicated Children's Emergency Department. It is a major private pediatric referral center and a leading provider of specialized care for children and adolescents in the country.

Data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records, comprising 44,476 pediatric admissions during the study period. Of these, 20,556 hospital admissions involved children presenting with respiratory symptoms and fever (defined as ≥37.5°C). The study was conducted at the hospital admission level, with each admission initially considered a separate clinical episode. However, to minimize overrepresentation of prolonged or recurrent detection, repeated positive test results for the same pathogen in the same patient within six months were considered part of the same infectious episode and excluded from pathogen-specific analyses.

Syndromic nucleic acid multiplex tests

Nasopharyngeal swabs (NPS) were collected upon admission and processed according to the manufacturer's instructions. BioFire® testing was performed at the discretion of the treating clinician as part of routine clinical care and was not used as a universal screening test for all hospitalized children with respiratory symptoms. During the COVID-19 pandemic, patients underwent routine SARS-CoV-2 screening according to institutional protocols, while BioFire® testing remained clinician-directed and was requested when multiplex respiratory pathogen testing was considered clinically indicated. Although no formal institutional policy governing BioFire® use changed during the study period, its clinical utilization increased following the pandemic. The present study included only hospitalized pediatric patients; outpatient and emergency department testing not resulting in hospital admission was excluded. Samples were tested using the FilmArray Respiratory Panel 2.1 (BioFire®, bioMérieux Italia S.p.A., Florence, Italy), a fully automated, qualitative real-time nested multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay that simultaneously detects 22 respiratory viruses and bacteria from a single NPS. The assay has an overall sensitivity of 97.1% and a specificity of 99.3%. A positive result indicates the presence of the detected pathogen, and multiple pathogens may be identified within the same sample. Test results were automatically synchronized with the hospital's electronic medical record system.

Timeline of COVID-19 key events in Malaysia

Malaysia reported its first COVID-19 case in January 2020 [8]. A major outbreak linked to a religious gathering in Sri Petaling, Kuala Lumpur in late February to early March 2020 triggered widespread community transmission and led to the implementation of a nationwide Movement Control Order (MCO) on 18 March 2020 [8]. Restrictions were subsequently adjusted in phases, transitioning to the Conditional MCO (CMCO) in May 2020 and the Recovery MCO (RMCO) in June 2020 as the country sought to balance outbreak control with economic activity.

By September 2021, 80% of adults had completed vaccination, contributing to reductions in severe disease and mortality. In early 2022, Malaysia experienced a large Omicron-driven wave, characterized by high transmissibility but lower clinical severity relative to the Delta variant. Although case numbers increased substantially, widespread vaccination and prior natural infections effectively limited hospitalizations and deaths [9]. By the end of 2021, Malaysia had officially reported approximately 2.75 million cases and 31,485 deaths; however, seroprevalence data indicate that about 32.8% of the population had been infected, suggesting substantial under-ascertainment of mild or asymptomatic cases [18]. By mid-2022, Malaysia had transitioned to a post-pandemic phase with the reopening of economic sectors and continued surveillance for emerging variants.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses of patient demographics, pathogen positivity rates, co-infection status, hospitalization patterns, and length of stay were stratified by COVID-19 periods: pre-pandemic (2018–2019), pandemic (2020–2021), post-pandemic (2022–2023), and the overall study period (2018–2023). To minimize overrepresentation of prolonged or recurrent detection, repeated positive BioFire® results for the same pathogen in the same patient within six months were considered part of the same infectious episode and excluded from the analysis. Positive detections occurring more than six months apart or involving different pathogens were considered independent events.

Age at admission was grouped into infants (<1 year), toddlers (1-4 years), primary school-aged children (5-12 years), and adolescents (>12 years). Pathogen positivity counts and pathogen-specific detection rates were analyzed independently for each pathogen, including instances where they appeared as part of co-infections. Temporal trends in respiratory pathogen detection were visualized using stacked area plots. Pathogen-specific detection rates were further stratified by admission type (general ward vs. PICU) to compare pathogen distribution across clinical severity levels.

Heatmaps were generated to visualize pathogen-specific detection rates and the frequency of pathogen co-infections across the study period, enabling identification of commonly co-detected pathogens. Related pathogens were grouped for analysis, for example, Influenza A subtypes (A/H1, A/H3, A/H1-2009) were combined under “Influenza A,” and Parainfluenza virus types 1-4 were grouped under “Parainfluenza Virus (1-4).”

For the stacked area plots and pathogen-specific heatmaps, monthly pathogen-specific detection rates were calculated by dividing the number of BioFire®-tested hospital admissions with each detected pathogen by the total number of BioFire®-tested hospital admissions in the corresponding month. Hospital admissions with multiple pathogens contributed to the detection rate of each detected pathogen; therefore, the summed pathogen-specific detection rates could exceed 100%.

Comparisons of categorical variables across periods were performed using Fisher's exact test due to small sample cell counts. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test due to non-normal data distributions. To assess variability in monthly positivity rates while accounting for differing test volumes, coefficients of variation were calculated using both raw positivity rates and arcsine–square-root–transformed proportions (variance-stabilized). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported for all estimates. All analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R version 4.2.1 with appropriate statistical packages.

Results

Demographic characteristics of children hospitalized with respiratory symptoms and fever

A total of 20,556 hospital admissions involving pediatric patients from January 2018 to May 2023 were included in the analysis. The cohort was majority male (55.1%), and this distribution remained consistent across the pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods (p = 0.34). The median age at admission was 3.1 years (range, 28 days-18.8 years). Among all admissions, 7707 (37.5%) underwent multiplex respiratory panel (BioFire®) testing. The age and sex distributions in the tested subgroup closely mirrored those of the overall cohort. Length of hospitalization did not vary substantially over time, with a median stay of 2 days in all three periods. More than 99% of patients were admitted to the general pediatric ward, with a small and stable proportion requiring pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) care throughout the study duration (Table 1).

Respiratory pathogen positivity across study periods

Respiratory pathogen detection patterns varied across the three COVID-19 pandemic phases. The overall positivity rate for at least one pathogen was comparable between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods (50.4% and 50.9%, respectively). In contrast, the positivity rate increased markedly in the post-pandemic pandemic period to 86.2%. A similar pattern was observed for single infections, where the prevalence was highest post-pandemic (64.6%), compared with lower proportions detected during the pre-pandemic (46.4%) and pandemic (43.1%) periods. Multiple infections involving two or more pathogens demonstrated a clear rising trend over time, particularly after the onset of the post-pandemic period. The maximum number of pathogens detected concurrently in a single BioFire®-tested hospital admission was five (Table 1).

Respiratory pathogen temporal positivity trends

A total of 7707 BioFire® tests were performed across the study period. Of these, 949 tests were conducted during the pre-pandemic period (2018–2019), 613 during the pandemic period (2020–2021), and 6145 during the post-pandemic period (2022–2023), corresponding to 21, 24, and 17 months of observation, respectively (Table 2). The period-level mean monthly positivity rate was 50.6% (95% CI: 38.0-63.2) in the pre-pandemic period and 50.2% (95% CI: 36.1-64.4) during the pandemic period. In the post-pandemic period, the mean monthly positivity rate increased to 85.8% (95% CI: 82.8–88.7). Monthly variability differed substantially across periods; the coefficient of variation (CV) was high in both the pre-pandemic (58.2%) and pandemic periods (70.5%), reflecting considerable month-to-month fluctuation in monthly positivity rates during periods with lower monthly testing volumes. In contrast, the post-pandemic period showed lower variability (CV = 7.3%), consistent with persistently high positivity rates and larger monthly testing volumes. This lower variability remained unchanged after applying a variance-stabilizing (arcsine-square-root) transformation, indicating that it likely represents a true epidemiological pattern rather than an artifact of differing sample sizes.

Respiratory pathogen-specific trends across the study periods

Pathogen-specific detection rates varied across the pre-pandemic (2018–2019), pandemic (2020–2021), and post-pandemic (2022–2023) periods. In the pre-pandemic period, Mycoplasma pneumoniae accounted for the highest proportion of BioFire®-tested hospital admissions (21.8%), followed by Influenza A (7.3%), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) (6.3%), human Rhinovirus/Enterovirus (hRV/EV) (5.4%), and Influenza B (4.7%), respectively.

During the pandemic period, hRV/EV (22.5%) and RSV (12.2%) were the most frequently detected pathogens, whereas detection of Mycoplasma pneumonia decreased to 6.9%. Influenza A detection declined to 3.9%, while influenza B was not detected during this period.

In the post-pandemic period, detection of multiple respiratory pathogens increased. hRV/EV remained the most frequently detected pathogen (32.4%), followed by RSV (17.9%) and adenovirus (17.8%). Influenza A and B re-emerged, accounting for 9.0% and 4.9% of BioFire®-tested hospital admissions, respectively, reaching levels comparable to or exceeding those observed pre-pandemic. These temporal changes are illustrated in the stacked area plot (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 1).

Monthly respiratory pathogen diversity and persistence

Heatmap visualization of monthly pathogen detection rates demonstrated temporal variation in both pathogen diversity and detection rates (Fig. 5). During the pandemic period, pathogen detection was largely limited to adenovirus, HRV/EV, RSV, and Mycoplasma pneumonia. SARS-CoV-2 was first detected in BioFire®-tested hospital admissions in January 2020 and remained detectable thereafter, contributing to a relatively small proportion of total respiratory pathogen detections throughout the study period.

Heatmap of monthly pathogen-specific detection rates among BioFire®-tested hospital admissions across the study period. Each row corresponds to a respiratory pathogen, and each column to a calendar month. Color intensity reflects the magnitude of the pathogen-specific detection rate, with darker shades indicating higher detection rates. Vertical reference lines indicate the pre-pandemic (2018-2019), pandemic (2020-2021), and post-pandemic (2022-2023) periods, highlighting temporal changes in respiratory pathogen circulation.

In the post-pandemic period, a broader range of respiratory pathogens was detected across consecutive months. HRV/EV, RSV, and adenovirus showed sustained detection, while seasonal human coronaviruses (229E, HKU1, and OC43), which demonstrated low detection rates before and during the pandemic (0.1–1.5%), appeared more frequently post-pandemic (0.2–2.6%). Influenza A and B showed minimal detection during the pandemic but re-emerged with seasonal peaks after 2022. Parainfluenza viruses (types 1–4) remained at low overall detection rates across all periods, with intermittent increases observed post-pandemic. Detection of Mycoplasma pneumonia was highest in the pre-pandemic period (21.8%) and remained low thereafter (0.1%).

Respiratory pathogen co-infection patterns

A total of 1413 co-infection episodes were identified, defined as the detection of two or more respiratory pathogens within the same BioFire®-tested hospital admission. Co-infections increased over time, with 4.0% (n = 38/949) of BioFire®-tested hospital admission exhibiting co-infections during the pre-pandemic period and 7.8% (n = 48/613) during the pandemic period, followed by a substantial increase to 21.6% (n = 1328/6145) cases in the post-pandemic period. hRV/EV was the most frequently detected pathogen in co-infection episodes and was commonly identified in combination with adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and parainfluenza viruses. The proportion of hRV/EV–adenovirus co-infections reached up to 30% during the pandemic period, representing the highest co-infection proportion observed in the study. Co-infections involving RSV and adenovirus also increased across the study periods, rising from 3.4% in the pre-pandemic period to 11.7% during the pandemic and 9.4% in the post-pandemic period. Overall, the temporal increase in co-infection frequency coincided with the rising circulation of respiratory pathogens observed in the post-pandemic period (Fig. 6).

Respiratory pathogen positivity in PICU vs ward admissions

Pathogen-specific detection rates differed between pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) and ward admissions across the study periods. During the pre-pandemic period, hRV/EV (33.3%), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) (33.3%), and parainfluenza viruses (33.3%) had higher detection rates among PICU admissions than among ward admissions; however, these differences were not statistically significant.

During the pandemic period, SARS-CoV-2 was detected more frequently among BioFire®-tested PICU admissions than among BioFire®-tested ward admissions (20.6% vs 1.6%). In contrast, hRV/EV had the highest detection rate among ward admissions during this period (22.7%) compared to other respiratory pathogens.

In the post-pandemic period, a wider range of respiratory pathogens was observed among PICU admissions, with RSV (27.4%, n = 17/62), rhinovirus/enterovirus (22.6%, n = 14/62), and adenovirus (21.0%, n = 13/62) representing the most frequently detected pathogens. Influenza A was more commonly detected among ward admissions than PICU admissions in the post-pandemic period (7.2% vs 0.0%, p < 0.05). Additional pathogens, including influenza B, human metapneumovirus, and coronavirus 229E, were detected among PICU admissions during the post-pandemic period, although their distributions did not differ significantly from those observed in ward admissions (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study retrospectively analyzed respiratory pathogen trends across three COVID-19 pandemic periods, pre-pandemic (2018-2019), pandemic (2020-2021), and post-pandemic (2022-2023) in hospitalized pediatric patients with respiratory symptoms and fever.

We observed marked shifts in the circulation of respiratory pathogens across these phases. Following the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 and the declaration of a global pandemic on March 11, 2020, extensive non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) were implemented to curb transmission. During the pandemic, reduced community circulation of common pathogens may have led to waning population-level immunity and growing pool of susceptible individuals [19]. Prolonged reduction in community-acquired pathogen exposure may have reduced population-level exposure to common respiratory pathogens [20], particularly in adults who subsequently transmitted infections to young children. This may have contributed to increased susceptibility to respiratory pathogens once restrictions were lifted. A higher respiratory pathogens positivity rate among hospitalized children who underwent BioFire® testing was observed in our cohort during the post-pandemic period, accompanied by atypical and asynchronous seasonal peaks. Although changes in testing practices may have contributed to the magnitude of the observed increase, the temporal pattern is consistent with global reports describing the re-emergence of respiratory pathogens following relaxation of NPIs [21]. The concept of “immunity debt” was first introduced in 2021 to explain unusually large epidemics observed following extended periods of low viral and bacterial circulation [20]. Contributing factors may include reduced maternal exposure to respiratory infections such as RSV, resulting in lower transplacental antibody transfer to infants [10,12], and disruptions to routine immunization programs that further expanded the pool of susceptible individuals during the pandemic. In this context, preventive strategies such as vaccination, particularly seasonal influenza immunization, remain essential for limiting transmission and reducing infection severity, especially for influenza B [22].

In our cohort, the rebound of influenza occurred at temporal peaks distinct from pre-pandemic patterns, suggesting a possible contribution of immunity debts on viral circulation. Influenza A and B rebounded post-pandemic and returned to positivity rates comparable to pre-pandemic levels, unlike several other respiratory pathogens that exhibited exponential increases. Influenza circulation is heavily shaped by antigenic drift, and the reduction in influenza circulation during the pandemic likely led to evolutionary bottleneck, limiting opportunities for mutation and antigenic evolution [23]. The atypical timing of influenza peaks in our data aligns with observations from other post-lockdown settings [19,[24], [25], [26]].

NPIs such as gathering restrictions, stay-at-home orders, and universal masking effectively reduced COVID-19 incidence [27], although their impact differed substantially across respiratory pathogens. Non-enveloped viruses such as hRV, hEV and adenovirus exhibited high resilience due to their structural stability and lower sensitivity to environmental conditions and disinfectants [28]. Transmission of non-enveloped viruses appears to have increased following school reopening and increased social interaction, consistent with our findings. Co-infections involving rhinovirus, enterovirus and adenovirus were frequent, consistent with previous studies showing high codetection rates [24,[29], [30], [31]].

Despite widespread SARS-CoV-2 circulation, we observed a relatively low co-infection rate with other respiratory pathogens. Although multiplex PCR assays detect SARS-CoV-2 regardless of symptom severity, many pediatric SARS-CoV-2 infections are reported to be asymptomatic or mild in community setting [32]; thus, asymptomatic infections are less likely to result in hospitalization [32,33]. Co-infection was not associated with increased proportion of PICU admissions in our cohort, consistent with previous studies reporting no consistent association between pediatric respiratory pathogen co-infections and severe clinical outcomes, including PICU admission, mechanical ventilation, or mortality. Similarly, although increased BioFire® testing after the pandemic may have influenced overall positivity estimates, it is less likely to fully account for the marked shifts in pathogen composition and seasonality observed across the study periods. Nonetheless, certain pathogens, particularly RSV and influenza, remain major drivers of pediatric hospitalization due to their higher virulence and prevalence [[34], [35], [36]].

Post-pandemic, the prevalence of co-infections increased, highlighting the potential for viral interference, particularly with hRV dominance. Viral interference is supported by elevated interferon levels during hRV infection, which activate innate immune responses against other respiratory pathogens, and vice versa for other RNA viruses, including influenza, RSV, parainfluenza, coronaviruses, and human metapneumovirus [37]. Early in the pandemic, studies suggested that hRV infection induces strong interferon responses that may temporarily reduce susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2, supported by modeling analyses [15] and in vitro data showing reduced SARS-CoV-2 infectivity in hRV-primed airway epithelium [16]. hRV activates robust type I and III interferon responses via RIG-I and MDA5 signaling; however, these pathways are developmentally attenuated in infants [38]. Young children have lower baseline interferon activity and require stronger viral stimulation to mount comparable responses, which may contribute to the reduced effectiveness of hRV-mediated interference in this age group [17].

hRV/EV was also frequently co-detected with adenovirus, RSV, and parainfluenza viruses. This pattern suggests that these viruses possess immune evasion strategies sufficient to overcome the interferon-rich environment induced by hRV. Adenoviruses express E1A/E1B and E3 proteins that antagonize interferon pathways [39]; parainfluenza viruses encode C proteins that inhibit STAT1/STAT2 signaling [40]; and RSV uses NS1 and NS2 proteins to disrupt RIG-I/MDA5 and STAT2 activity [41]. These mechanisms likely facilitate simultaneous replication despite strong innate immune activation and may have implications for disease pathogenesis in pediatrics.

Theoretical models suggest that immunity debt is transient, as immunity to most respiratory viruses’ wanes within weeks to a few months [42,43]. The reduced exposure to respiratory viruses during the pandemic is expected to restore normal population immunity within one to three respiratory seasons once circulation resumes. Thus, although immunity debt contributed to the post-pandemic surge and temporarily strained healthcare systems, their impact is not anticipated to persist long-term. Although population-level immunity theft likely to have contributed substantially to the observed rebound, other factors, including transient immunological effects following COVID-19 may also contribute. Acute COVID-19 can induce lymphopenia, T-cell exhaustion, and innate immune dysregulation [13], which may temporarily increase susceptibility to other infections. However, current evidence suggests that these effects are temporary and unlikely to be a major driver of the large epidemiological changes observed following NPIs relaxation, a pattern described across age groups at the population-level [14,25,26,44]. Although transient immune effects may occur at the individual level, particularly after acute infection, post-pandemic surge in respiratory infections are more consistently attributed to population-level changes in exposure and susceptibility associated with prolonged NPIs.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and reliance on electronic medical records limited the availability of detailed clinical information, including prior respiratory infections, vaccination status, baseline immunity, oxygen requirement, respiratory support, mechanical ventilation, and comorbidities, thereby limiting a more comprehensive assessment of disease severity and individual susceptibility. Second, the study period spanned January 2018 to May 2023, resulting in unequal durations across the pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic phases. Third, the proportion of hospitalized children undergoing BioFire® testing increased substantially over time, introducing potential selection bias due to changes in testing indications and clinical practice. Consequently, comparisons of overall BioFire® positivity rates across study periods should be interpreted with caution, although the marked temporal shifts in pathogen composition and seasonality are unlikely to be explained solely by increased testing. Fourth, because the BioFire® multiplex PCR assay detects pathogen nucleic acids rather than viable organisms, positive results may represent residual nucleic acid from recent infections and cannot distinguish active infection from prolonged shedding or asymptomatic carriage. Finally, the analyses were primarily descriptive and did not account for seasonality, testing volume, or other potential confounders through advanced time-series modelling. Despite these limitations, the temporal patterns observed were broadly consistent with reports from other settings.

Conclusion

Among hospitalized pediatric patients who underwent BioFire® testing, the post-pandemic period was characterized by increased respiratory pathogen positivity, higher pathogen diversity, and temporal disruptions of commonly circulating respiratory pathogens such as influenza and RSV. Despite these shifts, no increase in PICU admissions was observed. The findings also suggest the effectiveness of non-pharmaceutical interventions in reducing the transmission of COVID-19 and other respiratory pathogens. Together, these results underscore the importance of pandemic preparedness and public health strategies that protect vulnerable populations, particularly newborns and young children, while accounting for changes in pathogen circulation following relaxation of public health measures.
