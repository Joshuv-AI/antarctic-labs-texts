On the Maximal Gaussian Perimeter of Convex Sets, Revisited
Shivam Nadimpalli, Caleb Pascale

Abstract
We revisit Nazarov’s construction [ Naz03 ] of a convex set with nearly maximal Gaussian surface area and give an alternate analysis based on the notion of convex influence introduced in [ DNS21 , DNS22 ] .



1
Introduction

The
Gaussian surface area
of a (Borel) set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

\mathbf{GSA}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(K_{t}\setminus K)}{t}\,,

where
K_{t}
is the
t
-enlargement of
K
(that is,
K_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\exists y\in K~\text{s.t.}~\|x-y\|\leq t\}
) and
\mathrm{Vol}(K)
denotes the measure of
K
under the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution
N(0,I_{n})
.
It is a fundamental complexity measure in high-dimensional geometry, arising naturally in the context of noise stability and isoperimetry
[
Bor75
,
ST78
]
.
It has also found applications to problems in probability theory
[
Ben86
,
Ben03
,
Rai19
]
and theoretical computer science
[
KOS08
,
Kan10
,
DMN19
,
DNS24
]
.

For convex sets (and more generally, sets with smooth boundary), the above definition coincides with the following (see
[
Naz03
]
):

Definition 1
.

If
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex, then

\mathbf{GSA}(K)=\int_{\partial K}\varphi_{n}(x)\,d\sigma(x)\,,

where
d\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the surface measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varphi_{n}(x)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/2}
is the density of the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian measure.

Motivated by problems in high-dimensional probability, Ball
[
Bal93
]
considered the following reverse isoperimetric problem for the Gaussian measure: how large can
\mathbf{GSA}(K)
be for a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
?
It will be convenient to write

\Gamma(n)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup{\left\{\mathbf{GSA}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\text{convex}~K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}}\,.

Ball established the following uniform bound on the Gaussian surface area of convex sets:

Theorem 2

(Theorem 4 of
[
Bal93
]
)
.

We have
\Gamma(n)\leq 4n^{1/4}
.

This order of growth is sharp: Nazarov
[
Naz03
]
showed the existence of a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with surface area on the order of
\Omega(n^{1/4})
for
n
sufficiently large.
Nazarov’s construction has since found applications in learning theory
[
KOS08
]
, polyhedral approximation
[
DNS24
]
, and property testing
[
CDN+25
]
.

Theorem 3

(
[
Naz03
]
)
.

We have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n)}{n^{1/4}}\geq e^{-5/4}\,.

Nazarov
[
Naz03
]
lamented that his proof of
Theorem

3
(and of his sharpening of
Theorem

2
; see below) was “a pretty boring and technical computation” and speculated that “there should exist some simple and elegant way leading to the result,” even encouraging the reader to “stop reading and (try to) prove the theorem themselves.”
This note attempts to give such a proof.
In particular, our argument avoids the heavier analytic machinery (such as the Laplace asymptotic formula) and technical estimates of Nazarov’s original proof.
Our key idea is to analyze an isoperimetric quantity closely related to Gaussian surface area, namely
convex influence
(introduced in
[
DNS21
,
DNS22
]
and recalled below in
Section

2.2
), which is considerably easier to calculate.

We note that a gap remains between the best known upper and lower bounds for
\Gamma(n)
.
Nazarov
[
Naz03
]
himself sharpened Ball’s bound (
Theorem

2
), and a subsequent refinement by Raič
[
Rai19
]
obtained the following estimate, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the current state of the art:

\Gamma(n)\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}+0.59(n^{1/4}-1)\,.

Theorem

3
continues to be the best known lower bound on
\Gamma(n)
; note that
e^{-5/4}\approx 0.2865
.

Organization.
We recall useful preliminaries in
Section

2
and present our proof of
Theorem

3
in
Section

3
.

2.1
Distributions and Tail Bounds

We write
N(0,I_{n})
for the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
\varphi_{n}(\cdot)
.
When the dimension
n
is clear from context, we will sometimes write
\varphi
instead of
\varphi_{n}
.
The Gaussian measure of a (measurable) set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
\mathrm{Vol}(K)
, i.e.,

\mathrm{Vol}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\in K\right]}\,.

We recall a standard concentration bound for univariate Gaussian random variables:

Proposition 4

(Proposition 2.1.2 of
[
Ver18
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,1)
.
Then for all
t>0
, we have

{\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{t^{3}}\right)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\geq t\right]}\leq\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,.

We will also require the following concentration bound on the norm of a Gaussian random vector:

Proposition 5

(Equation 3.3 of
[
Ver18
]
)
.

Let
t>0
.
We have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\|\boldsymbol{x}\|-\sqrt{n}\right|}\geq t\right]}\leq 2e^{-Ct^{2}}\,,

where
C
is an absolute constant.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\|x\|=1\}
denote the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the uniform measure.
It is known (see, for example, Appendix D of
[
KOS08
]
) that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\geq 0
, we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}=\tau_{n}\int_{-1}^{\min{\left\{\frac{r}{\|x\|},1\right\}}}(1-z^{2})^{\frac{(n-3)}{2}}\,dz\qquad\text{where}~\tau_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\Gamma{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\Gamma{\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}}\,.

(1)

Stirling’s approximation (or alternatively, Gautschi’s inequality
[
Gau59
]
) yields the following:

\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}(1+O(n^{-1}))\leq\tau_{n}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}\,.

(2)

We will also require the following estimate on the measure of spherical caps:

Lemma 6
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\geq 0
such that
\|x\|\geq r
. Then

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}\geq 1-\tau_{n}\cdot\frac{\|x\|}{r(n-3)}\cdot e^{-\frac{r^{2}(n-3)}{2\|x\|^{2}}}\,.

Standard estimates on the measure of spherical caps—see, for example,
[
Bal97
,
Tko12
]
—obtain the coarser bound

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{nr^{2}}{2\|x\|^{2}}}\,.

Lemma

6
is sharper when
\|x\|\ll r\sqrt{n}
; this is exactly the regime that will arise later in our proof of
Theorem

3
.

Proof.

Thanks to
Equation

1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}>r\right]}

\displaystyle=\tau_{n}\int_{\frac{r}{\|x\|}}^{1}(1-z^{2})^{\frac{(n-3)}{2}}\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{n}\int_{\frac{r}{\|x\|}}^{\infty}e^{\frac{-z^{2}(n-3)}{2}}\,dz

(
1+y\leq e^{y}
)

\displaystyle=\tau_{n}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n-3}}\cdot\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}\sim N(0,1)}{\left[\boldsymbol{y}>\frac{r\sqrt{n-3}}{\|x\|}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{n}\cdot\frac{\|x\|}{r(n-3)}\cdot e^{-\frac{r^{2}(n-3)}{2\|x\|^{2}}}\,.

(
Proposition

4
)

The result follows immediately.
∎

2.2
Convex Influence

The following quantity was introduced in
[
DNS21
,
DNS22
]
as a convex set analogue of the well-studied notion of
total influence
from the analysis of Boolean functions
[
O’D14
]
:

Definition 7

(Total convex influence)
.

Given a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define its
total convex influence

\mathbf{I}[K]
as

\mathbf{I}[K]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Vol}{\left((1+t)K\right)}\bigg|_{t=0}\,.

We will also make use of the following alternative characterization of total convex influence from
[
DNS24
]
, and give a self-contained proof for completeness:

Lemma 8

(Lemma 67 of
[
DNS24
]
)
.

Given a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\mathbf{I}[K]=\int_{\partial K}(x\cdot\nu_{x})\,\varphi(x)\,d\sigma(x)

where
\nu_{x}
denotes the unit normal to
\partial K
at
x
and
d\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the surface measure.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}[K]=\frac{d}{dt}{\left(\int_{(1+t)K}\varphi(x)\,dx\right)}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}{\left(\int_{K}(1+t)^{n}\,\varphi((1+t)y)\,dy\right)}\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{K}{\left(\frac{d}{dt}(1+t)^{n}\,\varphi((1+t)y)\right)}\bigg|_{t=0}\,dy

(Fubini)

\displaystyle=\int_{K}(n-\|y\|^{2})\,\varphi(y)\,dy

(3)

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\mathcal{L}\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2}\,\varphi(y)\,dy\,,

where, for a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define
\mathcal{L}f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Delta f(x)-\nabla f(x)\cdot x
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}
is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator (cf. Definition 11.24 and Proposition 11.26 of
[
O’D14
]
).
Integrating by parts then gives

\mathbf{I}[K]=\int_{\partial K}(x\cdot\nu_{x})\,\varphi(x)\,d\sigma(x)\,.

as desired.
∎

Note that the proof of
Lemma

8
does not require convexity of
K
and in fact applies more generally to any set with finite Gaussian surface area.
We additionally note that the expression in
Equation

3
is the original definition of convex influence from
[
DNS21
,
DNS22
]
; we record a few easy consequences of it that rely on Hermite analysis (see
Appendix

A
or Chapter 11 of
[
O’D14
]
).

Equation

3
can be rewritten as

\mathbf{I}[K]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[K(\boldsymbol{x})(n-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|)^{2}\right]}\,,

where we identify
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with its
0/1
-valued indicator function.
Writing
e_{i}
for the
i^{\text{th}}
standard basis vector, we have

\mathbf{I}[K]=-\sqrt{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{K}(2e_{i})\,,

where
\{\widehat{K}(2e_{i})\}_{i}
are the degree-
2
Hermite coefficients of
K
(see
Appendix

A
and
Equation

16
).
Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\mathbf{I}[K]\leq{\left(2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{K}(2e_{i})^{2}\right)}^{1/2}\,.

(4)

In particular, for all (measurable)
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\mathbf{I}[K]\leq\sqrt{2n}
by Parseval’s formula (
Equation

17
) since
K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is Boolean valued.

A Simple Proof of

As in Nazarov’s original argument

, we will show the existence of a convex set with large Gaussian perimeter via the probabilistic method.
We first give a sketch of why (a slight modification of) Nazarov’s construction has

Gaussian surface area in

3.1
Intuition: An
\Omega(n^{1/4})
Lower Bound on Gaussian Surface Area

Recent work
[
DNS24
]
on polyhedral approximation under the Gaussian measure gives a quick way to see the existence of a convex set with maximal Gaussian perimeter (up to constant factors).
We briefly sketch this argument below, leaving full details to the interested reader.
The aim here is to give intuitive justification for the more explicit calculation carried out in
Section

3.2
.

Let
w,s>0
be parameters that we will set later, and let
\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)
denote the distribution over convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where a draw
\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)
is generated as follows:

1.

Draw
\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)},\dots\boldsymbol{g}^{(s)}\sim N(0,I_{n})
and set

\boldsymbol{H}^{\prime}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\cdot\boldsymbol{g}^{(i)}\leq w\}~\text{for}~i\in[s]\,.

2.

Output
\boldsymbol{K}\sim\cap_{i}\boldsymbol{H}^{\prime}_{i}
.

This distribution—a slight modification of Nazarov’s construction which we will use in
Section

3.2
—was shown by De, Nadimpalli, and Servedio
[
DNS24
]
to well-approximate the
\ell_{2}
-ball of Gaussian measure
1/2
for appropriate
w
and
s
.
In particular, it follows from Theorems 27 and 70 of
[
DNS24
]
that for
w=\Theta(n^{3/4})
and
s=\exp(\Theta(\sqrt{n})
,

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big]\geq\Omega(\sqrt{n})\,.

(5)

To be precise, the above fact relies on an observation made during the proof of Theorem 70 of
[
DNS24
]
: see the paragraph preceding the final displayed equation in its proof.
It is also possible to directly obtain
Equation

5
by computing
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]]
via
Equation

3
.
1
1

1

Indeed, note that it follows from
Equation

3
and Fubini’s theorem that

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\right)}^{s}{\left(n-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\right)}^{2}\right]}\,,

and this can be readily estimated using standard tail bounds for univariate Gaussian random variables (cf.
Proposition

4
) and chi-squared random variables
[
LM00
]
.

Next, we will use
Lemma

8
to obtain an upper bound on the L.H.S. of
Equation

5
.
We say that
K
in the support of
\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(r,s)
is
good
if, for some (large) absolute constant
c^{\prime}

\|g^{(i)}\|\leq c^{\prime}\sqrt{n}
for all
i\in[s]
, and will say that it is
bad
otherwise.
Note that
Proposition

5
and a union bound together imply that
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{K}~\text{is good}]\geq 1-e^{-C^{\prime}n}
for some absolute constant
C^{\prime}
.
We thus have

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big|\boldsymbol{K}~\text{is good}\big]+e^{-C^{\prime}n}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big|\boldsymbol{K}~\text{is bad}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big|\boldsymbol{K}~\text{is good}\big]+e^{-\Theta(n)}

\displaystyle\leq\Theta(n^{1/4})\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}^{\prime}(w,s)}{\left[\mathbf{GSA}(\boldsymbol{K})\big|\boldsymbol{K}~\text{is good}\right]}+e^{-\Theta(n)}\,,

(
Lemma

8
)

where the second inequality relied on the fact that
\mathbf{I}[L]\leq O(\sqrt{n})
for all
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(cf. the discussion following
Equation

4
), and the final inequality also relied on the choice of
w=\Theta(n^{3/4})
.
Together with
Equation

5
, this immediately gives the existence of a good
K
with
\mathbf{GSA}(K)\geq\Omega(n^{1/4})
.

3.2
Recovering Nazarov’s Constant

We now refine the argument sketched in
Section

3.1
to prove
Theorem

3
.
Let
r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n^{1/4}
and
s>0
be a parameter that we will set later.
(We write
r
instead of
w
to distinguish this section’s construction from that of the previous one.)
Let
\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)
denote the distribution over convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where a draw
\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)
is generated as follows:

1.

Draw
\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}^{(s)}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\boldsymbol{H}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\leq r\}~\text{for}~i\in[s]\,.

\boldsymbol{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigcap_{i}\boldsymbol{H}_{i}
.

Note that for every
K
in the support of
\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)

(x\cdot\nu_{x})=r

The following is then immediate from

:

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big]=r\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}{\left[\mathbf{GSA}(\boldsymbol{K})\right]}\,.

(6)

On the other hand, computing
\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]

Definition

7
gives

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}{\left[\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Vol}((1+t)\boldsymbol{K})\bigg|_{t=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}{\left[\frac{d}{dt}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\in(1+t)\boldsymbol{K}\right]}\bigg|_{t=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\frac{d}{dt}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\in(1+t)\boldsymbol{K}\right]}\bigg|_{t=0}\right]}

(Fubini)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\frac{d}{dt}{\left(\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq(1+t)r\right]}\right)}^{s}\bigg|_{t=0}\right]}\,.

Applying the chain rule, we get

\frac{d}{dt}{\left(\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq(1+t)r\right]}\right)}^{s}\bigg|_{t=0}=s\cdot\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}^{s-1}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}{\left(\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r(1+t)\right]}\right)}\bigg|_{t=0}\,.

It is readily verified using the Leibniz rule that

\frac{d}{dt}{\left(\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r(1+t)\right]}\right)}\bigg|_{t=0}=\tau_{n}\cdot\frac{r}{\|x\|}\cdot{\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{\|x\|^{2}}\right)}_{+}^{(n-3)/2}\,,

where
(a)_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max\{a,0\}
.
We thus have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(r,s)}\big[\mathbf{I}[\boldsymbol{K}]\big]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[s\cdot\frac{r}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}\cdot\tau_{n}\cdot{\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}}\right)}^{(n-3)/2}_{+}\cdot\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}^{s-1}\right]}\,.

(7)

The remainder of the argument will establish

\text{R.H.S.~of \eqref{eq:inf-dilation-equality}}\geq e^{-5/4}\cdot\sqrt{n}(1-o(1))\,,

(8)

which, together with
Equation

6

Theorem

.
Let
t=n^{1/4}
, and let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\|x\|\in[\sqrt{n}-t,\sqrt{n}+t]\}
.
2
2

2

We note that any choice of

\omega(1)
but

By

5
, we have
\mathrm{Vol}(A)\geq 1-2\exp(-Ct^{2})=1-o(1)
.
Since the quantity inside the expectation in

\text{R.H.S.~of \eqref{eq:inf-dilation-equality}}\geq{\left(1-o(1)\right)}\cdot\inf_{x\in A}{\left\{s\cdot\frac{r}{\|x\|}\cdot\tau_{n}\cdot{\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{\|x\|^{2}}\right)}^{(n-3)/2}_{+}\cdot\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}^{s-1}\right\}}\,.

Let

From

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[x\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\leq r\right]}\geq 1-\frac{\tau_{n}\|x\|}{r(n-3)}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}(n-3)}{2\|x\|^{2}}\right)}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1-G(x)\,.

We take

where

To control

\frac{F(x)}{G(x)}=\frac{r^{2}(n-3)}{\tau_{n}\cdot\|x\|^{2}}\cdot{\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{\|x\|^{2}}\right)}^{(n-3)/2}_{+}\exp{\left(\frac{r^{2}(n-3)}{2\|x\|^{2}}\right)}\,.

Recall that

Consequently, for

3.3
Discussion: Upper Bounds on GSA via Convex Influence

It is natural to ask whether the notion of convex influence can also be used to derive effective
upper
bounds on Gaussian surface area.
For simplicity, we assume throughout that
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex and contains the origin.
Next, let
r_{\mathrm{in}}(K)
denote the radius of the largest origin-centered ball contained in
K
.
Since
0^{n}\in K
, we have
r_{\mathrm{in}}(K)\geq 0
.
It follows from the definition of
\mathbf{I}[K]
(cf.
Definition

7
) that

\mathbf{I}[K]=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}((1+t)K\setminus K)}{t}\geq\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(K_{tr_{\mathrm{in}}(K)}\setminus K)}{t}=r_{\mathrm{in}}(K)\cdot\mathbf{GSA}(K)\,.

Combining this with
Equation

4
gives

\mathbf{GSA}(K)\leq\frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{in}}(K)}\cdot{\left(2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{K}(2e_{i})^{2}\right)}^{1/2}\,.

(14)

Using Parseval’s identity (see
Equation

18
), we can obtain a more convenient expression:

\mathbf{GSA}(K)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2n}\cdot\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[K]}}{r_{\mathrm{in}}(K)}\,.

(15)

Equation

15
is particularly effective when
\mathrm{Vol}(K)
is large, as then
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[K]
is close to zero and
r_{\mathrm{in}}(K)\geq\Omega(1)
by convexity.

One might optimistically hope that the right-hand side of
Equation

15
could be shown to be
O(n^{1/4})
in general, yielding an alternative proof of Ball’s bound (
Theorem

2
).
Unfortunately, this is false even in simple cases: for example, in one dimension, the bound fails for a thin slab
S=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|x|\leq\theta\}
as
\theta\to 0
.
It remains possible, however, that the refined bound from
Equation

14
or
Lemma

8
itself could furnish a new proof of Ball’s theorem.
We leave this as an open direction for future work.

Appendix A
Hermite Analysis

Our notation and terminology follow Chapter 11 of
[
O’D14
]
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we write
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},N(0,I_{n}))
to denote the space of functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that have finite second moment under the Gaussian distribution, i.e.
f\in L^{2}
if

\|f\|^{2}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{n})}\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})^{2}\right]^{1/2}<\infty\,.

When the dimension
n
is clear from context, we will write
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},N(0,I_{n}))
for simplicity.
We view
L^{2}
as an inner product space with

\langle f,g\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[f(\boldsymbol{x})g(\boldsymbol{x})]\,.

We recall the Hermite basis for
L^{2}
:

Definition 9

(Hermite basis)
.

The
Hermite polynomials

(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are the univariate polynomials defined as

h_{j}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

In particular, one can check that

h_{2}(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(16)

The following fact is standard:

Fact 10

(Proposition 11.33 of
[
O’D14
]
)
.

The Hermite polynomials
(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
form a complete, orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},N(0,1))
. For
n>1
the collection of
n
-variate polynomials given by
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
where

h_{\alpha}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}}(x)

forms a complete, orthonormal basis for
L^{2}
.

Given a function
f\in L^{2}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we define its
Hermite coefficient on

\alpha
as
\widehat{f}(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle f,h_{\alpha}\rangle
. It follows that
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be uniquely expressed as

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)h_{\alpha}

with equality holding in
L^{2}
.
One can check that Parseval’s identity holds in this setting:

{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,.

(17)

It is also readily verified that for
f\in L^{2}
,

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[f(\boldsymbol{x})]=\widehat{f}(0^{n})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[f(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)^{2}\,.

(18)
