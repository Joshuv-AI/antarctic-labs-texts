Prolyl endopeptidase is a multifunctional host factor required for FMDV infection
Authors: Kun Ma, Rongzeng Hao, Tianran Liu, Yi Ru, Tao Feng, Xiaolan Qi, Yuzhe Wang, Bingzhou Lu, Sen Wu, Dan Li, Ran Zhang, Yaofeng Zhao, Haixue Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) poses a significant global threat to livestock production, underscoring the urgent need for a deeper understanding of virus-host interactions to develop effective prevention strategies. In this study, we identify prolyl endopeptidase (PREP) as a critical host factor in FMDV infection. We demonstrate that PREP knockout (PREP-KO) significantly enhances host resistance to FMDV infection by upregulating the expression of RIG-I and MDA5 mRNA. Specifically, PREP-KO destabilizes the PUM1 protein via proteasomal and apoptotic degradation pathways, thereby increasing RIG-I promoter activity. Additionally, PREP-KO inhibits the FMDV life cycle at the replication stage. In vivo experiments further reveal that PREP inhibition or knockout provides protection against FMDV infection following a sublethal challenge in mice. Our findings identify PREP as a novel antiviral target, providing valuable insights for development of antiviral therapies and breeding of disease-resistant livestock.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05941-0.Keywords: Foot-and-mouth disease virus, Prolyl endopeptidase, Pumilio protein 1, Innate immunity, Virus life cycle

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a globally significant viral disease, transmitted through various routes and causing widespread outbreaks in livestock [1]. FMD is caused by FMDV, a highly contagious pathogen capable of infecting over 70 species, including pigs, cattle, sheep, and other major livestock [2]. The World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) classifies FMD as a notifiable animal disease because of its severe economic impact. FMDV, a member of the Picornaviridae family and Aphthovirus genus, is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus responsible for numerous major outbreaks in recent decades [3]. Its genome contains a large open reading frame (ORF) encoding a polyprotein, which is processed by viral proteases into four structural proteins (VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4) and eight non-structural proteins (L, 2 A, 2B, 2 C, 3 A, 3B, 3 C, and 3D). FMDV displays remarkable genetic diversity, comprising seven distinct serotypes (O, A, C, Asia I, and Southern African Territories (SAT) 1, 2, and 3) [4]. This variability poses significant challenges to the development of effective vaccines and antiviral strategies, as the virus rapidly mutates and evades immune responses.

Despite advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of FMDV, significant knowledge gaps remain, particularly in understanding of host genetic factors that influence susceptibility and resistance to FMDV infection. Host factors exploited by viruses to facilitate replication are promising therapeutic targets [5, 6]. The key host factors play a critical role in determining the outcome of viral infection, as they can either promote viral replication or activate the host immune response to inhibit it. For example, during FMDV infection, histone deacetylase 8 (HDAC8) enhances the type I interferon (IFN) signaling pathway, which inhibits FMDV replication [7]. Similarly, Transducer of ERBB2.1 (TOB1) plays a critical role in inhibiting FMDV infection through multiple mechanisms. Firstly, TOB1 knockout enhances the expression of RIG-I and MDA5, which subsequently promotes the production of IFN-β and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), further inhibiting FMDV replication. Additionally, TOB1 knockout suppresses FMDV entry via the EGFR/ERBB2 signaling pathway [8]. Understanding FMDV-host interactions more deeply is essential for identifying novel antiviral targets and advancing strategies to breed disease-resistant animals.

To address these challenges, we previously performed a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen using a porcine library developed in our laboratory [8]. This analysis identified the PREP gene as a critical host factor in FMDV infection. PREP (also known as prolyl oligopeptidase, POP) is an intracellular serine protease classified within the S9 family of proteases [9]. While previous studies have predominantly focused on PREP′s role in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer′s disease [10–14], Parkinson′s disease [15], and multiple sclerosis [16, 17], its role in viral infections has remained largely unexplored.

In this study, we demonstrate that PREP-KO significantly reduces FMDV infection by activating the type I IFN signaling pathway and thus inhibiting viral replication. Specially, PREP-KO enhances RIG-I and MDA5-mediated innate immune responses, resulting in significant antiviral defense. In addition, PREP-KO inhibits the FMDV life cycle at the viral replication stage. In vivo experiments further show that both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockout of PREP confer protection against FMDV infection. These findings suggest that the enhanced resistance to FMDV observed in PREP-KO cells is mediated by multiple antiviral mechanisms.

Results

PREP-KO significantly inhibits FMDV infection

To investigate the role of PREP in FMDV infection, we generated PREP-KO cells from FMDV-susceptible IBRS-2 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Sanger sequencing showed that all three PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells contained nucleotide insertions or deletions, resulting in frameshift mutations in the PREP coding regions (Fig. 1A). In PREP-KO2 and KO3 IBRS-2 cells, both insertions and deletions were observed, suggesting activation of DNA repair mechanisms at different points within the target region. Western blot analysis further verified the complete absence of PREP protein expression in these cells (Fig. 1B). When challenged with FMDV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01, all three PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells demonstrated a robust resistance to FMDV induced cytopathic effects (CPE), with CPE appearing at 30 h post-infection (hpi), delayed compared to sgNC cells (Fig. S1A). The FMDV structural protein VP1 was undetectable in infected PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells, whether inoculated with low (MOI = 0.01) or high (MOI = 1) viral titers (Fig. 1B). These results were corroborated by viral plaque (Fig. 1C) and indirect immunofluorescence assays (IFA) (Fig. S1B and Fig. 1D). PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells were infected with FMDV at two different MOIs (MOI = 0.01 and MOI = 1) for 16.5 h and 4 h, respectively, both of which confirmed a significantly reduced infection effects in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells. Virus titer analysis revealed that PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells showed markedly reduced FMDV titers compared to sgNC control cells at 6 hpi with FMDV at an MOI of 1. The most pronounced reduction, almost 2.70 log TCID₅₀/0.1 mL, was observed in PREP-KO2 cells (Fig. 1E). Infection in all three PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells was delayed by approximately 4 h compared to sgNC cells (MOI = 1). Similar results were observed in PREP-KO PK-15 cells, where Sanger sequencing verified a nucleotide insertion in the PREP gene (Fig. S1C). Western blot analysis and viral titer quantification demonstrated a significant decrease in both FMDV VP1 protein expression and viral titers in PREP-KO PK-15 cells challenged with FMDV at MOI of 0.01 for 24 h (Fig. S1D, E). Infection in PREP-KO PK-15 cells was delayed by approximately 12 h, and viral titers were nearly 4 log TCID₅₀/0.1 mL lower than those in sgNC control cells. Furthermore, restoration of the PREP expression in PREP-KO cells reinstated both FMDV VP1 protein expression and viral infectivity, confirming the functional specificity of the PREP knockout (Fig. 1F, G).

To further confirm the role of PREP in promoting FMDV proliferation, we generated two PREP overexpression IBRS-2 cell lines: GFP-PREP (N-terminal GFP tag) and PREP-GFP (C-terminal GFP tag). Fluorescence microscopy confirmed successful expression of GFP-tagged PREP protein (Fig. 1H). After FMDV infection at an MOI of 0.01 in two PREP-overexpression cell lines for 18 h, both cell lines exhibited significantly enhanced FMDV infection (Fig. 1I). These findings highlight PREP as a critical host factor for FMDV infection and suggest its involvement in the viral life cycle.

The type I IFN signaling pathway is significantly activated in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the resistance of PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells to FMDV infection, we performed transcriptome sequencing on total RNA isolated from uninfected PREP-KO cells and sgNC control cells.

The three control samples of IBRS-2 cells (sgNC-1, sgNC-2, and sgNC-3) were clustered together, while the three PREP-KO cells (KO1, KO2, and KO3) formed a separate cluster. Differential expression analysis identified 2,160 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 949 upregulated and 1,211 downregulated (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the DEGs revealed enrichment in biological processes such as defense response to virus, type I IFN signaling pathway, innate immune response, and negative regulation of viral genome replication (Fig. 2B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified multiple viral infection pathways, including herpes simplex virus I, human papillomavirus, kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus, hepatitis B, influenza A, hepatitis C, and measles viruses, as well as the RIG-I-like receptor and JAK-STAT signaling pathways (Fig. 2C).

Analysis of the top 50 upregulated genes revealed significant induction of IFN and ISGs. Notably, OASL mRNA levels increased nearly 5,000-fold, while IFN-β1 mRNA levels increased approximately 700-fold. Among the top 20 upregulated genes, USP18 [18], MMP25 [19], and DDX60 [20] were linked to the positive regulation of the type I IFN signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). RT-qPCR confirmed these trends, demonstrating a strong correlation with RNA-seq results (Fig. 2E). Rescue experiments revealed that reintroduction of PREP into PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells led to a partial reduction in IFN and ISGs expression (Fig. S2). Additionally, both PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells were treated with either porcine IgG or varying concentrations of porcine IFN-β antibody for 8 h to block type I IFN signaling. Treatment with porcine IFN-β antibody significantly downregulated ISGs expression in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells, confirming the involvement of the type I IFN pathway (Fig. 2F).

Interestingly, when PREP-KO was performed in PK-15 and HEK293T cells, no upregulation of IFN or ISGs was observed (Fig. S3A–D). This indicates that the elevated IFN and ISGs expression in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cell is cell-specific. Taken together, these findings suggest that PREP modulates type I IFN signaling in a cell-specific manner, potentially contributing to the resistance of IBRS-2 cells to FMDV infection.

PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells upregulate RIG-I mRNA expression by reducing pumillo protein 1 (PUM1) stability

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the upregulation of IFN-β and ISGs in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells, we examined key adaptor molecules in the type I IFN signaling pathway. Western blot analysis showed elevated protein levels of RIG-I, IRF3, IκBα, and p65 in all three PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells compared to sgNC control cells (Fig. 3A). In contrast, stable GFP-PREP-expressing IBRS-2 cells exhibited reduced RIG-I expression, suggesting that PREP negatively regulates the type I IFN signaling pathway by modulating adaptor molecules, particularly RIG-I (Fig. 3B). RT-qPCR further revealed significant upregulation of RIG-I and MDA5 mRNA levels in PREP-KO cells, while MAVS mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3C). Due to mRNA stability being a critical factor affecting mRNA levels, we explored whether it contributed to the upregulation of RIG-I mRNA. We assessed RIG-I mRNA stability and found it significantly reduced in PREP-KO cells compared to sgNC control cells, suggesting that the elevated RIG-I mRNA levels in PREP-KO cells were not due to increased stability (Fig. S4).

Previous studies have shown that the RNA-binding protein PUM1 can regulate RIG-I and MDA5 mRNA expression [21]. Consistent with this, our transcriptome analysis revealed reduced PUM1 mRNA levels in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells. RT-qPCR and western blot validation confirmed substantial decreases in both PUM1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3D, E). To assess the functional impacts of reduced PUM1 expression, we treated IBRS-2 cells with PUM1 siRNA. Knockdown of PUM1 significantly increase the expression of IFN and ISGs at 48 h post-transfection, consistent with the changes observed in PREP-KO cells (Fig. 3F and Fig. S5). These findings suggest that reduced PUM1 expression enhances IFN and ISGs levels in IBRS-2 cells.

To explore the relationship between PREP and PUM1, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays in HEK293T cells, due to the low transfection efficiency of porcine IBRS-2 cells. These assays confirmed a direct interaction between PREP and PUM1 (Fig. 3G). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiment showed that PUM1 protein stability was significantly reduced in the absence of PREP (Fig. 3H). To determine the degradation pathway involved, we treated cells with inhibitors targeting the proteasome (MG132), lysosome (chloroquine (CQ)), and caspases (Z-VAD-FMK). Both MG132 and Z-VAD-FMK restored PUM1 protein levels, indicating that PREP maintain PUM1 stability through proteasomal and apoptotic pathways (Fig. 3I). Further analysis with caspase-specific inhibitors revealed that caspases 3, 7, and 8 (inhibited by Z-DEVD-FMK and Z-IETD-FMK, respectively) were involved in PUM1 degradation (Fig. 3J).

Finally, we examined the role of PUM1 in RIG-I expression using a dual-luciferase reporter assay with a 2,200 bp porcine RIG-I promoter construct. PUM1 silencing significantly enhanced RIG-I promoter activity (Fig. 3K). These findings suggest that PREP-KO destabilizes PUM1, leading to the upregulation of RIG-I mRNA via transcriptional activation, thereby activating the type I IFN signaling pathway.

PREP-KO does not affect FMDV binding or internalization but impairs viral replication

PREP-KO displayed resistance to FMDV infection in both IBRS-2 and PK-15 cells. However, up-regulation of type I IFN signaling pathway was only observed in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells, suggesting that other mechanisms also contribute to the observed phenotype. To address this question, we further assessed whether PREP-KO affects the entry stage of FMDV. The binding of FMDV to PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells was assessed after incubating the cell-virus mixture on ice for 1 h. We observed that the fluorescence signal of the FMDV VP1 protein was localized on the cell membrane of both PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells, suggesting that viral particles can still bind to PREP-KO cells (Fig. 4A). Additionally, following the ice incubation, the cell-virus mixture was transferred to 37 °C for 30 min. The cells were then washed and treated with proteinase K (500 ng/mL) on ice for 1 h. We then examined the co-localization of FMDV particles with EEA1, a marker for early endosomes, in both PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells. No significant differences were observed in the co-localization patterns of FMDV and EEA1 between PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells, indicating that early endosome-mediated internalization was unaffected by PREP knockout (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, RT-qPCR further confirmed that no differences of FMDV RNA in binding and internalization stage between PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells (Fig. 4C, D).

To explore PREP′s role in post-entry stages, we transfected PREP-KO and sgNC control IBRS-2 cells with FMDV reverse genetics plasmids, bypassing the binding and internalization stages. Virus titers measured at various time points revealed significantly lower titers in PREP-KO cells compared to controls (Fig. 4E). Similarly, the expression of FMDV RNA-dependent RNA polymerase 3D was significantly reduced in PREP-KO cells following transfection (Fig. 4F).

To determine whether the viral replication is affected by PREP-KO, we analyzed the kinetics of FMDV RNA and protein synthesis in IBRS-2 cells. FMDV RNA levels began to increase by 10 hpi, and viral RNA polymerase 3D and structural protein VP1 were detectable at 8 hpi and 14 hpi, respectively (Fig. S6A, B). Focusing on viral replication events (MOI = 0.01, up to 12 hpi), we found significantly reduced accumulation of FMDV VP1 and 3D proteins in PREP-KO cells compared to sgNC controls (Fig. 4G).

Given PREP′s role in regulating FMDV protein translation, we further investigated whether PREP affects FMDV internal ribosome entry site (IRES)-driven translation. To this end, we utilized a bicistronic luciferase reporter containing a cap-dependent Renilla luciferase (RLuc) and an FMDV IRES-driven Firefly luciferase (FLuc) (Fig. S7A, B). PREP-KO cells exhibited a modest reduction in IRES activity (from 100% to 83%), but this decrease was insufficient to explain the reduced FMDV protein accumulation (Fig. S7C, D).

Further analysis of viral replication showed significantly suppressed RNA synthesis in PREP-KO cells (Fig. 4H). Since viral mRNA translation and mRNA synthesis closely linked during FMDV infection, we further analyzed whether the effects of PREP-KO are on replication or translation. When translation was inhibited with CHX, viral RNA replication remained suppressed in PREP-KO cells indicating that the primary defect lies in RNA replication rather than translation (Fig. 4I, J). These results suggest that PREP is essential for efficient FMDV replication following viral entry into host cells.

The FMDV replication is inhibited in PREP inhibitor baicalein treated cells

To determine whether the PREP inhibitor affects FMDV replication, we treated FMDV-susceptible cell lines (IBRS-2, PK-15, and immortalized porcine alveolar macrophage (iPAM) cells) with the PREP inhibitor baicalein and evaluated its effects. This approach enabled us to evaluate the specific effects of PREP inhibition on FMDV genome replication, without interference from the significant activation of the type I IFN signaling pathway in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells. The cells were pretreated with 100 µM of the PREP inhibitor baicalein for 3 h prior to FMDV infection, and samples were collected at different time points post infection for analysis. The results showed that baicalein treatment significantly reduced the accumulation of FMDV VP1 protein (Fig. 5A) and impaired FMDV replication in all three cell lines (Fig. 5B-D). Subsequently, we conducted time-of-drug-addition experiments, adding the PREP inhibitor baicalein at different time points relative to FMDV infection (−3, −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, and +3 h). Samples were collected at 24 hpi for analysis. The results showed that baicalein had its strongest inhibitory effects when added post-infection, with notable effects observed in both IBRS-2 and PK-15 cells (Fig. 5E–H). Although the biological half-life of baicalein was not measured in our study, a previous report found it to be 8.42 ± 2.68 h in rats [22]. These findings further confirm that PREP is critical for FMDV replication.

Inhibition or knockout of PREP increases the survival rate of mice following FMDV challenge

FMDV relies on host factors, including PREP, for critical processes such as replication. In vitro, the selective PREP antagonist, baicalein, effectively inhibited FMDV replication. To assess the in vivo antiviral effects of baicalein, we employed a murine FMDV infection model. Four-week-old female C57BL/6J mice pretreated with 30 mg/kg or 50 mg/kg of baicalein showed significantly improved survival rates after challenge with 103 TCID50 of FMDV, compared to DMSO-treated controls (Fig. 6A, B). Moreover, the viral load in the hearts of the 50 mg/kg baicalein-treated group was significantly reduced at day 3 compared to the DMSO-treated group. A similar reduction in viral load was observed in the livers, lungs, and kidneys of these mice (Fig. 6C). Histopathological analysis revealed extensive inflammatory infiltration and tissue damage in the hearts, kidneys, lives and lungs of DMSO-treated mice while baicalein-treated mice showed markedly fewer pathological changes (Fig. 6D). To evaluate the therapeutic potential of baicalein in FMDV infection, we treated FMDV-infected mice with 50 mg/kg baicalein at 0-, 0.5-, and 1-day post-infection (Fig. 6E). Mice treated with baicalein showed significantly improved survival rates compared to DMSO-treated controls (Fig. 6F). These results suggest that baicalein has both prophylactic and therapeutic effects against FMDV.

To further investigate the antiviral effects of PREP knockout in vivo, we generated PREP-KO mice using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Sanger sequencing confirmed a 1,995 bp deletion spanning exons 3–4 of the PREP gene, resulting in the loss of PREP protein expression (Fig. S8A–D). The resulting truncated protein is predicted to be 6.3 kDa. Following challenge with 103 TCID50 of FMDV, PREP-KO mice exhibited higher survival rates compared to WT mice (p = 0.0786) (Fig. 6G). Additionally, mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) from PREP-KO mice showed significantly reduced FMDV replication compared to WT MEFs infected with FMDV at an MOI of 1 (Fig. 6H). These findings suggest that both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockout of PREP confer protection against FMDV infection highlighting PREP as a promising target for antiviral drug development and for breeding disease-resistant livestock.

Discussion

Genome-wide CRISPR-based screening approaches have been instrumental in identifying key host factors involved in viral entry, replication, and host-virus interactions across diverse virus families. For instance, CRISPR screens have identified phospholipid scramblase 1 (PLSCR1) as a key modulator of SARS-CoV-2 entry, revealing that its knockout restricts viral entry independently of IFN induction [23]. Other studies have identified lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) [24] and major facilitator superfamily domain containing 6 (MFSD6) [25] as novel host entry receptors for SFTSV and EV-D68, respectively. CRISPR screens targeting coronaviruses have identified host factors such as high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1) and the switch/sucrose non-fermenting (SWI/SNF) chromatin remodeling complex, which regulate ACE2 expression and facilitate viral entry—revealing conserved therapeutic vulnerabilities across SARS-CoV-2 and related coronaviruses [26]. These studies demonstrate that genome-wide CRISPR-based screening is an effective approach for screening host factors critical for viral infection.

PREP is an atypical serine protease that plays a unique role in the maturation and degradation of peptide hormones and neuropeptides, particularly those smaller than 3 kDa and containing proline residues [27]. This distinctive function makes PREP a promising pharmacological target for treating various neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer′s disease [28]. Although PREP has been widely studied in neurodegenerative disorders, its role in viral infection and immunomodulatory signaling pathways remains poorly understood. In this study, we used a porcine cell-based model of FMDV infection to explore the role of the PREP gene in FMDV pathogenesis. Our findings reveal a novel function for PREP in the RIG-I and MDA5-mediated innate immune response, as well as its involvement in inhibition of FMDV replication. Furthermore, our in vivo experiments provide the first evidence of PREP-KO′s protective role against FMDV infection.

During viral infection, the host′s innate immune response triggers the generation of IFNs and antiviral cytokines, which are crucial for suppressing viral replication [29]. In this study, PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells showed significantly increased expression of IFN-β and ISGs. In contrast, low levels of IFN and ISGs were detected in the absence of viral infection in sgNC control cells. Mechanistic studies revealed that the activation of the type I IFN signaling pathway in PREP-KO cells was driven by increased mRNA levels of RIG-I and MDA5, which resulted from reduced PUM1 protein expression. Notably, PREP interacts with PUM1, and PREP-KO destabilizes PUM1 protein through both proteasomal degradation and apoptotic pathways, leading to enhanced RIG-I promoter activity. These findings establish a critical link between PREP and the type I IFN signaling pathway in IBRS-2 cells.

Previous studies have identified several proteins that interact with PREP, including alpha-synuclein (aSyn) [30], growth-associated protein 43 (GAP-43) [31], and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) [32]. Additionally, the transcription factor PU.1 has been identified as an interactor of PREP [33]. In this study, we identified PUM1 as a novel PREP interactor. Interestingly, we found that TBK1 expression remained unaffected in PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells, consistent with previous findings indicating that TBK1-mediated signaling to IRF3 in the type I IFN signaling pathway is inhibited in IBRS-2 cells [34].

However, the effects of PREP-KO on IFN signaling were not observed in PK-15 and HEK293T cells, indicating cell specific regulation. Although the antiviral phenotypes were observed across various cell lines, the underlying mechanisms may differ. Further studies are needed to explore how PREP-KO in IBRS-2 cells triggers PUM1 degradation via both proteasomal and apoptotic pathways, and how this reduction in PUM1 expression contributes to the enhanced activity of the porcine RIG-I promoter.

The FMDV life cycle involves several stages: binding, internalization, uncoating, genome replication, translation, capsid assembly, virion maturation, and release [6]. Our study found that PREP-KO significantly inhibited FMDV replication without affecting viral binding, internalization, or the activity of the FMDV IRES. A previous study showed that macropinosomes containing FMDV rapidly colocalize with EEA1-marked early endosomes after dissociating from the plasma membrane [35], which is consistent with our findings. The discrepancy between the marked reduction in viral titers and the relatively smaller decrease in 3D protein levels likely reflects differences in assay sensitivity and the particular aspects of viral replication measured by each assay. The 3D protein signal primarily reflects viral RNA replication and translation, and does not necessarily correlate directly with production of infectious virions. Additionally, we observed inhibition of FMDV replication in PK-15 and iPAM cells. Further studies are also needed to explore the mechanisms by which PREP-KO inhibits FMDV replication.

PREP-KO demonstrates a strong antiviral phenotype in porcine cells and provides protection to mice against sublethal FMDV doses. Additionally, mice treated with a PREP inhibitor baicalein exhibited significantly improved survival rates, highlighting the potential of PREP as a target for antiviral drug development. Despite the limited sample size of the animal experiments, the results of this study have preliminarily demonstrated the function of PREP-KO in resisting FMDV infection in vivo and have also laid a solid foundation for future research to confirm and expand the results in a larger study population. When evaluating the antiviral effects of the PREP inhibitor, we primarily used female mice. For experiments involving PREP-KO mice, wild-type controls were matched to the KO group in both gender and age, with equal numbers of males and females. Therefore, observed differences in antiviral effects may be influenced by these variations in gender and age. Baicalein, a plant-derived flavonoid, exhibits diverse biological activities and modulates host cellular pathways. It has been reported to disrupt the host mitochondrial electron transport chain, significantly inhibiting replication of SARS-CoV-2 and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) in Vero E6 cells. This inhibition results from interference with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), reduction of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), decreased oxygen consumption, and increased expression of ATF4, which enhances cellular stress responses [36, 37]. In addition, baicalein modulates Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling. For example, a liposomal formulation of baicalin protects mice against lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury by reducing alveolar wall thickening, pulmonary congestion, and fibrous hyperplasia. This protective effect is linked to decreased levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β), downregulation of TLR4 expression, and inhibition of NF-κB, JNK, and ERK phosphorylation [38]. Previous studies have not investigated the antiviral effects of baicalein specifically targeting PREP. We propose that baicalein′s antiviral activity arises from both its nonspecific effects and its inhibition of PREP.

One example of molecular breeding is gene editing in pigs to disrupt the PRRSV receptor CD163, effectively blocking PRRSV infection without affecting growth or development [39]. Since PREP is a novel gene associate with viral infection in this study, and PREP-KO mice show protection against high-fat-diet induced lipid accumulation and reduced liver inflammatory cell accumulation [40], further research is warrant to investigate whether PREP-KO mice offer additional protective effects. Moreover, understanding the detailed mechanisms of the PREP-virus interaction is essential, as this knowledge could inform precise gene editing strategies to maintain PREP′s normal physiological function while conferring an anti-FMDV phenotype in genetically modified pigs.

In summary, this study identifies PREP as a novel host factor associated with FMDV infection. The resistance observed in PREP-KO cells results from the activation of innate immune signaling pathway and the inhibition of FMDV replication (Fig. 7). These findings position PREP as a promising target for the development of antiviral drugs and the breeding of FMDV-resistant animals.

PREP is a multifunctional host factor required for FMDV infection. In IBRS-2 cells, PREP interacts with PUM1, while PREP-KO reduces the stability of PUM1. The destabilization of PUM1 enhances porcine RIG-I promoter activity, upregulating RIG-I and MDA5, thereby activating the type I IFN signaling pathway. In other FMDV-susceptible cells, PREP-KO inhibits the FMDV life cycle at the replication stage

Viral strain

The FMDV type O strain O/BY/CHA/2010 was obtained from the National Foot and Mouth Diseases Reference Laboratory, Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. All experiments involving FMDV were conducted in the Biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities in Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute of Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, approved by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs.

Plasmid construction

The FMDV reverse genetics plasmid and FMDV IRES dual-luciferase plasmid were preserved by Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute. The porcine RIG-I dual-luciferase plasmid were constructed by Azenta (Suzhou, China). pCRISPR-sg4, piggyBac transposase expression vector pPBase, doxycycline-induced Cas9 expression vector pCRISPR-S10 and piggyBac plasmid pPB-EF1a-eGFP-puro were maintained by the State Key Laboratory of Animal Biotech Breeding, China Agricultural University. The coding sequence of the PREP gene (Ensembl: ENSSSCG00000004368) was PCR-amplified from cDNA from IBRS-2 cells. The PCR products were cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector (Invitrogen) linearized with Kpn I and EcoR I. To construct the overexpression vector for the generation of GFP-PREP stable cell lines, the coding sequences of PREP and GFP were cloned into the pPB-EF1a-eGFP-puro vector, which was linearized with Hind III and Not I using the HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB, USA). PREP-GFP plasmid were generated by using the same method. All DNA constructs were verified by sequencing.

Cell culture, viral infection, and transfection

The IBRS-2, PK-15, iPAM, and HEK293T cell lines were purchased from ATCC (USA). The IBRS-2 cell line is free from Classical Swine Fever Virus (CSFV) contamination. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco′s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and incubated at 37˚C with 5% CO2. Cell transfections were performed using the JetPRIME (PolyPlus Transfection) reagent according to the manufacturer′s instructions. sgNC and PREP-KO cells were seeded into 12-well plates and transfected with 1 µg plasmid DNA when the cells were approximately 80% confluent. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were infected with FMDV O/BY/CHA/2010 strain at 37˚C with 5% CO2. After 1 h infection, the inoculum was removed and replaced with 1 mL of DMEM containing 2% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 10 to 36 h of infection, immunofluorescence, RT-qPCR, and western blot assays were conducted.

Generation of PREP-KO cell lines

An individual sgRNA targeting the PREP gene sequence (ATATGACTACGTGACCAACG) was cloned into the pCRISPR-sg4 vector. The pCRISPR-sg4 vector, along with the pCRISPR-S10 and pPBase plasmids, were co-transfected into IBRS-2 and PK-15 cells for 24 h. The polyclonal and monoclonal cells were generated as described in a previous study [8]. Non-knockout cells served as sgRNA negative control in this study.

Generation of stable PREP overexpression cell lines

IBRS-2 cells were transfected with plasmids encoding GFP-PREP and PREP-GFP, both based on the pPB-EF1a-eGFP-puro backbone. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were treated with 3 µg/mL puromycin. When approximately 80% of the cells had died, puromycin selection was stopped, and the medium was replaced with normal medium for recovery. One week later, GFP expression in the overexpressed cells was observed using a fluorescent microscope.

RNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis

High-throughput RNA-seq was performed by constructing RNA libraries for each group. Control IBRS-2 cells (sgNC-1, sgNC-2, and sgNC-3) were grouped together, and PREP-KO IBRS-2 cells (KO1, KO2, and KO3) were grouped separately. mRNA library construction and sequencing were performed using an Illumina HiSeq instrument with a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration, following the manufacturer′s instructions. The quality-trimmed reads were aligned to Sus scrofa reference genome (v11.1) using HISat2 (v2.0.1). Gene expression profiling was quantified by counting the reads mapped to each gene. FPKM values for individual unigene were calculated using HTSeq (v0.6.1) to estimate gene expression levels. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.18.3), with a P-value significance threshold of ≤ 0.05 and an absolute fold-change of ≥ 1.

Transfection of siRNAs

siRNAs targeting specific genes were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). IBRS-2 cells were seeded into 12-well plates and transfected with 80 pmol of siRNA when they reached approximately 60% confluence. Transfections were performed using JetPRIME, following the manufacturer′s instructions. Negative control siRNA (siRNA-NC) was also transfected as a reference. Cells were then used for further functional analyses 24 to 48 h post-transfection.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

For the porcine RIG-I dual-luciferase reporter assay, IBRS-2 cells were cultured in 24-well plates and transfected with siRNA targeting PUM1 using JetPRIME (reached 60% confluence). After 24 h, cells were transfected with porcine RIG-I dual-luciferase reporter plasmids for another 24 h. For the FMDV IRES dual-luciferase reporter assay, the sgNC and PREP-KO cells were transfected with FMDV IRES dual-luciferase reporter plasmids and incubated for 24 h. Samples were then collected using lysis buffer. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, USA) following the manufacturer′s protocol. The ratio of FLuc to RLuc expression indicates the relative activity of the porcine RIG-I promoter and FMDV-IRES.

FMDV binding and internalization assay

For FMDV binding assays, PREP-KO and sgNC IBRS-2 cells were incubated with FMDV (MOI = 10) on ice for 1 h. After washing with ice-cold PBS, the cells were either fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min on ice and analyzed by immunofluorescence or collected for RNA extraction, followed by qPCR analysis. For FMDV internalization assays, the cell-virus mixture was first incubated on ice, then transferred to 37 °C for 30 min. Following this, the cells were washed and treated with proteinase K (500 ng/mL) on ice for 1 h. Afterward, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min on ice for subsequent immunofluorescence analysis. Additionally, RNA was extracted from the cells, and the samples were subjected to qPCR analysis.

Immunofluorescence assay

FMDV VP1 protein expression was analyzed in sgNC, PREP-KO, and PREP-complemented PREP-KO cells cultured in 12-well plates. Cells were infected with FMDV at various MOIs, and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% TritonX-100 in PBS for 10 min. They were then incubated overnight at 4˚C with primary antibodies: a rabbit anti-FMDV VP1 protein polyclonal antibody (prepared in our laboratory) or anti-EEA1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #2411, 1:100). Primary antibodies were detected using Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluro 647 conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam) at a 1:1,000 dilution. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma, #D9542) for 2 min at room temperature. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific EVOS), and the proportion of positive cells was quantified using Image J software. The normalized mean fluorescence intensity was calculated by dividing each fluorescence intensity by the maximum fluorescence intensity. The colocalization analysis of EEA1-positive endosomes with FMDV was conducted using Coloc 2 (Mander′s colocalization coefficients) in Fiji.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer′s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScrip RT reagent Kit (Takara). For real-time qPCR, reactions were prepared with SYBR Green Mix (Bi-Rad, USA) and run on a CFX384 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The same amount of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis in each experiment, and cDNA served as a template for detecting the expression of host mRNA and FMDV RNA. The relative expression of mRNA was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, normalizing against GAPDH or β-actin mRNA. For absolute RT-qPCR, viral RNA (2 µL) was used as a template for cDNA synthesize with the One Step PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit (Takara). Specific primers for the FMDV 3D gene were used, in a final volume of 20 µL. FMDV copy numbers were calculated using the formula: FMDV copy number = 10^ ((42.85-CT value)/3.416). The FMDV 3D protein-encoding cDNA sequence (GenBank accession number: AH012984.2) was cloned into the pMD19-T vector and used as an internal reference for quantifying FMDV copy numbers. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates, and no-template controls were included to monitor potential contamination or genomic DNA presence. For absolute RT-qPCR, a negative control without sample addition was included in each experiment to ensure result accuracy and to rule out contamination.

Virus plaque assay

To assess virus plaque formation, sgNC and PREP-KO cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated with FMDV at various MOIs for 1 h at 37˚C with 5% CO2, gently swirling every 15 min. After the incubation, the inoculum was discarded, and the cells were overlaid with prewarmed 2× DMEM containing 1% Low Melting Point Agarose (Gibco), 2% FBS (fetal bovine serum), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated for 48 h, then fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 1 h and stained with 1% crystal violet to visualize plaques.

Virus titration

In our experiments, the MOI for FMDV inoculation used to label each cell type was determined by plaque assays conducted in different cell lines. After each inoculation, virus titers were measured using the TCID50 assay in BHK-21 cells for experimental convenience. Confluent cell monolayers in 12-well plates were inoculated with FMDV at an MOI of 0.01, or transfected with 1 µg of FMDV reverse genetics plasmids, or treated with varying amounts of PREP inhibitors. At different time points, cell supernatants were collected, and virus titers were determined.

Confocal microscopy

Immunofluorescence assays were performed to monitor FMDV binding and internalization in host cells, as described previously. Afterward, the images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica).

Immunoblot analysis

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) on ice for 20 min to obtain the total protein fraction. The cell lysates were centrifuged (12,000 rpm) for 10 min at 4 °C. Proteins were denatured with 1× SDS loading buffer, separated by 12% SDS PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and then probed with primary antibodies: PREP (Bethyl, #A305-348 A, 1:1,000), β-actin (Proteintech, #66009-lg, 1:2,000), PUM1 (Proteintech, #26256-1-AP, 1:1,000), VP1 (prepared in our laboratory, 1:1,000), 3D (prepared in our laboratory, 1:1,000), RIG-I (prepared in our laboratory, 1:1,000), TBK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #3504, 1:1,000), IRF3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4302, 1:1,000), IκBα (Cell Signaling Technology, #4812, 1:1,000), p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, #4764, 1:1,000), Myc (Cell Signaling Technology, #2278, 1:1,000), Flag (Cell Signaling Technology, #2368, 1:1,000). After incubation with primary antibodies, the membranes were probed with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Reactive bands were detected using a Bio-Rad imaging system. The relative expression levels of proteins were analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

HEK293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids for 24 h. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer. The supernatants were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with specific antibodies or IgG (negative control). The immune complexes were incubated with protein G-agarose beads (GE Healthcare, USA) for 6 h at 4 °C, washed three times with lysis buffer, and eluted using 2 × SDS-PAGE sample buffer for western blot analysis.

Generation of PREP-KO mice

Prep−/− mice on the C57BL/6J background were generated at the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). A gDNA fragment spanning exons 3 to 4 of the PREP gene was deleted by co-injecting sgRNAs and Cas9 mRNA into fertilized mouse eggs. Genotyping was performed via PCR amplification of gDNA extracted from mouse tails, followed by sanger sequencing. PCR genotyping was performed using the primers: P1, 5′-GGCAGCTGTACTTGGGGTAG-3′; P2, 5′-AGATCCACTCCCTCGCTGTA-3′; and P3, 5′-TTCATTAGGAAGGGCTGGCA-3′. Amplification of the wild-type allele with primers P1 and P2 yielded a 536-bp fragment, and the PREP-KO allele with primers P2 and P3 yielded an 839-bp fragment. Prep−/− mice were bred under specific-pathogen-free conditions.

In vivo FMDV infection

For the PREP inhibitor in vivo assays, groups of four-week-old C57BL/6J female mice (n = 12 per group) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Mice were pretreated with either 30 mg/kg or 50 mg/kg baicalein (a PREP inhibitor), or solvent control (DMSO) via intraperitoneal injection on days − 2, −1 and 0. The pretreated mice were then challenged with 103 TCID50 of mouse-adapted FMDV at day 0 via intraperitoneal injection. The survival rates of each group were recorded daily for 10 days post-challenge. Tissue sections were fixed in 4% formalin solution, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-µm tissue sections. Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and imaged under a light microscope. To assess FMDV loads in various tissues, total RNA was extracted from the tissue samples. The FMDV load was quantified by absolute RT-qPCR, determining the FMDV copy numbers in 1 µg of total RNA extracted from different tissues. For the therapeutic treatment assay, 4-week-old C57BL/6J female mice were first challenged with 103 TCID50 of mouse-adapted FMDV via intraperitoneal injection on day 0. Subsequently, the mice were treated with either 50 mg/kg baicalein or DMSO via intraperitoneal injection on days 0, 0.5 and 1. The survival rates of the mice were monitored daily for 10 days post-challenge.

For Prep−/− mice in vivo assays, sex- and age-matched WT and Prep−/− mice (approximately 5 weeks old, n = 8) were subjected to intraperitoneal injection with mouse-adapted FMDV (103 TCID50) in 100 µL PBS. The control group received an equal volume of PBS via the same route. The survival rates were monitored daily for 10 days post-challenge.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism Version 9 (GraphPad). The means ± S.D. was determined for each treatment group in the separated experiments. Two-tailed Student′s t-test was used to determine significant differences between treatment and control groups (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; ns, no significant). For mouse survival studies, log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was used to determine significant differences between treatment and control groups.
