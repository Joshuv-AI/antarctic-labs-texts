BOLT: Universal Classifier Training via a Bayes-Error Upper Bound
Mohammadreza Tavasoli Naeini, Ali Bereyhi, Morteza Noshad, Ben Liang, Alfred O. Hero

Abstract
This work introduces a novel approach to compute a universal upper bound on the ber ( ber ) from classifier-output samples, which serves as a Bayes-error-related performance criterion for the underlying classification task. We show that the bound is tight in the binary case and characterize the max-sum gap that appears in the aggregate multiclass formulation. Based on this bound, we define a new loss function, called Bayes optimal learning threshold (BOLT), for training parameterized classifiers. For each data sample, the BOLT loss computes a term whose expectation upper-bounds the Bayes error, so that during training the model parameters can be updated toward minimizing this bound. Numerical experiments with both synthetic and real-world datasets show that, in the reported settings, BOLT training often attains lower held-out classification error than cross-entropy training.

Introduction

A fundamental concept in machine learning is the inherent limit of classifier accuracy dictated by the
ber
(
ber
)
[
12
,
11
]
.
For a given classification problem, the
ber

is defined as the minimum probability of misclassification that any classifier can achieve
[
11
]
. The classifier that achieves this error rate is
Bayes optimal
, as it minimizes the Bayes risk of the classifier with respect to the
0
-
1
loss.
An example is the
map
(
map
) classifier, which assigns each data point to the class having the largest posterior probability
[
12
,
3
]
. Understanding the
ber
is crucial, as it provides a benchmark for comparing the performance of classifiers.

When the true underlying data distribution is known, the
ber
has an explicit integral representation. Consider the case of
m
-class classification, where the unknown class of a feature instance
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

C\in\left\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{m}\right\}
. Assume that the class-conditional data densities
p_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
are known, as are the prior class probabilities
\pi_{i}=\Pr\left\{C=C_{i}\right\}
. In this case, the
ber

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
is computed as
[
3
]

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=1-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{i\in[m]}\left\{\pi_{i}\,p_{i}(x)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1)

The expression in (
1
) is computationally hard in high-dimensional data spaces, even when the class-conditional densities are known. Moreover, in most machine learning applications, the class-conditional densities are unknown. This has motivated the development of approximations and bounds on the
ber
. This work develops a novel upper bound on the
ber
that can be empirically estimated from training data when the data distribution is unknown.

I-A

Contributions

We establish a novel upper bound on the
ber
, which can be empirically approximated by sampling from a learning model. The bound is
universal
in the sense that it can be computed from output samples of an
arbitrary
learning model, e.g., a neural network. We show that the bound is tight in the binary case. For the multiclass case, we characterize the max-sum gap between the aggregate BOLT bound and the
ber
and derive explicit upper bounds on its size.
Motivated by these results, we propose a novel loss function that can be used to train a parameterized classifier toward minimizing the Bayes-error upper bound. We call this loss

bolt
(
bolt
)
. Using
bolt
, the model training objective is aligned with minimizing a Bayes-error upper bound, with binary tightness and a bounded multiclass gap. Our experiments compare BOLT with conventional cross-entropy training.
The key contributions of this paper are four-fold.

1.

We establish a universal upper bound on the
ber
for a general multiclass classification problem. The bound is derived by relating the Bayes error to an
f
-divergence between class-conditional distributions, evaluated by a hinge function.

2.

Invoking the universal bound, we propose a framework for estimating an upper bound on the
ber
from output samples of an arbitrary learning model. This
ber
estimator defines a risk, i.e., expected loss, that can be estimated from data samples. We call the corresponding loss function
bolt
and use it as a supervised surrogate to minimize a bound on the
ber
.

3.

We characterize the tightness of the proposed bound and the statistical accuracy of its sample estimator by deriving bias and variance bounds for a likelihood-ratio plug-in estimator of the optimized BOLT bound. In the binary case, the max-sum gap vanishes, and the estimator is asymptotically unbiased as the likelihood-ratio estimation error vanishes and the sample size grows. In the multiclass case, the bias additionally contains the max-sum gap, whose size is explicitly bounded in terms of the number of classes and the
ber
.

4.

We illustrate the universal upper bound and evaluate BOLT training through numerical experiments.
We show that, on the reported held-out test splits, BOLT often attains lower classification error than the standard cross-entropy baseline. We also compare the learned representations through qualitative feature projections.

Characterizing the

[

,
17
]
. Direct computation of the
ber
is intractable in most applications because of the unknown data distribution and computational complexity, especially in high-dimensional spaces. This has led to several approaches for bounding or approximating the

II-A

Estimating Bayes Error

Estimating and bounding the
ber
has been a longstanding challenge in statistical pattern classification. Foundational work includes Fano’s inequality
[
13
,
8
]
, Chernoff bounds
[
7
]
, and Kovalevskij’s entropy-error bound
[
21
]
. Hoeffding concentration bounds
[
19
]
and Vapnik–Chervonenkis theory
[
41
]
address sampling deviations and generalization. Classical pattern-recognition bounds based on Mahalanobis and Bhattacharyya distances have also been widely used, as they capture class separability
[
26
,
2
]
. In high-dimensional spaces, these methods are affected by the curse of dimensionality, where the complexity of estimating distances or densities grows rapidly with feature dimension
[
11
,
14
]
. Entropic quantities, including cross-entropy and
kl
(
kl
) divergence, can characterize aspects of class separation, but they do not directly coincide with the
ber

[
8
,
3
]
.

Empirical methods for estimating Bayes error have shown substantial progress in recent years. Berisha et al.
[
1
]
introduced empirically estimable classification bounds based on a nonparametric divergence measure, and Sekeh et al.
[
35
]
developed multiclass
ber
bounds based on generalized Henze-Penrose divergence. Ishida et al.
[
20
]
proposed a direct model-free estimator for binary classification that averages class-assignment uncertainties to produce unbiased and consistent estimates. The
\varepsilon
-ball estimator framework by Noshad et al.
[
31
]
ensures consistent Bayes-error estimates using local density estimators and can be applied to optimize classifiers. More recently, Ushio et al.
[
40
]
studied estimation of the optimal classification error using soft labels and calibration. Although these estimators are statistically attractive, neighborhood-based or direct nonparametric procedures can become expensive as the sample size and feature dimension grow.

Most existing methods rely on specialized estimators, explicit modeling assumptions, or distinct ensembling strategies to approximate or bound the
ber
. In contrast, this work introduces an alternative method that uses a classifier model only through its output samples. This enables estimation of a model-dependent upper bound on the
ber
via the
bolt
loss. The method is designed to align the training objective with the goal of reducing a
ber
upper bound. This approach imposes the same per-epoch time complexity (FLOPs) as standard empirical risk minimization. This is in contrast to the neighborhood-based
ber
estimator proposed by Noshad et al.
[
31
]
, which incurs substantially higher computational cost in high-dimensional spaces.

II-B

Using Divergence Measures for Learning

Divergence measures have been applied to quantifying class separability and have significantly influenced the development of robust machine learning models. The
kl
divergence was an influential early measure, laying the groundwork for subsequent divergence measures. The notion of
f
-divergence, introduced by Csiszár in
[
9
]
and expanded by Liese and Vajda in
[
24
]
, provides a versatile measure for assessing distributional differences.

Among various
f
-divergence-based error bounds, a line of work develops nonparametric divergence estimators that can be used to characterize the
ber
. Moon and Hero
[
27
]
developed ensemble density plug-in estimators of multivariate
f
-divergence. Sricharan et al.
[
37
]
studied weighted ensembles of plug-in estimators for entropy and related information measures. Noshad and Hero
[
30
]
introduced a weighted nearest-neighbor graph estimator of the Henze–Penrose divergence for bounding binary classification error without explicit density estimation. More recent work has adopted these tools to benchmark modern neural networks against Bayes-optimal performance. Theisen et al.
[
39
]
exploited normalizing flows and the invariance of the
ber
under invertible transformations to approximate it on standard image benchmarks and to quantify how close state-of-the-art classifiers are to this limit. Chen et al.
[
5
,
6
]
studied Bayes-error estimation through identification of Bayes-noise samples, including an efficient method based on hierarchical clustering and approximate nearest neighbors. Wheat et al.
[
42
]
compared
k
-nearest-neighbor, generalized Henze–Penrose, and kernel-density estimators in synthetic binary-classification settings, examining their accuracy and uncertainty.

The above-mentioned lines of work treat the
ber
(or its bounds) primarily as a post-hoc assessment of trained models. In this study, we deviate from this convention by proposing a novel approach in which a divergence-based upper bound on the
ber
is integrated into the training objective itself. This enables the training loop to reduce an upper bound on the Bayes error.
Connections between classification losses and
f
-divergences were studied by Nguyen et al.
[
28
]
and Reid and Williamson
[
33
]
. Variational estimation of divergences and likelihood ratios was developed by Nguyen et al.
[
29
]
.

III
Universal Bound on Bayes Error

Consider an
m
-class classification problem on
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with class label
C\in\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\}
. Let
\Lambda\in[m]
be the class index and denote by
\lambda
its realization. Let
\pi_{\lambda}:=\Pr\{C=C_{\lambda}\}>0
, and let
P_{\lambda}
and
p_{\lambda}
denote, respectively, the conditional distribution and density of
X
given
C=C_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda\in[m]
.
The Bayes optimal classifier is given by the
map
estimator, which estimates the class of sample
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle\hat{C}(x)=C_{\argmax_{\lambda\in[m]}\Pr\{C=C_{\lambda}|x\}}.

(3)

The posterior
\Pr\left\{C=C_{\lambda}|x\right\}
is computed from the prior probabilities
\pi_{\lambda}
and likelihoods
p_{\lambda}(x)
via Bayes’ rule. The
ber
is given by the expression in (
1
). In the sequel, we present some key notions and results that are used throughout the paper.

III-A

Preliminaries

Following
[
31
]
, the multiclass
ber
admits an exact decomposition that is useful for our development. For
\lambda\in[m]
, define

r_{\lambda}(x):=\max_{i\in[\lambda]}\pi_{i}p_{i}(x).

(4)

Then the
ber
can be written as

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=1-\pi_{1}-\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl[r_{\lambda}(x)-r_{\lambda-1}(x)\bigr]\,dx.

(5)

This representation decomposes the multiclass
ber
into incremental terms obtained by adding one class at a time, and it serves as the starting point for the divergence-based formulation developed in this section. The decomposition in (
5
) is connected to
f
-divergence by observing that each incremental term admits a hinge-based divergence representation. Let us first recall the definition of
f
-divergence.

Definition 1

(
f
-Divergence
[
9
]
)
.

Let distributions
P
and
Q
be defined on a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
with the probability density functions
p(x)
and
q(x)
, respectively. Let
f:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be convex. Then, the
f
-divergence between
P
and
Q
is defined as

D_{f}(P\parallel Q)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q(x)f\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(6)

where
f
is referred to as the generator function. At
p=0<q
, we use
f(0)=\lim_{u\downarrow 0}f(u)
; at
q=0<p
, the integrand is
pf_{\infty}
, where
f_{\infty}=\lim_{u\to\infty}f(u)/u
; and the integrand is zero if
p=q=0
. The usual normalization is
f(1)=0
, while unnormalized generators define generalized divergence functionals.

The
f
-divergence can be bounded in terms of population expectations. The relevant variational bound is stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(Variational lower bound on
f
-divergence
[
32
]
)
.

Let
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
denote the Fenchel conjugate of the generator function
f
, defined as
[
34
]

f^{*}(s)=\sup_{u\in(0,\infty)}\{su-f(u)\}.

(7)

Then, the
f
-divergence between
P
and
Q
satisfies

D_{f}(P\|Q)\geq\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[f^{*}(h(X))]\right\},

(8)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a class of integrable functions
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
h\leq f_{\infty}

P
-almost everywhere on
\{q=0\}
. For the hinge generators, this condition is
h\leq 0
there.

Using the variational bound above in (
8
), we can derive an upper bound on the
ber
for a classification task. The derivation follows two main steps: (
i
) we relate the
ber
to the
f
-divergence, and (
ii
) we use the variational bound to bound the
ber
. For tractability, we first derive this bound for binary classification and then extend it to the general case.

III-B

Bounding Binary BER

The first step of the derivation rests on a key observation: the
ber
can be expressed in terms of the
f
-divergence when
f
is set to be the hinge loss. To see this point, let us first define the hinge loss.

Definition 2

(Hinge Loss)
.

For
u\in\mathbb{R}
, the hinge loss function is defined as

f_{\mathrm{hng}}(u)=\max\{0,1-u\}.

(9)

The hinge loss is known for its role as a surrogate loss for classification, e.g., in support vector machines. Using this function, we can directly represent the
ber
as an affine function of an
f
-divergence with hinge generator.

Lemma 2

(Binary Bayes error with uniform priors)
.

In binary classification with equal prior probabilities
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}=1/2
, the
ber
satisfies

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}D_{f_{\mathrm{hng}}}\!\left(P_{1}\|P_{2}\right).

(10)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Using Lemma
2
, we can bound the binary
ber
directly via the variational bound on the
f
-divergence. This establishes a bound that can be estimated by sampling a learning model, i.e., the function
h
in Lemma
1
.
This observation
can be extended to a general binary classification with arbitrary priors through the
shifted
hinge loss and its Fenchel conjugate. To this end, let us first define the shifted hinge loss.

Definition 3

(Shifted Hinge Loss)
.

For
u\in\mathbb{R}
and shift
t>0
, the shifted hinge loss
f_{\mathrm{shng}}
is defined as

f_{\mathrm{shng}}(u;t)=\max\{0,t-u\}.

(11)

The Fenchel conjugate of the shifted hinge loss is linear on a bounded interval, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(Fenchel conjugate of shifted hinge)
.

The Fenchel conjugate of the shifted hinge loss restricted to
u>0
is given by

f_{\mathrm{shng}}^{*}(s)=\begin{cases}-t,&\text{if }s<-1,\\
st,&\text{if }-1\leq s\leq 0,\\
+\infty,&\text{if }s>0.\end{cases}

(12)

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

In a binary classification with an arbitrary prior, the
ber
is an affine function of the
f
-divergence with the shifted hinge loss as generator. This enables us to directly bound the error via the variational bound as given in the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Binary BER Bound)
.

Consider a binary classification task whose classes
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
occur with prior probabilities
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, respectively. For any integrable function
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[-1,0]
, the
ber

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq

\displaystyle\;\pi_{2}+\pi_{2}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{2}}[h(X)]-\pi_{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[h(X)].

(13)

Proof.

Starting with the expression for the
ber
in (
1
), we have in the binary case

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}

\displaystyle=1-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max\left\{\pi_{1}p_{1}(x),\pi_{2}p_{2}(x)\right\}\mathrm{d}x,

(14a)

\displaystyle=1-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\pi_{1}p_{1}(x)+[\pi_{2}p_{2}(x)-\pi_{1}p_{1}(x)]_{+}\right)\mathrm{d}x.

(14b)

Using the identity
\max\{a,b\}=a+\max\{0,b-a\}
and the fact that
\pi_{2}=1-\pi_{1}
, we can write

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}

\displaystyle=1-\pi_{1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathcal{X}}[\pi_{2}p_{2}(x)-\pi_{1}p_{1}(x)]_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(15a)

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}-{}\pi_{1}\int_{\{x:p_{2}(x)>0\}}\max\Biggl\{0,\,\frac{\pi_{2}}{\pi_{1}}-\frac{p_{1}(x)}{p_{2}(x)}\Biggr\}p_{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(15b)

The positive-part integrand is zero wherever
p_{2}(x)=0
, which justifies the restriction in the last integral. The integrand is the shifted hinge loss applied to the likelihood ratio

{p_{1}(x)}/{p_{2}(x)}

whose shift is at
{\pi_{2}}/{\pi_{1}}
. As a result, the integral determines the
f
-divergence with shifted hinge generator. We can hence write

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{2}-\pi_{1}D_{t}(P_{1}\|P_{2}),

(16)

where we define
D_{t}
to be the
f
-divergence with shifted hinge generator at
t={\pi_{2}}/{\pi_{1}}
, i.e.,

D_{t}(P_{1}\|P_{2})=\int_{\{x:p_{2}(x)>0\}}f_{\mathrm{shng}}\left(\frac{p_{1}(x)}{p_{2}(x)};t\right)p_{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

For
t>1
,
f_{\mathrm{shng}}(1;t)=t-1
, so
D_{t}
is the generalized divergence functional defined above. We now use the variational bound with the shifted hinge generator to bound
D_{t}(P\|Q)
. For any integrable
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
h\leq 0
on
\{q=0\}
, it gives

\displaystyle D_{t}(P\|Q)\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[f_{\mathrm{shng}}^{*}(h(X))].

(17)

Lemma
3
implies that if
h>0
on a set of positive
Q
-measure, then the variational lower bound is trivial. Values below
-1
can be clipped to
-1
, since this increases
\mathbb{E}_{P}[h]
while leaving
f_{\mathrm{shng}}^{*}(h)
unchanged. We hence restrict
h
to
h:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]
, for which we can write
f_{\mathrm{shng}}^{*}(h(X))=h(X)t
. This concludes that

\displaystyle D_{t}(P_{1}\|P_{2})\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[h(X)]-t\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{2}}[h(X)].

(18)

Substituting this bound in (
16
) and recalling that
t={\pi_{2}}/{\pi_{1}}
, the bound in Theorem
1
is concluded.
∎

Theorem
1
holds for any measurable
h
with values in
[-1,0]
. A suitable subclass of such measurable functions can be approximated by a parameterized model, e.g., a neural network. This leads to a straightforward implication of the result, stated in the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Computational bound on binary BER)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\left\{h_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]:\theta\in\Theta\right\}

be a set of integrable functions parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
,
where
\Theta
is the parameter space defining the family of functions. The

satisfies

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq{}&\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Bigl\{\pi_{2}+\pi_{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{2}}[h_{\theta}(X)]-\pi_{1}\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[h_{\theta}(X)]\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(19)

Proof.

The
ber
is bounded as in Theorem
1
for all
h_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}
. We can hence conclude that it is also bounded by the infimum of these upper bounds over
\mathcal{H}
.
∎

Corollary
1
gives a model-based approach to estimate an upper bound on the
ber
. We show below in Corollary
2
that this bound is tight when
\mathcal{H}
contains all measurable functions taking values in
[-1,0]
. Section
V-A
provides a binary Gaussian illustration. A parameterized family approaches this value if it approximates a binary risk minimizer in
L^{1}(P_{1}+P_{2})
, since the objective is linear in

.

III-C

Extension to Multiclass Classification

A multiclass classification task can be viewed as a sequence of nested binary classifications. In particular, each class
C_{\lambda}
is compared with the preceding classes
C_{1},\ldots,C_{\lambda-1}
. This is the basis for the extension of the bound in Theorem
1
to a classification with an arbitrary number of classes.

Theorem 2

(Multiclass BER bound)
.

Consider a classification task with
m
classes
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
on the data space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let

h=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}],

where
h_{1}(x)\equiv-1
and
h_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]
is integrable for
i\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
. Then, the
ber
is bounded as

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq 1-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{m}\pi_{\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda},

(20)

where, for each
\lambda\in[m]
, we define

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}\left[\sum_{i=\lambda+1}^{m}h_{i}(X)-h_{\lambda}(X)\right].

(21)

Sketch of the Proof.

The proof starts with the Bayes error expression in (
5
)
with
r_{\lambda}
being defined in (
4
). Define the increment

\displaystyle\Delta_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq r_{\lambda}(x)-r_{\lambda-1}(x),

(22)

which captures the contribution of adding class
C_{\lambda}
to the MAP rule. For each
\lambda\geq 2
, we bound the
\lambda
-th increment using
a binary decision between class
C_{\lambda}
and the preceding classes
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{\lambda-1}\}
. Define their aggregate prior as

\displaystyle\rho_{\lambda-1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{i}.

(23)

Let
Q_{\lambda-1}
denote the distribution with density

\displaystyle q_{\lambda-1}(x)\coloneqq\rho_{\lambda-1}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{i}p_{i}(x).

The normalized priors of this binary subproblem are
\pi_{\lambda}/(\pi_{\lambda}+\rho_{\lambda-1})
and
\rho_{\lambda-1}/(\pi_{\lambda}+\rho_{\lambda-1})
.
This defines a binary subproblem whose Bayes error
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}

controls the integral of
\Delta_{\lambda}(x)
. Applying the upper bound in Theorem
1
to this binary subproblem with function
h_{\lambda}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]
yields an upper bound on
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}
in terms of
P_{\lambda}
and
Q_{\lambda-1}
. Substituting these bounds into
(
5
)
and regrouping terms by classes the bound in (
20
) is concluded. The detailed derivation is given in Appendix
C
.

IV
Bayes Optimal Learning Threshold

As shown in Theorem
2
, an upper bound on the
ber
can be estimated from data samples using a suitable measurable function
h
. This insight motivates a new framework for supervised learning in which a parameterized model is trained to minimize its estimated error bound. In this section, we formulate this framework through defining the BOLT loss. We then analyze the proposed framework through characterization of the BOLT population risk and its optimizer.

IV-A

BOLT Loss

Consider a supervised learning problem with the class-labeled dataset

\mathbb{D}=\left\{(x_{i},\lambda_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times{[m]}:i\in[n]\right\},

(24)

whose samples are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to a joint distribution that is determined by the class priors
\{\pi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda=1}^{m}
and the class-conditional distributions
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda=1}^{m}
. For the underlying discriminative learning task, we directly parameterize a bounded model

h_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]^{m},

(25)

where the model returns a set of scores for the classes, i.e.,

h_{\theta}(x)=\bigl[h_{\theta,1}(x),\ldots,h_{\theta,m}(x)\bigr]^{\top},

(26)

with the convention

h_{\theta,1}(x)\equiv-1,

(27)

and the remaining coordinates being learnable bounded scores. In practice, these coordinates can be obtained by appropriate activation in the parameterized model, e.g., the negative sigmoid activation applied to real-valued logits in the last layer of a neural network.

Using the parameterized model
h_{\theta}
, we can estimate an upper bound on the
ber
from the samples in
\mathbb{D}
. We propose training the model by minimizing this estimator. Our later analysis establishes binary tightness and bounds the multiclass gap between the optimized BOLT risk and the
ber
. Considering this proposed framework, we now define the resulting sample-level estimator of the
ber
bound as a function, which we refer to as the BOLT loss.

Definition 4

(BOLT Loss)
.

For an input
x\in\mathcal{X}
with class index
\lambda\in[m]
, let

h_{\theta}(x)=\bigl[h_{\theta,1}(x),\ldots,h_{\theta,m}(x)\bigr]^{\top},

(28)

where
h_{\theta,1}(x)\equiv-1
and
h_{\theta,\lambda}(x)\in[-1,0]
for
\lambda\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
. The BOLT loss between the model output and the label is defined as

\ell_{\text{{BOLT}}}\bigl(h_{\theta}(x),\lambda\bigr)=1-\sum_{i=\lambda+1}^{m}h_{\theta,i}(x)+h_{\theta,\lambda}(x).

(29)

The BOLT population risk determines the universal upper bound on the
ber
. Let us write this risk as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,\Lambda}\!\left[\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}(h_{\theta}(X),\Lambda)\right],

(30a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{m}\pi_{\lambda}\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}\!\left[\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}(h_{\theta}(X),\lambda)\right],

(30b)

where the second equality follows from the joint distribution of
(X,\Lambda)
.

Lemma 4
.

The BOLT population risk bounds the
ber
from above, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
.

Proof.

Substituting the
bolt
loss into the risk gives

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=1-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{m}\pi_{\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\lambda}}\left[\sum_{i=\lambda+1}^{m}h_{\theta,i}(x)-h_{\theta,\lambda}(x)\right].

(31)

Using the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
in Theorem
2
, this can be written as

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=1-\sum_{\lambda=1}^{m}\pi_{\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda},

(32)

which coincides with the upper bound in Theorem
2
. The result is then concluded straightforwardly from that theorem.
∎

Lemma
4
suggests that minimizing the BOLT population risk guides the model to compute scores that yield a tighter bound on the
ber
. In practice, we can estimate this risk from the samples in
\mathbb{D}
and use a stochastic gradient-based optimizer to update the model. Although intuitive, the properties of the model trained with this approach remain an open question. In the sequel, we characterize the minimizer of the BOLT population risk and its gap to the
ber
. Our results reveal two key points: (i) an endpoint BOLT risk minimizer gives a Bayes-optimal classifier in binary classification and a bounded excess error in multiclass classification, and (ii) the optimized BOLT population risk has an explicitly bounded gap above the
ber
. We also characterize a likelihood-ratio plug-in estimate of the optimized bound.

IV-B

BOLT Population Risk Minimization

We characterize the BOLT population risk in two steps. (i) We first derive an alternative expression for the
ber
, similar to the upper bound in Theorem
2
, as a sum of hinge terms involving pairwise likelihood ratios. (ii) We then show that the BOLT population risk is minimized by a sum-based threshold minimizer. This enables us to explicitly characterize the resulting gap between the BOLT population risk and the
ber
. For the sake of brevity, we drop the dependence of
h
on the model parameter
\theta
in the sequel, wherever it is clear from the context. We start our derivation with the following lemma.

Lemma 5

(Bayes error as a hinge sum)
.

For each class
\lambda\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
, define the class likelihood ratios

U_{j/\lambda}(x):=\frac{p_{j}(x)}{p_{\lambda}(x)}\quad\text{on }\{p_{\lambda}(x)>0\}

(33)

for
1\leq j<\lambda
, and the hinge expression for real-valued arguments
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\lambda-1}

t_{0}^{(\lambda)}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\lambda-1}):=\left[\pi_{\lambda}-\max_{1\leq j<\lambda}\pi_{j}u_{j}\right]_{+},

(34)

where
\left[x\right]_{+}=\max\left\{0,x\right\}
. Then, the
ber
can be written as

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=1-\pi_{1}-\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\lambda}}\!\Big[t_{0}^{(\lambda)}\!\big(U_{1/\lambda}(x),\ldots,U_{(\lambda-1)/\lambda}(x)\big)\Big].

(35)

Proof.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

Theorem 3

(BOLT minimizer and max-sum gap)
.

Consider the BOLT population risk in (
31
), with dependence on
\theta
suppressed, where
h_{1}(x)\equiv-1
and
h_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]
for
i=2,\ldots,m
. For
\lambda\geq 2
, define

\displaystyle S_{\lambda-1}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{j}p_{j}(x),

(36a)

\displaystyle r_{\lambda-1}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{1\leq j<\lambda}\pi_{j}p_{j}(x).

(36b)

Then, a pointwise minimizer is given by
h_{1}^{\star}(x)\equiv-1
and

h^{\star}_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&\pi_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}(x)>S_{\lambda-1}(x),\\[2.0pt]
0,&\pi_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}(x)\leq S_{\lambda-1}(x),\end{cases}

(37)

for
\lambda=2,\ldots,m
. Furthermore, the minimal risk
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
is given by

\mathcal{L}^{\star}=1-\pi_{1}-\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\pi_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}(x)-S_{\lambda-1}(x)\right]_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(38)

and its gap to the
ber
, i.e.,
\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}^{\star}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
, is

\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}=\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\min\left\{\left[\pi_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}(x)-r_{\lambda-1}(x)\right]_{+},S_{\lambda-1}(x)-r_{\lambda-1}(x)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(39)

Proof.

See Appendix
F
.
∎

Theorem
3
has interesting implications. The first key point is the fact that we are able to build a classifier directly from the learned score model
h
. For any endpoint-valued score model
h:\mathcal{X}\to\{-1,0\}^{m}
with
h_{1}(x)\equiv-1
, define

C_{h}(x)\coloneqq C_{\max\{i\in[m]:h_{i}(x)=-1\}}.

(40)

The maximization set is nonempty because
h_{1}(x)=-1
. For an endpoint-valued score model, the BOLT loss is zero for the class selected by (
40
) and is at least one for every other class. Hence,

\mathbbm{1}\{C_{h}(x)\neq C_{\lambda}\}\leq\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\bigl(h(x),\lambda\bigr).

(41)

The second key point is that the classifier induced by the endpoint population minimizer has error at most the
ber
plus the max-sum gap. More precisely,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq\Pr\!\left\{C_{h^{\star}}(X)\neq C\right\}\leq\mathcal{L}^{\star}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}.

(42)

Thus, the gap is zero in the binary case and can be nonzero in the multiclass case. The class order affects the bound, the minimizer and the induced classifier.
For continuous bounded scores, a minimum-loss rule is
C_{\argmin_{\lambda\in[m]}\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}(h(x),\lambda)}
, with ties resolved by the smallest index. It agrees with (
40
) at endpoint-valued scores, to which the inequalities above apply.

Corollary 2

(Binary BOLT minimizer)
.

In the binary case, i.e.,
m=2
, define
t\coloneqq{\pi_{2}}/{\pi_{1}}
and
U(x)\coloneqq{p_{1}(x)}/{p_{2}(x)}
on
\{p_{2}>0\}
. Then, the binary
ber
is written as

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=\pi_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{2}}\!\bigl[t_{0}(U(x))\bigr],

(43)

for the hinge function
t_{0}(u):=\big[\pi_{2}-\pi_{1}u\big]_{+}
. Moreover, let
h_{1}^{\star}(x)\equiv-1
, and

h_{2}^{\star}(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&p_{2}(x)>0\text{ and }U(x)<t,\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(44)

Then, the BOLT population risk is tight at
h^{\star}=(h_{1}^{\star},h_{2}^{\star})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{\star}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
, and the
map
classifier is recovered from
h^{\star}
as

C_{\mathrm{MAP}}(x)=\begin{cases}C_{1},&\text{if }h_{2}^{\star}(x)=0,\\[2.0pt]
C_{2},&\text{if }h_{2}^{\star}(x)=-1.\end{cases}

(45)

Proof.

See Appendix
E
.

Remark 1

(Relation to the binary hinge loss)
.

Using the bipolar representation in the binary case, i.e.,
s=+1
for
\lambda=1
and
s=-1
for
\lambda=2
, and defining
g(x)\coloneqq 2h_{2}(x)+1

Because
h_{2}(x)\in[-1,0]
, we have
g(x)\in[-1,1]
and therefore
1-sg(x)\geq 0
. Thus, the positive-part operator in the standard hinge loss is redundant in this bounded-score parameterization.

\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\bigl(h(x),\lambda\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-sg(x)\bigr).

(46)

This is the same unit-margin hinge term used in support vector machines (SVMs), scaled by
1/2
and evaluated on a bounded score. If
h_{2}(x)=-\sigma(z(x))
, then the binary loss is
\sigma(sz)
, a symmetric sigmoid loss studied in

.

Theorem 4

(Upper bound on the max-sum gap)
.

Let

\Delta^{\star}\coloneqq\min\left\{\left(1-2^{-(m-2)}\right)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}},\frac{m(1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}})-1}{2}\right\}.

(47)

The max-sum gap
\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}
is bounded from above as

0\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}\leq\Delta^{\star}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}.

(48)

Let

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{\star}\coloneqq\frac{m-1}{m+2\left(1-2^{-(m-2)}\right)}.

(49)

The upper bound
\Delta^{\star}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

If
0\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{\star}
, then

\Delta^{\star}=\left(1-2^{-(m-2)}\right)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
.

2.

If
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{\star}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq(m-1)/m
, then

\Delta^{\star}=\bigl[m(1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}})-1\bigr]/2
.

Proof.

See Appendix
G
.
∎

The upper bound in Theorem
4
is always less than or equal to the
ber
and is small when the
ber
is small, which is often the case in practice. Combining (
42
) and (
48
), the classifier built from the BOLT population risk minimizer can never have error larger than
2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
. The bound depends on the number of classes and the
ber
. Its second branch also vanishes in the random-label regime with uniform posterior mass, where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=(m-1)/m
.

IV-C

Learning via Empirical BOLT Risk Minimization

In practice, the BOLT algorithm estimates the risk via a batch estimator computed on a mini-batch
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{D}
. This means that at each iteration of a gradient-based optimization algorithm, the BOLT risk is estimated as

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}(y_{i},\lambda_{i}),

(50)

with
y_{i}=h_{\theta}(x_{i})
being the sample model output. The risk gradient in this case is estimated as

\nabla_{\theta}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\mathrm{BOLT}}(y_{i},\lambda_{i}),

(51)

and used to update the model parameter
\theta
. Noting that BOLT is computed by an analytic expression, we can readily compute the gradient estimator at each iteration via standard backpropagation.

An independent test-sample average of the BOLT loss estimates the trained model’s population risk
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
. We next characterize a separate likelihood-ratio plug-in estimator of the optimized BOLT bound. In general, this estimator contains the deterministic max-sum gap identified in Theorem
3
, as well as ratio-approximation and finite-sample errors. We characterize its conditional bias and variance. To this end, let

\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}(x):=\bigl(\widehat{U}_{1/\lambda}(x),\ldots,\widehat{U}_{(\lambda-1)/\lambda}(x)\bigr),

(52)

for
\lambda\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
, denote nonnegative measurable estimates, fitted on an auxiliary set of samples
\mathcal{T}_{U}
, of the likelihood ratios in the vector

U_{\cdot/\lambda}(x):=\bigl(U_{1/\lambda}(x),\ldots,U_{(\lambda-1)/\lambda}(x)\bigr),

(53)

with
U_{j/\lambda}(x)
defined in Lemma
5
. These ratio estimates are additional inputs, since bounded BOLT scores alone do not determine them. Let
M_{\lambda}>0
denote the fixed number of samples from class
C_{\lambda}
, used to estimate the optimized bound, and let

M:=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{m}M_{\lambda}

(54)

be the total number of test samples. Define the aggregate hinge term as

t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(u_{1/\lambda},\ldots,u_{(\lambda-1)/\lambda}):=\left[\pi_{\lambda}-\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{j}\,u_{j/\lambda}\right]_{+}.

(55)

The plug-in estimator of the optimized BOLT bound is then given by

\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}:=1-\pi_{1}-\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\frac{1}{M_{\lambda}}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{\lambda}}t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}\bigl(\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}(x_{\lambda,i})\bigr),

(56)

where
x_{\lambda,i}\sim P_{\lambda}
. Its bias relative to the
ber
includes the max-sum gap.

Theorem 5

(Bias and variance of the optimized-bound plug-in estimator)
.

Let
\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}
be the max-sum gap in Theorem
3
. Assume that the target priors
\pi_{\lambda}
are known and that all test samples are independent of
\mathcal{T}_{U}
and of one another, and that, almost surely in
\mathcal{T}_{U}
,

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\lambda}}\!\left[\|\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}(x)-U_{\cdot/\lambda}(x)\|_{\infty}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{T}_{U}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{0},

(57)

for
\lambda\in\left\{2,\ldots,m\right\}
, and let

M^{\star}\coloneqq\min\left\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{m}\right\}
.
Then, the conditional absolute bias relative to the
ber
is bounded as

\left|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\right|\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}+(m-1)\varepsilon_{0},

(58)

and its conditional variance is

\Var\!\left(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{M^{\star}}\right).

(59)

Proof.

See Appendix
H
.
∎

Theorem
5
indicates that the plug-in estimator is asymptotically exact for the
ber
when the BOLT population risk is tight, the likelihood-ratio approximation error vanishes, and
M^{\star}
grows. Binary classification is one such setting because the max-sum gap is zero. The sum-based hinge is used here to characterize the optimized BOLT bound.

V
Numerical Experiments

Next, we illustrate the binary tightness result and evaluate the BOLT framework using both synthetic and real-world data. To this end, we consider the following experimental settings:

1.

We first study a binary Gaussian classification problem where the BOLT population risk is tight and the Bayes-optimal classifier is derived analytically.

2.

We evaluate the BOLT framework on a synthetic multiclass classification dataset with samples whose Bayes-optimal decision regions are nonlinear and radial, and examine whether the framework can closely approximate the
ber
.

3.

We next train multiple classifiers on realistic visual and textual datasets and compare the BOLT framework with standard cross-entropy minimization.

4.

Finally, we examine the learned embeddings and the behavior of BOLT-trained models under magnitude pruning.

V-A

Univariate Gaussian Data

We consider a binary classification task in which the classes
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are Gaussian distributed with different means and identical variances. Specifically, the class-conditional probability density functions for
i\in\{1,2\}
are

\displaystyle p_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\mu_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

(60)

We assume a uniform class prior, i.e.,
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}=0.5
. For this problem, the
ber
is given by
[
3
]

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=Q\left(\frac{|\Delta\mu|}{2\sigma}\right),

(61)

where
\Delta\mu=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}
and
Q(x)
denotes the Q-function,

\displaystyle Q(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{x}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}u.

(62)

The Bayes-optimal classifier is the MAP classifier

\displaystyle\widehat{C}(x)=\begin{cases}C_{1},&\Delta\mu\,(x-\bar{\mu})\geq 0,\\
C_{2},&\Delta\mu\,(x-\bar{\mu})<0,\end{cases}

(63)

where
\bar{\mu}\coloneqq(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})/2
. This sign-aware form covers both positive and negative values of
\Delta\mu
used in the experiment.

From Corollary
2
, we know that the universal bound in this case is tight. In the sequel, we verify this result analytically and evaluate BOLT training through a simple neural network.

Validation of Tightness

From Theorem
1
, the
ber
satisfies

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq\pi_{2}+\pi_{2}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{2}}[h(X)]-\pi_{1}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{1}}[h(X)],

(64)

where
h:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,0]

\displaystyle h^{\star}(x)=\dot{f}_{\mathrm{shng}}\left(\frac{p_{1}(x)}{p_{2}(x)}\right),

(65)

where
\dot{f}_{\mathrm{shng}}(u)=-1
for
u<1
and
0
otherwise. Here,
t=\pi_{2}/\pi_{1}=1

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1}(x)}{p_{2}(x)}=\exp\left\{\frac{\Delta\mu}{\sigma^{2}}(x-\bar{\mu})\right\}.

(66)

Comparing this likelihood ratio with
t=1
gives the threshold
\bar{\mu}
. Thus,

\displaystyle h^{\star}(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&\Delta\mu\,(x-\bar{\mu})<0,\\
0,&\Delta\mu\,(x-\bar{\mu})\geq 0.\end{cases}

(67)

Remark 2
.

In addition to demonstrating the tightness of the bound, this result confirms that the optimal classifier can be constructed directly from the function
h^{\star}(x)
that optimizes the bound.

Pruning and Model Separation

Pruning

For this experiment, we consider training a CNN on the Fashion-MNIST dataset. The model has three convolutional blocks with 32, 64, and 128 filters. Each block performs convolution, batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max-pooling. The classifier head has two fully connected layers of sizes 128 and 64, followed by a 10-class output layer. We train the same architecture with BOLT and CE.

We then apply global unstructured magnitude pruning to the convolutional and fully connected weights
[
16
]
. Bias terms and batch-normalization parameters are not pruned. At epochs 10, 20, and 30, we remove 20% of the weights that remain unpruned. After the three stages, the effective sparsity is
1-(0.8)^{3}=48.8\%
. Training continues with the same loss after every pruning step so that the remaining weights can be fine-tuned.

Table
III
reports the first post-pruning checkpoint and the final checkpoint after the complete pruning-and-fine-tuning protocol. CE is initially more accurate after the first pruning step. Nevertheless, as training proceeds, the BOLT-trained model recovers and reaches 92.74% accuracy, compared with 92.47% for CE at the same 48.8% sparsity. This result indicates that BOLT remains compatible with iterative magnitude pruning and adapts effectively during post-pruning fine-tuning.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we established a new training framework based on a universal bound on the
ber
. The proposed BOLT framework trains a learning model by minimizing the empirical BOLT risk, offering an alternative training approach to risk minimization with conventional loss functions. Our numerical experiments demonstrated that, for the reported datasets and matched architectures, BOLT often attained lower classification error than the corresponding cross-entropy baselines. Extending the BOLT framework to a wider set of learning tasks, such as regression, is an interesting direction for future work. Work in this direction is currently ongoing.

k\in[m]
, define

a_{k}\coloneqq\pi_{k}p_{k}(x),\qquad S_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j},\qquad r_{k}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq j\leq k}a_{j}.

(133)

Since
a_{j}\geq 0
for every
j
, we always have

0\leq r_{k}\leq S_{k}.

(134)

The relation
r_{k}\leq S_{k}
will be used repeatedly below; it also orders the two thresholds that define the three induction cases.

For
k\geq 2
, define the pointwise gap increment

g_{k}\coloneqq\left[a_{k}-r_{k-1}\right]_{+}-\left[a_{k}-S_{k-1}\right]_{+},

(135)

and the accumulated pointwise gap

G_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{q=2}^{k}g_{q}.

(136)

Restoring the dependence on
x
, we have
r_{k-1}(x)=r_{k-1}(x)
. Hence, by (
130
),
G_{m}(x)
is exactly the pointwise integrand of the max–sum gap:

\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}G_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(137)

Thus,
G_{m}(x)
is the pointwise quantity to be bounded before integration. Moreover, because
r_{k-1}\leq S_{k-1}
and the positive-part map is nondecreasing, each
g_{k}
is nonnegative.

For later use, (
134
) gives the following explicit update when class
k+1
is added:

g_{k+1}=\begin{cases}0,&a_{k+1}\leq r_{k},\\[2.0pt]
a_{k+1}-r_{k},&r_{k}<a_{k+1}\leq S_{k},\\[2.0pt]
S_{k}-r_{k},&a_{k+1}>S_{k}.\end{cases}

(138)

In every case,

S_{k+1}=S_{k}+a_{k+1},\qquad r_{k+1}=\max\{r_{k},a_{k+1}\},\qquad G_{k+1}=G_{k}+g_{k+1}.

(139)

First pointwise bound.

We first prove, by a coupled induction, that for every
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle S_{k}-r_{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-2}\bigl(S_{k}-r_{k}-G_{k}\bigr),

(140a)

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-1}\bigl(r_{k}-G_{k}\bigr).

(140b)

The first inequality will give the desired pointwise bound; the second is an auxiliary inequality needed to propagate the first one through the induction.

For the base case
k=2
,
S_{1}=r_{1}=a_{1}
, and therefore

g_{2}=\left[a_{2}-a_{1}\right]_{+}-\left[a_{2}-a_{1}\right]_{+}=0,\qquad G_{2}=0.

(141)

Consequently, (
140a
) holds with equality. Also,
S_{2}=a_{1}+a_{2}\leq 2\max\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=2r_{2}
, so (
140b
) holds as well.

Now assume that both (
140a
) and (
140b
) hold at some
k\geq 2
. Since their left-hand sides are nonnegative, the induction hypotheses also imply

S_{k}-r_{k}-G_{k}\geq 0,\qquad r_{k}-G_{k}\geq 0.

(142)

We verify the two inequalities at
k+1
in the three cases of (
138
).

Case 1:
a_{k+1}\leq r_{k}
.

Here,

g_{k+1}=0,\qquad r_{k+1}=r_{k},\qquad G_{k+1}=G_{k}.

(143)

Using (
140a
),
a_{k+1}\geq 0
, and (
142
), we obtain

\displaystyle S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(S_{k}-r_{k})+a_{k+1}

(144)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-2}(S_{k}-r_{k}-G_{k})+a_{k+1}

(145)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-1}\bigl(S_{k}-r_{k}-G_{k}+a_{k+1}\bigr)

(146)

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}\bigl(S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}\bigr).

(147)

This proves (
140a
) at
k+1
. Furthermore,
a_{k+1}\leq r_{k}\leq S_{k}
, where the last inequality is (
134
). Therefore, by (
140b
),

\displaystyle S_{k+1}

\displaystyle=S_{k}+a_{k+1}

(148)

\displaystyle\leq 2S_{k}

(149)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}(r_{k}-G_{k})

(150)

\displaystyle=2^{k}(r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}),

(151)

which proves (
140b
) at
k+1
.

Case 2:
r_{k}<a_{k+1}\leq S_{k}
.

In this case,

g_{k+1}=a_{k+1}-r_{k},\qquad r_{k+1}=a_{k+1},\qquad G_{k+1}=G_{k}+a_{k+1}-r_{k}.

(152)

The first inequality follows from the auxiliary induction hypothesis (
140b
):

\displaystyle S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}

\displaystyle=S_{k}

(153)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-1}(r_{k}-G_{k})

(154)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-1}\bigl(r_{k}-G_{k}+S_{k}-a_{k+1}\bigr)

(155)

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}\bigl(S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}\bigr),

(156)

where the second inequality uses
S_{k}-a_{k+1}\geq 0
. For the auxiliary inequality,
a_{k+1}\leq S_{k}
and

r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}=r_{k}-G_{k}
, so

\displaystyle S_{k+1}

\displaystyle=S_{k}+a_{k+1}

(157)

\displaystyle\leq 2S_{k}

(158)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}(r_{k}-G_{k})

(159)

\displaystyle=2^{k}(r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}).

(160)

Case 3:
a_{k+1}>S_{k}
.

Now,

g_{k+1}=S_{k}-r_{k},\qquad r_{k+1}=a_{k+1},\qquad G_{k+1}=G_{k}+S_{k}-r_{k}.

(161)

Since

S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}=S_{k},\qquad S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}=r_{k}-G_{k},

(162)

(
140b
) directly gives

S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}\leq 2^{k-1}\bigl(S_{k+1}-r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}\bigr).

(163)

To prove the auxiliary inequality, consider the difference between its right- and left-hand sides. Using the updates above,

\displaystyle 2^{k}(r_{k+1}-G_{k+1})-S_{k+1}

(164)

\displaystyle\quad=2^{k}(a_{k+1}-G_{k}-S_{k}+r_{k})-(S_{k}+a_{k+1})

(165)

\displaystyle\quad=\bigl[2^{k}(r_{k}-G_{k})-2S_{k}\bigr]+(2^{k}-1)(a_{k+1}-S_{k}).

(166)

The first bracket is nonnegative by (
140b
), and the second term is positive because
a_{k+1}>S_{k}
. Hence,

S_{k+1}\leq 2^{k}(r_{k+1}-G_{k+1}).

(167)

This completes the coupled induction.

Rearranging (
140a
) gives, for every
k\geq 2
,

G_{k}\leq\bigl(1-2^{-(k-2)}\bigr)(S_{k}-r_{k}).

(168)

At
k=m
,
S_{m}(x)=p_{X}(x)
and

r_{m}(x)=\max_{j\in[m]}\pi_{j}p_{j}(x)
. Therefore, using (
137
),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}G_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(169)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(1-2^{-(m-2)}\bigr)\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(p_{X}(x)-\max_{j\in[m]}\pi_{j}p_{j}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x

(170)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1-2^{-(m-2)}\bigr)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}.

(171)

Second pointwise bound.

We next prove independently, again by induction, that

G_{k}\leq\frac{kr_{k}-S_{k}}{2},\qquad k\geq 2.

(172)

For
k=2
,
G_{2}=0
and
2r_{2}-S_{2}\geq 0
, so the claim holds. Assume it holds for some
k\geq 2
. We again use the three cases in (
138
).

Case 1:
a_{k+1}\leq r_{k}
.

Since
g_{k+1}=0
and
r_{k+1}=r_{k}
,

\displaystyle G_{k+1}

\displaystyle=G_{k}

(173)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{kr_{k}-S_{k}}{2}

(174)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(k+1)r_{k}-S_{k}-a_{k+1}}{2}

(175)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+1)r_{k+1}-S_{k+1}}{2},

(176)

where the second inequality follows from
a_{k+1}\leq r_{k}
.

Case 2:
r_{k}<a_{k+1}\leq S_{k}
.

Here,
g_{k+1}=a_{k+1}-r_{k}
and
r_{k+1}=a_{k+1}
. Thus,

\displaystyle G_{k+1}

\displaystyle=G_{k}+a_{k+1}-r_{k}

(177)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{kr_{k}-S_{k}}{2}+a_{k+1}-r_{k}

(178)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-2)r_{k}+2a_{k+1}-S_{k}}{2}

(179)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{ka_{k+1}-S_{k}}{2}

(180)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+1)r_{k+1}-S_{k+1}}{2}.

(181)

The last inequality holds because

(k-2)(a_{k+1}-r_{k})\geq 0
.

Case 3:
a_{k+1}>S_{k}
.

Here,
g_{k+1}=S_{k}-r_{k}
and
r_{k+1}=a_{k+1}
, yielding

\displaystyle G_{k+1}

\displaystyle=G_{k}+S_{k}-r_{k}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{kr_{k}-S_{k}}{2}+S_{k}-r_{k}

(183)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-2)r_{k}+S_{k}}{2}

(184)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{ka_{k+1}-S_{k}}{2}

(185)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+1)r_{k+1}-S_{k+1}}{2}.

(186)

For the last inequality, (
134
) and
a_{k+1}>S_{k}
give

(k-2)r_{k}+S_{k}\leq(k-1)S_{k}<ka_{k+1}-S_{k}.

(187)

This completes the second induction.

Taking
k=m
in (
172
) and using (
137
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(m\max_{j\in[m]}\pi_{j}p_{j}(x)-p_{X}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x

(188)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}})-1}{2},

(189)

where we used

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{j}\pi_{j}p_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x=1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}

and
\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x=1
.

The two inductions give two valid upper bounds on the same quantity, so we take their minimum:

0\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}\leq\min\left\{\bigl(1-2^{-(m-2)}\bigr)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}},\frac{m(1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}})-1}{2}\right\}=\Delta^{\star}.

(190)

The lower bound follows from
g_{k}\geq 0
, and

\Delta^{\star}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
follows from

0\leq 1-2^{-(m-2)}\leq 1
.
Equating the two branches gives

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{\star}=\frac{m-1}{m+2\bigl(1-2^{-(m-2)}\bigr)},

(191)

which is (
49
). The first branch is nondecreasing in
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
and the second is decreasing in
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
; hence, the first is the minimum for
0\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{\star}
, and the second is the minimum for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{\star}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\leq(m-1)/m
. This proves all claims in Theorem
4
.
∎

Appendix H
Proof of Theorem
5

Condition throughout on the auxiliary set of samples
\mathcal{T}_{U}
. The ratio estimates are then fixed, and the evaluation samples remain independent with their specified class distributions. For each
\lambda\geq 2
, define the population aggregate hinge

t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(u)\coloneqq\left[\pi_{\lambda}-\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{j}u_{j/\lambda}\right]_{+}

(192)

and the empirical average

A_{\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M_{\lambda}}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{\lambda}}t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}\bigl(\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}(x_{\lambda,i})\bigr).

(193)

Then,

\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}=1-\pi_{1}-\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}A_{\lambda}.

(194)

We can show that the mapping
t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}
is Lipschitz with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Since
z\mapsto\left[z\right]_{+}
is 1-Lipschitz, for any vectors
u
and
v
,

\displaystyle\left|t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(u)-t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(v)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{j}(u_{j/\lambda}-v_{j/\lambda})\right|

(195)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{j}|u_{j/\lambda}-v_{j/\lambda}|

(196)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda-1}\pi_{j}\right)\|u-v\|_{\infty}

(197)

\displaystyle\leq\|u-v\|_{\infty}.

(198)

Let

\varepsilon_{\Sigma}\coloneqq 1-\pi_{1}-\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}\left[t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}\bigl(U_{\cdot/\lambda}(X)\bigr)\right]

(199)

denote the BOLT population risk at its pointwise minimizer. By Theorem
3
,

\varepsilon_{\Sigma}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}.

(200)

Applying the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\varepsilon_{\Sigma}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]-\varepsilon_{\Sigma}\right|

(201)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}+\left|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]-\varepsilon_{\Sigma}\right|.

(202)

For each
\lambda\geq 2
, take conditional expectations and add and subtract the population hinge evaluated at
\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}\left[t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(U_{\cdot/\lambda}(X))\right]-\mathbb{E}[A_{\lambda}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}\left[t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(U_{\cdot/\lambda}(X))-t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}(X))\,\middle|\,\mathcal{T}_{U}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}\left[t_{\Sigma}^{(\lambda)}(\widehat{U}_{\cdot/\lambda}(X))\,\middle|\,\mathcal{T}_{U}\right]-\mathbb{E}[A_{\lambda}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]\right).

(203)

The first term is at most
\varepsilon_{0}
in absolute value by (
198
) and the assumption of Theorem
5
. The second term is zero, since with known priors and independent test samples,
A_{\lambda}
is conditionally unbiased for the hinge expectation at the fitted ratio estimates. Therefore, the test-sample size affects variance but adds no term to the conditional bias. Summing over
\lambda
gives

\left|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]-\varepsilon_{\Sigma}\right|\leq(m-1)\varepsilon_{0}.

(204)

Substituting this into (
202
), we obtain

\left|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}]-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\right|\leq\Delta_{\mathrm{ms}}+(m-1)\varepsilon_{0}.

(205)

For the variance, each
A_{\lambda}
in (
193
) is an average of conditionally independent terms in
[0,\pi_{\lambda}]
, since the ratio estimates are nonnegative. Hence,

\Var(A_{\lambda}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U})\leq\frac{\pi_{\lambda}^{2}}{4M_{\lambda}}=\mathcal{O}(M_{\lambda}^{-1}).

(206)

For arbitrary random variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{q}
, the conditional Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\Var\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}Z_{j}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{T}_{U}\right)\leq q\sum_{j=1}^{q}\Var(Z_{j}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}).

(207)

Applying this bound to the
m-1
terms
A_{2},\ldots,A_{m}
, and noting that
\pi_{1}
is fixed, we have

\displaystyle\Var\!\left(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{BOLT}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{U}\right)

\displaystyle=\Var\left(\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}A_{\lambda}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{T}_{U}\right)

(208)

\displaystyle\leq(m-1)\sum_{\lambda=2}^{m}\mathcal{O}(M_{\lambda}^{-1})

(209)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{M^{\star}}\right),

(210)

where
M^{\star}=\min\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{m}\}
. This proves the theorem.
∎
