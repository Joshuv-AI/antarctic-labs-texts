Fungi and colorectal cancer: from dysregulation to clinical translation
Authors: Wu Yinhang, Han Shuwen, Zhuang Jing, Feng Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research on the gut microbiota has primarily focused on bacterial communities. However, the role of fungi-the second-largest eukaryotic component of the gut microbiome-in the development and progression of colorectal cancer (CRC) has been less extensively studied compared to bacteria. Recent studies have revealed significant fungal dysbiosis in the intestines of CRC patients, where the enrichment of specific pathogenic fungi (e.g., Aspergillus spp.) is closely associated with tumor progression, while the reduction of certain commensal fungi may weaken their protective effects. This fungal imbalance not only directly promotes tumorigenesis through carcinogenic mechanisms but also indirectly accelerates CRC progression by reshaping the bacteria-fungi interaction network and the host immune microenvironment. This article reviews the structural alterations of fungal communities in CRC, the changing patterns of specific fungal species, the relationship between fungal dysbiosis and clinicopathological features, the potential molecular mechanisms of fungi in CRC pathogenesis, the significance of cross-kingdom microbial interactions, as well as the potential diagnostic and therapeutic applications of fungi in CRC and the challenges and prospects of novel fungal-modulating therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common malignancy worldwide. According to 2022 global cancer statistics, there are over 1.9 million new cases and approximately 900,000 deaths annually, with numbers continuing to rise [1]. The development of CRC is a multifactorial, multistep process, involving genetic mutations [2], epigenetic modifications [3], chronic inflammation [4], and microenvironment remodeling [5]. In recent years, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota (including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea) has been closely linked to CRC initiation, progression, and treatment response [5, 6].

Traditionally, gut microbiota research has primarily focused on bacterial communities, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum [7], pks+ Escherichia coli [8] and enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) [9]. These pro-oncogenic bacteria promote CRC development by activating carcinogenic signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin), secreting genotoxins (e.g., colibactin), and inducing an immunosuppressive microenvironment. However, fungi, as the second-largest eukaryotic component of the gut microbiome, though accounting for only 0.1% of microbial abundance, play crucial roles in host metabolism regulation, immune homeostasis, and mucosal barrier integrity [10, 11].

The gut mycobiota is predominantly composed of Candida, Aspergillus, Saccharomyces, and Cryptococcus [12], with colonization dynamics influenced by host genetics, diet, and antibiotic use [13, 14]. Recent studies have identified four stable fungal enterotypes across diverse populations: Sacc_type (dominated by Saccharomyces), Can_type (dominated by Candida), Asp_type (dominated by Aspergillus), Asc_type (characterized by Ascomycota in ITS1 and Saccharomycetales in ITS2). These fungal enterotypes are associated with various diseases, and cross-kingdom interactions between fungi and bacteria, as well as functional differences in fungal communities, have been observed [15].

Recent metagenomic studies have revealed significant alterations in the gut fungal community structure of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, characterized by an enrichment of pathogenic fungi (e.g., Malassezia globosa and Aspergillus rambellii) and depletion of protective commensal fungi (e.g., Saccharomycetes and Pneumocystidomycetes classes) [16, 17]. Notably, the intratumoral fungal community demonstrates progressively increasing heterogeneity during the adenoma-carcinoma sequence, with groundbreaking research revealing significant associations between fungal community heterogeneity and key oncogenic molecular features including KRAS mutations and microsatellite instability - well-established risk factors for colorectal carcinogenesis [18]. Fungal dysbiosis contributes to CRC pathogenesis through multiple mechanisms: (1) direct genotoxicity, as exemplified by Candida albicans-secreted candidalysin which induces DNA damage and chromosomal instability in intestinal epithelial cells [19]; (2) immunomodulatory disruption mediated through fungal cell wall components (e.g., β-glucan) that activate Dectin-1/CARD9 signaling via pattern recognition receptors, triggering pro-inflammatory cytokine release (IL-1β, IL-6) that drives chronic inflammation-to-cancer transition [20, 21] ] and (3) cross-kingdom synergy through fungal-bacterial co-aggregation forming polymicrobial biofilms that enhance carcinogenic bacterial adhesion and invasion [22, 23].

These findings have spurred research into fungal-based diagnostic biomarkers, therapeutic targeting of fungus-host interactions, and mycobiome-modulating intervention strategies. This review synthesizes current understanding of gut fungi’s roles in CRC pathogenesis, diagnostic/therapeutic applications, and their interactions with bacterial communities and host immune-metabolic networks, aiming to provide a comprehensive framework for developing mycobiome-based precision medicine approaches in CRC management.

Association between gut mycobiota dysbiosis and colorectal cancer

Specific alterations of gut mycobiota during CRC development

The progression of colorectal cancer (CRC) is accompanied by significant structural changes in gut fungal communities, manifested not only in overall abundance and diversity but more importantly in the dynamic fluctuations of specific fungal taxa (Fig. 1). Multiple metagenomic studies have demonstrated that CRC patients typically exhibit lower fungal α-diversity compared to healthy controls [17, 24], indicating fundamental reorganization of fungal community structure associated with CRC pathogenesis.

Current evidence identifies at least 20 fungal species showing significant alterations during CRC development. Investigations of fungal community variations in precancerous lesions revealed that Ascomycota, Glomeromycota, and Basidiomycota dominate the fungal microbiota in both adenomas and adjacent mucosal tissues. Notably, Phoma and Candida (constituting 45% of the fungal microbiota) emerge as potential core opportunistic pathogens during adenoma progression [25]. Characteristic mycobiota dysbiosis in colorectal polyps and CRC includes markedly reduced fungal α-diversity, increased Ascomycota/Basidiomycota ratio, and enriched opportunistic pathogens such as Trichosporon and Malassezia [26].

Moreover, in the context of colitis-associated cancer, a distinct pattern of fungal microbiota dysbiosis has been identified in the fecal samples of IBD patients. This dysbiosis manifests in two primary aspects: first, alterations in biodiversity, where both ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) patients exhibit significant reductions in fungal α-diversity, mirroring the observed decrease in bacterial diversity; second, characteristic compositional changes, including an increased Basidiomycota/Ascomycota ratio, decreased abundance of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and elevated proportions of Candida albicans in the IBD gut. Crucially, this study also revealed the disruption of normal symbiotic coexistence between fungi and bacteria during intestinal inflammation, demonstrating a disease-specific cross-kingdom imbalance in ecological networks (where the inflammatory gut environment favors fungal proliferation at the expense of bacterial growth) [27].

The niche competition theory provides a conceptual framework for understanding these structural alterations [28]. Under healthy conditions, intestinal bacteria and fungi maintain a delicate equilibrium through nutrient competition and spatial occupation. However, when this balance is disrupted, certain potentially pathogenic fungi may gain competitive advantages and undergo excessive proliferation.

A meta-analysis integrating 7 studies comprising 1,329 fecal metagenomic datasets systematically identified CRC-associated fungal biomarkers, revealing six fungal species - Aspergillus rambellii, Cordyceps sp. RAO-2017, Erysiphe pulchra, Moniliophthora perniciosa, Sphaerulina musiva and Phytophthora capsici - that were significantly enriched in CRC patients, while fungi such as Aspergillus kawachii showed decreased abundance [17]. During disease progression from healthy status to adenoma and then to CRC, the gut mycobiota demonstrated distinct stage-specific evolutionary patterns forming a unique “CRC-associated fungal signature profile”: gradual depletion of commensal fungi (e.g., certain Saccharomyces species) accompanied by marked enrichment of pathogenic fungi (e.g., specific Aspergillus and Candida species) [17, 29]. Furthermore, two fungal species (Cordyceps sp. RAO-2017 and Phytopythium vexans) showed consistent and significant enrichment, while one species (A. kawachii) decreased progressively from precancerous lesions to early and advanced CRC [17], suggesting their potential causal roles in CRC progression.

Collectively, several commensal fungal species commonly found in healthy individuals were significantly reduced in CRC patients [27]. For instance, certain strains of Saccharomyces are considered to possess potential anti-inflammatory and epithelial protective properties [30], and their depletion may compromise the gut’s natural defense mechanisms against tumorigenesis. This dual phenomenon of “beneficial fungi” depletion coupled with “harmful fungi” overgrowth constitutes the CRC-associated fungal dysbiosis that may synergistically promote tumor development.

The structural alterations in fungal communities may exhibit heterogeneity across different CRC subtypes. Variations in mycobiota reorganization patterns have been observed according to tumor molecular characteristics (e.g., microsatellite instability status, specific KRAS mutations) [18], anatomical location (left-sided vs. right-sided colon, proximal vs. distal) and tumor stage [17, 26]. This heterogeneity underscores the importance of considering tumor molecular and clinical features when investigating fungus-CRC associations to more precisely elucidate the relationships between specific fungal taxa and different CRC subtypes.

Clinical research of fungal dysbiosis in CRC progression

The dysbiosis of gut fungal communities demonstrates significant correlations with clinicopathological characteristics of colorectal cancer (CRC), manifesting not only in tumorigenesis risk but also in disease progression, treatment response, and prognosis.

Fungal dysbiosis contributes to increased metastatic risk in CRC. Studies reveal that elevated intestinal colonization of the pathogenic strain Candida albicans promotes tumor cell invasion through secretion of aspartic proteases (SAPs) that degrade extracellular matrix, thereby inducing CRC liver metastasis [31]. Higher tumor levels of Candida species correlate strongly with pro-inflammatory tumor gene activity and enhanced cancer cell adhesion - features predictive of advanced cancer stages, increased distant organ metastasis risk, and poor patient outcomes [29].

Furthermore, fungus-induced immunosuppression represents a key mechanistic link between fungal dysbiosis and CRC clinical progression. Increased fungal burden accompanies significant expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which suppress anti-tumor immune responses and create a microenvironment favorable for tumor growth and metastasis [23].

Consequently, elevated pathogenic fungal load often indicates more advanced CRC clinical staging [23, 32]. Multiple studies report progressive increases in specific pathogenic fungi (e.g., Candida albicans, Aspergillus rambellii) as tumors develop from early to advanced stages [29]. In late-stage CRC patients, these fungi not only colonize the gut but also invade tumor tissues and even metastatic lesions, forming “intratumoral fungal communities” [33]. This invasion may relate to local immunosuppressive microenvironments while potentially accelerating tumor progression through secretion of pro-inflammatory factors or carcinogenic metabolites. Patients with detectable fungal DNA or toxins in tumor tissues typically exhibit more aggressive biological behavior and poorer prognosis.

Research demonstrates significant alterations in intratumoral fungal communities during adenoma-to-carcinoma transition, with increased heterogeneity closely associated with host gene mutations (KRAS/MSI) [18]. Malassezia (particularly M. restricta) may drive CRC progression through pro-inflammatory mechanisms, its abundance showing positive correlation with disease stage. Fungal biomarkers (e.g., Malassezia, Aspergillus) serve as potential predictors for KRAS mutation and microsatellite instability (MSI) status, offering new directions for precision medicine in CRC. In CRC immunotherapy response, gut fungi demonstrate superior predictive accuracy over bacteria for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response phenotypes [34]. Modulating fungal communities to alleviate tumor-associated immunosuppression may emerge as a novel strategy to improve CRC treatment efficacy and prognosis.

Multifaceted molecular mechanisms of fungi in colorectal carcinogenesis

The gut mycobiota participates in CRC initiation and progression through diverse molecular mechanisms, encompassing both direct carcinogenic effects of fungal metabolites and complex immunomodulatory processes involving cross-kingdom microbial interactions. A comprehensive understanding of these molecular pathways is crucial not only for elucidating CRC pathogenesis but also for developing novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This section systematically examines the genotoxic effects of fungal toxins, fungal-mediated remodeling of the immune microenvironment, and the molecular basis of fungus-bacteria synergistic carcinogenesis, revealing how fungal communities promote malignant progression of CRC through multiple avenues (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Fungal pathogens and their potential mechanisms in colorectal cancer progression

Direct oncogenic effects of fungal metabolites

Fungal-derived toxins represent key molecular mediators of fungal carcinogenic effects, with these secondary metabolites promoting tumorigenesis through direct DNA damage or interference with cellular signaling pathways.

Candidalysin, a small peptide toxin secreted by Candida albicans (C. albicans), exerts pathogenic effects by disrupting epithelial cell membrane integrity. This toxin directly interacts with its target CCNH, inducing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) while simultaneously binding CCNH to activate CDK kinases that suppress DNA damage repair pathways, ultimately leading to intestinal mucosal damage [35]. Additionally, candidalysin mediates a cascade of stress responses including reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, intracellular ATP depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cytochrome C release [36]. The toxin also activates the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway, causing epithelial damage and triggering innate epithelial immune responses [37–39]. During fungal infections, candidalysin stimulates innate immunity through pro-inflammatory cytokine release, neutrophil recruitment [40, 41], and Th17 cell activation via IL-17 receptor signaling transduction [42, 43].

Multiple Candida species (e.g., C. albicans, C. tropicalis) can oxidize ethanol to acetaldehyde (ACH) and generate nitrosamine carcinogens, causing DNA damage and proto-oncogene activation [44]. The cytotoxic mechanisms of acetaldehyde manifest through two primary pathways: First, acetaldehyde induces oxidative stress by increasing intracellular ROS levels and Ca²⁺ concentration, activating Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) which triggers Drp1 phosphorylation. This signaling cascade leads to abnormal mitochondrial fission and dysfunction, ultimately causing cellular toxicity and DNA damage [45]. Second, excessive ROS further exacerbates genomic instability, accelerating tumor progression and enhancing invasiveness [46]. Moreover, acetaldehyde disrupts intestinal epithelial tight junction structures, impairing normal gut mucosal barrier function [47].

Immunoregulation and chronic inflammation

Fungal-host immune interactions play a pivotal role in CRC development, with these interactions - similar to bacterial ones - being primarily mediated through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [48]. C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) serve as PRRs in dendritic cells, macrophages, neutrophils and other immune cells to recognize pathogen-derived ligands [49]. For instance, Dectin-1 and Dectin-2 can identify fungal components like β-glucans and high-mannose carbohydrates [50, 51].

Commensal gut fungi signal through the Syk-CARD9 pathway to activate NF-κB and other pathways, effectively inducing inflammasomes to suppress colitis and colorectal cancer [23, 52]. Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes that form in response to fungal PRR recognition [53, 54]. The well-characterized Dectin-1 receptor triggers the Syk-CARD9 signaling axis upon fungal component binding [21, 55–57], promoting inflammasome assembly, Th17 polarization, and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18 [52, 58]. IL-18 enhances intestinal epithelial repair and activates CD8 + T cells to produce interferon-γ [52, 58]. Dectin-2 indirectly couples with Syk through association with the FcRγ chain, similarly activating immune and inflammatory responses via the Syk-CARD9 axis [59, 60].

In AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated cancer models, CARD9-deficient mice exhibit significantly attenuated inflammatory responses and tumor burden, with fungal load inversely correlating with colonic CARD9 expression levels [61]. Moreover, genetic deletion of the fungal-specific PRR Dectin-3 predisposes to CRC development with markedly increased pathogenic fungal colonization [31, 62]. These findings suggest that dysbiosis in pathogenic/commensal fungal ratios may impair Syk-CARD9-mediated inflammatory activation, thereby increasing cancer risk. However, some studies indicate that intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) can recognize C. albicans via Dectin-1 to activate Wnt signaling and promote CRC progression [50].

Additionally, recent studies identified a novel fungal-induced c-Cbl/RelB axis critical for regulating fungal-triggered intestinal inflammation and maintaining gut homeostasis. Fungal-derived α-mannans activate the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl through Dectin-2 and Dectin-3 in dendritic cells (DCs), promoting c-Cbl-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of RelB, a non-canonical NF-κB family member. Specific c-Cbl deficiency in DCs enhances α-mannan-induced RelB activation, which suppresses p65-mediated IL-10 transcription, ultimately increasing susceptibility to DSS-induced colitis. Notably, antifungal treatment with fluconazole can ameliorate colonic inflammation [63].

Fungal-induced immunosuppression represents a critical mechanism promoting colorectal carcinogenesis [23]. Pathogenic fungi enriched in the gut significantly enhance the recruitment and activation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which impair CD8⁺ T cell function [20, 61, 64], exacerbating inflammatory lesions and tumor burden.

Another hallmark of fungal involvement in CRC is the induction of inflammatory responses. Certain fungi (e.g., C. albicans) trigger robust Th17 polarization [65]. Th17 cells differentiate under stimulation by interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and IL-23, subsequently secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-17 A and IL-17 F [66]. IL-17 promotes tumor growth via STAT3-dependent pathways and is implicated in CRC development and progression. Cytokines of the IL-17 family exert their effects through IL-17 receptor A (IL-17RA), activating mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling to drive carcinogenesis [67]. Intestinal IL-17/IL-17RA deficiency may elevate circulating IL-17 levels through microbiota-driven mechanisms, enabling systemic dissemination to tumors. IL-17 stimulates tumor cells to produce oxidases, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) that accelerate tumor growth [68, 69].

However, contrasting evidence indicates that in advanced CRC, IL-17RA may foster antifungal antitumor immunity by modulating macrophage function. Intestinal epithelial cell (IEC)-specific IL-17RA deficiency increases fungal invasion in tumors. Treatment with the antifungal agent amphotericin B exacerbates tumor aggressiveness, suggesting that proper inflammasome activation and IL-18 release in macrophages following Dectin-1 recognition depend on IL-17RA signaling [70].

Interleukin-22 (IL-22) emerges as a pivotal mediator in fungal-regulated inflammation-to-cancer transition. Studies demonstrate that IL-22 directly induces oncostatin M receptor (OSMR) expression in IECs, synergistically activating STAT3 to propel colitis-associated cancer (CAC) progression [71]. C. albicans expansion upregulates IL-7 via the HIF-1α-glycolysis axis in macrophages, driving γδ T cells and group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) to secrete IL-22. Notably, tumor-associated IL-22 levels correlate strongly with fungal burden [31].

Conversely, beneficial commensal fungi like Candida tropicalis strain MYA-3404 colonize the gut and elevate IL-17 A/IL-22 production through Th17 and ILC3 activation. This enhances epithelial proliferation (evidenced by increased Ki-67⁺ cells), reduces intestinal permeability, and fortifies the mucosal barrier. Strain MYA-3404 further modulates vitamin B3 metabolism to elevate the nicotinic acid/nicotinamide ratio, amplifying IL-17 A expression and epithelial renewal, thereby ameliorating intestinal inflammation [72] (Fig. 2). Thus, fungi contribute to colonic inflammation and carcinogenesis through metabolic reprogramming and immune cell crosstalk. Fungal-driven immunoinflammatory responses exhibit a “double-edged sword” characteristic: while participating in antifungal host defense, they may also collaborate with STAT3/NF-κB pathways to promote tumor progression. The ultimate biological effects depend on the homeostasis of the tumor microenvironment and the balance state of the fungus-immune axis. Targeting pathogenic fungal subpopulations or inflammatory factors to restore immunosuppressive environments, or alternatively modulating beneficial commensal fungi to enhance immune responses, may emerge as novel strategies for future CRC immunotherapy.

The potential of fungal-bacterial cross-kingdom interactions in CRC regulation

Cross-kingdom microbial interactions within the gut ecosystem play a complex yet critical role in colorectal cancer development (Fig. 3). While traditional research on microbiota-CRC relationships has primarily focused on bacterial components, emerging evidence reveals that intricate fungal-bacterial networks are equally indispensable in shaping the tumor microenvironment. Metagenomic analyses demonstrate that compared to healthy individuals, CRC patients exhibit predominantly positive fungal-bacterial correlations in their intestines, with these cross-kingdom interaction networks actively contributing to CRC progression [17, 24, 73]. The intricate interplay between fungi and bacteria can influence host physiology through various mechanisms including physical/chemical interactions, environmental/host-mediated regulation, competition for nutrients/adhesion sites, and formation of mixed-species biofilms [74, 75].

For instance, certain microorganisms can suppress the colonization and invasion of other microbial species in the gut by competing for nutritional resources or producing specific metabolites, thereby mitigating colitis. Studies demonstrate that commensal anaerobic bacteria in mouse intestines, particularly Clostridium clusters IV/XIVa and Bacteroides, reduce gastrointestinal colonization and invasiveness of C. albicans by upregulating hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) expression, activating innate immune responses, and enhancing antimicrobial peptide LL-37 production [76]. Similarly, the gut commensal bacterium Lactobacillus rhamnosus counters C. albicans pathogenicity through multiple mechanisms: generating anti-pathogenic compounds, depleting C. albicans-preferred nutrient sources, and remodeling the metabolic microenvironment [77]. In the oral cavity, bacterial species including Streptococcus sobrinus, S. mitis, and Lactobacillus casei restrict C. albicans growth via glucose competition [78]. Furthermore, lysozyme derived from Acremonium alcalophilum serves as a pivotal regulator of intestinal homeostasis by promoting mucus secretion and modifying gut bacterial composition (e.g., reducing Akkermansia muciniphila and Oscillospira abundance), consequently alleviating colitis [79].

The formation of polymicrobial biofilms by fungi and bacteria represents a potential mechanism that synergistically enhances microbial invasiveness and promotes CRC development. Fungal-bacterial synergistic pathogenesis constitutes a crucial pattern within the CRC microbial interaction network. Pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Serratia marcescens can form mixed biofilms with Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis). These polymicrobial biofilms not only strengthen microbial resistance against host defenses and antibiotics [80, 81], but also create a tumorigenic microenvironment through continuous release of pro-inflammatory factors and carcinogenic metabolites [82]. Notably, biofilms formed by C. tropicalis with E. coli or Streptococcus mutans exhibit abundant fungal hyphae and even demonstrate fusion phenomena between bacterial and fungal cells [80, 81]. Experimental studies treating DSS-induced colitis mice with colistin (targeting Enterobacteriaceae) and supplementing with Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745 or Candida albicans revealed significantly reduced correlations between bacterial and fungal relative abundance. Both the anti-colitic effect of S. boulardii and the pro-colitic effect of C. albicans were attenuated. Remarkably, reintroduction of E. coli to restore Enterobacteriaceae populations reinstated the functional effects of both fungal species [83], demonstrating how Enterobacteriaceae can facilitate fungal activity in the gut.

Current understanding of CRC-associated fungal-bacterial networks can be summarized as involving competition, mutualism, and symbiosis between bacteria and fungi. While these cross-kingdom interactions primarily influence fungal/bacterial invasiveness or intestinal colonization, direct evidence establishing their causal roles in CRC initiation and progression remains limited. This knowledge gap presents important research opportunities for elucidating the mechanistic basis of fungal-bacterial interactions in CRC pathogenesis. Furthermore, these microbial interaction mechanisms offer promising foundations for developing precision intervention strategies, potentially opening new therapeutic avenues for CRC treatment.

Application of fungi in colorectal cancer treatment

With deepening understanding of gut mycobiota’s role in CRC pathogenesis, fecal fungal signatures (e.g., A. rambellii) have emerged as potential non-invasive diagnostic tools, while multi-kingdom microbiome models (fungi+bacteria) show enhanced sensitivity for early detection. Although conventional chemotherapy and targeted therapies have achieved certain success, limitations including drug resistance and side effects restrict their long-term efficacy. Given fungi’s multifaceted involvement in CRC, fungal-targeted interventions are becoming a promising research direction in comprehensive CRC treatment strategies (Fig. 4).

Conventional therapies vs. fungal-targeted approaches: a mechanistic contrast

To appreciate the novelty of fungal-based strategies, it is essential to contrast their mechanisms with those of conventional treatments. Traditional chemotherapy, such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) [84] and oxaliplatin [85], primarily exerts anti-cancer effects through direct cytotoxicity. 5-FU, an antimetabolite, disrupts DNA synthesis by inhibiting thymidylate synthase, while oxaliplatin forms DNA adducts, leading to replication arrest and apoptotic cell death. Targeted therapies, like anti-EGFR (Cetuximab) [86, 87] or anti-VEGF (Bevacizumab) [88] antibodies, block specific signaling pathways (PI3K-Akt pathway, Ras/RAF/MEK/MAPK pathway) crucial for tumor proliferation and angiogenesis. More recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs, e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) have revolutionized CRC treatment by reactivating T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [89]. Despite their efficacy, these approaches often face challenges of acquired resistance and significant off-target toxicity due to their mechanisms of action on rapidly dividing cells or systemic pathway inhibition [90].

In contrast, fungal-targeted approaches operate through fundamentally different principles aimed at remodeling the tumor ecosystem (Fig. 4). Current therapeutic approaches include: (1) Dietary interventions that modulate gut microbiota to enhance anti-cancer metabolites like SCFAs; (2) Probiotic and postbiotic applications that restore intestinal barrier function and exert immunomodulatory effects; and (3) Antifungal drug combinations that repurpose agents to enhance chemo-/immunotherapy efficacy. Emerging innovative strategies comprise: (1) Engineered yeast-based drug delivery systems for tumor-localized drug synthesis or immune checkpoint inhibitor secretion; (2) Fungal metabolite-targeted therapies using compounds; and (3) Microbial interaction modulation to selectively eliminate pro-carcinogenic fungi. This mechanistic shift from cytotoxicity to ecosystem remodeling, metabolic intervention, and precision delivery represents the distinction of fungal-based therapies.

Therapeutic potential of fungal-targeted approaches in CRC

Dietary modulation, probiotic supplementation, and antifungal agents represent potential synergistic approaches for CRC management. Dietary interventions can reshape gut fungal composition to reduce CRC risk. Increased consumption of Mediterranean diets [91], high-fiber foods [92], and polyphenol-rich nutrients [93, 94], combined with reduced intake of high-fat, high-sugar, and high-protein foods [95], promotes beneficial fungal growth while suppressing pathogenic fungi (Candida and Aspergillus species) [96]. These dietary patterns also stimulate production of anti-inflammatory compounds like short-chain fatty acids, improving gut microecological balance and reducing CRC risk [93, 97].

Probiotic administration represents the most direct approach to modulate gut mycobiota and may offer novel CRC prevention/therapeutic options. Saccharomyces boulardii (S. boulardii), the most extensively studied fungal probiotic, has demonstrated significant efficacy in clinical trials for treating and preventing various diarrheal diseases and inflammatory bowel conditions [98]. Its mechanisms include restoring and strengthening intestinal barrier function, repairing epithelial tight junctions [99], and alleviating colonic inflammation [100]. Furthermore, S. boulardii inhibits CRC progression by remodeling gut microbiota structure - reducing Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and decreasing abundance of CRC-associated bacteria like Escherichia-Shigella, Alistipes, and Clostridium XIVa [101]. Postbiotics also show promise in enhancing intestinal barrier function and immune regulation, with their immunomodulatory activity depending on their ability to balance anti-/pro-inflammatory cytokine production and Th1/Th2 responses. Non-viable cells and biofilms of Lactobacillus rhamnosus and L. casei exhibit antifungal activity against C. albicans [102], while Saccharomyces cerevisiae fermentation products can lower IL-6, NF-κB, and IL-1β levels while increasing tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin-1), thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective effects [103].

The antifungal drug terbinafine, a squalene epoxidase inhibitor, shows unique advantages in CRC treatment. It synergizes with oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil to inhibit CRC growth, suggesting its potential repurposing for CRC therapy [18]. Terbinafine reduces fungal load while significantly decreasing MDSC infiltration and restoring CD8⁺ T cell function. Its combination with PD-1 inhibitors enhances response rates in anti-PD-1 resistant models [34, 64]. Mechanistically, terbinafine lowers NADP+/NADPH ratio by inhibiting glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), leading to dNTP depletion and cancer cell cycle arrest [64]. Another antifungal agent, itraconazole, suppresses Wnt signaling pathway and eliminates dormant intestinal cancer cells, effectively inhibiting Wnt-driven cancer growth and metastasis [104].

Innovative fungal-based engineering strategies for CRC treatment

In recent years, engineered gut microbiota-based therapeutic approaches for CRC have emerged as a research hotspot, with their core concept focusing on precise intervention through modulating microbial dysbiosis and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment [105]. In the field of synthetic biology, traditional probiotics (such as Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 [106–108] and Bifidobacterium [109]) have been extensively developed as living drug delivery vectors due to their advantages including ease of genetic modification, scalability in cultivation, and high safety profile, making them relatively well-established research subjects.

In contrast, although the application of fungal engineering in CRC treatment started later, it demonstrates unique potential. Despite fewer reported studies on engineering human gastrointestinal fungal communities compared to bacteria, certain fungi (such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae) have been successfully engineered for targeted therapy by virtue of their natural gut colonization capacity, excellent biocompatibility, and efficient genetic manipulation systems. Fungal-based CRC treatment innovatively integrates three mechanisms: biological targeted delivery, microbiota-immune regulation, and metabolic intervention, with specific implementations as follows.

Application of engineered fungal vectors in targeted drug delivery

Fungi, particularly Saccharomyces cerevisiae, have emerged as ideal drug carriers for CRC treatment due to their natural intestinal tropism, biocompatibility, and genetic plasticity. In enzyme-prodrug therapy (EPT), researchers developed an orally administered engineered yeast system (SC@CS@5-FC) that specifically accumulates in tumor tissues. This system releases the prodrug 5-fluorocytosine (5-FC) in response to hyaluronidase (HAase) overexpression in the tumor microenvironment, followed by intracellular conversion to the highly toxic chemotherapeutic agent 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) via yeast-encoded cytosine deaminase (CD), enabling in situ drug generation. Concurrently, yeast cell wall components (e.g., β-glucans) and zinc-coordinated chitosan nanoparticles activate antigen-presenting cells like dendritic cells, enhancing anti-tumor immune responses [110].

Another study developed a novel oral delivery system (Sb_haPD-1) based on engineered Saccharomyces boulardii (Sb). Through genetic modification, researchers integrated a gene encoding miniaturized anti-PD-1 (haPD-1) into Sb, creating the Sb_haPD-1 recombinant strain. The modified yeast survives and secretes functional haPD-1 proteins that specifically bind PD-L1 on tumor cells, reversing immune suppression and activating T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. This approach enables localized intestinal delivery of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), overcoming systemic toxicity associated with intravenous administration. The Sb_haPD-1 system significantly reduced tumor burden in APCmin mice and induced unique local immune signatures, including marked reduction in Tregs and increased CD4+TNF-α+ T cell populations [111].

This fungal-based delivery platform demonstrates three key advantages: (1) precise tumor targeting through natural microbial tropism, (2) in situ production of therapeutic agents minimizing off-target effects, and (3) synergistic combination of direct cytotoxicity and immune activation. The successful engineering of yeast for both chemotherapy and immunotherapy applications highlights the versatility of fungal systems in CRC treatment, offering solutions to current limitations in drug delivery and immune modulation.

Targeting cancer cells via fungal metabolites

Fungal engineering extends beyond carrier functions, as their metabolites can directly modulate the intestinal microenvironment and tumor metabolic pathways. For instance, small-molecule inhibitors targeting metabolic enzymes-such as the terpene-nonadride heterodimers bipoterprides A-L (1–12) and their monomers (13–20), isolated from the endophytic fungus Bipolaris victoriae—specifically bind to the novel colorectal cancer target human dCTP pyrophosphatase 1 (DCTPP1). By inhibiting DCTPP1’s nucleotide metabolic activity, these compounds disrupt amino acid metabolic reprogramming, inducing CRC cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and autophagic death. Clinical samples confirm that high DCTPP1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in CRC patients. Notably, compound 2, the first natural inhibitor targeting DCTPP1, exhibits potent antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo without significant toxicity [112].

Other strategies

Synergistic Probiotic-Chemotherapy Systems: Orally administered xylan (a prebiotic) and capecitabine co-loaded nanoparticles (SCXN) enable drug sustained release under gut microbiota action while simultaneously increasing probiotic abundance (e.g., Lactobacillus) and reducing carcinogenic bacteria (e.g., Proteobacteria). This approach elevates short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) levels, which maintain intestinal barrier integrity and suppress the NF-κB–IL-6–STAT3 inflammatory axis, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment. SCXN treatment boosts intratumoral CD8 + T cell infiltration, reduces regulatory T cells, and enhances antitumor immunity, improving tumor suppression rates from 5.29% to 71.78%. Its excellent biocompatibility extends median survival in CRC mice to 33.5 days [113].

Bacterial-Fungal Interaction-Based Therapy: Synthetic biology-engineered probiotics (e.g., Escherichia coli Nissle 1917) designed to specifically recognize and adhere to pathogenic fungi not only enable antifungal therapy [114] but also open new avenues for targeted CRC treatment.

In summary, the core advantage of fungal-engineered therapeutics lies in their multimodal synergistic mechanisms. Fungi can respond to tumor microenvironment characteristics (e.g., specific enzymes or pH levels) to achieve site-specific drug activation, thereby reducing systemic toxicity. Through microbiota remodeling, metabolic targeting, and immune adjuvant effects, they effectively disrupt tumor immune evasion barriers. Additionally, these approaches overcome the limitations of traditional intravenous chemotherapy, significantly improving patient compliance. However, critical aspects such as the safety profiles, large-scale production feasibility, and population applicability of fungal-based delivery systems (e.g., SC@CS@5-FC) and naturally derived fungal small-molecule drugs require further investigation. Future research directions should focus on optimizing fungal targeting efficiency, developing genetically engineered fungi capable of expressing immune checkpoint antibodies, and exploring combination strategies with radiotherapy or targeted therapies to enhance treatment efficacy for advanced-stage CRC.

Conclusion

The gut mycobiota profoundly influences colorectal cancer pathogenesis through metabolic modulation, immune regulation, and cross-kingdom interactions. Future research should integrate multi-omics data with synthetic biology approaches and advanced biomaterials to develop fungus-based precision diagnostics and therapeutics, while comprehensively investigating their complex interplay with host systems and bacterial communities to accelerate clinical translation.
