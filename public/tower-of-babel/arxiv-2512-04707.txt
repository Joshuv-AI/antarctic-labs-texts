Abstract
. This paper presents a groundbreaking advancement in the theory of operators defined on octonionic Hilbert spaces, successfully resolving a fundamental challenge that has persisted for over six decades. Due to the intrinsic non-associative nature of octonions, conventional linear operator theory encounters profound structural difficulties. We make use of an original conceptual framework termed “para-linearity”, an innovative generalization of linearity that naturally accommodates the octonionic algebraic structure. Within this newly established paradigm, we systematically develop an appropriate algebraic setting by defining a carefully designed operator algebra and an adjoint operation which, together, recapture essential analytic properties previously inaccessible in this context. We identify a geometric structure—the “slice cone”—as the fundamental object encoding spectral properties typically derived through sesquilinear forms. We obtain a rigorous characterization of self-adjointness which indicates how to introduce a new notion of strong eigenvalues. For every compact, para-linear, self-adjoint operator with strong eigenvalues, we can establish the spectral decomposition theorem and functional calculi.

The octonion algebra

1.1.
Motivations from Physics:

Algebraic formulations of quantum theory are fundamentally rooted in

[

“Since the heart of quantum theory is a

Physically, non-associativity mirrors the non-locality expected in quantum gravity, while

may be a natural framework for Grand Unified Theories. Key manifestations include:

•

10D superstring backgrounds:
Spacetime factorizes as

•

Exceptional Lie algebras:

Octonionic structures accommodate exactly 72 fermion states—matching the three generations of the Standard Model

1.2.
Mathematical Obstacles:

Extending quantum mechanics to octonionic Hilbert spaces faces three core obstructions:

(1)

Spectral theory:
The lack of associativity prevents projection-valued measures, obstructing the spectral theorem.

(2)

Probability interpretation:
Gleason’s theorem fails as non-associativity undermines additive probability measures on the projection lattice.

(3)

Canonical commutation relations:
Operator ordering ambiguities render consistent formulations of the classical commutation relation
[x,p]=i\hbar
between the position operator
x

p

Subsequent workarounds—Jordan algebras
[

[

[
21

—either restricted to associative subalgebras or embedded

1.3.
Para-Linearity: A Geometric Resolution.

We resolve these challenges through
para-linearity
, a geometrically motivated non-associative analog of linearity. In the theory of octonionic Hilbert spaces, para-linear rather than octonionic-linear operators are the natural objects, as demonstrated by the octonionic Riesz representation theorem
[

.
For a map
T:H_{1}\to H_{2}
between
\mathbb{O}
-modules, this is characterized by:

(1.1)

\operatorname{Re}\left(T(x)p-T(xp)\right)=0,\quad\forall x\in H_{1},p\in\mathbb{O}.

This condition preserves the geometric essence of linearity while respecting octonionic non-associativity.
Here
\operatorname{Re}
denotes the real part in an
\mathbb{O}
-module
[
25

. This condition retains the geometric core of linearity while accommodating octonionic non-associativity. Notably, when the octonion algebra
\mathbb{O}
is replaced by the quaternion algebra

[

The concept of para-linearity also finds applications in octonionic function theory, as demonstrated in works such as

1.4.
Principal Contributions.

Within this framework, in this paper we establish the first complete spectral theory for self-adjoint operators in octonionic Hilbert spaces. Our main results are:

(1)

Involutive operator algebra.
The space
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{O}}(H)
of bounded para-linear operators on an octonionic bimodule

is an octonionic involutive Banach algebra with respect to the regular composition
\circledcirc
defined in Definition

. The involution is given by the corrected adjoint

, characterized by

\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle\;=\;\langle Tx,y\rangle\;+\;[y,T,x],

for all
x,y\in H
. These properties are established in Theorems
5.6
,
5.7
, and
5.9
.

(2)

Geometric self-adjointness:
A para-linear operator

is self-adjoint if and only if
\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all

in the
slice cone

\mathbb{C}(H):=\bigcup_{J\in\mathbb{S}}(\operatorname{Re}H+J\operatorname{Re}H)
, where
\mathbb{S}=\{J\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{O}:|J|=1\}
(Theorem

).

(3)

Spectral decomposition:
Every compact self-adjoint para-linear operator with

admits a complete spectral decomposition:

(1.2)

T=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}P_{z_{k}},

where
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,

are eigenvectors, and

are para-linear projections (Theorem

(4)

Functional calculus:
For real-analytic functions

\Phi(f)=\sum_{k}P_{z_{k}}\odot f(\lambda_{k}),\quad\Psi(f)=\sum_{k}f(\lambda_{k})\odot P_{z_{k}},

satisfying
\Phi(q^{k})=T^{\circledcirc k}
,
\operatorname{Re}\Phi(fg)=\operatorname{Re}(\Phi(f)\circledcirc\Phi(g))
for

1.5.
Impact and Applications.

This work transforms non-associativity from an obstruction into a structured feature:

•

It resolves problems open since Goldstine–Horwitz (1964) and provides foundations for non-associative functional analysis.

•

The slice cone

2.
Algebraic Foundations of Para-Linear Operators

This section establishes the algebraic framework for para-linear operators in octonionic Hilbert spaces. We begin by recalling essential properties of the octonion algebra, then define module structures and para-linearity, and finally Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and the Riesz representation theorem.

2.1.
Octonionic algebra

The octonions
\mathbb{O}
form an
8
-dimensional normed division algebra over
\mathbb{R}
with basis
\{e_{0}=1,e_{1},\dots,e_{7}\}
satisfying the following

Fano Plane Multiplication:
Basis elements multiply via cyclic rules encoded in the Fano plane:

e_{i}e_{j}=\epsilon_{ijk}e_{k}-\delta_{ij},\quad i,j,k\geq 1,

where
\epsilon_{ijk}=1
for oriented triples
(123)
,
(145)
,
(176)
,
(246)
,
(257)
,
(347)
,
(365)
.

Fig.1
Fano mnemonic graph

Key properties include:

(1)

Conjugation and Norm:
For
x=x_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_{i}e_{i}
,

\overline{x}:=x_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_{i}e_{i},\quad|x|:=\sqrt{x\overline{x}}.

(2)

Non-Associative Structure:
The associator
[x,y,z]:=(xy)z-x(yz)
is fully antisymmetric. The commutator
[x,y]=xy-yx
measures non-commutativity.

(3)

Moufang Identities:
For all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{O}
:

(xyx)z=x(y(xz)),\quad z(xyx)=((zx)y)x,\quad x(yz)x=(xy)(zx).

(4)

Five-Terms Identity:
For all
x,y,z,w\in\mathbb{O}
:

(2.1)

x[y,z,w]+[x,y,z]w=[xy,z,w]-[x,yz,w]+[x,y,zw].

2.2.
Octonionic Module Theory

We now define a module structure compatible with octonionic non-associativity.

Definition 2.1
.

An
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
M
is a right
\mathbb{O}
-module if it is equipped with a right scalar multiplication satisfying the alternativity of right associators:

[x,p,q]:=(xp)q-x(pq)=-[x,q,p],\quad\forall x\in M,\,p,q\in\mathbb{O}.

Similar definition for a
left
\mathbb{O}
-module
.

Definition 2.2
.

A right
\mathbb{O}
-module
M
is an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule if there is a left multiplication satisfying

[p,q,x]=[q,x,p]=[x,p,q]=-[q,p,x],\quad\forall p,q\in\mathbb{O},\,x\in M.

Here the left associators and middle associators are defined canonically.

The regular bimodule
\operatorname{Reg}\mathbb{O}
, which as a set is just the
\mathbb{O}
, and scalar multiplications wherein are just defined by the product in
\mathbb{O}
, is the unique irreducible
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
[

]
. This enables the definition of the real part of an

-bimodule
[
25
]
:

Proposition 2.3

(Real Part Operator)
.

For an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
M
, the
real part

\operatorname{Re}M
is defined as

\operatorname{Re}M:=\{m\in M:[p,q,m]=0,\ \forall p,q\in\mathbb{O}\}.

The projection
\operatorname{Re}:M\to\operatorname{Re}M
satisfies:

(1)

\operatorname{Re}M=\mathscr{Z}(M):=\{m\in M:pm=mp,\ \forall p\in\mathbb{O}\}
.

(2)

M\cong\operatorname{Re}M\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{O}
as
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules.

(3)

\operatorname{Re}
is given explicitly by

(2.2)

\operatorname{Re}{m=\frac{5}{12}m-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}me_{i},\ \forall m\in M.}

2.3.
Para-Linear Maps

Para-linearity generalizes linearity to the non-associative setting by constraining the associator’s real part. The notion of para-linearity is first introduced in
[
22
]
and then extended into a more general setting in
[
25

Definition 2.4

(Right Para-Linear Map)
.

Let
M
be a right
\mathbb{O}
-module and
M^{\prime}
an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule. A map
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(M,M^{\prime})
is
right para-linear
if

\operatorname{Re}B_{p}(f,x)=0,\quad\forall p\in\mathbb{O},\,x\in M,

where the
second right associator

B_{p}
is defined by

B_{p}(f,x):=f(x)p-f(xp),

and
\operatorname{Re}:M^{\prime}\to\operatorname{Re}M^{\prime}
is the real part operator. The set of right para-linear maps is denoted by
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{RO}}(M,M^{\prime})
. Left para-linearity is defined analogously.

For next result we refer to Theorem 3.10 in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Characterization of para-linearity)
.

Let
M
be a right
\mathbb{O}
-module and
M^{\prime}
an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule. For

and

. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

is right para-linear.

(2)

For each

f_{i}(x)=-\,f_{\mathbb{R}}(xe_{i}).

(3)

For all

2.5.
Properties of Left Multiplications and Real Part

So far we have established properties of the right multiplication by an octonion, however in the sequel we shall make use of properties of the left multiplications which are studied in the next results.

Lemma 2.12

(Adjoint of Left Multiplication)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule. For all
u,v\in H
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
the following properties hold:

(1)

Real symmetry:
\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle pu,v\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

(2)

Full correction:
\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle=\langle pu,v\rangle+B_{p}(u,v)
.

Proof.

Decompose
u=\sum_{i}u_{i}e_{i}
,
v=\sum_{j}v_{j}e_{j}
with
u_{i},v_{j}\in\operatorname{Re}H
.

Point (1):
Using Lemma
2.9
, we have

\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\sum_{i,j}\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle\operatorname{Re}\left(\overline{e_{i}}(\overline{p}e_{j})\right).

Using
\operatorname{Re}(\overline{e_{i}}\,\overline{p}e_{j})=\operatorname{Re}((\overline{pe_{i}})e_{j})
and reality of
\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle
by Theorem
2.11
:

{\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\sum_{i,j}\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle\operatorname{Re}\left(\overline{pe_{i}}e_{j}\right)=\langle pu,v\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.}

Point (2):
From (1),
\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle-\langle pu,v\rangle
is purely imaginary. Express:

\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle-\langle pu,v\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{7}\lambda_{k}e_{k},\quad\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}.

The coefficients satisfy:

-\lambda_{k}=\operatorname{Re}\left(\left(\langle u,\overline{p}v\rangle-\langle pu,v\rangle\right)e_{k}\right)=\langle u,[\overline{p},v,e_{k}]\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=-\langle u,[v,e_{k},p]\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},

by associator antisymmetry. Thus
\lambda_{k}=\langle u,[v,e_{k},p]\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, yielding the result.
∎

Lemma 2.13

(Real Part Operator Properties)
.

The operator
\operatorname{Re}
satisfies

(1)

Self-adjointness:
\langle\operatorname{Re}x,y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle x,\operatorname{Re}y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
for all

;

(2)

Determination:
x=0
if and only if
\langle x,y\rangle=0
for all

.

Proof.

(1):
Using equality (

) Lemma

(2) and Lemma

(1):

\langle\operatorname{Re}x,y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{5}{12}\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{7}\langle e_{i}xe_{i},y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{5}{12}\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{7}\langle x,\overline{e_{i}}y\overline{e_{i}}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle x,\operatorname{Re}y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

(2):
If

for all

3.
Regular Composition: Resolving Non-Associative Obstructions

This section introduces the
regular composition

\circledcirc
to overcome the failure of the classical composition of operators while working in octonionic Hilbert spaces. Moreover, non-associativity prevents a standard definition of adjoint, as
\langle T\cdot,\cdot\rangle
may not be para-linear. The regular composition provides an associative-like product compatible with octonionic structures.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and
T:H_{1}\to H_{2}
be a bounded real-linear operator. The map
\phi_{T,y}(x):=\langle Tx,y\rangle
denoted, for short,
\phi_{y}(x)
is
not
necessarily para-linear. Consequently, there may be no
z\in H_{1}
satisfying

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,z\rangle\quad\forall\ x\in H_{1},

thus making the classical adjoint definition
T^{*}y=z
invalid.

Indeed, for
p\in\mathbb{O}
, non-associativity implies in general that

\text{Re}\,\phi_{y}(xp)=\text{Re}\,\langle T(xp),y\rangle\neq\text{Re}\,\langle Tx\cdot p,y\rangle=\text{Re}\,\langle Tx,y\rangle p=\text{Re}\,\phi_{y}(x)p,

since
T
is not
\mathbb{O}
-linear. That is,
\phi_{y}
fails to be right para-linear. Counterexamples exist for left multiplication operators
L_{q}
with
q\notin\mathbb{R}
.

To generalize the concept of adjoint operators in octonionic Hilbert spaces, we have to introduce the notion of regular composition.

Definition 3.1

(Regular Composition)
.

Let
M,M^{\prime}
be right
\mathbb{O}
-modules and
M^{\prime\prime}
be an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule and let
f:M^{\prime}\to M^{\prime\prime}
para-linear and
g:M\to M^{\prime}
real-linear. The regular composition
f\circledcirc g:M\to M^{\prime\prime}
is the unique para-linear map (determined by Lemma
2.6
) satisfying:

(3.1)

\operatorname{Re}\big((f\circledcirc g)(x)\big)=\operatorname{Re}\big(f(g(x))\big),\quad\forall x\in M.

Equivalently (by Theorem
2.5
):

(f\circledcirc g)(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\operatorname{Re}\big(f(g(x\overline{e}_{i}))\big).

Definition 3.2

( Composition Associator)
.

The associator for regular composition is defined by:

{[f,g,x]_{\circledcirc}}:=(f\circledcirc g)(x)-f(g(x)).

If there are no confusions, we shall omit the subscript
\circledcirc
.

Lemma 3.3

(Composition associator formula)
.

Let
M
be a right
\mathbb{O}
-module and
M^{\prime}
an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule. Fix the standard basis
\{e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{7}\}
of
\mathbb{O}
with
e_{0}=1
. For
g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(M,M^{\prime})
and
f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(M^{\prime},M^{\prime})
, the composition associator
[f,g,x]
(see Definition
3.2
) satisfies, for every
x\in M
,

[f,g,x]\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\;\operatorname{Re}\!\big(f(B_{e_{i}}(g,x))\big).

Proof.

From Definition
3.1
and Theorem
2.5
,

\displaystyle(f\circledcirc g)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\operatorname{Re}\big((f\circledcirc g)(x\overline{e}_{i})\big)

\displaystyle f(g(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\operatorname{Re}\big(f(g(x)\overline{e}_{i})\big).

Thus:

{[f,g,x]}=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\operatorname{Re}\Big(f\big(g(x\overline{e}_{i})\big)-f\big(g(x)\overline{e}_{i}\big)\Big)=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}\operatorname{Re}\big(f(B_{e_{i}}(g,x))\big).

∎

When the associator vanishes, the regular composition is just the ordinary composition.

Remark 3.4
.

The space
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
is closed under
\circledcirc
; this fact is the algebraic foundation for Section
5
.

We remark that the definition of regular composition of para-linear mappings has first been given in
[
25
]
. The definition in
[
25
]
only requires the mappings to be real linear.
Nevertheless Definition
3.1
is a more straightforward way to understand the notion of regular composition in the case that the map
f
is para-linear. The above lemma shows that these two definitions coincide when the map
f
is para-linear. As a consequence,
the results derived in
[
25
]
can be directly applicable in this case.

We point out that the symbol of associators defined in Definition
3.2
is different from that in
[
25
]
for right para-linear mappings. We change the symbol to preserve the order so that in form we have

[a,b,c]=(ab)c-a(bc)

for the notation of associators.

Lemma 3.5

(Vanishing of Composition Associator)
.

Let all the modules in Definition
3.1
be
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and let
f,g
be para-linear maps.
The associator
[f,g,x]=0
and
(f\circledcirc g)(x)=f(g(x))
under any of
the following conditions:

(1)

g
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear, i.e.,
g(xp)=g(x)p
for any
p\in\mathbb{O}
;

(2)

x\in\text{Re}\,M
;

(3)

f
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear.

Proof.

In case (1) holds,
B_{p}(g,x)=0
, so Lemma
3.3
gives
[f,g,x]=0
. Case (2) follows from part (3) of Theorem
2.5
. Finally, if
f
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear, then by Remark 3.2 in
[
24
]
, one has

f(px)=pf(x),\qquad f(xp)=f(x)p

for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
. In view of identity (
2.2
)
f
commutes with the real part operator
Re
and hence

\text{Re}\,f(B_{e_{i}}(g,x))=f(\text{Re}\,B_{e_{i}}(g,x))=0.

It follows from Lemma
3.3
that
[f,g,x]=0
and this concludes the proof.
∎

We now collect some properties of the left multiplication operators in an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
M

\displaystyle L_{p}:M

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto

It is easy to check that
L_{p}
is a right para-linear operator. With arguments similar to those in

, Lemmas 3.31 and 3.32]
, one can prove the properties in the following lemma in which we use the notation
\odot
defined in (

).

Lemma 3.6

(

)
.

For all

and

, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle{[L_{p},f,x]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{p}(f,x);

(3.3)

\displaystyle L_{p}\circledcirc f

\displaystyle=

(3.4)

4.
Adjoint Operators via Regular Composition

This section resolves the central challenge of defining adjoint operators in octonionic Hilbert spaces. As we already pointed out, the traditional approaches fail due to the fact that non-associativity prevents
\langle T\cdot,\cdot\rangle
from being para-linear. We overcome this through the
regular composition

\circledcirc
and then establishing the properties of the adjoint.

4.1.
Adjoint Operator Definition

In this subsection,
let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(H_{1},H_{2})
be a real linear operator.

For
y\in H_{2}
, define the para-linear functional

f_{y}(x):=(\langle\cdot,y\rangle\circledcirc T)(x)

where, for simplicity, in the notation we omit the dependence on
T
.
By the Riesz Representation Theorem (Theorem
2.10
), there exists a unique
\hat{y}\in H_{1}
such that
f_{y}(x)=\langle x,\hat{y}\rangle
.

Definition 4.1

(Adjoint Operator)
.

The adjoint of
T
is
T^{*}:H_{2}\to H_{1}
mapping
y\mapsto\hat{y}
, characterized by

(4.1)

\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle=\langle Tx,y\rangle+{[y,T,x]},

where the triple associator is

(4.2)

{[y,T,x]}:=[\langle\cdot,y\rangle,T,x]=\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\left<y,B_{e_{i}}(T,x)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

Remark 4.2
.

From (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), it can be seen that the adjoint
T^{*}
coincides with the real adjoint
T^{*_{\mathbb{R}}}
(namely the adjoint of
T
in the real Hilbert space
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}})
) under
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
:

\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle Tx,y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},\quad\forall x\in H_{1},y\in H_{2}.

Thus

(1)

(S+T)^{*}=S^{*}+T^{*}
;

(2)

T^{**}=T
;

(3)

\|T^{*}\|=\|T\|
.

In view of the definition of the associator (
4.2
), we offer a reformulation of Lemma
3.5
.

Lemma 4.3

(Vanishing Associator)
.

If
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
, then
[y,T,x]=0
when:

(1)

T
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear;

(2)

x\in\operatorname{Re}H_{1}
;

(3)

y\in\operatorname{Re}H_{2}
.

4.2.
Core Properties

To further understand the nature of the adjoint operator
T^{*}
, it is crucial to understand the relation between the associators related to
T
and
T^{*}
.

Lemma 4.4

(Associator Symmetry)
.

Let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be two Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{R}}(H_{1},H_{2})
.
For any
x\in H_{1}
,
y\in H_{2}
and any
p\in\mathbb{O}
, the following relations hold:

(4.3)

\displaystyle{[y,T,x]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{[x,T^{*},y]};

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left<y,B_{p}(T,x)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<x,B_{p}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

We first prove (
4.3
).
Using (
4.1
), (
4.2
), and Remark
4.2
, for all
x,y\in H
we compute

\displaystyle[y,T,x]

\displaystyle=\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle-\langle Tx,y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle-\langle T^{**}x,y\rangle

\displaystyle=-\,\overline{\langle y,T^{**}x\rangle-\langle T^{*}y,x\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\,\overline{[x,T^{*},y]}

\displaystyle=[x,T^{*},y],

where in the last step we use that associators have vanishing real part, hence
-\overline{[x,T^{*},y]}=[x,T^{*},y]
.
The second equality relies on the boundedness of
T
, which ensures that the double adjoint
T^{**}
coincides with
T
.

We next prove (
4.4
). By synthesizing (
4.3
) and (
4.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\left<y,B_{e_{i}}(T,x)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\left<x,B_{e_{i}}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

This leads us to conclude

\left<y,B_{e_{i}}(T,x)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<x,B_{e_{i}}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

for every

i=1,\ldots,7
. As a result, we get (
4.4
).
∎

As an immediate consequence, we show that
T^{*}
is also para-linear when
T
is para-linear.

Theorem 4.5

(Para-Linearity of Adjoint)
.

If
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
is a right para-linear operator, then
T^{*}\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{2},H_{1})
is also right para-linear.

Proof.

By assumption,
T^{*}
is a bounded real linear operator. It suffices to show that
T^{*}
is para-linear. Fix arbitrarily
y\in H_{2}
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
.
For any
x\in H_{1}
, we employ both Lemma
2.13
and relation (
4.4
) to get

\left<x,\text{Re}\,B_{p}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<\text{Re}\,x,B_{p}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<B_{p}(T^{*},\text{Re}\,x),y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=0.

This forces that
\text{Re}\,B_{p}(T^{*},y)=0
, i.e.,
T^{*}
is a para-linear operator as desired.
∎

5.
The Involutive Banach Algebra of Para-Linear Operators

This section establishes that bounded para-linear operators on a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
H
form an octonionic involutive Banach algebra under regular composition and the adjoint operation, overcoming the issues due to non-associativity.

5.1.
Banach
\mathbb{O}
-Bimodule Structure

Let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be two Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
. The left and right scalar multiplication is defined by (see Theorem
2.7
)

(5.1)

\displaystyle(T\odot r)(x):

\displaystyle=T(rx)-B_{r}(T,x);

(5.2)

\displaystyle(r\odot T)(x):

\displaystyle=rT(x)+B_{r}(T,x)

for any
x\in H_{1}
and
r\in\mathbb{O}
.
As proved in
[
25
, Theorem 3.22]
,
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
is an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule with respect to the octonionic scalar multiplications (
5.1
) and (
5.2
). The real part of
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
is exactly the set of all bounded octonionic linear operators
\mathscr{B}_{{\mathbb{O}}}({H_{1}},H_{2})
(
[
25
, Theorem 3.23]
), i.e.,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})=\mathscr{B}_{{\mathbb{O}}}({H_{1}},H_{2}).

We shall prove that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
is a Banach
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule. To this end, we follow Lemma 5.1 in
[
22
]
, and we further generalize the Moufang identity for para-linear operators. Reasoning as for the following identities for associators, obtained in
[
25
, Propositions 3.6,3.15,3.19]
in the case of left para-linear operators:

A_{r}(x,T)=-A_{\overline{r}}(x,T),\qquad A_{r}(\overline{r}x,T)=rA_{r}(x,T)=A_{r}(x,T)\overline{r},\qquad rA_{r}(x,T)=A_{r}(xr,T),

where
T
is a
left para-linear map
,
r
is an arbitrary octonion,
A_{r}(x,T):=T(rx)-rT(x)
is the
second left associator
, one can establish the following identities for the
second right associators
:

(5.4)

\displaystyle B_{r}(T,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B_{\overline{r}}(T,x),\

(5.5)

\displaystyle B_{r}(T,xr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{r}(T,x)\overline{r}\ =\ rB_{r}(T,x),

(5.6)

\displaystyle B_{r}(T,x)r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{r}(T,rx).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be two Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
. Then for any octonion
r\neq 0
,
we have

(5.7)

\displaystyle(r\odot T)(x)

\displaystyle=rT(xr^{-1})r;

(5.8)

\displaystyle(T\odot r)(x)

\displaystyle=T(rxr)r^{-1}

for all
x\in H
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume
\left|r\right|=1
and hence
r^{-1}=\overline{r}
.
Let
x\in H_{1}
.
It follows from identities (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) that

\displaystyle r^{-1}(r\odot T)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{-1}(r(Tx)+B_{r}(T,x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tx+\overline{r}B_{r}(T,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tx-\overline{r}B_{\overline{r}}(T,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tx-B_{\overline{r}}(T,x\overline{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tx+B_{r}(T,x\overline{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(xr^{-1})r.

This implies (
5.7
).

We conclude from identities (
5.4
) and (
5.6
) that

\displaystyle(T\odot r)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(rx)-B_{r}(T,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(rx)+B_{\overline{r}}(T,\overline{r}(rx))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(rx)+B_{\overline{r}}(T,(rx))\overline{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(rx)-B_{r}(T,(rx))\overline{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(rx)-(T(rx)r)\overline{r}+T(rxr)\overline{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(rxr)r^{-1}.

This proves (
5.8
).
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules and let
\{T_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})

be right para-linear operators such that
T_{n}\to T
in operator norm.
Then
T
is right para-linear, i.e.

\operatorname{Re}\,B_{p}(T,x)=0\qquad\forall\,x\in H_{1},\ \forall\,p\in\mathbb{O},

where
B_{p}(T,x):=T(xp)-T(x)\,p
is the second (right) associator.

Proof.

Fix
x\in H_{1}
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
. Since
T_{n}\to T
in operator norm and the real-part
projection
\operatorname{Re}:H_{2}\to\operatorname{Re}H_{2}
is a bounded real-linear map by (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\,B_{p}(T,x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(T(xp)-T(x)\,p\bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\bigl((T-T_{n})(xp)\bigr)+\operatorname{Re}\bigl(T_{n}(xp)-T_{n}(x)\,p\bigr)+\operatorname{Re}\bigl((T_{n}-T)(x)\,p\bigr).

Because each
T_{n}
is para-linear,
\operatorname{Re}\bigl(T_{n}(xp)-T_{n}(x)\,p\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}\,B_{p}(T_{n},x)=0
.
For the remaining two terms, using the boundedness of
\operatorname{Re}
, we obtain

\bigl\|\operatorname{Re}\bigl((T-T_{n})(xp)\bigr)\bigr\|\;\leq\;C\,\|T-T_{n}\|\,\|xp\|\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl\|\operatorname{Re}\bigl((T_{n}-T)(x)\,p\bigr)\bigr\|\;\leq\;C^{\prime}\,\|T_{n}-T\|\,\|x\|\,|p|.

Hence both terms tend to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Therefore
\operatorname{Re}\,B_{p}(T,x)=0

for all
x
and
p
, i.e.
T
is right para-linear.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be two Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodules. Then, endowed with the octonionic scalar multiplications (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) and the usual operator norm,

\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
is a Banach
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule.

Proof.

The completeness of
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
follows from Theorem
5.2
.
It suffices to prove

\|r\odot T\|=|r|\|T\|=\|T\odot r\|

for any
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H_{1},H_{2})
and any
r\in\mathbb{O}
. Without loss of generality we can assume
r\neq 0
. By identity (
5.8
), we have

(5.9)

\displaystyle\|T\odot r\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|x\|\leqslant 1}\|({T\odot r})(x)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|x\|\leqslant 1}\|T(rxr)r^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle|r^{-1}|\ \|T\|\ \|rxr\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|r|\|T\|.

Since
r
and
T
are arbitrarily fixed, we can replace
r
with
r^{-1}
and
T
with
T\odot r
to get

\|(T\odot r)\odot r^{-1}\|\leqslant|r^{-1}|\|T\odot r\|.

Utilizing (
5.9
) again, we get

\|T\|=\|(T\odot r)\odot r^{-1}\|\leqslant|r^{-1}|\|T\odot r\|\leqslant|r^{-1}||r|\|T\|=\|T\|.

This forces

\|T\odot r\|=|r|\|T\|

as desired. The proof of equality
|r|\|T\|=\|r\odot T\|
is similar.
∎

5.2.
\mathbb{O}
-Algebra Structure

Definition 5.4
.

An
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule
\mathcal{U}
with multiplication
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
is an
\mathbb{O}
-algebra if:

(1)

The multiplication is para-bilinear, i.e., the left (right) multiplication operator is right (left) para-linear;

(2)

There exists an element
e\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{U}
, called the
unit
of
\mathcal{U}
, such that

(pe)x=px,\qquad x(pe)=xp

for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
.

Remark 5.5
.

There is an algebraic embedding of
\mathbb{O}
into an
\mathbb{O}
-algebra
\mathcal{U}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{U},

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle pe.

Indeed, since
e\in\text{Re}\,\mathcal{U}
, we have
(pq)e=p(qe)
for any
p,q\in\mathbb{O}
. Then by part (2) of Definition
5.4
, we obtain
(pq)e=(pe)(qe)
as desired.

Our next goal is to prove that
(\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H}),\circledcirc)
is an
\mathbb{O}
-algebra. To show this, we recall the notation introduced in
[
25
]

(5.10)

\displaystyle[f,g,h]:=(f\circledcirc g)\circledcirc h-f\circledcirc(g\circledcirc h)

for para-linear mappings
f,g,h
. By
[
25
, Lemma 3.30]
, we have

(5.11)

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,[f,g,h]=0.

Note that the real part operator
Re
here is defined for a para-linear map, and
(\text{Re}\,f)(x)\neq\text{Re}\,(f(x))
. In fact,
\text{Re}\,f
is an octonionic linear map and by (
2.2
),

\text{Re}\,f=\frac{5}{12}f-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\odot f\odot e_{i}.

We now prove the following interesting result:

Theorem 5.6
.

The space
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{O}}(H)
, endowed with the regular composition
\circledcirc
from Definition
3.1
, is an
\mathbb{O}
-algebra.

Proof.

For any
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H})
, we first prove that the right algebraic multiplication

L_{T}:\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H})\to\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H}),\qquad S\mapsto T\circledcirc S

is right para-linear.
By definition, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{p}(L_{T},S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{T}(S)\odot p-L_{T}(S\odot p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T\circledcirc S)\odot p-T\circledcirc(S\odot p).

In view of Lemma
3.6
, we conclude

\displaystyle B_{p}(L_{T},S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T\circledcirc S)\circledcirc L_{p}-T\circledcirc(S\circledcirc L_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[T,S,L_{p}].

It follows from (
5.11
) that

\text{Re}\,B_{p}(L_{T},S)=0.

Hence
L_{T}
is right para-linear as desired.
The fact that the right algebraic multiplication

R_{T}:\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H})\to\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H}),\qquad S\mapsto S\circledcirc T

is left para-linear can be proved in a similar manner.

We next show that the identity operator
I\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{O}}({H})=\text{Re}\,\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H})
satisfy axiom
(2)
in Definition
5.4
.
In view of Lemma
3.6
, we have

(p\odot I)\circledcirc T=L_{p}\circledcirc T=p\odot T

and similarly,

T\circledcirc(p\odot I)=T\circledcirc L_{p}=T\odot p.

This proves that
(\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H}),\circledcirc)
is an
\mathbb{O}
-algebra.
∎

Under a modified norm,
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
is an octonionic Banach algebra.

Theorem 5.7
.

For
S,T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
:

(1)

\|S\circledcirc T\|\leqslant 8\|S\|\|T\|

(2)

Under
\|T\|^{\prime}:=8\|T\|
,
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
is an octonionic Banach algebra.

Proof.

Let
x=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i}
with
x_{i}\in\operatorname{Re}H
. Then

\|(S\circledcirc T)x\|=\Big\|\sum_{i=0}^{7}S(Tx_{i})\,e_{i}\Big\|\;\leq\;\sum_{i=0}^{7}\|S(Tx_{i})\|\;\leq\;\|S\|\,\|T\|\sum_{i=0}^{7}\|x_{i}\|.

By (
2.2
) the real-part projection is contractive, hence
\|x_{i}\|\leq\|x\|
for each
i
, and therefore

\|(S\circledcirc T)x\|\;\leqslant\;8\,\|S\|\,\|T\|\,\|x\|.

It follows that
\|S\circledcirc T\|\leqslant 8\,\|S\|\,\|T\|
. In particular, for the rescaled operator norm
\|\cdot\|^{\prime}
(so that
\|S\circledcirc T\|^{\prime}\leqslant\|S\|^{\prime}\,\|T\|^{\prime}
), the multiplication is submultiplicative. The claim then follows from Theorems
5.3
and
5.6
.
∎

5.3.
Involution and
\mathbb{O}
-Involutive Algebra

Definition 5.8
.

An
\mathbb{O}
-algebra
\mathcal{U}
is involutive if it can be equipped with a conjugate
\mathbb{O}
-linear map
*:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
satisfying:

(1)

(rx)^{*}=x^{*}\overline{r}
,
(xr)^{*}=\overline{r}x^{*}

(2)

x^{**}=x

(3)

(xy)^{*}=y^{*}x^{*}

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{U}
and any
r\in\mathbb{O}
.

Theorem 5.9
.

(\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H),\circledcirc,*)
is an
\mathbb{O}
-involutive algebra.

Proof.

We first prove that for any
r\in\mathbb{O}
,

(r\odot T)^{*}=T^{*}\odot\overline{r}.

By definition, for all
x,y\in H
,

\text{Re}\,\langle x,(r\odot T)^{*}y\rangle=\langle(r\odot T)x,y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle rT(x)+B_{r}(T,x),y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

On the other hand, it follows from Lemma
2.12
that

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\left<x,(T^{*}\odot\overline{r})y\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\left<x,{T^{*}}(\overline{r}y)-B_{\overline{r}}(T^{*},y)\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,\overline{r}y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}+\left<x,B_{r}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<rTx,y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}+\left<x,B_{r}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

We conclude from identity (
4.4
) that

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\big(\left<x,(r\odot T)^{*}y\right>-\left<x,(T^{*}\odot\overline{r})y\right>\big)

\displaystyle=\left<B_{r}(T,x),y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}-\left<x,B_{r}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=0.

Thus we obtain
(r\odot T)^{*}=T^{*}\odot\overline{r}
. By the same method, we can also prove
(T\odot r)^{*}=\overline{r}\odot T^{*}.

Next, we prove the involution applied to composition. For any
S,T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H})
,
by definition, we have

\displaystyle\left<x,(T\circledcirc S)^{*}y\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<(T\circledcirc S)x,y\right>+[y,T\circledcirc S,x]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<T(Sx),y\right>+\left<[T,S,x],y\right>+[y,T\circledcirc S,x].

Similarly,

\displaystyle\left<x,(S^{*}\circledcirc T^{*})y\right>

\displaystyle=\left<T(Sx),y\right>+\left<x,[S^{*},T^{*},y]\right>+[y,T,Sx]+[T^{*}y,S,x].

Applying Lemmas
3.5
and
4.3
, we conclude that the difference of the two expressions vanishes for all
x,y\in\text{Re}\,H
:

\displaystyle\left<x,(S^{*}\circledcirc T^{*})y\right>-\left<x,(T\circledcirc S)^{*}y\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[y,T,Sx]+[T^{*}y,S,x]+\left<x,[S^{*},T^{*},y]\right>-\big(\left<[T,S,x],y\right>+[y,T\circledcirc S,x]\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

By using the second assertion of Lemma
2.13
, we can infer that

(S^{*}\circledcirc T^{*})y=(T\circledcirc S)^{*}y

for all
y\in\text{Re}\,{H}
.
Note that
(S^{*}\circledcirc T^{*})
is para-linear and
(T\circledcirc S)^{*}
is also para-linear by Theorem
4.5
. Then it follows from Lemma
2.6
that
S^{*}\circledcirc T^{*}=(T\circledcirc S)^{*}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain a general relation of associators.

Corollary 5.10

(Associator-Adjoint Duality)
.

For
S,T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
and
x,y\in H
:

(5.12)

\langle[S,T,x],y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle x,[T^{*},S^{*},y]\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

Proof.

From Theorem
5.9
we deduce the following chain of equalities:

\langle[S,T,x],y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle(S\circledcirc T-S\circ T)(x),y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle x,(S\circledcirc T-S\circ T)^{*}y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle x,[T^{*},S^{*},y]\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},

which prove the assertion.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

So far, we introduced various types of associators and we deduced some relations among which (
5.12
) is the most general one. In fact, from (
5.12
), we can derive both (
4.4
) and
(
2.4
).

Let
S=L_{p}
, where
p\in\mathbb{O}
. For any
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}({H})
,
x,y\in H
it is evident from (
5.12
) that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\left<[L_{p},T,x],y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<x,[T^{*},L_{p}^{*},y]\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

Combining with Lemma
3.6
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left<B_{p}(T,x),y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<x,-B_{\overline{p}}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<x,B_{p}(T^{*},y)\right>_{\mathbb{R}},

namely (
4.4
).
Letting
T=L_{q}
for any
q\in\mathbb{O}
in (
4.4
), we deduce

\left<[x,p,q],y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<[q,x,p],y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<B_{p}(x,L_{q}),y\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=\left<x,B_{p}(y,L_{\overline{q}})\right>_{\mathbb{R}}=-\left<x,[y,p,q]\right>_{\mathbb{R}}.

This establishes (
2.4
).

6.
Octonionic Polarization Identity and the Slice Cone

This section establishes an octonionic analog of the polarization identity, a cornerstone of spectral theory in associative settings. The non-associative nature of octonions necessitates restricting quadratic forms to geometrically significant subsets—the
slice cones
—to allow well-defined
sesquilinear forms. We define these structures and derive the polarization identity, which underpins our characterization of self-adjoint operators (Theorem
6.2
).

6.1.
Slice Decomposition and Sesquilinear Forms

Let
H
be a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule and
T
be a right para-linear operator on
H
. Let us set
\phi_{T}(x,y):=\left<Tx,y\right>
and let us denote, for short:

\phi(x,y):=\left<Tx,y\right>

for any
x,y\in H
.
Let
Q{(x)}:=\left<Tx,x\right>
.
We aim to express
\phi
in terms of
Q
.

Firstly, we point out that
\phi
is determined by its value on
\text{Re}\,H\times\text{Re}\,H
.
Indeed, for any
x,y\in H
, we have the decompositions

x=\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}e_{i},\quad y=\sum_{i=0}^{7}y_{i}e_{i}

with
x_{i},y_{i}\in\text{Re}\,H
for
i=0,\dots,7
. It follows that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\phi(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi\left(\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}e_{i},\sum_{j=0}^{7}y_{j}e_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{7}\big(\overline{e_{i}}\phi(x_{i},y_{j})\big){e_{j}}.

Thus,
\phi
is uniquely specified by its values
\phi(x_{i},y_{j})
for
x_{i},y_{j}\in\operatorname{Re}H
.

The key insight is that the quadratic form
Q(x)
on the
slice cone
determines
\phi
. This notion is adapted from slice analysis
[
19
]
.

Definition 6.1

(Slice Cone)
.

The
slice cone
of
H
is the set

\mathbb{C}(H):=\bigcup_{J\in\mathbb{S}}\left(\operatorname{Re}H+J\operatorname{Re}H\right),

where
\mathbb{S}=\{J\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{O}:|J|=1\}
is the sphere of unit imaginary octonions. Elements
z=u+Jv\in\mathbb{C}(H)
with
u,v\in\operatorname{Re}H
are called slice paravectors.

6.2.
Octonionic Polarization Identity

To express
\phi(x,y)
for
x,y\in\operatorname{Re}H
in terms of
Q
, let us define the auxiliary function:

m(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\left(Q(x+y){-}Q(x-y)\right)=\langle Tx,y\rangle+\langle Ty,x\rangle.

For
i,j=1,\dots,7
and
x,y\in\operatorname{Re}H
, define:

\displaystyle A_{ij}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=m(x,y)+e_{i}m(x,y\overline{e_{i}})+m(x,y\overline{e_{j}})e_{j},

\displaystyle B_{ij}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\left[e_{i}m(x,y(\overline{e_{i}e_{j}}))\right]e_{j},

\displaystyle C_{ij}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=e_{i}\left[m(x,y(\overline{e_{i}e_{j}}))e_{j}\right].

The polarization identity is then given by

Theorem 6.2

(Octonionic Polarization Identity)
.

For
x,y\in\operatorname{Re}H
,

(6.2)

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\frac{1}{56}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{7}(2A_{ij}+B_{ij}+C_{ij})(x,y)-\frac{1}{98}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}m(x,y).

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote
A_{ij}(x,y)=A_{ij}
and similar notations for
B_{ij},C_{ij}
.
Recall that
x,y\in\text{Re}\,H
. With Lemma
2.9
in mind, for all
i,j=1\dots,7
, we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(x,y)+e_{i}m(x,y\overline{e_{i}})+m(x,y\overline{e_{j}})e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,y\right>+\left<Ty,x\right>+e_{i}(\left<Tx,y\overline{e_{i}}\right>+\left<T(y\overline{e_{i}}),x\right>)+(\left<Tx,y\overline{e_{j}}\right>+\left<T(y\overline{e_{j}}),x\right>)e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,y\right>+\left<Ty,x\right>+e_{i}(\left<Tx,y\right>\overline{e_{i}}+e_{i}\left<Ty,x\right>)+(\left<Tx,y\right>\overline{e_{j}}+e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>)e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,y\right>+\left<Ty,x\right>+e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>\overline{e_{i}}-\left<Ty,x\right>+\left<Tx,y\right>+e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left<Tx,y\right>+e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>\overline{e_{i}}+e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}.

We next compute
B_{ij}
for
i\neq j
. By direct calculations, we have

(6.4)

\displaystyle B_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[e_{i}m(x,y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})})]e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[e_{i}\left(\left<Tx,y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})}\right>+\left<T(y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})}),x\right>\right)\right]e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(e_{i}\big[\left<Tx,y\right>(\overline{e_{i}e_{j}})\big]+e_{i}\big[(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>\big]\Big)e_{j}.

It follows from
i\neq j
that

\overline{e_{i}e_{j}}=-e_{i}e_{j}.

We conclude from the Moufang identity

x(a(xy))=(xax)y

that the first term
\Big(e_{i}\big[\left<Tx,y\right>(\overline{e_{i}e_{j}})\big]\Big)e_{j}
is

(6.5)

\displaystyle\Big(e_{i}\big[\left<Tx,y\right>(\overline{e_{i}e_{j}})\big]\Big)e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(-e_{i}\big[\left<Tx,y\right>({e_{i}e_{j}})\big]\Big)e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(-\big[e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>e_{i}\big]e_{j}\Big)e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>e_{i}.

Similarly, we conclude from the Moufang identity

y(xax)=((yx)a)x

that the second term
\Big(e_{i}\big[(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>\big]\Big)e_{j}
becomes

(6.6)

\displaystyle\Big(e_{i}\big[(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>\big]\Big)e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}\Big(\big[(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>\big]e_{j}\Big)+[e_{i},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>,e_{j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}\Big(e_{i}\big[e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}\big]\Big)-[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}-[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>].

Substituting (
6.5
) and (
6.6
) into (
6.4
), we obtain for
i\neq j
,

(6.7)

\displaystyle B_{ij}=e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>e_{i}-e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}-[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>].

Similarly, for
i\neq j
, we have

(6.8)

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}[m(x,y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})})e_{j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}\Big((\left<Tx,y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})}\right>+\left<T(y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})}),x\right>)e_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}\Big((\left<Tx,y\right>\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})}+(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>)e_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e_{i}\Big(\big(\left<Tx,y\right>{(e_{i}e_{j})}\big)e_{j}\Big)+e_{i}\Big(\big((e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>\big)e_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e_{i}\Big(\left<Tx,y\right>\big((e_{i}e_{j})e_{j}\big)+[\left<Tx,y\right>,e_{i}e_{j},e_{j}]\Big)+e_{i}\Big(e_{i}(e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>e_{i}-e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}-e_{i}[\left<Tx,y\right>,e_{i}e_{j},e_{j}].

By the Five-Terms identity (
2.1
),
we have

(6.9)

\displaystyle[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Tx,y\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}[e_{j},e_{i}e_{j},\left<Tx,y\right>]+[e_{i},e_{j},e_{i}e_{j}]\left<Tx,y\right>-[e_{i}e_{j},e_{i}e_{j},\left<Tx,y\right>]+[e_{i},e_{j}(e_{i}e_{j}),\left<Tx,y\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}[e_{j},e_{i}e_{j},\left<Tx,y\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e_{i}[\left<Tx,y\right>,e_{i}e_{j},e_{j}]

Substituting (
6.9
) into (
6.8
), we obtain

(6.10)

C_{ij}=e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>e_{i}-e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}+[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Tx,y\right>].

We next compute
\sum A_{ij},\sum_{i\neq j}(A_{ij}+B_{ij})
and
\sum_{i\neq j}(A_{ij}+C_{ij})
for
x,y\in\text{Re}\,H
.
We denote

\alpha:=\left<Tx,y\right>,\beta:=\left<Ty,x\right>

for simplicity.
By identity (
2.3
), it is easy to obtain

12\text{Re}\,x-5x=\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}x\overline{e_{i}}.

It follows that

(6.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 98\left<Tx,y\right>+\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}e_{i}\left<Tx,y\right>\overline{e_{i}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}e_{j}\left<Ty,x\right>e_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 98\left<Tx,y\right>+7(12\text{Re}\,\left<Tx,y\right>-5\left<Tx,y\right>)-7(12\text{Re}\,\left<Ty,x\right>-5\left<Ty,x\right>)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 63\left<Tx,y\right>+35\left<Ty,x\right>+84(\text{Re}\,\left<Tx,y\right>-\text{Re}\,\left<Ty,x\right>)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 63\alpha+35\beta+84(\text{Re}\,\alpha-\text{Re}\,\beta).

Recall the identity (identity (3.4) in
[
24
]
):

(6.12)

\displaystyle\mathit{Im}\,p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{48}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})p]

for any
p\in\mathbb{O}
.
It follows that

(6.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(A_{ij}+B_{ij})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}2\left<Tx,y\right>-\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 84\left<Tx,y\right>-\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Ty,x\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 84\left<Tx,y\right>+48\mathit{Im}\,\left<Ty,x\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 84\alpha+48\mathit{Im}\,\beta

and

(6.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(A_{ij}+C_{ij})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}2\left<Tx,y\right>+\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Tx,y\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 84\left<Tx,y\right>+\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}[e_{i},e_{j},(e_{i}e_{j})\left<Tx,y\right>]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 84\left<Tx,y\right>-48\mathit{Im}\,\left<Tx,y\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 84\alpha-48\mathit{Im}\,\alpha.

Hence

(6.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}-\dfrac{84}{48}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(A_{ij}+B_{ij})-\dfrac{84}{48}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(A_{ij}+C_{ij})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 63\alpha+35\beta+84(\text{Re}\,\alpha-\text{Re}\,\beta)-\dfrac{84}{48}\cdot 84\alpha-84\mathit{Im}\,\beta-\dfrac{84}{48}\cdot 84\alpha+84\mathit{Im}\,\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-147\alpha-49\beta.

Combining this with

(6.16)

\displaystyle m(x,y)=\left<Tx,y\right>+\left<Ty,x\right>=\alpha+\beta,

we have

(6.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}-\dfrac{84}{48}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(A_{ij}+B_{ij})-\dfrac{84}{48}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(A_{ij}+C_{ij})+49m(x,y)=-98\alpha.

Solving for
\alpha
gives (
6.2
). ∎

Example 6.3

(Identity Operator)
.

For
T=I
and
x,y\in\operatorname{Re}H
, let
\alpha=\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
. Then:

•

m(x,y)=2\alpha

•

A_{ij}=2\alpha
(since
m(x,y\overline{e}_{i})=0
by Lemma
2.9
)

•

B_{ij}=C_{ij}=0
(by associator antisymmetry).

Substituting in (
6.2
) we obtain:

\langle Ix,y\rangle=\frac{1}{56}{\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}}4\alpha-\frac{1}{98}{\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}}2\alpha-\alpha=\frac{168\alpha}{56}-\frac{98\alpha}{98}-\alpha=\alpha,

confirming Theorem
6.2
for the identity operator.

Remark 6.4
.

The quaternionic case admits a significantly simpler polarization identity due to associativity
[
34
]
. For a quaternionic Hilbert space (considered in the left-module setting) and operators acting on it, the polarization identity reduces to:

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(m(x,y)+e_{1}m(x,e_{1}y)+m(x,e_{2}y)e_{2}+e_{1}m(x,e_{3}y)e_{2}\right),

where
\{1,e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
is a standard basis for the quaternions
\mathbb{H}
with
e_{1}e_{2}=e_{3}
. In contrast, the octonionic version (Theorem
6.2
) requires:

•

Summation over all index pairs
(i,j)
,

•

Correction terms
B_{ij}
and
C_{ij}
,

•

Additional coefficients accounting for non-associative deviations.

This structural complexity reflects the fundamental role of non-associativity in octonionic functional analysis, necessitating more intricate formulas to recover sesquilinear forms from quadratic restrictions.

6.3.
General Sesquilinear Reconstruction.

For arbitrary elements
x,y\in H
, we use the real decomposition

x=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i},\quad y=\sum_{j=0}^{7}e_{j}y_{j}\quad(x_{i},y_{j}\in\operatorname{Re}H)

and the component-wise polarization identity (Theorem
6.2
) to reconstruct the full sesquilinear form. Substituting into the expansion (
6.1
) yields:

Corollary 6.5

(General Polarization Identity)
.

Let
H
be an Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule and let
T
be a right para-linear operator.
The sesquilinear form
\langle Tx,y\rangle
is completely determined by the quadratic form on the slice cone, i.e.:

(1)

For general
x=\sum_{i=0}^{7}e_{i}x_{i}
,
y=\sum_{j=0}^{7}e_{j}y_{j}
(
x_{i},y_{j}\in\operatorname{Re}H
):

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\\
\sum_{\ell,k=0}^{7}\overline{e_{\ell}}\left(\frac{1}{56}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{7}\Big(2A_{ij}(x_{\ell},y_{k})+B_{ij}(x_{\ell},y_{k})+C_{ij}(x_{\ell},y_{k})\Big)-\frac{1}{98}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}(x_{\ell},y_{k})-\frac{1}{2}m(x_{\ell},y_{k})\right)e_{k}.

(2)

If
\langle Tx,x\rangle=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}(H)
, then
T=0

This foundational result establishes
\mathbb{C}(H)
as the definitive geometric domain for spectral analysis in octonionic Hilbert spaces. The restriction of the quadratic form to the slice cone completely determines para-linear operators, a property that underpins our characterization of self-adjointness (Theorem
7.6
) and spectral decomposition (Theorem
10.2
).

7.
Para-Linear Self-Adjoint Operators

Building on the algebraic and geometric foundations developed in the previous sections, this section turns to a central object in operator theory, namely para-linear self-adjoint operators. In classical Hilbert spaces, self-adjointness plays a pivotal role in spectral theory, but its extension to octonionic settings has long been obstructed by non-associativity. Within the framework of para-linearity and the slice cone geometry, we introduce and rigorously characterize self-adjoint para-linear operators. By using the octonionic polarization identity, we establish that self-adjointness corresponds precisely to real-valued quadratic forms on the slice cone. This geometric criterion forms the cornerstone for the spectral decomposition results that follows.

Throughout this section, let
H
be a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule equipped with an
\mathbb{O}
-inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Definition 7.1
.

A para-linear operator
T:H\to H
is
self-adjoint
if
T=T^{*}
, where
T^{*}
is the adjoint defined in (
4.1
).

Remark 7.2
.

The self-adjointness condition
T=T^{*}
coincides with the real-linear adjoint since
T^{*}=T^{*_{\mathbb{R}}}
when viewed in the real Hilbert space
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}})
.

We first characterize self-adjointness for
\mathbb{O}
-linear operators, where associativity simplifies the analysis:

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
T:H\to H
be (right)
\mathbb{O}
-linear, i.e.,
T(xp)=(Tx)p
for all
x\in H
and
p\in\mathbb{O}
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

T
is self-adjoint,

(b)

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle
for all
x,y\in H
,

(c)

\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in H
.

Proof.

Since
T
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear, it follows from (
4.2
) that

[y,T,x]=0
for all
x,y\in H
. Thus
\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle
. The equivalence (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(b) and (b)
\Rightarrow
(c) follow classically. For (c)
\Rightarrow
(b), see the sufficient part of the proof of Theorem
7.6
below which is independent from Lemma
7.3
.
∎

For general para-linear operators, the characterization requires careful handling of non-associativity.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
T
be para-linear and self-adjoint. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle
for all
x,y\in H
;

(b)

\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in H
;

(c)

T
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear.

Proof.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(a) follows from Lemma
7.3
, and (a)
\Rightarrow
(b) is immediate. We prove (b)
\Rightarrow
(c). Since
T
is self-adjoint,

\langle Tx,x\rangle=\langle x,T^{*}x\rangle+[x,T,x]=\langle x,Tx\rangle+[x,T,x].

Condition (b) implies
\text{Im}\,(\langle Tx,x\rangle)=0
, so
[x,T,x]=0
. For arbitrary
x,y\in H
, the identity
[x+y,T,x+y]=0
yields

[y,T,x]=-[x,T,y]=-[y,T^{*},x]=-[y,T,x],

where we used Lemma
4.4
. Thus
[y,T,x]=0
for all
x,y\in H
. By (
4.2
),
\langle B_{e_{j}}(T,x),y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=0
for all
j
and
y
, so
B_{e_{j}}(T,x)=0
. Hence
T
is
\mathbb{O}
-linear.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

A consequence of the previous result is that if a para-linear, self-adjoint
T
is not
\mathbb{O}
-linear, there exists
x\in H
such that
\langle Tx,x\rangle\notin\mathbb{R}
.

The central result of this section provides a characterization of self-adjointness through the
slice cone

\mathbb{C}(H):=\bigcup_{J\in\mathbb{S}}(\operatorname{Re}H+J\operatorname{Re}H)
.

Theorem 7.6
.

A para-linear operator
T:H\to H
is self-adjoint if and only if
\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}(H)
.

To prove this result, we need to establish the following lemma at first.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
T
be a para-linear operator on
H
. Let us set

Q(x):=\left<Tx,x\right>,\qquad 2m(x,y):=Q(x+y)-Q(x-y).

Then for all
x,y\in\text{Re}\,H
and all
J\in\mathbb{S}
, we have

(7.1)

\displaystyle m(x,yJ)+m(y,xJ)=[m(x,y),J].

Proof.

Note that
x,y\in\text{Re}\,H
. In view of Lemma
2.9
, we have

\displaystyle m(x,yJ)+m(y,xJ)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,yJ\right>+\left<T(yJ),x\right>+\left<Ty,xJ\right>+\left<T(xJ),y\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\left<Tx,y\right>+\left<Ty,x\right>)J+\overline{J}(\left<Ty,x\right>+\left<Tx,y\right>)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[m(x,y),J].

∎

Proof of Theorem
7.6
.

We first prove the sufficiency.
Assume that
T
is self-adjoint.
Then for all
x\in\text{Re}\,H
, we have

\left<Tx,x\right>=\left<x,T^{*}x\right>=\left<x,Tx\right>=\overline{\left<Tx,x\right>}

and hence
\left<Tx,x\right>\in\mathbb{R}
.
For any
z=x+yJ\in\mathbb{C}(H)
with
x,\,y\in\text{Re}\,H
and
J\in\mathbb{S}
, we conclude from Lemma
2.9
that

\displaystyle\left<Tz,z\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,x\right>+\left<T(yJ),x\right>+\left<Tx,yJ\right>+\left<T(yJ),yJ\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<Tx,x\right>+\left|J\right|^{2}\left<Ty,y\right>+2\text{Re}\,(\left<Tx,y\right>J)\in\mathbb{R}.

Next we prove the necessity. Fix
x
,
y\in\text{Re}\,H
arbitrarily. Let

A_{ij}=m(x,y)+e_{i}m(x,y\overline{e_{i}})+m(x,y\overline{e_{j}})e_{j},

and

B_{ij}=[e_{i}m(x,y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})})]e_{j},\quad C_{ij}=e_{i}[m(x,y\overline{(e_{i}e_{j})})e_{j}]

as usual.
Since
\left<Tx,x\right>\in\mathbb{R}\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{C}(H)
, it follows that

2m(x,ye_{i}):=Q(x+ye_{i})-Q(x-ye_{i})\in\mathbb{R}

for
i=0,\dots,7
. Hence

(7.2)

\displaystyle B_{ij}(x,y)=C_{ij}(x,y).

Noticing that
x,y\in\text{Re}\,H
, it follows that
\left<x,T^{*}y\right>=\left<Tx,y\right>.

In view of Theorem
6.2
, we conclude from (
7.2
) that

(7.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\left<x,T^{*}y\right>=\left<Tx,y\right>=\dfrac{1}{56}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(2A_{ij}+2B_{ij})(x,y)-\dfrac{1}{98}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}(x,y)-\dfrac{1}{2}m(x,y).

We now show that

\left<x,T^{*}y\right>=\left<x,Ty\right>.

To this end, we compute
\overline{A_{ij}(y,x)}
. Since
m(x,ye_{i})\in\mathbb{R}
for each
i=0,\dots,7
, it follows from (
7.1
) that

\displaystyle\overline{A_{ij}(y,x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(y,x)-e_{i}m(y,x\overline{e_{i}})-e_{j}m(y,x\overline{e_{j}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(y,x)+e_{i}m(x,y\overline{e_{i}})+e_{j}m(x,y\overline{e_{j}}).

Noticing that

m(x,y)=m(y,x),

we conclude that

(7.4)

\displaystyle\overline{A_{ij}(y,x)}=m(x,y)+e_{i}m(x,y\overline{e_{i}})+e_{j}m(x,y\overline{e_{j}})=A_{ij}(x,y).

Similarly, we also have

(7.5)

\displaystyle\overline{B_{ij}(y,x)}=B_{ij}(x,y).

Utilizing (
7.4
) and (
7.5
), we conclude from Theorem
6.2
again that

\displaystyle\left<x,Ty\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\left<Ty,x\right>}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{56}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}2\overline{A_{ij}(y,x)}+2\overline{B_{ij}(y,x)}-\dfrac{1}{98}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}\overline{A_{ij}(y,x)}-\dfrac{1}{2}\overline{m(y,x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{56}\sum_{i,j=1,\atop i\neq j}^{7}(2A_{ij}+2B_{ij})(x,y)-\dfrac{1}{98}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}(x,y)-\dfrac{1}{2}m(x,y).

Combing this with (
7.3
), we obtain that

{\left<x,T^{*}y\right>}=\left<x,Ty\right>

for arbitrarily fixed
x,\,y\in\text{Re}\,H
.
This yields
Ty=T^{*}y
for arbitrarily fixed
y\in\text{Re}\,H
.
Note that
T^{*}
is also para-linear by Theorem
4.5
. Thus we get from Lemma
2.6
that
T=T^{*}
.
∎

Remark 7.8
.

Theorem
7.6
holds analogously for quaternionic Hilbert bimodules, emphasizing the universality of the slice cone characterization.

8.
Strong Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

The non-associativity of octonions entails a refined approach to spectral theory, particularly regarding the eigenvalue problem. The classical eigenvalue equation
Tz=\lambda z
fails to maintain coherence within the framework of para-linear operators, since the left multiplication by
\lambda
does not align with right para-linearity. Thus, we focus on slice paravectors, elements contained entirely within a single complex slice, and introduce the spectral parameter acting on the right. This naturally motivates the concept of a
strong eigenpair
.

In this section,
H
denotes a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule, while
\mathbb{C}(H)
denotes the slice cone.

8.1.
Definition and Fundamental Properties

Definition 8.1

(Strong eigenpair)
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{O}}(H)
. A nonzero slice paravector
z\in\mathbb{C}(H)
is called a
strong eigenvector
associated to a
strong eigenvalue

\lambda\in\mathbb{O}
if

Tz=z\lambda.

Remark 8.2
.

The choice
Tz=z\lambda
is consistent with right para-linearity, preserving slice coherence. Restricting
z
to
\mathbb{C}(H)
ensures compatibility with the octonionic geometric structure, thus establishing a meaningful spectral theory.

In this paper we have been considering the notion of right para-linearity. Although the notions of left and right para-linearity are different, but somewhat equivalent from a mathematical point of view, for physical applications the side on which linearity (and so para-linearity) is considered is crucial. This is relevant in quaternionic quantum mechanics, see the well-known Adler’s book
[
1
]
, in which it is shown that right linearity and right eigenvalues are the relevant concepts while the left versions seem less useful. This is the ground for our choice of the side of para-linearity.

We note that quaternionic spectral theory is well established since the definition of the S-spectrum in 2006 (see the Introduction of
[
10
]
for some historical remarks). Since then, there has been an extensive development of the theory, including new functional calculi and spectral decompositions tailored specifically to quaternionic linear operators, see e.g.
[
13
,
12
,
18
,
3
,
7
]
,
the books
[
14
,
10
,
9
]
and the references therein.
This theory makes use of the appropriate notion of spectrum for quaternionic operators and allows an associative-based spectral decomposition approach.

Example 8.3
.

Consider the octonionic Hermitian matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}t&p\\
{\overline{p}}&s\end{pmatrix},\quad t,s\in\mathbb{R},\,p\in\mathbb{O}\setminus\{0\}.

Selecting
J\in\mathbb{S}
such that
p\in\mathbb{C}_{J}
, the restriction of
M
to
\mathbb{C}_{J}^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}(H)
with
H=\mathbb{O}^{2}
yields a classical complex Hermitian operator, whose eigenvalues are strong eigenvalues, each admitting strong eigenvectors within
\mathbb{C}_{J}^{2}
.

8.2.
Reality of Strong Eigenvalues for Self-adjoint Operators

Theorem 8.6 shows that self-adjoint operators are characterized by the fact that the quadratic form
\langle Tx,x\rangle
is real-valued, for all
x\in\mathbb{C}(H)
. The following result is an immediate consequence:

Corollary 8.4
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{O}}(H)
and
T=T^{*}
and suppose
Tz=z\lambda
, where
z\neq 0
is a strong eigenvector. Then
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
7.6
,
\langle Tz,z\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
. Using self-adjointness and Lemma
2.9
(3), we get

\langle Tz,z\rangle=\langle z\lambda,z\rangle=\overline{\lambda}\langle z,z\rangle.

Since
\langle z,z\rangle>0
, it follows that
\overline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

8.3.
Orthogonality and Weak Associative Bases

The properties of para-linear projections necessitate a refined notion of orthogonality to ensure that spectral decompositions behave as in the classical case within slices.

We begin with the notion of weak associative orthogonality
[
23
]
.

Definition 8.5
.

A family
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\subset H
is said to be:

•

orthonormal
if
\langle x_{\alpha},x_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
;

•

weak associative orthonormal
if it is orthonormal and satisfies
B_{p}(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})=0
for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
;

•

a
weak associative orthonormal basis
if it is maximal with respect to these properties.

This formulation ensures the validity of Parseval’s identity, as in the classical Hilbert space theory.

Theorem 8.6

(Parseval Identity
[
23
, Thm. 5.7]
)
.

Let
\{x_{\alpha}\}
be a weak associative orthonormal basis of
H
. For every
x\in H
,

x=\sum_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}\langle x_{\alpha},x\rangle,\quad\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha}|\langle x_{\alpha},x\rangle|^{2}.

Moreover, eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal and weakly associative, in fact we have the following:

Theorem 8.7

(Orthogonality of distinct eigenvectors)
.

Suppose
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{O}}(H)
,
T=T^{*}
and
Tz_{k}=z_{k}\lambda_{k}
for
z_{k}\in\mathbb{C}(H)\setminus\{0\}
, with distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
. Then:

(1)

[z_{2},T,z_{1}]=0
;

(2)

\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle=0
;

(3)

\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
is weak associative orthonormal.

Proof.

Write
z_{k}=x_{k}+J_{k}y_{k}
for
x_{k},y_{k}\in\operatorname{Re}H
and
J_{k}\in\mathbb{S}
(
k=1,2
). By Corollary
8.4
, we know that
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us prove point (1). From (
4.1
) and self-adjointness of
T
we have

(8.1)

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle=\langle Tz_{1},z_{2}\rangle-\langle z_{1},Tz_{2}\rangle=-{[z_{2},T,z_{1}].}

By Lemma
4.3
and (
4.2
) we deduce

(8.2)

{[z_{2},T,z_{1}]}=\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\langle z_{2},B_{e_{i}}(T,J_{1}y_{1})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\langle Ty_{1},y_{2}\rangle[e_{i},J_{2},J_{1}]\bigr).

•

Case
J_{1}=\pm J_{2}
: The associator
[e_{i},J_{2},J_{1}]
vanishes by antisymmetry, so the right-hand side is
0
.

•

Case
J_{1}\neq\pm J_{2}
:
Define
J_{2}^{\prime}:=J_{2}-c\,J_{1}
with
c=\langle J_{2},J_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
J_{1}\perp_{\mathbb{R}}J_{2}^{\prime}
, where
\perp_{\mathbb{R}}
denotes orthogonality in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{O}\cong\mathbb{R}^{8}
. In particular,

[e_{i},J_{2},J_{1}]=[e_{i},J_{2}^{\prime},J_{1}]\qquad(i=0,\dots,7).

By a suitable scaling and change of basis, we may assume without loss of generality that

J_{1}=e_{1},\qquad J_{2}^{\prime}=e_{2}.

Let
\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{2}}
be the quaternionic subalgebra generated by
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, and let
\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{2}}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}
denote its Euclidean orthogonal complement in
\mathbb{O}
. A direct computation shows that, for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in H
,

[z_{2},T,z_{1}]=\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\,\operatorname{Re}\!\big(\langle Ty_{1},y_{2}\rangle\,[e_{i},e_{2},e_{1}]\big)\;\in\;\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{2}}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}},

where
[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]
is the associator on
\mathbb{O}
. On the other hand, by direct calculations we deduce

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle\in\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{2}},

and hence (
8.1
) forces

[z_{2},T,z_{1}]\in\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{2}}\cap\mathbb{H}_{e_{1},e_{2}}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=\{0\}.

Therefore
[z_{2},T,z_{1}]=0
.

Let us prove point (2). Point (1) and (
8.1
) imply
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle=0
.

Let us prove point (3). To show
B_{p}(z_{1},z_{2})=0
for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
:

B_{p}(z_{1},z_{2})=\langle J_{1}y_{1},J_{2}y_{2}\rangle p-\langle J_{1}y_{1},(J_{2}y_{2})p\rangle=[\overline{J_{1}},J_{2},p]\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle.

•

Case
J_{1}=\pm J_{2}
: The associator vanishes identically.

•

Case
J_{1}\neq\pm J_{2}
: Expand
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle=0
:

\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle+\overline{J_{1}}\langle y_{1},x_{2}\rangle+\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle J_{2}+\overline{J_{1}}J_{2}\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle=0.

Since
\{1,J_{1},J_{2},J_{1}J_{2}\}
is linearly independent in the real vector space
\mathbb{O}
, it follows that
\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle=0
. Thus
B_{p}(z_{1},z_{2})=0
.

This completes the proof.
∎

The framework of strong eigenpairs isolates the slice-compatible part of the spectrum and underpins a rigorous spectral decomposition. The following key facts will be used (note that items (1)–(2) have already been established):

(1)

Strong eigenvalues of self-adjoint para-linear operators are real.

(2)

Strong eigenvectors associated with distinct eigenvalues form orthogonal, weakly associative systems.

(3)

For
z\in C(H)
, the slice projections
P_{z}:x\mapsto z\langle z,x\rangle
are para-linear, self-adjoint, and idempotent, enabling spectral diagonalization (see Section
9
).

9.
Projection Operators in Octonionic Hilbert Spaces

This section develops the theory of projection operators associated with slice paravectors in octonionic Hilbert spaces. These operators are fundamental for the spectral decomposition of para-linear self-adjoint operators that will be established in Section
10
.

9.1.
Slice Projection Operators

Let
H
be a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule. For any
slice paravector

z\in\mathbb{C}(H)
, we define the associated
slice projection operator

P_{z}:H\to H
by

P_{z}(x):=z\langle z,x\rangle,\quad x\in H.

The requirement
z\in\mathbb{C}(H)
is essential for the following fundamental properties.

Theorem 9.1

(Properties of Slice Projections)
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}(H)
with
\|z\|=1
, the operator
P_{z}
satisfies:

(1)

Para-linearity:
P_{z}\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
,

(2)

Self-adjointness:
P_{z}^{*}=P_{z}
,

(3)

Idempotence:
P_{z}\circledcirc P_{z}=P_{z}
,

(4)

Range and kernel:
\ker P_{z}=z^{\perp}
and
\operatorname{Ran}P_{z}=z\mathbb{O}
.

Proof.

Write
z=u+Jv
with
u,v\in\operatorname{Re}H
and
J\in\mathbb{S}
.

(1) Para-linearity
: For
p\in\mathbb{O}
and
x\in H
, consider the second associator

B_{p}(P_{z},x)=P_{z}(x)p-P_{z}(xp)=(z\langle z,x\rangle)p-z\langle z,xp\rangle.

Using the real part operator and Lemma
2.9
(3), we deduce

\operatorname{Re}B_{p}(P_{z},x)=\operatorname{Re}\left([z,\langle z,x\rangle,p]+zB_{p}(z,x)\right)=\operatorname{Re}(zB_{p}(z,x)).

For
z=u+Jv\in\mathbb{C}(H)
with
u,v\in\operatorname{Re}H
, we compute from (
2.5
) and Lemma
2.9
(4) that:

\displaystyle B_{p}(z,x)

\displaystyle=B_{p}(u+Jv,x)

\displaystyle=B_{p}(Jv,x)

\displaystyle=-B_{p}(x,Jv).

Then:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(zB_{p}(z,x))

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Re}((u+Jv)B_{p}(x,Jv))

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Re}(uB_{p}(x,Jv))-\operatorname{Re}(JvB_{p}(x,Jv))

\displaystyle=-v\operatorname{Re}(JB_{p}(x,Jv)).

Now, using identity (
5.5
) we get:

-v\operatorname{Re}(JB_{p}(x,Jv))=-v\operatorname{Re}(JB_{p}(\langle x,\cdot\rangle,vJ))=0

This completes the proof that
\operatorname{Re}B_{p}(P_{z},x)=0
.
Thus
P_{z}
is para-linear.

(2) Self-adjointness
: By Theorem
4.5
,
P_{z}^{*}
is para-linear. For
x,y\in H
,
it follows from Lemma
2.9
that

\displaystyle\langle P_{z}(x),y\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\operatorname{Re}\left(\overline{\langle z,x\rangle}\langle z,y\rangle\right)=\operatorname{Re}\left(\overline{\langle z,y\rangle}{\langle z,x\rangle}\right)=\langle P_{z}(y),x\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

In view of Remark
4.2
, we have
P_{z}^{*}=P_{z}
.

(3) Idempotence
: By (
2.5
) and
\|z\|=1
:

P_{z}\circledcirc P_{z}(x)=z\langle z,z\langle z,x\rangle\rangle=z\left(\langle z,z\rangle\langle z,x\rangle\right)=z\langle z,x\rangle=P_{z}(x).

(4) Range and kernel
: Immediate from the definition and
\langle z,z\rangle=1
.
∎

Remark 9.2
.

The slice condition
z\in\mathbb{C}(H)
is necessary for para-linearity. For counterexamples such as
z=(e_{1},e_{2})\in\mathbb{O}^{2}\setminus\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{O}^{2})
, the map
x\mapsto z\langle z,x\rangle
fails to be para-linear.

9.2.
Interplay with Spectral Theory

Projection operators commute with para-linear operators when restricted to eigenspaces, as proved in the next result.

Theorem 9.3

(Eigenprojection Commutation)
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
be para-linear and self-adjoint, and
z\in\mathbb{C}(H)
a strong eigenvector with real eigenvalue
\lambda
(Corollary
8.4
). Then

T\circledcirc P_{z}=P_{z}\odot\lambda.

Proof.

For
x\in\operatorname{Re}H
:

(T\circledcirc P_{z})(x)=T(P_{z}(x))=T(z\langle z,x\rangle).

Noting that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
Tz=z\lambda
, it follows that

T(z\langle z,x\rangle)=(Tz)\langle z,x\rangle-B_{\langle z,x\rangle}(T,z)=\lambda z\langle z,x\rangle-B_{\langle z,x\rangle}(T,z).

Since
z=u+Jv
with
u,v\in\operatorname{Re}H
, by part (3) of Theorem
2.5
and part (1) of Theorem
2.11
, we have

\displaystyle B_{\left<z,x\right>}(T,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\text{Re}\,T([z,\left<z,x\right>,e_{i}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{7}e_{i}\text{Re}\,T([a+Jb,\left<a+Jb,x\right>,e_{i}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{7}\left<b,x\right>e_{i}\text{Re}\,T(b[J,\overline{J},e_{i}])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

The result follows by para-linearity and agreement on
\operatorname{Re}H
.
∎

The commutation relation proved above is pivotal for the spectral decomposition in Section
10
, where self-adjoint operators are diagonalized via slice projections.

10.
Spectral Decomposition of Compact Self-Adjoint Operators

This section establishes the spectral decomposition for compact self-adjoint para-linear operators on octonionic Hilbert spaces, resolving another fundamental challenge in non-associative functional analysis. The absence of associativity necessitates a geometrically constrained spectral theory, culminating in a Hilbert-Schmidt-type theorem that generalizes classical results, while respecting the octonionic structure.

10.1.
Strong Eigenvalues and Standard Spectral Data

Let
H
be a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule and
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
be a
compact self-adjoint para-linear operator
.
For simplicity, we confine ourselves to the point spectrum case. The spectrum
\sigma(T)
consists of
\lambda\in\mathbb{O}
where the eigenspace

E_{\lambda}(T):=\{x\in H:Tx=x\lambda\}

is non-trivial. To ensure spectral significance, we restrict to strong eigenvalues compatible with slice geometry.

By Corollary
8.4
, strong eigenvalues are real. For the spectral decomposition, we require:

Definition 10.1

(Standard Strong Eigenvalue)
.

A strong eigenvalue
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
is called a
standard strong eigenvalue
if there exists an orthonormal system
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{C}(H)
of strong eigenvectors such that

(1)

Tz_{i}=z_{i}\lambda
for all
i\in\Lambda
;

(2)

E_{\lambda}(T)\subset{\operatorname{span}}_{\mathbb{O}}\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\Lambda}:=\left\{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}z_{i_{k}}p_{k}:N\in\mathbb{N},\ i_{k}\in\Lambda,\ p_{k}\in\mathbb{O}\right\}
.

This ensures that eigenspaces are generated by slice-orthogonal systems in
\mathbb{C}(H)
.

10.2.
Main Spectral Theorem

The following represents a fundamental result in octonionic spectral theory:

Theorem 10.2

(Hilbert-Schmidt Theorem)
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
be compact, self-adjoint, and para-linear with all eigenvalues standard strong. Then there exists a weak associative orthonormal basis
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\cup\{z_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
such that:

(1)

Tz_{i}=\lambda_{i}z_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
for
i\in\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is finite or countable. If
\mathcal{N}
infinite,
\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}=0
;

(2)

Tz_{\alpha}=0
for
\alpha\in\Lambda
;

(3)

For any
x\in H
,

Tx=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}x

with norm convergence, where
P_{z_{i}}(x)=z_{i}\langle z_{i},x\rangle
is the para-linear projection.

Proof.

Since each eigenvalue of
T
is a standard strong eigenvalue and
T
is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear compact operator, its real spectrum
\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(T)
coincides with
\sigma(T)
. By classical theory for real linear compact operators,
\sigma(T)
is at most countable with
0
as the only possible accumulation point. Thus,
\mathcal{N}
is at most countable and
\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}=0
.

By Definition
10.1
, for each
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
, there exists an orthonormal system
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\Pi}
of strong eigenvectors such that

E_{\lambda}(T)\subset{\operatorname{span}}_{\mathbb{O}}\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\Lambda}.

For any
z_{i},z_{j}
with
i\neq j\in\Lambda
, if there exists
J\in\mathbb{S}
such that
z_{i},z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}_{J}(H)
, then

B_{p}(z_{i},z_{j})=[p,\bar{J},J]\left<y_{i},y_{j}\right>=0,

where
y_{i}=\text{Re}\,(z_{i}\bar{J})
and
y_{j}
is defined similarly.

Now suppose
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{I}(H)
,
z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}_{J}(H)
with
I\neq\pm J
. From
\left<z_{i},z_{j}\right>=0
and Theorem
8.7
(3), we obtain
B_{p}(z_{i},z_{j})=0
. By Theorem
8.7
, the set
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\cup\{z_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
forms a weak associative orthonormal set.

Viewing
T
as a real linear compact operator, we have

H=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathcal{N}}E_{\lambda_{i}}(T)\oplus\ker T\subset{\operatorname{span}}_{\mathbb{O}}\left(\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\cup\{z_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\right)\subset H.

Thus,
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\cup\{z_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
is a weak associative orthonormal basis for
H
.

Next, we show that
Tx=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}(x)
for all
x\in H
. Since
\lim_{i\to\infty}\lambda_{i}=0
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|\lambda_{i}|\leqslant\epsilon
for
i>N
. By the Parseval Theorem (Theorem
8.6
),

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}(x)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}z_{i}\left<z_{i},x\right>\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\lambda_{i}\left<z_{i},x\right>|^{2}+\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{i}\left<z_{i},x\right>|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\lambda_{i}\left<z_{i},x\right>|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\|x\|^{2}.

This proves norm convergence, so the operator
Px=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}(x)
is para-linear. To show
T=P
, by Lemma
2.6
(2), it suffices to verify
Tx=Px
for
x\in\operatorname{Re}H
. The Parseval Theorem gives

x=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}P_{z_{i}}(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}P_{z_{\alpha}}(x).

Since
x\in\operatorname{Re}H
and
T
is para-linear and continuous, Lemma
9.3
yields

\displaystyle Tx

\displaystyle=T\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}P_{z_{i}}(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}P_{z_{\alpha}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}(T\circledcirc P_{z_{i}})(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}(T\circledcirc P_{z_{\alpha}})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}(P_{z_{i}}\odot\lambda_{i})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}(x).

The last equality holds because
T\circledcirc P_{z_{\alpha}}=0
for
z_{\alpha}\in\ker T
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 10.3
.

If
T
can be decomposed into a sum of projections

T=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}

with
\left<z_{i},z_{j}\right>=\delta_{ij}
, then by the requirement of para-linearity, we must have
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(H)
and

Tz_{i}=\lambda_{i}z_{i}.

This implies each
\lambda_{i}
is a standard eigenvalue of
T
. That is, to decompose a para-linear operator
T
into a sum of projections, it is natural to require the eigenvalues to be standard strong.

10.3.
Finite Rank Operators

The finite rank case admits a particularly elegant decomposition.

Definition 10.4

(Finite Rank Operator)
.

T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
has
rank

n
if
\operatorname{Ran}T
is an
\mathbb{O}
-submodule and

\dim_{\mathbb{O}}(\operatorname{Ran}T):=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Re}(\operatorname{Ran}T)=n.

Theorem 10.5

(Finite Rank Spectral Decomposition)
.

Let the self-adjoint, para-linear
T
have rank
n
, with
n
pairwise orthogonal strong eigenvectors
\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}(H)
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

T=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}P_{z_{k}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
10.2
,
\{z_{k}\}
is weak associative orthonormal. Let
M=\operatorname{Ran}T
. If there exists
w\in M
with
\|w\|=1
and
\langle w,z_{k}\rangle=0
for any
k
, then

\mathcal{S}=\{w\}\cup\{z_{k}e_{i}:1\leqslant k\leqslant n,0\leqslant i\leqslant 7\}

is orthonormal in
(M,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}})
. But
|\mathcal{S}|=1+8n>\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M=8n
, contradiction. Thus
\{z_{k}\}

\mathbb{O}
-spans
M
, and decomposition follows from Theorem
10.2
.
∎

Corollary 10.6
.

If
T
has rank
n
, is self-adjoint and para-linear, and has
n
distinct strong eigenvalues, then
T=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}P_{z_{k}}
.

10.4.
Geometric Interpretation

The spectral decomposition reveals how octonionic non-associativity shapes diagonalization:

•

Slice-Constrained Eigenvectors
: Eigenvectors in
\mathbb{C}(H)
ensure para-linearity of projections
P_{z}
(Theorem
9.1
).

•

Weak Associativity
: Weak associative orthogonality
B_{p}(z_{i},z_{j})=0
enables convergent projection sums and

(10.1)

P_{z_{j}}P_{z_{i}}=\delta_{ij}P_{z_{i}}\quad\forall\ i,j.

•

Real Spectrum
: Strong eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
reflect self-adjointness (Corollary
8.4
).

This framework transforms non-associativity from an obstruction into a structured feature, enabling rigorous spectral analysis in octonionic Hilbert spaces.

11.
Functional Calculus for Compact Self-Adjoint Operators

This section develops a functional calculus for compact, self-adjoint, para-linear operators with standard strong eigenvalues on octonionic Hilbert spaces.
The non-associative nature of
\mathbb{O}
requires a specific approach, where the
real part
of operators and functions plays a central role. We establish left and right functional calculi
\Phi
and
\Psi
, prove some core properties, and demonstrate independence from spectral decompositions.

11.1.
Preliminaries and Function Space

Let
H
be a Hilbert
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule and
T\in\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
a compact self-adjoint para-linear operator whose eigenvalues are
standard strong eigenvalues
(Definition
10.1
). By Theorem
10.2
, there is a weak associative orthonormal basis consisting of strong eigenvectors
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\cup\{z_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
such that

Tz_{i}=\lambda_{i}z_{i},i\in\mathcal{N}\qquad Tz_{\alpha}=0,\alpha\in\Lambda,

and
T
admits a spectral decomposition:

T=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}.

The
spectrum

\sigma(T)=\{\lambda_{i}\}\cup\{0\}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
.

Define
\mathcal{R}
as the algebra of
\mathbb{O}
-valued functions
f
on
\sigma(T)
such that

\sup_{q\in\sigma(T)}|f(q)|<+\infty.

Equip
\mathcal{R}
with:

•

Octonionic multiplication
:

(pf)(q):=pf(q),\quad(fp)(q):=f(q)p,\quad p\in\mathbb{O}.

•

Product
: For
g\in\mathcal{R}
,

(fg)(q):=f(q)g(q).

•

Involution
:

f^{*}(q):=\overline{f(q)}.

•

Norm
:

\|f\|:=\sup_{q\in\sigma(T)}|f(q)|.

Then
\mathcal{R}
is an
\mathbb{O}
-involutive Banach algebra. The
real part

\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
consists of real-valued functions.

11.2.
Right and Left Functional Calculi

The non-associativity of
\mathbb{O}
necessitates distinct left and right functional calculi, defined via the spectral decomposition. Note that
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\cup\{z_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
is a weak associative orthonormal basis. This enables us to define

P_{0}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}P_{z_{\alpha}}.

Definition 11.1

(Functional Calculi)
.

The
right functional calculus

\Phi:\mathcal{R}\to\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
is
defined by

\Phi(f)(x):=(P_{0}\odot f(0))(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(P_{z_{i}}\odot f(\lambda_{i}))(x),

where
\odot
is the octonionic scalar multiplication for operators (see Theorem
2.7
). The series converge uniformly in norm by Parseval’s Theorem (Theorem
8.6
).
For simplicity, we can write

\Phi(f)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\cup\Lambda}P_{z_{i}}\odot f(\lambda_{i}).

The
left functional calculus

\Psi:\mathcal{R}\to\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
is
defined by

\Psi(f)(x):=(f(0)\odot P_{0})(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(f(\lambda_{i})\odot P_{z_{i}})(x).

11.3.
Key Properties

The functional calculi satisfy the following properties, generalizing the spectral theorem to octonionic spaces.

Theorem 11.2

(Properties of the functional calculi)
.

The map
\Phi
satisfies:

(1)

Power Preservation
: For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Phi(q^{k})=T^{\circledcirc k},

where
T^{\circledcirc k}
is the unique para-linear operator satisfying
T^{\circledcirc k}(x)=T^{k}(x)
for
x\in\operatorname{Re}H
.

(2)

Right Para-Linearity
: For
f,g\in\mathcal{R}
,
p\in\mathbb{O}
,

\Phi(f+g)=\Phi(f)+\Phi(g),\quad\operatorname{Re}\left(\Phi(f)\odot p-\Phi(fp)\right)=0.

(3)

Real-part Multiplicative Property
: For
f\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
,
g\in\mathcal{R}
,

\operatorname{Re}\Phi(fg)=\operatorname{Re}\left(\Phi(f)\circledcirc\Phi(g)\right).

Note that here
\operatorname{Re}
represents the real part operator on
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
.

(4)

Involution
:

\Phi(f^{*})=\Psi(f)^{*}.

(5)

Norm Bounds
:

\displaystyle\|\Phi(f)\|\leqslant\|f\|\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ for }\quad f\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R};

\displaystyle\|\Phi(f)\|\leqslant 8\|f\|\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ for }\quad f\in\mathcal{R}.

Analogous results hold for
\Psi
.

Proof.

(1)
Proceed by induction. For
k=0,1
,
\Phi(1)=\text{Id}
by Theorem
8.6
, and
\Phi(q)=T
by Theorem
10.2
. Assume
\Phi(q^{k})=T^{\circledcirc k}
for some
k\geqslant 1
. For
x\in\operatorname{Re}H
, by the definition of
T^{\circledcirc k}
and the induction hypothesis,

T^{\circledcirc(k+1)}(x)=T(T^{k}(x))=T(T^{\circledcirc k}(x))=T\left(\Phi(q^{k})(x)\right)=T\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{k}P_{z_{i}}(x)\right).

In view of (
10.1
), we have

T\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}P_{z_{i}}(x)\right)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}T(P_{z_{i}}(x))=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}(T\circledcirc P_{z_{i}})(x)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k+1}P_{z_{i}}(x)=\Phi(q^{k+1})(x),

where we used Theorem
9.3
to deduce
T\circledcirc P_{z_{i}}=\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}}
. Since both sides are para-linear and agree on
\operatorname{Re}H
, they are equal by Lemma
2.6
(2).

(2)
Additivity is immediate from linearity of the series. For scalar homogeneity,

\displaystyle\Phi(fp)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{0}\odot(f(0)p)+\sum_{i}P_{z_{i}}\odot\left(f(\lambda_{i})p\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(P_{0}\odot f(0))\odot p-\left[P_{0},f(0),p\right]_{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}+\sum_{i}\left(P_{z_{i}}\odot f(\lambda_{i})\right)\odot p-\left[P_{z_{i}},f(\lambda_{i}),p\right]_{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(f)\odot p-\left[P_{0},f(0),p\right]_{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}-\sum_{i}\left[P_{z_{i}},f(\lambda_{i}),p\right]_{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}.

Thus by the continuity of the real part operator on
{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}
,

\operatorname{Re}\left(\Phi(f)\odot p-\Phi(fp)\right)=\sum_{i}\operatorname{Re}\left[P_{z_{i}},f(\lambda_{i}),p\right]_{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}=0.

Here we use the fact that
{\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)}
is an
\mathbb{O}
-bimodule (Theorem
2.7
) which yields the real part of the associators vanish.

(3)
Let
f\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
and
g\in\mathcal{R}
. Then
f(\lambda_{i})\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i
. Assume first that
f(0)=g(0)=0
. Using Lemma
3.6
and the associator identity (
5.11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\!\big(\Phi(f)\circledcirc\Phi(g)\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\sum_{i,j}f(\lambda_{i})\,\Big(P_{z_{i}}\circledcirc\big(P_{z_{j}}\odot g(\lambda_{j})\big)\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}f(\lambda_{i})\,\operatorname{Re}\!\left(P_{z_{i}}\circledcirc\big(P_{z_{j}}\circledcirc L_{g(\lambda_{j})}\big)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}f(\lambda_{i})\,\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\big(P_{z_{i}}\circledcirc P_{z_{j}}\big)\circledcirc L_{g(\lambda_{j})}\right).

Applying (
10.1
) and Lemma
3.6
yields

\operatorname{Re}\!\big(\Phi(f)\circledcirc\Phi(g)\big)=\sum_{i}f(\lambda_{i})\,\operatorname{Re}\!\left(P_{z_{i}}\circledcirc L_{g(\lambda_{i})}\right)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\sum_{i}P_{z_{i}}\odot\big(f(\lambda_{i})g(\lambda_{i})\big)\right)=\operatorname{Re}\,\Phi(fg).

For general
f,g
, write
f=a+f_{1}
and
g=b+g_{1}
with
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b\in\mathbb{O}
, and
f_{1}(0)=g_{1}(0)=0
. Using the right para-linearity of
\Phi
(Theorem
11.2
(2)) and the equality
\Phi(p)=\mathrm{Id}\odot p
for
p\in\mathbb{O}
, we reduce to the previous case and conclude

\operatorname{Re}\,\Phi(fg)=\operatorname{Re}\!\big(\Phi(f)\circledcirc\Phi(g)\big).

(4)
By Theorem
5.9
and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, we deduce

\Phi(f^{*})=P_{0}\odot\overline{f(0)}+\sum_{i}P_{z_{i}}\odot\overline{f(\lambda_{i})}=(f(0)\odot P_{0})^{*}+\sum_{i}\left(f(\lambda_{i})\odot P_{z_{i}}\right)^{*}=\Psi(f)^{*}.

(5)
For
f\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
, Parseval’s Theorem (Theorem
8.6
) gives

\displaystyle\|\Phi(f)(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}z_{\alpha}\langle z_{\alpha},x\rangle f(0)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}z_{i}\langle z_{i},x\rangle f(\lambda_{i})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}|\langle z_{\alpha},x\rangle|^{2}|f(0)|^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}|\langle z_{i},x\rangle|^{2}|f(\lambda_{i})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}\|x\|^{2}.

Thus
\|\Phi(f)\|\leqslant\|f\|
.

For general
f
, decompose
f=\sum_{k=0}^{7}f_{(k)}e_{k}
with
f_{(k)}\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
. By Theorem
5.3
,

\|\Phi(f)\|\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{7}\|\Phi(f_{(k)})\odot e_{k}\|\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{7}|e_{k}|\,\|\Phi(f_{(k)})\|\leqslant 8\|f\|.

∎

11.4.
Independence of Spectral Decomposition

The functional calculus is independent of the choice of spectral decomposition.

Theorem 11.3
.

\Phi(f)
is independent of the orthonormal basis
\{z_{i}\}
in the spectral decomposition of
T
.

Proof.

For
f\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
, approximate uniformly by polynomials
f_{n}(q)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{k}a_{k}
with
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. By Theorem
11.2
(1),
\Phi(f_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}T^{\circledcirc k}
depends only on
T
, not the basis. Since
\Phi
is continuous (
\|\Phi(f)-\Phi(g)\|\leqslant 8\|f-g\|
),

\Phi(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi(f_{n})

is independent of the basis.

For general
f\in\mathcal{R}
, decompose
f=\sum_{k=0}^{7}f_{(k)}e_{k}
with
f_{(k)}\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
. By right para-linearity,

\Phi(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{7}\Phi(f_{(k)})\odot e_{k},

and each
\Phi(f_{(k)})
is independent of the basis.
∎

11.5.
Remark

The functional calculi
\Phi
and
\Psi
exhibit intrinsic non-associative features, in particular:

•

Homomorphism limitation
: Multiplicativity holds only for the real part (Theorem
11.2
(3));

•

Left-right distinction
:
\Phi\neq\Psi
but
\Phi(f^{*})=\Psi(f)^{*}
(Theorem
11.2
(4));

•

Norm deviation
: The factor
8
in
\|\Phi(f)\|
quantifies octonionic non-associativity.

This framework provides a consistent spectral-based functional calculus, laying foundations for extensions to broader operator classes in non-associative functional analysis.

12.
Concluding Remarks

In this section we summarize the main contributions of this paper. This work resolves fundamental challenges in non-associative functional analysis by establishing the first complete spectral theory for self-adjoint operators on octonionic Hilbert spaces, a problem which has been open since Goldstine and Horwitz’s foundational 1964 work. The core difficulty stemmed from the incompatibility of associative operator theory with non-associativity of octonions, which obstructed spectral decomposition, adjoint operators, and a consistent definition of a functional calculus. Our approach is based on
para-linearity
, a geometrically motivated algebraic framework that respects octonionic structure while enabling robust operator theory. The principal contributions are:

Key Innovations

(1)

Para-linear formalism.
We introduce para-linearity (Definition
2.4
) as the definitive non-associative analog of linearity, characterized by the vanishing real part of the second associator:

\operatorname{Re}(B_{p}(T,x))=0,\quad B_{p}(T,x):=T(x)p-T(xp).

This recovers the essential analytic results, among which Riesz representation (Theorem
2.10
), Parseval’s theorem (Theorem
8.6
), and Hahn-Banach extension Theorem (see
[
26
]
).

(2)

Involutive operator algebra.
We construct the first octonionic involutive Banach algebra
\mathscr{B}_{\mathcal{RO}}(H)
of bounded para-linear operators (Theorems
5.7
and
5.9
) via the following tools:

•

Regular composition

\circledcirc
(Definition
3.1
), defined by

\operatorname{Re}((f\circledcirc g)(x))=\operatorname{Re}(f(g(x))),

which resolves associativity failures.

•

A corrected adjoint
T^{*}
(Definition
4.1
) satisfying

\langle x,T^{*}y\rangle=\langle Tx,y\rangle+[x,y,T],

where the associator
[x,y,T]
quantifies non-associative deviation.

The algebra has norm
\|T\|^{\prime}=8\|T\|
and scalar multiplications
p\odot T
,
T\odot p
compatible with octonionic bimodule structure.

(3)

Geometric spectral theory.
We prove an
octonionic polarization identity
(Theorem
6.2
):

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\frac{1}{56}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{7}(2A_{ij}+B_{ij}+C_{ij})(x,y)-\frac{1}{98}\sum_{i,j=1}^{7}A_{ij}(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}m(x,y)

for
x,y\in\operatorname{Re}H
, demonstrating that sesquilinear forms are determined by quadratic forms restricted to the
slice cone
:

\mathbb{C}(H)=\bigcup_{J\in\mathbb{S}}(\operatorname{Re}H+J\operatorname{Re}H),\quad\mathbb{S}=\{J\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{O}:|J|=1\}.

This yields a geometric characterization of self-adjointness (Theorem
7.6
):
T=T^{*}
if and only if
\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}(H)
.

(4)

Spectral decomposition.
For compact, self-adjoint, para-linear operators with
standard strong eigenvalues
(Definition
10.1
), we establish a spectral theorem (Theorem
10.2
):

T=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\lambda_{i}P_{z_{i}},\quad P_{z_{i}}(x)=z_{i}\langle z_{i},x\rangle,

where
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are eigenvalues,
\{z_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{C}(H)
are orthonormal strong eigenvectors, and convergence is norm-topological. Eigenvectors exhibit
weak associativity
:
B_{p}(z_{i},z_{j})=0
for all
p\in\mathbb{O}
(Theorem
8.7
).

(5)

Functional calculus.
We develop left and right functional calculi
\Psi
,
\Phi
(Theorem
11.2
) for octonion-valued functions
f\in\mathcal{R}
:

\Phi(f)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\cup\Lambda}P_{z_{i}}\odot f(\lambda_{i}),\quad\Psi(f)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\cup\Lambda}f(\lambda_{i})\odot P_{z_{i}},

satisfying

•

Power preservation:
\Phi(q^{k})=T^{\circledcirc k}
.

•

Para-linearity:
\text{Re}\,\Phi(fp)-\Phi(f)\odot p=0
.

•

Real-multiplicativity:
\operatorname{Re}\Phi(fg)=\operatorname{Re}(\Phi(f)\circledcirc\Phi(g))
for
f\in\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{R}
.

•

Norm bound:
\|\Phi(f)\|\leqslant 8\|f\|_{\infty}
.

The calculi are decomposition-independent (Theorem
11.3
).
