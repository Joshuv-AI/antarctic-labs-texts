Evaluation of the Quality of Educational Content of YouTube Videos on Silver Diamine Fluoride
Authors: Satish Vishwanathaiah, Prabhadevi C Maganur, Yazeed K Al-Shomrani, Haider H Alhijji, Almaha Yahya A Koriri, Jubarah Mohammed M Mergami, Fatimah Balqasim M Hakami, Ruba Mohammed A Salem, Ismail A Alaama, Razan Abdullah A Mahdi, Hanan Hussain Najmi, Apathsakayan Renugalakshmi, Ganesh Jeevanandan, Varsha Manoharan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe aim of this research is to assess the accuracy and educational content concerning silver diamine fluoride (SDF) videos available on YouTube.Materials and methodsVideos were sought on YouTube using relevant keywords related to SDF in pediatric dentistry via the Google Trends application. There were a total of 70 videos accessible. Following the application of exclusion criteria, 54 videos were chosen for analysis in this study. Video data were reviewed, including the video type, quantity of likes and dislikes, view count, upload duration, and the efficacy of the videos was evaluated.ResultsThe majority of the assessed videos were classified as educational (81.4%), uploaded by healthcare professionals, and were moderately useful. A significant difference was observed in the duration (p = 0.02) between the educational videos (4.15 ± 2.17) and testimonial videos (2.12 ± 1.8). The association between viewing rate and usefulness score was found to be statistically significant, with videos categorized as moderately useful having the highest viewing rate (p = 0.04). Correlation analysis revealed that the usefulness score had a statistically significant negative correlation with the number of likes (r = –0.22) and a positive correlation with the number of views (r = 0.67) and view rate (r = 0.514).ConclusionThe information available on YouTube regarding SDF was found to be satisfactory to some extent, and healthcare professionals should be encouraged to upload quality educational and testimonial videos on the uses and applications of SDF.How to cite this articleVishwanathaiah S, Maganur PC, Al-Shomrani YK, et al. Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is a disease that remains largely untreated in children due to various factors, including a lack of understanding and accessibility to health services in marginalized communities.1,2 ECC presents a treatment challenge, particularly in children whose lack of cooperation may hinder the provision of conventional treatment or cause delays, thus requiring surgical intervention.3,4 Minimally invasive, cost-efficient, and widely available fluorides can be used to manage and arrest carious lesions when standard dental treatments are not an option or are inaccessible.5 Fluorides are effective in preventing tooth decay because they impede the carious process.6,8 Numerous materials have been tested to inhibit the development of caries at both community and clinical levels.

Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) is a preventive agent, a clear, odorless liquid indicated for desensitizing noncarious tooth lesions, molar-incisor hypomineralization, and arresting carious lesions in individuals who cannot tolerate invasive treatment, including the elderly, medically compromised patients, or those requiring additional care and support.9 Due to its significant advantages, SDF has gained prominence at both the community and clinical levels.

Unlike earlier times when parents were the primary source of information, the digital era brings the world into our hands with ready, easy, and fast access to information from various social platforms. Almost 80% of patients with internet access use it to seek medical information because it is affordable, readily accessible, and quick.10,11 Videos on social networks containing valuable dental and clinical information are trending, with YouTube™ being the leader, attracting >2 billion views daily.12 However, lingering doubts regarding the authenticity of this information raise numerous concerns among healthcare professionals.13

Low-quality information can disrupt the understanding of professional students and users, thereby misleading them. To address this, various studies have been conducted to evaluate the authenticity of information posted on YouTube™ related to different health topics, including dentistry14,16 and medicine.17,19 Based on our observation, no previous studies have appraised the quality of YouTube videos on SDF. As a complex subject, this necessitates continuous knowledge updates by both physicians/dentists and patients, highlighting the need to compare the available quality and authenticity of data on YouTube with previous studies.

The main objective of this study is to assess the content of videos related to SDF available on YouTube. The primary focus is to evaluate the usefulness of these videos and gain a better perspective on this platform to assist dentists and patients with an updated data repository concerning these issues.

Methodology

Study Design

This study used a cross-sectional design.

Search Strategy

YouTube video selection was made using specific search criteria with keywords such as “silver diamine fluoride” or “SDF,” and all videos uploaded until the 30th of August 2024 were included. Search engines and Google Trends indicated that “SDF” or “silver diamine fluoride” were the most common terms identified, and information obtained from all these videos was utilized to inspect the following conditions. Only those videos that met the following criteria, such as having a video quality of 240p or more and being recorded entirely in English, were included in the study. There was no restriction imposed on the length of the video, and the principal video content was set as SDF. Exclusion criteria included videos recorded in any language other than English, those that did not have any sound or explanation, and those that were repeated.

Content Evaluation

YA, one of the researchers did a cautious analysis of the videos included in the study and evaluated them depending on the video's length, the duration of the video, video type, number of views, number of likes and dislikes, viewership comments, and subscriptions. Before any of this was done, a Kappa value of 0.92 was concluded from intra observer calibration. The source of the video upload was deciphered and categorized into three groups named healthcare professionals, people/blogs, and science and technology depending on the individual who had uploaded the videos. Videos were grouped into educational or testimonial videos resulting in two prominent video categories. The below-given viewing rate formulas and interaction index were used to gauge interaction with viewers.12

Five exclusive domains, including (1) etiological and risk factors, (2) clinical features/findings, (3) various treatment modalities and options, (4) promoting prevention, and (5) images of habits, were utilized to evaluate the videos, and we derived a usefulness score (Table 1) using these domains. The individual scores from each of these criteria were totaled to derive an overall score ranging from 0 to 10. A score of zero was given to videos with no scientific information, categorizing them as “not useful.” Videos with a score of 1–3 were termed “slightly useful,” videos with a score of 4–6 were categorized as “moderately useful,” and videos with a score of 7–10 were tagged as “very useful.”

Usefulness scoring system

Discrepancies or doubts regarding scoring and categorizing videos were resolved with the help of PCM, a specialist in pediatric dentistry, and SV, a specialist in pedodontics, through discussion and review of the videos until a unanimous decision was reached.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software for Windows (v.20; IBM SPSS Statistics Inc., Chicago, Illinois, United States). Descriptive statistics, such as percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated. An unpaired t-test was utilized to compare two independent groups based on quantitative data, while analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to quantitatively compare all clinical indicators among the four groups. Comparison of the groups was performed using Chi-squared and Fisher's exact tests. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value below 0.05.

Discussion

The internet has transformed the way information is exchanged and obtained. Retrieving information is simpler now than it has ever been.20 With the advent of contemporary search engines, social media platforms, and widespread access via devices like smartphones, tablets, and laptops, information is readily available at people's fingertips virtually anytime.21 In recent times, more and more individuals are turning to YouTube for information. YouTube serves as more than just a video platform; it functions as a search engine for knowledge. Video content is preferred over text format because it is user-friendly, accessible, and efficient for accessing specific information.21 These benefits highlight the effectiveness of using video to disseminate complex medical information.22 The increase in viewership demonstrates a rising pattern of individuals actively interacting with health-related content on YouTube. Audiences are not just consuming information but also using the platform to connect with others who have similar health conditions, seek solutions to their health queries, and gather insights before undergoing medical procedures.23,24 The influence of YouTube on individuals’ health-related actions and choices is significant. Numerous studies have been conducted to assess the credibility of health-related content on YouTube, and healthcare professionals have acknowledged the platform's role as a source of patient education.25

The introduction of SDF has made the management of dental caries noninvasive and easy to apply.9 Unlike before, dental students, patients, and guardians are more interested in seeking information about any topic on YouTube due to its popularity. In our research, we reviewed approximately 70 videos, but only 54 were suitable for evaluation. The majority of the videos included in our study were educational and moderately useful, with an average duration of 5 minutes. Various scoring methods have been used in the past to assess the quality of video content, such as Global Quality Score (GQS)26 and VIQI,27 but we have utilized a usefulness scoring system in this research,28 which was then categorized into four groups, revealing that a variety of educational videos were shared from different origins. However, only a few were rated as “extremely or very useful,” with most falling under the “moderately useful” category, and the remaining videos were considered “slightly useful.”

A significant statistical difference was noted in the duration of videos, with educational videos generally being longer than testimonial videos. This finding aligns with the study by Bakshi et al.,29 where educational videos from the source category hospital/physician/academic association had a longer duration, suggesting that providing thorough and precise information by healthcare providers requires more time. He also reported that the most viewed and liked videos were from the patient category, likely due to their accessibility to individuals without medical expertise.

A significant difference was noted in the study done by Hakami et al.30 regarding the effectiveness of videos and the source of upload, where the majority of videos were educational and slightly helpful, with a large portion uploaded by individual users. In contrast, in our study, the majority of videos were uploaded by healthcare professionals, although this difference was not statistically significant. Moderately useful videos had a significantly higher viewing rate in our study, with the lowest rate for videos deemed not useful. Additionally, moderately useful videos were found to be longer in length and had a higher mean interaction index score, whereas Cokakoglu and Cakir31 found that longer videos with quality content had lower view numbers and rates. This difference can be attributed to waning interest from the audience when the length of YouTube videos is extended.

In another study by Hassona et al.,12 it was noted that there was no substantial correlation between the usefulness of videos and metrics such as viewing rate, viewer interaction, and video length. This contrasts with our results, where a significant positive correlation was found between the number of views and the view rate. Another noteworthy finding in our study was the negative correlation identified between the usefulness score and the number of likes on YouTube videos. A similar observation was made by Bakshi et al.,29 who reported a negative correlation between the usefulness score and the number of views, likes, comments, and likes-to-views ratio. The significant disparity between the value of videos and the substantial amount of views and likes presents a notable conundrum. This issue has been highlighted in prior research,32 showing that videos lacking scientific credibility or quality tend to garner more views and likes compared to those containing accurate and reliable information. This highlights the need for healthcare providers to improve in creating patient-friendly videos that are easy to understand and contain accurate scientific information. Healthcare professionals should also assist patients in finding trustworthy and accurate medical information on YouTube.

The accuracy of the content shared in YouTube videos is debated in both the medical and dental fields. YouTube allows for the free posting of videos related to oral health that have not been peer-reviewed, and it's not always clear where the information is coming from.33 As a result, viewers may question the reliability of the videos. There are no standardized approaches for evaluating the quality of online videos. YouTube users are allowed to upload video clips regardless of their qualifications, expertise, and educational credentials. Research shows that nonspecialists tend to include patients' experiences in their content. This can lead to the spread of inaccurate information when compared to videos shared by experts.34,35

We have made an attempt to assess YouTube videos pertaining to the English language only, which can be considered an inherent limitation of this research. Another limitation was the lack of children/parents-uploaded videos to provide insight into their experiences. The study revealed a demand for new videos, especially those that capture the perspectives of children who have undergone the treatment, as well as very good videos that provide full information regarding SDF.

Conclusion

YouTube has a wide range of content and information about SDF. Most of the included videos in our study were educational, with only a handful classified as “very useful.” The majority of the included videos were uploaded by healthcare professionals. The highest mean viewing rate, interaction index, and video duration were observed for moderately useful videos. The view ratio and number of views showed a significant positive correlation with the usefulness score. Encouraging healthcare professionals and individuals to share more quality educational and testimonial videos can foster a greater comprehension of the uses and applications of SDF, benefiting parents and patients alike.
