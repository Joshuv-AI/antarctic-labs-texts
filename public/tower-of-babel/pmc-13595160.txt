Malate–aspartate shuttle inactivation drives pulmonary hypertension through glucose carbon flux rewiring and α-ketoglutarate-dependent epigenetic remodeling
Authors: Ying Wang, Qi Liang, Yongheng Gao, Jing Geng, Xiangshu Cheng, Meng Xin, Dong Guo, Danni Sun, Lang Hu, Yan Li, Faguang Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary hypertension (PH) is characterized by progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling, primarily driven by the hyperproliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) and their resistance to apoptosis. Metabolic reprogramming, particularly enhanced glycolysis, has been proposed to be involved in PASMCs activation. However, the implicated mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, using in-vitro cultured PASMCs and in-vivo PH models, we showed that malate–aspartate shuttle (MAS), a critical system connecting glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, was significantly inhibited in PASMCs during PH progression. The contribution of MAS-mediated NADH shuttling to energy supply markedly decreased, while fatty acid oxidation capacity and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation were activated as compensatory mechanisms to meet energy demands. Notably, Slc25a11, a key component of the MAS, were significantly decreased by hypoxia, whose silencing lead to evidenced PASMC activation and further recapitulated the major pathological phenotype of PH. Conversely, restoring MAS activity via Slc25a11 overexpression suppressed PASMC hyperproliferation, thereby preventing hypoxia-induced PH progression. Mechanistically, the downregulation of Slc25a11 and subsequent MAS inactivation redirected glucose-derived carbon flux towards the pentose phosphate pathway, generating NADPH to support the antioxidative system and protect PASMCs from oxidative stress. Furthermore, Slc25a11 deficiency hindered the mitochondrial-cytoplasm transfer of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), resulting in cytoplasmic and nuclear α-KG deficiency. Insufficient nuclear α-KG reduced the activity of the histone demethylase KDM5, increasing H3K4 tri-methylation and activating transcription of proliferation-related genes. Therapeutically, α-KG supplementation restored nuclear α-KG levels, normalized histone demethylation, and ameliorated PASMC hyperproliferation in both in vitro and in vivo PH models. Collectively, this study identified MAS as a critical mediator linking metabolic changes to PASMC phenotype alterations through the modulation of glucose-derived carbon flux and epigenetic modifications. Restoration of MAS activity or α-KG supplementation may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for clinical intervention in patients with PH.Keywords: Pulmonary hypertension, Glycolysis, Malate–aspartate shuttle, PASMC proliferation, Histone methylation

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive, life-threatening cardiopulmonary disorder marked by sustained pulmonary arterial pressure elevation, increased pulmonary vascular resistance, and eventual right ventricular failure [1,2]. Current targeted vasodilator therapies provide only temporary symptom relief, while the long-term prognosis for PH patients remains poor [3]. The most recognized mechanism in systemic pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is mutation of the bone morphogenetic protein receptor-2 (BMPR2), a member of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily [4]. Recently, sotatercept, an inhibitor targeting members of the TGF-β superfamily, has been reported to improve survival rates in PH patients by reducing pulmonary vascular resistance. Nevertheless, the link between TGF-β superfamily mutations and PH progression is insufficient to establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship since BMPR2 deletion alone is typically insufficient to induce PAH in most cases [[5], [6], [7]]. This therapeutic gap for PAH highlights the need for a comprehensive understanding of the complex molecular mechanisms driving disease development.

Despite the heterogeneous etiologies of PH, vascular remodeling is widely acknowledged as a common pathological hallmark and a key structural alteration driving disease progression [8]. This remodeling process involves pathological changes in various vascular-associated cell types, including endothelial cells, pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), fibroblasts, and immune cells. Among these, the hyperactivation, proliferation, and migration of PASMCs significantly contribute to increased pulmonary vascular resistance and the clinical progression of PH [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. However, the underlying mechanisms driving these phenotypic changes in PASMCs remain poorly understood. Recent evidence suggests that metabolic programming, akin to that observed in cancer, plays a vital role in PASMC activation and PH development [13]. Enhanced aerobic glycolysis represents one of the most consistently observed metabolic abnormalities in PH and is strongly associated with PASMC activation. Similar to proliferating cancer cells, PASMCs in PH preferentially increase glycolytic flux even under normoxic conditions, facilitating rapid cell growth and survival. This glycolytic shift has long been believed to support PASMC proliferation by providing ATP and biosynthetic intermediates [14]. Notably, an increased glycolytic process in PASMCs is often accompanied by alterations in mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism [15]. Growing evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming is not merely a passive response to heightened energetic demands but may actively regulate cell fate, signaling pathways, redox homeostasis, and epigenetic states [16]. However, how metabolic changes, particularly alterations in glycolysis, contribute to PASMC activation during PH progression remains unclear and requires further investigation.

The malate–aspartate shuttle (MAS) serves as a fundamental route for NADH/NAD+ exchange between intra- and extramitochondrial compartments. Through MAS, cytosolic reducing equivalents generated by glycolysis are transferred into mitochondria for ATP production, thereby functionally linking glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation [17]. In addition to this transfer, MAS coordinates the exchange of malate, aspartate, α-ketoglutarate, and glutamate, contributing to the maintenance of cytosolic and mitochondrial redox homeostasis, which in turn influences cellular energy metabolism and functional states [18]. Despite the recognition of PASMC metabolic reprogramming as a key factor in pulmonary vascular remodeling, the dysregulation of MAS in PASMCs under conditions of PH and its role in PASMC remodeling remain largely unexplored.

This study presents compelling evidence that both the MAS process and MAS-derived ATP production are significantly decreased in PASMCs during PH development. In vivo and in vitro data further indicate that MAS inhibition plays a critical role, rather than being a mere concomitant phenomenon, in hypoxia-induced PASMC activation by integrating metabolic remodeling with epigenetic reprogramming. Restoration of MAS effectively suppressed hypoxia-induced PASMC hyperproliferation, thereby preventing hypoxia-induced PH progression. Consequently, this study identifies MAS inhibition as a novel pathological process regulating PASMC proliferation and PH development. Manipulating MAS may represent a potential strategy for the prevention and treatment of clinical patients with PH.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Air Force Medical University. Male C57BL/6J mice aged 8 weeks and weighing 20–25 g were used in this study. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with free access to standard chow and water under a 12 h light/dark cycle. To generate smooth muscle cell-specific Slc25a11 knockout mice, Slc25a11 fl/fl mice were crossed with Myh11-CreERT2 mice to obtain inducible conditional knockout mice, designated Slc25a11KO. Cre-mediated recombination was induced by oral gavage of tamoxifen at 75 mg/kg/day for 5 consecutive days. Experiments were initiated after a 10–14 day washout period to allow efficient recombination and tamoxifen clearance. Slc25a11fl/fl littermates receiving the same tamoxifen treatment were used as controls. For smooth muscle cell-specific Slc25a11 gain-of-function studies, Rosa26 conditional Slc25a11 knock-in mice carrying a CAG-loxP-STOP-loxP-Kozak-Slc25a11-3xFLAG-WPRE-pA cassette at the Rosa26 locus were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing. These mice were crossed with Myh11-CreERT2 mice to obtain inducible smooth muscle cell-specific Slc25a11-overexpressing mice, designated Slc25a11Tg. Following tamoxifen oral administration, Cre-mediated excision of the floxed STOP cassette enabled Slc25a11-3xFLAG expression driven by the CAG promoter in smooth muscle cells. Littermate mice lacking Cre or not carrying the knock-in allele but receiving identical tamoxifen treatment were used as controls where appropriate. Slc25a11 overexpression or knockout efficiency was verified in pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells by immunofluorescence co-staining for α-SMA and Slc25a11 and Western-blot analysis.

Hypoxia/SU5416-induced pulmonary hypertension model

Experimental pulmonary hypertension was induced using a combined chronic hypoxia and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor blockade model. Briefly, mice were exposed to normobaric hypoxia (10% O2) in a controlled hypoxic chamber for 4 weeks. Oxygen concentration was continuously monitored and automatically regulated to maintain stable hypoxic conditions. SU5416 (20 mg/kg; dissolved in DMSO and diluted in appropriate vehicle) was administered subcutaneously once per week throughout the hypoxia exposure period, starting on day 0 of hypoxic conditioning. Control mice were maintained under normoxic conditions and received equivalent vehicle injections. The hypoxic chamber was briefly opened at regular intervals for cage maintenance, including food and water replacement, with minimal disturbance to oxygen stability. At the end of the exposure period, mice were returned to normoxic conditions for terminal analyses.

For metabolic intervention studies, mice subjected to hypoxia/SU5416 treatment received α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) at 300 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection once daily for 4 consecutive weeks as previously described [19]. Control groups received equivalent volumes of vehicle. At the end of experimental protocols, mice underwent comprehensive phenotyping, including transthoracic echocardiography, invasive hemodynamic measurements, pulmonary vascular angiography, histological assessment, and molecular analyses.

Echocardiographic assessment

Cardiac structure and pulmonary hemodynamics were evaluated using high-resolution transthoracic echocardiography (VEVO 3100 imaging system, VisualSonics Inc., Toronto, Canada) equipped with a high-frequency linear array transducer. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane for induction and maintained at 1.5% isoflurane on a temperature-controlled platform. Heart rate was continuously monitored and maintained within a physiological range (400–450 beats per minute) throughout data acquisition to minimize anesthesia-induced cardiac depression. Pulmonary arterial flow was assessed in the right ventricular outflow tract using pulsed-wave Doppler imaging with the sample volume positioned at the level of the pulmonary valve. Pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAT) was defined as the interval from the onset of systolic flow to peak velocity, and pulmonary artery ejection time (PET) as the duration from flow onset to flow termination [20]. The PAT/PET ratio was calculated as an index of pulmonary vascular load and right ventricular afterload. Right ventricular systolic function was quantified by tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), obtained in the apical four-chamber view using M-mode echocardiography and defined as the longitudinal displacement of the tricuspid annulus between end-diastole and end-systole. All parameters were averaged from at least three consecutive cardiac cycles and analyzed offline by an investigator blinded to experimental allocation.

Hemodynamic measurement and right ventricular hypertrophy assessment

Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) was measured using a high-fidelity micropressure catheter (Millar Mikro-Tip, Millar Instruments, Houston, TX, USA) under general anesthesia. The right jugular vein was surgically exposed, and the catheter was advanced into the right ventricle under continuous pressure waveform monitoring. Correct catheter positioning was confirmed by a characteristic right ventricular pressure tracing, defined by a rapid systolic upstroke and low diastolic baseline without arterial pulsatility. After a stabilization period of 5–10 min, steady-state pressure recordings were acquired, and RVSP was extracted from the corresponding pressure tracings.

Following hemodynamic assessment, mice were euthanized, and hearts were rapidly excised. The right ventricle (RV) was carefully dissected from the left ventricle plus interventricular septum (LV + S) under a dissecting microscope to ensure anatomical precision. Right ventricular hypertrophy was quantified using the Fulton index, defined as RV/(LV + S) [21].

Pulmonary angiography

Pulmonary vascular architecture was assessed using Microfil-based vascular casting. Following euthanasia, the thoracic cavity was opened, and heparin was administered via the right ventricle or pulmonary artery to prevent intravascular coagulation. The pulmonary circulation was then flushed with warm phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood, followed by perfusion with Microfil compound (Flow Tech Inc., USA) consisting of polymer, diluent, and curing agent. Perfusion was continued under controlled pressure until uniform filling of distal pulmonary vessels was achieved and venous effluent became clear, indicating complete vascular casting. Lungs were then stored at 4°C for polymerization (2–4 h), excised, rinsed in PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Microvascular architecture was visualized using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), and three-dimensional reconstructions of the pulmonary vascular tree were generated. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ, including total vascular length, number of branch points, and junction density in distal pulmonary regions, providing an index of pulmonary vascular rarefaction and network integrity.

Histology, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence staining

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate pulmonary vascular morphology. Elastic van Gieson (EVG) staining was used to assess elastic lamina integrity and medial wall remodeling of pulmonary arteries. For immunohistochemistry, lung sections were incubated with anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibody to evaluate the degree of pulmonary arterial muscularization. Positive staining was visualized using standard DAB chromogenic detection. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against α-SMA, Slc25a11, HK2, PFK1, or PKM2. After washing, sections were incubated with species-appropriate fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence or confocal laser scanning microscope under identical acquisition settings across groups. Quantitative analysis, including fluorescence intensity and vascular wall thickness, was performed using ImageJ software. For morphometric analysis, small pulmonary arteries (external diameter 25–100 μm) were randomly selected, and medial wall thickness was calculated as (external diameter − internal diameter)/external diameter. TUNEL staining was performed using a commercial apoptosis detection kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. TUNEL-positive cells surrounding pulmonary arteries were quantified as the percentage of total nuclei in the corresponding field.

PASMC isolation and culture

Primary pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) were isolated from mouse pulmonary arteries under a stereomicroscope. Briefly, pulmonary arteries were carefully dissected and freed from surrounding connective tissue. The vessels were enzymatically digested using collagenase type I and elastase at 37°C, and released cells were collected and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. For hypoxic exposure, PASMCs were cultured in a tri-gas incubator (1% O2, 5% CO2, balanced N2) for 48 h. Normoxic controls were maintained at 21% O2 and 5% CO2 under identical culture conditions. Primary PASMCs were used between passages 2 and 5. Throughout this study, all in-vitro experiments presented were performed under identical DMEM+10% FBS conditions for the corresponding control and experimental groups. All cultures were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed to characterize the phenotype and purity of isolated pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). Briefly, isolated PASMCs were harvested, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in staining buffer. For cell-specific markers staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized, followed by incubation with an AF488-conjugated anti-α-SMA antibody and PE-conjugated antibodies against CD31, CD45, or PDGFRα, respectively. Appropriate negative controls were included to establish the gating thresholds. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended in staining buffer, and fluorescence signals were acquired using a flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. PASMCs were characterized by positive expression of α-SMA while negative expression of CD31, CD45, and PDGFRα.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and analysis of public dataset

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE228643 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [22]. Individual samples were imported, merged, and normalized using the Seurat R package. To minimize potential batch effects among samples, Harmony was applied for batch-effect correction and data integration. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was subsequently performed for dimensionality reduction and visualization. Cell types were annotated according to the expression of canonical lineage-specific marker genes. Epithelial cells were identified by SFTPA1, SFTPB, and NAPSA; fibroblasts by DCN, LUM, and MMP2; pericytes by NOTCH3, GJA4, and RERGL; smooth muscle cells (SMCs) by ACTA2, MYH11, and CNN1; endothelial cells by PECAM1, VWF, and CLDN5; lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) by PROX1, FLT4, and CCL21; macrophages by CD68, CD163, and CD14; dendritic cells (DCs) by CD1C, CD1E, and FCER1A; mast cells by KIT, TPSAB1, and TPSB2; natural killer (NK) cells by PRF1, GNLY, and KLRD1; T cells by CD3D, CD3E, and IL7R; and B cells by CD79A, MS4A1, and IGHM. The expression patterns of these canonical markers across the annotated cell populations were visualized using a dot plot. Following cell-type annotation, SMCs were subsetted for downstream analysis. The expression level of Slc25a11 in SMCs was compared among the indicated disease groups and visualized using violin plots. Differences in Slc25a11 expression between groups were assessed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Measurement of mitochondrial and cytosolic malate and aspartate

PASMCs were harvested and subjected to subcellular fractionation to separate the mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions. Briefly, cells were collected and homogenized in ice-cold isolation buffer, followed by differential centrifugation to obtain the cytosolic supernatant and mitochondrial pellet. The mitochondrial pellet was subsequently resuspended in the appropriate extraction buffer. Malate and aspartate levels in the mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions were determined using commercially available enzymatic assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance signals were measured using a microplate reader, and metabolite concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated in parallel. Malate and aspartate levels were normalized to the protein concentration of the corresponding mitochondrial or cytosolic fraction. Relative metabolite levels were expressed relative to those of the corresponding Ad-NC control group.

Adenoviral transduction and siRNA knockdown

For Slc25a11 knockdown, PASMCs were transduced with adenovirus carrying Slc25a11-specific shRNA (Ad-shSlc25a11), while adenovirus carrying a non-targeting sequence (Ad-NC) served as control. For overexpression experiments, PASMCs were infected with adenovirus expressing Slc25a11 (Ad-Slc25a11). Knockdown or overexpression efficiency was confirmed by western blotting prior to downstream assays. For KDM5B silencing, PASMCs were transfected with siRNA targeting KDM5B (si-KDM5B) or non-targeting control siRNA (si-NC) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for western blotting, ChIP-qPCR, gene expression analysis, and metabolite assays.

Pharmacological treatments

To modulate metabolic pathways, PASMCs were treated with the following compounds under indicated conditions: GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876 (1 μM, 24 h) to inhibit glucose uptake, Aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA, 1 mM) to inhibit malate–aspartate shuttle (MAS) activity, Etomoxir to inhibit CPT1-mediated fatty acid oxidation, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG, 5 mM, 24 h) for in-vitro supplementation. For detection of MAS-mediated ATP production, PASMCs were preconditioned under normoxia or hypoxia (48 h), washed with HBSS, and then incubated with glucose-containing medium in the presence or absence of AOAA for 6 h.

Cell proliferation assays

PASMC proliferation was assessed using EdU incorporation and CCK-8 assays. For EdU labeling, cells were incubated with EdU reagent according to the manufacturer's protocol, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized, and subjected to click-chemistry detection. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and EdU-positive cells were quantified as a percentage of total nuclei. For CCK-8 assays, PASMCs were seeded in 96-well plates and treated as indicated. CCK-8 reagent was added and incubated for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All proliferation assays were performed after serum starvation (0.5% FBS, 24 h) to minimize baseline proliferative activity.

Apoptosis analysis

PASMCs apoptosis was evaluated using western blotting, and flow cytometry. Protein expression of apoptosis-related markers, including Bax, Bcl-2, and cleaved caspase-3, was analyzed by western blotting. For flow cytometry, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and apoptotic populations were quantified using flow cytometric analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from PASMCs or lung tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and RNA integrity was verified prior to reverse transcription. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a real-time PCR system. Gene expression levels were quantified based on cycle threshold (Ct) values and analyzed using a relative quantification approach. Target gene expression was normalized to β-actin as an internal reference gene. Relative mRNA abundance was calculated by comparing normalized Ct values between experimental and control groups. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates, and each biological sample was independently analyzed. Target genes included cell cycle-related genes (Ccnd1, Ccna2, Ccne1, Ccne2, Cdk1, Cdk4, Cdk6) and apoptosis-related genes (Bcl-2, Bax) (Table S1).

Seahorse metabolic flux analysis

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were measured using a Seahorse XF extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies). For glycolysis stress tests, PASMCs were seeded in Seahorse XF plates and equilibrated in assay medium prior to sequential injection of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 μM), and 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG, 50 mM). Glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve were calculated according to manufacturer's instructions. For mitochondrial respiration analysis, OCR was measured under sequential injection of oligomycin (ATP synthase inhibitor), FCCP (uncoupler), and rotenone/antimycin A (complex I/III inhibitors). Fatty acid oxidation-dependent respiration was assessed by comparing OCR responses in the presence or absence of etomoxir, and basal and maximal respiration attributable to fatty acid oxidation were calculated accordingly.

Measurement of ATP, NADH/NAD+, NADPH, and α-KG

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using a luciferase-based ATP assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. NADH/NAD+ ratios and NADPH levels were measured using commercially available colorimetric/fluorometric assay kits following the manufacturer's protocols. For α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) quantification, cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were isolated using a subcellular fractionation kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. α-KG levels were determined using a commercially available enzymatic assay kit. Absorbance or fluorescence signals were measured using a microplate reader, and metabolite concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated in parallel. All metabolite levels were normalized to total protein content of each sample.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and superoxide detection

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in PASMCs were measured using DCFH-DA fluorescent probe according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37°C for 30 min under dark conditions, followed by washing with PBS. Fluorescence intensity was detected using fluorescence microscopy and quantified using ImageJ software.

Superoxide production in pulmonary artery segments was assessed using dihydroethidium (DHE) staining. Fresh pulmonary arteries were incubated with 5 μM DHE at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescent images were acquired under identical exposure settings and quantified as mean fluorescence intensity.

To measure H2O2 production, pulmonary artery segments were incubated with 10 μM H2O2-sensitive fluorescent probe Amplex Red reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence intensity was recorded every 3 min for a total duration of 30 min using a BioTek microplate reader under standardized excitation/emission settings. H2O2 production was calculated based on the linear increase in fluorescence over time using linear regression analysis, as previously described. All measurements were normalized to protein concentration and expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU).

Protein carbonylation assay

Protein carbonylation levels were assessed using an OxyBlot™ Protein Oxidation Detection Kit (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, PASMCs samples were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer, and total proteins were extracted and divided equally for parallel analyses. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by either Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining (Sigma-Aldrich) to determine total protein loading or electrotransfer onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes for immunoblotting. Membranes were blocked and incubated with anti-2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (anti-DNPH) antibody to detect derivatized carbonyl groups. Protein carbonylation levels were quantified by densitometric analysis of immunoblot signals and normalized to corresponding Coomassie-stained total protein signals to control for loading variability.

Measurement of intracellular GSH and GSSG levels

Cellular antioxidant capacity was assessed by measuring glutathione redox status. Intracellular reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels were quantified using a glutathione recycling assay kit based on the enzymatic reaction of glutathione with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) in the presence of glutathione reductase and NADPH, generating 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB), which was detected spectrophotometrically. Prior to measurement, samples were deproteinized using the kit-provided precipitation solution to eliminate interference from protein thiols. Total glutathione and GSSG were measured separately, and GSH content was calculated by subtraction. The GSH/GSSG ratio was used as an index of cellular redox homeostasis and antioxidant buffering capacity.

Targeted metabolomics and 13C-glucose tracing

For targeted metabolomics, PASMCs were cultured under indicated conditions and metabolites were extracted using pre-chilled 80% methanol. Samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Quantified metabolites included glycolytic intermediates and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolites, such as glucose-6-phosphate, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, citrate derivatives, isocitrate, α-KG, succinate, fumarate, and malate. Metabolite abundance was analyzed and subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. For stable isotope tracing, PASMCs were cultured in glucose-free medium supplemented with uniformly labeled 13C-glucose for 6 h. Isotopic enrichment was corrected for natural abundance. Incorporation of 13C into glycolytic, pentose phosphate pathway, and TCA cycle intermediates was quantified using mass spectrometry to determine glucose-derived carbon flux.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from PASMCs, pulmonary arteries, or lung tissues using TRIzol reagent. RNA integrity was assessed prior to library construction. Sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered and aligned to the mouse reference genome. Gene expression quantification and differential analysis were performed using DESeq2. Genes with a fold change greater than 2 or less than 0.5, together with an adjusted P value < 0.05, were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG pathway analysis, heatmaps, volcano plots, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), were performed using standard bioinformatics pipelines.

ChIP sequencing and ChIP-qPCR

ChIP-seq was performed to assess genome-wide H3K4me3 enrichment. PASMCs were crosslinked with formaldehyde, quenched with glycine, lysed, and sonicated to obtain chromatin fragments. Chromatin was immunoprecipitated using anti-H3K4me3 antibody or IgG control. After reverse crosslinking and DNA purification, ChIP DNA was used for library preparation and high-throughput sequencing. Sequencing reads were aligned to the mouse genome. H3K4me3 distribution around transcription start sites and gene bodies was analyzed using heatmaps and genome browser tracks. Differential peaks were identified and associated genes were subjected to GO enrichment analysis. For ChIP-qPCR validation, immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed using primers targeting promoter regions of cell-cycle genes, including Ccna2, Ccnd2, Ccne1, Ccne2, Cdk1, Cdk2, and Cdk4 (Table S2). The resulting data were calculated as a percentage of input DNA. For relative comparison across conditions, values were further normalized to the corresponding control group.

Western blotting

Cells or tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors under ice-cold conditions. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against metabolic enzymes, transporters, mitochondrial proteins, histone modifications, and apoptosis-related proteins, including Slc25a11 (Proteintech, #12253-1-AP), Slc25a12 (Proteintech, #26804-1-AP), GOT1/2 (Cell signaling technology, #34423) (Cell signaling technology, #71692), MDH1/2 (Proteintech, #15904-1-AP) (Proteintech, #15462-1-AP), GLUT1 (Cell signaling technology, #12939), HK2(Abcam, # ab227198), PFK1 (Proteintech, #55028-1-AP), PKM2 (Cell signaling technology, #4053), LDHA (Proteintech, #21799-1-AP), MCAD (Abcam, # ab110296), LCAD(Abcam, # ab128566), CPT1 (Abcam, #ab134988), PPAR-γ (Abcam, #ab209350), ACSL1 (Proteintech, #13989-1-AP), CS (Proteintech, #16131-1-AP), IDH2 (Proteintech, #15932-1-AP), OGDH (Proteintech, #15212-1-AP), Bax (Abcam, #ab32503), Bcl-2(Proteintech, #26593-1-AP), cleaved caspase-3(Abcam, #ab32042), 4-HNE(Abcam, # ab46545), G6PD(Proteintech, # 66373-1-Ig), 6PGD(Proteintech, # 14718-1-AP) and histone modifications [H3K4me2/3 (Proteintech, #91321) (Cell signaling technology, #9751), H3K9me2/3 (Proteintech, #39041) (Abcam, #ab8898), H3K27me3 (Proteintech, #39055), H4K20me1/2/3 (Proteintech, #39027) (Proteintech, #39539) (Proteintech, #91107)], as well as β-actin (Proteintech, #66009-1-Ig) as loading controls. Primary antibodies were used at 1:1000 dilution (β-actin at 1:5000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at 1:5000 for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and visualized under standardized exposure conditions. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to β-actin, depending on subcellular localization.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. Each dot represents an independent biological replicate or individual animal, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism or equivalent statistical software. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's or Bonferroni post hoc test was used as appropriate. For experiments involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's or Bonferroni post hoc test was applied where appropriate. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Boosted glycolysis while decreased glucose-derived energy supply proportion were observed in hypoxia-treated PASMCs

To investigate the role of glycolysis in PASMC activation during PH, primary PASMCs were exposed to hypoxia for 48 h. As illustrated in Fig. 1A and B, hypoxia significantly enhanced PASMC proliferation, as demonstrated by increased EdU incorporation and elevated cell viability reflected by OD450 values. Concurrently, apoptosis markers such as Bax and cleaved-caspase-3 were significantly suppressed, whereas the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl-2 exhibited an increase in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. 1C). RNA sequencing analysis was subsequently conducted to assess the transcriptional profile changes in PASMCs under hypoxic conditions. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed numerous significantly altered genes following hypoxic exposure (Fig. 1D). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were predominantly associated with pathways related to cell proliferation, vascular smooth muscle contraction, and apoptosis regulation (Fig. 1E). Consistent with transcriptomic findings, quantitative PCR analysis further confirmed the upregulation of several proliferation-associated and anti-apoptotic genes in hypoxic PASMCs (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that in vitro hypoxia treatment effectively induced extensive PASMC activation. Next, the levels of major glycolysis-related proteins were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 1G, the expression of Glut1, HK2, PFK1, PKM2, and LDHA significantly increased in response to hypoxia. Seahorse extracellular flux analysis was then performed to assess glycolytic activity. As presented in Fig. 1H–J, hypoxia significantly increased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve, indicating enhanced glycolytic activity in hypoxia-treated PASMCs. Given the established role of glycolysis in rapid ATP production during PASMC activation, total cellular ATP production was also investigated. As anticipated, total ATP production significantly increased in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. 1K), suggesting upregulated cellular energy metabolism. The Glut1-selective inhibitor BAY-876 was utilized to determine the contribution of glucose-derived ATP generation. Interestingly, BAY-876 significantly reduced ATP levels in normoxic PASMCs, whereas ATP levels in hypoxia-treated PASMCs exhibited only a modest decline following BAY-876 treatment (Fig. 1L). These findings indicate a non-energy-supplying role of glycolysis in hypoxia-induced PASMC proliferation, despite the notable enhancement of glycolytic activity. Considering the high efficiency of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for energy supply, potential utilization of fatty acids as an energy substrate by PASMCs was evaluated. FAO was further assessed through Seahorse analysis, which revealed a marked enhancement of basal mitochondrial respiration fueled by fatty acids under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 1M and O). Additionally, Western blot analysis demonstrated increased expression of key proteins involved in FAO and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. S1A–D). These results suggest that PASMCs upregulated FAO and the mitochondrial TCA cycle to meet energy demands associated with hypoxia-induced proliferation.

MAS inhibition failed to suppress but instead exacerbated hypoxia-induced PASMC activation

Glycolysis-derived reducing equivalents contribute to ATP production via two pathways: substrate-level phosphorylation and the MAS-mediated NADH shuttle, with the latter accounting for nearly 70% of glycolysis-derived ATP production in normal PASMCs [23,24] (Fig. 2A). We next wonder if this proportion would alter in hypoxia-treated PASMCs. Here, primary PASMCs isolated from the mouse pulmonary arteries for in-vitro experiments. The purity of isolated PASMCs was evaluated by flow cytometry. Isolated cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with antibodies against α-SMA. Non-PASMC contamination was assessed using CD31, CD45, and PDGFRα to identify endothelial cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts, respectively. As shown in Fig. S2A, the proportion of α-SMA positive cell were more than 95%, while the proportion of CD31, CD45, or PDGFRα positive cell were all below 5%, indicating a high purity of isolated PASMCs. Then, isolated PASMCs were then cultured under normoxic or hypoxic conditions for 48 h and washed with HBSS. Subsequently, cells were exposed to glucose as a single substrate in the presence or absence of aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA), a pharmacological inhibitor of MAS (Fig. 2B). AOAA treatment markedly reduced ATP production in normoxic PASMCs, while ATP production in hypoxia-treated PASMCs was only modestly affected by AOAA (Fig. 2C–D), suggesting a decreased contribution of the MAS-mediated NADH shuttle to energy supply in hypoxia-treated PASMCs. To investigate whether this alteration influences PASMC activation, the effects of AOAA treatment were assessed. As expected, AOAA treatment resulted in a significant accumulation of cytosolic NADH and an increased NADH/NAD+ ratio, indicating notable MAS inhibition (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, AOAA-induced MAS inhibition led to enhanced cell proliferation in both normoxic and hypoxia-treated PASMCs, as evidenced by increased EdU-positive cells and CCK8-detected cell viability (Fig. 2F–H). Correspondingly, quantitative PCR analysis showed upregulation of several major proliferation-related genes following AOAA treatment (Fig. 2K). Furthermore, AOAA treatment led to a significant reduction in pre-apoptotic indices and an elevation in anti-apoptotic indices. Specifically, AOAA treatment significantly decreased protein and mRNA levels of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, along with a reduction in the percentage of apoptotic cells, while increasing Bcl-2 levels (Fig. 2I and K). Although AOAA is commonly used to inhibit MAS activity, prior biochemical studies have indicated that AOAA also inhibits cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE), and acts as an ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) inhibitor. To directly assess whether inhibition of these off-target enzymes accounts for the observed PASMC phenotype, additional inhibitor and genetic experiments were conducted. CSE and OAT were individually inhibited using specific inhibitors and siRNA, respectively. In contrast to AOAA treatment, OAT knockdown did not significantly promote PASMC proliferation while inhibiting CSE induced a mild increase in PASMC proliferation, resulted in effects far below those observed with AOAA treatment (Fig. S3A–D). These findings indicate that MAS inhibition is not merely a concomitant process but serves as a central contributor to PASMC activation during the pathogenesis of PH.

Slc25a11 inhibition was observed in hypoxia-treated PASMCs and contributed to PASMC activation

Given the observed MAS inhibition in hypoxia-induced PASMC activation, specific molecular factors responsible for this alteration were explored. Key transporters and enzymes involved in the MAS process were examined in hypoxia-treated PASMCs. As shown in Fig. 3A, among these transporters and enzymes, Slc25a11 and Slc25a12, responsible for the transport of metabolic substrates across the mitochondrial membrane, were significantly decreased, with Slc25a11 exhibiting the most pronounced reduction. Concurrently, hypoxia treatment resulted in significant accumulation of cytosolic NADH (Fig. 3B). To validate these findings in an in vivo context, wild-type C57BL/6 mice were subjected to hypoxia in combination with subcutaneous SU5416 injection for 4 weeks, establishing a SuHx-induced PH model. Pulmonary angiography revealed a marked loss of distal pulmonary vessels in SuHx mice, as evidenced by reduced pulmonary vascular density, total vascular length, branch number, and junction number (Fig. 3C–F). Moreover, echocardiographic analyses indicated abnormal pulmonary artery flow and reduced tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion in SuHx mice (Fig. S4A–D), signifying increased pulmonary vascular resistance and impaired right ventricular function. Consistently, right ventricular systolic pressure and the ratio of right ventricular weight to left ventricle plus septum weight [RV/(LV + S)] showed significant elevation (Fig. S4E and F), confirming the successful establishment of experimental PH in these mice. Histological analysis further evaluated pulmonary vascular remodeling, with results from H&E staining, α-SMA immunostaining, and EVG staining confirming enhanced vascular wall remodeling and medial thickening (Fig. S4G–I). Additionally, TUNEL staining showed reduced apoptotic signals in remodeled pulmonary vessels (Fig. S5A). These results suggest significant pulmonary vascular remodeling and PASMC activation in the PH mice. Importantly, immunofluorescence co-staining revealed decreased Slc25a11 expression in the remodeled pulmonary vasculature of PH mice (Fig. 3G). We further analyzed publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets from patients with PH to determine the relevance of Slc25a11 in clinical PH progression. As shown in Fig. S6, Slc25a11 expression was significantly reduced in SMCs from patients with PH when compared with healthy controls (Fig. S6). These data suggested that Slc25a11 inhibition is implicated in in vivo PH progression. To further explore the role of Slc25a11 inhibition in the pathogenesis of PH, recombinant adenoviral vectors encoding Slc25a11 shRNA were transfected into PASMCs, with knockdown efficiency verified by Western blot analysis (Fig. 3H). Similar to pharmacological MAS inhibition by AOAA in vitro, Slc25a11 knockdown induced cytosolic NADH accumulation and increased the cytosolic NADH/NAD+ ratio (Fig. 3I). Moreover, Slc25a11 knockdown also impeded the shuttle of malate and aspartate between mitochondria and cytosol, as indicated by significantly increased cytosolic malate level while decreased mitochondrial aspartate levels (Fig. S7). These data suggested that Slc25a11 knockdown blocked MAS in PASMCs. Moreover, silencing Slc25a11 led to significant PASMC proliferation and reduced apoptosis, as evidenced by increased EdU incorporation, elevated CCK8-detected cell viability, and higher levels of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, alongside decreased levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 3J–M). RNA sequencing analysis was conducted to examine the effects of Slc25a11 inhibition on gene transcription in PASMCs. DEG analysis revealed numerous significantly altered genes following Slc25a11 knockdown (Fig. 3N), with multiple proliferation and cell cycle-related genes upregulated in Slc25a11-deficient PASMCs (Fig. 3O). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with DNA replication, vascular smooth muscle contraction, and cell cycle regulation, findings further confirmed by quantitative PCR analysis (Fig. 3P and Q). These results suggest that Slc25a11 inhibition is a major contributor to MAS inhibition and subsequent PASMC activation during the progression of PH.

Considering the critical role of metabolic changes in PASMC activation, the metabolic alterations in Slc25a11-deficient PASMCs were examined. Western blot analysis revealed that Slc25a11 knockdown increased the expression of key glycolysis-related proteins, including HK2, PFK1, PKM2, and LDHA (Fig. S8A). Seahorse extracellular flux analysis demonstrated significantly elevated ECAR, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve (Fig. S9A–C), indicating enhanced glycolytic activity in Slc25a11-deficient PASMCs. Concurrently, the levels of major proteins involved in FAO and the TCA cycle, along with FAO-related mitochondrial oxygen consumption, were also significantly increased in Slc25a11-deficient PASMCs (Fig. S11A–D and S12A). Notably, despite the decreased MAS-mediated NADH shuttle, total cellular ATP production levels increased following Slc25a11 knockdown (Fig. S10A). These findings suggest that Slc25a11 inhibition is sufficient to induce glycolytic activation and mitochondrial energy production, representing the metabolic reprogramming observed in hypoxia-treated PASMCs.

SMC-specific deletion of Slc25a11 induced vascular remodeling to promote PH progression

Given that Slc25a11 silencing induced PASMC activation and metabolic reprogramming in in vitro models, this study examined whether in vivo SMC-specific Slc25a11 knockdown directly contributes to pulmonary vascular remodeling. Inducible smooth muscle cell-specific Slc25a11 knockout mice (Slc25a11KO) were generated by crossing Slc25a11 flox/flox mice with Myh11-creERT2 transgenic mice (Fig. 4A). Cre expression was induced at 6 weeks of age using a tamoxifen diet. Successful deletion of Slc25a11 was confirmed by Blot analysis and immunofluorescence co-staining of α-SMA and Slc25a11 in Slc25a11flox/flox Myh11-creERT2 mice compared to littermate controls (Slc25a11flox/flox mice) (Figure S13A & Fig. 4B). Following successful induction, the mice were maintained under normoxic conditions with a chow diet for 10 weeks. Pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular function were then assessed. As depicted in Fig. 4C–E, SMC-specific Slc25a11 deletion led to significant pulmonary remodeling in normoxia-treated mice, characterized by marked thickening of pulmonary arterial walls, enhanced muscularization of distal pulmonary arteries, and evident vascular structural remodeling. Additionally, TUNEL staining showed significantly reduced apoptotic signals within the pulmonary vascular cells of Slc25a11KO mice (Fig. 4F), suggesting an apoptosis-resistant phenotype. Next, PH, cardiac function, and pulmonary hemodynamics were evaluated. Echocardiographic analysis indicated abnormal pulmonary artery flow patterns and significantly reduced tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion in Slc25a11KO mice (Fig. 4G–H). Correspondingly, right ventricular systolic pressure and the ratio of RV/(LV + S) were both significantly elevated in Slc25a11KO mice compared to control littermates (Fig. 4I–J). Moreover, pulmonary angiography demonstrated obvious distal pulmonary vascular rarefaction and reduced vascular branching (Fig. 4K–N). These data suggest that in vivo SMC Slc25a11 deletion induces significant PASMC activation, promoting vascular remodeling in normoxia-treated mice.

To further characterize the gene expression alterations induced by Slc25a11 deficiency in vivo, RNA sequencing analysis was performed using lung tissues from control and Slc25a11KO mice. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with cell cycle regulation, cell proliferation, vascular remodeling, and metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 4O–P). Restoration of MAS via SMC-specific Slc25a11 overexpression attenuates PH in mice

We next investigated whether restoration of Slc25a11 could rescue MAS activity to attenuate hypoxia-induced PASMC activation. Here, PASMCs were transfected with a recombinant adenovirus encoding Slc25a11 to induce Slc25a11 overexpression, followed by exposure to hypoxia for 48 h. Western blot analysis demonstrated that Ad-Slc25a11 effectively increased Slc25a11 expression in both normal and hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. S14A). As anticipated, overexpression of Slc25a11 significantly reduced cytosolic NADH accumulation in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, indicating a restoration of the MAS-mediated NADH shuttle (Fig. S14B). Furthermore, Ad-Slc25a11 markedly decreased EdU incorporation, CCK8-determined cell viability, and proliferation-related gene expression in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. S15A–C). Additionally, pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3 levels increased significantly, while the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 decreased in response to Slc25a11 overexpression (Fig. S16A). These findings suggest that Slc25a11-induced MAS restoration effectively prevented in vitro activation of PASMCs induced by hypoxia.

The potential therapeutic effects of Slc25a11 restoration on PH were then evaluated using conditional SMC-specific Slc25a11-overexpressing mice. SMC-specific Slc25a11 transgenic (Slc25a11TG) mice were generated by crossing Rosa26-CAG-Loxp-Stop-Loxp-Slc25a11 knock-in mice with Myh11-CreERT2 mice. Blot analysis of Slc25a11 in pulmonary artery tissue and immunofluorescence co-staining of α-SMA and Slc25a11 confirmed SMC-specific Slc25a11 overexpression (Figure S17A& Fig. 5A). Both Slc25a11TG mice and their control littermates were subjected to hypoxia combined with SU5416 treatment. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, hypoxia-induced Slc25a11 inhibition was significantly reversed in Slc25a11TG mice. Consequently, SMC-specific Slc25a11 overexpression attenuated PASMC activation and vascular remodeling in SuHx-treated mice, as evidenced by reduced vascular wall thickness, improved muscularization, and alleviated vascular structural remodeling (Fig. 5B–D). Furthermore, TUNEL staining indicated that the reduction of apoptotic signals in SuHx-treated pulmonary vessels was partially reversed by Slc25a11 overexpression (Fig. 5E). Further assessment of vascular remodeling showed that this improvement prevented the progression of PH. Parameters such as RVSP, RV/(LV + S) ratio, and pulmonary artery flow in SuHx-treated mice were restored in Slc25a11TG mice (Fig. 5F–I). Notably, pulmonary angiography results revealed that SMC-specific Slc25a11 overexpression reduced distal pulmonary vascular rarefaction (Fig. 5J–M). In summary, these observations indicate that SMC-specific Slc25a11 overexpression restores MAS function to inhibit PASMC activation and slow the progression of PH.

Slc25a11 inhibition activated the pentose phosphate pathway to escape oxidative stress damage and apoptosis

After establishing the critical role of Slc25a11 inhibition in PASMC proliferation and anti-apoptotic phenotype, further exploration of the underlying mechanisms was conducted. Hypoxia treatment has been reported to elevate superoxide production in PASMCs [25], which is closely associated with superoxide-dependent apoptosis. In this study, superoxide production was assessed in pulmonary artery segments of PH mice. As illustrated in Fig. 6A–C, pulmonary artery segments from PH mice exhibited a marked increase in superoxide production, evidenced by significantly elevated H2O2 production and enhanced DHE fluorescence density. Additionally, ROS levels were significantly increased in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, corroborating the rise in superoxide production (Fig. 6D). However, the final products of oxidative damage, including protein carbonylation and 4-Hydroxynonenal accumulation, did not show a significant increase in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, suggesting a potential mechanism to evade superoxide-induced damage (Fig. 6E and F). To investigate the role of Slc25a11 inhibition in this protective effect, Slc25a11-silenced PASMCs were treated with H2O2 to induce oxidative damage. As depicted in Fig. 6G–K, H2O2 resulted in significant 4-HNE accumulation and subsequent apoptosis in Ad-NC-transfected PASMCs, effects which were largely reversed in Slc25a11-silenced PASMCs, indicating that Slc25a11 inhibition conferred protection against peroxide-induced damage. Importantly, the activity of glutathione reductase, a key component of the cellular antioxidant system, was significantly increased due to Slc25a11 inhibition (Fig. 6L). The glutathione reductase system utilizes NADPH as an electron donor to reduce superoxide. A further increase in NADPH content was observed in PASMCs following Slc25a11 inhibition (Fig. 6M). In animal cells, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) is a critical source of antioxidants through its generation of NADPH. To assess whether Slc25a11 inhibition was involved in the redistribution of carbon flux and the activation of the PPP, 13C-glucose tracing was performed in PASMCs transfected with Ad-NC or Ad-sh-Slc25a11. The tracing strategy is outlined in Fig. 6N. Knockdown of Slc25a11 significantly enhanced 13C incorporation into glucose-6-phosphate and PPP-related intermediates (Fig. 6O–Q). Conversely, downstream glycolytic intermediates, such as F-6-P, G-3-P, and acetyl-CoA, showed only modest increases (Fig. 6R–T). Additionally, central carbon metabolome analysis using LC/MS indicated a significant elevation in overall metabolite content of the PPP, including G-6PD, following hypoxic exposure. KEGG classification of altered metabolites revealed an enrichment of pathways related to glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the PPP in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. S18A and B). Moreover, expression levels of G-6-PD and 6-PGD, key enzymes of the PPP, were significantly elevated following Slc25a11 silencing, further supporting the activation of the PPP (Fig. 6U). These data suggest that Slc25a11 inhibition shifts glucose-derived carbon flux toward the PPP to enhance NADPH generation and bolster the antioxidant system, thereby protecting PASMCs from oxidative stress-induced damage and apoptosis under hypoxic conditions.

Slc25a11 deficiency leads to nuclear α-KG deprivation, thereby triggering H3K4me3 methylation

Given the extensive transcriptional profile changes observed in hypoxia-treated and Slc25a11-silenced PASMCs, the potential role of Slc25a11 and MAS in modulating gene expression related to PASMC activation was investigated. Epigenetic modifications, particularly histone methylation, significantly impact various cellular processes, including proliferation and differentiation [26]. Of note, distinct from the well-established role of H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 in transcriptionally repressive, H3K4me3 is widely recognized as a chromatin mark enriched at the promoters and transcription start sites of actively transcribed genes [27]. Histone methylation markers were first assessed in hypoxia-treated PASMCs. Fig. 7A demonstrates that hypoxia elevated several histone methylation marks, including H3K4me2, H3K4me3, and H4K20me1, with H3K4me3 levels also increased by Slc25a11 silencing (Fig. 7B). To investigate whether changes in H3K4me3 were involved in PASMC activation, the genomic distribution of H3K4me3 was analyzed through ChIP-seq in Slc25a11-deficient PASMCs. As indicated in Fig. 7C, Slc25a11 knockdown led to genome-wide alterations in H3K4me3 enrichment. GO biological process analysis revealed that genes with heightened H3K4me3 peaks were enriched in pathways associated with angiogenesis, positive regulation of vasoconstriction, cell migration, the cell cycle, and cell differentiation (Fig. 7D). IGV visualization illustrated increased H3K4me3 occupancy at several proliferation-related genes, including Ccna2, Ccnd2, Ccne2, Cdk1, Cdk2, and Cdk4 (Fig. 7E). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed the increased H3K4me3 enrichment at these gene loci following Slc25a11 silencing (Fig. 7F). In addition, H3K4me3 ChIP-seq was conducted in hypoxia-treated PASMCs to assess whether hypoxia induced a similar remodeling pattern of H3K4me3-marked genes. Fig. S19A shows that hypoxia also induced genome-wide changes in H3K4me3 enrichment. GO enrichment analysis indicated that genes with increased H3K4me3 peaks were primarily associated with DNA binding, cell migration, cellular response to growth factor stimuli, cell differentiation, and cell population proliferation (Fig. S19B). The elevation of H3K4me3 markers on proliferation-related genes was corroborated through IGV visualization and ChIP-qPCR (Fig. S19C and D). These data suggest that Slc25a11 inhibition enhances the expression of proliferation-associated genes by promoting H3K4me3 modification in hypoxia-treated PASMCs.

Histone methylation is regulated by histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and histone demethylases (HDMs). The activity of histone demethylases was critically dependent on α-KG [28], an intermediate metabolite of the mitochondrial TCA cycle. Slc25a11 functions as a transmembrane transporter that facilitates the cross-mitochondrial exchange of malate and α-KG (Fig. 7G). Subcellular fractionation analysis revealed a decrease in α-KG levels in both cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions following Slc25a11 knockdown, with a more pronounced reduction in the nuclear fraction (Fig. 7H). Similar decreases in α-KG levels were also found in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, Similar reductions in cytoplasmic and nuclear α-ketoglutarate were also observed in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. 7I).These findings suggest that Slc25a11 deficiency may induce H3K4me3 activation through the depletion of nuclear α-KG. To further explore the role of α-KG-dependent histone demethylation in regulating the expression of proliferation-related genes in PASMCs, KDM5, a critical HDM, was knocked down using siRNA transfection in Slc25a11-overexpressing PASMCs. Slc25a11 overexpression restored nuclear α-KG content, thereby reducing H3K4me3 levels in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, an effect that was reversed by si-KDM5 transfection (Fig. 7J and Fig. S22A). ChIP-seq analysis of H3K4me3 further demonstrated a restored occupancy of H3K4me3 after KDM5 knockdown. GO biological process analysis indicated that genes with altered H3K4me3 peaks were enriched in pathways related to cell proliferation, angiogenesis, regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation, vascular-associated smooth muscle cell proliferation, and cell growth (Fig. 7K and L). Additional IGV visualization and ChIP-qPCR results confirmed that KDM5 knockdown mitigated the inhibitory effects of Slc25a11 overexpression on H3K4me3 occupancy at proliferation-related genes, including Ccna2, Ccne1, Cdk1, and Cdk4 (Fig. 7M–N and Fig. S21A). These data indicate that Slc25a11 deficiency impedes cross-mitochondrial α-KG transport, leading to nuclear α-KG depletion, which subsequently triggers H3K4me3 methylation and enhances the expression of proliferation-associated genes.

α-KG supplementation prevented PASMC hyperproliferation to attenuate PH

Given that nuclear α-KG deficiency is implicated in the H3K4me3 accumulation resulting from Slc25a11 deficiency, the potential of exogenous α-KG supplementation to reverse PASMC activation was investigated. In vitro α-KG supplementation significantly restored nuclear α-KG levels in Slc25a11-silenced and hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Figure S22A and Fig. 8A). Consequently, reductions in H3K4me3 accumulation and proliferation-related gene expression were observed (Fig. S21B and C and Fig. 8B and C). Functional assays demonstrated that α-KG supplementation significantly decreased EdU incorporation and CCK8-detected cell viability in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. 8D and E). RNA-sequencing analysis was subsequently performed to assess the effect of α-KG supplementation on the transcriptional profile of hypoxia-treated PASMCs. DEGs analysis, combined with GSEA, indicated that cell cycle- and proliferation-related gene signatures were negatively enriched following α-KG supplementation (Fig. 8F and G). These findings suggest that α-KG supplementation effectively inhibits Slc25a11 deficiency-induced epigenetic and transcriptional activation.

The therapeutic efficacy of α-KG was further evaluated in in vivo PH models. Mice underwent hypoxia combined with SU5416 treatment and received intraperitoneal injections of α-ketoglutarate at a dosage of 300 mg/kg for 4 weeks. As depicted in Fig. 8H–K, α-KG supplementation significantly attenuated PASMC activation and vascular remodeling in SuHx-treated mice, as indicated by reduced vascular wall thickness, improved muscularization, and alleviated vascular structural remodeling. Additionally, parameters such as RVSP, RV/(LV + S) ratio, and pulmonary artery flow in SuHx-treated mice were restored following α-KG supplementation (Fig. 8L–O). Pulmonary angiography results further demonstrated that α-KG supplementation mitigated distal pulmonary vascular rarefaction (Fig. 8P–S). These findings support the notion that α-KG supplementation may represent a feasible therapeutic strategy for PH by restoring nuclear α-KG levels and further inhibiting PASMC activation.

Discussion

In the present study, MAS inhibition was identified as a key contributor to PASMC activation and the subsequent progression of PH. Despite a significant increase in glycolytic activity, hypoxia-treated PASMCs exhibited a decreased reliance on ATP derived from the MAS-mediated NADH shuttle. Specifically, hypoxia induced a reduction in Slc25a11, thereby impeding cross-mitochondrial substrate transport and further disrupting MAS-dependent NADH shuttling. Through both in vivo and in vitro models, a direct cause-and-effect relationship between MAS inhibition and PASMC activation was established. SMC-specific ablation of Slc25a11 resulted in a PH-like phenotype, while restoration of MAS via SMC-specific Slc25a11 overexpression mitigated PH progression by preventing hypoxia-induced PASMC activation. Mechanistically, MAS inhibition uncoupled glycolysis-derived reducing equivalents from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and redirected glucose-derived carbon flux toward the PPP for the generation of ribose 5-phosphate and NADPH. Importantly, as a critical cross-mitochondrial transporter, Slc25a11 inhibition disrupted mitochondrial-to-cytosol substrate communication, leading to further depletion of nuclear α-KG, the cofactor for nuclear histone demethylases. The loss of nuclear α-KG impaired KDM5-dependent H3K4me3 demethylation, activating sustained cell-cycle transcriptional programs, while α-KG supplementation restored its nuclear abundance and suppressed PASMC hyperproliferation. These findings highlight MAS as a central process linking cellular metabolic state, glucose carbon rewiring, and epigenetic activation in pulmonary vascular remodeling. Accordingly, MAS inhibition may serve as a potential intervention target for PH treatment.

Enhanced glycolysis is widely regarded as a metabolic hallmark of PASMC activation in PH [29]. Moreover, the effect of hypoxia on proliferation and glycolysis in PASMC is also well documented, establishing a causal-and-effect relationship between glycolysis and PASMCs activation [30,31]. However, whether this glycolytic shift directly fuels proliferation or merely reflects a metabolic epiphenomenon remains unclear. The classical interpretation suggests that increased glycolysis supports rapid ATP generation and provides biosynthetic intermediates [32]. Besides substrate-level phosphorylation, glycolysis-derived cytosolic reducing equivalents enter mitochondria to facilitate ATP production via the MAS-mediated NADH shuttle [33]. In normally cultured PASMCs, the MAS-mediated NADH shuttle accounts for over 60% of ATP production. However, our data indicate that the MAS-dependent contribution of glucose to mitochondrial ATP production was markedly reduced in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, despite a significant boost in glycolysis. Moreover, inhibition of MAS-mediated ATP production exacerbated PASMC activation, suggesting that hypoxia-induced glycolytic activation does not signify an increased reliance on glycolysis-derived mitochondrial ATP. Consequently, enhanced glycolysis may promote PASMC activation via a non-energetic-dependent pathway. This study proposes that the proliferative advantage of increased glycolysis lies in redirecting glucose-derived carbons away from mitochondrial oxidation and toward alternative pathways such as the PPP. This perspective shifts the interpretation of glycolytic activation in PH from a bioenergetic adaptation to a carbon rerouting strategy that supports anabolic metabolism and redox homeostasis.

The role of fatty acid metabolism in PH remains contentious. Some studies have reported increased expression of FAO-related proteins or enhanced fatty acid utilization in pulmonary vascular cells [34], whereas others have indicated impaired mitochondrial FAO under hypoxic or pathological conditions [35]. In the present study, both increased FAO-related protein expression and fatty acid-stimulated oxygen consumption were observed in PASMCs. However, the presence of increased FAO signatures does not necessarily imply unrestricted FAO within hypoxic pulmonary vascular lesions. Given the high oxygen demand associated with FAO, an increase in FAO capacity should not be interpreted as evidence of unrestricted FAO flux in vivo. Instead, the enhanced fatty acid-responsive oxidative capacity serves as a compensatory adaptation that maintains ATP homeostasis when MAS-mediated utilization of glycolysis-derived reducing equivalents is compromised. Consistent with our findings, Akagi et al. compared human PH-PASMCs with non-PH-PASMCs under normoxic and hypoxic conditions, demonstrating that PH-PASMCs exhibited increased ECAR alongside relatively preserved or enhanced mitochondrial respiration and ATP production under hypoxia [30]. This suggests that PH-PASMCs undergo adaptive changes in their energy production strategy in response to hypoxic stress, with a greater contribution from mitochondrial respiration and FAO toward ATP production. Beyond serving as oxidative substrates, fatty acids can be incorporated into phospholipids and other complex lipids, supporting plasma membrane and intracellular membrane synthesis during cell cycle progression. In this regard, increased fatty acid uptake or utilization may offer both energetic and structural advantages: FAO contributes to ATP maintenance, while lipid incorporation into membrane phospholipids facilitates biomass accumulation and promotes proliferative growth [36,37]. Furthermore, lipid metabolic intermediates may also participate in redox adaptation and pro-survival signaling, enhancing resistance to apoptosis under hypoxic stress [38]. Thus, glycolytic and lipid metabolic remodeling should not be viewed in isolation; together, they support PASMC proliferation by coordinating energy adaptation, membrane biosynthesis, redox homeostasis, and stress resistance. This collaborative metabolic strategy may elucidate why targeting a single metabolic enzyme often yields only partial efficacy in addressing pulmonary vascular remodeling.

A key unresolved issue is whether MAS dysfunction is merely a downstream consequence of hypoxic metabolic remodeling or an active driver of PASMC activation. As the primary mechanism for transferring glycolysis-derived reducing equivalents into mitochondria, MAS links cytosolic redox balance to oxidative phosphorylation. Beyond its energetic role, MAS-mediated cross-mitochondrial substrate exchange integrates glycolytic activity with metabolite compartmentalization and signaling [39]. In the present study, Slc25a11 inhibition emerged as a major contributor to MAS suppression in PASMCs during the progression of PH. Notably, Slc25a11 knockdown recapitulated key features of hypoxia-induced PASMC activation, including NADH accumulation, glycolytic remodeling, activation of cell-cycle genes, enhanced proliferation, and reduced apoptosis. Unlike mutations in BMPR2 or the constitutive activation of HIF1α, which typically require additional stressors to fully manifest PH, SMC-specific deletion of Slc25a11 alone was sufficient to induce pulmonary vascular remodeling, hemodynamic deterioration, distal rarefaction, and right ventricular hypertrophy under normoxic conditions. Conversely, SMC-specific overexpression of Slc25a11 mitigated SuHx-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling and improved hemodynamic abnormalities. These gain- and loss-of-function data suggest that Slc25a11 suppression is not a secondary epiphenomenon; rather, it acts as a causal driver of PASMC-mediated vascular remodeling, positioning MAS dysfunction upstream of many previously described metabolic abnormalities associated with PH. In addition to its role as a redox shuttle, MAS dysfunction has been shown to profoundly affect cellular state and metabolic flexibility. In neuronal cells, MAS is tightly regulated by fluctuations in Ca2+ concentration. Low Ca2+ levels promote MAS activity, sustaining oxidative phosphorylation, while high Ca2+ levels suppress MAS, forcing cells to rely on anaerobic glycolysis, indicating that MAS serves as a central hub connecting extracellular signals to intracellular metabolic decisions [40]. Moreover, disruption of MAS components (GOT1/2) leads to NADH accumulation and reductive stress, impairing both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in red blood cell precursors [41]. These observations suggest that MAS functions as a metabolic rheostat that, when dysfunctional, triggers cell-type-specific responses—from energy failure in neurons to proliferative activation in cancer-like cells. Our findings extend this conceptual framework to pulmonary vascular remodeling. In normoxic PASMCs, MAS maintains redox balance and supports oxidative phosphorylation. However, under hypoxic stress, Slc25a11 suppression fosters a “permissive metabolic state” in which the cell compensates by activating the PPP and upregulating FAO. This metabolic rewiring not only sustains ATP production but also supplies biosynthetic precursors and enhances antioxidant capacity, actively promoting PASMC proliferation and apoptosis resistance. Thus, MAS dysfunction in PH is not merely a passive consequence of hypoxic stress but an active enabler of the PASMC hyperproliferative phenotype. This shift distinguishes the pulmonary vascular response from the MAS deficiencies noted in other tissues.

How transient metabolic stress is transformed into stable transcriptional activation remains a fundamental question in vascular remodeling. A central implication of our study is that Slc25a11 deficiency impacts not only bulk cellular metabolism but also compartment-specific metabolite availability. α-KG serves both as an intermediate in the TCA cycle and as a critical cofactor for α-KG-dependent dioxygenases, including HDMs [42,43]. Consequently, its subcellular distribution is vital for linking mitochondrial metabolism to chromatin regulation. Our subcellular fractionation analysis demonstrated that both hypoxia and Slc25a11 loss led to reductions in cytoplasmic and nuclear α-KG, with a particularly pronounced decrease in the nuclear fraction. This nuclear α-KG depletion was accompanied by an increase in H3K4me3 abundance and enhanced H3K4me3 occupancy at loci associated with proliferation-related genes. Consistent with this, Slc25a11 overexpression restored nuclear α-KG availability and reduced H3K4me3 enrichment, while KDM5 knockdown mitigated this protective effect. Thus, the pathogenic consequences of Slc25a11 loss extend beyond reduced MAS activity or altered overall α-KG metabolism; they involve a disruption of mitochondrial-to-nuclear metabolite communication that stabilizes a proliferative chromatin state. The role of α-KG as a nexus linking metabolism to epigenetic regulation has been documented in various biological contexts. For instance, a landmark study published in Nature demonstrated that inhibiting FAO in cardiomyocytes led to α-KG accumulation, activating the α-KG-dependent demethylase KDM5, which erased H3K4me3 modifications on genes associated with cardiac maturation and shifted cardiomyocytes into a less mature, proliferative state [44]. These studies establish the α-KG–KDM5–H3K4me3 axis as a conserved metabolic rheostat that connects nutrient availability, mitochondrial metabolism, and chromatin state. In the context of PH, α-KG deprivation preserves PASMCs in a proliferative state by maintaining H3K4me3 marks on cell cycle genes. Thus, the α-KG–KDM5–H3K4me3 axis functions as a bidirectional epigenetic rheostat capable of licensing or restraining proliferation depending on metabolic context and the repertoire of target genes involved.

In addition to the α-KG-dependent epigenetic mechanism, Slc25a11-dependent MAS dysfunction also reshapes glucose carbon allocation, especially by favoring the PPP. In proliferating cells, glucose is not solely oxidized for ATP production but can also be redirected into anabolic pathways that generate reducing equivalents and biosynthetic precursors. The PPP is particularly significant in this context, as it produces ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis and NADPH for antioxidant defense and reductive biosynthesis [45]. In pulmonary vascular remodeling, activated PASMCs must duplicate DNA, expand biomass, remodel membranes, and survive under hypoxic and oxidative stress [46]. Thus, increased PPP activity may confer a metabolic advantage by simultaneously supporting macromolecular synthesis and maintaining redox homeostasis. In the present study, a marked increase in superoxide production was observed in the pulmonary artery segments of PH mice and in hypoxia-treated PASMCs. Given the close association between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and FAO, the elevated superoxide levels in PH cells may be attributed to compensatory FAO activation. Previous studies have also reported significant increases in long-chain fatty acid oxidation and oxidative stress during PH progression [47]. Despite the substantial activation of glucose metabolism and glycolysis, hypoxia was found to increase glucose and glucose-6-phosphate accumulation, suggesting that glucose-derived carbon was not fully processed through glycolysis for mitochondrial ATP production. Furthermore, Slc25a11 deficiency enhanced the incorporation of glucose-derived carbon into PPP-related intermediates and increased NADPH levels. These findings indicate that Slc25a11-dependent MAS dysfunction redirects glucose metabolism toward PPP-dependent anabolic and antioxidant programs during PASMC activation. The increased availability of NADPH may help protect PASMCs from hypoxia-associated oxidative injury by supporting antioxidant systems [48] and providing ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis, which are essential for membrane biogenesis, DNA replication, and cell-cycle progression [49,50]. Notably, Pak et al. showed that acute hypoxia (1% O2 for 10 min) was associated with increased total cellular superoxide and mitochondrial ROS in PASMCs, whereas ROS levels decreased in PASMCs following chronic hypoxic exposure (10% O2 for 4 weeks). They also found that mitochondria-targeted antioxidant treatment attenuated acute hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction but did not prevent the development of chronic hypoxia-induced PH [51]. Our results indicate that, despite the increase in ROS, protein carbonylation and 4-HNE accumulation were not significantly elevated in hypoxia-treated PASMCs, suggesting an adaptive change in the anti-redox system in chronic hypoxia-treated PASMCs. These observations may partially explain the failure of mitochondria-targeted antioxidant treatments in preventing chronic hypoxia-induced PH. While direct crosstalk remains to be further explored, our data support a model where Slc25a11 deficiency induces acute metabolic adaptations to meet the high substrate demands required for proliferation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our genetic and pharmacological studies consistently support a pathogenic role for Slc25a11 deficiency, the upstream mechanisms responsible for hypoxia-induced Slc25a11 suppression remain unknown. Future studies should determine whether transcriptional repression, post-transcriptional regulation, mitochondrial stress, or hypoxia-responsive signaling pathways contribute to Slc25a11 downregulation. In addition, despite the decreased mRNA level of Slc25a11 has been observed in PASMCs from patients with PH, clinical relevance of Slc25a11 deficiency and PH phenotype remains to be validated.

In conclusion, this study defined Slc25a11-mediated MAS suppression as a key contributor to metabolic reprogramming and the phenotypic alteration of PASMCs during PH progression. Manipulating MAS activity or α-KG supplementation may represent promising therapeutic strategies for preventing chronic pulmonary vascular remodeling in patients with PH.
