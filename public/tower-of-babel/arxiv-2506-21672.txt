Quantum Transition Rates in Arbitrary Physical Processes
Adolfo del Campo, András Grabarits, Dmitrii E. Makarov, Seong-Ho Shinn

Abstract
We introduce a framework for computing time-dependent quantum transition rates (QTRs) that describe the pace of evolution of a quantum state from a given subspace to a target subspace. QTRs are expressed in terms of flux-flux correlators and are shown to obey two complementary quantum speed limits. Our framework readily accommodates the generalization of Hamiltonian dynamics to arbitrary open quantum evolution, including quantum measurements. We illustrate how QTRs can be controlled by counterdiabatic driving.



Quantum speed limits (QSLs) are widely used to estimate the minimum time for a process to unfold
Mandelstam and Tamm 1945
;
Margolus and Levitin 1998
;
Deffner and Campbell 2017
;
Gong and Hamazaki 2022
. More precisely, they determine the minimum time for the time-evolving state to travel a given distance in state space, measured from the initial state. However, QSLs do not account for the direction in state space. A given evolution may leave the initial subspace at a given rate, but may travel in the ‘wrong’ direction for achieving a given task. As such, QSLs are not suitable for determining the completion of a specific process. In fact, while they can be saturated for specific evolutions
Margolus and Levitin 1998
;
Giovannetti et al. 2003
;
Levitin and Toffoli 2009
;
Campaioli et al. 2018
;
Campaioli et al. 2019
, QSLs are often too conservative. This is apparent when estimating the timescale of physical processes, such as thermalization, where estimates based on QSLs can be off by orders of magnitude from the experimentally observed timescales
Gogolin and Eisert 2016
;
Gong and Hamazaki 2022
.

To avoid this limitation, it is necessary to identify the target subspace of the evolution for a specific process to be completed. Let us define a process as a dynamics in which the quantum state of the system moves from a given subspace
A
to a different target subspace
B
. This involves conditioning the evolution so that the initial quantum state is found in
A
and the final state is in
B
. This conditioning, lacking in the formulation of QSLs, is implicit in the definition of transition probabilities
Feynman and Hibbs 1965
, a fundamental object in nonequilibrium phenomena with broad applications from the foundations of physics
Muga et al. 2008
;
Muga et al. 2009
to chemistry, biophysics
Makarov 2015
, and quantum technologies.

The notion of a directed quantum evolution towards a target subspace is essential in chemical reaction rate theory
Yamamoto 1960
;
Levine 1999
;
McLafferty and Pechukas 1974
;
Miller 1974
;
Miller et al. 1983
;
Miller 1993
;
Miller et al. 2003
;
Hänggi et al. 1990
;
Lidar and Wang 1999
;
Schatz and Ratner 2002
.
A particularly relevant formulation is that of Yamamoto
Yamamoto 1960
and Miller
Miller 1974
;
Miller et al. 1983
;
Miller 1993
;
Miller et al. 2003
.
The focus in chemical reaction theory involves the dynamics along a reaction coordinate. For illustration, consider a collinear chemical reaction that admits a one-dimensional reaction coordinate
\hat{s}
with conjugate momentum
\hat{p}
. The Hamiltonian is then
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+\hat{V}(s)
, that of a particle moving in one dimension along
s
in the presence of a potential barrier
\hat{V}(s)
. The subspace associated with the projector
\hat{\Pi}_{A}=\Theta(-\hat{s})
is associated with the reactants, and
\hat{\Pi}_{B}=\Theta(\hat{s})
is associated with its products. The reaction rate is given by the expression

k=\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tr\left[\hat{J}e^{-(\beta/2-it/\hbar)\hat{H}}\Theta(\hat{s})e^{-(\beta/2+it/\hbar)\hat{H}}\right],

(1)

where
Z(\beta)
is the partition function of the reactants and the quantum flux takes the conventional expression
\hat{J}=\{\hat{p},\delta(\hat{s})\}/(2m)
.
In general, computing this expression is challenging
Lidar and Wang 1999
.
Yet, it can be recast in terms of an integral of the flux-flux correlator,

C_{JJ}(t)=\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}\tr\left[\hat{J}e^{-(\beta/2-it/\hbar)\hat{H}}\hat{J}e^{-(\beta/2+it/\hbar)\hat{H}}\right],

(2)

as

k=\int_{0}^{t}dtC_{JJ}(t).

(3)

This relation
Miller et al. 1983
, reminiscent of the linear response theory
Yamamoto 1960
, has spurred substantial progress in the calculation of chemical reaction rates
Thompson and Miller 1997
;
Chakraborty and Truhlar 2005
;
Venkataraman and Miller 2007
;
Bose and Makri 2017
.

Despite its success, we notice the following limitations inherent to such formulation: (i)
It relies on the long-time asymptotic and scattering theory. As such, it is unsuitable to capture the short and intermediate time dependence of the pace of the underlying physical process
Fonda et al. 1978
;
Beau et al. 2017
;
Bose and Makri 2017
;
Ness et al. 2021
, which is particularly relevant for quantum control
Rice and Zhao 2000
. (ii) It is restricted to Hamiltonian evolution. A generalization to arbitrary dynamics that includes dissipation and decoherence remains to be elaborated, despite related progress
Christie et al. 2024
. The current formulation is not suitable for accommodating, for example, non-Markovianity associated with memory effects
Rivas et al. 2014
and quantum measurements
Jordan and Siddiqi 2024
. (iii) It is designed for chemical reactions characterized by a well-defined reaction coordinate, in terms of which the subspaces of reactants and products are identified, with the condition
\hat{\Pi}_{A}+\hat{\Pi}_{B}=\mathbb{I}
.

In this work, we formulate a theory of quantum transition dynamics between two subspaces
A
and
B
of the Hilbert space of the system. Our formulation applies to arbitrary quantum evolution and readily accommodates the inclusion of quantum measurements. Furthermore, it enables us to identify fundamental quantum bounds on the time scale to complete the process. Such bounds provide a natural generalization of QSLs while conditioning the evolution towards a target subspace.

Quantum Transition Rates (QTRs).
—
Consider a quantum mechanical system described by a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with two subspaces of interest,
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, associated with projectors
\hat{\Pi}_{A}
and
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
, respectively.
We consider the general case in which the resolution of the identity reads
\sum_{X=A,B,C,\dots}\hat{\Pi}_{X}=\mathbb{I}
, with
\hat{\Pi}_{X}\hat{\Pi}_{Y}=\hat{\Pi}_{X}\delta_{XY}
.
Said differently,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
need not be the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
.
We focus on the transition rate from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, which we denote by
k_{A\rightarrow B}
.
To this end, we start with the classical definition of a rate

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}P(B,t|A),

(4)

where
P(B,t|A)=P(B,t|A,0)
is the probability of finding the system at time
t
in
\mathcal{H}_{B}
provided that it was at time
t=0
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}
.
We first consider unitary dynamics generated by a possibly time-dependent Hermitian Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{S}\left(t\right)
in the Schrödinger picture.
The corresponding time evolution operator from
t=0
to time
t
is denoted by

\hat{U}=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\hat{H}_{S}(\tau)/\hbar\right)
, where
\mathcal{T}
is the time ordering operator.
From now on, we will use the Heisenberg picture and write the associated Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}\left(t\right)\coloneqq\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{S}\left(t\right)\hat{U}
.
Consider the quantum circuit in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
QTRs refine QSLs by using the conditional probability
P(B,t|A,0)
. An initial state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
is found in the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{A}
by means of a projective measurement of
\hat{\Pi}_{A}
. Time evolution is described by a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}
and a second projective measurement, of
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
, determines the probability that the state reaches the target subspace
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.

Given an initial quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
, the quantum expression for
P(B,t|A)
reads

\displaystyle P(B,t|A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P_{A}}\tr[\hat{U}\hat{\Pi}_{A}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\Pi}_{A}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}_{B}],

(5)

where
P_{A}=\tr(\hat{\rho}_0\hat{\Pi}_A)
. Alternatively, we note that
P(B,t|A)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)]
, where we have defined the projection of the state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
into the subspace
A
as
\hat{\rho}_{A}=\hat{\Pi}_{A}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\Pi}_{A}/P_{A}
,
with
\tr\hat{\rho}_{A}=1
. Further, the projection operator in the Heisenberg picture is given by

\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)=\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{U}
.
It follows that

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\dot{\hat{\Pi}}_{B}(t)]=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{A}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t),\hat{H}(t)]\right),

(6)

where the second equality holds under unitary dynamics generated by a Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
. When the two projectors resolve the identity
\hat{\Pi}_{A}+\hat{\Pi}_{B}=\mathbb{I}
, it follows that

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=-\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\dot{\hat{\Pi}}_{A}(t)]=\frac{i}{\hbar}\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{A}[\hat{\Pi}_{A}(t),\hat{H}(t)]\right).

(7)

In chemical reaction rate theory, it is customary to consider a time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+\hat{V}(s)
and complementary projectors
\hat{\Pi}_{A}=\Theta(-\hat{s})
and
\hat{\Pi}_{B}=\Theta(\hat{s})
, whence it follows that

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{J}_{B}(t)]=\frac{1}{P_{A}}\tr[\hat{\Pi}_{A}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\Pi}_{A}\hat{J}(t)]
,
with the quantum flux operator
\hat{J}_{B}=\hat{J}=\frac{1}{2m}\{\hat{p},\delta(\hat{s})\}
.
Note that the symmetrized flux naturally arises as
\hat{J}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\Theta(\hat{s}),\hat{p}^{2}/2m+\hat{V}(s)]=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{\Pi}_{B},\hat{H}]
.
By analogy, it proves convenient to define an operator for the generalized flux into the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{B}
as

\hat{J}_{B}(t)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t),\hat{H}(t)]=:\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t),

(8)

where the last equality follows from the Heisenberg equation of motion.
As
\hat{H}(t)
and
\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)
are Hermitian,
\hat{J}_{B}(t)
represents a canonical observable with real eigenvalues.
In terms of it, the QTR is given by

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{J}_{B}(t)],

(9)

as the expectation value of the flux operator
\hat{J}_{B}(t)
on the initial quantum state within the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. We note that for a time-dependent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
,
\hat{J}_{B}(t)
generally inherits an intrinsic time dependence as

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{J}_{B}(t)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{J}_{B}(t),\hat{H}(t)]+\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}\left(t\right),\dot{\hat{H}}\left(t\right)]
.

QTRs from flux-flux correlators.
—
One advantage of chemical reaction theory is its ability to determine reaction rates using the flux-flux correlation function, offering both computational efficiency and physical insight
Makarov 2015
;
Miller et al. 1983
;
Schatz and Ratner 2002
. This result can be extended to QTRs.
Consider the rate of variation of
k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)
given by
\frac{d}{dt}k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\frac{d}{dt}\hat{J}_{B}(t)]
. Its explicit computation, upon integration, yields

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\tr[\hat{J}_{B}(t^{\prime})\hat{J}_{A,t}\hat{\rho}_{A}]

(10)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\tr\left[\hat{J}_{B}\left(t^{\prime}\right)\left[\left[\hat{H}\left(t^{\prime}\right),\hat{\rho}_{A}\right],\hat{\Pi}_{A}\right]\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\tr[\hat{J}_{B}(t^{\prime})\hat{\Pi}_{A}[\hat{H}(t^{\prime}),\hat{\rho}_{A}]\hat{\Pi}_{A}]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t^{\prime}),\dot{\hat{H}}(t^{\prime})]],

where

\hat{J}_{A,t}\coloneqq\left[\hat{\Pi}_{A},\hat{H}\left(t\right)\right]/i\hbar
.
When
\hat{\Pi}_{A}=\mathbb{I}-\hat{\Pi}_{B}
, it follows that

\hat{J}_{A,t}=-\hat{J}_{B,t}
.
Furthermore, when
[\hat{\rho}_{A},\hat{H}_{S}(t)]=0
, Eq. (
10
) reduces to

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=2\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{J}_{B}(t^{\prime})\hat{J}_{B,t}].

(11)

Thus, the QTR is obtained as the integral of the generalized flux-flux autocorrelation
C_{J_{B}J_{B}}(t)=2\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{J}_{B}(t^{\prime})\hat{J}_{B,t}]
, which is reminiscent of the known result in chemical reaction dynamics for time-independent Hamiltonians
Miller et al. 1983
.
The generalized flux-flux relation (
11
) relies on the fact that
the flux-flux correlator vanishes at
t=0
, i.e.,
C_{J_{B}J_{B}}(t)\left(0\right)=0
.

Similarly, for a time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S}(t)=\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}
, the last term in Eq. (
10
) trivially vanishes. At
t=0
,
\hat{J}_{A,t}\rightarrow\hat{J}_{A}\coloneqq\hat{J}_{A}(0)=\left[\hat{\Pi}_{A},\hat{H}\right]/(i\hbar)
and provided that
\left[\hat{\rho}_{A},\hat{H}\right]=0,\,\hat{\Pi}_{A}=\mathbb{I}-\hat{\Pi}_{B}
, the QTR simplifies to

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=2\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{J}_{B}(t^{\prime})\hat{J}_{B}].

(12)

Comparison and generalizations.—

Our approach offers several advantages over the standard formulation of the chemical reaction theory
Levine 1999
;
Miller 1974
;
Miller et al. 1983
;
Miller 1993
;
Miller et al. 2003
. First, it applies to arbitrary subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
of the Hilbert space of the system, without requiring them to be complementary or defined through projectors on a reaction coordinate. Thus, QTRs are applicable beyond the dynamics of chemical reactions and encompass systems with discrete and continuous variables and generic subspaces. By way of example, the computation of
P(B,t|A)
and QTR is provided for the Bixon-Jortner model
Freed and Jortner 1968
for different choices of
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
in
SM
.
Second, our formalism does not rely on scattering theory, and, as the descriptions of non-equilibrium chemical reaction rates
Makarov and Metiu 1997
;
Bose and Makri 2017
,
it applies to any finite time
t
, without involving infinite-time asymptotics.
As a result, QTRs are generally time-dependent.
In addition, our approach is grounded on the probabilistic interpretation of QTRs and quantum measurement theory. The von Neumann postulate states that the post-measurement state following a projective quantum measurement is given by
\hat{\rho}_{A}
, justifying the definition of the QTR in (
9
) without ad hoc regularizations.
In doing so, the formulation is closer in spirit to that of QSLs
Deffner and Campbell 2017
. In addition, it is not restricted to unitary evolution or a specific dissipation mechanism
Hänggi et al. 1990
and can be generalized to arbitrary quantum dynamics, including driving and control, dissipation and decoherence, and quantum measurements.

Consider a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}
that implements a CPTP map describing the evolution of any initial quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{0}
such that
\hat{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{E}_{t}(\hat{\rho}_{0})
.
We define the quantum flux into the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{B}
in terms of the adjoint quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}
as

\hat{J}_{B}\left(t\right)=\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{t}^{\dagger}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}]
,
whence it follows that the rate takes the form
k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{J}_{B}(t)]=\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{A}\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{t}^{\dagger}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}]\right)
.

As an example, let us combine unitary time evolution with projective measurements associated with a projector
\hat{\Pi}
. For measurements at regular time intervals
\delta t=t/n
, the evolution is governed by the dynamical map

U_{n}(t)=[\hat{\Pi}\hat{U}(t/n)\hat{\Pi}]^{n}
.
For a time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}
, it is known that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{n}(t)=\exp(-it \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}\hat{\Pi}/\hbar)

Facchi and Pascazio 2008
. It follows that when
\hat{\Pi}
is chosen as
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
, see
SM
, the QTR
k_{A\rightarrow B}
vanishes identically as a result of the quantum Zeno effect.

For finite time intervals
\delta t=t/n
, Taylor expanding
\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)=U(t,0)^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}_{B}(0)\hat{U}(t,0)
shows that
P(B,t|A)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}]t^{2}/\hbar^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
.
Thus, sequential projective measurement of
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
at intervals
\delta t
satisfying

\delta t\ll\hbar/\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}]^{1/2}
suppress the QTR, as detailed in
SM
.

This result generalizes to driven quantum systems.
Using the Dyson series for the time evolution operator generated by
\hat{H}(t)
,
one finds, neglecting
\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
corrections, that

\displaystyle P(B,t|A)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}dt_{2}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{H}(t_{1})\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}(t_{2})],\quad

(13)

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\left(\hat{H}(t_{1})\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}(t)+h.c.\right)].

This generalizes the condition on
\delta t
in the case of
\hat{H}(t)
to be smaller than the inverse of the square root of the term involving the double integral. Thus, projective measurements at intervals satisfying
P(B,\delta t|A)^{1/2}\ll 1
can be used to suppress QTRs.

Quantum Speed Limits to QTRs.
—
The QTR in Eq. (
9
) is given as the expectation value of the flux operator
\hat{J}_{B}
in the time-dependent state
\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)
. This makes it possible to bound the rate of change of the QTR as well as the QTR itself.
A Mandelstam-Tamm bound to the QTR can be obtained by applying the Robertson inequality for mixed states
Gong and Hamazaki 2022
,

\Delta_{\hat{\rho}}A\Delta_{\hat{\rho}}B\geq\frac{1}{2}\left|\left\langle[\hat{A},\hat{B}]\right\rangle_{\hat{\rho}}\right|
,
where the variance of the observable
\hat{O}
in the state
\hat{\rho}
reads
\Delta_{\hat{\rho}}^{2}O=\tr(\hat{\rho} \hat{O}^2)-\tr(\hat{\rho} \hat{O})^{2}
.
Using the definition of the QTR and the generalized flux in Eq. (
8
), it follows that

|k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)|\leq\frac{2}{\hbar}\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}H(t)\sqrt{\langle\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)\rangle_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}\left(1-\langle\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)\rangle_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}\right)},

(14)

where we have used the fact that the projector is idempotent, and thus

\Delta^{2}_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)=\tr[\hat{\rho}_A \hat{\Pi}_B(t)](1-\tr[\hat{\rho}_A \hat{\Pi}_B(t)])
.
As a result, the QTR is upper bounded in terms of energy fluctuations with respect to the conditioned state
\hat{\rho}_{A}
.

QSLs are generally formulated in the language of quantum information geometry
Amari and Nagaoka 2000
;
Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2017
. For a trace-class projector
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
, it is possible to introduce the target quantum state
\hat{\pi}_{B}=\hat{\Pi}_{B}/d_{B}
, with
d_{B}=\tr(\hat{\Pi}_B)
. Then one can use the Bures length
Uhlmann 1992
to set the distance between the time-evolving state
\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)=\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}
and the target state as

\mathcal{L}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t),\hat{\pi}_{B})=\cos^{-1}\sqrt{F(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t),\hat{\pi}_{B})},

(15)

defined in terms of the Uhlmann fidelity
F(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t),\hat{\pi}_{B})=\tr(\sqrt{\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_A(t)}\hat{\pi}_B\sqrt{\hat{\rho}_A(t)}})^{2}
. By employing the super fidelity bound
Chen et al. 2002
;
Miszczak et al. 2008
,
F(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t),\hat{\pi}_{B})\leq P(B,t|A)d^{-1}_{B}+M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)}M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}
, where
M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)}=\sqrt{1-\tr(\hat\rho^2_A(t))},\,M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}=\sqrt{1-\tr(\hat\pi^2_B)}
denote the mixedness of the density operators, the Bures length can be lower bounded as
\mathcal{L}(\tau)=\mathcal{L}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{B})\geq\cos^{-1}\sqrt{P(B,\tau|A)d^{-1}_{B}+M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)}M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}}
. As a result, the minimal time for the evolution to sweep through this length obeys the inequality

\displaystyle\tau\geq\frac{\hbar\cos^{-1}\sqrt{P(B,\tau|A)d^{-1}_{B}+M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)}M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}}}{\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}H(t)dt}.

(16)

Remarkably, if at least one of the density matrices,
\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)
or
\hat{\pi}_{B}
, is pure both the Bures length and the speed limit will depend only on the conditional probability,
\mathcal{L}(\tau)=\cos^{-1}\sqrt{P(B,\tau|A)d^{-1}_{B}}
.

A complementary QSL results from applying the Mandelstam-Tamm time-energy uncertainty relation to the rate of change of the QTR.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle\hbar\left|\dot{k}_{A\rightarrow B}(t)\right|\leq\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}J_{B}(t)\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}H(t)+\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}\dot{H}(t),

which holds for an arbitrary time-dependent Hamiltonian.
For completeness, we note that in the time-independent case, the rate of change of the QTR is solely upper-bounded by the product of the energy fluctuations times the flux fluctuations.
This equation can then be rewritten as a time-energy uncertainty relation
Mandelstam and Tamm 1945
. To this end, one can introduce the characteristic time scale in which the QTR varies by a factor comparable to
\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}J_{B}
, that is,

\tau_{\rm QTR}:=\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}J_{B}/\left|\dot{k}_{A\rightarrow B}\right|
,
whence it follows the Mandelstam-Tamm inequality.

\tau_{\rm QTR}\,\Delta_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}H\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}.

(18)

Example: QTRs under Counterdiabatic Driving.

Counterdiabatic driving (CD) is a quantum control technique that allows speeding up or slowing down a reference quantum evolution by means of an auxiliary counterdiabatic Hamiltonian
Demirplak and Rice 2003
;
Demirplak and Rice 2005
;
Demirplak and Rice 2008
;
Berry 2009
.
Thus, CD can be used to control, enhance, or suppress QTR between two subspaces. Consider a driven system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}(t)=\sum_{n}E_{n}(t)|n_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|
. When the system is prepared in an eigenstate
|n_{0}\rangle
, the adiabatic trajectory under slow driving reads
|\psi_{n}^{\rm ad}(t)\rangle=|n_{t}\rangle\exp[i\phi_{n}(t)]
, where
\phi_{n}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}E_{n}(t^{\prime})/\hbar+i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\langle n_{t^{\prime}}|d_{t^{\prime}}n_{t^{\prime}}\rangle
. CD guides the evolution along the adiabatic trajectory
|\psi_{n}^{\rm ad}(t)\rangle
without relying on slow driving. The corresponding time-evolution operator admits the representation
\hat{U}_{\rm CD}=\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}^{\rm ad}(t)\rangle\langle n_{0}|
, and is generated by the CD Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm CD}(t)=\hat{H}_{0}(t)+\hat{H}_{1}(t)
, where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}(t)=i\hbar\sum_{n}\left[|d_{t}n_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|-\langle n_{t}|d_{t}n_{t}\rangle|n_{t}\rangle\langle n_{t}|\right].

Under CD, the conditional probability reads

\displaystyle P(B,t|A)=\sum_{nm}\langle m_{0}|\hat{\rho}_{A}|n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{t}|\hat{\Pi}_{B}|m_{t}\rangle e^{i(\phi_{m}-\phi_{n})}.\quad

(19)

Given the time-dependent flux in Eq. (
8
), the time derivative of
P(B,t|A)
yields the QTR in the form

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\sum_{n}\langle m_{0}|\hat{\rho}_{A}|n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{0}|\hat{J}_{B}(t)|m_{0}\rangle,

(20)

which is consistent with the identity (
9
). As shown in
SM
, using the Robertson inequality

|k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)|\leq\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{n,m}\lvert\langle n_{0}|\hat{\rho}_{A}|m_{0}\rangle\rvert\Delta_{|m_{t}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\rm CD}\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}.

(21)

The energy dispersion of the CD Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm CD}
in a single eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{0}
greatly simplifies
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Funo et al. 2017
. In particular, consider the case of parametric driving, where
H_{0}(t)=H(\lambda_{t})
, the vector
\lambda_{t}=(\lambda^{1},\lambda^{2},\cdots)
specifies the time dependence of the Hamiltonian parameters and
|n_{t}\rangle=|n(\lambda_{t})\rangle
. Then,

\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\rm CD}^{2}=\langle n_{t}|\hat{H}_{1}^{2}|n_{t}\rangle
. In addition,
the last term can be written in terms of the real part of the quantum geometric tensor defined as
Provost and Vallee 1980
,

Q_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}=\langle\partial_{\mu}n|(1-\hat{P}_{n})|\partial_{\nu}n\rangle
,
associated with the
n
-th eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{0}
.
In terms of
g_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}(t)={\rm Re}[Q_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}]

Boixo et al. 2007
to upper bound the variance of the projector as
\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}^{2}_{B}(t)\leq 1/4
, it follows that the QTR satisfies the quantum geometric bound

|k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}\lvert\langle n_{0}|\hat{\rho}_{A}|m_{0}\rangle\rvert\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}^{(m)}(t)\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}}.

(22)

This result further simplifies to a single sum when
\left[\hat{\rho}_{A},\hat{H}_{0}(0)\right]=0

Under CD, the QRT is thus upper bounded by an intrinsic geometric quantity, the weighted average of the line elements associated with each eigenstate
|n_{t}\rangle
. Note that this is the case regardless of the definition of
\hat{\Pi}_{B}
.
The conditional probability can thus be upper bounded using the triangle inequality
P(B,t|A)\leq\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}|k_{A\rightarrow B}(t^{\prime})|
as

(23)

where we have identified the natural distance,

\mathcal{L}(|m_{0}\rangle,|m_{t}\rangle)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}^{(m)}(t^{\prime})\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}}=\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{t}}\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}^{(m)}\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\mu}\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\nu}}
,
between the
m

The geometric bounds for

and
k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)
are governed by the distance traveled by each eigenmode, reflecting the fact that CD generates parallel transport while suppressing transitions among different energy eigenstates.

Summary.
We have introduced a theoretical framework for the description of QTRs in arbitrary quantum processes. QTRs characterize the rate at which a quantum state moves from an initial subspace to a target subspace. As such, they provide an alternative to QSLs in identifying the characteristic timescales of physical processes. QTRs can also be understood as a generalization of chemical reaction rates, applicable to arbitrary subspaces and dynamical processes, without resorting to a scattering approach and focusing instead on finite-time evolution.
They can be formulated beyond Hamiltonian dynamics, encompassing both Markovian and non-Markovian processes, and incorporating the effects of quantum measurements as described by a quantum channel.
As such, we expect QTRs to find broad applications in nonequilibrium physics, ranging from the foundations of physics to quantum technologies.

By way of example, we consider the QTRs in the celebrated Bixon-Jortner model, describing the intramolecular radiationless transitions in an isolated molecule. Appendix C
Super fidelity bound and speed limit

The Uhlmann fidelity can be upper bounded using the super fidelity bound
Miszczak et al. 2008
,

\displaystyle F(\tau)=F(\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{B})=\tr(\sqrt{\sqrt{\hat\rho_A(\tau)}\hat\pi_B\sqrt{\rho_A(\tau)}})^{2}\leq\tr(\hat\rho_A(\tau)\hat\pi_B)+M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)}M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}=P(B,\tau|A)d^{-1}_{B}+M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)}M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}},

where
M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)}=\sqrt{1-\tr(\hat\rho^2_A(\tau))}
and
M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}=\sqrt{1-\tr(\hat\pi^2_B)}
denote the mixedness of the two density matrices of
\hat{\rho}_{A}
and
\hat{\pi}_{B}
, respectively.
We note that this bound cannot be derived from the Fuchs van de Graaf inequality
Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2017
and also provides a tighter bound than it.
Upon using it, the Mandelstam-Tamm bound naturally follows from the inequality between the Bures length and the quantum Fisher information
\mathcal{L}(\tau)=\cos^{-1}\left(\sqrt{F(\tau)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t\Delta_{\rho_{A}(t)}H(t)
,

\displaystyle\tau\geq\frac{\hbar\mathcal{L}(\tau)}{\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t\Delta_{\rho_{A}(t)}H(t)}\geq\frac{\hbar\cos^{-1}\left[\sqrt{P(B,\tau|A)d^{-1}_{B}+M_{\hat{\rho}_{A}(\tau)}M_{\hat{\pi}_{B}}}\right]}{\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t\Delta_{\rho_{A}(t)}H(t)}.

(44)

In deriving these results, we have invoked the notion of the maximally mixed state
\hat{\pi}_{B}
, which weights equally each rank-one projector spanning the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
Alternative constructions can be envisioned. For instance, whenever the projector associated with the target subspace admits the form
\hat{\Pi}_{B}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{B}}|j\rangle\langle j|
, one can consider the set of Bures lengths
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(t),|j\rangle\langle j)=\cos^{-1}\sqrt{\langle j|\hat{\rho}_{A}(t)|j\rangle}
and find the shortest distance minimizing over
j
. Such an approach is closer in spirit to, e.g., the formulation of the time of arrival problem and chemical reaction rates, in which
j
is generally considered as a continuous idex.

Appendix D
Quantum Zeno effect

The dynamical map resulting from unitary evolution combined with projective measurements at time intervals
\delta t=t/n
is written as
U_{n}(t)=\left(\hat{\Pi}\hat{U}(t/n)\hat{\Pi}\right)^{n}
with an arbitrary
\hat{\Pi}
projector. For intervals sufficiently smaller than any relevant timescale of the system, this becomes
U_{n}(t)\approx\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{n}=\exp\left[-it\hat{\Pi}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}/\hbar\right]

Facchi and Pascazio 2008
. The time-evolved projector, in turn, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)=\exp\left[-it\hat{\Pi}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}/\hbar\right]\hat{\Pi}_{B}\exp\left[it\hat{\Pi}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}/\hbar\right]=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[1-i\frac{t}{n}\hat{\Pi}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}/\hbar\right]^{n}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\left[1+i\frac{t}{n}\hat{\Pi}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}/\hbar\right]^{n}.

(45)

For
\hat{\Pi}=\hat{\Pi}_{B}
and using that
\left[\hat{\Pi}_{B},\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right]=0
, one finds

\displaystyle\left[1-i\frac{t}{n\hbar}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right]\hat{\Pi}_{B}\left[1+i\frac{t}{n\hbar}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right]=\hat{\Pi}_{B}+\frac{it}{n\hbar}\left[\hat{\Pi}_{B},\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right]+O(t^{2}/n^{2}),

(46)

where the second term identically vanishes.
Thus, the final result up to the leading-order correction is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\hat{\Pi}_{B}+O(t^{2}/n^{2})\right)^{n}=\hat{\Pi}_{B}.

(47)

where the leading-order correction is suppressed as
O(1/n)
.

Next, we show how to arrive at the leading-order expression for the transition probability in the time-independent case. Consider the short-time power series expansion of the time evolution operator.

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t)=\mathbb{I}-i\frac{t}{\hbar}\hat{H}-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\hat{H}^{2}+O(t^{3}).

(48)

Using it, the time-evolved projector reads

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)\simeq\hat{\Pi}_{B}+i\frac{t}{\hbar}[\hat{\Pi}_{B},\hat{H}]+\frac{t^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\left(\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{H}^{2},\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right\}\right)+O(t^{3}).

(49)

Taking the trace with
\hat{\rho}_{A}
, only the third term survives as the subspaces
A
and
B
are disjoint,
\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{\Pi}_{B}=0
. As a result, the leading order expansion of the transition probability reads

\displaystyle P(B,t|A)=\frac{t^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}]+\mathcal{O}(t^{3}).

(50)

Using this, the leading-order behavior of the QTR is given by

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}=\frac{2t}{\hbar^{2}}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}]+O(t^{2}).

(51)

For sufficiently small intervals
\delta t
, the transition rate can be written as

k_{A\rightarrow B}\approx P(\delta t)/\delta t
. The condition for negligible transition probabilities
\delta t\ll\hbar/\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\hat{H}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}]^{1/2}
implies that
k_{A\rightarrow B}(\delta t)\ll\delta t^{-1}
.

One can also generalize this conclusion to driven quantum systems.
Using the Dyson series for the time evolution operator generated by
\hat{H}(t)
,

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t,0)=\mathbb{I}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{i\hbar}\right)^{n}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\dots\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_{n}\hat{H}(t_{1})\hat{H}(t_{2})\cdots\hat{H}(t_{n}).

(52)

The time-evolved projector expanded as

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)\simeq\hat{\Pi}_{B}+\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t_{1}[\hat{\Pi}_{B},\hat{H}(t_{1})]+\frac{t^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\hat{H}(t_{1})\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{H}(t_{2})-\frac{t^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\left\{\hat{\Pi}_{B},\hat{H}(t_{1})\hat{H}(t_{2})\right\}.\quad

(53)

Taking the trace with
\hat{\rho}_{A}
, only the third term contributes to
P(B,t|A)
, leading to the result in the main text, Eq. (
13
).

In the time-dependent case, the rate of change of the QTR can be upper-bounded as

where in the last step the Robertson inequality has been applied for both terms.

Appendix F
QTR for Liouvillian dynamics

When the Liouville von Neumann equation reads

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}(t)=\lim_{dt\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{t+dt}(\hat{\rho}_{0})-\mathcal{E}_{t}(\hat{\rho}_{0})}{dt}=\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}(t)],

(55)

the quantum flux is given by

\hat{J}_{B}\left(t\right)=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)],

(56)

and the QTR equals

k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\tr\left(\hat{\rho}_{A}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)]\right).

(57)

Consider the decomposition of the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}(\circ)=[\hat{H},\circ]/(i\hbar)+\mathcal{D}(\circ)
, involving the Hamiltonian (possibly including a Lamb shift) and the dissipator
\mathcal{D}
. It follows that the quantum flux has the following contributions, one Hamiltonian and the second dissipative:
\hat{J}_{B}(t)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t),\hat{H}]+\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)]
. In the specific case of Markovian dynamics, the dissipator can be written in the Lindblad form and the adjoint Lindblad generator acting on an observable
O
reads
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{O}(t)]=i[\hat{H},\hat{O}(t)]/\hbar+\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}(\hat{L}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\left(t\right)\hat{L}_{n}-\{\hat{L}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{n},\hat{O}\left(t\right)\}/2)
.

Appendix G
QTR using the operator sum representation

Any quantum channel admits a (Kraus) operator-sum representation. The dynamics can be derived from the unitary evolution of the system coupled to an environment. Let
\{|e_{k}\rangle\}
denote a complete basis in the Hilbert space of the environment, and
\hat{U}_{SE}(t)
describe the global time-evolution operator. Choosing the initial state of the environment in a purified state
|e_{0}\rangle\langle e_{0}|
,
\hat{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho_{0})=\langle e_{k}|\hat{U}_{SE}(t)\hat{\rho}_{0}\otimes|e_{0}\rangle\langle e_{0}|\hat{U}_{SE}(t)^{\dagger}|e_{k}\rangle=\hat{M}_{k}(t)\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{M}_{k}(t)^{\dagger}
with
\hat{M}_{k}(t)=\langle e_{k}|\hat{U}_{SE}(t)|e_{0}\rangle
. The adjoint quantum channel reads
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\rho_{0})=\sum_{k}\hat{M}_{k}(t)^{\dagger}\rho_{0}\hat{M}_{k}(t)
and the QTR is given by

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=2{\rm Re}\sum_{k}\hat{M}_{k}\tr[\hat{\rho}_{A}\dot{M}_{k}(t)^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\hat{M}_{k}(t)].

(58)

Appendix H
QTRs under counterdiabatic driving: general case

In this section, we provide essential details for deriving the bound for the QTR under CD without the assumption of
\left[\rho_{A},H_{0}(0)\right]=0
. By inserting the identity in the trace,
\tr(\hat\rho_A\hat J_B(t))
, the general expression for the transition rate in the basis of
H_{0}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)=\sum_{n,m}\langle m_{0}\lvert\hat{\rho}_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{0}\lvert\hat{J}_{B}(t)\rvert m_{0}\rangle.

(59)

Applying now the triangular and Heisenberg inequality for an off-diagonal matrix element in energy basis,
\left\lvert\left\langle n_{0}\left\lvert\left[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t),\hat{J}_{B}(t)\right]\right\rvert m_{0}\right\rangle\right\rvert\leq\hbar\left[\Delta_{|n_{0}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)\Delta_{|m_{0}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}+\Delta_{|m_{0}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)\Delta_{|n_{0}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}(t)\right]
, one finds the upper bound for the absolute value of the transition rate,

\displaystyle\lvert k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\hbar\sum_{n,m}\lvert\langle m_{0}\lvert\rho_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle\rvert\left[\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}\Delta_{|m_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}+\Delta_{|m_{t}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right]

(61)

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum_{n,m}\lvert\langle m_{0}\lvert\rho_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle\rvert\left[\sqrt{g^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(t)\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}}\Delta_{|m_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}+\sqrt{g^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}(t)\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}}\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}_{B}\right],

where we switched back to the Schrödinger
picture and used again the relation,
\hbar^{2}\Delta_{|n_{t}\rangle}\hat{H}_{\rm CD}^{2}=g^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(t)\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}
.

As the absolute value of the density matrix element is symmetric by using the simple inequality of
\Delta_{|m_{t}\rangle}\hat{\Pi}^{2}_{B}=\langle m_{t}\lvert\hat{\Pi}_{B}\rvert m_{t}\rangle\left(1-\langle m_{t}\lvert\hat{\Pi}_{B}\rvert m_{t}\rangle\right)\leq 1/4
, as the curve
f(x)=x(1-x)\leq 1/4
for
x\in[0,1]
. Alternatively, one can use the semi-norm inequality
Boixo et al. 2007
along with the fact that the eigenvalues of the projector are
\{0,1\}
.
The bound then simplifies to

\displaystyle\lvert k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}\lvert\langle m_{0}\lvert\hat{\rho}_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle\rvert\sqrt{g^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(t)\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}},

(62)

which is a quantum geometric quantity. Specifically, for a monotonic driving of the control parameters
\lambda^{\mu}
, the QTR upper bound is independent of the specific modulation in time.
In addition, under the minimal assumption of
\left[\hat{\rho}_{A},\hat{H}_{0}(0)\right]=0
, the double sum simplifies to a single one, by orthogonality,
\langle m_{0}\lvert\hat{\rho}_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle=\delta_{nm}\langle n_{0}\lvert\hat{\rho}_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\lvert k_{A\rightarrow B}(t)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\lvert\langle n_{0}\lvert\hat{\rho}_{A}\rvert n_{0}\rangle\rvert\sqrt{g^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(t)\dot{\lambda}^{\mu}\dot{\lambda}^{\nu}}.

(63)
