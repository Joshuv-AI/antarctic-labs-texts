Factors Associated With Surgical Site Infections in Adults Following a Replacement of Weight‐Bearing Joints: A Systematic Review
Authors: Ut T Bui, Jane O'Brien, Kathryn Kynoch, Rosie Glynn, Christina Parker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSurgical site infection may occur within 90‐days following joint replacement surgery and, in patients undergoing weight‐bearing joint replacement, can complicate recovery and adversely affect quality of life. Although numerous studies have identified factors associated with surgical site infections, synthesised and comprehensive evidence specific to weight‐bearing arthroplasty remains limited. This systematic review determined the epidemiological associations between intrinsic and extrinsic factors and the development of surgical site infection in adults undergoing hip, knee and/or ankle arthroplasty, using the JBI methodology for systematic reviews of aetiology and risk, and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023482491). Studies were retrieved from CINAHL, Medline, Embase, Emcare, the Cochrane Library, and reference lists. English‐language observational studies reporting associations between one or more risk factors and surgical site infection following weight‐bearing joint replacement were eligible for inclusion. All included studies underwent methodological quality appraisal. Fifty‐seven studies met the inclusion criteria. The number of risk factors investigated varied across studies, ranging from a single factor to multiple factors, with multiple factors identified as being significantly associated with an increased risk of surgical site infections at the multivariable levels of analysis. This evidence indicates the need for comprehensive strategies to address these risk factors throughout the patient journey in arthroplasty.Keywords: arthroplasty, joint replacement, risk factors, surgical site infection, systematic review

Introduction

Joint replacement or arthroplasty is a common surgical procedure that aims to restore the function of a damaged joint and ease pain [1] by replacing the damaged bone and/or cartilage with prosthetic material [1]. When the cartilage at the end of the joint is damaged due to conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and osteoarthritis (OA), this can lead to patients' experiencing joint pain and disability [2]. Joint‐related pain is often managed with medical treatment [3], however, when the use of medical treatments is no longer effective, joint replacement or arthroplasty is recommended to restore the function of a damaged joint [3, 4]. The three main weight‐bearing joints (hips, knees and ankles) are responsible for primarily bearing the weight of the body while mobilising and often require replacement when damaged. Hip, knee and ankle arthroplasty are surgical procedures used to restore function of damaged weight‐bearing joints [5]. Depending on the level of damage of a joint, a total joint arthroplasty to replace a damaged joint with prothesis material may be required, such as total knee arthroplasty (TKA), total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total ankle arthroplasty. In some cases, partial joint arthroplasty may be required, such as partial hip arthroplasty (PHA) or hemiarthroplasty, partial knee arthroplasty and/or partial ankle replacement. Arthroplasty is classified as either primary arthroplasty or revision arthroplasty, such as primary total knee arthroplasty (pTKA) or primary total hip arthroplasty (pTHA), referring to the first time the procedure is performed in the relevant joint [1]. When issues arise with the prosthesis joint, such as infection, instability or loosening, a revision procedure may be performed, such as revision of total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) or revision of total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) [6]. Arthroplasties can deliver significant improvements to a patient's quality of life and functional outcomes [7, 8, 9], including improved standing balance, reduced fear of falling, reduced incidence of falls [10] and reduced pain [7, 8, 9]. However, there are several complications that can negatively affect recovery from these types of procedures, such as bleeding, surgical site infections (SSIs) and loosening of prosthetic parts [7].

Surgical site infection in arthroplasty is defined as an infection that occurs at site of surgery within 90 days following surgery [11, 12, 13]. There are three types of SSIs: superficial incisional SSIs, which occur within the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the incision; deep incisional SSIs, wheich involve the deep soft tissues of the incision; and organ or organ/space SSIs, which involve deeper anatomical structures beyond the fascial or muscle layers [11, 12]. Common signs and symptoms of SSIs include localised redness, pain, tenderness and warmth at the surgical site, and systemic symptoms, such as fever [11, 12]. Depending on the specific categories of SSI, some additional signs may also be obvious, such as the sign of discharge or pus from the surgical site and the surgical site reopening [11, 12].

The incidence of SSIs in joint replacement surgeries varies within the literature; reported rates vary between 1.1% and 7.0% in people with weight‐bearing joint replacements [14, 15, 16]. SSIs impact the healthcare system by prolonging patient hospital stays and increasing utilisation of healthcare resources [14, 17, 18]. For patients, the occurrence of SSIs results in the potential for complex reconstructive procedures and/or orthopaedic hardware failure/infections [14, 19], which can lead to further serious complications, such as prosthetic joint infection (PJI), amputation, sepsis and mortality [14] and significant economic burden [20]. It is predicted that the economic burden of SSIs in patients receiving arthroplasty will exceed 50% of the total resources available for inpatients by 2030 [18].

The risk for SSIs is multifactorial [21] and is influenced by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors [21]. Intrinsic factors are related to the hosts ability to defend against microorganisms, such as age, general health state, nutritional status, cognitive and functional impairment, and/or any comorbidities, the persons own microbiota, and their lifestyle behaviours [21]. Extrinsic factors can include environmental (i.e., prolonged hospital stay), type and/or level of complexity of a procedure (open or closed) and staff factors (i.e., hand hygiene, infection control precautions) [21]. These factors can relate to before (pre‐), during (intra‐) or after (post‐) or operative period [13, 22] and can be grouped as either pre‐operative, intra‐operative and/or postoperative risk factors [22, 23].

There have been some previously published systematic reviews that have identified risk factors associated with developing SSIs in patients undergoing joint replacement surgery. However, these reviews focused on identifying risk factors associated with SSIs in all types of surgeries [24], deep SSIs only [25], SSIs with deep infection following TKA [26], or in patients following hemiarthroplasty due to a femoral neck fracture [23, 27]. Other systematic reviews only investigated the relationship between one single factor and SSIs [28, 29, 30, 31, 32], or investigated risk assessment scores for SSIs or PJI [17]. We did not find any systematic reviews or registered protocols for systematic reviews that specifically focused on identifying all risk factors for SSIs in adults following a weight‐bearing joint replacement including arthroplasties of hips, knees and ankles. Understanding these risk factors could assist clinicians in preventing SSIs and further complications for patients undergoing these types of procedures. The systematic review addressed the following question:

What risk factors are associated with the development of surgical site infections in adult patients following weight‐bearing joint replacement surgery?

Methods

The systematic review was conducted following the JBI methodology for systematic reviews of aetiology and risk [33]. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023482491).

Selection Criteria

Participants

This systematic review included studies of adults (≥ 18 years of age) who had undergone at least one elective primary or revision weight‐bearing joint replacement procedure of either knees, ankles, or hips. Studies were excluded if they analysed data from participants who had undergone either a joint replacement procedure of either knees, hips, or ankles as a result of an accident or fracture.

Exposure of Interest

This review included studies examining associations between pre‐operative, intra‐operative, and post‐operative risk factors and the development of superficial and/or deep incisional SSIs in adults undergoing weight‐bearing joint procedures. Risk factors were defined and classified using the same conceptual framework described earlier in the manuscript [21] and were considered “attributes, characteristics, or exposures that increase the likelihood of developing a disease or health disorder” [34, para. 1]. Where reported, risk factors were identified as self‐reported, clinically observed, or derived from medical records or registry data, and information on timing, duration, intensity, and modifiability was extracted. Risk factors were further categorised as modifiable or non‐modifiable and as preoperative, intraoperative, or postoperative related factors, to support consistent interpretation and to highlight implications for clinical practice and future research.

Outcomes

The outcome of interest for this review was risk factors for incisional SSIs. SSIs in joint replacement are infections occurring at or near the surgical incision within 90 days (≤ 90 days) of procedures involving prosthetic material implantation as per the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines [35]. In this systematic review only studies that reported on SSIs that were superficial and/or deep incisional SSIs [12] were included. Superficial incisional SSIs were defined as SSIs involving only skin and subcutaneous tissue of the surgical incision, while deep incisional SSIs were defined as SSIs involving the deep soft tissue of the surgical incision [12]. Studies were excluded if the outcomes focused on organ/space SSIs, PJIs, SSIs diagnosed more than 90 days postoperatively, or if incisional SSIs were not reported separately.

Types of Studies

This systematic review included the following types of study designs: prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, and cross‐sectional studies [33].

Search Strategy

The search aimed to find published studies. The search followed a validated three‐step process developed in collaboration with a health librarian (RG). Stage one, an initial search of the literature with a basic search strategy of (MH “surgical wound infection”) OR ((“surgical site*” OR “surgical wound*” OR “acute wound*” OR “surgical incision*” OR post‐operati* OR “post operati*” OR postoperati* OR SSI*) AND infect*) AND ((“joint replace*” OR arthroplasty* OR “prosthetic joint” OR periprosthetic* OR “prosthetic implant” OR prosthesis OR hemiarthroplasty OR “total‐joint arthroplast*” OR TJA* OR TKA OR THA OR replace*) AND (knee* OR hip* OR ankle*)) OR (MH “arthroplasty, replacement, knee+”) OR (MH “arthroplasty, replacement, hip”) AND ((MH “Risk Factors+”) OR “risk factor*” OR predictor* OR “prognosis factor*” OR factor* OR determinant*). Stage two of the search strategy was to adapt to other databases, CINAHL (via EBSCOhost), Medline (via EBSCOhost), Embase, Emcare, Cochrane Library, and APA PsycINFO (via EBSCOhost). Grey literature sites were also searched to identify relevant papers. The search was reviewed by a second health librarian using the Peer Review for Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) Checklist to ensure accurate translation of the research question, appropriate use of language headings, and precise use of search operators [36]. The feedback was incorporated to achieve an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity while maintaining the reproducibility of the search. Searches were limited to peer‐reviewed papers and studies in humans that had been published by January 2017–30th June 2025. This timeframe was selected due to the large volume of literature on this topic and earlier published reviews. The third stage in the search strategy was to review all references in reference lists of the included articles. Full search strategies can be found in Table S1. Finally, the reference lists of included studies were scanned to locate any additional papers.

Study Screening and Selection

Following the searches, all identified records were uploaded to Covidence. Prior to formal screening, a pilot screening exercise was conducted on a subset of records to refine and ensure consistent application of the inclusion criteria among reviewers. Two independent reviewers (CP, UB) then screened titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria. Studies deemed potentially eligible underwent full‐text review, which was conducted independently by two reviewers (UB, JO) following a similar process. that met the inclusion criteria had full texts reviewed independently by two reviewers (removed for blinded review). Throughout all stages, discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (KK). Consistency between reviewers was further supported through ongoing discussion during the screening process [33].

Assessment of Methodological Quality

All reviewers were involved in assessing the methodological quality of included studies (UB, JOB, CP, JH, KK). A pilot test was conducted on three included studies by three reviewers (UB, CP, JOB), all of whom had participated in the screening process, to refine and standardise the data extraction form. Eligible studies were divided among reviewers and the standardised critical appraisal checklists from JBI for case control, cohort and analytical cross‐sectional studies were used [33]. Any disagreements that arose during data extraction or methodological quality assessment were resolved through discussion to achieve consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (KK) adjudicated. The same third reviewer was involved across all phases of the review. All studies were included in the review regardless of methodological quality.

Confounding factors were identified and controlled for in multivariate analyses in most included studies. The most adjusted confounders included patient age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, smoking status, and perioperative factors such as operative duration. These variables were typically controlled using multivariable logistic regression models to estimate adjusted associations between risk factors and SSI outcomes. For example, Chaudhry et al. [37] adjusted for age, diabetes, and smoking status using multivariable logistic regression, while Damonti et al. [38] additionally accounted for BMI and operative duration in their analysis. This consistent use of regression‐based adjustment across studies strengthens confidence in the reported associations.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Data were extracted from studies included in the review by two independent reviewers using the standardised JBI data extraction tool (UB, JO). An initial pilot test of the data extraction process was conducted by three reviewers on three included studies. The remaining included articles were then divided among and extracted by all reviewers. The data extracted from studies included name of author(s), year of publication, study title, journal, study aim, country where the study was conducted, study design, sample size, study setting, study duration and follow‐up; characteristics of participants (such as mean age, gender, demographics, social and medical background information); types of joint replacement and SSI rates. All risk factors were investigated to determine whether a statistically significant association existed between each factor and incisional SSIs. Data analysis methods, adjustments for confounding factors, effect size measures (e.g., Odds Ratio [OR], Risk Ratio [RR], or Hazard Ratio [HR]), significant outcomes (p‐values), and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were extracted from the included studies.

Data from included studies were narratively synthesised and reported, including tables and figures where possible to enhance data presentation. Data were synthesised narratively as meta‐analysis was not possible due to the heterogeneity of the included papers. Data from the included studies were synthesised narratively in accordance with the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) guidelines [39]. Meta‐analysis was not performed due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in study design, populations, definitions of surgical site infection, measurement and categorisation of risk factors, follow‐up periods, and effect measures reported, as well as variability in covariates included in multivariable models, which precluded meaningful statistical pooling. Studies were grouped by risk factor and summarised using tables and figures, with extraction of the direction of effect and statistical significance of associations reported in multivariable analyses where available. To assess the overall body of evidence for each risk factor, a structured vote‐counting approach based on the direction of effect was used, emphasising consistency of findings across studies rather than statistical significance alone, while considering study size and risk of bias in the narrative interpretation. Inconsistencies between studies were explored with reference to methodological and clinical differences, and the limitations of narrative synthesis without quantitative pooling are acknowledged.

Results

Study Inclusion

A total of 2857 records were identified from searches; after 940 duplicates were removed, 1898 records underwent title and abstract screening. The full text of 521 records was retrieved and assessed for eligibility against the inclusion criteria. Fifty‐seven records met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review (see Figure 1). Of the 464 studies excluded at full text review, including 54 that were excluded due to ineligible populations such as people with a fracture of a weight bearing joint or an emergency procedure, 356 were excluded due to an ineligible outcome (i.e., PJI), and 54 studies were ineligible due to various reasons, such as wrong study design or unclear methods (see Figure 1).

Search results and study selection and inclusion process [40].

Detailed characteristics of included studies are reported in Table 1. The majority of included studies were conducted in the United States (43 studies). Single studies were conducted in Bazil [51], China [2], France [58], Germany [46], Iceland [77], Serbia [15], Sweden [16], Spain [62], Switzerland [38], Taiwan [48], Turkey [70], and Canada [47]. Of these 57 included studies, there were 54 cohort studies, two case–control studies [67, 70], and one cross‐sectional study [65]. Thirty‐three studies reported data from adults with pTHA, 27 studies with pTKA, and two study with pTAA [56, 82]. Six studies included patients with rTHA and eight studies included patients with rTKA.

Methodological Quality

The results of the assessment of methodological quality of the cohort studies have been summarised in Table S2, case–control studies in Table S3 and the one cross‐sectional study are in Table S4. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was assessed as having moderate to high quality with moderate to low risk of bias. Most included studies measured the risk factor/s in valid and reliable ways and two groups (with and without investigated risk factors) were similar and recruited from the same population. Four studies did not clearly define the diagnosis of incisional SSIs [71, 73, 78, 82], however they either were presented separately from PJIs or organ/space SSIs [71, 78], or specifically focused on superficial wound infections [73, 82]. Confounding factors were identified and controlled for in multiple variate analyses in most included studies.

Incisional Surgical Site Infection Rates

All included studies followed the CDC guidelines in diagnosis and classifying SSIs [35], however, 25 included studies only assessed SSIs within 30 days after surgery and 32 studies assessed SSIs within 90 days after surgery. The incidence of SSIs varied between studies with reported rates between 1% and 10%. However, compared to pTKA and pTHA, people who received revision procedures had higher rates of SSIs overall (15.6% after rTKA vs. 21.0% after pTKA and 8.6% after rTHR vs. 2.1% for pTHR, respectively [52]). See Table 1 for further included study characteristics.

Factors Associated With SSIs in Patients Following a Weight‐Bearing Joint Replacement

Many factors investigated were grouped into preoperative related, intraoperative related and postoperative related risk factors.

Preoperative Related Risk Factors

A total of 37 preoperatively related factors were investigated and 27 preoperative factors found to be associated with the development of SSIs in adults receiving a primary weight‐bearing joint replacement. Fourteen preoperatively related factors were investigated and 12 preoperative factors found to be associated with the development of SSIs in adults receiving a revision weight‐bearing joint replacement. Details of these risk factors and the effects on SSIs are reported in Table 2. Factors, such as homelessness [72], older age [16, 38, 73], smoking [15, 65, 69, 75], male gender [37, 38, 46, 58, 66, 75], having a BMI > 30 [16, 47] or malnutrition [54], hyponatraemia [86], diabetes [51, 52, 58, 65, 89], cardiac illness [47, 52, 65], chronic renal disease [47] and anaemia [59], and mental health‐related diseases (depression [82] and psychosis [52]) were strong predictors for SSIs in multivariable analyses. Four studies found patients who were older had a greater risk of SSIs [16, 38, 69, 73]. Male patients were at higher risk of SSIs compared to female counterparts [38, 45, 46, 58, 66, 75], however, females with a BMI ≥ 40 were found to have a higher risk of SSIs at day 30 (aOR = 1.40, 95% CI = 1.10–1.79, p = 0.007) [37]. Compared to non‐smokers, patients who smoked within a year of surgery had a greater risk of SSI [15, 69, 75]. An increased risk for SSI was also found in patients who had a history of hospital stay ≥ 4 days (OR = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.35–1.87, p < 0.0001) [58], and of being hospitalised with complicated diabetic foot ulcers (OR = 2.72, 95% CI = 1.84–3.94, p < 0.001 in pTKA) and (OR = 2.40, 95% CI = 1.52–3.70, p < 0.001 in pTHA) [67]. Older patients aged ≥ 60 years old, who smoked cigarettes within 1 year prior to a TKA were 11.532 times more likely to have a superficial SSI (OR = 11.532, 95% CI = 1.95–55.85, p = 0.0054, n = 650) [2]. Patients with preoperative functional dependence had a greater risk of a SSI at 30 days (OR = 1.96, 95% CI = 1.27–3.01, p = 0.002) [50].

A total of 10 studies investigated any relationship between patient nutritional status and the risk of SSIs, using body mass index (BMI) [16, 47, 58, 65, 69, 75, 77], nutritional risk screening tools [54], and/or serum albumin [88]. These studies found a significant association between patient nutrition status and the development of SSIs. Comorbid conditions, including diabetes [2, 6, 52, 58, 65, 89, 90], depression [82], psychosis [52], paralysis [52], anaemia [59], a positive COVID‐19 diagnosis prior to surgery [63], and hidradenitis suppurativa [57], were also identified as risk factors for SSIs. Many other comorbid conditions also found to have a significant impact on the development of SSI, such as metastasis cancer [52, 58], cardiac illness [47, 52, 65], acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDs), peripheral vascular disease, bleeding disorder, chronic renal disease, and dyspnoea. Several medications were identified as risk factors for SSIs. These included blood transfusions [78], or opioid dependent [81, 91] which were all identified as predictors of SSIs. Two studies found peri‐operative transfusion [65] or perioperative transfusion of red blood cells [83] were associated with SSIs. Sax et al. [74] found patients who received intra‐hip injections within 3 months prior to having a pTHA increased the risk of an SSI within 90 days OR = 2.39 (95% CI = 1.75–3.26, p < 0.01).

The association between two pre‐operative classification scores (the American Society of Anaesthesiologists physical status classification (ASA score) [15, 16, 38, 75] and National Healthcare Safety Network risk index (NHSN) score) and SSI was also investigated. Four studies used ASA score [15, 16, 38, 75] and two studies used NHSN score (NHSN was calculated based on a total summarised score from ASA score, classification of wound contamination and operative time) [62]. The ASA score aimed to document the patient's overall health status prior to the surgical procedure [92]. Patients with a higher ASA score [15, 16, 38, 75] or NHSN score [62] were at a greater risk of SSIs.

Chronic use of corticosteroids was found to be a significant risk factor [2, 65, 69, 70, 71] however, the use of corticosteroid was not significantly associated with incisional SSIs in rTHA [84], and in TAA [56]. No difference in the incident rate of incisional SSIs between patients who were on long‐term anticoagulation therapy (LTAC) compared to those who were not [55] while LTAC was found increased odds of SSIs (OR = 3.7, 95% CI = 2.0–6.5, p = < 0.001), controlled for Charlson Comorbidity Index only [87].

In combined data of patients pTHA and pTKA, no significant differences in 90 days SSI between group used forced‐air warmers (FAWs) versus group used FAWs with high efficiency particulate air filters (FAW‐HEPA) (OR = 1.38, 95% CI = 0.79–32.47; p = 0.831) [49].

Intra‐Operative Related Factors

A total of six predictors during the intra‐operative period including blood transfusion, blood loss, prolonged operative time, not using an intra‐operative warming device, cement application during procedure and if procedure performed in the summer were significantly associated with SSI. Details of these risk factors are presented in Table 3. Operative time, referred to as the duration from the start of creating the skin incision until the incision was completely closed, was investigated in eight studies. Overall, all studies reported a prolonged operative time was a significant risk factor for developing SSIs [2, 38, 61, 65, 70, 80]. Li and colleagues found that patients who developed SSIs had a prolonged operative time compared to those who did not develop SSIs following the procedure operative time (x ± s, minutes) was 120.11 ± 28.86 versus 97.92 ± 26.88, respectively with p = 0.003 [2]. Overall, patients with a prolonged operative time were 1.014 times more likely to develop SSIs (OR = 1.014, 95% CI = 1.006–1.023, p = 0.0009) [2]. In the study by Held and colleagues in 2021, when grouping patients based on the operative time, the longer the operative duration the higher the proportion of patients developing SSIs with 0.79% of those who had a duration of < 90 min compared to 0.85% in group with 90–120 min and 1.50% in the group with a duration of > 120 min [61]. Patients who underwent a > 120‐min procedure were 1.76 times more likely to develop SSIs compared to those with a < 90 min procedure (OR = 1.76, 95% CI = 1.03–3.01, p = 0.038) [61]. Transfusion in those who experienced blood loss [2] were also predictors for SSIs. Two studies investigated the impact of seasons and environmental temperature with the development of SSI. The rates of SSIs after hip and knee arthroplasties increased when the procedures were performed in summertime where higher environmental temperatures had a higher risk of SSIs compared to procedures performed in winter [46] or in autumn [38]. In TKA, those received cemented TKA had a greater risk of SSIs compared to cementless TKA (OR = 1.96, 95% CI = 1.41–2.72, p < 0.001) [68]. Some other factors were investigated but not found to be significantly associated with SSIs, such as: history of CABG within 3 years [64], or immune thrombocytopenic purpura [43]. There was also no difference in 90‐days SSI rate in patients undergoing either elective pTHA or elective pTKA, regardless if they received or did not receive intra‐operative dexamethasone [60].

Discussion

This systematic review reported risk factors associated with developing an SSI for adult patients undergoing a weight‐bearing joint replacement procedure. The review provides a comprehensive synthesis of evidence across the full patient journey, from pre‐operative risk stratification and intra‐operative considerations to postoperative management, thereby clarifying how these factors may independently or cumulatively contribute to infection risk.

Surgical site infection is a serious complication with significant impacts on patients after weight‐bearing joint replacement surgery. Many risk‐factors were identified to be associated with developing SSIs including preoperative related risk factors, intraoperative risk factors and postoperative risk factors. A total of 27 preoperative‐related factors, five intraoperative related factors and three postoperative factors were identified to be predictors for incisional SSIs in adults following primary weight‐bearing joint replacement. Regarding revision weight‐bearing joint replacement, 11 preoperative factors were found to be increased the likelihood of incisional SSIs, while there was conflict evidence on how diabetes influence the likelihood of incisional SSIs. COPD was not found to be a predictor for incisional SSIs in those received revision procedure (as shown in Table 2). These findings confirmed the influence of multifactorial influences of both intrinsic and extrinsic factors in the development of SSI. factors [13, 21, 22, 23]. Many of these risk factors are modifiable factors, such as smoking [15, 65, 69, 75], having a BMI > 30 [16, 47] or malnutrition [54, 94], hyponatraemia [86], uncontrolled glucose levels if diabetes [51, 52, 58, 65, 89], anaemia [59], intra‐hip injections within 3 months prior to surgery [74], prolonged operative time [2, 38, 61, 65, 70].

The findings from the 57 studies included in this review are similar to other reports within the literature. A systematic review by Gillespie and colleagues [95] that aimed to estimate the worldwide incidence of SSI and identify associated factors in adult general surgical patients found that 11 out of 100 general surgical patients are likely to develop an infection 30 days after surgery which aligns with the incidence of SSI reported in this review. The authors also reported that the development of an SSI was closely associated with the length of surgery. This systematic review also identified length of surgery time as a risk factor for patients developing SSI post‐joint replacement [95]. Similarly, other reviews investigating risk factors across all types of surgeries [96] or in ankle fracture fixation surgery [97] reported that patient comorbidities were a major contributing factor to developing SSIs post‐surgery [96, 97]. post‐operative related factors, such as prolonged hospital stay [23]. Kim and Kim [29] in their systematic review and meta‐analysis also found that depression was a predictor for SSIs and PJI.

These results of this systematic review emphasised the need for comprehensive and systematic prevention strategies that target not only pre‐operative factors but also procedural and post‐operative factors. A multifaceted approach ensuring rigorous pre‐operative assessments of patients and tailored interventions from interdisciplinary healthcare professionals in all settings from outpatient clinics, in‐patient services to the community and general practice would minimise the risk of patients developing SSIs.

Limitations

This systematic review explored the association of risk factors for developing SSIs in patients undergoing joint‐replacement surgery. Several limitations were identified including few full‐text papers that were unable to be retrieved which may have impacted on the overall results of the study. In addition, due to the heterogeneity of the included papers, studies were unable to be combined in a meta‐analysis. In addition, the predominance of retrospective cohort and case–control designs is a significant limitation in terms of being able to identify the overall impact of each risk factor. However, these studies included have provided a broad view of overall factors that were significantly associated with SSIs in weight‐bearing joint replacement. However, a minority of studies relied on univariate analyses or did not clearly report adjustment for potential confounders. The absence of confounder control in these studies may have resulted in residual confounding, potentially inflating or attenuating the observed associations and should be considered when interpreting the overall findings of this review.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Overall, this review has identified several risk factors that are associated with developing a surgical site infection in adults following weight‐bearing joint replacement. These include specific risk factors related to preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative time. This evidence suggests that comprehensive strategies to address these risk factors should be considered throughout the patient's journey of receiving arthroplasty.

Recommendations for Practice

The review emphasised the practical implications for clinicians in practice. This included conducting a comprehensive assessment of risks for surgical site infection at the preoperative period, screening the risk for SSI, minimising hospital‐related factors intraoperatively and optimising patient management postoperatively, such as optimisation of glucose control for patients with diabetes during the operative period [76, 89, 90] or better management of depression [29]. This was to ensure appropriate managements to prevent surgical site infection.

Recommendations for Research

Further high‐quality studies are warranted to better understand the patient and other related risk factors associated with SSI for patients undergoing weight‐bearing joint replacement procedures. As the current review found most included studies seem to have been done in preoperative period and very few in intra and postoperative periods, more studies focused on these important times of perioperative period are recommended. Longitudinal studies that assess how modifying specific risk factors (e.g., preoperative nutritional interventions or smoking cessation) impact SSI rates. Additional research should focus on the use of identified risk‐factors to prevent incisional SSI in patients including the development and testing of risk‐assessment tools or predictive models or SSI risk calculators that incorporate identified risk factors.
