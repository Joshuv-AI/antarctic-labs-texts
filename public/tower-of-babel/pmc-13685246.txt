Impact of nasal septal deviation, age, and sex on anterior, middle, and posterior clinoid process pneumatization: a morphological computed tomography study
Authors: Rukiye Soyal, Betül Digilli Ayaş, Aynur Emine Çiçekcibaşı, Demet Aydoğdu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinoid process pneumatization is a critical anatomical variation that influences surgical exposure and complication risks in skull base approaches. This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the pneumatization patterns of the anterior (ACP), middle (MCP), and posterior clinoid processes (PCP) and to determine the effects of age, sex, and nasal septal deviation (NSD) parameters, specifically angle severity and laterality, on these aeration patterns.MethodsComputed tomography images of 250 adults (125 females, 125 males; aged 18–70 years) were retrospectively evaluated. Pneumatization patterns of the ACP and PCP were classified using a four-type grading system (Type 0–III). In addition, the presence of the MCP and its pneumatization status were evaluated. NSD was evaluated for laterality and angle severity (mild:<9°, moderate:9°–15°, severe:>15°). Statistical associations were assessed using chi-square tests and multivariate regression models.ResultsType 0 (no pneumatization) predominated across all processes (ACP:81.8%, MCP:82.4%, PCP:82.6%), while Type III (total pneumatization) was rare (ACP:2.0%, PCP:2.2%). While univariate analysis showed age-related associations across all regions, multivariate logistic regression identified an independent association between age and ACP pneumatization, with younger individuals (18–34 years) exhibiting lower degrees of aeration (p < 0.01). Male sex was independently associated with bilateral MCP pneumatization (OR:3.016, p < 0.001) and right PCP pneumatization (OR:2.234, p = 0.032). Severe NSD angle (> 15°) was strongly associated with bilateral PCP pneumatization (p < 0.001) and higher-grade ACP aeration (p < 0.01). Furthermore, MCP presence showed strong ipsilateral clustering with ACP and PCP pneumatization patterns (p < 0.001).ConclusionClinoid process pneumatization demonstrates distinct anatomical associations with NSD angle severity, age, and sex. A severe NSD angle (> 15°) showed the strongest independent association with higher-grade pneumatization, particularly in the ACP and PCP, whereas NSD laterality was not independently associated with pneumatization. These findings support the inclusion of NSD angle severity in the radiological assessment of patients undergoing preoperative skull base CT, especially when extensive clinoid pneumatization may influence surgical planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02610-x.Keywords: Anterior clinoid processes, Clinoidectomy, Middle clinoid processes, Nasal septal deviation, Pneumatization, Posterior clinoid processes, Skull base surgery, Sphenoid sinus

Introduction

Safe management of skull base lesions in neurosurgery relies on detailed anatomical understanding due to the close relationship with critical neurovascular structures [1]. In this context, the sella turcica— a saddle-shaped structure located on the superior surface of the sphenoid body that houses the hypophysial gland—represents a key anatomical region of surgical interest. The sella turcica is bounded anteriorly by the tuberculum sellae and posteriorly by the dorsum sellae, and is surrounded by three pairs of bony projections: the anterior clinoid processes (ACP) anteriorly, the middle clinoid processes (MCP) centrally, and the posterior clinoid processes (PCP) posteriorly [2–4].

A detailed understanding of the complex anatomy of the sellar region plays a critical role in preventing technical difficulties and complications during the surgical management of pathologies such as optic gliomas, meningiomas, and aneurysms [5]. In skull base surgery, obtaining adequate exposure for radical aneurysm neck clipping and gross-total tumor resection without manipulating the optic nerve or brain parenchyma frequently requires additional bone work, including removal of the ACP, MCP, and PCP, the optic strut, or the petrous bone [6]. In lesions involving the suprasellar and parasellar regions as well as the cavernous sinus, resection of the ACP (anterior clinoidectomy) constitutes a fundamental step to expand the surgical corridor and achieve optimal control of adjacent neurovascular structures [5–10]. Conversely, for lesions located in the retrosellar region and the interpeduncular cistern, surgical removal of the PCP (posterior clinoidectomy), which otherwise forms a rigid anatomical barrier, is pivotal in enabling safe and sufficient surgical access [3, 4, 11].

Current publications have generally focused on pneumatization of the ACP or PCP [3–9, 11, 12], whereas evidence regarding MCP pneumatization (MCPP) remains limited and is largely derived from small case series [13]. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively evaluate and compare the pneumatization patterns and degrees of all three clinoid processes (ACP, MCP, and PCP) within the same cohort. In addition to detailing pneumatization rates and type distributions, potential associations with demographic variables, including age and sex, were investigated. Moreover, given that nasal septal deviation (NSD) may reflect broader aeration dynamics of the skull base rather than a purely localized phenomenon, NSD was classified according to deviation angle severity and laterality, and its relationship with clinoid pneumatization patterns was examined. By integrating these anatomical and demographic parameters, this study sought to provide a more comprehensive anatomical characterization of clinoid process pneumatization and its associations with demographic variables and NSD, thereby contributing to preoperative CT assessment of the sellar and parasellar regions.

Methods

Ethical approval

For the retrospective study, approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine (No 2025/6033). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study sample

Computed tomography (CT) images of 250 patients recruited via a balanced stratified sampling design (125 females and 125 males) aged 18–70 years who were referred to the Department of Radiology between 2019 and 2024 for non-rhinogenic complaints that were not expected to affect skull base pneumatization or sinonasal anatomy were included in the study. Patients with loss of bony landmarks due to extensive pathologies such as tumors in the sellar region; scans demonstrating trauma, deformity, or congenital abnormalities that could affect the regional anatomy or structural integrity; and patients whose CT images were not evaluable due to imaging artifacts were excluded from the study. Bilateral CT datasets were obtained for all participants, allowing evaluation of 500 structures each for the ACP, MCP, and PCP. To ensure internal consistency, all radiological assessments were performed by a single investigator. Additionally, participants were stratified into three age cohorts: young adulthood (18–34 years), middle adulthood (35–49 years), and older adulthood (50–70 years). These categories were established to evaluate potential age-related morphological variations following the completion of paranasal sinus and skull base pneumatization, which typically stabilizes by late adolescence or early adulthood. Furthermore, this specific stratification ensured a well-balanced distribution of sample sizes across all subgroups, thereby maintaining adequate statistical power for reliable comparative analyses.

Imaging procedure

All CT examinations were performed using a 256-slice multidetector CT scanner (Somatom Drive; Siemens Healthineers, Germany). The acquisition parameters included a slice thickness of 0.625 mm, a field of view of 220 mm, tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 74 mA (60 mAs), and a gantry rotation time of 0.28 s. The images were stored in DICOM format and subsequently imported into the open-source software 3D-Slicer, where multiplanar reconstructions were generated to visualize the datasets in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes. For all morphological and architectural evaluations within the software, high-resolution bone window settings were strictly applied with a standardized window width (WW) of 3500 HU and a window level (WL) of 500 HU to ensure optimal contrast, structural visibility, and absolute reproducibility of the fine osseous borders.

MCPE and MCPP were evaluated according to the methodology described by Miller et al. [13]. For each clinoid process, the CT slice providing the clearest visualization of the anatomical boundaries and the maximum extent of pneumatization was selected for evaluation in the corresponding imaging planes.

Nasal septal deviation classification with side and angle-based severity

For NSD assessment, the coronal CT slice providing the best visualization of the osteomeatal complex was selected. The absence (Fig. 4A) or presence of NSD, as well as its laterality (right (Fig. 4B) / left (Fig. 4C)), was recorded. A vertical reference line was then drawn from the crista galli to the anterior nasal spine. Subsequently, a second line was drawn from the crista galli to the most prominent point of the deviated septum. The angle between these two lines was measured as the NSD angle (NSDangle). According to the method described by Kapusuz Gencer et al. [14], NSDangle was classified as mild (< 9°) (Fig. 4D), moderate (9°–15°) (Fig. 4E), and severe (> 15°) (Fig. 4F).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v27.0. To minimize the risk of false-positive findings and Type I error inflation, a strict analytical hierarchy was established based on predefined outcomes. The primary outcome was defined as the prevalence, grading, and side-specific multivariable modeling of ACPP, MCPP, and PCPP variations in relation to NSDangle severity and laterality. The secondary outcomes included the assessment of baseline demographic variations (age and sex), their interaction profiles, and the regional clustering of MCPE with surrounding skull base aeration patterns. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Initial baseline associations between demographic factors, NSD characteristics, and clinoid process pneumatization patterns were assessed using the Chi-square test. To evaluate the magnitude of these categorical associations, Cramer’s V was calculated as the effect size measure, where values of approximately 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 were interpreted as indicating small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. To account for the paired nature of the bilateral structures and avoid patient-level clustering bias, the 500 sides were not pooled into a single database. Instead, multivariable regression models were constructed completely separately for the right and left sides (parallel independent models) based strictly on the patient-level baseline cohort (n = 250). Multivariate logistic regression served as the principal inferential framework to evaluate the independent effects of NSDangle severity, sex, age groups, and NSDside. Since these adjusted models simultaneously account for all covariates within a unified mathematical structure, separate post-hoc alpha corrections were not required. Ordinal logistic regression was applied to regions where the proportional odds assumption (test of parallel lines) was strictly met (RACPP, LACPP, and LPCPP) to preserve and analyze the full gradient of pneumatization grades (0–3). Conversely, for other regions (RMCPP, LMCPP, and RPCPP), this parallel lines assumption was mathematically violated due to skewed distributions and low cell frequencies in specific extreme categories; therefore, to ensure model stability and mathematical validity, these parameters were collapsed into a binomial layout and analyzed via binary logistic regression (Presence vs. Absence). Results were reported as regression coefficients (B) or Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To test intra-observer reliability, a randomly selected portion of the sample (n = 50) was re-evaluated under blinded conditions by the same investigator after a 2-week interval. Due to the categorical nature of the parameters, consistency was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistics. The overall kappa coefficient was calculated as 0.940 (95% CI: 0.820–1.000, p < 0.001), demonstrating an almost perfect intra-observer reliability. Finally, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) to verify sample size adequacy. Based on a medium effect size (Cohen’s w = 0.3), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and 2 degrees of freedom, the statistical power (1-β) of the study was calculated as 0.992, confirming that the sample size (n = 250) provides excellent statistical power.

Results

In this retrospective study, patients’ age ranged from 18 to 70 years (mean age 41.27 ± 13.30 years). As for the ages of females and males, the means were 38.97 ± 13.75 and 43.58 ± 12.47 years, respectively.

Overall distribution of MCPE, clinoid process pneumatization patterns, and NSD characteristics

The overall prevalence of the MCP was 43.2% (216/500) across all investigated sides, demonstrating an equal distribution between the right (n = 108) and left (n = 108) sides. MCPP was absent in 82.4% (412/500) of cases and present in 17.6% (88/500). Crucially, when evaluating the severity of pneumatization strictly among the anatomically existing MCP (n = 216), MCPP was identified in 45.4% (49/108) of the right sides and 36.1% (39/108) of the left sides. The pneumatization rates of the ACP, and PCP were as follows: Type 0 in 81.8% (409/500), 82.6% (413/500); Type I in 10.8% (54/500), 10.8% (54/500); Type II in 5.4% (27/500), 4.4% (22/500); Type III in 2% (10/500), 2.2% (11/500), respectively.

Among the 250 participants, NSD was absent in 49 (19.6%) individuals, while right- and left-sided NSD were identified in 101 (40.4%) and 100 (40.0%) individuals, respectively. According to the predefined NSDangle severity classification, 60 (24.0%) participants were classified as having mild (< 9°), 69 (27.6%) moderate (9°–15°), and 72 (28.8%) severe (> 15°) NSD (Table 1).

Sex- and age-group comparisons of MCPE, clinoid process pneumatization patterns, and NSD characteristics

Sex-based comparisons showed no significant differences in ACPP, MCPP, and PCPP patterns on either side (p > 0.05), with small effect sizes. NSDside was not significantly associated with sex, although it approached significance (χ²=5.839, p = 0.054, Cramer’s V = 0.153), whereas NSDangle severity differed significantly between females and males (χ²=9.497, p = 0.023, Cramer’s V = 0.195) (Table 1).

In univariate analysis, ACPP, MCPP, and PCPP patterns showed significant variation across age groups on both the right and left sides (all p < 0.05). In contrast, neither NSDside nor NSDangle severity differed significantly across age groups (p = 0.367 and p = 0.287, respectively) (Table 2; Fig. 5).

Multivariate analysis of factors influencing clinoid process pneumatization

An independent samples t-test demonstrated that the baseline age difference between female and male patients was statistically significant (p = 0.006). To control for this baseline discrepancy and rule out potential confounding, multivariable models were used, and interaction analyses (Age x Sex) were specifically evaluated; no statistically significant interaction effects were found on any pneumatization parameters (p > 0.05). Multivariate ordinal logistic regression models, adjusted for age, sex, and NSDside, demonstrated that NSDangle severity showed the strongest independent association with higher-grade ACPP on both sides (RACPP: p = 0.002; LACPP: p < 0.001). Severe deviation (> 15°) significantly increased the probability of higher-grade pneumatization (RACPP: p = 0.011; LACPP: p < 0.001), whereas the NSDside showed no statistically significant impact on either side (p > 0.05). Additionally, younger age groups (18–34 years) were associated with significantly lower pneumatization degrees (RACPP: p = 0.005; LACPP: p = 0.001) (Fig. 8).

In the MCPP, even moderate deviation (9°–15°) significantly influenced the presence of pneumatization independently of other factors (RMCPP: p = 0.016; LMCPP: p = 0.020). Binary logistic regression models validated these findings, revealing that male sex is associated with a 3-fold higher risk of MCPP on both sides (OR = 3.016, p < 0.001). Neither NSDside nor age group showed a significant relationship with MCPP on either side (Fig. 8).

For the PCPP, the impact of NSDangle severity remained significant primarily for the severe (> 15°) category (RPCPP: p < 0.001; LPCPP: p < 0.001). Binary logistic regression analysis demonstrated an independent association between male sex and RPCPP (OR = 2.234, p = 0.032), whereas neither NSDside nor age group showed a statistically significant effect (p > 0.05) (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive, multi-regional analysis of clinoid process pneumatization, offering a novel perspective on the interplay between sinonasal anatomy and skull base morphology. Although the risks associated with pneumatized clinoids—such as neurovascular injury and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage—are well-documented, previous investigations have largely focused on isolated anatomical regions [3–9, 11–13]. By analyzing the ACP, MCP, and PCP within a single cohort and integrating NSD metrics, this research moves beyond simple prevalence reporting to provide a comprehensive anatomical framework for understanding skull base aeration. Evidence suggests that clinoid pneumatization is not a stochastic occurrence but a multifactorial phenomenon, in which NSDangle severity shows a strong independent association with extensive aeration. These findings suggest that clinoid process pneumatization represents a multifactorial anatomical characteristic and highlight the value of comprehensive anatomical assessment during preoperative CT evaluation for neurosurgical planning.

Anterior clinoidectomy and ACPP

Because of its close relationship with critical neurovascular structures, including the optic nerve, oculomotor nerve, trochlear nerve, and ophthalmic artery, surgical manipulation of the ACP inherently carries a risk of iatrogenic injury. Extension of sphenoid sinus aeration into the ACP through the optic strut may create a hollow structure with a thin cortical bony shell, resulting in the optic nerve being separated from the aerated cavity by only a delicate osseous lamella [5–10, 12]. Çetinkaya et al. [12] reported ACPP in 28% of cases and found that this variation was frequently associated with thinning or indentation of the optic canal wall (23%).

In addition to neurovascular vulnerability, opening a pneumatized ACP during clinoidectomy may create a communication with the sphenoid sinus, increasing the risk of postoperative rhinorrhea. Accordingly, extradural anterior clinoidectomy is generally considered the preferred approach when ACPP is present because it enables controlled management of exposed air cells before dural opening [6, 15–17]. Similarly, during endoscopic endonasal surgery, a pneumatized ACP may form the lateral opticocarotid recess, where the optic nerve may be covered by only a thin bony shell or may even be dehiscent, highlighting the importance of careful preoperative CT evaluation [6, 18].

Regarding the prevalence of ACPP, the literature reports that Type 0 is the most common finding (70.5%-91.2%), followed by Type I (3.3%-14.0%), Type II (2%-12%), and Type III (0%-5.26%), respectively [5–9, 12] (Table 5). In the present study, right-sided ACPP distributions were 81.2% Type 0, 10.8% Type I, 5.2% Type II, and 2.8% Type III; left-sided distributions were 82.4% Type 0, 10.8% Type I, 5.6% Type II, and 1.2% Type III (Table 5). Moreover, the findings of the present study, together with previous reports, suggest that ACPP does not demonstrate a clear predisposition in terms of sex or laterality [5–9, 12].

Middle clinoidectomy and MCPP

MCPP is clinically relevant in sellar and parasellar surgery because perforation of a pneumatized MCP during drilling may create a communication between the sinonasal cavity and the intracranial compartment [1, 13, 19].

Reported MCP prevalence ranges from 7% to 42% across studies using dry skulls, cadaveric specimens, and CT imaging [1, 20–26] (Table 5). Although most studies have not demonstrated significant differences by sex or laterality, MCP prevalence has been reported to increase with age, and to be significantly higher in White individuals (41.3%) than in Black individuals (19.3%) (p = 0.02) [1, 2, 13, 27]. In the present study, MCP was present in 43.2% (216/500) of participants, and this was significantly higher in males than in females (p = 0.022). Importantly, MCPE was strongly associated with ipsilateral pneumatization patterns of all clinoid processes (p < 0.001) (Table 3), suggesting segmental clustering of skull base aeration.

Posterior clinoidectomy and PCPP

PCPP has significant clinical relevance in surgery involving the retrosellar region and the interpeduncular cistern. Posterior clinoidectomy may become a necessary surgical step to achieve safe access to the target area in selected lesions, including ophthalmic aneurysms, giant basilar artery bifurcation aneurysms, abducens nerve schwannomas, craniopharyngiomas, and tuberculum sellae meningiomas [3, 4, 11, 28–31]. In a study by Alpergin et al. [3], the prevalence of PCPP in patients with Chiari type I malformation was approximately twice that of an age-matched control group (38.5% vs. 19.7%). The authors cautioned that this nearly 50% increase in pneumatization may elevate the risk of CSF leakage during posterior clinoidectomy. Likewise, Burulday et al. [4] suggested that posterior clinoidectomy should be avoided in individuals with Type III PCPP because of the increased risk of complications such as CSF fistula formation. In such cases, alternative surgical routes, including high pterional or cranio-orbital approaches, have been recommended for the management of supraclinoid and paraclinoid aneurysms.

Reported PCPP rates in adults range from approximately 7% to 20% [3, 4, 32]. In the present study, PCPP was observed in 17.6% of right sides and 17.2% of left sides, consistent with this range. In a pediatric series of 180 CT scans (1–18 years), Alpergin et al. [11] reported pneumatized PCP in only 10% of children (18/180), with nearly all cases occurring during adolescence (10–18 years), consistent with progressive sphenoid sinus pneumatization toward surrounding bony structures during this period.

Common clinical considerations of pneumatized clinoid processes

Pneumatized clinoid processes should be regarded not merely as anatomical variations but also as clinically relevant anatomical features that may be considered during preoperative evaluation for skull base surgery. Previous studies have reported that pneumatization of the ACP, MCP, and PCP may be associated with technical challenges during clinoidectomy, including potential injury to adjacent internal carotid artery (ICA) segments during drilling, disruption of dural integrity, and the development of CSF fistulas [4, 6, 13]. Failure to recognize clinoid process pneumatization before surgery may present anatomical challenges during clinoidectomy. As reported in previous studies, inadvertent opening of an unrecognized pneumatized air cell may create a communication between the subarachnoid space and the sphenoid sinus, potentially resulting in CSF leakage [6, 33]. In such situations, various reconstruction techniques, including bone wax, fat grafts, muscle tissue (e.g., the “muscle plug” or “yo-yo” technique), and endoscopic endonasal repair of the sphenoid sinus defect, have been described [6, 33].

Therefore, preoperative identification of clinoid process pneumatization may facilitate anatomical assessment and surgical planning before clinoidectomy and other skull base procedures.

Developmental characteristics and impact of demographic factors

From a developmental perspective, ossification of the sphenoid bone and its associated structures is not a synchronous process but rather progresses through temporally distinct stages. Previous studies have demonstrated that the ACP and PCP do not ossify during the same period and that maturation of these structures may extend into childhood and adolescence [2, 3, 11]. This temporal variation in ossification provides a biological basis for the variability in clinoid morphology and pneumatization patterns observed in adulthood. Although age-related differences were identified in several regions in the univariate analyses, the independent effect of age remained significant only for ACP in the multivariate models (Fig. 8). In particular, the lower degrees of aeration observed in the anterior regions of younger individuals (18–34 years) suggest that skull base pneumatization may represent a progressive process associated with osteological maturation. Regarding PCP, Burulday et al. [4] reported that PCPP may decrease with advancing age; however, univariate analyses performed in the present study demonstrated a shift toward higher-grade PCPP patterns (Types II–III) in older age groups (right: p = 0.020; left: p = 0.007). Nevertheless, the loss of statistical significance in the multivariate analyses suggests that age may not be an independent determinant of PCPP (p > 0.05).

Although no significant sex-related differences in MCPP have been reported in the literature (p > 0.99) [13], the binary logistic regression analyses performed in the present study demonstrated that male sex was independently associated with bilateral MCPP (OR = 3.016; p < 0.001). Similarly, male sex remained independently associated with right-sided PCPP (OR = 2.234; p = 0.032). However, it is worth noting that the independent association between male sex and right-sided PCPP presented a borderline significance level (p = 0.032). Therefore, this specific demographic correlation should be interpreted with caution, as borderline p-values across parallel models may carry a higher vulnerability to multiple comparison fluctuations and require validation in broader populations. These findings are consistent with the results of Burulday et al. [4], who reported a significantly higher prevalence of PCPP in males (p = 0.001). It is worth noting that a significant baseline age difference existed between the female and male cohorts in our study (p = 0.006). Such demographic discrepancies inherently pose a risk of confounding bias, potentially intertwining the effects of physiological aging with biological sex. However, our multivariable logistic regression framework effectively controlled for this variance, and subsequent interaction analyses (Age x Sex) yielded no statistically significant synergistic effects on any pneumatization parameters (p > 0.05). This confirms that biological sex acts as a true, standalone, and independent driver of skull base aeration, rather than being an artifact of sample age distribution. Previous studies have demonstrated that males generally exhibit larger sphenoid sinus volumes, greater skull base dimensions, and more extensive craniofacial pneumatization areas [34]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed sex-related differences in clinoid process pneumatization may reflect underlying morphological characteristics in addition to aerodynamic factors.

The impact of nasal septal deviation angle on clinoid process pneumatization

NSD is known to alter aerodynamic airflow patterns within the nasal cavity, increasing nasal resistance and potentially influencing the ventilation and pneumatization processes of the paranasal sinuses [35]. However, studies investigating the relationship between NSD and clinoid process pneumatization remain limited, and the available findings are conflicting. Yazıcı [36] reported an increased frequency of ACPP in individuals with NSD, whereas Şahin et al. [37] found that NSD did not significantly affect the prevalence of ACPP. Furthermore, to the best of current knowledge, no previous study has examined the relationship between NSD and MCP or PCP. In this regard, the present study is noteworthy as one of the first comprehensive analyses to evaluate the association between NSD and ACPP, MCPP, and PCPP.

Furthermore, the findings of the present study suggest that NSD’s influence on skull base pneumatization patterns may extend beyond localized alterations in airflow dynamics and may be related to broader mechanisms governing skull base aeration. One of the key findings of the multivariate analysis performed in the present study was that NSDside was not a significant determinant of the presence or degree of pneumatization in any anatomical region. This observation suggests that developmental or anatomical predispositions triggered by the mechanical stress and long-term aerodynamic changes associated with the severity of deviation may play a more prominent role in pneumatization development than side-specific airflow alterations. This interpretation is consistent with the findings of Tang et al. [35], who proposed that the severity of NSD, rather than its mere presence or laterality, may be a more important determinant of sphenoid pneumatization. Moreover, the emergence of statistical significance for LACP in the multivariate models, after adjustment for factors such as age and sex, highlights the importance of advanced statistical modeling in evaluating anatomical variations.

The marked sensitivity of the MCP to NSD is particularly noteworthy. Whereas only severe (> 15°) deviations showed significant associations in the anterior and posterior regions, even mild (< 9°) and moderate (9°–15°) deviations influenced pneumatization in the middle region, suggesting that this area may be more responsive to alterations in nasal cavity aeration dynamics. Furthermore, severe NSDangle (> 15°) was associated with more extensive clinoid process pneumatization. However, because the present study was retrospective and cross-sectional, the chronological onset of NSD could not be determined. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted as an anatomical relationship identified in an adult population rather than evidence of a causal developmental mechanism. Nevertheless, these findings may contribute to a better understanding of the developmental characteristics of skull base aeration.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study including only a Turkish population, the generalizability of these findings to different ethnic and geographic populations may be limited. Second, measuring the NSDangle from two-dimensional CT images may not fully capture the complex 3D morphology of septal deformities. Third, the absence of a universally accepted classification system for MCPP in the literature suggests that these findings require external validation across different populations and centers. Another limitation is that sex-specific craniofacial morphological parameters, such as skull base dimensions, skull width, or sphenoid sinus volume, were not directly evaluated. In addition, because this was a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted in an adult population, the timing of NSD development could not be determined. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify this developmental relationship.

Furthermore, present study was based on radiological morphological analysis, and the direct relationship between clinoid pneumatization patterns and intraoperative technical difficulties, CSF fistula formation, ICA injury, or neurological complication rates could not be evaluated. Therefore, future prospective surgical series investigating the impact of pneumatization types on intraoperative complications and surgical outcomes would be valuable. In addition, the relatively small sample sizes in certain subgroups, particularly rare variants such as Type III pneumatization, may limit statistical generalizations about these uncommon anatomical configurations. This scarcity of cases within extreme pneumatization categories poses an inherent risk to the overall stability and standard errors of the logistic regression models. Larger multicenter studies may provide a more accurate understanding of the true prevalence and clinical significance of rare clinoid variations.

Conclusion

This CT-based study provides a comprehensive evaluation of pneumatization patterns of the ACP, MCP, and PCP. The findings demonstrate that clinoid aeration follows measurable anatomical trends rather than a random distribution. NSDangle severity, but not NSDside was associated with clinoid process pneumatization. Descriptive analyses showed that the middle region was sensitive even to mild (< 9°) and moderate (9°–15°) NSD angles, whereas multivariate analyses showed the strongest independent association between severe NSDangle (> 15°) and higher-grade pneumatization, particularly in the anterior and posterior regions. Age-related progression of ACPP, together with male predominance in bilateral MCPP and RPCPP, further supports the multifactorial nature of skull base aeration. Based on these findings, patients with severe NSD angles (> 15°) may warrant increased attention during preoperative thin-slice CT evaluation, as they are more likely to exhibit extensive clinoid pneumatization. Careful preoperative imaging assessment may facilitate recognition of these anatomical variations during planning for clinoidectomy and other sellar/parasellar skull base procedures.
