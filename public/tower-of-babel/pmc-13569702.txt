Silk–Hyaluronic Acid Laryngoplasty Outcomes: An Independent Tertiary Center Experience
Authors: Benjamin Wajsberg, Anastasia M. Christ, Sarah E. Benyo, Connor A. Keuchel, Radhika Duggal, Richard Wu, Michael S. Benninger, Rebecca C. Nelson, Paul C. Bryson, William S. Tierney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate the safety and efficacy of silk protein–hyaluronic acid (silk‐HA) injection laryngoplasty for glottic insufficiency at an independent tertiary academic voice center and compare outcomes and complication rates with those of HA injection augmentation.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients undergoing SilkVoice augmentation between June 2022 and August 2025 at a single institution. Outcomes included patient‐reported vocal improvement, emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and operative interventions. For patients with prior HA injection, silk‐HA outcomes were compared using a within‐patient design.ResultsThirty‐three patients underwent 44 silk‐HA injections (mean age 73.4 years; 51.5% female). Outcome data were available for 29 patients (87.9%). Most procedures (95.5%) were office‐based with a mean follow‐up of 12.7 months. Overall, 86.2% of patients reported at least minimal vocal improvement and 79.3% reported partial to significant improvement. In a within‐patient comparison of 20 patients who received both silk‐HA and prior HA, median improvement scores were equivalent (p = 0.776). Compared with a historical HA cohort from the same institution (691 patients, 1146 injections), operative intervention was significantly higher in silk‐HA (6.8% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.005); two cases were elective revisions for overaugmentation, and one occurred in the setting of lung cancer progression.ConclusionSilk‐HA injection laryngoplasty demonstrates safety and efficacy for glottic insufficiency with voice outcomes comparable to HA and no increase in serious adverse events despite higher operative revision rates. These findings from an independent academic voice center provide external validation supporting silk‐HA as a viable office‐based augmentation option.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Glottic insufficiency, characterized by incomplete vocal fold closure during phonation, most commonly arises from vocal fold paralysis or paresis and age‐related atrophy (presbylarynges). These structural changes result in breathy dysphonia, dysphagia, weak cough, aspiration, and diminished quality of life [1, 2]. As the population ages, the prevalence of glottic insufficiency is expected to rise, underscoring the need for effective and durable treatment strategies [3].

Injection laryngoplasty has become a cornerstone intervention for glottic insufficiency, offering a minimally invasive means of augmenting vocal fold volume and improving glottic closure [4]. The procedure can be performed in the office under local anesthesia or in the operating room with comparable outcomes [5, 6]. A variety of injectable materials are available, including calcium hydroxyapatite, collagen‐based products, hyaluronic acid (HA), and autologous fat, each with distinct durability, biomechanical properties, and safety profiles [7].

HA has gained widespread adoption for injection laryngoplasty due to its biocompatibility and viscoelastic properties, which approximate those of the native vocal fold lamina propria [8]. Systematic reviews confirm that HA improves glottic closure and patient‐reported voice outcomes; however, the duration of effect typically ranges from several months to 1 year, and repeat injections are frequently required [8]. This limitation has motivated investigation into alternative injectable materials that may provide greater durability while maintaining biomechanical compatibility with vocal fold tissue.

Silk fibroin, a biodegradable protein derived from Bombyx mori silkworm cocoons, has emerged as a promising candidate. Preclinical studies demonstrate that silk‐HA composite injectable (SilkVoice, Sofregen Medical, Medford, MA)—consisting of silk fibroin microparticles suspended in HA—is biocompatible, non‐immunogenic, and rheometrically similar to native porcine vocal fold tissue [9]. In vivo rat and canine models confirmed slow degradation and no material migration [9, 10]. Clinically, a retrospective series of 160 silk‐HA injection procedures reported steroid‐responsive hemilaryngeal edema in 3.8% of cases and statistically significant improvement in VHI‐10 scores through 1 year and beyond [11]. Long‐term follow‐up in a small cohort has demonstrated sustained voice improvement for at least 3 years, with benefit maintained at 4 years in two patients [12].

However, existing clinical evidence originates exclusively from the institution involved in silk‐HA's development, and independent validation is lacking, with no study directly comparing silk‐HA with conventional HA injection. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of silk‐HA injection laryngoplasty for glottic insufficiency at an independent tertiary academic voice center, and to compare patient‐reported voice outcomes and complication rates with those of a historical same‐institution HA cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective case series conducted at a single tertiary academic voice center. Given the retrospective nature of this study, the Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for informed consent. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained for this study (IRB #24‐669). Patients who underwent silk protein–HA (silk‐HA) injection laryngoplasty (SilkVoice) for glottic insufficiency between June 1, 2022, and August 31, 2025, were identified through electronic medical review using CPT codes for vocal fold augmentation or injection (31571, 31573, 31574).

Data Collection

Medical records were reviewed to extract demographic, clinical, and procedural data. Demographic data included sex, race, ethnicity, and age at first injection. Clinical variables included indication for augmentation, presenting symptoms, and number of prior vocal fold injections. Procedural characteristics included setting (office or operating room) and injection approach (transnasal, transoral, or transthyrohyoid). All data were entered into a standardized REDCap data collection tool.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was patient‐reported degree of vocal improvement as documented by the treating clinician at follow‐up visits, and categorized as significant, partial, minimal, none, or worsened. Safety outcomes included emergency department visits, hospitalizations, operative interventions, and intubations. Additional post‐procedural symptoms were recorded as reported.

For patients who had previously undergone HA injection laryngoplasty, vocal improvement following silk‐HA was compared directly with their documented response to prior HA injection, with each patient serving as their own control. Improvement categories were converted to an ordinal scale (worsened = −1, none = 0, minimal = 1, partial = 2, significant = 3) for comparative analysis.

A historical same‐institution cohort of patients who underwent HA injection laryngoplasty was identified for cohort‐level comparison of complication rates. Complication data including emergency department visits, hospitalizations, operative interventions, hyaluronidase injections, and intubations were extracted from this cohort using the same data collection methods.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive and comparative statistical analyses were performed using JMP 10.4 (SAS Corporation, Cary, NC). Within‐patient comparison of silk‐HA versus prior HA vocal outcomes was performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, with improvement categories converted to an ordinal scale (worsened = −1, none = 0, minimal = 1, partial = 2, significant = 3). Cohort‐level comparison of complication rates between the silk‐HA cohort and a historical same‐institution HA cohort was performed using Fisher's exact test, which was selected given the small cell counts in several complication categories. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.025 using Bonferroni correction for two primary complications (operative intervention and hyaluronidase injection rates). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Thirty‐three patients underwent 44 silk‐HA injection laryngoplasty procedures. Outcome data were available for 29 patients (87.9%) following the first injection. The cohort included 17 females (51.5%) with a mean age of 73.4 ± 10.2 years at first injection (range: 48–89). Most patients identified as White (90.9%) and non‐Hispanic/Latino (93.9%; Table 1).

Dysphonia was present in all patients. Dysphagia (6.1%) and dyspnea (3.0%) were also reported.

Twenty‐five patients had undergone at least one prior HA injection before their first silk‐HA injection (median interval 417 days, range 87–1942 days), and eight had received two or more prior HA injections. Among patients with a documented rationale for transitioning from HA to silk, improved longevity was cited in 14 cases. Additionally, two patients on anticoagulation were preferentially offered office‐based silk‐HA over OR‐based fat injection.

Procedural Characteristics

The majority of injections were performed in the office (42/44, 95.5%), with two performed in the operating room. Office‐based approaches included transnasal flexible laryngoscopy (52.4%), transoral (45.2%), and transthyrohyoid (2.4%). Injection approach was not documented in two cases. Most patients received a single silk‐HA injection (n = 23); 10 underwent multiple injections, primarily to treat bilateral atrophy across staged sessions (n = 6), with a mean interval of 53 days between stages, or to further augment a single side (n = 3), with a mean interval of 412 days (1.13 years) between first and second injection in the latter group. One patient underwent three injections, with the second administered to the contralateral side at 423 days and the third for further augmentation of one side at 298 days following the second injection.

Clinical Outcomes

Mean follow‐up after the first silk‐HA injection was 1.06 ± 0.94 years (range 0.08–3.08). Overall, 86.2% reported at least minimal improvement and 79.3% reported partial to significant improvement. Significant improvement was noted in 16 patients (55.2%), partial improvement in 7 patients (24.1%), and minimal or no improvement in 4 patients (13.8%). Two patients experienced worsening and required elective return to the operating room for silk removal and hyaluronidase injection. A third patient underwent urgent operative intervention for airway compromise in the setting of contralateral vocal fold paralysis from lung cancer progression. Three patients subsequently underwent HA injection, and one underwent silastic medialization revision.

Among the subset of patients who underwent multiple injections, incremental benefit was observed in several cases. One patient experienced minimal improvement after the first injection but achieved partial improvement following a second augmentation. Another progressed from partial to significant improvement after their second injection. One patient achieved significant improvement after the first injection and maintained significant benefit through two subsequent augmentations.

Voice Handicap Index Outcomes

Changes in Voice Handicap Index (VHI) scores were available for a limited subset of 10 patients following silk‐HA injection. The mean improvement was 8.7 ± 13.7 points (median 8 points; range −17 to +26).

For comparison, VHI change following prior HA injection was available in 4 of these patients, with a mean improvement of 17 ± 19.3 points (median 12 points; range 0 to +44).

Comparison With HA

Vocal outcomes following silk‐HA were compared directly with each patient's prior HA response among 20 patients who had received both treatments. Outcomes were scored on an ordinal scale (worsened = −1, none = 0, minimal = 1, partial = 2, significant = 3).

The median improvement score was 2 (partial improvement) for both treatments.

Significant improvement occurred in 9 patients (45%) following both silk‐HA and HA injection. Silk‐HA produced greater improvement than prior HA in 5 patients (25%), equivalent outcomes in 8 (40%), and lesser improvement in 7 (35%). Two patients worsened after silk‐HA compared with none after HA (Figure 1). Per‐patient delta scores (silk‐HA minus HA) ranged from −3 to +2 (Figure 2). A Wilcoxon signed‐rank test did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between treatments (p = 0.776).

Safety and Complications

All 33 patients tolerated silk‐HA injection without intraoperative complications. Three patients (9.1% of patients; 6.8% of injections) required operative intervention: two for overaugmentation resulting in strained voice (one due to superficial injection technique, one following a second injection after prior significant benefit), and one for silk removal to improve glottic airway after contralateral vocal fold paralysis due to lung cancer progression. The latter procedure was performed urgently in the setting of contralateral vocal fold paralysis from lung cancer progression rather than a complication of the injection. One patient was hospitalized for generalized weakness felt to be unrelated to the procedure. There were no emergency department visits, acute airway events, or intubations.

When compared with a historical HA cohort from the same institution (691 patients, 1146 injections), operative intervention and hyaluronidase injection rates were significantly higher in the silk‐HA cohort (6.8% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.005 and 6.8% vs. 1.2%, p = 0.022, respectively), though two operative interventions were elective returns for overaugmentation and one was performed urgently for progressive airway compromise due to underlying lung cancer progression rather than a complication of the injection itself. Rates of hospitalization (0% vs. 1.0%), ED visits (0% vs. 2.0%), and intubation (0% vs. 0.4%) were lower in the silk‐HA cohort (Table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that silk‐HA injection laryngoplasty provides patient‐reported voice improvement comparable to conventional HA injection with an overall favorable safety profile in an independent tertiary academic voice center. The primary objectives were to evaluate safety and efficacy at an institution unaffiliated with SilkVoice's development and to directly compare outcomes with conventional HA injection. To our knowledge, this represents the first independent validation study of silk‐HA outcomes from an institution unaffiliated with its development, providing independent supportive evidence of findings from prior single‐center series.

The durability of silk‐HA over conventional HA is a primary rationale for its use, and the most compelling long‐term data to date come from Schettini and Carroll, who demonstrated sustained vocal improvement and stable laryngovideostroboscopic findings for at least 3 years, and up to 4 years in two patients, following silk‐HA augmentation [12]. In our cohort, improved longevity was the primary stated reason for transitioning from HA to silk‐HA, reflecting real‐world clinical motivation consistent with these long‐term data. However, our follow‐up of approximately 1 year is insufficient to directly confirm this durability advantage, and prospective studies with longer follow‐up are needed to fully characterize it. Among the 16 patients with significant improvement, 5 maintained this response at follow‐up exceeding 1 year, including one patient followed for 2.67 years, and notably each of these patients had received only a single injection. Additionally, one patient who underwent three sequential injections achieved significant improvement after each, with intervals of 423 days between the first and second injection and 298 days between the second and third. Our independent findings provide complementary external evidence that silk‐HA performs comparably to HA in the short term, with potential for greater durability.

The majority of patients in this cohort achieved meaningful vocal improvement following silk‐HA injection, with 79.3% reporting partial to significant benefit and 55.2% achieving significant improvement. These findings are consistent with the largest published silk‐HA series to date by Dwyer et al., which reported statistically significant improvement in VHI‐10 and CAPE‐V scores at all follow‐up intervals through and beyond 1 year in 160 procedures [11].

In a within‐patient comparison of 20 patients who received both treatments, no statistically significant difference in vocal improvement was detected between silk‐HA and prior HA injection (p = 0.776), with a median ordinal improvement score of 2 (partial improvement) for both. While silk produced greater improvement than prior HA in 25% of patients and equivalent outcomes in 40%, lesser improvement was observed in 35%, and two patients worsened following silk‐HA compared with none after HA. The within‐patient matching design strengthens the validity of this comparison by controlling for inter‐patient variability; however, several limitations apply. As HA was uniformly administered as the first injection, the potential contribution of carryover effects between treatments cannot be excluded. Additionally, the small sample size limits statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences.

Cohort‐level comparison with a historical HA series (in press) from the same institution similarly demonstrated no significant difference in the rate of partial to significant improvement between silk‐HA and HA (79.3% vs. 79.0%), with nearly identical rates of significant improvement (55.2% vs. 54.3%). The higher rate of worsening in the silk‐HA cohort (6.9% vs. 0%) should be interpreted as a safety observation warranting attention rather than a reflection of inferior efficacy and is consistent with the greater clinical consequence of overaugmentation with a durable material.

The overall complication profile of silk‐HA was favorable when compared with a historical HA cohort from the same institution. Operative intervention and hyaluronidase injection rates were significantly higher in the silk‐HA cohort (6.8% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.005 and 6.8% vs. 1.2%, p = 0.022, respectively). Two of these procedures represented elective returns for overaugmentation, and one occurred in the setting of contralateral vocal fold paralysis from lung cancer progression rather than material‐related morbidity. This may reflect technique‐ and durability‐related consequences rather than intrinsic material toxicity, consistent with the greater clinical risk of overaugmentation with a long‐lasting material compared with a temporary filler. As Schettini and Carroll emphasize, silk‐HA is not intended for overcorrection, and a conservative, staged approach with planned second‐look evaluation is advisable to avoid overcorrection with a material whose effects may persist for years [12]. The occurrence of strained voice from overaugmentation in two patients highlights the clinical consequence of overcorrection with a long‐lasting material, reinforcing the need for a conservative, staged injection strategy. Among patients with suboptimal or limited response, three subsequently underwent HA injection, demonstrating that silk‐HA does not preclude subsequent augmentation with alternative materials.

No cases of acute airway compromise attributable to silk‐HA, emergency take‐back, intubation, or unplanned escalation of care occurred. Rates of ED visits (0% vs. 2.0%), hospitalization (0% vs. 1.0%), and intubation (0% vs. 0.4%) were lower in the silk‐HA cohort. One patient in our cohort was hospitalized for generalized weakness following injection, felt to be unrelated to the procedure. Steroid‐responsive hemilaryngeal edema, reported at 3.8% in the Dwyer series [11], was not observed in our cohort, though the sample size limits meaningful comparison of rare adverse events.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Outcome data were unavailable for 4 of 33 patients (12.1%) due to loss to follow‐up, introducing the potential for follow‐up bias and limiting the reliability of conclusions drawn from the available cohort. The retrospective design and single‐center experience may further limit generalizability. Patient‐reported outcomes were assessed using patient‐reported improvement extracted from chart documentation rather than prospectively collected validated instruments, and VHI data were available in only a subset of patients, precluding robust quantitative outcome analysis. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized patient‐reported outcome measures to enable more rigorous assessment. The within‐patient HA comparison, while methodologically strengthened by its paired design, is subject to the confounders described above. The cohort is predominantly older, White, and non‐Hispanic, limiting applicability to more diverse populations. Finally, follow‐up duration of approximately 1 year is insufficient to fully characterize the long‐term durability that represents silk‐HA's primary theoretical advantage over conventional HA.

Conclusion

These findings from an independent tertiary academic voice center support silk‐HA injection laryngoplasty as a viable office‐based option for glottic insufficiency, with comparable voice outcomes to HA. Future prospective studies with standardized outcome measures, longer follow‐up, and larger sample sizes are needed to better define the durability advantage of silk‐HA and to identify patient and technical factors that optimize outcomes.
