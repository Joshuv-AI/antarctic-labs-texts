LIMS2 downregulation is associated with tumor progression and immune landscape alterations in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Huanhuan Wang, Chunyu Li, Yuan Gao, Wanqi Zeng, Xingye Li, Jingjing Wang, Weiwei Wang, Wenting Yang, Yaqiong Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLIM and senescent cell antigen-like domains protein 2 (LIMS2) is a focal adhesion–associated protein involved in cytoskeletal organization and cell–matrix interactions. However, its expression pattern and biological significance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) have not been fully elucidated. Transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas were analyzed to assess LIMS2 expression across cancer types and its prognostic relevance in LUAD. Protein–protein interaction analysis and functional enrichment were performed to explore biological processes potentially associated with LIMS2. Gain-of-function experiments were conducted in A549 and NCI-H358 cells to evaluate the effects of LIMS2 overexpression on cell proliferation and migration. In addition, multiple computational algorithms were applied to investigate the relationship between LIMS2 expression, immune cell infiltration, and immune checkpoint–related genes in LUAD. LIMS2 expression was significantly decreased in LUAD tissues compared with normal lung tissues and was associated with poorer clinical outcomes. Functional assays demonstrated that LIMS2 overexpression markedly inhibited LUAD cell proliferation and migratory capacity. Bioinformatic analyses indicated that LIMS2-related genes were mainly enriched in cytoskeleton-associated processes and signaling pathways such as MAPK and Hippo. Immune-related analyses further revealed that LIMS2 expression was significantly correlated with the infiltration of multiple immune cell populations and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, including PDCD1, CTLA4, and CD274. These results suggest that LIMS2 is frequently downregulated in LUAD and may be associated with tumor progression through its involvement in cellular behavior and the tumor immune landscape. Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for a substantial proportion of cancer incidence and deaths each year. Among its histological subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) represents the most prevalent form of non-small cell lung cancer and is characterized by marked molecular heterogeneity and complex biological behavior [1]. Although advances in targeted therapy and immune checkpoint blockade have improved outcomes for selected patient subsets, the overall prognosis of LUAD remains unsatisfactory, largely due to late-stage diagnosis, therapeutic resistance, and limited durable responses [2]. These challenges highlight the urgent need to identify additional molecular determinants involved in LUAD progression.

Cell adhesion-associated proteins have emerged as critical regulators of tumor initiation and progression by coordinating cytoskeletal dynamics, mechanotransduction, and intracellular signaling [3]. The LIM and senescent cell antigen-like domain (LIMS) protein family, consisting mainly of LIMS1 and LIMS2, functions as adaptor proteins within focal adhesion complexes and participates in integrin-mediated signaling pathways [4, 5]. Previous studies have demonstrated that LIMS1 promotes tumor cell survival under metabolic stress conditions and contributes to aggressive tumor phenotypes in several cancer types [6, 7]. LIMS2 has been suggested as a tumor suppressor in some studies. For example, low LIMS2 expression is associated with poor prognosis in Ewing’s sarcoma [8]. However, the expression pattern and biological function of LIMS2 in lung adenocarcinoma are still unclear, and the mechanism of LIMS2 in tumorigenesis and development still needs to be further elucidated.

Moreover, LIMS2 interacts with key focal adhesion components such as PARVA, PARVB, indicating a potential role in regulating cytoskeletal organization and cell motility [9]. Despite these observations, the expression pattern, biological function, and clinical relevance of LIMS2 in lung adenocarcinoma have not been systematically investigated. In particular, whether LIMS2 is associated with tumor progression and immune-related features in LUAD remains largely unclear.

In this study, we performed an integrated analysis to explore the expression characteristics and prognostic significance of LIMS2 in lung adenocarcinoma using large-scale public datasets. We further evaluated the functional impact of LIMS2 on LUAD cell proliferation and migration through in vitro assays. In addition, protein interaction analysis and functional enrichment were conducted to gain insight into the potential biological processes associated with LIMS2. Given the growing importance of the tumor immune microenvironment in lung cancer, we also examined the relationship between LIMS2 expression, immune cell infiltration, and immune checkpoint-related features [10, 11]. Collectively, this work aims to provide a comprehensive and cautious assessment of the potential role of LIMS2 in LUAD, thereby offering new perspectives for future mechanistic and translational studies.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and data sources

Human lung adenocarcinoma cell lines BEAS-2B, A549 and NCI-H358, PC9, H1299 were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). Cells were routinely authenticated by the supplier and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. The LIMS2 overexpression plasmid and corresponding empty vector control were synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China).

Publicly available transcriptomic and clinical data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project via the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and corresponding clinical information for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) were retrieved from the TCGA-LUAD cohort, while pan-cancer analyses were performed using the TCGA Pan-Cancer (PANCAN) cohort.

Reagents and instruments

RPMI-1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin–streptomycin solution, and trypsin were purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Dojindo Laboratories (Japan). Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size) were obtained from Corning (USA). TRIzol reagent, reverse transcription kits, and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix were obtained from Takara (Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Expression analysis of LIMS2

Normalized RNA-seq data were analyzed in transcripts per million (TPM) format and log2-transformed [log2 (TPM + 1)] for subsequent analysis. Differential expression of LIMS2 between tumor and normal tissues was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Paired analyses were conducted when matched tumor–normal samples were available. Visualization was performed using violin plots and boxplots generated with the ggplot2 package in R.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic significance of LIMS2 expression in LUAD patients. Disease-specific survival (DSS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were analyzed. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of LIMS2. Survival differences were assessed using the log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Analyses were conducted using the survival and survminer R packages.

Protein–protein interaction and functional enrichment analysis

The STRING database (version 11.5) was used to predict potential protein–protein interactions associated with LIMS2. Interaction networks were visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1). Genes interacting with LIMS2 were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses to explore potential biological processes and signaling pathways. Enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Cell culture and transfection

A549 and NCI-H358 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were transfected with LIMS2 overexpression plasmid or empty vector using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The full-length human LIMS2 coding sequence was cloned into a mammalian expression vector (oe-LIMS2). An empty vector was used as the negative control (NC). A549 and NCI-H358 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to ~ 60–70% confluence before transfection. For each well, plasmid DNA (oe-LIMS2 or NC; e.g., 2.0–2.5 µg) was diluted in Opti-MEM and mixed with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to form DNA–lipid complexes (15–20 min, room temperature). Complexes were added to cells in serum-free medium, and complete medium was replaced after 4–6 h. Cells were harvested at 24–48 h post-transfection for downstream assays. Small interfering RNAs targeting human METTL3 (si-METTL3) and a non-targeting siRNA (si-NC) were used for transient knockdown. Cells were seeded to reach ~ 40–50% confluence at the time of transfection. siRNA duplexes were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Opti-MEM, following the manufacturer’s protocol. A final siRNA concentration of 50 nM (range 25–100 nM) was used unless otherwise specified. Medium was replaced with complete medium after 4–6 h, and cells were collected at 24–48 h for mRNA validation and at 48–72 h for protein validation and phenotypic assays. si-METTL3-1 (sense): GCAAGAAUUCUGUGACUAUTT, antisense: AUAGUCACAGAAUUCUUGCTT.

Cell proliferation and migration assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Transfected cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and optical density values were measured at 450 nm at indicated time points. For colony formation assays, transfected cells were seeded into six-well plates at low density and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted. Cell migration was assessed using Transwell and wound healing assays. For Transwell assays, cells were seeded into the upper chamber in serum-free medium, while medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h, migrated cells were fixed, stained, and counted. For wound healing assays, a linear scratch was generated using a sterile pipette tip when cells reached approximately 90% confluence. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h, and migration was quantified by measuring wound closure.

Immune infiltration analysis

The association between LIMS2 expression and immune cell infiltration was evaluated using multiple computational algorithms, including TIMER, CIBERSORT, EPIC, MCPcounter, QUANTISEQ, and xCELL. Correlations between LIMS2 expression and immune checkpoint–related genes (such as PDCD1, CTLA4, and CD274) were also analyzed. All immune-related analyses were based on the TCGA-LUAD cohort.

Xenograft model and immunohistochemistry

A subcutaneous xenograft model was established using male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old). All experimental procedures in this research received approval from the Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (2402333). NCI-H358 or A549 cells transfected with negative control (NC) or LIMS2 overexpression plasmid (oe-LIMS2) were suspended in serum-free medium, and 5 × 10⁶ cells (100 µL) were injected subcutaneously into the flank of each mouse (n = 5 per group). At the experimental endpoint, mice were sacrificed and tumor tissues were harvested for histological analysis. Tumor samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against LIMS2 (Cat. No. 55300-1-AP), anti-phospho-YAP (p-YAP, Cat. No. 29018-1-AP), anti-YAP (Cat. No. 13584-1-AP), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation. Signals were visualized using DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Staining intensity was semi-quantitatively analyzed in randomly selected fields using ImageJ software in a blinded manner.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. GAPDH was used as an internal control, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for quantitative real-time PCR were as follows:

LIMS2 forward, 5′-ACACCTGAGACAGAGACAGAG-3′; LIMS2 reverse, 5′-CGCAGCTGAGGTCAGAGGAG-3′. GAPDH forward, 5′-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3′; GAPDH reverse, 5′-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3′; METTL3 forward, 5′-AGCCTTCTTCAGGCTTACCTG- 3′; METTL3 Reverse, 5′-CTGCTGTTGGTGTTGATGGT- 3′.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from transfected A549 and NCI-H358 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. After incubation on ice for 30 min, lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (for phosphorylated proteins) in TBST buffer for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies (Proteintech, China) were used: anti-LIMS2 (Cat. No. 55300-1-AP), anti-phospho-p38 (p-p38, Cat. No. 28796-1-AP), anti-phospho-JNK (p-JNK, Cat. No. 80024-1-RR), anti-phospho-YAP (p-YAP, Cat. No. 29018-1-AP), anti-YAP (Cat. No. 13584-1-AP), and anti-GAPDH (Cat. No. 60004-1-IG). All primary antibodies were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions (typically 1:1,000 unless otherwise specified). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) and normalized to GAPDH. Phosphorylated protein levels were analyzed relative to total protein expression where applicable. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3) or GraphPad Prism 8. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test or nonparametric tests, as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression profile of LIMS2 in pan-carcinoma and lung adenocarcinoma

To characterize the baseline expression pattern of LIMS2, RNA tissue specificity analysis was first performed using publicly available transcriptomic datasets. LIMS2 displayed a tissue-enhanced expression pattern, with relatively higher expression levels observed in intestinal tissues, particularly the colon, suggesting a degree of tissue specificity under physiological conditions (Fig. 1A).

LIMS2 expression was significantly decreased in all LUAD cell lines. Subsequently, the expression profile of LIMS2 across multiple cancer types was systematically evaluated using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort. Comparative analysis between tumor and corresponding normal tissues revealed that LIMS2 expression was significantly reduced in a broad range of malignancies (Fig. 1B). Notably, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) exhibited a marked decrease in LIMS2 expression compared with normal lung tissues, consistent with a tumor-associated downregulation pattern.

To further validate the expression pattern of LIMS2 at the cellular level, we examined its mRNA expression in a panel of lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, including NCI-H358, A549, PC9, and H1299, with the normal bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B serving as a control. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings from TCGA, LIMS2 expression was markedly reduced in all LUAD cell lines compared with BEAS-2B (Fig. 1C).

To further validate these findings, paired tumor–normal sample analyses were conducted in several cancer types. LIMS2 expression was consistently lower in tumor tissues than in matched adjacent normal tissues across multiple cohorts, including LUAD, reinforcing the robustness of the observed expression difference at the individual-patient level (Fig. 1D).

In addition, a pan-cancer overview of LIMS2 expression distribution across TCGA tumor types was performed. LIMS2 expression varied among different cancers but generally remained at a relatively lower level in several solid tumors, including lung adenocarcinoma, further supporting its potential relevance in tumor biology (Fig. 1E).

Collectively, these results indicate that LIMS2 exhibits tissue-specific expression in normal conditions and is broadly downregulated in multiple cancer types, with a pronounced decrease observed in lung adenocarcinoma, providing a rationale for subsequent functional and mechanistic investigations.

Association of LIMS2 expression with clinical characteristics and prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma

To investigate the clinical relevance of LIMS2, pan-cancer survival analyses were performed using TCGA cohorts. In disease-specific survival (DSS) analysis, LIMS2 expression exhibited heterogeneous prognostic associations across different cancer types. Notably, in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), higher LIMS2 expression was significantly associated with improved DSS (hazard ratio < 1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Consistent results were observed in progression-free interval (PFI) analysis, where elevated LIMS2 expression correlated with prolonged progression-free survival in LUAD patients (Fig. 2B).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was further conducted to evaluate the discriminatory capacity of LIMS2 expression between tumor and normal tissues in LUAD. The results demonstrated a strong classification performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.992 (95% CI: 0.986–0.998), indicating that LIMS2 expression effectively distinguishes LUAD tissues from normal lung tissues at the transcriptomic level (Fig. 2C).

Additionally, the relationship between LIMS2 expression and pathological T stage was examined in LUAD. Patients with advanced tumors (T2–T4) exhibited significantly lower LIMS2 expression compared with those at early stage (T1) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that reduced LIMS2 expression is associated with tumor progression and unfavorable clinical features in lung adenocarcinoma.

Genomic and epigenetic alterations of LIMS2 across cancers and in LUAD

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying LIMS2 dysregulation in lung adenocarcinoma, we systematically analyzed its genomic alterations and epigenetic features across TCGA pan-cancer cohorts. First, the mutation landscape of LIMS2 was assessed across multiple tumor types. LIMS2 exhibited a relatively low mutation frequency in most cancers, with the highest alteration rate observed in UCEC, followed by HNSC and SKCM (Fig. 3A). In LUAD, the overall mutation frequency of LIMS2 was low, suggesting that somatic mutation is unlikely to be the primary driver of its dysregulation. Consistent with this observation, protein structure mapping revealed that LIMS2 mutations were sparsely distributed along the protein sequence, without clear enrichment within conserved LIM domains (Fig. 3B). These alterations were predominantly missense mutations, further indicating limited functional disruption at the genomic mutation level. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis demonstrated that LIMS2 alterations across cancers were mainly characterized by copy number loss rather than amplification (Fig. 3C). In LUAD, shallow deletion was the predominant CNV event, while deep deletion and amplification occurred at relatively low frequencies, supporting the notion that structural genomic loss may contribute to reduced LIMS2 expression. Given the limited contribution of genetic alterations, we further evaluated the epigenetic regulation of LIMS2. Promoter methylation analysis revealed a significantly higher methylation level of the LIMS2 promoter region in LUAD tumor tissues compared with normal lung samples (Fig. 3D). This finding suggests that aberrant promoter hypermethylation may represent an important mechanism underlying LIMS2 transcriptional suppression in LUAD. Collectively, these results indicate that LIMS2 downregulation in LUAD is more likely driven by epigenetic modification and copy number loss rather than recurrent somatic mutation.

Exploration of m6A-related regulation as a potential contributor to LIMS2 downregulation

To further explore potential mechanisms contributing to LIMS2 downregulation beyond genomic and promoter methylation alterations, we examined whether post-transcriptional regulation might be involved. In silico analysis revealed several putative m6A modification sites distributed along the LIMS2 transcript, with predicted sites located within relatively stable RNA secondary structures (Fig. 4A–B), suggesting that LIMS2 may be permissive to m6A-associated regulation.

B Predicted RNA secondary structures of LIMS2 highlighting putative m6A-related sites. C, D qRT-PCR analysis of METTL3 and LIMS2 mRNA levels in NCL-H358 (C) and A549 (D) cells following METTL3 knockdown. Given the central role of METTL3 in m6A writing, METTL3 was silenced in NCL-H358 and A549 cells [12]. qRT-PCR confirmed effective METTL3 knockdown, accompanied by a modest but consistent increase in LIMS2 mRNA expression (Fig. 4C–D). These results suggest that m6A-related regulatory processes may be associated with LIMS2 expression regulation, although direct m6A modification of LIMS2 transcripts requires further investigation.

LIMS2 overexpression suppresses proliferation and clonogenic capacity of LUAD cells

To evaluate the functional role of LIMS2 in lung adenocarcinoma cells, LIMS2 overexpression models were established in NCL-H358 and A549 cell lines. PCR analysis confirmed that LIMS2 mRNA levels were significantly increased in cells transfected with the LIMS2 overexpression vector compared with negative controls in both cell lines (Fig. 5A–B). Cell proliferation was subsequently assessed using CCK-8 assays. As shown in Fig. 5C–D, LIMS2 overexpression resulted in a significantly reduced growth rate in both NCL-H358 and A549 cells over the 5-day observation period, as reflected by lower OD values at 450 nm compared with control cells. Furthermore, colony formation assays were performed to examine long-term proliferative potential. Representative images and quantitative analysis demonstrated that the number of colonies formed by LIMS2-overexpressing cells was markedly decreased relative to control groups in both cell lines (Fig. 5E, F). Collectively, these results indicate that elevated LIMS2 expression is associated with reduced proliferative and clonogenic abilities of LUAD cells in vitro.

LIMS2 overexpression is associated with reduced migratory capacity of LUAD cells

To further investigate whether LIMS2 influences the migratory behavior of lung adenocarcinoma cells, Transwell migration and wound healing assays were performed in NCL-H358 and A549 cells following LIMS2 overexpression. In the Transwell migration assay, a significantly lower number of cells migrated through the membrane in the LIMS2-overexpressing group compared with the negative control in both NCL-H358 and A549 cell lines (Fig. 6A–B), suggesting an association between elevated LIMS2 expression and reduced migratory potential. Consistently, wound healing assays showed that cells overexpressing LIMS2 exhibited a slower closure of the scratch area compared with control cells. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significantly lower wound closure percentage at 24 h in the oe-LIMS2 group in both cell lines (Fig. 6C–D). Taken together, these results indicate that increased LIMS2 expression is associated with attenuated migration ability of LUAD cells.

LIMS2-associated protein interaction network and functional enrichment analysis

To explore the potential molecular functions of LIMS2, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database. As shown in Fig. 7A, LIMS2 was closely connected with multiple cytoskeleton- and focal adhesion–related proteins, including PARVA, PARVB, VCL, FERMT2, RSU1, and other LIM domain–containing proteins. Extraction of the core network highlighted a highly interconnected module centered on LIMS2 (Fig. 7B), suggesting its involvement in adhesion-related protein complexes.

H GO enrichment overview showing broader cellular context of LIMS2-associated proteins

Subcellular localization enrichment analysis revealed that LIMS2-associated proteins were predominantly enriched in focal adhesion, cell–substrate junction, actin cytoskeleton, and adherens junction compartments (Fig. 7C, D). Consistently, Gene Ontology (GO) biological process analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in cell–substrate adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton organization, integrin-mediated signaling, and cell migration–related processes (Fig. 7E). Molecular function analysis further indicated that these proteins were mainly involved in actin binding, cytoskeletal protein binding, and integrin binding (Fig. 7F).

Pathway enrichment analysis based on KEGG revealed that LIMS2-associated genes were significantly enriched in focal adhesion, MAPK signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, and pathways in cancer (Fig. 7G). In contrast, broader GO terms related to protein metabolism and intracellular transport were also identified (Fig. 7H), reflecting the general cellular context of the interaction network. Collectively, these results suggest that LIMS2 is closely associated with cytoskeleton organization and adhesion-related signaling pathways, potentially linking structural regulation to MAPK and Hippo signaling in cancer cells.

LIMS2 overexpression is associated with modulation of MAPK- and Hippo-related signaling in vitro and in vivo

Based on pathway enrichment analyses indicating potential involvement of MAPK- and Hippo-associated signaling, we further explored whether LIMS2 overexpression was accompanied by alterations in key pathway components in LUAD cells. Western blot analysis confirmed successful overexpression of LIMS2 at the protein level in both NCI-H358 and A549 cells (Fig. 8A–B). Notably, LIMS2 overexpression was associated with decreased phosphorylation levels of p38 and JNK, whereas the phosphorylation level of YAP was increased compared with the negative control group. Total YAP protein expression showed no significant change between groups. Densitometric quantification across independent experiments supported these observations, suggesting coordinated modulation of MAPK and Hippo pathway–related signaling following LIMS2 upregulation.

To further evaluate whether similar alterations were observed in vivo, xenograft tumors derived from control and LIMS2-overexpressing HCL-H358 cells were subjected to immunohistochemical analysis. As shown in Fig. 8C–F, LIMS2 protein expression was markedly elevated in the oe-LIMS2 tumor tissues, confirming sustained overexpression in vivo. Consistent with the in vitro findings, phosphorylated YAP staining intensity was reduced in control tumors but relatively increased in the LIMS2-overexpressing group, while total YAP expression exhibited no obvious difference between groups. Semi-quantitative analysis further supported these trends.

Taken together, these findings suggest that LIMS2 overexpression is accompanied by alterations in MAPK- and Hippo-associated signaling both in cultured LUAD cells and in xenograft tumor tissues. Although additional mechanistic studies are warranted, the data indicate that modulation of these signaling pathways may contribute to the biological effects of LIMS2 observed in LUAD models.

Association of LIMS2 expression with immune infiltration patterns and immunotherapy-related features

To further explore the potential immunological relevance of LIMS2 in lung adenocarcinoma, multiple computational algorithms were applied to systematically assess the association between LIMS2 expression and tumor immune infiltration in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Across several deconvolution methods, including TIMER, CIBERSORT, EPIC, MCPcounter, xCELL, and QUANTISEQ, LIMS2 expression showed consistent correlations with multiple immune cell populations (Fig. 9A–F). Notably, LIMS2 expression was positively associated with immune effector–related cell types in several algorithms, whereas negative correlations were observed with selected stromal or suppressive components, although the magnitude of correlation varied across methods.

In addition, correlation analysis revealed that LIMS2 expression was significantly associated with a broad panel of immune checkpoint–related genes, encompassing both inhibitory and stimulatory molecules (Fig. 9G). These findings suggest that LIMS2 expression may reflect distinct immune-related transcriptional features within the tumor microenvironment. Consistently, higher LIMS2 expression was positively correlated with the tumor immune score in the TCGA-LUAD cohort (Fig. 9H). Moreover, analysis of a public immunotherapy-treated dataset (CT26_ERP114266) demonstrated that Lims2 expression was higher in responder samples compared with baseline or non-responder samples following combined anti–CTLA-4 and anti–PD-1 treatment (Fig. 9I).

To provide experimental support for the computational observations, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were performed in LUAD cell lines. Overexpression of LIMS2 in NCL-H358 and A549 cells resulted in increased secretion levels of the chemokines CXCL10 and CCL5 in culture supernatants compared with control cells (Fig. 9J–K). Collectively, these results indicate that LIMS2 expression is closely associated with immune infiltration characteristics, immune checkpoint–related gene expression, and immunomodulatory chemokine production, suggesting a potential link between LIMS2 and the tumor immune microenvironment in LUAD.

Discussion

LIM domain–containing proteins play critical roles in cell–matrix adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and signal transduction, primarily through their participation in focal adhesion complexes and integrin-associated scaffolds [13, 14]. Members of the LIMS family, particularly LIMS1 (PINCH1), have been relatively well studied and are known to participate in tumor cell survival, migration, and therapy resistance across multiple cancer types, including pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, and lung cancer [4, 15, 16]. In contrast, LIMS2 (PINCH2) has received considerably less attention, and its biological role in malignancy remains incompletely defined.

Existing studies on LIMS2 are scarce and largely context-dependent. Limited evidence suggests that LIMS2 may function as a negative regulator of tumor progression in certain settings. For example, LIMS2 downregulation has been reported in osteosarcoma, where its restoration suppressed cell growth and migration, implicating a potential tumor-inhibitory role [8]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, LIMS2 has been included in integrin-related prognostic signatures and was associated with immune-related features, although mechanistic validation was not the primary focus [17]. Importantly, systematic investigations of LIMS2 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) are currently lacking, and its expression pattern, clinical relevance, regulatory mechanisms, and functional consequences in this disease context remain largely unexplored.

Against this background, the present study was designed to fill a knowledge gap rather than extend an already mature research line. Unlike LIMS1, which has been extensively linked to oncogenic signaling and therapy resistance, LIMS2 has not been comprehensively characterized in LUAD at either the transcriptomic or functional level. By integrating pan-cancer and LUAD-specific datasets with in vitro functional assays, our work provides a structured and disease-focused evaluation of LIMS2.

Our results consistently indicate that LIMS2 expression is reduced in LUAD and that lower expression is associated with unfavorable clinical features and outcomes. These findings extend prior observations from other tumor types into the LUAD context and suggest that LIMS2 downregulation may represent a recurrent but understudied event in solid tumors. Importantly, rather than attributing this reduction to a single dominant mechanism, we systematically assessed multiple regulatory layers—including copy number variation, promoter methylation, and m6A-related post-transcriptional regulation—reflecting the current understanding that tumor suppressor-like genes are often regulated through combined genomic and epigenetic mechanisms.

Functionally, previous reports on LIMS2 have largely been descriptive or correlative. In contrast, our gain-of-function experiments in LUAD cell lines provide direct evidence that LIMS2 restoration is associated with reduced proliferative and migratory capacities. These phenotypic effects are consistent with the known biological roles of focal adhesion–associated proteins and align with the enrichment of LIMS2-interacting networks in adhesion- and cytoskeleton-related pathways [18, 19]. While MAPK and Hippo signaling emerged from enrichment analyses and phosphorylation profiling, we deliberately interpret these findings as pathway-level associations rather than definitive mechanistic claims, in line with current standards for exploratory mechanistic studies.

Another area in which prior evidence remains limited is the relationship between LIMS2 and tumor immunity. Although integrin- and adhesion-related molecules are increasingly recognized as modulators of immune cell trafficking and tumor–immune interactions, direct studies focusing on LIMS2 and immune regulation are rare [20]. Recent studies have further emphasized that tumor progression is not solely driven by intrinsic genetic alterations of cancer cells, but is profoundly shaped by dynamic interactions within the tumor immune microenvironment (TME). Emerging evidence highlights that alterations in stromal architecture, immune cell composition, and cytokine signaling collectively influence tumor growth, immune evasion, and therapeutic responsiveness. In particular, the spatial distribution and functional states of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, macrophage subsets, and immune checkpoint-expressing cells have been shown to determine the balance between anti-tumor immunity and immune suppression. These studies also underscore that gene expression changes associated with cytoskeletal remodeling and cell–matrix interactions may indirectly modulate immune cell recruitment and checkpoint signaling within the TME [21–23]. In this context, our findings that LIMS2 expression correlates with multiple immune cell populations and immune checkpoint molecules suggest that its downregulation in LUAD may not only influence intrinsic tumor cell behavior but also be associated with alterations in the immune landscape. Although the precise mechanistic links remain to be clarified, these observations are consistent with the growing understanding that structural and signaling molecules involved in cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization can contribute to shaping immune contexture and potentially affect immunotherapeutic outcomes.

To complement these computational findings, we performed ELISA-based validation of chemokines with established roles in immune cell recruitment. The observed increase in CXCL10 and CCL5 secretion following LIMS2 overexpression provides preliminary experimental support for a link between LIMS2 status and immune-modulatory signals. However, given the absence of clinical tissue validation and in vivo models, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating, consistent with the early stage of research on LIMS2 in cancer immunity.

One limitation of the present study is the lack of independent immunohistochemical validation of LIMS2 protein expression in LUAD clinical specimens. Although transcript-level downregulation was consistently observed, further protein-level confirmation using well-annotated patient cohorts will be necessary to establish its clinical applicability.

Taken together, while the broader biological functions of LIM domain proteins have been widely studied, LIMS2 itself remains an underexplored molecule in cancer biology, particularly in LUAD. The present study does not seek to redefine established oncogenic pathways but rather to introduce LIMS2 as a candidate suppressive and immune-associated factor in LUAD, supported by multi-layered bioinformatic analyses and targeted experimental validation. By situating our findings within the limited but growing body of LIMS2-related research, this work provides a foundation for future studies aimed at clarifying its mechanistic role and potential clinical relevance.
