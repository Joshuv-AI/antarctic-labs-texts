Neurohumoral Biomarker Profile Associated With Blood Pressure Response After Renal Denervation
Authors: Wei Liang, Yingdong Wang, Long Ai, Runmin Sun, Xu Zhao, Heng Yu, Xiaofang Zhang, Caie Li, Jianshu Chen, Ruixin Ma, Qiongying Wang, Xiaowei Zhang, Jing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBlood pressure (BP) response after renal denervation (RDN) remains heterogeneous. This study explored whether baseline neurohumoral and inflammation‐related biomarkers were associated with a pronounced ambulatory BP response after RDN. This single‐center post hoc analysis included 40 patients with uncontrolled essential hypertension who underwent RDN in four clinical trial programs. High response was defined as a reduction of ≥10 mmHg in 24‐h ambulatory systolic blood pressure (24‐h SBP) at 6 months. Baseline clinical, anatomical, procedural, and biomarker variables were compared between high‐ and low‐response groups. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic analyses were performed to evaluate candidate biomarkers. RDN was associated with reductions in ambulatory BP at 24 h and 6 months. At 6 months, office SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) decreased by 21.57 ± 9.90, 9.70 ± 9.94 mmHg, and 24‐h ambulatory SBP and DBP decreased by 14.38 ± 13.55/9.25 ± 8.57 mmHg. BP reductions were broadly comparable across the four catheter subgroups. Baseline sex, age, ablation number, renal artery diameter and length, aldosterone did not differ between response groups. High responders had higher baseline upright plasma renin activity, 24‐h urinary norepinephrine, serum neuropeptide Y, and plasma adenosine triphosphate levels. These four biomarkers were associated with high response in logistic regression, and their combined model showed high apparent discrimination. Patients with a pronounced ambulatory BP response after RDN showed a biomarker profile consistent with higher neurohumoral activation. This profile may help characterize RDN responsiveness, but validation in larger prospective cohorts is required.Keywords: biomarker, blood pressure, hypertension, predicting model, renal denervation, renal denervation response

Introduction

Renal denervation (RDN) has re‐emerged as a catheter‐based interventional option for selected patients with uncontrolled or resistant hypertension, supported by randomized trials and registry evidence demonstrating reductions in office and ambulatory blood pressure (BP) [1, 2]. Despite these advances, individual BP responses to RDN remain heterogeneous [3, 4]. This variability remains an important barrier to broader implementation and highlights the need to better understand biological factors associated with treatment response. Several clinical and procedural factors have been proposed to influence RDN response, including baseline BP, medication burden, renal artery anatomy, ablation strategy, and sympathetic activation [5, 6]. However, existing tools for identifying likely responders have not been consistently validated across populations, and no universally accepted biomarker‐based approach is currently available for routine clinical decision‐making.

Because renal sympathetic nerves interact with the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), catecholamine release, vascular tone, and immune‐inflammatory signaling, baseline neurohumoral biomarkers may help characterize biological phenotypes associated with RDN responsiveness [7]. In this study, we evaluated several candidate indicators that have not been routinely incorporated into previous RDN response analyses, with the aim of generating hypotheses for future validation rather than establishing a clinically ready prediction model. Sympathetic overactivity in hypertension may be reflected, in part, by circulating and urinary catecholamine measures, including norepinephrine (NE) and epinephrine, which may capture biological pathways targeted by RDN [8]. Neuropeptide Y (NPY), a neuropeptide abundantly expressed in the cardiovascular system and autonomic nerve fibers, has also been implicated in BP regulation. Experimental evidence suggests that plasma NE and NPY levels are positively correlated with BP in hypertensive rat models, indicating a potential mechanistic link [9]. Furthermore, inflammation has been recognized as a key pathophysiological contributor to essential hypertension, particularly T cell–mediated immune activation and oxidative stress, which are believed to be central mechanisms in resistant hypertension. Therefore, inflammation‐related biomarkers were included at baseline to explore their association with the antihypertensive response to RDN [10, 11].

Accordingly, we conducted an exploratory single‐center post hoc analysis of patients undergoing RDN within four clinical trial programs. The primary objective was to examine whether baseline neurohumoral biomarkers were associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in 24‐h ambulatory systolic blood pressure (24‐h SBP) measured by ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (24‐h ambulatory BP) at 6 months. A secondary, descriptive objective was to summarize BP changes across device subgroups.

Methods

Study Subjects

Patients enrolled in four RDN clinical trials conducted at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024, were included in this study. The four trials were as follows: the Randomized, Sham‐Controlled, Multi‐Center Iberis‐HTN Trial [1] (Shanghai Antong Medical Technology Co., NCT02901704); the Netrod six‐electrode radiofrequency RDN system for uncontrolled hypertension: a sham‐controlled trial [12] (Shanghai Meiliweiye Medical Technology Co., NCT03261375); a prospective, multicenter, blinded, randomized controlled trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of a disposable renal artery ultrasound ablation catheter system (Shanghai Hantong Medical Technology Co., PID 247651); and FlashPoint RDN Under Columbus 3D Mapping System Guidance for Treating Hypertension(Shanghai Hongdian Medical Technology Co., NCT05590871). Follow‐up for all participants was completed as of May 2025. All participants provided written informed consent, and the protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University. The trials were strictly conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Cohort

Adult patients (18–65 years) with uncontrolled essential hypertension for ≥6 months were included. Prior to enrollment, all patients provided informed consent and were transitioned to protocol‐defined antihypertensive regimens as part of standardized therapy. After a 4‐week stabilization period, uncontrolled hypertension was confirmed by office blood pressure (OBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP) 150–180 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mmHg) and 24‐h SBP ≥135 mmHg, thereby minimizing white‐coat effects. Anatomical suitability for RDN was verified during a second screening by renal angiography, requiring a renal artery diameter ≥3 mm and length ≥20 mm. After RDN, the protocol‐defined antihypertensive regimen was intended to remain unchanged throughout follow‐up unless modification was clinically required for safety reasons. Furthermore, individuals identified with secondary hypertension or fulfilling any exclusion criteria were excluded from the study. The standardized antihypertensive regimens, medication‐management procedures for each trial were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

RDN Procedure

RDN was performed using commercially available denervation systems, including radiofrequency‐based catheters (Iberis, Netrod and Flashpoint catheters, Shanghai, China), and an endovascular ultrasound‐based system (Hawksonic, Shanghai, China). All procedures were carried out via transfemoral arterial access, with both renal arteries treated during the same intervention. Comprehensive procedural details have been previously described in the literature [1, 12]. Patients who achieved a reduction of ≥10 mmHg in 24‐h SBP at 6 months post‐procedure were classified as the high‐response group. Based on the change in 24‐h SBP, the cohort was stratified into high‐response and low‐response groups, and baseline characteristics were compared between the two groups.

Patient Characteristics

Patient demographics, baseline blood pressure parameters, renal artery characteristics, and relevant laboratory indicators, including plasma renin activity (PRA, upright position), aldosterone levels, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), electrolytes, and 24‐h urinary NE were collected before the procedure. Patients were followed according to the study protocol, and clinical efficacy outcomes were collected, including OBP and 24‐h ambulatory BP after the procedure and at the 6‐month follow‐up.

Serological Biomarkers

Fasting peripheral venous blood was collected and processed to obtain serum and plasma, which were aliquoted and stored at −80°C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. Serum, plasma, and preserved 24‐h urine samples were collected and processed according to standardized procedures for storage and laboratory analysis (The analytical procedures and reagents are provided in the Supplementary File). Serum tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases‐1 (TIMP‐1) and matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9) were quantified by a double‐antibody sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs) and NPY were determined by competitive ELISA. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) was measured via chemiluminescence, whereas PRA and aldosterone were assayed using chemiluminescent immunoassays (Detailed procedures and reagents are provided in the Supplementary File).

Definition of High and Low Responders

Previous studies have used different operational definitions of blood pressure response after RDN. Some studies defined responders as patients with a reduction of ≥10 mmHg in office systolic blood pressure after RDN, whereas others used a reduction of ≥5 mmHg in 24‐hSBP or mean 24‐h ambulatory BP as the response criterion [13, 14]. Because OBP may be influenced by the white‐coat effect and may not fully reflect out‐of‐office BP burden, 24‐h ambulatory BP has been widely used to confirm sustained hypertension and to assess BP changes after RDN [15]. Therefore, for the purpose of this analysis, high responders were defined as patients with a reduction of ≥10 mmHg in 24‐hSBP from baseline to 6 months after RDN [16].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed before analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non‐normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Between‐group comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Between‐group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using independent‐samples t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Changes in blood pressure from baseline were summarized as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. Between‐subgroup differences in treatment effects were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for baseline values. Variables showing significant differences between responders and non‐responders in baseline comparisons were entered into binary logistic regression analysis to evaluate their associations with response to RDN. The predicted probabilities derived from the multivariable logistic regression model were used to construct the combined ROC curve. ROC analyses were also performed for individual candidate biomarkers, and the AUC with corresponding 95% confidence intervals was calculated. A nomogram was constructed based on the multivariable logistic regression model. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Figures were created using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and R software.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 40 patients were included in the study, with 10 patients assigned to each device group. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 40 patients enrolled. The mean age was 42.65 ± 9.90 years, and 75% were male, with an average body mass index (BMI) of 26.51 ± 3.95 kg/m2. Left ventricular hypertrophy was observed in 57.5% of patients. Baseline OBP was markedly elevated, with values of 161.83 ± 14.37/96.33 ± 9.42 mmHg, 24‐h SBP and 24‐h DBP were 147.00 ± 11.33 mmHg and 92.80 ± 8.42 mmHg, respectively. The baseline 24‐h mean heart rate was 77.80 ± 7.40 bpm. Renal function was preserved, and electrolyte levels were within normal ranges. Serological biomarkers, including PRA, aldosterone, TIMP‐1, MMP‐9, AGEs, urinary norepinephrine, NPY, and ATP, were summarized (Table 1).

Efficacy of RDN

24‐hour ambulatory BP demonstrated an early reduction in BP 24‐h after RDN, with mean decreases of 17.53 ± 10.67 mmHg in SBP and 10.85 ± 9.06 mmHg in DBP compared with baseline. At 6 months, office SBP and DBP were reduced by 21.57 ± 9.90 mmHg and 9.70 ± 9.94 mmHg, and the reductions in 24‐h SBP and DBP were 14.38 ± 13.55 mmHg and 9.25 ± 8.57 mmHg, confirming the sustained antihypertensive effect of RDN. No significant changes were observed in overall heart rate, day‐time heart rate, or night‐time heart rate at 6 months post‐procedure compared with baseline (Figure 1).

Comparison of Different RDN Devices

Across the four RDN device groups, reductions in 24‐h ambulatory BP were observed at both 24 h and 6 months after the procedure. At 24 h, there were no significant between‐group differences in changes in 24‐h SBP or DBP, day‐time BP, night‐time BP, or heart rate parameters. At 6 months, reductions in 24‐h SBP remained comparable among the Iberis, Netrod, Flashpoint, and Hawksonic groups (p = 0.320). Most 24‐h ambulatory BP and heart rate parameters did not differ significantly across device groups at 6 months, except for night‐time DBP (p = 0.017). Changes in eGFR also differed among device groups (p = 0.008), although the magnitude of change was small (Table 2).

High and Low RDN Responder

The cohort was stratified into high‐response (≥10 mmHg reduction) and low‐response (<10 mmHg reduction) groups, and their baseline characteristics were compared. There were no significant differences between groups in sex, age, number of ablation applications, or renal artery diameter and length (p > 0.05). Likewise, plasma aldosterone, serum TIMP‐1, serum MMP‐9, and serum AGEs levels did not differ significantly (p > 0.05). In contrast, PRA, 24‐h urinary norepinephrine, serum NPY, and plasma ATP levels differed significantly between high and low responders (p < 0.05, Table 3, Figure 2).

Prediction Model of RDN Response

Multivariable logistic regression (high response defined as a ≥10 mmHg reduction in 24‐h ambulatory BP SBP at 6 months) identified four independent predictors: PRA (OR 3.42; 95% CI 1.06–11.11; p = 0.040), 24‐h urinary NE (nmol/24 h: OR 1.02; 95% CI 1.00–1.04; p = 0.030), serum NPY (per pg/mL: OR 1.08; 95% CI 1.01–1.16; p = 0.034), and plasma ATP (per µmol/L: OR 9.94; 95% CI 1.02–96.97; p = 0.048). A nomogram incorporating these variables estimated individual response probability (The regression table and nomogram are shown in the Supplementary File). In ROC analyses, discrimination was highest for ATP (AUC 0.84; 95% CI 0.70–0.99), followed by PRA (0.80; 0.66–0.95), NPY (0.75; 0.59–0.91), and urinary norepinephrine (0.62; 0.45–0.80); the four‐marker model achieved an AUC of 0.97 (0.92–1.00) (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this exploratory single‐center post hoc analysis of 40 patients undergoing RDN, three findings should be emphasized. First, RDN was followed by reductions in 24‐h ambulatory BP at 24 h and 6 months. Second, baseline upright PRA, 24‐h urinary NE, serum NPY, and plasma ATP were higher in patients who achieved a clinically meaningful 6‐month 24‐h SBP response. Third, although a four‐biomarker panel showed high apparent discrimination, the model was not externally validated and should be interpreted only as hypothesis‐generating. In addition, the ultrasound‐based Hawksonic RDN catheter showed BP reductions comparable to those observed in the other ablation catheter subgroups. Although the sample size remained limited, this pattern was in line with findings from the RADIOSOUND‐HTN trial [17]. Nevertheless, confirmation awaits the publication of the full study results.

Over the past decade, randomized trials, including the SPYRAL program, have demonstrated the BP–lowering efficacy of RDN, and large real‐world registries such as the Global SYMPLICITY Registry have provided supportive evidence for its long‐term effectiveness and safety [2, 18]. Our results are consistent with these data, showing sustained reductions in 24‐h ambulatory BP beyond the periprocedural period. Across the four device subgroups, BP reductions were observed at both early and 6‐month follow‐up, with broadly comparable changes in most 24‐h ambulatory BP parameters. In this context, device selection in routine practice may reasonably incorporate operator experience, renal artery anatomy, device availability, and procedural logistics.

Nevertheless, response heterogeneity remains the principal limitation: moving from “main‐artery only” to “main‐plus‐branch” ablation raises the responder rate only from 66% to 73%, leaving roughly one‐third of patients classified as non‐responders [19, 20]. This residual variability likely reflects patient‐specific factors; hence, exploring candidate biomarkers associated with RDN response remains clinically relevant. The core contribution of this study is to propose a biomarker‐based perspective for identifying patients who may be more likely to experience a pronounced blood pressure response after RDN. Baseline sex, age, number of ablations, main renal artery diameter and length, plasma aldosterone, serum TIMP‐1, serum MMP‐9, and serum AGEs did not differ between the high‐ and low‐response groups (p > 0.05). In contrast, the high‐response group showed significantly higher levels of upright PRA, 24‐h urinary NE, serum NPY, and plasma ATP. ROC analysis showed that the combined use of these four biomarkers yielded high apparent discrimination for a pronounced RDN response, although this finding requires further validation. To our knowledge, this is among the first exploratory clinical analyses to evaluate these neurohumoral and inflammation‐related indices together in the context of RDN response.

Upright PRA may be particularly relevant to RDN responsiveness because it links renal sympathetic efferent activation with RAAS‐mediated blood pressure regulation. Increased renal sympathetic nerve activity contributes to hypertension by stimulating renin release, enhancing renal tubular sodium reabsorption, and reducing renal blood flow [21, 22]. This interpretation is supported by the SPYRAL HTN‐OFF MED analysis, in which RDN significantly reduced plasma renin activity and aldosterone levels compared with sham control, and higher baseline plasma renin activity was associated with greater reductions in both office and 24‐h systolic blood pressure after RDN [23]. Therefore, the higher upright PRA observed in the high‐response group in our study may indicate a renal sympathetic–RAAS phenotype that is more susceptible to blood pressure reduction after RDN.

Emschermann et al. [24]. reported that higher baseline NE concentrations were associated with greater BP reductions after RDN. Because forearm venous NE is posture dependent and can underestimate renal sympathetic outflow, catecholamine metabolites measured in urine may better reflect renal sympathetic activity [25]. In particular, 24‐h urinary NE excretion is a clinically accessible surrogate of systemic sympathetic activation, although it does not directly quantify renal sympathetic nerve activity. The higher 24‐h urinary NE level observed in the high‐response group further supports the presence of greater sympathetic activation. Notably, although 24‐h urinary NE was the most direct clinically available marker of sympathetic activation in this study, it showed the lowest AUC (0.62, 0.45–0.80) among the four biomarkers. This finding suggests that urinary NE alone may not be sufficient for individual‐level discrimination of RDN response, despite its significant between‐group difference. The modest AUC may reflect substantial interindividual variability, overlap between response groups, and the fact that urinary NE reflects systemic rather than renal sympathetic activity. Thus, urinary NE may be better interpreted as one component of a broader neurohumoral profile rather than as a standalone predictor of RDN responsiveness.

NPY is co‐released with NE from sympathetic varicosities, augments vasoconstriction and modulates central sympathetic outflow through pathways involving the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus [26]. Accordingly, heightened sympathetic tone is accompanied by concurrent increases in NPY and NE, supporting serum NPY as a candidate biomarker for predicting response to RDN. ATP, a key mediator of immune and inflammatory signaling, increases under hemodynamic stress; erythrocyte‐derived ATP can activate antigen‐presenting cells via P2×7 receptors, thereby amplifying T‐cell–mediated vascular inflammation and raising BP [21, 27]. Because RDN has been shown to attenuate systemic inflammation, elevated plasma ATP may identify patients whose hypertension is driven by sympathetic–inflammatory cross‐talk and who may therefore derive greater benefit from RDN. In line with these mechanisms, the higher baseline serum NPY and plasma ATP levels observed in the high‐response group may reflect a neurohumoral and sympathetic–inflammatory phenotype associated with greater blood pressure reduction after RDN. Notably, although ATP was independently associated with high RDN response in the logistic regression analysis, the confidence interval was wide, indicating limited precision of the estimate. Therefore, the large odds ratio should be viewed as supportive of a potential biological association rather than as evidence of a stable standalone predictor.

The association between baseline upright PRA, 24‐h urinary NE, serum NPY, plasma ATP, and RDN response may reflect a neurohumoral phenotype in which blood pressure is more dependent on renal sympathetic and related neurovascular pathways. Therefore, exploring the relationship between these biomarkers and a pronounced RDN response is clinically relevant, because it may help distinguish patients with a more sympathetically or neurohumorally driven form of hypertension and provide biological support for identifying individuals who are more likely to derive benefit from RDN.

In contrast, plasma aldosterone, serum TIMP‐1, serum MMP‐9, and serum AGEs did not differ significantly between the high‐ and low‐response groups. Aldosterone has been linked to resistant hypertension through sodium retention, volume expansion, vascular inflammation, and fibrosis [28, 29]. In the present study, baseline aldosterone concentrations were generally within the reference range and did not differ significantly between the high‐response and low‐response groups. This finding may reflect the exclusion of patients with confirmed primary aldosteronism, the differential effects of the four standardized antihypertensive regimens on renin and aldosterone concentrations, and the limited statistical power associated with the relatively small and unequal response groups [30, 31]. Accordingly, the absence of a significant between‐group difference should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or as excluding an important role for aldosterone in resistant hypertension. MMP‐9 and TIMP‐1 are involved in extracellular matrix turnover and vascular remodeling, and MMP/TIMP imbalance has been associated with arterial stiffness and hypertension‐mediated vascular damage [32, 33]. AGEs are associated with endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, oxidative stress, and glycation‐related vascular injury in hypertension [34, 35]. Thus, these negative findings support the central interpretation of this study: high responders appeared to be characterized more by neurohumoral activation than by structural vascular remodeling or glycation‐related vascular injury.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‐center post hoc analysis with a small sample size, and each device subgroup included only 10 patients; therefore, comparisons across catheter systems should be considered descriptive. Second, the biomarker analyses were exploratory and may be influenced by posture, sodium intake, concomitant medications, renal handling, completeness of 24‐h urine collection, and pre‐analytical factors, particularly for plasma ATP. Third, these biomarkers do not directly measure renal sympathetic nerve activity, and the observed associations should be interpreted as surrogate evidence of neurohumoral activation. Fourth, the combined biomarker model was not internally or externally validated, and its apparent discrimination may overestimate performance. Finally, although the parent trials incorporated standardized antihypertensive medication run‐in periods and restrictions on postprocedural medication changes, medication‐related confounding cannot be completely excluded. The four trials used different standardized regimens, and several antihypertensive drug classes may directly influence plasma renin activity, aldosterone, and sympathetic biomarkers.

In conclusion, RDN was associated with sustained reductions in 24‐h ambulatory BP across catheter subgroups. High responders showed higher baseline upright PRA, 24‐h urinary NE, serum NPY, and plasma ATP levels, supporting a neurohumoral phenotype linked to greater RDN response, although this biomarker profile requires validation in larger prospective cohorts.
