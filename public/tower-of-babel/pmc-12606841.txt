Rotation of Corydalis yanhusuo with different crops enhances its quality and soil nutrients: a multi-dimensional analysis of rhizosphere microecology
Authors: Jia Liu, Qiang Yuan, Kejie Zhang, Xiaoxiao Sheng, Zixuan Zhu, Ning Sui, Hui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe medicinal value of the herb Corydalis yanhusuo W. T. Wang has driven its cultivation expansion. However, long-term monoculture of C. yanhusuo causes severe soil degradation and increases the risk of soil-borne diseases. Crop rotation improves soil sustainability and reduces pathogen pressure. Further research is necessary to establish successful crop rotation systems for C. yanhusuo and to comprehend their effects on soil microbial populations and physicochemical qualities.MethodsIn this study, we established three crop cultivation systems, including CR (C. yanhusuo–rice rotation), CS (C. yanhusuo–soybean rotation), and CN (continuous cropping of C. yanhusuo), to examine sustainable models suitable for C. yanhusuo cultivation and their regulatory effects on the soil. To assess alterations in the physicochemical characteristics of the soil, we employed chemical analysis. The microbial makeup and roles of bacterial and fungal communities were compared using high-throughput amplicon sequencing. We investigated the effects of these rotations on soil ecosystem functions through the use of structural equation modeling.ResultsThe results showed that CR and CS increased C. yanhusuo tuber yield and tetrahydropalmatine and protopine contents compared to CN. The concentrations of soil organic matter, total/hydrolysable nitrogen, total/available phosphorus, and soil enzyme activity were significantly higher in CR and CS compared to CN. Firmicutes grew in rhizosphere soil in CS, while Actinobacteria abundance increased in CR. The abundance of Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Rhodanobacter, and Trichoderma increased. Meanwhile, that of Flavobacterium, Fusarium, and Plectosphaerella decreased in CR and CS. CR and CS had increased bacterial and fungal community network linkages and modularity. Structural equation modelling revealed that crop rotation affects soil nutrients through microbial community dynamics. These soil nutrients, in turn, influence the yield and quality of C. yanhusuo.Conclusion In this study, we have deepened our understanding of the effects of rice and soybeans on the microbial community and physicochemical properties of C. yanhusuo cultivation soil. The findings can inform the development of ecological restoration. methods for the sustained production of C. yanhusuo, increasing green agricultural development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07594-4.Keywords: Rhizosphere microorganism, Corydalis yanhusuo, Crop rotation, Soybean, Rice

Background

Corydalis yanhusuo W. T. Wang is a common herb used in traditional Chinese medicine. Its medicinal value has resulted in a significant expansion in the scale of planting in recent years [1]. Clinically, the medicinal properties of the dried tubers of C. yanhusuo enhance blood circulation, eliminate blood stasis, regulate qi, and alleviate pain [1]. The core active ingredients are benzoisoquinoline alkaloids such as tetrahydropalmatine, protopine, and tetrahydroberberine [2]– [3]. Modern pharmacological research has confirmed that the analgesic effect of total alkaloids in C. yanhusuo can reach up to 40% of that of morphine [2]– [3]. The significant rise in the medicinal demand for C. yanhusuo has led to continual cropping, which poses challenges that are the primary impediment to the sustainable development of the traditional medicine sector. Continuous cropping of C. yanhusuo can lead to soil deterioration, and reduced tuber yield and quality. This phenomenon is underlain by an ecological mechanism that is similar to that of soil acidification, nutrient imbalance, and pest and disease outbreaks caused by continuous crop cultivation [4]– [5].

Crop rotation, a key technology in sustainable agriculture, enhances soil nutrient availability and suppresses the incidence of soil-borne diseases by reconstructing the structure and functional network of the rhizosphere microbial community [6]. Compared with monocultures, diversified crop rotations have accelerated nutrient cycling based on increased contents of bioavailable nitrogen, soil organic matter, and available potassium, and higher soil microbial metabolic capacity [7]. Notably, in a diversified crop rotation system, rotation patterns formed by different crop combinations exhibit unique manifestations of soil ecological functions and nutrient cycling. Typical examples of such systems are rotations with soybean or rice [8]. These two crop rotation models have common characteristics. For example, to a certain extent, they can both improve soil properties and create a favourable soil environment for crop growth [9]. However, these models differ in their influence on soil microbial diversity and their effectiveness in pest and disease control [10]. Soybean rotation successfully achieves nitrogen self-sufficiency through the unique mechanism of nitrogen fixation by rhizobia. The nitrogen fixation process in soybeans influences a unique microbial community involved in the nitrogen cycle. In rotation systems, soybean roots secrete phenolic acids that stimulate the activity of nitrogen-fixing bacteria within the phyla Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, leading to increased soil nitrogen content [11]. Northeast China’s corn-soybean rotation model has demonstrated remarkable soil improvement capabilities [12]. Soybean rotation significantly reduces the relative abundance of various pathogens, such as Fusarium, while increasing the number of biocontrol agents, such as Trichoderma. It also enhances the soil organic matter and nitrogen accumulation [13]– [14]. Rice rotation plays a key role in regulating the redox potential and influencing the soil microbial community structure, primarily through water layer management. In rice rotation, the relative abundance of fungi significantly decreases, whereas that of Acidobacteria increases. These changes are associated with an enhanced rate of soil organic matter mineralization [15]– [16]. Rice rotation markedly enhances the activity of soil microbes involved in the carbon cycle. In the rice–soybean rotation, soil microbial biomass carbon increases, which is associated with the increased abundance of Actinobacteria and Chloroflexi [17]. The alternating wet–dry environment promotes the decomposition of recalcitrant organic matter. For instance, the rice–Astragalus sinicus rotation increases the rate of soil organic carbon mineralization and the proportion of lignin-degrading bacteria [18]. Research indicated that a rice-rice-Chinese milk vetch rotation pattern significantly reduced the incidence of panicle neck blasts [19]. Thus, diversified rotations, such as soybean and rice rotations, hold great potential for the control of soil nutrients, microbial ecology, and the prevention and control of diseases, among other aspects.

Soil microorganisms are essential for the decomposition of organic matter, the cycling of nutrients in the soil, and the degradation of pollutants. The diversity of the microbial community and its functions directly affect the stability and productivity of soil ecosystems [20–22]. The ecological effects of crop rotations, implemented to improve the soil nutrient status, are mainly achieved through a complex biochemical reaction network driven by soil microorganisms [23]. Differences in exudation patterns across the root systems of different crops select for specific microbial groups, resulting in the specialisation of interactions between root systems and microorganisms [24]– [25]. For instance, leguminous crop rotations can directly supplement soil nitrogen through nitrogen fixation by rhizobia [26]. Flavonoids secreted by leguminous root systems can induce Pseudomonas in the rhizosphere to produce iron carriers and promote the activation of insoluble phosphorus [27]. Significant disparities exist in the regulatory effects of various crop rotations on microorganisms. For example, leguminous and non-leguminous crop rotations stimulate nitrogen-fixing bacteria more than gramineous crop rotations [28], while the rotation of water and dry land may alter the microbial community via the redox potential, significantly enhancing the metabolic activities of methanogens and sulphate-reducing bacteria [29]. Rice-rapeseed rotation can significantly change soil microbial communities, reducing the relative abundance of Acidobacteria and increasing that of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes [30]. In the Proteobacteria phylum, autotrophic bacteria carrying the cbbL gene have been shown to enhance the soil microbial carbon sequestration capacity through the RubisCO enzyme. This promotes the transformation of soil organic carbon. Methylibium petroleiphilum, specifically enriched in rice-rapeseed rotation, participates in methane oxidation and short-chain hydrocarbon degradation, optimizing the carbon cycle and releasing nutrients. These metabolic products stimulate the decomposition of organic matter by Bacteroidetes, further optimizing the soil carbon cycle and providing nutrient support for subsequent crop growth [31]– [32]. Thus, the complex interactions between crop rotations and soil microorganisms highlight the crucial function of microbial communities in maintaining soil health and productivity [33]. Considering the significance of soil microorganisms in supporting plant growth and health, it is reasonable that similar principles also apply to the cultivation of Chinese medicinal herbs.

We performed experiments in continuous C. yanhusuo cultivation (CN), rice-C. yanhusuo rotation (CR), and soybean-C. yanhusuo rotation (CS) systems to systematically reveal the ecological effects of these rotations, assess soil physical and chemical characteristics, the content of medicinally active components, and microbial community composition using high-throughput sequencing. We specifically quantified changes in soil physical and chemical properties in the different systems; detected differences in microbial community composition in the different systems through analysis of bulk soil (BS), rhizosphere soil (RS), and roots (R); and identified key microbial groups and their association with the biomass and accumulation of active ingredients of C. yanhusuo. Our findings elucidate the intricate relationships between medicinal plants, soil ecosystems, and rhizosphere microbiota. The results from these experiments across different rotation systems provide novel insights into the effects of various cropping patterns on soil conditions, microbial ecosystems, and the quality of C. yanhusuo, along with the underlying mechanisms, which will aid in optimizing cultivation strategies to enhance yield and medicinal value.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and field experiment

A field test of the Corydalis yanhusuo cultivation site was performed in Dongyang City, Zhejiang Province (29°16’N, 116°36’E). The seeds of C. yanhusuo were gathered in Dongyang City, Zhejiang Province, and verified by Professor Shuili Zhang of Zhejiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine as C. yanhusuo. The voucher specimens of C. yanhusuo (No. ZJ00100157) were deposited in the Zhejiang institute for Food and Drug Control, Hangzhou, China. Permission to collect C. yanhusuo was obtained from Zhejiang Zhennong Chinese Medicine Technology Co., Ltd. The study protocol adhered to the relevant guidelines and legislation at the institutional, national and international levels. This area belongs to the subtropical monsoon climate zone, and the soil texture was loam. Before the experiment, the fundamental physical and chemical properties of the soil were assessed: pH 6.53, soil organic matter (SOM) 13.72 g/kg, total nitrogen (TN) 1.79 g/kg, total phosphorus (TP) 0.71 g/kg, total potassium (TK) 6.50 g/kg, hydrolysable nitrogen (HN) 200.20 mg/kg, available phosphorus (Ava-P) 43.43 mg/kg, and available potassium (Ava-K) 300.15 mg/kg.

To address the challenges caused by continuous cropping of C. yanhusuo, we propose C. yanhusuo - rice (Oryza sativa) and C. yanhusuo - soybean (Glycine max) rotations. According to systematic observations of the phenological stages of C. yanhusuo, its suitable growth period spans from late September to May, with May being the most concentrated harvesting period. The approximately 4–5 months of land idleness provides a time window for the implementation of rotation systems. Through the spatiotemporally coupled cultivation of rice or soybean, land use efficiency can be improved. Moreover, based on the ecological functional complementarity of these two crops, synergistic effects on improvements in soil quality and disease prevention and control can be achieved.

The test period was set from June 2020 to May 2023. This study adopted a randomized grouping design. The experimental area was divided into three experimental groups, each consisting of three replicated plots. Three groups were set up for treatment as follows: (1) CN: C. yanhusuo was planted continuously for three years; (2) CR: C. yanhusuo was planted on plots in which rice had been planted previously, with a rotation period of 3 years; (3) CS: C. yanhusuo was planted in plots in which soybeans had been planted before, with a rotation period of 3 years. Uniform cultivation and management measures were adopted for each plot. The timing of crop planting is detailed in Fig. 1A. Each plot was spatially adjacent to ensure consistent microenvironmental conditions in the field.

The CR system involved the following rice cultivation and management practices: Rice was sown in early June at a seeding rate of 150 kg/ha. Before transplanting, basal fertiliser (N: P2O5:K2O = 15:15:15, 300 kg/ha) was applied without using top-dressing fertiliser. Conversely, during cultivation, a long-term flooding strategy was adopted; water depth was maintained at 5–10 cm. Following rice harvest, the water was drained to prepare for planting C. yanhusuo. Meanwhile, the soybean cultivation and management practices in the CS system were as follows: Soybean was sown in mid-June at a seeding rate of 60 kg/ha. Basal fertiliser (N: P2O5:K2O = 15:15:15, 300 kg/ha) was applied without using top-dressing fertiliser. Supplementary irrigation was provided during critical growth stages (flowering/grain filling). For C. yanhusuo cultivation, uniform planting and management practices were adopted across the three treatment fields. Following rice and soybean harvest, as well as in the fields without preceding crops, the land was uniformly ploughed to a depth of 25 cm. For C. yanhusuo planting, a uniform basal fertiliser was applied to all plots, again, without using top-dressing fertiliser during the growth period. Other management practices, such as irrigation and pest control, were consistent across all systems.

Collection and yield calculation of C. yanhusuo

Corydalis yanhusuo was planted on a per-hole basis in each plot. The row spacing between holes was 10 cm. Samples were harvested in early to mid-May, during which the tubers of C. yanhusuo from each hole were separately collected and placed in sampling bags. Field plots measuring 1 m×1 m were selected and excavated to serve as yield-testing plots. Three replicates were set up to conduct the yield measurement work. After washing, the medicinal tubers were oven-dried at 45 °C until a constant weight was achieved. The average dry weight per hole (DW, in kg) was then measured. The total number of holes in each plot was recorded as N, the area of each plot as S (m2), and the total yield for each treatment as Y . Gathering samples of bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots

To ensure the representativeness of the sample space and repeatability of the experiment, 15 independent plants were randomly selected as biological repeat units from the four corners and the central area of each experimental plot. During this process, the potential impact of edge effects was considered. Therefore, a 1 m-wide buffer zone was established around each plot; no sampling was conducted within these buffer zones. The experimental site was located in an area with uniform land cover and microclimate conditions, minimizing spatial heterogeneity. Bulk soil collection: The base of the plant stem was held, and a depth of 30 cm was used as the excavation boundary. The root system was gently shaken to thoroughly mix the naturally detached loose soil. Rhizosphere soil collection: A sterile soft-bristled brush was used to gently remove the soil layer closely adhering to the root. The rhizosphere soil samples were filtered through a 2-mm aperture sterile sieve to remove plant residues and then transferred into sterile centrifuge tubes. The pretreatment of root samples was based on the root surface disinfection process established by Herrera et al. [34]. The root samples were successively submerged in 75% ethanol for 3 min, rinsed with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 4 min, soaked in 75% ethanol for 45 s, and finally rinsed five times with sterile water for gradient purification to ensure the complete removal of surface microorganisms and soil particles.

Identification of C. yanhusuo protopine and tetrahydropalmatine

C. yanhusuo plants (15 biological replicates) from each system were collected and their tubers pulverised into a powder after being oven-dried to a consistent weight. Twenty milliliters of a 20:1 methanol-ammonia solution were used to extract 0.5 g of powdered tubers at room temperature for one hour in an ultrasonic bath. Prior to high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis, the extracts were stored at 4 °C after passing through a Millipore filter with a pore size of 0.22 μm. A stock solution was created by precisely weighing and dissolving the standards tetrahydropalmatine (Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and protopine (Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 46 µg/mL and 35 µg/mL, respectively. Subsequently, several working solutions were prepared by dilution with methanol. HPLC was performed using a 2998 PDA detector with an e2695 separation module equipped with a C18 column (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Eluents A (acetonitrile) and B (0.1% phosphoric acid in water, pH 6.0 with triethylamine) formed the mobile phase.

Assessment of soil physicochemical characteristics and enzymatic activity

The methods for determining various indicators of soil physical and chemical qualities were based on classical literature, as follows [35]. SOM was determined using the K2CrO7 oxidation titration method. TN was quantified using an elemental analyser based on the Kjeldahl method. The soil was decomposed with H2SO4/HClO4, and the molybdenum blue method was employed to quantify TP. Flame photometry was employed to quantify TK after aqua regia digestion. In compliance with the manufacturer’s specifications, assay kits (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were employed to quantify HN, Ava-P, and Ava-K. Using a phenol-sodium hypochlorite colorimetric method, soil urease (S-UE) was measured. 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetry was used to quantify the activity of soil sucrase (S-SC). Soil acid phosphatase (S-ACP) was measured using the disodium phenyl phosphate colorimetric technique.

Extraction of DNA from soil, amplification with PCR, and reading with illumina miseq

We used a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and 1% w/v electrophoresis agarose gel to check the concentration and purity of the DNA. This was done after the samples were treated with the Omega Bio-tek soil DNA extraction kit (Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR primer pairs targeted the bacterial 16 S rRNA V4-V5 region and the fungal ITS1 region were in Table S1. The 10 µL PCR reaction mix included 2 µL of 5× FastPfu buffer, 1.25 mM dNTPs, 0.4 µL of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 0.2 µL of FastPfu DNA polymerase, and 5 ng of template DNA. The PCR amplification process involved initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. After a 10-min extension at 72 °C, the reaction ended at 10 °C. For every sample, three technical replicates were examined. Amplified products were subjected to gel electrophoresis, the bands were purified, and paired-end sequencing was performed using Illumina’s MiSeq PE 250 platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Raw sequencing data (SRR33852956 - SRR33853009) were uploaded to the NCBI.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Raw FASTQ files were concatenated using FLASH (v1.2.7) [36] after being quality-filtered using Fastp (v0.19.6) [37] and demultiplexed using an internal Perl script. After optimising the sequences, UPARSE 7.1 was used to categorise them into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity [38]. Rarefaction curves and alpha diversity indices were generated by Mothur (v1.30.1), which incorporated observed OTUs, Chao 1 richness, and the Shannon index [39]. The Vegan software (v2.5-3) was employed to evaluate the similarity of microbial communities across different samples by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) utilizing Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) revealed multiple bacterial and fungal taxa (phyla to genera) in the groups (LDA score >2, P < 0.05) [40]. FAPROTAX was used to make functional predictions for bacteria based on OTUs [41]. The FUNGuild database was utilized to functionally categorize soil fungal communities, elucidating the relationship between environmental variables and the ecological responsibilities of microorganisms [42]. Structural equation models (SEM) were parameterized using our dataset and adjusted to meet the criteria of a Chi-square test P-value >0.05 and a Fisher’s C test P-value >0.05 to confirm the normal distribution of the data.The standardised total effects of microbial abundance and soil nutrients on the yield and quality of subsequent crops were determined. Microbial abundance was represented by the microbial a-diversity of each treatment group. Soil nutrients were indicated by the PCoA1 score for each treatment group. Visualisation was performed with Gephi (v0.9.2). All data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v24.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance was used to examine group differences.

Results

Effects of different crop rotations on yield and the content of active ingredients of C. yanhusuo

We collected C. yanhusuo biomass in different crop management practices: CN, CR, and CS. For CN, the overall yield of C. yanhusuo tubers was 7,966 kg/ha, while the yields for CR and CS were 12,766 kg/ha and 13,583 kg/ha, respectively (Fig. 1B-C). These results indicate that crop rotations with both rice and soybean can increase the yield of C. yanhusuo by 60.25% and 70.51%, respectively. In addition, the content of the main active ingredients in traditional Chinese medicinal materials is an important quality trait. The content of active components of C. yanhusuo, protopine and tetrahydropalmatine, increased in CR and CS compared to that in CN (Fig. 1D-E). The contents of tetrahydropalmatine and protopine were the highest in CS, with a significant increase by 52.27% and 78.57% relative to the contents in CN, respectively. Similarly, compared to CN, the content of tetrahydropalmatine in CR significantly increased by 45.45%, and that of protopine significantly increased by 64.28%. In conclusion, rotations integrating C. yanhusuo with soybeans and rice boosted C. yanhusuo yield and quality, especially with soybeans.

Crop rotations affect soil physicochemical characteristics and enzyme activity

The physicochemical and enzymatic activities of the soil were tested in all cropping systems (CN, CR, and CS). The physical and chemical features of the soil in CR and CS differed from those in CN. The pH of the soil in CR and CS was increased in comparison to that in CN, although not statistically significant (Fig. 2A). Compared with CN, TN, TP, SOM, HN, and Ava-P contents were increased in CR and CS. The contents in CS were substantially higher than those in CR, with respective values of 2.04 g/kg, 0.98 g/kg, 18.21 g/kg, 307.53 mg/kg, and 68.50 mg/kg (Fig. 2B-C and E-G). TK and Ava-K values increased in both CS and CR, with no significant difference between the two (Fig. 2D and H). Compared with CN, both CR and CS had increased S-SC, S-UE, and S-ACP values, the latter showing the highest increase, with values of 5.98 U/g, 470.28 U/g, and 1375.80 U/g, respectively (Fig. 2I-K). Compared with CR, CS had significantly increased TN, TP, SOM, HN, Ava-P, S-SC, S-UE, and S-ACP values, whereas there were no significant differences in PH, TK, and Ava-K values (Fig. 2L). Figure 2M-N shows a comparison of the percentage effect of soil indicators between CR, CS and CN. Both the CR and CS showed significantly better effects on various soil indicators than the CN, and the CS had a greater effect on SOM, TN, TP, HN, Ava-P, S-UE, and S-ACP.

Impact of different crop rotations on bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root microbial community characteristics

Analysis of sample rarefaction curves (Figure S1) showed that the number of OTUs in the 27 samples from the different cropping systems strongly increased, and the slope of the dilution curves exhibited a declining trend that slowed over time. To explore microbial community diversity in the sequencing results in detail, optimised sequences were clustered, classified, and assigned to OTUs based on similarity. Amplicon sequencing yielded 1,930,083 reads in the bacterial 16 S rRNA region and 2,087,826 reads in the fungal ITS region (Table S2). The total numbers of bacterial OTUs in BS, RS, and R samples were 13,012, 11,478, and 4,385, respectively (Figure S2A). Among the bacteria, the number of OTUs in bulk and rhizosphere soil samples was the greatest in CS, followed by CR, with CN exhibiting the lowest count. In the root samples, the number of OTUs in CR and CS was similar and higher than that in CN. The total number of fungal OTUs in BS, RS, and R samples was 2,964, 2,933, and 956, respectively (Figure S2B). The number of OTUs for fungi and bacteria showed similar trends.

The Chao-1 index indicates microbial richness, while the Shannon index reflects microbial diversity. According to the Chao-1 index, the bulk soil in CS field (4502) had the highest bacterial richness, followed by that in CR (3754). The Chao-1 index of the rhizosphere soil in CS and CR was markedly elevated compared to that in CN. The Chao-1 index in CR and CS root samples was 1670 and 1551, respectively, which was noticeably greater than that in CN (Fig. 3A). Similarly, at the fungal level, the Chao-1 index of the bulk and rhizosphere soils in CS was the highest, followed by that in CR, while that in CN was the lowest. However, fungal richness in root samples did not differ significantly among the three investigated cropping systems (Fig. 3B). The Shannon index (Fig. 3C) for bacteria in CS-bulk soil, CS-rhizosphere soil and CS-root samples was 6.66, 6.42, and 4.53, respectively, substantially higher than those in CN. The Shannon indices of rhizosphere soil and roots in CR were 6.26 and 4.25 respectively, which was also considerably higher than those in CN. CS and CR exhibited a substantially greater fungal diversity than CN, while fungal diversity did not significantly differ between CS and CR (Fig. 3D). We employed PCoA to calculate β-diversity and delve into the similarities or differences in soil community structure among samples from the three distinct cropping systems. The results revealed that, at both the bacterial and fungal levels, the confidence intervals of samples from the three cropping systems were separated to a certain extent. They were specifically strongly separated between the BS, RS, and R samples (Fig. 3E-F). The data reveal disparities in the composition of soil microbial communities across the different cropping systems.

Differences in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root microbial community composition in different cropping systems

To examine alterations in the microbial community’s composition, we selected the 10 most prevalent phyla for investigation based on taxonomic annotation outcomes (Fig. 4). We found that the dominant phyla among bacteria were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, Acidobacteriota, and Chloroflexi (Fig. 4A). However, the abundance of these dominant microbial phyla significantly differed in the different cropping systems. The most abundant bacterial phylum in CN was Proteobacteria (CN-bulk soil, 10%; CN-rhizosphere soil, 10%; CN-root, 19%). However, in CR, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria was decreased (CR-bulk soil: 8%, CR-rhizosphere soil: 8%, CR-root: 16%). In CS, Proteobacteria exhibited the lowest relative abundance (CS-bulk soil, 8%; CS-rhizosphere soil, 7%; CS-root, 15%). In the crop rotations, the relative abundance of Firmicutes increased relative to that in CN; CS-rhizosphere soil had the highest relative abundance of Firmicutes. The relative abundance of Actinobacteria also increased in the crop rotations vs. that in CN. CR-root had the highest relative abundance of Actinobacteria. Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Mortierellomycota were the most prevalent fungal phyla (Fig. 4B). In CR and CS, the relative abundance of Ascomycota increased in both bulk and rhizosphere soil; CS-bulk soil had the highest relative abundance of Ascomycota, at 16%. There was a discernible decline in the relative abundance of Mortierellomycota in CR and CS relative to that in CN. In contrast, the relative abundance of Basidiomycota increased upon crop rotation. In CR-bulk soil, CR-rhizosphere soil, and CR-root, Basidiomycota accounted for 25%, 11%, and 20% of the fungal community, respectively. In CS-bulk soil, CS-rhizosphere soil, and CS-roots, Basidiomycota accounted for 8%, 18%, and 8%, respectively.

Composition of soil microbial communities in the different cropping systems. Circos diagram illustrating (A) bacterial and (B) fungal composition at the phylum level (B). Heatmap depicting the relative abundance of (C) bacteria and (D) fungi at the genus level

The dominant bacterial genera detected in BS, RS, and R samples were Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Paenibacillus, Bradyrhizobium, and Rhodanobacter (Fig. 4C). In CR and CS, the relative abundances of Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Paenibacillus, and Bradyrhizobium were significantly increased compared to those in CN. Compared to CR, CS had greater relative abundances of these genera. Compared to CN, we also found a significantly increased relative abundance of Rhodanobacter in CR. The relative abundance of Rhodanobacter was higher in CR than in CS. The relative abundances of Dyella, Flavobacterium, and Bordetella decreased in both CR and CS. At the fungal genus level (Fig. 4D), the relative abundance of Trichoderma in bulk and rhizosphere soil was significantly increased in CR and CS relative to that in CN. Furthermore, in comparison to CR, the relative abundance of Trichoderma was elevated in CS. In both CR and CS, the relative abundances of Fusarium, Plectosphaerella, Chordomyces, Cyphellophora, Cladosporium, and Neocosmospora were decreased in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root compared with those in CN. Among these, the abundance of Fusarium, Plectosphaerella, Chordomyces, and Neocosmospora was strongly decreased in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root in CR.

Indicator species analysis in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots in different cropping systems

LDA LEfSe was performed to identify dominant microorganisms in the different cropping systems (Fig. 5). The LDA results revealed significant differences in the relative abundance of bacteria among the three cropping systems. In CN bulk soil, Flavobacterium (LDA = 4.31), Sporosarcina (LDA = 4.13), and Bordetella (LDA = 3.81) were present in large quantities (Fig. 5A). In CR bulk soil, bacteria such as Rhodanobacter (LDA = 3.74), Thermodesulfovibrionia (LDA = 3.71), and Desulfobacca (LDA = 3.36) were abundant. In addition, Bacillus (LDA = 4.30), Sphingomonas (LDA = 4.30), and Nocardioides (LDA = 3.82) were significantly enriched in CS bulk soil. The most abundant bacteria in the rhizosphere were Flavobacterium (LDA = 4.55), Rhodanobacter (LDA = 3.80), and Bacillus (LDA = 4.62) in CN-, CR-, and CS-rhizosphere soil, respectively (Fig. 5B). In root, Kineosporia (LDA = 4.07) and Acidibacter (LDA = 3.92) in CR, and Bacillus (LDA = 4.03) and Pedosphaera (LDA = 3.62) in CS were the most abundant (Fig. 5C). Among the fungi, 10, 10, and 12 fungal biomarkers were identified in the bulk soils of CN, CR, and CS, respectively (Fig. 5D). Relatively abundant fungal taxa in bulk soil included Chordomyces (LDA = 4.54) and Linnemannia (LDA = 4.28) in CN, Trichocladium (LDA = 4.71) and Emericellopsis (LDA = 4.40) in CR, Paraphaeosphaeria (LDA = 4.49) and Perisporiopsis (LDA = 4.19) in CS (Fig. 5D). In rhizosphere soil, a significant relative abundance of Trichocladium (LDA = 5.17) was noted in CR. A significant rise in the prevalence of Paraphaeosphaeria occurred (LDA = 4.83) in CS-rhizosphere soil (Fig. 5E). Within roots, Acaulopage (LDA = 4.17) and Chaetomium (LDA = 3.94) were enriched in the CR, whereas Mortierella (LDA = 4.96) and Leptobacillium (LDA = 4.02) were enriched in CS (Fig. 5F).

Both Flavobacterium and Bordetella were present in CN-bulk soil, CN-rhizosphere soil, and CN-root. Functions of bacterial and fungal communities in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots in the different cropping systems

The ecological roles of microbial communities were forecasted utilizing FAPROTAX and FUNGuild, revealing significant discrepancies among the various cropping systems. Bacterial functional predictions were mainly associated with processes such as nitrate denitrification, plant pathogenicity, hydrocarbon degradation, sulphate respiration, and methanotrophy (Figure S3A). The percentage of bacterial taxa in bulk soil that cause sulphate respiration, dark sulphide oxidation, methanotrophy, and iron respiration was significantly higher in CR than in CN. In contrast, compared with CN, CS showed a notably increased proportion of bacterial taxa associated with nitrogen respiration, nitrate denitrification, and nitrification in bulk soil. Additionally, compared to CN, CS exhibited a higher functional allocation towards xylanolysis in rhizosphere soil and roots. Notably, in CR and CS, the abundance of bacterial taxa associated with plant pathogens was lower than that in CN in both bulk soil and rhizosphere soil, suggesting that CR and CS contributed to reducing the number of harmful bacteria in these environments.

Fungal taxa were primarily categorised into several major groups, including saprotrophs, symbiotrophs, saprotroph-symbiotrophs, pathotrophs, pathotroph-saprotrophs, and pathotroph-symbiotrophs (Figure S3B). Among these, saprotrophs represented the most predominant trophic mode within the fungal community and exhibited higher proportions in both bulk and rhizosphere soil in CR. Notably, the trend for symbiotrophs was distinct, showing a significant increase in RS, and R samples in both CS and CR groups compared to CN group. Pathotrophs, pathotroph–saprotrophs, and pathotroph–symbiotrophs were significantly decreased in BS, RS, and R samples in both CS and CR groups compared to CN group.

Impact of crop rotation on rhizosphere microbial co-occurrence networks

Next, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the effects of different crop rotation patterns on the community structure and interactions of bacteria and fungi in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root samples. To this end, we integrated data from bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots to jointly construct microbial co-occurrence networks (Fig. 7), thereby capturing the microbial interactions within different treatments. The relevant results and data based on the threshold are presented in Supplementary Table S3, providing a more detailed and comprehensive overview of the microbial network characteristics under different conditions. Compared with CN, CR and CS showed an increased complexity and density of microbial networks. The number of soil bacteria identified in the networks for CN, CR, and CS was 117, 173, and 187, respectively, whereas the number of fungi was 58, 80, and 99, respectively. The number of bacterial network links for CN, CR, and CS was 560, 1434, and 2003, respectively, whereas the number of fungal network links was 165, 254, and 708, respectively. These results indicated that the impact of CS on the network was relatively pronounced. Moreover, the average path length of the soil bacterial network decreased from 3.00 in CN to 2.82 in CS, and the length of the fungal network decreased by 12.76% in CS relative to that in CN. The modularity of the bacterial network increased from 0.42 in CN to 0.51 in CS, and that of the fungal network increased by 65.71% in CS relative to that in CN (Table S4). Compared with CN, both CR and CS had an increased number of positive links between the microbial networks. In CR, the number of positive correlation links between the bacterial and fungal networks increased to 47.97% and 55.68%, respectively, compared to that in CN. In CS, the number of positive correlation links between bacterial and fungal networks increased to 50.77% and 57.20%, respectively, compared to that in CN (Fig. 7C). The degrees of freedom for network nodes in the bacterial and fungal networks also showed an increasing trend in both CR and CS compared with CN (Fig. 7D). This indicates that both CS and CR are more effective in shaping complex soil bacterial and fungal communities than continuous cropping of C. yanhusuo.

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to investigate correlations among the dominant microbial genera, soil physicochemical properties, and yield, as well as the active components of C. yanhusuo (Fig. 8). Among bacteria, Bacillus and Sphingomonas were positively correlated with soil parameters such as HN, Ava-P, SOM, TN, and TP (Fig. 8A). Discussion

Different crop rotations exert a strong influence on soil nutrient dynamics

Different crop rotation patterns markedly affect the yield and active ingredient content of C. yanhusuo. The soil physicochemical properties directly influence crop development, as well as yield and quality. In this study, notable differences in soil physicochemical characteristics were observed between the CR and CS rotation compared to the CN treatment. Compared with CN, both CR and CS showed significantly improved soil physicochemical properties and increased contents of various soil nutrients. Specifically, CS and CR outperformed CN in terms of TN, TP, SOM, HN, and Ava-P contents (Fig. 2). Moreover, these parameters were higher in CS than in CR. This can perhaps be attributed to the fact that rotation with soybean, a leguminous plant possessing nitrogen-fixing capabilities, can convert atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogen forms readily taken up and utilised by plants [43]. Research showed that soybean-maize rotation increased soil carbon storage compared with continuous cropping, along with nitrogen storage [44]. This type of rotation further boosted topsoil enzyme and urease activities, promoted soil nitrogen fixation and growth-promoting bacteria via rhizosphere secretions, improving soil structure, carbon sequestration, and nutrient efficiency [45, 46]. Rotating soybeans with crops (e.g., maize and wheat) boosts soil nitrogen fixation and growth-promoting bacteria via root secretions [47, 48]. In addition to crop rotations involving legumes, paddy-upland rotation (rice rotation) plays an essential role in enhancing soil properties and nutrient status [49]. Rice rotation regulates the redox potential by controlling the duration of soil flooding. During the flooding period, anaerobic decomposition promotes the accumulation of organic matter, whereas under aerobic conditions during the dry farming period, the mineralisation of organic matter is accelerated [50]. Research has shown that rice-upland crop rotations boost soil chemical (e.g., available nitrogen, phosphorus, exchangeable potassium, and organic matter) and physical (porosity and water-holding capacity) properties, significantly enhancing fertility and reducing nutrient inputs [51, 52]. Different crop rotations can alleviate imbalances in soil nutrients caused by continuous cropping. By enabling different crops to absorb and return nutrients in distinct ways, rotations contribute to a more balanced and enriched soil nutrient profile.

Different crop rotation patterns enhance microbial community diversity and optimise microbial community structure

In this study, high-throughput sequencing technology was employed to systematically elucidate the impacts of different cropping systems (CN, CR, and CS) on the microbial community diversity in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root compartments. The results revealed that crop rotation patterns significantly affected microbial richness and diversity indices. CS significantly enhanced bacterial richness in both bulk and rhizosphere soil, followed by CR, resulting in a gradient of bacterial richness as follows: CS >CR >CN (Fig. 3). The high richness in CS may be attributed to nitrogen enrichment by the nitrogen-fixing ability of soybean root nodules [53]. As a key element in microbial metabolism, nitrogen promotes the proliferation of bacterial groups that rely on the nitrogen cycle to form dominant bacterial communities [54]. Compared with soybean, the contribution of rice root exudates to carbon sources is relatively limited in diversity, which may restrict the survival of bacterial groups that rely on complex carbon sources. In flooded soils, the abundance of anaerobic microorganisms increases, and their metabolic byproducts may alter soil pH and redox potential, thereby indirectly affecting bacterial community structure [55]. Under long-term continuous cropping, the decomposition rate of soil organic matter decreases, the availability of carbon sources is reduced, and functional diversity is diminished [56]. Continuous cropping leads to intensified competition among bacteria for resources owing to nutrient simplification, thereby suppressing the survival of certain sensitive bacterial groups.

Crop rotation significantly regulated microbial community composition at the phylum level, with prominent crop-specific effects. In CN, Proteobacteria (10%−19%) emerged as the dominant phylum, which was potentially linked to the enrichment of pathogens that exacerbated the obstacles associated with continuous cropping. The abundance of Proteobacteria substantially diminished in CR (by 8%−16%) and CS (by 7%−15%; Fig. 4); the most significant decrease was noted in CS. The abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria was markedly increased in CS-rhizosphere soil (20% for Firmicutes) and CR-roots (16% for Actinobacteria). These two phyla encompass a substantial number of nitrogen-fixing, phosphate-solubilising, and biocontrol bacteria. The significant enrichment of Firmicutes in CS may be associated with the high C/N ratio of soybean residues, which promotes the proliferation of saprophytic Firmicutes. This microbial group secretes antimicrobial peptides and extracellular enzymes that suppress soil diseases [57]. The notable enrichment of Actinobacteria in CR may be related to the promotion of actinomycete spore germination by rice root exudates. This microbial group inhibits pathogens and competing microorganisms by producing siderophores and antibiotics during the dry farming period [58]. CS exhibited a significant enrichment of bacterial genera such as Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, Bacillus, Sphingomonas, Paenibacillus, and Bradyrhizobiums (with relative abundances higher than those in CR), which was possibly linked to the specific colonisation of soybean rhizobia and the synergistic effects of non-symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria. The abundance of Rhodanobacter was substantially increased in CR and greater than that in CS. Some strains of the Rhodanobacter genus possess denitrification capabilities, which are potentially associated with the anaerobic denitrification process promoted by the flooded environment during rice rotation [59].

CS had a substantially increased abundance of the fungal genus Ascomycota, with the maximum abundance occurring in bulk soil. Trichoderma and Aspergillus within Ascomycota can efficiently decompose lignin and cellulose in soybean residues, thereby promoting nutrient cycling [60, 61]. Trichoderma can suppress pathogenic fungi such as Fusarium through competition, hyperparasitism, and induced resistance [62]. CR had a significantly increased abundance of Basidiomycota, possibly due to relatively easily decomposable rice straw, which promotes the growth of saprophytic basidiomycetes. This fungal group accelerates organic matter decomposition through mycelial networks. At the fungal genus level, pathogens were suppressed in both CS and CR. The abundance of Fusarium, Plectosphaerella, Chordomyces, and Neocosmospora significantly decreased in both CR and CS. In particular, the abundance of Plectosphaerella, Chordomyces, and Neocosmospora in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots were strongly decreased in CR, possibly because the flooded environment in rice rotation inhibited spore germination.

Core microbial genera across different compartments under diverse crop rotation systems

This study revealed the variation patterns of core microbial communities in different compartments (bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root) under various crop rotation systems (Figs. 5 and 6). The results indicate that different rotation crops influence unique microbial community structures. In the CN system, Flavobacterium and Bordetella were simultaneously present in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots, suggesting that these two microorganisms exhibited strong adaptability and a wide ecological niche within this system. Certain strains of Flavobacterium can cause columnaris disease [63], whereas some strains of Bordetella are capable of producing phytotoxins [64]. In contrast to CN, the key bacterial genera in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots under the CR system shifted to Rhodanobacter, Anaeromyxobacter, and Citrifermentans. This change reflects the remarkable influence of rotation crops on soil microbial communities. During the rice growth period, the soil remains submerged for extended periods, resulting in substantial alterations to the soil environment, including a decrease in oxygen content and a decline in redox potential [65, 66]. These environmental changes provide favourable living conditions for anaerobic bacteria and other microorganisms adapted to anaerobic environments, enabling them to become dominant groups. Rhodanobacter may play a crucial role in soil nutrient cycling processes [67]. In the CS system, the primary bacterial genera in bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and roots were Bacillus and Gaiella. Soybeans can fix nitrogen; their root exudates contain abundant sugars, amino acids, and other substances, all of which serve as a rich nutritional source for microorganisms such as Bacillus, attracting them to aggregate in large numbers around the rhizosphere and roots. Bacillus has the ability to promote absorption and utilisation of nitrogen by plants [68]. Gaiella has the potential to contribute substantially to crucial processes, such as carbon cycling in the soil ecosystem, playing a positive role in maintaining soil fertility [69].

Soil microbial networks in the different cropping systems

The crop rotations significantly enhanced the complexity of microbial networks. CS had the most prominent effect on network complexity, with a notable increase in both the number of network nodes and links (Fig. 7). This indicated that CS significantly enhanced the topological complexity of the microbial network by introducing more species and interactions. Network modularity increased from 0.42 in CN to 0.51 in CS, with network modularity showing an increase of 65.71%. Enhancement of the modular structure implies that the network was divided into tighter subcommunities, which enhances the network’s functional redundancy and resistance to environmental disturbances [70]. The proportion of positive correlations in both bacterial and fungal networks increased from 44.46% to 51.55% in CN to 50.77% and 57.20% in CS, respectively, suggesting that CS promoted synergistic interactions among microorganisms. CR also exhibited a certain degree of network stability. Co-occurrence network analysis demonstrated that CR and CS had an increased quantity of network links and modularity in the bacterial and fungal communities than CN, indicating improved ecological stability (Fig. 7). Microbial interactions under flooded conditions lead to network remodelling. Long-term flooding of rice paddies reduces the soil redox potential, thus facilitating the coexistence of obligate and facultative anaerobes [71]. However, because of the limited species diversity in anaerobic environments, the network complexity in CR remained lower than that in CS.

Microbial community changes are closely related to alterations in soil physicochemical properties

As revealed by Pearson analysis and db-RDA results, a significant association exists between microbial communities and soil physicochemical properties (Fig. 8). At the bacterial level, multiple dominant genera, such as Bacillus and Sphingomonas, exhibit positive correlations with soil parameters, including HN, Ava-P, SOM, TN, and TP. Certain species within the Bacillus genus possess capabilities that enable them to break down complex organic substances in the soil and release nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, thereby increasing the contents of TN and TP in the soil [72]. Pseudolabrys and Rhodanobacter also show significant positive correlations with Ava-K, pH, and TK. This suggests that these bacteria may play a crucial role in soil nutrient cycling and transformation processes. They are likely involved in the fixation, mineralisation, and transformation of elements, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, thus influencing the availability and forms of nutrients in the soil [73]. In terms of fungi, the genera Paraphaeosphaeria and Talaromyces, among others, showed significant positive correlations with numerous soil parameters. Fungi belonging to the Talaromyces genus can decompose complex organic substances, such as cellulose in the soil, providing a carbon source for the soil while participating in the nutrient transformation processes [74].

Mantel test analysis further clarified the close relationship between microbial OTUs and soil properties (Fig. 8). Specifically, microbial OTUs exhibited extremely significant positive correlations with soil TN, HN, SOM, and Ava-P. For instance, one study demonstrated that an increase in bacterial community diversity can significantly enhance the soil’s capacity for nutrient transformation and utilisation [75]. A diverse bacterial community contains a wider range of functional genes, enabling it to participate in various biochemical processes related to nutrient cycling, such as nitrogen fixation, nitrification, denitrification, and phosphorus solubilization [75]. In soils with a rich bacterial diversity, the decomposition rate of organic matter is higher, thereby enhancing the release of nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. This is because different bacterial taxa have distinct substrate preferences and metabolic pathways [76].

This study used SEM to elucidate the impact pathways of crop rotation patterns on microbial communities and soil nutrients. The SEM analysis revealed that microbial communities play a pivotal role in mediating the effects of crop rotation on soil nutrients and crop yield. Specifically, crop rotation modulates microbial communities, which in turn influence the soil-crop system. In the CR and CS rotation systems, the relative abundance of bacteria exhibited an extremely significant positive correlation with soil nutrients. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies, which have demonstrated that crop rotation can affect bacterial community structure and subsequently regulate nutrient cycling and availability in the soil [75]. For instance, a study on maize-tobacco rotation systems indicated that rotation enhanced the abundance of microbial functional groups involved in nitrogen mineralisation and phosphorus solubilisation, thereby promoting nutrient release [77]. Similarly, diversified crop rotations have been shown to enrich the bacterial communities responsible for nitrogen fixation, thereby enhancing the soil’s nitrogen supply capacity [78]. In a peanut continuous cropping system, the application of microbial fertilisers significantly increased the soil’s available phosphorus and potassium contents, leading to improved peanut yields [79]. SEM analysis also indicated that soil nutrients had a direct and positive impact on the yield and quality of C. yanhusuo, with a slightly higher contribution rate under the soybean rotation system than the rice rotation system. It is speculated that soybean rotation significantly enhanced soil nutrient levels through biological nitrogen fixation, thereby contributing more to the yield of C. yanhusuo. The microbial network in the soybean rotation system exhibited higher complexity, possibly attributed to the directional recruitment of microorganisms by root exudates. In contrast, the rice rotation system developed a unique anaerobic microbial community due to prolonged flooding conditions, resulting in different pathways that contribute to nutrient cycling. These findings highlight the importance of considering microbial communities as a crucial mediator in the relationship between soil and crop systems.

Furthermore, although high-throughput amplicon sequencing has facilitated our understanding of microbial community composition and its prospective influences on soil ecosystem functions under various crop rotation systems, we must acknowledge its limitations. It may fail to accurately detect low-abundance but functionally important microbes. Given the complexity of microbial metabolic networks, even microbes of the same genus can have vastly different functions due to genomic differences [80]. To address these issues, future research should conduct metagenomics and isolated bacterial culture experiments for complementary validation. Metagenomics provides insights into microbial diversity at the species and strain levels, whereas bacterial culture experiments enable the study of the physiological and metabolic traits of individual strains under controlled conditions, further confirming functional roles inferred from sequencing data. In sequence processing, OTU clustering and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) analysis are two mainstream methods, each with unique traits and research significance. OTU clustering groups sequences by a preset similarity threshold, widely used in microbial research for a long time, offering a basic analytical framework. In contrast, ASV analysis, relying on 100% sequence similarity, provides higher taxonomic resolution. Despite the advantages associated with ASV, OTU clustering remains prevalent; it is still used in high-level research [81, 82]. OTU clustering has a solid foundation in microbial research, ensuring consistency with past studies and facilitating experiment execution [83]. For large-scale community pattern changes or comparing major taxonomic group differences, the resolution of OTU clustering is sufficient, making complex ASV analysis unnecessary. Due to constraints, we did not use ASV in this study, but recognise its potential for cross-study microbial community comparison. In future research, we intend to integrate ASV analysis and reanalyse existing datasets with it.

In addition to what has been discussed, future research can significantly enhance the ecological relevance and statistical reliability of our findings on how crop rotation systems impact soil ecosystems. A promising strategy is to include a wider variety of crop species in controlled experiments. Currently, our study centres on a fairly small number of crops. By introducing more crop types, we can foster a more complex and dynamic soil environment. Different crops have unique root structures, exude different substances from their roots, and have varying nutrient needs, which can trigger a wider range of microbial activities and interactions in the soil. Furthermore, a diverse crop rotation system can effectively replicate the conditions of natural ecosystems, where multiple plant species coexist and interact.

Conclusions

To explore the sustainable use of cultivation soil for C. yanhusuo and effective crop rotation systems, we investigated two crop rotation systems, namely, C. yanhusuo–rice rotation and C. yanhusuo–soybean rotation. Both CR and CS can enhance the tuber yield of C. yanhusuo and the amount of its active components, that is, tetrahydropalmatine and protopine. In CR and CS, SOM, TN, TP, HN, and Ava-P contents, together with the activities of S-SC, S-UE, and S-ACP, showed an increase relative to CN. The succession of dominant microbial groups was notable. The abundance of beneficial bacterial genera increased in CR and CS, whereas that of pathogenic genera decreased. CR and CS also strengthened the connections and modularisation within the bacterial and fungal community networks. The SEM further revealed that crop rotation affects soil nutrients through the dynamics of microbial communities, which, in turn, influence crop yield and quality. This study revealed the beneficial effects of rice and soybean rotations on the microbial community and physicochemical properties of the soil cultivated with C. yanhusuo. Our findings will inform the development of ecological restoration methods for the sustainable cultivation of C. yanhusuo.
