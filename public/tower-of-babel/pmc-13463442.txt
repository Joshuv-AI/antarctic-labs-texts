Design, synthesis, and stability evaluation of semaglutide-derived lactam-stapled peptide scaffolds for GLP-1R targeting
Authors: Tingting Liu, Xintian Ren, Yanchen Li, Jin Wang, Jiaxin Chen, Roujia Lin, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) ligands including semaglutide play an important role in drug discovery. Herein, a short semaglutide-derived GLP-1R-engaging segment was used as the basis for scaffold construction, and conformational restabilisation was introduced through lactam stapling and bulky aromatic non-natural amino acid substitution. A total of 108 stapled peptide candidates were designed by structure-guided modelling and virtual screening. Among them, 35 peptides were synthesised and characterised. Most stapled analogues showed improved serum and proteolytic stability relative to semaglutide, and 11CP-17B, 11CP-17N, and 11CP-19N showed the most favourable stability profiles. Molecular dynamics simulations and MM-GBSA analysis were consistent with receptor-compatible poses and favourable predicted interaction patterns for these representative analogues. Collectively, these results define a practical strategy for constructing stabilised semaglutide-derived peptide scaffolds and provide a basis for subsequent functional optimisation of GLP-1R-targeting peptides.Keywords: Semaglutide, GLP-1R, stapled peptides, peptide stability, peptide scaffolds

Introduction

Obesity has become one of the most important global health challenges of the twenty-first century and is closely associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and several malignancies1–4. Current therapeutic options include lifestyle intervention, pharmacotherapy, and bariatric surgery5,6. Long-term efficacy remains difficult to maintain with lifestyle measures alone, whereas surgical treatment is invasive and unsuitable for a large proportion of patients6,7. The development of safe and effective anti-obesity agents therefore continues to be a major therapeutic priority. Among currently available pharmacological strategies, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) ligands have emerged as a particularly important class because of their established effects on glucose homeostasis, appetite regulation, and body weight control8–10. Semaglutide is one of the most successful agents in this category and has shown marked clinical benefit in both glycaemic control and weight reduction11,12. Recent outcome data have further extended its clinical relevance in obesity-related cardiovascular risk reduction13. Longer-term studies have also supported the durability of weight reduction with once-weekly semaglutide in adults with overweight or obesity14. Its clinical success is closely associated with sequence and pharmacokinetic engineering, including Aib-mediated DPP-4 resistance, albumin-binding side-chain extension, and prolonged systemic exposure15.

Peptide agonist-bound GLP-1R structures provide a structural basis for identifying receptor-facing regions within GLP-1R peptide ligands, and the semaglutide-bound GLP-1R–Gs complex offers a direct template for analysing the receptor-bound conformation of semaglutide16,17. Sequence truncation can reduce molecular complexity and generate a compact scaffold for systematic side-chain and conformational engineering18–20. Comparable GLP-1R-oriented studies have shown that conformationally constrained short hydrophobic peptides and hydrocarbon-stitched peptide agonists can preserve receptor-relevant peptide conformations, supporting the feasibility of compact constrained frameworks for GLP-1R-directed design18,21. These precedents provide an important rationale for further exploring whether a truncated semaglutide-derived receptor-engaging segment can be converted into a chemically stabilised peptide scaffold. Based on our analysis of the receptor-bound semaglutide conformation, the His7-Ser17 segment was selected in this study as a truncated semaglutide-derived receptor-engaging scaffold. This segment preserves a local receptor-facing region while removing the wider extracellular-domain-contacting, conformational, and pharmacokinetic architecture of full-length semaglutide15,17. Accordingly, the His7-Ser17 scaffold was advanced as a simplified platform for chemical restabilisation and subsequent GLP-1R-oriented optimisation.

Chemical restabilisation is a central requirement for converting a truncated receptor-engaging fragment into a tractable peptide scaffold. In peptide drug discovery, conformational restriction, macrocyclisation, and non-natural amino acid incorporation are widely used to improve proteolytic stability, reduce conformational flexibility, and enhance drug-like properties19,20. Peptide stapling provides a particularly useful approach for this purpose. Early all-hydrocarbon stapling studies established that covalent side-chain cross-linking can enhance peptide helicity and metabolic stability, and subsequent work has further defined the principles, scope, and applications of stapled peptides22,23. For the present lactam-stapled design, side chain-to-side chain macrocyclisation was therefore used to restrict backbone flexibility, favour conformational preorganisation, and improve resistance to proteolytic degradation22–24. These properties are especially relevant when a short receptor-engaging segment is separated from the full-length therapeutic sequence. In the present study, aromatic non-natural amino acid substitution was introduced at the C-terminal receptor-facing positions of the His7-Ser17 scaffold based on our analysis of the semaglutide-bound GLP-1R structural model. Bip, Nal, and Btl were selected in this study as a small representative aromatic side-chain panel. Bip was included to provide an extended biphenyl surface, Nal to provide a more compact fused aromatic system, and Btl to introduce sulfur-containing heteroaromatic character. Beyond hydrophobicity, this panel was designed to compare side-chain volume, aromatic surface area, ring shape and rigidity, potential π-associated contacts, and possible heteroaromatic or sulfur-associated contributions within the local receptor-facing environment of the truncated scaffold19,20,25,26.

In this study, semaglutide was used as the structural starting point for developing stabilised peptide scaffolds for GLP-1R-oriented optimisation. Based on the semaglutide-bound receptor structure, the His7-Ser17 segment was retained as a truncated receptor-engaging scaffold, and chemical restabilisation was introduced through lactam stapling together with aromatic non-natural residue substitution. The resulting peptide set was prioritised by molecular superimposition and docking, synthesised by solid-phase peptide synthesis, and evaluated by analytical characterisation and in vitro stability assays. Representative analogues with favourable stability profiles were further examined by molecular dynamics simulation and MM-GBSA analysis to assess their conformational behaviour and predicted receptor interaction patterns. Overall, this work aimed to define a tractable semaglutide-derived stapled peptide scaffold framework with experimentally improved in vitro stability and receptor-compatible simulated interaction patterns, thereby providing a chemically defined starting point for subsequent GLP-1R functional optimisation.

Materials and methods

Structure retrieval and preparation

The experimentally determined structure of the semaglutide-bound GLP-1R–Gs complex (PDB ID: 7KI0) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org)17. The deposited complex contains GLP-1R, the bound semaglutide ligand, Gs protein components, and Nb35. The bound semaglutide was used as the structural reference for defining the receptor-engaging segment. The GLP-1R model used for docking was prepared after removal of the reference ligand and non-essential molecules. The receptor structure was preprocessed using the Prepare Protein protocol implemented in Discovery Studio 2019 (BIOVIA, San Diego, CA, USA), including removal of water molecules and non-receptor heteroatoms where appropriate, addition of hydrogen atoms, and repair of missing side chains.

Structural design of stapled peptides

Based on analysis of the binding mode between semaglutide and GLP-1R, the His7-Ser17 segment, which occupies the TMD-facing region in the receptor-bound structure, was retained as the scaffold basis, and residues 18–36 were truncated to simplify the peptide sequence. Considering that residues 16 and 17 are positioned near the TMD cavity and appear suitable for side-chain exploration in the structural model, they were replaced with bulky aromatic non-natural amino acids, including Bip (biphenylalanine), Nal (2-naphthylalanine), or Btl (benzothienylalanine), thereby generating three derivative series. Bip provides an extended biphenyl surface, Nal offers a compact fused aromatic system, and Btl introduces a sulfur-containing heteroaromatic scaffold. These residues were used to explore how aromatic size, shape, rigidity and heteroaromatic character influence local packing and receptor-facing conformational behaviour. On the basis of truncation and mutation, an i,i + 3 or i,i + 4 lactam stapling strategy was introduced. Selected residues were replaced with Asp/Lys or Glu/Lys pairs to form side chain-to-side chain intramolecular lactam bridges, resulting in stapled peptide structures. All peptide sequences were N-terminally acetylated (Ac-) and C-terminally amidated (-NH2). In total, three series comprising 108 stapled peptide derivatives were designed.

Conformational optimisation and main-chain superimposition analysis

Three-dimensional structures of all designed stapled peptides were constructed in Discovery Studio 2019. Energy minimisation was performed using the CHARMm force field with the default convergence criteria in Discovery Studio 2019. The truncated semaglutide (7–17) structure was used as the reference conformation. Main-chain atom superimposition was performed for all stapled peptides, and the main-chain RMSD values were calculated to evaluate backbone preservation relative to the truncated semaglutide-derived reference conformation.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed using the ZDOCK module implemented in Discovery Studio 2019. The GLP-1 receptor was treated as the receptor molecule, and the energy-minimised stapled peptides were used as ligands. ZDOCK employs a fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based rigid-body docking algorithm to conduct global conformational sampling and generate multiple binding poses27. Docking results were ranked according to the ZDOCK scoring function, and top-ranked poses were selected for interface analysis. All 108 stapled peptides were subjected to docking calculations. Peptides with lower main-chain RMSD values relative to the truncated reference conformation were retained to ensure backbone preservation. ZDOCK-ranked poses were inspected for receptor-interface complementarity and compatibility with the TMD-facing binding region. Peptides showing both favourable backbone preservation and plausible receptor-facing poses were selected, while maintaining representation across different stapling positions and aromatic substitution types. Using this workflow, 35 candidates were selected for synthesis and subsequent experimental evaluation.

Peptide synthesis and structural characterisation

Solid-phase peptide synthesis

Stapled peptide derivatives were synthesised using standard Fmoc-SPPS on Rink-Amide-MBHA resin (loading: 0.33 mmol·g−1). The resin was swollen in DMF/DCM (1:1, v/v) prior to synthesis. Fmoc deprotection was carried out using 20% piperidine in DMF. Amino acid coupling was performed using Fmoc-AA-OH in the presence of HATU/HOBt and DIPEA under standard solid-phase peptide synthesis conditions. Completion of coupling reactions was monitored by the Kaiser test. Chain elongation was performed sequentially from the C-terminus to the N-terminus according to the designed peptide sequences.

N-terminal acetylation and lactam cyclisation

After completion of peptide assembly and final Fmoc removal, N-terminal acetylation was performed using acetic anhydride and DIPEA in DCM. Side-chain protecting groups at the designated cyclisation sites (the O-2-PhiPr protecting group of Asp/Glu and the 4-methyltrityl protecting group of Lys side chains) were selectively removed using TFA/TIS in DCM. On-resin lactam cyclisation was then carried out using PyBOP and HOBt in the presence of DIPEA. The cyclisation reaction was repeated to ensure complete conversion.

Cleavage and purification

Peptides were cleaved from the resin using a cleavage cocktail consisting of TFA/H2O/DODT/1,3-dimethoxybenzene/TIS (90:2.5:2.5:2.5:2.5, v/v). The cleavage mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 h. The crude peptides were precipitated with cold diethyl ether, collected by centrifugation, and dissolved in DMSO. Peptides were purified by RP-HPLC using a C18 reversed-phase column. The mobile phase consisted of 0.05% TFA in water (A) and 0.05% TFA in acetonitrile (B). The gradient was as follows: 20% B (0–2 min), 20–50% B (2–8 min), 50–75% B (8–38 min), 75–90% B (38–40.5 min), 90% B (40.5–42 min), 90–20% B (42–43.5 min), and 20% B (43.5–45 min). The flow rate was 1 ml·min−1, the injection volume was 100 μL, and the detection wavelength was 254 nm. Fractions corresponding to the target peak were collected and lyophilised to afford the purified stapled peptides.

Analytical characterisation

Purity of the purified peptides was analysed by RP-HPLC using the chromatographic conditions described above. Peptide identity was confirmed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. The observed molecular masses were consistent with calculated theoretical values.

In vitro stability evaluation

Serum stability assay

Serum stability was evaluated using foetal bovine serum (FBS) to simulate physiological degradation conditions. Purified stapled peptides and semaglutide reference compound were dissolved in FBS to a final concentration of 1 mg·mL−1 and incubated at 37 °C with gentle agitation (45 rpm). Samples were collected at predetermined time points (0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36 and 72 h). At each time point, aliquots were mixed with acetonitrile to terminate degradation and precipitate serum proteins, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was diluted with acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) before RP-HPLC analysis. The stability assays were used for comparative profiling, and the remaining peptide percentage was calculated by normalising the RP-HPLC peak area of the intact peptide at each time point to that at 0 h. Chromatographic conditions were as follows: C18 reverse-phase column; mobile phase A, 0.05% TFA in water; mobile phase B, 0.05% TFA in acetonitrile; gradient elution: 20% B (0–3 min), 20–90% B (3–25 min), 90% B (25–28 min), 90–20% B (28–29 min), 20% B (29–35 min); flow rate 1 ml·min−1; column temperature 37 °C.

Proteolytic stability assays

Proteolytic stability was evaluated against trypsin, α-chymotrypsin and pepsin under their respective assay conditions. Trypsin and α-chymotrypsin stock solutions were prepared at 0.1 mg·mL−1 in PBS (pH 7.4). Peptides were incubated with the enzyme solution at a final peptide concentration of 1 µg·mL−1 at 37 °C with gentle agitation (45 rpm) for 2 h. Aliquots (5 µL) were withdrawn at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min and immediately quenched with 95 µL acetonitrile containing 0.1% TFA. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was analysed by RP-HPLC under the same chromatographic conditions described above. Pepsin stock solution (0.1 mg·mL−1) was prepared in acetate buffer (pH 3.5). Peptides (1 μg·mL−1) were incubated at 37 °C with gentle agitation for 2 h. Aliquots (5 μL) were withdrawn at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min and quenched with 95 µL of 2% aqueous ammonia. After centrifugation, samples were analysed by RP-HPLC under the same chromatographic conditions described above. The remaining peptide percentage was calculated by normalising the RP-HPLC peak area of the intact peptide at each time point to that at 0 min.

MD simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to evaluate the structural stability of the GLP-1R–ligand complexes. The representative docked conformations of stapled peptides in complex with GLP-1R were subjected to 100 ns MD simulations using the Desmond module (Schrödinger 2024–4)28,29. The systems were prepared using the System Builder panel. Each complex was solvated in an orthorhombic box with TIP3P explicit water molecules, ensuring a minimum distance of 10 Å between the protein and the box boundaries. Na+ and Cl− ions were added to neutralise the system and to achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M. The OPLS4 force field was employed for both protein and ligands30. Energy minimisation was first performed using the default relaxation protocol to remove steric clashes. Subsequently, equilibration was carried out under the NVT ensemble followed by the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1.01325 bar. Production simulations were conducted for 100 ns with a 2 fs time step. Trajectory snapshots were recorded every 100 ps. The structural stability of the complexes was evaluated by calculating: Root mean square deviation (RMSD), Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), Radius of gyration (Rg). Trajectory analyses were performed using the Simulation Interaction Diagram (SID) tool of Desmond. Trajectory frames used for subsequent MM-GBSA calculations were extracted from the equilibrated portion of the simulations.

MM-GBSA binding free energy calculation

The binding free energies of the GLP-1R–stapled peptide complexes were estimated using the Prime MM-GBSA module implemented in Schrödinger 2024–4. Representative structures were extracted from the equilibrated portion of the MD trajectories (80–100 ns). Five frames (80, 85, 90, 95 and 100 ns) were selected for each complex to perform binding free energy calculations. Results and discussion

Structure-based design and virtual screening of stapled peptides

This study was based on the experimentally determined structure of the semaglutide-bound GLP-1R–Gs complex (PDB ID: 7KI0). Structural inspection and molecular docking analysis indicated that semaglutide primarily binds within the V-shaped cavity formed by the transmembrane domain (TMD) of GLP-1R. Its N-terminal His7-Ser17 segment adopts an α-helical conformation that penetrates deeply into the binding pocket, whereas the C-terminal region mainly interacts with the extracellular domain (ECD). Local inspection indicated that the His7-Ser17 region contributes several receptor-facing polar contacts within the TMD-binding region and was therefore retained as the scaffold basis for further design (Figure 1(A,B)). Based on these structural insights, residues Ser18-Arg36 were truncated while retaining the His7-Ser17 segment observed in the receptor-bound conformation to simplify the peptide sequence. Given that residues 16 and 17 are located near the transmembrane domain (TMD) cavity and were selected for side-chain exploration in the structural model, they were substituted with bulky aromatic noncanonical amino acids (Bip, Nal, or Btl), to systematically compare extended biphenyl, compact fused aromatic, and sulfur-containing heteroaromatic scaffolds. This design enabled a systematic evaluation of aromatic size, shape, rigidity, and heteroaromatic character within the receptor-facing region. To introduce local conformational constraint into the truncated scaffold, an i,i + 3 or i,i + 4 lactam stapling strategy was introduced on the basis of truncation and mutation. Selected residues were replaced by Asp/Lys or Glu/Lys pairs to form intramolecular lactam bridges, thereby restricting backbone flexibility and increasing conformational rigidity. In addition, the N-terminal acetylation was introduced as a terminal capping modification in the present scaffold design. Following this design strategy, three series comprising 108 stapled peptide derivatives were generated. Main-chain RMSD analysis was performed to evaluate structural preservation, and the results for each series are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3. ZDOCK molecular docking was conducted separately to assess receptor interface complementarity. Based on structural similarity and docking performance, 35 candidates exhibiting favourable conformational stability and binding interface compatibility were selected for synthesis and subsequent experimental evaluation (Table 1). Synthesis and structural characterisation of stapled peptides

Based on the structure-based screening results, 35 stapled peptide candidates of three series were synthesised by Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis using Rink-Amide-MBHA resin as the solid support (Table 1, Figure 2(A)). After sequential elongation of the peptide chain from the C-terminus, N-terminal acetylation and side-chain deprotection at the designated stapling sites were performed, followed by on-resin lactam cyclisation to generate the intramolecular stapled structure. After completion of cyclisation, the peptides were cleaved from the resin using a TFA-based cleavage cocktail and precipitated with cold diethyl ether. The crude products were purified by preparative reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC), affording the target stapled peptides after purification. All purified peptides were characterised by analytical RP-HPLC and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. The observed molecular weights were consistent with the calculated values, supporting the formation of the expected stapled peptide products. In vitro stability evaluation of stapled peptides

To assess whether lactam stapling and incorporation of non-natural amino acids improved peptide stability, the synthesised stapled peptides were subjected to serum and proteolytic stability assays, using semaglutide as a pharmacological reference compound. Serum stability was evaluated by incubating peptides in foetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C for up to 72 h. The percentage of remaining peptide was quantified by analytical RP-HPLC. As shown in Figure 3(A), semaglutide exhibited progressive degradation over time. Most stapled analogues retained a higher proportion of intact peptide than semaglutide in the serum assay, with 11CP-9N, 11CP-17B, 11CP-17N, and 11CP-19N showing particularly favourable retention after 72 h. The remaining percentages at 36 h and 72 h are summarised in Table 2, supporting improved serum stability for most stapled analogues under the present assay conditions. These findings are consistent with the intended stabilising effect of intramolecular lactam stapling and non-natural amino acid incorporation in the serum assay.

Proteolytic stability was further investigated using trypsin, α-chymotrypsin, and pepsin under their respective assay conditions. As shown in Figure 3(B–D), under the present assay conditions, semaglutide showed a faster decrease in intact RP-HPLC peak area than most stapled analogues, consistent with the expected substrate preferences of the proteases used in the assay. The stapled derivatives retained higher proportions of intact peptide in these enzymatic systems, indicating improved resistance to proteolytic degradation. In particular, analogues incorporating bulky aromatic substitutions (Bip, Nal, or Btl) at positions 16/17 showed favourable stability profiles across the proteolytic assays. These observations are consistent with the intended stabilising effect of lactam-mediated conformational constraint combined with aromatic non-natural amino acid incorporation. Overall, intramolecular lactam stapling and non-natural amino acid substitution improved the serum and proteolytic stability profiles of the stapled analogues under the present assay conditions.

Based on the overall serum and proteolytic stability profiles, 11CP-17B, 11CP-17N, and 11CP-19N were selected as representative peptides for further computational analysis. These three candidates combined favourable experimental stability with closely related backbone designs, allowing a focused comparison of representative stabilised analogues. All three peptides contain a central 10–13 residue lactam constraint within the truncated helical segment, a feature expected to support conformational preorganisation while preserving geometric compatibility with the GLP-1R transmembrane binding region. Based on these stability profiles, the next step was to examine the predicted conformational behaviour of these representative peptides in GLP-1R-compatible receptor-associated models.

MD analysis of representative GLP-1R–stapled peptide complexes

To compare the predicted structural behaviour of representative GLP-1R–peptide complexes, 100 ns MD simulations were performed for 11CP-17B, 11CP-17N, and 11CP-19N. These simulations were used to assess relative conformational behaviour and receptor-compatible pose retention over the simulation timescale. As shown in Figure 4(A), the ligand RMSD values relative to the protein backbone initially increased during the early equilibration stage and subsequently fluctuated within relatively stable ranges throughout the simulations. The RMSD values of 11CP-17B and 11CP-19N were mainly maintained between approximately 4–9 Å, whereas 11CP-17N exhibited slightly higher fluctuations around 9–11 Å, indicating moderate conformational flexibility while remaining within the receptor-facing region in the simulated complex. No abrupt structural displacement or dissociation events were observed during the simulation trajectories.

(B) Backbone RMSD of GLP-1R in the indicated complexes. (C) Residue-wise RMSF profiles of GLP-1R. (D) Radius of gyration (Rg) of the complexes over the simulation period.

The backbone RMSD of GLP-1R in all three complexes stabilised after the initial equilibration period (Figure 4(B)), indicating that the receptor model retained its overall fold during the simulations. No major global disruption of the receptor model was observed. Residue-level flexibility analysis (Figure 4(C)) showed that higher RMSF values were primarily distributed in the N-terminal region, extracellular loops, and C-terminal segments, whereas the transmembrane helices exhibited relatively low fluctuations, consistent with the structural characteristics of GLP-1R. Among the three complexes, the GLP-1R–11CP-17N system displayed comparatively reduced residue fluctuations across several regions, suggesting a relatively lower fluctuation profile in the simulated receptor model. The radius of gyration (Rg) values of the complexes remained stable over the 100 ns simulations (Figure 4(D)), indicating that the overall compactness of the receptor-peptide models was preserved without obvious expansion or collapse.

The three stapled peptides remained accommodated in receptor-compatible simulated poses over the simulation timescale, consistent with the intended scaffold design. Representative conformations extracted from the equilibrated MD trajectories further illustrate the predicted accommodation of the stapled peptides within the GLP-1R binding region (Figure 5). Overall, the MD trajectories were consistent with receptor-compatible pose retention for all three representative stapled peptides over the simulation timescale, although differences in conformational fluctuation were observed among the complexes. Because global metrics such as RMSD, RMSF, and Rg mainly describe overall dynamic behaviour, a more detailed interaction-level analysis was then performed to clarify how hydrogen-bonding patterns and conformational compactness contributed to the relative simulated profiles of each complex.

Hydrogen-bond network and conformational compactness analysis

To define the molecular basis underlying the different dynamic behaviours observed in Section 3.4, hydrogen-bond formation, intrapeptide conformational stability, and solvent exposure were analysed throughout the MD trajectories. As shown in Figure 6(A), the number of hydrogen bonds formed between the stapled peptides and GLP-1R remained relatively stable during the simulations. The 11CP-17B complex maintained the highest number of receptor-peptide hydrogen bonds, generally fluctuating between approximately 7 and 11 interactions. The 11CP-17N system exhibited a slightly lower but still persistent hydrogen-bond profile, typically ranging from 6 to 9 interactions. In contrast, the 11CP-19N complex displayed fewer intermolecular hydrogen bonds. These results suggest that 11CP-17B formed more persistent predicted hydrogen-bond contacts with the receptor-binding region.

(C) Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of the peptides during the simulations. (D) Hydrogen-bond occupancy of GLP-1R residues interacting with the stapled peptides. (E) Interaction frequency profile of GLP-1R residues contacting 11CP-17B.

In addition to receptor-peptide interactions, intramolecular hydrogen bonds within the peptides were analysed to evaluate their conformational behaviour. As illustrated in Figure 6(B), both 11CP-17B and 11CP-17N maintained relatively high numbers of intramolecular hydrogen bonds during the simulation trajectories. In contrast, 11CP-19N exhibited fewer intramolecular hydrogen bonds and larger fluctuations, indicating a comparatively less constrained peptide conformation. The higher intramolecular hydrogen-bond content observed for 11CP-17B and 11CP-17N is consistent with a more conformationally restricted peptide state in the simulated receptor environment.

The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of the peptides was further analysed to evaluate conformational compactness (Figure 6(C)). The SASA values of 11CP-17B and 11CP-17N remained relatively stable during the simulation trajectories, fluctuating mainly between approximately 350 and 450 Å2. In contrast, the SASA of 11CP-19N showed a gradual increase during the simulation, indicating a more exposed and flexible conformation in the receptor-associated model. Residue-level hydrogen-bond occupancy analysis (Figure 6(D)) further revealed recurrent predicted contacts between the stapled peptides and GLP-1R. For the 11CP-17B complex, residues such as Tyr152, Arg190, Arg299, and Thr391 exhibited relatively high hydrogen-bond occupancy, indicating persistent predicted contacts within the receptor-binding region. In the 11CP-17N system, residues including Asp372, Arg380, and Lys383 displayed high interaction frequencies, suggesting an alternative predicted hydrogen-bonding pattern. In contrast, the 11CP-19N complex showed fewer residues with high occupancy values, consistent with its lower intermolecular hydrogen-bond numbers observed during the simulations.

Residue interaction frequency analysis further showed that several GLP-1R residues maintained recurrent contacts with the stapled peptides during the simulations. As illustrated in Figure 6(E), residues located in the transmembrane binding region formed recurrent predicted hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic contacts with 11CP-17B, consistent with the predicted accommodation of the peptide. Similar interaction patterns were observed for 11CP-17N and 11CP-19N (Figures S71 and S72). Additional ligand-protein contact diagrams and ligand-level property profiles are provided in Figure S73, which further support the MD-based assessment of peptide compactness and receptor-associated conformational behaviour. Collectively, these results indicate that 11CP-17B and 11CP-17N displayed more persistent predicted receptor contacts and more compact peptide conformations than 11CP-19N during the simulations. This interaction pattern is consistent with the comparatively more favourable simulated conformational profiles inferred from the trajectory analyses. To further compare the energetic favourability of these simulated receptor-associated states, MM-GBSA calculations were subsequently performed using representative frames extracted from the equilibrated portions of the trajectories.

MM-GBSA binding free energy analysis

To further compare the predicted binding free energies of the selected GLP-1R–peptide complexes, MM-GBSA calculations were performed using representative structures extracted from the equilibrated MD trajectories. As summarised in Table 3, 11CP-17B gave the most favourable MM-GBSA estimate among the three representative complexes, followed by 11CP-17N, whereas 11CP-19N showed a less favourable MM-GBSA profile. Energy decomposition analysis indicated that van der Waals and lipophilic terms were the major favourable contributors in these calculations, while the 11CP-19N complex displayed a less favourable and more variable electrostatic component. These energetic trends were broadly consistent with the hydrogen-bonding and conformational analyses described above and support 11CP-17B and 11CP-17N as more favourable representatives within this computational comparison.

Conclusion

In this study, a structure-guided series of semaglutide-derived stapled peptide scaffolds targeting GLP-1R was designed and evaluated through peptide synthesis, stability testing, and computational analysis. From 108 designed candidates, 35 analogues were selected for synthesis on the basis of structural preservation and docking performance. The resulting peptide set showed broad improvement in serum and proteolytic stability under the present assay conditions, indicating that lactam stapling combined with bulky aromatic side-chain substitution provides an effective route for restabilising short semaglutide-derived scaffolds. Among the synthesised analogues, 11CP-17B, 11CP-17N, and 11CP-19N displayed the most favourable stability profiles and were therefore selected for further structural evaluation. Molecular dynamics simulations and MM-GBSA analysis were consistent with receptor-compatible poses and comparatively favourable predicted interaction patterns for these representative peptides, with 11CP-17B and 11CP-17N showing the most favourable overall profiles within this computational comparison. Taken together, the present work establishes a practical framework for the construction and evaluation of stabilised semaglutide-derived GLP-1R-targeting peptide scaffolds. The data support the feasibility of extracting a compact receptor-engaging segment from the semaglutide-bound structure and converting it into a chemically tractable scaffold through local restabilisation. Further pharmacological studies will be required to define receptor activation and signalling properties, and the current scaffold set provides a suitable basis for that next stage of optimisation.
