Chirality-Induced Spin Currents in a Fermi Gas
Camen A. Royse and J. E. Thomas

Abstract
We observe and model spin currents arising from chirality and effective spin-exchange interactions in a weakly interacting 6 Li Fermi gas. Chirality is introduced by a static displacement between the center of the trapped atoms and the center of an applied magnetic bowl, which produces spatially varying spin rotation. Spin-selective spin current is observed via oscillations in the centers of mass of the spin-up and spin-down components, which appear to bounce off of or pass through one another, depending on the relative size of the chirality and s-wave spin scattering interactions. We show that this behavior obeys a driven oscillator equation with an effective spin-dependent driving force.



Chiral or twisted materials are of great interest for generating and controlling spin-polarized electron currents
Ray et al. 1999
to enable “spintronics” technologies. In such technologies, information is transmitted via spin currents instead of electron currents, greatly reducing Joule heating, which limits the scalability of computers. Chiral molecules
Shang et al. 2022
can act as passive spin filters, efficiently transporting electrons for one polarization while impeding the transmission of the other. Large chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS) has been observed in the asymmetric transmission of polarized electrons through thin films of chiral molecules, including DNA
Guo and Sun 2012
;
Göhler et al. 2011
. While spin-orbit coupling is known to play a role in this effect, no unifying theory of the role of chirality has been able to quantitatively reproduce measurements. Spin transport in chiral materials is now widely studied
Bloom et al. 2024
;
Bentmann 2025
;
Miki et al. 2025
;
Moharana et al. 2025
for manipulating spin-selective spin currents. Ultracold Fermi gases provide experimental simulators for spintronics concepts
Duine 2008
, which may offer new insights into chiral control methods.

We report on the measurement of chirality-induced spin currents in a trapped, weakly interacting Fermi gas of
6
Li atoms, confined in the combined potential of a cigar-shaped CO
2
laser trap and a magnetic bowl, Fig.
1
. A chiral spin texture (i.e., a spin spiral/twist) is created by simply displacing the center of the laser trap from the center of the magnetic bowl, which causes the coherently prepared spin medium to evolve into a twisted state. We show that the observed spin-selective spin current obeys a 1D driven oscillator equation, with an effective spin-dependent driving force that arises from s-wave scattering in the twisted medium.

In summary, we have measured chirality-induced spin current in a coherently prepared weakly interacting Fermi gas that evolves into a twisted medium. The vanishing of s-wave scattering for parallel spins, according to the Pauli principle, makes the spin-twisted medium spin orienting. Although the difference between the spin-dependent mechanical forces is negligible, spatially asymmetric spin waves appear as oscillations in the centers of mass of the spin components, which are driven by effective spin-dependent forces. The resulting spin selectivity for spin-up and spin-down atoms provides a cold gas version of the CISS effect in chiral materials.

\displaystyle C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{+},{\mathbf{x}}_{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{12}\left\{\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\downarrow}\bar{n}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})+\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\uparrow}\bar{n}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\right.

(S14)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt\left.-\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\downarrow}\bar{\rho}_{\uparrow\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})-\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\uparrow}\bar{\rho}_{\downarrow\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+g_{12}\left\{\delta_{\alpha\downarrow}\bar{n}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})+\delta_{\alpha\uparrow}\bar{n}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt\left.-\delta_{\alpha\downarrow}\bar{\rho}^{*}_{\uparrow\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})-\delta_{\alpha\uparrow}\bar{\rho}^{*}_{\downarrow\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})\right\}\,.

Using these results, eq.
S10
yields

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{t}+\frac{\mathbf{p}}{m}\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{F}}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})}{2}\cdot\nabla_{p}\right]\,\hat{W}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{p}})=i\,\Omega_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,\hat{W}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle\hskip 18.06749pt-\frac{\hbar}{8}\sum_{i}(k_{i\alpha}-k_{i\alpha^{\prime}})\,\partial^{2}_{p_{i}}\hat{W}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{p}})+\frac{i}{\hbar}\,\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,e^{\frac{i\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{\hbar}}\,C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{+},{\mathbf{x}}_{-})\,,

(S15)

where the time argument is suppressed in the Heisenberg operators and

\Omega_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=\omega_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\hbar}[V_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})-V_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})]\,.

(S16)

The evolution equation for the thermal averaged density matrix
\bar{\rho}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x},t)
is found by integrating eq.
S15
over
{\mathbf{p}}
using

\int\frac{d^{3}{\mathbf{p}}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,e^{\frac{i\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{\hbar}}=\delta(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\,.

(S17)

With
\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\delta(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\,C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{+}{\mathbf{x}}_{-})=C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})
, we can see from eq.
S14
that the thermal average
\langle C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})\rangle=0
. Thermal averaging yields

\partial_{t}\bar{\rho}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\nabla_{x}\cdot{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x},t)=i\,\Omega_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,\bar{\rho}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x},t)\,,

(S18)

where
{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}
is the current density for each component of the density matrix.

To find the evolution equation for the current densities, we multiply eq.
S15
by
{\mathbf{p}}/m
and integrate over
{\mathbf{p}}
. We assume that the momentum distribution remains close to thermal equilibrium and is nearly isotropic, so that
\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\rangle
is time independent and independent of
\alpha\,,\alpha^{\prime}

Oktel and Levitov 2002
;
Williams et al. 2002
;
Tim
. The force terms are evaluated using integration by parts, yielding

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},t)+\frac{\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\rangle}{3m^{2}}\,\nabla_{x}\bar{\rho}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},t)-\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{F}}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})}{2m}\,\bar{\rho}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},t)=

\displaystyle\hskip 54.2025pt=i\,\Omega_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},t)+\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,.

(S19)

The interaction term is

\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=\int d^{3}{\mathbf{p}}\,\frac{\mathbf{p}}{m}\,\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,e^{i\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{\hbar}}\,\langle C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{+},{\mathbf{x}}_{-})\rangle\,.

(S20)

We evaluate eq.
S20
in terms of thermal averaged Wigner operators,
\langle\hat{W}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{p}})\rangle
using a method similar to that of ref.
Ebling et al. 2011
. In eq.
S14
, we note that each term of
C^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{+},{\mathbf{x}}_{-})
takes the form

\bar{f}({\mathbf{x}}_{\pm})\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}}_{+})\hat{\psi}_{\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{-})
. We can write

\bar{f}({\mathbf{x}}_{\pm})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{l!}\left(\pm\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{2}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)^{l}\,\bar{f}({\mathbf{y}})|_{{\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{x}}}

(S21)

and

e^{i\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{\hbar}}\left(\pm\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{2}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)^{l}\,\bar{f}({\mathbf{y}})=\left(\pm\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\nabla_{p}\cdot\nabla_{y}}{2}\right)^{l}\,e^{i\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{\hbar}}\bar{f}({\mathbf{y}})\,.

(S22)

Then, eq.
S20
becomes

\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=\sum_{l}\frac{1}{l!}\int d^{3}{\mathbf{p}}\,\frac{\mathbf{p}}{m}\,\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\pm\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\nabla_{p}\cdot\nabla_{y}}{2}\right)^{l}\,\int\frac{d^{3}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,e^{i\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{\hbar}}\,\bar{C}^{\,int}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}}_{+},{\mathbf{x}}_{-})|_{{\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{x}}}\,,

(S23)

where the signs
\pm
and the
{\mathbf{y}}
argument in
\bar{C}^{\,int}
correspond to the argument
{\mathbf{x}}_{\pm}
of the first factor for each term in eq.
S14
.

The
l=0
terms in eq.
S23
are proportional to
{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}
. For the
l=1
terms, integration by parts removes the
{\mathbf{p}}
factor, yielding contributions proportional to gradients of the density matrix. For
l\geq 2
, the integrals vanish.

We find the evolution equations for
\bar{\rho}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
and
\bar{\rho}_{\uparrow\uparrow}
from eq.
S18
. Using eqs.
S19
and
S23
, it is straightforward to find the evolution equations for
{\mathbf{J}}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
and
{\mathbf{J}}_{\uparrow\uparrow}
. By interchanging
\uparrow
and
\downarrow
, these results determine the evolution equations for all components of the spin density
{\mathbf{s}}
using

\displaystyle s_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\rho}_{\uparrow\downarrow}+\bar{\rho}_{\downarrow\uparrow}}{2}

\displaystyle s_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\rho}_{\uparrow\downarrow}-\bar{\rho}_{\downarrow\uparrow}}{2i}

\displaystyle s_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\rho}_{\uparrow\uparrow}-\bar{\rho}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}{2}=\frac{\bar{n}_{\uparrow}-\bar{n}_{\downarrow}}{2}\,.

(S24)

and similarly for the components of the spin current tensor
{\mathbf{J}}_{x}
,
{\mathbf{J}}_{y}
, and
{\mathbf{J}}_{z}
.

We write the evolution equations in a frame rotating about the z-axis at the hyperfine resonance frequency,
\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}
. In this frame, eq.
S16
requires
\Omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}\rightarrow\Omega_{z}=-\Omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}
with
\Omega_{z}\equiv\frac{1}{\hbar}[V_{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}})-V_{\downarrow}({\mathbf{x}})]
determined from eq.
S2
and
\Omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}=\Omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}=0
. Then, the spin density components
s_{i}=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
obey

\dot{s}_{i}({\mathbf{x}},t)+\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{J}}_{i}({\mathbf{x}},t)=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times{\mathbf{s}})_{i}\,,

(S25)

where the spatially varying rotation rate is

{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,\frac{m\,\omega_{0}\delta\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}}{\hbar}\,(x-x_{os})^{2}\,.

(S26)

Now we use eq.
S19
to explicitly write the evolution equations for the current densities
{\mathbf{J}}_{i}
in eq.
S25
, which enables a clear discussion of the negligible contributions. The x-component of the current density tensor obeys

\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{x}+\frac{\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\rangle}{3m^{2}}\,\nabla s_{x}-\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}+{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow}}{2m}\,s_{x}=-\Omega_{z}{\mathbf{J}}_{y}+\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{x}\,,

(S27)

where

\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{x}=-\frac{2g_{12}}{\hbar}(s_{y}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{z}-s_{z}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{y})+\frac{g_{12}}{2m}(\bar{n}\nabla s_{x}-s_{x}\nabla\bar{n})\,,

(S28)

with the total density
\bar{n}=\bar{n}_{\uparrow}+\bar{n}_{\downarrow}
.

Similarly, the y-component of the current density tensor obeys

\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{y}+\frac{\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\rangle}{3m^{2}}\nabla s_{y}-\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}+{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow}}{2m}\,s_{y}=\Omega_{z}{\mathbf{J}}_{x}+\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{y}\,,

(S29)

where

\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{y}=-\frac{2g_{12}}{\hbar}(s_{z}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{x}-s_{x}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{z})+\frac{g_{12}}{2m}(\bar{n}\nabla s_{y}-s_{y}\nabla\bar{n})\,.

(S30)

Finally, the z-component of the current density tensor obeys

\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{z}+\frac{\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\rangle}{3m^{2}}\nabla s_{z}-\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}+{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow}}{2m}\,s_{z}=\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}-{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow}}{2m}\,\frac{\bar{n}}{2}+\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{z}\,,

(S31)

where

\dot{\mathbf{J}}^{int}_{z}=-\frac{2g_{12}}{\hbar}(s_{x}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{y}-s_{y}\,{\mathbf{J}}_{x})+\frac{g_{12}}{2m}(\bar{n}\nabla s_{z}-s_{z}\nabla\bar{n})\,.

(S32)

The evolution equation for the total density
\bar{n}=\bar{n}_{\uparrow}+\bar{n}_{\downarrow}
is needed to complete the evolution equations. With
{\mathbf{J}}_{n}\equiv{\mathbf{J}}_{\uparrow\uparrow}+{\mathbf{J}}_{\downarrow\downarrow}
, we obtain the continuity equation

\dot{\bar{n}}+\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{J}}_{n}=0\,.

(S33)

The current density obeys

\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{n}+\frac{\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}\rangle}{3m^{2}}\nabla\bar{n}-\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}+{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow}}{2m}\,\bar{n}=\frac{{\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}-{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow}}{m}\,s_{z}+\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{int}\,,

(S34)

where

\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{int}=\frac{g_{12}}{m}\nabla\left({\mathbf{s}}^{2}-\frac{\bar{n}^{2}}{4}\right).

(S35)

Using eq.
S34
, it is easy to show that the density profile
\bar{n}({\mathbf{x}})
is time-independent, as observed in our experiments. First, the differences between the spin-up and spin-down forces is negligible for our experimental parameters, as noted in the main text. Further, just after the
\pi/2
radiofrequency pulse is applied,
s_{x}=\bar{n}/2
is the only nonvanishing spin density component, so that
\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{int}=0
in eq.
S35
. Hence, the right hand side of eq.
S34
initially vanishes.
In thermal equilibrium, we expect that the total density is determined by the balance of the pressure
p
and trap forces,
\nabla p+{\mathbf{F}}\bar{n}=0
. With
{\mathbf{F}}=({\mathbf{F}}_{\uparrow}+{\mathbf{F}}_{\downarrow})/2
the average force and the pressure
p=\bar{n}m\langle v_{x}^{2}\rangle
, the sum of the second and third terms on the left side of eq.
S34
also vanish. Then both
\dot{\mathbf{J}}_{n}=0
and
{\mathbf{J}}_{n}=0
just after the
\pi/2
pulse, so that
\dot{\bar{n}}=0
for all
t
. In modeling the data, we find that spatial derivatives of the mean field potential are negligible, i.e., the second terms on the right hand side of eqs.
S28
,
S30
, and
S32
are negligible. Further, the force difference term on the right hand side of eq.
S31
is negligible.

A.1.2
One-Dimensional Approximation

As our experiments are performed in a cigar-shaped trap oriented along the x-axis with
\omega_{\perp}\simeq 25\,\omega_{0}
, we simplify the description further by making a one-dimensional approximation, using transverse-averaged densities and currents that depend only on
x
, and the average force near the cloud center is
F_{x}=-m\omega_{0}^{2}x
. We take
\nabla\rightarrow\partial_{x}
in the evolution equations and define a spin current density vector
{\mathbf{J}}\equiv(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})
, where the vector denotes the direction of the spin component carried by the current flowing along x. Assuming a near equilibrium momentum distribution as discussed above, we take
\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{2}/3\rangle=\langle p^{2}\rangle=m\,k_{B}T
, where
T
is the effective temperature. Dropping spatial derivatives of the mean field potential and force difference terms as discussed above, we obtain the simplified 1D evolution equations,

\dot{\mathbf{s}}+\partial_{x}\,{\mathbf{J}}={\mathbf{\Omega}}\times{\mathbf{s}},

(S36)

\dot{\mathbf{J}}+\frac{k_{B}T}{m}\partial_{x}\,{\mathbf{s}}+\omega_{0}^{2}\,x\,{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{\Omega}}\times{\mathbf{J}}-\frac{8\pi\hbar a_{s}}{m}\,{\mathbf{s}}\times{\mathbf{J}}\,,

(S37)

where
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
is given by eq.
S26
.

To model the evolution after the initial
\pi/2
radio-frequency pulse, we take the only non-vanishing initial condition for the spin density to be
s_{x}(x,0)=\frac{1}{2}\,n_{0}(x)
. For simplicity, we fit the density profile with
n_{0}(x)=\bar{n}_{0}\,\exp(-x^{2}/\sigma_{x}^{2})
, which defines the effective temperature
k_{B}T=m\omega_{0}^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}/2=\langle p^{2}/m\rangle
. The one-dimensional approximation implies that the strength of the
{\mathbf{s}}\times{\mathbf{J}}
interaction term, which is proportional to the square of the density, should be averaged over the transverse dimensions. For this reason, we take
\bar{n}_{0}
to be a transverse averaged peak 3D density. Numerical integration determines the spin density profiles
n_{\uparrow,\downarrow}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\,n_{0}(x)\pm s_{z}(x,t)
and the corresponding centers of mass

\langle x\rangle_{\uparrow\downarrow}=\frac{1}{N_{\uparrow\downarrow}}\int dx\,x\,n_{\uparrow\downarrow}(x,t)=\pm\frac{1}{N_{\uparrow\downarrow}}\int dx\,x\,s_{z}(x,t)\,,

(S38)

where

N_{\uparrow\downarrow}\equiv\int dx\,n_{\uparrow\downarrow}(x,t)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,\bar{n}_{0}\,\sigma_{x}\,.

(S39)

Eq.
S38
shows that
\langle x\rangle_{\downarrow}=-\langle x\rangle_{\uparrow}
for a 50-50 mixture, as predicted previously
Ebling et al. 2011
and observed in our experiments.
