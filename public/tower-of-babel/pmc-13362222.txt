The impact of multidimensional mutual-aid care services on social functions: evidence from a case study in China
Authors: Ye He, Jia Xu, Jianzeng An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe unmet care needs of older adults are rapidly increasing in China. Informal family caregiving is weakened by the migration of the rural labor force to urban areas in search of better income and job opportunities. Formal care services are also insufficient due to insufficient governmental financial support, limited availability of formal caregivers, and challenging economic development conditions. A novelty solution is required to address these issues.MethodsThis study draws on existing mutual-aid care services for older adults in rural China and proposes a theoretical framework to examine the impact of multidimensional mutual-aid care services. Key areas of focus include service design, delivery, and accessibility, as well as their implications for the social functioning of older adults. The model was tested through a case study in South China, involving exploratory interviews with 40 participants associated with the village’s mutual-aid care service system and an analysis of 112 implementation documents from 2000 to 2020.ResultsOur findings address significant research gap by emphasizing the importance of understanding the social implications of multidimensional mutual-aid care services in the community. This understanding can facilitate a better alignment of care policies with societal objectives and provide a more informed response to the challenges posed by aging population. We identify four structural dimensions of multidimensional mutual-aid care service including perceptions, regulations, providers and resources, and analyze their differential impacts on socio-cultural, social orientation, social integration and social communicational functions.ConclusionsBased on our findings, we propose four directions to guide future research and encourage discussion on policies aimed at aligning mutual-aid care service development with societal expectations for older adults. This study offers constructive insights, analyzes the delivery and accessibility of social care services, and highlights valuable lessons from China’s experiences, setting a modest example of efficient care service implementation on a local scale while maintaining an international perspective.Keywords: Mutual-aid care service, Social function, Older adult, Qualitative case study, Policy alignment, Aging population

Introduction

Population aging is one of society’s most pressing challenges, particularly in rural areas of China, where the demand for care services among older adults is growing rapidly. Both informal family care and formal institutional care services for older adults have weakened in rural China [1, 2]. This decline is largely due to the migration of much of China’s labor force to urban areas in search of better income and job opportunities [3, 4]. Additionally, formal public care institutions in rural areas are underdeveloped, suffering from a lack of governmental financial support, insufficient market-driven labor resources, and poor economic development conditions [5]. Moreover, while there is a gradual promotion of professional and market-oriented institutions, there is reluctance among professionals in the field of care services to work in rural care settings [3]. Consequently, formal care services are inadequate, and many rural older adults find these services unaffordable due to low pension income [6]. As a result, the unmet care needs of rural older adults are increasing.

To address this issue, a new care service model is needed—one that bridges the gap between the unmet care needs of rural older adults and the delivery of care services. A promising solution is the development of multidimensional mutual-aid care services (MCS), which should be promoted not only in China but also worldwide, to improve care service delivery and access for potential recipients in our aging societies.

In this study, we define MCS as an organized system of care provision in which community members, including older adults, volunteers, neighbors, and formal organizations, exchange support and services based on principles of reciprocity, mutual obligation, and shared responsibility [6, 7]. Unlike informal mutual aid (e.g., occasional neighborly help), MCS is characterized by structured arrangements [8] and formal or semi-formal rules, and it often involves coordination by government agencies, non-governmental organizations (NGOs) [9], or community-based organizations [9, 10]. Alternative terms used in the literature include “time banking,” “community-based mutual support,” or “reciprocal care networks” [10, 11].

Conceptually, MCS can be analyzed through four structural dimensions as follows: perceptions, regulations, providers, and resources. Perceptions refer to the cultural and cognitive foundations that shape mutual aid behaviors; regulations encompass formal rules and norms guiding MCS operations; providers include the diverse actors (government, communities, NGOs) delivering services, and resources cover the material and symbolic assets that sustain MCS. These dimensions, derived from institutional theory, [11, 12] form the analytical backbone of our study.

However, the definition of MCS remains contested in the literature [10]. Most current research reflects a bias toward organizational dimensions with the aim of building a solid knowledge base on care services and provide pathways within these frameworks [8, 10]. Meanwhile, the social functions of rural care services have received little academic attention and remain poorly conceptualized [8, 9]. Nevertheless, given the ongoing population aging, the development of MCS has garnered considerable attention in the literature, particularly its three key characteristics. First, MCS is viewed as a low-cost, socially supportive, emotionally comforting, and culturally adaptable model for elderly care. [7] This perception hinders evidence-based discussions about integrating MCS with existing social care services and understanding the sociocultural functions underlying these services [7, 8]. Second, Chinese MCS is generally considered as a response to the challenges posed by public financial constraints in supporting care services for rural older adults as well as the lack of human resources to provide such support [9]. Third, there is a notable bias in the literature toward addressing care needs of rural older adults without adequately exploring the social impact of structural changes within MCS [8, 9, 13].

Reflecting on the existing literature, we argue that a better understanding of the social implications of multidimensional MCS could lead to improved alignment of care policies with societal objectives and a more informed response to the challenges of an aging population. Therefore, this study aims to enhance both the theoretical and empirical understanding of the multiple structural dimensions of MCS and their impact on the social landscape.

Case study

In this study, we conducted a case analysis in Sanzhou Village, which is located in a rural district of South China. Its borders extend along the western mountains, and the landscape comprises mountainous regions interspersed with tea plantations [6, 14]. With approximately 1,500 inhabitants and 490 households, the village grapples with challenges typical of rural areas, including low population density (476 inhabitants/km2) [6]. In comparison, the province encompassing Sanzhou Village has a high average age (55 years), experiences outward migration of young people, and faces a shortage of skilled workers [6]. Approximately 400 residents (26.6%) are over 65 years old, emphasizing the aging issue. Of the 700–900 residents who had lived in the village for over six months at the time of our interviews, half were aged over 60 years, including 80 individuals who lived alone. [14] More than half the working population is employed outside the district and commutes to neighboring areas or the nearest major city, Hangzhou [15, 16].

In 2007, MCS for older adults was initially implemented in Sanzhou Village based on the traditional cultural concept of mutual-aid relationships among acquaintances [17]. The prototypes of MCS—the MCS Volunteer Team for Older Adults in Sanzhou Village (fewer than 10 members) and the Mutual Aid Association for Older Adults in Sanzhou Village (10 members, including both relatives and friends)—were spontaneously formed and organized toward the end of 2011 [6, 13, 17]. Starting in 2015, an MCS system was established with support from the government, rural communities, NGOs, and local residents.

As what we found, this village experienced a significant decline in its local labor force as residents migrated from rural areas to urban centers between 2000 and 2020. Based on national census data and village archives, the working-age population declined from approximately 1,120 in 2000 to 860 in 2020, and the proportion of those out-migrating (for ≥ 6 months) increased from 18% to 52%. Over the same period, the older population (aged 60+) rose from 260 (about 15%) to 530 (about 35%), while over 90% of these older adults resided in the village year-round. Consequently, the village’s older population is facing substantial unmet care needs while there lacks care resources in down. Our analytical perspective builds on the existing literature regarding the structural dimensions of MCS. Specifically, we theoretically conceptualize the social impacts of MCS on social functions and employe a systematic perspective that considers the full range of structural dimensions to gain a deeper understanding of its effects on various social functions within the selected region.

The MCS model was tested through exploratory interviews with 40 participants associated with the village’s MCS system, including older adults, staffs from professional NGOs, directors and associate directors of rural community and community staffs, as well as an analysis of 112 service implementation documents from 2000 to 2020. Our findings suggest a significant relationship between the care service model and the unmet care needs of older adults in rural areas.

How Sanzhou village managed MCS

Based on our analytical framework, we aimed to capture and understand the various dimensions of MCS implemented in Sanzhou Village. We identified four dimensions as follows: perceptions, regulations, providers, and resources. This categorization emerged during the coding process and revealed that these dimensions varied in terms of their role in implementing and utilizing MCS for older adults. We determined the impact of MCS structures on the social functions of older people, and the corresponding results are explained below.

Perception, identified as the most fundamental structural dimension of MCS, serves as a prerequisite for its establishment. From a macro perspective, this dimension includes societal cognitions such as traditional culture, social customs, behavioral habits, and ethics, particularly those among older adults. These factors shape conventions and public opinion. In the context of Chinese traditional society, clan culture, which emphasizes respect for older adultshas historically fostered mutual assistance networks for them [18, 19]. This perception helps extend care for older adults beyond family boundaries, promoting mutual assistance within neighborhoods and creating a culture of good neighborliness and friendship that serves as a foundation for supporting older adults in rural areas.

From a micro perspective, recognizing and participating in MCS while also actively contributing to it is driven by rational choices and preferences for welfare behavior [20, 21]. Rational individuals cooperate to achieve goals, recognizing that their individual efforts are limited and that collective action is necessary to accomplish shared objectives. While perception fosters mutual support among network members, it can also lead to rational decisions and behavioral strategies that enable the active construction of MCS networks for older adults based on professional expertise rather than subjective factors alone. [22] Therefore, it is crucial to understand that perception can encompass the idea of providing MCS with attributes such as specialization, institutionalization, sustainability, intelligence, efficiency, and legalization, particularly in rural areas.

The regulation dimension refers to formal norms, including laws, policy documents, charters, and rules, which guide older adults’ mutual aid behavior. This dimension is characterized by three key aspects: (i) Regulations established through legal procedures, formalized in documents, and characterized by clarity enhance the guidance provided by rules, encourage expected behaviors, and promote stability [23] (ii) Regulations help generate consensus among mutual aid members by reflecting their shared knowledge and values [24]; (iii) Mandatory regulations encourage residents to actively cooperate, thereby fostering trust on the regulation and enforcement of norms, rules, and interpersonal communication [25]. Thus, the effective and sustainable development of mutual aid cooperation depends on the rationality and relevance of reciprocity norms as well as their authority and enforceability [26].

The provider dimension includes key actors such as the government, communities, and NGOs. It has been argued that modernizing rural mutual support systems for older adults requires government intervention [10]. Specifically, the government is viewed as a crucial resource that guides the direction, purpose, and functions of rural mutual care services. The government further plays a leading role in creating a cultural environment that fosters mutual aid and raises society’s awareness of mutual support systems for older adults by establishing an MCS system at the regulatory level.

Rural communities also play a significant role in providing MCS to older adults. Villages with better MCS often have a strong internal drive for mutual support [27], such as rural communities enable an atmosphere conducive to mutual aid; promote relevant policies; and spread awareness through various means such as bulletin boards, leaflets, broadcasts, meetings, and door-to-door conversations. Communities also encourage mutual aid initiatives by developing rules, forming organizations, creating service teams, and facilitating service delivery [28]. Given their organizational strengths, communities can also provide venues and facilities in rural areas and develop human resources by mobilizing community leaders and forming volunteer teams, including party members, enthusiastic villagers, and active seniors.

NGOs are another crucial provider of MCS. We identified two types of NGOs involved in MCS for older adults. The first is externally embedded professional NGOs, which are drawn to rural areas through government-purchased service projects that benefit from financial subsidies and tax incentives. These NGOs organize MCS activities to meet the diverse needs of older adults, establish mutual aid groups, empower older adults in the community, raise awareness of mutual aid, foster a cooperative culture, expand support networks, and strengthen the connections between older adults and broader social support systems.

The second type consists of community-based social organizations that are initiated by the villagers themselves. These organizations extensively engage villagers to leverage local human resources for promoting mutual aid for older adults and fostering a strong sense of community support.

The resource dimension consists of three key aspects: (i) practical equipment, including kitchens, restaurants, entertainment facilities, sports areas, medical care, rehabilitation spaces, and infrastructure such as beds, tables, chairs, electrical appliances, related instruments, and internet support; (ii) Service production and delivery resources, which covers staff salaries, volunteer subsidies, catering, accommodations, and other operational costs such as water, electricity, and food expenses; and (iii) Signs and symbols, including nameplates, sculptures, logos, brands, and trademarks, which represent the mutual aid networks for older adults. While symbolic artifacts do not directly influence the production and delivery of MCS [9], they play an important role in building an organization’s image, uniting members, aggregating resources, and motivating individuals. For instance, if a mutual aid organization’s cultural logo holds symbolic significance, it can heighten older adults’ awareness of mutual aid within the team and boost their collective sense of pride. This logo can also shape the organization’s social influence and attract public welfare resources to support the development of rural MCS for older adults.

Recognizing the interplay between multiple structural dimensions of MCS, we propose an integrated analysis of their impact on social functions. Based on a systematic literature review exploring social care services for older adults in China, we identified four key social functions [29–31]. These functions reflect the impact of MCS’s various structural dimensions and demonstrate that a strong sense of perception is closely related to the increasing alignment of care service activities with rural care service principles. These activities have: (a) Enhanced socio-cultural functions, as mutual aid regulations have led more older adults to engage in voluntary work with NGOs supporting MCS; (b) Strengthened social orientation functions, driven by diversified support from formal MCS providers that have become more integral to rural care service activities; (c) Reinforced social integration functions, as sustained resources have promoted efficiency and deepened the impact of MCS, and (d) Improved social-communication functions. We adopted this categorization in our data analysis and results structure.

Methods

Analytical framework

The literature indicates that the structural dimensions of MCS can be developed based on mutual support for older adults, maintained through emotional bonds and informal family care, and strengthened by the establishment of standardized, stable institutional regulations. These regulations may consist of a “mixture” of rules, conventions, customs, and beliefs [11, 12]. Drawing on institutional theory, we conceptualize MCS structures through three institutional pillars, namely, regulative (rules and laws), normative (values and expectations), and cultural-cognitive (shared beliefs and meanings) [11, 12]. Further, researchers have proposed that the structural dimensions of MCS should also focus on institutions that support older adults, encompassing regulatory, normative, and cultural-cognitive dimensions that are vital to social life and resource allocation [12]. Existing literature suggests that the structural dimensions of MCS also involve the procedures or codes of action that older adults are expected to follow, as well as emotional perspectives tied to social values and cultural backgrounds that reflect historical contexts [12, 13]. Thus, the structural dimensions of MCS can be understood as an analytical framework addressing MCS’s role in meeting societal demands and the unmet care needs of older adults.

To fully explore MCS’s potential, a clear articulation of its multiple structural dimensions is essential. In a significant contribution to the literature, the current study proposes that the concept of MCS’s structural dimensions serves as the foundation for our analytical perspective, providing an overarching framework to illustrate the multiple interactions between care service structures and their impacts on the social environment across disciplines. Based on this understanding and identification of MCS, we developed a theoretical approach that examines the association between MCS’s multiple structural dimensions and their social impacts on systematic social functions for older adults. We propose that in promoting its four dimensions—perceptions, regulations, providers, and resources— MCS should significantly influence the relevant social functions (Fig. 1). These four dimensions emerged from our thematic analysis and were subsequently interpreted through Scott’s institutional pillars, in which perceptions correspond to the cultural-cognitive pillar, regulations to the regulative pillar, providers to the normative pillar (as they embody social roles and expectations), and resources to the material foundation supporting all pillars (also see Table 3 in the Appendix 3) [12].

As shown in Fig. 1, we argue that, based on our theoretical approach to MCS, interview data, and institutional document data, a strong sense of perception is closely related to care service activities increasingly aligned with rural care service logics, which in turn have enhanced socio-cultural functions. Regulations have led to greater engagement of older adults in voluntary work, as well as increased contributions from NGOs to MCS, thereby implying an enhanced social orientation function [37]. The diverse support from formal providers has gained significance in relation to rural care service activities, reinforcing several social integration functions. Moreover, the continuity of resources has effectively deepened the impact of care services for older adults, thereby enhancing social-communicational functions [38]. We acknowledge that this functionalist framing serves as an analytical heuristic, not as a normative prescription. It is possible that some people may not have accepted these functions or may have experienced them differently [39]. We address this limitation by including critical perspectives in our results and discussion sections.

Data and methods

We adopted a case study to explore the multi-functionality of mutual support care services and their structural dimensions on social functions for older adults in China. We examine an instrumental case to better understand a more general phenomenon rather than one that is merely intrinsically interesting. Sanzhou Village was selected as it constitutes a “bounded system” with clear geographical and administrative boundaries that are reflected in the residents’ social reality. Furthermore, potential respondents in Sanzhou Village have typical unmet care needs coupled with relatively weak responses from care services. The first and second authors possess prior research experience in the region; the village is easily accessible; and the authors have the expertise necessary for verifying the collected data.

Data collection and dataset

Data collection began in 2022 with a field visit and exploratory interviews. Following our initial insights, we selected 37 out of 40 interview partners through purposive stratified sampling. To enhance the validity of our qualitative data, we aimed to include diverse perspectives from older recipients who had either received MCS services or had continuous access to such services for more than six months prior to the date of our interview. Additionally, we included suppliers, which comprised older residents who had provided MCS to other residents continuously and flexibly for more than six months, community staffs working responsible for implementing MCS in the case study region, and NGO staffs who had delivered services to older adults under MCS.

To remain open to new ideas that emerged during data collection and to incorporate a participatory element into our sampling, we employed the snowball sampling selection and invited local government officers to participate. The sample size was determined by data saturation, which is defined as the point at which no new themes emerged from three consecutive interviews. Saturation was achieved after 35 interviews, with 5 additional interviews conducted to confirm saturation.

Information on interviewees

The interview formats were designed to accommodate the varying backgrounds, perspectives, and roles of the participants. They were based on open-ended questions that directly and indirectly addressed the role of MCS within their social context. For example, older adults who receiving MCS were asked about their interactions with neighbors, relatives, friends, and the village community, the role of these services in fulfilling their care needs, and their knowledge and opinions about MCS. Community staffs were asked to describe their understanding of older adults’ satisfaction with MCS and their thoughts on collaborating with NGOs in providing these services. NGO staffs were requested to explain the extent to which their organization had integrated into the service-providing programs and the challenges they had faced during this process.

Subsequently, further open-ended questions were posed and discussed. Data collection concluded when the dataset was deemed rich enough to generate insights into social functions and their relationship to MCS structures, as well as to draw connections to contemporary literature. The interviews were conducted by university students trained in interview skills, each with over two years of experience in conducting residents interviews. The research team evaluated all interview questions. Each interview lasted for at least 30 min; some lasted for 45–90 min, with an average duration of approximately 40 min.

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the authors’ university (approval number: AHNU-ET-2022046), and all interviewees provided informed consent to participate in the interviews after being briefed on the study’s purpose. Each interview was transcribed in Chinese, translated into English, and cross-checked by professional language editing services.

Furthermore, the 112 service implementation documents (2000–2020) were analyzed to triangulate and validate the four structural dimensions (perceptions, regulations, providers, resources) identified in interviews, rather than to construct a chronological narrative of change. Documents such as village regulations, government funding records, and NGO service contracts provided objective evidence for the existence and formalization of these dimensions. Additionally, they served to cross-verify the interviewees’ accounts (e.g., claims about government funding were checked against fiscal records), thereby enhancing the credibility of our findings.

Data analysis

The interview transcripts were thematically analyzed to explore how variations in the structural changes of MCS impacted the social functions of older adults in rural communities. NVivo 12 software was used to organize and code the interview transcripts. Three researchers independently coded the data and held regular meetings to resolve discrepancies. Inter-coder reliability was assessed through consensus coding, with agreement exceeding 90% after reconciliation. Instead of constructing a historical narrative, the analysis focused on identifying the structural dimensions underpinning MCS and their relationships with social functions. Thematic analysis was performed to systematically identify the patterns related to perceptions, regulations, providers, and resources across both the interview and documentary data, which were also subsequently interpreted through Scott’s institutional framework [12]. The four social functions (socio-cultural, social orientation, social integration, social-communicational) were derived from the literature on North’s institutional framework and used as outcome categories in the results [11]. Table 3 (Appendix 3) maps the alignment between interview topics, structural dimensions, and social functions.

Three members of the lead research team read and coded the transcripts, focusing on MCS, and then collated all themes and subthemes into one document. Additional points that did not fit the initial themes were also recorded, along with quotations to illustrate key points. The second and third authors then organized all subthemes for the final synthesis. The themes and subthemes related to topics for which the most information was provided (i.e., structural changes in MCS) were summarized. Even if an issue was mentioned by only one interviewee, it was included in the case study. However, many interviewees raised similar themes. Rather than attributing specific points to individual interviewees, the main findings from across the interviews were collated. The quotations are included for illustrative purposes only.

Results

How the structured dimensions of MCS impact multiple social functions

This section examines how the various structured dimensions of MCS for older adults influence social functions in our case study [40]. Interviewees typically discussed the 2010–2020 period along with participants’ expectations for the next decades. By analyzing their statements about the development of the MCS system, we identified several connections that help fulfill multiple social functions. These connections are summarized in Fig. 1 and elaborated below through four core patterns characterizing the interactions among MCS dimensions and their impacts on social functions.

Strong mutual aid perception: enhanced socio-cultural functions

Individuals’ actions in any given situation are influenced by their preferences and beliefs [41, 42, 12]. Our findings indicate that participation in MCS is driven by a mixture of rational choices and cultural expectations. For rational choices, the “Good Points” system offered tangible incentives (e.g., points exchangeable for goods, and regarding cultural expectations, traditional values of reciprocity and filial piety motivated participation even without material rewards [43, 44]. A strong sense of mutual aid was a key driver of the rapid development of MCS in Sanzhou Village [44]. The community staffs attributed the success of the MCS to stakeholders’ affection for and willingness to help one another, their dedication to supporting older adults, their adherence to rules, and their responsiveness to the government’s call to institutionalize neighborhood mutual assistance (Interview data: Interview 2022 W, community staff director). The local culture of mutual support in Sanzhou Village provided a cultural foundation for these perceptions. This foundation—the “inertia” of mutual aid actions—also provided impetus for the subsequent development of the MCS system, encouraging older adults to become more aware of their role in helping others and inspiring them to actively participate in the MCS system.

Our case study identified a strong socio-cultural inertia in the mutual aid activities of Sanzhou Village. With a history spanning over a thousand years, the village has developed a deep-rooted culture of mutual assistance. The residents, especially older individuals, have lived in close proximity for generations, fostering strong emotional bonds. Although modern society has encouraged smaller family sizes and urbanization for younger generations, [6, 7] the older adults in Sanzhou Village continue to uphold traditional cultural values, choosing to remain in their village, particularly as their health declines, which has increased their reliance on MCS. This cultural commitment led to the spontaneous formation of the Sanzhou Village Volunteer Team for Mutual Aid for Older People in 2007, followed by the Sanzhou Village Elderly Mutual Aid Association. These initiatives encouraged residents to embrace MCS, as shared by many of the older interviewees.

We have a shared understanding of supporting each other. This tradition of helping older people is part of our culture. Like others in their 60s, I am still strong and can contribute a lot to our village, with no expectation of repayment. (Interview 2022 W, community staff director)

The establishment of the MCS in Sanzhou Village has been positively reinforced by this longstanding mutual aid culture, as reflected in the participants’ statements.

Helping each other is just normal, don’t you think? But now, if we join the MCS, we earn points or coupons instead of cash, which we can use to purchase necessities at the local supermarket. (Interview 2022A1, older adult)

Furthermore, we observed that the local community staffs played a crucial role in strengthening this mutual aid culture, thereby supporting the development of MCS for older adults. They actively promoted the integration of mutual assistance values into MCS, demonstrating how effective patterns of mutual care could be explored and applied despite the challenges involved in establishing an MCS. Sanzhou Village serves as an exemplary model for advancing MCS for older adults in small-town settings.

It is important for community staff to encourage residents to participate in MCS, either on their own or with family. Voluntary engagement is different from enforced regulations. MCS benefits everyone, and older adults especially gain when young family members work far from home. (Interview 2022 S, community staff associate director)

Similar sentiments emerged from interviews with older adults who provide MCS to their neighbors.

We participate in MCS because we’re encouraged by the community staff, who believe strongly in mutual aid. Some older people aren’t even sure what they need until they come to us. The community helps bridge that gap. (Interview 2022A4, older adult)

A staff from NGO added:

“The government’s financial support is important, but what really makes this work is the people’s willingness. Without that cultural foundation, no amount of money would make MCS sustainable.” (Interview 2022B3, staff from community NGO) In summary, the strong sense of mutual aid culture in Sanzhou Village is closely related to the rural care service model, which has enhanced the village’s socio-cultural functions through mutual care activities.

Comprehensive mutual aid regulations: strengthening social orientation functions

In recent decades, mutual aid regulations and documentation have been implemented to support MCS for older adults in rural areas. Sanzhou Village participated in the first wave of the MCS pilot project, benefiting from government-provided administrative and financial support. The village has since standardized its MCS framework, formalized regulatory documents to bolster MCS development, and established a long-term pilot service model grounded in government regulations (Interview 2022 W, community staff director). As a result of these structured guidelines, MCS in Sanzhou Village has shifted from relying on “moral solidarity” or traditional cultural values to a system of organized cooperation. This approach is based on comprehensive regulations designed to guide participant behavior, standardize services for care recipients and providers, and establish clear roles for NGOs.

Our analysis of Sanzhou Village’s comprehensive mutual aid regulations revealed three key aspects. First, the village established general MCS regulations, such as the “Mutual Aid Governance Regulations in Sanzhou Village” (2019) and “Implemented Suggestions for Enhancing Mutual Aid Governance Regulations in Sanzhou Village” (2019), aligning with both national and provincial regulations, including “Grade Classification and Evaluation of Elderly Care Institutions” (GB/T37276-2018) and “Implementation Rules for the Grade Evaluation of Elderly Care Institutions in Zhejiang Province” (Zhejiang People’s Care [2020] No. 111). These regulations addressed MCS development challenges, guiding participants in both their actions and areas for improvement.

It is necessary to implement regulations at various government levels since we receive financial support from them. However, we also need to develop our own documents tailored to our MCS system’s unique characteristics. Our aim is to provide high-quality MCS for older adults, not just casual assistance, which requires us to benchmark our document standards. (Interview 2022 W, community staff director)

The development of these comprehensive regulations established a foundation for effective and sustainable MCS practices, fostering mutually supportive behaviors that are publicly recognized and valued.

Second, MCS regulations were strengthened by promoting the internal structure of Sanzhou Village’s mutual aid system. For example, to encourage participation in MCS, a “Good Points” system was established, allowing participants to use points instead of cash to purchase goods at designated markets. These points, similar to coupons, were included in residents’ agreements, motivating older adults to engage in MCS. Additionally, internal management standards were established, covering emergency response, material storage, disease prevention, property management, and sanitation. Specifically, the Mutual Aid Association for Older People developed a charter outlining voluntary care standards to encourage older adults to participate in volunteer activities.

Volunteering is exciting. I can attend a free training class—even at 70! It takes me back to my youth when I could bring water to a neighbor in need. Now, I know how to provide care services. I even keep a checklist of tasks in my bag at all times. (Interview 2022 S, community staff associate director)

The implementation of these standards has reinforced the structured practice of mutual aid, promoting a consistent and stable MCS system in Sanzhou Village and serving as a model for other communities.

Third, we found that other regulations involved agreements with professional NGOs, which have significantly supported the development of MCS in Sanzhou Village. These NGOs contribute professional expertise to the village’s care services while fostering awareness among older adults about the benefits of timely care. For instance, Sanzhou Village signed “Entrusted Operations” and “Assisting Doctors” agreements with a professional social service organization and the village clinic, respectively. Under the MCS system, both caregivers and care recipients sign agreements with the social service organization, which clarify their respective rights and responsibilities. This approach to shared values brings older adults’ actions into a clear and reliable framework, enhancing the effectiveness of MCS. Through these regulatory agreements, more older adults are participating in MCS, and NGOs involvement have increased, reflecting strengthened social orientation functions in Sanzhou Village.

Diversified mutual aid providers: reinforcing social integration functions

Sanzhou Village has successfully coordinated diverse providers, including government entities, rural communities, and social service organizations, to support MCS initiatives. Our findings highlight the essential role of government at various levels in establishing regulations and supporting MCS in Sanzhou Village. Financial support has been especially critical; for instance, in 2019, the local government invested approximately 300,000 RMB to build care service centers for older adults in the village and has provided annual funding to support daily MCS operations.

Without the government’s financial support, we would not have been able to sustain MCS. We are grateful for this assistance and do our best to maximize its impact. (Interview 2022 W, community staff director)

In addition to funding, various government departments oversee MCS operations, ensuring effective service delivery, and encouraging older people to participate. This supervision has increased the efficiency of care services and motivated engagement in MCS (Interview 2022 S, community staff associate director)

Rural communities also play a supportive role in MCS development by providing annual financial support of 20,000–30,000 RMB. Although modest, these subsidies contribute significantly to MCS functionality. Additionally, rural communities offer free venues for activities, including care and recovery rooms in community centers equipped with seasonal temperature adjustments and drink supplies. They also monitor meal services in Sanzhou Village’s dining facilities for older adults, mediating conflicts between care recipients and providers as needed. By encouraging voluntary work among older adults, the community helps to strengthen their self-efficacy, enhance their confidence, improve their quality of life, and promote active aging. For example, during major festivals and celebrations, community staff, along with the Mutual Aid Associations and Care Service Centers for Older People, organize events to enrich the lives of older residents. Many MCS members voluntarily contribute to these events, finding a sense of joy and purpose in doing so.

Providing care can sometimes be challenging, but we manage to overcome these obstacles with support from the community and the government … Festivals and celebrations make me feel valued and supported. (Interview 2022A3, older adult)

Furthermore, NGOs have enhanced social integration between MCS recipients and service providers. For instance, Sanzhou Village’s dining facility is managed by a social service agency staffed by social work and nursing professionals. Through methods including group and individual support, the agency provides both spiritual comfort and daycare services to older adults. By organizing mutual aid groups that encourage residents to share meals at community dining halls, the agency has strengthened social networks and increased trust between care recipients and providers, enriching the MCS framework in the village. As one NGO representative noted:

MCS has a profound impact on these older adults. We often sense their loneliness and need for assistance, even though they don’t voice these needs to their children, who are often busy working. We see the need for basic support, even for simple tasks like cooking. (Interview 2022B1, head of professional NGO)

Another example of community-based support is the Mutual Aid Association for Older People, which fosters a sense of belonging through mutual aid activities, such as visiting neighbors and offering support. This association also serves as a platform for mobilizing and organizing volunteers to participate in MCS efforts.

One morning, I wasn’t feeling well, and my children were in town. When no one answered my call, an MCS member visiting for regular checks took me to the doctor. Later, I was diagnosed with a stroke. Without that visit, the outcome could have been much worse. (Interview 2022 S, community staff associate director)

A professional NGO staff member added:

“The challenge is that we are only funded for specific projects. Once the project ends, our presence becomes uncertain. The village needs a more sustainable funding mechanism.” (Interview 2022B2, staff from professional NGO) In summary, the diversification of formal support providers has become increasingly important in rural care service initiatives, significantly reinforcing social integration functions in Sanzhou village.

Continuous mutual aid resources: strengthening social and communicative functions

Our research demonstrated that Sanzhou Village successfully enhanced communication between service providers and care recipients by drawing on diverse resources. These included government funding, village collective resources (such as venues and additional funds), contributions from external NGOs with professional expertise, social donations, and support from local NGOs promoting a culture of mutual aid and volunteerism. This resource integration fostered a higher quality and more efficient practice of MCS for older adults, going beyond typical spontaneous aid services. In particular, the financial and village collective resources have strengthened the foundation for mutual support by enabling partnerships with professional institutions, such as NGOs, that offer specialized services, thus laying a solid groundwork for MCS’s ongoing professional development.

Notably, Sanzhou Village’s social service organizations introduced an innovative brand management approach. For example, a distinctive MCS logo was designed for the village and displayed on volunteer vests, small red flags, and leaflets. This branding increased members’ sense of belonging and cohesion while encouraging other residents to join MCS. We find this strategy to be highly effective in enhancing social communication within the village.

Wearing this volunteer vest [with the logo in the center] feels like wearing a uniform. It makes me feel younger, more connected, and I end up talking to more people when I wear it.” (Interview 2022 H, community staff associate director)

We wore the same clothes, visited and assisted older people, and organized activities. I cannot quite describe the feeling—it’s wonderful. (Interview 2022A5, older adult)

The continuity of these resources has significantly improved the efficiency and broadened the impact of MCS in Sanzhou Village, enhancing its social and communicative functions among older adults.

Gender differences in MCS participation and perception

Our analysis revealed notable gender differences in MCS participation patterns. Female participants were more likely to volunteer as providers (67% of female participants vs. 41% of male participants), whereas male participants were more likely to be care recipients (71% of male recipients vs. 52% of female recipients). This pattern reflects the traditional gender roles in rural China, wherein women are often expected to take on caregiving responsibilities and men are more commonly positioned as recipients of care. A female community staff member explained:

In our village, women naturally take on these roles. When we organize MCS activities, it is usually the wives who come forward. The men are more reluctant to ask for help or to offer it, but they are more willing to accept care when offered. (Interview 2022 H, community staff associate director)

Some older women expressed pride in their caregiving roles but also acknowledged the burden.

I help my neighbor every day with cooking and cleaning. It is what we do. But sometimes I am tired too. Who helps me? (Interview 2022A19, older adult)

Conversely, some older men expressed reluctance to participate in MCS, preferring to rely on family or formal services.

I don’t need strangers helping me; my family should be here. This MCS is for people who have no one. (Interview 2022A21, older adult)

These findings suggest that gender norms shape both willingness to participate in MCS and the types of roles assumed by older adults. Future MCS programs should consider using gender sensitive approaches to ensure equitable participation and address the potential overburdening of female caregivers.

Challenges and gaps in MCS implementation

The MCS system in Sanzhou Village has achieved noteworthy success. Nonetheless, our interviews and document analysis also revealed significant challenges and gaps that warrant attention. First, funding instability emerged as a persistent concern. Although the local government provided initial funding and annual subsidies, these allocations were not guaranteed over a long term. A community staff member noted:

We never know if the funding will continue next year. We plan year by year, which makes it hard to invest in training or infrastructure. (Interview 2022 W, community staff director)

Second, limited professional capacity constrained the quality of services. Although professional NGOs contributed expertise, their involvement was often project-based and temporary. A professional NGO staff member explained:

We are only contracted for specific projects. Once the project ends, we leave. The village needs local capacity, not just external support. (Interview 2022B2, staff from professional NGO)

Third, not all older adults embraced MCS. Some expressed reluctance due to privacy concerns, distrust of non-family caregivers, or a preference for traditional family care. An 82-year-old male recipient stated:

I don’t like having people I don’t know in my home. My daughter-in-law comes when she can. That is enough. (Interview 2022A7, older adult)

Fourth, generational differences emerged in attitudes toward MCS. Younger-old adults (aged 60–70) were more willing to participate as volunteers. Additionally, the oldest old (aged 80+) were more likely to be recipients but sometimes felt uncomfortable receiving help from neighbors. A community staff member observed:

The younger ones see it as a way to stay active and earn points. The older ones are more traditional and sometimes feel they are losing face by accepting help. (Interview 2022 S, community staff associate director)

Fifth, geographic barriers remained challenging. The village’s mountainous terrain made it difficult for some isolated older adults to access MCS services, even when such services were available. A community staff noted:

There are households up in the hills that we cannot reach easily. They are the ones who need help the most, but we cannot get to them regularly. (Interview 2022 W, community staff director)

These challenges highlight the need for more sustainable funding mechanisms, investment in local capacity building, culturally sensitive outreach, and innovative solutions to overcome geographic barriers.

Discussion

The challenges identified in the Results Section, such as funding instability, limited professional capacity, resistance from some older adults, generational differences, and geographic barriers, informing the following discussion of policy and practice as well as the lessons from our case study.

This case study explored the social embeddedness of MCS in Sanzhou Village, highlighting the trend toward multidimensional development of care services for older adults. Given that structural changes and social functions are highly specific to time and place, generalizing these results to other settings requires careful consideration of the unique challenges in developing MCS within those contexts. Despite the specific nature of our case study, we argue that this single-case analysis of Sanzhou Village offers four key contributions to China’s academic and policy discussions on MCS and its social functionality.

First, our findings underscore the importance of carefully cultivating public perceptions of MCS to evaluate its impacts effectively and to foster an active MCS model for older adults (Claim 1), in which responding to RQ1 so as to identify and conceptualize the multiple structural dimensions of MCS in rural China. Second, we argue that current regulatory gaps hinder the development of MCS systems in rural areas, which limits awareness of its social benefits (Claim 2), in which responding to RQ2 so as to examine how these structural dimensions influence various social functions among older adults. Third, we highlight the critical role of introducing two types of NGOs to support MCS for older adults, as existing resources are insufficient to meet care needs (Claim 3), in which responding to RQ3 so as to identify the challenges and gaps in MCS implementation and propose policy-relevant recommendations. Fourth, we emphasize the need for sustained resources to support MCS, using Sanzhou Village’s model as a promising example of continuous mutual aid resources, though certain resource gaps persist (Claim 4).

Claim 1. Enhancing mutual aid perceptions among older adults

The perception of mutual aid is a foundational element for implementing MCS among older adults in rural areas. However, this case study reveals significant challenges in fostering this perception. First, public understanding of MCS is limited to a temporary support role, suggesting that broader institutionalization and standardization of MCS are overdue. A robust perception of mutual aid is not yet embedded in the society. Second, many older adults have a limited understanding of the MCS model, viewing it as a government-led charitable service rather than an individual responsibility. As a result, older adults may not distinguish between MCS services and informal neighborly help, leading to weak perceptions of mutual aid. Third, our interviews indicated that rural residents rely heavily on traditional family caregiving, particularly from wives, daughters, and daughters-in-law, and may feel reluctant to seek care outside the family. Some older adults also feel uncomfortable expressing their care needs to non-family members. These perceptual barriers align with the resistance and generational differences noted in the Results Section. For instance, the reluctance of some older adults to accept non-family care and the differing attitudes between the “younger-old” and “oldest-old” underscore that perceptions are not uniform and must be addressed through tailored strategies.

Our results suggest that developing a strong mutual aid perception is essential for the success of MCS initiatives. We recommend broadening public understanding of mutual aid through diverse outreach efforts, such as social media, television, the Internet, and offline platforms such as community bulletin boards and brochures. Additionally, based on our findings, we propose implementing a pilot project to build rural MCS networks for older adults, which could serve as a foundation for a national MCS system in China. This case study offers valuable insights and a potential dataset for developing such a system.

Claim 2. Toward comprehensive mutual aid regulation: reevaluating policies for MCS development

This case study on the regulatory impact on Sanzhou Village’s MCS system demonstrates that while governments issue policy documents to support MCS for older adults and encourage its expansion, there are significant institutional shortcomings. First, most regulations are issued without specific guidelines on how MCS should be structured or the direction it should take. While policy documents emphasize the importance and urgency of developing MCS, frontline policy implementers often lack clear instructions on operationalizing these services. As one government policy executor shared:

We have been informed about the ideas and directions for MCS development for older people, but detailed policy implementation plans are still being explored. (Interview 2022 H, community staff)

Second, our study observed insufficient regulatory support for NGOs to assist in MCS development in rural areas through incentives such as tax reductions and financial aid. Third, existing regulations lack a focus on MCS-specific management. Despite MCS pilot programs in rural areas, the mechanisms for standardized operation remain undefined, even in well-regarded cases such as Sanzhou Village. Furthermore, most MCS systems have evolved from preexisting senior care services without promoting broader social functions (Interview 2022 W, community staff). These regulatory gaps mirror the funding instability and limited professional capacity highlighted in the Results. Without clear guidelines and sustainable financial frameworks, MCS systems remain vulnerable to interruptions and cannot attract or retain qualified providers.

We argue that the current regulations for MCS development are inadequate for its expansion into rural areas, which partly explains why the social functions of MCS remain underrecognized. As it is essential to strengthen comprehensive regulations, we propose that MCS policy improvement should begin with a well-defined policy design and local guidance documents should offer specific directions to institutionalize rural MCS. Moreover, relevant state departments should set standards for diverse MCS models and establish frameworks to regulate NGOs and other service providers.

Claim 3. Expanding professional mutual aid providers’ perspectives to strengthen social multifunctionality in MCS

A robust, multidimensional MCS sector contributes significantly to a multifunctional society and relies on support from diverse service providers. We argue that effectively developing MCS for older adults in rural areas requires the involvement of both professional and community-based NGOs. These NGOs can be introduced through incubation platforms that leverage venture philanthropy and research initiatives with government financial support and tax incentives (Interview 2020B4, staff from community NGO). NGOs are vital to building a foundation of professional mutual aid providers, which is essential for operating sustainable MCS systems for older adults. In addition to increasing the number of NGOs and fostering professional caregiving teams, strengthening the service capabilities of these NGOs is crucial.

Our case study reveals persistent gaps between the availability of MCS in rural communities and residents’ actual care needs. These gaps are partly due to a lack of positive internal perspectives on MCS within local communities. For example, some community staffs lack confidence in MCS systems and are insufficiently motivated to promote their growth, resulting in outdated awareness and neglect of MCS’s institutional infrastructure. Therefore, developing MCS in rural areas requires careful consideration of community capability-building and the selection of qualified staffs. Furthermore, it is essential to improve external perspectives by strengthening coordination and linkages to support MCS expansion. The limited professional capacity documented in the Results, particularly the project-based nature of NGO involvement, emphasize the need for long-term investment in local expertise rather than relying on short-term external support.

Claim 4. Building sustainable mutual aid resources through diverse and integrated support

Our case study highlights multiple resources essential to sustaining MCS in rural areas. We argue that the approach taken by Sanzhou Village offers a valuable model for establishing continuous mutual aid resources, even as some gaps remain. First, governments at various levels could incorporate mutual aid pensions into the care service system and increase financial subsidies for MCS. The central government’s Department of Civil Affairs, in collaboration with departments for finance, medical security, development and reform, natural resources, and poverty alleviation, could establish a dedicated mutual aid fund for older adults. The process of applying for subsidies should be streamlined to shorten approval times and allow flexible use of funds. In areas with economic challenges or urgent needs for elderly support, a portion of funds could be allocated upon project approval.

Second, MCS cannot rely solely on state financial support. Contributions from other social capital sources, such as material aid from NGOs, affordable supplies from companies and individuals, free rentals, and donations, can enhance the effectiveness of mutual aid organizations and improve MCS operational mechanisms. Social capital should encompass not only funds but also essential resources such as materials, venues, facilities, and equipment.

Third, it is critical to address the supply-demand balance in MCS. We recommend establishing a scientific mechanism for assessing care needs, which can be integrated into existing long-term care needs assessment systems. This would enable more accurate identification of care demands and facilitate a more responsive MCS model. The persistent funding instability and geographic barriers identified in the Results directly inform these recommendations. Without predictable financial resources and innovative solutions for remote areas, even well-designed MCS systems may struggle to achieve equitable coverage.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, it focuses on a single typical case; future research should include more cases and comprehensive interviews to explore the research questions across diverse contexts. Second, this study proposes its evidence based only on qualitative research outcomes; therefore, mixed research methods should be included in future research to triangulate our findings. Third, different groups of population should be included in future studies instead of only older adults, which this study focused on. Fourth, we acknowledge that interviewees may have perceived the research team as evaluators or as having connections to the local government, which may have introduced social desirability bias. Participants may have emphasized positive aspects of MCS while downplaying the challenges. We mitigated this by triangulating the interview data with document analysis, including multiple stakeholder groups (older adults, providers, NGO staffs, community staffs), and cross-checking responses across different interview types. However, this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, the retrospective nature of some interview questions (e.g., changes from 2000 to 2020) may be subject to recall bias. We addressed this by supplementing the retrospective accounts with contemporaneous document analysis and by asking participants to provide specific examples to support their recollections. Sixth, our findings are specific to the rural South China context and may not be directly transferable to urban settings or other countries. However, the theoretical framework and analytical approach may be adapted for use in other contexts. Seventh, we acknowledge that gender differences in MCS participation and perception were not systematically analyzed in this study. Although our sample included both female and male participants, the interview guide and analytical framework were not designed to explore gender as a central variable. Future research should employ gender-sensitive approaches to examine how traditional gender roles shape engagement with outcomes of MCS.

Conclusions

This study examined the case of a rural region in South China to assess how different structural dimensions of mutual-aid care services for older adults influence social dynamics. Our findings demonstrate that the four structural dimensions—namely, perceptions, regulations, providers, and resources—contribute distinctively to social functions. Furthermore, a strong mutual-aid culture (perceptions) enhanced socio-cultural functions, comprehensive regulations strengthened social orientation, diversified providers reinforced social integration, and sustained resources improved social-communicational functions. Our analysis also revealed significant challenges that temper these positive outcomes. Funding instability limited professional capacity, while resistance from some older adults, generational differences in attitudes, and geographic barriers constrained the reach and sustainability of MCS in Sanzhou Village. These findings suggest that although MCS holds considerable promise, its successful implementation requires not only cultural and regulatory support but also stable funding, local capacity-building, and targeted outreach to overcome resistance and inequities in access.

The study’s insights aim to inform future research and facilitate policy debates on aligning MCS development more closely with societal expectations regarding care for older adults. By clarifying both the strengths and challenges of MCS in a typical rural Chinese village, this study makes a significant contribution to the literature by addressing gaps in empirical evidence on MCS, particularly regarding its structural implementation, utilization, and the broader impacts of structural changes on its multifunctional roles.
