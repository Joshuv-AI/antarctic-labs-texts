Dynamic Ultrasound Assessment of Axial Extensor Mechanism Alignment in Adolescents With and Without Patellofemoral Instability
Authors: Elliot M Greenberg, Bridget DeSandis, Sean Owens, Caroline Kim, Naomi Brown, Brendan Williams, J Todd R Lawrence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Patellofemoral instability (PFI) is common among adolescents. Dynamic evaluation of extensor mechanism alignment during early knee flexion may improve the understanding of recurrent instability risk.Purpose:To evaluate the use of point of care ultrasound (POCUS) to dynamically measure axial extensor mechanism alignment in patients with and without PFI.Study Design:Cohort study (diagnosis); Level of evidence, 3.Methods:Patients diagnosed with either patellar subluxation or dislocation and aged between 10 and 18 years were eligible. Control participants included those without any knee pain, nor any history of PFI, fracture, or musculoskeletal developmental disorder. Axial extensor mechanism was visualized with POCUS and the distance between the midpoint of the patellar tendon and lateral trochlear ridge (MPT-LTR) and lateral edge of the patellar tendon and LTR (LPT-LTR) was measured under 4 conditions: (1) active knee hyperextension (ie, quadriceps set), (2) full passive extension, (3) 15° of knee flexion, and (4) 30° of knee flexion. Univariate statistical analyses compared MPT-LTR and LPT-LTR distances across cohorts and knee positions. The area under the curve (AUC) evaluated measurement performance.Results:The cohort (PFI; n = 19) and controls (n = 19) were similar with respect to age, height, and weight (P > .05). In both groups, the MPT-LTR and LPT-LTR were greatest in active hyperextension and progressively medialized with increasing knee flexion. Patients with PFI demonstrated statistically significantly more lateral MPT-LTR and LPT-LTR distance in all testing conditions (P≤ .01) when compared with controls. AUC analysis demonstrated acceptable performance across measures, ranging from 0.712 to 0.837.Conclusion:Patients with PFI consistently exhibited a more laterally oriented patella while performing active knee hyperextension and throughout the first 30° of knee flexion when measured using a novel POCUS evaluation.Keywords: knee, patella; diagnostic ultrasound; clinical assessment/grading scales; pediatric sports medicine

The stability of the patellofemoral (PF) joint is a complex interplay between osseous structures, soft tissue restraints, dynamic muscular stabilizers, and lower limb biomechanics. Instability occurs when the laterally directed forces encountered by the patella exceed the capacity of these restraining structures, allowing the patella to displace beyond the boundary of the lateral trochlear ridge (LTR). PF instability (PFI) is a common condition among pediatric and adolescent athletes with a 6-fold higher incidence in 10- to 17-year-olds compared with adults. 19 Unfortunately, adolescents face a high risk of recurrence, with studies demonstrating repeat dislocations in 30% to 54% of cases.8,13 The initial treatment for PFI is largely nonoperative with brief periods of immobilization, bracing, activity modification, and referral to physical therapy. The decision of whether or not to pursue surgical intervention is based on the interplay of multiple variables in the history and physical examination, but also relies heavily on measurements of PF joint anatomy using radiography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or computed tomography (CT). Recent literature has highlighted the importance of axial imaging–based parameters in determining the likelihood of repeat PF dislocation, the need for surgical intervention, and the selection of surgical procedures.9,17 While these measurements are impactful and have significant clinical utility, they may be incomplete in their assessment of dynamic PF kinematics.

PFI typically occurs during active, weightbearing movements when the patella is most susceptible in early degrees of knee flexion. 6 Thus, functional activities such as stair climbing or sit-to-stand and athletic activities such as running or jumping replicate the joint loading scenarios in which injuries occur. Current standard of care involves using static imaging procedures with the knee in near-full extension, which fails to evaluate the PF joint in the position of injury and ignores the dynamic nature of PFI pathophysiology. 4 Over the past decade, there has been growing interest and increased use of point of care ultrasound (POCUS) in orthopaedics to assist with differential diagnosis and clinical decision making.10,12,14 Musculoskeletal ultrasound offers real-time visualization of anatomy, while also giving clinicians the ability to easily assess dynamic movements. POCUS therefore serves as an ideal modality to evaluate the PF joint and may provide opportunity for enhancing risk prediction and treatment decision-making in PFI.

Recent research has demonstrated that a newly derived measurement of axial alignment of the PF joint using ultrasound had excellent reliability and face validity. 5 However, this protocol only focused on static imaging procedures with the knee in full extension; thus, the potential for ultrasound to help visualize the dynamic movement of the PF joint remains unknown. The purpose of this study was to expand on this work and perform an exploratory study evaluating the utility of a dynamic ultrasound-based measurement protocol of axial extensor mechanism alignment in patients with and without PFI across multiple knee positions. We hypothesized that extensor mechanism alignment would change throughout early knee motion.

Methods

Patient Recruitment

This was a prospective case-control study. Patients with (case) and without (control) a clinical history of PFI were recruited from the Division of Orthopedics or physical therapy clinics within the Sports Medicine and Performance Center at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia. All recruited participants were between 8 and 21 years of age. Prior to enrollment, the participant and/or parent filled out a brief electronic questionnaire that included demographic information, sports activities, and injury history to evaluate for inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. Inclusion criteria for the patellar instability cohort required the patient to have ≥1 patellar subluxation or dislocation event that was diagnosed by a fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic sports medicine specialist or pediatric orthopaedic surgeon. The time from dislocation event to ultrasound assessment was not controlled. The control group consisted of patients being evaluated for nonknee issues at our institution who had no history of knee-related complaints. Participants were excluded from both groups if they had any history of knee surgery or any preexisting developmental disorder or previous knee injury that may have affected PF morphology (eg, patellar fracture). The study was approved by our institutional review board. Parent and participant consent and/or assent were obtained prior to the initiation of any study procedures.

Measurement Procedures

Patient Positioning

All images were obtained using a GE LOGIQ ultrasound unit and a 4-cm linear transducer. Participants began the assessment procedure positioned in a long sitting position with the knee relaxed and in full extension. A foam donut was placed under the heel to stabilize the limb, keeping the limb in a neutral position (defined as foot in vertical position) and limiting any rotational movement during the procedure. Images were obtained in order in the following positions: (1) full knee extension, quadriceps active (ie, quad set); (2) full knee extension with quadriceps relaxed; (3) knee supported by towel roll in 15° flexion; and (4) knee supported by towel roll in 30° flexion. Limb assessment order was randomized (Figure 1). All data collection procedures and postprocessing measurements were performed independently by 2 investigators (E.M.G. and B.D.).

Depiction of measurement position for the Dynamic Ultrasound for Patellofemoral Lateralization Examination protocol. (A) Active Hyperextension position with the quadriceps muscle contracted. (B) Full knee extension with the quadriceps muscle at rest. (C) 15 degrees of knee flexion. (D) 30 degrees of knee flexion. Quad, quadriceps.

Ultrasound Imaging Acquisition

Axial plane PF alignment was assessed via ultrasound in the following manner, consistent with previously published literature. 5 The ultrasound probe was placed on the anterior aspect of the patient's knee and angled slightly superiorly to allow for visualization of the patellar tendon and trochlear groove within a single view. To standardize the viewing window, the examiner first identified the inferior pole of the patella and then moved the ultrasound probe inferiorly to the point at which the most proximal aspect of the patellar tendon was in full view. With the patellar tendon in view, the probe was oriented to also capture the trochlear groove within the same image. Images were then captured and transferred to the hospital's picture archiving and communication system for measurement. The same procedure was performed twice for each limb and the mean of the 2 measures was utilized for data analysis.

Ultrasound Measurement Procedures

The linear distance from the midpoint of the patellar tendon to the LTR (MPT-LTR) was determined by first measuring the mediolateral width of the patellar tendon in the axial plane and marking the midpoint using a perpendicular line. The peak of the LTR was operationally defined as the apex of the curve, and a perpendicular line was drawn to bisect this point (Figure 2). The distance between the MPT and LTR was then determined by measuring the distance between these 2 points and recorded in millimeters. In this measurement procedure, a smaller MPT-LTR value indicates a more laterally oriented patellar tendon position relative to the trochlea.

The linear distance from the lateral edge of the patellar tendon to the LTR (LPT-LTR) was also calculated. The LTR was identified using the same methods described previously in this manuscript. A perpendicular line indicating the lateral-most edge of the patellar tendon was drawn and the horizontal distance between these 2 points was recorded in millimeters. In this measurement, the value was recorded as positive if the LPT was lateral to the LTR and negative if the LPT was medial to the LTR. Thus, a higher, positive number indicates a more laterally oriented patellar tendon, while smaller values or negative values indicate a relatively medially located patellar tendon relative to the trochlea (Figure 2).

Statistical Analysis

Summary statistics analyzed group characteristics. Separate independent-samples t tests were utilized to compare patients with PFI to healthy controls in both the involved and the uninvolved limbs for each measure procedure. In order to analyze the change in patellar position between the varying positions or measurement constructs, the difference between each measurement (eg, change between full extension at rest to 15° flexion) was calculated for all, and the differences were compared using independent-samples t tests. Measurement performance to discriminate those with PFI from healthy controls was performed for each position and was analyzed with area under the curve (AUC) analysis. Receiver operating characteristic curves were performed to determine the sensitivity and specificity of each parameter. The cutoff threshold demonstrating optimal balance of sensitivity and specificity was determined using the Youden index.

Results

A total of 40 participants (20 cases, 20 controls) were evaluated in this study. There were 2 participants (1 case, 1 control) who were removed from data analysis because of issues with image quality. The remaining participants were similar in demographic features (Table 1). For the patients with PFI, 79% had unilateral involvement while 21% had bilateral instability symptoms. Eleven (58%) were diagnosed as having patellar dislocation, while the remaining patients were diagnosed with patellar subluxation.

MPT-LTR Distance

The MPT-LTR distance followed a similar pattern across both groups and within both knees, with the patella being located most laterally while performing a quad set, then progressing medially as the knee was in full extension (0°) to 15° and finally 30° of flexion (Figure 1). There was a statistically significant difference between patients with PFI and healthy controls in all measurement positions, on both the involved and the uninvolved legs (Table 2).

Measurement Performance

The AUC analysis demonstrated acceptable performance with AUC values ranging from 0.712 to 0.837 across all measures (Tables 4 and 5). Overall, the knee flexed to 15° demonstrated the highest discrimination ability across both measurements with a sensitivity and specificity of 73.6% and 94.7%, respectively, for MPT-LTR and 78.9% and 89.4%, respectively, for LPT-LTR.

Discussion

This feasibility study with preliminary construct validation aimed to evaluate the Dynamic Ultrasound for Patellofemoral Lateralization Examination (DUPLEX) protocol measuring axial extensor mechanism alignment in multiple knee positions. Our findings indicate that when assessed with the DUPLEX protocol, patients with PFI demonstrated a more lateral patellar tendon position across all knee positions when compared with healthy peers (Figure 4). This difference was statistically significant across both the MPT-LTR and the LPT-LTR measures. The AUC analysis demonstrated acceptable levels of discriminate ability across all knee positions, but assessment with the knee flexed to 15º demonstrated the highest sensitivity and specificity for both MPT-LTR and LPT-LTR. These findings support the preliminary validity of this newly developed measure and support the need for future research evaluating the prognostic performance of these measures and the relationship of DUPLEX to MRI measures of extensor mechanism alignment to determine its role in treatment decision-making.

The results of this study build upon recent evidence highlighting the importance of axial extensor mechanism imaging and dynamic CT or MRI for determining the likelihood of repeat PF dislocation and treatment decision-making.1,9,16,18 While MRI and CT certainly offer much more information relative to specific features of soft tissue and bony anatomy, which is necessary for diagnosing loose bodies or articular injury, POCUS is a promising imaging modality and may help enhance clinical decision-making for pursuing nonoperative versus surgical management. Previously published work has noted there is excellent reliability and face validity of POCUS assessment of axial patellar alignment in relaxed, full knee extension. 5 The current study expands upon this work, leveraging the efficiency of POCUS, which allows clinicians to perform multiple assessments with ease and evaluate extensor mechanism alignment in a variety of positions. Within the currently described DUPLEX protocol, by positioning the knee at 0°, 15°, and 30° of knee flexion, we are able to evaluate how effectively the extensor mechanism medializes relative to the trochlea during knee flexion. Patients with PFI had a more laterally oriented extensor mechanism compared with controls at all measurement positions. The extensor mechanism for both groups did translate medially as the knee progressed through the initial phases of knee flexion; however, the difference between groups remained significant. Thus, the patients with PFI began with a more laterally oriented extensor mechanism, which remained more laterally oriented throughout the entire range from 0° to 30° of knee flexion. Further work will seek to determine what magnitudes of these measures most predict recurrent instability and what role they may play in treatment decision-making.

Assessing the extensor mechanism position during a quadriceps contraction in full knee extension is another unique feature of the current study. In current clinical practice, this is typically categorized dichotomously via the presence of a lateral J-sign. The presence or absence of a lateral J-sign does not specify severity of patellar translation, and observational grading systems of J-sign severity demonstrate limited reliability. 7 If POCUS can be utilized by clinicians to directly measure the amount of lateral movement the quadriceps muscle exerts on the extensor mechanism, it would allow enhanced utility of this routine clinical assessment. In the current study, a positive lateral J-sign was noted in 8 patients within the PFI group. When comparing those patients, those with a positive lateral J-sign demonstrated a smaller mean MPT-LTR value (2.69 mm vs 6.96 mm) and greater LPT-LTR value (7.7 mm vs 4.6 mm), indicating a more laterally oriented patella during quad set within the DUPLEX protocol. Future studies should seek to evaluate whether this direct quantification extensor mechanism lateralization is related to recurrent instability risk and offers more utility beyond standard visual grading of lateral J-sign.

The relative alignment of the tibial tubercle to the trochlear groove (TT-TG) is a widely studied anatomic risk factor for recurrent instability and is based upon the inference that the larger the offset (ie, lateral alignment of the tubercle relative to the trochlear groove) between these landmarks, the larger the lateral force vector encountered by the PF joint. 15 As the quadriceps muscle is the main muscular stabilizer of the PF joint, it exerts a strong influence on whether or not the PF joint will remain stable. Our results demonstrate that the patella was positioned more laterally during quadriceps contraction in those with PFI compared with controls in both MPT-LTR and LPT-LTR measures. Quantifying the lateral translation of the patellar tendon during quadriceps contraction allows for direct assessment of the lateral force on the PF joint. Several factors, including knee valgus, femoral torsion, and tibial torsion, may all affect the lateral force vector on the patella during movement. While the current study is unable to directly analyze the relationship between specific anatomic abnormalities and traditional MRI-based measures due to a small subgroup with available imaging, this work is intended to be interpreted within the broader context of extensor mechanism alignment assessments, including TT-TG, TT-LTR, and PT-LTR. We hypothesize that increasing lateralization of the extensor mechanism is associated with greater lateral patellar translation during quadriceps activation. Given their shared anatomic relationship to the extensor mechanism, LPT-LTR may correlate with PT-LTR, reflecting similar underlying biomechanical drivers. Future work from this group of investigators aims to prospectively evaluate the relationships among these measures and determine whether dynamic DUPLEX-derived metrics provide added clinical value beyond static MRI-based alignment measures.

Interestingly, there were statistically significant differences in the MPT-LTR and LPT-LTR distance between both the involved and the uninvolved limbs of those with PFI, compared with healthy controls. These results indicate that adolescents with a history of patellar instability demonstrate a more lateralized patella on both the affected and the unaffected limb. Within our sample, 4 out of 19 (21%) of the patients with PFI had a history of bilateral patellar instability. This finding may be congruent with previous research that has demonstrated a high risk of contralateral patellar dislocation, particularly among adolescents, after a primary dislocation event.2,3,11 The ease of performing bilateral assessments using the DUPLEX protocol described in this study is another advantage over traditional imaging modalities. The potential for bilateral examination or serial monitoring to gain further insight into ipsilateral or contralateral injury risk and changes in patellar position that may be associated with nonoperative or operative treatment is easy and cost-efficient when using POCUS. Future research should be directed toward longitudinal assessment of this dynamic measure to understand how it relates to recurrent and contralateral instability as well as whether it changes in response to nonoperative treatments (bracing or physical therapy).

Limitations

The current work is not without limitations. Because of the small sample size, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution and considered preliminary. Patients were recruited from the orthopaedic clinic after sustaining an instability event. This may introduce selection bias, as these patients may have more severe anatomical abnormalities than the general population of patients with PFI. In addition, the time from patellar instability event to the time of assessment was not controlled, which may affect the results. Examiners were also not blinded, which may have introduced further bias. The ultrasound-based assessment described in this study is an extension of previously published work that has established the reliability of POCUS in assessing extensor mechanism alignment. 5 However, the reliability of the entire DUPLEX protocol was not included. Given the technique-dependent nature of musculoskeletal ultrasound, further work is necessary to evaluate the reliability of this measurement protocol. Last, patient-specific factors such as body mass index, patellar height, or trochlear dysplasia could affect the reliability of this measurement; however, these factors were unable to be evaluated given the size of the studied cohort. Finally, while the control group did not have any patellar instability events, imaging to specifically determine if any anatomical abnormalities of the extensor mechanism existed within the control group was not available.

Conclusion

While the results of this study should be considered preliminary, the DUPLEX protocol may be a cost-effective and efficient adjunct to improve clinical decision-making in patients with PFI. Both the MPT-LTR and the LPT-LTR distances shifted more medially as the knee moved into flexion; however, patients with PFI consistently had a more laterally oriented patellar tendon throughout the first 30° of knee flexion in both measures. In some patients with PFI, there was a more pronounced lateral shift in patellar tendon location when performing a quad set, which may indicate more severe extensor mechanism dysfunction. Further exploration of the relationship of this ultrasound-based protocol to existing radiographic risk factors in PFI and the risk of recurrence to establish clinical relevance is warranted.
