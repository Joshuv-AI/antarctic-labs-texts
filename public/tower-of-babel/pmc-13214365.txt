Epidemiology, antifungal susceptibility, risk factors, and mortality of adult candidemia at a tertiary hospital in Eastern China from 2020 to 2025
Authors: Yu-qiong Zhang, Wen-jing Li, Ning Su, Ya-nan Wang, Jing-jing Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the epidemiology, antifungal resistance profiles, and risk factors for 30-day mortality in adult patients with candidemia in Eastern China, ultimately informing improved clinical management strategies.MethodsData on clinical characteristics, blood culture isolates, and antifungal susceptibility were retrospectively collected from the laboratory information system and analyzed for adult patients with culture-confirmed candidemia from 2020 to 2025. Independent risk factors for 30-day mortality were identified using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses.ResultsA total of 111 candidemia cases were identified during the study period. Patients were predominantly male (68/111, 61.26%), with the largest proportion aged 60–74 years (44/111, 38.74%). Nearly half of all cases (51/111, 45.95%) were identified in the intensive care unit (ICU). Candida albicans was the most frequently isolated species (52/111, 46.85%), followed by Nakaseomyces glabratus (formerly Candida glabrata) (24/111, 21.62%), Candida parapsilosis (19/111, 17.12%), Candida tropicalis (15/111, 13.51%), and Pichia kudriavzevii (formerly Candida krusei) (1/111, 0.90%). No significant shift in species distribution was observed over the six-year period (P > 0.05). Antifungal susceptibility testing revealed that all isolates were susceptible to amphotericin B and 5-flucytosine. However, Candida tropicalis showed the highest resistance rates to voriconazole (9/15, 60.00%) and fluconazole (7/15, 46.67%). Resistance in Candida albicans was notable for itraconazole (8/52, 15.38%). The overall 30-day mortality rate was 43.24% (48/111). On multivariate analysis, cardiac disease was an independent risk factor for mortality (adjusted OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.36–7.88, P = 0.008).ConclusionAdult Candidemia was associated with high mortality in Suzhou, China. Although Candida albicans remained predominant, the clinical burden of non-albicans species and high local resistance, especially to voriconazole in Candida tropicalis, underscored the necessity of targeted therapy. Cardiac disease was an independent predictor of mortality, emphasizing the importance of cardiovascular risk assessment, particularly in the elderly.Keywords: Candidemia, Candida, Risk factor, Epidemiology, Antifungal susceptibility, Mortality

Introduction

Candidemia is a major invasive fungal infection, contributing to considerable global morbidity and mortality [1]. It is primarily caused by Candida albicans (C. albicans) and increasingly by non-albicans Candida (NAC) [2]. NAC species often exhibited reduced susceptibility to azoles and other first-line antifungals, complicating treatment decisions [3]. For instance, Candida glabrata frequently demonstrated fluconazole resistance, while Candida parapsilosis (C. parapsilosis) was associated with biofilm formation on medical devices, increasing the risk of persistent infections [4]. Furthermore, based on recent molecular and phylogenetic analyses, Candida krusei and Candida glabrata has been reclassified and renamed as Pichia kudriavzevii (P. kudriavzevii) [5] and Nakaseomyces glabrata (N. glabratus) [6]. Given the inherent resistance of P. kudriavzevii to fluconazole [7], this reclassification is particularly important in clinical practice. Global surveillance data revealed considerable geographical variation in Candida resistance patterns. The highest fluconazole resistance rates were observed in N. glabratus from North America (10.6%) and Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis) from the Asia-Pacific region (9.2%), while echinocandin resistance remained low but showed a slow, steady emergence particularly among N. glabratus and C. tropicalis isolates [8]. According to the results of a nationwide surveillance program, the China Hospital Invasive Fungal Surveillance Net (CHIF-NET), spanning over a decade, Candida remained a common pathogen in invasive yeast infections in China [9]. Overall, there is a decreasing trend in the susceptibility of Candida to azoles, while it still exhibits high susceptibility rates (typically > 98%) to echinocandins and amphotericin B with resistance rates remaining below 2% susceptibility [10]. Collectively, these data highlighted the geographic variability in antifungal resistance, underscoring the critical need for local surveillance to guide empiric therapy.

Risk stratification models for candidemia have evolved in recent years. Several studies have identified invasive procedures, such as central venous catheterization and mechanical ventilation, as independent predictors of mortality [11, 12]. However, the relative importance of these factors varied across patient populations. For instance, in elderly patients, comorbidities like chronic kidney disease appeared more influential than procedural factors [13]. This heterogeneity underscored the need for population specific risk assessment tools.

The objective of the present study was to investigate the epidemiological characteristics, antifungal drug susceptibility, and risk factors influencing 30-day mortality in patients with candidemia in Suzhou region in recent years. This investigation was conducted via a retrospective analysis of clinical data from patients diagnosed with candidemia at a tertiary hospital in Suzhou from 2020 to 2025. The findings of this study are expected to provide a reference for the clinical diagnosis, treatment and prevention of candidemia.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This retrospective study was conducted at a Grade A tertiary general hospital with six campuses in Eastern China, which has 4,600 inpatient beds, 48 clinical departments. Patients aged ≥ 18 years old with a confirmed diagnosis of candidemia from January 2020 to December 2025 were included in this study. Candidemia was clinically defined as the isolation of Candida species from at least one positive blood culture, accompanied by corresponding clinical symptoms and signs [14]. Inclusion required fulfillment of this definition and the availability of complete clinical records. Patients were excluded if they had received prophylactic antifungal therapy prior to Candida detection or had mixed Candida species infections. Pediatric patients (aged < 18 years) were excluded from this study. During initial data screening, 9 cases of candidemia in pediatric patients (all neonates) were identified; however, they were excluded because the clinical characteristics and risk factors for mortality in pediatric populations differ fundamentally from those in adults [15, 16]. This exclusion also helped minimize confounding effects of prior antifungal exposure on susceptibility data and ensured a clear species‑outcome correlation for single-species infections. For analysis, anonymized data were collected across clinical dimensions, including demographics, admitting department, underlying comorbidities, nosocomial infections, recent surgery (within 3 months), hospital stay before fungal detection, duration of prior antibacterial therapy, and antifungals use after fungemia onset. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. K-2024-144-K01). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Methods

Blood culture samples were collected in accordance with national health industry standards [17]. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, blood was inoculated into both aerobic and anaerobic culture vials and deposited in the BD BACTEC™ FX200 automated blood culture system (BD Company, USA). Upon receiving the positive blood culture alert from the instrument, the sample was promptly inoculated onto Columbia blood agar, MacConkey agar and Chocolate agar to ensure viability and purity. Simultaneously, Gram staining and microscopic examination were conducted for preliminary identification. Species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer (MALDI-TOF MS, Bruker Daltonics, Germany).

Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the ATB FUNGUS 3 kit (bioMérieux, France), which assesses five antifungal agents, including amphotericin B, 5-Flucytosine, and three azoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole). Quality control was ensured using reference strains Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019 and P. kudriavzevii ATCC 6258. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined and interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M27M44S-ED3 [18] to ensure methodological consistency. For antifungal agents with established clinical breakpoints (i.e., fluconazole and flucytosine), the formal CLSI clinical breakpoints (CBPs) were applied. For other antifungal drugs lacking defined clinical breakpoints (e.g., amphotericin B), the CLSI epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs) were used to distinguish between wild-type (WT) and non-wild-type (non-WT) phenotypes. For N. glabratus lacking established clinical breakpoints, ECVs were applied for resistance determination [19]. To ensure accuracy and avoid potential overestimation of azole MICs associated with automated systems, all ATB FUNGUS 3 strips were interpreted via manual visual reading by two experienced microbiologists [20].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics software version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables (e.g., age, hospital stay duration) were expressed as median (Q₁, Q₃) using the Mann-Whitney U test for two independent samples, while categorical variables (e.g., comorbidities, procedure history) were expressed as n (%) using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test . Multivariate logistic regression identified independent mortality predictors, with variables selected based on univariate significance (P < 0.2) or clinical relevance. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Due to limited statistical power and model stability considerations, Candida species with fewer than 5 cases (P. kudriavzevii) were excluded from formal comparative analyses and multivariable regression models, but remained included in descriptive statistics.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of candidemia cases

A total of 111 candidemia cases were included in the analysis. The proportion of males was significantly higher than that of females (68/111, 61.26% vs. 43/111, 38.74%; P = 0.029). Age distribution differed significantly (P < 0.001), with the majority occurring in patients aged 60–74 years (44/111, 38.74%) and 75–89 years (37/111, 33.33%). C. albicans was the predominant species (52/111, 46.85%), followed by N. glabratus (24/111, 21.62%), C. parapsilosis (19/111, 17.12%), C. tropicalis (15/111, 13.51%), and P. kudriavzevii (1/111, 0.90%) (P < 0.001). The intensive care unit (ICU) accounted for the highest proportion of cases (51/111, 45.95%), followed by the emergency intensive care unit (EICU, 15/111, 13.51%) (P < 0.001). Statistical analysis showed no significant difference in the annual incidence rates across the six years (P = 0.472) (Table 1).

Distribution of Candida species

Species distribution remained stable across the study period, with no significant year-to-year differences observed for C. albicans, N. glabratus, C. tropicalis, or C. parapsilosis (all P > 0.05). C. albicans consistently predominated, ranging from 30.77% (4/13) to 54.17% (13/24) annually. Despite the overall stability, notable fluctuations were observed in NAC species. The percentage of N. glabratus ranged from 10.34% (3/29) to 47.06% (6/17), while the percentage of C. parapsilosis showed marked variation, peaking at 31.03% (9/29) in 2025 after complete absence in 2024. C. tropicalis was not detected in 2022 but subsequently accounted for 16.67–28.57% of isolates in 2023–2024. P. kudriavzevii remained rare, detected in only one case throughout the study period (Table 2).

Drug resistance patterns of Candida species

Overall, 25 out of 111 isolates were resistant to at least one tested antifungal drug. The in vitro antifungal susceptibility profiles revealed distinct, species-specific drug resistance patterns among the four prevalent Candida species (P. kudriavzevii was excluded to only one isolate being available ) (Table 3). Overall, amphotericin B and 5-flucytosine demonstrated potent universal in vitro activity, with all 110 isolates across the four species exhibiting 100% susceptible (S) or wild-type (WT) phenotypes. C. tropicalis exhibited the most severe azole resistance profile, with high proportions of isolates classified as resistant (R) or non-WT to voriconazole (9/15, 60.00%), itraconazole (8/15, 53.33%), and fluconazole (7/15, 46.67%). C. albicans isolates showed a moderate azole resistance pattern, with non-susceptibility rates ranging from 11.54% (6/52) to 15.38% (8/52). Notably, while frank fluconazole resistance was absent in N. glabratus, all isolates (100%) were categorized as susceptible-dose dependent (SDD) in accordance with CLSI breakpoints; additionally, non-WT phenotypes were observed for itraconazole (1/24, 4.17%) and voriconazole (8/24, 33.33%) [21]. Conversely, C. parapsilosis was the most susceptible to azoles overall, showing complete susceptibility to itraconazole (100%) and exhibiting relatively low resistance rates to voriconazole (2/19, 10.53%) and fluconazole (1/19, 5.26%).

Risk factors associated with mortality in candidemia patients

Among 111 candidemia patients, the overall mortality rate was 43.24% (48/111). Univariate analysis identified cardiac disease (P = 0.005), surgical procedures (P = 0.040), and urinary catheterization (P = 0.037) as factors significantly associated with mortality. However, age, sex, hospitalization duration, antimicrobial use duration, comorbidities (pulmonary infection, liver/renal dysfunction, diabetes, hypoproteinemia, solid tumor, cerebrovascular disease, hematological disease, hypertension, and urinary system infection), other invasive procedures (endotracheal intubation, central venous catheter, dialysis, parenteral nutrition, and blood transfusion), Candida species, antifungal treatment, and fluconazole resistance showed no significant associations (all P > 0.05) (Table 4).

Multivariate logistic regression confirmed cardiac disease as an independent risk factor for mortality (OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.36–7.88, P = 0.008), indicating that patients with pre-existing cardiac conditions had more than three-fold increased odds of death. Conversely, a history of surgery was independently associated with a lower risk of 30-day mortality (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.16–0.86). Urinary catheterization, despite univariate significance, did not retain statistical significance in the multivariate model (P = 0.066) (Table 5).

Discussion

Candidemia is recognized to be a deadly global health threat [22]. This six-year retrospective study analyzed 111 candidemia patients at a tertiary hospital in Eastern China. The male predominance observed in this study was consistent with other studies suggesting sex-based differences in susceptibility to candidemia, potentially attributable to higher rates of invasive procedures and healthcare exposures among hospitalized males [8]. The age distribution revealed striking concentration in older adults, with 81.08% (90/111) of cases occurring in patients aged 60 years or older, reflecting the convergence of immunosenescence, increased comorbidity burden, and greater healthcare-associated exposures in the elderly population [13]. The distribution of fungal species revealed that C. albicans was predominant, followed by N. glabratus, C. parapsilosis, and C. tropicalis. This was consistent with the contemporary epidemiological trends in China recorded by the CHIF-NET surveillance program [23, 24], and also aligns with studies conducted in South Korea and Belgium [25, 26]. The relatively high proportion of NAC (59/111, 53.15%), observed in our study aligns with the current epidemiological trend toward a greater prevalence of azole-resistant strains [4]. The overwhelming concentration of cases in intensive care settings emphasized that candidemia remained predominantly a disease of critically ill patients requiring intensive monitoring [27]. Temporal variation in case numbers might reflect the impact of the COVID-19 policy shift in China in 2023, while the increase in 2025 likely stemmed from an institutional merger that expanded patient coverage.

The present study identified significant species-specific azole resistance differences among four predominant Candida species causing candidemia in Eastern China. C. tropicalis exhibited the highest azole resistance rates (> 45% for fluconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole), consistent with global surveillance data showing increasing multidrug resistance in this species, likely driven by widespread clinical azole exposure and cross-resistance mechanisms [23, 28, 29]. Studies have shown that hotspot mutations (such as Y132F) in the ERG11 gene and amplification of the ERG11 gene in C. tropicalis are the main mechanisms leading to cross-resistance to fluconazole and voriconazole [30]. Regarding N. glabratus, our in vitro data requires careful interpretation based on specific CLSI guidelines. In our cohort, 100% of the N. glabratus isolates were categorized as susceptible-dose dependent (SDD) to fluconazole. It is crucial to note that CLSI does not define a fully “Susceptible” category for this species-drug combination due to its inherently reduced susceptibility, meaning clinical success with fluconazole necessitates higher dosing [18]. Conversely, for voriconazole and itraconazole, which lack established clinical breakpoints, epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs) must be applied. We identified that 33.33% (8/24) and 0% (0/24) of the isolates were non-wild-type (non-WT) to voriconazole and itraconazole, respectively. The appearance of voriconazole non-WT isolates alongside 100% fluconazole-SDD isolates should not be misinterpreted as the species being “more susceptible” to fluconazole. Instead, these distinct categories (SDD vs. WT/non-WT) cannot be directly compared for clinical efficacy. The presence of non-WT strains indicates that a subset of our local isolates likely harbors acquired resistance mechanisms. Globally, N. glabratus exhibits reduced fluconazole susceptibility and varying azole cross-resistance [31]. This phenotype is primarily driven by ERG11 mutations or overexpressed efflux pumps (CgCDR1/2) [31, 32]. Differential pump affinities elegantly explain why voriconazole MICs slightly exceeded the epidemiological threshold while fluconazole remained masked within its broad SDD range (≤ 32 µg/mL). Consequently, non-azole agents (such as echinocandins) should remain the priority for empirical therapy when N. glabratus is suspected, and local surveillance must remain vigilant for the emergence of non-WT phenotypes [31].

The resistance rate of C. albicans to azole drugs ranges from 11.54% (6/52) to 15.38% (8/52), which is consistent with multiple global and regional research findings [10, 33], indicating that there is some fluctuation in its resistance rate and continuous monitoring of resistance evolution is necessary, as the resistance mechanisms are complex and may vary over time and across regions [4, 34]. Despite the overall resistance rate of C. parapsilosis being low, global antifungal surveillance data indicates that resistance is concentrated in specific geographic regions, and fluconazole resistance shows a significant prevalence and rising trend worldwide [35]. It is noteworthy that all isolates were completely sensitive to amphotericin B and 5-fluorocytosine, a finding that aligns with the global antifungal drug management guidelines. In accordance with these guidelines [36], amphotericin B and echinocandins are considered the preferred treatment options for NAC bloodstream infections, owing to their extensive spectrum of activity. However, some NAC (such as C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis) have exhibited high MIC values, indicating potential drug resistance risks [37]. 5-Fluorocytosine, as a nucleic acid synthesis inhibitor, also maintains good activity, but it is prone to resistance when used alone, so it is often used in combination with other antifungal drugs [34].

The high prevalence of NAC species (59/111, 53.15%), combined with significant azole resistance (fluconazole resistance exceeding 11% in major species), underscored the limitations of empiric fluconazole therapy in critically ill patients [38]. Given that 59.46% (66/111) of cases occurred in ICU and EICU with overall fluconazole resistance exceeding 15%, empiric echinocandin therapy appeared justified in our hospital. The consensus recommendation for de-escalation to fluconazole once clinical stability is achieved and susceptibility is confirmed emphasizes the importance of timely species identification and susceptibility testing to optimize antifungal stewardship [39]. However, detailed data on the use of echinocandins were not documented in this study, which constitutes a limitation. The significant inter-species differences in drug resistance underscore the necessity of fungal species identification and AFST for targeted treatment. The results of this study support the cautious use of empirical azole therapy and ongoing monitoring of fungal resistance to optimize treatment outcomes and provide a basis for regional antifungal drug management.

In this study, the overall 30-day mortality rate was 43.24% (48/111), which was consistent with other studies reporting mortality rates between 30% and 60% in similar patient populations [27, 40]. More importantly, our multivariate analysis identified cardiac disease as an independent risk factor (adjusted OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.36–7.88, P = 0.008), while a history of surgery was independently associated with a significant reduction in the odds of 30-day mortality. These findings provide actionable insights for risk stratification and management optimization.

The relationship between invasive procedures and candidemia outcomes in our cohort revealed complexity. While urinary catheterization showed a significant association with mortality in univariate analysis (OR = 2.72, P = 0.041), this association was not maintained in the multivariate analysis (OR = 2.60, P = 0.066). Contrary to prior reports, other common invasive devices (central venous catheters, endotracheal intubation, and parenteral nutrition catheters) showed no significant associations with 30-day mortality in our analysis [41, 42]. For instance, the recent multinational ECMM Candida III study unequivocally highlighted the critical role of central venous catheters (CVCs) in candidemia outcomes. It demonstrated that early source control, specifically prompt CVC removal, is pivotal for improving patient survival [43]. This discrepancy might reflect differences in patient case-mix, catheter management practices, or the relative contribution of other risk factors in our specific institutional setting. Secondly, institutional differences in the timing of source control, especially early extubation when device-related infections are suspected, can significantly alter mortality outcomes [44]. After adjusting for severity indicators (cardiac disease, surgical status, and diabetes), the independent contribution of devices became non-significant, suggesting host factors may dominate short-term prognosis.

Among the host factors examined, cardiac disease emerged as the most robust predictor of mortality, with affected patients experiencing more than a three-fold increased risk even after adjustment for other significant variables. While it is challenging to distinguish whether the observed mortality is a direct consequence of candidemia or reflects the high baseline risk associated with advanced cardiac conditions, our multivariate analysis suggests that cardiac disease acts as an independent contributor to poor outcomes. This finding aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that cardiovascular comorbidities substantially compromise host defense mechanisms and hemodynamic stability during fungal sepsis [2, 45]. The systemic inflammation and increased metabolic demands associated with candidemia may exacerbate underlying cardiac dysfunction, resulting in a synergistic increase in mortality [46]. Previous studies have identified cardiopathy as a major independent risk factor for mortality in candidemia, with odds ratios ranging from 8.91 to as high as 19.36, indicating a strong association beyond baseline fragility [13, 47]. Patients with underlying cardiac disease often exhibit impaired tissue perfusion, reduced immune cell trafficking, and limited physiological reserve to withstand the massive inflammatory cascade and hemodynamic stress triggered by candidemia [2]. In our study, 81.08% (90/111) of patients were aged 60 or older, a population particularly vulnerable to this “double hit” of infection and chronic organ failure.

Conversely, multivariate analysis demonstrated that surgical history was independently associated with lower 30‑day mortality (OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.16–0.86, P = 0.020). This result appears contradictory, since major abdominal surgery is a well‑known risk factor for candidemia related to mucosal injury and invasive procedures [37]. However, the association between surgery and lower mortality should be interpreted carefully due to potential clinical and statistical confounding factors. This association may be explained by selection bias. Patients who tolerate surgical intervention usually have better physiological reserve and less severe comorbidities than those who are not eligible for surgery [48]. Surgical patients also receive effective source control, including abscess drainage, debridement and infected device removal, which is essential for improving survival in fungal sepsis. In addition, intensive perioperative surveillance in surgical settings allows early identification and management of clinical deterioration [36]. In this relatively small cohort (n = 111), statistical associations may not fully align with clinical causality. The apparent “protective” effect of surgery should be interpreted as a reflection of better baseline clinical status and the feasibility of source control, rather than an inherent survival benefit of the procedure itself. These findings suggest that although healthcare‑associated exposures initiate infection, prognosis is primarily determined by host factors, particularly cardiovascular reserve and surgical candidacy.

It is imperative to acknowledge the limitations of this study. Firstly, the single-center, retrospective design and relatively small sample size (n = 111) of the study may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduce potential selection bias. Furthermore, key risk factors, including APACHE II scores, corticosteroid use, and specific surgical procedures, were not uniformly captured in electronic medical records. This may have resulted in an underestimation of certain clinical predictors. Secondly, the research revealed a paucity of detailed information regarding antifungal regimens, the timing of treatment initiation, and the adequacy of source control. Consequently, we were unable to correlate clinical outcomes (mortality vs. survival) directly with the appropriateness of empiric or targeted azole therapy, which limits the clinical interpretation of the observed resistance patterns. Thirdly, antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the ATB FUNGUS 3 kit, which does not cover echinocandins. Given that echinocandins constitute first-line therapy for candidemia, the absence of MIC data for this drug class limits the direct clinical applicability of the findings with respect to initial empiric treatment. Fourthly, the exclusion of patients with prior antifungal prophylaxis or mixed fungal infections, implemented to ensure data homogeneity, may have resulted in underestimation of mortality and antifungal resistance among patients who developed candidemia despite preventive measures. Finally, despite the use of multivariate analysis, the choice of all‑cause 30‑day mortality as an endpoint may, to some extent, reflect progression of underlying cardiac disease rather than candidemia itself. In addition, certain findings, such as the lower risk associated with surgical history, may be influenced by the relatively small sample size and inherent selection biases of this retrospective single-center study. These observations should therefore be validated in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts to ensure their clinical relevance. Hence, the development of risk prediction tools that integrate cardiac function and surgical candidacy assessments is recommended for future multicenter studies.

Conclusions

In summary, candidemia in our setting carries a high mortality burden. Although C. albicans remains the most prevalent pathogenic species, C. tropicalis presents a major clinical challenge due to its significantly reduced susceptibility to azoles. Cardiac disease was identified as an independent risk factor for 30-day mortality. This study provides updated epidemiological and antifungal susceptibility data to inform the management and prevention of candidemia among adult patients in Suzhou, China.
