Elevation-structured viral ecological strategies along glacier-fed rivers on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Yinghao Li, Tianyi Chen, Pengwei Li, Guohua Zhang, Yucheng Tian, Jiawen Wang, Jinren Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, a climate-vulnerable source of Asia’s major rivers, harbors underexplored viral communities critical to ecosystem functions. By integrating 597 metagenomes from the Yangtze, Yellow, Lancang, and Yarlung Tsangpo rivers with 85 public available glacial metagenomes (Tibetan Glacier Genome and Gene catalog), we built the Glacier-to-River Virome Catalogue, encompassing 36,358 vOTUs and 897,250 viral protein clusters, to decode viral adaptation and ecological influence across elevation gradients. Our results reveal that high-altitude conditions favor viruses with elevated Guanine-Cytosine content, larger genomes and more cold-adaptation genes. A central finding is a systematic viral lifestyle shift from temperate in glaciated regions to lytic viruses downstream, accompanied with decline of pathogens carrying antibiotic resistance genes along the glacier-to-river gradients. Further, viral auxiliary metabolic genes transition from glacier nutrient scavenging (e.g., nitrogen and sulfur transporters) to downstream mineralization processes (e.g., denitrification) in plains highlights their role in biogeochemical cycling. These findings position viruses as pivotal regulators of microbial community structural and functional dynamics to glacier-to-river gradient change and biogeochemistry in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, providing critical insights into climate response in vulnerable Asian water towers.

Introduction

Extreme environments host microbial communities uniquely adapted to harsh conditions, serving as models for ecological resilience and adaptation1. The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP), the highest and one of the most vulnerable ecosystems on Earth, functions as Asia’s primary high-mountain headwater system, often referred to as the Asian Water Tower, by storing water in glaciers, snow, permafrost, and lakes and regulating its release to sustain major river systems such as the Yangtze, Yellow, Lancang, and Indus Rivers2,3. The QTP is widely regarded as an extreme environment due to its high elevation, low temperatures, intense ultraviolet radiation, hypoxia, and oligotrophic conditions4–6. At the same time, the QTP exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity, with strong southeast-to-northwest gradients in precipitation, air temperature, and the frequency and intensity of extreme climatic events7–9. Ongoing climate change is not only reshaping the magnitude, frequency, and spatial patterns of extreme conditions across the QTP10, but is also progressively undermining the sustainability of this high-altitude system11. Climate-induced glacier retreat and permafrost thaw are deeply altering the hydrology and biogeochemistry of these rivers10–15, yet the ecological responses of viral communities remain largely unknown.

The extreme environment of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau imposes strong selective pressures on biological adaptation. Studies on macroscopic organisms have shown widespread tolerance to cold, hypoxia, and intense ultraviolet radiation at high altitudes. For example, low- and mid-altitude fish species are largely unable to migrate onto the plateau16, plateau frogs exhibit reduced metabolic rates and metabolic reorganization under lower temperature and dissolved oxygen conditions17, and plants form adaptation at morphological, physiological, and molecular levels through enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, strengthened antioxidant systems, and other molecular regulatory mechanisms18,19. Host-associated microbial communities also contribute substantially to adaptation to the harsh plateau environment. Rapid and persistent shifts in gut microbiota have been observed in humans20,21 and other plateau animals22,23 following high-altitude exposure, with enriched microbial taxa linked to altered energy metabolism, antioxidant capacity, and physiological regulation under hypoxic and high-radiation conditions. Environmental microorganisms also show strong adaptation to plateau conditions, with total microbial biomass generally declining along elevational gradients, while functional investment in resource acquisition becomes increasingly pronounced24,25. Soil microbial communities on the plateau exhibit elevation-dependent reorganization toward facultatively anaerobic, stress-tolerant, and saprotrophy-dominated strategies26. Diverse plateau lakes harbor microbial communities exhibiting multifaceted functional adaptations related to carbon cycling, energy metabolism, and stress tolerance27,28.

Genes and genomes of microbes recovered from QTP glaciers are valuable resources for discovering novel microbial lineages and unknown functions5. Meanwhile, the water bodies originating from the glaciers also serve as essential habitats for a multitude of microorganisms6. Large number of rare but active taxa exist in benthic biofilms of glacier-fed streams29 and rare taxa can thrive along rivers and eventually become dominant30. Mechanisms including energy acquisition pathways, exploitation of different resources and resistance to low temperatures and high radiation contribute to the success of complex biofilms in glacier-fed streams4. In addition, glacial retreat exerts significant influence on microbial community dynamics which poses a threat to indigenous species due to the proliferation of generalist and invasive microbial taxa31,32. Furthermore, the accelerated melting of glaciers alters the physicochemical characteristics of the environment, disrupting biodiversity patterns and functions along the downstream rivers33,34.

Although studies have begun cataloging microbial diversity and functional adaptations within QTP glaciers5 and rivers6, viral communities, their adaptive strategies and their ecological impacts, remain largely unexplored. Viruses mediate microbial evolution, nutrient cycling, and pathogenicity through infection, lysis and horizontal gene transfer (HGT)35,36. Viruses may drive host adaptation through auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) or serve as reservoirs of antibiotic resistance and virulence factors that enhance competitive fitness1,37–40. Furthermore, the release of ancient or emerging viruses from melting ice poses potential ecological and health risks41–44. Meanwhile, bacteria and archaea develop the immune system like CRISPR-Cas to defend against these foreign invaders38–40,45–48. The interactions between viruses and their hosts may lead to co-evolutionary changes that enhance fitness through selection events and alter the biogeochemical cycling49–51. Despite recent efforts to characterize viral diversity in glacial and permafrost environments44,52–54, fewer studies have systematically investigated viral biogeography, adaptation, and ecosystem function along the glacier-to-river gradients.

Here, we bridge this critical knowledge gap through a large-scale metagenomic analysis of viral communities spanning the elevational and environmental gradients of the QTP’s major river systems. We integrate new sampling from the Yangtze, Yellow, Lancang, and Yarlung Tsangpo rivers with published datasets to construct a comprehensive Glacier-to-River Virome Catalogue (GtRVC) consisting of 36,358 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) and a viral protein dataset of 897,250 viral protein clusters (vPCs). We examine how environmental gradients shapes viral genomic features, lifestyle transitions, and functional traits, and evaluate the consequences for host adaptation, biogeochemical cycling, and pathogenic genes control. Our findings reveal viruses as biogeographic regulators of ecosystem processes and provide insights into microbial resilience and viral-mediated ecological outcomes in a warming world.

Sampling overview and environmental characteristics of glacier-to-river systems

To provide a clearer environmental context for viral communities, we compiled environmental and surface energy flux data for a subset of sampling sites to characterize environmental differences. Due to the lack of environmental metadata for glacier samples obtained from public databases, comparisons of environmental and heat flux variables were restricted to monitoring sites along plateau and plain rivers, with the exception of elevation (Fig. 1b). The mean elevations of glacier, plateau river, and plain river sites were 5,168.85 m, 3,341.18 m, and 448.51 m, respectively, revealing a pronounced elevational gradient (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 9). Plateau rivers exhibited significantly lower (Wilcoxon test, P < 2.22e-16) mean temperatures (7.73 °C) compared to Plain rivers (14.60 °C). Similarly, several nutrient-related parameters were significantly lower in plateau rivers than in Plain rivers. In contrast, surface energy flux variables showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 9), with plateau rivers characterized by significantly higher values of surface solar radiation (Wilcoxon test, P < 2.22e-16), surface latent heat flux (Wilcoxon test, P < 2.22e-16), and surface sensible heat flux (Wilcoxon test, P = 3.8e-16) compared to plain rivers.

Composition and distribution of viral communities across glacier-to-river gradients

From 59,997 identified viral contigs, we derived 36,358 non-redundant vOTUs (clustered at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI), 85% coverage using CD-HIT55, Supplementary Fig. 1a), including 17,848 medium-quality (≥10 kb), 9351 high-quality, and 9159 complete genomes. Genome sizes ranged from 1.58 kbp to 1.99 Mbp, with ∼84% between 10–100 kb and ~4.5% ≥100 kb (Supplementary Fig. 1b, Supplementary Data 2). Protein clustering (80% coverage, 60% average amino acid identity (AAI)) yielded 897,250 viral protein clusters (vPCs) from 1,804,791 genes. Notably, based on CD-HIT clusters, 99.9% vOTUs and 78.4% vPCs did not include any sequences in aquatic part of IMG/VR v456 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). 99.37% vOTUs showed no BLAST hits to IMG/VR v456 (≥90% identity, ≥80% coverage, Supplementary Fig. 2a). The majority of GtRVC viral sequences showing significant BLAST matches to the IMG/VR database were derived from host-associated and environmental sources. Within the environmental category, most matched viruses were associated with freshwater and marine habitats (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Taxonomically, more than 98.5% of these matched viruses belonged to the realm Duplodnaviria, primarily represented by the class Caudoviricetes, and to the realm Monodnaviria, represented by the class Malgrandaviricetes (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

NMDS revealed clear regional separation along NMDS1 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The gene-sharing network analysis formed a total of 5318 viral clusters (Supplementary Data 8). Among these, only 0.45% of viral clusters contained sequences originating from glacier, plateau, plain, and RefSeq datasets simultaneously. 1.65% of viral clusters comprised viral sequences shared among glacier, plateau, and plain environments, while 18.1% of viral clusters were shared between plateau and plain rivers. Notably, viral clusters composed exclusively of viral sequences assembled in plain rivers accounted for 58.8% of all clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Viral α-diversity increased significantly with decreasing elevation (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3b, Wilcoxon test, P < 2.22e-16). More than 85% of vOTUs from glaciers and plateau rivers were also detected in plain rivers (Supplementary Fig. 4a), and 63.5–90.9% vOTUs present in glaciers or plateau rivers displayed significantly higher relative abundance downstream (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c).

Using BACPHLIP57, we classified 5,044 vOTUs as temperate and 31,314 as virulent (Supplementary Data 2). Temperate viruses comprised 63.5% of dominant vOTUs (top 200 vOTUs ranked by average relative abundance) in glaciers, decreasing to 45% in plateau and 16% in plain rivers (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Taxonomic assignment with geNomad58 classified 87.3% of vOTUs spanning 4 major DNA viral realms (Supplementary Data 2), largely belonging to Duplodnaviria. These vOTUs were predominantly assigned to unknown Caudoviricetes (79.8%) which represent the most viruses in many other ecosystems and are thought to infect hosts across almost all bacterial lineages59. Only 3.3% were assigned to families (e.g., Autographiviridae). In addition, we identified 193 NCLDV vOTUs (Nucleocytoviricota) which infect a wide range of eukaryotes, such as protists and algae60, and 470 virophages (Lavidaviridae)61 which can parasite on NCLDVs62 (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1e).

Virus-host interaction and dynamics across glacier-to-river gradient

To investigate viral impacts on microbes across regions, we identified 18,422 virus-host pairs connecting 3,960 vOTUs (10.8%) and 3342 prokaryotic MAGs using three prediction methods (Supplementary Data 3). Predicted hosts spanned 30 bacterial and 3 archaeal phyla, with Pseudomonadota (46.1%), Bacteroidota (20.6%), and Actinomycetota (15.3%) being the most frequent (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Archaeal hosts included Thermoproteota, Thermoplasmatota, and Halobacteriota. Based on relative abundance with statistical testing (See Methods), we identified viruses significantly enriched in each region. Viral relative abundance was positively correlated with host relative abundance at both species and phylum levels for different lifestyles in different regions (Supplementary Fig. 6a, c). However, the virus-host relative abundance ratio (VHR) decreased with increasing host relative abundance across the glacier-to-river gradient regardless of lifestyle (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This negative correlation was observed across nearly all host phyla (Supplementary Fig. 6c). VHR varied by host lineage, showing high values for Deinococcota (radiation-resistant63) across all regions: Cyanobacteriota and Bacillota in glacial ice and snow, Myxococcota in plateau rivers, and Fusobacteriota and Bacillota_C in plain rivers (Supplementary Fig. 7). In sediment, VHR was higher for Chloroflexota and Planctomycetota in glaciers, and high for Patescibacteria in plain rivers (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Hosts in glaciers were infected by 1.81 ± 1.10 (mean ± SD) virulent and 3.33 ± 3.01 temperate viruses, compared with 1.54 ± 1.01 and 2.53 ± 1.81 in plateau rivers, and 2.31 ± 4.80 and 2.35 ± 1.89 in plain rivers, respectively. VHR decreased significantly along the gradient (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 3e). The relative abundance of temperate virus also declined downstream (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3d). These patterns along glacier-to-river gradients were robust when viral lifestyles were analyzed separately and different viral genome quality thresholds were applied (Supplementary Fig. 16). Viruses in glaciers more frequently carried anti-defense systems (e.g., anti-restriction modification (anti-RM), anti-Thoeris, Supplementary Data 5) and infected hosts with defense mechanisms (Fig. 2e).

Given the low temperatures and intense ultraviolet radiation characteristic of the QTP, we investigated related adaptative genes in viral hosts. Cold-adaptation-related genes were widespread across hosts, particularly within Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, and Bacteroidota (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Their densities were similar between water and sediment, with the highest values in plateau rivers (0.0321 and 0.0313 per kbp), exceeding those in glaciers (0.0295 and 0.0299 per kbp) and plain rivers (0.0307 and 0.0303 per kbp) (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Viruses themselves encoded the highest density of cold-adaptation genes in glaciers (>0.33 COGs per kbp), exceeding those observed in downstream (Supplementary Fig. 8c). ROS resistance genes in hosts (e.g., SOD FeMn64, catalase–peroxidase65, glutathione peroxidase66) showed a consistent pattern, peaking in plateau river water and sediment, compared with glaciers and plain rivers (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In contrast, light utilization genes (e.g., photosynthetic reaction centers67, proteorhodopsin68,69, chlorophyll-binding proteins) displayed an increasing trend from glaciers to plateau and plain rivers, although not all differences were statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

Genomic features and potential functions of viruses

The genomic features of viruses differed among three regions. The vOTUs assembled from glacial samples showed higher Guanine–Cytosine (GC) content, larger genome size and lower average molecular weight (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The average genome size of glacial viruses was comparable to that observed in other extreme aquatic environments (thermal springs, deep subsurface, and industrial wastewater) (Supplementary Fig. 10b). In addition, protein encoded by glacial vOTUs possessed lower carbon atoms per residue side chain (C-ARSC) but higher nitrogen and sulfur atoms per residue side chain (N-ARSC and S-ARSC).

Potential protein-coding genes encoded by viruses were annotated by searching against the eggNOG database70. 756,340 viral open reading frames (ORFs) were classified as known orthologous groups, of which 491,565 were further classified into specific COG categories including 207,907 ORFs belonging to the functionally unknown category (COG category S) (Fig. 3a). Numerous functional genes associated with viral reproduction and protein modification were identified across multiple COG categories. Category L (78,191 genes), M (36,184 genes), K (25,163 genes), and O (17,747 genes) were the four categories with most members, together accounting for 55.4% of all functionally annotated genes (Fig. 3a). Each category was characterized by its most represented functions, for example, COG category L included genes related to DNA methyltransferases, ATP-dependent helicase, and replicative DNA helicases. COG category M comprised genes including L-malate glycosyltransferase which can initiate bacillithiol biosynthesis and enable redox homeostasis and antibiotic resistance in Gram-positive bacteria71. The chaperonin GroES in category O acts as a co-chaperonin that caps GroEL, forming an isolated folding chamber that ensures efficient and accurate protein folding72. Several identified genes were involved in carbohydrate and lipid synthesis and degradation. For instance, endoglucanase catalyzes the hydrolysis of β−1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose, generating new chain ends, which are further processed by exoglucanase73 and exoZ participates in the biosynthesis of exopolysaccharide74. The fabG gene was also detected, which catalyzes the first of the two reductive steps in the fatty acid synthesis cycle and plays a key role in lipid biosynthesis75. To further investigate the functional traits of viral communities across three regions, we identified viral auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) to assess the functional potential of viral communities. In total, 22,965 potential AMGs were predicted by DRAMv78 and then annotated by kofamscan79 (Supplementary Data 10). Among viruses enriched in different regions, genes associated with DNA replication, prokaryotic defense systems, and transcriptional regulation exhibited higher occurrence ratios than other functional categories, particularly in glacier-enriched temperate viruses, where their occurrence ratios exceeded 10% (Supplementary Fig. 11). Replication-related genes included DnaC80, which functions as an ATP-dependent helicase loader facilitating the recruitment of DnaB to replication origins, and Pif181, a DNA helicase that resolves replication-blocking DNA and RNA structures, thereby promoting replication fork progression and genome stability. Prokaryotic defense–related genes such as McrA and McrB82, which specifically recognize and restrict methylated foreign DNA, were also frequently detected. Viruses carrying bacterial motility–related genes accounted for 1.07% of viral populations in glaciers, exceeding those in plateau (0.35%) and plain rivers (0.83%) (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Within glaciers, temperate viruses exhibited a nearly threefold higher carriage rate of bacterial motility genes (1.10%) compared with virulent viruses (0.38%) (Supplementary Fig. 12c). These genes were dominated by over 200 flagellar structure genes, including flgA, flgE, flgF, flgG, flgJ and flgL, which encode core components of flagellar assembly83 and have been reported to be involved in stress response84. Similarly, antimicrobial resistance genes were detected in 1.15% of glacial viral populations, compared with 0.35% in plateau rivers and 0.75% in plain rivers, with 145 catB and 119 vat genes representing the predominant ARG types, both associated with acetylation-mediated antibiotic inactivation85. DNA repair and recombination genes were most prevalent in plateau rivers (9.6%), exceeding those in glaciers (2.4%) and lowland rivers (7.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 12a). These genes were dominated by 4,663 ribonucleotide reductase genes, particularly nrdA and nrdB, which catalyze the rate-limiting step of DNA synthesis86, contribute to DNA damage regulation, and are strongly induced under UV exposure87. Genes involved in biosynthesis and sugar metabolism accounted for 4.5–7.9% in plain rivers more than those in plateau rivers (3.5–7.0%), and glaciers (0.76–0.92%), especially within virulent viruses (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Among these genes, 1611 GalE homologs were identified, which participate in the Leloir pathway by converting galactose into glucose-1-phosphate and supplying precursors for polysaccharide biosynthesis88. lpxA and lpxD, which can catalyze the initial steps of lipid A and lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis from UDP-GlcNAc89, were also detected.

We also identified and compared viral AMGs involved in methane, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus metabolism across glacier-to-river gradient (Supplementary Data 4). In the nitrogen cycle, although the overall number of nitrogen-cycling genes was limited (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 13), viruses encoding nrtB, nrtC, and nrtD (nitrate/nitrite transport) showed markedly higher relative abundance in glaciers (111.4), being 3.1-fold and 17.3-fold higher than in plateau and plain rivers, respectively (Fig. 3c). Viruses carrying denitrification genes (nirS, nirK) and anaerobic ammonium oxidation genes (HZS subunit) were relatively more abundant in plain rivers (2.63) than in plateau rivers (0.115) and glaciers (0.0025). Notably, nrfA peaked in glaciers (9.11) but remained at very low levels (<10⁻³) in both plateau and plain rivers. The nitrification-related amoC was also detected (Fig. 3b, d).

Numerous AMGs related to phosphorus acquisition and utilization were identified90 (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 13). Key genes such as phoH, DUT, dcd, and ribonucleotide reductase were ubiquitous across regions. The phoH gene, induced under phosphate stress91, was consistently detected at high relative abundance (Fig. 3c), indicating pervasive phosphorus limitation. The genes ppaX, ppa, and phoD, which contribute to phosphate generation and mobilization90, were observed. In addition, genes involved in phosphate (pstS, pstC, pstB) and phosphonate (phnC) transport were also identified.

Sulfur cycling AMGs included genes for sulfate transport, assimilatory/dissimilatory reduction, oxidation, and cysteine biosynthesis (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 13). Viruses with sulfate transport gene cysA showed the highest relative abundance in glaciers, being 3.7-fold and 17.2-fold higher than in plateau and plain rivers, respectively (Fig. 3c). Sulfur oxidation genes soxX and soxD which can mediate thiosulfate oxidation under low-oxygen conditions, exhibited highest relative abundances in plateau rivers, being 126.9-fold and 13.2-fold higher than those observed in glaciers and plain rivers (Fig. 3c). In contrast, viruses in glaciers encoding tsdA involved in thiosulfate-to-tetrathionate conversion and previously reported in deep-sea systems92, exceeded those in plateau and plain rivers by approximately four orders of magnitude. Meanwhile, viruses carrying assimilatory sulfate reduction genes (cysC, PAPSS, cysNC, and cysH93) were most abundant in plain rivers (590.66), compared with plateau rivers (504.67) and glaciers (195.68) (Fig. 3c, d).

For methane metabolism, viral AMGs contributed to both oxidation (pmoC, mmoC) and methanogenesis (mtaC94, mttC, mtbC, mtmC). Notably, viruses with the ortholog K00400, which encodes a subunit of methyl coenzyme M reductase (mcr) and is essential for methanogenesis and anaerobic methane oxidation, was more abundant in glaciers (Fig. 3c) and surprisingly predicted to infect bacterial hosts.

Host with potential pathogenicity gradually declined across glacier-to-river gradient

Pathogenic microorganism enclosed in cryosphere, posing significant threats and impacts to public health, are receiving increasing attention due to climatic change42,43,95. We investigated the interactions between bacteria with antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and virulence factor genes (VFGs), and viruses (Supplementary Data 7). 44.4%, 34.1%, 29.5% of all MAGs from glacier, plateau rivers and plain rivers, respectively, had virulence factors genes and were recognized as potential pathogens (carrying/not carrying ARGs, Fig. 4a). Notably, in glaciers, the proportion of potential pathogens carrying ARGs reached 9.5%. The VHR for potential pathogens and bacteria without VFGs all exhibited a continuous decrease along glacier-to-rivers gradients (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 3f). ARG-carrying pathogens exhibited the highest VHR across the glacier-to-river gradient (Fig. 4a), exceeding 100 in glaciers and consistently higher than that of other pathogens and non-pathogens. Viruses carrying anti-defense systems strongly infected potential pathogens, peaking in glaciers (Fig. 4c). Among these, viruses with anti-RM systems are particularly notable, as their hosts encompass not only pathogen with VFGs and ARGs but also a substantial number of non-pathogens (Fig. 4c). Moreover, viruses carrying VFGs and ARGs exhibit higher relative abundance in glaciers, with a predominance of lysogenic over lytic lifestyle (Fig. 4d). Discussion

Elevational gradients in physicochemical conditions and surface energy fluxes

The observed elevational gradient is associated with clear differences in physicochemical conditions and surface energy fluxes between QTP and plain (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 9). Plateau rivers exhibit lower temperatures and reduced nutrient concentrations, consistent with previous studies98,99. Differences in dissolved oxygen likely reflect the combined influence of temperature and reduced atmospheric pressure in QTP100. In contrast, surface energy fluxes, including solar radiation as well as latent and sensible heat fluxes, are elevated in plateau rivers, reflecting strong radiative forcing, large diurnal temperature variability, and persistent wind101–103. Together, these patterns characterize a distinct physicochemical and energetic flow for plateau, providing essential background context for interpreting biological patterns along the glacier-to-river gradient.

Diverse viral communities in rivers originating from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau

As the smallest biological entities, viruses are ubiquitous across nearly all natural ecosystems104–106. However, viral communities along glacier-to-river gradients remain poorly understood. The GtRVC comprises over 90% novel vOTUs and vPCs compared with the IMG/VR4 database56, spanning viral lineages across multiple domains and exhibiting significant differences among regions along the glacier-to-river gradient (Fig. 1c, ANOSIM R = 0.8776, P = 0.001). Viral diversity peaked in downstream plain rivers, where more favorable environmental conditions and anthropogenic impacts, such as input of exogenous microorganisms107 and pollutants108, reshape microbial and viral assemblages. However, the majority of viruses were first detected with appreciable abundance in plateau rivers (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings highlight that the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (QTP) is not only a “microbial tower”, as described in previous studies6, but also represents a “viral tower”.

It is important to note that water and particle samples in the GtRVC are primarily derived from metagenomes of size-fractionated microbial communities (>0.22 µm). As such, the recovered viral genomes predominantly represent viruses that are closely associated with microbial hosts, including intracellular and actively replicating viruses, rather than the full assemblage of free viral particles. This host-linked viral fraction is particularly informative for understanding virus–host coupling along environmental gradients109.

Shifts in viral lifestyle from temperate to virulent along the environmental gradient

We observed a progressive shift from temperate to virulent lifestyles along the elevational gradient. In glacial and plateau river environments, temperate viruses likely integrate into host genomes, enhancing both their own persistence and host adaptability through auxiliary viral genes38. It should be noted that our analyses for water samples primarily reflect host-associated (cell fraction) viruses, as free viral particles smaller than 0.22 μm released from lytic events were not comprehensively captured (see Methods). In addition, viral lifestyle inference is influenced by genome quality and tool-specific assumptions. The lifestyle analyses in this study only include high-quality and complete sequences. Future work may be needed to validate potential biases arising from restricting analyses to complete genomes to satisfy some tools’ requirements, given the substantial contribution of incomplete sequences to the whole community in metagenome analyses.

This lysogenic strategy provides a stable refuge under harsh conditions. In contrast, downstream rivers may favor lytic infections, where viruses exploit abundant hosts to maximize replication and dissemination, potentially reducing the relative abundance of bacteria with VFGs and ARGs through lysis. VHR correlated negatively with host relative abundance within the glacier-to-river gradient (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Previous studies indicate that virus–host interactions follow a continuum of ecological strategies110,111. Virus-to-microbe ratios often peak at intermediate host densities and decline as host abundance increases or decreases, reflecting continuum of strategies among Piggyback-the-Loser (PtL)111, Kill-the-Winner (KtW)112, and Piggyback-the-Winner (PtW)110 dynamics. Under cold, oligotrophic and high radiation conditions characteristic of glacial and plateau rivers, low host density and slow growth may favor lysogenic persistence when lytic replication is inefficient, consistent with PtL-like dynamics. In plain rivers with higher host densities, increased encounter rates and frequent coinfection could again promote lysogeny (PtW)113, and higher host diversity favors broader viral host ranges which may further reduce per-host infection efficiency114. It’s noticeable that host prediction results included only a subset of viral populations, therefore VHR-related results may not reflect patterns across entire viral communities. However, the hosts predicted to be infected account for nearly 70% of recovered MAGs, suggesting that the observed VHR trends still provide meaningful insight into virus–host abundance relationships along the glacier-to-river gradient. Crucially, current metagenomic approaches cannot discriminate between productive and unsuccessful infections, which may lead to an overestimation of VHR if a substantial fraction of detected viral genomes didn’t result in effective host infection or progeny production. Furthermore, absolute quantification using virus-like particle and microbial cell counts, flow cytometry, metatranscriptome109 (targeting successful infection-related markers110,115), and experimental validation is required to distinguish viral DNA presence from successful infection and replication116.

Functional adaptation strategies of viruses in QTP

Viruses play essential roles in ecological processes and ecosystem stability117 by shaping host population dynamics and cellular processes, while also depend on host to buffer against environmental stress. Following the framework proposed in recent perspective118, we interpret viral genes as part of a broader functional continuum rather than AMGs. Genes involved in DNA replication, nucleotide metabolism, ribosomal proteins and other essential viral processes are treated as core viral functions that primarily support viral propagation. In contrast, genes not directly required for viral core processes are discussed in terms of ‘auxiliary viral genes’ (AVGs)118, including AMGs, auxiliary physiology genes (APGs) and auxiliary regulatory genes (AReGs). Although viruses don’t directly participant in biogeochemical cycling, they influence the pools of cellular- and virus-derived materials via host lysis and modulate host metabolism through AVGs39.

A substantial fraction of viral genes identified in this study are involved in DNA replication, nucleotide metabolism, transcription, translation, and other genes related to essential viral processes (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 11). These genes are involved in core viral functions supporting viral fitness and reproduction, and are ubiquitously present among viruses across all regions. The higher ratio of DNA replication proteins, transcription factors and chromosome and associated genes in glacial viruses, especially among temperate lifestyle (Supplementary Fig. 12c), likely reflects increased selective pressure on viral replication efficiency and genome maintenance under cold, nutrient-poor, and physically stressful conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12a). The higher proportion of viruses carrying DNA repair and recombination proteins in plateau rivers may reflect adaptation to increased genomic damage under intense radiation conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12a).

Beyond these core functions, many viruses encode genes directly associated with host metabolic processes. EggNOG70 annotation of viral ORFs revealed 7117 and 13,872 genes related to “Energy production and conversion” and “Carbohydrate transport and metabolism” respectively, which may enhance metabolic capacity during infection (Fig. 3a). Some AVGs showed pronounced regional variation, with viruses from nutrient-limited glacial and plateau river environments carrying higher proportions of genes related to nutrient transporter (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 12a). Viral AMGs were linked to methane, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur cycling, exhibiting distinct spatial patterns along the glacier-to-river gradient which may reflect their potential influence on biogeochemical processes119,120 (Fig. 3). For example, sulfur metabolism–related genes were at higher ratio in riverine viruses, whereas glacial viruses were with higher ratio of energy-associated AMGs within pathways such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the citrate cycle, and methane metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 13). Several genes showed higher relative abundance in glacial viruses, such as nrfA, which can confer an advantage under nitrate-limited conditions121, nrtC/D which are involved in nitrate/nitrite transport enhancing host nitrogen acquisition under nutrient-limited conditions122, and the phosphorus transport genes phnC and pstB (Fig. 3b, c), suggesting viral involvement in host nutrient uptake in oligotrophic environments. In contrast, phosphorus cycle genes were widespread across all regions, indicating universal phosphorus limitation among microorganism (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 13). In plain rivers, lytic viruses were instead at higher ratio of genes related to energy metabolism and substrate degradation, potentially facilitating rapid viral replication within hosts (Supplementary Fig. 12).

In addition to metabolic genes, viruses also carried APGs associated with host physiological traits and stress responses. These APGs include genes involved in bacterial motility proteins, flagellar assembly, and antimicrobial resistance. Such genes were particularly occurring in glacial viruses and were predominantly associated with temperate lifestyles (Supplementary Fig. 12). In addition, viruses from glaciers and plateau rivers showed signatures of cold adaptation, including proteins with lower molecular weight and a higher density of cold-adaptation-associated genes (Supplementary Fig. 8c), encoding GroEL/GroES chaperonins, Csp family protein, and few antifreeze proteins. These patterns are consistent with Southern Ocean viromes123 and suggesting shared viral responses to low-temperature stress. Rather than directly altering host metabolism, these APGs may enhance host survival and competitiveness under harsh conditions by improving motility, defense against microbial antagonists, and tolerance to environmental stress, thereby indirectly supporting viral persistence during lysogeny.

Variation in viral microdiversity also reflects functional adaptation. Glaciers exhibited the lowest nucleotide diversity, likely due to environmental constraints and limited host diversity, while plateau and plain rivers supported higher diversity under more heterogeneous conditions. Specialist viruses from glaciers showed higher SNP frequencies and pN/pS ratios than generalists, indicating strong positive selection and rapid adaptation to narrow niches in the QTP (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 14). In contrast, generalist viruses in plain rivers were under purifying selection, maintaining stability across diverse hosts. This suggests that specialist viruses drive innovation under environmental stress, while generalists promote functional persistence, collectively influencing microbial adaptation and ecosystem processes along the glacier-to-river gradient.

Enhanced viral infection of host with potential pathogenicity in glacial environments

Hosts showed higher VHR in glaciers (Fig. 2c) and host-virus defense/anti-defense interactions were more intense in glaciers than in downstream rivers (Fig. 2e), reflecting dynamic co-evolutionary arms races. Given growing concerns about potential pathogens and viruses released from melting glaciers, we investigated the interactions between viruses and potential pathogen. Prokaryotes carrying VFGs and/or ARGs were widespread across all regions but experienced stronger viral infection in glaciers. Potential pathogens carrying ARGs were particularly heavily targeted in glacier and plateau river sections (Fig. 4a). These patterns are consistent with intense defense and anti-defense interplay, whereby viruses encoding anti-defense systems infected a greater number of pathogenic hosts in glaciers (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, the relative abundance of bacteria and viruses carrying ARGs and VFGs both decreased with declining elevation (Fig. 4a, d). Although temperate viruses may confer ecological advantages to their hosts, including auxiliary gene carriage and protection against other phages infections via superinfection exclusion45,124, lysogeny also imposes metabolic and regulatory costs associated with prophage maintenance. In plain rivers dominated by virulent viruses, these costs, together with possibility to prophage induction under environmental perturbations125,126, may reduce the competitive fitness of lysogenized hosts. This dual effect likely contributes to the reduced relative abundance of potential pathogens downstream.

Our Glacier-to-River Virome Catalogue (GtRVC) provides insights into how viral diversity, genomic features, and ecological functions shift across the extreme environmental gradients from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. We reveal a systematic transition in viral lifestyle strategy, i.e., temperate phages prevail in glacial environments, leveraging lysogeny to foster host adaptation and horizontal gene transfer, while lytic viruses dominate downstream, where they might exert strong predatory control and significantly reshape microbial communities. Furthermore, virus-encoded auxiliary metabolic genes might play roles in biogeochemical cycling from nutrient scavenging in oligotrophic high-altitude zones to denitrification and anaerobic ammonium oxidation in nutrient-rich downstream regions. Importantly, viral activity influences dynamics of potential pathogens and serves as a natural biocontrol mechanism in rivers. These findings establish viruses as central ecological players that both respond to and shape ecosystems sensitive to environmental change, highlighting their crucial role in maintaining stability and function in climate-threatened, rapidly warming headwater regions.

Methods

Data sampling and filtration

The four rivers involved in this study originate from the eastern and southern parts of the Tibetan Plateau, namely the Yellow River, Yangtze River, Lancang River, and Yarlung Tsangpo River. Additionally, this study integrates glacier data5 from the Tibetan Plateau to construct a glacier-to-river ecosystem encompassing glaciers, plateau rivers, and plain rivers in order to explore differences in viral communities across regions. A total of 682 samples were collected and compiled in this study which were sampled from 2014 to 2018. Among these samples, there were 85 glacier samples (retrieved from the TG2G database5) and 597 samples from four rivers (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data 1). Raw sequencing reads from the glacial samples were downloaded and processed using the same pipeline as the river samples, starting from read quality control, followed by assembly, binning, gene annotation and all subsequent downstream analyses.

The latitude, longitude, and elevation of each sampling site were recorded using a handheld GPS device. All samples were subsequently preserved at −80 °C until downstream DNA extraction. All field sampling was conducted responsibly and in compliance with local, national, and international regulations. Sampling procedures followed the Chinese national standards GB/T 14581-1993, HJ 493-2009, HJ 494-2009, and HJ/T 91-2002, ensuring proper environmental protection and minimal habitat disturbance. At each sampling site, approximately 10 L of river water was collected from ~0.5 m below the water surface and transferred into two sterile polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles for downstream processing. All samples were transported in refrigerated conditions (~0-4 °C) to local laboratories for further processing. For the Yellow River, Lancang River, and Yarlung Tsangpo River, the water samples were first filtered through 3.0 μm polycarbonate membranes (Millipore, USA) to capture particle-associated microbes. Then, the filtrate obtained from the 3.0 μm membrane filtration was filtered again through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes (Millipore, USA) to capture free-living microbes and cellular fraction viruses. Both the 3.0 μm and 0.22 μm size fractions were subjected to metagenomic sequencing, and the resulting samples were designated as “particle” and “water” types, respectively. As for the Yangtze River, the total planktonic community was directly collected by filtering through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes (Millipore, USA). All pre-treated filters were stored in 50 mL sterilized centrifuge tubes. In the TG2G5, ice and snow samples were also melted and filtered through 0.22 μm polycarbonate membranes prior to DNA extraction. Surface sediment samples for each rivers were sampled at sites with a water depth of approximately 0.5 m, transferred into sterile 50-mL polypropylene tubes, and then kept on dry ice during transport to the laboratory, just like procedures described in our previous studies127,128.

DNA extraction and sequencing

For water and particle samples, two filter membranes were transferred into sterile centrifuge tubes, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and mechanically disrupted prior to DNA extraction. For sediment samples, frozen material was retrieved from −80 °C storage and allowed to thaw. After brief centrifugation to remove water, approximately 0.5–1.0 g of sediment was subsampled into sterile centrifuge tubes for subsequent DNA extraction.

DNA extraction was performed using the FastDNA® SPIN Kit for soil (MP Biomedicals, USA), following the instructions provided with the kit for extracting DNA. Each environmental sample underwent 3–5 rounds of DNA extraction, and the resulting DNA extracts were pooled together as the final microbial DNA for sequencing. A 5 μL aliquot of DNA sample was used for assessing the integrity of DNA through 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). DNA (DNA amount >1 μg and concentration >10 ng μL−1) was used for shotgun metagenomic libraries construction, and then was sequenced on Illumina’s HiSeq platform (Majorbio Company, Shanghai, China) using a paired-end strategy (2 × 150 bp with an insert size of 300 bp).

Metagenomic reads processing, assembly, binning and taxonomic classification

All sequencing paired-end raw reads were quality-controlled using TrimGalore v0.6.4 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore, --stringency 5; -e 0.1; --length 35; --paired; --max_n 0). MEGAHIT v1.2.9129 were employed for de novo assembly with a minimum contig length threshold of 500 bp. Assembled contigs from 682 metagenomes were binned using the binning module (--metabat2 –maxbin2 –concoct –run-checkm) within the metaWRAP v1.2.2130. The generated MAGs were merged and refined using bin_refinement module of metaWRAP which the MAGs with completeness >70% and contamination <10% were selected as putative prokaryotic MAGs. All MAGs were dereplicated using dRep v3.4.5131 (-sa 0.95 -nc 0.30), resulting in 4903 MAGs for analysis. The taxonomy of each MAGs was classified using GTDB-tk v2.3.2132 based on Genome Taxonomy Database Release r214133. The phylogenetic tree of prokaryotic MAGs were also inferred from concatenations of 120 bacterial and 53 archaeal marker genes respectively using GTDB-tk and trees was visualized and displayed using iTOL134.

Virus identification, taxonomic classification and lifestyle prediction

All assembled contigs were subjected to virus identification using six methods (VIBRANT v1.2.1135, ViralVerify v1.1136, PPR-Meta v1.1137, Virsorter2 v2.2.4138, DeepVirFinder v1.0139, Earth Virome Pipeline106,140). VIBRANT, ViralVerify, Virsorter2 and PPR-Meta were used with default parameters (Supplementary Data 11). The putative viral sequences predicted by DeepVirFinder with scores >0.8 and p < 0.05 were retained. These tools rely on different principles, including protein annotation–based classification, machine-learning models, and reference-free deep-learning approaches, thereby compensating for individual methodological limitations. To minimize false positives introduced by this broad identification strategy, all predicted viral contigs were subjected to stringent downstream quality control. CheckV v1.0.1141 was used to estimate the completeness and quality of all putative viral sequences. The viruses were selected for subsequent analysis based on following standards: (1) Medium-quality sequences with length exceeding 10 kb; (2) High-quality and complete sequences. All validated viral contigs were dereplicated and clustered at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) using CD-HIT v4.8.155 (-c 0.95 -aS 0.85). The representative sequences of each cluster were denoted as vOTUs. Taxonomic classification of vOTUs was performed on geNomad v1.5.258 with default parameters. The lifestyles of vOTUs were predicted by BACPHLIP57 with default parameters. We also generated viral clusters by gene-sharing networks using vConTACT2 v0.11.3142. The gene-sharing network contained vOTUs from the GtRVC and prokaryotic viruses from the NCBI Viral RefSeq (v201).

Viral host assignment

The virus-host assignment was conducted using the obtained vOTUs and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from the previous steps. Three in-silico methods were employed to predict virus-host relationships based on different genomic features. (1) CRISPR spacer match: CRISPR refers to an immune mechanism within prokaryotes that recognizes and records genomic fragments of invading viruses. CRISPRs were identified from MAGs using minced v0.4.2143 and were then BLASTn144 matched against vOTUs with ≤1 mismatch and 100% coverage. (2) Transfer RNA (tRNA) match: As viruses undergo metabolism inside the host, some viruses may exhibit similar codon usage patterns to adapt to the cellular environment of their hosts under evolutionary pressure. ARAGORN v1.2.41145 with the ‘-t’ option was used to identify tRNAs from MAGs and vOTUs. Then, BLASTn144 was performed with 100% sequence identity and 100% length coverage for sequence alignment to determine the virus-host relationships. (3) Nucleotide sequence homology: Viruses and hosts may share genomic fragments during long-term molecular coevolution. BLASTn144 was used for sequence comparison between vOTUs and MAGs, with parameter thresholds set at ≥ 90% minimum nucleotide identity and covering length of virus and putative host sequences ≥2 kb. The results from three methods were merged as putative virus-host pairs.

Calculation of genomic features and comparisons with IMG/VR database

The genomic features (GC content, molecular weight, carbon/nitrogen/sulfur atoms per residue side chain) were calculated using the custom scripts ‘get_gc_and_narsc.py’ (https://github.com/faylward/pangenomics/). Based on the metadata provided by the IMG/VR database56, the sequences annotated as high-confidence and high-quality from aquatic ecosystems were selected for comparison. BLASTn144 was used to align the sequences obtained in this study and IMG/VR v4 database56. To further identify overlapping vOTUs and protein with public database, all vOTUs were clustered at 95% identity over 85% coverage and protein sequences were clustered at 60% identity over 80% coverage using CD-HIT v4.8.155. These thresholds are widely used in large-scale viral comparative genomics, with the vOTU-level cutoff reflecting species-level delineation146 and the protein-level cutoff designed to identify homologous proteins across diverse viral lineages while preserving functional diversity104,147,148.

Relative abundance profiling

The relative abundances of vOTUs and MAGs were represented using RPKM (Reads per kilobase per million mapped reads) values. Bowtie2 v2.3.5.1149 was used to build indexes for vOTUs and MAGs and map clean reads to the sequences. The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM files and sorted using samtools v1.9150. Subsequently, the sorted BAM files were imported into CoverM v0.6.1151 in “contig” mode for vOTUs and “genome” mode for MAGs (--min-read-percent-identity 95, --min-read-aligned-percent 90), generating corresponding relative abundance tables for viruses and MAGs.

Functional annotation and identification of auxiliary metabolic genes

Gene ORFs were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3152. The proteins of vOTUs and MAGs were both annotated by eggnog-mapper v2.1.1270. We also used the MagicLamp (https://github.com/Arkadiy-Garber/MagicLamp) to identify genes associated with ROS and light utility, generating gene count files normalized by the total number of genes in each MAG.

VirSorter2138 (--prep-for-dramv) was used to generate annotation files for DRAM-v v1.4.678, where genes assigned with M/F labels and auxiliary scores ≤ 3 were considered putative AMGs (auxiliary metabolic genes). To avoid false positives, only AMGs located between two virus-associated or virus marker genes, as well as those adjacent to virus-associated or virus marker genes, were selected for downstream data analysis. The detailed functions of AMGs were predicted using kofamscan v1.3.079. We categorized the AMGs based on their corresponding pathway and manually inspected genes involved in methane, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus metabolism as annotated in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes153,154 (KEGG).

Antibiotic resistance gene, virulence gene and (anti)defense systems identification

All predicted ORFs of MAGs and vOTUs were search against SARG v3.2 database155 and Virulence Factor DataBase156 (VFDB) using BLASTp144 to identify VFGs and ARGs, respectively. Then the output of BLASTp144 was filtered with > 80% amino acid identity and > 80% query coverage for MAGs (>40% query coverage for virus). The defense systems against viruses within prokaryotic MAGs were detected using DefenseFinder v1.3.0157 and ‘–antidefensefinder-only’ options was employed for anti-defense systems within viral sequences.

Potential gene transfer between viruses and their prokaryotic host

We investigated potential gene transfer between virus and their host using reciprocal best BLAST hits158. In brief, ARGs and VFGs encoded by viruses were queried against genes from MAGs using BLASTp144 with an e-value < 1e-5. The best cellular homology of viral genes was queried against the database constructed from MAGs and viral genes. If the top BLASTp144 hit of best cellular homology were the same as viral genes queried in the first BLASTp144 search (excluding the cellular homology sequence itself), we would regard this pair as a potential virus-prokaryote gene transfer event.

Identification of dominant, enriched and generalist/specialist viruses

To better understand the distribution and dynamics of viruses along the glacier-to-river gradient, we identified distinct viral groups. First, we recognized the dominant species within each region. A species was considered dominant if it appeared in ≥80% of the sites within a region and ranked in the top 200 in average relative abundance among all species. Pairwise comparisons were conducted to compare differences between regions. Briefly, for each species, Mann–Whitney U test was performed on relative abundance between two regions, and the p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. Species with an adjusted p < 0.05 were selected. Additionally, Cohen’s d value was calculated based on species relative abundance. A species was deemed significantly enriched in a region only if it showed significant differences compared to all other regions with an effect size > 0.

Considering the ecological roles of viruses, we defined generalist viruses as those distributed across a broad range of habitats or regions, indicating a wide ecological niche, whereas specialist viruses were defined as those restricted to a narrower range of habitats or regions, indicating a narrow ecological niche. We used the spec.gen function from the EcolUtils (https://github.com/GuillemSalazar/EcolUtils) package to assess viral specialist and generalist across different regions. Specifically, we applied three niche calculation methods provided in this function: Levins, Shannon, and occurrence, and conducted 1000 random permutations to establish the null distribution of niche width indices for each virus. Based on these simulations, viruses with niche width indices exceeding the upper 95% confidence interval were classified as generalists, those below the lower 95% confidence interval were classified as specialists, and the remaining viruses were classified as opportunists. A virus was classified as a generalist or specialist only if it was identified as such by at least two of the three methods.

Assessing evolutionary dynamics and microdiversity of specialist/generalist viruses and their hosts

To confirm the evolutionary characteristics of viral hosts, we employed the Binary-State Speciation and Extinction (BiSSE) model159. We defined the hosts of generalist viruses and those of specialist viruses as two distinct evolutionary states and calculated evolutionary rate parameters, including speciation, extinction, and state-transition rates. The BiSSE model selects the parameters most likely to have produced the observed data. Briefly, we first selected hosts of generalist and specialist viruses and extracted the corresponding subtree from the phylogenetic tree. We then used the ape160 R package to reconstruct an ultrametric tree. The BiSSE model was implemented using the diversitree161 R package, as in previous studies162. We used the starting.point.bisse function to obtain initial speciation and extinction rates consistent with the two states and then employed the maximum likelihood method for parameter estimation. A 1000-step Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation, conducted using the mcmc function, ensured the stability of the final estimates. The speciation and extinction rates of generalists and specialists, as well as the transition rates between them, were obtained after running the model.

We also utilized inStrain v.1.9.0163 (-l 0.95 -f 0.05 -fdr 1e-06) to assess the microdiversity. This software compares mapped reads to vOTUs, allowing us to characterize virus populations through metrics such as nucleotide diversity, counts of single nucleotide substitutions (SNS), and single nucleotide variations (SNV).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2. Alpha and Beta diversity of viral communities were calculated using the vegan164 package. Beta-diversity of viral communities was visualized through non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray‒Curtis distances derived from the relative abundances of vOTUs using vegdist function. Differences in diversity indices were tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Pearson correlation was computed using the cor function.
