Genome-resolved surveillance and predictive ecological risk modeling of urban microbiomes
Authors: Suleiman Aminu, AbdulAziz Ascandari, Morad M Mokhtar, Achraf El Allali, Rachid Benhida, Rachid Daoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman-built environment microbiomes mediate pathogen persistence and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) circulation, yet their ecological organization and resilience remain poorly quantified. Hospitals, sewage systems, ambulances, and public transport form interconnected microbial networks where contamination potential and compositional stability define biosurveillance risk. Understanding these dynamics requires genome-resolved frameworks capable of linking community composition to ecological behavior.MethodsWe analyzed 767 publicly available Illumina metagenomes from four urban environments using the GRUMB workflow. Quality-filtered reads were assembled into 10,834 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and dereplicated into 1542 species-level representatives. Functional annotation with CARD and VFDB identified ARG- and VF-carrying species, producing a genome-resolved abundance matrix used for ecological and predictive modeling. Alpha and beta diversity, indicator taxa, and prevalence were assessed in R, while machine learning (Random Forest, scikit-learn) achieved a nested cross-validation balanced accuracy of 0.97 ± 0.01. Synthetic donor–recipient simulations (α = 0–1) implemented in Python modeled compositional blending, entropy-based uncertainty, and Minimal Detectable Contamination (MDC) thresholds.ResultsMicrobial communities exhibited strong environment-specific structure (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.12, p < 0.001). Hospital sewage contained the highest richness and compositional heterogeneity, whereas ambulances and hospital environments showed low-diversity, surface-filtered microbiomes. Machine learning identified consistent ecological predictors (Pseudomonas_E fragi, Sphingomonas sp000797515, Acinetobacter variabilis, Roseomonas mucosa) that delineated environmental identity. Synthetic blending revealed a directional source–sink hierarchy with hospital sewage acting as the primary donor (MDC = 0.2–0.3), while hospital environments displayed the greatest compositional resilience (MDC ≥ 0.8). Entropy-based uncertainty analysis identified tipping zones (α = 0.3–0.5), and dominance mapping highlighted hospital environments as stabilizing ecological nodes. WHO-priority pathogens (Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli) occupied central positions in the network, bridging environmental and clinical compartments.ConclusionsThis genome-resolved and simulation-driven framework reveals a directional microbial continuum across urban infrastructures governed by dominance, resilience, and clinical connectivity. Hospital sewage functions as a microbial donor, while hospital environments act as ecological stabilizers anchoring built-environment microbiomes. These findings advance biosurveillance from descriptive profiling to predictive ecological modeling, offering quantitative metrics for risk-informed infrastructure design. Significance statement

Built environments such as hospitals, ambulances, and public transport are dynamic microbial ecosystems that influence pathogen persistence and antimicrobial resistance transmission. By integrating genome-resolved metagenomics with simulation-driven machine learning, this study advances biosurveillance from descriptive profiling to predictive ecological modeling. We reconstructed over 10,000 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and developed a framework to quantify microbial resilience, contamination thresholds, and ecological risk across urban infrastructures. Our results reveal a directional source–sink continuum in which hospital sewage acts as a dominant microbial donor, while hospital environments serve as ecological stabilizers. This integrative approach provides actionable ecological indicators for infrastructure-level infection control and establishes a scalable blueprint for predictive, One Health–aligned biosurveillance.

Introduction

Microbial communities in human-built and urban environments are increasingly recognized as critical players in public health ecosystems. These microbiomes act not only as passive indicators of environmental conditions but also as active participants in the transmission, persistence, and evolution of pathogens, including those harboring AMR determinants [1–3]. Infrastructures such as ambulances, hospital environments, sewage systems, and public transport form dynamic microbial exchange networks, linking human activity with microbial ecology in ways that challenge traditional models of infection prevention and control [4–7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has underscored the urgency of AMR containment through its 2024 update to the global list of priority pathogens [8]. These organisms are increasingly detected in both clinical and environmental reservoirs, highlighting the need for integrated surveillance spanning healthcare facilities, waste systems, and public spaces [9, 10]. Recent policy frameworks, including the 2024 Global Leaders Group report on AMR, have called for biosurveillance strategies that extend beyond patient diagnostics to include environmental and infrastructure-level monitoring [10]. As Hart et al. (2023) note, environmental regulators now emphasize that effective AMR control depends on capturing microbial exposure in real-world, high-contact settings such as wastewater systems and public transport [11].

In response to these calls, large-scale initiatives such as the MetaSUB Consortium have mapped urban microbiomes across public transport systems and metropolitan environments [12, 13]. While these studies have illuminated the taxonomic diversity of non-clinical microbiomes, most remain descriptive, lacking genome-resolved reconstruction, functional annotation, and predictive modeling necessary for actionable biosurveillance [14–16]. Amplicon-based or taxonomic approaches, while informative, cannot reliably distinguish pathogenic from commensal strains or resolve virulence and resistance [17]. Although these methods offer compositional snapshots, they lack the resolution to differentiate pathogenic from commensal strains, detect virulence and resistance genes, or capture the ecological behavior of microbial lineages [18–21]. These limitations render them insufficient for risk-informed biosurveillance, particularly in infrastructure-rich, high-contact environments where strain-level tracking and functional annotation are essential.

Genome-resolved metagenomics provides a transformative solution by reconstructing MAGs to recover near-complete microbial genomes directly from metagenomic data [15, 16, 20, 22]. These assemblies enable simultaneous taxonomic identification and functional annotation, including virulence and AMR genes profiling. Yet, despite their analytical power, genome-resolved methods have rarely been applied to low-biomass, high-heterogeneity built environments, where strain diversity, uneven microbial load, and environmental filtering complicate assembly and comparability [23–25]. Consequently, the human-built environment, where human exposure and microbial exchange are most intense, remains one of the least understood frontiers in genome-resolved microbiology.

To address these analytical gaps, we developed the GRUMB, an integrated workflow that unifies genome-resolved metagenomics with machine learning–driven ecological modeling [26]. GRUMB enables systematic assembly, dereplication, and annotation of MAGs while incorporating statistical modules that quantify ecological resilience and contamination dynamics across environments. The framework models microbial communities as dynamic ecological systems rather than static compositional profiles, capable of identity transitions, contamination propagation, and directional dominance. Machine learning (ML) provides the predictive engine behind this framework, uncovering structure in complex, high-dimensional sequencing data [27, 28]. This enables simulation of microbial transfer, stability, and cross-environmental influence, which are key parameters for biosurveillance and ecological risk assessment [29–31].

Hence, biosurveillance frameworks aligned with AMR stewardship must move beyond simple species detection to incorporate functional profiling and predictive modeling. This approach focuses not only on identifying which pathogens are present, but also on assessing how stable their presence is, the ecological communities they inhabit, and the likelihood that they will spread or persist under real-world environmental pressures [32, 33]. Such predictive and function-oriented insights are essential for risk-informed surveillance, pathogen containment, and infrastructure-level infection control, particularly as environmental pathways of AMR transmission become increasingly recognized [34–36].

Using the GRUMB, we reconstructed more than 10,000 MAGs from 767 urban metagenomes spanning hospital environments, hospital sewage, ambulances, and public transport [12, 37–45]. We then applied machine learning to classify environmental origin, identify key microbial indicators, and simulate synthetic contamination scenarios that quantify ecological stability and risk. Together, these analyses transform microbial monitoring from a descriptive task into a predictive, mechanistic discipline, providing a transferable framework for urban biosurveillance and AMR risk assessment under the One Health paradigm. Methodological details of the GRUMB v2.0 workflow are described below.

Methods

Overview of the GRUMB workflow

All analyses were conducted using GRUMB v2.0 which integrates read preprocessing, assembly, genome binning, dereplication, functional annotation, ecological modeling, and simulation-driven machine learning within a reproducible, SLURM-compatible pipeline. Key upgrades over the original implementation include CheckM2 for genome quality estimation, dRep for non-redundant dereplication, GTDB-Tk for standardized taxonomy, CoverM for normalized abundance quantification, and newly incorporated modules for ecological simulation and risk scoring. A schematic of the workflow is shown in Fig. 1, and detailed command parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods 1–3.

Metagenomic data acquisition and preprocessing

Publicly available shotgun metagenomes were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (accessed 25 August 2025), representing four human-built environments (hospital environments, hospital sewage, public transport systems, and ambulances). Metadata, including geographic origin, sequencing platform, and project accession numbers, were curated and harmonized (Table S1). All datasets consisted of Illumina paired-end reads (75–150 bp) [46],

Quality control and preprocessing followed the GRUMB v2.0 workflow. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed using BBTools (v37.62), and non-microbial contaminants (including host-associated and vector-derived reads) were removed using FastQ Screen (v0.15.3) with a custom multi-genome reference database [47]. Only high-quality, decontaminated read pairs were retained for downstream assembly. Sequencing coverage and redundancy were assessed using Nonpareil (v3.5.5) [48] to ensure sufficient depth across environments (Supplementary Methods S1).

Assembly, genome binning, and quality control

High-quality reads were assembled using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with parameters optimized for complex metagenomes [49]. Contigs shorter than 1.5 kb were excluded, and assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST (v5.2.0) (Supplementary Methods 2). Genome binning was conducted with MetaBAT2 (v2.18) based on contig coverage profiles derived from BBMap [50], followed by quality assessment with CheckM2 (v1.0.1). Only bins with ≥ 80% completeness and ≤ 10% contamination were retained for downstream analyses [51]. Nonredundant species-level genomes were generated by dereplicating bins across environments using dRep (v3.6.2) at a 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) threshold [52]. Taxonomic classification of representative metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) was performed with GTDB-Tk (v2.4.1) against the GTDB Release R226 database (downloaded on 29 August 2025), yielding standardized genome-to-species assignments [53] (Supplementary Methods 2). This ensured that each species entry in the matrix represented at least one dereplicated genome with confirmed genomic and functional annotation (ARG/VF linkage), thereby maintaining genome-level resolution while reducing redundancy for statistical modeling.

Functional annotation of antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes

Functional annotation was performed on the dereplicated MAG catalog to identify genes associated with antimicrobial resistance (ARGs) and virulence factors (VFs) genes. ARGs were detected using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI: v6.0.4) with the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD 2025) [54], while VFs were identified through DIAMOND (v2.1.10) blastx searches against the VFDB core 2025 dataset [55, 56]. Stringent filtering thresholds were applied to ensure high-confidence assignments (Supplementary Methods 3).

To focus analyses on ecologically and clinically relevant taxa, only species carrying at least one ARG or VF were classified as pathogen-associated and retained for downstream abundance quantification and ecological modeling.

Abundance quantification and genome-to-species mapping

We quantified abundance at two levels.

(i)Species (genome) level where paired reads were mapped using CoverM (genome mode) to the dereplicated representative MAG catalog [57]. CoverM reported transcripts per million (TPM), reads per kilobase million (RPKM), relative abundance, and covered_fraction. MAG-level TPMs were then aggregated to GTDB-defined species to generate species-level abundance profiles used for ecological and predictive analyses.

For MAGs, TPM values were collapsed to the species level using GTDB-Tk taxonomic assignments, with genus-level suffixes (spp.) applied where species names were unresolved. Species-level abundance matrices were then merged with ARG and VF annotations. To generate integrated pathogen-associated species profiles, the matrices were filtered to retain only pathogen-associated species (ARG- or VF-positive, or both), ensuring that all ecological and machine-learning analyses were driven by functionally relevant taxa.

Although the v2 model further reduced residual variance, Project_ID was partially confounded with environment type (for instance, all ambulance samples originated from a single study), leading to inflated between-group separation (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.31–0.53). In contrast, the v1 model balanced technical noise reduction with preservation of true biological structure (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.10–0.12) and was therefore retained for all downstream ecological and predictive analyses (Table S2; Fig. S1e).

Alpha diversity was estimated from TPM-normalized species matrices using Shannon, Simpson, and observed richness indices implemented in the vegan R package [59]. Richness was calculated both unfiltered and at abundance thresholds of TPM ≥ 0.1, 1, and 10. Group-level differences were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc comparisons and Benjamini–Hochberg correction (dunn.test v1.3.6). Beta diversity was computed on CLR-transformed, batch-corrected matrices using Euclidean distances, followed by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination. Community-level differences were evaluated using PERMANOVA (adonis2, 999 permutations), ANOSIM, and PERMDISP tests implemented in vegan [60–62].

Species prevalence was defined as the proportion of samples in which TPM > 0, and taxa were classified as core (≥ 30%), secondary (10–30%), or peripheral (< 10%). This classification was used to construct environment-overlap networks in igraph (v2.1.4), where node size represented species richness and edge width denoted shared taxa between environments.

Indicator species were identified using the IndVal.g statistic (multipatt, 999 permutations) implemented in indicspecies v1.8.0 in R [63], excluding taxa present in fewer than two samples or in environments with fewer than three replicates. Clinically relevant and WHO-priority pathogens were annotated by exact species or genus-level matches to the WHO 2017 Priority Pathogen List, complemented by additional taxa previously described as nosocomial or opportunistic pathogens in built-environment and healthcare microbiome studies [44]. Their relative abundances were visualized using ComplexHeatmap in R.

Building on these ecological insights, we next applied supervised machine-learning models to test whether environment-specific microbial signatures could be predicted from the abundance profiles and to identify the key taxa driving environmental differentiation.

Machine learning framework

Machine-learning models were applied to classify environmental origin, identify key microbial indicators, and evaluate ecological predictability. Analyses were implemented in Python 3.11 using scikit-learn (v0.21.3). The input dataset comprised species-level abundance matrices that had been CLR-transformed and batch-corrected (v1 model), as described above. Samples (n = 767) were labeled by environment, and features corresponded to 1542 species. Data were split into 80% training and 20% hold-out sets using stratified sampling to preserve class balance.

Five supervised classifiers (Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Logistic Regression (LR), and Support Vector Machine (SVM)) were benchmarked.

Performance was first assessed by 10-fold stratified cross-validation using four metrics (accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro F1, and Cohen’s kappa), averaged as mean ± SD across folds.

Model rankings were further evaluated using a Friedman test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction (Table S3).

For the best-performing algorithms (DT and RF), we performed nested cross-validation (inner = 3, outer = 5 folds) with grid-search hyperparameter tuning. Parameter grids explored tree depth (5–20), estimator count (200–500), feature-subset size (√, 0.3, 0.5), and minimum leaf size (1–5).

The RF achieved the highest reproducibility and generalization (balanced accuracy = 0.97 ± 0.01 in nested CV; 0.965 on hold-out; macro F1 = 0.968; κ = 0.955), and was therefore selected for downstream ecological and simulation analyses (Fig. S6). Feature relevance was quantified using Gini impurity, DT split-based relevance, and permutation importance, repeated over 50 × 5 stratified folds (250 runs). Normalized importance ranks were averaged to compute a consensus feature-stability score, identifying species that consistently predicted environment type (Table S4). All modeling scripts, parameter grids, and intermediate outputs are available in the GRUMB v2.0 repository, and a complete step-by-step workflow, including statistical comparisons and stability analysis, is described in Supplementary Methods 5.

Model validation and performance reporting

To ensure unbiased generalization estimates, model development followed a nested cross-validation strategy (inner = 3, outer = 5 folds) applied only to the training subset (80%). Hyperparameter tuning and model selection were confined to these folds. After the optimal configuration was identified, the final model was retrained on the complete training data and evaluated on an independent hold-out set (20%) that was never used during training or cross-validation. All reported metrics reflect performance on this unseen test dataset.

Synthetic blending simulations

To explore how microbial communities respond to compositional perturbation and to quantify cross-environmental vulnerability, we developed a synthetic blending simulation pipeline implemented in Python 3.11. This simulation-based approach extends beyond conventional diversity analyses by explicitly modeling microbial identity resilience, contamination tipping behavior, and ecological vulnerability, providing a new predictive framework for environmental biosurveillance. Glossary of key analytical terms

Contamination simulation

An in silico experiment where microbial community profiles from two distinct environments are linearly blended at defined fractions (α = 0–1), simulating real-world cross-contamination events such as the transfer of sewage-associated microbes into clinical surfaces or public transport systems

Recipient environment

The original microbial community that is perturbed during contamination simulation; its resilience represents ecological resistance to external microbial influx

Donor environment

The source of microbial input contributing to contamination, representing an external contamination pressure

Synthetic centroid mixing

A deterministic form of blending based on the mean (centroid) community profile of each environment. This approach models idealized, noise-free transitions between environments, enabling comparison of real versus theoretical contamination dynamics

Minimal Detectable Contamination (MDC)

The smallest donor fraction (α) at which the classifier consistently predicts the mixed community as belonging to the donor environment with ≥ 80% probability. Lower MDC values indicate higher environmental sensitivity or fragility to external inputs

Override frequency (identity fragility)

The number of times an environment loses its predicted identity before reaching a 50:50 blend during simulation. High override frequency indicates unstable microbial signatures and low ecological resilience

Prediction confidence

The probability assigned by the machine learning model to its most likely predicted class, reflecting the strength of classification certainty

Prediction entropy

A measure of classification uncertainty derived from Shannon entropy (H = − ∑ pi log₂ pi), where pi represents the predicted probability for class i and k is the number of possible environments. Higher entropy indicates lower model confidence and greater ecological ambiguity

Entropy trajectory

The curve describing how prediction uncertainty (entropy) changes as the donor fraction (α) increases, often revealing ecological tipping points where communities lose distinct identity

Ecological richness

The mean number of distinct microbial species per sample, serving as a proxy for biodiversity and ecological capacity

Community variance

The intra-environmental variability in species composition, representing ecological heterogeneity or instability across samples of the same habitat

Contamination risk score

A composite index integrating override frequency, prediction entropy, richness, and variance to quantify the vulnerability of each environment to microbial intrusion and compositional destabilization

In this donor–recipient blending framework, microbial community profiles were linearly mixed across contamination fractions (α = 0–1). Synthetic mixtures were generated from both individual samples and environment centroids, and predictions were performed using the tuned Random Forest (RF) classifier. At each α step, the probability of donor assignment was recorded, and the Minimal Detectable Contamination (MDC) was defined as the smallest α at which the donor was predicted with ≥ 80% probability. Pipeline benchmarking and runtime profiling

All GRUMB v2 analyses were profiled for runtime, memory, and Input/Output (I/O) usage across 767 metagenomic samples. Stepwise runtime metrics were recorded for over 20 major modules, including adapter trimming, assembly, binning, annotation, and mapping, representing more than 4,800 individual workflow executions. Average runtime per step ranged from 8.6 ± 22.3 min for low-complexity environments (ambulance, hospital environments, and public transport) to 22.4 ± 45.5 min for high-complexity hospital sewage assemblies. Global dereplication and annotation averaged 23.1 ± 56.5 min per stage. All computations were executed on 56-thread nodes with 96 GB RAM, and I/O sizes ranged from 224 to 512 MB per sample. These benchmarks demonstrate GRUMB v2’s scalability and reproducibility for high-throughput, multi-environment metagenomic biosurveillance and provide quantitative guidance for computational resource planning (Table S5).

Results

Genome reconstruction and dereplicated MAG catalog

Prior to genome assembly, sequencing depth and coverage sufficiency were evaluated using Nonpareil modeling [48] to confirm adequate capture of community diversity across 767 metagenomes spanning hospital environments, hospital sewage, ambulances, and public transport (Fig. 2a i–iv). Most samples achieved > 95% estimated coverage at sequencing depths of approximately 1 Gbp, indicating near-saturating data suitable for genome-resolved reconstruction (Table S2). Hospital sewage showed the highest redundancy and rapid saturation, consistent with high microbial load, whereas ambulance and hospital-environment samples exhibited shallower curves reflecting low-biomass, high-stochasticity communities, a pattern typical of disturbed built-environment microbiomes [68].

Ambulance and hospital-environment samples reached near-saturation at lower sequencing depth, whereas hospital sewage exhibited broader, higher-complexity curves. b Distribution of MAG recovery, completeness, and contamination across environments, with most assemblies exceeding 90% completeness and < 5% contamination. c t-SNE visualization before and after batch correction, showing removal of technical clustering and recovery of environment-specific patterns. d NMDS ordination based on CLR-transformed TPM matrices, showing clear community segregation by environment (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.12). e Alpha diversity indices (richness, Shannon, Simpson) illustrating highest diversity in hospital sewage and lowest in ambulances

High-quality reads were assembled and binned into 10,834 MAGs using the GRUMB workflow and dereplicated at 95% ANI using dRep, yielding 1542 (915 from hospital sewage, 458 from ambulances, 85 from hospital environments, and 84 from public transport samples) non-redundant, species-level representative genomes (Fig. 2b i).

Across environments (Fig. 2b ii–iii), MAG completeness peaked near 95%, while contamination remained below 5%, fulfilling MIMAG medium- to high-quality thresholds [69]. GC content (45–65%) and coding densities (> 0.85) confirmed the structural integrity of assemblies. Hospital sewage produced the most contiguous and complete genomes [70], while ambulance and hospital-environment datasets, representing low-biomass, surface-associated environments, were more fragmented, consistent with their ecological constraints (Table S2).

Batch correction and data harmonization across studies

To minimize technical bias across sequencing projects, batch effects were evaluated using PERMANOVA and corrected with limma::removeBatchEffect (see Table S2). The v1 model (adjusting for sequencing instrument and center) substantially reduced technical variance while preserving genuine ecological structure, whereas the more aggressive v2 model that included Project_ID overcorrected due to confounding between project and environment. As shown in t-SNE ordination (Fig. 2c), batch correction with the v1 model improved data harmonization and revealed clear, environment-specific clustering. This harmonized, batch-corrected dataset was used for all subsequent ecological and predictive analyses.

Ecological structure and diversity of built-environment microbiomes

Beta-diversity analyses revealed strong environment-driven segregation of microbial community composition. NMDS based on CLR-transformed species abundance matrices showed distinct clustering by environment (Fig. 2d). PERMANOVA confirmed that environment explained 12.3% of total community variance (R2 = 0.123; p < 0.001, Table S3), indicating clear ecological differentiation across built environments. Pairwise comparisons identified hospital sewage as the most compositionally distinct habitat, showing the greatest dissimilarity relative to all other environments (R2 = 0.13; p < 0.001). Dispersion analysis supported these results, with hospital sewage exhibiting significantly higher within-group variance (F = 533; p < 10−186), consistent with its diverse and heterogeneous microbial inputs.

Alpha-diversity patterns mirrored these compositional trends (Fig. 2e). Hospital sewage displayed the highest richness and Shannon diversity, while ambulances exhibited the lowest diversity across all metrics (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 10⁻44). These trends were consistent across richness thresholds (TPM ≥ 0.1, 1, and 10), confirming the robustness of the observed patterns. Simpson indices further highlighted dominance by a few abundant taxa in ambulance and hospital-environment samples, contrasting with the more even community structure of sewage and public-transport microbiomes. Post-hoc Dunn tests confirmed significant pairwise differences between most environments (p < 0.001), except between hospital environments and public transport, which showed partial overlap in diversity profiles (Table S3). Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that microbial community structure in urban built environments is strongly shaped by ecological context [68, 71], with hospital sewage representing a highly diverse and compositionally distinct reservoir, whereas ambulances and hospital environments harbor low-diversity, surface-filtered microbiomes characteristic of high-contact, low-biomass environments [72]. Having established broad ecological differentiation, we next explored the frequency and persistence of individual taxa to identify environment-specific microbial signatures.

Species prevalence and ecological concordance across environments

Species prevalence analysis revealed strong habitat asymmetry, with most taxa occurring sporadically and only a minor fraction forming environment-specific cores (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Fig. S2a–b). Across all environments, 92.9% of species were peripheral (present in < 10% of samples), 6.9% were secondary (10–30%), and 0.2% were core (≥ 30%) (Fig. S2a-b). This rarity bias highlights the transient nature of most environmental microbes in built systems [68, 71, 73].

Hospital sewage contained the largest number of core species (n = 155), followed by public transport (n = 36), hospital environments (n = 22), and ambulances (n = 3) (Fig. S2c, Table S3). Core sewage taxa included functionally diverse genera such as Acidovorax, Pseudomonas, Aeromonas, Acinetobacter, and Aliarcobacter, consistent with its role as a microbial convergence point between clinical and environmental flows [74, 75]. In contrast, ambulance and hospital-environment microbiomes were dominated by low-prevalence surface taxa (Telluria, Psychrobacter, Pseudomonas E spp.) (Fig. 3a), which may reflect consistent disinfection and recolonization cycles [71, 76]. Shared-species analyses revealed substantial but asymmetric overlap between environments (Fig. S2d–e). The UpSet plot (Fig. S2d) indicated that while each environment harbored distinct community subsets, hospital sewage shared the largest number of taxa with all other environments. The network representation further illustrated this connectivity, with hospital sewage and public transport exhibiting the strongest inter-environmental overlap (273 shared species; 68.7%), followed by sewage–hospital environment (266 shared; 67.7%) and hospital environment–ambulance (177 shared; 59.6%). Node scaling by species richness revealed that sewage and public transport formed the dominant hubs within the urban microbiome network, while ambulances remained peripheral, with minimal shared taxa and low overall diversity (Fig. S2e).

To test whether these ecological patterns were reflected in predictive modeling, we integrated prevalence data with indicator species analysis and RF classification outputs (Fig. 3c–e; Tables S3-S4). The RF models achieved near-perfect environment classification accuracy while maintaining biological interpretability (Fig. S3). Top predictive species closely matched indicator and core taxa from prevalence analyses, demonstrating that the models captured genuine ecological structure rather than technical artifacts.

Indicator species analysis (IndVal.g) supported these trends and identified clear habitat-specific associations (Fig. 3B; Table S3). Hospital sewage displayed the greatest taxonomic distinctiveness, with 83% of all significant indicators (p < 0.001) including Aliarcobacter cryaerophilus A, Azonexus spp., Pseudomonas E alcaligenes, and Thauera phenylacetica, characteristic of redox-variable, nutrient-rich, wastewater conditions [70, 77]. Hospital-environment microbiomes were enriched in desiccation-tolerant taxa such as Bradyrhizobium denitrificans, Pelomonas aquatica, Sphingomonas sp000797515, and Methylobacterium radiotolerans, consistent with persistent surface colonizers under dry and disinfected conditions [71, 76]. Public-transport samples were dominated by human-associated Actinobacteria including Acinetobacter pittii, Kocuria marina, and Tsukamurella tyrosinosolvens, reflecting current transfer through skin and contact surfaces. Ambulances contained fewer but distinct indicators (Telluria timonae, Pseudomonas B luteola, Stenotrophomonas spp.), consistent with transient microbial deposition and turnover driven by disinfection cycling [44].

Across environments, consistent microbial signatures emerged (Fig. 3d). In ambulances, Pseudomonas, Stutzerimonas, Psychrobacter faecalis, and Telluria spp. dominated both indicator and RF predictor lists (Fig. 3e), representing stress-tolerant colonizers adapted to mobile, disinfected surfaces [71, 76, 78]. In hospital sewage and environments, Acinetobacter, Klebsiella, and Enterobacter species formed an ecological continuum, with K. pneumoniae, E. asburiae, and A. nosocomialis appearing as both high-prevalence and high-dominant taxa [75]. The co-occurrence of redox-flexible genera (Desulfovibrio, Thauera, Azonexus) (Fig. 3e) reflected mixed aerobic–anaerobic biofilm conditions characteristic of hospital effluents [79]. Public transport microbiomes featured recurrent human-contact taxa (Roseomonas mucosa, Telluria varians A, Acinetobacter pittii, Streptococcus thermophilus) (Fig. 3e) that remained peripheral in abundance yet stable across model runs, emphasizing their diagnostic persistence [37, 41]

Together, these results demonstrate a coherent ecological and predictive hierarchy, with abundant, infrastructure-adapted taxa dominating sewage and hospital environments, whereas ambulances and public transport harbor rare but diagnostically consistent colonizers. The strong agreement between prevalence, indicator analysis, and machine learning reinforces the ecological validity and interpretability of the GRUMB framework, forming a robust foundation for downstream modeling of clinically relevant taxa and contamination dynamics.

Clinically relevant species and WHO-priority pathogens

To contextualize ecological patterns within a biosurveillance framework, we cross-referenced all reconstructed species against the WHO priority pathogen list and related clinical reports (Fig. 4a–c; Table S3). Clinically relevant taxa were detected across all environments but showed striking differences in abundance, persistence, and ecological influence. Hospital sewage emerged as the dominant reservoir, harboring the broadest and most abundant repertoire of WHO-priority species, whereas hospital environments exhibited lower but overlapping diversity, and ambulances and public transport contained primarily transient, low-prevalence detections.

Clinically relevant taxa and WHO-priority pathogen ecology across built environments. a Heatmap of relative abundance (log₁₀[TPM + 1]) of WHO-priority and clinically relevant species across environments. b Co-occurrence network (ρ ≥ 0.5) highlighting WHO-listed taxa (red nodes) and their ecological connectivity. c Network centrality metrics (degree, betweenness, eigenvector) showing E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and C. freundii as dominant hubs linking clinical and environmental networks

Critical and high-priority pathogens, including A. baumannii, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus, displayed distinct habitat stratification (Fig. 4a). Hospital sewage supported the greatest diversity of Enterobacteriaceae, with K. pneumoniae, E. coli, Enterobacter asburiae, and Citrobacter freundii reaching secondary-to-core prevalence levels (≥ 25%), consistent with findings from global sewage metagenomes [74]. These species frequently co-occurred with facultative anaerobes such as Thauera, Bacteroides, and Aeromonas, forming a metabolically versatile community indicative of redox-variable effluent niches. Hospital environments shared several of these taxa, albeit at lower abundance, reflecting periodic introduction through patient and staff movement followed by rapid attrition due to disinfection [76]. Ambulance and public-transport surfaces contained mainly peripheral occurrences of A. baumannii, S. aureus, and S. maltophilia, suggesting short-lived deposition and minimal long-term colonization [41].

Network-level analysis revealed that these pathogens occupy central structural roles within the urban microbiome (Fig. 4b). E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and C. freundii exhibited the highest degree and eigenvector centrality (0.43–0.69), forming a dense Enterobacteriaceae hub that linked hospital, sewage, and community compartments (Fig. 4b). A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa served as ecological bridges with elevated betweenness values, connecting Gram-negative and Gram-positive modules, while S. aureus maintained high clustering coefficients (> 0.9) consistent with surface-associated co-occurrence patterns. Radar analysis further identified E. coli as the most integrative and globally connected taxon, uniting clinical and environmental subnetworks (Fig. 4C, Fig. S2 f-g).

These results demonstrate that clinically important species are not isolated anomalies but integral components of the built-environment microbiome, structured along a clear source–sink gradient. Hospital sewage functions as the primary microbial donor, continuously replenishing clinically relevant taxa that disperse, often transiently, into hospital environments, ambulances, and public spaces. The central network positioning of WHO-priority pathogens underscores their ecological resilience and potential for re-emergence across interconnected infrastructures, providing a possible mechanistic link between environmental persistence, clinical relevance, and contamination-risk potential.

Synthetic contamination simulations and ecological dominance patterns

To quantify cross-environmental influence and compositional resilience, we simulated incremental microbial blending between built-environment compartments (α = 0–1) and reclassified the mixed profiles using the tuned RF classifier (Fig. 5a i–iv; Table S4). The resulting dose–response trajectories revealed asymmetric dominance patterns, defining a structured source–sink continuum across urban microbiomes.

Synthetic donor–recipient contamination simulations and Minimal Detectable Contamination (MDC) analysis. a Dose–response curves depicting donor prediction probability (P(donor | α)) across contamination gradients (α = 0–1). Ambulances showed rapid identity loss, while hospital environments and sewage remained resilient. b, c MDC heatmaps summarizing the smallest contamination fraction (α) where donor classification reached ≥ 80%. Light red = fragile communities; dark red = resilient. Average MDC values confirmed highest stability in hospital environments (0.72) and lowest in ambulances (0.43)

When ambulances served as recipients (Fig. 5a i), their microbiomes showed the lowest ecological resilience, shifting rapidly toward donor identity at minimal contamination levels (α = 0.27–0.35). Mixing with public-transport or hospital-environment donors produced early classification flips, while hospital-sewage donors required slightly higher α to override. These sharp transitions illustrate the high vulnerability of low-biomass, surface-associated microbiomes to external microbial influx. In contrast, hospital-environment communities (Fig. 5a ii) exhibited moderate compositional stability, maintaining their identity until α equals 0.66–0.67 when blended with hospital-sewage or public-transport donors, and requiring α at approximately 0.82 under ambulance input. This pattern identifies hospital environments as intermediate-resilience systems capable of absorbing external taxa while preserving their core compositional signature.

Hospital sewage displayed bidirectional dynamics (Fig. 5a iii). As a recipient, it was sensitive to hospital-environment donors, flipping at α equals 0.35 to 0.44, but highly resistant to public-transport and ambulance inputs (α = 0.75–0.90). Conversely, as a donor, sewage communities required moderate contamination fractions (α = 0.65–0.66) to override other environments, suggesting that despite their diversity and metabolic flexibility, they are context-dependent rather than universally dominant. Public-transport microbiomes (Fig. 5a iv) occupied an intermediate ecological position, with contamination thresholds typically ranging from 0.46 to 0.85 depending on donor type, reflecting their bidirectional connectivity between community and clinical spaces.

Quantitative patterns in the MDC matrices (Fig. 5b, c; Table S4) corroborated these asymmetries. Lower MDC values indicate stronger donor effects and weaker recipient resistance. Hospital-environment donors exerted the broadest compositional influence, overriding other communities at the lowest contamination levels (MDC = 0.35–0.46), while public transport exerted targeted but strong donor effects on ambulances (MDC = 0.20–0.27). By contrast, hospital-sewage donors were comparatively moderate (MDC = 0.65–0.66), and ambulances acted as the weakest donors overall (MDC = 0.75–0.90). From the recipient perspective, ambulances remained the most easily perturbed, while hospital environments and hospital sewage exhibited the highest compositional resilience, resisting reclassification until α ≥ 0.65–0.80 depending on donor type. Public-transport communities showed intermediate stability, with transitions typically occurring at α approximately 0.46–0.85.

Together, these results define a reproducible dominance hierarchy across the built-environment network. As donors, hospital environments exerted the strongest compositional influence, followed by public transport, hospital sewage, and ambulances. As recipients, hospital environments and hospital sewage were the most compositionally stable, followed by public transport, whereas ambulances were the most fragile and easily overridden. The sigmoidal dose–response trajectories across contamination gradients (Fig. 5a) highlight threshold-like tipping behavior in community identity, providing a quantitative basis for defining ecological stability boundaries across interconnected urban microbiomes. These dominance asymmetries mirror classical source-sink and resilience dynamics observed in ecological meta-communities, where high diversity donor systems exert disproportionate compositional influence on less stable recipients, yet structured environments maintain local resistance to invasion [80].

Entropy-based uncertainty, environmental dominance, and risk scoring

To extend the contamination modeling beyond deterministic transitions, we quantified compositional uncertainty, ecological dominance, and biosurveillance risk across all pairwise environment blends (Fig. 6a–f; Table S4). Synthetic mixtures were generated in 10% increments (α = 0–1), and RF predictions were used to trace classification confidence and Shannon entropy as indicators of stability and resilience.

Entropy-based uncertainty, dominance hierarchy, and composite ecological risk scoring. a RF prediction-confidence trajectories across contamination gradients showing tipping points where recipient identity collapses. b Shannon entropy trajectories illustrating uncertainty peaks (α = 0.3–0.5) during transitions. c Switch-point heatmap defining contamination thresholds for each donor–recipient pair. d Environment-dominance matrix summarizing directional ecological wins, with hospital environments showing the highest dominance. e Composite contamination risk scores under three weighting schemes (primary, equal, PCA) showing consistent environment rankings. f Radar chart summarizing normalized entropy, richness, variance, and override contributions, identifying public transport and hospital sewage as highest-risk environments

Across all pairings (Fig. 6a), prediction-confidence trajectories revealed reproducible switch points corresponding to the contamination fraction at which recipient identity transitioned to that of the donor. Hospital-sewage mixtures exhibited early transitions (α = 0.2–0.3), confirming their strong donor potential, whereas ambulance-based mixtures required markedly higher α values (≥ 0.7–0.9) to reach the same threshold. Entropy profiles (Fig. 6b) peaked at intermediate contamination fractions (α = 0.3–0.5), defining tipping zones of maximal uncertainty. Hospital sewage displayed broad, moderate entropy peaks, indicative of diverse, mixed-source communities, while ambulance microbiomes showed sharp, high-amplitude peaks consistent with abrupt compositional collapse in low-biomass systems.

The switch-point heatmap (Fig. 6c) further established the hierarchy of ecological resistance. Hospital sewage rapidly overrode public transport at α = 0.1, and public transport displaced ambulances at the same fraction, revealing that these recipients are compositionally fragile and easily overridden. In contrast, hospital sewage required α = 0.8 to override hospital-environment communities, demonstrating that hospital environments are the most compositionally resistant to microbial intrusion. This asymmetry defines a directional susceptibility gradient across the built-environment continuum, following the order hospital sewage → public transport → ambulance, with hospital environments forming the stability anchor.

Dominance outcomes summarized in the environment-dominance matrix (Fig. 6d) confirmed this relationship. Hospital environments emerged as the overall ecological winners, dominating in four of six pairwise simulations, followed by public transport with two wins, while both hospital sewage and ambulances lost in all simulations. These findings indicate that hospital environments not only resist compositional displacement but also regain their native microbial configuration following perturbation, serving as ecological stabilizers within the urban network.

We next integrated dominance frequency, mean entropy, richness, and variance into a composite contamination-risk index calculated under three weighting schemes (primary, equal, and PCA-derived). All weighting strategies yielded highly correlated results (ρ ≥ 0.6), confirming the robustness of the ranking (Table S4). Public transport and hospital sewage exhibited the highest composite risk (0.58–0.62), reflecting their combination of high entropy, moderate override frequency, and elevated variance. Ambulances displayed intermediate risk (0.47–0.55), driven by frequent overrides but low internal entropy, while hospital environments maintained the lowest overall risk (< 0.46) due to their low variance and minimal override frequency (Fig. 6e, f).

Collectively, these analyses establish a dual ecological axis linking donor strength with stability dominance with hospital sewage and public transport functioning as dynamic, high-risk microbial donors, while hospital environments act as the principal compositional anchors and ecological stabilizers. This directional yet resilient source–sink structure provides a mechanistic basis for interpreting microbial persistence and contamination potential within urban biosurveillance frameworks. These findings emphasize that microbial stability and contamination potential are emergent system properties rather than static compositional traits, linking ecological structure directly to biosurveillance function.

Discussion

Our results shed light on the ecological architecture of built-environment microbiomes, revealing a structured source–sink continuum in which hospital sewage functions as a potent microbial donor, while hospital environments act as a stabilizing compositional anchor, and ambulances and public-transit surfaces occupy more transient, fragile positions. This layered network of dominance and resilience has both theoretical and applied implications for biosurveillance, pathogen control, and the design of resilient built environments.

Ecological implications: source strength vs. compositional stability

The asymmetric contamination behavior we observed, where sewage-derived communities rapidly override other systems at modest mixing fractions (α), but themselves resist intrusion only at high thresholds, emphasizes a distinction between donor strength and compositional resilience. Sewage is rich in microbial diversity and metabolic flexibility, enabling it to colonize or destabilize lower biomass systems such as ambulances and public transports. Yet, it lacks the structural robustness to resist mixing from more stabilized environments like hospital environments. This duality aligns with ecological theory about community invasibility and priority effects, where diverse donor pools can invade simplified recipients but may themselves be restructured under strong opposing forces [81–83].

In contrast, hospital-interior environments emerged as the most stable in the dominance matrix. They dominated most pairwise competition, required high contamination thresholds to flip, and maintained low entropy under mixing. This suggests that environment surfaces harbor a persistent core assemblage of taxa resistant to exogenous disturbance. This resilience is likely a product of repeated colonization, strong selective pressure from disinfection regimes, and niche filtering that favors stress‐tolerant taxa [76, 84]. In ecological terms, hospital environments behave like stable attractors in the microbial state space, offering resistance to invasion.

Ambulance and public-transport communities, by contrast, reflect sink-like endpoints with weak internal structuring. Their rapid transitions in contamination simulations and narrow entropy peaks indicate limited ecological inertia and susceptibility to external microbial influx. Such systems are analogous to ephemeral habitats in ecology, existing only through continual input and prone to collapse when inputs change [81].

This architecture resonates with broader microbial ecology in built spaces, where microbial communities are shaped by a balance of dispersal, extinction, and selection [68, 85]. The built-environment is increasingly recognized as a network of connected microbial compartments, linked by air, water, and human contact, and shaped by cleaning practices, occupancy, and material design [68, 86, 87]. Our results provide a quantifiable mechanistic framework for that connectivity where we can estimate contamination tipping thresholds (α) and connect them with network dominance.

Clinical and pathogen surveillance implications

From a public health and biosurveillance perspective, the topology we uncovered has critical implications. Because hospital sewage is a strong microbial donor, it represents a persistent source of opportunistic pathogens and antibiotic-resistance genes into downstream water systems [74, 88]. Hospital plumbing, sinks, and wastewater drains have repeatedly been identified as reservoirs and transmission routes for multidrug-resistant organisms including P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and A. baumannii [89–91]. These environments provide moist, nutrient-rich biofilm habitats where horizontal gene transfer and persistence of resistance determinants are favored.

The finding that sewage can override transit and ambulance microbiomes at low contamination fractions suggests that interventions in plumbing and wastewater flows could exert disproportionate control over environmental pathogen dissemination. This aligns with evidence that effluent management and drain disinfection significantly reduce downstream AMR burden and nosocomial transmission [75, 92].

Conversely, the observed dominance and compositional stability of hospital environments offer a potential ecological “buffer zone.” If the hospital microbiome remains resilient, it may impede the ingress and establishment of sewage-derived pathogens, provided that cleaning protocols, ventilation systems, and surface materials are managed to promote neutral or beneficial microbial assemblages rather than complete sterilization [84, 93]. In practice, designing disinfection and material strategies that reinforce a stable, low-entropy microbial core could thus enhance ecological barriers’ contamination.

Finally, the entropy-based uncertainty and risk-scoring framework adds a predictive dimension to biosurveillance, identifying “tipping zones” of instability before compositional collapse occurs. Similar early-warning dynamics have been recognized in ecological regime-shift modeling [65], and microbial resilience studies [94]. Incorporating such predictive markers into real-time hospital surveillance could enable proactive interventions at the onset of instability rather than reactive control after outbreak establishment.

Design, engineering, and microbial control

Our findings suggest that built-environment design, including plumbing architecture, drainage gradients, airflow, and surface materials, plays a defining role in shaping microbial topology and tipping thresholds. For example, reducing connectivity between plumbing systems and environment surfaces may increase the contamination fraction (α) required for microbial invasion, thereby enhancing ecological resilience.

The emerging field of microbial biocontrol in built environments proposes harnessing benign or commensal taxa to outcompete pathogens on surfaces, HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) systems, and water lines [68, 89]. However, the success of such interventions depends on the persistence landscape of introduced microbes, specifically, whether they can survive, integrate, and establish within the resident community under the selective pressures of cleaning and environmental fluctuation [86, 95]. The contamination-based models developed here offer a quantitative framework for mapping such “fitness valleys” and identifying the ecological thresholds that determine establishment success.

Moreover, hospital plumbing and sink design features, such as P-trap geometry, splash reduction, and drainage configuration, have been shown to mediate microbial transfer and biofilm development [89]. Architectural and fluid-dynamics optimizations that minimize upward splash and inter-surface flow can therefore reduce pathogen transfer risk while maintaining stable background microbiota. Integrating these engineering principles with microbial ecological models, as demonstrated in this study, offers a pathway toward hospitals that are not only physically clean but also microbially resilient, capable of maintaining compositional stability under perturbation and resisting colonization by pathogenic or AMR-associated taxa [68, 95].

Limitations and future directions

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the contamination simulations presented here assume idealized blending and do not explicitly capture spatial heterogeneity, micro-gradients, or biofilm structure. In real systems, micro-environments such as crevices and moisture gradients may provide hideout that alters effective contamination thresholds (α). Second, the modeling framework is correlative and empirical. Experimental perturbations or controlled synthetic inoculation experiments will be necessary to validate the predicted directional dynamics. Third, the present analysis treats microbial communities as temporally static, whereas real built-environment microbiomes evolve under fluctuating selective pressures. Incorporating temporal metagenomic data would enable evaluation of how resilience and tipping thresholds shift over time.

Future work should expand in several directions. Integrating strain-level tracking, functional gene flow analyses (for instance, resistome and mobilome dynamics), and metabolite exchange will help identify keystone taxa and metabolic gatekeepers of resilience. Coupling building engineering models, such as airflow, water hydraulics, and surface materials with microbial community models could further enable predictive design of microbiome-conscious infrastructures.

This study focused on pathogen-associated taxa to quantify biosurveillance risk, yet inclusion of non-pathogenic community members in future resilience modeling will illuminate background ecological buffering and competitive exclusion processes that stabilize built-environment microbiomes. Moreover, although genome-resolved MAGs provided high-confidence species-level and functional linkage, plasmid- and phage-borne resistance genes remain underrepresented. Integrating long-read sequencing or plasmidome capture approaches will be essential to quantify horizontal gene transfer potential and the mobility-associated AMR risk landscape in urban environments.

Finally, benchmarking genome-resolved workflows against contig- and read-level approaches will clarify trade-offs between taxonomic resolution, functional sensitivity, and ecological interpretability. Such comparisons are particularly relevant for biosurveillance applications where mobile genetic elements and strain-level variants mediate critical pathways of persistence and dissemination.

Conclusions

In sum, this study provides a quantitative blueprint of microbial continuity and resistance across built environments. Through synthetic blending and entropy-driven modeling, we show that hospital sewage is a potent microbial donor, but hospital environments act as the ecosystem’s stabilizer, while ambulances and public-transit surfaces sit on the periphery of instability. These insights bridge ecological theory and infection control, offering a pathway toward infrastructure design and surveillance systems grounded in microbial systems ecology. In the era of rising antimicrobial resistance and hospital-acquired infection risk, such ecological models offer a new frontier for biosafety-informed building design and microbial risk mitigation.
