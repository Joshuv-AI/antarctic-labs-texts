Cutibacterium acnes infection of the acromioclavicular joint: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ivan A. Garces, Angel E. Herrera, Kenai A. Gupte, Andrew J. Chi, Derek F. Papp
Published: 2026

Introduction

Cutibacterium acnes (C acnes) is a slow growing, anaerobic gram-positive bacillus that resides as a commensal organism on the skin, particularly in sebaceous areas such as the axilla.1 While historically regarded as a contaminant, its clinical significance has become increasingly recognized in orthopedic surgery, particularly in post-operative shoulder infections and cases involving orthopedic implants.4 Its ability to persist intracellularly and elicit low grade inflammation allows the bacterium to evade immune responses and delay diagnosis, often leading to poor recognition in clinical settings.12

C acnes infections typically occur in the setting of prior surgical intervention or implanted hardware.8 In these cases, diagnosis can be difficult due to absent systemic symptoms, normal inflammatory markers, and nonspecific imaging.12 However, growing evidence suggests that C acnes may exist as an intracellular and intra-articular commensal bacterium in native joints, raising concern for noniatrogenic infections that can present insidiously and mimic more common mechanical or degenerative shoulder conditions.5,11

We present a case of C acnes isolated from the native acromioclavicular (AC) joint of a 69-year-old male with no prior history of shoulder surgery or invasive procedures. The patient's symptoms were initially attributed to rotator cuff tendinopathy and degenerative joint disease. Although C acnes was found in the AC joint, it is uncertain if joint colonization slowly contributed to the patient's AC joint degeneration and symptomatology. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential in patients with shoulder pain and contributes to the evolving understanding of the clinical spectrum of C acnes infection in native joints.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old gentleman presented with right shoulder pain persisting for eight weeks. He denied any previous trauma or specific inciting event. He reported constant pain unrelieved by oxycodone/acetaminophen (prescribed by another provider for his low back pain) or anti-inflammatory medications.

Physical examination demonstrated active forward flexion and abduction to 150°, external rotation to 85°, and internal rotation to 45°. Strength was 5/5 in the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and subscapularis muscles. There was mild tenderness to palpation over the AC joint; Neer and Hawkins impingement signs were positive, while O'Brien test was negative. The patient received a corticosteroid injection consisting of 1 cc of Kenalog-40, 2 cc of 1% lidocaine, and 2 cc of 0.25% Marcaine into the subacromial space without complication. A diagnosis of right rotator cuff tendinitis with subdeltoid bursitis was made. The patient was advised to initiate physical therapy and avoid overhead and pressing activities for six weeks.

The patient returned 6 weeks later with persistent pain. He had been unable to attend physical therapy due to work obligations and had since retired. While symptoms had slightly improved after the injection, he continued to experience pain with overhead activity. Physical examination was unchanged from the prior visit. As conservative measures such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acetaminophen, activity modification, therapeutic injection, and physical therapy had failed, a right shoulder magnetic resonance imaging without contrast was ordered.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated degenerative joint disease of the right AC joint with edema of both the distal clavicle and acromion (Fig. 1, A-D). Further questioning and exam findings demonstrated the patient's AC joint as the source of his ongoing pain. After a detailed discussion of surgical risks and benefits, the patient elected to proceed with right open distal clavicle resection. The procedure included open resection of the distal clavicle using the senior author's technique. Following a standard approach to the AC joint, the periosteum was dissected anteriorly and posteriorly, and we removed what remained of the intra-articular disc. One centimeter of the distal clavicle was marked and resected. A bur was used to smooth the bone edges. There was no evidence of impingement or instability at the resection site. The wound was irrigated thoroughly, and intraoperative radiograph confirmed adequate resection. The surgeon cut the resected bone in half—one-half was sent to pathology, which demonstrated reparative and degenerative changes. We sent the other half en bloc for culture and gram stain (which showed moderate WBCs and no organisms).

The patient returned to clinic on post-operative day 9. The patient reported adequate pain control and no systemic symptoms. The incision was clean, dry, and intact, without erythema or induration. Active forward flexion and abduction were easily 90°. Intraoperative cultures obtained from the AC joint demonstrated light growth of C acnes. The patient denied any history of shoulder surgery but reported prior motorcycle accidents, raising the possibility of remote traumatic seeding of the joint. Given the absence of systemic symptoms and implanted hardware, the patient was prescribed a 14-day course of oral cephalexin 500 mg twice daily. Sutures were removed, Steri-Strips were applied, and wound care instructions were reviewed. The patient was referred to physical therapy for a short course focused on inflammation control, range of motion, and gradual strengthening, along with repeated instructions to call back if any systemic symptoms arose. After 6 weeks, the patient had regained full ROM and strength. The patient returned again at 3 months with no recurrence of symptoms or signs of infection. He declined the need for further follow-up.

Discussion

This case highlights a case of AC joint osteoarthritis with the additional finding of C acnes at the time of distal clavicle resection. AC joint degenerative changes are a common source of shoulder pain in older adults, with radiographic evidence of AC osteoarthritis present in over half of patients above 50 year old.3 Patients often report pain with overhead or cross-body motions, and exam findings can include a positive impingement sign (Neer, Hawkins) and AC joint tenderness, as seen in our patient. Initial management of AC joint arthritis is conservative. This includes activity modification, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and corticosteroid injections. These injections do not often provide lasting relief; 1 study reported that steroid injection into the AC joint yielded on average around 20 days of pain relief, and 67% of patients still required surgical intervention subsequently.3 When symptoms persist despite conservative therapy, distal clavicle resection (the Mumford procedure) is the definitive treatment. This can be performed open or arthroscopically and involves excising around 1 cm of the distal clavicle to eliminate the arthritic AC joint. Distal clavicle excision reliably alleviates pain and has high success rates in appropriately selected patients.3 In our case, because of the diagnostic uncertainty and the presence of bone marrow edema on both sides of the joint, the surgeon proceeded with open resection to allow direct visualization and the collection of intraoperative tissue for pathology and culture.

Notably, cultures from the resected AC joint grew C acnes, an anaerobic gram-positive bacillus that resides in sebaceous glands and is a common commensal of the skin, especially in the shoulder region.1,6 It is a low-virulence organism not typically associated with acute infections; in fact, C acnes rarely causes septic arthritis in native joints without prosthetic material.6 Most reported cases involve iatrogenic introduction, via surgery or injections.6 He did report a number of motorcycle accidents in the past, which could theoretically seed the joint. In our patient, there was no history of shoulder surgery, but he did receive a corticosteroid injection 6 weeks prior, which could have introduced the organism. Indeed, C acnes infection has been reported years after intra-articular corticosteroid injections in the shoulder.2

Overall, the presence of C acnes in this context is curious as there were no overt signs of infection such as fever, significant erythema, or elevated inflammatory markers. Unfortunately, the specimen was sent en bloc with only 1 culture grown. We asked the pathologist to evaluate the other half for malignancy/metastatic disease (though unlikely given the imaging findings). The authors recognize the possibility of specimen contamination, as a recent systematic review concluded that C acnes is best conceptualized as a common commensal and perhaps a frequent contaminant, with deep tissue cultures positive in approximately 21% of nonarthroplasty shoulder surgeries.9 However, the authors wish to propose that C acnes joint colonization might act as a catalyst for arthritic change in some patients. This would align with the known clinical behavior of C acnes in general, which often present as chronic pain or stiffness with minimal systemic symptoms.1 Clinicians are cautioned not to dismiss a positive C acnes culture as mere contamination without careful correlation to the clinical picture,6 especially in the shoulder joint, where this organism is prevalent.

Our case adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting C acnes may play a contributory role in chronic shoulder pathology. C acnes is a leading cause of periprosthetic shoulder infections, often identified in patients with painful “aseptic” shoulder arthroplasties.4 For example, 1 study found positive C acnes cultures in 41% of patients undergoing revision shoulder arthroplasty for loosening.4 More provocatively, Levy et al cultured C acnes from 23 of 55 patients (42%) undergoing primary shoulder arthroplasty for osteoarthritis, despite no obvious signs of infection. They postulated that an unrecognized low-grade C acnes infection might contribute to glenohumeral arthritis.7 Similarly, Hudek et al5 used immunohistochemical techniques to detect C acnes in patients with degenerative shoulder conditions and found the bacterium present intracellularly in 75% of AC joint samples, far more than what standard cultures detected. Their study supports the hypothesis that C acnes may exist as an “intracellular commensal” in the shoulder joint and might initiate or exacerbate osteoarthritis through chronic inflammation.5 We hope to add to this literature and promote further investigation/discussion regarding C acnes joint colonization as a potential risk factor for glenohumeral joint arthritis.

Managing an unexpected C acnes finding in this setting poses dilemmas. Septic arthritis of the AC joint is rare and usually caused by more virulent organisms like Staphylococcus aureus, particularly in immunocompromised or IV drug use patients.10 These cases typically require surgical drainage and prolonged IV antibiotics.10 In contrast, our case was chronic and indolent, with no systemic symptoms, and C acnes is known for low-grade infections with subtle presentation.1 There is no consensus on how to manage C acnes in a native joint post-operatively. While some regard it as a contaminant, others recommend treating it as a true infection given its potential to persist and form biofilms. In our case, a 14-day course of oral cephalexin was prescribed; C acnes is typically sensitive to beta-lactams, including penicillin's and first-generation cephalosporins.1 Since the resection likely removed the infected tissue, and there was no hardware, a brief oral course was deemed sufficient, with close clinical monitoring. Fortunately, the patient's outcome was favorable.

This case highlights several key points. First, AC joint arthritis unresponsive to conservative therapy is well managed with distal clavicle resection. Second, obtaining cultures, even in “noninfectious” joint surgery, can reveal occult pathogens like C acnes. Third, clinicians must recognize the unique, often subtle behavior of C acnes in shoulder pathology and consider treatment on a case-by-case basis. Finally, this case supports emerging theories that chronic musculoskeletal degeneration may, in some cases, involve a microbial component. Further research is needed to distinguish pathogenic involvement from colonization in such presentations.
