Safeguarding artemisinin: integrating bioproduction innovation and resistance management for malaria eradication
Authors: Karim Farmanpour Kalalagh, Ali Rezaei, Saeid Hazrati, Silvana Nicola, Sousan Moradi, Sanaz Davarpanah Dizaj, Neda Tariverdizadeh, Arman Beyraghdar Kashkooli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Malaria persists as a global health emergency, with Plasmodium falciparum resistance to artemisinin (ART) critically undermining elimination campaigns. This review consolidates dual challenges of ART production and resistance, presenting a unified analysis of countermeasures. The examination encompasses biotechnological advances in ART biosynthesis, including metabolic engineering strategies applied in both native and heterologous hosts, alongside an assessment of approaches to mitigate agricultural constraints in Artemisia annua cultivation. Concurrently, the analysis focuses on the molecular mechanisms underpinning ART resistance, with particular emphasis on pfk13 mutations and their implications for therapeutic efficacy. Current treatments, notably artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT), and novel strategies like triple and quadruple ACTs (TACTs/QACTs) are critically evaluated, emphasizing advantages, drawbacks, and geographical suitability. By bridging production and resistance paradigms, this review outlines a sustainable malaria control roadmap, advocating interdisciplinary collaboration to ensure ART’s enduring efficacy.

Background

Malaria remains a critical global health threat, disproportionately affecting populations in tropical regions, particularly across Africa [1]. The urgency to combat this disease has spurred extensive research into eradication strategies and therapeutic innovations [2]. In 2023 alone, malaria caused approximately 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths worldwide, showing its persistent burden. The pathogen responsible for the deadliest form of malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, is transmitted through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [3].

Artemisinin (ART), a lipophilic sesquiterpene lactone containing 15 carbon atoms derived from the glandular trichomes of Artemisia annua (Asteraceae), has emerged as a cornerstone of antimalarial therapy [4, 5]. Its efficacy, alongside derivatives like dihydroartemisinin (also known as dihydroqinghaosu, or artenimol), extends beyond malaria treatment, demonstrating possible antitumour [6], anti-cancer, anti-viral properties, anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity [7, 8]. Artemisia annua, commonly termed Qinghao in traditional Chinese medicine [1] and annual wormwood, sweet wormwood, and sweet annie in English [9], is a diploid, cross-pollinated species [1], distributed in southeastern Asia, southeastern South America, western and central Europe, and southeastern North America, and cultivated in different countries [10, 11]. Historically utilized in China to treat fevers [1] and in Europe as a flavoring agent, this plant gained prominence following the isolation of ART [12].

The discovery of ART’s antimalarial activity arose from Project 523, a Chinese government initiative launched in the 1960 s to screen traditional remedies. In the 1970 s, a research team led by Professor Tu Youyou, later awarded the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, identified A. annua extracts as highly effective against Plasmodium parasites, achieving 100% inhibition in murine models [13–15]. By the late 1970 s, ART was established as a first-line antimalarial, addressing rising resistance to chloroquine and other therapeutics [1, 15, 16].

Despite its efficacy, ART production remains constrained by low natural yields in A. annua, exacerbated by environmental variability and cultivation challenges [17, 18]. Chemical synthesis has proven economically unfeasible due to structural complexity and environmental concerns [19]. Consequently, biotechnological approaches, including heterologous production of ART precursors in microbial systems and metabolic engineering in plants, are being actively pursued. Advances in synthetic biology now enable ART pathway reconstruction in diverse hosts, though scaling production remains a hurdle [4, 20, 21].

As ART-based therapies face emerging parasite resistance, this review examines current strategies to ART biosynthesis and production challenges, the emergence of ART resistance, and therapeutic strategies to sustain malaria control efforts.

Literature search and review methodology

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize a comprehensive and critical overview of the current knowledge on ART, from its biosynthesis to therapeutic applications. An extensive literature search was conducted across major scholarly databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, to ensure a thorough and transparent foundation for this review. The search strategy employed a combination of key terms and phrases such as “artemisinin biosynthesis,” “Artemisia annua metabolic engineering,” “artemisinin resistance mechanisms,” “artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT),” “triple ACT (TACT),” and “artemisinin production challenges.” The selection of literature for inclusion was not governed by strict, pre-defined eligibility criteria as in a systematic review but was guided by the objective of identifying seminal, high-impact, and recent studies that collectively provide a deep and interpretive understanding of the field’s landscape, key debates, and future directions.

Artemisinin biosynthesis and production challenges: from plant trichomes to cell factories

Artemisinin biosynthetic pathway

A comprehensive understanding of the key biosynthetic pathway and associated genes governing ART production is critical for enhancing yields in A. annua and reconstructing the pathway in heterologous systems. ART is synthesized alongside arteannuin B (ARB) within glandular trichomes (GTs) localized to the leaves and floral structures of A. annua [4, 22]. Notably, GT density is highest in young leaves, buds, and flowers [22]. ART biosynthesis proceeds through the terpenoid biosynthetic pathway, which derives isoprene units from two distinct pathways: the cytosolic mevalonate (MVA) pathway and the plastidial 2-C-methylerythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway [23].

The pathway initiates with the condensation of isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP) to form farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP), a pivotal precursor in sesquiterpene metabolism [24]. The cyclization of FPP to amorpha-4,11-diene, catalyzed by amorpha-4,11-diene synthase (ADS), represents the first committed step in ART biosynthesis [25, 26]. Subsequent oxidation by cytochrome P450 hydroxylase (CYP71AV1) converts amorpha 4, 11-diene to artemisinic alcohol. Artemisinic alcohol is then oxidized to artemisinic aldehyde primarily by the action of A. annua alcohol dehydrogenase 1 (AaADH1) [27], and the aldehyde is further oxidized to artemisinic acid [28, 29]. Artemisinic aldehyde serves as a branching point: it is either reduced to dihydroartemisinic aldehyde by artemisinic aldehyde reductase (DBR2) and aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH1) [26, 30] or channeled through CYP71AV1 and ALDH1 to yield artemisinic acid. Furthermore, a recently identified dihydroartemisinic acid dehydrogenase (AaDHAADH) from A. annua provides a direct enzymatic route for the bidirectional conversion between artemisinic acid and DHAA [31]. Non-enzymatic oxidation of dihydroartemisinic acid (DHAA) and artemisinic acid under UV light and aerobic conditions generates ART and ARB, respectively [2, 26, 32] (Fig. 1). Agricultural optimization

ART yield is influenced by genotype (e.g., hybrids with ≥ 2% dry weight) [33], photoperiod and light quality (16 h supplemental green and UV-B light increases trichome density by 11% and 9%, respectively) [34], and elicitors (chitosan elevates ART by 53% [18]). Tetraploid induction (56% yield increase [35]), utilizing a consortium of endophytes [36] and precision farming (foliar selenium + nanosilicon: 1.4 × ART [37] exemplify field-ready strategies.

Biotechnological and chemical innovations

Metabolic engineering in Artemisia annua

Genetic and metabolic engineering have significantly advanced the biosynthesis of ART in A. annua. Efforts have focused on enhancing ART content through various biotechnological methods in the native host plant. One approach involves the use of the truncated AaActin1 promoter, which has shown higher activity than the commonly used CaMV35S promoter in directing gene expression in peltate glandular secretory trichomes, thus providing a novel tool for metabolic engineering [38]. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation systems have been established to transfer genes related to ART biosynthesis into A. annua, aiming to increase ART content in transgenic plants [39]. Advanced strategies include enhancing metabolic flux, blocking competing pathways, and using TFs to increase ART yields [40]. Co-transformation of biosynthetic pathway genes and trichome-specific TFs has resulted in significant increases in ART content, demonstrating the potential of genetic engineering to stabilize and enhance production [41]. Additionally, research has shown that non-glandular trichome cells can also express the ART biosynthetic pathway, suggesting new avenues for metabolic engineering [42]. Co-overexpression of key biosynthetic enzymes has led to substantial increases in ART production, highlighting the potential of transgenic technology to optimize the biosynthetic pathway [43].

Transcription factors (TFs) regulation

TFs play a crucial role in regulating trichome density and ART production in A. annua. The R2R3 MYB TFs TrichomeLess Regulator 1 (TLR1) and TrichomeLess Regulator 2 (TLR2) have been identified as negative regulators of trichome density and ART levels. Overexpression of TLR1 and TLR2 results in reduced trichome development and lower ART content, while their suppression leads to increased trichome density and ART levels [44]. Conversely, the bHLH TF AaMYC2-type positively influences both trichome density and ART biosynthesis. Overexpression of AaMYC2-type enhances the expression of genes involved in trichome development and ART biosynthesis, leading to increased ART accumulation [45]. Similarly, the AP2 TF trichome and artemisinin regulator 1 (TAR1) is essential for trichome development and ART biosynthesis, with its overexpression significantly boosting ART content [46]. Another MYB TF, trichome and artemisinin regulator 2 (TAR2), also positively regulates trichome development and ART production, with its overexpression increasing both trichome numbers and ART content [47]. The synergistic interaction of two bHLH TFs, Aa6119 and Aa7162, further enhances ART biosynthesis and trichome development, indicating a complex regulatory network [48]. The WRKY TF glandular trichome-specific wrky 1 (AaGSW1) specifically promotes ART biosynthesis in glandular trichomes by regulating key biosynthetic genes [49]. AaMYC3, another bHLH TF, bridges the regulation of trichome density and ART biosynthesis, acting as a co-activator with other bHLH proteins to enhance ART yield [50]. The AP2/ERF TF AaORA is a positive regulator of ART biosynthesis and also contributes to disease resistance, highlighting its multifaceted role [51]. The R2R3-MYB TF AaMYB17 is crucial for the initiation of glandular secretory trichomes, thereby increasing ART content without affecting trichome morphology [52]. Lastly, the MYB TF AaMYB108-like, induced by light and jasmonic acid, positively regulates the initiation of glandular secretory trichomes, further enhancing ART production [53]. These findings collectively indicate the complex interplay of TFs in regulating trichome density and ART biosynthesis, offering potential targets for genetic manipulation to increase ART yield.

In planta artemisinin production

The production of ART precursors and ART itself in in planta platforms, such as Nicotiana benthamiana and Physcomitrella patens, has seen significant advancements. In N. benthamiana, the production of ART precursors has been achieved through agro-infiltration techniques. This involves the expression of biosynthetic genes leading to artemisinic acid, a precursor of ART. The process includes the introduction of amorpha-4,11-diene synthase, which results in the production of amorpha-4,11-diene, a key precursor. However, the expected oxidation products like artemisinic acid were not detected, suggesting further metabolism by endogenous glycosyl transferases, leading to the accumulation of artemisinic acid-12-β-diglucoside [54]. Additionally, the introduction of specific lipid transfer proteins from A. annua has been shown to enhance the accumulation of ART precursors in N. benthamiana by preventing the reflux of these compounds back into the cells, thus boosting production [4].

In Physcomitrella patens, a stable and scalable production platform for ART has been developed by engineering the moss with the complete set of ART biosynthesis genes. This approach has resulted in the successful in planta biosynthesis of ART without further modifications, achieving a high initial production rate [21, 55]. The integration of the full biosynthetic pathway into P. patens has demonstrated the moss's potential to complement the ART pathway with its endogenous enzymes, leading to the production of ART and related compounds like arteannuin B [56].

Semi-synthetic and microbial production of artemisinin and its precursors

The heterologous production of ART and its precursors have progressed from academic research to industrial-scale manufacturing, most notably through a semi-synthetic process. This approach is exemplified by the commercial production of semi-synthetic ART [27, 57]. This landmark achievement demonstrated the importance of supplying a key pharmaceutical ingredient at scale [58, 59], with ongoing research focused on further optimizing the commercial production of semi-synthetic ART [60]. Alongside this commercial pathway, significant advancements continue across various microbial hosts. In Escherichia coli, the engineering of a synthetic amorpha-4,11-diene synthase gene and the mevalonate isoprenoid pathway from Saccharomyces cerevisiae has enabled the production of amorphadiene, a precursor to ART, reaching concentrations of 24 μg caryophyllene equivalent/ml [61]. Further improvements in the heterologous mevalonate (MEV) pathway and fermentation processes have achieved commercially relevant titers of 27.4 g/L of amorpha-4,11-diene [62]. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the pioneering production of artemisinic acid was achieved through the engineering of the MEV pathway and the introduction of a novel cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (CYP71AV1), yielding titers of up to 100 mg/L [29]. Subsequent optimization efforts have significantly advanced this production, with reported titers reaching up to 25 g/L [27]. Additionally, Yarrowia lipolytica has been explored for its potential in amorphadiene production by harnessing its lipogenic acetyl-CoA pathways and endogenous MEV pathway, achieving a production level of 171.5 mg/L [63]. In Bacillus subtilis, metabolic engineering efforts have focused on improving amorphadiene production by upregulating plant-derived amorphadiene synthase and optimizing the expression of key enzymes, resulting in a yield of 416 ± 15 mg/L [64]. Aspergillus terreus has been used for the microbial transformation of ART, resulting in the production of derivatives such as 1-deoxyartemisinin and 4α-hydroxy-1-deoxyartemisinin [65]. These advancements across different microbial platforms highlight the potential for scalable and sustainable production of ART and its precursors in microbial heterologous hosts to meet increased ART global demand.

The remarkable success in enhancing ART supply through agricultural and biotechnological innovations has been pivotal for addressing the global demand driven by the implementation of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) as first-line treatment against Plasmodium falciparum malaria. However, this very success, by facilitating widespread and sometimes suboptimal use of ART, has inadvertently contributed to selective pressures that are now a primary concern. The increased availability and deployment of ACT have directly influenced the evolutionary landscape of the parasite, indicating a critical link between production scalability and therapeutic sustainability. Consequently, the challenge has progressively shifted from one of mere supply to one of preserving clinical efficacy, thereby making the understanding of ART resistance mechanisms an urgent and inseparable consequence of these production achievements.

Emergence of artemisinin resistance

Genetic drivers

Plasmodium falciparum, a protozoan parasite responsible for vector-borne malaria, remains a leading cause of global mortality. Its prevalence across tropical regions poses significant challenges, particularly in developing nations [66]. Antimalarial agents, from quinine and chloroquine to sulfadoxine/pyrimethamine, mefloquine, and artemisinin-based therapies (ARTs), have been pivotal in parasite clearance [67]. ARTs, widely deployed as first-line treatments, are central to malaria management [68]. However, emerging resistance to these drugs, particularly ART, threatens global control and eradication efforts [69–71]. This resistance has contributed to increased treatment failures [72] and complicated therapeutic decision-making [66]. Resistance arises through the selection of parasites harbouring specific single-point mutations that shorten the effective lifespan of antimalarials [73–75].

Notably, mutations in specific parasite genes drive drug resistance. For example, cytochrome B (cytb) mutations confer atovaquone resistance [76], while polymorphisms in chloroquine resistance transporter (pfcrt) and multidrug resistance 1 (pfmdr1) genes are linked to resistance against amodiaquine, chloroquine, quinine, lumefantrine, mefloquine, piperaquine, primaquine, etc. [77, 78]. Similarly, mutations in dihydropteroate synthase (pfdhps) and dihydrofolate reductase-thymidylate synthase (pfdhfR) genes underlie sulfadoxine/pyrimethamine resistance [66].

Following the initial detection of artemisinin resistance (ART-R) in 2007 [79], proposed mechanisms include activation of the unfolded protein response [80], impaired hemoglobin processing [81], oxidative stress modulation [82], dysregulation of phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K)-mediated proteostasis [83], and altered protein ubiquitination [84]. Additional hypotheses suggest ART-R involves enhanced ring-stage survival, reduced heme-dependent drug activation, and diminished phototoxicity [85]. Despite mechanistic variations, consensus highlights roles for ribosome biogenesis factors, RNA metabolism, and exported proteins in ART-R [86]. Non-synonymous polymorphisms in multidrug resistance protein 2 (mrp2), ferredoxin (fd), apicoplast ribosomal protein S10 (arps10), chloroquine resistance transporter (pfcrt) genes [87], and autophagy-related gene 18 (atg18) [88] also correlated strongly with resistance.

Critically, mutations in the Plasmodium falciparum Kelch 13 propeller (pfk13) gene on chromosome 13, altering the β-propeller domain of the Kelch-13 protein, have been identified as a primary ART-R determinant [69, 85, 89–94]. The pfk13 gene encodes six Kelch motifs, which mediate diverse cellular functions [95]. The K13 protein (726 amino acids) shares homology with Kelch/BTB/POZ (Broad complex Tramtrack Bric a brac/Pox virus Zinc finger) ubiquitination adaptors. These adaptors, alongside Cullin-3 E3 ubiquitin ligase, regulate ubiquitin conjugation to target proteins, influencing endocytosis, membrane trafficking, transcription, and cell cycle progression [89, 96, 97].

K13 localizes to the cytostome, facilitating hemoglobin uptake, a critical process for amino acid acquisition. During haemoglobin digestion, toxic heme is detoxified into haemozoin [85, 89, 93]. ART disrupts this pathway, but resistance mutations, particularly the prevalent C580Y variant [89, 98–100], attenuate haemoglobin endocytosis [89], alter protein-binding affinities, and elevate specific parasite protein levels [101]. These adaptations neutralize ART’s efficacy, indicating the urgency of global ART-R surveillance to guide drug development, combination therapies, and next-generation treatments [102, 103].

Global surveillance

Global surveillance of ART resistance is crucial due to the increasing spread of ART-resistant P. falciparum, particularly in Southeast Asia and parts of Africa. In Southeast Asia, particularly the Greater Mekong Subregion, resistance has been well-documented, with specific K13 mutations, such as C580Y being prevalent [104–106]. These mutations have led to delayed parasite clearance, posing a significant threat to malaria control efforts [105, 107]. In Africa, although the resistance is not as widespread, there have been reports of K13 mutations, such as A675V and C469Y, which are associated with prolonged parasite clearance times [108, 109]. Surveillance efforts are being enhanced through genomic studies and the development of rapid diagnostic tools, such as qPCR-based assays, which facilitate the detection of resistance even in resource-limited settings [108, 110]. The global effort to contain ART resistance involves mapping the distribution of K13 mutations and implementing strategies to prevent the spread of resistant strains, including the use of combination therapies and enhanced monitoring systems [104, 107, 111]. These efforts are critical to maintaining the efficacy of current malaria treatments and preventing a resurgence of the disease in regions where it has been controlled [112, 113].

Therapeutic strategies: from ACT to QACT

ACT: efficacy and limitations

Following sustained global efforts to reduce malaria over the past decade, the identification of P. falciparum Kelch 13 propeller (pfk13)-mediated ART-R has provided critical insights for refining eradication strategies, optimizing disease control protocols, and advancing the development of novel therapeutic regimens [114]. Preventing or delaying drug resistance remains an indispensable priority for achieving long-term malaria control and elimination. Network meta-analysis (NMA) shows that ART monotherapy carries a high risk of recurrence due to its rapid action and short half-life, which often fails to ensure complete parasite clearance [115]. To mitigate this risk, current international guidelines mandate the use of at least two antimalarial agents with distinct mechanisms of action and cellular targets, ensuring synergistic parasite suppression [116]. In alignment with these principles, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends ART-based combination therapy (ACT) as first-line treatment in regions with confirmed ART-R, emphasizing their role in curbing resistance evolution [68].

ACT functions by combining two antimalarials that target the P. falciparum haem detoxification pathway, a critical vulnerability in the parasite’s life cycle [117]. These regimens typically pair a short-acting ART derivative, which rapidly reduces parasite biomass and alleviates acute clinical symptoms, with a long-acting partner drug designed to eliminate residual parasites and prevent recrudescence [88, 116, 118]. Comparative studies have documented the variable efficacy and safety profiles of ART with amodiaquine, azithromycin, chloroquine, clindamycin, fosmidomycin, lumefantrine, mefloquine, naphthoquinone, piperaquine, primaquine, pyronaridine, and quinine (Table 1). Notably, ACT administration has not been associated with increased genetic diversity in the Pfk13-propeller domain, a key marker of ART-R evolution [119]. Despite their historical success, the emergence of parasites resistant to both ART and PD components has increasingly compromised ACT efficacy, contributing to documented rises in treatment failures across multiple regions [68, 120, 121]. Of particular concern is evidence suggesting that pre-existing reductions in ART susceptibility within ACT regimens may accelerate the development of partner drug resistance in certain epidemiological settings, further complicating control efforts [122].

Clinical evaluations in countries such as Cameroon, Tanzania, Senegal, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo have demonstrated high ACT efficacy, with PCR-corrected cure rates frequently surpassing 90%, indicating their continued utility in these regions [123–126]. However, the emergence of ART resistance poses a significant threat to malaria control, particularly in Southeast Asia, where resistance patterns have been rigorously documented [122, 127]. In contrast, the absence of validated resistance markers in many African regions suggests that artemisinin-based combinations retain their therapeutic effectiveness there, though vigilance remains critical [124–126].

Persistent limitations of ACT include high costs, uneven accessibility in resource-limited settings, and insufficient safety data for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and children, necessitating further research [128, 129]. Additionally, the potential for accelerated partner drug resistance, facilitated by pre-existing ART resistance, highlights the urgent need for sustained molecular surveillance and the development of next-generation antimalarial strategies [122, 127]. Collectively, these findings affirm that ACT remains a strategy for global malaria treatment. However, addressing these multifaceted challenges, spanning resistance, accessibility, and safety, is imperative to safeguarding their long-term efficacy and achieving malaria elimination goals.

TACTs/QACTs: balancing benefits and risks

The emergence of resistance to artemisinin-based combination therapy necessitates novel therapeutic approaches to mitigate the spread of ACT-resistant P. falciparum [130–132]. In response, Triple artemisinin-based combination therapy (TACT) has been proposed, incorporating an additional partner drug with intermediate half-life and complementary protective effects to existing ACT components, as outlined in recent studies (Table 1) [133–137]. This approach aims to achieve Single Encounter Radical Cure and Preventive Therapy (SERCAP), a foundational principle of TACT efficacy. By targeting multiple vulnerabilities in the parasite’s life cycle, TACTs may reduce the likelihood of de novo resistance development [130, 132, 138], establishing their potential as critical interventions in regions with confirmed ACT resistance [102, 139].

However, as ACT resistance continues to evolve in endemic regions, the prospect of future resistance to TACTs remains a significant concern. Although preliminary studies highlight the efficacy of fourth-line PDs (Table 1), the potential necessity for quadruple ART-based combination therapy (QACT), four-drug combinations, indicates the urgency for adaptive, forward-looking strategies. Geographic variability in resistance patterns has led the WHO to advocate for region-specific treatment protocols (Table 2). Expanding on these challenges, the success of TACT faces potential threats from the emergence of triple-resistant genotypes, which could jeopardize elimination efforts in low-transmission settings [102]. In Africa, where ACT remains largely effective, TACT deployment could preemptively curb resistance escalation; however, ethical, regulatory, and market-access barriers must be resolved before implementation. The adoption of TACT in African countries will hinge on demonstrable superiority over existing artemisinin-based combinations, robust WHO advocacy, and compatibility with current distribution frameworks [138]. Conversely, QACT demonstrates comparable efficacy and safety profiles to triple therapies, though their relevance to malaria management requires further justification through targeted research. Collectively, TACTs and QACTs represent promising avenues to counteract antimalarial resistance, yet their sustainable implementation demands rigorous evaluation of clinical, logistical, and socioeconomic factors to ensure long-term viability. Crucially, antimalarial regimens, whether monotherapies or multidrug combinations, demand meticulous customization for distinct patient populations (e.g., infants, children, pregnant individuals, and adults) under expert clinical supervision to maximize therapeutic efficacy and mitigate adverse effects across both uncomplicated and severe malaria cases.

Conclusion and future directions

The fight against malaria hinges on dual pipelines: scaling artemisinin production via metabolic engineering and synthetic biology, and delaying resistance through spatially-adaptive therapies. Emerging tools, genome-edited hosts, PfK13 inhibitors, and drug combinations offer hope. Prioritizing equitable access and resistance surveillance will determine if artemisinin remains a “silver bullet” or succumbs to evolutionary inevitability.
