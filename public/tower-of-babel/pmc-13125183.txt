Houttuynia cordata extract protects against Salmonella infection by targeting type III secretion system 1
Authors: Tao Wang, Siyu Cai, Jingjing Zhang, Jianfeng Wang, Xuming Deng, Kelong Ma, Hongtao Liu, Jiazhang Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salmonella is a major zoonotic pathogen and a leading cause of foodborne illness worldwide, posing serious threats to public health and livestock production. The rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant strains underscores the urgent need for alternative anti-infective strategies. Antivirulence therapies targeting pathogen virulence factors (e.g., the type III secretion system 1, T3SS-1) have gained increasing attention because they mitigate infection and minimize resistance development. Here, we systematically evaluated the protective efficacy of Houttuynia cordata extract (HCE) in both murine and economically relevant chick infection models of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella Pullorum. HCE significantly improved host pathology, alleviated intestinal barrier damage, reduced bacterial burden in target organs and modulated inflammatory responses. Mechanistically, HCE downregulated HilA, the central transcriptional regulator of T3SS-1, thereby suppressing effector protein secretion and bacterial invasion. These findings provide robust in vivo evidence supporting HCE as a low-cost, natural antivirulence candidate for the prevention and control of Salmonella infections in the poultry farming industry.

Introduction

Salmonella is a prevalent foodborne pathogen primarily transmitted through contaminated food and water sources (Zheng et al., 2026). It can cause acute gastroenteritis, septicemia and systemic infections in both humans and animals (Feng et al., 2023). This bacterium not only impairs growth performance and feed conversion rates in poultry farming but also leads to significant economic losses (Obe et al., 2023). The existence of over 2,600 distinct Salmonella serotypes poses challenges for vaccine development, as current vaccines offer limited cross-protection among different serotypes (Demirtas et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). Currently, the treatment of Salmonella heavily relies on antibiotic therapy. However, the widespread misuse and overuse of antibiotics have exacerbated the global issue of antimicrobial resistance (Giorgio et al., 2024). These severe challenges collectively underscore the critical need for developing novel infection control strategies and alternative therapeutics against Salmonella infection.

In recent years, antivirulence strategies have emerged as an alternative to traditional antibiotics in developing new anti-infective agents by targeting specific virulence factors to disarm pathogens and reduce selective pressure for resistance (Xu et al., 2025). Notably, in poultry production systems where antibiotic use is increasingly restricted, antivirulence interventions offer a rational and non-bactericidal approach for disease control, and may reduce the selective pressure that drives antimicrobial resistance. Among the virulence factors targeted by antivirulence strategies, the type III secretion system 1 (T3SS-1) has been widely recognized as a central virulence mechanism in Salmonella pathogenesis (Hotinger et al., 2021). T3SS-1 is a complex transmembrane multiprotein apparatus that enables the direct translocation of bacterial effector proteins into the host cell cytoplasm (Jia and Zhu, 2025). This system secretes effector proteins such as SipA, which modulate the host cytoskeleton, induce membrane ruffling and facilitate bacterial internalization. Disruption or deletion of T3SS-1 components results in markedly attenuated virulence, underscoring the indispensable role of T3SS-1 in Salmonella infection (He et al., 2024). These insights highlight T3SS-1 as a promising molecular target for antivirulence therapeutics (Miao et al., 2025). The development of effective T3SS-1 inhibitors represents an innovative approach for the control and management of Salmonella infections.

Houttuynia cordata Thunb. is a traditional medicinal herb with broad pharmacological activities. However, studies on the efficacy of Houttuynia cordata extract (HCE) against Salmonella infection remain limited. Most existing reports have mainly focused on its antiviral (Zhu et al., 2023) and anti-inflammatory effects (Wang et al., 2023). Several compounds, such as quercetin (Tsou et al., 2016) and hyperoside (Zhang et al., 2022), have been identified as inhibitors of T3SS-1. Nevertheless, current research has largely concentrated on purified single molecules. Importantly, most reported T3SS-1 inhibitors have been evaluated mainly in vitro, with limited validation in animal infection models. This has hindered their translational application (Lingling et al., 2015). Notably, evidence from economically relevant livestock models, such as poultry, remains particularly scarce.

Given the significant threat that Salmonella infections pose to poultry production, this study evaluated the efficacy of HCE against highly virulent Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella Pullorum. Both murine and chick infection models were employed. In parallel, we systematically explored the molecular mechanisms underlying HCE-mediated protection. Our findings provide compelling evidence for the anti-Salmonella potential of HCE. These results also lay a solid foundation for its translational application in livestock and poultry health management.

Materials and methods

Strains and agents

The Salmonella Typhimurium SL1344 (ST) strain (Luria-Bertani, LB medium; 40 µg/mL streptomycin) and its isogenic ΔinvA mutant strain were provided by Professor Liu Xiaoyun at Peking University. The invA gene encodes the T3SS-1 component protein InvA in Salmonella. Deletion of invA prevents assembly of the entire T3SS-1 apparatus, which results in substantially reduced bacterial invasion of host cells. The Salmonella Pullorum C79-13 (SP) strain (LB medium), ST-hilA-pJL03 strain (LB medium; 30 µg/mL kanamycin) and SP-hilA-pJL03 strain (LB medium; 30 µg/mL kanamycin) were constructed and preserved by the veterinary pharmacology laboratory at Jilin University. ST-hilA-pJL03 and SP-hilA-pJL03 strains were used in hilA overexpression experiments to determine whether the effect of HCE on T3SS-1 effector expression was mediated through HilA. The HilA and SipA polyclonal antibodies were prepared by the same laboratory. Salmonella was cultured in LB medium containing 0.4 M NaCl to induce T3SS-1 gene expression.

Caco-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The traditional Chinese medicine Houttuynia cordata Thunb. (storage No. CMD028) is stored in the veterinary pharmacology laboratory and sourced from Huaihua, China. Reference standards of rutin (CAS: 153-18-4), hyperoside (CAS: 482-36-0) and quercetin (CAS: 117-39-5) with a purity of > 98% were purchased from Chengdu Refens Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China) for high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis.

HPLC analysis of flavonoid components in HCE

To characterize the major flavonoid constituents of HCE, HPLC analysis was performed. Dried Houttuynia cordata Thunb. powder (10 g) was extracted with 100 mL of 50% (v/v) methanol under reflux for 90 min. The extract was filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure to obtain a crude residue, which was re-dissolved in 20% methanol and diluted to 10 mL. Prior to HPLC analysis, the solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. Reference standards of rutin, hyperoside and quercetin were prepared as stock solutions (1 mg/mL) in methanol and serially diluted to generate calibration curves.

HPLC separation was performed on an Acclaim R120 C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) using a Shimadzu system. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.05% phosphoric acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile) with gradient elution. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the injection volume was 5 μL, the detection wavelength was 260 nm, and the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting peak area (Y) against concentration (X), and linear regression was used to obtain the regression equations.

Growth curve and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

Growth curve and MIC assays were performed to determine whether HCE directly inhibited Salmonella growth, thereby distinguishing antibacterial activity from anti-virulence effects. Overnight bacterial cultures (OD600 = 0.3) were diluted 1:40 in LB medium containing HCE (0–150 µg/mL), with the 0 µg/mL HCE group used as the control, and incubated at 37 °C with shaking. Growth was monitored by measuring OD600 and growth curves were generated.

The MIC of HCE against Salmonella was determined according to the method described by Li et al. (2024), with appropriate modifications for the present study. Overnight bacterial cultures (OD600 = 0.3) were diluted 1:40. The diluted bacterial suspensions were added to 96-well plates containing LB medium, 0.01% resazurin, and HCE at final concentrations of 0–2048 μg/mL. The 0 µg/mL HCE group served as the control. Plates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 16–18 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest HCE concentration that prevented visible bacterial growth, as indicated by the absence of resazurin color change. Each condition was tested in triplicate and repeated independently three times.

Biofilm detection

To investigate the effect of HCE on a key virulence-related phenotype, biofilm formation was assessed (Thames et al., 2023). Overnight cultures were diluted to OD600 = 0.2 and inoculated into 96-well polystyrene plates containing HCE at final concentrations of 0–150 µg/mL. The plates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 18–24 h to allow biofilm formation. After incubation, the culture medium was discarded, and the wells were washed three times with PBS to remove planktonic cells. The attached cells were fixed with formaldehyde for 15 min and air-dried. Biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (100 μL/well) at room temperature for 30 min. Bound dye was dissolved in 200 μL of 33% acetic acid for 15 min, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

Cytotoxicity assay

Before assessing Salmonella invasion, it was important to confirm that HCE does not exert cytotoxic on host cells. Caco-2 cells (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC, USA) were cultured in DMEM and seeded into culture plates. When cell confluence reached approximately 80%, the cells were treated with different concentrations of HCE for 12 h, with 0 μg/mL HCE serving as the control. After treatment, CCK-8 solution (Biotime, C0039) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance was then measured at 450 nm. Cell viability was expressed relative to the control (100%) after subtracting blank values.

Salmonella adhesion and invasion assay in Caco-2 cells

Adhesion and invasion assays were performed to determine whether HCE reduced the ability of Salmonella to adhere to and invade intestinal epithelial cells. Salmonella strains were pretreated with different concentrations of HCE and used to infect Caco-2 cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for 50 min. For the adhesion assay, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria, lysed with 0.3% saponin, and plated for colony counting.

Intracellular invasion was evaluated using a gentamicin protection assay (Liu et al., 2024). After infection, Caco-2 cells were treated with 100 μg/mL gentamicin for 1 h to eliminate extracellular bacteria. Cells were then washed three times with PBS, lysed, and plated for enumeration of intracellular colony-forming units (CFU).

Bacterial invasion was further visualized by confocal microscopy. In the immunofluorescence assay for bacterial invasion, cells infected with the ΔinvA strain were included as a negative control. After washing with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 25 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min. The cells were blocked with 4% goat serum at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with a mouse anti-Salmonella antibody (ab69238, 1:1000, Abcam). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, A-21057) was used for detection, and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 3342 (MedChemExpress, HY1559). Fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal microscope (Olympus IX83).

Salmonella effector protein secretion and endogenous expression assays

To explore whether HCE affects Salmonella invasion through suppression of T3SS-1, we assessed both secreted and intracellular levels of key effector proteins (Shu et al., 2022). ST and SP strains were cultured in LB medium supplemented with 0.4 M NaCl to induce T3SS-1 expression. Overnight cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.2 and incubated with different concentrations of HCE (0–150 µg/mL) for 4 h. For secreted protein analysis, culture supernatants were collected, and proteins were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TCA), washed with cold acetone, and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Western blotting was then performed to detect SipA.

For intracellular protein analysis, bacterial pellets were lysed, and intracellular SipA expression was assessed by Western blot. The primary antibodies used were anti-SipA (1:1000), anti-HilA (1:2000), and anti-isocitrate dehydrogenase (ICDH, 1:3000), with ICDH serving as a loading control. The secondary antibody was HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:20,000; Beyotime). To further assess whether HilA mediated the effect of HCE, ST-hilA-pJL03 and SP-hilA-pJL03 were treated under the same conditions, and HilA and SipA expression levels were subsequently analyzed.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jilin University (Approval No. SY202504008).

Salmonella-infected mouse model

Sixty female BALB/c mice (20–22 g, Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were acclimated for 3 days and then received streptomycin water (5 g/L) for 3 days. Thirty mice were used for survival analysis and the remaining 30 mice were used for efficacy evaluation. The mice were randomly assigned to a control group, an ST-infected group and an ST-infected group treated with HCE (ST + HCE group). For survival analysis, mice in the ST-infected group and the ST + HCE group were orally challenged with a lethal dose of ST (1 × 107 CFU). HCE was administered orally at 100 mg/kg every 12 h for 4 consecutive days after infection. Mice in the control group received an equal volume of PBS on the same schedule.

In a separate cohort, mice were similarly assigned to three groups (n = 10 per group). Mice in the ST-infected group and the ST + HCE group were orally challenged with a sublethal dose of ST (5 × 106 CFU) to evaluate the efficacy. HCE was administered orally at 100 mg/kg body weight every 12 h for 4 consecutive days after infection. Mice in the control and ST-infected groups received an equal volume of PBS on the same schedule. At 7 days post-infection, liver, spleen, and intestine samples were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, or snap-frozen at −80 °C for further analysis. Blood samples were centrifuged to obtain serum. Serum levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Wuhan, China).

Salmonella-infected chick model

A total of 100 one-week-old female broiler chicks (45–55 g, Chunhui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changchun, China) were randomly assigned to two independent infection models, the ST infection model and the SP infection model, with 50 chicks in each model. Within each model, chicks were further assigned to five groups (n = 10 per group), including a control group, an infected group (either the ST-infected group or the SP-infected group), and three infected groups treated with HCE (50, 100, or 200 mg/kg).

Chicks were acclimatized for 7 days before infection. Chicks in the infected group and HCE treated groups were orally challenged once daily for 3 consecutive days from day 7 to day 10 (5 × 109 CFU of ST per chick or 1 × 109 CFU of SP per chick, depending on the infection model). HCE was administered orally every 12 h for 4 consecutive days from day 10 to day 14. Chicks in the control group and infected group received an equal volume of PBS according to the same dosing schedule. Chicks were maintained under normal feeding conditions from day 14 to day 21 and euthanized on day 21 for sample collection. Tissues were collected, fixed, or stored at −80 °C. Housing conditions included proper ventilation, hygiene, and a 23:1 light:dark cycle.

Growth performance

Growth performance was monitored to determine whether HCE improved the overall health and production status of infected chicks (Wang et al., 2025a). Body weight was measured on days 1, 7, 10, 14, and 21 of the experimental period. Daily feed intake was recorded for each chick, and average daily feed intake (ADFI) was expressed as the mean daily feed intake per chick over the entire experimental period. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated for each chick as the total feed consumed over the entire experimental period divided by the total body weight gain, reflecting overall feed efficiency from day 1 to day 21.

Determination of organ indices and bacterial loads

Organ indices and bacterial loads were measured to quantify organ injury and systemic bacterial colonization after infection. Liver and spleen were collected aseptically and weighed to calculate the organ index (organ weight/body weight, g/g). Tissues were homogenized in sterile PBS. For ST, serial tenfold dilutions were plated onto LB agar containing 40 µg/mL streptomycin and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For SP, serial dilutions were plated onto xylose lysine deoxycholate (XLD) agar without antibiotics, and colonies were identified by their smooth red morphology. Bacterial counts were expressed as CFU per gram of tissue (CFU/g).

Histopathological analysis

Histopathological examination was performed to assess tissue damage caused by Salmonella infection and its alleviation by HCE treatment. Tissues were collected aseptically, fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Histopathological changes were assessed by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining. The histopathological scoring method was adapted from Liu et al. (2024). Lesions in the liver, spleen and cecum were scored on a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 4 according to the severity of pathological changes. The scoring criteria included liver hemorrhage and damage, splenic enlargement and hemorrhage, and cecal inflammation and damage. The total pathology score for each animal (0–12) was calculated as the sum of the three organ scores.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

qPCR was performed to evaluate host inflammatory responses in intestinal tissue and Salmonella gene expression under in vitro conditions. Total RNA was extracted from intestinal tissue samples and in vitro cultured Salmonella using TRIzol reagent (Cat#15596018; Thermo Fisher), as described previously with minor modifications (Li et al., 2024). One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the RevertAid RT Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat#K1691; Thermo Fisher). qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green fluorescent dye (Cat#KTSM1401; AlpaLife, China) on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher). The primer sequences are listed in Table 2. ACTB was used as the internal reference gene for intestinal tissue samples, whereas DNA gyrase subunit B (gyrB) served as the reference gene for Salmonella samples. Each sample was run in at least three technical replicates. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.50 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, and one-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons. For survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier curves were generated, and differences between groups were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical significance was indicated as **** P < 0.0001; *** P < 0.001; ** P < 0.01; * P < 0.05; ns P > 0.05.

Results

HPLC quantification of major flavonoids in HCE

Calibration curves were established using rutin, hyperoside, and quercetin reference standards. The regression equation for rutin was Y = 36805X + 103.73 (r² = 1.0000), for hyperoside was Y = 40805X + 535.7 (r² = 0.9998) and for quercetin was Y = 56902X − 2629.6 (r² = 0.9981). Based on these calibration curves, the contents of rutin, hyperoside and quercetin in the HCE were determined to be 115 mg/kg, 416 mg/kg and 710 mg/kg, respectively. Representative chromatograms of the mixed standards and sample extract are shown in Fig. S1.

To simulate these events in vitro, we employed Caco-2 cells as a model of the intestinal epithelium. HCE exhibits in vitro antibacterial activity against Salmonella

To determine whether the inhibitory effect of HCE on invasion was attributable to direct antibacterial activity, we next assessed bacterial growth, MIC and biofilm formation in vitro. MIC assays revealed that the MIC values of HCE exceeded 2048 µg/mL for both ST (Fig. 2A) and SP (Fig. 2D), suggesting that the observed inhibition of invasion is not due to direct antibacterial activity. Consistently, bacterial growth curves showed that HCE at concentrations ranging from 0 to 150 µg/mL did not significantly affect the proliferation of either strain (Fig. 2B and 2E). HCE reduces the secretion and intracellular expression of Salmonella T3SS-1 effector proteins

The Salmonella T3SS-1 mediates effector translocation into host cells and plays a central role in epithelial cell invasion. Since HCE suppressed invasion without affecting bacterial growth, we next examined whether this effect was associated with inhibition of T3SS-1.

To examine this possibility, TCA precipitation assays were conducted to evaluate the secretion of T3SS-1 effector proteins into the culture supernatants of ST and SP strains. HCE inhibited the secretion of SipA and SipC in both ST (Fig. 3A) and SP (Fig. 3B) in a dose-dependent manner. Western blot analysis confirmed the suppression of SipA secretion (Fig. 3C–3E). Discussion

Salmonella serovars such as Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella Pullorum continue to pose significant threats to poultry health and public safety (Orimaye et al., 2024). As poultry production becomes increasingly intensive, the risk of Salmonella transmission and its associated public health burden continues to increase (Wang et al., 2025b). In light of the escalating crisis of antibiotic resistance, the development of novel anti-infective strategies and agents has become increasingly imperative.

Antivirulence strategies that target bacterial pathogenicity without affecting viability have gained momentum as promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics (Shi et al., 2025). In this context, T3SS-1, a major virulence determinant of Salmonella invasion, represents an ideal molecular target (Miao et al., 2025). Overall, our in vitro and in vivo findings indicate that HCE attenuates Salmonella virulence by suppressing the HilA regulated T3SS-1 pathway and provides consistent protection in both murine and chick infection models.

A key finding of the present study is that HCE attenuated Salmonella virulence at concentrations that did not measurably affect bacterial growth. This pattern indicates that HCE acts primarily through an antivirulence mechanism rather than through general bacteriostatic activity. HCE inhibited epithelial adhesion, invasion, and T3SS-1 effector protein expression. These effects closely parallel the suppression of the invasion program and support this as a major mechanism of action. Regulatory data further support this conclusion. HCE reduced the expression of several upstream regulators within the SPI-1 regulatory cascade, with the most pronounced effect observed for hilA, and also decreased HilA protein abundance. More importantly, hilA overexpression restored SipA production in the presence of HCE. Together, these findings indicate that suppression of the HilA-regulated T3SS-1 pathway is a central component of the antivirulence activity of HCE. Furthermore, HCE reduced Salmonella biofilm formation without affecting bacterial growth, further supporting the view that it preferentially interferes with Salmonella virulence-associated phenotypes rather than basal bacterial viability.

These in vivo findings were in line with the molecular effects observed in vitro. In both animal models, HCE treatment was associated with reduced organ colonization by Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella Pullorum, alleviated tissue injury, and attenuated inflammatory responses, suggesting that inhibition of the invasion process was accompanied by reduced tissue colonization and an overall decrease in infection severity. In chicks, the concurrent improvement in body weight, average daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio further suggests that the protective effect of HCE extended beyond pathological relief and translated into better physiological performance under infection conditions. These production-related benefits likely result from reduced intestinal colonization and concomitant improvements in intestinal function during infection, rather than from a direct growth-promoting effect.

From a translational application perspective, the chick model is particularly important (Lin et al., 2025). Previous studies of Salmonella T3SS-1 inhibitors have relied mainly on in vitro assays or murine models, whereas poultry represents the setting most directly relevant to practical disease control (Lv et al., 2021). The protective effects of HCE against both Salmonella Typhimurium and the poultry-adapted pathogen Salmonella Pullorum indicate that its antivirulence activity remains effective in a host context directly relevant to poultry production. HCE was also associated with improved growth performance and reduced infection burden, further supporting its practical value under infection conditions. These results are consistent with previous findings that traditional herbal extracts can enhance disease resistance and overall health in poultry by modulating immune responses and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity (Wen et al., 2024). The different dose responses observed between the two serotypes further suggest that in vivo responsiveness may vary in a serotype-dependent manner. Although HCE showed clear antivirulence activity, no significant differences were observed between the medium and high doses with respect to anti-inflammatory or barrier-protective effects. This finding suggests that increasing the dose beyond a certain range may not provide additional benefit. Future studies should therefore combine pharmacokinetic analyses of HCE constituents, formulation optimization, and field trials in commercial poultry systems to more precisely define an appropriate dosing regimen.

Although several purified natural compounds, such as cinnamomum bejolghota (Liu et al., 2020), tannic acid (Shu et al., 2022) and harmine (Shi et al., 2022a), have been reported as T3SS-1 inhibitors, their preparation requires complex extraction or synthesis. In contrast, HCE is a low-cost, plant-derived extract that contains multiple bioactive components, including quercetin (Tsou et al., 2016) and hyperoside (Zhang et al., 2022), both of which have been reported to inhibit Salmonella T3SS-1. HPLC analysis confirmed the presence of major flavonoids, including rutin, hyperoside and quercetin, although their concentrations were lower than those commonly applied for purified compounds in vitro. Notably, despite these low concentrations, the protective efficacy of HCE in the lethal mouse infection model was comparable to that reported for tannic acid (Shu et al., 2022), suggesting that the observed antivirulence effects likely result from the concerted action of multiple constituents. These components likely act in concert to mediate the antivirulence effects of HCE rather than through a single dominant compound. Further fractionation and component-specific validation will be required in future studies to elucidate the precise contributions of each constituent.

Conclusion

This study identifies Houttuynia cordata extract (HCE) as a promising antivirulence agent against Salmonella infection. By modulating the HilA-regulated T3SS-1 pathway, HCE effectively inhibited effector protein secretion and bacterial invasion without affecting bacterial viability. These mechanistic effects were further supported in both murine and chick infection models, highlighting the translational potential of HCE. Collectively, these findings indicate that HCE is a low-cost, plant-derived candidate for the development of alternative antivirulence strategies.
