Sustainable supply chain management using Bi-GRU network optimized by hybrid maritime search and rescue algorithm
Authors: Zhenjie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe agricultural and food supply chain faces various challenges that include waste reduction, traceability, and profit optimization, with one being dynamic disturbances such as the weather events and transportation delays. As traditional methods have constraints in addressing these challenges due to a lack of adaptability and scalability, this study proposes a hybrid framework to resolve the problem. The study combines the Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (Bi-GRU) networks and a Hybrid version of Maritime Search and Rescue (HMSR) for sustainable supply chain management. The production and storage prediction problem is handled using IoT sensors and market trends-based time series data by the Bi-GRU model, and the HMSR algorithm is used to optimize the decisions based on constraints, which can include production capacity, storage constraints, and market demand. Applied to the Global Food and Agriculture Statistics dataset on Kaggle and synthetic disruption scenarios, our framework reaches 92.4% accuracy in making storage decisions, reduces waste by 34.2% and maximizes profit margins at 28.7%, outperforming GRU, LSTM, GA, PSO, and HGSODL-ASCM baselines. Introduction

Humans need proper and valuable nutrition to survive and continue living. The agricultural industry is responsible for providing these vital substances in the first step, which includes growing various plants, vegetables, greens, and oilseeds1. Providing and making available agricultural products, given the vastness of the Earth and the increasing population, requires a precise, agile, and minimally risky supply chain2. For this reason, logistics and supply chain are considered very important in the agricultural industry3.

Agricultural and food supply chains (SCs) are an important part of the global food security4. However, it faces sustainability and profitability challenges. The main issues in this direction are:

High Waste Rates: Around 30% of perishable goods (like fruits and vegetables) are wasted because of inappropriate storage, ineffective logistics, and poor demand forecasting5.

Traceability, trade, and transparency: Fragmented stakeholder coordination and manual record-keeping create opaque supply chains that make it difficult to comply with safety regulations or consumer demand for transparency6.

Traceability, trade, and transparency: Fragmented stakeholder coordination and manual record-keeping create opaque supply chains that make it difficult to comply with safety regulations or consumer demand for transparency6.

Profit volatility: Chaotic market prices, varying demand, and operational hiccups (like energy costs associated with cold storage) compress profit margins for farmers and distributors7.

Profit volatility: Chaotic market prices, varying demand, and operational hiccups (like energy costs associated with cold storage) compress profit margins for farmers and distributors7.

In addition, these challenges are worsened due to the dynamic nature of SC environments, where disruptions (e.g., weather events or transportation delays) necessitate real-time adaptive decision-making.

These challenges can be addressed by emerging transformative technologies8.

Pioneered by blockchain technology, it facilitates immutable, decentralized record-keeping that maintains the integrity of the information blockchain provides, such as the journey of produce from farm to retailer, with transparency and traceability that offers anti-fraud capability and improved compliance with safety standards9.

On the other hand, smart contracts provide automated agreements, e.g., payment on delivery, that reduce administrative delays10. The IoT (Internet of Things) sensors enable real-time monitoring of environmental changes, such as humidity and temperature, in storage facilities and transport vehicles, and help to mitigate spoilage by issuing alerts and corrective actions.

However, past research has addressed blockchain for traceability, IoT for monitoring, and AI for forecasting, and three key gaps remain:

Lack of Unified Frameworks: Many existing studies concentrate on specific technologies (e.g., blockchain or GRU models) and do not build them into unified frameworks.

Static Decision-Making: Conventional AI models (e.g., LSTMs) and optimization algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms) do not sufficiently adapt to real-time disruptions in SC contexts.

Limited Scalability: Existing hybrid approaches are often limited to single stakeholders and cannot be scaled to cooperative, multi-stakeholder settings where conflicting objectives (e.g., farmer profits and retailer costs) complicate coordination.

Static Decision-Making: Traditional AI methods (e.g., LSTMs) and optimization methods (e.g., genetic algorithms) do not respond to real-time SC environmental perturbations.

Limited Scalability: Existing hybrid approaches demonstrate limited scalability in multi-stakeholder scenarios where different objectives are misaligned (for example, farmer profits vs. retailer costs), making coordination difficult.

In this research, AI is considered, and Bi-GRU networks (bidirectional gate recurrent units) are used to capture both temporal dependencies in time-series data, like previous yields and price variations, to forecast recommended production and storage strategies.

Furthermore, a modified metaheuristic algorithm, the HMSR (Hybrid Maritime Search and Rescue optimizer) algorithm, emulate adaptive searching patterns to address complex optimization challenges, including profit maximization within time-varying constraints.

The concern of this research relates to issues surrounding agriculture and food supply chains with significant waste, especially for perishables, which can be up to 30%.

Poor traceability, profitability that varies with each supply chain cycle, risks from dynamic disruption including weather disruption and delays in transportation, which benefits the eradication of sustainability and prudent economics.

Traditional supply chain management methods are still using a form of static models or independent technologies such as blockchain or basic AI technologies which are not flexible, scalable, or in real-time decision making, which are the core to be able to address this complex interconnected issues.

Previous work investigated individual technologies (e.g., LSTM networks for time series forecasting or the use of metaheuristic algorithms such as GA and PSO for optimization), but little attempts were made at integrating predictive modeling with adaptive constraint-aware optimization. Many studies analyze individual components of the supply chain, usually together with deep learning paradigms that are disconnected from dynamic decision framework for addressing real-time disruptions and multiple stakeholders constraints. These strategies can operate less than optimal under dynamic uncertainty, perishability conditions, and demand variability. This research introduces a hybrid framework that operates collaboratively by employing Bi-Directional Gated Recurrent Unit (Bi-GRU) for a context-rich temporal forecasting and Hybrid Maritime Search and Rescue (HMSR)-based real-time constraint optimization, which ultimately allows for accurate forecasting of production and storage requirements, but more importantly, allows for the ability to fine-tune decisions based on changing operating and environmental conditions and addresses vital gaps in scalability, adaptability, and responsiveness in sustainable agricultural supply chains.

Jianying et al.12 used a neural network for assessing the fresh grape supply chain’s risk. Initially, the supply chain’s potential factors of risk were recognized, and a risk assessment index system was offered. After that, risk assessment methods due to the optimized BP and single BP or PSO-BP and GABP neural networks were developed. Finally, the methods were tested, trained, and assessed through data from a survey of the supply chain. The outcomes revealed that the fresh grape supply chain’s risk was low. However, the risk of links was inconsistent. Actually, the greatest risk was the risk amid the chain’s links or R0, and the highest risk dimensions were social, cooperation, and economic risk. The majority of dangerous events occurred in the 2nd quadrant (high damage and low probability risks). The model’s testing and training outcomes revealed that the method outperformed the single BP neural network’s risk assessment within the sustainable supply chain of grapes, while the PSO-BP method was more suited, with fewer assessment mistakes and a higher R2. Also, the findings identified the risk elements, which contributed most to the grape sustainable supply chain’s general risk. The current study theoretically enriched supply chain risk assessment of the method and made practical recommendations for stable operation, avoidance of risk, and sustainability development in the supply chain of fresh grapes.

The current framework’s application had suggestions for improving organizational effectiveness through reducing waste, saving expenses, and creating a green culture amid employees. The study helped to build policies and gain a better understanding of the way to adopt green innovation strategies.

Xu et al.14 conducted a Hunger Games Search Optimization based on a Deep Learning Model for HGSODL-ASCM or management of food supply chain and Sustainable agricultural. The HGSODL-ASCM approach’s main focus was on enhancing decision-making procedures for food commodities and agricultural production for maximizing profits. The offered HGSODL-ASCM approach used a Bi-LSTM or bidirectional long short-term memory method for calculating the quantity of needed storage and productivity in order to enhance income. The current work applied the HGSO algorithm to improve the Bi-LSTM categorization procedure’s enactment. Actually, the HGSODL-ASCM approach could establish SCM policies by interacting with intricate and adaptable settings. The simulations were carried out to guarantee that the HGSODL-ASCM approach performed well. The outcomes showed the better enactment of the HGSODL-ASCM technique compared to other approaches.

Yuan and Wang15 addressed the inventory management and resource distribution difficulties within systems of supply chain through developing a supply chain optimization method due to the ISCO-DQ or Deep Q-Networks that prioritized eco-effectiveness. First, the project created a supply chain method, which included supplier-customer connections that were led by means of green computing concepts to reduce environmental effects. The model implemented Markov Decision Processes in order to create a structure for maintainable supplier inventory management, with a focus on waste decrease and resource implementation. Actually, the method prioritized energy-efficient inventory management processes while achieving intelligent resource allocation using Deep Q-Networks’ function approximation abilities. The ISCO-DQ inventory managing method, in turn, converged to − 181,300 and − 41,400 after 300 and 100 cycles with normal client request distributions. Compared to common single-period random and fixed-order amount inventory managing approaches, the ISCO-DQ method decreased overall prices by mans of an average of 6.7% and 16.6% through decreasing carbon emissions from excess inventory and overproduction. Additionally, the ISCO-DQ method significantly alleviated costs from ambiguity in requests by responding quickly to volatility, as well as enhancing inventory approaches by assisting in circular economy. It also emphasized how the ISCO-DQ inventory management method effectively addresses the rigidity and inefficiencies inherent in traditional supply chain management, ultimately promoting organizational sustainability and environmental stewardship.

The application of these models to dynamic, real-world settings, such as agriculture and food systems, where perishability, demand variability, and multi-stakeholder coordination can complicate decision-making, remains challenging.

Materials and methods

This paper introduces a new hybrid method involving a hybrid Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit and Hybrid Maritime Search and Rescue (HMSR) metaheuristic algorithm as an effective framework for agricultural and food supply chain management (SCM). It seeks to exploit the nature of these environments to make the best possible decisions with respect to production and storage while maximizing profit in an environmentally sustainable and resilient fashion. The Bi-GRU model leverages learning of temporal dependencies from time-series data, such as logs from sensors, market trends and temporal conditions, to offer predictive input on optimal productivity and storage levels. The HMSR act as a metaheuristic optimization method that refines these predictions by optimally arranging the learning and predictions from the Bi-GRU to include temporal constraints and assessments of actual experiences. The previous section highlighted both components, clarified their interplay, and justified those design approaches.

Bi-GRU model: predicting productivity and storage needs

In this study, the Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (Bi-GRU) serves as the predictive engine that accurately predicts production amounts and storage needs within agricultural and food supply chains from temporal dependencies within multi-source time-series data.

By integrating sequences in both the forward and reverse context, the Bi-GRU can derive a more complete historical context, including historical yields and weather, historical changes in market prices, and capture a forward context such as demand forecast and anticipated disruptions which can enhance adaptive decision-making.

The Bi-GRU merges information from IoT sensors and market trends alongside operational logs that we have pre-processed and input into the model to create dynamic decision-making predictions for production () and storage (). The outputs from the model are processed inputs, and are not stand-alone decision variables, as they serve as the input into the Hybrid Marine Search and Rescue (HMSR) optimization algorithm which revised the model outputs against real-world constraints such as capacity limits, demand uncertainties, and environmental conditions.

Thus, the Bi-GRU serves as the backbone in providing context-based, highly accurate forecasts, enabling proactive, data-driven, and resilient supply chain management of the hybrid framework to maximize profit, cut back on waste, and realign in real-time against dynamic disruptions.

In the following, different parts of the Bi-GRU model for productivity and storage prediction have been explained in detail.

Bidirectional processing for capturing Temporal dependencies

Bi-GRU is a type of RNN that uses bidirectional processing to capture both the past and future context of the sequences. Conventional unidirectional RNNs can only process sequences in one direction (e.g., left to right), which means they cannot consider future dependencies. Bi-GRUs, on the other hand, read sequences in both forward and backward directions; they therefore capture richer representations of temporal patterns. Input data and feature engineering

Data of series from several sources is entered into the Bi-GRU model to account for place-monitoring environmental state (e.g., temperature, humidity, and soil moisture level) via Internet of Things (IoT) sensors and trade data, which consists of past and future prices, interest demand, and competitors’ actions, and operational data, including production capacity, transport schedule, and stock limits. Therefore, these inputs are pre-processed during their passage to the model itself. For example, by imputing missing values via interpolation methods16, normalizing features17 to avoid models becoming biased towards any variable that has a larger scale than others, and dividing time-series into fixed-size windows (look-back steps) for the model to learn through time. Parameters of the Bi-GRU model

The Bi-GRU model has dependencies to different hyperparameters, each of which provides an important effect on the trade-off between accuracy, complexity, and computational efficiency. The look-back steps (L) determine how many time steps back you want to include when you make predictions- a larger look-back will yield more context, but also be more expensive to train. For example, L = 30 would make sense when predicting monthly yield, whereas L = 7 would be sufficient for weekly storage adjustments. Neurons in a layer increase representation power but can also lead to overfitting if too high, with common maximum values of 64, 128, or 256 neurons per layer. Dropout rate (D) avoids overfitting by randomly dropping neurons during training, with common values in the range [0.2, 0.5].

Output: profit and storage decisions

The Bi-GRU model gives two outputs, one for predicted production levels () for when to harvest the crops based on historical data and environmental conditions, and storage requirements (), to find out the storage space needed to minimize spoilage and maximize revenue. These predictions are inputs used by the HMSR algorithm to dynamically make optimal decisions. As its name implies, the Bi-GRU model combines innovations in recurrent neural networks (RNNs) known as GRUs, with a bidirectional learning process that enables the predictive model to leverage both past and future data when determining outcomes or making predictions.

When the model predicts excess produce (P_t > Demand_t), the HMSR algorithm determines how the excess inventory would be distributed across the storage facilities with the lowest cost.

The primary benefit of using Bi-GRU over other models is the intelligent and efficient combination of bidirectionality and computational power, the flexibility of adaptive decision-making during interruptions, and the ability to easily couple with an optimization algorithm like GA and PSO in running simulations. For agricultural supply chains, an optimal set of key features-waste management, profitability, and sustainability enables decision making to be precise, situationally aware, and resilient in decision making. Empirical evidence demonstrated that none of the other models in the comparison set (GRU, LSTM, GA, PSO, HGSODL-ASCM) displayed a comprehensive performance based on accuracy, profitability, and operational efficiency. It follows that the Bi-GRU/HMSR combination will be a disrupter in the management of sustainable supply chains in the future.

The preprocessing pipeline was comprehensive in developing the time series data used in the Bi-GRU/HMSR framework to assure that accurate predictions and robust predictions could be made from high-quality performance inputs. As earlier described in Sect. Input data and feature engineering, the preprocessing included missing data imputation, where interpolation methods were used to infer missing data values from surrounding data points so as not to obliterate temporal continuity.

There was also normalization of features so that all input features would have to scale to the range [0, 1], as this would prevent the model from becoming biased toward variables of greater magnitudes and would hasten convergence during training. In addition, the data were further segmented into fixed-size time windows (based on look-back steps) for the Bi-GRU model to learn sequentially.

Such preprocessing was performed on those heterogeneous data sources, like IoT sensor readings (e.g., temperature, humidity), market trends (e.g., prices, demand forecasts), and operational logs (e.g., production capacity, transport schedules), so that the model would learn effectively from heterogeneous real-world agricultural supply chains.

Hybrid maritime search and rescue algorithm

This study’s Hybrid Maritime Search and Rescue (HMSR) algorithm will serve as a metaheuristic optimization engine, dynamically optimizing production and storage decisions while refining and enhancing the predictive outputs of the Bi-GRU model against real-world constraints such as production capacities, market demand variability, and environmental disruptions.

HMSR was inspired by search and rescue operations on water and combines the global exploration capabilities of the MSRA (Maritime Search and Rescue Algorithm) with the local exploitation strength of Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to avoid local optima and accelerate convergence toward global optima.

It fine-tunes critical hyperparameters of the Bi-GRU model (look-back steps, number of neurons, and dropout rate) for the dual purpose of balancing accuracy and computational efficiency while also reallocating resources in response to synthetic disruptions like transportation delays or weather impacts.

The integrated adaptive optimization mechanisms of the HMSR enable the hybrid framework to maximize profit margins (28.7%); minimize wastage (34.2%); and maintain resilience in dynamic and multi-stakeholder agricultural supply chain environments.

To eliminate these limitations, the MSRO method is merged with the PSO (Particle Swarm Optimization) algorithm. Despite the promising outcomes of the MSRO optimizer, it does have its limitations, which include a proneness to sticking in local optima and a slower speed of convergence. These limitations become more important when the problem set is high dimensional.

This combination provides various benefits as compared to adopting each approach alone. Actually, by expanding the search space investigation, this combination helps MSRO avoid becoming trapped in less-than-ideal solutions.

PSO establishes a balance between exploration and exploitation in the solution space to accelerate the convergence of MSRO. Results and analysis

The Bi-GRU model is trained through the use of MSE loss and the Adam optimizer, allowing a reliable and stable learning implementation with multi-source time-series input data. The three most critical hyperparameters: look-back (L = 30), neurons (N = 128), and dropout rate (D = 0.3) capture the parameters that were chosen to reflect time dependency while avoiding overfitting and keeping computational efficiency intact.

In this part, we will evaluate the proposed Bi-GRU/HMSR framework with five baseline models based on the state-of-the-art models, which are GRU18, LSTM19, GA (Genetic Algorithm)20, PSO (Particle Swarm Optimization)21, and HGSODL-ASCM sustainable supply chain management (HGSODL-ASCM)22. Most interestingly, they are not fixed and adaptively modified by the HMSR algorithm, enabling the hybrid framework to acquire performance results of 92.4% accuracy, 34.2% waste reduction, and 28.7% profit margin against GRU, LSTM, GA, PSO, and HGSODL-ASCM baselines. This brings forth the integration of deep learning as a dynamic element in metaheuristic optimization and contributed synergistically to the resilience, extensibility, and suitability of the model for sustainable agricultural supply chain management in practice. The innovation baseline models were based on the same preprocessed dataset from Global Food and Agriculture Statistics information on Kaggle that contained time-series data on crop production, price in market commodity, and environmental variables that were normalized in the range of [0,1] followed by fixed-size segment windows with a look-back of 30 steps. GRU and LSTM models were trained with the Adam optimizer and with a learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 32, and 150 epochs, having 128 hidden units and a dropout rate of 0.3 to avoid overfitting. HGSODL-ASCM model, as one of the hybrid benchmarks, is based on the Bi-LSTM architecture but incorporates Hunger Games Search Optimization (HGSO) for hyperparameter tuning, in which its input and objective function are similar to the proposed framework. Metaheuristic baselines used a population size of 100 and a maximum of 200 iterations for both GA and PSO, where GA had a crossover rate of 0.8 and a mutation rate of 0.1, while the PSO setting had an inertia weight of 0.9 and acceleration coefficients of 1.5. All models are under these conditions, including synthetic disruption scenarios to ensure fair metrics inaccuracy, profit margin, waste reduction, and computation efficiency.

We target the analysis to three key areas: accuracy, profit margin, computational efficiency, traceability and waste reduction, and resilience to disruptions. Findings were validated against Kaggle’s Global Food & Agriculture Statistics dataset and synthetic disruption scenarios.

Statistical significance testing: one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tukey’s HSD

To appropriately measure the statistical significance of the differences between the performance of the new Bi-GRU/HMSR hybrid model versus the baseline models, we used a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to measure if the mean accuracy measure differences from multiple experimental runs across all six models would be considered significant.

This method was chosen specifically to assess means in the various independent groups, thereby testing the null hypothesis that there are no differences in predictive accuracy due to model architectures. Figure 11 shows the ANOVA test.

The ANOVA test.

The ANOVA was run with model type as the independent variable (which had six levels: Bi-GRU/HMSR and five baselines) and prediction accuracy as the dependent variable, all built upon an observational dataset of 36 (6 models×6 replicates to withstand variability). The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were validated using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively, thereby confirming that the data maintain parametric assumptions (p > 0.05 for both). Figure 12 shows the Tukey’s HSD post-hoc comparisons.

Tukey’s HSD post-hoc comparisons.

The direct ANOVA, whose results of utmost significance showed a model type main effect on prediction accuracy, yielded F(5, 30) = 48.73, p < 0.001, with an associated large effect size (η² = 0.89), so that differences in model design, rather than random error, is primarily determining variation in accuracy scores. This leads to a rejection of the null hypothesis, corroborating the conclusion that at least one of the models is better performing than the other models. Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was then applied in the context of a family-wise error rate to identify pairwise differences that might exist. The post-hoc results indicated that the Bi-GRU/HMSR model was superior to all baseline models at a very high level of stringency (p < 0.01), and indicated significant differences against HGSODL-ASCM (p = 0.008), Bi-LSTM (p = 0.002), and GRU (p = 0.005). This supports the claim of nonrandom improvements.

In addition to this statistical support, it adds empirical further evidences for the effectiveness of the Bi-GRU/HMSR framework, and the combined benefits of the Bi-GRU mechanism for improving temporal dependency modeling and HMSR model for improved feature extraction. By showing real, and meaningful, improvements in predictive performance that cannot be accounted for by mere random sampling fluctuations, this analysis enhances the empirical rigor of the study, and presents opportunities for many more extensions in the future: sensitivity analysis, and applications to closely-related domains like time-series analysis in healthcare or finance. It may be of reference to consider including other metrics like Cohen’s d for effect sizes in their pairwise comparisons to help better contextualize the practical meaningfulness of these improvements as well.

Conclusions

Agricultural and food supply chains are fundamental to the sustainability of the global food system. Unfortunately, these supply chains deal with challenges of excessive waste, limited traceability, and limited profits, which are further exacerbated shocks including weather shocks and delays in transport. These changing complexities create dynamic characteristics, which outflank the traditional methods of supply chain management (SCM) that are primarily developed under static models or using isolated technologies. To address both issues outlined above, this study presented a Hybrid version of the Maritime Search and Rescue (HMSR) algorithm to the system and a Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (Bi-GRU) networks, respectively, to improve decision-making elements concerning the production, storage and profit maximization in the food supply chain. The Bi-GRU was used to build predictions about future crop yields and storage needs based on its ability to learn bidirectional temporal relationships from the historical data. The HMSR algorithm had a dynamic approach that provided it the ability to optimize decisions effectively in real world constraints, such as capacity and demand changes. The framework is tested on Kaggle’s Global Food and Agriculture Statistics dataset, and in synthetic disruption scenarios, where it demonstrates state-of-the-art performance with 92.4% accuracy in storage decisions, a 34.2% reduction in waste, and a maximization of profit margins of 28.7%, compared to state-of-the-art baselines (GRU, LSTM, GA, PSO and HGSODL-ASCM). Hyperparameters with significant impact like look-back steps and dropout rate play a key role in finding the balance between performance and efficiency as evident from the sensitivity analysis. Using the sensor data, the proposed method achieved transparency, reduces spoilage, and improves disruptions robustness. These results highlighted the transformative capabilities of AI-enhanced hybrid strategies in realizing sustainable and resilient agricultural SCM. While Bi-GRU/HMSR framework produced promising results, it already has some drawbacks. Foremost, even though it achieved considerable accuracy and profitability, the computational cost (420 s) required to train the model makes it unfit for use in low-resources or real-time embedded systems, especially for small-scale farms. Second, IoT sensors contain a blockchain mechanism for traceability and real-time monitoring, however this will pose challenges in terms of high infrastructure costs, energy consumption, data privacy, and interoperability across heterogeneous platforms, thus stalling adoption in developing regions. Third, evaluation is based on synthetic disruption scenarios (e.g., weather shocks, transportation delays, etc.), and while informative, it may not encompass all of the complexities and unpredictability of real events, and as such have potential implications for generalizability of the model. Future research will address this limitation through exploring model compression methods (e.g. pruning, quantization) to reduce their computing demands. Following that, lightweight versions suitable for the edge-computing framework will be developed and ultimately, we will assess the system through real-world pilot implementations with agricultural cooperatives. In addition to that, federated learning and permissioned blockchain paradigms will improve data privacy and scalability to maintain that framework efficient, efficient, and strong enough to operate in diverse operational settings.
