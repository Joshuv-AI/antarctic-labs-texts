One-carbon metabolism in cancer: moonlighting functions of metabolic enzymes and anti-tumor therapy
Authors: Xuedi Cao, Yunyun Guo, Zhengyang Guo, Yang Liu, Yali Dou, Lixiang Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
One-carbon (1C) metabolism is a central metabolic network that supports nucleotide biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and redox homeostasis, playing a critical role in cellular proliferation. In cancer, dysregulation of this pathway fuels tumor progression by satisfying the heightened biosynthetic and energetic demands of malignant cells. Beyond their canonical metabolic roles, metabolites and enzymes within the one-carbon metabolic network exhibit moonlighting functions that contribute to tumorigenesis, immune evasion, and metastasis. These non-canonical functions include modulation of gene expression, epigenetic remodeling, and rewiring the oncogenic signaling pathways, underscoring their multifaceted roles in cancer biology. This review explores the production and utilization of one-carbon units, with a focus on the metabolic enzymes involved and their moonlighting functions in tumorigenesis. Additionally, we summarize the progress of ongoing clinical trials targeting the 1C network, highlighting the potential to exploit metabolic vulnerabilities for cancer therapy.

Introduction

One-carbon (1C) metabolism is crucial for generating and transferring one-carbon units, such as methyl groups, to support important cellular processes including nucleotide synthesis, amino acid metabolism, and redox homeostasis [1]. The one-carbon metabolic network integrates several interconnected metabolic cycles, including the folate cycle, methionine cycle, and trans-sulfuration pathway, to generate and transfer one-carbon units from serine, glycine, and methionine to biomolecules [2, 3]. Most common 1 C units within organisms include methyl (-CH₃), methylene (-CH₂-), methine (= CH-), hydroxymethyl (-CH₂OH), formyl (-CHO), and iminomethyl (-CH = NH) groups [4]. In addition to the regulation of 1 C metabolism, emerging evidence shows that enzymes in the one-carbon metabolic network also have important non-canonical functions, commonly referred to as ‘‘moonlighting’’ functions [5]. These moonlighting activities encompass non-metabolic functions distinct from their canonical catalytic roles, often arising from dynamic changes in post-translational modifications, ligand binding, redox state, or oligomerization that induce conformational or subcellular localization shifts. Through these mechanisms, such enzymes engage in diverse processes such as transcriptional regulation, chromatin modifications, and DNA damage response [6]. These moonlighting functions directly integrate metabolic status with gene expression and signaling pathways, thereby equipping cancer cells with remarkable plasticity. Such plasticity allows them to dynamically navigate challenges including metabolic stress, nutrient deprivation, and therapeutic pressure. Consequently, these non-canonical activities of 1 C metabolism enzymes contribute to multiple stages of tumorigenesis, underscoring that 1 C enzymes act not only as metabolic effectors but also as key regulatory nodes in cancer progression.

In this review, we provide an overview of 1 C metabolites and enzymes with a focus on their moonlighting functions in cancer. We will discuss the moonlighting functions in regulation of the transcription and epigenetic landscapes and modulation of important biological processes that facilitate or hinder tumorigenesis. We will also discuss ongoing clinical trials that exploit metabolic vulnerabilities in the 1 C network and explore nutrient modulation as a strategy for cancer treatment.

One-carbon metabolism in cancer

Tumor cells exhibit a heightened demand for 1 C units and other metabolic intermediates to sustain rapid growth and proliferation. Limited availability of 1C units can significantly impact cellular methylation reactions, thereby reshaping the epigenetic landscape of tumor cells. By contrast, cancer cells can modulate folate and other metabolite levels, reshaping the tumor microenvironment to promote immune evasion and enhance survival. These metabolic reprogramming events underscore the intricate relationship between 1 C metabolism and tumor biology, offering valuable insights into novel therapeutic targets for cancer treatment [7, 8]. Generation of 1 C units

One-carbon units are generated through multiple interconnected metabolic pathways, including serine and glycine metabolism, the glycine cleavage system (GCS), and the catabolism of histidine and tryptophan (Fig. 1). The non-essential amino acids serine and glycine are major contributors of one-carbon units. Serine availability is derived from exogenous dietary uptake and endogenous synthesis via the serine synthesis pathway (SSP), which depends on glycolytic intermediates. Serine is subsequently catabolized to glycine and a 1C unit attached to tetrahydrofolate (THF) by the action of serine hydroxymethyltransferase 1 and 2 (SHMT1/2) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, respectively [69]. Glycine is also a source of 1 C units through the GCS. This system facilitates the oxidative cleavage of glycine, resulting in the production of CO₂, NH₃, and a transferable one-carbon unit in the form of 5,10-methylene-THF, which participates in the folate cycle [70]. Both PHGDH and SHMT1/2 are rate-limiting enzymes in their respective pathways and play crucial roles in tumorigenesis. Specifically, upregulation of PHGDH activity through PHGDH amplification or overexpression is reported in various cancer cells [71] and is closely associated with tumor growth, metastasis, chemoresistance, and poor patient survival [72]. As a result, inhibiting PHGDH, via RNA interference or pharmacological approaches, is able to hinder the proliferation of cancer cells [14, 73, 74]. Similarly, SHMT1 and SHMT2 have been considered potential therapeutic targets in many cancers [17, 75, 76]. In addition to serine and glycine, histidine and tryptophan also participate in one-carbon metabolism (Fig. 1). Histidine degradation generates 5-formimino-THF, whereas tryptophan is a precursor of formyl-kynurenine, which releases formate during its conversion to kynurenine. Both 5-formimino-THF and formate enter the folate cycle [8, 77, 78]. Enzymes catalyzing tryptophan degradation, including indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) and tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), are associated with modulation of the tumor microenvironment and facilitation of immune evasion [79]. Upregulation of IDO1 facilitates the proliferation and immunomodulatory activity of Tregs and MDSCs, while reducing tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. This immunosuppressive shift ultimately promotes tumor progression [80]. Clinical development of IDO1 inhibitors such as Epacadostat and Navoximod has demonstrated promising efficacy in treating solid tumors and hematological malignancies [81].

Transfer of 1 C units

The transfer of 1 C units is primarily carried out in metabolic processes such as the folate cycle, methionine cycle, and the transsulfuration pathway [82]. Dietary folate (vitamin B9) undergoes initial conversion to dihydrofolate (DHF), which dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) subsequently reduces to tetrahydrofolate (THF), the active carrier of 1 C units in the folate cycle [7]. The folate cycle is carried out in both the mitochondria and the cytoplasm, each compartment containing distinct but parallel enzymatic systems that interconvert THF derivatives such as 5,10-methylene-THF, 5,10-methenyl-THF, and 10-formyl-THF to support nucleotide and methyl group biosynthesis. In the cytosol, the trifunctional enzyme MTHFD1 catalyzes dehydrogenase, cyclohydrolase, and formyltetrahydrofolate synthetase reactions to interconvert these THF intermediates and formate, whereas in mitochondria,these interconversions are carried out by MTHFD2/MTHFD2L (dehydrogenase/cyclohydrolase) and MTHFD1L (formyltetrahydrofolate synthetase), respectively (Fig. 1). This compartmental specialization underpins the coordinated metabolic flow between mitochondrial formate production and cytosolic 1 C utilization. Formate is a key product of mitochondrial 1 C metabolism. In normal cells, folate metabolism often exceeds the demand for 1 C units, resulting in excess formate that is excreted. Cancer cells display even higher formate overflow. Accumulating evidence indicates that formate itself actively benefits cancer cells, promoting purine biosynthesis and energy metabolism, while its depletion reduces AICAR levels and AMPK activation. Circulating formate varies among tumor types. Inhibition of MTHFD1L or MTHFD2 reduces formate production, whereas ALDH1L2 loss redirects 10-formyl-THF toward formate. Formate flux is elevated in “oxidative cancers” [83], correlating with increased NAD⁺/NADH ratios. In cancer, these anabolic reactions are reprogrammed to ensure a continuous nucleotide supply required for DNA replication and rapid cell proliferation. Consequently, key enzymes in these pathways, such as SHMT and MTHFD, are frequently overexpressed in various cancers, driving tumor progression and often correlating with poor prognosis.

Whether and how folic acid regulates tumorigenesis remains ongoing debate. Epidemiological studies suggest that low folic acid levels are linked to higher cancer risk. A meta-analysis of 83 studies has associated low serum folate with increased cancer risk [84, 85]. Folic acid deficiency leads to genomic instability, DNA hypomethylation, alteration of gene expression for both oncogenes and tumor suppressors [86]. Interestingly, folic acid supplementation enhances anti-PD-1 therapy by promoting CD8+ T cell function [87]. However, folic acid supplementation in prostate cancer leads to 24% increase of risk in a meta-analysis of 10 clinical trials [88]. It has been proposed that timing of folic acid supplementation may be critical. It may prevent tumor formation before precancerous lesions but could accelerate cancer growth afterward by providing DNA precursors supporting insatiable proliferation [89, 90].

The methionine cycle is another key component of 1 C metabolism. There are two outputs from the methionine cycle, homocysteine(Hcy) and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) (Fig. 1). Hcy can enter the transsulfuration process that transforms it into cysteine, It can also accept one 1 C unit from 5-methyl-THF, produced from 5,10-methylene-THF in the folate cycle by MTHFR, and is converted to methionine by vitamin B12-dependent methionine synthase (MS). Methionine is subsequently converted to SAM by methionine adenosyltransferase (MAT) in an ATP-dependent process [91, 92]. Glycine N-methyltransferase (GNMT) transfers a methyl group from SAM to glycine, producing sarcosine and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). This methylation reaction serves as a metabolic sink for excess SAM [93]. SAM is a substrate for many chromatin modification enzymes, capable of donating one methyl-group for protein and DNA methylation [94]. A case–control study involving 216 pairs of colorectal adenoma patients and healthy controls has shown that elevated SAH level, a product of methylation reaction, is significantly associated with increased risk of tumor development [95]. Changes in global DNA methylation are commonly observed in various cancer types. Global DNA hypomethylation contributes to tumorigenesis through mechanisms including higher mutation rates, loss of chromosome heterozygosity, and aberrant oncogene activation [96–98]. Furthermore, aberrantly high DNA methylation as a result of elevated SAM production due to increases of 1 C intermediates from glucose and glutamine metabolism is reported in liver kinase B1 (LKB1)-deficient tumors, making them vulnerable to DNA hypomethylation agents [99, 100].

Although the transsulfuration pathway is not a direct participant in 1 C transfer, it functions as a critical sink for clearing surplus sulfur and 1 C units (Fig. 1).This pathway is intricately linked to the methionine cycle through sulfur-containing Hcy, a key metabolic branch point whose flux is regulated by SAM. Under conditions of SAM scarcity, Hcy is preferentially remethylated to regenerate methionine, thereby conserving methyl groups within the 1 C cycle. Conversely, when SAM levels are elevated, reflecting sufficient methylation potential, SAM allosterically activates cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), the rate-limiting enzyme of the transsulfuration pathway, while concurrently inhibiting MTHFR. This dual regulation redirects Hcy flux away from remethylation toward cysteine and glutathione biosynthesis, enhancing the cell’s antioxidant capacity [101, 102]. The transsulfuration pathway functions as a metabolic “overflow valve,” coordinating 1 C unit utilization with redox homeostasis. It ensures efficient methylation and nucleotide synthesis when needed, while channeling excess sulfur toward protective antioxidant defense. This cytoprotective function is frequently exploited by cancer cells to establish a robust antioxidant defense that sustains their survival and malignant progression. Due to inherently high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) arising from rapid proliferation and metabolic dysregulation, tumors often upregulate the transsulfuration pathway. This metabolic rewiring drives excessive glutathione production, which neutralizes oxidative stress and prevents ROS-induced cell death [103]. By strengthening their antioxidant capacity through this pathway, cancer cells not only gain resilience against intrinsic metabolic stress but also acquire resistance to therapeutic challenges, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Moreover, this adaptive mechanism promotes survival under nutrient-deprived and hypoxic conditions, thereby facilitating tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [104–106].

Utilization of 1 C units

The 1 C units generated through folate-mediated reactions are essential for multiple biosynthetic processes. Within the cytosolic folate cycle, 5,10-methylene-THF serves as the one-carbon donor for the conversion of deoxyuridine monophosphate (dUMP) to deoxythymidine monophosphate (dTMP), a reaction catalyzed by thymidylate synthase (TYMS) that is critical for pyrimidine synthesis. Similarly, 10-formyl-THF provides formyl groups for de novo purine biosynthesis, a process facilitated by GAR transformylase (GARFT) and AICAR transformylase (AICARFT) [3, 4, 107]. In parallel with nucleotide synthesis, 5-methyl-THF serves as a methyl donor in the methionine cycle, regenerating methionine from Hcy and sustaining SAM-dependent methylation reactions [108].

Beyond these cytosolic and nuclear roles, mitochondrial 1 C metabolism uniquely supports formylation of methionyl-tRNA (formyl-Met-tRNA),which is essential for initiating mitochondrial translation. Within mitochondria, 10-formyl-THF serves as the formyl donor for the modification of mitochondrial tRNA-Met, and its generation depends on SHMT2 and MTHFD2/2L [109] (Fig. 1). The enzyme mitochondrial methionyl-tRNA formyltransferase (MTFMT) utilizes 10-formyl THF to produce formyl-Met-tRNAs. This formylation enables the initiation of translation on mitochondrial ribosomes, thereby maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis and respiratory function [110]. Loss functions in MTFMT disrupt this process, leading to impaired mitochondrial translation and defective oxidative phosphorylation [111]. Moreover, serine availability and autophagy-derived metabolites dynamically modulate this pathway, linking nutrient status and 1 C metabolism to mitochondrial translational efficiency [112]. Collectively, these findings highlight that the utilization of 1 C units extends beyond classical anabolic pathways to include a pivotal role in sustaining mitochondrial translation and energy metabolism, thereby integrating nutrient sensing with cellular bioenergetic homeostasis.

Moonlighting functions of 1 C enzymes in cancer

In cancer, a new concept emphasizes that metabolic enzymes have non-traditional roles beyond producing metabolic intermediates, influencing cancer onset, development, and resistance to treatment via unique mechanisms [6]. Notably, growing evidence indicates that enzymes involved in 1 C metabolism exhibit a remarkable diversity of moonlighting activities, including the regulation of gene transcription, modulation of post-translational modifications, alteration of mitochondrial activity, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. These discoveries have significantly reshaped our understanding of 1 C metabolism, reframing it from a conventional biosynthetic route into a dynamic regulatory nexus intimately linked to tumor development.

PHGDH

PHGDH, the rate-limiting enzyme in the SSP, has emerged as a multifunctional regulator with unexpected non-catalytic roles in tumorigenesis. Notably, these non-catalytic functions are closely associated with its subcellular localization, suggesting that PHGDH may exert distinct regulatory effects depending on its spatial distribution within the cells (Fig. 2). Specifically, nuclear PHGDH has been shown to perform transcriptional regulatory functions across various tumor types. In glioblastoma, PHGDH binds to and stabilizes the transcription factor FOXM1, sustaining the expression of downstream targets (e.g., VEGF, CCND1, CHK2, and MMP-2) to drive glioma cell proliferation, invasion, and tumorigenicity [113] (Fig. 2A). Similarly, in liver cancer, the ACT domain of PHGDH directly interacts with c-MYC, forming a transcriptional activation complex with p300 and AF9 to upregulate chemokines (CXCL1, IL8) (Fig. 2B). These chemokines recruit neutrophils and tumor-associated macrophages, reshaping the immune microenvironment to promote tumor progression [114]. Metabolic stress further regulates the nuclear functions of PHGDH. Under glucose restriction, p38-dependent phosphorylation at Ser371 promotes its nuclear translocation, where PHGDH moonlights as a metabolic sensor and transcriptional regulator (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, phosphorylation of PHGDH at Ser55 induces a conformational shift that augments its alternative catalytic activity of malate oxidation. This modification enables PHGDH to sense energy stress and elevate nuclear NADH levels while depleting NAD⁺, thereby inhibiting PARP1 activity. Beyond its nuclear functions, cytoplasmic PHGDH acts as a molecular scaffold, that modulates mRNA translation, signaling, and metabolic reprogramming. In pancreatic cancer, PHGDH binds to translation initiation factors eIF4A and eIF4E, enhancing the formation of eIF4F complex at 5' cap of target mRNAs (Fig. 2F). This scaffolding activity promotes the translation of EMT-associated mRNAs, thereby facilitating tumor progression [116]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), glucose deprivation reduces intracellular levels of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA), a metabolite that normally binds PHGDH to maintain its metabolic function. Upon dissociation of 3-PGA, PHGDH undergoes a conformational and functional shift, promoting the assembly of multi-protein assembly composed of PHGDH, AXIN, HIPK2, TIP60, and p53 (Fig. 2E). Within this complex, HIPK2-mediated phosphorylation of p53 at Ser46 triggers apoptosis, revealing a tumor-suppressive role for PHGDH [117]. Additionally, in breast cancer, PHGDH interacts with glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase platelet isoform (PFKP). Disruption of this interaction activates the hexosamine and sialic acid biosynthetic pathway, leading to increased production of sialic acid. Consequently, the cellular pool of nucleotide sugars such as CMP-sialic acid expands, fueling elevated protein glycosylation, including sialylation of integrin αvβ3. This terminal modification adds negatively charged sialic acid residues to cell surface receptors, altering the adhesive properties of integrin αvβ3, enhancing cell motility and invasiveness, and ultimately driving cancer cell dissemination and metastasis [118] (Fig. 2D). Remarkably, 15–20% of PHGDH localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) in hepatoma cells, where it binds ANT2 to recruit mitochondrial elongation factor G2 (mtEFG2) (Fig. 2G). This interaction enhances mitochondrial ribosome recycling, boosts translation of mtDNA-encoded electron transport chain (ETC) components, and amplifies oxidative metabolism to fuel tumor progression [119]. Collectively, accumulating evidence underscores that the non-catalytic functions of PHGDH are intimately tied to its subcellular localization, positioning this metabolic enzyme as a spatially regulated hub for cellular signaling.

MTHFD2

MTHFD2, traditionally recognized for its enzymatic roles in folate metabolism, has recently emerged as a multifaceted regulator with crucial non-enzymatic functions, including the regulation of DNA damage repair, cell cycle progression, RNA metabolism, and RNA methylation (Fig. 3).Nuclear localized MTHFD2 stabilizes the phosphorylation of exonuclease 1 (EXO1) by modulating cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) activity, thereby facilitating homologous recombination (HR) repair and safeguarding genomic integrity [120] (Fig. 3A). In the nucleus, MTHFD2 also binds to Poly (ADP-Ribose) Polymerase 3 (PARP3), enhancing the efficiency of non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), a crucial DNA repair pathway in tumor cells (Fig. 3B). This interaction is particularly important in tumors harboring p53 deletions or mutations, which exhibit increased dependence on NHEJ. Pharmacological inhibition of MTHFD2 disrupts DNA repair mechanisms and markedly sensitizes tumor cells to the chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin [121]. Beyond its function in DNA repair, MTHFD2 interacts with CDK2 to enhance Rb phosphorylation, triggering the release and activation of E2F1 (Fig. 3C). Marked by increased E2F1 acetylation and transcriptional activity, this cascade promotes G1–S phase transition and accelerates cell cycle progression in bladder cancer cells [122]. In addition, Costas et al. identified a noncanonical role of MTHFD2 in regulating RNA processing and translation. Mechanistically, MTHFD2 physically associates with nuclear proteins implicated in RNA metabolic processes and translational regulation, including several heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) family members and components of small ribosomal subunit. Functionally, depletion of MTHFD2 impairs cell proliferation and stress adaptation, resembling the cellular phenotypes observed upon disruption of ribosomal proteins, thereby highlighting its role in maintaining translational homeostasis [123]. In addition, MTHFD2 has been newly characterized as a regulator of RNA methylation in renal cell carcinoma (RCC). MTHFD2 promotes N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of HIF-2α mRNA, enhancing its translation (Fig. 3D). Elevated HIF-2α, in turn, drives glycolytic gene expression and MTHFD2 transcription, forming a feedforward loop that links RNA methylation to metabolic reprogramming in RCC [124]. PSAT1 and PSPH

Analogously, the oncogenic roles of PSAT1 and PSPH extend well beyond their metabolic functions (Fig. 4). A landmark study in 2020 was among the first to underscores the non-enzymatic role of PSAT1 in promoting metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma. Specifically, PSAT1 overexpression suppresses the IFNγ–STAT1–IRF1-IFIH1 signaling axis, attenuating IFNγ-mediated responses and fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Fig. 4). Notably, this pro-metastatic effect occurs independently of alterations in serine biosynthesis, indicating a non-catalytic mechanism of action, although the underlying regulatory basis remains unclear [125]. Subsequent research further demonstrates that aberrant upregulation of PSAT1 is closely associated with erlotinib resistance in lung cancer. Mechanistically, PSAT1 activates STAT3 signaling pathway through its interactiopm with IQGAP1, and this effect remains even in the presence of catalytically inactive PSAT1 mutants [126] (Fig. 4). These findings underscore the emerging recognition of PSAT1’s non-canonical, scaffolding functions as crucial contributors to tumor progression. For instance, in tumors harboring p5372P variant, PSAT1 interacts competitively with the mutant without relying on its enzymatic function, promoting the nuclear import of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α) and thereby increasing mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and TCA cycle processes (Fig. 4). Disrupting the PSAT1–p5372P interaction through aminooxyacetic acid (AOA) significantly impairs these mitochondrial processes, suggesting a potential therapeutic vulnerability [127]. Similarly, PSPH has been shown to directly interact with insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1), promoting its dephosphorylation and subsequent activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 4). SHMT1/2

Emerging evidence underscores the functional complexity of SHMT1/2 beyond their traditional roles in 1 C metabolism (Fig. 4). SHMT1 exhibits a moonlighting function as an RNA-binding protein, regulating both its own expression and that of SHMT2. Specifically, SHMT1 binds to the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of its own mRNA to suppress translation, while also interacting with the 5′ UTR of SHMT2 mRNA to reduce transcript stability and protein expression (Fig. 4). This dual regulatory mechanism ensures coordinated control of serine metabolism between the cytosol and mitochondria, maintaining a dynamic balance between cytosolic and mitochondrial fluxes [129].

Notably, the non-catalytic roles of SHMT2 have been more extensively characterized and are remarkably diverse. One well-characterized example involves its role as a crucial structural component of the BRISC (BRCC36 isopeptidase complex), a deubiquitinating enzyme complex that specifically cleaves K63-linked ubiquitin chains. In this context, SHMT2 initially functions as a scaffold protein that recruits BRISC to ubiquitinated IFNAR1, promoting K63-linked deubiquitination and preventing receptor degradation to sustain type I interferon signaling [130]. Subsequent structural and biochemical studies revealed that, in the absence of its cofactor pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP) or substrate, SHMT2 forms an inactive dimer that binds BRISC complex and sterically blocks BRCC36, acting as an endogenous competitive inhibitor of BRISC’s nonspecific deubiquitinating activity [131, 132] (Fig. 4). Thus, SHMT2 integrates basal inhibition and substrate-specific facilitation of BRISC, illustrating a broader regulatory paradigm in which a metabolic enzyme moonlights to fine-tune ubiquitin signaling, with potential implications for tumor immune responses and therapeutic outcomes [133–136]. This functional diversity is further exemplified by alternative splicing of SHMT2 gene, which generates two distinct isoforms: the canonical mitochondrial SHMT2 and a shorter variant, SHMT2α, lacking the mitochondrial targeting sequence due to exon 1 exclusion. Unlike its mitochondrial counterpart, SHMT2α undergoes cell cycle-dependent nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, accumulating in the nucleus during S/G2/M phases and remaining cytoplasmic in G1 (Fig. 4). This localization pattern reflects tight spatiotemporal coordination with DNA replication and repair. Although SHMT2α retains metabolic activity, its nuclear function is independent of mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism. Mechanistically, nuclear SHMT2α collaborates with SHMT1 to form a thymidylate synthesis complex at replication sites, channeling dTMP production precisely where it is needed. This spatial organization enhances nucleotide utilization efficiency and minimizes precursor loss [137]. Extending its immune-regulatory and nuclear roles, SHMT2 can directly interact with cytoplasmic p53, inhibiting its binding with the E3 ubiquitin ligase HDM2 and thereby preventing p53 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation (Fig. 4). This stabilization of p53 suppresses autophagy and enhances the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents such as 5-FU [138]. In parallel, SHMT2 interacts with β-catenin in the cytoplasm to prevent its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Moreover, β-catenin recruits the transcription factor TCF4 to enhance SHMT2 expression, forming a positive feedback loop that sustains oncogenic signaling and promotes colorectal cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [139]. In gastric cancer, SHMT2 exerts a non-metabolic role by interacting with hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) and inhibiting its ubiquitination, thereby stabilizing HIF1α protein under hypoxic conditions. This stabilization activates the HIF1α/VEGF/STAT3 signaling axis, contributing to redox homeostasis and enhancing tumor cell adaptation to hypoxic stress [140] (Fig. 4). Collectively, these findings reveal that SHMT1/2 extend far beyond its metabolic origins, functioning as a versatile signaling hub that integrates 1 C metabolism with diverse processes including immune regulation, genome maintenance, and oncogenic transformation.

IDO

IDO1 is a well-established immunosuppressive enzyme, depleting tryptophan and generating kynurenine to inhibit effector T cells. Beyond its catalytic role, IDO1 converts tryptophan to N-formylkynurenine, releasing formyl groups that feed the one-carbon pool to support nucleotide biosynthesis and methylation. Emerging evidence further implicates IDO1’s non-enzymatic functions in immune regulation. However, emerging evidence has unveiled a critical role for IDO1’s non-enzymatic functions in immune regulation. A pivotal study by Pallotta et al. [141] first demonstrated that IDO1 contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) within its non-catalytic domain, which mediates active immunosuppression. Upon stimulation by TGF-β, the ITIM motif recruits the tyrosine phosphatases SHP-1 and SHP-2, forming an IDO1–SHP signaling complex that activates NF-κB pathway. This activation induces de novo transcription of IDO1, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains immune tolerance. Conversely, under pro-inflammatory conditions, elevated IL-6 stimulates production of cytokine signaling inhibitor 3 (SOCS3), which binds to IDO1 and targets it for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation, thus terminating its immunosuppressive effects and facilitating immune activation [141]. More recently, studies using glioblastoma models have highlighted the clinical relevance of IDO1’s non-catalytic activity. Specifically, IDO1 facilitates the transcriptional upregulation of complement factor H (CFH) and its isoform FHL-1 independent of its enzymatic activity in the kynurenine pathway. These immunosuppressive proteins contribute to the establishment of an immunologically suppressive tumor microenvironment and are associated with poorer survival outcomes in preclinical glioblastoma models. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which IDO1 regulates CFH and FHL-1 remains to be elucidated [142]. In summary, IDO1’s non-enzymatic functions constitute a biologically and clinically significant immunoregulatory axis that operates independently of its canonical role in tryptophan metabolism.

Others

In addition to the enzymes mentioned above whose non-enzymatic functions have been extensively characterized, GNMT and MAT2α have also emerged as important regulators of gene expression through non-enzymatic mechanisms. MAT2α, a nuclear-localized isoform of methionine adenosyltransferase, has been shown to regulate gene expression independently of its role in SAM synthesis. Specifically, nuclear MAT2α interacts with the transcription factor MAF and functions as a corepressor that suppresses the expression of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) [143]. GNMT catalyzes the conversion of SAM to SAH, thereby contributing to the regulation of SAM/SAH homeostasis. Early studies demonstrated that, in the presence of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) such as Benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P), GNMT forms homodimers and functions as a PAH-binding receptor independently of its enzymatic activity [144, 145]. Subsequent research revealed that even non-PAH compounds like aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) can induce the nuclear translocation of GNMT [146, 147]. Within the nucleus, GNMT acts as a transcriptional modulator by directly binding to the promoter sequences of transcription factors like Nrf2 or CAR/PXR. Through this mechanism, GNMT influences the expression of detoxification-related enzymes, including NQO1 and CYP3A44 [148, 149].

In conclusion, we summarize the spatiotemporal regulation of 1 C enzymes and discuss how their moonlighting functions contribute to oncogenesis by orchestrating key cellular programs such as mitotic control, growth signaling, cell survival, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Unraveling these pleiotropic roles may uncover previously unrecognized regulatory networks and identify targetable vulnerabilities for precision oncology interventions.

1 C metabolism and anti-tumor therapy

As discussed earlier, enzymes and metabolites involved in the synthesis and utilization of 1 C units are critical drivers of tumorigenesis and progression, either through their canonical enzymatic activities or through non-canonical functions. Consequently, this pathway has emerged as a significant target for anti-tumor therapies [150–152]. Currently, numerous therapeutic strategies targeting 1 C metabolism are undergoing clinical evaluation for their efficacy and safety.

Antifolate drugs

As early as the 1940 s, the discovery that folate is essential for DNA synthesis laid the foundation for the development of antifolate agents. These compounds represent the earliest examples of targeting 1 C metabolism for cancer therapy, establishing the conceptual basis for metabolic intervention in oncology. Classical antifolate drugs, including methotrexate (targeting DHFR) [153] and fluoropyrimidine agents (targeting TYMS) [154], induce cell death in rapidly proliferating malignancies by specifically inhibiting key enzymes in 1 C metabolism, thereby depleting nucleotide pools and causing replication stress [101]. Novel antifolates are in various stages of development in attempts to improve their efficacy and toxicity profile or to decrease the various known mechanisms of resistance to antifolate therapy [155].

Methotrexate remains a widely used antifolate in oncology, targeting DHFR and promoting the intracellular accumulation of polyglutamylated folate analogs. These analogs directly inhibit key enzymes such as TYMS and GARFT, leading to depletion of reduced folate substrates and impaired synthesis of purine nucleotides, thymidylate, and certain amino acids, ultimately resulting in cell death. This cytotoxic process, however, is frequently counteracted by resistance mechanisms such as impaired uptake via the reduced folate carrier, DHFR gene amplification, and diminished polyglutamylation, which collectively reduce the intracellular levels of active drug. Critically, cancer cells can bypass DHFR inhibition by engaging an alternative pathway that sustains folate cycle activity. Although methotrexate effectively blocks the de novo regeneration of THF through DHFR inhibition, the major circulating folate, 5-methyl-THF, serves as an alternative extracellular folate source [156]. Taken up via folate transporters such as RFC and PCFT, 5-methyl-THF donates its methyl group to Hcy through MS, a vitamin B12-dependent reaction, regenerating THF. This DHFR-independent pathway replenishes the intracellular THF pool, sustaining 1 C metabolism for nucleotide synthesis and methylation reactions, thereby maintaining cancer cell proliferation and contributing to acquired resistance to DHFR-targeted antifolate therapies [157, 158]. Together, these findings highlight the long-standing clinical relevance of metabolic pathways targeted by antifolate therapy and underscore the importance of physiological folate forms, such as 5-methyl-THF, in modulating drug sensitivity and resistance.

Nutrient therapy

Tumor cells typically increase the uptake of extracellular precursors such as serine and glycine to meet the demand for 1 C units required for proliferation. Although serine and glycine are non-essential amino acids, chronic deprivation in p53-deficient murine tumor models reduces tumor burden and prolongs survival. Serine deprivation activates the serine synthesis pathway and reroutes carbon flux from glycolysis to the TCA cycle. Transient p53-p21 activation and cell cycle arrest redirect limited serine to glutathione synthesis, maintaining redox balance and cell survival. In contrast, p53-deficient cells fail to mount this adaptive response, resulting in ROS accumulation, impaired proliferation, and reduced tumor growth in vivo [159, 160]. These findings suggest that dietary serine restriction may be an effective strategy for treating p53-deficient tumors.

Moreover, micronutrient intake can modulate cancer risk by influencing 1 C metabolism. Folic acid, a B vitamin, plays a crucial role in DNA methylation, and its deficiency has been associated with an increased cancer risk. Folic acid supplementation has been shown to prevent Helicobacter pylori-related gastric cancer [161]. However, some studies suggest that low folic acid intake may actually reduce the risk of gastric cancer [162]. Additionally, vitamin B6 and choline also serve as essential cofactors in 1 C metabolism, facilitating methyl group transfer for nucleotide synthesis, epigenetic regulation, and redox homeostasis. Higher dietary intake of vitamin B6 and choline has been linked to a reduced incidence of pancreatic cancer [163]. Given the intricate interactions between diverse cell types and their microenvironments, which play a decisive role in dictating distinct metabolic demands and utilization modalities, it becomes of utmost importance to conduct a comprehensive assessment of these elements when formulating dietary interventions. Such an evaluation is essential to guarantee both the precision and the therapeutic effectiveness of the interventions.

Targeting enzymes of IC metabolism

In recent years, targeting 1 C metabolism has emerged as a promising approach for cancer treatment. While classical antifolates like methotrexate inhibit DHFR and nucleotide synthesis [164], recent drug development has expanded to other key enzymes such as MTHFD2, PHGDH, and SHMT2, with inhibitors now in clinical or preclinical development, as summarized in Table 1. However, these efforts have focused solely on inhibiting canonical enzymatic activities, whereas targeting the non-canonical ("moonlighting") functions of these enzymes remains an unexplored frontier.

PHGDH inhibitors

Suppression of serine uptake or PHGDH expression has been shown to suppress the proliferation of cancer cells [165]. AstraZeneca conducted fragment-based screening targeting the NAD⁺ binding pocket of PHGDH, followed by combinatorial chemical synthesis to generate a large library of compounds. Among these, an indole derivative demonstrated a strong inhibitory effect on PHGDH (Kd = 0.18 μM) [12]. Pacold et al. identified NCT-503 through high-throughput screening. In cell-based assays, NCT-503 not only inhibited proliferation and induced apoptosis in multiple PHGDH-overexpressing cell lines but also blocked the incorporation of serine-derived carbon into nucleotides in both intracellular and extracellular contexts [9]. Although various PHGDH inhibitors have been developed (including ketothioamide, BI-4924, indoleamide, piperazine-1-thiourea inhibitors, and allosteric inhibitors) and have demonstrated anti-tumor effects, no PHGDH inhibitors have been approved by the FDA for clinical application, as a result of challenges such as metabolic compensation, drug selectivity, resistance, toxicity, and the complexity of drug development [165, 166].

SHMT1/2 inhibitors

Inhibiting both SHMT1 and SHMT2 is essential to prevent tumor cells from utilizing serine to generate 1 C units. SHIN1 has IC50 values of 5 nM and 13 nM for SHMT1 and SHMT2, respectively. It has been demonstrated that the concurrent inhibition of these enzymes results in pronounced antitumor activity in vivo models as well as in vitro experimental settings [18]. However, the inhibitors exhibit unsatisfactory stability, bioavailability and pharmacokinetic properties in vivo, limiting their potential as cancer therapeutics. Combination strategies are currently being explored involving SHIN1 and other chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, or immunotherapeutic drugs [167]

MTHFD2 inhibitors

The crystal structure of MTHFD2 has been elucidated, and four inhibitors have been developed. Ly345889 is the first reported inhibitor, but it lacks sufficient selectivity, with IC50 value of 96 nM for MTHFD1 and 663 nM for Exo1 [168]. Carolacton, a natural compound, inhibits the growth of adenocarcinoma and human oral epidermal carcinoma cells, demonstrating an EC50 of 42 μM and a maximum inhibition rate of 70% when U-937 cells are cultured in folate-free medium [169].

More recently, DS44960156 has been reported to exhibit high selectivity for MTHFD2, with an IC50 of 1.6 μM for MTHFD2 and an IC50 greater than 30 μM for MTHFD1 [170]. Further optimization led to the development of DS18561882, which shows an impressive IC50 of 6.3 nM for MTHFD2. Oral administration of DS18561882 significantly inhibits tumor growth in mouse xenograft models. This compound is a promising MTHFD2 inhibitor for the clinical treatment of breast cancer [171]. Its biological effects in other cancer types warrant urgent investigation.

IDO1 inhibitors

IDO1 has emerged as an important immune checkpoint, and IDO1 inhibitors can represent an effective alternative strategy for tumor treatment [172]. Several novel classes of inhibitors, including indoximod, epacadostat, and navoximod, have advanced into clinical trials [173, 174]. These inhibitors not only act as tumor immune modulators, but are also combined with immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Numerous Phase I to III clinical trials have been conducted for epacadostat, encompassing both solid tumors and hematological malignancies [20, 21]. Gangadhar et al. conducted a Phase I/II open-trial of epacadostat combined with pembrolizumab in treating advanced tumors [175]. The results indicate that this combination significantly enhances the anti-tumor effect, improving clinical remission rates and demonstrating good safety with a low incidence of adverse reactions. Soliman et al. conducted a Phase I/II clinical trial assessing indoximod administered alongside an adenovirus p53-DC vaccine in patients suffering from advanced breast cancer [176–178]. They found that the combination is well-tolerated and exhibits a synergistic effect. These findings suggest that combining IDO1 inhibitors with cancer vaccines may potentiate therapeutic outcomes.

Regulating methylation

1 C metabolism produces the essential methyl donor, followed by SAM, which transfers methyl groups to DNA, RNA, and proteins, enabling methylation modifications as the key readout. Methylation is a key epigenetic modification implicated in tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance. Several drugs targeting enzymes involved in the methylation process are currently under preclinical evaluation and early clinical trials, offering potential therapeutic avenues for cancers driven by epigenetic alterations [179].

DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors

DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors, including azacitidine and decitabine, have been approved by FDA for clinical use. They inhibit DNMT activity, disrupt DNA methylation, induce demethylation, reactivate tumor suppressor genes, and inhibit tumor growth [180]. These drugs are currently widely used in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), and other cancers characterized by significant epigenetic alterations [181]. In addition, SGI-110 (guadecitabine), a prodrug of decitabine, has proven to be a more stable small-molecule DNA methylation inhibitor compared to some existing nucleoside analogs. Preclinical studies in ovarian cancer mouse models have shown that SGI-110 can enhance cisplatin sensitivity, suggesting that combination therapy with SGI-110 may imporve outcomes for patients with platinum-resistant ovarian cancer [51, 182].

Histone methylation inhibitors

Histone methylation inhibitors are a class of small-molecule drugs that target histone methyltransferases (HMTs) [140]. Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) inhibitors can suppress tri-methylation of H3K27, thereby affecting tumor cell proliferation. Tazemetostat, the first-in-class EZH2 inhibitor, was FDA-approved in 2020 for relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma and epithelioid sarcoma. Following this, Valemetostat, a dual EZH1/2 inhibitor, gained approval in Japan in 2022 for relapsed/refractory adult T-cell lymphoma. Most recently, SHR2554, a selective EZH2 inhibitor by Hengrui Pharma, was approved in China. These developments represent a significant advance in epigenetic cancer therapy [58, 59, 63]. In addition to EZH2 inhibitors, other histone methyltransferase inhibitors are under investigation, including 2-n-propylpentanoic acid, suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) [183], belinostat [184], and SNDX-275 [185]. These compounds are currently in the clinical trial stage, with SAHA demonstrating good oral absorption and low cytotoxicity. Regulating histone modifications for cancer treatment is expected to offer a promising new direction in oncology.

m6A methylation inhibitors

Several inhibitors targeting m6A modification have shown potential for clinical application, including METTL3, FTO, and ALKBH5 inhibitors [186]. Meclofenamic acid (MA) selectively inhibits FTO activity by competing with FTO (but not ALKBH5) for binding to m6A-containing RNA. Entacapone directly binds to and inhibits FTO activity in vitro. The small-molecule inhibitors FB23 and FB23-2 bind to FTO and selectively inhibit its m6A demethylase activity. In vitro, FB23-2 suppresses FTO expression, significantly inhibiting proliferation and promoting differentiation and apoptosis in human AML cell lines and primary AML cells [64]. Scientists have been trying to develop inhibitors against METTL3 since the crystal structure of METTL3-METTL14 complex was solved in 2016. STORM Therapeutic based in the UK, developed the first METTL3 small molecule inhibitor STM2457 for leukemia treatment and advanced it into Phase I clinical trials [67]. The stapled peptide inhibitor RSM3 exhibits broad-spectrum anti-cancer properties and has shown excellent anti-tumor effects across multiple tumor models.

Conclusions and perspectives

Over the past decade, advances in cancer metabolism research have significantly reshaped our understanding of metabolic enzymes, revealing that they possess diverse, non-canonical "moonlighting" functions in addition to their traditional enzymatic roles [187]. In addition, with the growing insights into the production and utilization of one-carbon units in cancer cells, multiple therapeutic strategies have been proposed, including nutrient therapy and targeting enzymes in one-carbon metabolism pathways. Most currently available inhibitors are still designed to suppress the canonical enzymatic activity of 1 C metabolic enzymes, while largely overlooking their non-canonical functions. One one hand, regarding the enzymatic targeting approach, identifying appropriate windows for targeting metabolic pathways, as well as developing strategies to modulate 1 C flux and metabolic intermediates, requires further investigation. One the other hand, with the moonlighting functions are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to tumor progression and therapeutic resistance, more attention should be paid on this point. Such as emerging strategies increasingly focus on targeting the stability and regulatory functions of 1 C metabolic enzymes. PROTAC-based approaches have been developed to induce selective degradation of enzymes such as DHFR, thereby eliminating both their catalytic and non-catalytic activities and potentially overcoming resistance linked to active-site mutations [188]. In parallel, efforts to modulate allosteric sites or disrupt protein–protein interactions offer alternative means to fine-tune metabolic flux without direct catalytic blockade. Epigenetic and transcriptional interventions, including BET and HDAC inhibitors, can further suppress oncogenic upregulation of 1 C enzymes such as SHMT2 and MTHFD2 [189–192]. Together, these approaches expand the therapeutic landscape by integrating enzyme inhibition, targeted degradation, and metabolic network rewiring to more effectively disrupt tumor-associated 1 C metabolism. A more promising strategy involves the combined use of drugs targeting both the metabolic and non-metabolic functions of 1 C metabolism enzymes, which may enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome some of limitations associated with single-target therapies.
