Comparative genomics reveals avian Acinetobacter baumannii as antimicrobial resistance reservoirs and bovine strains with elevated pathogenicity
Authors: Dadimi Bhargavi, Sandeep Ghatak, Kandhan Srinivas, Madesh Angappan, Daniel Aibor Pyngrope, John Pynhun Lamare, Samir Das, A. A. P. Milton, Chendu Bharat Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii is primarily known as an opportunistic nosocomial pathogen but is increasingly recognized as an emerging contaminant in foods of animal origin. In the present study, we characterized the A. baumannii strains from raw meat samples and undertook a comparative genomic analysis to explore the resistome, virulome, mobilome (integrons and transposons) and pathogenic potential of the A. baumannii strains from food animals and animal origin foods. Avian strains of A. baumannii were found to carry a significantly higher number of antimicrobial resistance genes (P < 0.05) and also possess mobile genetic elements (transposons, integrons), while bovine strains were found to possess significantly higher pathogenic potential (P < 0.05) than A. baumannii strains from other sources. Pan-genome and pan-resistome analyses indicated an open nature for both across all sources. Phylogenetic analysis revealed spatial clustering of the isolates, and isolates sequenced in the present study clustered closely with Chinese isolates. Taken together, being the first comparative genomic study on A. baumannii strains from food animals and foods of animal origin, the present study provided insights into the genomic features of A. baumannii strains while highlighting the potential role of avian strains of A. baumannii in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance through animal origin foods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33117-0.

Introduction

Various kinds of bacterial infections are associated with the consumption of raw or undercooked meats around the globe. Bacteria isolated from meat are of both pathogenic and commensal in nature, including Staphylococcus aureus, Campylobacter jejuni, Yersinia, Acinetobacter spp., E. coli, Salmonella etc1–4. Among these, Acinetobacter spp., particularly Acinetobacter baumannii, has emerged as a significant global concern4. A. baumannii is a gram-negative coccobacillus belonging to the family Moraxellaceae. It is a strictly aerobic, catalase-positive, oxidase-negative, non-fastidious, and non-fermenting organism. This opportunistic pathogen is notorious for developing antimicrobial resistance and causing nosocomial infections with high morbidity and mortality rates5. It colonizes various body systems, causing pneumonia, surgical site infections, UTIs, RTIs, meningitis, and osteomyelitis in humans and animals6,7. The World Health Organization has classified carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii as a critical priority pathogen, underscoring the urgent need for new treatment options8. Owing to their importance in clinical settings, less attention has been paid to their existence in food and their connection to infection/dissemination pathways. However, foods derived from animals have also been found to contain pathogenic Acinetobacter species, which may serve as a reservoir and source of infection for people9. The reservoirs of A. baumannii were found to be very diverse. It has been recovered from various sources such as humans, animals, the environment, and food. Prevalence of A. baumannii in food samples varies globally: 26.6% raw meat samples in Iran2, 26.5% in Saudi Arabia (sheep: 46.55%, chicken: 32.50%, cow: 15.00%, camel: 9.68%9, and 10% in ready-to-eat salads in Spain5. A. baumannii rapidly develops multidrug resistance due to the presence of mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as insertion sequences (ISs), transposons, and resistance islands10. Due to the ability of this organism to spread and adapt to the nosocomial environment, it is important to identify and monitor the possible sources. Capsular (KL) and outer core locus (OCL) types are key genomic features of A. baumannii that aid in pathogenicity and immune evasion. The KL and OCL loci encode surface polysaccharides critical for bacterial virulence, immune resistance, and biofilm formation11. These loci are highly diverse, with over 237 KL types and 22 OCL types reported, highlighting the variability in capsular and lipooligosaccharide structures. KL-defined capsular polysaccharides are major virulence factors, protecting against host immune defenses and limiting antibiotic penetration11. Similarly, OCL types define outer membrane glycolipid composition, influencing immune recognition and bacterial persistence11.

While A. baumannii is often considered a neglected veterinary pathogen, its epidemiology and genomic features outside human clinical settings remain underexplored. In India, studies have primarily focused on human-derived A. baumannii strains, with limited data on the genomic characteristics of strains from animal-derived foods. Comparative genomics facilitates genome-wide assessment of pathogenicity, evolution, and resistance mechanisms14.

Despite increased research on A. baumannii in animals, whole-genome sequence analyses and comparative genomic studies of strains from animals or their food products are scarce. The distribution of KL and OCL types and their association with sequence types (STs) in A. baumannii from food animal origins has not been systematically characterized. The emergence of A. baumannii in food-producing animals raises concerns about zoonotic transmission and its role as a reservoir for antimicrobial resistance genes. Understanding the diversity of KL and OCL types in these isolates is critical for assessing their pathogenic potential, transmission dynamics, and public health implications.

To address these gaps, this study investigates the resistome, virulome, mobilome, and pathogenic potential of A. baumannii strains from foods of animal origin in India. It also conducts comparative genomic analyses with strains from animals and their foods, and systematically characterizes the distribution of KL and OCL types and their correlation with sequence types in isolates recovered from raw meat samples. To our knowledge, this is the first study globally to explore the genomic features of A. baumannii strains from foods of animal origin, providing critical insights into their role in public health and antimicrobial resistance.

Results

In the present study, we included 9 previously isolated A. baumannii strains (n = 5 from chicken; n = 4 from pork). All the isolates were isolated on Leed’s Acinetobacter medium and appeared as light pink colonies with pinkish discoloration of the medium (Fig. 1A). Molecular confirmation of the isolates was achieved by PCR amplification of 16 S rRNA gene with 280 bp amplicon size for genus level confirmation (Fig. 1B) and blaOXA-51-like gene amplification with 353 bp product size for A. baumannii species confirmation (Fig. 1C).

Antimicrobial susceptibility of A. baumannii of animal origin foods

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that all 9 isolates were susceptible to all the antibiotics tested, except for 1 isolate exhibiting resistance to imipenem. However, intermediate resistance was noticed towards various antibiotics including imipenem (n = 1), gentamicin (n = 3), piperacillin-tazobactam (n = 2), cefepime n = 2), tigecycline (n = 1), polymyxin B (N = 3) (Table 1). Further, in the e-test analysis, one isolate from pork (PCN_IN_23_3) displayed the growth of resistant sub-population in the zone of inhibition on Mueller Hinton agar plates indicating possible hetero-resistance.

Biofilm formation, hemolytic activity and serum resistance in A. baumannii

Biofilm formation among the A. baumannii isolates was evaluated by measuring cell adhesion to the wells of the polystyrene microtitre plate after incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. Biofilm formation assay under these conditions revealed that all the isolates of the present study possess a very strong ability to form biofilms with OD values ranging from 0.567 to 1.3433 (Fig. 2A).

Hemolytic assay revealed that none of the A. baumannii isolates from the present study were able to produce hemolysis on 5% sheep blood agar, and S. aureus, which was used as a positive control, displayed strong β-hemolysis (Fig. 2B).

Serum sensitivity of the A. baumannii isolates was evaluated after 2 h incubation in 40% human serum by comparing the viable counts at 0 h and 2 h time points. A significant increase in viable bacterial count was observed after 2 h incubation in human serum in 8 isolates of A. baumannii from the present study, indicating resistance to human serum. However, 1 isolate of porcine origin was found sensitive to human serum (PCN_IN_23_3). (Fig. 2C).

Isolate characteristics and genome features

The size of the A. baumannii genomes included in the study ranged from 3.7 to 4.2 MB. The years of isolation of the strains ranged from 2006 to 2023. Majority of the genomes were of porcine origin (n = 26), followed by avian (n = 25), bovine (n = 6), and ovine (n = 3). Genomes from the ovine origin were excluded from statistical analyses because of their smaller number.

MLST analysis

In silico MLST analysis by Oxford scheme revealed the presence of 23 Sequence types, of which ST235 (n = 7) was found to be more prevalent, followed by ST1158 (n = 3), ST642 (n = 3). Pasteur scheme identified 26 STs, and the most prevalent ST identified was ST162, followed by ST23 (n = 5), ST2252 (n = 4), and ST1512 (n = 4). Further, 11 strains were untypable by Pasteur scheme and 16 isolates by Oxford scheme. Given that the Pasteur MLST scheme was more capable of differentiating between closely related isolates, we adopted it for all further analysis. Further, isolates sequenced in the present study belonged to ST1512 (n = 4), ST2140 (n = 2), ST23 (n = 2), ST52 (n = 1). Isolates belonging to the same sequence type were found to have similar AMR gene profiles except for the isolates belonging to ST23 (Supplementary Table 1).

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes in the genomes of A. baumannii

RGI-CARD analysis revealed that there are 117 different antimicrobial-resistance genes among all the genomes. Point mutations in parC (V104I, D105E) conferring resistance to fluoroquinolones were present in all the genomes, while mutation in gyrA (S81L) gene was observed in 17 genomes (Supplementary Table 2). All the 60 genomes of A. baumannii carried 6 antimicrobial resistance ontologies (AROs), namely AmvA, abeS, adeF, AbaQ, parC, including point mutations in parC gene (V104I, D105E).

Kruskal-Wallis and Bonferroni tests identified that the avian strains carried a significantly higher number (n = 520) (p < 0.05) of AMR genes than bovine (n = 111) and porcine-origin strains (n = 284). A total of 34 OXA variants were detected conferring resistance to carbepenems and monobactams, while one avian strain from China (AVN_CN_17_1) harbored blaNDM-1 gene. The β-lactam group exhibited the greatest diversity (63 genes) (Fig. 3). The blaTEM variants identified in the present study include TEM-1, TEM-2 and were detected in avian strains. Further, genes conferring resistance to tigecycline tet(X6), and tet(X5) were identified among avian strains from China. Tetracycline resistance genes (tetB, tetR), and sulfonamide resistance genes (sul1, sul2) were also identified. Additionally, colistin resistance gene MCR-4.3 was present in a porcine strain (PCN_CN_18_1), though no MCR genes were found in sequenced isolates. Gamma values for pan-resistome and core-resistome were found to be 0.19 and 0.04 respectively, indicating the openness of the pan-resistome. The trends in pan-resistome and core-resistome developments were fitted to a power law model with R2-values of 0.99 and 0.97, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Virulence genes of A. baummannii

Virulome analysis of 60 A. baumannii genomes identified 134 distinct virulence genes (Supplementary Table 3), spanning categories such as adherence, biofilm formation, immune evasion, iron uptake, regulation, natural transformation, and genes encoding resistance to human serum. Among these, 66 genes were conserved across all genomes, including genes for biofilm formation (n = 7), iron uptake (n = 16), pilin (n = 2), secretion systems (n = 13), immune evasion (n = 7), adherence (n = 15), outer membrane protein (ompA), serum resistance (pbpG), and enzymes like phospholipase C and D. The highest number of virulence genes (n = 122) was observed in an avian strain from Brazil (AVN_BR_12_1), while the lowest (n = 88) was in a porcine strain from India (PCN_IN_19_2). PathogenFinder analysis revealed that all the strains from the present study were determined to be pathogenic to humans, with a mean pathogenic potential score of 0.859. Interestingly, the pathogenic potential scores of the 22 strains were found to be slightly higher than the mean pathogenic probability scores (Fig. 4). Kruskal–Wallis and Bonferroni tests revealed that the pathogenic probabilities of the bovine strains of A. baumannii were significantly higher than the strains from other sources (p < 0.05).

Mobilome (transposons and integrons)

In the mobilome analysis, a total of 11 transposon types were identified in all the genomes namely Tn1213, Tn2008, Tn2, Tn402, Tn6022-Δ1, Tn6171, Tn6172, Tn801, TnaphA6, Tn7, and Tn4 (Supplementary Table 4). These transposons were predominantly detected in avian isolates, with a single occurrence in an ovine isolate (OVN_PA_12_1). The largest number of transposons (n = 8) was identified in an ovine strain from Pakistan. Interestingly, Tn2008 was identified in the ovine isolate, which also harbored the blaOXA-23 gene, a key determinant of carbapenem resistance.

A total of 5 genomes were found to harbor integrons, among which 4 isolates were from avian origin and 1 strain from ovine origin (Fig. 4). However, none of the isolates sequenced in the present study harbored any integrons. Among the 5 genomes that harbored integrons, 4 were found to possess 1 integron each, while one avian strain from Brazil (AVN_BR_12_1) harbored 2 integrons.

Pangenome of A. baumannii

Pangenome analysis of A. baumannii revealed 11,496 coding sequences of which 2557 represent core genome and the rest 8939 represent dispensable genes (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Application of Heaps-law on pan-genome development revealed the openness of the pan-genomes as the Gamma values ranged between 0.147 ± 0.028 to 0.326 ± 0.005, satisfying the condition for open pan-genome (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Genome diversities assessed from RCP (ratio of core genome to pan-genome) revealed that genomes of avian (0.334) origin were found to be more diverse than those of porcine (RCP = 0.350), bovine (0.583) or ovine (0.726) origin. Based on the GWAS analysis, no statistically significant genetic markers were identified that differentiate A. baumannii isolates from different sources.

Phylogenetic analysis

Clusters analysis with the median clade distance method grouped 60 A. baumannii genomes into 15 clusters. Isolates sequenced in the present study were grouped in 2 clusters and are associated with avian strains from China and Germany in one cluster (light purple) and with one porcine isolate from the US and one avian isolate from China in another cluster (yellow) (Fig. 4). Geographical clustering of the isolates was observed as seen in case of porcine isolates from Scotland (light green), Canada (grey), VietNam (light indigo), and Indian isolates (bright blue). However, porcine isolates from India, which were not sequenced in the present study, were scattered through the phylogram without forming any source-wise or geographical clustering with other isolates. Further, one avian strain from Brazil and one bovine strain from the United States didn’t cluster with any of the isolates and remained separate in the phylogram.

KL and OCL typing

A genomic analysis of 60 A. baumannii isolates identified 33 distinct capsular polysaccharide (KL) types, with KL208 and KL209 being the most prevalent (Supplementary Table 1). Outer core lipooligosaccharide (OCL) typing revealed 13 unique variants, predominantly OCL1 (n = 20) and OCL2 (n = 18). Among the subset of isolates sequenced in this study, five KL types were detected: KL9 (n = 3), KL169 (n = 2), KL8 (n = 2), KL57 (n = 1), and KL192 (n = 1), along with two OCL types: OCL1 (n = 3) and OCL2 (n = 6).

Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) analysis, based on the Pasteur scheme (MLST(P)), revealed significant associations between specific sequence types (STs) and both KL and OCL types, as indicated by standardized residuals exceeding 2 (P < 0.05). Notable KL-ST correlations included: ST465–KL126, ST1303–KL111, ST1153–KL49, ST2253–KL54, ST1904–KL197, ST2399–KL79, ST2400–KL65, ST2426–KL72 (residuals up to 7.62); ST1093/ST2140–KL136/KL169 (7.49); ST1512–KL9 (6.26); ST25–KL14 (4.25); and ST23–KL62 (4.49). For OCL types, significant associations were identified between ST79 and OCL10, ST283 and OCL12, ST504 and OCL22, ST1904 and OCL21, and ST1303 and OCL9, each with a residual of 7.62. Further correlations included ST499 with OCL3 (residual = 6.01), ST375 with OCL6 (residual = 5.29), ST19, ST25, and ST762 with OCL7 (residual = 4.25), ST465 and ST1111 with OCL4 (residual = 2.85), ST23, ST1512, and ST2252 with OCL2 (residuals ranging from 2.56 to 2.86).

Discussion

In this study, A. baumannii isolates recovered from chicken meat and pork were characterized for their antimicrobial resistance and virulence properties while also uncovering the genomic features of A. baumannii through a comparative genomics approach. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the characterization of A. baumannii from foods of animal origin and elucidating its genomic features from India.

Antimicrobial resistance is a growing global concern, significantly impacting healthcare. Acinetobacter baumannii is one of the most challenging nosocomial pathogens due to the widespread antibiotic resistance. Antimicrobial susceptibility analysis of the A. baumannii isolates from the present study revealed that all the isolates of A. baumannii sequenced in the present study were susceptible to all the antibiotics tested, except for imipenem resistance observed in one isolate from chicken (AVN_IN_23_4). In contrast to the findings of the present study, several studies reported the occurrence of multi-drug resistance in A. baumannii isolates of human origin, as well as from animal origin15. The high antibiotic susceptibility of the isolates from the present study may be linked to their source, with the presence of A. baumannii strains in raw meat samples indicating potential introduction through environmental contamination during processing or handling2. Studies have shown that environmental isolates of A. baumannii tend to be more susceptible to antibiotics compared to clinical isolates16,17.

Pathogenesis in A. baumanniii has been attributed to a combination of factors such as pili, biofilm-associated proteins, porins, phospholipases, iron acquisition systems, protein secretion systems, and lipopolysaccharides, which collectively enable host infection. Biofilm formation is a key virulence trait aiding colonization, persistence, and environmental survival. All the isolates sequenced from the present study were found to be strong biofilm formers (OD values 0.567 to 1.3433), plausibly due to the presence of various biofilm-related genes, including ompA, csu pili genes (csuA, csuB, csuC, csuD, csuE), PNAG genes (pgaA, pgaB, pgaC, pgaD) and biofilm-associated protein gene (bap) as detected in the virulome analysis. These findings align with Elbehiry et al.9, who also reported a correlation between biofilm formation and biofilm-associated genes in A. baumannii from raw meat.

The two virulence factors pbpG and LpsB associated with serum resistance were found in all the 60 genomes of A. baumannii including the isolates sequenced in the present study. Phenotypic resistance of the A. baumannii isolates to human serum observed in the present study possibly be explained by the presence of these virulence factors. Several studies reported the resistance of the clinical strains of A. baumannii to human serum18,19 Serum resistance and biofilm formation are significant contributors to the pathogenesis of A. baumannii. High rate of resistance to human serum and strong ability to form biofilms among the isolates sequenced in the present study highlights the risk of highly virulent A. baumannii strains spreading through food.

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility profile of the isolates from the present study indicates the high susceptibility of the isolates to various antibiotics. It was observed that all the isolates of A. baumannii exhibiting strong biofilm formation were not resistant to multiple antibiotics. These findings are in agreement with the German research conducted in 2013 by Rühl-Teichner et al.20. Conversely, Yang and colleagues observed the reverse effect and demonstrated that MDR isolates were found to have the ability to form stronger biofilms than non-MDR isolates. Given these contentious findings, more studies in this area are necessary to fully comprehend the connection between AMR and biofilm production.

Pathogenic potential analysis of A. baumannii genomes revealed that all the genomes had a high probability of being pathogenic to humans highlighting the importance of non-human origin strains of A. baumannii ability to be pathogenic to humans. On the other hand, A. baumannii strains of bovine origin were found to be more pathogenic than the strains from other sources highlighting the role of food animals as a source of pathogenic A. baumannii as opined earlier2. However, this finding contrasts with the presence of mobile genetic elements and AMR genes, which were found in significantly higher numbers among avian and porcine strains of A. baumannii.

Among the various resistance mechanisms in bacteria against antibiotics, efflux pumps have gained major attention recently and emerged as an important resistance determinant because of their broad substrate specificity. Resistome analysis in this study identified 17 efflux pump associated genes indicating a high potential for antimicrobial resistance. Kaviani et al.21 reported the high occurrence of antibiotic resistance linked with efflux pumps associated genes in clinical strains of A. baumannii. This mechanism is a significant factor in the multidrug resistance observed in A. baumannii making it challenging to treat the infections caused by this pathogen.

Colistin resistance gene mcr4.3, a plasmid-mediated genetic element, was detected in a porcine strain from China (PCN_CN_18_1) in the resistome analysis of the genomes of the present study. Colistin resistance has been reported earlier in A. baumannii strains of non-human origin including dogs and cats22–24. The current study also identified tet(X5) and tet(X6) tigecycline resistance genes in avian strains from China, consistent with earlier reports in avian and animal-origin strains25,26. However, none of the isolates sequenced in the present study displayed phenotypic resistance to tigecycline. Also, in the present study, hetero-resistance has been observed in one of the isolates exhibiting intermediate resistance in broth microdilution assay. The observed hetero-resistance could be because of the upregulation of AdeABC efflux pumps due to ISAba1 insertion in adeS as mentioned earlier27. This hetero-resistance may result in treatment failures because of the selection of resistant sub-populations. However, it has to be noted that A. baumannii strains are intrinsically resistant to tigecycline28. In the in-silico AMR gene mining, various ESBL genes, including blaTEM, blaPER, blaOXA, bla AmpC genes were identified which is in line with various studies reported earlier29,30.

All the A. baumannii strains carry chromosomally encoded OXA-51-like genes. We found 15 types of OXA-51-like genes (OXA-51, OXA-64, OXA-65, OXA-66, OXA-67, OXA-68, OXA-70, OXA-78, OXA-91, OXA-95, OXA-98, OXA-132, OXA-104, OXA-106, OXA-208) in the 60 genomes of A. baumannii. In our study, we observed resistance towards imipenem, a carbapenem group antibiotic in one isolate and reduced susceptibility in another isolate. Nevertheless, all the isolates in the present study carried OXA-51-like genes conferring resistance to carbapenems. However, it has to be noted that the presence of insertion sequences such as ISAbaI upstream to OXA-51-like genes provides a strong promoter region leading to overexpression of the genes thus causing resistance to carbapenems31.

Transposons are a type of mobile genetic elements that has the property of jumping across DNA molecules and integrating genetic elements into other organisms, capable of transferring antimicrobial resistance genes horizontally across other bacteria32. Among the transposons identified in the present study, Tn2008, is found to be significant. The transposon Tn2008 was detected in isolate OVN_PA_12_1. Resistome analysis (Supplementary Table 2) using the CARD database corroborated the presence of the blaOXA-23 gene in this isolate, conferring resistance to carbapenems. Presence of these transposons could explain the increasing occurrence of carbapenem resistance among A. baumannii strains globally due to the presence of carbapenem hydrolyzing enzymes such as oxacillinases on these transposons33.

Integrons, on the other hand, are the DNA elements capable of capturing genes by a site-specific recombination mechanism that often carry gene cassettes containing antibiotic-resistance genes34. Various studies have found antibiotic-resistance genes located on integrons in Acinetobacter species35,36. In the present study, integrons have been detected in avian (n = 5) and ovine (n = 1) isolates. The present findings, combined with the presence of a high frequency of AMR genes and the existence of integrons in avian strains, underscore their possible involvement in the spread of AMR to other organisms.

In the MLST analysis of the present study, Pasteur scheme assigned the 49 (81.66%) isolates to distinct STs, while the Oxford scheme was successful in assigning only 36 isolates (60%) to distinct STs. The findings of the present study are consistent with the findings of Gaiarsa et al.37 who recently showed that Pasteur scheme is more suitable for population biology, and epidemiological studies of A. baumannii. Isolates sequenced in the present study were assigned to 4 different types. Considerable diversity was observed in A. baumannii strains investigated in the present study. The most common ST in the present study was ST162 observed in A. baumannii strains from Scotland. ST162 and ST79 have been previously reported from Brazilian hospitals38,39 indicating the global distribution of clones and highlighting the potential zoonotic transmission. Further ST52 assigned to an Indian and one Chinese isolate was previously identified in Germany among clinical strains23. ST2 was earlier found in A. baumannii strains from hospitals in Japan40 and Lebanon41. These findings of the distribution of similar clones among animal and human populations underscore the need for integrated surveillance and control strategies across both human and veterinary medicine to address the spread of such pathogens.

The genomic analysis of 60 A. baumannii isolates in this study revealed a remarkable diversity of capsular polysaccharide (KL) and outer core lipooligosaccharide (OCL) types, with 33 distinct KL types and 13 OCL variants identified. The predominance of KL208 and KL209, alongside OCL1 (n = 20) and OCL2 (n = 18), aligns with previous reports of capsular type prevalence in A. baumannii populations. For instance, Cahill et al.12 similarly noted the frequent occurrence of specific KL types, such as KL2 and KL3, in globally distributed isolates, suggesting that certain capsular configurations may confer selective advantages, potentially related to immune evasion or environmental resilience12. The prominence of OCL1 and OCL2 in our dataset further corroborates findings by Kenyon and Hall42, who highlighted the recurrence of specific OCL types in multidrug-resistant strains, indicating a possible linkage between lipooligosaccharide structure and resistance phenotypes42. Within the subset of isolates sequenced here, the distribution of KL9 (n = 3), KL169 (n = 2), KL8 (n = 2), KL57 (n = 1), and KL192 (n = 1), alongside OCL1 (n = 3) and OCL2 (n = 6), underscores a localized diversity that may reflect niche-specific adaptations or clonal expansion within our sampled population.

Significant associations between MLST(P) profiles and KL/OCL types (standardized residuals > 2, P < 0.05) indicate genetic linkage or co-evolution in A. baumannii. Strong correlations of MLST 465, 1303, 1153, 2253, 1904, 2399, 2400, and 2426 with KL126, KL111, KL49, KL54, KL197, KL79, KL65, and KL72 (residuals up to 7.62), and MLST 1093, 2140, and 1512 with KL136, KL169 (residual = 7.49), and KL9 (residual = 6.26), suggest non-random capsular diversity tied to clonal lineages, consistent with Holt et al.43 and Hamidian et al.44. For OCL types, associations of MLST 79, 283, 504, 1904, and 1303 with OCL10, OCL12, OCL22, OCL21, and OCL9 (residual = 7.62), and MLST 499 with OCL3 (residual = 6.01) and MLST 375 with OCL6 (residual = 5.29), align with Kenyon and Hall42, linking OCL variation to membrane stability and resistance. Multiple associations, like MLST 25 (KL14, OCL7) and MLST 23 (KL62, OCL2), suggest coordinated evolution of capsular components, as noted by Geisinger et al.45.

In the present study, we determined the pan-and core genome of the A. baumannii strains isolated from food animals and avians. The pan-genome of A. baumannii comprised of 11,496 genes of which core genome consists of 2557 (22.41%) genes, and the remaining 8939 (77.7%) are the dispensable genes. Due to the existence of a vast pool of dispensable genes, the pan-genome seemed large. The diverse pan-genome suggests the frequent occurrence of horizontal gene transfer events46. Various studies on the pangenome of the A. baumannii strains of human origin large and open pan-genome47,48. However, pan-genome analysis of A. baumannii from food animal origin is not available. The open pan-genome of A. baumannii reveals that we may reasonably expect to find new genes, many of which may be linked to AMR or virulence genes.

Phylogenetic analysis of A. baumannii genomes revealed the spatial clustering of the isolates, indicating the similar lineages of this organism circulating in the respective geographical regions. Further, the A. baumannii isolates sequenced in the present study were clustered with the majority of Chinese isolates. While we could not establish an epidemiological link for this observation, the role of migratory birds in the spread of A. baumannii has been previously highlighted49, and given that the Indian subcontinent lies along a major winter migration flyway50, this aspect warrants further investigation.

Despite the significant findings, our study has certain limitations. First, the genomes included were not evenly distributed across all hosts and geographical regions due to the limited availability of sequences in public databases, which was beyond our control. Second, the analyses were based on draft genomes and heterogeneous public assemblies, and variations in sequencing and assembly pipelines may have influenced gene detection and subsequent analyses. Third, the cross-sectional design of the study limited our ability to assess the temporal dynamics or directionality of Acinetobacter baumannii transmission among animals, food, and humans. Additionally, the absence of corresponding human or environmental comparator isolates constrained our capacity to evaluate potential zoonotic links.

Conclusion

In the present study, we characterized the A. baumannii isolates recovered from foods of animal origin, including chicken and pork. Whole genome sequencing was performed on 9 A. baumannii isolates, and comparative genomics analyses were conducted on 60 genomes (including 9 from this study and 51 from various countries worldwide). The analyses focused on the resistome, virulome, mobilome, MLST, KL, OCL typing, and phylogenetic relationships. Avian strains were found to carry a significantly higher number of antimicrobial resistance genes, and those of bovine origin were found to have more potential for being pathogenic to humans. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind on A. baumannii strains of food-animal origin. The results highlight the importance of avian origin of A. baumannii strains in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance owing to the carriage of high antimicrobial resistance genes and mobile genetic elements.

Materials and methods

A. baumannii strains

In the present study, we investigated 9 A. baumannii isolates recovered from chicken and pork samples during ongoing investigations of food samples from India in 2023. Genomic DNA of the isolates was extracted Qiagen DNA mini kit. All the isolates were confirmed molecularly by PCR targeting 16 S rRNA gene (280 bp) for genus confirmation51 and blaOXA-51-like gene (353 bp)35 for A. baumannii species confirmation.

Phenotypic characterization of the isolates

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility of the isolates was assessed towards 7 different antibiotics by estimating MIC by broth microdilution method employing a commercial kit from HiMedia (India) containing dehydrated antimicrobials in serial 2-fold dilutions. Test was performed as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Antibiotics included in the study were: Imipenem (0.12–8 mcg/ml), Gentamicin (0.5 to 32 mcg/ml), Piperacillin tazobactam (2/4 to 128/4 mcg/ml), Cefepime, Tigecycline (0.06 to 4 mcg/ml), Polymixin B (0.25 to 16 mcg/ml), Ampicillin Sulbactam (1 to 64 mcg/ml). Results were interpreted as per CLSI guidelines52. Isolates exhibiting intermediate resistance were subjected to epsilometer test to detect possible hetero resistance among the isolates to respective antibiotics.

Biofilm formation assay

Quantitative biofilm assay was performed on microtitre plate as described by Zhang et al.53. Overnight-grown bacterial cultures were normalized to an OD600 0.5 and 200 µl of the aliquots were inoculated to each well of the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. All the planktonic cells were removed and wells were washed thrice with PBS, air dried, stained with crystal violet (1%), and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Wells were washed thrice with distilled water. The dye was solubilized with ethanol (95%), OD was measured at 540 nm, and the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Hemolysis assay

Hemolytic activity of the isolates was assessed as described earlier54. All the A. baumannii isolates were grown in LB broth overnight at 37 °C and the cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5 turbidity. Adjusted cultures were streaked onto 5% sheep blood agar and incubated at 37 °C for up to 6 days and observed for hemolytic activity. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and A. baumannii 19,606 were used as positive and negative controls respectively.

Assay for resistance to human serum

Assay for resistance to human serum was performed as described by Kim et al.18. Briefly, the isolates were grown in trypticase soy broth overnight at 37 °C. The cells were washed and the turbidity was adjusted to 1.0 at OD600 with sterile PBS (Ph-7.4). Human serum was collected from 5 healthy individuals, pooled together, and diluted to 40% with sterile PBS (Ph-7.4). Bacterial suspensions were added to the serum and the final concentration was adjusted to 1 × 107 CFU and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Viable bacterial count was determined at 0 h and 2 h time points and the results were interpreted.

Whole genome sequencing and assembly

Genomic DNA of the isolates was extracted using QIAGEN DNA mini kit following manufacturer’s guidelines. Whole genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform (NovaSeq) by outsourcing from Himedia, Mumbai, India. The raw data were checked for quality using the FastQC and QUAST tool55,56. After the quality check, the raw reads were assembled using Shovill V1.1.0 run with Spade assembler57. using default parameters except using appropriate switches for genus (Acinetobacter) and species (baumannii) and minimum contig length set to 200 bp with trim function turned on to trim the adapter sequences, besides “read error”, and “post-assembly correction” switches. Assembled genomes were subjected to species identification using Kraken2 v.2.0.7 tool58 against “minikraken2_v2_8GB” database and completeness check through “lineage workflow” of CheckM tool V0.1.3 with default settings of the tool59. Assembled genomes were submitted to NCBI.

Public genome dataset of A. baumannii

A. baumannii genomes (n = 51) from food animals, avians, and foods of animal origin submitted to NCBI database up to 12th June 2024 were downloaded for this study. The genomes were represented from various sources including avian, bovine, ovine, and porcine origins. Further,6 genomes from raw meat sources whose species origin information was unavailable were excluded from the study. The genomes were coded based on their source, country, and year of isolation for ease of analysis. We assigned the codes to each genome based on their isolation source in the format X_Y_Z, where “X” indicated the isolation source (species), “Y” indicated the Country of isolation, “Z” indicated the year of isolation, and “A” indicates the number of the isolate recovered in the same year. All the downloaded genomes were annotated with Prokka v1.14.6 and further proceeded for downstream analysis. Prokka run parameters included following switches – “add genes”, “add mRNA”, “genus Acinetobacter”, “species baumannii”, “kingdom bacteria”, “gcode 11”, “use genus”, and “rfam”. Nine genomes sequenced from isolates in the present study were analyzed together with publicly available genome datasets. The details of the genomes (n = 60; 51 downloaded from NCBI; 9 from the present study) and the corresponding metadata are furnished in Supplementary Table 1.

Multilocus sequence typing

In silico multi-locus sequence typing of all the genomes was performed submitting the assembled genomes to the MLST database (https://pubmlst.org/abaumannii/), according to the Pasteur scheme (60) and Oxford scheme as described by Bartual et al.61.

Resistance gene mining

Resistome analysis for all the genomes of A. baumannii included in this study was undertaken by using the Resistance Gene Identifier tool along with CARD database updated till 30.07.2024) to detect the AMR genes as well as to predict the mutations62. The tool was run with default parameters. RGI-CARD-Select Criteria: Perfect and strict hits only, Nudge ≥ 95% identity, Loose hits to Strict: Exclude nudge, Sequence Quality:-High quality/coverage. The PRAP tool63 was used to construct the pan- and core-resistomes. The resulting curves for both the pan- and core-resistomes were then fitted to a power law model, as previously outlined by Tettelin et al.64.

In silico virulence factor analysis and pathogenic potential

Analysis of virulence genes in the genomes of A. baumannii was performed by employing ABRicate tool v1.0.1 with the virulence factor database (VFDB) updated upto 26 April 202465. Pathogenic potential of the isolates was ascertained by submitting the assembled genomes to the PathogenFinder web tool using default parameters66.

Mobilome analysis (transposons and integrons)

A.baumannii genomes were searched for the presence of transposons and integrons deploying Bacant tool v3.3.3 using integronDB v.2.0 and transposonDB v.2.0 database updated till 28 July 2024 with similarity threshold set at 80%67.

KL and OCL typing

KL and OCL typing of A. baumannii genomes was conducted using the Kaptive tool v3.1.0 with the Kaptive database updated to 25.02.2025 using the default tool parameters68.

Pangenome analysis

Phylogeny Enhanced Pipeline for pan-genome (PEPPAN) tool v1.0.5 was employed to analyze the pan-genome encompassing both core and accessory genes of the Acinetobacter baumannii genomes using the gff files from prokka annotation and was run with 95% identity in amino acid sequences69. Power-law functions were used to estimate the trends in the development of pan-genome and core-genome as described earlier64. The gene presence–absence matrix generated during pan-genome analysis was channeled into the Scoary tool along with Benjamini–Hochberg’s method for multiple comparisons correction, to estimate presence or absence of specific genes among isolates originating from different hosts70.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic tree of all the 60 genomes of A. baumannii strains was constructed to ascertain the genetic relationship among the isolates. Initially, all the genomes of A. baumannii (n = 60) were aligned with Roary tool v.3.13.0 with PRANK module and the alignment was refined with Gblocks v0.91b tool using default settings to remove inaccurately aligned regions. Further, phylogenetic tree was constructed with core gene alignments using IQtree multicore v2.2.0.3 run with modelfinder tool integrated in IQTree software with default parameters alongwith 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replications and a fixed seed value to ensure reproducibility. Following ModelFinder run the best model was identified as TIM + F + R8 which was then used for construction of the phylogenetic tree by the tool. Clusters in the phylogenetic tree were identified with TreeCluster tool v1.0.332. Initially, the best threshold for cluster identification was identified with a threshold-free run. The best threshold value (t = 0.0013) was then used for the identification of clusters with median-clade distance method as described by Balaban et al.71. The resultant dendrogram was visualized using FigTree version 1.4.572.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were undertaken with R studio v.2022.02.3. Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni correction was performed to analyse the statistical association between the source and other factors (resistance genes, virulence factors, transposons, and pathogenic potential) while the statistical significance was set as p < 0.05 at 95% confidence level.
