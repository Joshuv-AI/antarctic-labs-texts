Risk assessment and prediction of early blood transfusion after joint replacement surgery: a clinical decision support model based on machine learning
Authors: Tianyou Xing, Jincai Duan, Tianjie Xiao, Zhihui Wang, Huigeng Zhao, Wei Qin, Di Wu, Changjiang Shi, Yuanliang Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative blood transfusion remains a major challenge in joint arthroplasty, with substantial variability in clinical decision-making. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate machine learning models for early postoperative transfusion risk assessment to support early postoperative decision-making, with the clinical objective of minimizing unnecessary transfusions and improving perioperative blood resource utilization after joint arthroplasty.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 1,954 patients who underwent primary or revision hip or knee arthroplasty between June 2019 and December 2024. Patients were categorized according to whether they received allogeneic transfusion within 72 h postoperatively. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and validation (30%) cohorts. Logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM) models were developed based on 15 key variables identified through LASSO regression. Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were evaluated using ROC curves, Hosmer–Lemeshow tests, Brier scores, calibration slopes/intercepts, and decision curve analysis (DCA). SHAP analysis was used to interpret variable importance.ResultsAmong all patients, 172 (8.8%) received transfusions. All three models showed good discrimination (AUC > 0.75). The RF model achieved the best overall performance (training AUC = 1.000; validation AUC = 0.808, sensitivity = 87.5%, specificity = 59.0%; Brier score = 0.0651). The LR model also demonstrated stable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.097, Brier = 0.0679), while the SVM model achieved moderate discrimination (validation AUC = 0.776) but weaker calibration. DCA confirmed that the RF model provided the greatest net clinical benefit within a threshold range of 0.1–0.75. SHAP analysis of RF model showed that postoperative hemoglobin, preoperative hemoglobin and operation time were the most important predictors.ConclusionPostoperative and preoperative haemoglobin levels, along with operative duration, were important predictors of early postoperative transfusion. The RF model demonstrated good predictive performance and clinical value, supporting its potential use for individualized transfusion risk assessment in patients undergoing joint arthroplasty.Keywords: Joint arthroplasty, Blood transfusion, Machine learning, SHAP, Clinical prediction

Introduction

With improving quality of life and progress in the medical field, the average life expectancy is increasing steadily. UN projections indicate that the average lifespan will exceed 80 years. In China, the proportion of people aged 60 and above increased from 13.32% in 2010 to 18.73% in 2020 [1, 2], reflecting a growing ageing population. This demographic shift has led to an increased incidence of age-related musculoskeletal disorders, such as osteoarthritis (OA), femoral head necrosis, femoral neck fractures, periprosthetic fractures, and prosthetic loosening [3], which cause pain, joint deformity, and impaired mobility [4], severely harming patients’ mental health and quality of life.

Total joint replacement (TJR) is the primary treatment for advanced joint diseases (e.g., end-stage OA and femoral head osteonecrosis) and primary treatment for patients who require revision due to traumatic injury [5, 6]. The demand for TJR, especially for hip and knee arthroplasty, is increasing with these demographic changes—the number of knee and hip arthroplasty procedures is projected to increase by 110% and 129%, respectively, by 2030 [7]. However, perioperative complications, including overt and occult blood loss, are noteworthy. Anaemia reduces blood oxygen-carrying capacity, slows recovery, prolongs hospital stays, and increases transfusion rates [8]. Carson et al. [9] reported that a Hb concentration < 60 g/L is correlated with significantly greater postoperative complications and mortality.

Postoperative transfusion is a key measure for addressing perioperative anaemia-related complications. Current guidelines [10] suggest that blood transfusion should be performed when the haemoglobin (Hb) concentration is less than 70 g per litre (for patients with heart diseases, it should be less than 80 g per litre). When the Hb concentration is between 70 and 100 g per litre, the need for transfusion should be determined based on factors such as the patient’s cardiac and pulmonary compensatory capacity, metabolic rate, age, and other clinical factors. Despite clear Hb thresholds, clinical practice varies because of comorbidities, intraoperative blood loss, and surgeon judgement, resulting in transfusion rates ranging from 2.5% to 70% [11, 12]; this variation indicates inconsistent decision-making and the potential occurrence of overtransfusion.

The literature shows that advanced age, comorbidities, the female sex, a low preoperative Hb (pre-Hb), and regional differences are linked to postoperative transfusion, with preoperative anaemia as a major risk factor [13]. Decreased perioperative Hb levels often necessitate allogeneic transfusion [14], which increases the risks of haemolytic/allergic reactions, transfusion-associated circulatory overload, acute lung injury, infections, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality [15–17]. Post-TJR transfusion increases the risk of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), which is a catastrophic complication, by 2.1 times (95% CI: 1.1–3.9) [18]. Additionally, unnecessary transfusions waste medical resources and increase the burden on patients.

The accurate identification of patients at high risk for postoperative blood transfusion is crucial for personalized perioperative management and could help reduce surgical risks, postoperative complications, and excessive blood product consumption, which would be significant for optimizing medical care and lowering patient medical costs. The best clinical actions triggered by high-risk predictions may vary significantly depending on the clinical context (e.g., elective surgery versus surgery for traumatic fractures). Therefore, customized intervention measures for high-risk patients may range from preoperative optimization (e.g., correcting anaemia and postponing elective surgery) to enhanced perioperative preparations (e.g., ensuring the availability of blood products) [19]. Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial intelligence that possesses powerful data processing capabilities. It has the ability to simulate nonlinear relationships, automatically detect interactions, and handle high-dimensional data without the need to explicitly specify the interaction terms beforehand [20–22]. Although previous studies have applied ML in similar contexts, they were limited by small data sets or insufficient validation, restricting model generalizability [23].

The aim of this study was to construct and validate several machine learning-based models to predict the risk of allogeneic blood transfusion in the early stage after joint replacement surgery, that is, transfusion within 72 h after surgery [24]. A moderately large, real-world dataset was used to train and evaluate the models, incorporating clinically accessible variables. We hypothesized that machine learning models could outperform conventional logistic regression by capturing nonlinear interactions among perioperative variables. The ultimate goal is to facilitate the identification of high-risk patients and assist surgeons in tailoring personalized interventions, thereby reducing the transfusion rate, avoiding transfusion-related complications, and optimizing the use of blood resources.

Materials and methods

Patient source and grouping

This retrospective study included patients who underwent knee arthroplasty (unicompartmental knee arthroplasty and total knee arthroplasty), hip arthroplasty (bipolar hemiarthroplasty and total hip arthroplasty) or prosthesis revision surgery at the Department of Joint Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical College, from June 2019 to December 2024. All surgeries were performed by a fixed team of five senior attending orthopaedic surgeons, all of whom are chief physicians specializing in joint surgery with more than 15 years of experience. Each surgeon on this team performs more than 200 joint replacement procedures annually.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of hip or knee osteoarthritis, femoral head necrosis, femoral neck fracture, rheumatoid arthritis, or articular tuberculosis through imaging examinations; (2) complete medical and laboratory data; (3) treatment with unilateral joint replacement surgery or prosthesis revision surgery. The exclusion criteria were (1) contraindications to blood transfusion and (2) incomplete medical records.

After applying these criteria, a total of 2,534 patients were screened, with no perioperative deaths observed. Of these, 1,954 cases (77.1%) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Among these patients, 172 (8.8%) received an allogeneic blood transfusion. Data segmentation was accomplished using the “caret” package in R. To ensure the reproducibility of the results, the random seed was set first using “set.seed(123); subsequently, the “createDataPartition” function was used to randomly divide the dataset into a training set and an internal validation set at a ratio of 7:3. Finally, the training set (70%, n = 1,370) and the internal validation set (30%, n = 584) were obtained. The flowchart depicting patient selection is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of patient selection

Transfusion status was determined according to the “Guidelines for Perioperative Blood Transfusion and Adjunctive Therapy” [10] and our hospital’s transfusion protocol; for those patients whose Hb levels are near the threshold, the decision on whether to administer a blood transfusion is made based on the clinical symptoms and signs that correspond to the blood transfusion guidelines at that time. This study used whether transfusion was received within 72 hours postoperatively as the outcome and divided the cohort into two subgroups: a transfusion group and a nontransfusion group.

Variable collection

The relevant variables related to the perioperative period (from hospital admission to 72 h postoperatively) collected through our hospital’s electronic medical record system included the following: (1) Demographic and clinical characteristics: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), blood type, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, cerebral infarction, and others). (2) Surgery-related variables: initial diagnosis, surgical method, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, anaesthesia method, operative duration, tourniquet duration (applicable only to knee arthroplasty cases; for hip arthroplasty patients, this variable was coded as zero to represent the absence of tourniquet use—all variables were standardized and processed through feature selection methods [e.g., LASSO, RF, and SVM] to minimize potential bias from procedure-specific differences), intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion, intraoperative and postoperative administration of tranexamic acid via the intravenous route, and total postoperative drainage volume (from the time the drainage tube was inserted until it was removed). (3) Laboratory variables: routine blood tests (red blood cells, Hb, and platelets) and biochemical indicators (albumin, alanine aminotransferase, creatinine, potassium, sodium, chloride, and calcium) at admission and 24 h after surgery; and coagulation function indicators (i.e., D-dimer level, prothrombin time, prothrombin activity, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen level, and fibrin degradation products) only at admission. The events-per-variable (EPV) ratio exceeded 10 for the selected predictors, satisfying commonly accepted criteria for model stability and preventing overfitting.

Missing data were imputed using a nonparametric random forest algorithm implemented in the missForest package (R version 4.4.2). This approach iteratively predicts missing values based on the remaining observed variables, minimizing imputation bias in complex clinical datasets. The overall proportion of missing values across variables was less than 5%. To verify the robustness of the results, a multiple imputation procedure (mouse package, predictive mean matching, m = 5) was conducted, which yielded similar model performance.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the administration of an allogeneic blood transfusion in the early postoperative period (within 72 h).

Model Building and evaluation

Model construction (training cohort)

Logistic regression (LR) model:

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to screen the included variables, and the optimal λ value was selected by 10-fold cross-validation. The selected variables were included in univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, and the significant variables selected by LASSO were used to construct the final LR model and construct a nomogram. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to evaluate multicollinearity among the 15 significant predictor variables selected by LASSO. Although preoperative and postoperative haemoglobin levels and red blood cell counts showed high VIF values because of their inherent physiological relevance, these parameters were retained to maintain clinical interpretability.

Random forest (RF) model:

The RF model was implemented using the randomForest package in R. The model was constructed with 500 decision trees (ntree = 500), which was determined to be sufficient because the out-of-bag (OOB) error stabilized well before this point. For other key hyperparameters, we employed the package defaults: hyperparameters, including maximum tree depth (max_depth) and minimum number of samples per split (min_samples_split), were optimized using a grid search with 5-fold cross-validation. The final selected parameters were max_depth = 10, min_samples_split = 2, and max_features = ‘sqrt’. These settings allow the individual trees to fully capture complex patterns and interactions within the data, with the ensemble mechanism effectively mitigating the risk of overfitting. The model was generated via bootstrap resampling from the training cohort only.

Support vector machine (SVM) model:

The SVM model was implemented using the e1071 package in R. A radial basis function (RBF) kernel was selected because of its proven ability to capture complex, nonlinear decision boundaries between classes, which is often the case in clinical data. Prior to model training, all predictor variables were centred and scaled based on the mean and standard deviation of the training cohort, and these same parameters were subsequently applied to the validation cohort. For the hyperparameters, the parameters C and gamma were tuned by 10-fold cross-validation over a grid search. The optimal configuration was C = 10 and gamma = 0.01. These default settings provide a robust baseline for model performance. The model was trained exclusively on the training cohort to construct a hyperplane that maximized the margin between the transfusion and nontransfusion groups.

Model evaluation (validation cohort)

The model underwent the following effective evaluations:

Primary model performance metric:

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis: ROC curves were plotted for each model, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. When the AUC was ≥ 0.75, the model was considered to have good predictive value.

Secondary performance metrics:

Decision curve analysis (DCA): DCA was used to evaluate the benefit of the constructed model in actual clinical decision-making.

Decision curve analysis (DCA): DCA was used to evaluate the benefit of the constructed model in actual clinical decision-making.

Calibration curve: The consistency and accuracy between the risk predicted by the model and the actual observed risk were assessed, and the fitting performance of the model was evaluated.

Sensitivity, specificity and 95% confidence interval (CI) at the optimal Youden index cut-off point.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis: The best model for this study, SHAP analysis, was used to interpret the predictions of the model and quantify the contribution of important variables to the outcome. If the SHAP value of a feature was positive, the feature was considered to increase the predicted risk of the current sample to a level greater than the average predicted risk of all the samples; the larger the absolute value of the SHAP value of a feature, the stronger the influence of the feature on the prediction result of the current sample [25].

The overall transfusion rate was 8.8%, indicating moderate class imbalance. Because this imbalance was within an acceptable range and the ensemble models were relatively robust to such imbalance, no resampling or class weighting was applied. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using AUC, calibration, and decision curve analyses.

Data analyses

All data were analysed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 4.4.2; http://www.Rproject.org). In this study, the Shapiro–Wilk (SW) test was used to assess the normality of all continuous variables. Normally distributed data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and independent sample t tests were used for group comparisons. Nonnormally distributed data are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentages) and were analysed using Pearson’s chi-square test. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Clinical features

A total of 1,954 patients were ultimately included in this study, of whom 766 (39%) were men and 1,188 (61%) were women. Overall, 172 patients (8.8%) received an allogeneic transfusion within 72 h after surgery. However, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of blood type, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, cerebral infarction, and others), ASA classification, or anaesthesia method (P > 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences between the training cohort (N = 1,370) and the validation cohort (N = 584) (P > 0.05).

Feature selection via LASSO and multivariate logistic regression

To avoid variable redundancy in the model, we used LASSO regression to select features from 49 candidate variables. Fifteen key predictors were retained at the optimal λ = 0.0067 (Fig. 2): age, BMI, length of stay, operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, pre-Hb, preoperative creatinine, preoperative APTT, postoperative Hb (pos-Hb), postoperative albumin, postoperative ALT, total drainage, surgical procedure, and intraoperative and postoperative use of tranexamic acid. The LASSO algorithm excluded 34 of the 49 initially included variables, primarily those with low variance, strong multicollinearity, or minimal contribution to the predictive performance of the model. Importantly, clinically meaningful factors such as liver disease, anticoagulant use, and coagulation abnormalities were excluded largely because of their low prevalence (< 2%) rather than statistical irrelevance. Their potential effects are likely represented indirectly by correlated predictors such as haemoglobin level and operative duration. Although several variables were not independently significant in the multivariate logistic regression (Table 2), all 15 predictors selected by the LASSO algorithm were retained in the final model, as LASSO selection prioritizes overall model performance and accounts for variable interactions. The results of the VIF analysis revealed high collinearity among the pre- and postoperative haematologic variables (maximum VIF = 33.8 for postoperative RBC), which is expected given their biological association. The other variables had acceptable VIF values (< 5).

A nomogram was subsequently developed based on these 15 predictors to estimate the risk of postoperative blood transfusion (Fig. 3). In the training cohort, the logistic regression (LR) model achieved an AUC of 0.839 (specificity = 79.1%, sensitivity = 75.8%, 95% CI: 0.797–0.876), and in the validation cohort, the AUC was 0.783 (specificity = 66.6%, sensitivity = 81.2%, 95% CI: 0.723–0.840) (Fig. 4A, B). The calibration curves for both cohorts demonstrated good visual agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities, with mean absolute errors of 0.008 and 0.010, respectively (Fig. 4C, D). Quantitative calibration metrics further supported these findings: the LR model showed acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.097, Brier score = 0.0679, calibration slope = 7.10, intercept = − 3.18). These results indicate good agreement in the midrange probabilities, although the relatively steep slope suggests overestimation at higher risk levels. Decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed greater net clinical benefit than the “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies within a risk threshold of 0.1–0.7 (Fig. 4E, F).

RF model

After hyperparameter tuning, the RF model achieved an OOB error rate of 8.54%, and the top 15 key variables were ultimately displayed (Fig. 5). The AUC of the RF model in the training cohort was 1.000 (specificity = 100%, sensitivity = 100%, 95% CI: 1.000–1.000). In the validation cohort, the AUC of the model was 0.808 (specificity = 59.0%, sensitivity = 87.5%, 95% CI: 0.747–0.861) (Fig. 6A, B). The observed decline in validation performance compared with the perfect training AUC indicates some degree of overfitting, which is expected in ensemble-based methods trained on moderately imbalanced datasets. Nevertheless, the validation AUC and calibration metrics (Brier score = 0.0651, slope = 8.15, intercept = − 3.48) confirm good generalizability and model stability (Fig. 6D). DCA demonstrated superior net clinical benefit across the thresholds of 0.1–0.75 (Fig. 6F).

SVM model

Using an RBF kernel and 10-fold cross-validation for parameter tuning, the SVM model achieved an AUC of 0.943 in the training cohort (specificity = 97.4%, sensitivity = 88.7%, 95% CI: 0.915–0.968) and 0.776 in the validation cohort (specificity = 76.5%, sensitivity = 68.8%, 95% CI: 0.697–0.848) (Fig. 7A, B). The calibration plot of the SVM model showed moderate agreement with the observed outcomes, as reflected by a Brier score of 0.0688 and a slight overestimation tendency at higher predicted risks (slope = 7.14, intercept = − 3.15) (Fig. 7D). Discussion

In this retrospective study, after data preprocessing, feature selection, and model construction, we identified key predictors of postoperative blood transfusion and developed three machine learning models—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM) models. Among them, the RF model demonstrated the best overall predictive performance based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, decision curve analysis (DCA), and quantitative calibration metrics. Specifically, the RF model demonstrated the greatest discrimination (AUC = 0.808, sensitivity = 87.5%) and the lowest Brier score (0.0651), with good agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities. Although the OOB error rate (8.54%) was numerically close to the transfusion incidence (8.8%), this reflects proportional misclassification rather than baseline-level prediction, and the validation AUC confirms adequate discriminative ability. The LR model also exhibited stable and interpretable performance (AUC = 0.783, H–L P = 0.097, Brier score = 0.0679), with good calibration across clinically relevant probability ranges. In contrast, the SVM model achieved acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.776, Brier score = 0.0688) but demonstrated inferior calibration compared with the LR and RF models (Fig. 7D), a known limitation of SVM-based probabilistic estimation. This discrepancy arises from the intrinsic design of SVM models, which optimize classification margins rather than probability calibration. Consequently, probability outputs generated through Platt scaling may be unstable in relatively small or imbalanced datasets, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of true transfusion probabilities. Clinically, such miscalibration may limit the interpretability of SVM-derived risks for individualized decision-making or transfusion threshold adjustment.

In contrast, the LR and RF models yielded more reliable probability estimates and better alignment between the predicted and observed outcomes, supporting their use in clinical decision support systems. The RF model, in particular, demonstrated robust calibration and the broadest net benefit range in DCA, highlighting its potential to optimize blood resource utilization and enhance perioperative patient safety. The SHAP analysis further revealed the top 15 key variables in the RF model, among which postoperative haemoglobin, preoperative haemoglobin, and operative duration were the most influential predictors. Future research should focus on external multicentre validation, prospective application, and exploration of calibration improvement methods such as isotonic regression or ensemble postprocessing to further enhance model generalizability and clinical reliability.

Pos-Hb, the most important risk factor in this study, can directly reflect the patient’s physiological status and transfusion need in a timely manner, with lower levels significantly increasing the likelihood of transfusion, underscoring the importance of routine postoperative haematologic monitoring. Previous studies have demonstrated that advanced age and pre-Hb are important risk factors for perioperative transfusion in joint arthroplasty. Adunsky et al. [26] reported that lower preoperative Hb levels increased the risk of perioperative transfusion by 5-fold but that age was not a risk factor. The reason for this significant difference may be related to population characteristics, sample size, and statistical methods. Therefore, actively correcting anaemia before surgery by supplementation with iron or erythropoietin (EPO) to increase the patient’s Hb level can effectively reduce the need for postoperative transfusion.

This study revealed that the duration of surgery was positively correlated with the risk of postoperative blood transfusion. A prolonged operation exacerbates the consumption of coagulation factors, delays the coagulation process, and increases intraoperative blood loss, thus increasing the need for transfusion. Additionally, a longer surgery may induce systemic inflammatory responses that impair coagulation function [27]. Therefore, meticulous preoperative planning and efficient surgical techniques are essential to minimize the operative duration and reduce transfusion requirements. The use of tranexamic acid (TXA) inhibits fibrinolysis by competitively blocking the binding of plasminogen to fibrin, thereby stabilizing the formed blood clots and reducing intraoperative bleeding.

Interestingly, postoperative blood calcium was also identified as a key predictor of transfusion. Louis et al. [28] reported that hypocalcaemia was independently associated with increased transfusion risk. This association may be related to the function of calcium as a cofactor in multiple steps of the coagulation cascade. Decreased calcium levels can impair prothrombin complex formation and delay thrombin generation, potentially prolonging the coagulation time and promoting bleeding [29, 30]. Although this explanation remains speculative, our findings underscore the potential importance of monitoring postoperative calcium levels in transfusion risk stratification.

Notably, differences were observed in the key variables identified by the LR and RF models. In the LR model, the risk of transfusion progressively increased as the BMI decreased, and greater intraoperative blood loss further elevated the likelihood of transfusion. Previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between BMI and transfusion risk—some have shown a negative correlation, whereas others have suggested that a higher BMI may increase intraoperative blood loss [31]. One possible explanation is that patients with a higher BMI generally have a larger circulating blood volume and greater physiological tolerance to blood loss, thereby reducing the need for transfusion. In contrast, BMI and intraoperative blood loss were not among the most important features in the RF model. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in how the two algorithms process data structures and feature interactions rather than true contradictions in clinical reasoning. The logistic regression model, which is linear, may emphasize independent additive effects, whereas the random forest model captures nonlinear and interaction effects between features. For example, the impact of intraoperative blood loss may be indirectly represented through correlated variables such as Hb. The observed disagreement in feature importance rankings among the LR, RF, and SVM models reflects the distinct analytical perspectives of each algorithm rather than inconsistency or unreliability. LR highlights linear additive effects, random forest captures nonlinear and interaction terms, and SVM emphasizes multidimensional separability. This variation indicates that transfusion risk is influenced by both linear and nonlinear mechanisms, which cannot be fully represented by any single model. Importantly, several major predictors—such as pre-Hb, operative duration, and postoperative calcium—remained consistently important across the models, reinforcing their robustness. Conversely, discrepancies in secondary variables (e.g., BMI and intraoperative blood loss) suggest that model interpretation should focus on reproducible predictors, while model disagreement itself provides valuable insight into the multidimensional nature of clinical data. These findings emphasize the need for multimodel validation before a prediction model can be translated into a clinical decision support system.

Although primary and revision arthroplasties differ in terms of surgical complexity and typical blood loss, this study intentionally developed an integrated predictive model for all joint replacement types. This approach reflects real-world clinical workflows, where perioperative management protocols are often shared across arthroplasty procedures. A unified model simplifies clinical application by allowing clinicians to perform transfusion risk assessment without selecting among multiple procedure-specific tools. Moreover, procedure type was incorporated as a categorical feature within the model, enabling the algorithm to internally account for risk differences among total knee, total hip, and revision arthroplasty. Similar integrative modelling strategies have been used successfully in previous studies combining heterogeneous surgical populations [32]. While this approach enhances usability and generalizability, we acknowledge that stratified modelling could further refine accuracy and will explore this approach in future multicentre validation studies.

Although some studies have attempted to use traditional logistic regression or scoring systems to predict the perioperative blood transfusion risk, models specifically targeting the joint replacement surgery population are still relatively scarce. In the TKA postoperative blood transfusion model constructed by Mohammed et al. [33], the AUCs of the LR, gradient boosting machine, and RF models were 0.7074, 0.797, and 0.783, respectively, and the AUC of the RF model constructed by Chen et al. [34] for predicting whether blood transfusion is needed after TKA was 0.773. In contrast, the AUC of the random forest model in this study was 0.808, suggesting a certain improvement in discrimination performance. The advantages of this model lie not only in the improvement of predictive performance but also in its clinical practicality and interpretability by combining preoperative and early postoperative factors, which can provide decision support for individualized blood transfusion management. However, the present model was not directly compared with existing models. Future multicentre studies will include such comparisons to further verify the external applicability and generalizability of the model.

A SHAP analysis was also conducted for the best model of this study to uniformly assess the contribution of each feature, thereby enabling a clearer understanding of the contribution of important predictive factors. Although different models involve varying assessments of feature importance, the results collectively suggest that transfusion risk is multifactorial. Future studies need to combine larger samples and dynamic intraoperative data to more accurately analyse the synergistic mechanisms of multiple factors.

The ultimate goal of a predictive model is to inform and enhance clinical decision-making. Our model, which leverages readily available preoperative and early postoperative data, can be integrated into clinical workflows at two critical time points: preoperatively for elective cases and immediately postoperatively for all patients. For patients undergoing elective joint arthroplasty (e.g., for osteoarthritis), the identification of high-risk patients prior to surgery allows proactive optimization—such as correcting preoperative anaemia through iron supplementation or erythropoietin therapy, considering autologous blood donation, or applying restrictive transfusion thresholds. This “optimize-then-operate” approach can effectively reduce perioperative transfusion needs and improve overall outcomes. In contrast, for patients requiring urgent surgery (e.g., for femoral neck fracture), surgical delay is not feasible. In these cases, the value of the model lies in preparedness rather than prevention—a high-risk prediction should prompt enhanced vigilance, including ensuring cross-matched blood availability, meticulous haemostasis, aggressive intraoperative tranexamic acid use, and close post-Hb monitoring. Among the three models developed, the random forest (RF) model provided the best balance between discrimination and clinical net benefit. Although its specificity was moderate (59.0%), the resulting false-positive rate is clinically acceptable for perioperative risk management, where the priority is to minimize missed high-risk patients rather than avoid overprediction. The logistic regression model, visualized as a nomogram, provides straightforward interpretability, whereas the RF model can be transformed into a web-based or EMR-integrated decision-support tool to deliver real-time risk alerts.

Rather than aiming to identify new predictors, the primary contribution of this study lies in translating existing clinical knowledge into a robust, generalizable, and easily applicable decision-support framework that can assist clinicians in early risk stratification across different surgical types, without the need for separate models for each procedure. This integrative approach enhances the clinical practicality of machine learning and demonstrates how data-driven modelling can be used to operationalize evidence-based medicine in perioperative transfusion management.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre retrospective study without external validation. The performance reported here may therefore overestimate real-world generalizability, as model performance typically decreases when applied to new settings with different patient populations, surgical techniques, or transfusion protocols. Second, the cohort combined both primary and revision arthroplasties of the hip and knee, which may introduce heterogeneity, and procedure-specific models could yield greater predictive precision. Third, temporal variations over the 5.5-year study period (2019–2024) may have influenced transfusion practices and TXA use, affecting model calibration. Fourth, the tourniquet duration was recorded only for knee arthroplasty patients and was coded as zero for hip arthroplasty, which could introduce minor systematic bias. Finally, future studies using large multicentre datasets with external validation are warranted to enhance model generalizability and clinical applicability.

Conclusions

This study developed a machine learning–based model for early identification of patients at risk of postoperative transfusion following joint arthroplasty. While the model demonstrated promising discrimination and potential utility, several constraints must be recognized. Because key predictors include postoperative laboratory variables, its applicability for preoperative decision-making is limited. In addition, the single-centre development and the RF model’s modest specificity indicate that further refinement and external validation are necessary before clinical implementation.

The potential benefits of streamlined workflow and individualized risk stratification remain theoretical and require prospective studies to confirm real-world effectiveness.

Future work should prioritize the development of preoperative-only prediction tools, procedure-specific model refinement, and integration of validated models into electronic clinical decision support systems, alongside multicentre prospective evaluation to enhance generalizability.
