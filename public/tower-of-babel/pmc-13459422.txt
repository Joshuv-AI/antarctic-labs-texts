Temporal and spatial-adapted metal ions delivery system for spinal cord injury repair with Mg-Zn-doped bilayer bioactive glasses
Authors: Su Xinjin, Wu Yuantao, Xu Zheng, Kong Qingjie, Huo Shicheng, Chen Xiongsheng, Zhu Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In clinical practice, ion supplementation therapy has demonstrated excellent clinical efficacy in spinal cord injury (SCI) repair. However, due to the spatiotemporal dynamics of ion imbalance following SCI, targeted phase-adapted ion supplementation strategies for SCI repair remain challenging. To address this issue, a spatiotemporally adapted metal-ion delivery system, Mg-Zn-doped bilayer bioactive glasses (Mg-Zn/BGs), was developed. It had a diameter of approximately 125 nm and comprised an Mg/BGs outer shell and a Zn/BGs inner core. The core-shell structure of Mg-Zn/BGs enables phase-specific sustained release of Mg2+/Zn2+ during SCI pathological progression. The early release of Mg2+ attenuated macrophage-induced inflammation by promoting macrophage polarization toward M2 type, which further inhibited scar formation, and thereby created a favorable microenvironment for subsequent neural regeneration. The release of Zn2+ at the late phase effectively promoted neural cell proliferation and regeneration, which was accompanied by axonal regeneration and re-myelination, leading to significant behavioral recovery of SCI mice. This study highlights the essential regulatory functions of metal ions across distinct stages of SCI recovery, demonstrating the development of a core-shell structured delivery platform capable of stage-optimized ion release. The engineered Mg-Zn/BGs effectively bridges the therapeutic gap between inflammation modulation and neuronal tissue regeneration, offering a potential solution for SCI treatment through its dual-phase ion coordination mechanism.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04630-9.

Introduction

Metal ions homeostasis plays a critical role in maintaining normal physiological functions of the spinal cord [1, 2]. For example, magnesium (Mg²+) serves as a cofactor for over 300 enzymes, including ATPases, glycolysis-related enzymes (such as phosphofructokinase), and DNA/RNA polymerases [3]. It maintains intracellular and extracellular ion gradients by activating Na+/K+-ATPase and participates in ATP generation, providing energy support for neurons. Additionally, zinc ions (Zn2+) promote neural regeneration and synapse formation while exerting antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects [4, 5]. Through their regulatory effects on cellular signaling pathways, enzyme functionality, and biochemical metabolism, these metal ions play a crucial role in ensuring the proper development and maintenance of normal neurological functioning [6, 7].

The disruption of metal ions homeostasis is one of the central pathological mechanisms underlying spinal cord injury (SCI) [8]. The alterations in metal ion concentrations following SCI critically influence secondary injury processes and neural repair, with dynamic fluctuations in magnesium ions (Mg2+) and zinc ions (Zn2+) playing a pivotal role [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that magnesium ion (Mg2+) concentrations exhibit a persistent decline during the initial 7-day period following SCI [9]. This ionic depletion mechanism primarily stems from acute-phase pathological changes in transmembrane ionic regulation, involving both inflammatory-driven modifications to membrane permeability and subsequent breakdown of intra/extracellular ionic equilibrium. As an endogenous NMDA receptor antagonist, diminished Mg2+ levels reduce its regulatory inhibition of these receptors, resulting in excessive calcium ion (Ca2+) influx and subsequent neuronal degeneration [10, 11]. This hypo-magnesemic state also triggers the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, compromises antioxidant defenses, and disrupts cellular energy metabolism, thereby collectively amplifying secondary injury mechanisms [12]. Early intervention with magnesium supplementation demonstrates neuroprotective potential by counteracting NMDA receptor overactivation and attenuating oxidative damage [13]. Supporting this, Temkin et al. [14] demonstrated that administering magnesium sulfate within the first post-injury week markedly improves tissue preservation and neural network excitability. Hence, Mg2+ supplementation is recommended during the early phase of SCI.

Additionally, as an essential cofactor for enzymatic processes involving DNA/RNA polymerases and carbonic anhydrase, Zn2+ are extensively liberated from neuronal storage pools into the extracellular space, resulting in marked increases in interstitial Zn2+ levels [15]. Notably, serum Zn2+ concentrations progressively decrease during this process, while compensatory elevation of Zn2+ levels around the injury site facilitates neural repair [16, 17]. This phenomenon indicates that dynamic redistribution of zinc ions within local tissues establishes a microenvironment conducive to neural regeneration. Such spatially specific regulatory mechanisms of zinc ions may exert beneficial effects on axonal regrowth and myelin remodeling. However, the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells during the acute phase necessitates substantial Zn2+ sequestration from the injury microenvironment, with consumption rates directly proportional to mechanical trauma severity, ultimately inducing zinc deficiency [18]. Paradoxically, this micronutrient demonstrates indispensable dual functionality during the regenerative phase. It impedes astroglia scar maturation through matrix metalloproteinase modulation while concurrently acting as integral structural components for zinc finger transcriptional regulators that orchestrate neural repair-associated gene expression programs [18]. Therefore, targeted restoration of metal ion homeostasis across distinct SCI progression phases holds therapeutic potential for enhancing neural repair and regeneration post-SCI.

Achieving stable and precise spatiotemporal delivery of metal ions remains challenging. The biphasic therapeutic demands that acute-phase Mg2+ supplementation to mitigate secondary injury cascades and regenerative-phase Zn2+ maintenance to orchestrate axonogenesis and remyelination, necessitate smart-responsive systems that can dynamically interface with the evolving lesion microenvironment. In recent years, core-shell structured materials, particularly polymer-based systems and liposomal membranes, have been investigated for SCI therapeutic applications [19]. Although these constructs exhibit core-shell morphology, the inherent rigidity of their constituent materials imposes constraints on achieving precise spatiotemporal control over the release kinetics of encapsulated drugs or cytokines [20, 21]. Additionally, polymeric carriers may suffer from complex degradation behavior and potential inflammatory responses, and liposome-based systems often exhibit limited structural stability and rapid drug leakage [20, 21].

In the present study, we designed and synthesized a novel bilayer bioactive glass system co-doped with Mg and Zn (Mg-Zn/BGs) to enable spatiotemporally adaptive metal ion delivery for SCI therapeutics (Scheme 1). In the initial phase, the Mg-doped outer layer of bioactive glasses (Mg/BGs) undergoes progressive degradation, releasing Mg2+ to mitigate inflammatory cell infiltration and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, thereby establishing a favorable microenvironment for subsequent neural regeneration. Subsequently, the Zn-doped inner layer (Zn/BGs) gradually degrades, liberating Zn2+ to coordinately promote axonal regrowth and remyelination. Compared with previous core-shell structured materials, Mg-Zn/BGs exhibits several advantages, including: Inorganic stability and inherent bioactivity; Controlled and sustained ion release behavior; Potential for spatiotemporal regulation through core–shell design; Good biocompatibility and biodegradability. The effects of Mg-Zn/BGs on SCI repair were comprehensively evaluated by in vitro and in vivo studies. Based on the results, the core-shell constructed Mg-Zn/BGs may be an alternative ions therapy for SCI repair.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Mg-Zn/BGs

Figure 1a showed the typical transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs. The Zn/BGs exhibited uniformly spherical morphology, composed predominantly of C, O, N, Si, Ca and Zn (Figure S1). The results were consistent with previous studies [22]. In contrast, Mg-Zn/BGs displayed distinct core-shell architectures, where EDS mapping revealed magnesium enrichment in the outer layer and zinc concentration within the core region (Fig. 1b). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) demonstrated a monodisperse distribution pattern for Mg-Zn/BGs, with a mean particle diameter of 125.3 nm, significantly higher than Zn/BGs nanoparticles (Fig. 1c). While, no significant difference was observed in Zeta potential between Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs, indicating that the bilayer bioactive glasses (BGs) did not significantly alter the physicochemical properties of the monolayered BGs.

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) revealed a characteristic broad halo spanning 20–30° in the 2θ range, a signature feature confirming the non-crystalline structural arrangement of the Mg-Zn/BGs nanoparticles (Fig. 1d). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) investigations identified three distinct vibrational modes: a prominent absorption band at 806 cm− 1 corresponding to Si-O-Si bending vibrations, a high-intensity peak at 1192 cm− 1 associated with asymmetric stretching of the same bonds, and a weaker feature at 499 cm− 1 indicative of Si-O-Si rocking motions (Fig. 1e). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling (Fig. 1f and g) provided elemental verification, with distinct Mg 2p and Zn 2p photoelectron signals confirming the homogeneous incorporation of both metallic species into the glass matrix. This coordinated spectroscopic evidence collectively demonstrated successful ionic coordination between Mg and Zn within the amorphous BGs framework.

Fluctuations in the ion concentrations were monitored following the immersion of Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs into phosphate buffer over various durations. As depicted in Fig. 1h, Zn/BGs exhibited progressive degradation over time with gradual Zn2+ liberation, reaching peak release rates between days 5–7 and achieving nearly complete degradation by day 10, which was consistent with previous studies. In contrast, Mg-Zn/BGs demonstrated sequential ion release kinetics: initial Mg2+ predominance during early stages (days 1–7) with minimal Zn2+ release, followed by reversed release profiles where Zn2+ liberation rates surpassed Mg2+ outputs starting around day 11 (Fig. 1i). The observed trace Zn2+ release from Mg-Zn/BGs in the initial phase (days 1–7) originates from partial PBS permeation through the porous outer Mg/BGs layer, enabling limited contact between the encapsulated Zn/BGs core and dissolution medium, though with constrained interfacial exposure area resulting in suppressed release kinetics. Additionally, the cumulative release profiles of Mg2+ and Zn2+ presented in Fig. 1j provided conclusive validation of the sequential ion release kinetics exhibited by Mg-Zn/BGs, demonstrating time-programmed liberation patterns where Mg ions precede zinc release, a behavior governed by the core-shell architecture.

In this study, to adapt to the spatiotemporal dynamic ionic imbalance phenomenon after SCI, the shell-core thickness ratio of the Mg-Zn/BGs nanoparticles is approximately 1:4. This ratio was determined after multiple experimental attempts. At this ratio, the early-stage sustained release of Mg2+ ions lasts for approximately 1–2 weeks, and the later-stage release of Zn2+ lasts for approximately 3–4 weeks.

Mg-Zn/BGs inhibited early inflammation in vitro

Macrophage cell line Raw 264.7 was used to test the inflammation-inhibiting properties of Mg-Zn/BGs, and the activation was induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL). First, ELISA was used to detect the levels of inflammatory factors on day 7 after co-culturing with different samples (Figure S2). The results revealed that Mg-Zn/BGs treatment elicited a statistically significant suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6), while concurrently promoting elevated expression levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10) relative to untreated controls, indicating that Mg-Zn/BGs inhibited macrophage-mediated inflammation in vitro.

Subsequently, the flow cytometry analysis was performed to determine macrophage phenotype dynamics. As depicted in Fig. 2a and b, Mg-Zn/BGs treatment significantly decreased the percent of CD86-positive (M1 marker) cells and increased the proportion of CD206-positive (M2 marker) cells. Specifically, the CD86-positive cell proportions were 43.4%, 42.9%, 35.2% and 25.0% in the control, BGs, Zn/BGs, and Mg-Zn/BGs groups, respectively. While the CD206-positive cells were 0.16%, 0.18%, 5.43% and 10.6% in the control, BGs, Zn/BGs, and Mg-Zn/BGs groups, respectively. These results were further confirmed by the iNOS/CD206 immunostaining. As shown in Fig. 2c and e, LPS stimulation induced significant induction of iNOS expression (pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage marker). In contrast, Mg-Zn/BGs treatment significantly decreased the fluorescence intensity of green signal (iNOS) while increasing the CD206 signal intensity. Additionally, RT-PCR was performed to detect the gene expression of iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and Arg1 on day 7 (Fig. 2f). The results showed that the expression of pro-inflammatory factors (iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β) significantly down-regulated after Mg-Zn/BGs treatment, while the anti-inflammatory factor (IL-10) significantly up-regulated.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that Mg-Zn/BGs facilitated macrophage polarization toward M2 phenotype, decreased the production of pro-inflammatory factors, and thereby significantly inhibited macrophage-mediated inflammation in vitro. Specifically, Zn/BGs also showed in vitro inhibitory effect on pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages. This may be due to the release of Zn ions, consistent with previous studies [22].

To further elucidate the mechanism by which Mg-Zn/BGs regulate macrophage polarization towards the M2 type, transcriptomic profiling via RNA-seq (Fig. 2g) was employed. Applying rigorous thresholds (P < 0.05, FC ≥ 2), we identified 814 regulated transcripts (417 upregulated; 397 downregulated) versus controls. Dual enrichment analyses revealed predominant involvement in immunoregulatory processes: GO terms highlighted immune-inflammatory modulation, while KEGG pathways showed dysregulation in cytokine interactions, IL-17 signaling, PI3K-Akt cascades, TNF/NF-κB networks. Recently, Lu et al. [23] reported that Mg2+ supplementation directly regulates macrophages toward an M2 phenotype by fine-tuning these pathways. Additionally, previous studies have well-documented the regulatory effects of Mg2+ on macrophage phenotypic transition, which aligns with our observed transcriptomic changes and supports the mechanistic role of Mg2+ release from Mg-Zn/BGs in mitigating inflammation post-SCI [24, 25]. Building on this, we speculate that Mg2+ may directly modulate these pathways by serving as a critical cofactor for enzymes involved in immunoregulation. For example, it can inhibit TNF/NF-κB signaling by reducing oxidative stress and stabilizing IκB, thereby curtailing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, while simultaneously enhancing PI3K-Akt activation to promote M2 polarization through Akt-dependent anti-inflammatory cascades. However, Western blot and PCR validation of key pathway components (e.g., phosphorylated forms of ERK, AKT) and inhibitor-based experiments are required in future studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanism.

The Effects of Mg-Zn/BGs on neuronal apoptosis and NSCs differentiation in vitro

After SCI, secondary factors such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and hypoxia can further lead to neuronal apoptosis, exacerbating neurological dysfunction [26]. Preventing this process helps promote the recovery of neurological function and is an important direction in current research on SCI repair. Therefore, the apoptosis of neurons in response to inflammatory stimuli was detected. Specially, to mimic the in vivo inflammatory microenvironment after SCI, the activated astrocytes conditioned medium (CM) was used according to previous studies [27]. As shown in Fig. 3a-b, exposure to CM induced neuronal apoptosis, a process that was not significantly affected by BGs treatment. In contrast, both Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs treatments reversed this effect, with the latter demonstrating a superior ability to markedly reduce apoptosis triggered by inflammatory stimuli. The effects of Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs on neuronal apoptosis may be due to the release of Mg2+ and/or Zn2+.

Current studies have shown that while typical NSCs (neuronal stem cells) are absent in white matter regions, there may exist cell populations with neurogenic potential in specific areas [28]. For instance, ependymal cells surrounding the central canal can exhibit stem cell properties after injury but rarely generate neurons under normal physiological conditions [28]. Additionally, in the subpial zone of the adult spinal cord, there exist scattered neural precursor cells with proliferative and differentiation potential [29]. These cells were namely endogenous NSCs mentioned by many previous studies. Promoting endogenous NSCs differentiation into neurons rather than glial cells contributes to the repair of SCI. Based on this, the effects of Mg-Zn/BGs on NSCs differentiation were subsequently determined by co-culturing with NSCs isolated from postnatal rat. As depicted in Fig. 3c-d, treatment with Zn/BGs resulted in a marked downregulation of GFAP expression, alongside an enhanced density and brightness of NF-200 green fluorescence signals relative to both the BGs and control groups. Furthermore, the Mg-Zn/BGs group demonstrated a significantly lower GFAP intensity and a higher NF-200 signal intensity compared to the Zn/BGs group. These observations were further quantitatively corroborated in Fig. 3e-f. Gene expression analysis (Fig. 3g) revealed that both Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs significantly increased the expression of neuronal markers MAP2 and TUJ1, while suppressing GFAP expression. Collectively, these results suggest that the released Mg2+ and/or Zn2+ ions from the bioactive glasses have neuro-regenerative properties, consistent with previous studies [30].

Additionally, to further elucidate the mechanism behind the neuronal survival-promoting effects of Mg-Zn/BGs, we conducted transcriptomic profiling using RNA-seq. Comparative analysis against control groups, under stringent thresholds (p < 0.05, FC ≥ 2), identified 479 differentially expressed transcripts (221 upregulated and 258 downregulated). Dual enrichment analyses revealed a predominant association with antioxidant functions. Gene Ontology (GO) terms were significantly enriched for antioxidant activity and calcium binding, while KEGG pathway analysis indicated dysregulation in the Nrf2 signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling, and JAK-STAT networks (Fig. 3h-i). Mg2+/Zn2+ exert anti-apoptotic effects through multiple pathways. Li et al. [31] reported that Zn2+ can promote functional recovery after SCI by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 defense pathway and inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation in neural cells. Furthermore, Zn2+ can act as secondary messengers, participating in the transduction of downstream signals from growth factor receptors (such as TrkB), activating PI3K and its downstream effector Akt (protein kinase B). The activated Akt exerts anti-apoptotic effects by phosphorylating a variety of proteins [32]. Additionally, Mg2+ can antagonize Ca2+, reduce Ca2+ influx, stabilize the mitochondrial membrane potential, and prevent the abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). Consequently, this inhibits the release of apoptotic factors such as cytochrome C from the mitochondria, thereby blocking the mitochondrially mediated intrinsic apoptotic pathway [33]. The findings of RNA-sequencing are consistent with previously reported mechanisms for Mg2+/Zn2+ in supporting neuronal survival (Fig. 3j). However, Western blot and PCR validation of key pathway components and inhibitor-based experiments are required in future studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanism.

Degradation and metabolism of Mg-Zn/BGs In Vivo

In this part, a standardized weight-drop impact at the T10 thoracic level was used to establish murine SCI models according to previous studies [27, 34], followed by immediate in situ delivery of PBS, BGs, Zn/BGs, and Mg-Zn/BGs during the initial surgical intervention (Fig. 4a).

According to previous studies, fluorescent labeling serves as a robust methodology for biomaterial degradation assessment, enabling quantitative tracking of structural disintegration kinetics through real-time fluorescence signal attenuation analysis [35]. Therefore, to study the degradation and metabolism of Mg-Zn/BGs in vivo, indocyanine green (ICG) was used to label Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs, and real-time fluorescence images of the ICG signal were captured at various time points after in situ injection of Zn/BGs/ICG, and Mg-Zn/BGs/ICG. As shown in Fig. 4b, the ICG signals in both Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs groups gradually decreased over 28-day period. The fluorescence intensity observed in the Zn/BGs group on day 14 post-injection was minimal, indicating the monolayered bioactive glass exhibits a 14-day in vivo degradation profile, consistent with its in vitro dissolution behavior. While the Mg-Zn/BGs group retained detectable fluorescence signals on day 28 post-injection, indicating that the bilayer bioactive glass exhibits a markedly extended degradation timeline compared to its monolayered counterpart. This was further confirmed by the quantitative analysis (Fig. 4c). The extended persistence of Mg-Zn/BGs (28 days) can be mechanistically explained by its unique bilayer core-shell structure, where the dense Zn/BGs core is encapsulated by a porous Mg/BGs outer layer; this architectural complexity inherently retards degradation by limiting direct exposure of the inner core to physiological fluids, necessitating sequential dissolution—first of the Mg-doped shell (releasing Mg2+ early) and later the Zn-doped core (releasing Zn2+ post-day 10), as corroborated by the ion release kinetics.

Subsequently, the bio-distribution and metabolism of Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs were studied. Following euthanasia at 14 days post-injection, spinal cord and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys) were harvested and underwent ex vivo fluorescence quantification via IVIS imaging. As shown in Fig. 4d, ICG fluorescence signals were detected in spinal cord tissues from both Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs groups. The Mg-Zn/BGs cohort exhibited significantly higher ICG signal intensity compared to Zn/BGs, likely attributable to accelerated Zn/BGs degradation (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, ICG fluorescence signals were detected in liver and kidney from both Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs groups, indicating that Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs predominantly undergo hepatorenal clearance (Fig. 4f and g). Although ICG fluorescence signals were detected in cardiac tissue across both groups, this phenomenon may be attributable to nanoparticle translocation into the circulatory system via paracellular pathways.

Additionally, the release kinetics of Mg2+ and Zn2+ ions were monitored at the injury site. As illustrated in Fig. 4h and i, Mg2+ levels decreased in the early phase after SCI, while Zn2+ deficiency was observed at later stages, which aligns with prior reports [35]. Implantation of Mg–Zn/BGs led to a peak in Mg2+ concentration by day 5 post-injury, after which it gradually declined. Similarly, the use of core–shell Mg–Zn/BGs resulted in a peak in Zn2+ levels around day 14, followed by a reduction. Notably, Zn2+ levels showed an initial rise immediately after SCI but dropped by day 5, possibly due to ion compensatory mechanisms. These findings indicate that Mg2+ and Zn2+ ions are released from the Mg–Zn/BGs in a sequential manner under in vivo conditions, which complements the lack of ionic dynamics following SCI.

Mg-Zn/BGs promoted motor functional recovery of SCI mice

Figure 5a exhibits the representative images of hind limbs on day 28 in the different groups. Complete hindlimb paralysis persisted in the control and BGs groups (no flexion and weight-bearing ability), while Zn/BGs-treated mice exhibited intermittent limb flexion. Notably, the Mg-Zn/BGs group demonstrated coordinated flexion with partial weight-bearing capacity, confirming the therapeutic superiority of ionic sequential therapy in SCI treatment. This observation was further supported by the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scores over the 4-week measurement period post-SCI (Fig. 5b). Postoperative assessment revealed complete paralysis (BMS score = 0) in all groups initially. While control and BGs groups maintained persistently low scores (< 3) reflecting impaired self-repair, Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs groups exhibited progressive motor recovery. Notably, Mg-Zn/BGs demonstrated equivalent BMS scores to Zn/BGs at week 1 but showed superior recovery by week 2, attributed to Mg2+-mediated early-phase immunomodulation transitioning to Zn2+-driven neural regeneration in later stages, collectively validating the therapeutic superiority of Mg-Zn dual-ion sequential release in SCI rehabilitation.

Footprint behavioral assays (Fig. 5c) revealed distinct gait abnormalities in the control and BGs groups, characterized by compensatory forelimb weight-bearing (blue-marked forelimbs) and pronounced hind limb dragging (red-marked limbs), indicating significant motor dysfunction. By day 28 post-injury, Zn/BGs treatment showed improved hind limb strength restoration compared to controls, though some limb dragging remained. The Mg-Zn/BGs group exhibited the most notable functional recovery, with substantially restored hind limb mobility and reduced gait impairments. Quantitative analysis further confirmed these findings, demonstrating a significant reduction in toe dragging and increased stride length in SCI mice following Mg-Zn/BGs treatment (Fig. 5d and e).

The swimming test was used to further assess locomotor recovery in SCI mice, with representative images and Louisville Swim Scale (LSS) scores displayed in Fig. 5f and g. While the control and BGs groups exhibited significant locomotor impairments, including hindlimb paralysis, predominant forelimb propulsion, and trunk instability, the Zn/BGs group showed partial functional restoration with intermittent hindlimb activity and improved body balance. The Mg-Zn/BGs group outperformed all others, demonstrating better hindlimb function and decreased reliance on forelimbs. These findings were supported by LSS scores, which indicated sustained motor improvement over 28 days in Mg-Zn/BGs-treated mice. Similar to BMS results, the Mg-Zn/BGs and Zn/BGs groups had comparable swimming scores on day 7, but Mg-Zn/BGs showed greater progress by week 2. This enhanced recovery stems from the combined effects of Mg2+-induced early immunomodulation and subsequent Zn2+-promoted neural regeneration, highlighting the advantage of Mg-Zn dual-ion sequential delivery for SCI treatment.

Urological dysfunction represents a prevalent SCI complication and in turn serves as a key therapeutic efficacy indicator. As depicted in Fig. 5h, H&E staining revealed bladder morphology across the various groups, with quantitative analysis (Fig. 5i and Figure S3) indicating that Mg-Zn/BGs therapy markedly reduced bladder weight and volume, and increased the thickness of both the mucosal and muscular layers, highlighting the functional recovery of SCI mice after Mg-Zn/BGs treatment.

Mg-Zn/BGs inhibited early inflammation in vivo

Many studies have demonstrated that Mg2+ concentrations exhibit a persistent decline during the initial 7-day period following SCI [2]. The hypo-magnesemic state triggers the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, compromises antioxidant defenses, and collectively amplifys secondary injury [12]. Early intervention with Mg2+ supplementation demonstrates neuroprotective potential by counteracting NMDA receptor overactivation and attenuating inflammatory response [13]. Therefore, enlightened by the in vitro results, the effects of Mg-Zn/BGs on early inflammation post-SCI were further studied.

CD68 (a marker of macrophages) immunostaining was performed to assess macrophage infiltration at the injured sites in the different groups. As shown in Fig. 6a, substantial macrophage infiltration at lesion sites were observed in both control and BGs groups. Zn/BGs treatment significantly reduced macrophage density and distribution compared to the control group, which may be attributed to Zn2+-mediated immunomodulation [36]. Notably, the Mg-Zn/BGs group exhibited the lowest fluorescence intensity, indicating that Mg-Zn/BGs effectively attenuated acute-phase inflammation post-SCI via Mg2+ supplementation. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6b) further validated these findings.

To further characterize macrophage phenotypes at the lesion sites after treatment, iNOS and Arg1 immuno-staining was performed to detect M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. As shown in Figure S4a-4d, post-injury iNOS levels were markedly elevated in the control group, while Mg-Zn/BGs treatment significantly attenuated this response, concurrently enhancing Arg1+ cell populations. These results were then verified by the flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 6c and f). Specifically, the percentage of CD86 positive cells was 35.6%, 31.1%, 20.8%, and 11.3% in the control, BGs, Zn/BGs, and Mg-Zn/BGs groups, respectively. Meanwhile, the proportion of CD206 positive cells was 3.53%, 4.97%, 17.1%, and 27.5% in the control, BGs, Zn/BGs, and Mg-Zn/BGs groups, respectively. These findings indicated that Mg-Zn/BGs inhibited the early inflammation by modulating macrophage polarization.

Additionally, RT-PCR was employed to determine the gene expression of iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and Arg1 in spinal cord on day 7 post-injury. As depicted in Fig. 6g, the expression of pro-inflammatory factors (iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-1β) significantly decreased in the Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs groups. While the expression of anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10, and Arg1) increased. The results were confirmed by subsequent ELISA detection (Fig. 6h). The pro-inflammatory factors levels, including TNF-α, CXCL10, and IL-6 were markedly reduced while the IL-4 and IL-10 were significantly elevated in the Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs group.

Collectively, the findings reveal that Mg-Zn/BGs exert early-phase anti-inflammatory effects in spinal cord injury (SCI) by compensating for Mg2+ deficiency through initial magnesium release. This therapeutic mechanism likely involves Mg2+-mediated suppression of macrophage infiltration, inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and modulation of macrophage phenotypic polarization. Additionally, while Zn/BGs demonstrated measurable anti-inflammatory properties, their efficacy proved less pronounced than Mg-Zn/BGs. This disparity may stem from both the superior anti-inflammatory capacity of Mg2+ compared to Zn2+ and the differential ion homeostasis in acute SCI, where severe Mg2+ depletion contrasts with elevated Zn2+ levels, rendering early Mg2+ replenishment more impactful. These results underscore that stage-specific metal ion supplementation achieves superior functional recovery compared to indiscriminate administration, emphasizing the necessity of pathophysiological phase-adapted therapeutic strategies in SCI management.

Mg-Zn/BGs promoted neuronal retention and regeneration

Previous studies have reported that Zn2+ exerted anti-apoptotic effects through chelation of catalytic cysteine residues in caspase-3’s active domain, effectively blocking programmed neuronal death [37, 38]. Preclinical evidence has indicated that Zn2+ supplementation enhances neuronal survival [39]. Additionally, as an integral cofactor for Cu/Zn-SOD, it directly quenches superoxide radicals, achieving 40–60% reduction in lipid peroxidation biomarkers [4, 40]. Furthermore, Zn2+ upregulated cytoprotective heme oxygenase-1 by activating Nrf2/ARE pathway, rebalancing the glutathione redox couple (GSH/GSSG) to combat oxidative cascades and promoting neuronal regeneration [41]. Although Zn2+ levels are transiently elevated during the acute phase following SCI, inflammatory activation triggers excessive Zn2+ sequestration into immune cells and glia [18]. This dysregulated redistribution decreased Zn2+ level in the later phase, ultimately impairing neural repair. Therefore, the effects of Mg-Zn/BGs on neuronal retention and regeneration were studied.

Initially, NeuN immunostaining was conducted to identify the number of surviving neurons in sagittal sections of the spinal cord. Figure 7a illustrated the distribution of NeuN-positive cells in the Z1-Z4 regions adjacent to the lesion core on day 28. The Zn/BGs group exhibited a higher number of NeuN+ neurons compared to the control. Importantly, when comparing all four regions, the Mg-Zn/BGs treatment group showed the highest number of NeuN+ cells, surpassing other groups (Fig. 7b). These findings indicate that Mg-Zn/BGs exerts neuroprotective effects on neuronal survival in areas adjacent to the lesion core. Furthermore, Nissl staining was utilized to assess spinal cord motor neuron preservation. Figure 7c illustrates surviving neurons characterized by dark blue Nissl bodies (indicated with black arrows). Quantitative evaluation (Fig. 7e) demonstrated that Zn/BGs administration significantly increased motoneuron density compared to the control and BGs groups, which exhibited minimal neuronal survival. The Mg-Zn/BGs group exhibited maximal neuronal preservation, indicating the effective preservation of motor neurons in the spinal cord of SCI mice.

The results showed that after Mg-Zn/BGs treatment, the expression of MAP2 (a marker of neural regeneration) significantly increased, while the expression of GFAP (a marker of glial scarring) significantly decreased. Previous studies reported that Zn2+ may enhance MAP2 expression and suppress GFAP expression by inactivating RhoA [42].

Collectively, these results indicated that Mg-Zn/BGs with spatiotemporally precise delivery of Mg2+ and Zn2+ promoted neuronal retention and regeneration. This neuroprotective effect of Mg-Zn/BGs could be attributed to two reasons. First, the early release of Mg2+ suppresses spinal cord inflammation, thereby reducing inflammation-induced damage and glial scar formation. Subsequently, the precise and timely delivery of Zn2+ during later stage decreases oxidative stress levels, consequently reducing neuronal apoptosis.

Mg-Zn/BGs promoted axonal regeneration and re-myelination

SCI typically cause axonal disruption, impairing neural signal transmission, making axonal regeneration critical for repair [43]. However, excessive neuroinflammation, inhibitory molecules, and glial scars can hinder regeneration [26]. Previous studies have reported that appropriate Zn2+ concentrations promoted axonal growth by activating metallothionein-3 (MT3) signaling and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) phosphorylation [44]. Therefore, the effects of Mg-Zn/BGs on axonal regeneration were explored.

To evaluate the regenerative effects of Mg-Zn/BGs, immunostaining was conducted for NF-200 (a neurofilament marker) and GFAP (an indicator of glial scarring). Representative images in Fig. 8a revealed that both the control and BGs groups displayed significant GFAP-positive gliosis, while weak green fluorescence were observed. After Zn/BGs treatment, the GFAP signals decreased, while NF-200 signals intensified. This can be attributed to two reasons. First, the Zn2+ gradually released from Zn/BGs inhibited secondary inflammation, which dampens the regeneration of neural tissues. Second, Zn/BGs provided appropriate Zn2+ and promoted axonal growth by regulating MT3 and ERK signaling pathways. Besides, compared with the Zn/BGs group, Mg-Zn/BGs further suppressed astrocyte formation and enhanced NF-200 upregulation at the injury site. This was verified the quantification in Fig. 8b and c. Mg-Zn/BGs drastically increased the NF-200 positive areas, accompanied by greatly decreased fluorescence intensity of GFAP. This finding strongly underscores the necessity of spatiotemporally sequential therapeutic interventions rather than indiscriminate supplementation.

Existing evidence has established endogenous myelin regeneration as a critical determinant of locomotor functional restoration post-SCI [45, 46]. To investigate this phenomenon across treatment modalities, myelination status was quantitatively assessed via myelin basic protein (MBP) immunohistochemical analysis. Mirroring NF-200 staining patterns, both control and BGs cohorts exhibited minimal MBP+ cellular fluorescence (Fig. 8d). Strikingly, Mg-Zn/BGs administration induced a marked increase in MBP+ signal density and intensity, demonstrating potent pro-myelination efficacy. According to the quantification in Fig. 8e, MBP staining ratios of total area were 11.28%, 10.72%, 40.73%, and 49.35% in the control, BGs, Zn/BGs, and Mg-Zn/BGs groups, respectively.

Taken together, results of NF-200 and MBP immunostainings proved that Mg-Zn/BGs could promote neural tissues regeneration after SCI. The different results in Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs strongly underscores the necessity of spatiotemporally sequential therapeutic interventions rather than indiscriminate supplementation. Supplementation with lost metal ions at different pathological stages of SCI may significantly improves the overall prognosis, superior to conventional mono-ionic therapies.

Actually, inspired by the crucial functions and therapeutic potential of metal ions in SCI, various approaches have been developed to deliver these ions for treatment. For instance, Zn@MOF nanozymes mitigate oxidative damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS) elimination and MMP-9 suppression, thereby enhancing neuronal survival and regeneration [47]. Similarly, MgO/PLCL scaffolds incorporating sonic hedgehog (Shh) and retinoic acid (RA) have been shown to facilitate the differentiation of neural stem cells [11]. However, these mono-ionic delivery approaches fail to recapitulate the endogenous spatiotemporal ionic dynamics post-SCI, resulting in suboptimal reparative outcomes due to temporal mismatch with phase-specific neural repair requirements. Recently, Liu et al. [35] designed a chimeric hydrogel microsphere with Mg2+-crosslinked methacrylate gelatin as the “shell” and Zn2+-loaded PLGA (poly lactic-co-glycolic acid) as the “core”, which allow continuous delivery of Mg2+ or Zn2+ at the exact required phase in SCI pathological process. However, this novel ion-delivery system exhibits divergent release profiles across media types, coupled with premature in vivo degradation that truncates Zn2+ release duration below therapeutic thresholds.

To address these issues, based on the single-layer BGs, we constructed a double-layer BGs capable of spatiotemporally sequential delivery of Mg and Zn ions. This system not only enables the gradual release of Mg ions in the early stage (7 days) but also significantly prolongs the release duration of Zn ions in the later stage compared to previous studies. Moreover, its well-documented property of slow degradation and ion release can compensate for the drawbacks of burst release [48]. Additionally, BGs nanoparticles exhibit an ultrahigh specific surface area that allows multi-therapeutic cargo loading, which expands their applicability across precision medicine and neural regeneration [49]. More importantly, by adjusting the compound ratios and reaction times during the synthesis of the double-layer BGs, the proportion of the inner and outer layers can be controlled, thereby modulating the release cycles of ions from each layer, which should be further explored in the future studies.

Biosafety of Mg-Zn/BGs

Twenty-eight days after injury, cardiac, hepatic, splenic, pulmonary, and renal tissues from experimental murine groups were systematically collected for histological assessment of biosafety. Histopathological examination under light microscopy demonstrated preserved tissue architecture across all specimens, with no pathological abnormalities detected in any organ system (Figure S5a). Complementary serum analysis revealed stable hepatic functional markers (ALT, AST) and renal metabolic indicators (UA), with all biochemical parameters remaining within normal physiological ranges across treatment cohorts (Figure S5b). This comprehensive evaluation confirmed the favorable biocompatibility of Mg-Zn/BGs, as evidenced by the absence of histological damage and maintenance of vital organ functionality. The demonstrated tissue compatibility and negligible systemic toxicity collectively support the material’s translational potential for therapeutic applications.

Conclusion

In this study, a temporal- and spatial-adapted metal ions delivery system, Mg-Zn-Doped Bilayer Bioactive Glasses (Mg-Zn/BGs), was successfully designed and synthesized, in which Mg/BGs as the outer shell and Zn/BGs as the inner core. The core-shell structure of Mg-Zn/BGs allows phase-specific sustained release of Mg2+/Zn2+, aligning with the pathological progression of SCI. In the early stage, the outer Mg/BGs degraded and released Mg2+, which attenuated macrophage-induced inflammation by promoting macrophage polarization toward M2 type, inhibited scar formation, and thereby created a favorable microenvironment for subsequent neural regeneration. The release of Zn2+ at the late phase effectively promoted neural cell retention and regeneration, which was accompanied by axonal regeneration and re-myelination, leading to significant behavioral recovery of SCI mice. Based on the in vitro and in vivo results, the engineered Mg-Zn/BGs may provide an alternative ion-delivery strategy for SCI treatment.

Even though the study expands the application of BGs, there are some limitations requiring further attention in the future studies. First, considering the ultrahigh specific surface area of BGs which allows multi-therapeutic cargo loading, future studies could investigate the combinatorial efficacy of spatiotemporally programmed ionic sequential therapy (e.g., Mg2+ to Zn2+ phase transition) and pharmacological co-delivery (e.g., anti-inflammatory agents plus neurotrophic factors) to address stage-specific pathophysiology in SCI repair. Second, although Mg-Zn/BGs nanoparticles have sedimentation properties and most can settle at the injury site through in situ injection, we have found in practical operations that due to the influence of flowing cerebrospinal fluid, even in situ injection cannot achieve 100% accumulation of Mg-Zn/BGs at the lesion core. Therefore, we propose concrete solutions (e.g., hydrogel encapsulation) to improve lesion-site retention in the future studies.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of Mg-Zn/BGs

Bilayer-structured bioactive glasses (Mg-Zn/BGs) were fabricated via a stepwise sol-gel method. The inner dense Zn-doped BGs core was synthesized by mixing tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) with anhydrous ethanol at a molar ratio of 1:4. A 0.1 M nitric acid solution was then added dropwise to adjust the pH to 1.5, followed by magnetic stirring for 30 min to form a transparent sol. Subsequently, a precursor solution containing 5 mol% zinc nitrate was introduced, and the mixture was stirred in a 60 °C water bath for 2 h to promote hydrolysis and condensation reactions. The sol was aged statically for 24 h and then cast into polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) molds. Solvent evaporation was performed in an oven at 50 °C for 72 h to obtain a gel body, which was then sintered in a muffle furnace at 650 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 2 °C/min to yield a dense Zn/BGs core.

To construct the porous Mg-based outer layer, we prepared a transparent sol containing 15 mol% magnesium nitrate and 20 wt% polyethylene glycol 600 (PEG 600) following a similar method as described above. Subsequently, the pre-sintered Zn/BGs core was immersed in the sol and withdrawn at a constant speed of 2 mm/s to ensure uniform coating. The resulting wet coating was pre-dried under 40% relative humidity for 12 h, then transferred to a supercritical CO2 drying system (31 °C, 73 bar) for 6 h to stabilize the porous network structure. Finally, a two-step calcination was performed: first, heating at 1 °C/min to 250 °C with a 1-hour dwell to remove the PEG porogen; followed by heating at 5 °C/min to 550 °C and holding for 1.5 h to complete the sintering of the outer layer. These parameters were selected based on commonly reported sol-gel bioactive glass processing principles and our preliminary optimization to ensure preservation of the porous architecture and controlled degradation behavior [22, 48].

Characterization of Mg-Zn/BGs

The surface morphology and elemental distribution of Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM2100, Hitachi, Japan) coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental mapping. Zeta potential and particle size distribution were assessed via Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA; ZetaView PMX 110, Particle Metrix, Germany). Crystalline phases were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (2θ = 10–80°). Functional groups were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Nicolet 6700, Thermo Scientific, USA) using KBr pellets (1:100 sample ratio, 4 cm− 1 resolution, 4000–400 cm− 1 range). Surface chemistry was further investigated through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Scientific). To evaluate ion release kinetics, Mg-Zn/BGs were immersed in phosphate buffer, and Mg/Zn concentrations were monitored over time. Additionally, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent Technologies, USA) quantified the leaching profiles of Mg and Zn at predetermined intervals (day 1 to day 21).

Cell culture

The Raw 264.7 macrophage cell line was obtained from the cell banks of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. These cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and kept in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air.

Detection of the macrophage phenotype transition

To study phenotype transition, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Specifically, RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded on the 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL (500 µL/well), and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) for 24 h, which is a widely adopted condition for inducing classical (M1-type) macrophage activation in vitro. And then, the cells were co-cultured with different samples (BGs, Zn/BGs, Mg-Zn/BGs, 2.0 × 10¹¹ particles/mL, 50 µL) for 7 days. After co-cultivation, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 for an additional 10 min, and then blocked with 1% BSA in PBS. Primary antibodies—iNOS and CD206 (Abcam)—were applied at a 1:50 dilution and left overnight at 4 °C. Following the removal of unbound primary antibodies, secondary antibodies (donkey anti-rat IgG and donkey anti-rabbit IgG, both Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated, 1:100 dilution, Abcam) were introduced for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Flow cytometry was used to analyze the macrophage surface markers CD86 (M1 phenotype) and CD206 (M2 phenotype), following a previously described protocol. Briefly, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded onto the samples at 2 × 104 cells/well and co-cultured for 7 days. After incubation, the cells were gently detached, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, they were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, followed by another PBS wash. For staining, the cells were incubated for 1 h on ice (protected from light) with APC-conjugated anti-CD86 and PE-conjugated anti-CD206 antibodies. Finally, 300 µL of cell suspension was analyzed using a flow cytometer (Millipore, Burlington, MA), and data were processed with FlowJo v10.0.7 software.

ELISA detection of inflammatory factors

Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10 were quantified via ELISA. In detail, cell culture supernatants were collected and analyzed following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RT-PCR

Gene expression levels of iNOS, TNF-α, CD206, and IL-10 were assessed by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), with the primers listed in Table S1 (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). In detail, RAW264.7 cells were seeded on the samples at 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by cDNA synthesis with a PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR analysis was conducted on an ABI7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using SYBR Green chemistry, with data processing performed on QuantStudio 7 Flex software. β-actin served as the internal reference gene for normalization, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method.

NSCs isolation and culture

Neural stem cells (NSCs) were isolated from the hippocampal tissue of embryonic Sprague-Dawley rats. The tissue was digested with Accutase® solution (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C for 20 min and then mechanically dissociated under sterile conditions to obtain a single-cell suspension. The resulting suspension was passed through a 200-µm mesh filter to remove cellular aggregates. Purified cells were grown as neurospheres in T15 flasks (Corning, USA) using DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 2% B27 supplement (Gibco, USA), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech, USA), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; PeproTech, USA), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA), under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Cells from passages 2–4 were used in subsequent experiments.

Evaluation of Mg-Zn/BGs’ effects on NSCs differentiation and apoptosis

To initiate neural stem cell (NSC) differentiation, neurospheres were first dissociated into single cells with Accutase® (Gibco, China) and plated in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. The cells were then exposed to BGs, Zn/BGs, or Mg-Zn/BGs (2.0 × 10¹¹ particles/mL, 50 µL) for 14 days to promote differentiation. Phenotypic evaluation was performed by immunocytochemical staining using antibodies against GFAP (CST, USA) and NF-200 (CST, USA), which serve as specific markers for astrocytes and neurons, respectively.

According to previous studies, the activated astrocyte-conditioned medium (CM) was used to induce neuronal apoptosis by mimicing the in vivo inflammatory microenvironment after SCI [27]. The preparation details of CM were as follows: Astrocytes were stimulated using LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h to induce an inflammatory phenotype. The supernatant was then collected, centrifuged to remove debris, and used as CM for subsequent neuronal culture. After co-culturing with different samples for 48 h, the flow cytometric analysis was used to assess the neuronal apoptosis in different groups through Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining, following a previously described protocol [27].

SCI animal model

A spinal cord contusion model was established in female C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks. Subjects were randomly divided into four treatment cohorts: control, BGs-treated, Zn/BGs-treated, and Mg-Zn/BGs-treated groups. Surgical procedures adhered to standardized protocols. Under intraperitoneal isoflurane anesthesia, a midline dorsal incision was performed between the T9-T11 vertebrae. Following T10 laminectomy to expose spinal tissue, controlled impact trauma was administered using a 5 g weight dropped from 5 cm height with an RWD precision impactor (USA). Therapeutic agents were administered via in situ injection at the injury site immediately post-trauma. Postoperative management involved layered tissue suturing, preventive antibiotics, and twice-daily bladder evacuation until normal micturition returned. All experimental protocols were conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines and were approved by Renji Hospital’s Animal Care Committee at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No.2025–033421).

Assessment of biodegradation and metabolism in vivo

In this study, to investigate the degradation of Mg-Zn/BGs in vivo, indocyanine green (ICG) was used to label Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs, and real-time fluorescence images of the ICG signal were captured at various time points after in situ injection of Zn/BGs-ICG, and Mg-Zn/BGs-ICG. Specifically, ICG (MCE, USA) was integrated into Zn/BGs and Mg-Zn/BGs nanoparticles. Equivalent concentrations of Zn/BGs-ICG and Mg-Zn/BGs-ICG were administrated through in situ injections at the injury site immediately post-trauma. The degradation dynamics and metabolic clearance of nanoparticles were monitored over time using the IVIS® Lumina Series III platform (PerkinElmer, USA) under predefined spectral conditions (excitation: 785 nm, emission: 810 nm). Additionally, the metabolism of these nanoparticles were studied. At the 14-day post-administration, experimental subjects were sacrificed for tissue collection, including spinal cord and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidneys). Ex vivo fluorescence quantification was conducted on excised biological samples to assess residual nanoparticle concentrations.

Evaluation of motor functional recovery

First, the hind limbs of SCI mice in the different groups were observed on day 28, and the representative images was captured. Subsequently, the footprint assay was performed four weeks after SCI in experimental subjects. Forelimbs were stained with blue ink, while hind paws were marked with red ink. Subjects traversed a sterile acrylic surface under controlled conditions, facilitating high-resolution digital recording of locomotor patterns. Stride length and toe dragging were calculated using specialized image processing tools. Functional restoration was objectively measured through the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS), which assessed posterior limb coordination (0–9 scale) through systematic open-field monitoring sessions. Additionally, the swimming test and Louisville Swim Score (LSS) were performed to examine recovery progression by analyzing anterior limb propulsion efficiency, posterior extremity kinematics, and axial alignment. Three independent evaluators conducted all analytical procedures under double-blind conditions with protocol-driven consistency.

Flow cytometry analysis in vivo

The flow cytometry analysis was used to detect the phenotype transition of macrophages in vivo. Detailly, on day 7 post-treatment, mice were euthanized and transcardially perfused with PBS. The spinal cord was rapidly dissected and digested with trypsin to generate a single-cell suspension, which was then filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer to remove tissue debris. After washing with PBS, cells were stained with F4/80 (general macrophage marker). The CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) antibodies were further used to distinguish macrophage subsets. Appropriate isotype controls and unstained samples were included to account for nonspecific binding and autofluorescence. Post-staining, cells were resuspended in Flow cytometry buffer and analyzed using a Cytoflex ADP instrument (Beckman Coulter, USA). Data were processed with FlowJo v10.0.7 software.

Histological analysis

Animals were sacrificed at 7- and 28-day post-SCI. Systemic vascular perfusion was initiated via cardiac puncture using sequential phosphate-buffered saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde fixation. Extracted spinal cord specimens underwent secondary fixation, paraffin embedding, and microtome sectioning into 20 μm coronal slices. Dual-axis histological evaluations combined immunofluorescence quantification of inflammatory markers and regenerative indicators. Macrophage subpopulations were characterized through sequential immunostaining: pan-macrophage identification employed CD68 and F4/80 antibodies (Abcam, USA), with polarization subtypes differentiated via iNOS (pro-inflammatory) and Arg1 (anti-inflammatory) markers (Abcam, USA). Tissue remodeling parameters were quantified using lineage-specific probes: astrogliosis via GFAP (Boster, China), axonal integrity through NF200 (Abcam, USA), and myelination status using MBP (CST, USA). Neurogenesis was evaluated through TUJ1-labeled newly borned neurons and NeuN-stained mature neurons (Abcam, USA), complemented by Nissl histochemistry for neuronal survival assessment.

Biosafety evaluation

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was used to examine tissue micro-architecture in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney). Complementary evaluation of hepatorenal functional status was conducted through quantitative serum biochemistry panel measuring aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), and uric acid (UA) concentrations.

Statistical analysis

Experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Relevant sample sizes and statistical parameters (p-values with significance levels) are detailed in corresponding figure legends. Statistical analysis employed IBM SPSS Statistics and GraphPad Prism software. The t-test is used for the statistical analysis of two groups of measurement data. One-way ANOVA assessed single-variable differences across multiple groups, while two-way ANOVA evaluated interaction effects between two variables in multi-group comparisons. Post-hoc analyses using Tukey’s test clarified significant main effects identified in ANOVA models. A p value of less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance across all tests (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.).
