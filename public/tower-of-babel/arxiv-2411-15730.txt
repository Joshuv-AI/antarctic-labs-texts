Finite-volume quantization condition from the N / D N/D representation Preprint: JLAB-THY-24-4216
Sebastian M. Dawid, Andrew W. Jackura, Adam P. Szczepaniak

Abstract
We propose a new model-independent method for determining hadronic resonances from lattice QCD. The formalism is derived from the general principles of unitarity and analyticity, as encoded in the N / D N/D representation of a partial-wave two-body amplitude. The associated quantization condition relates the finite-volume spectrum to the infinite-volume numerator, 𝒩 \mathcal{N} , used to reconstruct the scattering amplitude from dispersive relations. Unlike the original Lüscher condition, this new formalism is valid for energies coinciding with the left-hand cuts from arbitrary one- and multi-particle exchanges.

We base our approach on the
N/D
representation of the scattering amplitude
Omnes 1958
;
Chew and Mandelstam 1960
;
Bjorken 1960
. It is a general parametrization based on dispersion relations, satisfying the requirements of the S-matrix unitarity and analyticity. In this representation, the partial-wave amplitude
\mathcal{M}
is written as a fraction,
\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{D}
, where the numerator,
\mathcal{N}
, is defined to contain the left-hand singularities, and the denominator,
\mathcal{D}
, contains the right-hand unitarity cuts. The
N/D
representation has been applied extensively in experimental analyses to parametrize scattering data in a way that respects fundamental S-matrix principles
Badalian et al. 1982
;
Oller and Oset 1999
;
Albaladejo and Oller 2012
;
Jackura et al. 2018
;
Rodas et al. 2019
. Here, we show how to employ it in the context of FV lattice computations. We derive a quantization condition that relates the FV energy spectrum, given by the zeros of the FV denominator function,
\mathcal{D}_{L}
, to the FV numerator,
\mathcal{N}_{L}
. We demonstrate that
\mathcal{N}_{L}
matches the IV representation,
\mathcal{N}
, up to corrections exponentially suppressed with the volume, which allows us to replace
\mathcal{N}_{L}\to\mathcal{N}
in the quantization condition. Thus, provided a suitable model of the left-hand cuts is available, the quantization condition allows one to constrain
\mathcal{N}
and then reconstruct the full scattering amplitude from the well-known
N/D
integral equations. For simplicity, we derive the formalism for a single-channel
2\to 2
reaction of spinless particles. The derived condition is applicable at energies arbitrarily below the threshold and, thus, extends the applicability of the canonical FV formalism.

The structure of the paper is as follows. We begin by reviewing the analytic properties of the scattering amplitudes in the
N/D
representation. This is followed by the derivation of the new quantization condition. We conclude with an overview of the method and discuss its potential extensions. Some technical details are relegated to the supplemental material.

Infinite-volume scattering:
To illustrate our idea, let us first review the
N/D
parametrization in the infinite volume. We consider a single-channel elastic scattering of two scalar particles of masses
m_{i}
, where
i\in\{1,2\}
. They collide with initial four-momenta
p_{i}=(\omega_{i}(\bm{p}),\bm{p}_{i})
and emerge from the reaction with final four-momenta
p^{\prime}_{i}=(\omega_{i}(\bm{p}^{\prime}),\bm{p}_{i}^{\prime})
. Here,
\omega_{i}(\bm{p})=(\bm{p}_{i}^{2}+m_{i}^{2})^{1/2}
is the single-particle on-shell energy. The total four-momentum
P=(E,\bm{P})=p_{1}+p_{2}
and the invariant mass squared
s=P^{2}
. Other Mandelstam variables are
t=(p_{1}-p^{\prime}_{1})^{2}
and
u=(p_{1}-p^{\prime}_{2})^{2}
, and they satisfy the relation
s+t+u=2(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2})
. In the center-of-momentum (c.m.) frame (denoted by a “
\star
” superscript) the total four-momentum is
P^{\star}=(E^{\star},\bm{0})
and initial (final) particle has momentum
\bm{p}^{\star}\equiv\bm{p}_{1}^{\star}=-\bm{p}_{2}^{\star}
(
{\bm{p}^{\prime}}^{\star}\equiv{\bm{p}^{\prime}_{1}}^{\star}=-{\bm{p}_{2}^{\prime}}^{\star}
). Its magnitude is given by,

\displaystyle p^{\star}=|\bm{p}^{\star}|=|{\bm{p}^{\prime}}^{\star}|=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{s}}\,\lambda^{1/2}(s,m_{1}^{2},m_{2}^{2})\,,

(1)

where
\lambda(x,y,z)=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-2xy-2yz-2zx
is the Källén triangle function. The total scattering energy is given by
E=\sqrt{(E^{\star})^{2}+\bm{P}^{2}}
and energy conservation implies that
E^{\star}=\omega_{1}(\bm{p}^{\star})+\omega_{2}(\bm{p}^{\star})
.

Given the S matrix,
S=\mathbbm{1}+iT
, the scattering amplitude is an element of the transition operator,

\displaystyle\langle\bm{p}_{1}^{\prime}\bm{p}_{2}^{\prime}|\,T\,|\bm{p}_{1}\bm{p}_{2}\rangle=(2\pi)^{4}\,\delta^{(4)}(P^{\prime}-P)\,\mathcal{M}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})\,.

(2)

To simplify comparison with the FV formalism, we write the amplitude as a function of nine variables,
\bm{p}^{\prime}
,
\bm{p}
,
\bm{P}
, many of which are redundant. Given energy-momentum conservation and Lorentz invariance of the amplitude in the infinite volume, one can reduce this set of degrees of freedom to two variables, e.g.,
s
and
t
, or
p^{\star}
and
\hat{\bm{p}}^{\prime\star}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}^{\star}=\cos\theta^{\star}
, where
\theta^{\star}
is the scattering angle in the c.m. frame,
\mathcal{M}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})\equiv\mathcal{M}(p^{\star},\hat{\bm{p}}^{\prime\star}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}^{\star})
.

Probability conservation enforces that transition operators satisfy the unitarity relation
T-T^{\dagger}=iT^{\dagger}T
. For scattering amplitudes, this gives the unitarity condition,

\displaystyle 2\im\mathcal{M}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})=\xi\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\frac{\pi\delta\big(E-\omega_{1}(\bm{k})-\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})\big)}{2\omega_{1}(\bm{k})\,2\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})}\,\mathcal{M}^{*}(\bm{k},\bm{p}^{\prime};\bm{P})\,\mathcal{M}(\bm{k},\bm{p};\bm{P})\,,

(3)

where we normalize the single-particle scattering states according to
\langle\bm{p}^{\prime}_{i}|\bm{p}_{j}\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\,2\omega_{i}(\bm{p})\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta^{(3)}(\bm{p}-\bm{p}^{\prime})
. The symmetry factor
\xi
is
1/2
if the particles are identical and
1
otherwise. We also restrict the energy domain to the elastic scattering region,
E^{\star}\leq E^{\star}_{\mathrm{inel.}}
, where
E^{\star}_{\mathrm{inel.}}
is the first inelastic threshold, see Fig.
1
.

The scattering amplitude is decomposed into partial waves,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})=4\pi\,\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell m_{\ell}}Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{p}}^{\prime\star})\,\mathcal{M}_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell}}(s)\,Y_{\ell m_{\ell}}(\hat{\bm{p}}^{\star})=\sum_{\ell}(2\ell+1)\,\mathcal{M}_{\ell}(s)\,P_{\ell}(\hat{\bm{p}}^{\prime\star}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}^{\star})\,,

(4)

where angular momentum
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and its
z
-axis projection
-\ell\leq m_{\ell}\leq\ell
. In the first equality, we used Lorentz invariance to write the partial-wave projection as a function of
s
, and in the second equality we used angular momentum conservation to write
\mathcal{M}_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell}}(s)=\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m_{\ell}^{\prime}m_{\ell}}\,\mathcal{M}_{\ell}(s)
as well as the addition theorem for spherical harmonics. It is convenient to extract from the amplitude factors representing threshold suppression at high angular momenta; therefore we define
\mathcal{M}_{\ell}(s)\equiv(p^{\star})^{2\ell}\,\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s)
. Introducing this expansion in Eq. (
3
), transforming the integral to the c.m. frame, and evaluating the integral yields the partial-wave unitarity relation,

\displaystyle\im\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s)=\rho_{\ell}(s)\,\lvert\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s)\rvert^{2}\,\Theta(s-s_{\mathrm{thr}})\,,

(5)

where
s_{\mathrm{thr}}=(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}
and
\rho_{\ell}(s)=\xi(p^{\star})^{2\ell+1}/8\pi\sqrt{s}
is the two-body phase space factor with barrier factors. Here,
\Theta
is the Heaviside function.

Through the Schwartz reflection principle, one identifies the imaginary part of the amplitude with its discontinuities. The phase-space factor in Eq. (
5
) gives a branch cut called the “right-hand” or “unitarity” cut. In general, amplitudes have both right-hand cuts (from direct channel unitarity, as shown) and left-hand cuts, which arise from cross-channel processes. Likewise, partial-wave amplitudes can have left-hand cuts induced from partial-wave projecting these cross-channel processes. To capture the generic analytic structure of the amplitude, we introduce a decomposition called
N/D

Omnes 1958
;
Chew and Mandelstam 1960
;
Bjorken 1960
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s)\equiv\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)\,\mathcal{D}_{\ell}^{-1}(s)\,.

(6)

Here, by definition,
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)
has only right-hand cuts. Zeros of
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)
on the physical sheet can only be on the real axis and correspond to bound states. Zeros in the complex energy plane on the unphysical Riemann sheet describe resonances or virtual states. The left-hand cuts lie below the physical region and are contained in the numerator function
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)
. Therefore, the partial-wave unitarity condition separates into one condition for
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)
,

\displaystyle\im\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)=-\rho_{\ell}(s)\,\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)\,\Theta(s-s_{\mathrm{thr}})\,,

(7)

and another expression for the imaginary part of
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)
,

\displaystyle\im\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)=\im\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s)\,\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)\,\Theta(s_{\rm lhc}-s)\,,

(8)

where
s_{\rm lhc}
is the location of the nearest left-hand cut. These conditions have known solutions in terms of coupled linear integral equations,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=1-\int_{s_{\mathrm{thr}}}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}{\pi}\,\frac{\rho_{\ell}(s^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}-s-i\epsilon}\,\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s^{\prime})\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{s_{\rm lhc}}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}{\pi}\,\frac{\im\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}-s-i\epsilon}\,\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s^{\prime})\,,

(10)

which are obtained from the Cauchy theorem under the assumption that
|\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}(s)|
and
|\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)|\to 0
fast enough as
|s|\to\infty
. In particular, we assume
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
decays faster than
(p^{\star})^{-2\ell}
with
|s|\to\infty
, such that the integral in (
9
) is convergent. In practice, various parametrizations of the amplitude do not satisfy this criterion, and subtracted dispersion relations must be used (or the behavior of the parametrization tuned at infinity) to eliminate a potential integral divergence and the ambiguity in the overall normalization of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{D}
functions (which can be simultaneously multiplied by any holomorphic function without affecting the amplitude.) We discuss it further in the supplemental material and here keep the simplest
N/D
form for clarity of the presentation. Note that we assume that
\mathcal{D}
is normalized according to
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(\infty)=1
.

Finite-volume scattering:
We now consider the constraint unitarity imposes on the system in a finite cubic volume of side length
L
, subjected to periodic boundary conditions so that momenta are quantized as
\bm{p}=2\pi\bm{n}/L
for
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. The condition for the imaginary part of the FV amplitude
\mathcal{M}_{L}
takes the form of Eq. (
3
) with the integral replaced by a sum performed over all discrete components of the intermediate momentum
\bm{k}
,

\displaystyle\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\to\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,,

(11)

and the
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}_{L}
switch. Importantly, it is assumed that the FV ground states have masses that for large enough volumes are exponentially close to the IV mass,
m_{i,L}=m_{i}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-m_{\pi}L})
. The finite-volume amplitude is decomposed into partial waves as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{L}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})=

(12)

\displaystyle 4\pi\,\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell m_{\ell}}Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}_{\ell}}(\hat{\bm{p}}^{\prime\star})\,\mathcal{M}_{L;\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}_{\ell},\ell m_{\ell}}(p^{\prime\star},p^{\star};\bm{P})\,Y_{\ell m_{\ell}}^{*}(\hat{\bm{p}}^{\star})\,.

The above expression has to be treated with caution. Since rotational symmetry is broken in the box, spherical harmonics no longer form a set of orthonormal, linearly independent states of well-defined angular momentum. Thus, it is difficult to unambiguously define partial-wave projection and its inverse for discrete amplitudes
Li et al. 2020
;
Miyamoto et al. 2020
.
\mathcal{M}_{L}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})
appearing in (
12
) has to be understood as an extension of the half-off-shell amplitude from discrete to continuous momenta. We provide a more thorough discussion of the finite-volume amplitudes, their properties, and the definition of the partial waves in the supplemental material.

Since rotational symmetry is broken, one cannot reduce the expansion further. The FV amplitude is a function of
\bm{P}
and not just
s
(or equivalently
p^{\prime\star}=p^{\star}
), as the scattering depends on the orientation of
\bm{P}
with respect to the box. The partial-wave unitarity relation becomes,

\displaystyle 2\im\mathcal{M}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})=

\displaystyle\frac{\xi}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,\frac{\pi\delta(E-\omega_{1}(\bm{k})-\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k}))}{2\omega_{1}(\bm{k})\,2\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})}\,(4\pi)\,\left[Y^{\,{\dagger}}(\hat{\bm{k}}^{\star})\,\mathcal{M}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})\right]^{\dagger}\otimes\left[Y^{\,T}(\hat{\bm{k}}^{\star})\,\mathcal{M}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})\right]\,,

(13)

where we introduced a notation in which amplitudes are considered matrices in the
(\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell})
space; for instance
[\mathcal{M}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})]_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell}}=\mathcal{M}_{L;\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell}}(p^{\star},p^{\star};\bm{P})
. Spherical harmonics become vectors in this notation,
[Y(\hat{\bm{k}}^{\star})]_{\ell m_{\ell}}=Y_{\ell m_{\ell}}(\hat{\bm{k}}^{\star})
. We note that under the sum,
k^{\star}=p^{\star}
due to the Dirac delta function and both momentum magnitudes are fixed by the invariant mass
s
. Similarly to the infinite volume, we remove the threshold barrier factors from the amplitude by defining,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{L}({p}^{\star},\bm{P})=Q(p^{\star})\,\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{L}({p}^{\star},\bm{P})\,Q(p^{\star})\,,

(14)

where the matrix
[Q(p^{\star})]_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell}}=\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m_{\ell}^{\prime}m_{\ell}}\,(p^{\star})^{\ell}
. The analytic structure of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})
is determined by a collection of simple right-hand poles in the
E^{\star}
variable originating from the direct channel. There are also finite-volume bound-state poles and the cross-channel cuts or/and poles that are considered as left-hand singularities. We can then write the
N/D
decomposition for the FV amplitude,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{L}({p}^{\star},\bm{P})=\mathcal{N}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})\,\big(\mathcal{D}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})\big)^{-1}\,,

(15)

in which the IV
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{D}
functions become matrices in angular momentum space. Note that the partial-wave mixing in the finite volume effectively turns the FV
N/D
representation into a “coupled channel” problem. We stress that by construction, the FV energy levels (right-hand and bound-state poles of the amplitude) are given solely by zeros of
\mathcal{D}_{L}
and not by the potential left-hand poles in
\mathcal{N}_{L}
. These two functions are related to each other, and any right-hand pole introduced in a given parametrization of
\mathcal{N}_{L}
can be replaced by a zero of
\mathcal{D}_{L}
without affecting the scattering amplitude
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{L}
. This is obtained, for instance, by multiplying
\mathcal{N}_{L}
and
\mathcal{D}_{L}
by a holomorphic function with zeros coinciding with the relevant poles of
\mathcal{N}_{L}
.

By demanding that
\mathcal{N}_{L}
contains the left-hand singularities only,
\im\mathcal{N}_{L}(p^{\star})=0
in the physical direct channel, and therefore performing matrix manipulations analogous to those in Ref.
Bjorken and Nauenberg 1961
, we find,

\displaystyle\im\mathcal{D}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,\frac{2s}{E}\,\frac{\pi\delta\big(s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}\big)}{2\omega_{1}(\bm{k})2\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})}\,\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\left(\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}\,\mathcal{N}_{L}(k^{\star},\bm{P})\right)\,,

(16)

where we introduced
E^{\star}(\bm{k})=\omega_{1}(\bm{k}^{\star})+\omega_{2}(\bm{k}^{\star})
and
\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})=\sqrt{4\pi}\,Q(k^{\star})\,Y(\hat{\bm{k}}^{\star})
, and rewrote the Dirac delta as a function of the Mandelstam
s
variable. It should be understood that
p^{\star}
is considered a function of
s
, as given in Eq. (
1
). The solution to the unitarity equation for
\mathcal{D}_{L}
is given by a sum of poles,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}+\int_{s_{\rm thr}}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}{\pi}\,\frac{\im\mathcal{D}_{L}(q^{\star},\bm{P})}{s^{\prime}-s-i\epsilon}=\mathbbm{1}-\frac{\xi}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}-s-i\epsilon}\,\mathcal{N}_{L}(k^{\star},\bm{P})\,,

(17)

where
q^{\star}
is a momentum corresponding to the
s^{\prime}
variable and we define the kinematic factor,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})=\frac{E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}{2\omega_{1}(\bm{k})\,2\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})\,\sqrt{E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}+\bm{P}^{2}}}\,.

(18)

Three comments are in order. Firstly, let us note that the energy denominator becomes zero (leads to a
\mathcal{D}_{L}
pole) at free energies
s=E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}
. Thus, just like the finite-volume amplitude,
\mathcal{D}_{L}
considered a function of
s=(E^{\star})^{2}
has a non-zero imaginary part above the threshold. Since one is interested in the interacting energies—corresponding to zeros and not poles of
\mathcal{D}_{L}
—the
i\epsilon
can be safely removed from the denominator. This holds only if the interacting energy levels do not coincide with the free ones. Furthermore, it is assumed that
\mathcal{N}_{L}
decays fast enough for large momenta, such that the sum in Eq. (
17
) is convergent. If this cannot be ensured, one needs to regulate the sum by including subtractions, as discussed in the supplemental material.

Finally, we note that
\mathcal{N}_{L}
has no singularities in the physical momentum region, over which the sum is performed. Therefore, the FV numerator function can be replaced in the above expression by its IV version,
\mathcal{N}
, if one neglects corrections that are exponentially suppressed with the size of the box. In this context,
\mathcal{N}
is considered a diagonal matrix with elements given by
[\mathcal{N}]_{\ell^{\prime}m_{\ell}^{\prime};\ell m_{\ell}}=\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m_{\ell}m_{\ell}^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(k^{\star})
, where we wrote
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
as a function of
k^{\star}
,
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s^{\prime})\equiv\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(k^{\star})
. A detailed argument for this replacement is provided in the supplemental material.

Given this replacement, one can write,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{L}(p^{\star},\bm{P})=\mathbbm{1}+\frac{\xi}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}\,\mathcal{N}(k^{\star})\,,

(19)

and the eigenenergies of the FV theory are given by,

\displaystyle\det_{\ell m_{\ell}}\mathcal{D}_{L}=0\,,

(20)

where the determinant is taken in the angular momentum space
(\ell,m_{\ell})
. Therefore—just like the Lüscher quantization condition—Eq. (
20
) relates the spectrum of the theory in a spatial box to the IV scattering information; in this case the numerator function
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)
. Unlike in the derivation of the Lüscher QC, at no point were we required to assume
s>s_{\rm lhc}
, and the provided formula applies at energies below the nearest left-hand cut branch point. The key difference is that our formalism involves only the
\mathcal{D}
function, while the Lüscher’s approach involves both
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{D}
through the K matrix. This does not affect the elastic energy region, where both approaches are equivalent, as we demonstrate in the supplemental material. However, for energies below the threshold, the model-independent separation of the singularities between
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{N}
allows one to determine the spectrum of the FV theory without evaluating singular parts of the amplitude from the cross-channel processes, which leads to unaccounted power-law volume dependence in naïve extrapolation of Lüscher QC.

We stress that the
N/D
form, just like the commonly used
K

.1
Formal aspects of the FV amplitudes

The FV transition operator is defined in complete analogy to its IV version. Namely, we define a theory in a box,
H_{L}=H_{0,L}+V_{L}
, where
H_{0,L}
is the free and
V_{L}
is the interaction Hamiltonian. The transition operator is,

\displaystyle T_{L}(z)=V_{L}+V_{L}\,G_{L}(z)\,V_{L}\,,

(21)

where
G_{L}(z)=(z-H_{L})^{-1}
is the Hamiltonian’s resolvent (Green’s operator.) By construction, it inherits the singularity structure of
G_{L}(z)
, e.g., the poles of
T_{L}(z)
in the
z
variable coincide with the spectrum of the theory. The main difference between the FV and IV theories comes from the spaces of scattering states they are built upon. In the FV case, periodic boundary conditions mean that the three-dimensional coordinate space is topologically equivalent to a torus. Its size is assumed to be much larger than the range of interactions, such that the asymptotic behavior of the hadrons is affected only by the boundary conditions. The finite-volume space of states consists of quantized momentum kets,
|\bm{p}\rangle
, where
\bm{p}=2\pi\bm{n}/L
,
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. They are normalized to Kronecker deltas, while the IV states correspond to a continuous momentum spectrum and are normalized to Dirac deltas. The set of FV scattering states can be given a well-defined meaning of a Hilbert space, while such definition in the infinite volume is accomplished in the sense of the rigged Hilbert space
Gel’fand and Vilenkin 2014
;
Maurin 1968
;
de la Madrid 2005
.

The half-off-shell FV scattering amplitude,
\mathcal{M}_{L}
, is defined as the matrix element of
T_{L}(z)
with
z=E+i0^{+}
, taken between (discrete) momentum eigenstates of hadrons moving inside the torus. Starting from the definition of the FV transition operator and following the same derivation method as in the infinite volume, one finds that the imaginary part of
{\cal M}_{L}
satisfies the off-shell unitarity relation,

\displaystyle 2\im\mathcal{M}_{L}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})=\frac{\xi}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,\frac{\pi\delta(E-\omega_{1}(\bm{k})-\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k}))}{2\omega_{1}(\bm{k})\,2\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})}\,\mathcal{M}_{L}^{*}(\bm{k},\bm{p}^{\prime};\bm{P})\,\mathcal{M}_{L}(\bm{k},\bm{p};\bm{P})\,.

(22)

Certain care is required when interpreting this relation. In particular, unlike in the infinite volume, this equation alone cannot be used to infer the singularity structure of the amplitude without additional input, e.g., from Eq. (
21
). The FV amplitude has poles at interacting energies that occur above the threshold, which implies that its imaginary part is non-zero outside of the set of free energies,
\omega_{1}(\bm{k})+\omega_{2}(\bm{P}-\bm{k})
, despite being naïvely canceled by the explicit vanishing Dirac delta in Eq. (
22
). This can be inferred from the operator relation
T_{L}(z)-T_{L}(z^{*})=2\pi i\,V_{L}\,\delta(z-H_{L})\,V_{L}=2\pi i\,T_{L}(z)\,\delta(z-H_{0,L})\,T_{L}(z^{*})
obtained from the first resolvent identity. Moreover, as can be seen directly from the definition of the transition operator, the FV amplitude vanishes at free energies (assuming they are separated from the interacting energies), removing the explicit contributions from the free-energy Dirac deltas in the unitarity equation. Nonetheless, as described in the main text, one may manipulate the FV unitarity relation into a simpler equation that describes singularities of its inverse, i.e., the equation for the imaginary part of the
\mathcal{D}_{L}
function, Eq. (
16
), which exhibits intuitive analytic properties (poles at free energies and zeros at interacting energies.) This is analogous to the situation in the infinite volume, where the unitarity equation for the inverse of the amplitude gives its right-hand discontinuity,
\im\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell}^{-1}(s)=-\rho_{\ell}(s)
.

Because the set of relative momenta and their orientations is discrete, spherical harmonics no longer define orthonormal, linearly independent states of well-defined angular momentum,
|\ell,m_{\ell}\rangle
. To define the partial-wave projection of
\mathcal{M}_{L}(\bm{p}^{\prime},\bm{p};\bm{P})

\displaystyle\det_{\ell m_{\ell}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}+\xi\,{\rm p.v.}\!\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}\,\mathcal{N}(k^{\star})

\displaystyle+\xi\left[\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}-{\rm p.v.}\!\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\right]\,\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}\,\mathcal{N}(p^{\star})\Bigg]=0\,.

(45)

The two p.v. integrals can be combined into one and replaced by a sum (up to exponentially suppressed corrections) since the integrated function is regular,

\displaystyle{\rm p.v.}\!\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}\,\left[\mathcal{N}(k^{\star})-\mathcal{N}(q^{\star})\right]

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}\,\left[\mathcal{N}(k^{\star})-\mathcal{N}(q^{\star})\right]+\mathcal{O}(e^{-m_{\pi}L})\,.

(47)

The term proportional to
\mathcal{N}(p^{\star})
cancels with the momentum sum in the second line of Eq. (
45
). It leads to,

\displaystyle\det\Bigg[

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}+\frac{\xi}{L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\,\mathcal{L}(\bm{k},\bm{P})\,\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\star})\otimes\mathcal{Y}(\bm{k}^{\star})^{T}}{s-E^{\star}(\bm{k})^{2}}\,\mathcal{N}(k^{\star})\Bigg]=0\,.

(48)

which is the quantization condition proposed in Eq. (
19
). This completes the demonstration of its equivalence to the Lüscher condition for energies above the nearest left-hand singularity.

.5
Parametrizations of
\mathcal{N}

In the following, we suggest two example models for
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
that may become useful in practical applications.

.5.1
The pole model

The simplest non-trivial model for the left-hand singularity structure of a partial-wave amplitude includes a finite number of poles that mimic a branch cut,

\displaystyle\im\mathcal{M}_{\ell}(s)=-\pi\sum_{i=0}g_{i}\,\delta(s-s_{i})\,,

(49)

where
s_{i}
are (ordered) positions of the poles,
s_{i+1}<s_{i}
, and
-\pi g_{i}
are their residues. Models of this type, although simplistic, were shown to approximate well certain types of long-range interactions
Bjorken and Goldberg 1960
;
Aly and Müller 1966
;
Kamal 1966
;
Kok et al. 1972
;
Kok et al. 1973
and in some cases can be related to the effective-range expansion of the K matrix
Burkhardt 1969
. Assuming a contribution from just one pole at
s_{0}=s_{\rm lhc}
, the solution of the equation for
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)
depends on a single constant,
g_{0}=g
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)=\frac{g\,\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s_{0})}{s-s_{\rm lhc}}\,.

(50)

Once the
N/D
quantization condition, Eq. (
20
), fixed its value from the known lattice QCD energy levels, Eq. (
50
) would be then used in the (unsubtracted) equation for
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)
, Eq. (
9
). After ensuring consistency of the solution at
s=s_{\rm lhc}
, it gives,
\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(s)=1-\lambda I_{\ell}(s)/(1+\lambda I_{\ell}(s_{\rm lhc}))
, where
I_{\ell}
is the integral,

\displaystyle I_{\ell}(s)=\int_{s_{\mathrm{thr}}}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}{\pi}\,\frac{\rho_{\ell}(s^{\prime})}{(s^{\prime}-s-i\epsilon)(s^{\prime}-s_{\rm lhc})}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,\rho_{\ell}(s)\,\frac{1}{(s-s_{\rm lhc})}\,\log\left(\frac{s-(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}{s_{\rm lhc}-(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}\right)\,.

(51)

The final amplitude is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\ell}^{-1}(s)=\frac{s-s_{\rm lhc}}{g}-\rho_{\ell}(s)\,\frac{1}{\pi}\log\left(\frac{s-(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}{s_{\rm lhc}-(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}}\right)\,.

(52)

It has the correct right-hand branch cut and a built-in left-hand pole singularity at
s=s_{\rm lhc}
. In addition, it has dynamical poles given by the zeros of Eq. (
52
).

.5.2
The one-particle exchange model

A more sophisticated choice is to consider a
t
-channel, one-particle exchange (OPE) amplitude,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(s,t)=\frac{g^{2}}{t-\mu^{2}+i\epsilon}\,,

(53)

where
t=-2q^{\star\,2}(1-\cos\theta^{\star})
,
g
is a coupling, and
\mu
is the exchanged particle mass
Luming 1964
;
Du et al. 2024
. Using the completeness condition,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{z^{\prime}-z}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\,Q_{\ell}(z^{\prime})P_{\ell}(z)\,,

(54)

where
P_{\ell}
and
Q_{\ell}
are the Legendre functions of the first and second kind, respectively, we find the partial-wave-projected amplitude,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)=-\frac{g^{2}}{2p^{\star\,2}}\,Q_{\ell}(\zeta+i\epsilon)\,,

(55)

with
\zeta=1-\mu^{2}/2p^{\star\,2}
. This amplitude is analytic everywhere in the complex
s
plane, except for a branch cut with branch points defined by
\zeta=\pm 1
. In choosing a parametrization for
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
, the most rigorous construction is provided by the integral equation for
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
itself, such as Eq. (
31
). We note here that if one were to study a coupled channel system, e.g., in the partial waves such as
NN
or in particle species such as
\pi\pi,K\bar{K}
, then in principle, one must make a model for
\mathcal{T}
and feed it through Eq. (
31
) to maintain the symmetry of
\mathcal{M}
. For single channel systems, such as those discussed in this work, one may choose instead to use Eq. (
55
) as motivation for the structural form of
\mathcal{N}
. In other words, one can consider a parametrization such as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(s)=\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(s)\,Q_{\ell}(\zeta+i\epsilon)\,,

(56)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
is a rational function in
s
(or
q^{\star\,2}
) provided it is chosen to ensure convergence of the dispersion integral at
s\to\infty
, as was required by our derivation. This function absorbs not only any residual short-distance effects associated with couplings of OPE but also local interactions that we do not control. The
Q_{\ell}
function inherits the OPE cut structure, which is kinematically known and thus fixed.

Note that near the threshold
p^{\star}\sim 0
,
\zeta\sim p^{\star\,-2}
, thus
Q_{\ell}\sim p^{\star\,2\ell+2}
. So, the partial-wave OPE behaves as
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\sim(p^{\star})^{2\ell}
. In the high-energy limit,
p^{\star}\sim\sqrt{s}/2
and
\zeta\sim 1+O(s^{-1/2})
, thus
Q_{\ell}\sim\log(s)
, and therefore
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\sim\log(s)/s
and one is forced to either include subtractions as discussed in
.2
, or parameterize
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\sim s^{-\ell}
in this limit. Once the parameters of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
are fixed from the lattice QCD energies via Eq. (
20
), then the amplitude is reconstructed from Eqs. (
6
) and (
9
).
