Novel biallelic MEI1 variants cause female infertility characterized by multiple pronuclei formation and aberrant embryonic development
Authors: Xuedan Jiao, Yuqin Zhu, Wenyi Liu, Yihua Liang, Xiaoyu Zuo, Ling Zhou, Yingying Xia, Qingxue Zhang, Hui Chen, Ping Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiallelic variants in meiosis inhibitor protein 1 (MEI1) have recently been implicated in embryonic developmental arrest. However, the genomic and transcriptomic consequences of these MEI1 variants remain only partially understood. This study aims to expand the known MEI1 variant spectrum and elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which these variants affect early embryonic development.ResultsTwo unrelated infertile women experiencing embryonic developmental arrest were identified to carry novel biallelic MEI1 variants using whole-exome sequencing. The functional impact of these variants was characterized through a combination of in vivo and in vitro analyses. Additionally, genomic and transcriptomic sequencing was performed on embryos from one of the patients. Patient 1 carried compound heterozygous variants c.622T > C (p.Ser208Pro) and c.2647 C > T (p.Arg883Ter), while Patient 2 harbored c.3037T > G (p.Trp1013Gly) and c.2546_3525del (EX21-31del) variants. Genomic analysis of embryos demonstrated extensive chromosomal abnormalities and maternal triploidy, indicating a compromise in meiotic integrity due to biallelic MEI1 variants. Transcriptomic profiling revealed downregulation of meiotic cell cycle genes. Additionally, downregulation of epigenetic regulatory genes, particularly those involved in histone demethylation and deacetylation, was observed, suggesting abnormal transcriptional activation and genomic instability.ConclusionsOur findings broaden the spectrum of MEI1 variants associated with embryonic developmental arrest and highlight the essential role of MEI1 in oocyte meiosis and early embryonic chromosomal stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01890-1.

Background

Embryonic developmental arrest (EDA), characterized by the cessation of embryo growth at early stages from zygote to blastocyst, poses significant challenges in vitro fertilization (IVF) cycles [1]. Early embryonic development is a complex biological process starting from the gradual transition from maternal gene silencing to zygotic genome activation, known as the maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) [2]. This transition involves complex biological mechanisms, including the regulation of maternal effect products, zygotic mitosis, maternal nucleolar proteins, epigenetic reprogramming (such as DNA methylation and histone modifications), and genome stability maintenance [3, 4]. Maternal effect genes, such as those encoding the subcortical maternal complex (SCMC), governs these processes [5]. Mutations in maternal effect genes have been implicated in EDA, which was discussed in recent literatures [3, 4, 6, 7].

The MEI1 (meiotic double-stranded break formation protein 1) gene is essential for meiotic double-strand break (DSB) formation and chromosome synapsis, playing a critical role in gametogenesis and proper chromosomal segregation [7]. Mei1 mutant mice exhibits female infertility without litter production, impaired ovarian morphology and function, and aberrant chromosomal arrangement [8]. The oocytes from Mei1-null mouse are capable of fertilization but lead to androgenetic zygotes due to impaired homologous chromosome synapsis and recombination [9]. In humans, MEI1 variants have been associated with many reproductive disorders, including non-obstructive azoospermia in males [10–15] and recurrent hydatidiform mole (HM), recurrent implantation failure, and embryonic arrest in females [9, 16–18]. However, the variant spectrum of MEI1 associated with embryonic arrest and the molecular mechanisms by which these variants affect early embryonic development have not been fully established. While chromosomal abnormalities and altered DNA methylation were reported in embryos derived from oocytes carrying MEI1 variants [17], the transcriptomic consequence of MEI1 variants on developmental embryos has not been illustrated.

In this study, we employed exome sequencing to identify four novel MEI1 variants in two unrelated infertile women experiencing EDA. Unlike previously reported MEI1-related phenotypes, the primary characteristic observed in these patients was fertilization resulting in tri-pronuclear (3PN) or poly-pronuclear (PPN) embryos following ICSI. Notably, 3PN/PPN embryos were capable of developing into blastocysts, whereas two-pronuclear (2PN) embryos failed to progress. The functional impact of these variants was characterized through both in vivo and in vitro assays. Moreover, we performed genomic and transcriptomic sequencing on patient-derived embryos to investigate the molecular consequences of MEI1 variants on EDA. Our study expands the spectrum of known MEI1 variants and provides comprehensive insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying MEI1-related early embryonic arrest. These findings may contribute to the development of a diagnostic gene panel for MEI1-related fertility disorders, offering potential clinical implications for genetic screening and reproductive medicine.

Methods

Study participants

Over the past three years, twenty female patients with a high rate of abnormal fertilization during ICSI-assisted reproduction at the Reproductive Medicine Center of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (Guangzhou, China) have underwent whole-exome sequencing (WES) to screen for genetic variants associated with abnormal fertilization. Among these 20 patients, MEI1 variants were identified in two infertile women (F1_II:4 and F2_II:2). These two probands, along with their parents, were subsequently recruited in the present study. Both probands had a history of repeated failures in in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). For proband in family 1 (F1_II:4), discarded embryos from day 1 to day 6 were collected for whole-genome sequencing to assess aneuploidy through preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) and for quantitative parental contamination testing (qPCT) [19]. Additionally, whole-transcriptomic sequencing was performed following our previously established protocol [20]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun-Yat Sen University (2020ShengZhiLunShen No.03), and informed consents were obtained from all participants.

Embryo culture and evaluation

Oocytes were assessed for fertilization approximately 20 h post-injection, based on the presence of two pronuclei and the second polar body. Embryos were cultured in G1-plus/G2-plus sequential media (Vitrolife, Sweden) under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% O₂ and 6% CO₂. Following the observation time points and criteria established by the Istanbul Consensus Workshop on Embryo Assessment [21], cleavage-stage embryos on day 3 were evaluated 68 ± 1 h post-fertilization. Blastocyst grading was performed according to the Gardner grading system [22], where blastocysts graded 1–2 were classified as early blastocysts, and those graded 3–6 were classified as late blastocysts. All embryo scoring was independently verified by two experienced embryologists to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and bioinformatic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples of the probands and their family members. Exome sequencing libraries were prepared using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end (PE) 150 bp sequencing was performed on the MGISEQ-T7 platform (MGI, China). Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using SOAPnuke and aligned to the human reference genome (NCBI GRCh38) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Variant calling was conducted using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK). Variants were annotated using multiple public databases, including dbSNP (NCBI), 1000 Genomes Project (IGP), gnomAD (Broad Institute), ClinVar (NCBI), and OMIM (OMIM). Variants were kept based on the following criteria: [1] Sequencing depth of ≥ 10 reads and minor allele frequencies (MAF) < 1% in both gnomAD and 1000 Genomes databases; [2] Only protein-altering variants, including nonsynonymous, start-loss, stop-gain, splicing, frameshift, and in-frame variants, were considered; [3] Based on an autosomal recessive inheritance model, homozygous or compound heterozygous variants were prioritized. Genetic variants were designated according to the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) recommendations, with + 1 corresponding to the A of the ATG translation initiation codon in the MEI1 reference cDNA sequence (GenBank: NM_152513.4). Candidate variants were validated using Sanger sequencing and quantitative PCR (qPCR) in both probands and their family members. The specific PCR primers used are listed in Table S1. Protein sequences (Homo sapiens, Pan troglodytes, Bos taurus, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus) were retrieved from UniProt database for evolutionary conservation analysis. The impact of missense variants on the three-dimensional (3D) structure of MEI1 protein were predicted using the online AlphaFold server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) and were visualized with PyMOL (v2.5.2).

Whole-genome amplification and sequencing, and quantitative parental contamination test

Single-cell whole-genome amplification (WGA) and the quantitative parental contamination test (qPCT) were performed on three discarded embryos at the zygote, blastomere, and blastocyst stages from the proband in Family 1 (F1:II_4), following previously described [19]. Briefly, single-cell WGA was conducted for embryos from the proband couple in Family 4 using a single-cell WGA kit (Yikon Genomics Ltd., Suzhou, China). The amplified DNA was sequenced on MGISEQ-T7 platform (MGI, China), and genome-wide copy number variation (CNV) analysis was subsequently performed. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from parental peripheral blood samples. Both gDNA and amplified embryonic DNA were processed for genotyping and B allele frequency (BAF) analysis at each single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) site using the Iaap-cli gencall algorithm.

Parent-embryo relationship inference was performed using the Kinship-based INference for Genome-wide association studies (KING) algorithm, as previously described [23]. A kinship coefficient between 0.2 and 0.3 was indicative of a first-degree relationship, whereas a coefficient < 0.022 suggested an unrelated pair. The kinship coefficient dot plot was generated using R (v4.10.1).

Determining the origin of triploidy based on cell division

To infer the parental origin of genomic material in triploid embryos, we selected homozygous loci where the maternal and paternal genotypes were distinct. At such loci, variant allele frequency (VAF), defined as the proportion of the alternative allele in the embryo, was used to evaluate allelic bias. For example, at loci where the father is homozygous reference (0/0) and the mother is homozygous alternative (1/1), a VAF < 0.5 in the embryo suggests a paternal bias (i.e., more paternal than maternal contribution), while a VAF >0.5 suggests a maternal bias. The numbers of loci supporting maternal or paternal bias within the copy number variation (CNV) region were then counted. For triploid embryos, the parent contributing the majority of biased loci is considered the likely source of the additional genome set [24].

To further investigate maternal-origin triploidy, loci with heterozygous maternal genotypes (0/1) and homozygous paternal genotypes (0/0 or 1/1) were analyzed. There are two situations: [1] at maternal 0/1 and paternal 0/0 loci, an embryonic VAF around 0.33 indicates inheritance of both maternal haplotypes, supporting both parental homologs (BPH) model. Conversely, a VAF near 0 or 0.67 suggests inheritance of only one maternal haplotype, supporting the single parental homolog (SPH) model [2]. Similarly, at maternal 0/1 and paternal 1/1 loci, a VAF around 0.67 supports BPH, while a VAF near 0.33 or 1 supports SPH [25]. All loci classified as BPH or SPH were sorted by genomic position and subjected to segmentation using the circular binary segmentation (CBS) algorithm [26]. The presence of a BPH segment on a chromosome indicates meiotic-origin triploidy. Furthermore, for meiotic triploids, if BPH segments flank the centromere, the error likely occurred during meiosis I; if SPH segments flank the centromere, the error likely arose during meiosis II [25].

RNA sequencing analysis on embryos

Three discarded embryos on day 3 from the proband of family 1 (F1:II_4) were collected for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. As controls, four donated embryos from two naturally conceived women were included, following previously described protocols [20]. Paired-end sequencing with 100 bp read length was conducted on MGISEQ-T7 platform (MGI, China). Quality control, reads processing and quantification followed the bioinformatic pipeline as previously described [20]. Differential expression analysis was performed using Deseq2, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with a fold change >2 (upregulated) or < 0.5 (downregulated) and an adjusted P < 0.05. Transcripts per million reads (TPM) values were used for principal component analysis (PCA), correlation analysis, and data visualizations. Over-representation-based Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for DEGs was performed using “clusterProfiler” R package (v4.10.1) [27].

Plasmid construction and cell transfection

The coding sequences of MEI1 (NM_152513.3), including both wild-type and variant forms (c.622T > C, c.2647 C > T, c.T3037G and c.E21-E31del), were inserted into pcDNA3.1-EGFP expression vectors (Hitrobio, China). All constructs were engineered to incorporate an N-terminal HA tag. Following sequence verification by Sanger sequencing, HEK293T cells were transfected with the expression constructs using Lipofectamine® 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) at a concentration of 1.6 µg plasmid per well. Cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) and harvested 30 h post-transfection for subsequent analysis. All the primers are detailed in Table S1.

Reverse transcription and quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples of the probands as well as from a control woman who has given birth naturally, using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (PreAnalytiX, Switzerland) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, reverse transcription was carried out using Evo M-MLV Plus Reverse Transcriptase (Accurate Biology, China). Simultaneously, RNA was extracted from HEK293T cells using TRIzol Reagent (CWBIO, China) following standard protocols. The isolated RNA was then reverse transcribed to cDNA using the HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China), which includes gDNA wiper to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. For validation of RNA-seq results, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed directly on the amplified DNA products. qPCR was performed using a LongGene Q2000B instrument (LongGene, China) with 2× M5 HiPer Dual SYBR Green PCR SuperMix (Mei5bio, China). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer (PC101, Epizyme Biotech, China) containing protease inhibitor. Protein concentrations were determined and equal amounts were resolved on 4–12% SDS-PAGE gels before transfer to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (HATF00010, Millipore, China). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, then probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HA tag (mouse anti-HA, CSB-MA000141M0m, 1:10,000; Cusabio, China) and β-actin (mouse anti-β-actin, 66009-1-Ig, 1:20,000; Proteintech, China). To minimize variation caused by transfection, eGFP from the same expression plasmid was also used as control (66002-1-Ig, 1:50,000; Proteintech, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (SA00001-1, 1:5,000; Proteintech, China). Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system (Shenhua Technology, China) and quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (v9.0). Student’s t test was mainly used as the statistical analysis method. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05. For omics-data analysis and visualization were performed using R software (v4.10.1).

Results

Clinical phenotypes of the patients

The proband in family 1 (F1_II:4) was a 32-year-old woman with a 4-year history of primary infertility and no prior pregnancies. She had regular menstrual cycles and a normal ovarian reserve. Laparoscopy revealed a pelvic adhesion. Her husband’s semen analysis was within normal parameters. Karyotype analysis confirmed that both the proband and her husband had normal chromosomal profiles. She underwent controlled ovarian stimulation following an antagonist protocol. After gonadotropin stimulation, 25 oocytes were retrieved, of which 22 were mature. Of these, 10 oocytes underwent ICSI, while 12 oocytes were fertilized using conventional IVF. Among ICSI-fertilized oocytes, eight developed into3PN or PPN embryos. Among IVF-fertilized oocytes, five exhibited normal 2PN formation, while three developed into 3PN/PPN embryos (Fig. 1A). All 2PN-derived embryos from conventional IVF failed to form blastocysts, and no viable embryos were frozen or implanted. In contrast, two out of three 3PN/PPN-derived embryos from ICSI developed into late-stage blastocysts (Table 1). The proband in family 2 (F2_II:2) was a 35-year-old woman presented with an 8-year history of primary infertility. She had regular menstrual cycles and normal ovarian function. Laparoscopic examination revealed patent bilateral fallopian tubes. She had previously undergone two unsuccessful IVF cycles before attending our center (Table 1). At our center, she underwent a third ICSI cycle, during which 11 oocytes were retrieved, including 8 mature oocytes. Among these oocytes, three developed into normal 2PN embryos, while one was classified as a one-pronuclear (1PN) embryo (Fig. 3A; Table 1). Finally, she got three medium- to high- quality embryos. Two of the three viable frozen embryos were implanted; however, implantation failed to result in pregnancy. The 1PN embryo failed to form blastocyst.

Interestingly, most embryos on day 3 from both probands developed into high-quality embryos at the 7–9 cell stage. Notably, in F1_II:4, the 3PN-derived embryos successfully progressed to the blastocyst stage (Fig. 3A).

Identification of compound heterozygous variants in ME11 gene

WES identified four novel MEI1 variants in both probands. The proband from family 1 (F1_II:4) carried compound heterozygous variants: c.622T > C (p.Ser208Pro) and c.2647 C > T (p.Arg883Ter) (Fig. 1B; Table 2). The proband from family 2 (F2_II:2) carried a compound heterozygous genotype for c.3037T > G (p.Trp1013Gly) and c.2546_3525del (EX21-31del) variants (Fig. 1B and S1; Table 2). The c.2546_3525del variant was predicted to cause a copy number loss encompassing Exons 21 to 31 of MEI1 gene, including the c.3037 position (Fig. S1). As a result, the F2_II:2 patient appeared to be homozygous for c.3037T > G variant in WES and Sanger sequencing data (Fig. 3B). qPCR further validated the CNV loss in Exons 21 and 31 (Fig. 1B). All identified variants affected amino acids that are highly conserved across multiple species (Fig. 2A). All four novel identified variants were located within the Immune Signaling and Apoptosis Regulator domain (Fig. 2C), and structural alterations in the MEI1 protein might be likely to disrupt normal meiotic chromosome synapsis [28]. Our findings broaden the spectrum of MEI1 variants associated with defective embryonic development arrest and other reproductive diseases (Fig. 2C; Table S2).

Impact of ME11 variants on mRNA and protein expression

We investigated the effects of four MEI1 variants on gene and protein expression through in vivo and in vitro experiments. RT-qPCR analysis of peripheral blood samples from two probands (F1_II:4 and F2_II:2) revealed significantly elevated MEI1 mRNA expression compared to controls (Fig. 3A). In vitro analysis using HEK293T cells transfected with wild-type and mutant MEI1 expression plasmids revealed variant-specific effects. At the mRNA level, the p.Arg883Ter variant significantly increased MEI1 expression (P = 0.0004), while the other three variants showed no significant effect (Fig. 3B). This discrepancy was less likely to be due to the differences in transfection efficiency (Fig. S2). At the protein level, western blot analysis revealed that both p.Arg883Ter variants reduced MEI1 protein abundance (P = 0.031), consistent with nonsense-mediated decay of the truncated transcript (Fig. 3, C-D; Fig. S3). In contrast, p.Trp1013Gly increased protein levels (P = 0.016, Fig. 3, C-D). The p.Ser208Pro and E21_E31del variants did not alter protein expression (Fig. 3, C-D). These findings suggested that MEI1 variants differentially impacted transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, potentially affecting protein stability and function.

Identification of copy number variation and kinship in embryos derived from oocytes carrying ME11 variants

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of embryos from the proband (F1:II_4) in family 1 revealed significant chromosomal abnormalities (Fig. 4A). Notable chromosomal aneuploidies were detected in the 3PN-derived blastocyst and 2PN-derived blastomere, both carrying heterozygous variants (c.622T > C and c.2647 C > T). In contrast, the 3PN-derived zygote appeared euploid, carrying only one heterozygous variant (c.2647 C > T) (Fig. 4A). However, qPCT confirmed that all embryos were triploid, with the majority of chromosomes originating from the maternal side (Fig. 4B). Identification of the origin of triploidy based on cell division in in abnormal human embryos

Resolving the origin of triploidy from WGS data, we found that all three embryo samples exhibited BPH segments, indicating that they were triploid of meiotic origin (Fig. 4D). For example, on chromosome 22, which harbors the MEI1 gene, the presence of an acrocentric short arm limits reliable detection by next-generation sequencing (NGS). Therefore, the classification of the meiotic division stage was based on the segment type downstream of the centromere. In the 3PN zygote, the region downstream of the centromere on chromosome 22 was classified as an SPH segment, suggesting that the triploidy resulted from meiosis II errors. In contrast, both the 3PN blastocyst and the 2PN blastomere exhibited BPH segments downstream of the centromere, indicating that these two embryos originated from meiosis I errors.

RNA-seq analysis of embryos derived from the patient

To investigate the transcriptomic consequences of MEI1 variants on embryonic development, we performed RNA-seq analysis on the day 3 embryos from the patient (F1:II_4) and control women. Distinct gene expression profiles were observed between patient and control embryos (Fig. 5, A-B), with considerable heterogeneity among the patient’s embryos (Fig. 5B), suggesting disordered transcriptional activation in embryos derived from affected oocytes. We identified 2,005 downregulated genes and 1,904 upregulated genes in patient’s embryos (Fig. 8C; Table S3). Notably, MKI67 (classical cell proliferation marker) and WEE2 (key regulator of oocyte meiotic maturation) exhibited markedly dysregulated expression in the patient’s embryos (Fig. 5D). While MEI1 gene expression appeared reduced, the change was not statistically significant (Fig. 5D). GO enrichment analysis revealed that downregulated genes were strongly enriched in pathways related to meiotic cell cycle, beta-catenin binding, and epigenetic programming of transcription activation, including transcription coactivator activity, histone demethylase/deacetylase, and RNA polymerase mediated transcription (Fig. 8E; Fig. S4; Table S4). Importantly, maternal-effect genes exhibited significant dysregulation in the patient’s embryos, including FBXO43, OOEP, and WEE2, while KHDC3L, CENPH, and COX15 genes were upregulated (Fig. 5F). To validate the RNA-seq result, we performed qPCR on the available embryo samples for four downregulated genes, including MEI1, MKI67, WEE2 and ASTL. Discussion

In this study, we identified four novel biallelic MEI1 variants (c.622T > C [p.Ser208Pro], c.2647 C > T [p.Arg883Ter], c.3037T > G [p.Trp1013Gly], and c.2546_3825del) in women experiencing recurrent aberrant embryonic development, expanding the mutational spectrum of MEI1-related reproductive disorders. Through genomic and transcriptomic profiling of patient-derived embryos, we revealed that these variants induce widespread chromosomal instability and maternal triploidy, and that disrupt gene expression of essential meiotic cell cycle regulators (e.g. WEE2, MKI67) and epigenetic reprogramming modulators (e.g. HDAC1/2, KDM5B). Additionally, our work provides transcriptomic hints that MEI1 dysfunction may impair maternal-to-zygotic transition by silencing maternal effect genes (OOEP, NLRP5) at early embryonic development. These findings support MEI1’s role in oocyte meiosis and embryonic chromosomal stability and expand knowledge towards molecular mechanisms of MEI1 variants. Serving as an example of genetic diagnosing, our work highlights the importance of genetic counseling for idiopathic embryonic arrest and infertility cases, such as MEI1-related infertility.

MEI1 variants, mostly bi-allelic, have been identified in multiple reproductive disorders, including non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) in males and embryonic development arrest (EDA), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), and hydatidiform mole (HM) in females [4, 6, 7]. We summarized the distributions of identified MEI1 variants. Intriguingly, we found that they were broadly scattered across the entire gene, with certain highly recurrent variants being common to both male and female cases. Interestingly, NOA-associated variants tend to locate towards the N-terminal, while EDA-related variants - including three novel variants identified in our study – tends to cluster toward the C-terminal. This pattern may imply potential differential interactions with downstream proteins involved in distinct biological pathways. However, this hypothesis requires further validation through comprehensive functional assays and large-cohort replication studies.

MEI1 plays a crucial role in meiotic spindle formation and meiotic double-strand breaks (DSBs) formations [12, 29]. This MEI1-mediated DSB formation machinery undergoes precise cyclic regulatory mechanisms during gametogenesis, from initiating DSBs via an epistatic interaction with DMC1 in meiotic prophases to suppressing DSBs through binding to the ZFP541 in mid-pachytene stage [30, 31]. Absence of Mei1 in mice impaired chromosome synapsis and disrupted RAD51 loading onto meiotic chromosomes [32], indicating the critical role of MEI1 in maintaining chromosome stability. Indeed, our genome sequencing of patient-derived embryos provides further evidence of the genomic instability caused by variants in the MEI1 gene. This instability could be, at least partially, attributed to MEI1-induced impaired synapsis. Our results support the previously proposed hypothesis that MEI1 variants may disrupt spindle-related functions during embryo development [17].

Unlike previous reports, our patients predominantly showed tri-pronuclear (3PN) or poly-pronuclear (PPN) embryos after ICSI. Genetic analysis confirmed these embryos contained excessive maternal genetic material. We propose that MEI1 variants impair meiosis I by abnormal chromosome synapsis. Despite the apparent second polar body extrusion, the egg contains additional chromosome sets which should be assigned to the second polar body. Notably, these 3PN/PPN embryos successfully developed to blastocyst stage, suggesting they could implant and potentially develop into hydatidiform moles in natural pregnancies. Our research provides morphological characterization of MEI1-related hydatidiform moles prior to implantation and suggest the importance of maternal effect underlying triploid blastocysts. For clinical practice, we recommend MEI1 variant screening in patients showing abnormal fertilization patterns, especially multiple pronuclei following ICSI. In these cases, even embryos with apparently normal 2PN morphology may be triploid, which would significantly compromise assisted reproduction outcomes. Incorporating MEI1 screening into clinical workflows could improve patient counseling and treatment planning.

The molecular mechanism and downstream pathways underlying MEI1-induced EDA remain incompletely understood. In this study we addressed this question by conducting transcriptomic analysis on patient’s embryos. Our results revealed a notable downregulation of genes related to meiotic and mitotic cell cycle. It implies that MEI1 variants disrupt the intact regulation of cell cycles, likely due to the crucial role of MEI1 in spindle formation during cell cycle. Notably, we also found that MEI1 variants may suppress epigenetic programming and transcriptional regulation of zygotic genome, which potentially lead to failure of zygotic genome activation [3, 6]. This finding was further corroborated by the inactivation of key maternal effect genes (e.g. FBXO43 and OOEP), which have been implicated in embryonic development arrest [6]. OOEP is a critical component of the subcortical maternal complex (SCMC), while FBXO43, involved in meiosis regulation and spermatogenesis. Thus, our results may propose a dual mechanism hypothesis for the impact of MEI1 variants. First, there is a direct effect on the impairment of meiotic and mitotic cell cycles and genomic stability. Second, MEI1 variants dysregulate epigenetic processes, thereby disrupting zygotic genome activation. The coordinated disruption of both genomic and epigenetic stability may provide new understanding for the mechanism of MEI-induced EDA, although further research is needed to confirm this hypothesis.

We acknowledge that the current study has several limitations. Firstly, the RNA-seq data of control embryos were sourced from our previous work. This may potentially introduce a batch effect, despite our application of standardized and identical experimental pipelines across both studies. Given this, we advocate for a cautious interpretation of the results and for future replications under stringent criteria. Secondly, the functional evidence is currently derived from in vitro assays. In vivo assays such as transgenic mouse models with point mutation, are necessary to confirm the pathogenicity of these variants. Another plausible approach is to microinject mRNA of Mei1 carrying these variants into Mei1-null mouse oocytes followed by IVF to investigate the embryo development. Intriguingly, we did not detect a significant decline in the protein level resulting from the large-fragment CNV deletion (c.2546_3825del). This suggests that the protein with this deletion could be theoretically stable, yet it may have either lost its normal function (loss-of-function) or exerted some abnormal functions (gain-of-function). Further studies are warranted to clarify the unsolved questions.

Conclusions

Our findings broaden the spectrum of MEI1 variants associated with embryonic developmental arrest and highlight the essential role of MEI1 in oocyte meiosis and early embryonic chromosomal stability. By elucidating how MEI1 variants compromise genomic stability, our study advances etiological understanding of embryonic arrest and underscores genetic counseling for idiopathic embryonic arrest and infertility cases to improve IVF outcomes.
