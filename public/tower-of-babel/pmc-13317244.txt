The prevalence and associations of stroke history with self-reported sleep disturbances in China: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuxiang Yang, Changzheng Yuan, Martin H. de Borst, Kun Huang, Wei Piao, Shuya Cai, Mulei Chen, Fusheng Li, Jing Nan, Dongmei Yu, Xiang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep is essential for stroke prognosis and recovery. While stroke history has been associated with sleep disturbances, evidence from China remains limited. This study aimed to characterize the prevalence of multiple sleep disturbances and to examine their associations with stroke history based on the China Nutrition and Health Surveillance 2015–2017.Methods157,135 respondents were included in the current study. Stroke history was defined as being first diagnosed over one year ago. Sleep disturbances were defined as non-optimal sleep duration (< 6 or ≥ 9 h), self-reported sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), and insomnia symptoms. The number of sleep disturbances was further used as an indicator of overall sleep quality. All analyses were weighted to obtain national representativeness. Weighted multivariable Poisson regression and multinomial logistic regression were applied to evaluate the associations between stroke history and sleep disturbances.ResultsThe prevalence of sleep disturbances was significantly higher among the stroke population than non-stroke population (non-optimal sleep duration: 41.7% vs. 26.3%; self-reported SDB: 42.1% vs. 27.6%; insomnia symptoms: 71.6% vs. 44.7%, all P < 0.01). In addition, the prevalence of having at least one sleep disturbance among the stroke population was higher than in non-stroke population (87.4% vs. 66.4%, P < 0.0001). Positive associations were observed between stroke history and prevalence of non-optimal sleep duration and insomnia symptoms (non-optimal sleep duration: multivariable-adjusted PR = 1.28; insomnia symptoms: PR = 1.12, all P < 0.0001), whereas no association was observed for self-reported SDB (PR = 1.01, P = 0.8045). Moreover, the strength of association between stroke and sleep disturbances increased with the number of co-occurring sleep disturbances (for 1 type: PR = 1.20; 2 types: PR = 1.40; 3 types: PR = 2.13, all P < 0.0001).ConclusionsStroke history was associated with an increased burden of sleep disturbances across multiple dimensions, particularly non-optimal sleep duration, insomnia symptoms, and sleep disturbance co-occurrence. The current results highlight the multidimensional nature of post-stroke sleep disturbances and the importance of comprehensive sleep assessment and management in post-stroke care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26635-7.

Background

Stroke is presently the leading cause of long-term disability and mortality, placing an enormous burden on healthcare systems and society [1, 2]. In addition to the primary prevention of stroke occurrence, post-stroke management remains a major challenge, particularly given the large stroke population and the gap between guideline recommendations and clinical practice in China [1]. Thus, identifying modifiable risk factors to improve outcomes and rehabilitation after stroke is essential. Sleep is considered a key component of post-stroke health, given its associations with physiological processes such as regulating metabolic, endocrine hormones, and inflammation [3, 4]. Previous studies have increasingly regarded suboptimal sleep as both a consequence of stroke and a potentially modifiable factor influencing recovery and long-term prognosis [5–8]. Poor sleep is likely to follow the occurrence of stroke and might even worsen the outcomes post-stroke in the long term, such as impacting self-care ability, cognitive function, overall health, and quality of life and increasing the risk of stroke recurrence [9–13]. Nevertheless, sleep disturbances remain insufficiently recognized and managed in post-stroke care, partly due to limited routine screening and a limited understanding of underlying mechanisms [14–16].

Sleep could be evaluated by examining single sleep disturbances, such as abnormal sleep duration, sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), and insomnia [17]. However, these sleep components are usually interrelated, and one component’s biological effects frequently depend on the others’ presence to affect overall sleep quality [18]. The multifaceted interactions highlight the importance of evaluating and modifying sleep by comprehensive measures. Accordingly, the number of sleep disturbances, a concept used in previous studies, integrates several aspects of sleep behavior that may compensate for one another [17, 19]. It provides a comprehensive tool to assess sleep quality and further associations with health outcomes. Moreover, from the perspective of the whole sleep quality, it could also indicate multifaceted interventional sleep-related targets regarding the promotion of disease prevention and prognosis, as well as enhance the applicability of intervention from the perspectives of either clinical or public health [17].

Despite accumulating evidence on sleep disturbances in the post-stroke stage, most existing studies have focused on a single sleep disturbance, relied on small samples, or were conducted mainly among Western populations [20, 21]. Moreover, limited attention has been paid to the co-occurrence of multiple sleep disturbances [22], which may better reflect overall sleep profiles among stroke survivors. Evidence from a large, nationally representative study in the Chinese population, particularly among individuals in the chronic phase of stroke, remains understudied. Therefore, the current study aimed to comprehensively examine both individual sleep disturbances and their co-occurrence among stroke and non-stroke populations, and to further evaluate the associations between stroke history and sleep disturbances at the population level in China.

Methods

Study design

The current study was conducted based on the China Nutrition and Health Surveillance (CNHS) 2015–2017, a national cross-sectional survey that contained the most recent available information regarding major chronic diseases and related factors, nutritional status, and lifestyle behaviors in China. A stratified, multistage, and random sampling method was applied to obtain nationwide samples of over 6-year-old from 31 provinces (municipalities/autonomous) in mainland China. Considering the urbanization rate, population size, mortality, and no response rate (set to 10%), 189,605 respondents were surveyed in the adult section (except for pregnancy) of CNHS 2015–2017, which were deemed to have national representativeness. Specific information regarding monitoring contents, investigation, data collection, and quality control for CNHS was detailed in the previous study [23].

The project was approved by the Ethnic Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention on 15-06-2015 (protocol code: 201519-B). All the participants were informed and signed the informed consent form in advance.

Sample selection

Participants were included by following exclusion criteria: (1) participants with incomplete information on basic demographics (n = 2,245); (2) without completed dietary survey data (n = 7,903); (3) had no available data on physical examinations and laboratory tests (n = 14,971); (4) missing data on the diagnostic date or the age (in years) at the time stroke was diagnosed, and/or had experienced a stroke within the past year, to ensure that only participants in the chronic phase of stroke were included, as sleep disturbances might precede stroke onset or improved after the acute or subacute post-stroke phases [12, 24] (n = 1,154); (5) missing data on sleep-related information (n = 1,311); (6) reported to have taken any hypnotic and/or soporific medication in the past 30 days, which might be a confounder regarding the associations between post-stroke and sleep disturbances [21] (n = 4,886). Finally, a total of 157,135 participants were eligible for further analysis. Detailed information on the selection process was shown in Fig. 1.

Basic information

Basic information collection was based on the unified questionnaire in the CNHS 2015–2017, including socioeconomic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, and major chronic disease status. All the related information was investigated and recorded by well-trained investigators through face-to-face interviews.

Sleep outcomes

Primary sleep outcomes were binary based on face-to-face survey by trained investigators and were evaluated by three aspects, namely sleep duration, self-reported SDB, and insomnia symptoms in the CNHS 2015–2017. Sleep duration was defined as optimal sleep duration (6–8 h) or non-optimal sleep duration (< 6 or ≥ 9 h) based on the threshold given by previous study [25]. Self-reported SDB and insomnia symptoms were based on the frequencies of at least three times a week within the past 30 days. For instance, the question defined self-reported SDB, “Did you have a problem in snoring, choking or breath-holding during sleep?” and insomnia symptoms was defined by any one of difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty staying asleep, or waking up too early [26]. Additionally, secondary outcomes, including short sleep duration (< 6 h), long sleep duration (≥ 9 h), difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty staying asleep, and waking up too early, as defined by the above questions, were also included in the analysis. Further information was provided in Table S1.

Furthermore, we scaled the number of sleep disturbances corresponding to the methods in the previous study by summing up the amount of primary sleep outcomes to evaluate the whole profile of sleep quality and interaction [27]. 0 point was the best, which indicated the respondent was without any sleep disturbance, while a higher score indicated worse sleep quality.

Diet assessment

CNHS 2015–2017 included dietary survey and non-dietary survey respondents. A 64-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was applied for full-dietary survey respondents, while a 12-item semi-quantitative FFQ was applied to simplified-dietary survey respondents. For both types of respondents, the frequency of consumption (times per day, week, month, or year) and the amount per intake (grams or milliliters per time) of vegetables, fruits, and alcohol were assessed. The average daily intakes (grams or milliliters per day) of the above food groups were further calculated based on the frequencies and amount per intake [28].

The survey on edible oils and condiments was conducted at a household level and was administered to all the respondents. Briefly, the average daily intakes of edible oils and condiments per family member were estimated by the number of family members who usually eat at home per meal (breakfast, lunch, and dinner) and total consumption of edible oils and condiments in the past 30 days [28]. Furthermore, we estimated the average daily sodium intake for each respondent based on condiment consumptions using the China Food Consumption Table (Standard Edition) [29].

Physical examinations and laboratory tests

Respondents were required to conduct this part on an empty stomach in the morning. All the related equipment and machines were unified to the same brand and type in all the monitoring locations to reduce measurement bias. Physical examinations including body height (cm), weight (kg), and blood pressure (mmHg) were measured by well-trained staff from the local Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Laboratory tests on blood indices, including fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C), High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C), and glycohemoglobin (HbA1c) were tested using 8 mL of overnight fasting blood for each respondent. All the processes were taken by professionals under strict quality control.

Disease definitions

Self-reported physician-diagnosed stroke history was based on the question, “Were you diagnosed with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke in a Township Health Center/Community Healthcare Center or senior medical institute?“. Participants who reported having a history of stroke were asked to provide the accurate date (month-year) or age of the initial diagnosis. The time interval to diagnosis was obtained by subtracting the date or age on the day of diagnosis from the date or age on the day of investigation. Meanwhile, the available information on the time interval since stroke onset was only accurate to the year, as some participants reported their age at diagnosis rather than the exact date. Therefore, our analysis focused on stroke individuals in the chronic phase (≥ 1 year after stroke onset).

The current study included several chronic diseases that may be confounding variables [8, 30–32]. In addition to a self-reported physician-diagnosed disease history, hypertension was defined based on 3-time average systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and (or) diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg and (or) taking any anti-hypertensive medications; diabetes mellitus was defined based on FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L and (or) HbA1c ≥ 6.5% and (or) taking glucose-lowering medications; and dyslipidemia was defined based on serum TC ≥ 6.2 mmol/L and (or) TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L and (or) LDL-C ≥ 4.14 mmol/L and (or) HDL-C ≤ 1.04 mmol/L and (or) taking any lipid-lowering medications [28]. Other chronic diseases, atherosclerosis heart disease (ASCVD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, bone and joint diseases, waist and neck diseases, chronic digestive diseases, chronic renal diseases, and cancers were defined based on self-reported physician-diagnosed disease history.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous studies that reported associations between stroke and sleep disturbances [8, 33–38]. Model I was the crude model without adjusting covariates to obtain primary associations between stroke and sleep disturbances. Model II was adjusted for sex (male/female), age group (18~/45~/60~), Body Mass Index (BMI) (underweight/normal/overweight/obese) [28], education (primary school or below/middle school/senior high school or above), household income (per capita, not given/< 10,000/10,000 ~/25,000 ~ CNY), living area (urban/rural), marital status (married/other status), current smoking (No/Yes), excessive drinking (No/Yes, according to daily pure alcohol intake > 15 g) [39], physical activity (low/moderate/high) [40], sedentary behavior (< 2/2~/4 ~ hours), excessive sodium intake (No/Yes, according to daily sodium intake > 2,000 mg) [41], vegetable intake (in tertiles), and fruit intake (in tertiles). Model III was further adjusted for family history of stroke (No/Yes) and chronic disease status mentioned above.

Statistical analysis

Statistical processes were weighted using individual sampling weights according to the Sixth Chinese Nationwide Population Census data and incorporated into the complex survey design analysis. Specifically, the weighting procedure incorporated sampling stratification (urban/rural), clustering (surveillance site), and corresponding population weights by age and sex to ensure national representativeness. All the variables were categorized into categorical variables and were described with numbers and weighted proportions (n, %). Rao-Scott Chi-square test was applied to compare the statistical differences between groups. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of sleep disturbances might vary from stroke recovery phases (i.e., acute, subacute, and chronic) [42]. Thus, we further evaluate the prevalence of sleep disturbances by post-stroke duration (i.e., after 1/2/3~/5 ~ years). Previous studies have shown that using the prevalent ratio (PR) could lessen the overestimation of associations compared with using odds ratio (OR) under the condition that the prevalence of outcomes is high (> 10%) [43, 44]. Accordingly, we analyzed the associations between stroke history and sleep disturbances by weighted Poisson regression with three adjustment models to calculate PRs and related 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We also applied multinominal logistic regression to compare the associations between stroke history and further multi-categories of sleep duration/the number of sleep disturbances, as the reference of non-stroke respondents without non-optimal sleep duration/sleep disturbance. For this part, we used ORs and the weighted prevalences of sleep disturbance in non-stroke respondents (p0) to estimate the PRs and 95% CIs following the formula [43]:

Moreover, we conducted subgroup analysis by sex, age group, BMI, current smoking, excessive drinking, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus as potential interactors in the corresponding regression models [45]. The probability of interaction between stroke and the subgroup factors on the risk of sleep disturbances was calculated using the likelihood ratio test by additionally including the corresponding product term in the full-adjustment model [46].

All the statistical analysis was conducted by SAS (version 9.4), and the SAS macro %surveygenmod was applied to conduct weighted Poisson regression [47]. The two-tailed P value was considered statistically significant at the level of 0.05. Besides, given multiple comparisons, P for interaction (P-int) was considered statistically significant in subgroup analysis when the corresponding value < 0.01. Plots were drafted by R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Basic characteristics

Among 157,135 respondents, a total of 4,599 participants (2.9%) had self-reported physician-diagnosed stroke, while the weighted stroke prevalence was 1.9%. A comparison of characteristics between non-stroke and stroke populations is shown in Table 1 and Table S2. Briefly, the majority were males, elderly, overweight, and had lower education levels and household income. The proportions of stroke participants with suboptimal lifestyle behaviors, family history, and other chronic diseases were shown to be significantly higher than those who were non-stroke, except for excessive drinking (all P < 0.05). However, the compositional difference between stroke and non-stroke populations was insignificant between living area (P = 0.1), current smoking (P = 0.06), and sedentary behavior (P = 0.16).

Prevalence of sleep disturbances

Overall, the prevalence of all sleep disturbances among the stroke population was significantly higher than that of those without stroke (Table 2). Specifically, non-optimal sleep duration (< 6 or ≥ 9 h), self-reported sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), and insomnia symptoms were all more prevalent in the stroke population (41.7% vs. 26.3%, 42.1% vs. 27.6%, and 71.6% vs. 44.7%, respectively; all P < 0.01).

Discussion

Previous studies mainly focused on individual sleep disturbances or were based on Western populations, while the present study provides a more comprehensive, population-based assessment of post-stroke sleep disturbances by jointly examining multiple sleep dimensions and their co-occurrence in a nationally representative Chinese sample. In this study, we found that sleep disturbances were highly prevalent among individuals with a history of stroke. Compared with participants without stroke, stroke survivors had significantly higher prevalence of non-optimal sleep duration, self-reported SDB, and insomnia symptoms. After multivariable adjustment, stroke history remained positively associated with non-optimal sleep duration and insomnia symptoms, whereas the association with self-reported SDB was attenuated. In addition, individuals with stroke history were more likely to experience multiple co-occurring sleep disturbances.

Notably, nearly 90% of participants with stroke reported at least one sleep disturbance, indicating the great burden of sleep problems in this population. However, because sleep disturbances were assessed using a 30-day recall period and the survey was conducted throughout the year, the observed prevalence may have been influenced by seasonal variation in sleep behaviors [48]. Nevertheless, our findings are consistent with evidence from the Canadian Community Health Survey, which also reported a higher prevalence of sleep disturbances among adults with stroke, although direct comparisons are limited by differences in survey instruments and definitions [8].

The current study showed that stroke survivors were more likely to experience non-optimal sleep duration. The positive association between stroke history and suboptimal sleep duration has also been reported in previous studies [8, 49, 50]. Notably, our results showed that the association between stroke history and long sleep duration was more pronounced than that with short sleep duration. This pattern is consistent with previous studies showing a tendency toward prolonged sleep or hypersomnia among stroke survivors, even several years after stroke onset [20, 21, 51]. One possible explanation is that prolonged sleep may reflect a compensatory response to central nervous system injury, thereby facilitating neural repair and the clearance of neurotoxic waste [12, 52]. However, as prolonged sleep has also been identified as a potential marker of cardiovascular risk and mortality [53], maintaining sleep duration within an optimal range may be particularly important for long-term post-stroke health.

SDB is common among stroke survivors and is considered a potentially modifiable factor affecting stroke prognosis [54]. In our results, 42.1% of participants with stroke history reported SDB, which was similar to the estimate in previous studies [24, 55]. The current association between stroke history and SDB was positive in Models I and II, but was attenuated after full adjustment for family history and comorbid conditions. This finding is consistent with previous evidence that SDB is prevalent both before and after stroke and may be strongly influenced by coexisting chronic diseases (e.g., asthma and COPD), which could partly explain the attenuation of the observed association [55–57]. In addition, the previous study has also shown that the prevalence of SDB may decrease over time following stroke as post-stroke recovery progresses, which may partly explain the findings observed in the chronic stage of our study population [42].

Insomnia is an important component of overall sleep quality and has been shown to predict recovery outcomes after stroke [58]. Previous studies have reported that approximately 60% of stroke survivors experience insomnia symptoms, with a consistently higher prevalence than that observed in the general population [13, 21]. The current study found that 71.6% of stroke patients had insomnia symptoms. The relatively higher prevalence observed in the current study may be partly attributable to differences in symptom-based definitions and the shorter recall period used in the investigation [49, 59]. Seasonal variation in sleep patterns may also have influenced symptom reporting [48]. Nevertheless, insomnia symptoms remained significantly more prevalent among individuals with stroke than among those without stroke, supporting a positive association between stroke history and lower sleep quality.

The current study further examined the co-occurrence of multiple sleep disturbances and their association with stroke history. The co-occurrence of sleep disturbances, reflecting multiple facets of sleep behaviors, has been shown to be an influencing factor for all-cause mortality [17]. Non-optimal sleep duration, self-reported SDB, and insomnia symptoms were selected as indicators of overall sleep quality based on their high prevalence after stroke and their relevance to stroke recovery [20]. These sleep disturbances may interact and co-occur after stroke since sleep is affected by the emergence of stroke and combines multiple aspects of behavior [8, 20]. Hence, targeting one single sleep behavior may be insufficient, highlighting the importance of modifying sleep in an integrated manner [6, 22]. Previous studies have shown that nearly two-thirds of stroke survivors report at least one sleep problem, and that 78.2% experience multiple sleep-related disturbances [8, 22]. In the current study, at least one sleep disturbance was reported by 66.4% of participants without stroke and by nearly 90% of those with stroke, indicating a great burden of sleep problems not only among stroke survivors but also in the general adult population. Meanwhile, the risk of co-occurring sleep disturbances was 1.43–2.21 times higher among individuals with stroke than among those without stroke, consistent with findings from a Canadian population-based study [8].

In the interaction analyses, significant effect modification by several lifestyle factors, including sex, current smoking, and excessive drinking, was observed for the association between stroke history and self-reported SDB. A positive association between stroke history and self-reported SDB was observed among individuals with normal BMI, whereas positive but non-significant tendencies were observed among males, non-current smokers, and non-excessive drinkers. Sex-related anatomical and hormonal differences in upper airway structure and neck adipose tissue accumulation may contribute to differences in prevalence of SDB between sexes and further explain the observed results [60–62]. Individuals with higher BMI and unhealthy lifestyle habits (e.g., smoking and excessive alcohol consumption) may already be at increased risk of SDB through mechanisms such as inflammatory responses, neuromuscular dysfunction, and reduced upper airway stability [3, 63–65]. However, the associations between stroke history and SDB appeared to differ across BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption subgroups. This may partly be explained by the fact that individuals with higher BMI, current smoking, or excessive drinking were already at elevated risk, therefore the association between stroke history and SDB might be masked in the current results.

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine the associations between stroke history and multiple sleep disturbances in a large, nationally representative Chinese population study, providing population-level evidence on post-stroke sleep management. However, some limitations should be taken into consideration in further studies. First, due to the cross-sectional design, the current study cannot draw the causal relationship between stroke history and sleep disturbances. Moreover, sleep conditions before stroke were not assessed, which might bias the observed associations [21]. Second, stroke history was self-reported, which may have led to underestimation, and information on stroke recurrence was not available, limiting further evaluation of its impact on sleep disturbances [16]. Third, detailed stroke-related clinical information, including stroke severity and subtype, which may influence sleep outcomes, was not sufficiently available to support robust subtype-specific analyses in the current dataset [8, 66]. Fourth, sleep was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire rather than objective measurements, and several sleep-related factors, including chronotype, daytime sleepiness, and unrefreshing sleep, were not collected in this study, which may have restricted a more comprehensive evaluation of sleep disturbances [8, 27]. Finally, the 30-day recall period may not fully reflect long-term sleep patterns. Despite these limitations, the current study provides important insights into the burden and co-occurrence of sleep disturbances among adults with stroke in China and may help inform future longitudinal and interventional studies.

Conclusion

In this large, nationally representative study of Chinese adults, stroke history was associated with a substantially increased burden of sleep disturbances across multiple dimensions, including non-optimal sleep duration, insomnia symptoms, and sleep disturbance co-occurrence. Beyond confirming that sleep problems are common after stroke, our findings show that sleep disturbances frequently coexist, indicating the limitations of single-aspect approaches to post-stroke sleep assessment. By providing population-level evidence from a non-Western population, this study extends existing literature and underscores the importance of integrated sleep evaluation and management in post-stroke care. These results suggest that addressing sleep as a multidimensional condition may represent an important target for improving long-term recovery and quality of life among stroke survivors.
