Kernel-Adaptive PI-ELMs for Forward and Inverse Problems in PDEs with Sharp Gradients
Vikas Dwivedi, Balaji Srinivasan, Monica Sigovan, Bruno Sixou

Abstract
This paper introduces the Kernel Adaptive Physics-Informed Extreme Learning Machine (KAPI-ELM), an adaptive Radial Basis Function (RBF)-based extension of PI-ELM designed to solve both forward and inverse Partial Differential Equation (PDE) problems involving localized sharp gradients. While PI-ELMs outperform the traditional Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) in speed due to their single-shot, least square optimization; this advantage comes at a cost—their fixed, randomly initialized input layer limits their ability to capture sharp gradients. To overcome this limitation, we introduce a lightweight Bayesian Optimization (BO) framework that, instead of adjusting each input layer parameter individually as in traditional backpropagation, learns a small set of hyperparameters defining the statistical distribution from which the input weights are drawn. This novel distributional optimization strategy—combining BO for input layer distributional parameters with least-squares optimization for output layer network parameters—enables KAPI-ELM to preserve PI-ELM’s speed while matching or exceeding the expressiveness of PINNs. We validate the proposed methodology on several challenging forward and inverse PDE benchmarks, including a 1D singularly perturbed convection-diffusion equation, a 2D Poisson equation with sharp localized sources, and a time-dependent advection equation. Notably, KAPI-ELM achieves state-of-the-art accuracy in both forward and inverse settings. In stiff PDE regimes, it matches or even outperforms advanced methods such as the Extended Theory of Functional Connections (XTFC), while requiring nearly an order of magnitude fewer tunable parameters. These results establish the potential of KAPI-ELM as a scalable, interpretable, and generalizable physics-informed learning framework, especially in stiff PDE regimes.

Introduction

PINNs
(
Raissi et al., 2019
)
and operator learning frameworks such as DeepONets
(
Lu et al., 2021
)
and Fourier Neural Operators
(
Li et al., 2021
)
(FNO) have shown great promise in solving forward and inverse problems governed by PDEs. However, they often struggle
(
Lee et al., 2025
;
Du et al., 2023
;
You et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2024
;
Lin et al., 2023a
)
when applied to singularly perturbed problems, which are characterized by sharp spatial gradients or stiff temporal dynamics. These challenges are primarily due to spectral bias
(
Rahaman et al., 2019
;
Tancik et al., 2020
;
Zhi-Qin et al., 2020
)
, making it difficult to accurately capture localized or high-frequency features essential for resolving stiff regions.

To overcome these issues, several enhancements to the PINNs have been proposed. For example, NTK-guided PINNs
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
leverage insights from the neural tangent kernel theory to inform training dynamics and reduce spectral bias. Self-adaptive PINNs
(
McClenny and Braga-Neto, 2023
)
employ attention-inspired trainable weights to focus learning on stiff regions. Semi-analytic PINNs
(
Gie et al., 2024
)
integrate corrector functions derived from boundary layer analysis to handle singularities. VS-PINNs
(
Ko and Park, 2025
)
use variable scaling to zoom in on stiff regions during training. Curriculum learning-driven PINNs
(
Krishnapriyan et al., 2021
)
gradually increase the complexity of the problem, progressing from non-stiff to stiff cases. Operator learning-enhanced PINNs
(
Lin et al., 2023b
)
use pretrained operators to initialize PINNs, enabling faster convergence on difficult problems. Theory-guided PINNs
(
Arzani et al., 2023
)
model inner and outer boundary layers using separate parallel subnetworks. Although diverse in methodology, these approaches share a common strategy: first identify stiff regions, then respond by adapting the network architecture, rescaling the loss function, refining the sampling, or hypothesis. Despite their success, they remain computationally expensive and can suffer from stability issues.

In contrast, randomized fixed-weight networks such as Physics-Informed Extreme Learning Machines (PI-ELMs)
(
Dwivedi and Srinivasan, 2020
;
Dong and Li, 2021
)
and the Extended Theory of Functional Connections (XTFC)
(
Schiassi et al., 2021
)
provide lightweight alternatives for forward problems. These models offer fast training by avoiding backpropagation and leveraging fixed input-layer weights. Several recent papers
(
Calabrò et al., 2021
;
De Florio et al., 2024
)
demonstrated XTFC’s capability to resolve singular perturbations in convection-diffusion equations by satisfying the boundary conditions analytically. However, these methods require a large number of neurons, limiting their scalability to higher dimensions. Similarly, PI-ELMs are highly sensitive to hyperparameters in stiff regimes and struggle with inverse problems due to the inherent nonlinearity, even when the forward PDE is linear
(
Dong and Yang, 2022
)
.

In summary, two contrasting paradigms exist for solving PDEs:
(1) Deep neural networks such as PINNs, which are highly versatile and effective for both forward and inverse problems, but computationally expensive and often unstable in stiff regimes; and
(2) Fixed-weight models like PI-ELMs and XTFC, which are efficient and well-suited for linear non-stiff forward problems, but limited in generality, scalability, and inverse problem capabilities.

In this paper, we ask:
Can we reconcile these two contrasting paradigms by developing adaptive PI-ELMs that retain the computational efficiency of fixed-weight models while incorporating the flexibility and expressiveness of PINNs?

A natural but inefficient approach to adaptivity in PI-ELMs could be alternating optimization—iteratively updating the input-layer parameters while fixing the output layer, and vice versa. However, due to large number of weights, this method introduces significant computational overhead and results in slower performance than even baseline PINNs.

Our Contributions

To address these limitations, we propose the following novel ideas in this paper:

•

Distributional Optimization
: Rather than directly tuning a large number of individual RBF centers and widths, we introduce a low-dimensional set of hyperparameters that governs their statistical distribution as well as total number, significantly reducing the search space and therefore computational cost.

•

Efficient Alternating Optimization Strategy
: We decouple the distributional parameter tuning from the closed-form least-squares solution of PI-ELMs. Unlike PINNs, which rely on end-to-end backpropagation, our approach enables external optimization of kernel distributions, preserving the efficiency of the PI-ELM framework.

•

Physics-Informed Bayesian Optimization
: Since RBF centers and widths have clear physical interpretations, their distributional hyperparameters are also physically meaningful. This allows us to define informed search bounds, enabling efficient Bayesian optimization over a low-dimensional space.

2
Preliminaries: PI-ELM and PINN

PI-ELM Formulation

Consider a 2D linear PDE of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}(u)=R(x),\quad(x,y)\in\Omega,

(1)

where
R(x)
is the source term,
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
is a differential operator depending on parameter
\nu
, subject to boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
.

In the RBF-based PI-ELM framework
(
Dwivedi and Srinivasan, 2020
;
Dong and Li, 2021
)
, the solution
u(x,y)
is approximated as a weighted sum of
N^{*}
Gaussian RBFs:

\hat{u}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N^{*}}c_{i}\,G_{i}(x,y),

(2)

with each basis function defined as

G_{i}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i})=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{(x-\alpha_{i}^{*})^{2}}{2\sigma_{x,i}^{2}}+\frac{(y-\beta_{i}^{*})^{2}}{2\sigma_{y,i}^{2}}\right)\right),

(3)

where centers
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{*}=(\alpha_{i}^{*},\beta_{i}^{*})
and widths
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}=(\sigma_{x,i},\sigma_{y,i})
are fixed, and coefficients
c_{i}
are tunable.

For a compact neural-network-style representation (see Figure
1
), the basis function can also be expressed using,
m_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{x}}
,
m_{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{y}}
,
b_{x}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{x}}\alpha^{*}
and
b_{y}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{y}}\beta^{*}
:

G_{i}(x,y;\boldsymbol{m}_{i},\boldsymbol{b}_{i})=\exp(-\left({(m_{x,i}x+b_{x,i})^{2}}+{(m_{y,i}y+b_{y,i})^{2}}\right))

(4)

Applying
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
to
\hat{u}
with fixed kernel parameters yields a linear system in
\mathbf{c}
. Let
\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{c}}\subset\Omega
be residual points, and
\{(x_{k}^{b},y_{k}^{b})\}_{k=1}^{N_{b}}\subset\partial\Omega
be boundary points. Enforcing the PDE residual and boundary conditions at these locations leads to a linear system for solving
\mathbf{c}
.

Assembling all equations, we obtain the linear system:

H\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{r},

(5)

where
H\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{c}+N_{b})\times N^{*}}
is the design matrix, with each row representing the evaluation of the PDE operator or boundary condition at a given point;
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{c}+N_{b})}
is the target vector (zero for residuals in the interior); and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{*}}
is the vector of unknown weights. A similar formulation works for 1D linear PDEs as well. For full details, see
Dwivedi et al., 2025
.

3
Proposed KAPI-ELM

In this section, we describe KAPI-ELM in detail. Figure
1
illustrates the schematics of an RBF-based PI-ELM and a PINN for a 1D steady boundary value problem. To emphasize the similarities and differences, we describe KAPI-ELM using the same 1D example, although the methodology naturally extends to higher-dimensional and time-dependent PDEs.

3.1
Physics-Informed Distributional Optimization

Figure 2:
Kernel Adaptive PI-ELM architecture, blending elements of PI-ELM (least-squares PDE solving) and PINNs (iterative parameter updates). While it shares the iterative nature of PINNs, it differs in two key aspects: it uses Bayesian optimization instead of backpropagation, and it optimizes distributional parameters of model weights rather than model weights directly.

Figure
2
illustrates the overall flowchart of the KAPI-ELM framework. In this approach, the RBF kernel centers
(\alpha^{*})
, widths
(\sigma)
, and, in inverse problems, the PDE parameter
(\nu)
are treated as random variables. Their joint distribution is governed by a low-dimensional set of hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{w}}
, where
N_{w}\ll N^{*}
.

The process begins with sampling
\alpha^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{*}}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{*}}
from parameterized distributions
p(\alpha^{*};\boldsymbol{w})
and
p(\sigma;\boldsymbol{w},\nu)
, respectively. For inverse problems, the PDE parameter
\nu
is sampled from a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\nu;\mu_{\nu},\sigma_{\nu})
.

Using the sampled kernel parameters, the weight and bias matrices are defined as

m_{1,i}(\alpha_{i}^{*},\sigma_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{i}},\quad b_{1,i}(\alpha_{i}^{*},\sigma_{i})=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{i}}\alpha_{i}^{*}.

These are used to construct Gaussian radial basis functions,

G_{i}(x;\alpha^{*},\sigma)=\exp(-(m_{1,i}x+b_{1,i})^{2}),

whose linear combination forms the model solution
u(x)
(see Section
2
).

Following the PI-ELM methodology, the coefficients
\mathbf{c}
are computed in closed form via least squares, and the total physics-informed loss
J
is evaluated. If
J<J_{\text{tol}}
, the current hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{w}_{\text{opt}}
are stored. Otherwise,
\boldsymbol{w}
is updated to improve the kernel distribution. This process repeats until convergence.

Having outlined the overall workflow, we now describe the four key components of KAPI-ELM in detail:

1.

RBF-Kernel Mixture Model
: We propose the sampling strategy for the parameters of radial basis functions (RBFs)—specifically the centers and widths—using a multi-component mixture distribution. This distribution comprises
N_{adap}+1
components: a fixed global baseline distribution and
N_{adap}
localized adaptive distributions. The fixed baseline component ensures global domain coverage, while the localized components focus on sharp-gradient regions of the solution.

(a)

Fixed Kernels
: To determine the fixed kernel parameters, we adopt a curriculum learning strategy proposed by
Krishnapriyan et al., 2021
. For a parametric PDE of the form
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}(u)=R
, where
\nu
is a governing parameter, we initialize the PI-ELM with a baseline configuration consisting of
N_{r}
uniformly distributed RBF centers and a fixed kernel width
\sigma_{F}
, selected heuristically to ensure: (A) sufficient overlap between neighbouring kernels and (B) inverse scaling with the number of RBFs
(N_{r})
.

For example, in the case of a 1D problem where the computational domain has length equal to one,
\sigma_{F}
is selected as:

\sigma_{F}=10\times\frac{1}{N_{r}}.

Given that the PDE becomes increasingly stiff as
\nu
decreases, we solve the problem sequentially for increasing values of
\nu
. While the baseline PI-ELM may perform poorly at low
\nu
, its accuracy typically improves at higher values of
\nu
.

To identify regions with sharp gradients in the solution, we apply DBSCAN clustering (see Algorithm
2
). The resulting number of distinct gradient regions, denoted by
N_{adap}
, determines the number of adaptive components used in the final mixture-based kernel configuration. Figure
14
shows an example of a trial function with
N_{adap}=3
.

(b)

Adaptive Kernels
: To control the allocation of basis functions, we assign weights
\pi_{base}
to the uniform component and
\pi_{adap,k}
to the
k
-th normal component, satisfying the normalization condition:

\pi_{base}+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{adap}}\pi_{adap,k}=1.

(6)

Assuming a total of
N^{*}
RBFs, we let the adaptive components collectively contribute
f
times as many RBFs as the baseline component. The mixture weights are then:

\pi_{base}=\frac{1}{1+f},\quad\pi_{adap,k}=\frac{f_{k}}{1+f},\quad\text{where}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{N_{adap}}f_{k}=f.

(7)

This parameterized weighting scheme enables the model to concentrate more RBFs in regions of high solution complexity by increasing
f
, while smaller values retain broader domain coverage.

2.

Sampling Procedure
: The RBF centers and widths in
D
-dimensional space (
D\in\{1,2\}
) are generated using the following sampling procedure:

(a)

Centers Sampling
:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}\sim\pi_{base}\cdot\mathcal{U}(\Omega)+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{adap}}\pi_{adap,k}\cdot\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),

(8)

where
\pi_{base}
and
\pi_{adap,k}
are the weights of the uniform and Gaussian components, respectively.
In the 1D case,
\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Sigma_{k}=(\eta\tau_{k})^{2}
. In the 2D case,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}=[\mu_{x,k},\mu_{y,k}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}((\eta\tau_{x,k})^{2},(\eta\tau_{y,k})^{2})
, where
\eta
is a fixed scale and
[\mu_{x,k},\mu_{y,k},\tau_{x,k},\tau_{y,k}]
are tunable parameters. For domain length
|\Omega|
,
\eta
should typically satisfy the following to maintain locality and ensure proper scaling:

\log_{10}\eta\leq\log_{10}|\Omega|-1

(9)

Please note that both collocation points and RBF centers are sampled from the same distribution. However, the RBF centers form a subset of the collocation points. Specifically, the uniform component includes fewer RBF centers than collocation points, whereas in the adaptive (Gaussian) component, RBF centers and collocation points are co-located.

(b)

Widths Sampling
:
For RBFs drawn from the baseline component, fixed widths are assigned as
\sigma=\sigma_{F}
. For adaptive components, the width distribution is given by

\sigma_{k}^{(d)}=\min\left(\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\xi_{k}^{(d)}}\right|,\sigma_{F}\right),\quad\forall d\in\{1,\ldots,D\},

(10)

where
\xi_{k}^{(d)}\sim\mathcal{U}\left[-\frac{\zeta_{k}^{(d)}}{2},\frac{\zeta_{k}^{(d)}}{2}\right]
is a uniformly sampled inverse-scale parameter in each dimension. The inverse-scale bound
\zeta_{k}^{(d)}
is dynamically adapted to the PDE stiffness parameter
\nu
via:

\zeta_{k}^{(d)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{F}}\cdot\nu^{-(1+\lambda_{k})},

(11)

where
\lambda_{k}
is a tunable decay factor controlling the width sensitivity to stiffness.

Our choice of the inverse-scale parameter distribution is inspired by the approach of
De Florio et al., 2024
in the XTFC framework, where RBF widths are reduced linearly with the number of neurons. However, we employ an exponential decay of RBF widths with respect to the PDE parameter
\nu
, enabling sharper localization in stiff regimes.

(c)

Hyperparameter Space Dimensions
: The dimension of the hyperparameter space
(N_{w})
is determined by the number of mixture components used to capture regions of high solution gradients. Table

Physics-Informed Bayesian Optimization
:

(a)

Physics-Informed Loss Function
: Since the RBF parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
are drawn from a distribution parameterized by
\boldsymbol{w}
, the design matrix can be written for a given value of
\boldsymbol{w}
as
H(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\boldsymbol{w}
). The PDE residual and boundary conditions can be written as:

H(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\boldsymbol{w})\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{r},

(12)

where
\mathbf{r}
encodes collocation and boundary data.

We define the loss as the residual norm:

\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\boldsymbol{w})=\|H(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\boldsymbol{w})\mathbf{c}_{\text{opt}}-\mathbf{r}\|_{\infty},

(13)

with
\mathbf{c}_{\text{opt}}=H^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\boldsymbol{w})\mathbf{r}
computed using the Moore-Penrose pseudo inverse and where
\hat{J}
is the physics-informed loss evaluated at deterministic samples of the kernel distribution for a given
\boldsymbol{w}
.

(b)

Distributional Parameter Optimization
: KAPI-ELM effectively reframes parametric PDE solving as distributional optimization over a low dimensional hyperparameter space. Classical bayesian methods for the optimization of the hyperparamters are based on the estimation of the evidence by marginalization
(
MacKay, 1995
)
over the position and widths for a given value of
\boldsymbol{w}
. This evidence is evaluated with samples and Monte-Carlo type integration. In contrast, because our adaptive kernel distribution is sharply concentrated and regularized by the physics-informed loss, we bypass the marginalization step and directly approximate the objective as follows:

J(\boldsymbol{w})\approx\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{w}).

(14)

The iterative optimization procedure is detailed in Algorithm
1
. At each step, the RBF parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\}
are sampled, and the design matrix
H(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})
is assembled. A least-squares solution gives optimum weights
\mathbf{c}_{opt}
, and the loss
J(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})
is evaluated. In the optimization step,
BayesStep
employs Gaussian Process (GP) regression to model the objective and selects optimal parameters
\boldsymbol{w_{opt}}
and via expected improvement (EI
1
1

https://fr.mathworks.com/help/stats/bayesopt.html
). The procedure iterates until a convergence threshold on
J
or parameter changes is met.

(c)

Physics-Based Search Bounds
: The distributional hyperparameters, collectively denoted by
\boldsymbol{w}
, include parameters such as the number of adaptive RBF kernels, the mean and standard deviation of the RBF-center distribution, and the decay characteristics of the RBF widths. Therefore, we can leverage the physically intuitive nature of the KAPI formulation to define meaningful search ranges for each parameter. For a related application of this concept in physics-informed image segmentation, we refer readers to
Dwivedi et al., 2024
.

4
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the performance of KAPI-ELM on several benchmark cases including forward PDE solutions and inverse parameter estimation. The overall plan and the selected test cases are as follows:

1.

Forward Problems
:

•

Singularly Perturbed 1D Convection–Diffusion Equation:
This problem is challenging for standard PINNs and PI-DeepONet due to the presence of a single or multiple sharp boundary layers. Among existing ML-based methods, XTFC has shown the best performance. Therefore, we solve this problem using KAPI-ELM and compare its accuracy and efficiency with XTFC.

•

2D Steady Poisson Equation With A Sharp Source:
While XTFC yields accurate solutions, it does not scale effectively to higher-dimensional problems. To demonstrate the scalability of KAPI-ELM, we apply it to a 2D Poisson equation and compare its performance with that of traditional PINNs.

•

Time-Dependent Linear Advection Equation:
This example is included to demonstrate the applicability of KAPI-ELM to unsteady problems with time-dependent, propagation of localized gradients.

2.

Inverse Problems
:
We validate the proposed Bayesian optimization framework by applying it to inverse problems, where the goal is to recover unknown physical parameters from noisy observations. In particular, we consider the following two cases.

•

Linear Advection Equation Without Sharp Gradients:
Included to show that Bayesian optimization can be effectively applied even with standard PI-ELMs.

•

Convection–Diffusion Equation With Sharp Gradients:
Included to demonstrate the efficacy of KAPI-ELM on inverse problems with sharp gradients.

All the experiments are conducted in Matlab R2022b environment running on a 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-12700H, 2.30 GHz CPU and 16GB RAM Asus laptop.

The results provide two useful pieces of information: (A) PI-ELM accurately solves the PDE for

N_{n}

KAPI-ELM Results

In our experiments, for
\nu=[0.01,0.001]
, we configure the PI-ELM with
[N_{c},N_{r},\sigma_{F},N_{n}]=[500,250,0.04,1]
for the Type-1 problem, and
[1000,500,0.02,2]
for the Type-2 problem, where
N_{c},N_{r},\sigma_{F},N_{n}
denote the number of fixed collocation points, number of RBFs, fixed RBF width, and number of adaptive components, respectively. For the very stiff case (
\nu=0.0001
), the configuration
[N_{c},N_{r},\sigma_{F}]=[1500,750,0.013]
is used for both types of problems.

After configuring the uniform RBF kernel component, the next step involves initializing the adaptive kernel components and tuning their distributional hyperparameters using Bayesian Optimization (BO). In our framework, the PI-ELM loss function serves as the objective that BO seeks to minimize. The optimization process is carried out over a small set of interpretable hyperparameters that define the distribution of the adaptive RBF kernels.

The physically intuitive nature of the distributional parameters allows us to define meaningful search ranges. Specifically, the location parameter
\mu
is confined to the range
(0.9,0.99)
, targeting regions near the sharp gradients. The width parameter
\tau
is restricted to
(0.05,0.5)
to reflect the narrow shock-like structures typically found in stiff solutions. The fraction parameter
f
, controlling the proportion of adaptive kernels, is almost fixed to
0.5
to encourage high local resolution. Finally, the decay rate
\lambda
is chosen from
(0.5,0.9)
to accommodate steep transitions. These parameter ranges are not only physically interpretable but also flexible. They can be tightened for highly stiff PDEs or relaxed for milder stiffness, depending on the value of
\nu
.

Florio et al.
(
De Florio et al., 2024
)
recently benchmarked the performance of XTFC, PINNs, and PI-ELMs on a stiff 1D convection-diffusion problem (Type-1 problem). As anticipated, standard PINNs fail to capture sharp layers for
\nu<0.01
due to spectral bias (also see Figure

\nu=10^{-4}
. XTFC’s advantage stems from its structured inverse RBF width distribution, which increases linearly with the number of neurons. However, this design incurs high computational costs, requiring up to 10,000 neurons and collocation points even in 1D, thus limiting its scalability to higher dimensions.

Unlike XTFC, which places neurons uniformly across the domain, KAPI-ELM selectively positions its kernel centers and adaptively adjusts both their widths and quantity to minimize the physics-informed loss. The smaller number of neurons in KAPI-ELM accelerates pseudo-inverse computations during inner iterations, and allows it for scale for higher dimensional problems.

KAPI-ELM Solution

KAPI-ELM was originally formulated to resolve sharp spatial gradients localized at a point. However, in the case of linear advection with small
\nu
, the sharp gradient persists along a characteristic line in the
(x,t)
-space, forming an extended structure rather than a pointwise feature. While, in principle, such patterns could be modeled using multiple Gaussian functions parameterized by
(\mu_{x},\mu_{y},\tau)
, we opt for a simpler approach. Instead of solving the PDE across the entire computational domain simultaneously, we partition the domain into one hundred time blocks and solve the advection equation sequentially.

In the first time block, we first locate the region of sharp gradients in the initial condition and then track its evolution along the characteristic path
dx/dt=a
, constructing a mask that restricts the adaptive placement and scaling of RBFs to this area. This approach allows us to bypass the use of adaptive Gaussian distributions parameterized by
(\mu_{x},\mu_{y},\tau)
, while still employing the original width sampling strategy. The configuration of the fixed (baseline) component is defined as:

[N_{c},N_{bc},N_{ic},N_{r},N_{n}]=[600,150,450,150,1],

where
N_{c}
,
N_{bc}
,
N_{ic}
,
N_{r}
, and
N_{n}
correspond to the number of collocation points, boundary points, initial condition points, RBFs, and adaptive components, respectively. Each adaptive component is governed by three parameters. The first two,
f
and
\lambda
, are tunable;
\lambda
is defined as in previous experiments, and
1+f
represents the ratio of adaptive RBFs to residual points. In the context of linear advection, we also treat the fixed RBF width
\sigma_{F}
as a tunable parameter. The associated search intervals for these distributional parameters are:

f\in[1,1.5],\quad\lambda\in[1,1.5],\quad\sigma_{F}\in[2.5,4.5].

We solve the linear advection equation within the first time block using a candidate triplet
(f,\lambda,\sigma_{F})
, then use the resulting solution to generate the initial condition for the subsequent time block. This sequential procedure continues until all time blocks are processed. The tunable parameters are optimized by minimizing the residual. Figure
11
compares the solution predicted by KAPI-ELM with the exact solution, along with the corresponding training history. Clearly, the KAPI-ELM predicted solution accurately captures the exact solution. The total training time is close to 40 minutes.

1.

Applicability to Linear PDEs:
The current formulation is restricted to linear PDEs. Extending it to nonlinear PDEs remains an important direction for future research.

2.

Scalability Issues:
The use of the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse limits the scalability of our method when dealing with large-scale systems. Future work will focus on exploring more efficient solvers or iterative alternatives to address this bottleneck.

3.

Baseline PI-ELM Selection Strategy:
While curriculum learning helps guide the selection of the baseline PI-ELM, the process is not fully automated or guaranteed to yield optimal choices. Further work is needed to develop a more robust and adaptive selection mechanism.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced the Kernel Adaptive Physics-Informed Extreme Learning Machine (KAPI-ELM), a novel framework that unifies the computational efficiency of fixed-weight shallow PI-ELMs with the flexibility of fully-trainable deep PINNs. By shifting the optimization focus from individual input weights to a low dimensional set of physically interpretable distributional hyperparameters, KAPI-ELM employs Bayesian Optimization (BO) to efficiently optimize the RBF kernel distribution for forward and inverse PDE problems, particularly in stiff regimes with sharp gradients. Extensive numerical experiments across 1D, 2D, steady and time-dependent PDEs demonstrate that KAPI-ELM achieves state-of-the-art accuracy with significantly fewer tunable parameters, outperforming both traditional PINNs and even advanced frameworks like XTFC. These results highlight KAPI-ELM as a scalable, interpretable, and computationally efficient alternative for physics-informed learning, especially suited for localized, high-gradient phenomena.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

To the best of our knowledge, KAPI-ELM is the first adaptive extension of standard PI-ELM that efficiently solves stiff PDEs with sharp gradients in both forward and inverse settings. It bridges the gap between fixed-weight PI-ELMs and fully trainable PINNs by introducing distributional optimization over kernel distribution hyperparameters, enabling performance that surpasses both.

2.

Unlike traditional deep learning methods, KAPI-ELM operates in a low-dimensional space of physically interpretable network hyperparameters—namely, the RBF center locations, widths, and their count. This not only enhances model interpretability but also enables the use of domain-informed priors and bounded optimization.

3.

By decoupling the optimization of input-layer distributions from the closed-form least-squares output solver, KAPI-ELM preserves the hallmark speed and efficiency of PI-ELMs while extending their applicability to inverse problems, time-dependent dynamics, and higher-dimensional domains.

4.

KAPI-ELM has been tested on a diverse set of benchmark problems, including 1D convection–diffusion, 2D Poisson equation, unsteady advection and inverse parameter estimation. Across all these cases, it achieves state-of-the-art accuracy using far fewer tunable parameters than PINNs or XTFC, promoting sustainable and resource-efficient physics-informed machine learning.

Future research will focus on extending KAPI-ELM to nonlinear PDEs and making it scalable to large scale systems.

epsilon

minPts
