Abstract
. Part I of the paper considered infinite orthogonal sums of regular subspaces in a Kreĭn space (that is, of subspaces which are themselves Kreĭn spaces). How precisely these sums should be defined and conditions for when such a sum is itself regular were examined. These included, for example, a boundedness condition for the sum of the corresponding orthogonal projections. The same problem is addressed here for (quasi-)pseudo-regular subspaces. Such subspaces happen to be the orthogonal direct sum of a regular space and an isotropic, or neutral, subspace. Alternate characterizations of such subspaces are given, along with various examples and counter-examples. The infinite orthogonal sum of these is then considered.

1.
Introduction and background

A fundamental result in the study of Hilbert space geometry is that
orthogonal sums of Hilbert spaces are themselves Hilbert spaces. This
works whether the number of spaces involved is finite or infinite.
Kreĭn spaces are indefinite analogues of Hilbert spaces, and so it is
natural to ask which properties of Hilbert spaces carry over to these.

Beginning in 1979, Brian McEnnis made a detailed study of this topic
in a series of papers considering shift operators on Kreĭn spaces

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Part I of the current
series
[
14
]
contains an elaboration and expansion of
McEnnis’ work. This, an earlier work of the third author

[
13
]
, and McEnnis’ papers were primarily concerned with

regular spaces
, which are the natural analogue in Kreĭn spaces of closed subspaces of Hilbert spaces, in that they are
themselves Kreĭn spaces and orthogonal sums are automatically direct.

Part II will examine orthogonal sums of
pseudo-regular
and

quasi-pseudo-regular
or
qpr
spaces (formally defined
in Definition
2.1
), which generalize regular spaces in
that they are the orthogonal direct sum of regular spaces and neutral
subspaces (where all inner products are
0
). A natural question is
how the orthogonal sum of such subspaces should be defined, and what
conditions are needed to guarantee that orthogonal sums exist. Here,
even for finitely many orthogonal spaces, the sum need not exist (as a
space of the same type), or it may not be direct, along with other
possible difficulties.

Theorem
1.5
originates in Part I of this
paper
[
14
]
and highlights the status regarding
orthogonal sums of regular spaces. There, infinite sums of regular
spaces are well-defined in terms of rearrangement invariance of
infinite direct sums of vectors from these spaces, or equivalently, by
means of a boundedness condition of related projection operators.
While it is perhaps too much to ask that such definitive results hold
for pseudo-regular and quasi-pseudo-regular spaces, analogues are
nevertheless available — see Theorems
5.1
and
6.1
,
as well as Corollary
6.3
. The first of these
theorems is a strengthening of Theorem 4.3 in
[
13
]
, the
main ideas of which were discovered by the first two authors and
communicated to the third.

Quasi-pseudo-regular (or qpr) spaces are new, being an outgrowth of
work of Aurelian Gheondea on pseudo-regular
spaces
[
5
,
6
]
. The latter spaces are
assumed to be closed, while this is relaxed in the
quasi-pseudo-regular setting to the assumption that they are operator
ranges. Generalizations of Gheondea’s results form the bulk of
Section 2. A deeper understanding of infinite sums of operator ranges
is required when considering orthogonal sums of quasi-pseudo-regular
spaces. Section 3 takes this up, expanding on fundamental work of
Fillmore and Williams
[
4
]
. Section 4 deals with nets of
projections in Kreĭn spaces, playing a key part in understanding
orthogonal sums of pseudo-regular spaces. The last two sections put
all of this together in theorems on infinite orthogonal sums of
pseudo-regular and quasi-pseudo-regular spaces.

Now for a brief summary of the needed background in Kreĭn space
theory.

Recall that a Kreĭn space is a complex inner product space

({\mathcal{H}},{\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>}_{{\mathcal{H}}})
which admits a
fundamental
decomposition

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{+}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
such that
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
is a Hilbert space and

{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
is the anti-space of a Hilbert space (that is, a Hilbert space
with the inner product replaced by its negative). Every Kreĭn space

{\mathcal{H}}
has a unique

which is induced by any
such decomposition, though in general the decomposition is not unique.
A subspace
{\mathcal{M}}
is called

if it is closed and is
itself a Kreĭn space in the inner product of
{\mathcal{H}}
.

In analogy with Hilbert spaces, the range of a selfadjoint projection
operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
is a regular subspace, and every regular subspace is
the range of a unique selfadjoint projection. Here

selfadjoint
means with respect to the indefinite inner
product.

.

Let be

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}

In what follows,

stands for the
vector space of finite sums of

Finding conditions for when an orthogonal sum exists for various
classes of subspaces is the central goal of this paper.

Recall from part I that for a Banach space
X
with norm
\|\cdot\|

and a sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
in
X

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}x_{j}
is termed
unconditionally convergent

if every rearrangement is convergent. An infinite series can also be
viewed as an indexed sum with no regard to order. An arbitrary
indexed sum
\sum_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}x_{\lambda}
of vectors in
X
is said to be

Moore-Smith convergent
with
sum
or

Moore-Smith sum

x
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
finite subset
{\mathcal{F}}
of
\Lambda
such that

\big\|x-\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{G}}}x_{\lambda}\big\|<\varepsilon
for
every finite subset
{\mathcal{G}}
of
\Lambda
that contains
{\mathcal{F}}
. Such a

is unique and denoted

x=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}x_{\lambda}
.

Equivalently, suppose
\{x_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}\subset X
. The
collection of all finite subsets
\mathcal{F}=\{F\}
of
\Lambda

ordered by inclusion is a directed set. Define

x_{F}:=\sum_{\lambda\in F}x_{\lambda}
. Then
(x_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}}

is a net. Convergence of this net to
x
(that is,
x_{F}\to x
) means
that given any
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
F\in\mathcal{F}
such
that for all
G\in\mathcal{F}
with
G\geq F
,

\|x-x_{G}\|<\varepsilon
. Since the space
X
is Hausdorff, limits
are unique. The existence of a limit

for

is
equivalent to

x=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}x_{\lambda}
. The
definition of the directed set
\mathcal{F}
ensures that the sum is
rearrangement invariant
[
8
]
.

Definition 1.3

Let

\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}x_{\lambda}

Any space

If the subspaces are pairwise orthogonal, call

an

If additionally, the sum is direct

,
{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, is

\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\bigoplus_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}

It is worth emphasizing that all the sums are assumed to consist of
elements which are Moore-Smith sums. Clearly, the span of any finite

collection

of subspaces,

\mathrm{span}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\subseteq\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}

As will be demonstrated in Example

be unique, even if the spaces being summed happen to be closed. This
occurs for example if the spaces considered are viewed as operator

There will be circumstances where uniqueness does hold. If the spaces
in the class being summed are assumed to be closed (for example,
regular spaces, and the sum is then regular), the next lemma shows

If

2.
Pseudo-regular and quasi-pseudo-regular subspaces

There are various ways to weaken the condition of regularity,
including those defining pseudo-regularity and
quasi-pseudo-regularity. A compelling reason for the interest in
pseudo-regular subspaces is that every closed subspace of a Pontryagin
space (that is, a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
where either
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
or
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}

is finite dimensional) is pseudo-regular (see Proposition 3.2.9
in
[
6
]
or
Corollary
2.11
below), while in
any Kreĭn space, pseudo-regular spaces are quasi-pseudo-regular and
as will be seen, the latter have better properties under orthogonal
direct sums. Several equivalent ways of defining pseudo-regular
subspaces exist (see Gheondea
[
6
, §3.2]
). The one
highlighting its connection with regularity is chosen here.

Definition 2.1
.

A subspace
{\mathcal{S}}
of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
is

quasi-pseudo-regular

(
qpr
for short
)
;
respectively,
pseudo-regular
, if

(1)

{\mathcal{S}}
is an operator range; respectively, closed; and

(2)

{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is regular and
{\mathcal{N}}
is
a neutral subspace.

Lemma
2.4
shows that the sum in
part (2) of Definition
2.1
is in fact direct.

An
operator range
is the range of a bounded Hilbert space
operator. Since Kreĭn spaces can be identified with not necessarily
unique, but all equivalent, Hilbert spaces, the range of any Kreĭn space operator is an operator range in this sense. Using the
polar decomposition, one sees that any operator range is the range of
a positive (that is, positive semi-definite) Hilbert space operator.

As noted, closed subspaces of Pontryagin spaces are pseudo-regular,
though this does not hold for general Kreĭn spaces. Moreover,
orthogonal direct sums of pseudo-regular subspaces may fail to be
pseudo-regular, making the consideration of direct sums of such spaces
more complex.

Example 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Kreĭn space with fundamental decomposition
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{+}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{-}

such that both
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
are infinite dimensional. Then

{\mathcal{H}}
contains a closed neutral subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
with

\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=\infty
. Choose closed subspaces
{\mathcal{N}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
of

{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
in such a way that
{\mathcal{N}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}=\{0\}
while the angle
between them is zero (Halmos
[
7
, §15]
). Since

{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is neutral,
{\mathcal{N}}\perp{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
. Then
{\mathcal{N}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
is not
closed, and so while
{\mathcal{N}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
are pseudo-regular,

{\mathcal{N}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
is not.

By contrast, as will be shown in Proposition
2.14
,
quasi-pseudo-regular subspaces are closed under the formation of
orthogonal sums.

As the next lemma shows, the difference between a qpr and
pseudo-regular space is that for the latter, the neutral summand is
necessarily closed. Pseudo-regular subspaces are quasi-pseudo-regular
since any closed space is the range of a bounded Hilbert space
projection. Regular subspaces are pseudo-regular spaces with zero
neutral component.

In any qpr space,
{\mathcal{N}}
in part (2) of Definition
2.1

is uniquely determined.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Kreĭn space and assume that
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
,
where
{\mathcal{R}}
is regular and
{\mathcal{N}}
is neutral. Then
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{S}}^{0}
,
where
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}={\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is the isotropic part of
{\mathcal{S}}
.
Furthermore,
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}={\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}}^{0}
. Also,
{\mathcal{N}}

is closed, respectively, an operator range, if and only if
{\mathcal{S}}
is
pseudo-regular, respectively, qpr.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{N}}
is neutral,
{\mathcal{N}}\perp{\mathcal{N}}
, and so
{\mathcal{N}}\perp{\mathcal{S}}
because

{\mathcal{N}}\perp{\mathcal{R}}
; hence
{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
. Clearly,

{\mathcal{R}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{S}}\supseteq{\mathcal{N}}
. Conversely, suppose

x\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{S}}
, and write
x=u+v
,
u\in{\mathcal{R}}
,
v\in{\mathcal{N}}
.
For any
u^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{R}}
,

0=\left<x,u^{\prime}\right>=\left<u+v,u^{\prime}\right>=\left<u,u^{\prime}\right>,

so
u\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}
and by the nondegeneracy of
{\mathcal{R}}
,
u=0
and
hence
x=v\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Thus
{\mathcal{R}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{N}}
. So

{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{N}}
, and hence

{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{N}}
.

Since
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}
by regularity
of
{\mathcal{R}}
, it follows that

{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}=\{0\},

and so

{\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}}^{0}=\overline{({\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}})}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=({\mathcal{R}}+\overline{{\mathcal{N}}})\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=\overline{{\mathcal{N}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=\overline{{\mathcal{N}}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}.

For the last statement, if
{\mathcal{S}}
is pseudo-regular, and so closed,
then
{\mathcal{N}}
is closed as it is the intersection of closed spaces.
Likewise, if
{\mathcal{S}}
is an operator range,
{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is also an
operator range as it is closed, and so
{\mathcal{N}}
being the intersection
of operator ranges is then an operator range

[
4
, Cor. 2, p. 260]
.
∎

Next, a couple of simple yet useful lemmas.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}
is a regular subspace of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
and

{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}
is closed
(
respectively, an operator
range
)
, then
{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{M}}=\{0\}
and

{\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{R}}\,\dot{\oplus}\,{\mathcal{M}}
is closed

(
respectively, an operator range
)
.

Proof.

By regularity,
\{0\}={\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}\supseteq{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{M}}
.
Choose a fundamental symmetry
J
such that
J{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}
and write

|{\mathcal{H}}|
for the associated Hilbert space. Then the Hilbert space
orthogonal complement of
{\mathcal{R}}
in
|{\mathcal{H}}|
equals
{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}
, and so

{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}
are Hilbert space orthogonal. If
{\mathcal{M}}
is assumed
to be closed, it then follows that
{\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}
is closed. On
the other hand, if
{\mathcal{M}}
is an operator range, since
{\mathcal{R}}
is the
range of a Hilbert space projection it follows by Theorem 2.2 of

[
4
]
that the sum is an operator range.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is a qpr subspace of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
, then

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is closed.

Proof.

Write
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is regular and

{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{S}}^{0}={\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is neutral. Since

{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is closed, it follows
by Lemma
2.4
that

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}

is closed.
∎

If
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}
are subspaces of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
, then the sum

{\mathcal{R}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
is automatically orthogonal since

{\mathcal{S}}^{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
, though it may not be direct, even if
{\mathcal{S}}

is qpr. However, if
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr and the sum
is
direct, the
proof of the next proposition shows that
{\mathcal{R}}
is a Kreĭn space. A
subtle point is that this may not be enough to ensure that
{\mathcal{R}}
is
regular, since while it is closed in the induced inner product, it may
not be closed as a subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
. As the next example indicates,
something more is needed to guarantee regularity.

Example 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be an infinite dimensional Hilbert space,
x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}
,

\|x\|=\|y\|=1
and
\left<x,y\right>=0
. Set

{\mathcal{H}}_{0}={\mathcal{H}}\ominus\,\mathrm{span}\,\{x,y\}
,

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}={\mathcal{H}}\ominus\,\mathrm{span}\,\{y\}
. Define a fundamental
symmetry on
{\mathcal{H}}
by
J|{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=1_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}
and
Jx=y
(so
Jy=x
).
Then
{\mathcal{N}}:=\mathrm{span}\,\{x\}
is a neutral subspace both Kreĭn and Hilbert space orthogonal to
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. It is a standard result
that there exists an unbounded everywhere defined linear functional

\varphi
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
with
\varphi(x)=1
. Furthermore,

{\mathcal{R}}:={\rm ker\,}\varphi
is not closed and
{\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}
,
which is closed. Hence
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is pseudo-regular since

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\bot}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{0}={\mathcal{N}}
. While the space
{\mathcal{R}}
is dense in

{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, it is not an operator range, for if it were, as will be
shown below in
Theorem
2.13
, it would
be regular, and so in particular closed, in which case it would
equal
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
.

By the way, another, more concrete, example of a subspace which is
not an operator range will be given in
Example
3.5
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a qpr subspace of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
. If
{\mathcal{R}}
is an
operator range and
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
, then
{\mathcal{R}}
is
regular.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr, there is a regular subspace
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
such
that
{\mathcal{S}}=\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
. Choose a fundamental
symmetry
J
fixing
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
and write
|{\mathcal{H}}|
for the
associated Hilbert space.

Let
P
be the (both Hilbert and Kreĭn space) orthogonal projection
onto
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
. Then

P({\mathcal{R}})=P({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{0})=P(S)=\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
. If
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
,
then
x=\tilde{x}+n
,
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
,

n\in{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
, so
P(x)=\tilde{x}
. Hence if
x\in{\rm ker\,}P
, then

x\in{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
. Thus since it is assumed that the sum of
{\mathcal{R}}
and

{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
is direct,
P:{\mathcal{R}}\to\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
is a bijection.

Decompose
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}=\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{+}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{-}
, where

\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{+}
is closed and uniformly positive and
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{-}

is closed and uniformly negative. Define

{\mathcal{R}}^{\pm}=\left\{x\in{\mathcal{R}}:P(x)\in\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{\pm}\right\}.

If
x\in{\mathcal{R}}^{\pm}
, then
x=\tilde{x}+n
,

\tilde{x}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{\pm}
,
n\in{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
, and so

\left<x,x\right>=\left<\tilde{x}+n,\tilde{x}+n\right>=\left<\tilde{x},\tilde{x}\right>=\left<Px,Px\right>.

Hence
P|_{{\mathcal{R}}^{\pm}}:{\mathcal{R}}^{\pm}\to\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{\pm}
are isometric
isomorphisms. Then since
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{+}
and
|\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}^{-}|
are
Hilbert spaces, the same is true for
{\mathcal{R}}^{+}
and
|{\mathcal{R}}^{-}|
. Similar
reasoning shows that
{\mathcal{R}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{-}
are (Kreĭn space)
orthogonal, and so
{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}^{+}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{R}}^{-}
is a Kreĭn space.

Under the assumption that
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr,
Lemma
2.5
implies that

{\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is closed. By

[
4
, Theorem 2.3]
,
{\mathcal{R}}
an operator range leads to the
conclusion that
{\mathcal{R}}
is closed in
{\mathcal{H}}
, so as desired,
{\mathcal{R}}
is
regular.
∎

If a space
{\mathcal{S}}
is pseudo-regular, then
{\mathcal{S}}
is closed by
definition, and by Lemma
2.5

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is
closed. Gheondea proved that these two conditions characterize
pseudo-regularity
[
5
]
. It is natural to wonder if
there is a similar characterization for qpr spaces.
Lemma
2.5
says that if
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr, then

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is closed. Also, it is clear from
Lemma
2.3
that the closure of a qpr space is
pseudo-regular. So this combined with Gheondea’s result might lead
one to guess that
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
being closed implies that
{\mathcal{S}}
is
qpr. The following example rules this out.

Example 2.8
.

There is no converse to Lemma
2.5
; that is,
simply assuming that
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed is not enough to
guarantee that a space
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr. To see this, using the spaces
constructed in Example
2.6
, let

{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}
. As noted there,
{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\bot}
, and so

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, which is closed. Also,
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}=\{0\}

since
{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
. So if
{\mathcal{S}}
were qpr, it would
necessarily be regular and so closed, giving a contradiction.

While it was seen in the last example that the assumption that

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
closed does not characterize quasi-pseudo-regularity,
the space
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
does have some nice properties.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr, then
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}
,
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
, and

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
are pseudo-regular. Also, if
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is
pseudo-regular and
{\mathcal{S}}
is closed, then
{\mathcal{S}}
is pseudo-regular.

Proof.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr, then
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is
regular and
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
is neutral. By regularity of
{\mathcal{R}}
, there is a
fundamental symmetry
J
such that
J{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}
, and so
{\mathcal{R}}
and

{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
are also orthogonal in the associated Hilbert space
|{\mathcal{H}}|
.
Notice that
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
,

{\mathcal{N}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
by
Lemma
2.3
. As
{\mathcal{N}}
is closed and neutral,

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}
is pseudo-regular. By the choice of
J
,

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}{\mathcal{N}}
. It follows from
Lemma
2.5
that
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is
closed. Since

(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})^{\perp}={\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}\cap\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}={\mathcal{N}}
,

{\mathcal{H}}=(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}J{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}{\mathcal{N}}\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}J{\mathcal{N}},

where
{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{N}}\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is
closed. So for
{\mathcal{L}}:={\mathcal{N}}\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}J{\mathcal{N}}
, which is closed,

J{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}
, and so
{\mathcal{L}}
is regular. This then implies that

(2.1)

{\mathcal{H}}=({\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}})\oplus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}{\mathcal{L}}=({\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}})\,\dot{\oplus}_{{\mathcal{H}}}\,{\mathcal{L}},

and regularity of
{\mathcal{L}}
leads to the conclusion that

{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is also regular. The sum
{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is
direct, and it is also Kreĭn space orthogonal because

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}^{\perp}

(recall
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
). Then since
{\mathcal{R}}
is regular, so is

\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
.

The last equality in (
2.1
) shows that
{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}

is Kreĭn space orthogonal to
{\mathcal{L}}
and hence to
{\mathcal{N}}
. Thus

{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=\tilde{\mathcal{R}}\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
, and since
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed,
it is pseudo-regular by Definition
2.1
. The equality

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=({\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}})\oplus{\mathcal{N}}
similarly shows that

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is pseudo-regular since it is closed by Lemma

2.5
. The last statement of the lemma is
immediate from what has already been shown.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is any subspace, then
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}
is
pseudo-regular. So if
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed, it is
pseudo-regular, and if the sum is also direct, then it is regular.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{S}}
is an arbitrary subspace of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Then since

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}=\overline{\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}=(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})^{\perp},

and
{\mathcal{N}}:=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is a closed neutral
subspace (so pseudo-regular), it follows that

{\mathcal{N}}^{\perp}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}
is pseudo-regular. Hence if

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed, it is pseudo-regular. If the sum is
direct, then

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}=\{0\}
. Then by
Lemma
2.3
,
{\mathcal{N}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{0}=\{0\}
. Hence

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is regular.
∎

The next result is Proposition 3.2.9 in
[
6
]
. The
proof given here is somewhat different and uses the previous
corollary.

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Pontryagin space and
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
a closed
subspace. Then
{\mathcal{S}}
is pseudo-regular. Conversely, if
{\mathcal{H}}
is a
Kreĭn space for which every closed subspace is pseudo-regular, then

{\mathcal{H}}
is a Pontryagin space.

As is seen in the proof that follows, neutral subspaces of Pontryagin
spaces are finite dimensional, and hence closed. Consequently,
quasi-pseudo-regular subspaces are pseudo-regular in this context.

Proof of Corollary

2.11
.

Assume
\dim{\mathcal{H}}^{-}<\infty
(the case where
\dim{\mathcal{H}}^{+}<\infty

being handled identically). Define
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
, a
closed neutral subspace. Observe that

(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}})^{0}:=(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}})\cap(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}})^{\bot}={\mathcal{N}}
. By
Corollary
2.10
,

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}}={\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
, where
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is
regular. Note that
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, as a regular subspace of a Pontryagin
space, is a Pontryagin space. Set
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
and

{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}\cap{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. Both are closed, they are orthogonal,
and
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}=({\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
. So

\overline{{\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}}={\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, and thus
{\mathcal{M}}
is
non-degenerate in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. As a closed, non-degenerate subspace of
the Pontryagin space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{M}}
is regular (see, for
example,
[
6
, Prop. 3.1.8]
). Thus
{\mathcal{S}}
is
pseudo-regular.

For the converse, assume that
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Kreĭn space which is not a
Pontryagin space, so
\dim{\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}=\infty
. Define a Hilbert
space contraction
T\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{S}}^{+},{\mathcal{S}}^{-})
with the property that

\|Tx\|<\|x\|
for all
x\in{\mathcal{S}}^{+}
and
\|T\|=1
. By the
Closed Graph Theorem, the graph of
T
is a closed subspace which is
strictly positive, but not uniformly positive (so not regular).
∎

Lemma
2.9
could have been proved using
Gheondea’s characterization of pseudo-regular subspaces cited above.
The lemma indicates that pseudo-regular spaces have analogous
properties to regular spaces. For example,
{\mathcal{S}}
is (pseudo-)regular
if and only if
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is (pseudo-)regular, while if
{\mathcal{S}}
is

(pseudo-)
regular, then
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is (pseudo-)regular.

By both Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.3
, if

{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr, then
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
is closed and

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}={\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}}^{0}
. So one might suspect that
these conditions imply that
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr. The next example shows that
this may fail.

Example 2.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
be an infinite dimensional Hilbert space,

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{0}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. Make
{\mathcal{H}}
in to a Kreĭn space by defining a fundamental symmetry

J=\begin{pmatrix}1_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}&0&0\\
0&0&1_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{H}}_{0}\oplus\{0\}\oplus\{0\}
and

{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\oplus\{0\}
. Fix
x\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\|x\|=1
, and set
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}=\mathrm{span}\,\{x\}
. The space

{\mathcal{R}}
is a Hilbert space (so regular) while
{\mathcal{N}}
is isotropic, so

{\mathcal{N}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
. Note too that

{\mathcal{N}}_{0}^{\bot}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
. As in the last example, define an
unbounded linear functional
\varphi
, this time on

{\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
, with
\varphi(x)=1
. Let
{\mathcal{S}}={\rm ker\,}\varphi
.
Then
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}:={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{0}={\mathcal{S}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
is closed and

{\mathcal{S}}
is dense in
{\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
.

Define a bounded operator acting on
{\mathcal{N}}
with dense range
{\mathcal{W}}
not
containing
x
. Then
{{\mathcal{W}}}^{\bot}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
(as a subspace of

{\mathcal{H}}
). Let
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}={\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{W}}
. Then

\begin{split}{\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}}^{\bot}&={\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}\cap{\mathcal{W}}^{\bot}\\
&=({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{0})^{\bot}\cap({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}})\\
&={\mathcal{R}}^{\bot}\cap{\mathcal{N}}_{0}^{\bot}\cap({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}})\\
&=({\mathcal{N}}+J{\mathcal{N}})\cap{\mathcal{N}}_{0}^{\bot}\cap({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}})\\
&={\mathcal{N}}\cap({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}})\\
&={\mathcal{N}},\end{split}

which contains
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
since
{\mathcal{W}}\subseteq{\mathcal{N}}
, so

\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}+{\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}}^{\bot}=({\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{W}})+{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{S}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{0}+{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{R}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{0}+{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}},

which is closed. It is easy to see that
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{0}={\mathcal{W}}
, so

{\overline{\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}^{0}}}={\mathcal{N}}
. Also it can be checked that

{\overline{\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}}}^{0}={\mathcal{N}}

Suppose that
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}
is qpr. Then

\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}=\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{W}}
, where
\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
is
regular, and so closed. By assumption,
{\mathcal{W}}
is an operator range,
hence in this case
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}
is also an operator range, as is
the closed space
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. By construction,

{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. On the other hand, if

x\in\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
,
x=s+n
,
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
,

n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{0}
. Thus

x-s=n\in{\mathcal{W}}\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{1}={\mathcal{W}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}_{1}=\{0\}
. So
n=0

and
x=s\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Hence
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{1}={\mathcal{S}}
.
However, by
[
4
, Cor. 2, p. 260]
, the intersection of two
operator ranges is an operator range, and by
Example
2.8
it was seen that

{\mathcal{S}}
is not an operator range. Therefore
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}
is not an
operator range. Hence
\tilde{{\mathcal{S}}}
is not qpr.

The following is the proper generalization to qpr spaces of Gheondea’s
characterization of pseudo-regular spaces, and also contains his
result since closed spaces are operator ranges.

Theorem 2.13
.

A subspace
{\mathcal{S}}
of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
is qpr if and only if

(1)

{\mathcal{S}}
is an operator range, and

(2)

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed.

Proof.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr, the forward implication follows directly by the
definition of qpr and Lemma
2.5
.

Now assume
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
is an operator range and that

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed. By
[
4
, Th. 2.4]
, there exist
closed subspaces
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
,
such that

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}.

Hence
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{M}}_{2}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})
.

Fix a fundamental symmetry
J
and associated Hilbert space
|{\mathcal{H}}|
.
Define on this Hilbert space, the closed space

{\mathcal{R}}:={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\ominus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}).

Then
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
,

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})
, and

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{2}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp},

where the latter sum is direct since

{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{R}}\cap({\mathcal{M}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})=\{0\}
.
Hence

{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}\cap({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{0}.

Let
Q
be the (not necessarily orthogonal) projection on

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
with range
{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
and kernel
{\mathcal{R}}
. This is
bounded since
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
. As

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is closed, by continuity, the
pre-image
Q^{-1}(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}})=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}+{\mathcal{R}}
is
closed and contains
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{R}}
. Hence

Q^{-1}(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}})\supseteq\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{R}}}
,
while the reverse inclusion always holds. Therefore,

\overline{S}=\overline{{\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{R}}.

Arguing as above with
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}
in place of
{\mathcal{S}}
, one has

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}=({\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})\cap\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}={\mathcal{R}}+(\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp})={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,(\overline{S})^{0}.

Consequently, since
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}\subseteq(\overline{S})^{0}
,

{\mathcal{N}}:=\overline{{\mathcal{S}}^{0}}=(\overline{S})^{0}.

The space
{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
, so the sum is both orthogonal
and direct; that is,

\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}.

By parallel arguments with

\tilde{R}={\mathcal{M}}_{2}\ominus_{|{\mathcal{H}}|}({\mathcal{M}}_{2}\cap\overline{{\mathcal{S}}})
, one
has
\tilde{R}
closed and

{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}.

Then

{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=\overline{S}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}=({\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}})+{\mathcal{N}}.

By Corollary
2.10
,
{\mathcal{S}}+{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is
qpr, and
{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is an operator range because it is the sum
of operator ranges. Since
{\mathcal{R}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
are orthogonal
to
{\mathcal{N}}
,
{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is orthogonal to
{\mathcal{N}}
. If

{\mathcal{N}}\ni n=r+r^{\prime}
, with
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
r^{\prime}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}
, then

r=-r^{\prime}+n\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}
. As
{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}^{\bot}=\{0\}
,
conclude that
r=0
and
r^{\prime}=n
. Also,

\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
, so
r^{\prime}=n=0
, meaning that

({\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{{\mathcal{R}}})\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
. Therefore by Proposition

2.7
,
{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is regular.

The sum
{\mathcal{R}}+\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is also orthogonal and direct. Since the
sum is regular, it follows that
{\mathcal{R}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
are regular.
The decomposition
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{S}}^{0}
obtained above is also
orthogonal because
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
.
Therefore
{\mathcal{S}}
is qpr.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
be qpr subspaces of a Kreĭn space such that

{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\perp{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
. Then
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}+{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
is qpr.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}={\mathcal{R}}_{i}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{i}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
is regular and
{\mathcal{N}}_{i}
is
neutral,
i=1,2
. Then
{\mathcal{R}}_{1},{\mathcal{R}}_{2},{\mathcal{N}}_{1},{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
are pairwise
orthogonal. Thus
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}+{\mathcal{S}}_{2}={\mathcal{R}}+{\mathcal{N}}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}_{1}+{\mathcal{R}}_{2}

is regular,
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{N}}_{1}+{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
is neutral, and
{\mathcal{R}}\perp{\mathcal{N}}
. If

x\in{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}
, then
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
x\perp{\mathcal{R}}
; hence
x=0
by the
nondegeneracy of a regular subspace. So
{\mathcal{R}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
, and
therefore
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}+{\mathcal{S}}_{2}={\mathcal{R}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
is qpr.
∎

Of course this last proposition can be extended to finite families of
qpr spaces. The extension to infinite families is explored in
Theorem
6.1
.

3.
Sums of operator ranges

Quasi-pseudo-regular spaces are characterized in terms of operator
ranges, and closed subspaces are obviously also operator ranges. For
this reason, the study of orthogonal sums of regular, pseudo-regular,
and qpr spaces, leads naturally to the study of sums of operator
ranges. Finite sums of operator ranges are operator
ranges
[
4
, Th. 2.2]
. The consideration of infinite sums of
such spaces is more subtle. The main issues here are already present
for Hilbert spaces, but our applications involve Kreĭn spaces.

Attention therefore turns to sums of operator ranges

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}={\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}
acting on copies of a fixed Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
indexed by
\Lambda
. By Definition
1.3
,
such a sum
{\mathcal{M}}
is an operator range such that

\mathrm{span}_{\lambda}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{\lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}.

In general this will not be unique (see Example

3.5
). One way of constructing such a
T
is as
an operator from a Cartesian product space into
{\mathcal{H}}
. If
{\mathcal{H}}
is a
Kreĭn space and
\Lambda
is an index set, define the Kreĭn space

{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
as in
[
14
, Section 4]
. For any associated
Hilbert space
|{\mathcal{H}}|
for
{\mathcal{H}}
,
|{\mathcal{H}}|^{\Lambda}
is an associated
Hilbert space for
{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
.

Our first condition for the existence of a sum is based on the Cauchy
criterion. As in
[
13
]
, given a family

\{a_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
of non-negative scalars, the notation

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}a_{\lambda}<\infty
means that all finite sums from this
set have a uniform upper bound. In this case,
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}a_{\lambda}

is Moore-Smith convergent, and its Moore-Smith sum (also denoted

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}a_{\lambda}
) is the supremum of all finite sums. Moreover
the Cauchy criterion is satisfied, and only countably many summands
are non-zero (see Lemma 2.2 and Theorem 2.3 of
[
13
]
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
be a collection of operator ranges in
a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
, and let
\{T_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
be operators
in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
with
{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}={\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
for each
{\lambda\in\Lambda}
. If

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|T_{\lambda}\|<\infty
relative to some and hence any
norm for
{\mathcal{H}}
, then the formula

(3.1)

Tx=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}x_{\lambda},\quad x=\{x_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda},

defines an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda},{\mathcal{H}})
whose range is a sum
of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
.

When
\Lambda
is countable, the condition

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|T_{\lambda}\|<\infty
in Theorem

3.1
can always be arranged by scaling, that
is, by multiplying the operators
T_{\lambda}
by positive constants.
Scaling does not change the relations
{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}={\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
,

{\lambda\in\Lambda}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For every
countable collection
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
of operator ranges in
a Kreĭn space, there is a bounded operator
T
with range a sum of

\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Choose and fix an associated Hilbert space
|{\mathcal{H}}|
and norm

\|\cdot\|
for
{\mathcal{H}}
. By assumption the scalars

\{\|T_{\lambda}\|\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
are Moore-Smith summable. By the Cauchy
criterion, for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a finite set

{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\subseteq\Lambda
such that

\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}\|<\varepsilon
for every finite
set
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\Lambda
disjoint from
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
. Consider some
fixed
x=\{x_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
. Given

\varepsilon>0
there is a finite set
{\mathcal{F}}_{0,x}\subseteq\Lambda

such that

\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}x\|<\varepsilon/\|x\|

for every finite set
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\Lambda
disjoint from

{\mathcal{F}}_{0,x}
. Then for all such
{\mathcal{F}}
,

(3.2)

\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda}\|\leq\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda}\|\leq\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}\|\|x_{\lambda}\|\leq\|x\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}\|<\varepsilon.

It follows that the family
\{T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

Moore-Smith summable
[
14
, Theorem A.2(3)]
. Since
x

is arbitrary, the correspondence

T\colon x\to\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda}
is an everywhere defined mapping from
{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
into

{\mathcal{H}}
. The mapping is clearly linear. The first three inequalities
in (
3.2
) hold for every finite subset
{\mathcal{F}}
of
\Lambda
,
and the sums
\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}\|
in the fourth
term of (
3.2
) have a uniform upper bound. Thus
T
is
bounded and hence continuous. It is immediate from the formula
defining
T
that
{\rm ran\,}T
is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
.
∎

The same conclusion holds with a weaker condition on operator norms.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
be a collection of operator ranges in a
Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
,
\{T_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
operators in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
with

{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}={\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
for each
{\lambda\in\Lambda}
. The following are
equivalent:

(1)

For some and hence any norm for
{\mathcal{H}}
there is a constant

C>0
such that
\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}T_{\lambda}\|\leq C

for every finite
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\Lambda
.

(2)

The formula

(3.3)

Tx=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda},\quad x=\{x_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda},

defines an operator in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda},{\mathcal{H}})
.

In this case
{\rm ran\,}T
is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
.

Proof.

Assume (1). Choose and fix an associated Hilbert space
|{\mathcal{H}}|
and
norm
\|\cdot\|
for
{\mathcal{H}}
. Then
|{\mathcal{H}}|^{\Lambda}
is an associated
Hilbert space for
{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
. An equivalent form of our
assumption is that
\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}T_{\lambda}^{*}\|\leq C

for every finite
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\Lambda
. As in the proof of

[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
, this implies that

\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}^{*}u\|^{2}\leq 4C^{2}\|u\|^{2}
,

u\in{\mathcal{H}}
, for all finite
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\Lambda
, and thus

\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}\|T_{\lambda}^{*}u\|^{2}\leq 4C^{2}\|u\|^{2},\qquad u\in{\mathcal{H}}.

Hence there is an operator
W\in{\mathcal{L}}(|{\mathcal{H}}|,|{\mathcal{H}}|^{\Lambda})
such that

Wu=\{T_{\lambda}^{*}u\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda},\qquad u\in{\mathcal{H}}.

Then also
W\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}},{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda})
, and

W^{*}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda},{\mathcal{H}})
.

Suppose
x=\{x_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
. Fix

\mu\in\Lambda
, and let

x^{(\mu)}=\{x^{(\mu)}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
, where

x^{(\mu)}_{\mu}=x_{\mu}
and all other terms are zero. Then

W^{*}x^{(\mu)}=T_{\mu}x_{\mu}.

For if
u
is any element of
{\mathcal{H}}
, then

\displaystyle{\left<W^{*}x^{(\mu)},u\right>}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle={\left<x^{(\mu)},Wu\right>}_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}}={\left<\{x^{(\mu)}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda},\{T_{\lambda}^{*}u\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\right>}_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt={\left<x^{(\mu)}_{\mu},T_{\mu}^{*}u\right>}_{{\mathcal{H}}}={\left<T_{\mu}x_{\mu},u\right>}_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Since
x
is the Moore-Smith sum of
\{x^{(\mu)}\}_{\mu\in\Lambda}
,
and
W^{*}
is continuous,
W^{*}x
is the Moore-Smith sum of

W^{*}x^{(\mu)}=T_{\mu}x_{\mu}
,
\mu\in\Lambda
, in
{\mathcal{H}}
. Renaming

W^{*}
as
T
, (
3.3
) is obtained. It is immediate from
(
3.3
) that
{\rm ran\,}T
is a sum of the family

\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
.

Conversely, if (2) holds, then for each
f
in
{\mathcal{H}}
any finite sum
of vectors
\{T_{\lambda}f\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is uniformly bounded (see
Theorem A.2 of
[
14
]
). By the Uniform Boundedness
Principle, the finite sums of operators
\{T_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
have
a uniform bound; that is, (1) holds.
∎

Here are a few examples illustrating aspects of the last theorems.

Example 3.4
.

As observed in Example
2.6
and subsequent
examples, in a separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
there is a space
{\mathcal{R}}

which is
not
an operator range. For each
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
with

\|x\|=1
, set
{\mathcal{M}}_{x}=\mathrm{span}\,x
. Being one
dimensional, these spaces are obviously the ranges of projection
operators, and
{\mathcal{R}}=\mathrm{span}_{x}\,M_{x}
. Of course,

\bigvee_{x}{\mathcal{M}}_{x}=\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}
is the range of the projection
onto
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}
. It is an open question whether there is an
operator range
{\mathcal{M}}
containing
{\mathcal{R}}
and properly contained in

\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}
.

Example 3.5
.

On the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(=\ell^{2})
, consider the
Toeplitz operator

T:=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0&\cdots\\
1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

This is easily seen to be positive with dense, non-closed range.
Write
c_{k}
for the
k
th column of
T
, and
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
for the span of

c_{k}
. Then the elements of the family
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\}_{k}
are pairwise
disjoint and
\mathrm{span}_{k}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\subseteq{\rm ran\,}T
. Let

\{e_{k}\}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
to be the standard basis for
{\mathcal{H}}
, so that

c_{k}=Te_{k}
. Evidently, any
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
is a Moore-Smith sum,

x=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{k}x_{k}e_{k}
, and so

Tx=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{k}x_{k}Te_{k}=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{k}x_{k}c_{k}
. Thus

{\rm ran\,}T\subseteq\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{k}{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
, and so

{\rm ran\,}T
is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\}
. (With
T_{j}=TP_{j}
,
P_{j}
the
orthonormal projection onto
\mathrm{span}\,e_{j}
,
j\geq 1
, this
illustrates Theorem
3.3
).

Let
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
be any vector with infinite support; that is, with infinitely
many non-zero entries. Define the diagonal operator

D_{h}
with
(n,n)
-entry equal to
h_{n}
if
h_{n}\neq 0
and

1
otherwise. Then
{\rm ran\,}D_{h}
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}
and
h\notin{\rm ran\,}D_{h}
. Define
T_{h}=TD_{h}
. Thus
Th\notin{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
. Note that
T_{h}e_{k}
spans

{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
. Consequently,
\mathrm{span}_{k}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\subseteq{\rm ran\,}T_{h}\subset{\rm ran\,}T
, where the second containment is proper. So
{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\}
.

By
[
4
, Cor. 2, p. 260]
, the intersection of any two
operator ranges is an operator range. So it is natural to wonder if
there is a bounded operator
\tilde{T}
with minimal range, in the
sense that its range is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\}
contained in any other
sum of these spaces. There is no loss in generality in assuming
that, if it exists,
\tilde{T}\geq 0
. By construction,

{\rm ran\,}\tilde{T}\subseteq{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
for all
h
with infinite
support, and so
Th\notin{\rm ran\,}\tilde{T}
for all such
h
.

For each
k
, choose
\tilde{e}_{k}\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that

\tilde{T}\tilde{e}_{k}=c_{k}
, and set

\alpha_{k}=1/(2^{k}\|\tilde{e}_{k}\|
). Then

f=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\tilde{e}_{k}\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Suppose that

\tilde{T}f
has finite support. Then there is
n
such that for
all
k>n
, the
k
th entry of
\tilde{T}f
is
0
. This means
that
\alpha_{k+1}=-(\alpha_{k-1}+2\alpha_{k})<0
,
contradicting the fact that
\alpha_{k}>0
for all
k
. Hence

\tilde{T}f
has infinite support. Since

{\rm ran\,}\tilde{T}\subseteq{\rm ran\,}T
,
\tilde{T}f=Th
for some

h\in{\mathcal{H}}
. This
h
has infinite support since otherwise
Th

would have finite support. Recall that for
T_{h}
,
{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
is a
sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\}_{k}
with
h\notin{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
. By assumption,

{\rm ran\,}\tilde{T}\subseteq{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
, implying that

Th\in{\rm ran\,}T_{h}
, giving a contradiction. Hence there is no
minimal operator range
{\mathcal{M}}
which is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}\}_{k}
.
Moreover, this implies that
\mathrm{span}_{k}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
is a subspace
of
{\mathcal{H}}
which is
not
an operator range.

Example 3.6
.

In this example, the set
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
is taken to be be
an infinite collection of operator ranges in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}={\mathcal{M}}\neq\{0\}
for all
\lambda
. In this
case,
\mathrm{span}_{\lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}={\mathcal{M}}
so
{\mathcal{M}}
is a sum of
these operator ranges.

Alternately,
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}
is also a sum of these spaces.
Obviously,
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}
is an operator range since it is the
range of a projection. Now suppose that

x\in\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}\backslash{\mathcal{M}}
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, choose

x_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}
such that
x_{0}=0
and
z_{n}=x-x_{n}
satisfies

\|z_{n}\|=\|x-x_{n}\|\leq\|x\|/2^{n}
. Set

y_{n}=x_{n}-x_{n-1}=z_{n-1}-z_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
n\geq 1
. Then

\|y_{n}\|\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}+\tfrac{2}{2^{n}}\right)\|x\|=\tfrac{3}{2^{n}}\|x\|
and
y_{1}+y_{2}+\cdots+y_{n}=x_{n}\to x
as

n\to\infty
. Hence
\{y_{n}\}
is absolutely summable with

\sum\|y_{n}\|\leq 3\|x\|
. By the Cauchy criterion,
\{y_{n}\}
is
Moore-Smith summable, and
\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}y_{n}=x
.
In other words,

\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}{\mathcal{M}}=\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}
, and so

\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}
is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
.

Note that Example 6.3 of
[
14
]
shows more generally
that in an orthogonal direct sum of Kreĭn spaces, the closed span
and Moore-Smith may differ.

Example 3.7
.

Ranges of closed operators are operator ranges in the usual sense.
Unfortunately, if a condition like that required in
Theorem
3.3
does not hold, then the
operator
T
constructed there will generally not be closable, and
so the approach taken in the proof of
Theorem
3.3
does not appear to
immediately generalize. Here is a simple example illustrating the
problem.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}
, and suppose
T
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathbb{N}}
to
{\mathcal{H}}
is the
row operator with all entries
1
. Vectors in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathbb{N}}
which have
only finitely many non-zero entries (the set of which is dense) are
in the domain of
T
. Thus the vector
x_{n}
with the first
n

entries equal to
1/n
is in the domain of
T
and

\|x_{n}\|^{2}=1/n
. So
x_{n}\to 0
in norm with
n
. However,

Tx_{n}=1
for all
n
, meaning that
T
is not closable.

Admittedly, this example could have been dealt with since the number
of operators is countable, but uncountable variations are easily
constructed.

4.
Convergence of nets of projections

A more relaxed notion of projection is used when considering sums of
pseudo-regular spaces, as the selfadjoint projections which suffice
for regular spaces are no longer enough here. On a Kreĭn space

{\mathcal{H}}
, call
P\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
a
projection
if
P^{2}=P
. If
P
is a
projection, so is
1-P
; moreover,
{\rm ran\,}P={\rm ker\,}(1-P)
, and

{\rm ker\,}P={\rm ran\,}(1-P)
. The range of a projection is closed, and every
closed subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
is the range of a projection. If

{\mathcal{M}}={\rm ran\,}P
and
{\mathcal{N}}={\rm ker\,}P
for some projection
P\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
, then

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
. Conversely, any pair of closed subspaces
{\mathcal{M}}

and
{\mathcal{N}}
such that
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
has this form for a unique
projection
P\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
.

There is an even more general notion of projection on Kreĭn spaces
which allows for unbounded operators or even linear relations; see

[
2
]
and
[
1
]
. While interesting in the
context of quasi-pseudo-regular spaces, it is not addressed here.

Lemma 4.1

(​​
[
3
, p. 481]
)
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
be projections, and set
{\mathcal{M}}_{j}={\rm ran\,}P_{j}
and

{\mathcal{N}}_{j}={\rm ker\,}P_{j}
,
j=1,2
. Then

(1)

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
if and only if
P_{2}P_{1}=P_{1}
;

(2)

{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\supseteq{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
if and
only if
P_{2}P_{1}=P_{1}P_{2}=P_{1}
.

It is also known that a closed subspace
{\mathcal{M}}
of a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}

is pseudo-regular if and only if
{\mathcal{M}}={\rm ran\,}Q
, where
Q\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
is a

normal projection
, that is,
Q^{*}Q=QQ^{*}
. This is proved by
Maestripieri and Pería in Theorem 4.3 of

[
9
]
using the characterization of
pseudo-regular subspaces in
Theorem
2.13
. An
important difference with the regular case is that the normal
projection
Q
is not determined by
{\mathcal{M}}
. Observe that for any
normal projection
Q
,
Q^{*}
is a normal projection.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Kreĭn space,
\{P_{d}\}_{d\in D}
a net of projections in

{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
such that

(4.1)

P_{d}P_{d^{\prime}}=P_{d^{\prime}}P_{d}=P_{d}\quad\text{whenever}\quad d^{\prime}\geq d.

Assume that relative to some and hence any associated norm

\|\cdot\|
for
{\mathcal{H}}
, there is a
C>0
such that

(4.2)

\|P_{d}\|\leq C,\qquad{d\in D}.

Set
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}={\rm ran\,}P_{d}
,
{\mathcal{N}}_{d}={\rm ker\,}P_{d}
,
{d\in D}
, and

(4.3)

{\mathcal{M}}=\bigvee_{{d\in D}}{\mathcal{M}}_{d},\qquad{\mathcal{N}}=\bigcap_{d\in D}{\mathcal{N}}_{d}.

Then there is a projection
P\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
such that

(4.4)

\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}f=Pf,\qquad f\in{\mathcal{H}},

in the strong topology of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Moreover,
{\rm ran\,}P={\mathcal{M}}
and

{\rm ker\,}P={\mathcal{N}}
.

By Lemma
4.1
, the condition (
4.1
) implies that

(4.5)

{\rm ran\,}P_{d^{\prime}}\supseteq{\rm ran\,}P_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm ker\,}P_{d^{\prime}}\subseteq{\rm ker\,}P_{d},

whenever
d^{\prime}\geq d
. If the projections
P_{d}
,
d\in D
, commute, then
conversely (
4.5
) implies (
4.1
).

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Without loss of generality, assume that
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Hilbert space.
This can be done since the projections
\{P_{d}\}_{d\in D}
satisfy the
same conditions relative to any associated Hilbert space
|{\mathcal{H}}|
,
and strong convergence in
{\mathcal{H}}
is the same as strong convergence in

|{\mathcal{H}}|
.

First show that
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{N}}
. Fix
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
. For each
{d\in D}
,

P_{d}
is a projection, and hence
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}_{d}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}_{d}
. Write

f=u_{d}+v_{d},\qquad u_{d}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d},\;v_{d}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{d}.

By (
4.2
), each
u_{d}=P_{d}f,{d\in D},
is in the closed ball
centered at
0
of radius
C\|f\|
. Since the closed ball is
compact in the weak topology of
{\mathcal{H}}
, there is a subnet

\{u_{d_{i}},i\in E\}
of
\{u_{d},{d\in D}\}
that converges weakly to a
vector
u\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\|u\|\leq C\|f\|
. Thus

\lim_{i\in E}\left<u_{d_{i}},x\right>=\left<u,x\right>,\quad x\in{\mathcal{H}}.

Set
v=f-u
. Then
\{v_{d_{i}},i\in E\}
is a subnet of

\{v_{d},{d\in D}\}
such that

\lim_{i\in E}\left<v_{d_{i}},x\right>=\left<v,x\right>,\quad x\in{\mathcal{H}},

that is,
v=\lim_{i\in E}v_{d_{i}}
weakly.

Claim:

v\in{\mathcal{N}}_{d}
for each
{d\in D}
.

Fix
d\in D
. By the definition of a subnet, there is an
i_{0}
in

E
such that
i\geq i_{0}\Rightarrow d_{i}\geq d
. By replacing the net

\{v_{d_{i}},i\in E\}
by its truncation at
i_{0}
(that is,

\{v_{d_{i}},i\in E\text{\,and\,}i\geq i_{0}\}
), it can be assumed
that
i\geq i_{0}
for all
i\in E
. Then for all
i\in E
,

d_{i}\geq d
, hence

{\mathcal{N}}_{d_{i}}={\rm ker\,}P_{d_{i}}\subseteq{\rm ker\,}P_{d}={\mathcal{N}}_{d}.

Therefore
v_{d_{i}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{d}
for all
i\in E
, and hence

v\in{\mathcal{N}}_{d}
since a norm closed convex set is weakly closed. The
claim follows.

By the Claim,
v\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Thus each
f
in
{\mathcal{H}}
has the
form
f=u+v
with
u\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
v\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Thus
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}+{\mathcal{N}}
.

Next that
{\mathcal{M}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
and hence
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
.
The key step is to show that
\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}f=f
in norm for every

f\in{\mathcal{M}}
. By (
4.3
),
{\mathcal{M}}
is the closure of the set of all
finite sums of vectors in the subspaces
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
,
{d\in D}
. Suppose

h=h_{d_{1}}+\cdots h_{d_{r}}
, where
h_{i}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,r
.
By the definition of a net, there is a
d
in
D
such that

d\geq d_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
, and then
h\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
by (
4.5
).
It follows that

{\mathcal{M}}=\overline{\bigcup_{d\in D}{\mathcal{M}}_{d}}.

Hence for any
f\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
d_{0}\in D

and a vector
g\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d_{0}}
such that

\|f-g\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{C+1},

where
C
is the constant in (
4.2
). For any
d\geq d_{0}
,

P_{d}\,g=g
because
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}\supseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{d_{0}}
by (
4.5
).
Hence for all
d\geq d_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|f-P_{d}f\|

\displaystyle=\|f-g+P_{d}(g-f)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f-g\|+\|P_{d}\|\|g-f\|

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{C+1}+\frac{C\varepsilon}{C+1}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon,

which means that
\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}f=f
. Now if both
f\in{\mathcal{M}}
and

f\in{\mathcal{N}}
, then
\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}f=f
by what was just showed, and
also
\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}f=0
because
f\in{\mathcal{N}}
and

{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq{\mathcal{N}}_{d}={\rm ker\,}P_{d}
for each
{d\in D}
; hence
f=0
. It
follows that
{\mathcal{M}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
and hence
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
.

Since
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{M}}\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{+}}\,{\mathcal{N}}
, there is a unique projection
P\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
with
range
{\mathcal{M}}
and kernel
{\mathcal{N}}
. The preceding argument incidentally
shows that (
4.4
) holds separately for
f\in{\mathcal{M}}
and

f\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and hence it holds for all

. All of the
statements in the theorem follow.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

In Theorem
4.2
assume that the projections
P_{d}
,
{d\in D}
,
are all normal. Then
P
is normal. Moreover,

(4.6)

\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}^{*}g=P^{*}g,\qquad g\in{\mathcal{H}},

in the strong topology of
{\mathcal{H}}
, and

(4.7)

{\rm ran\,}P^{*}=\bigvee_{d\in D}{\rm ran\,}P_{d}^{*},\quad{\rm ker\,}P^{*}=\bigcap_{d\in D}{\rm ker\,}P_{d}^{*}.

In the special case that each
P_{d}
,
{d\in D}
, is selfadjoint,

is
selfadjoint and (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) reduce to (
4.3
)
and (
4.4
).

Proof.

The projections
P_{d}^{*}
,
{d\in D}
, satisfy (
4.1
) and
(
4.2
) with each
P_{d}
replaced by
P_{d}^{*}
. Set

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{d}={\rm ran\,}P_{d}^{*}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{d}={\rm ker\,}P_{d}^{*}
,

{d\in D}
, and

(4.8)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\bigvee_{{d\in D}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{d},\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}=\bigcap_{d\in D}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{d}.

By Theorem
4.2
there is a projection
\widetilde{P}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
such
that

(4.9)

\lim_{d\in D}P_{d}^{*}f=\widetilde{P}f,\qquad f\in{\mathcal{H}},

strongly, and
{\rm ran\,}\widetilde{P}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
and

{\rm ker\,}\widetilde{P}=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}
. Since

\left<P_{d}f,g\right>=\left<f,P_{d}^{*}g\right>,\qquad{d\in D},

in the limit one obtains

5.
Sums of pseudo-regular spaces

The theorem below is a generalization of Theorem 4.3 of

[
13
]
, and comes close to the form of
Theorem
1.5
for regular spaces. Since even finite
orthogonal direct sums of pseudo-regular spaces may not be
pseudo-regular (see Example
2.2
), some rather strong
conditions are needed to guarantee that orthogonal direct sums of
pseudo-regular spaces are again pseudo-regular.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\{Q_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be normal projections such that for all

{\lambda\in\Lambda}
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
the ranges of

Q_{\lambda},Q_{\lambda}^{*}
, and
{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda},\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}

the kernels of
Q_{\lambda},Q_{\lambda}^{*}
. Suppose that for all

\lambda\neq\mu
,

(5.1)

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\perp{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu},\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\perp\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu},\quad{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\perp\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

For some and hence any norm
\|\cdot\|
for
{\mathcal{H}}
, there
is a constant
C>0
such that

\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}Q_{\lambda}\|\leq C
for every finite
subset
{\mathcal{F}}
of
\Lambda
.

(2)

There is a normal projection
Q\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
such that for all

f
in
{\mathcal{H}}
,

(5.2)

Qf=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}Q_{\lambda}f.

In this case,
Q^{*}f=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}Q_{\lambda}^{*}f
, and

\begin{split}{\rm ran\,}Q&=\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda},\quad{\rm ker\,}Q=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda},\quad\text{and}\\
{\rm ran\,}Q^{*}&=\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda},\quad{\rm ker\,}Q^{*}=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}.\end{split}

Proof.

Assume (1). Let
D
be the set of all finite subsets
{\mathcal{F}}
of

\Lambda
, directed by
\supseteq
. For each
{\mathcal{F}}\in D
, set

P_{\mathcal{F}}=\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}Q_{\lambda}.

The relations (
5.1
) have the equivalent forms

Q_{\mu}^{*}Q_{\lambda}=0
,
Q_{\mu}Q_{\lambda}^{*}=0
, and

Q_{\mu}Q_{\lambda}=0
. By straightforward algebra, these equations
imply that for each
{\mathcal{F}}\in D
,

P_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}P_{\mathcal{F}}=P_{\mathcal{F}}P_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}=P_{\mathcal{F}},

that is,
P_{\mathcal{F}}
is a normal projection. Further algebra shows that
if
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
are in
D
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}\supseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, then

P_{\mathcal{F}}P_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}=P_{{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{F}}=P_{\mathcal{F}}.

By (1),
\|P_{\mathcal{F}}\|\leq C
,
{\mathcal{F}}\in D
. Thus the net of projections

\{P_{\mathcal{F}}\}_{{\mathcal{F}}\in D}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorems

4.2
and
4.3
. By (
4.4
) and (
4.6
), there is
a normal projection
Q\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
such that for all
f
in
{\mathcal{H}}
,

\lim_{{\mathcal{F}}\in D}P_{\mathcal{F}}f=Qf,\qquad\lim_{{\mathcal{F}}\in D}P_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}f=Q^{*}f

in the strong topology of
{\mathcal{H}}
. By the definition of Moore-Smith
convergence, this is equivalent to (
5.2
), and so (2) follows.
It is an elementary exercise to show that

Q^{*}f=\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}Q_{\lambda}^{*}f
.

Conversely, if (2) holds, using the argument in the proof of
Theorem
3.3
, the operators

\{Q_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
have a uniform bound, that is, (1) holds.

The final statement follows from Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
.
∎

As was pointed out, orthogonal subspaces of a Kreĭn space need not
have zero intersection. Nevertheless, it follows from the assumptions
of the theorem that for
\lambda\neq\mu
,

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\cap{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}=\{0\}
since

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\bot}
implies that

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\cap{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}^{\bot}\cap{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}={\rm ker\,}Q_{\mu}\cap{\rm ran\,}Q_{\mu}=\{0\}
. The equality

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\mu}=\{0\}
follows in the
same fashion.

Example 4.2 in
[
13
]
illustrates how normal projections
satisfying the conditions in the above theorem arise naturally as
wandering subspaces of isometries.

6.
Sums of quasi-pseudo-regular spaces

The goal now is to find a version of Theorem
1.5
which
applies to qpr spaces. The material from
Section
3
plays a key role here.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
be a collection of pairwise orthogonal
qpr spaces in a Kreĭn space
{\mathcal{H}}
. Assume there are regular spaces

\{{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
with corresponding selfadjoint projection
operators
\{E_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, and operators
\{T_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

in
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
having neutral ranges
\{{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, such that

{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}={\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}\oplus{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda},\qquad{\lambda\in\Lambda}.

Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

For some and hence any norm for
{\mathcal{H}}
there is a
C>0

such that both
\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}E_{\lambda}\|\leq C
and

\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}T_{\lambda}\|\leq C
for every finite
subset
{\mathcal{F}}
of
\Lambda
.

(2)

There is a selfadjoint projection
P\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}})
and an
operator
T\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda},{\mathcal{H}})
such that for all

f\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and for all
x=(x_{\lambda})_{\lambda}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
,

Pf=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}E_{\lambda}f,\qquad Tx=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda}.

In this case,
{\rm ran\,}P=\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\bigoplus}}\,_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}
,
{\mathcal{N}}:={\rm ran\,}T
is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, and

{\mathcal{M}}:={\rm ran\,}P\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,{\mathcal{N}}

is a sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (1) and (2) follows Theorems
1.5

and
3.3
with

{\rm ran\,}P=\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\bigoplus}}\,_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}
,
{\mathcal{N}}:={\rm ran\,}T
is a sum
of
\{{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
.

To see that
{\mathcal{N}}
is neutral,
consider any
f,g\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Then by (
3.3
), for some

x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\Lambda}
,

f=Tx=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda},\qquad g=Ty=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{\mu\in\Lambda}T_{\mu}y_{\mu},

and

\left<f,g\right>=\left<\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda},\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{\mu\in\Lambda}T_{\mu}y_{\mu}\right>=\sum_{\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda}\left<T_{\lambda}x_{\lambda},T_{\mu}y_{\mu}\right>=0,

because the spaces
\{{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
are neutral and
pairwise orthogonal.

A similar argument shows that
\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}
and
{\mathcal{N}}

are orthogonal. Thus

{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq(\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda})^{\perp}
, so that

(\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda})\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
because

\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}
is regular. Then by
[
4
, Theorem
2.2]
, the subspace

{\mathcal{M}}={\rm ran\,}P\,\,{\overset{\smash{\mathchoice{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\displaystyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\textstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptstyle.$}}}}}{{{\hbox{\scalebox{1.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle.$}}}}}}}{\oplus}}\,\,{\rm ran\,}T

is an operator range. Finally,
{\mathcal{M}}
contains the finite sums of

\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, and by construction,

{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}{\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}+\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}\subseteq\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}({\mathcal{R}}_{\lambda}+{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda})=\sum^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}.

Therefore
{\mathcal{M}}
is a sum of the
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

In Theorem
6.1
, when
\Lambda
is countable the
assumption that
\|\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}T_{\lambda}\|\leq C
for
every finite subset
{\mathcal{F}}
of
\Lambda
can be omitted.

Proof.

When
\Lambda
is countable, in the proof of Theorem
6.1
,
scaling ensures that

\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|T_{\lambda}\|<\infty
. Then Theorem

3.1
is used to define the operator
T
by
(
3.1
) and the proof is completed in the same way.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

In Theorem
6.1
, if the subspaces
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

are pseudo-regular and

\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}
is closed,
then
\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}
is
pseudo-regular and a unique sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.1
, there is a sum
{\mathcal{M}}
of

\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
of the form indicated in that theorem as qpr
spaces. By the assumption that

\sum^{\scalebox{0.4}{\text{MS}}}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}{\rm ran\,}T_{\lambda}
is closed,

{\mathcal{N}}
is closed, and so
{\mathcal{M}}
is closed. Hence by
Lemma
2.9
,
{\mathcal{M}}
is pseudo-regular. By
Lemma
1.4
, the sum is unique.
∎

Example 6.4
.

As already noted in Section
3
,
orthogonal sums need not be unique. As another example, consider
qpr spaces
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
under the assumption that the
regular parts are orthogonal and the neutral parts are all the same
operator range
{\mathcal{N}}
. Assume also that the regular parts are
summable. Then the sum of these is a unique space by
Theorem
1.5
. On the other hand, by
Example
3.6
any operator range

\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}
with

{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{N}}}
, including

\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}={\mathcal{N}}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}=\overline{{\mathcal{N}}}
, are sums of
the neutral spaces. The space
\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}
may or may not be
closed, and so the sum of
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
can be
pseudo-regular or just qpr, even when
{\mathcal{N}}
is not closed so that
the spaces in
\{{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}\}_{{\lambda\in\Lambda}}
are qpr.
