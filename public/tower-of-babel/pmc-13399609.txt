Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair With Autologous Biologic Augmentation Using Biceps Tendon Redirection and Incorporation
Authors: Erel Ben‐Ari, Luciano Ramires, Brian B. Gilmer, Sean C. Marvil, Dan Guttmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long head of the biceps tendon is a valuable autologous tissue for biologic augmentation in rotator cuff repair. Redirecting the long head of the biceps tendon can enhance its role as a humeral head depressor, limiting superior humeral head migration, and improving rotator cuff force couple and shoulder biomechanics. Incorporating the long head of the biceps tendon into the rotator cuff repair construct can enhance the repair by acting as a biologic internal brace, promoting load sharing and improving structural integrity. This technique may improve healing, repair durability, and functional outcomes, especially in large or revision tears with poor tissue quality.

The long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) plays a multifaceted role in shoulder biomechanics. In addition to its function as a primary forearm supinator and a secondary elbow flexor, the LHBT contributes to glenohumeral stability by acting as a dynamic humeral head depressor and anterior stabilizer. 1 , 2 , 3 This function becomes especially important in the setting of rotator cuff repair (RCR), where loss of the normal force couple can lead to superior migration of the humeral head. 3 , 4 Preserving and using the LHBT in such cases has gained interest for its potential to restore shoulder biomechanics and augment RCRs. 5 , 6

The concept of an “internal brace” has been described in various joints, including the ankle, elbow, and knee where suture tape constructs are used to protect healing ligaments by offloading stress during early motion. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 These constructs provide mechanical stability and serve as a scaffold for healing tissue. 8 , 10 Applying this concept to the shoulder, utilizing the LHBT as autologous tissue instead of synthetic material to augment the RCR introduces the idea of a biologic internal brace.

In this technical note, we describe a technique in which the LHBT is redirected and incorporated directly into the RCR construct. This method integrates the biceps tendon as a biologic augment, aiming to enhance both tendon healing and biomechanical properties.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning

The procedure is performed under general anesthesia, with an interscalene block for additional analgesia. The beach chair position is the surgeon's preferred approach for RCR, offering optimal access and visualization of the shoulder joint and subacromial space. Prior to final positioning, while the patient is still supine, an examination under anesthesia is conducted on the affected shoulder and compared with the contralateral side.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Portal Placement

Following standard sterile preparation and draping, the bony landmarks including the scapular spine, acromion, clavicle, and coracoid process are palpated and marked. PROCEDURE

Biceps Evaluation and Rerouting Technique

The surgical technique is shown in Video 1.

Biceps evaluation is performed via diagnostic arthroscopy through the posterior portal using a 30° scope. A blunt switching stick introduced through the superior anterolateral portal and rotator cuff tear is used to dynamically assess the biceps tendon and anchor for integrity, degeneration, tearing, or instability. If the tendon is deemed salvageable, it may be utilized as a biologic internal brace to augment RCR (Figure 2).

Salvageable biceps tendon despite shredding. Arthroscopic intra‐articular view of the left shoulder from the posterior portal using a 30° arthroscope showing (A) glenoid, (B) intact superior glenoid labrum, (C) intact biceps anchor, and (D) frayed but continuous, repairable biceps tendon suitable for incorporation into the rotator cuff repair.

The biceps tendon is initially tagged using the “Loop ‘N’ Tack” technique. 11 An arthroscopic grasper (Crab Claw, Arthrex, Naples, FL) or a suture passer (Scorpion Suture Passer; Arthrex, Naples, FL) loaded with a looped suture (TigerLink, FiberTape; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is used to pass and secure the looped end around the tendon. The free end is then retrogradely retrieved through the tendon using a suture passing device (Bird Beak; Arthrex, Naples, FL), securing the suture through the tendon (Figure 3).

The tagged biceps tendon is then transferred to the posterolateral portal to allow mobilization from the bicipital groove. A tenotomy is then performed approximately 0.5 cm distal to the tagging site, which can serve either as a definitive tenotomy or allow for subsequent supra/subpectoral biceps tenodesis, if preferred.

Incorporating the Biceps Tendon as Biologic Augmentation in RCR

Following subacromial debridement and rotator cuff mobilization, 2 triple‐loaded suture tape anchors (2.6 mm FiberTak; Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed at the greater tuberosity footprint near the articular surface to form the medial row. Four suture tapes (2 from each anchor) are passed through the rotator cuff using a Suture Lasso handoff technique. The 2 tapes closest to the biceps tendon (one from each anchor) are passed through the rotator cuff and then the biceps tendon, creating a mattress configuration (Figure 4). The biceps tendon tagging suture is incorporated and tensioned with the tapes in the lateral row, rerouting the tendon approximately 1 to 1.5 cm posterior to its native position in the bicipital groove, where it functions both as a humeral head depressor and a biologic internal brace to augment the repair (Figure 5). After all tapes are passed, they are crisscrossed and secured laterally with 3 anchors (4.75 mm SwiveLock Arthrex, Naples, FL), completing a double‐row modified expansion bridge repair, as previously described 12 (Figure 6). Postoperative Care

Following surgery, the arm is placed in a shoulder immobilizer in a neutral and slight abduction position to minimize stress on the repair and reduce the risk of internal rotation contracture. A modified conservative rehabilitation protocol for large or massive RCR is initiated within the first postoperative week, emphasizing patient education and passive range of motion exercises. Elbow, wrist, and finger motions are allowed immediately after surgery. In cases where biceps tenodesis is performed, active elbow flexion is restricted for the first 3 weeks. Transition to active‐assisted and active shoulder range of motion typically begins between 6 and 8 weeks postoperatively. Strengthening exercises are introduced at approximately 3 to 4 months, with a gradual return to full, unrestricted activity by 4 to 6 months.

DISCUSSION

Failure rates following large and massive RCRs remain a significant clinical challenge, with reported rates ranging widely from approximately 11% to 94%. 13 , 14 These variations are influenced by tear size and retraction, tendon quality, muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration, patient factors, and the repair technique employed. 13 , 14 This variability underscores the complexity of achieving durable healing in these extensive tears.

In recent years, augmentation of RCR using xenografts, human allografts, autografts, and synthetic materials has gained popularity as a strategy to improve structural integrity and biological healing. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Short‐ to midterm studies report promising clinical and imaging outcomes, suggesting these scaffolds can enhance tendon healing and reduce retear rates. 16 , 17 , 20 However, limitations persist, including graft availability, elevated costs, and potential adverse reactions, particularly inflammatory responses associated with xenografts and some synthetic materials. 21 , 22 These challenges have prompted ongoing investigation into alternative biologic rotator cuff augmentation options.

Emerging evidence has highlighted multiple roles for the LHBT in shoulder surgery beyond its traditional function. 4 , 6 , 23 Several studies have used the biceps tendon in RCR, primarily through side‐to‐side repair or partial superior capsule reconstruction techniques, suggesting potential benefits in RCR strength and healing. 2 , 4 , 5 However, these approaches typically use the LHBT as an adjunct rather than fully integrating it into the repair construct.

This study describes a technique in which the biceps tendon is incorporated directly into the RCR construct. This approach aims to create a more robust repair by acting as a biologic internal brace, offloading stress at the repair site thus promoting a more favorable healing environment. Additionally, by rerouting the biceps approximately 1 to 1.5 cm posterior to its native position within the bicipital groove, this technique may enhance the tendon's biomechanical role as a humeral head depressor and improve the overall rotator cuff force couple dynamics. Such biomechanical enhancement could translate to improved shoulder function and greater repair durability. Potential limitations of this technique include the risk of postoperative shoulder stiffness as well as persistent anterior shoulder pain related to the retained biceps tendon. Although these issues are uncommon, they should be discussed with patients preoperatively as part of shared decision‐making.

Incorporating the biceps tendon into the RCR construct may offer a viable and accessible biologic augmentation option that may improve RCR strength and healing potential. By serving as a biologic internal brace, this approach can mitigate limitations of other nonbiologic augmentation methods and offer potential advancement in the management of large and massive rotator cuff tears. Further clinical studies are warranted to evaluate long‐term outcomes and functional benefits of this technique.
