Different coffee consumption patterns affect HbA1c via propionic acid-producing gut microbiota
Authors: Zhaoming Cao, Yu An, Yage Du, Guangyi Xu, Jingya Wang, Yanhui Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the causal relationship between coffee consumption patterns, propionic acid-producing gut microbiota, and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels using Mendelian randomisation (MR). Genome-wide association study datasets from the UK Biobank and MiBioGen consortium were analysed, identifying a significant mediating pathway: unsweetened filter coffee consumption promotes Veillonella abundance, which in turn reduces HbA1c levels. The inverse variance weighted method served as the primary analytical approach, supported by sensitivity analyses to ensure robustness. These findings highlight the potential of unsweetened filter coffee as a dietary intervention to improve glycaemic control by modulating gut microbiota. This research provides novel insights into the coffee-intestinal microbiota-metabolism axis and offers practical guidance for diabetes management, emphasising the importance of coffee preparation methods and dietary habits in harnessing its therapeutic potential.Subject terms: Endocrine system and metabolic diseases, Nutrition, Quality of life

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the most prevalent and severe metabolic diseases globally,type 2 diabetes accounts for about 90% of all diabetics. According to the International Diabetes Federation, the number of diabetes patients worldwide exceeded 500 million by 2021, with projections reaching 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 20451. This trend poses a considerable challenge to global healthcare systems, as diabetes not only precipitates multiple complications, such as cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and retinopathy but also profoundly impacts patients’ quality of life. Therefore, achieving effective blood glucose control in diabetic patients is of paramount importance. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) has been widely recognized as a pivotal monitoring biomarker for diabetes management for a considerable period2–4. HbA1c is generated through non-enzymatic glycosylation reactions between hemoglobin in red blood cells and blood sugars (primarily glucose). This non-enzymatic reaction is continuous, gradual, and irreversible, resulting in HbA1c levels that reflect past, rather than current, blood glucose concentrations, regardless of factors such as fasting status, insulin administration, or hypoglycemic therapy. HbA1c concentration effectively reflects average blood glucose levels over the past 8–12 weeks5. HbA1c is comprised of HbA1a, HbA1b, and HbA1c isoforms, with HbA1c constituting approximately 70% of the total6. Owing to its stable structure properties, HbA1c is routinely utilized in clinical practice for monitoring diabetes management, aiding in the assessment of treatment efficacy and the prediction of complication risks. Therefore, maintaining HbA1c levels within reasonable ranges is crucial for the long-term health of diabetic patients.

In research on blood glucose management, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), specifically propionate, have garnered growing attention7–10. Recent clinical randomized controlled trials and basic experimental studies have found that propionate exerts significant effects on regulating mitigating systemic inflammation, and managing blood glucose levels. A study published in Nature Metabolism in 201911 clinically validated that propionate intake can significantly decrease fasting blood glucose levels and positively impact insulin sensitivity. Additionally, basic research has indicated that beneficial gut microbiota such as Veillonella12, Parabacteroides13, Akkermansia14, and Bifidobacterium15,16 can elicit anti-inflammatory and hypoglycemic effects through propionate production. However, whether the propionate-producing probiotics has a direct impact on on HbA1c remains uncertain, leaving ample space for further research.

Coffee stands as one of the most popular beverages globally, with its consumption increasing at an average annual rate of 1.05% between 2017 and 2022, ultimately reaching 10.0767 million tons in 202217. Meanwhile, numerous studies have confirmed coffee’s potential benefits in lowering blood glucose. For instance, a systematic review by Van Dam and Hu, published in JAMA18 indicated an inverse relationship between coffee consumption and the risk of type 2 diabetes, particularly among individuals with high coffee intake. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Carlstrom and Larsson, featured in Nutrition Reviews19, echoed this notion, revealing a dose-response relationship between coffee consumption and a decreased risk of type 2 diabetes. However, research findings concerning the benefits of coffee in lowering blood glucose have been inconsistent. For example, a study by Keijzers et al., published in Diabetes Care20 showed that caffeine may impair insulin sensitivity, thereby potentially nullifying its potential benefits for blood glucose regulation. Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial by Alperet et al., appearing in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition21, similarly failed to detect significant improvements in insulin sensitivity following coffee consumption. Moreover, studies have shown that certain coffee components, such as chlorogenic acid, may indirectly affect metabolic health by influencing gut microbiota composition and function22.

On the basis of this information, we hypothesize that the variability in coffee’s impact on blood glucose levels could be attributed to the variety of coffee consumption patterns (such as decaffeinated coffee, filtered coffee, milk-added instant coffee, etc.) and their interactions with beneficial gut microbiota. Nevertheless, comprehensive studies exploring the relationships between various coffee consumption patterns and specific gut probiotics, as well as their impact on blood glucose, remain scarce.

To overcome the constraints inherent in observational studies and randomized controlled trials, Mendelian Randomization (MR) has been introduced, employing genetic factors that are robustly associated with exposure as instrumental variables to assess causal relationships between the exposure and outcomes23. As an analytical approach that integrates genetic data, MR exhibits distinct advantages in elucidating causal relationships. Using genetic variants as instrumental variables, MR provides an effective method for exploring causal associations between exposure factors and outcomes. In contrast to traditional observational studies, which grapple with issues of reverse causation and confounding factors, MR studies can infer causality with relative robustness. MR research has become increasingly favored by researchers, especially in situations with constrained infrastructure or funding, owing to its straightforward methodology and compelling persuasiveness. This study aims to use MR to explore the associations between different coffee consumption patterns and HbA1c, assess the regulatory role of propionate-producing probiotics, and quantify the magnitude of their direct and indirect effects. This endeavor not only provides foundational data for future investigations but also provides guidance for promoting healthy lifestyle choices among the public.

Effect of different coffee consumption preferences on HbA1c levels

Among the six coffee consumption patterns examined, only filtered coffee demonstrated a statistically significant association with HbA1c levels. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) analysis revealed that filtered coffee consumption was significantly associated with decreased HbA1c levels (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, P = 0.04). For other consumption patterns, including sugar-added coffee (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.96–1.07, P = 0.64), artificial sweetener-added coffee (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.96–1.11, P = 0.41), milk-added instant coffee (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.95–1.05, P = 0.97), milk-added filtered coffee (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.93–1.04, P = 0.47), and decaffeinated coffee (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.92–1.07, P = 0.79), no statistically significant associations with HbA1c levels were observed (Table 3). Effect of different coffee consumption preferences on the abundance of propionic acid-producing probiotic gut microorganisms

According to the results of MR analysis, there were some differences in the effects of different coffee consumption preferences on four probiotic gut microorganisms capable of producing propionic acid, namely Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, Parabacteroides, and Veillonella. Mediating role of propionic acid-producing probiotic gut microbes between different coffee consumption preferences and glycosylated hemoglobin levels

Based on the mediation analysis, we identified a significant mediating role of Veillonella in the relationship between filtered coffee consumption and HbA1c levels. The analysis revealed that filtered coffee consumption was positively associated with the abundance of Veillonella (βa = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.03~0.49, P = 0.03), while Veillonella demonstrated a significant negative association with HbA1c levels (βb = -0.05, 95% CI: -0.10~0.00, P < 0.01). The indirect effect, calculated as the product of paths a and b, was statistically significant (βc’ = -0.013*, 95% CI: -0.02~-0.01, P < 0.01). Importantly, the direct effect of filtered coffee on HbA1c levels remained significant (βc = -0.03, OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.943–0.99, P = 0.04), indicating a partial mediation effect where the indirect effect through Veillonella accounts for 43.33% of the total effect (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The present study identified an important mediating pathway through a two-step, two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis: filter coffee reduces HbA1c levels by promoting Veillonella abundance. Specifically, filter coffee intake was significantly and positively associated with Veillonella abundance (βa = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.92-0.98, P < 0.01), whereas Veillonella was significantly and negatively associated with HbA1c levels (βb = -0.05, 95% CI: 0.923–0.98, P < 0.01). The indirect effect calculated by the product of paths a and b was statistically significant (βc’=-0.013, 95% CI: -0.023–0.01, P < 0.01). Notably, the direct effect of filtered coffee on HbA1c levels remained significant (βc = -0.03, OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.943–0.99, P = 0.04), suggesting that this was a partially mediated effect, with the indirect effect via Veillonella accounting for 43.33% of the total effect. This finding provides a new perspective for understanding the regulatory effects of coffee on glucose metabolism.

Firstly, we found that all four propionic acid-producing probiotics (Veillonella, Parabacteroides, Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium) included in the present study had a direct modulatory effect on HbA1c levels and all were negatively correlated. This is in line with previous studies, where a 12-month prospective cohort study conducted by Depommier et al.24 showed that diabetic patients with higher abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila at baseline had a more significant reduction in HbA1c levels (HR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.65-0.94). A study by Plovier et al.25 in published in Nature Medicine further demonstrated that purified Akkermansia muciniphila membrane proteins significantly improved metabolic status in obese and diabetic mice. In addition recent studies have shown that Akkermansia improves insulin resistance by enhancing intestinal barrier function, reducing endotoxin translocation and systemic inflammatory response14. For Bifidobacterium, several clinical trials have confirmed its hypoglycemic effect. a randomized controlled trial by Kobyliak et al.26 showed that oral Bifidobacterium supplementation for 3 months significantly reduced HbA1c levels in diabetic patients by an average of 0.54% (p < 0.05). In addition Bifidobacterium16 can produce short-chain fatty acids through fermentation of dietary fiber, increase GLP-1 secretion and inhibit the mechanism of action of pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Pan et al.13 found that Parabacteroides distasonis can significantly enhance insulin sensitivity by increasing the production of succinic acid and secondary bile acids Zhang et al.27 showed that Parabacteroides could also improve metabolic status by regulating glucocorticoid-induced disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism.The hypoglycemic mechanism of Veillonella has been more thoroughly studied than the other three gut microorganisms, and in 2014, it was found that Veillonella could promote GLP-1 secretion through activating G protein-coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43, improving the mechanism of pancreatic β-cell function28,29.

One of the more notable findings of this study was that filter coffee was the only coffee consumption pattern that significantly reduced HbA1c levels (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.943–0.99, P = 0.04). This finding is consistent with the results of a systematic review by Carlstrom and Larsson published in Nutrition Reviews19, who found that filtered coffee was associated with a dose-response relationship with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes. A recent study published in Nature Microbiology30 further demonstrated significant alterations in the composition of the intestinal flora in those who regularly consumed filter coffee (≥3 cups/day), with a significant increase in the abundance of propionic acid-producing bacteria. This unique effect may be closely related to the preparation method and compositional characteristics of filter coffee. Filtered coffee retains higher levels of chlorogenic acid and other polyphenols compared to other coffee preparations, while the filtration process removes coffee lipids (diterpenes found in coffee beans), and these compositional differences may be key to the different effects. A study by Hong et al.31 found that filtered coffee contained significantly higher levels of chlorogenic acid, which has been shown to increase the intestinal flora via the AMPK signaling pathway to increase skeletal muscle glucose uptake. It has also been found that polyphenols in filter coffee can regulate the intestinal flora through multiple pathways: first, they can enhance the antioxidant capacity of intestinal epithelial cells by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway, which provides a favorable environment for the colonization of beneficial bacteria32; second, these compounds can selectively promote the growth and metabolic activity of propionic acid-producing bacteria27; and third, they can optimize the growth and metabolic activity of propionic acid-producing bacteria by adjusting intestinal pH and the redox environment. redox environment, optimizing the survival conditions of propionic acid-producing bacteria. As for the finding that other coffee consumption patterns did not have a significant effect on HbA1c levels, this is broadly in line with the results of previous studies. van Dam et al.22, in a NEJM review, suggested that the addition of ingredients such as sugar and creamer may counteract the potential benefits of coffee by altering the intestinal microenvironment. Recent studies have shed further light on this complex interaction. A large number of existing studies point out that the addition of dairy products significantly alters the bioavailability of coffee polyphenols, and that interactions between phenolic compounds in coffee and milk proteins may be the main reason for the negative impact of milk on coffee functionality33, but this is less discussed in the literature at present, and in particular the role of the milk fat globules and their membranes has not been fully explored. The present study did not find a significant effect of sugar-containing coffee on hemoglobin, but this does not mean that sugar-containing coffee cannot influence glycolipid metabolic processes; a prospective cohort study conducted by researchers at Harvard University on which substance in coffee is the key factor in the promotion of weight loss demonstrated that unsweetened coffee, with or without caffeine, did not affect its weight loss effect, whereas the addition of sugar reduced this weight loss effect34.

The coffee-Veillonella-HbA1c regulatory axis identified in this study has important clinical translational value. Recent chemical analyses have shown that filtered coffee retains significantly higher levels of bioactive substances, including chlorogenic acid at 3.23–4.5 mg/mL and caffeoylquinic acid at 1.83–2.6 mg/mL31. These polyphenolic compounds regulate intestinal microecology through multiple mechanisms. Toydemir et al.35 confirmed that coffee polyphenols can enhance the antioxidant capacity of intestinal epithelial cells through the nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway, upregulate MUC2 gene expression, promote mucus secretion, and create a suitable microecological environment for Veillonella colonization through a human intestinal epithelial cell model. The lactate metabolism pathway, which is unique to Veillonella, converts lactate to propionic acid36, which improves β-cell function through activation of G protein-coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43, increasing intracellular calcium ion concentration, and promoting the cytosolic action of insulin-secreting granules32.

Based on the results of this study, we offer several important recommendations for clinical practice. First, moderate consumption of unsweetened filter coffee is recommended because studies have shown that the bioactive substances retained in it, especially chlorogenic acid and other polyphenolic compounds, can effectively promote the growth of beneficial intestinal flora. This type of consumption not only preserves the beneficial components of coffee, but also maximizes its modulatory effect on the intestinal flora. Second, the findings emphasize the importance of avoiding adding sugar or high-fat milk to coffee. These additives not only increase calorie intake, but more importantly may inhibit the metabolic activity of propionic acid-producing bacteria, thereby diminishing the health benefits of coffee. For those who are accustomed to adding sugar or milk to their coffee, it is recommended to gradually reduce the amount added and eventually transition to drinking plain coffee. At the same time, joint supplementation with prebiotics or dietary fiber can be considered, as these nutrients can further promote the growth of propionic acid-producing bacteria and enhance the metabolic regulatory effects of coffee. Finally, for patients with low diversity of intestinal flora or metabolic disorders, personalized dietary interventions under the guidance of a physician are recommended. This intervention should take into account the patient’s specific situation, including the degree of coffee tolerance, existing dietary habits, and metabolic status, to develop an individually appropriate coffee consumption program. At the same time, regular monitoring of blood glucose levels and intestinal flora is recommended for timely adjustment of the intervention strategy to achieve the best therapeutic effect.

The present study elucidates the potential molecular mechanisms by which coffee consumption affects glucose metabolism, but several methodological limitations remain. The first limitation stems from the nature of the source of the study data: analyses based on the existing GWAS database mainly reflect the genetic characteristics of populations of European descent, and this limitation of population representativeness not only affects the extrapolation of the results, but may also mask race-specific metabolic modulation patterns resulting from the interaction of genetic polymorphisms, epigenetic modifications, and environmental factors. Second, this study focused only on the mediating effects of four specific propionic acid-producing genera, failing to comprehensively assess the dynamic balance of the intestinal microecosystem and its synergistic network of metabolomes. In addition, the study failed to establish an accurate dose-effect relationship model, and the modulatory effects of different coffee preparation processes and intake doses on the composition of intestinal flora and their metabolic activities still need to be further quantified. The inherent limitations of the cross-sectional study design also constrained the in-depth analysis of the temporal evolution of microbial communities and their metabolic interactions with the host.

Based on the above limitations, future research should focus on the following aspects: firstly, there is an urgent need to carry out multi-center and large-scale population cohort studies to explore the regulatory effects of coffee intake on intestinal flora and glucose metabolism in different racial backgrounds through stratified analysis. Second, it is recommended to integrate multi-omics technology platforms, including but not limited to macro-genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics and proteomics, to build a systematic molecular network to comprehensively analyze the regulatory mechanisms of coffee bioactive components on the intestinal microecosystem. Particular attention needs to be paid to changes in short-chain fatty acid profiles, remodeling of bile acid metabolism, and signal transduction pathways in the microbial-host metabolic axis. In addition, a standardized dose-effect assessment system should be established to systematically study the effects of different coffee preparation processes and intake patterns on the ecological niche and metabolic phenotype of intestinal flora, so as to provide a scientific basis for the development of precise nutritional intervention strategies.

In conclusion, this study provides a new theoretical framework for elucidating the molecular mechanism of coffee regulation of glucose metabolism, but more in-depth mechanistic studies are still needed to improve the existing knowledge. Future studies should adopt a multidimensional and systematic research strategy to deeply analyze the regulatory network of the coffee-intestinal flora-host metabolism axis, so as to provide a more comprehensive theoretical basis and intervention targets for the prevention and treatment of metabolic diseases.

Data Organization

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci associated with HbA1c and six coffee consumption preferences, retrieved from the IEU Open GWAS project (mrcieu.ac.uk), were utilized for the targeted GWAS. To maintain an adequate number of instrumental variables (IVs) in mendelian randomization analysis, we selected P < 5 × 10-6 as our significance threshold. Independent SNPs (R2 < 0.001, distance >10000 kb) were identified and employed as instrumental variables. The European Thousand Genomes Project’s gene-wide information was referenced to assess linkage disequilibrium (LD) among these SNP loci. To ensure that the included SNP loci were associated with outcomes only through exposure factors, each SNP locus was screened using the PhenoScanner database, and the association hypothesis was further verified by calculating the F-value to assess whether there was a weak instrumental variable bias in the selected instrumental variables.

Statistical methods

The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the main analytical method, while supplementary methods included MR Egger regression37, the weighted median, weighted mode38, and robust adjusted curve score (MR-RAPS)39, all aimed at evaluating the causal link between exposure factors and outcomes. The IVW method is characterized by its regressions models, which omit the intercept term and utilize the inverse of the outcome variance as weights. This method yields the most precise results when all included SNP loci are all valid instrumental variables40. Sensitivity analyses were performed by heterogeneity tests and multiple validity assessments. For SNP loci meeting the three hypotheses, the Cochran’s Q test was employed to assess heterogeneity among individual genetic variants. The MR Egger intercept method was utilized to detect violations of MR hypotheses due to horizontal pleiotropy. Sensitivity was tested using the leave-one-out approach to identify potential outliers among the final SNP loci. The stability of funnel plots was examined for asymmetries, and MR PRESSO was applied to detect outliers and assess their impact on the results.

The mediated share (or mediation proportion) represents the percentage of the total effect that is explained through the mediator pathway. The mediated share is calculated as:Mediated Share = (Indirect Effect ÷ Total Effect) × 100%
