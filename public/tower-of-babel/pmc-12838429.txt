Comparison of the measurement properties of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 among patients with diabetes in China
Authors: Meixuan Li, Shitong Xie, Tuoer Li, Wenbo He, Yanfei Li, Xu Hui, Yanan Wu, Fuxing Shi, Jing Wu, Jinhui Tian, Xiuxia Li, Kehu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo validate and compare the measurement properties of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 among patients with diabetes in China.MethodsA quota sampling method was used to recruit a representative sample, social-demographic characteristics, self-reported EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 responses were collected through online survey. Test–retest reliability and the agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 utility values were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and the Bland–Altman plot. Hypotheses testing including convergent validity and known-group validity were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation and effect sizes, respectively. Sensitivity was compared using relative efficiency and receiver operating characteristic.ResultsA total of 600 respondents (53.67% male; mean age 60 years; 90% type 2, 10% type 1 diabetes) were included in this study. A higher ceiling effect was observed for EQ-5D-5L than for SF-6Dv2 (10.5% vs. 0.5%). The mean (SD) utility was 0.771 (0.196) for EQ-5D-5L and 0.671 (0.153) for SF-6Dv2. Agreement between the two instruments was good with an ICC of 0.776. The ICC values for the test-retest analysis were 0.982 for EQ-5D-5L and 0.986 for SF-6Dv2 among the subgroup (N = 100). The Spearman’s rank correlation (range: 0.312–0.661) indicated an acceptable convergent validity between the dimensions of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2. Hypotheses for known-group validity were fulfilled in both instruments. The EQ-5D-5L showed basically equivalent discriminative capacities with the SF-6Dv2 (ES: 0.92–2.08 vs. 1.03–2.05). The SF-6Dv2 had 14.7-445.8% higher efficiency than the EQ-5D-5L at revealing differences in self-reported health status, except for the group dichotomized by “very good, good, fair or bad versus very bad”.ConclusionsEQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 showed generally comparable reliability, validity, and sensitivity in this sample of Chinese patients with diabetes. However, the utility values derived from the two instruments differed, indicating that they may not be used interchangeably. Further research is needed to evaluate and compare their responsiveness.Keywords: Health-related quality of life, EQ-5D-5L, SF-6Dv2, Measurement properties, Diabetes

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the major public health challenges [1]. The global prevalence of diabetes has been increasing over recent decades [2, 3]. The latest estimate from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) showed that in 2021 there were 537 million (age 20–79 years) people with diabetes worldwide. It is expected to increase to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045, with a prevalence rate of 12.2% [2]. People with diabetes have an increased risk of developing many serious and life-threatening co-morbidities and complications resulting in higher medical care costs, reduced quality of life and increased mortality [2, 4–7]. In 2021, the global diabetes related health expenditure is estimated to be 966 billion US dollars, which is expected to reach 1.03 trillion US dollars by 2030 and 1.05 trillion US dollars by 2045 [2].

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a multidimensional concept that integrates information on people’ physical, mental and social health to reflect their health status living with specific conditions [8]. HRQoL measures can be categorized as either non-preference based or preference-based measures. Preference-based HRQOL measures are commonly used to generate quality-of-life weights for calculating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which is currently a recommended outcome measure to inform drug coverage or reimbursement decisions in many countries [9–11].

The EQ-5D and the Short Form Six-Dimension (SF-6D) are the two most frequently used preference-based HRQoL instruments [12] and are recommended as the standard instruments in the application of health economics evaluation in many countries [13–15]. The EQ-5D has two versions, and the EQ-5D-5 L is generally preferred due to its superior measurement properties, as demonstrated in both general population and the therapeutic contexts [16, 17]. The measurement properties of the EQ-5D and SF-6D have been validated and compared in the general population and various patient sets [18–35], of which only one study compared the measurement properties of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv1 among adults with type 2 diabetes in Canada [23]. This study concluded that both EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv1 had valid measurement performance in diabetes population. Nevertheless, it should be noted that this study had not compared the test-retest reliability, an important psychometric property of HRQoL instruments. More importantly, evidence evaluating the measurement properties of the EQ-5D and SF-6D in the Chinese diabetes populations is still lacking. As far as we know, no studies have validated and compared the measurement properties of the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 among patients with diabetes in China.

This study aimed to validate and compare the measurement properties of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 in Chinese diabetes population.

Methods

This study followed the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) [36]. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Institute of Health Data Science of Lanzhou University (Ethics review number: HDS-202309-02). Informed consent was obtained from all respondents.

Study sample

Study samples were recruited from North, Northeast, East, Central, South, Southwest and Northwest of China respectively according to the prevalence of diabetes in these seven geographical regions. A stratified sampling method was used, in which five quotas were set for sex, age, type of diabetes (type 1 and type 2), with/without complications, and area of residence (urban and rural), to make sure that these distributions of the sample resembled those of the Chinese diabetes population. Respondents were also required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) participants were diagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes patients; (2) No age limit; (3) having Chinese nationality, and having lived in China for the past five years; (4) Literacy, able to read text from a computer or mobile phone screen, and able to complete questionnaires on their own; (5) No diseases such as dementia that restrict cognitive function; (6) Provide informed consent.

There was currently no consensus on sample size calculation for instrument development and validation [37]. Empirical rules indicated that the sample size for psychometric validation was at least 10 times the total number of items included in the instrument [38]. The COSMIN recommended measurement properties validation with a sample size of 5–7 times the total number of instrument items and ≥ 100 [36]. This study considered meeting the above two criteria and included 600 valid samples.

Data collection

Data used in study were collected from an online survey conducted between December 2023 and January 2024. We first imported the designed survey questionnaire into the WJX online survey system, and then distributed the online questionnaire links to potential respondents who meet the inclusion criteria to collect data.

The design of the survey questionnaire consisted of three parts. The first part included participants screening questions and an informed consent form (6 items). Participants first read the informed consent statement and chose whether to participate in the survey. The screening items covered diabetes type, nationality, duration of residence in China, literacy, and cognitive ability, ensuring that only eligible respondents could proceed to the full survey. The second part collected sociodemographic information (16 items), including age, gender, ethnicity, height, weight, area of residence, education level, marital status, employment status, personal monthly income, medical insurance type, family history of diabetes, duration of diabetes, risk factors, complications and current treatment methods; The third part was the health-related questions (13 items) including a 5-level categorized self-reported health status (very good, good, fair, bad, very bad), SF-6Dv2 and EQ-5D-5L.

To evaluate the test-retest reliability of both instruments, a subset of the respondents (target N = 100) was recruited. Following the first survey, we randomly sent the survey link to the previous respondents again asked for the respondents’ consent to be online surveyed again (retest). The interval between the test and retest was set as two weeks [23, 35]. During the retest survey, respondents completed the same process as in the first survey. In the retest survey, the respondents were asked the question “Have there been any changes in your health status compared with the last survey?” and rated on a 5-level Likert scale (“no change”, “slightly change”, “some change”, “much change”, or “extremely change”). The respondents who reported “no change” was considered to have relatively stable health over the two tests and included in the test-retest data analysis [36, 37].

Data quality control was conducted by checking the answer pattern, answer logic, and monitoring the answer time. Respondents were excluded if (1) their answers clearly followed a certain pattern (e.g. level 1 was selected for all questions); (2) they completed EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 less than five seconds, respectively; (3) their answers existed obvious logical issues.

HRQoL instruments

EQ-5D-5 L was a generic, preference-based instrument with five dimensions, including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension used five-level response options (no, mild, moderate, severe, and extreme problems). Another part of EQ-5D-5 L was the Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS), which was a vertical line with an endpoint of 0 for the “worst health condition” and 100 for the “best health condition“ [39, 40]. 3,125 unique health states can be generated by the EQ-5D-5 L, with 11,111 being the best health state (full health), and 55,555 the worst health state. Chinese EQ-5D-5 L utility value sets ranged from − 0.391 (55555) to 1 (11111) [40].

SF-6Dv2 was an updated version of the generic instrument SF-6Dv1. The descriptive system of SF-6Dv2 had six dimensions, including physical functioning, role limitation, social functioning, pain, mental health, and vitality. Six-level response options was set for the pain dimension, while five-level for the other dimensions [41, 42]. The descriptive system of SF-6Dv2 can define 18,750 (= 5*5*5*6*5*5) unique health states [43]. Chinese SF-6Dv2 utility value sets ranged from − 0.277 (555655) to 1 (111111) [42].

This study used validated Chinese versions of EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 [34, 44].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Utility values of the two instruments as well as the characteristics of the respondents were described using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were shown as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented in the number and proportion of the sample within each group. The distribution of response levels across each EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 dimension was reported using histograms.

Agreement

The degree of agreement between utility values of EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland–Altman plot. The ICC was computed with the two-way mixed-effects model based on absolute agreement [45]. The ICC was classified into four levels of agreements as poor agreement (ICC < 0.50), moderate agreement (0.50 ≤ ICCs < 0.75), good agreement (0.75 ≤ ICCs < 0.90), and excellent agreement (ICCs ≥ 0.90) [46, 47].

Bland-Altman plot was presented as a scatter plot in which the x-axis represented mean utility values of EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2, while the y-axis showed the difference between the two instruments [48]. To provide more robust limits of agreement (LoA), bootstrap resampling with 2,000 iterations was performed to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) around the mean difference and LoA. In addition, the range of ± 0.10 was considered the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for health utility values, serving as a clinically meaningful reference for agreement interpretation.

Measurement properties of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2

We evaluated ceiling and floor effects, test-retest reliability, convergent validity, known-group validity, and sensitivity all of which are crucial for evaluating the measurement properties of preference-based measures.

Ceiling and floor effects. The ceiling and floor effects of the instruments were evaluated by calculating the proportion of participants reporting the best and worst health status, respectively. The ceiling and floor effects were considered to exist if more than 15% of the respondents achieved either extreme end of the instrument [49].

Test-retest reliability. The test-retest reliability of the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 was evaluated using ICC, which was computed with the two-way mixed-effects model based on absolute agreement. ICC value above 0.7 was considered as satisfactory reliability [50].

Convergent validity. Spearman’s rank coefficient (r) were used to assess the association between the dimensions of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2. As recommended by COSMIN, r ≥ 0.5 represents strong correlation, 0.3 ≤ r < 0.5 moderate correlation, and r < 0.3 weak correlation [51]. Higher correlations were hypothesized between conceptually similar dimensions across the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 since they attempt to capture the same aspects of health, for example, EQ-5D-5 L pain/discomfort and SF-6Dv2 pain, as well as EQ-5D-5 L anxiety/depression and SF-6Dv2 mental health dimension should be highly correlated.

Known-group validity. Known group validity was assessed to confirm the hypothesized differences in utility scores among subgroups that are theoretically expected to differ [52]. This study hypothesized that diabetes respondents with poor self-reported health status or lower EQ VAS scores, would have lower utility values [53]. We classified the EQ VAS scores into five groups, namely < 60, 60 to 69, 70 to 79, 80 to 89, and 90 to 100 [21, 34]. Because utility data did not fully satisfy the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, subgroup differences in the utility values of the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 were analyzed using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Besides, the discriminative capacity of the two instruments was estimated using effect sizes (ES), calculated as Cohen’s d, defined as the difference between the mean utility value of two sub-groups divided by the pooled standard deviation [54]. For polytomous variables, the effect size between the extreme subgroups (e.g., self-reported very good health state subgroup and self-reported very poor health state subgroup) was calculated. An ES value of 0.20 is defined as small, 0.50 as medium, and 0.80 as large [54]. In this study, the term discriminative capacity refers to the ability of an instrument to distinguish between groups of respondents differing in self-reported health status, rather than in comparison to an external gold standard.

Sensitivity. The efficiency of the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 to detect differences in self-reported health status was compared using the relative efficiency (RE). RE was calculated via the ratio of the square of t-statistics from the t-tests of the comparator instrument (SF-6Dv2) over that of the reference instrument (EQ-5D-5 L) [32, 35]. A RE value of 1 indicates SF-6Dv2 and EQ-5D-5 L have same efficiency at detecting differences, RE greater than 1 indicates SF-6Dv2 are more sensitive than the EQ-5D-5 L, while RE lower than 1 means the opposite [53]. Besides, the sensitivity of the two instruments was also assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [55], and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to compare the discriminative power of the two instruments [56]. The instrument with the larger AUC is thought to be more sensitive at detecting differences, an AUC score of 1.0 indicates that the instruments would have excellent discriminative ability, whereas an AUC score of 0.5 indicates instruments with no discriminative capacity [39]. The respondents’ self-reported health status was divided into four categories: (1) very good versus good, fair, bad, or very bad, (2) very good or good versus fair, bad, or very bad, (3) very good, good, or fair versus bad or very bad, (4) very good, good, fair or bad versus very bad. All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 29.0 software. All reported statistical tests were performed two-sided with a significance level of 0.05.

A total of 90.00% (N = 540) of the participants had type 2 diabetes, and 10% (N = 60) had type 1 diabetes. The mean (SD) duration of diabetes was 40.38 (56.46) months.

Agreement

The mean (SD) EQ-5D-5L utility value among the total sample was 0.771 (0.196), ranging from 0.023 to 1, and the mean SF-6Dv2 utility was 0.671 (SD = 0.153), ranging from 0.033 to 1. The agreement between EQ-5D-5L and SF-6D was good, with ICC of 0.776 (95%CI 0.723–0.817). The Bland–Altman plot (Fig. 1) demonstrated that the mean difference in utility values between these two instruments was 0.091, indicating that the EQ-5D-5L yielded slightly higher utility values than the SF-6Dv2 on average.

Measurement properties of the EQ‑5D‑5L and SF‑6Dv2

Ceiling and floor effects. The distribution of responses to the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 were presented in Figs. 2 and 3. Although neither instrument exceeded the conventional 15% threshold for defining a substantial ceiling or floor effect, the EQ-5D-5L exhibited a noticeably higher ceiling proportion (10.5%) compared with the SF-6Dv2 (0.5%), indicating a relatively greater clustering of respondents at the upper end of the EQ-5D-5L scale. No respondent reported the worst health state for both instruments.

Test-retest reliability. A total of 100 respondents provided valid test-retest data. As shown in Table 1, the majority of the respondents were male (54.0%), mean (SD) age of 59.75 (13.08) years. Both instruments showed good test-retest reliability. For the EQ-5D-5L, the ICC was 0.982 (95% CI 0.973, 0.988). For the SF-6Dv2, the ICC was 0.986 (95% CI 0.980, 0.991).

Convergent validity. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between the dimensions of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 ranged from 0.312 to 0.661 (p < 0.001); As expected, the EQ-5D-5L pain/discomfort dimension was strongly correlated with the SF-6Dv2 pain dimension (r = 0.607), and the EQ-5D-5L anxiety/depression dimension was highly correlated with the SF-6Dv2 mental health dimension (r = 0.661). Other dimension pairs demonstrated weak-to-moderate correlations, suggesting that these two instruments capture overlapping but not identical constructs of health status (Table 2).

Sensitivity. As shown in Table 4, the SF-6Dv2 had 14.7-445.8% higher efficiency than the EQ-5D-5L in revealing differences between self-reported health status groups dichotomized by “very good versus good, fair, bad, or very bad”, “very good or good versus fair, bad, or very bad”, and “very good, good, or fair versus bad or very bad”, while the EQ-5D-5L was found to be 76.2% more efficient than the SF-6Dv2 at detecting differences between self-reported health status groups dichotomized by “very good, good, fair or bad versus very bad”. The AUC values of both SF-6Dv2 and EQ-5D-5L were above 0.5 with statistically significant differences (p < 0.01) (Table 4). The SF-6Dv2 generated higher AUC scores than the EQ-5D-5L, indicating a possible sensitivity superiority.

Discussion

Systematically evaluating and comparing the measurement properties of health utility instruments is crucial for utility measurement in economic evaluation. As far as we know, this is the first study to compare the response patterns, utility values, and measurement properties of the EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 in a Chinese diabetes population. Overall, we found that EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 showed good agreement and comparable reliability, validity, and sensitivity when used in the Chinese diabetes population.

In this study, health utilities for patients with diabetes measured by the EQ-5D-5 L tend to be slightly higher than the SF-6Dv2, with a mean difference of 0.091. This finding was consistent with the study compared EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv1 in type 2 diabetes [23] and some other previous studies [21, 31, 53]. Possible reasons may be the discrepancy in the dimensions, and the recall period between the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv1 [53]. The ICC of EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 utility values was greater than 0.7, and Bland-Altman plot showed that about 96% of differences fell within 2 SDs of the mean difference, indicating good agreement between the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2. Similar findings were reported in other populations including patients with mental health, HIV/AIDS, asthma, colorectal Cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [57–60]. However, our findings were inconsistent with the results of one study comparing EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv1 in people with type 2 diabetes, in which the agreement of EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv1 was not good with ICC less than 0.7. This may be due to the fact that our study used the Chinese versions of EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2, but in that study, the SF-6Dv1 values set were derived in the UK and the EQ-5D-5 L used Canadian preferences.

While no floor effects were observed for either the EQ-5D-5 L or SF-6Dv2. The higher ceiling proportion observed for the EQ-5D-5 L compared with the SF-6Dv2 suggests that the EQ-5D-5 L may be less sensitive in differentiating between respondents with mild health problems, particularly in populations with relatively good health status. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that the SF-6Dv2 tends to produce lower ceiling effects due to its larger number of response levels and different domain weighting structure [21, 34, 35].

Regarding reliability, both the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 were demonstrated to have an acceptable test-retest reliability, with ICC greater than 0.98, implying these two instruments have ability to produce reproducible results from diabetes patients if the instrument was used repeatedly within a short period of time. The SF-6Dv2 (ICC = 0.986) performed slightly better than EQ-5D-5 L (0.982), similar findings were reported in previous studies [53, 61].

For convergent validity, as assumed, the EQ-5D-5 L pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression dimensions were strongly correlated with the SF-6Dv2 pain and mental health dimension. However, the remaining dimensions demonstrated weak-to-moderate associations, indicating that the two instruments share overlapping but not identical constructs of health. One explanation for this might be that the EQ-5D-5 L includes four of the five items for evaluating physical health, while the SF-6Dv2 includes an equal number of questions for mental and physical health. Our results are in line with earlier research [23, 30, 62], suggesting that patients with more physical issues should use the EQ-5D-5 L than those with psychological or mental issues.

In terms of known-group validity, the hypotheses for all tested groups were fulfilled, indicating that both the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 were able to discriminate between respondents with different levels of self-reported health status and EQ VAS scores. The effect size (ES) values, ranging from approximately 0.9 to 2.1, suggest that both instruments can effectively distinguish between extreme health states among individuals with diabetes. These findings imply that the EQ-5D-5 L and SF-6Dv2 are suitable for identifying broad health gradients in diabetic populations. Nevertheless, future research may further explore whether disease-specific or hybrid preference-based measures can provide greater sensitivity in detecting subtle variations in health-related quality of life [63].

As for sensitivity, the SF-6Dv2 had 14.7-445.8% higher efficiency than the EQ-5D-5 L at revealing differences in self-reported health status, except for the group dichotomized by “very good, good, fair or bad versus very bad”. The SF-6Dv2 generated higher AUC scores than the EQ-5D-5 L, indicating a possible sensitivity superiority. This may be also related to the differences in the recall period, and the descriptive system between the two instruments. These results are in line with earlier research that found that both instruments are sensitive to distinct groups and compared the EQ-5D-5 L with the SF-6Dv1 or SF-6Dv2 in both the general population and patients with various other sorts of disease [32, 34, 53, 64, 65].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the online survey format may have influenced the representativeness and validity of the data. Although eligibility screening and strict quality control procedures were implemented to enhance data reliability, participants recruited through online platforms may differ from the broader diabetes population in demographic or clinical characteristics. Second, the relatively small number of participants with type 1 diabetes (n = 60) prevented separate analyses of measurement properties by diabetes type. As a result, the measurement properties evaluated in this study may not fully reflect potential differences between patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to address this gap. Finally, we were unable to examine and compare the responsiveness of EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 due to the cross-sectional design. Longitudinal studies are required to determine how well these instruments detect changes in health status over time.

Conclusions

EQ-5D-5L and SF-6Dv2 demonstrated generally comparable reliability, validity, and sensitivity among Chinese patients with diabetes. Although the two instruments showed acceptable agreement, differences in utility values indicate that they may not be used interchangeably. Further research, particularly longitudinal studies, is needed to evaluate and compare their responsiveness.
