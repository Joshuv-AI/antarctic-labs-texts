Abstract
GHZ2 is among the highest-redshift galaxies discovered to date, exhibiting a spectrum rich with prominent emission lines in the rest-frame ultraviolet (UV) and optical. These features raise critical questions about the mechanism powering this nebular emission, in particular the extremely strong C IV λ \lambda 1548 emission (rest-frame EW = = 45 Å). Here we aim to quantify the AGN contribution within this system using the BEAGLE -AGN tool to simultaneously fit the spectrum and photometry of GHZ2. We consider a range of models with and without AGN components, allowing us to disentangle the stellar and AGN contribution of GHZ2 for the first time. We conclude that a partial contribution by an AGN is significantly favored based on the Bayes factor comparison to models without an AGN component, measuring an AGN contribution of 54 − 1 + 1 {}^{+1}_{-1} % and 26 − 2 + 4 {}^{+4}_{-2} % for the C IV λ \lambda 1548 and C III ] λ \lambda 1908 emission lines, respectively. We obtain an estimate for the black hole mass using the accretion luminosity ( L a ​ c ​ c L_{acc} ) from the best fit BEAGLE -AGN model, computing a value of log 10 (M BH /M ⊙ ) = 7.20 − 0.04 + 0.04 {}^{+0.04}_{-0.04} , for an Eddington ratio of η \eta = 0.5 (with a systematic uncertainty of ∼ \sim 1 dex). The inferred black hole mass to stellar mass ratio is 0.05 − 0.02 + 0.02 {}^{+0.02}_{-0.02} , consistent with other high redshift AGN systems. If the black hole interpretation is confirmed, GHZ2 would represent the most distant black hole identified to date, making it an ideal laboratory to study AGN growth and their role in shaping high-redshift galactic evolution.



I
Introduction

Since the launch of
JWST
, the frontier of what is measurable for galactic evolution in the early universe has undergone a paradigm shift.
JWST
is finding evidence that accreting black holes (BH) exist at earlier times and at masses higher than what was expected, given the lack of time that has elapsed at high redshift. Spectroscopic follow-up of high redshift galaxies have also shown that many of these systems host an active galactic nuclei (AGN), as evidenced through the presence of broad Balmer emission
(
Kokorev et al. 2023
;
Larson et al. 2023
;
Maiolino et al. 2024a
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
;
Taylor et al. 2024
;
Taylor et al. 2025
, e.g.)
, implying that high redshift black holes may be playing a larger role in the evolution of galaxies than previously thought. Studying systems that harbor an AGN requires careful analysis and modeling to decouple the AGN contribution from the stellar contribution. These ‘composite’ systems often reside in a region of parameter space where star forming (SF) galaxies and AGN overlap in diagnostic line-ratio diagrams, which makes determining the main ionization mechanism difficult, some noticeable ones are UHZ1 and GHZ9
(
Bogdán et al. 2024
;
Napolitano et al. 2024
;
Kovács et al. 2024
)
.

A complicating factor when using locally calibrated line ratios to determine the ionization source at high redshift is that the diagnostics that well-separate AGN and star-formation lose their classification power at higher redshift. For example, low metallicity AGN can fall in the SF region of common line ratio diagrams
(
Backhaus et al. 2022
;
Cleri et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. A compounding challenge is that the emission lines which most-accurately differentiate between AGN and star formation are often detected only at low significance, or are out of the spectral range of the instruments. Thus, traditional line ratio diagnostics struggle to discern whether the radiation is dominated by stars or AGN in these composite systems
(
Übler et al. 2023
;
Castellano et al. 2024
;
Scholtz et al. 2025
, see)
. However, tools are now being developed which can model the full spectra of galaxies, constraining both an AGN and stellar component, such as

-AGN
(
Vidal-García et al. 2024
;
Silcock et al. 2025
)
and
CIGALE

(
Boquien et al. 2019
;

)

An exceptional laboratory to test these tools is a galaxy at redshift 12.34, GLASS-z12 (GHZ2), discovered through data collected as part of the GLASS survey
(
Castellano et al. 2022
;
Naidu et al. 2022
;
Treu et al. 2022

III
Methods

Decoupling AGN and stellar components requires simultaneous modeling of both, achievable with the existing
BEAGLE
-AGN code
(
Vidal-García et al. 2024
)
, an extension of the
BEAGLE
framework
(
Chevallard & Charlot 2016
)
.
BEAGLE
-AGN uses the
MultiNest

(
Feroz et al. 2009
)
sampler to perform the exploration of the multi-dimensional parameter space and finds the best fitting parameters to the data. The
MultiNest
output provides the Bayesian evidence (Z), a key statistic that quantifies the integrated likelihood of a model given the data. Comparing the evidences between competing models offers a rigorous, probabilistic criterion for determining which model is most strongly supported.

A key advantage of
BEAGLE
-AGN is its ability to separate AGN emission in the narrow line region (NLR) from the stellar and H II contributions. We use this feature to assess the AGN flux contribution to the high-ionization UV lines in GHZ2.
BEAGLE
-AGN employs stellar templates that are based off of the
Bruzual & Charlot 2003
stellar population models, for a full description and updates of the stellar modeling see
Vidal-García et al. 2017
, and incorporates AGN templates from
Feltre et al. 2016a
see
Vidal-García et al. 2024
for full implementation of the grids. Since it was designed for type-2 AGN, the code assumes the AGN continuum is fully obscured by the torus and therefore sub-dominant to the stellar continuum—a limitation discussed in Section
V
. However, we find no evidence of GHZ2 violating this assumption (i.e: no evidence of broad line features in H
\beta
or H
\alpha
, though these were only observed in the R
\sim
100 MIRI/LRS mode, so this is not very constraining). The AGN power law slope is fixed to be at
\alpha
=
-
1.7, a standard value for the AGN power law index
(
Groves et al. 2004
)
.
BEAGLE
-AGN performs simultaneous spectro-photometric fitting by jointly modeling the observed spectrum, broadband photometry, and optionally, derived emission-line fluxes. The fitting process compares the input spectrum with a physically motivated model spectrum generated from a combination of stellar, nebular, and AGN components, allowing for a self-consistent determination of galaxy and AGN properties within a unified Bayesian framework.
Doing this analysis allows one to understand the interplay between the AGN and SF components and explore alterations to the models to gain insight if adding in other features could reproduce the data.
To assess the AGN contribution to GHZ2’s nebular emission, we perform simultaneous
BEAGLE
-AGN fits to the NIRCam photometry, NIRSpec spectra, and MIRI-derived optical fluxes from
Zavala et al. 2024a
, using the parameters outlined in Table

. For stellar-only runs, the AGN parameters were omitted.

BEAGLE
-AGN Models

To assess the ionization coming from GHZ2, a suite of models that incorporates different physical phenomena needs to be considered. We start off with a fiducial model which incorporates both SF and AGN components, the stellar IMF cutoff is 100

To ensure completeness, we run all permutations of these model grids as well as SF only models. A summary of the model types and their relationships is provided in Table
5
(see Appendix).

We assess model performance using differences in log-evidence (
\Delta\ln Z
) and the Bayes factor, which quantify the relative likelihood of models in reproducing the data. For model comparison, we adopt the
Jeffreys 1961
criteria and select the model with the highest

III.2
Computing the AGN Contribution

BEAGLE
-AGN outputs a high-resolution SED including stellar, nebular, and AGN contributions (hereafter the full SED), as well as an AGN-only spectrum at the same resolution. We also obtain a marginal SED, defined as the full SED convolved with the NIRSpec resolution curve via the supplied line spread function (LSF) parameters, representing the modeled observed spectrum. To evaluate AGN contributions to the emission lines, we convolved both the full and AGN-only SEDs with the NIRSpec and MIRI resolution curves using the flux-preserving resampling code
spectres

(
Carnall 2017
)
.

After convolution, we compute the AGN contribution fraction—the ratio of the convolved AGN SED to the convolved full SED—which quantifies the AGN contribution to the total spectrum. We perform this on all the posterior realization of the full SEDs and AGN SEDs and we acquire an uncertainty for the AGN contribution using the absolute difference of the 16th and 84th percentile against the median. For key emission lines, including
C
IV
\lambda
1548 and
C
III
]
\lambda
1908, the AGN contribution for individual lines is the AGN contribution fraction that is closest to the wavelength of the line.

III.3
Estimating Black Hole Mass

BEAGLE
-AGN provides a posterior of the accretion luminosity assuming a 10% covering fraction of the NLR, which we assume to approximate the bolometric luminosity (
log_{10}(L_{acc})\approx log_{10}(L_{bol})
). Under this assumption, we estimate the black hole mass (M
BH
) using the definition of the Eddington ratio as follows:

\eta=\frac{L}{L_{Edd}}=\frac{L}{3.2\times 10^{4}(\frac{M_{BH}}{M_{\odot}})L_{\odot}}

(1)

Using the accretion luminosity from
BEAGLE
-AGN and Equation
1
, we estimate black hole masses for a range of Eddington ratios: 0.1, 0.5, and 1, described in §4.3. These masses allow us to explore the co-evolution of the black hole and stellar components via the BH-to-stellar mass relation and to compare GHZ2 with other high-redshift AGN in the literature.

However, this conversion is highly uncertain. For example, our observational constraints on the accretion luminosity come solely from our detected emission lines, which only probe the portion of the spectrum at high enough energies to allow ionization
(
Vidal-García et al. 2024
, see Fig. 1;)
. The extrapolation to bolometric luminosity is thus heavily dependent on the slope of the accretion disk emission (assumed to be
\alpha
=
-
1.7 in our work). We approximate the amplitude of this uncertainty by exploring the
Kubota & Done 2019
AGN spectral energy distribution models, finding there is a scatter of
\sim
0.7 dex in the ratio of the number of ionizing photons to the bolometric luminosity. Better constraints on the black hole mass can be obtained via future modeling including the continuum luminosity, and future higher-resolution spectroscopy which could potentially detect broad permitted lines, for an independent kinematic tracer.

V
Discussion

Castellano et al. 2024
and
Calabrò et al. 2024
conducted a detailed analysis of GHZ2 to investigate the origins of its UV emission. They employed line ratio diagnostics involving
C
IV
\lambda
1548,
C
III
]
\lambda
1908, and
He
II
\lambda
1640, comparing the observed ratios to predictions from AGN models
(
Feltre et al. 2016a
)
and stellar models
(
Gutkin et al. 2016
)
. A key limitation of these model grids is the assumption that the ionizing spectrum is dominated by a single source—either AGN or stellar. Accurately characterizing the UV ionization in GHZ2 therefore requires a framework capable of incorporating a multi-component ionizing source. The
BEAGLE
-AGN approach addresses this limitation, as demonstrated by
Vidal-García et al. 2024
, by simultaneously modeling stars,
H
II
regions, and the narrow-line region (NLR) to reproduce nebular emission lines. Additionally, our approach models individual line-strengths, rather than just the line ratio.

V.1
Model Comparisons

After exploring a wide range of physical scenarios—including extending the IMF cutoff to 300
{\rm M}_{\odot}
, and elevated C/O abundances—we find that the best-fitting model features an IMF upper-mass cutoff of 100
{\rm M}_{\odot}
. While this model is preferred, variations in the IMF cutoff (100 vs. 300
{\rm M}_{\odot}
) have minimal impact on the fits, as evidenced by negligible differences in log-evidence (
\Delta\ln Z<4
) and the Bayes factors not being high enough to disfavor them. Additional tunable parameters, such as a flexible nebular C/O abundance, do not drastically improve the fits to the data. Crucially, SF-only models are strongly disfavored, all models tested have a
\Delta\ln Z>>100
and a large Bayes factor. We conclude that an AGN component is required to account for the high-ionization UV lines, most notably
C
IV
\lambda
1548 which drives the inclusion of an AGN component.

To explore another mechanism for reproducing the
C
IV
\lambda
1548 strength, we varied the C/O abundances in the
BEAGLE
-AGN fits. The model with the enhanced C/O got a best fit C/O ratio of 1.18 time solar with a median of 1.02
{}^{+0.12}_{-0.20}
times solar. Even with a derived C/O of 1.02 (1.18-best fit) times solar, the modeled
C
IV
\lambda
1548 under predicted the measured
C
IV
\lambda
1548 flux and produced slight improvements, and in some cases slightly worsened the fit as denoted by having a larger
\Delta\ln Z
when compared with the fiducial model. Thus, even with a C/O
>1
solar, it remains insufficient to reproduce the high ionization carbon we see in GHZ2. While stellar-only models can reproduce the UV continuum shape and the strength of
C
III
]
\lambda
1908, they consistently under predict
C
IV
\lambda
1548 (see Figure
4
) and this drives up the
\Delta\ln Z>>100
. We therefore conclude that stars alone cannot provide sufficient ionization to account for the observed
C
IV
\lambda
1548 emission in GHZ2, although they can reproduce the UV continuum and
C
III
]
\lambda
1908.

GHZ2 provides a valuable test case because line diagnostics place it in a region of parameter space intermediate between AGN and star formation, suggesting that a combination of both is required to explain its spectral features. The
BEAGLE
-AGN SED fitting supports this scenario. Only through
BEAGLE
-AGN’s self-consistent SED modeling can we assess evidence for AGN activity, as line diagnostics alone are limited by degeneracies in the ionization mechanisms
(
Calabrò et al. 2024
;
Castellano et al. 2024
;
Cleri et al. 2025
)
and we further quantify the extent of the AGN contribution.

V.2
Testing Black Hole Seeding

If the AGN interpretation for GHZ2 turns out to be correct, this would imply the earliest supermassive black hole (SMBH) observed to date. Indeed, such a high redshift of SMBH formation would provide an opportunity to push theoretical models of black hole seeding and growth to their limits
(
Smith & Bromm 2019
;
Woods et al. 2019
;
Inayoshi et al. 2020
)
. In general, to promote the accelerated emergence of SMBHs, one can consider heavy initial seed masses, earlier redshifts of formation, or more efficient growth through super-Eddington accretion. It is also conceivable that a combination of all these effects may be required to understand the black hole growth in GHZ2.

One leading scenario invokes high-mass seed black holes, with initial masses of
\sim 10^{4-6}
M
⊙
, as opposed to ‘light seed’ stellar remnants with
\lesssim
100 M
⊙
. Such ‘heavy’ seeds could form via the direct collapse of a massive pristine gas cloud, where vigorous fragmentation into a Population III star cluster is suppressed by keeping the gas temperature close to the (hydrogen) atomic cooling threshold
(
Bromm & Loeb 2003
;
Begelman et al. 2006
;
Lodato & Natarajan 2006
, e.g.,)
. These direct-collapse black hole (DCBH) models, however, require conditions that may be quite rare, such as the presence of strong soft (Lyman-Werner) UV fluxes
(
Habouzit et al. 2016
;
Bhowmick et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Other pathways which can form heavy seeds may also be in play, involving dynamical processes in dense star clusters, such as the merging of stars or their BH remnants
(
Reinoso et al. 2023
;
Kritos et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
.

Theoretical studies suggest that black hole seeds can reach higher masses at early times under extreme conditions. In highly-biased regions, corresponding to the tail in the Gaussian random field of density perturbations, stellar collapse can begin at
z>
20
(
Haemmerlé et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, providing a longer timeline for accretion. Furthermore, heavy seeds may reach 10
8

{\rm M}_{\odot}
if rotation
(
Shibata et al. 2016
;
Dennison et al. 2019
)
or dark collapse
(
Mayer & Bonoli 2019
)
are considered, although typical heavy-seed masses are
\sim
10
5-6

{\rm M}_{\odot}
(
Becerra et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. Invoking the self-annihilation of weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs), “dark stars” are also proposed as a channel for heavy seeds
(
Freese et al. 2016
)
. Another non-standard pathway is linked to massive primordial black holes (PBHs), formed in the ultra-early universe
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, possibly able to explain the peculiar case of the ‘naked’, extremely overmassive, SMBH in Abell 2744-QSO1, recently discovered by
JWST

(
Maiolino et al. 2025
)
.

Pursuing a different avenue towards accelerated early BH growth, multiple studies have explored super-Eddington accretion, including the conditions under which it can occur, and the duty cycles over which it may be sustained within realistic cosmological environments
(
Jeon et al. 2023
;
Lupi et al. 2024
;
Gordon et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. In any case, episodes of super-Eddington accretion may be required to explain the high-mass tail of the BH mass function at high redshifts, irrespective of the seeding model
(
Jeon et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

To illustrate these possibilities, we construct an idealized BH growth model
(
Salpeter 1964
;
Haiman & Loeb 2001
)
, under four sets of initial seeding and growth conditions: (1) a 10
6
–10
7

{\rm M}_{\odot}
heavy seed accreting at the Eddington limit at
z
= 20, (2) a 10
5
–10
6

{\rm M}_{\odot}
heavy seed accreting at twice the Eddington limit at
z=
20, (3) a 10
5
–10
6

{\rm M}_{\odot}
heavy seed accreting at the Eddington limit at
z=
30, and (4) a stellar-remnant seed of 10–10
2.47

{\rm M}_{\odot}
accreting at the Eddington limit at
z=
20. All models assume a radiative efficiency of 10%.
Figure

shows that, for select scenarios, this toy model can reproduce the inferred black hole mass of GHZ2 by
z

>
10

{\rm M}_{\odot}
), (ii) an early formation redshift (z

Linking observable properties to specific growth mechanisms—such as the direct collapse scenario, super-Eddington accretion, or early formation—would be particularly valuable. With ongoing theoretical studies and forthcoming observations of GHZ2, it will be possible to further constrain the formation and growth pathway of the AGN, which our analysis suggests to reside within this system. Given the current factor
\sim 10

For the high-mass value, the system would be extremely overmassive, similar to Abell 2744-QSO1, but pushing this extreme configuration to even higher redshifts. For such a possible

V.3
Modeling Caveats

While
BEAGLE
-AGN represents a significant step forward, there are important caveats in its modeling approach. Notably, it fails to reproduce the nitrogen lines in GHZ2, specifically N IV]
\lambda
1486 and N III]
\lambda
1746, even in the best-fitting models. This limitation likely arises from the parameter space covered by the available grids. Addressing this issue will require careful consideration of nitrogen enrichment in high-redshift systems. Observations indicate that some high-redshift galaxies exhibit enhanced nitrogen abundances, and
Cameron et al. 2023
explore possible mechanisms, including nitrogen-loud quasars, Wolf-Rayet stars enriching the ISM via winds, and mergers of dense star clusters. Any of these processes could contribute to the enhanced nitrogen in GHZ2; however, further investigation is required to understand nitrogen enrichment under the extreme conditions near AGN at high redshift.

Nitrogen enrichment is not the only factor to consider; other ionization mechanisms may also contribute. For example,
Flury et al. 2024
highlight the role of shocks in powering nitrogen lines in enriched systems. Understanding the sources of nitrogen excitation is critical, as they can mimic features typically attributed to AGN activity. This emphasizes the need for models capable of capturing the full complexity of these systems and disentangling their various components. A detailed understanding of high-redshift extreme systems is therefore essential to accurately model nitrogen-to-oxygen enhancement and to identify the diverse physical processes responsible for their emission.

As stated in Section 2.1, we mask the [Ne III]
\lambda
3868 line in our fitting. This decision is motivated by the observed behavior of this line in many high-redshift galaxies, where the [Ne III]
\lambda
3868 flux is often higher than in lower-redshift systems
(
Shapley et al. 2025
, e.g.)
and model coverage
(
Silcock et al. 2025
, see fig. 9 of)
. Such behavior may reflect atypical gaseous properties, such as super-solar Ne/O abundances. Consequently, when fitting with models that assume solar Ne/O ratios (e.g.
BEAGLE
-AGN), the derived AGN parameters
can
become biased, as the AGN component compensates for line fluxes the star-forming component uncharacteristically cannot reproduce alone. We note that this scenario is more applicable when the AGN constraints are inextricably tied to Ne-line observables
(
Silcock et al. 2025
, as in )
, which is not the case for GHZ2. Nonethless, to mitigate any potential biases, we therefore mask the [Ne III]
\lambda
3868 line as a precautionary measure.

Another caveat in our analysis concerns the treatment of gas density in the model grids. In
BEAGLE
-AGN, the SF and AGN grids are generated separately with distinct density thresholds using the photoionization code
Cloudy

(
Ferland et al. 2017
)
. High-redshift galaxies exist in environments that differ substantially from local systems, and the density of
H
II
regions directly affects level populations and the resulting emission lines. We tested only a couple of nebular densities 10
2
and 10
4
cm
-3
with
BEAGLE
-AGN and
Bagpipes
. However, it remains plausible that higher-density grids, modeled self-consistently including stars,
H
II
regions, and AGN, could improve these analyses. Exploring such extreme environments is beyond the scope of this work and is reserved for future studies.

A final limitation of our modeling is that the AGN spectrum lacks a continuum due to the NLR assumption. While our current data show no evidence for a broad-line region, deeper higher-resolution spectra could reveal a broadened emission line such as broad carbon emission in
C
IV
,
C
III
, or H
\alpha
(a MIRI/MRS spectrum is incoming in Cycle 4 which may reveal broad H
\alpha
; PID 7078, PI: Mitsuhashi). Accounting for an AGN continuum would alter both the inferred AGN contribution to the emission lines and the derived galactic properties, as the observed photometry would be a combination of stellar and AGN continuum. This highlights how sensitive spectral modeling is to the inclusion of AGN components. Nevertheless, this modeling approach offers valuable insight into how different AGN components influence the spectrum. Despite these limitations, we have explored all feasible avenues with the available tools and consistently find significant AGN contributions in GHZ2.

Improved modeling will also enable tighter constraints on key AGN properties, such as black hole mass—the fundamental observable for testing black hole growth models. As observations push the frontier to ever higher redshifts, refined analyses of these composite systems may help distinguish between different black hole seeding and growth scenarios, offering critical insight into the co-evolution of galaxies and their central black holes.
