Local electronic regulation of Na5V12O32 cathode suppressing structural distortion toward enhanced sodium storage
Authors: Xuexia Song, Jingjing Wang, Wenbin Li, Wei Xiao, Gaini Zhang, Shuling Liu, Yunkai Xu, Jun Lu, Xifei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
High-voltage layered oxide cathodes have received extensive attention for sodium-ion batteries owing to their potential high energy and power densities, but their stabilization remains a universal challenge. Herein, a stable high-voltage KxNa5-xV12O32 cathode is designed by synergistically tuning the irreversible phase and oxygen vacancies through the substitution of Na with K. The functional mechanism regulating the electronic structure of KxNa5-xV12O32 is elucidated: K substitution strengthens V 3d–O 2p hybridization and V 3d–orbital electron delocalization in the K0.147Na4.853V12O32 structure, enriching charge distribution and reinforcing the V3O8 structure. This promots electron transfer kinetics, suppresses irreversible phase transition, and lowers the Na+ diffusion energy barrier. Moreover, the reversible redox reaction of V5+/V4+ is significantly enhanced, delivering 250.1 mA h g−1 (1.5–4.3 V vs. Na/Na+), which increases the average operating voltage from 4.0 to 4.3 V and boosts the overall energy density. Consequently, the K0.147Na4.853V12O32 electrode significantly enhances cycling performance, retaining 98.2% of the capacity after 1000 cycles at 1300 mA g−1 and enabling stable cycling with 98.7% retention after 300 cycles in a hard carbon | | K0.147Na4.853V12O32 pouch cell. This strategy of electronic structure modulation offers avenues for developing high energy density stable vanadium-based cathode materials for sodium-ion batteries.

Introduction

Sodium-ion battery layered oxide cathodes have attracted extensive attention owing to their periodic layered structures, simple synthesis, and high theoretical capacity and operating voltage. Nevertheless, they still face fatal issues, including detrimental phase transitions, cycling instability, unsatisfactory performance, etc1,2. A phase transition usually emerges at high voltages, and restricting the high cutoff voltage appears to be a good way to hinder phase change, but it undoubtedly reduces the energy density. Furthermore, cathodes with high operating voltages are necessary for the development of high-energy-density sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), although the cycling stability of cathodes is still a pressing issue3.Therefore, how to reinforce the structure of layered oxides upon electrochemical insertion/extraction processes and restrain phase transitions to achieve high structural stability, rapid Na+ diffusion, and predominant comprehensive electrochemical performance has led to enormous research interest. Presently, the most straightforward and practical approach is to modulate the phase transformation by means of proper ion doping, reinforcing the crystal structure upon charge/discharge and guaranteeing rapid Na+ (de)intercalation4–6.

Layered Na5V12O32 (Na1.25V3O8) serves as an appealing layered oxide cathode for sodium-ion batteries, and delivers a theoretical specific capacity of 260 mAh g−1 with a wide voltage window (1.5–4.0 V, versus Na/Na+)7. Generally, Na5V12O32 materials have two major inherent issues: poor electronic conductivity and unstable structure, which lead to cycling instability and poor rate performance7. Various strategies for morphology and electronic conductivity modulations to improve the performance of Na5V12O32 have been reported8,9. Recent studies have shown that synthesized hierarchical zigzag Na1.25V3O8 nanowires delivered a capacity of 172.5 mAh g−1 at 100 mA g−1 for Na+ storage7. Cao et al.9 constructed sodium vanadate@polypyrrole (Na5V12O32@PPy) nanocomposites, and the PPy shells not only increased the electrical conductivity of the electrodes but also mitigated the structural distortion of rigid Na5V12O32 nanowires during the discharge process. Na5V12O32 nanowire arrays were fabricated and exhibited a discharge capacity of 161 mAh g−110. It has been reported that cationic dopants in layered oxide materials can depress phase transitions, enabling a significant improvement in cycling stability11–14. For example, ion doping has shown a beneficial effect on Na5V12O32, with a reversible capacity of 169 mAh g−1, and facilitates Na+ /electron transport12,14. In addition to good kinetics and structural stability11, the activation of multielectron redox behavior (such as V3+/V4+/V5+) is another major research interest for obtaining higher energy density15. The Al3+ substitution of partial V3+ in the NASICON matrix allows for access to the highly reversible redox reaction of the V4+/V5+ couple (4.0 V vs. Na/Na+)16. As reported by Sun et al., materials undergo a series of phase transitions due to the TMO2 layer glidin17,18. Thus, developing a stable Na5V12O32 cathode with good rate capability, structural stability, and multielectron redox activity has become a critical requirement for high-energy-density, high-safety sodium-ion batteries.

Herein, KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO (KNVO) was synthesized via a simple one-step posttreatment. This structure not only has good cycling stability, but also unlocks the redox activity at potentials up to 4.3 V. More importantly, the absorption energy for Na+ notably increases with the adoption of potassium ions, which elevates the discharge potential and overall energy density of the cathdoe. Moreover, the expected diffusion is acquired by delocalizing the nominal valence of the inserted Na+ over multiple atoms via p-d hybridization among the V-3d and O-2p orbitals. The irreversible phase transitions can be suppressed by K substitution, leading to enhanced cycling stability and rate capability. This work provides guidance for designing advanced cathodes with high energy density for practical SIBs.

Results

The hydrothermal process of synthesizing the KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO cathode is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The chemical formulas of the as-prepared KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO samples were determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP–AES, Supplementary Table 1). The exact formulas of KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO (KNVO; x = 0, 0.048, 0.147, and 0.246) are identified as Na5V12O32-rGO (NVO), K0.048Na4.952V12O32-rGO (KNVO-I), K0.147Na4.853V12O32-rGO (KNVO-II) and K0.246Na4.754V12O32-rGO (KNVO-III). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 3a confirm that the as-prepared NVO and KNVO materials present similar layered structures with P21/m space groups, indicating that the crystal structure did not change significantly after K+ substitution. All the peaks in both samples agree well with the monoclinic structure of Na5V12O32 (JCPDS no. 24-1156). This indicates that the d-spacing along the c-axis direction for KNVO-II is increased because of the insertion of potassium ions into the NVO. Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns (Fig. 1c–d and Supplementary Fig. 3a–b) revealed that all the diffraction peaks of both NVO and KNVO are well indexed to a typical layered structure with P21/m symmetry. Detailed XRD refinements are indicated in Supplementary Table 2, where the cell volume is increased in the presence of K+, and the expansion of the c axis is attributed to the larger ionic radius of K+ (0.69 Å) than that of Na+19,20. As shown in Fig. 1b inset and the refinement results in Supplementary Table 3, the average Na–O bond length (d(Na–O)) of KNVO-II is obviously shortened, and KNVO-II has a decreased layer spacing (d(O–V–O)) and increased Na layer spacing (d(O–Na–O)) compared with those of NVO. The interlayer spacing (dV3O8) and metal oxide layer thickness (d(O–V–O)) are ~4.11 Å and 7.379 Å in KNVO-II (4.39 Å and 7.32 Å in NVO), as shown in Fig. 1f, verifying the strengthened V3O8 skeleton and broadened Na+ diffusion paths21. The shrinkage of V3O8 slabs originating from the reinforcement of V–O bonds decreases the shielding effect of Na+ on adjacent oxygen layers, increasing the structural stability18,22. To explore the redox mechanism during Na+ (de)intercalation, high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were collected for NVO and KNVO, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. As shown in the V 2p spectra in Fig. 1e, the V4+ peak in the KNVO sample is significantly enhanced, whereas the V5+ peak is reduced upon the introduction of K+. For KNVO-II, the introduced K+ tunes the delocalized electron density around the V and O atoms, which is elucidated by the slight shifts in the V 2p (Fig. 1e) XPS peaks. The V 2p XPS peaks show that KNVO-II has a lower spin-energy separation (ΔE) of 7.3 eV compared with that of NVO (7.52 eV), and a negative shift of ≈0.22 eV can be detected for both the V 2p1/2 and V 2p3/2 peaks in KNVO-II, indicating the strongest electronic interaction. The decreased binding energies of V 2p in KNVO-II indicate increased electron intensity on V atoms21. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a, there is a slight shift in the Na 1 s peaks of 1071.28 eV for NVO and 1070.78 eV for KNVO-II, with ΔE = 0.5 eV. The increase in the binding energy can be attributed to the K+ ions riveting in the Na layers23. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5b, the two peaks of K 2p confirm the addition of potassium into the structure. The process of K-doping is schematically shown in Fig. 1f, in which partial K+ substitution increased the layer spacing, stabilized the structure, and induced abundant oxygen defects. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was carried out to further explore the oxygen defects in the sample with its essential role in the charge transfer process as the signal originates from unpaired electrons at the defect sites in an applied magnetic field24. Oxygen defects as well as V4+ can be identified by EPR on account of unpaired electrons25, as presented in Fig. 1g. The NVO and KNVO samples present a sharper signal at g = 2.003. The oxygen vacancy and V4+ concentration increased with the addition of K. The KNVO-III sample reveals the highest oxygen vacancy concentration among all the samples, which might be ascribed to the generation of extra oxygen vacancies associated with V4+ in KNVO, in addition to the original V4+ in NVO, through the incorporation of K+ ions—clearly demonstrating the existence of abundant oxygen vacancies in the lattice structure24,25. X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) measurements were performed to characterize the local chemical and electronic environments of NVO and KNVO-II. As shown in Fig. 2a, b, the V K-edge XANES spectra of NVO and KNVO-II are similar, and the valences of V in both samples are located mainly between V4+ and V5+, indicating that V possesses a similar chemical environment. The energy absorption edges of KNVO-II are lower than those of NVO, illustrating that K+ incorporation induces a lower vanadium oxidation state, which agrees well with the XPS results (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 5). The Fourier transformed (FT) extended EXAFS spectra in R-space are shown in Fig. 2c, f. The V K-edge EXAFS fitting results in R-space revealed that the peaks at 1–2 Å and 2–3 Å, corresponding to V–O and V–V coordination of NVO and KNVO-II in the first and second shells, respectively. The V–O bond is obviously compressed due to K+ insertion, and the decrease in the intensity of the V–O and V–V bonds of KNVO-II demonstrates bond opening (Fig. 2f inset), which is ascribed to the shorter bond lengths, indicating a more stable framework in the KNVO-II structure26–31. Additionally, as shown in Table S4 Supplementary Table 4, the coordination number CN of V–V shell for KNVO-II is clearly lower than that of NVO, and the radial distance R is shorter than that of NVO. The lower coordination number originates from its ultrathin structure with abundant dangling bonds29. The radial structure functions corresponding to the central absorption vanadium atom highlight that the interlayer spacing increases and that the distance from the oxygen located at the vertex to vanadium decreasea after K+ incorporation30–32. The wavelet transform (WT) of the V K-edge EXAFS clearly records the atomic dispersion of V for the two samples owing to its ability to divide backscattered atoms in R-space and K-space resolution. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves at 0.1 mV s−1 and corresponding galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) curves of NVO and KNVO-II over the range of 1.5–4.3 V are shown in Fig. 4a–d. The CV profiles of NVO show two pairs of redox peaks at 2.32/2.66 V and 3.3/3.46 V, while the two redox couples of KNVO-II are located at 2.4/2.59 V and 3.33/3.43 V, corresponding to the continuous redox reaction of V4+/V5+. The voltage hysteresis of the two redox couples is 190 mV and 100 mV for KNVO-II, whereas it is 340 mV and 160 mV for NVO, indicating better redox kinetics and higher electronic conductivity for KNVO-II. The galvanostatic discharge/charge (GCD) curves of the first 100 cycles of NVO and KNVO-II between 1.5 and 4.3 V at 26 mA g−1 are shown in Fig. 4b, d. The discharge profiles exhibit two distinct plateaus, and KNVO-II has a smoother voltage profile and a higher specific capacity of 250.1 mAh g−1 compared to 227 mAh g−1 for NVO (Supplementary Fig. 8), corresponding to 2.89 mol Na+ (de)intercalation per Na4.853K0.147V12O32. Furthermore, Fig. 4e shows a comparison of the long-term cycling stabilities of NVO and KNVO-II at 1300 mA g−1. Clearly, the KNVO-II electrode delivers a higher capacity and capacity retention (98.2%) after 1000 cycles with a high coulombic efficiency of ~100%, outperforming most of the reported layered sodium vanadate cathodes (Supplementary Table 5)[8]. In comparison, NVO retains 50.7% of the capacity, showing much worse cycling stability. Among the three KNVO samples, KNVO-II exhibited the highest discharge capacity retention compared to KNVO-I (94.4%) and KNVO-III (90.2%). The inferior cycling performance of the KNVO-I sample is caused mainly by the lower pinning effect, while the increased Na occupancy in the KNVO-III sample enhances the electrostatic repulsion between VO6 octahedra (Supplementary Table 2), and limits the Na-ion diffusion14,33. A rate capability comparison was also performed as shown in Fig. 4f. KNVO-II maintains 236, 228.8, 214, 189.3, 172.1, and 128 mAh g−1 capability at 52, 130, 260, 780, 1300, and 2600 mA g−1, implying better rate capability than NVO. This good rate performance can be ascribed to the enlarged Na layer after K-doping, which facilitates Na+ diffusion and maintains a more stable long-term cycling life. A comparison of charge/discharge differential capacity vs. voltage profiles(dQ/dV) in Fig. 4g–h and Supplementary Fig. 9 reveals the capacity degradation during cycling. From the 1st to the 100th cycle, NVO shows significant redox peak fading (Supplementary Fig. 9), which can be attributed to poor structural reversibility. From the 100th cycle to the 500th cycle (Fig. 4g), an irreversible oxidation peak at ~4.0 V appeared for NVO, which is caused by the degradation of NVO during prolonged cycling34–36. In comparison, the dQ/dV curve of KNVO-II possesses two pairs of similar redox peaks with better symmetry than NVO37. Even after 500 cycles, the irreversible oxidation peak at ~4.0 V is absent for KNVO-II, implying better structural and cycling stability (Fig. 4h). The GCD profiles agree well with the dQ/dV analysis, where Fig. 4i shows two distinct voltage plateaus for NVO: a plateau at ~3.3 V, which is attributed to Na+ insertion/extraction, and a long plateau at a high-voltage of ~4.0 V, which corresponds to the irreversible phase transition. For KNVO-II, no such irreversible charge plateau is observed38. This further confirms that the pinned K in Na layers can effectively suppress the gliding of V–O layers and stabilize the structure upon electrochemical cycling39.

To shed more light on the (de)sodiation kinetic differences between NVO and KNVO-II, Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 10a show the CV profiles measured at various sweep rates. According to i = avb, b value close to 0.5 illustrates an ion diffusion-controlled process, whereas b value close to 1.0 indicates a capacitive-controlled behavior39–41. Electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM) measurements were performed to explore the charge storage mechanism of the electrode during cycling. Figure 5c, d shows the CV profiles at 1.0 mV s−1 for the NVO and KNVO-II electrodes. Two pairs of redox peaks can be observed during the EQCM measurement, which is similar to the results obtained previously (Fig. 4a, c). The CV profile for NVO is discrete and steep in the first cycle between 3.8 and 4.3 V, whereas that of the KNVO-II electrode is overlapping and smoother, indicating that V dissolution is suppressed near 4.3 V after K-doping42. Interestingly, at the Na+ intercalation potential of ~2.4 V, KNVO-II presents only one sharp CV peak, whereas NVO has two separated peaks at 2.25 and 2.4 V, corresponding to the intercalation of Na+ and storage of Na+ in the electrolyte, respectively42. As shown in Fig. 5e–f, Faraday’s law was used to identify the molar mass (M/z) and distinguish the categories and quantities of adsorbed/desorbed ions41. Notably, four major stages appear during the discharge process of NVO, which suggests the presence of four major reaction processes. The m/z of the adsorbed ions calculated from the slope is 41.9 g mol−1 at the beginning of 4.3 V (Fig. 5e), which corresponds to a precipitation at 0.82 V (51 g mol−1). The curve of mass versus charge can be divided into three main stages (Fig. 5f) for KNVO-II, indicating the existence of three main reaction processes. Average molar weights of m/z = 125.7 g mol−1 and 100.2 g mol−1 were obtained in the high (approximately 3.5 V vs. Na/Na+) and low (approximately 2.4 V vs. Na/Na+) potential regions, respectively, implying that solvated Na+ ions are present on the surface of KNVO-II and that desolvated Na+ ions intercalate into the layers. Typically, in the region of 2.5–3.3 V, a large molar weight (m/z = 300 g mol−1) was obtained, and the reaction was governed by the intercalation of desolvated Na+ ions, along with a few adsorbed Na+ ions on the surface. During the charge/discharge process, the K+ ions remaining between the layers expedite Na+ diffusion. The layered structure is persistent, which is beneficial for reversible Na+ intercalation and extraction. The prominent Na+ storage process involves the desolvated Na+ (de)intercalation into the layers, which is a highly pseudocapacitive reaction associated with the V5+/V4+ redox couple43. These EQCM data reveal the mechanism and high reversibility of Na+ intercalation during the electrochemical processes of Na+ hosting in KNVO-II.

The enhancement mechanism of KNVO-II was investigated by in situ EIS. Figure 5g, h shows the EIS spectra of the NVO and KNVO-II electrodes at various discharge/charge states during the first and second cycles. According to the equivalent circuit (inset), all the Nyquist plots reveal analogous curve shapes, which are composed of a semicircle in the high-frequency region and a sloping line in the low-frequency region, representing the charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and Warburg impedance of Na+ diffusion in the electrode (Zw), respectively13,44.The Rct values of NVO initially increased upon discharging to 1.5 V and then declined upon charging from 1.5 to 4.3 V. Notably, the fitted values of Rct remained almost constant for KNVO-II during the entire sodiation/desodiation process, confirming the good conductivity and robust structural stability during cycling (Fig. 5i). The fluctuation in Rct for NVO is generated mainly by TM dissolution and compositional changes in the CEI film, resulting in poor cycling performance45. To further elucidate the reaction kinetics variations in the NVO and KNVO-II cathodes, the diffusion coefficients of Na+ (DNa+) during the charge/discharge process were explored by the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique,as shown in Fig. 5j (GITT). As shown in Fig. 5k, KNVO-II exhibited much higher DNa+ values of ~10−11 cm2s−1, which were approximately one order of magnitude greater than those of the NVO cathode (~10−12 cm2s−1). In contrast, the DNa+ values of NVO exhibit unstable fluctuations, which can be ascribed to the sluggish phase transition46.

Furthermore, the adsorption of Na+ on the (100), (111), and (001) planes of KNVO-II was calculated, and the most stable optimized structures for each plane and matching adsorption energies are shown in Supplementary Fig. 14. Notably, Na+ could be firmly anchored on the O top site of the (001) plane owing to the negative adsorption energy (−1.687 eV). For KNVO-II, all the crystal planes have prominent affinities for the Na ion, and the (001) plane is the steadiest, demonstrating the successful introduction of K into the Na layers, and agreeing well with previous experimental results. Compared with Na+ intercalation into the (001) plane of KNVO-II, oxygen-deficient sites can expand the vanadium oxide layer and permit Na+ diffusion/intercalation along the c-axis, thereby significantly increasing the reaction kinetics and electrochemical reactivity.

Because the electron interaction occurs chiefly in the exterior d-electron orbital, the projected density of states (PDOS) results of the d-electron orbital are further given for NVO and KNVO-II, which reveal the structural change with the axial effect. As shown in Fig. 7g–h, two orbital sets can be observed in the PDOS of the V 3 d orbitals: eg (dz2, dx2-y2) and t2g (dxy, dyz, dxz). The axial NVO has a strong influence on dz2 and this strong axial interaction cannot stabilize the dz2 structure and tailor the redox process53. For KNVO-II, strong interactions occur mainly in the dxz and dx2-y2 orbitals. Therefore, the electron delocalization of 3 dz2 implies that more electrons are transferred from the spin down orbital of the V center to the spin-up orbital to form the more stable V–O bond of KNVO-II51,54–56, revealing a high degree of electron delocalization and improved electron transfer ability in KNVO-II. Notably, KNVO-II (see Fig. 7i right) displays an embedded O 2p band and an increased overlap between the V 3 d and O 2p bands compared with NVO, and an entire peak of the V 3 d eg orbital in KNVO-II ascertains an unoccupied eg orbital and the semifilled t2g orbital, confirming the dominant V valence state of +4 in KNVO-II and enhanced structural integrity. Moreover, the large hybridization of the V 3 d band with O 2p along with an increase in the V 3d band also alleviated the structural evolution, which suppressed the occurrence of irreversible phase transition evolution, resulting in improved redox involvement. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 7j, V5+ with 3d0 (t2g0eg0) electrons were used, and the unpaired electron spins of V4+(t2g1eg0) ions can be transmitted to Na 3 s orbitals by O 2p orbitals. The V4+ and V5+ components are electrochemically active during the electrochemical process. The V4+ local environment in KNVO-II increases the intrinsic conductivity (V4+ contains one unpaired electron, whereas V5+ does not contain unpaired electrons) and increases the stability of the structure to promote Na+ diffusion. Nevertheless, for NVO, owing to structural damage during cycling, Na+ transfer is hampered and electrochemical polarization is increased, leading to the formation of an irreversible phase with poor Na+ utilization57,58.

Discussion

In summary, through the synergetic engineering of sodium-site substitution and oxygen vacancy strategies, a cathode material of K0.147Na4.853V15O32 with K+ partial riveting in the Na layers has been successfully designed and applied for highly stable sodium storage. As proven, K+ at the Na-site can serve as a pillar to strengthen the structure, and abundant oxygen vacancies induce increased electronic conductivity and rapid ion diffusion kinetics, thereby improving the performance of batteries. For sodium storage, K+ in Na layers relieves the variation in interlayer spacing and suppresses irreversible phase transition, particularly in the highly (de)sodiated state, ensuring a robust structure, in which the complete solid–solution (de)sodiation reaction occurs upon phase transition. Theoretical calculations further revealed that the synergetic effect induces a narrow bandgap and a low Na+ diffusion energy barrier, promoting rapid diffusion. Owing to electronic regulation, the delocalization of V 3 d orbital electrons in the KNVO structure enriched the charge distribution of V‒O‒C bonds and stabilized the V3O8 structure. Thus, it harvests a high capacity of 128 mAh g−1 at 2600 mA g−1 and a lower capacity loss of 0.0025% per cycle over 1000 cycles at 1300 mA g−1. This study proposes an effective modification strategy of alkali-metal site doping and vacancy construction for designing progressive cathode materials for other metal-ion batteries.

Methods

All chemicals of analytical grade were obtained from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. and were used without further purification (AR). The fabrication process of the free-standing Na5V12O32-rGO and KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO membranes is as follows. A series of KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO cathode materials (x = 0, 0.048, 0.147, and 0.246) were rationally designed.

Synthesis of the Na5V12O32-rGO membrane

All chemicals were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. A total of 909 mg (0.005 mol) of V2O5 powder (Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) was slowly added to 30 mL of H2C2O4 (Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) solution (0.5 M) and stirred for 1 h to form a sol. Then, 900 mg of sodium nitrate (Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) was added to the sol under vigorous stirring for 0.5 h at 25 °C. Various amounts of GO (Aladdin) dispersion (2 mg mL−1) was added to generate a homogeneous dispersion after sonication. Afterward, the sol was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave with a stainless-steel shell, heated at 200 °C for 12 h, and then naturally cooled to room temperature. The precipitates were collected using vacuum filtration, and washed with deionized water/ethanol three times. The self-standing Na5V12O32-rGO membrane was subsequently obtained by carefully peeling off the sediments from the filter paper dried at 50 °C under vacuum. Finally, the obtained membrane was heated at 500 °C in N2 for 3 h with a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1 to obtain the Na5V12O32-rGO membrane electrode, referred to as NVO. The mass ratio of rGO in NVO was 10%.

Synthesis of the KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO membrane

The KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO nanobelts were prepared by the same method as the NVO membrane. Different amounts of potassium nitrate (0.1%,0.3%, and 0.5%) was added to the sol under vigorous stirring for 0.5 h at 25 °C. The mixture was subsequently filtered to form a membrane, which was subsequently washed with water and ethanol, dried at 50 °C under vacuum, and peeled off from the filter paper, to obtain a self-standing membrane. Finally, the obtained membrane was heated at 500 °C in N2 for 3 h with a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1, and then cooled to achieve different ratios of KxNa5-xV12O32-rGO membrane electrodes, designated KNVO-I, KNVO-II, and KNVO-III.

Materials characterization

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a TA-SDTQ 600 instrument (Texas Instruments, Inc., New Castle, DE) at a heating rate of 20 °C min−1. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM-6510A) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2010) were used to observe the morphology of the as-prepared samples. High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (JEM-ARM200CF) was used to analyze the nanostructure of the obtained samples. Electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM) measurements were performed using a QSense Analyzer (Biolin Scientific AB, Sweden). The elemental specific ratios of the samples were checked by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‒AES).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were used to characterize the crystalline structure and phase of the electrode materials at 25 °C by a powder diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advanced) with a Ni filter at a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1 over the 2θ range of 10‒60° (Cu Kα source, λ = 0.154 nm). In situ XRD patterns of the KₓNa₅-ₓV₁₂O₃₂-rGO electrode material were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance Diffractometer for the first two cycles at 2θ values ranging from 10° to 50° and at a scanning rate of 5° min−1. The current density used for charging-discharging was 20 mA g−1. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements of the as-prepared samples were carried out at 25 °C using a Bruker spectrometer. Raman spectrua were collected using a Renishaw Via Raman Microscope. The valence state and chemical composition of V were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Escalab 250XI).

X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), including both X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) at the V K-edge, was conducted in transmission mode, at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF), China, operated at 3.5 GeV with a maximum injection current of 230 mA. The XANES and EXAFS data reduction and analysis were refined using Athena software.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were conducted using a Princeton Applied Research Versa STAT 4. The former was performed at various sweep rates within the potential range of 1.5–4.3 V (vs. Na/Na+) using the assembled 2032-type cells. EIS measurements were performed with a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz, which was applied at 5 mV of alternating current amplitude. Galvanostatic charge and discharge (GCD) measurements and the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) were carried out on a NEWARE Battery Test System (CT-4008Tn-5V10mA-164, Shenzhen, China). In the GITT measurements, current pulses of 50 mA g−1 were applied for 1200 s, followed by a 2 h relaxation period.

Electrochemical measurements

Both the cathode and anode electrodes were prepared for electrochemical testing. For the preparation of the cathode electrodes: the calcined NVO, KNVO-I, KNVO-II and KNVO-III membranes were punched into disc cathodes with a diameter of 14 mm, and the average active mass loading was ~1.86–2.03 mg cm−2 on each disc. To prepare the hard carbon anode electrode, hard carbon and sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) at a weight ratio of 95:5 were dispersed in deionized water. After mixing, the anode electrode slurry was cast on aluminum foil and then dried at 100 °C for 12 h in a vacuum electric oven. The electrode was cut into a 14 mm circle for coin-cell fabrication.

Coin-type and half-cells were assembled (2032-type) in a glove box under an argon atmosphere (with water and oxygen concentrations less than 0.1 ppm). A glass microfiber membrane (GF/D, Whatman) and metallic sodium foil were used as the separator and counter electrode (thickness: 0.5 mm), respectively, and 1 M NaClO4 dissolved in ethyl carbonate/dimethyl carbonate at 1:1 weight ratio with 5 wt% fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) was used as the electrolyte.

Before the full-cell assembly, the hard carbon anodes were electrochemically presodiated by undergoing five cycles of discharging and charging at a current density of 50 mA g−1 in half-cells. This process was conducted prior to reassembling the cells into full configurations to establish a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) film. The mass ratio of the cathode to anode electrodes was 1.27:1. The electrochemical properties were calculated on the basis of the total mass of the active materials in both the cathode and anode. The full cells operated within a voltage range of 1.4 to 4.1 V.

The feasibility of the practical application of SIBs was evaluated in full-cell devices. The assembly process of the pouch full battery was as follows: the free-standing K0.147Na4.853V12O32-rGO (KNVO-II) membrane (seven layers) and the hard carbon electrode were cut into rectangular shapes of a certain size (30 mm×35 mm, nominal capacity:15.2 mAh). Next, the cathode, separator, and anode were put together in order with a sandwich structure (cathode–separator–anode) and placed into an aluminum plastic film for packaging. Notably, the process of electrolyte injection was carried out in a glove box.

Effects of air instability on application

The KNVO samples were stored in two key air components for two weeks (water vapor and CO2 with water vapor, referred to as H-2W and HC-2W, respectively) and then characterized by XRD. At the same time, the as-synthesized material was placed in deionized water for 7 days and then dried at 120 °C overnight.

The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerh of (PBE) functional with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method was employed in the calculations carried out using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP). Structural optimization was performed using 3×3×1 K-points and a cut-off energy of 450 eV, and one tetrahedrally coordinated oxygen atom was removed to explore the influence of oxygen defects on the electronic structure of NVO and KNVO. The vacuum spacing in a direction perpendicular to the plane of the structure was at least 10 Å. Regarding the structure, the convergence criterion can be described as follows: the self-consistent force is less than 10−6 eV Å−1 and the difference in energy is less than 0.03 eV Å−1. Moreover, the barrier energies of Na+ ion migration were evaluated using the climbing nudged elastic band method. Spin polarization was considered in all the calculations.
