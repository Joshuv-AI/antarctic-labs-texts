Improving ricotta cheese shelf life using saffron petal extract double nanoemulsions stabilized by plant proteins and Lepidium sativum L. gum
Authors: Fereshteh Bakhshalipoor, Marzieh Bolandi, Fariborz Nahidi, Ahmadreza Abedinia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of double nanoemulsions of saffron petal extract (DN-SPE), stabilized with soybean (SPI) and pea protein isolates (PPI), alone or combined with Lepidium sativum L. seed gum (LSG) (SL or PL), on the quality and shelf life of ricotta cheese during refrigerated storage (4 ± 0.5 °C). In vitro analyses indicated that adding LSG to protein isolates significantly reduced (p < 0.05) droplet size and enhanced encapsulation efficiency, antioxidant activity, and zeta potential. SPI–LSG nanoemulsions exhibited strong antioxidant activity over time. Encapsulation improved antibacterial efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus, with no significant effect on Escherichia coli. Microscopy showed good encapsulation and particle distribution. SPI–LSG was the most stable formulation and had better thermal stability. The incorporation level of DN-SPE into ricotta cheese (1% w/w) was selected based on preliminary formulation optimization experiments evaluating physicochemical stability, antioxidant performance, and sensory acceptability of the cheese matrix. Treatments mitigated alterations in physicochemical attributes, suppressed microbial growth, and preserved sensory quality. Rheological analysis showed shear-thinning, gel-like behavior in all samples, while DN-SPEs significantly increased apparent viscosity, loss modulus, and storage modulus (p < 0.05). Cheese containing DN-SPE-PL exhibited the greatest rheological parameters and enhanced hardness, gumminess, and chewiness. Overall, DN-SPE emulsions containing of PL or SL provided strong antioxidant and antimicrobial effects, improved the physicochemical and sensory quality of ricotta cheese, and demonstrated potential to extend its short refrigerated shelf life within the 14-day storage period.

Introduction

Cheese is among the most widely consumed dairy products due to its high nutritional value. Various cheese types differ based on milk source and processing method1. Ricotta cheese is a fresh, soft, and unripened dairy product traditionally manufactured from whey proteins through heat- and acid-induced coagulation, typically at temperatures above 85–90 °C. It is characterized by high moisture content (70–80%), relatively low fat levels, and a delicate protein network primarily composed of albumins and globulins rather than caseins2. This high moisture content (70–80%) accelerates microbial proliferation and physicochemical deterioration, limiting the refrigerated shelf life typically to less than 10–14 days. Demand for healthier products enriched with functional additives is rising, and plant-derived bioactive compounds are of particular interest. Phenolic compounds are associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular and other chronic diseases3.

Saffron (Crocus sativus L.), a member of the Iridaceae family, is globally recognized as a valuable medicinal plant. Nearly 90% of global saffron cultivation occurs in Iran, yet its production generates substantial waste—about 78 kg of fresh flowers are needed to obtain 1 kg of dried stigma4. Saffron petals, the main by-product, are typically discarded despite being rich in phenolic compounds with strong antioxidant capacity, including quercetin, kaempferol, anthocyanins, and isorhamnetin5. Their antimicrobial and antioxidant properties have been well documented6. However, the direct incorporation of these phenolic-rich extracts into high-moisture dairy matrices remains limited due to their physicochemical instability and rapid degradation under processing and storage conditions.

Polyphenolic compounds are highly sensitive to temperature, light, and oxygen, and microencapsulation provides an effective strategy to enhance their stability and bioavailability7,8. Among delivery systems, double nanoemulsions (W1/O/W2) are particularly attractive due to their ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and lipophilic compounds9. However, their inherent thermodynamic instability leads to coalescence, creaming, and phase separation. Recent studies have shown that protein–polysaccharide complexes can significantly improve interfacial viscoelasticity, reduce droplet coalescence, and enhance kinetic stability by forming structured interfacial layers10,11.

Recently, plant-derived proteins have gained attention as sustainable emulsifiers due to their functional properties and lower allergenicity compared to animal proteins12,13. SPI offers strong interfacial adsorption and thermal stability, whereas PPI contributes to gelation and water-binding capacity. Lepidium sativum L. seed gum (LSG) is a non-toxic, low-cost polysaccharide widely used as a stabilizer and thickening agent14. The synergistic combination of protein isolates and LSG is expected to enhance interfacial structuring, thereby improving droplet stability and controlled release behavior in double nanoemulsion systems. Despite these advances, no study has systematically investigated the incorporation of saffron petal extract-loaded double nanoemulsions stabilized by plant protein–seed gum complexes into a high-moisture dairy matrix such as ricotta cheese.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to engineer and characterize saffron petal extract-loaded double nanoemulsions (DN-SPEs) stabilized by soybean and pea protein isolates, used alone or in combination with Lepidium sativum seed gum, and to evaluate their effectiveness as functional bioactive delivery systems for improving the physicochemical stability, antioxidant capacity, antimicrobial activity, rheological behavior, and refrigerated shelf life of ricotta cheese.

Materials and methods

Materials

Saffron petals were obtained from Khorasan Razavi (Iran). Watercress seeds were purchased from Attari shop in Tehran (Iran), and pea oil and soybean oil (Famila, Iran) were purchased from a local market in Tehran (Iran). SPI was purchased from YUWANG Co. (China). Gram-positive bacteria strain Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 14154) and Gram-negative bacteria strain Escherichia coli (ATCC 12435) were obtained from the Scientific and Industrial Research Organization of Iran. Span 80, culture media and other analytical-grade chemicals were purchased from Merck (Germany).

Preparation of SPE

After drying, saffron petals were ground and sieved through a 20-mesh. The extract was obtained by ultrasound. The plant powder was mixed with 70% ethanol (1:10 w/v), and ultrasound-assisted extraction was performed under controlled conditions (60 °C, 28 kHz, 3 min; ultrasound power 400 W) using an ultrasonic processor (model: UP400S, Hielscher Ultrasonics GmbH, Germany) to ensure reproducibility. The extract was then concentrated under vacuum at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator (model: Heidolph Hei-VAP Precision, Germany)15.

Preparation of LSG

To extract gum from watercress seeds, the seeds were first cleaned and the gum was extracted from the whole seeds at pH 10 using distilled water. The seed-to-water ratio was (1:30 w/v) and the water temperature was maintained at 35 °C. The heating process lasted 15 min and continuous stirring was carried out during this process. The gum was separated from the swollen seeds and the obtained solution was filtered through a vacuum filter and dried in an oven (60 ± 0.5 °C). The dried extracted gum was milled and, after sieving (through a 1-mm mesh), stored in polyethylene bags in a cool and dry place16.

Preparation of PPI

To obtain PPI, fat-free pea flour was first produced by grinding the peas and sieving them through a 60-mesh screen. The resulting flour was defatted by mixing it with n-hexane (1:5, w/v) and stirring the suspension for 1 h at ambient temperature (25 ± 2 °C). The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove the hexane phase containing extracted lipids. The defatted flour was then dispersed in distilled water (1:15, w/v), and the pH was adjusted to 9.5 using 1 N NaOH to solubilize the proteins. The suspension was stirred for 2 h at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and centrifuged again at 8000 × g for 10 min to separate insoluble components. The protein-rich supernatant was acidified to pH 4.5 with 0.5 M HCl to precipitate the proteins, followed by centrifugation at 7000 × g for 20 min. The recovered protein precipitate was freeze-dried and stored in polyethylene zip-seal bags under refrigerated conditions until further use14.

Preparation of DN-SPEs

Aqueous solutions of LSG (0.2%, w/v), SPI (6%, w/v), and PPI (6%, w/v) were prepared separately at pH = 7.0 ± 0.05 in distilled water and stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 2 h to ensure complete dispersion. The selected concentrations of LSG (0.2%) and protein isolates (6%) were based on preliminary optimization and literature evidence, where low LSG levels provide sufficient interfacial stabilization without excessive viscosity, while protein concentrations ≥ 5% are required to form viscoelastic interfacial films and ensure emulsion stability9. The solutions were subsequently stored at 4 ± 0.5 °C for 24 h to allow full hydration and molecular relaxation.

Gum and protein isolate solutions were then mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and stirred at 25 ± 2 °C for 2 h, followed by storage at 4 ± 0.5 °C for 24 h (Ahmadian et al., 2024). This ratio was selected to balance steric stabilization (from LSG) and interfacial film formation (from proteins), thereby enhancing the structural integrity of the double emulsion system. First, a water-in-oil (W/O) nanoemulsion was prepared. Specifically, 7% (w/w) SPE was gradually added to the oil phase composed of 68% (w/w) soybean oil and 25% (w/w) Span 80. The oil-to-emulsifier ratio was deliberately fixed at approximately 3:1 to ensure adequate surface coverage of dispersed aqueous droplets and to minimize coalescence, as insufficient emulsifier levels (< 20%) lead to unstable systems, whereas excessive levels (> 30%) may induce viscosity increase and phase inversion. The mixture was stirred at 1000 rpm until a transparent pre-emulsion was obtained and then homogenized using an Ultratorex homogenizer (8000 rpm, 2 min). The use of 68% soybean oil provides a continuous hydrophobic phase with appropriate viscosity and oxidative stability, while 25% Span 80 (HLB ≈ 4.3) was selected as an optimal lipophilic emulsifier for stabilizing W/O systems by promoting curvature toward the oil phase. To prepare the W/O/W double nanoemulsions, the primary W/O emulsion (30%, w/w) was gradually dispersed into the external aqueous phase (70%, w/w). Homogenization was performed using an Ultratorex homogenizer (12,000 rpm, 5 min), followed by probe sonication (20 kHz, 80 W, 3 min; pulse mode: 20 s on/3 s off). All emulsions were prepared in triplicate (n = 3), and the processing temperature was maintained below 30 ± 0.5 °C to prevent thermal denaturation of proteins. The resulting double nanoemulsions were denoted as DN-SPE-S, DN-SPE-P, DN-SPE-SL, and DN-SPE-PL.

Measurement of particle size and zeta potential

The size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were determined using a dynamic light scattering instrument (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK)17.

Measurement of encapsulation efficiency (EE)

The EE of nanoemulsions was determined based on the measuring the surface phenolic content and total phenolic content. For this purpose, 200 mg of each sample was mixed with 2 mL of methanol-acetic acid-water solution (ratio v/v/v 50:8:42). After stirring for 1 min, the mixture was sonicated for two cycles of 20 min at a frequency of 20 kHz. The samples were then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min and the total phenolic content in the supernatant was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. Morphology study

The surface morphology and microstructural features of the developed films were examined using a light microscope (BX53, Olympus Corporation, Japan) equipped with a high-resolution digital camera. Prior to imaging, film samples were cut into uniform Sect. (10 × 10 mm) and conditioned at 25 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 5% relative humidity for 48 h to minimize moisture-induced variability. The samples were placed on clean glass slides without any chemical treatment to preserve their native structure. Micrographs were captured at different magnifications (×10, ×40, and ×100) under consistent illumination conditions to ensure comparability between samples. At least three independent regions of each film were analyzed to account for structural heterogeneity, and representative images were selected for presentation. Special attention was given to the evaluation of surface homogeneity, phase distribution, pore formation, and the presence of aggregates, which are critical indicators of polymer compatibility and nanoemulsion dispersion within the biopolymer matrix (Abedinia et al., 2021).

Study of the stability of antioxidant activity during storage period

To measure the antioxidant activity of nanoemulsions and the free extract of saffron petals, the DPPH (1, 1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging method was used19, and the measurement was carried out on days 1 and 30 of storage at refrigerated temperature (4 ± 0.5 °C). To 0.2 mL of the sample, 0.1 mmol/L DPPH ethanolic solution (4 mL) was added, followed by distilled water (8 mL). After incubating the mixtures at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) in the dark for 30 min, absorbance was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) (As). FTIR spectroscopy

The chemical interactions of nanoemulsions, extract and wall materials were investigated using an FTIR spectrophotometer (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) in the range of 400 to 4000 cm− 1 and with a resolution of 4 cm− 1 and a scan speed of 1 cm/s18.

Investigation of X-ray diffraction pattern

The crystallinity of the nanoemulsions was investigated using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) at a scan speed of 0.04 °/min and in the 2ɵ range of 5 to 70 °17.

Investigation of thermal stability

The thermal stability of the capsules was investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (TGA Q500, TA Instruments, USA). The sample with an approximate weight of 20 mg was firmly attached to an aluminum pan and the sample was heated at a rate of 10 °C/min in the temperature range of 30 to 600 °C and a nitrogen flow rate of 30 mL/min was used20.

Investigation of antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the free extract as well as the encapsulated extracts against the Gram-positive bacterial strain S. aureus (ATCC 14154) and the Gram-negative bacterial strain E. coli (ATCC 12435) was evaluated using the disc diffusion agar method2. Fresh bacterial cultures were prepared and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard turbidity (1.5 × 10⁸ ± 0.2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL), corresponding to an absorbance of 0.10 ± 0.02 at 625 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Sterile Müller–Hinton agar plates were inoculated with 100 ± 2 µL of the standardized bacterial suspension using a sterile spreader to ensure uniform distribution. Sterile paper discs (6.0 ± 0.1 mm diameter) were impregnated with the test samples by loading 20 ± 1 µL per disc of free extract or nanoemulsion formulations at the corresponding experimental concentrations. The discs were allowed to dry under controlled aseptic conditions at 25 ± 2 °C for 30 ± 2 min to ensure complete solvent evaporation and uniform diffusion characteristics. It should be noted that slight variations in disc geometry were observed due to swelling and softening of the biopolymeric matrix upon contact with the moist agar surface; however, all discs were prepared with identical initial dimensions to maintain experimental consistency. The impregnated discs were then placed on the inoculated agar surface at equal distances. Plates were incubated at 37 ± 0.5 °C for 24 ± 1 h. Following incubation, the diameter of the inhibition zones was measured using a digital caliper with a precision of ± 0.01 mm, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (mm, n = 3).

Preparation of ricotta cheese treatments

The concentration of DN-SPEs incorporated into ricotta cheese was fixed at 1% (w/w), based on preliminary screening tests (data not shown) evaluating emulsion stability, antioxidant efficacy, and sensory compatibility within the cheese matrix. Although ricotta cheese is conventionally obtained from whey through heat–acid coagulation of whey proteins, in the present study a milk-based model system was intentionally designed to ensure compositional standardization and experimental reproducibility. This approach allows precise control over total solids, protein composition, and mineral balance, which are key factors influencing gel network formation, moisture retention, and bioactive compound interactions within the cheese matrix. Accordingly, reconstituted whole milk powder was used as a controlled dairy substrate to simulate a standardized ricotta-like matrix, enabling systematic evaluation of structure–function relationships without variability associated with whey-derived raw materials. This strategy is widely adopted in functional dairy system studies when the objective is controlled delivery and mechanistic assessment rather than industrial replication. To produce ricotta cheese, whole milk powder (120 ± 0.5 g/L) was dissolved in distilled water and the pH was adjusted to 7.00 ± 0.02 using sodium hydroxide. The solution was heated at 90 ± 1 °C for 30 ± 1 min, followed by the addition of citric acid (25.0 ± 0.5 mL/L) to induce protein coagulation. Curds were collected and molded (20.0 ± 0.1 mm × 43.0 ± 0.1 mm) to facilitate whey separation. The obtained cheese matrix was cooled and subsequently supplemented with free SPE or DN-SPEs at 1.00 ± 0.01% (w/w). All treatments were prepared in three independent batches (100.0 ± 0.5 g each) and stored at 4 ± 0.5 °C for 14 days. Analyses were conducted on days 0, 7, and 1421.

The pH measurement was performed using a pH meter (Metrohm 827 pH Lab, Switzerland) at 20 °C7. Acidity was determined by titration with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide2. The moisture content of the cheeses was measured using the oven method at 105 degrees Celsius (until constant weight was reached) (AOAC, 2000). The color of the cheese samples, including the lightness (L*), green-red (a*) and blue-yellow (b*) indices, was examined using a colorimeter. Measurement of antioxidant activity

The DPPH free radical scavenging activity method was used to evaluate the antioxidant activity of cheese samples22. Cheese extract was prepared by mixing 10 g of cheese with 90 mL methanol at 25 °C. After stirring and centrifugation at 10,000 rpm, the resulting supernatant was used for antioxidant assessment. Then, 0.5 mL of each cheese extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of DPPH methanolic solution and stirred thoroughly. After 60 min at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan).

Measurement of peroxide index

To determine the peroxide index of cheeses, the oil of each sample was first extracted. In this way, 10 g of each sample was mixed with 20 mL of chloroform and 40 mL of methanol for 2 min, and then 20 mL of chloroform and 20 mL of methanol were added to it. The resulting mixtures were filtered through Whatman filter paper No. 1 and their solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator, and the sample oil was obtained. After that, 0.1 g of each sample oil was mixed in 25 mL of a chloroform–glacial acetic acid mixture (v/v 2:3), and saturated potassium iodide (1 mL) was added to it. After the mixture was kept in the dark and at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 10 min, 20 mL of distilled water and then 1.5% starch solution were added and titration was performed with 0.02 N sodium thiosulfate23.

Investigation of rheological properties

The rheological properties of cheeses, including apparent viscosity, loss modulus (Gʺ) and storage modulus (Gʹ), were investigated using a controlled-stress rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar, Austria) in the frequency range of 0.1–100 Hz. The samples were allowed to rest on the rheometer for 20 min to eliminate the effect of any applied stresses applied to them. The linear viscoelastic range was determined by performing a strain sweep test. For this purpose, the frequency was set at 0.1 Hz and the strain percentage was varied from 0.01 to 2. Then, a strain value in the linear range (0.02) was selected, and a frequency sweep test was performed, in which the strain was set at 0.02 and the frequency was adjusted from 0.1 to 100 Hz21.

Examination of textural parameters

Textural parameters of cheese, including firmness (N), cohesiveness, gumminess (N) and chewiness (N.m) were examined using a texture analyzer (TA.XTplus, Stable Micro Systems, UK) and a cylindrical probe (height 20 mm and diameter 1.38 mm). The samples were first molded in dimensions of 20 mm × 20 mm. The shear rate was 1 mm/s and the maximum penetration rate was 10 mm (Azarashkan et al., 2022).

Microbial analysis

First, the required serial dilutions were prepared from each cheese sample. For the initial dilution, 10.0 ± 0.01 g of each sample was aseptically homogenized with 90.0 ± 0.1 mL of sterile peptone water (0.1%, w/v) for 60 ± 1 s using a stomacher (BagMixer 400, Interscience, France), followed by decimal serial dilutions. Plate Count Agar (PCA) was used for total viable count enumeration, and plates were incubated at 30 ± 0.5 °C for 48 ± 2 h. For mold and yeast enumeration, Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) acidified with sterile tartaric acid (final pH 3.5 ± 0.1) was used to selectively inhibit bacterial growth, in accordance with common practices for high-moisture dairy products (Rehim et al., 2023). The corresponding plates were incubated at 25 ± 1 °C for 5 ± 0.5 days. Staphylococcus aureus counts were determined using Baird–Parker agar following ISO6888-1.202124, with incubation at 37 ± 1 °C for 48 ± 2 h. Escherichia coli enumeration was performed using a Most Probable Number (MPN) approach based on Lauryl Sulfate Tryptose (LST) broth for presumptive detection, followed by confirmation in EC broth, in accordance with ISO 16649-2:200125. A three-tube MPN scheme was employed for each dilution level. Tubes showing gas production after incubation at 37 ± 0.5 °C for 24 ± 2 h in LST broth were considered presumptive positive and subsequently transferred to EC broth and incubated at 44 ± 0.5 °C for 24 ± 2 h for confirmation of thermotolerant E. coli. Additionally, positive tubes were streaked onto Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar for colony morphology verification, and indole production was assessed using Kovac’s reagent as a biochemical confirmatory test. The results were calculated based on standard MPN tables and expressed as log MPN/g20. All microbiological analyses were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (log CFU/g) to ensure statistical robustness and reproducibility.

Sensory evaluation

Evaluation of sensory parameters of cheeses, including taste, texture, color, odor and overall acceptability, was conducted using a 5-point hedonic scale with 20 panelists. Cheese samples were randomly assigned and equally sized in white, coded plastic containers, and panelists were provided with initial instructions on how to evaluate the samples26. This study does not involve the use of animals or humans and only involves the use of a sensory panel that was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from the sensory assessors and all evaluation procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis of data

A completely randomized experimental design was used in this study. Nanoemulsion data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 software and the one-way ANOVA, while cheese quality parameters were evaluated using two-way ANOVA (treatment × storage time). Normality and homoscedasticity were verified prior to analysis. Duncan’s test was applied for post-hoc separation (p < 0.05).

DN-SPE-P exhibited the largest particle size (298.4 ± 5.7 nm) and the highest PDI (0.316 ± 0.011), indicating a relatively heterogeneous droplet distribution. In contrast, DN-SPE-S showed a significantly smaller particle size (226.6 ± 3.8 nm) with a lower PDI (0.266 ± 0.017), suggesting improved emulsification efficiency. The incorporation of LSG further reduced droplet size and improved homogeneity, with DN-SPE-SL showing the smallest particle size (206.3 ± 3.5 nm) and a relatively low PDI (0.280 ± 0.013), confirming enhanced colloidal stability (p < 0.05).

The reduction in droplet size can be attributed to stronger protein–polysaccharide interactions, which improve interfacial adsorption and prevent droplet coalescence through steric stabilization mechanisms27. Similarly, Hasani, Ojagh28 reported that polysaccharide incorporation enhances electrostatic repulsion and reduces aggregation of protein-based nanoparticles. Comparable trends were observed by Ahmadian, Kenari9, who demonstrated smaller droplet sizes in SPI–chia gum systems compared to SPI alone. Aniya, Cao10 also confirmed that polysaccharide-stabilized double emulsions exhibit reduced droplet size due to improved interfacial film strength.

The zeta potential values ranged from − 25.3 ± 0.5 mV (DN-SPE-P) to − 32.5 ± 0.8 mV (DN-SPE-SL). Increasing the magnitude of negative surface charge in gum-containing systems indicates stronger electrostatic repulsion between droplets, which contributes to enhanced physical stability and reduced aggregation tendency29. Generally, emulsions with absolute zeta potential values above ± 30 mV are considered electrostatically stable; thus, DN-SPE-SL can be classified as the most stable formulation. This observation is consistent with Ahmadian, Kenari9 and Wang, Yang30, who reported similar improvements in surface charge upon polysaccharide–protein complexation.

Overall, the combined protein isolate–LSG system provided the most stable nanoemulsion, characterized by reduced droplet size, improved homogeneity, higher surface charge magnitude, and enhanced encapsulation efficiency, confirming the synergistic role of protein–polysaccharide interactions in nanoemulsion stabilization.

EE of nanoemulsions

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) is a critical indicator of the structural integrity and loading capacity of nanoemulsion systems. In the present study, EE values were significantly influenced by the type of biopolymer coating and their interfacial interactions (Table 1).

Among single-protein systems, DN-SPE-S (85.30 ± 1.39%) exhibited a significantly higher EE than DN-SPE-P (81.64 ± 1.10%) (p < 0.05). This improvement can be attributed to the more flexible molecular architecture of SPI, which facilitates the formation of a denser and more cohesive interfacial film compared to PPI, thereby enhancing retention of the encapsulated core material. Statistical analysis (one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s test, p < 0.05) confirmed significant differences among all formulations, as indicated by different superscript letters in Table 1. Incorporation of LSG into protein-based systems markedly enhanced EE. DN-SPE-SL showed the highest EE (89.52 ± 0.94%), followed by DN-SPE-PL (87.14 ± 1.17%). This enhancement reflects synergistic protein–polysaccharide interactions, which strengthen the interfacial viscoelastic network and reduce mass transfer of the entrapped compounds.

Compared with single-protein coatings, composite biopolymer systems generate a more compact interfacial layer, which effectively suppresses droplet coalescence and minimizes leakage of bioactive compounds under environmental stress conditions18. Furthermore, the presence of LSG increases the viscosity of the continuous phase, promoting the formation of a stronger steric barrier and improving the physical stability of droplets31. Similar trends have been reported in previous studies, where protein–polysaccharide complexes significantly improved EE in double emulsion systems containing anthocyanins10 and plant extracts9. Additionally, Li, Geng27 demonstrated that protein–polysaccharide interfacial complexes enhance emulsion stability through improved electrostatic and steric stabilization mechanisms. Overall, the progressive increase in EE from single-protein to composite systems confirms the dominant role of interfacial reinforcement and polymer synergy in improving nanoemulsion performance.

Surface morphology of nanoemulsions

The results of the morphology study of DN-SPEs by fluorescence microscopy are given in Fig. 1A and show that the extract is effectively encapsulated first by the oil phase and subsequently by the aqueous phase. A noticeable dispersion in droplet size was observed; however, nanoemulsions stabilized solely with protein isolates exhibited greater size variability compared with those stabilized with LSG-protein combinations. This is due to the gum-induced increase in viscosity that improves droplet stability and reduces aggregation. The most uniform droplet distribution and highest structural stability were observed in DN-SPE-SL, which aligns with the zeta potential results. Similar trends were reported by Guzmán-Díaz et al.31, where double nanoemulsions of green tea extract stabilized with chia seed gum and whey protein concentrate showed uniform spherical droplets without aggregation. Harimurti et al.32 also reported a spherical, homogeneous droplets in double nanoemulsions of dragon fruit extract, with some droplet size dispersion similar to that observed in this study.

Figure 1B shows the apparent stability of the nanoemulsions during two weeks of storage at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). On day 1, all formulations remained physically stable with no flocculation or creaming. By day 14, phase separation appeared in DN-SPE-S and DN-SPE-PL, and DN-SPE-P further showed visible creaming. In contrast, DN-SPE-SL exhibited neither phase separation nor creaming throughout the 14-day storage period, making it the most stable formulation. Aniya et al.10 similarly reported that double emulsions stabilized with PPI alone became creamy and flocculated after 20 days, whereas no flocculation occurred in emulsions stabilized with combined PPI–xanthan gum during the same period. Research indicates that in protein-stabilized emulsions, larger droplets tend to migrate toward the oil layer while smaller droplets disperse within the protein matrix, leading to phase separation. However, combining proteins with gum increases the viscosity of the aqueous phase and thickens the interfacial film, restricting droplet mobility and enhancing resistance to flocculation and phase separation14.

Stability of antioxidant activity of nanoemulsions during storage

The antioxidant activity of DN-SPEs stabilized with different biopolymers was investigated using the conventional DPPH radical scavenging method, and the stability of antioxidant activity of nanoemulsions was also investigated during 30 days of storage at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), and the results are presented in Fig. 1C. On the first day of storage, the DPPH radical scavenging activity of the free extract was 83.31%, indicating strong inherent antioxidant capacity. Phenolic and flavonoid compounds typically exhibit considerable antioxidant activity due to their hydroxyl groups capable of donating electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals, decompose peroxides, and quench reactive oxygen species18. Saffron petals are a source of various bioactive compounds such as crocin, crocin, and kaempferol, which are significant antioxidants and reduce oxidative stress6. The significant antioxidant activity of SPE was also confirmed by Naim et al.5. Encapsulation of SPE in DN-SPE-S (31.83%) or DN-SPE-PL (83.49%) did not significantly alter its DPPH radical scavenging activity, whereas DN-SPE-SL (85.31%) enhanced the antioxidant activity and DN-SPE-P (79.60%) reduced it (p < 0.05). The superior activity of DN-SPE-SL is attributed to its higher encapsulation efficiency, which allows greater retention of phenolic compounds compared with the other nanoemulsions. During the 30-day storage period, the antioxidant activity of the samples decreased (p < 0.05). As expected, the free extract exhibited the sharpest decline, reaching the lowest antioxidant activity (71.08%) on day 30. In contrast, DN-SPE-SL showed the highest antioxidant activity on the final day (82.96%). Microencapsulation within an emulsion system protects the core bioactive compound from oxidative degradation by limiting its exposure to oxygen, light, and peroxides, thereby slowing the loss of antioxidant capacity relative to the free extract18. This stabilization effect demonstrates that the combination of protein isolates with LSG enhances the structural integrity of the nanoemulsion, improving retention and controlled release of saffron petal polyphenols, thereby increasing their bioavailability. Consistent with these findings, Guzmán-Díaz et al.31 reported higher antioxidant activity in green tea double nanoemulsions stabilized with chia seed gum and whey protein concentrate compare to gum alone. Similarly, Pasban Noghabi33 found that a combined whey protein–maltodextrin wall material provided superior protection for saffron petal extract bioactives compared to single coatings.

The superior antioxidant and antimicrobial performance of DN-SPEs arises from their nano-scale interfacial behavior. Small-scale interfacial interactions between DN-SPEs create their superior antioxidant and antimicrobial performance. The protective effect of encapsulation stops phenolic hydroxyl groups from oxidation at an early stage while making more surface area available for radical scavenging. The protein–polysaccharide interfacial membrane results in improved structural stability and controlled release which leads to enhanced bioavailability of active compounds. At the microbial level, the small droplet size enables closer interaction with bacterial cell walls, promoting membrane disruption, leakage of intracellular components, and inhibition of essential metabolic pathways. The combined operation of these mechanisms leads to higher bioactivity in DN-SPE formulations than the free extract34.

FTIR spectroscopy of nanoemulsions

FTIR spectroscopy was used to investigate the active and functional groups present in SPE, SPI, PPI and DN-SPEs stabilized with these biopolymers and the corresponding spectra are shown in Fig. 2A. In the FTIR spectrum of the extract, the peak in the 3200 cm− 1 was attributed to the stretching vibrations of the N-H and O-H bonds and the peaks in the 2900 –2800 cm− 1 region were associated with aliphatic C-H bond35. The peak in the 1750 cm− 1 corresponded to C = O stretching vibrations and the peak in the 1600 cm− 1 region is related to the C = C bond of the phenolic and aromatic groups. The peaks in the 1360, 1120 and 740 cm− 1 assigned to C-H bending, C-O-C stretching and aromatic C-H, respectively36. In the FTIR spectrum of protein isolates, two distinct peaks appeared in the 1600–1500 cm⁻¹ region, corresponding to C = O stretching vibrations (amide I) and N–H bending vibrations (amide II). In addition, relatively weak peaks observed around 1200 cm⁻¹ indicated N–H bending vibrations associated with amide III bands. These bands were also reported in the FTIR spectrum of protein isolates in the research of Ahmadian et al.9 and Estrada-Fernández et al.37. In the FTIR spectrum of cress seed gum, the peaks in the 1600 –1400 cm− 1 region attributed to the COO symmetric stretching vibrations of uronic acid, and the peak in the 1120 cm− 1 region was assigned to the C-O-C stretching vibrations of the glycosidic bond (1←4) and the C-O-H bending vibrations, which are typical of polysaccharide structures38. In the FTIR spectra of the extract nanoemulsions, the same peaks found in the extract and biopolymers were also present. However, the intensity of the hydroxyl peak (around 3200 cm⁻¹) decreased, and this reduction was more pronounced in nanoemulsions stabilized with combined protein-isolate coatings than in those stabilized with a single protein isolate. This reduction is attributed to the formation of stronger hydrogen bonds between protein and polysaccharide molecules, which limits the number of free hydroxyl groups, indicating enhanced molecular interactions and a more cohesive nanoemulsion network. Similar findings were reported by Ahmadian et al.9. A reduction in the intensity of the hydroxyl peak of SPE loaded into zein nanofibers compared with the free extract was also documented by Najafi et al.39. Thus, FTIR analysis not only confirms the presence of functional groups but also indicates molecular interactions responsible for enhanced stability in nanoemulsions.

XRD pattern of nanoemulsions

A study of the XRD pattern results of the extract double nanoemulsions (Fig. 2B) showed that a weak peak was observed in the region of 2θ = 13.68°, which is related to the crystalline structure of the proteins. The intensity of this peak in the nanoemulsion stabilized with SPI alone was higher than that of PPI alone, reflecting greater relative crystallinity of the SPI structure. In all nanoemulsions, the intensity of this peak was relatively low, which can be attributed to the emulsification process, causing partial disruption of the crystalline order and enhancement of the amorphous regions in the biopolymer matrix. A prominent peak was observed in all patterns in the region of 2θ = 19.91°, corresponding to the amorphous phase. These results indicate that the structure of the double nanoemulsions produced in this study was predominantly amorphous, with minor crystalline domains. The combination of protein isolates with LSG resulted in sharper and more intense crystalline peaks, indicating an increase in crystallinity and structural order due to the polymeric nature of the gum. The highest crystalline peak intensity was associated with DN-SPE-SL, correlating with its enhanced stability and coherence. Devi et al.8 also reported that anthocyanin double nanoemulsions prepared by combining gelatin and acacia gum exhibited increased crystallinity with XRD peaks in the 2θ region of 19.37–20.87°.

Thermal stability of nanoemulsions

The thermal stability of DN-SPEs was investigated based on the weight loss pattern as a function of temperature by TGA test and the resulting thermograms are shown in Fig. 3. As can be seen in the figure, all samples lost weight in three main stages. The first stage at a temperature of about 50–150 °C was related to the evaporation of free and absorbed moisture in the nanoemulsion structure. The second stage, in which the highest amount of weight loss occurred, was observed in the temperature range of 210–330 °C. In this stage, the decomposition of volatile compounds present in the extract as well as the majority of proteins and polysaccharides occurred. Examination of the resulting curves showed that the samples containing LSG had a higher weight loss onset temperature and maximum weight loss temperature than the samples without gum. The highest thermal stability was associated with DN-SPE-SL, whose weight loss onset temperature and maximum weight loss temperature were 235 °C and 330 °C, respectively. In addition, the residue percentage at 500 °C in this nanoemulsion was estimated to be about 12%, which exceeded that of the other samples. In general, DN-SPE-S showed better stability than DN-SPE-P, and the combination of SPI with LSG also effectively improved the thermal stability of this protein isolate. The effect of gum on improving the thermal stability of the nanoemulsion is likely due to the formation of hydrogen and electrostatic bonds with the protein, resulting in a more stable network around the nanoemulsion10. The superior thermal stability of nanoparticles formed by gum compared to preine isolate was also observed in the study of Yao et al.40.

Antimicrobial activity of nanoemulsions

The antibacterial activity of free SPE and its stabilized double nanoemulsions against the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli and the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus was evaluated using the agar disk diffusion assay. To improve clarity and enable direct comparison, the inhibition zone diameters are presented in Fig. 4 as mean ± standard deviation (mm). As shown, the inhibition zones against E. coli remained nearly constant across all treatments (12.74 ± 0.03 to 12.98 ± 0.04 mm), indicating no statistically significant enhancement (p > 0.05) following nanoemulsification. In contrast, a marked and statistically significant increase (p < 0.05) was observed against S. aureus, where inhibition zones increased from 12.36 ± 0.05 mm (free SPE) to 16.19 ± 0.06 mm in DN-SPE-SL.

Phenolic compounds constitute the primary bioactive agents responsible for the antimicrobial activity of plant extracts. These compounds, particularly flavonoids with hydroxyl functionalities, interact with membrane proteins and intracellular targets, disrupting peptidoglycan integrity and interfering with essential enzymatic systems2. Similar antimicrobial effects of SPE have been attributed to its phenolic, aromatic, and monoterpene constituents5,41,42.

Microbiological safety in dairy products is achieved through rigorous regulatory frameworks that set specific pathogen and hygiene indicator limits to ensure consumer protection. The European Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005 establishes criteria for key pathogens in dairy products, with studies showing high compliance rates. Specifically, regulations mandate: (i) absence of Salmonella spp. in 25 g samples, (ii) limits for E. coli (typically < 10³–10⁵ CFU/g), and (iii) restrictions on coagulase-positive staphylococci. Available evidence suggests that a high proportion of cheese samples comply with these microbiological criteria; however, compliance rates are strongly influenced by factors such as milk origin, processing conditions, and hygienic practices. For instance, Primavilla, Roila43 reported a compliance rate of 97.4% under specific production conditions, which should not be generalized across all cheese types or processing systems.

The results of the present study also showed that SPE nanoemulsions exhibited enhanced antibacterial activity against the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus compared to the free extract, which can be attributed to the higher surface-to-volume ratio, enhanced reactivity, more controlled release, and longer effective contact time between nanoscale particles and bacterial cells44. These findings indicate that the nanoemulsion system, particularly when stabilized by protein–polysaccharide combinations, enhances antimicrobial efficacy and increases the bioavailability of active polyphenolic compounds. Nanoemulsions in general can result in more efficient cellular uptake of the extract and facilitate its entry into bacterial cells18. Improved antibacterial activity of nettle and wormwood extracts after encapsulation in nanoemulsions was also reported by Rahmani et al.45. Bains et al.46 also found that double nanoemulsions of red rice extract stabilized by gum arabic had antibacterial activity against bacteria compared to the free extract. The research findings demonstrate that S. aureus experiences stronger inhibition than E. coli because their bacterial cell walls have distinct structural characteristics. Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick but porous peptidoglycan layer that allows easier penetration of phenolic compounds and nanoemulsion droplets. The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria including E. coli contains lipopolysaccharides, which restricts the passage of hydrophobic and moderately polar bioactives18. The interaction between SPE phenolics and nanoemulsion interfaces with the cytoplasmic membrane of E. coli leads to poor results which prevents any major antibacterial effect from occurring. Of course, this method provides a semi-quantitative assessment and it is recommended that more quantitative assessments such as MIC, MBC, or time-kill curves, or CFU reduction in a food model, which can provide better analysis, be considered in future studies.

pH and titratable acidity of cheeses

Table 2 shows the changes in pH and titratable acidity of ricotta cheese during 14 days of refrigerated storage (4 ± 0.5 °C). At day 0, the control sample exhibited the highest pH (5.70 ± 0.02) and the lowest acidity (0.39 ± 0.01%), whereas incorporation of SPE significantly reduced pH (5.63–5.65 ± 0.01) and increased acidity (0.45–0.49 ± 0.02) (p < 0.05), with no significant differences among fortified treatments. This initial acidification can be attributed to the presence of phenolic acids in SPE, particularly chlorogenic acid, which may contribute to increased proton release and slight acidification of the cheese matrix47. During storage, all samples exhibited a progressive decrease in pH and a concomitant increase in titratable acidity (p < 0.05), although the magnitude of change was significantly lower in SPE-fortified samples compared to the control. By day 14, the control reached the lowest pH (5.42 ± 0.03) and highest acidity (0.97 ± 0.02%), indicating intensified post-acidification reactions. These changes are mainly driven by residual microbial metabolism, including lactose fermentation to lactic acid22, and lipolytic activity leading to free fatty acid release48. Importantly, SPE-treated samples showed a reduced rate of acidification, which may be associated with the antimicrobial activity of plant-derived bioactives, limiting microbial growth and metabolic acid production, as also reported in ricotta systems enriched with plant extracts2,26.

Moisture content of cheeses

Moisture content significantly influenced the physicochemical stability of ricotta cheese during storage (Table 2). At day 0, the control showed the lowest moisture content (53.10 ± 0.12%), while DN-SPE-fortified samples exhibited higher values (53.62–53.79 ± 0.10%), with no significant differences among encapsulated treatments (p > 0.05). The increase in moisture retention in DN-SPE samples may be associated with improved water-binding capacity induced by encapsulation, which enhances matrix–water interactions. During storage at 4 ± 0.5 °C, moisture content gradually decreased in all samples (p < 0.05), with the most pronounced reduction observed in the control. At day 14, the control reached 51.27 ± 0.15%, whereas DN-SPE samples retained significantly higher moisture levels (52.78–53.18 ± 0.11%). This moisture loss is primarily attributed to syneresis and water migration from the protein network to the surface followed by evaporation. However, phenolic compounds and biopolymeric carriers in DN-SPE may enhance water immobilization through hydrogen bonding, reducing free water mobility49. Similar improvements in moisture retention have been reported in dairy matrices fortified with plant-derived encapsulates, protein isolates, and hydrocolloids, which strengthen the gel network and reduce whey separation50–52.

Antioxidant activity of cheeses

The antioxidant activity of different ricotta cheese treatments in this study was investigated using the conventional DPPH free radical scavenging activity method. Table 2 shows the changes in DPPH free radical scavenging activity values of ricotta cheese during a 14-day refrigerated storage period (4 ± 0.5 °C). At the beginning of storage, the control sample exhibited the lowest antioxidant activity (20.81%). The control cheese still showed measurable antioxidant activity, attributable to whey-derived serum proteins such as beta-lactoglobulin and alpha-lactalbumin, as well as bioactive peptides and soluble amino acids with antioxidant properties53. Incorporation of different forms of SPE significantly increased (p < 0.05) DPPH radical scavenging activity, ranging from 51.88% to 57.23%. On this day, among fortified treatments, the highest antioxidant activity was observed in cheese containing DN-SPE-SL (57.23%), while the lowest was in cheese with DN-SPE-P (51.88%). Variations in antioxidant activity among fortified cheeses depended on the intrinsic antioxidant potential of the additive used.

During storage, particularly in the later days, a significant decline in antioxidant activity (p < 0.05) was observed, likely due to the participation of antioxidants in lipid and protein oxidation and their gradual inactivation21. Among SPE forms, the free extract exhibited the greatest decrease in antioxidant activity during storage. On the last day of storage, the control cheese had the lowest antioxidant activity (11.25%), whereas the highest was recorded for DN-SPE-SL–enriched cheese (48.60%). In agreement with the results of this study, El Gamal et al.53 also reported a gradual decrease in the antioxidant activity of ricotta cheese during a 28-day storage period at refrigerated temperature (4 ± 0.5 °C) and observed a significant effect of curcumin on improving the antioxidant activity of this cheese. In the study of Aktypis et al.54, the incorporation of saffron extract improved the antioxidant activity of the cheese. The significant effect of the microencapsulation process on maintaining the antioxidant activity of ricotta cheese enriched with broccoli sprout extract was also shown in the study of Azarashkan et al.21, and these researchers also reported a decrease in the antioxidant activity of ricotta cheese during cold storage. Lipșa et al.55 also showed that during a 21-day storage period, the antioxidant activity of ricotta cheeses decreased significantly (p < 0.05), and the addition of different levels of red onion peel powder was able to significantly improve the antioxidant activity of this cheese (p < 0.05).

Peroxide index of cheeses

Lipid oxidation is a major cause of quality deterioration in high-fat foods, adversely affecting taste, odor, texture, color, and nutritional value, ultimately leading to consumer rejection. The peroxide index is a widely used method for assessing oxidative rancidity in fat-rich foods, measuring primary products of lipid oxidation (hydroperoxides)56. Table 2 presents changes in peroxide index values of ricotta cheese during refrigerated storage (4 ± 0.5 °C). At the beginning of storage, peroxide index values ranged from 0.26 to 0.31 meq/kg among different treatments, with no statistically significant differences observed (p > 0.05). During storage, lipid oxidation and hydroperoxide formation caused a gradual increase in the peroxide index of all cheese samples. The greater oxidation observed in the control sample compared to extract-enriched treatments is likely due to its lower antioxidant activity. At the end of storage, nanoemulsion-enriched treatments exhibited lower peroxide indices than the free extract form, reflecting the protective effect of microencapsulation on bioactive compounds. On day 14, the control sample had the highest peroxide index (1.52 meq/kg), while the lowest value was observed in cheese containing DN-SPE-SL (0.71 meq/kg). An increase in the peroxide index of cheese during the storage period was also shown in the study of Saravani et al.23. Gadallah et al.57 also reported a significant effect of rosemary essential oil nanoemulsion on reducing the peroxide index of cream. In the study of Hosseini et al.26, an increase in the peroxide index of ricotta cheese was also observed during the storage period, and these researchers were also able to successfully reduce the rate of hydroperoxide production in this cheese using chia seed extract, especially its nanoencapsulated form. Abd El-Galeel and El-Zahar58 also used plant extracts to reduce the rate of hydroperoxide production in fresh cheese in their study.

In general, initial color changes in cheese due to SPE can be attributed to anthocyanin pigments in the extract. Rheological properties of cheese

In this study, the rheological properties of ricotta cheese treatments, including apparent viscosity, storage modulus, and loss modulus, were investigated as a function of shear rate. Figure 5A shows the changes in apparent viscosity of ricotta cheese samples enriched with free extract and SPE double nanoemulsions under increasing shear rates. As can be seen in this graph, the apparent viscosity of all samples decreased sharply with increasing shear rate, hence, all samples exhibited quasi-plastic (shear-thinning) behavior. This behavior is due to the gradual disruption of the network structure against the application of shear force, which is quite common in fresh dairy products62. Among them, the control sample and the sample containing free extract had the lowest viscosity. Incorporation of double nanoemulsions stabilized with PPI and SPI significantly increased apparent viscosity compared to the control (p < 0.05). Of these two protein isolates, the sample containing PPI showed higher viscosity than SPI at all shear rates, especially at low speeds, likely reflecting PPI’s greater ability to form protein–water and protein–protein interactions and its higher water retention capacity in the cheese matrix63. The highest apparent viscosity was generally associated with the cheese containing DN-SPE-PL. These results indicate that stronger synergistic interactions may have occurred between PPI and LSG and a stronger network structure was formed. In general, the presence of LSG together with protein isolates resulted in the formation of a stable and quasi-continuous gel network in the cheese matrix, which failed later and had a milder viscosity drop when subjected to shear.

In contrast, Azarashkan et al.21 reported a reduction in ricotta cheese viscosity after incorporation of broccoli sprout extract nanocapsules prepared with basil seed gum. The difference between their results and the present study is likely due to the nature of the additive; they used extract nanocapsule powder, whereas saffron petal extract was incorporated here as gel double nanoemulsions. The decrease in viscosity with increasing angular frequency was also reported by Hesarinejad et al.64. The thickening effect of gum in increasing the viscosity of PPI-stabilized astaxanthin emulsions was also confirmed by Boonlao et al.29.

In Fig. 5B, and C, the loss (G″) and storage (G’) moduli are presented as a function of angular frequency. The loss modulus represents the viscous (flowable) part of the material, i.e., the energy dissipated as heat during each deformation cycle. The storage model represents the elastic part in the material and represents the energy that is stored during each deformation cycle and causes the network to recover65. The results showed that the dynamic-mechanical properties of ricotta cheese were generally affected by the incorporation of DN-SPEs. With increasing angular frequency, both loss and storage moduli increased in all samples, confirming the gel-like and quasi-solid nature of the cheese. At high frequencies, the polymer chains do not have enough time to relax, while at low frequencies, relaxation occurs, hence, moduli increase with increasing frequency66. Comparison between treatments showed that the addition of DN-SPE-SL and DN-SPE-PL significantly increased both moduli compared to the control and free extract cheese (p < 0.05). Among them, the cheese containing DN-SPE-PL had the highest values of these moduli, followed by the cheese containing DN-SPE-SL. These results indicate strengthened protein networks and enhanced gel structure in the presence of these stabilized nanoemulsions.

In general, the samples with LSG, due to having higher values of both moduli, are more elastic and more viscous than other formulations, contributing to improved texture and better consistency during processing and storage. The results also showed that in all samples, G’ was higher than G” at all frequencies, indicating dominant elastic behavior and strong protein–protein interactions62. Joshi et al.67 also found in their study that cheeses containing higher protein content have higher values of loss and storage modulus. The higher storage modulus than the shrinkage modulus of ricotta cheese was also reported by Rubel et al.68.

Textural parameters of cheese

Table 4 shows the textural parameters of ricotta cheese during storage. At day 0, the control sample exhibited hardness, gumminess, and chewiness values of 12.02 ± 0.18 N, 4.93 ± 0.11 N, and 5.96 ± 0.14 N·m, respectively. Incorporation of free SPE slightly reduced hardness (11.35 ± 0.16 N), gumminess (4.21 ± 0.10 N), and chewiness (5.13 ± 0.12 N·m), indicating a weakening of the protein matrix in the early stage. In contrast, cheeses enriched with SPE nanoemulsions showed significantly higher textural strength than the control, with hardness ranging from 13.29 ± 0.21 to 15.24 ± 0.25 N, gumminess from 5.31 ± 0.12 to 6.71 ± 0.15 N, and chewiness from 6.65 ± 0.18 to 7.81 ± 0.22 N·m (p < 0.05). Among all treatments, DN-SPE-PL consistently exhibited the highest values, confirming its stronger structural reinforcement effect. During refrigerated storage (4 ± 0.5 °C), hardness, gumminess, and chewiness increased in all treatments; however, the rate of increase was significantly higher in the control compared to nanoemulsion-enriched samples (p < 0.05). This indicates that nanoemulsion systems mitigated progressive protein network densification typically induced by moisture loss and proteolytic rearrangements.

Cohesiveness values ranged between 0.37 and 0.44 and showed no significant differences among treatments or storage time (p > 0.05), suggesting that the internal bonding strength of the protein matrix remained relatively stable despite changes in firmness. The increase in hardness and chewiness can be attributed to a combination of reduced moisture content and enhanced protein–protein interactions during storage, which leads to a more compact gel structure55,69. However, nanoemulsion incorporation appeared to partially counteract this effect, likely due to improved water retention and structural interference within the casein network. The observed relationship between hardness and chewiness is consistent with previous findings, where increased firmness directly elevates mastication resistance60. Furthermore, DN-SPE-PL, which exhibited higher storage modulus and viscosity (data not shown), also demonstrated the highest hardness and chewiness, supporting the correlation between rheological strength and textural resistance.

Gumminess, defined as hardness × cohesiveness, followed the same trend as hardness, confirming that structural reinforcement rather than matrix cohesion governed texture development in nanoemulsion-treated samples. Overall, free SPE weakened the gel structure, whereas nanoemulsified SPE enhanced network stability. Similar trends in ricotta cheese have been reported during storage, although some studies showed no significant changes in cohesiveness or gumminess depending on formulation and bioactive inclusion7,53,60.

Microbial load of cheese

Microbial analysis of ricotta cheese samples showed that all treatments were free from Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli at the end of 14 days of refrigerated storage (4 ± 0.5 °C) (Fig. 6A), confirming initial microbiological safety. At day 0, no significant differences were observed among treatments in total aerobic mesophilic bacteria (TAMBC) or mold and yeast counts (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6B). During storage, both microbial groups increased significantly (p < 0.05). TAMBC increased from 2.68 ± 0.09–2.74 ± 0.10 log CFU/g to 3.56 ± 0.12–7.53 ± 0.18 log CFU/g, while mold and yeast counts increased from 0.75 ± 0.04–0.84 ± 0.05 log CFU/g to 1.10 ± 0.06–3.10 ± 0.08 log CFU/g by day 14. The highest microbial growth was consistently observed in the control sample, whereas DN-SPE-SL exhibited the lowest TAMBC at the end of storage. For mold and yeast, DN-SPE-SL also showed the lowest values, although differences among nanoemulsion treatments (DN-SPE-SL, DN-SPE-PL, DN-SPE-S) were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Overall, free SPE showed stronger antimicrobial activity in the early storage period (up to day 7), whereas nanoemulsion-based formulations maintained more stable inhibitory effects toward the end of storage. This shift suggests improved long-term bioavailability and protection of bioactive compounds within nanoemulsion carriers. The antimicrobial activity of plant polyphenols is mainly attributed to membrane disruption, enzyme inhibition, and leakage of intracellular components45. However, their effectiveness is often limited by instability and poor dispersion in complex food matrices.

Nanoemulsion systems overcome these limitations by reducing droplet size and increasing surface area, which enhances interaction with microbial cells while protecting active compounds from degradation70. Additionally, protein–polysaccharide-based encapsulation improves emulsion stability and controlled release13,71. The superior antimicrobial performance of nanoemulsions observed in this study is consistent with previous reports on encapsulated plant extracts and essential oils in dairy systems17,46,57. The ability of nano-sized droplets to interact more effectively with microbial membranes has also been previously emphasized (Pérez-Soto et al., 2021). Based on established microbial safety limits for fresh cheese (6 log CFU/g for TAMBC and 2 log CFU/g for molds and yeasts), the control exceeded acceptable levels after day 7, while nanoemulsion-treated samples remained within safe limits throughout storage17.

Sensory evaluation of cheese

The average scores of sensory attributes of different ricotta cheese treatments are compared in Fig. 6C. At the beginning of the storage period, all cheese treatments obtained flavor, odor, color (except for cheese containing free extract), texture and overall acceptability scores higher than 4, and the addition of free SPE significantly reduced flavor, odor, color, and overall acceptability compared with the control (p < 0.05). The other treatments showed no significant differences from the control (p > 0.05), which reflects the masking effect of encapsulation on the sensory impact of the extract. During the storage period at refrigerated temperature (4 ± 0.5 °C), all sensory scores gradually declined, with a significantly faster decline in the control sample due to lower oxidative stability and higher microbial load (p < 0.05). The reduction in taste and odor scores can be attributed to lipid oxidation, formation of secondary oxidation products (aldehydes, alcohols, hydrocarbons, etc.), microbial growth, production of metabolic by-products, and pH changes. The decrease in texture scores is likely related to moisture migration and proteolytic activity that weakens the cheese matrix. The decline in color scores may be associated with moisture loss, reduced light reflectance, and pigment oxidation.

At the end of the storage period, the control sample no longer showed acceptable sensory quality, whereas cheeses enriched with different SPE forms—especially those containing the nanoemulsion form—maintained scores above 3 and remained acceptable. In line with these results, in studies conducted by Hosiseni et al.26, and Azarashkan et al.21, where free and particularly encapsulated extracts of chia seeds and broccoli sprouts preserved the sensory quality of ricotta cheese throughout storage. These authors attributed the effect to the antioxidant and antimicrobial activity of the extracts and the sensory-masking and controlled-release properties of encapsulation. Lipșa et al.55 also showed that the adding onion peel powder (1 and 3%) did not negatively affect sensory acceptance, and Bhat et al.61 observed that saffron enrichment improved sensory scores of Himalayan cheese.

Conclusion

The results showed that combining protein isolates with LSG resulted in a smaller size and higher encapsulation efficiency, antioxidant activity, and zeta potential of the produced double nanoemulsions. Encapsulation of SPE in the nanoemulsion system resulted in enhanced preservation of the antioxidant activity of the extract during storage and improved antibacterial activity against S. aureus. The highest physical stability during storage was observed in the DN-SPE-SL. The combination of different forms of SPE improved the antioxidant activity, oxidative stability, and antimicrobial activity of fortified cheeses compared to the control, and these active additives were able to significantly mitigate the severity of changes in the physicochemical and sensory properties of ricotta cheese during storage compared to the control (p < 0.05). Different ricotta cheese treatments showed pseudoplastic or shear-thinning behavior, and DN-SPEs enriched cheeses had higher apparent viscosity and storage and loss moduli than the control, hence, these treatments also showed higher hardness, gumminess, and chewiness. Overall, the results indicated strong antioxidant and antimicrobial activity of DN-SPEs both in vitro and in the model system (ricotta cheese), and therefore it can be inferred that these nanoemulsions, especially DN-SPE-SL, can be used to improve the physicochemical properties, antioxidant and antimicrobial activity, and preserve the sensory quality of ricotta cheese during storage. These findings offer formulation-level guidance for the design of functional nanoemulsion-based preservatives in high-moisture, unripened cheeses, rather than direct extrapolation to other cheese categories with different compositional and processing characteristics. The current research took place in a laboratory setting yet the nanoemulsion system shows clear promise for application in dairy processing operations. The application of food-grade proteins with gums allows for scale-up production but these biopolymers cost less than synthetic stabilizers. The regulatory structures between different areas show differences but every component used in this study exists as an approved food ingredient which suggests positive industrial application potential.
