DNA breakpoints and free DNA fragments as potential predictors of infertility
Authors: Yue Zhou, Juan Wang, Fan Zhang, Liguo Pei, Yue Chang, Bei Yan, Hongyan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study investigated the impact of varicocele on male infertility by analyzing sperm samples from 42 varicocele patients and 22 healthy individuals. Basic sperm parameters such as concentration and vitality were assessed, along with the DNA Fragmentation Index (DFI) using the SCSA method. Additionally, the mean number of sperm DNA breaks (MDB) and free DNA fragments amount in seminal plasma (fDFA) were analyzed using the TDT-Strand Displacement Probe Technique. Results showed higher levels of DFI, MDB, and fDFA in varicocele-related infertility cases. Combining MDB and fDFA proved more effective in predicting fertility compared to individual assessments or the DFI alone. While these findings suggest a potential role for MDB and fDFA as predictors of male fertility, further rigorous studies are needed to validate their application in varicocele-related male infertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81252-x.

Background

Varicocele, a common disease impacting male fertility, affects around 15% of males. Among them, approximately 35% experience primary infertility, while 80% experience secondary infertility1–4. This disorder causes abnormal dilation, elongation, and twisting of the pampiniform plexus, leading to local tissue hypoxia, increased temperature, and accumulation of metabolic waste, thus influencing both the quality and quantity of sperm5,6. Due to insufficient DNA repair mechanisms, sperm are highly susceptible to DNA damage, resulting in sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF)7. The sixth edition of the WHO Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen underscores the significance of evaluating the DNA fragmentation index (DFI) as a crucial marker of male fertility8. Studies have also indicated that increased levels of reactive oxygen species in varicocele patients can compromise sperm DNA integrity5,6,9. Hence, precise evaluation of DNA integrity in varicocele patients is vital for comprehending and addressing related infertility problems. Sperm Chromatin Structure Analysis (SCSA) is presently among the most frequently utilized techniques for indirectly identifying sperm DNA fragmentation10,11. This technique utilizes flow cytometry to assess the denaturation characteristics of sperm DNA, particularly sensitivity to acid-induced denaturation. In SCSA testing, sperm are treated with acidic solution to identify damaged DNA, and upon binding with acridine orange, damaged DNA emits red or yellow fluorescence, while undamaged DNA emits green fluorescence11,12. The percentage of sperm emitting red fluorescence is termed the DNA fragmentation index (DFI), used to quantify the extent of sperm DNA damage. Despite controversies regarding the use of DFI in evaluating sperm function and predicting fertility outcomes, many studies have linked DFI with male infertility13,14.

TUNEL (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling) assay is another commonly used technique for direct detection of sperm DNA damage15,16. This technique labels DNA breaks with fluorescently labeled dUTP (e.g., FITC) using Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) at the 3’-hydroxyl end of DNA breaks, directly pinpointing DNA damage locations. Subsequently, samples can be analyzed under a microscope or flow cytometer for precise localization and quantification of DNA damage. Although the TUNEL technique provides accurate data on DNA damage, its operation is relatively complex and time-consuming, involving multiple staining and washing steps, which may present some limitations in routine laboratory use17. We have developed a new detection method, the Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and strand displacement (TdT/SD) biosensor, for evaluating sperm DNA integrity17. This technique allows for the direct quantification of DNA breaks in sperm. It offers improved sensitivity, efficiency, and accuracy, capable of detecting even minimal changes in sperm DNA integrity. By converting the number of DNA breaks into fluorescence signal values, we are able to provide the mean number of DNA break points (MDB). This technique offers a reliable evaluation of sperm DNA integrity at the molecular leve18. In this project, we have successfully detected the amount of free DNA fragments (fDFA) in the seminal plasma of patients with varicocele using the TdT/SD biosensor. To explore the association between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and infertility caused by varicocele, we have also investigated the levels of ROS in the two sample groups. So far, MDB and fDFA have not been applied in patients with varicocele. This study seeks to delve deeper into the predictive potential of MDB and fDFA in fertility assessment by analyzing sperm from varicocele-related infertility patients and fertile individuals.

Materials and methods

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study design

Semen samples were collected from 42 patients with varicocele and 22 normal males at the Reproductive Medicine Center of Yinchuan Maternal and Child Health Hospital and the Reproductive Medicine Center of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. The study was ethically approved by the Ethical Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (KYLL2021493), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patient selection

The study comprised 42 men diagnosed with varicocele during male infertility assessments. Varicocele severity was classified as grade 1, 2, or 3 according to the criteria by Dubin and Amelar. Varicocele diagnoses were confirmed through a clinical examination conducted by two physicians trained in physical exams. Color Doppler ultrasound was also used to verify blood reflux in the spermatic veins. A control group of 22 healthy, fertile men without varicocele was included for comparison, with their semen analysis falling within the normal range. Control participants were carefully selected based on the study’s specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Control participants in the elite control group had to have at least two children, with one child aged 2 or younger, to be deemed reproductively successful. Participants from both the varicocele and control groups were excluded if they had a history of smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, drug use, occupational exposure, previous varicocele surgery, other infertility-related conditions (e.g., cryptorchidism), exposure to substances or medications known to affect male fertility, urogenital infections, cancer, or endocrine disorders.

Semen collection and conventional semen test

Semen samples were collected after 3–5 days of abstinence. The computer-aided sperm analysis system (WLJY-9DOO; version 2.0; Beijing Weili New Century Science & Tech. Dev. Co., Ltd.) was utilized to evaluate sperm concentration and motility following liquefaction. Samples with a sperm concentration greater than 15 × 106 sperm/mL and progressive motility (PR) of 32% were classified as the normal group. Concentrations below 15 × 106 sperm/mL were categorized as oligospermia, while PR below 32% was classified as asthenospermia. Sperm morphology was assessed using the Diff-Quik rapid staining kit (cat. no. G1541; Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.), and the evaluation criteria were strictly based on the requirements outlined in The World Health Organization Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen, Fifth Edition.

Importantly, the evaluation criteria for normal semen were established according to the recommendations of The World Health Organization Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen, Fifth Edition: Volume ≥ 1.5 mL; Total sperm count ≥ 39 × 106; Progressive motility (PR) ≥ 32%; and a combination of PR and non-progressive (NP) motile sperm > 40%; normal morphology rate ≥ 4%.

Detection of ROS

Superoxide anion flow cytometry was used to measure the oxidative stress activity in sperm cells (cat.no. BKR 803; Wuhan Biocarry Technology Co., Ltd.). This technique assesses the concentration of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in sperm. For the experiment, 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), a dye that readily permeates cell membranes, was utilized. When oxidized by reactive molecules like hydrogen peroxide, peroxide, and peroxynitrite, DCFH-DA emits fluorescence. The experimental procedure was carried out in strict accordance with the product’s instructions (DCFH-DA: 10 µM/L; incubation: 37 °C for 20 min).

DFI test

Sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI) was determined using the sperm chromatin structure assay (SCSA). The basic principle is as follows: AO emits green fluorescence when it binds to double-stranded DNA and red fluorescence when it binds to single-stranded DNA. Sperm with DNA breaks will have fragmented DNA that dissociates into single strands under acidic conditions, emitting red fluorescence. The DFI (DNA fragmentation index) is calculated by determining the percentage of red fluorescence within the total fluorescence. In this study, the DFI kit (catalog number BKR802, Wuhan Biocarry Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was used. The procedure19 involved diluting semen to a concentration of 1–2 × 106/ml with Solution A(buffer solution). Then, 100 µl of Solution B (acidic processing solution)was added to denature the DNA over a period of 30 s, followed by staining with 1200 µl of Solution C(acridine orange staining solution). The stained samples were analyzed using flow cytometry. After initiating the sample flow, data from at least 5000 cells were recorded. Samples were further diluted if the flow rate exceeded 300 s.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted using the cell genome extraction kit (cat.no. BSC86S1E; Hangzhou Bioer BIOTECH Co., Ltd.) and an automated nucleic acid extraction and purification instrument (GenePure Pro 32E; Hangzhou Bioer BIOTECH Co., Ltd.). Seminal Plasma Free DNA was extracted following the protocol of the extraction kit (cat.no. DP339; Cellpro; Beijing TIANGEN BIOTECH Co., Ltd.).

MDB test

DNA concentrations were adjusted to 5 ng/µL, followed by the addition of 2 µL of 10x TdT buffer, 2µL of dATP (Sangon Biotech), and 2 µL of sperm DNA solution. Then, 8.5 µL of sterile deionized water was added, and the mixture was gently mixed by pipetting. Subsequently, 0.5 µL of TdT enzyme (NEB) was incorporated. The reaction mixture was incubated in a qPCR instrument at 37 ℃ for 60 min, then at 75 ℃ for 20 min. After incubation, 5 µL of SD probes adjusted to 400 nM were immediately added to the qPCR instrument for detection, with conditions set at 37 ℃, 30 s per cycle, for 10 cycles.

fDFA test

Referencing the above MDB testing protocol, the testing procedure is the same.

Statistical methods

All the sperm parameters were expressed in the form of mean ± SD and P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to the small sample size, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to analyze the normality of the data, and the Levene test was employed to assess the homogeneity of variances. Differences between the normal groups and VC (Varicocele) groups were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t-test with SPSS 25.0.

Sperm DNA extraction and seminal plasma free DNA extraction

When sperm is subjected to internal or external stimuli, such as reactive oxygen species, it can lead to sperm DNA fragmentation (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Initially, centrifugation at 300 g was used to separate the sperm into the bottom layer. Sperm DNA was extracted from the cells at the bottom, while the supernatant was used for seminal plasma free DNA extraction (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The DNA extraction process was optimized, resulting in significant improvements in both sperm concentration and purity, as indicated in Supplementary Table 1. Additionally, seminal plasma free DNA fragments were successfully extracted, with the extraction process detailed in Supplementary Fig. 2 and the extracted DNA concentrations listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Mean number of sperm DNA break points (MDB) and free DNA fragment amount (fDFA)

When sperm is exposed to excessive external stimuli, DNA undergoes breakage, leading to the generation of 3’-hydroxyl groups. The TdT enzyme is employed to identify and elongate these 3’-hydroxyl groups using dATP to create polyA. A strand displacement probe attaches to the polyA, segregating the short nucleotide chain containing a fluorescent group (FAM) from the quencher (BHQ-1), resulting in fluorescence emission (Fig. 1). This fluorescence signal is associated with the quantity of 3’-hydroxyl groups in the DNA.

The extracted sperm DNA and the developed detection system were placed in wells and then transferred to a fluorescence detection instrument. This procedure enabled the collection of fluorescence signal values produced post sperm DNA reaction. Following this, a standard concentration of DNA single strand (s-001) was utilized as a benchmark material, where each DNA single strand contributed a 3’-hydroxyl group. These DNA single strands were employed to construct a standard curve linking the quantity of 3’-hydroxyl groups with the fluorescence signal values, leading to the derivation of a linear equation (Fig. 2A). The equation was utilized to calculate the Mean Number of Sperm DNA Breakpoints (MDB). Considering the signal-to-noise ratio, it was found that an 18-nt short nucleotide chain provided the optimal ratio (Fig. 2B). The method was standardized using a standard single-stranded DNA (s-001), and the concentrations of dATP and TdT enzyme were optimized. It was determined that 1 mM dATP and 4 u/uL TdT enzyme (Fig. 2C) yielded the most favorable signal-to-noise ratio. The detection system was capable of detecting a minimum of 0.01 nmol of 3’-hydroxyl groups, with a linear range from 0.01 to 4 nmol, and an R-squared value of 0.9949 (Fig. 2D-E).

MDB, fDFA and DFI, ROS of sperm and conventional parameters

The Mean Number of Sperm DNA Breakpoints (MDB) was further calculated based on the fluorescence platform values of sperm DNA after PCR. The detailed calculation formula can be found in the Supplemental Calculation Methods. Thus, the specific number of sperm DNA breakpoints for each sample was obtained. Moreover, a standard curve was used to determine the fragment amount of seminal plasma free DNA. Additionally, the Sperm DNA Fragmentation Index (DFI) was obtained using the SCSA method.

The correlations among the conventional parameters of MDB, fDFA, and DFI in sperm were investigated in a study involving 64 cases. The results revealed negative correlations between PR and MDB, PR and fDFA, and PR and DFI (r=-0.458, P < 0.01; r=-0.400, P < 0.01; r=-0.301, P < 0.05, respectively). Sperm concentration exhibited negative correlations with MDB and fDFA, while no correlation was observed between DFI and concentration (r=-0.263, P < 0.05; r=-0.285, P < 0.05; r=-0.170, P = 0.178, respectively). Sperm morphology demonstrated negative correlations with MDB, sperm morphology and fDFA, and sperm morphology and DFI (r=-0.407, P < 0.01; r=-0.271, P < 0.05; r=-0.426, P < 0.01, respectively).

Our analysis revealed significant differences: MDB was significantly lower in the normal group than in the VC group (p < 0.0001). Similarly, fDFA was significantly higher in the VC group compared to the normal group (p < 0.0001), as depicted in Fig. 3A-B. DFI in sperm was significantly lower in individuals with normal fertility compared to varicocele patients, as shown in Fig. 3C.

Flow cytometry was utilized to assess the levels of sperm reactive oxygen species (ROS) in both groups. The results indicated a significant increase in ROS levels among varicocele patients compared to the control group (p = 0.0027), as illustrated in Fig. 3D. In the three-dimensional representation shown in Fig. 3E, the majority of data points representing sperm from healthy individuals clustered in the region associated with lower ROS levels, enhanced progressive motility (PR), and fewer DNA breakpoints. In predicting varicocele-related infertility (Fig. 3F), the combination of MDB and fDFA exhibited the highest AUC value (0.8988), followed by MDB alone (0.8593), and fDFA alone (0.8306). The SCSA methods also demonstrated strong discriminatory abilities, with an AUC value of 0.8025.

Discussion

Abnormalities in the reproductive system are observed in patients with varicocele, characterized by dilation, elongation, and twisting of the venous plexus. This condition results in increased levels of local reactive oxygen species, which can have a negative impact on spermatogenesis due to potential sperm DNA damage caused by excessive reactive oxygen species5. During the packaging of sperm chromatin heads, if damaged DNA cannot be repaired in a timely manner, or if repair capacity is insufficient to address the damage, these broken DNA strands will be encapsulated within the chromatin region and then progressively packaged by layers of proteins. This makes it difficult for current detection methods to accurately identify these DNA breakage points19,20. We hypothesize that this is also one of the reasons for the inconsistent results obtained with current techniques for assessing sperm DNA integrity, as current technologies often fail to fully evaluate the DNA within the dense regions inside sperm.

Additionally, in clinical practice, the commonly used DNA fragmentation index (DFI) serves as a metric representing the percentage of sperm with damage within a population. However, DNA fragmentation occurs at the molecular level, and DFI as a cellular-level parameter lacks precision19,20. Therefore, a molecular-level indicator to assess DNA integrity as a supplement to DFI is lacking in clinical practice. Accordingly, we previously developed a technique to detect sperm Mean DNA Breaks (MDB), which accurately calculates the mean number of DNA breaks in sperm20,21. Furthermore, considering previous studies22–24suggesting that dithiothreitol (DTT) can assist in decondensing the chromatin in the head of sperm, we incorporated DTT into the sperm DNA preparation process to effectively lyse the sperm and fully release DNA from chromatin regions, resulting in a higher yield of DNA.

In this project, we utilized this technique not only to detect sperm MDB but also to evaluate the level of free DNA fragments in seminal fluid, establishing a new indicator, termed seminal plasma free DNA fragment amount (fDFA), and successfully completed fDFA detection in both varicocele-associated infertility and normal fertility groups. We found that both sperm MDB and seminal plasma fDFA in the varicocele group were significantly higher than those in the normal control group. Additionally, the results of oxidative stress detection in both groups exhibited a similar trend9. This suggests that excessive reactive oxygen species in varicocele patients may lead to subtle changes in the sperm generation environment, thereby affecting sperm DNA integrity6,25,26 and simultaneously increasing DNA fragment release.

We also utilized SCSA to evaluate sperm DFI values, showing that sperm DFI values in the varicocele group were significantly higher than those in the normal group, consistent with results from other research groups6,27.Both methods displayed consistent trends; DFI provides the proportion of damaged sperm in a population as a percentage, while MDB provides the number of sperm DNA breakage points at the molecular level, offering complementary evaluations of sperm DNA integrity at different levels. Additionally, we conducted ROC curve analysis28,29 of various indicators to evaluate their predictive ability for fertility, finding that the combination of MDB and fDFA achieved superior predictive performance. Furthermore, in this project, we obtained MDB values for varicocele-associated infertility patients for the first time using this technique, with the MDB measured in varicocele-associated infertility patients being significantly higher than those in healthy individuals. We believe that implementing sperm MDB and fDFA detection in seminal fluid analysis of varicocele patients can enhance fertility prediction, completing assessments at the molecular level19, which serves as a good supplement to the cellular-level DFI indicator17,20.

To streamline the clinical application of this technique, we standardized the entire detection process. Additionally, we adopted a magnetic bead-based automated nucleic acid extraction system, significantly reducing labor costs and making the entire process more efficient. This work provides a selectable, reliable, and convenient clinical application method for detecting sperm DNA breakage points in varicocele-infertile patients. However, we also acknowledge certain limitations in this study. Firstly, the sample size is relatively small, and to ensure the reliability of the results and further explore the clinical value of MDB and fDFA, larger-scale studies are needed. Additionally, although we have explored sperm DNA fragmentation damage, we have not addressed other types of oxidative damage to sperm DNA, such as 8-oxoguanine. Future research directions will focus on further exploring these aspects to more comprehensively understand sperm DNA damage in varicocele-infertile patients.

Conclusion

The combination of MDB and fDFA has shown promising performance in predicting male fertility and can serve as one of the indicators for assessing fertility. However, rigorous prospective studies are still needed to further validate and strengthen their potential application as parameters for male infertility in patients with varicocele.
