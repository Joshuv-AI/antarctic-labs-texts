The effect of tranexamic acid in perioperative bleeding transfusion in radical cystectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Pedro Faria Makabe, Luís Fernando Ferreira Cavalcante, Bruno Yuamoto, Sophia Massafelli Battistuta, Giovanna Ristori Costa, Luís Guilherme Giacon Meloni, Felipe Endrigo Gonçalves Vilela, Isadora Silva Fanucci Bueno, Roni de Carvalho Fernandes, Luis Gustavo Morato de Toledo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifibrinolytic agent with demonstrated benefits in various surgical procedures, including urological surgeries. However, its effect on radical cystectomy (RC) remains uncertain, largely due to limited data from an earlier meta-analysis that combined RC with substantially different urological procedures, as well as conflicting results from two recently published meta-analyses specifically evaluating the impact of TXA on RC.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024628394) and conducted following PRISMA and Cochrane guidelines. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies comparing TXA versus no TXA in patients undergoing RC. Primary endpoints included perioperative blood transfusion (PBT) and venous thromboembolism (VTE). Random-effects models with Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment were used to calculate pooled risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThree studies (one RCT and two propensity-score matched observational studies) involving 1,344 patients (673 TXA; 671 control) were included. The pooled analysis for PBT showed a non-significant reduction in risk (RR 0.69; 95% CI 0.25–1.86; p = 0.25; I2 = 81%). Similarly, the risk of VTE was not significantly different between groups, although a potential trend toward increased risk was noted (RR 1.60; 95% CI 0.80–3.17; p = 0.10; I2 = 0%).ConclusionsCurrent evidence is underpowered to confirm the efficacy or safety of TXA during radical cystectomy. While no significant benefit or harm was identified, the wide confidence intervals necessitate further large-scale randomized trials to provide definitive clinical guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02162-w.Keywords: Tranexamic acid, Radical cystectomy, Perioperative blood transfusion

Introduction

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a synthetic derivative of the amino acid lysine that functions as an antifibrinolytic agent by inhibiting fibrinolysis. This mechanism is achieved through the blockade of lysine binding sites on plasminogen, thereby preventing the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, which is essential for fibrin degradation in blood clots [1]. The inhibition of this pathway can be clinically significant in various medical contexts, especially during selected surgeries [2].

Among urological surgeries, radical cystectomy (RC) – which is the cornerstone surgical treatment of muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) – is a surgical procedure with high operative time and may be associated with significant blood loss [3].

Bladder cancer (BC) occurs more frequently in males, with ratios varying from 6:1 to 2:1. Smoking is the most significant risk factor, accounting for approximately 50% of cases. The disease presents in various forms, ranging from indolent non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), which has a long natural history and is staged as either Tis, Ta or T1, to aggressive MIBC, which corresponds to either T2, T3 or T4 stages [4–6].

Microscopic hematuria may serve as an indicator of a lower disease stage in patients diagnosed with BC. Clinical presentations in such patients can include painless microscopic or gross hematuria, urinary tract symptoms, and flank pain. The gold standard for diagnosing BC remains cystoscopy, which is crucial for direct visualization and assessment of the bladder’s interior [6].

The management of BC typically involves surgical intervention; specifically, NMIBC is primarily treated through transurethral resection, while MIBC is predominantly addressed with RC [6, 7] In high-risk NMIBC (patients who have persistent or recurrent disease within one year after receiving two induction cycles of BCG or BCG maintenance therapy) RC is recommended [8].

RC refers to the surgical procedure involving the complete removal of the bladder, as well as adjacent organs and regional lymph nodes. In male patients, this procedure encompasses the excision of the prostate gland and seminal vesicles. In female patients, there may also be a concomitant removal of reproductive organs to optimize oncologic outcomes. This comprehensive approach is essential for effective treatment and management of certain malignancies associated with the bladder [7] Whereas robot-assisted RC (RARC) is associated with better perioperative outcomes, open RC is also considered the cornerstone of MIBC [7, 9].

A previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Kim et al. [3] assessed the impact of TXA on perioperative outcomes among urological surgeries. However, the representation of RC in this previous meta-analysis was represented by only a single study, Zaid et al. [10], which represents a reduced cohort that has since been significantly expanded by Ahmed et al. [11], a more comprehensive and recent study.

Furthermore, the evidence base has significantly evolved with the release of new data, including the randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Breau et al. [12] and the propensity-score matched analysis by Egen et al. [13], as well as the expanded cohort study by Ahmed et al. [11]. Despite these new findings, there remain notable discrepancies regarding the clinical utility of TXA in RC.

Recent meta-analyses by Costa de Sousa et al. [14] and Suartz et al. [15] have evaluated the effect of TXA in RC. However, their findings remain discordant. Furthermore, a critical appraisal of these studies reveals several methodological limitations, underscoring the requirement for a more rigorous and up-to-date meta-analysis.

To address the controversy among the results and the lack of significant representation of RC in previous systematic review and meta-analysis, we present this systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the role of TXA in perioperative blood transfusion (PBT) rate of patients undergoing RC.

Methods

The protocol of this review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the protocol CRD42024628394. Reporting and conduction of the systematic review and meta-analysis was performed as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [16].

Eligibility criteria

No restrictions were determined concerning the publication date, status, or language. Studies were considered eligible if they met all the following criteria: (1) RCT or observational study, (2) comparing the TXA administration with no TXA administration, and (3) patients undergoing RC. Our exclusion criteria were (1) overlapping patient populations, (2) conference abstracts, or (3) no outcomes of interest.

Search strategy and data extraction

We conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases from their inception up to December 2024 using the following specific search strategy as described in Supplementary Table 1. The references of all include studies and previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses were also searched manually for additional studies. Two authors (L.C. and P.M.) independently extracted the data following predefined search criteria, and disagreements were resolved through a panel discussion with both authors.

Endpoints

PBT, death, pulmonary embolism (PE), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and venous thromboembolism (VTE) were the outcomes assessed. The number of events and the total number of participants of the intervention and control groups were retrieved. Missing data for VTE was calculated by the sum of events of PE and DVT.

Quality assessment

Two independent authors (L.C and P.M.) assessed the risk of bias of the included studies using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB-2) [17] and the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS-I) [18] for observational studies as reported in Fig. 1.

Risk of bias assessment of included studies

Two independent authors (L.C and P.M) assessed the certainty of the evidence using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool [19] for each reported outcome based on the GRADE handbook [20] (Fig. 2).

GRADE assessment

Statistical analysis

Risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to compare treatment effects for categorical endpoints. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test and I2 statistics, with significance defined as P values < 0.10 and I2 > 50%. Statistical analyses were performed using the Mantel–Haenszel method and a random-effects model. To account for the limited number of included studies, we applied the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment to calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CI) for pooled risk ratios (RR). This method provides a more conservative estimate of uncertainty compared to traditional random-effects models.

A subgroup analysis was performed based on study design and a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted for outcomes reported by at least three studies with substantial heterogeneity. All analyses were implemented in Review Manager Version 8.1.1.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Our systematic search yielded 123 potential articles (Fig. 3). After removing duplicates and excluding studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria, six studies underwent full-text review. Zaid et al. [10] was excluded due to overlapping populations, and Prasad et al. [21] and Wright et al. [22] were excluded due to the lack of data for the RC subgroup.

The number of events and number of total participants in the intervention and control group of PBT were retrieved directly from the main text and tables of all included studies [11–13]; however, for the number of events of VTE, it was necessary to calculate a composite of PE and DVT for Ahmed et al. [11] and Breau et al. [12]. In contrast, the total number of Egen et al. [13] was retrieved directly from the main text and tables. The outcomes of death, PE, and DVT were not reported by all included studies [11–13]; therefore, their analysis was not feasible.

Pooled analysis of all studies

The pooled analysis for PBT (RR 0.69; 95% CI 0.25–1.86; p = 0.25; I2 = 81%; Fig. 4) suggested a possible 31% relative reduction in PBT risk; however, this result was not statistically significant, as the confidence interval crossed the line of no effect. The interval ranged from a 75% reduction to an 86% increase in risk. Given the substantial imprecision and heterogeneity, the analysis appears underpowered to rule out clinically meaningful effects. Accordingly, a definitive benefit or harm of TXA on PBT cannot be established based on the very low certainty of the evidence.

Perioperative Blood Transfusion (PBT)−2

The pooled analysis for VTE (RR 1.60; 95% CI 0.80–3.17; p = 0.10; I2 = 0%; Fig. 5) suggested a potential 60% relative increase in VTE risk; however, this result did not reach statistical significance, as the confidence interval crossed the line of no effect. The 95% CI ranged from a 20% reduction to a 217% increase in risk. This wide interval indicates that the available evidence is underpowered to detect or exclude a clinically relevant effect, and no firm conclusions regarding the impact of TXA on VTE can be made from the current data with low certainty of the evidence.

Subgroup analysis based on study design

A subgroup analysis based on study design revealed no significant difference in PBT rates for either RCTs (RR 0.99; 95% CI 0.75–1.30; p = 0.93; I2 = not applicable; Fig. 4) or propensity-matched cohorts (RR 0.60; 95% CI 0.18–1.97; p = 0.12; I2 = 3%; Fig. 4). Similarly, the incidence of VTE did not differ significantly between groups in the RCT subgroup (RR 1.19; 95% CI 0.37–3.81; p = 0.77; I2 = not applicable; Fig. 5) or the propensity-matched subgroup (RR 1.76; 95% CI 0.20–15.27; p = 0.19; I2 = 0%; Fig. 5).

Sensitivity analysis and quality assessment

We conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for PBT, which presented high heterogeneity. The analysis showed no statistically significant difference in PBT among patients undergoing RC when excluding the studies Ahmed et al. [11] (RR 0.68; 95% CI 0.00–194.24; p = 0.55; I2 = 77%; Fig. 6A), Breau et al. 2024 [12] (RR 0.60; 95% CI 0.18–1.97; p = 0.12; I2 = 3%; Fig. 6B) and Egen et al. [13] (RR 0.77; 95% CI 0.04–15.94; p = 0.47; I2 = 88%; Fig. 6C).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, encompassing one RCT and two propensity-matched retrospective cohorts with 1,344 patients who underwent RC, indicates that the impact of TXA on PBT remains inconclusive. At the same time, the effect on VTE remains uncertain and a potentially increased risk cannot be excluded.

Ahmed et al. [11] is an observational study in which 468 TXA recipients were matched to 468 TXA-non-recipient patients undergoing open or robot-assisted RC. The clinical tumor stage for both groups was predominantly T1 or inferior. TXA recipients received a dose of 10 mg/kg bolus and a continuous 2 mg/kg/hour infusion throughout the surgery.

Breau et al. [12] is an RCT of patients undergoing open RC in which 173 TXA recipients were compared to 171 patients who received a matching placebo. The clinical tumor stage for both groups was predominantly T2, whereas preoperative hemoglobin was 12.9 for both groups. TXA recipients received a dose of 10 mg/kg within 10 min before the surgical incision and a continuous 5 mg/kg/hour infusion until 20 min before skin closure.

Egen et al. [13] is an observational study in which 32 TXA recipients were matched to 32 TXA-non-recipient patients undergoing open RC. The clinical tumor staging for both cohorts was predominantly either low or high grade, with a lower representation of T2. TXA recipients received a dose of 1 g 30 min before surgery.

Considering the limited number of RCTs on this topic, observational studies were included to provide a more comprehensive synthesis of the currently available evidence, thereby allowing for a broader exploratory analysis of the current clinical landscape. However, even with the propensity-matched score analysis, some degree of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The inability to draw definitive conclusions is further emphasized by the substantial heterogeneity observed, likely resulting from significant variations in TXA administration and posology across the included studies. While Ahmed et al. [11] and Breau et al. [12] utilized a preoperative bolus dose of TXA followed by a continuous infusion during the surgical procedure. Egen et al. [13] employed a single, higher bolus dose of TXA administered 30 min before the procedure. This discrepancy in dosing strategies may contribute to the differences observed in the need for blood transfusion, highlighting the necessity for further research to determine the optimal dosing regimen and timing of TXA administration to achieve the best surgical outcomes.

In addition to dosing variations, different surgical techniques across the included studies likely contribute to the observed heterogeneity. While Breau et al. [12] and Egen et al. [13] focused exclusively on open RC, Ahmed et al. [11] included both open and robot-assisted RC in their cohort. These surgical discrepancies and lack of a standardized protocol account for the significant heterogeneity.

This clinical complexity is further compounded by variations in tumor staging, as T2 and T3 tumors are associated with a higher risk of bleeding; an individualized analysis of TXA administration in these subgroups could be an important point of discussion. However, the studies reviewed do not stratify TXA use by staging classification, thereby limiting this analysis. While Breau et al. [12] included a population predominantly staged as T2; Ahmed et al. [11] and Egen et al. [13] contained a higher proportion of T1 or lower-stage tumors. This reveals an important clinical imbalance regarding tumor staging and underscores the complexity of synthesizing data among different staging classifications which suggests the need for future studies to stratify outcomes by tumor stage to provide more granular clinical guidance.

Furthermore, a considerable number of cases staged as T1 were indicated for RC. The American Urological Association (AUA) describes the role of cystectomy in NMIBC in cases where the bladder-sparing modalities have failed; in high-risk patient with persistent high-grade T1 disease on repeat resection and in recurrent disease within one year following treatment with two induction cycles of BCG or BCG maintenance. In this review, the studies that performed RC for T1 or lower tumors do not specify which indications were present, a detail that impacts the discussion of TXA effectiveness [8].

Another point to be considered in our analysis is the substantial divergence regarding perioperative VTE prophylaxis protocols across the studies. While Ahmed et al. [11] and Egen et al. [13] implemented mandatory thromboprophylaxis for all patients, Breau et al. [12] adopted a more flexible approach, deferring the choice to the attending physician’s discretion. Beyond that, the absence of a standardized thromboprophylaxis protocol, characterized by marked variability in drug selection, dosing schedules, and the timing of administration represents a significant confounding factor that may influence the perioperative bleeding outcomes.

Related to vasculopathy is the impact of smoking as a major associated comorbidity. Smoking is well established as a risk factor for increased platelet aggregation, endothelial dysfunction and chronic vascular injury, all of which can interfere and exacerbate the prothrombotic state in BC patients [23]. These characteristics may also play a role in the complication of the hemostatic dynamics during surgery, potentially modifying TXA’s effect. It is important to consider that none of the included studies in this systematic review and meta-analysis had specifically addressed the relationship between smoking and surgical complications related to the TXA effectiveness.

A prior meta-analysis investigating the efficacy of TXA in urological surgeries concluded that the administration of TXA is associated with a reduced risk of blood transfusion. However, the results of our current study do not align with this conclusion. The urological surgical procedures included in the previous meta-analysis — percutaneous nephrolithotomy, radical prostatectomy, transurethral resection of the prostate, and RC — exhibit significant variability in both operative time and estimated blood loss, particularly when compared to RC. This variability may account for the discrepancies observed between our findings and those reported in the earlier study.

Recent meta-analyses by Costa de Sousa et al. [14] and Suartz et al. [15] have addressed the role of TXA in RC, yet their findings remain discordant. Costa de Sousa et al. suggest a reduction in PBT rates, whereas Suartz et al. conclude that TXA offers no such benefit. Importantly, both prior meta-analyses included the cohort reported by Zaid et al. [10] in their pooled analyses. This represents a significant methodological limitation, as the more recent study by Ahmed et al. [11], also analysed by both articles, comprises an expanded cohort that already encompasses the patient population previously described by Zaid et al. [10], thereby creating an overlap of patients. By excluding this redundant data in our analysis, we demonstrate that the effect of TXA on PBT and thromboembolic risk remains inconclusive.

While we have synthesized all currently available data, the small evidence base restricts statistical robustness and limits the certainty of our findings. By addressing this ongoing evidence gap, our study serves as an exploratory analysis that maps existing clinical uncertainties. Given these constraints, our meta-analysis should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Larger, methodologically rigorous, and adequate studies are necessary to provide conclusive guidance on the role of TXA during RC.

Limitations

Although our findings are based on robust methodology, with an emphasis on high-quality peer-reviewed evidence and the exclusion of overlapping populations, the small number of available studies, scarcity of RCTs on this topic, the inclusion of non-randomized data to ensure a more comprehensive synthesis of the currently available evidence and the limited size of the pooled sample constrain the strength and generalizability of our conclusions, supporting them as hypothesis-generating rather than as a basis for conclusive clinical application.

The analysis of estimated blood loss, operative time and all-cause mortality were not feasible due to the lack of available data across all included studies. Furthermore, formal subgroup analyses based on tumor stages, specific surgical approaches and differences in dosing of TXA were not feasible due to heterogeneous reporting and insufficient detail provided in the primary literature, which prevented a meaningful stratification of results. Moreover, our analysis is constrained by the lack of data on known bleeding risk-related factors, liver disease, and chronic anticoagulation therapy, all of which are significantly associated with hemorrhagic risk, were not uniformly reported across the included studies.

Another major limitation is the absence of uniform reporting regarding PBT thresholds across the included studies. In clinical practice, the decision to initiate a blood transfusion is often driven by institutional standards or the attending surgeon’s discretion rather than rigid hemoglobin levels. Given that such criteria directly influence the primary outcome of PBT, the lack of this information can lead to selection bias and contribute to the observed statistical heterogeneity.

Furthermore, the findings of ROBINS-I analysis should be interpreted in light of some methodological limitations. Several variables may act as potential confounding factors in Egen et al. [13] study, as they may influence both the likelihood of receiving the intervention and the risk of the outcome. These include preoperative hemoglobin levels, which are strongly associated with the probability of PBT; tumor stage, since more advanced disease may require more extensive surgical procedures and result in greater blood loss; and receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which may affect hematologic status and increase the risk of bleeding. Additionally, surgeon experience may influence intraoperative blood loss and transfusion practices.

Another factor contributing to the moderate risk of bias observed in the observational studies was the lack of blinding in patient assessment. Outcome assessors were aware of which participants belonged to the intervention and control groups, which could potentially influence the interpretation and assessment of the reported outcomes.

In addition, in Ahmed et al. [11], other baseline differences between groups may also have contributed to residual confounding. These include patient age, comorbidity burden, smoking status, year of surgery, and differences in surgical approach, such as the proportion of robotic versus open procedures. Variations in the extent of lymph node dissection may also influence intraoperative blood loss and PBT requirements. Although statistical adjustments were performed, the observational nature of the study means that unmeasured or incompletely controlled confounders may still have influenced the observed associations between TXA administration and perioperative outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings do not allow for a firm conclusion regarding any clinically relevant effect of TXA on reducing the risk of PBT or increasing the risk of VTE. Although the results are not statistically significant, future research should carefully consider patient selection, especially in light of the observed potential trend toward an increase in adverse events. Our findings underscore the need for further studies to identify specific patient populations that might benefit from TXA administration during RC. Additionally, dose standardization in future trials is warranted, as more evidence is required to clarify the efficacy and safety of TXA in this setting.
