Closed-Form Analysis and Extremal Bounds of Albertson and Sigma Indices in Trees with Prescribed Degree Sequences


Abstract
This study explores the irregularity properties of trees with prescribed degree sequences by analyzing two prominent topological indices: the Albertson index and the sigma index. With a particular emphasis on caterpillar trees—frequently used to model molecular chains—we derive a closed-form expression for the Albertson index: irr ⁡ ( 𝒞 ⁡ ( n , m ) ) = m ⁡ ( m + 1 ) ​ n − 2 ​ m + 2 , for ​ n ≥ 3 . \mathrm{irr}(\mathscr{C}(n,m))=m(m+1)n-2m+2,\quad\text{for }n\geq 3. Furthermore, we establish extremal bounds for both indices across tree families characterized by fixed degree sequences. The results yield a unified analytical framework for comparing linear and quadratic irregularity measures, and provide new structural insights relevant to applications in chemical graph theory and extremal graph analysis.

1
Introduction

The study of structural irregularity in graphs has garnered increasing attention in both theoretical and applied contexts, particularly in mathematical chemistry, network analysis, and algorithmic graph theory. A graph is said to be regular if all of its vertices have equal degrees; otherwise, it exhibits some degree of irregularity. Accurately quantifying this irregularity is essential for modeling complex systems and understanding the behavior of real-world networks.

One of the earliest and most influential measures of graph irregularity is the
Albertson index
, introduced in
[
3
]
, and defined for a simple graph
G=(V,E)
as:

\mathrm{irr}(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}|\deg_{G}(u)-\deg_{G}(v)|,

where
\deg_{G}(u)
denotes the degree of vertex
u
. This index captures local disparities between adjacent vertex degrees and has inspired numerous variants and generalizations
[
2
,
1
]
.

Parallel foundational contributions by Harary
[
17
]
and Chen
[
6
]
provided combinatorial frameworks that laid the groundwork for modern graph descriptors. To further enrich the understanding of irregularity, global measures such as the
total irregularity

\mathrm{irr}_{T}(G)
have been proposed
[
1
]
, satisfying inequalities that relate them to local measures (e.g.,
\mathrm{irr}_{T}(G)\leq(n-2)\,\mathrm{irr}(G)
for trees
[
11
]
).

More recently, the
Sigma index
was introduced as a quadratic analogue of the Albertson index
[
15
]
, defined by:

\sigma(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(\deg_{G}(u)-\deg_{G}(v))^{2}=F(G)-2M_{2}(G),

where
F(G)
is the sum of squared degrees over all edges, and
M_{2}(G)
is the second Zagreb index. This formulation amplifies large degree differences and provides a more sensitive measure of irregularity.

Beyond general graphs, a growing body of literature has focused on trees with constrained degree sequences. Studies by Molloy and Reed
[
18
]
and Broutin and Marckert
[
5
]
explored their asymptotic behavior, while Zhang et al.
[
21
]
investigated realizability and enumeration under degree constraints. These results have proven instrumental in analyzing extremal irregularity measures in structured tree families.

Among such families,
caterpillar trees
—characterized by a linear backbone with attached pendant vertices—have emerged as key models in chemical graph theory
[
22
]
. Their tractable structure allows the derivation of explicit analytical expressions. While prior work
[
8
,
19
]
has examined irregularity bounds in these trees, closed-form expressions for both the Albertson and Sigma indices remain scarce.

This paper addresses this gap by deriving new closed-form formulas for the Albertson and Sigma indices in caterpillar trees with prescribed degree sequences. In addition, we investigate extremal configurations that maximize or minimize these indices, and we establish structural criteria and comparative bounds. Our findings contribute a unified framework for analyzing linear and quadratic irregularity in trees, with implications for discrete mathematics, chemical graph theory, and network modeling.

Preliminaries

This section presents essential definitions and previously established results that form the theoretical foundation for our analysis of graph irregularity in trees. We introduce the primary irregularity indices studied in the literature, namely the Albertson and Sigma indices, along with related global and spectral measures. These tools are fundamental for understanding the structure and behavior of graphs with prescribed degree sequences, especially caterpillar trees. We begin with the concept of total irregularity, which provides a global measure of degree disparity across all pairs of vertices in a graph.

Definition 1

(Total Irregularity
[
1
]
)
.

The
total irregularity
of a graph
G
is defined by

\operatorname{irr}_{T}(G)=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq V(G)}|\deg_{G}(u)-\deg_{G}(v)|.

Next, we recall the notion of a degree sequence, which is a basic descriptor of a graph’s structure and plays a central role in our classification of trees.

Definition 2

(Degree Sequence
[
10
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph, where
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
, let
\mathscr{D}(G)=(\deg_{G}(v_{1}),\dots,\deg_{G}(v_{n}))
be a degree sequence of

where
\deg_{G}(v_{1})\geqslant\deg_{G}(v_{2})\geqslant\dots\geqslant\deg_{G}(v_{n})
. When
\mathscr{D}(G)=(k,k,\ldots,k)
, then
G
is regular of degree
k
. Otherwise, the graph is irregular.

The following result, specific to trees, establishes an upper bound on total irregularity in terms of the Albertson index.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
G
be a tree with
n\geq 3
vertices. Then:

\operatorname{irr}_{T}(G)\leq(n-2)\,\operatorname{irr}(G).

Several classical indices, such as the Zagreb indices, are also used to quantify structural features of graphs, particularly in chemical graph theory.

Definition 3

(Zagreb Indices
[
12
,
13
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. The first and second Zagreb indices are given by

M_{1}(G)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg_{G}(v)^{2},\quad M_{2}(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\deg_{G}(u)\deg_{G}(v).

The sigma index, introduced as a quadratic analogue of the Albertson index, emphasizes larger degree differences by squaring them. It provides a refined sensitivity to irregularity.

Definition 4

(
\sigma

Let
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{S})=\max\{\sigma(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{S}\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\mathcal{S})=\min\{\sigma(G)\mid G\in\mathcal{S}\}.

Definition 5
.

Let

and

denote the minimum and maximum degrees in graph
G

denote the average degree. We also denote by

n

The next proposition gives a lower bound for the Albertson index of a graph in terms of its extremal degrees, offering insight into the effect of degree disparity.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
8

)

Let

\operatorname{irr}(G)>\frac{\delta(\Delta-\delta)^{2}\cdot n}{\Delta+1}.

In addition to combinatorial descriptors, spectral parameters of the graph—such as the adjacency matrix’s largest eigenvalue—have been heuristically related to irregularity, as shown below.

Proposition 2.3

[

Let

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence with
n\geq 3
, such that

d_{n}>d_{1}>d_{n-2}>\dots>d_{3}>d_{2}>d_{n-1}.

Then the caterpillar tree with this ordering attains the maximum value of
\operatorname{irr}
among all caterpillar trees whose path vertices have the degree sequence
\mathscr{D}
.

Proof.

Let us consider the degree sequence
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})
, where

d_{n}>d_{1}>\dots>d_{2}>d_{n-1}.

Then the Albertson index is given by:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+(d_{n}-1)^{2}+(d_{1}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+2(n-2)+(d_{n})^{2}-2d_{n}+(d_{1})^{2}-2d_{1}+2

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+(d_{n})^{2}-2d_{n}+(d_{1})^{2}-2d_{1}+2n-2

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i=n\end{subarray}}(d_{i}-1)^{2}+2n-2.

We now consider the following three cases to identify the configuration that maximizes
\operatorname{irr}(G)
.

Case 1:

Consider the term
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}
compared to
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-1)^{2}
.
Since
d_{i}^{2}>(d_{i}-1)^{2}
for all
d_{i}>1
, it follows that the maximum bound is attained by
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}
.

Case 2:

Consider the term
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})
.
We compare it with
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}
under the assumption that
(d_{i}-d_{i+1})\leq d_{i}^{2}
,
which clearly holds. Thus, the quadratic term dominates in maximizing
\operatorname{irr}(G)
.
Also, the expression
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}
has a larger impact on the total compared to other lower-order terms.

Case 3:

We proceed by induction. Assume the inequality holds for
n
, and consider
n+1
. Then:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+2(n-3)+d_{1}+d_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+d_{1}+d_{n}+2n-6.

Note that the term
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)
remains constant under the ordering
d_{n}>d_{1}>\dots>d_{2}>d_{n-1}
.
Hence, the expression

\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+2n-6+d_{1}+d_{n}

is maximized regardless of specific values of the degrees under this strict ordering.

It remains to show that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|
is maximized under this ordering. We assume:

|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|\leq d_{i}^{2}.

This inequality holds in both cases:

•

If
d_{i}\geq d_{i+1}
, then
d_{i}^{2}\geq d_{i}-d_{i+1}
.

•

If
d_{i}<d_{i+1}
, then
d_{i}^{2}\geq d_{i+1}-d_{i}
.

Thus, in both scenarios,
|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|\leq d_{i}^{2}
is satisfied.

By adding the constant term
\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n}+2n-6
, which is maximal under the given ordering, the total value of
\operatorname{irr}(G)
is maximized. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{i_{1}},d_{i_{2}},\dots,d_{i_{k}},d_{j_{1}},d_{j_{2}},\dots,d_{j_{\ell}})

\operatorname{irr}
among all caterpillar trees whose path vertices have the degree sequence
\mathscr{D}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.5
, for

and the ordering

in Proposition

\operatorname{irr}
is attained when
d_{3}
is the degree of the central vertex of the caterpillar. This can be generalized: for any degree sequence of length
n
satisfying

d_{n}>d_{1}>d_{n-2}>\dots>d_{3}>d_{2}>d_{n-1},

we have

\operatorname{irr}(G)=(d_{n}-1)^{2}+(d_{1}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|.

To extend the result to
n+1
, let
\mathscr{D}
be a degree sequence with

d_{n+1}\geq d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{2}\geq d_{n}.

Since
d_{1}\geq d_{n}
, we write
d_{1}=d_{n}+r
with
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then,

(d_{n}-1)^{2}+(d_{1}-1)=d_{n}^{2}-d_{n}+r.

Additionally,

(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)=d_{i}^{2}-3d_{i}+2,

so

\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)=\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+2(n-3).

Therefore, the Albertson index is

\operatorname{irr}(G)=(d_{n}-1)(d_{n}-2)+(d_{n}-1)+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}d_{i}+2(n-3)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|.

We assume that the expression

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|-\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}d_{i}

(2)

is maximized when the sequence satisfies the ordering in the hypothesis. We now show that this remains true for
n+1
.

To proceed, compare

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+d_{1}+d_{n}

with the general definition of Albertson index.

Next, consider a degree sequence of the form
\mathscr{D}=(d_{i_{1}},d_{i_{2}},\dots,d_{i_{k}},d_{j_{1}},d_{j_{2}},\dots,d_{j_{\ell}}),

with
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. When the parity of both
k
and
\ell
is the same, we have
|k-\ell|=0
; otherwise,
|k-\ell|=1
.

The Albertson index in this case becomes:

\operatorname{irr}_{\mathscr{D}}(T)=\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}|d_{i_{m}}-d_{i_{m+1}}|+\sum_{n=1}^{\ell-1}|d_{j_{n}}-d_{j_{n+1}}|.

Since the absolute value function prevents direct telescoping, the following inequality always holds:

|d_{i_{1}}-d_{i_{k}}|\leq\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}|d_{i_{m}}-d_{i_{m+1}}|,

and similarly for the
j
-indexed terms.

Now assume that the quantity

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+d_{1}+d_{n}

is maximal under the given ordering. Then, by extension,

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+d_{1}+d_{n+1}

is also maximal when
d_{n+1}\geq d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{2}\geq d_{n}
.

Finally, we compare the two possible cases:

Case 1:

If
d_{i}\geq d_{i+1}
, then

\operatorname{irr}(G)=2d_{1}-d_{n}+d_{n+1}

is maximal, since
2d_{1}
accounts for two terms involving
d_{1}
.

Case 2:

If
d_{i}\leq d_{i+1}
, then

\operatorname{irr}(G)=d_{n}+d_{n+1}

is maximal, combined with the maximum term
d_{1}+d_{n+1}
.

Hence,

\operatorname{irr}(G)=\begin{cases}2d_{1}-d_{n}+d_{n+1},&\text{if }d_{i}\geq d_{i+1},\\
d_{n}+d_{n+1},&\text{if }d_{i}\leq d_{i+1},\end{cases}

attains its maximum when
d_{n+1}\geq d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{2}\geq d_{n}
, completing the proof.
∎

3.2
, a degree sequence
\mathscr{D}
was given to establish the Albertson index for the case of three degrees
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}

Lemma
3.3
extends this result to the degree sequence
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
T
be a tree, and let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})
be a degree sequence such that

d_{4}\geqslant d_{3}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant d_{1}
. Then the Albertson index of the tree
T
is given by:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert+\sum_{i=2}^{3}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2.

Proof.

Assume the degree sequence is
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})
, where
d_{4}\geqslant d_{3}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant d_{1}
. We examine the following cases to determine the maximum and minimum values of the Albertson index for trees with this sequence.

Case 1:

The largest values are
d_{1}
and
d_{4}
, while the smallest are
d_{2}
and
d_{3}
. The sequence is ordered as
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})
, and we have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+1)(d_{1}-1)+(d_{4}+1)(d_{4}-1)+|d_{1}-d_{2}|+|d_{2}-d_{3}|+|d_{3}-d_{4}|

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{2}+2)(d_{2}-1)+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+(d_{2}+2)(d_{2}-1)+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)-2.

Case 2:

The largest values are
d_{2}
and
d_{3}
, and the smallest are
d_{1}
and
d_{4}
. With the ordering
\mathscr{D}=(d_{2},d_{1},d_{4},d_{3})
, we get:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{2}+1)(d_{2}-1)+(d_{3}+1)(d_{3}-1)+|d_{2}-d_{1}|+|d_{1}-d_{4}|+|d_{4}-d_{3}|

\displaystyle=d_{2}^{2}+d_{3}^{2}+\sum_{i\in\{1,3\}}|d_{i+1}-d_{i}|+|d_{1}-d_{4}|+(d_{1}+2)(d_{1}-1)+(d_{4}+2)(d_{4}-1)-2.

Case 3:

The largest values are
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
, and the smallest are
d_{4}
and
d_{3}
. For the sequence
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{3},d_{4},d_{2})
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+1)(d_{1}-1)+(d_{2}+1)(d_{2}-1)+|d_{1}-d_{3}|+|d_{3}-d_{4}|+|d_{4}-d_{2}|

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)+(d_{4}+2)(d_{4}-1)

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}|d_{i}-d_{i+2}|+\sum_{i=3}^{4}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2.

Case 4:

The largest values are
d_{1}
and
d_{3}
, and the smallest are
d_{2}
and
d_{4}
. With ordering
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{3},d_{4},d_{2})

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+1)(d_{1}-1)+(d_{3}+1)(d_{3}-1)+|d_{1}-d_{2}|+|d_{2}-d_{4}|+|d_{4}-d_{3}|

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{2}+2)(d_{2}-1)+(d_{4}+2)(d_{4}-1)

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{2}+d_{3}^{2}+\sum_{i\in\{1,3\}}|d_{i}-d_{i+2}|+|d_{2}-d_{4}|+d_{2}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+d_{2}+d_{4}-6.

Case 5:

The largest values are
d_{2}
and
d_{4}
, and the smallest are
d_{1}
and
d_{3}
. Ordering
\mathscr{D}=(d_{2},d_{3},d_{1},d_{4})
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{2}+1)(d_{2}-1)+(d_{4}+1)(d_{4}-1)+|d_{2}-d_{3}|+|d_{3}-d_{1}|+|d_{1}-d_{4}|

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{1}+2)(d_{1}-1)+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)

\displaystyle=d_{2}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+|d_{2}-d_{3}|+|d_{3}-d_{1}|+|d_{1}-d_{4}|+d_{1}^{2}+d_{3}^{2}+d_{1}+d_{3}-6.

Case 6:

The largest values are
d_{3}
and
d_{4}
, and the smallest are
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. With ordering
\mathscr{D}=(d_{3},d_{1},d_{2},d_{4})
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{3}+1)(d_{3}-1)+(d_{4}+1)(d_{4}-1)+|d_{3}-d_{1}|+|d_{1}-d_{2}|+|d_{2}-d_{4}|

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{1}+2)(d_{1}-1)+(d_{2}+2)(d_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=d_{3}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}|d_{i}-d_{i+2}|+|d_{1}-d_{2}|+d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+d_{1}+d_{2}-6.

Summarizing the results across all cases, the irregularity index satisfies:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{irr}_{\max}(T)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}|d_{i}-d_{i+2}|+d_{3}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+d_{3}+d_{4}-6,\\
\operatorname{irr}_{\min}(T)=d_{3}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}|d_{i}-d_{i+2}|+|d_{1}-d_{2}|+d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+d_{1}+d_{2}-6.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.
∎

To generalize the computation of the Albertson index for more complex structures, we now extend the
analysis to degree sequences of length six. The following lemma provides an explicit formula for
\operatorname{irr}(T)

when the degree sequence
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{6})
is ordered in non-decreasing fashion.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T
be a tree, and let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{6})
be a degree sequence such that

d_{6}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{1}
. Then the Albertson index of
T
is given by:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{n}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{6}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert+\sum_{i=2}^{5}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2.

Proof.

Assume the sequence is
d=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4},d_{5},d_{6})
, where
d_{6}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{1}
. According to Lemma
3.3
, and in view of both the maximum and minimum values, we consider the following cases under which this sequence represents a valid graph. The sequence must satisfy certain conditions, as the degrees of the vertices are interrelated.

Case 1:

In this case, assuming that
d_{1}
represents the maximum value, we obtain:

\operatorname{irr}(T_{1})=\begin{cases}d_{1}^{2}+d_{6}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{5}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{1},d_{6},\\
d_{1}^{2}+d_{5}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{2,3,4,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{1},d_{5},\\
d_{1}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{2,3,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{1},d_{4},\\
d_{1}^{2}+d_{3}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{2,4,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{1},d_{3},\\
d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{3,4,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{1},d_{2}.\end{cases}

Case 2:

In this case, assuming that
d_{2}
is the maximum value, we derive:

\operatorname{irr}(T_{2})=\begin{cases}d_{2}^{2}+d_{6}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,3,4,5\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{2},d_{6},\\
d_{2}^{2}+d_{5}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,3,4,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{2},d_{5},\\
d_{2}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,3,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{2},d_{4},\\
d_{2}^{2}+d_{3}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,4,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{2},d_{3}.\end{cases}

Case 3:

In this case, assuming that
d_{3}
is the maximum value, we find:

\operatorname{irr}(T_{3})=\begin{cases}d_{3}^{2}+d_{6}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,2,4,5\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{3},d_{6},\\
d_{3}^{2}+d_{5}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,2,4,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{3},d_{5},\\
d_{3}^{2}+d_{4}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,2,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{3},d_{4}.\end{cases}

Now, let us suppose the maximum values are
d_{4},d_{6}
,
d_{4},d_{5}
, or
d_{5},d_{6}
, while the remaining vertices correspond to minimum values. Then we have:

\operatorname{irr}(T_{4})=\begin{cases}d_{4}^{2}+d_{6}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,2,3,5\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{4},d_{6},\\
d_{4}^{2}+d_{5}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,2,3,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{4},d_{5},\\
d_{5}^{2}+d_{6}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{1,2,3,4\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2&\text{for maximum }d_{5},d_{6}.\end{cases}

Therefore, from all the cases, we observe that
\operatorname{irr}(T_{1})
includes several fixed terms, and that all the cases share certain common constants. This leads to twelve possible configurations. Hence, we conclude:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{irr}_{\max}(T)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{6}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{5}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2,\\
\operatorname{irr}_{\min}(T)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|+\sum_{i\in\{3,4,5,6\}}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)-2.\end{cases}

As desired.
∎

Building upon the preceding cases and generalizing the observed patterns concerning the extremal values of the Albertson index,
we now turn to the study of a general tree of arbitrary order
n
.
Based on the structural properties revealed by specific degree sequences,
the following theorem presents the general formula for the maximum and minimum values of the Albertson
index with respect to a given degree sequence.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
T
be a tree of order
n
, and let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})
be a
degree sequence such that
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\dots\geq d_{n}
. Then the maximum and minimum
values of the Albertson irregularity index are given by:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=\sum_{i=1}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n}+2+\sum_{i=1}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert.

Proof.

We observe that the constant term is
\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert
, and the expression
(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)
is a quadratic function in
d_{i}
, where
d_{i}>2
. Thus, we have:

•

If
d_{i}=d_{i+1}
, then
(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)>\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert
.

•

If
d_{i+1}>d_{i}
, then
(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)\geq\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert
.

From Lemma
3.2
, for a tree
T
of order
n=3
, we have:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{irr}_{\max}(T)=(d_{1}-1)^{2}+(d_{2}-1)^{2}+(d_{3}^{2}-3d_{3}+2)(d_{1}d_{2}-d_{1}d_{3}-d_{2}d_{3}+d_{3}^{2}),\\
\operatorname{irr}_{\min}(T)=(d_{1}-1)^{2}+\left(d_{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}+(d_{3}-1)^{2}+\frac{3}{4}.\end{cases}

This holds if and only if
d_{i+1}>d_{i}
.
It follows from Proposition
2.5
that
(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)\geq\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert
.
Using Theorem
3.1
, for
n=4
, we have:

\operatorname{irr}(T)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{irr}_{\max}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}(d_{i}-1)^{2}+(d_{1}+d_{2})-(d_{3}+3d_{4})+2,\\
\operatorname{irr}_{\min}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}(d_{i}-1)^{2}+(d_{1}-d_{2}-d_{3}-d_{4})+2.\end{cases}

This aligns with Proposition
2.5
, and we note that
d(\operatorname{irr}_{\max},\operatorname{irr}_{\min})=2d_{1}-(d_{3}+d_{4})+5
.

Hence, the result holds for any sequence of order
3
. Consequently, the expression

\operatorname{irr}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n}+2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert

holds for
n
, under the condition
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\dots\geq d_{n}
.

To extend this to
n+1
, consider the sequence
d_{1},\dots,d_{n+1}
.
For consecutive differences over
n+1
terms, the sum involves
n
terms for both
(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)
and
\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert
, with the constant term being
d_{1}+d_{n+1}+2
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{irr}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n+1}+2+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}d_{i}^{2}+d_{1}-3d_{2}-3\sum_{i=3}^{n}d_{i}-2d_{n+1}+2(n+1)+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}-3\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}+2n+d_{1}+d_{n}+2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}-2d_{1}-3\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}d_{i}-2d_{n}+2(n+1)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=3}^{n+2}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n+2}+2+\sum_{i=3}^{n+1}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=3}^{n+2}d_{i}^{2}+d_{1}-3d_{3}-3\sum_{i=4}^{n+1}d_{i}-2d_{n+2}+2(n+1)+\sum_{i=3}^{n+1}\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert.

Now, observe that whenever
(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)\geq\lvert d_{i}-d_{i+1}\rvert
, the inequality remains valid for
n+1
and
n+2
, provided that
d_{i}>d_{i+1}
.
In this case, the constant term is
d_{1}+d_{n}
.
∎

4
Sigma Index in Trees With Degree Sequence

In this section, we extend the investigation initiated in Section
3
on the Albertson index
to study the Sigma index. Specifically, we focus on analyzing the Sigma index, which measures the
structural irregularity of a graph—particularly trees—by evaluating the sum of the squared differences
between the degrees of adjacent vertices. Throughout this section, we examine the Sigma index on trees under
specific cases of degree sequences
\mathscr{D}
, which form the basis for the general formulation.

Proposition 4.1
.

The Sigma index of the caterpillar tree
\mathscr{C}(n,m)
of order
(n,m)
is given by:

\sigma(\mathscr{C}(n,m))=\begin{cases}2m^{3},&\quad\text{if }n=2,\\
2m^{3}+m-2,&\quad\text{if }n\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

Assume the sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
and the pendant vertices
y_{1,1},\dots,y_{i,j}
are adjacent to
x_{i}
. Then
\deg x_{1}=\deg x_{n}=m+1
and
\deg x_{i}=m+2
for
2\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
. Hence, according to Proposition
3.1
we have

\operatorname{irr}(\mathscr{C}(n,m))=\begin{cases}m(m+1)n-2m+2,&\quad\text{if }n\geqslant 3,\\
m(m+1)n-2m,&\quad\text{if }n\in\{1,2\}.\end{cases}

According to Figure
1
, for
n=2
, the Sigma index of the caterpillar tree is given by:
\sigma=2m^{3}
.
For
n=3
, we have:
\sigma(\mathscr{C}(n,m))=2m^{3}+m-2
, where
\deg x_{1}=\deg x_{n}=m+1
at both ends of
\mathscr{C}(n,m)
and
\deg x_{2}=m+2

T
be a tree of order
n
, and let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence such that
d_{1}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{n}
. Then, the Sigma index of the tree
T
is given by:

\sigma(T)=(d_{n}-1)^{3}+(d_{1}-1)^{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2).

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence such that
d_{1}\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{n}
. At the two ends of the sequence, we consider the degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{n}
, which contribute the terms
(d_{1}-1)^{3}
and
(d_{n}-1)^{3}
, respectively. For the middle part of the sequence, from
d_{2}
to
d_{n-1}
, we have the terms
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2)
. Thus, the expression

\sigma(T)=(d_{n}-1)^{3}+(d_{1}-1)^{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2)

holds for
n
. Now, we aim to prove that the formula also holds for
n+1
and
n+2
. For
n+1
, we have:

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{n+1}-1)^{3}+(d_{2}-1)^{3}+\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n}(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2)

\displaystyle=(d_{n+1}-1)^{3}+(d_{2}-1)^{3}+d_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n}2d_{i}^{2}+d_{n+1}^{2}-2\sum_{i=2}^{n}d_{i}d_{i+1}+\sum_{i=3}^{n}(d_{i}^{3}-4d_{i}^{2}+5d_{i}-2)

\displaystyle=(d_{n+1}-1)^{3}+(d_{2}-1)^{3}+d_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n}(d_{i}^{3}-6d_{i}^{2}+5d_{i}-2d_{i}d_{i+1})+d_{n+1}^{2}-2d_{2}d_{3}-2(n-2).

It is clear that the constant term is
(d_{n+1}-1)^{3}+(d_{2}-1)^{3}+d_{2}^{2}-2(n-2)
. In this case, we distinguish between the following situations:

Case 1:

If
d_{i}>d_{i+1}
, then:

•

For the first summation, we assume the sequence
d_{2}>d_{3}>\cdots>d_{n+1}
, which implies
(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}>0
for all
i\in[2,n+1]
. Suppose
d_{i}=n+2-i
, so that
d_{2}=n
,
d_{3}=n-1
, …,
d_{n+1}=1
. Then
d_{i}-d_{i+1}=1
, and therefore
\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}=n
.

•

For the second summation, we analyze the values
d_{3}>d_{4}>\cdots>d_{n}
with respect to
(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2)
:

–

If
d_{i}>2
, then
(d_{i}-1)^{2}>0
and
(d_{i}-2)>0
, hence the term is positive.

–

If
1<d_{i}<2
, then
(d_{i}-1)^{2}>0
but
(d_{i}-2)<0
, so the term is negative, which is not accepted under the assumption
d_{3}>\cdots>d_{n}
.

–

If
d_{i}<1
, then
(d_{i}-1)^{2}>0
and
(d_{i}-2)<0
, so the term is also negative and not acceptable in this context.

Therefore, the second summation is valid under the condition
d_{i}>d_{i+1}
if and only if
d_{i}>2
.

Case 2:

If
d_{i}<d_{i+1}
, the sequence becomes
d_{n+1}>d_{n}>\dots>d_{3}
. For the first summation, we observe that although
d_{i}-d_{i+1}<0
, the square
(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}
remains positive. Regarding the second summation, the same condition
d_{i}>2
ensures that the term is valid, as discussed in Case 1.

Taking into account the constant term
(d_{n+1}-1)^{3}+(d_{2}-1)^{3}+d_{2}^{2}-2(n-2)
and the cases above, we find that the condition
d_{i}>2
must hold. Hence, the formula remains valid for
n+1
.

Now, we consider the case
n+2
. We obtain:

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{n+2}-1)^{3}+(d_{3}-1)^{3}+\sum_{i=3}^{n+2}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=4}^{n+1}(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2)

\displaystyle=(d_{3}-1)^{3}+\sum_{i=3}^{n+1}\left[(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+(d_{i+1}-1)^{2}(d_{i+1}-2)\right]+(d_{n+2}-1)^{3}

\displaystyle=(d_{3}-1)^{3}+(d_{3}-d_{4})^{2}+\sum_{i=4}^{n+1}\left[(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2)\right]+(d_{n+2}-1)^{3}.

This completes the proof.
∎
We now present an alternative closed-form expression for the Sigma index of a tree
T
based on
its degree sequence. This formulation refines the contribution of extremal and internal vertices
by incorporating shifted polynomial weights, offering deeper insight into the structure-sensitive nature of the index.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
T
be a tree of order
n
, and let
\mathscr{D}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence such that
d_{n}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{1}
. Then, the Sigma index of the tree
T
is given by:

\sigma(T)=\sum_{i\in\{1,n\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+2n-2.

Proof.

It is well known that the Sigma index is defined as
\sigma(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(d_{u}-d_{v})^{2}
. Let
d=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})
be a degree sequence of order
n\geqslant 1
, where
d_{n}\geqslant d_{n-1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{2}\geqslant d_{1}
. We consider the following cases.

We have previously established the Sigma index for a degree sequence as

\sigma(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}-2.

Additionally, in Lemma
4.2
, we obtained

\sigma(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{3}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}-2,

by considering the maximum and minimum values in both cases and the constant terms. Accordingly, we propose the Sigma index for a tree with a degree sequence as

\sigma(T)=\sum_{i\in\{1,n\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}.

For a degree sequence of order
6
, we have

\sigma(T)=\sum_{i\in\{1,n\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+10.

Thus, the relationship holds for
n\in[3,6]
. To prove it for a general
n
, we show that it holds for
n+1
.

Assume the sequence
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, where
d_{n}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{1}
. Then, we have:

Case 1:

In this case:

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+1)(d_{1}-1)^{2}+(d_{n}+1)(d_{n}-1)^{2}+(d_{1}-d_{2})^{2}+\dots+(d_{n-1}-d_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{2}+2)(d_{2}-1)^{2}+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)^{2}+\dots+(d_{n-1}+2)(d_{n-1}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{1,n\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}.

Case 2:

Now, consider shifting the sequence to
d=(d_{n-1},\dots,d_{2})
. Then:

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{2}+1)(d_{2}-1)^{2}+(d_{n-1}+1)(d_{n-1}-1)^{2}+(d_{2}-d_{3})^{2}+\dots+(d_{n-2}-d_{n-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)^{2}+(d_{4}+2)(d_{4}-1)^{2}+\dots+(d_{n-2}+2)(d_{n-2}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{2,n-1\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n-2}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}.

Thus, the relationship holds for
n
. Now we prove it for
n+1
, with the sequence
d=(d_{2},\dots,d_{n+1})
, where
d_{n+1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{2}
.

Case 3:

In this case:

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{2}+1)(d_{2}-1)^{2}+(d_{n+1}+1)(d_{n+1}-1)^{2}+(d_{2}-d_{3})^{2}+\dots+(d_{n}-d_{n+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{3}+2)(d_{3}-1)^{2}+(d_{4}+2)(d_{4}-1)^{2}+\dots+(d_{n}+2)(d_{n}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{2,n+1\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}.

Case 4:

Finally, consider shifting the sequence to
d=(d_{n},\dots,d_{3})
. Then:

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=(d_{3}+1)(d_{3}-1)^{2}+(d_{n}+1)(d_{n}-1)^{2}+(d_{3}-d_{4})^{2}+\dots+(d_{n-1}-d_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(d_{4}+2)(d_{4}-1)^{2}+(d_{5}+2)(d_{5}-1)^{2}+\dots+(d_{n-1}+2)(d_{n-1}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{3,n\}}(d_{i}+1)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=4}^{n-1}(d_{i}+2)(d_{i}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{n-1}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}.

Therefore, the relationship also holds for
n+1
. These cases represent the primary scenarios considered. Notably, the constant term arising from the summations is
2n-2
.

This completes the proof.
∎

5
Conclusion

This paper has presented a rigorous closed-form analysis of two prominent irregularity measures in graph theory—the Albertson index and the Sigma index—specifically within the context of trees with prescribed degree sequences. By systematically examining caterpillar trees and generalizing to arbitrary tree structures, we have derived exact expressions and extremal bounds that deepen the structural understanding of topological indices.

In the case of the Albertson index, Lemma
3.4
establishes the exact values for trees of order six under degree sequences ordered as
d_{6}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{1}
, while Theorem
2.5
provides a general formula for trees of arbitrary order
n
, showing that

\operatorname{irr}(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)+d_{1}+d_{n}+2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|d_{i}-d_{i+1}|.

This expression captures both the local and global aspects of degree deviation in a unified manner.

Similarly, the Sigma index has been thoroughly analyzed. Lemma
4.2
provides exact values for trees of order four, while Theorem
4.5
introduces a summation-based formulation involving shifted degree polynomials and adjacent degree differences. An alternative formulation is provided in Theorem
4.4
, where for trees with degree sequence
d_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant d_{n}
, the Sigma index is expressed as

\sigma(T)=(d_{n}-1)^{3}+(d_{1}-1)^{3}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_{i}-d_{i+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(d_{i}-1)^{2}(d_{i}-2).

These two formulations jointly capture both extremal contributions and inner-vertex irregularity with high sensitivity to degree variation.

Throughout the paper, structured examples and computed tables—such as Tables
4
and
3
—illustrate the theoretical claims and provide numerical verification across various degree sequences. The results emphasize that the maximal and minimal values of the irregularity indices are tightly dependent on the degree ordering, and particular configurations (e.g., placing the largest degrees at the boundaries) often yield extremal outcomes.

The work presented offers a unified analytical framework for comparing linear and quadratic measures of irregularity in trees, with precise formulas validated across fixed-size examples and general sequences. This framework not only strengthens the theoretical foundations of irregularity indices but also establishes a reference for further explorations in structured families such as caterpillar trees.

Future research could extend the current results in two directions. First, a closer inspection of summation ranges and index-shifted forms may reveal tighter bounds or simplifications. Second, the extremal configurations identified in this study could be further examined in light of algorithmic graph generation or chemical graph applications, especially in contexts where irregularity measures are used for classification or optimization.
