Synthetic redesign of Corynebacterium glutamicum for 1,4-butanediol production via L-glutamate-derived CoA-independent pathway
Authors: Jinyoung Kim, Hyeon Jin Kim, Sukjae Han, Moon Jeong Kim, Gibyuck Byun, Dae Geun Cha, Seongmin Kim, Kyung Jin Kim, Jae Hun Lee, Young Lyeol Yang, Sang Woo Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract1,4-Butanediol (1,4-BDO) is a key commodity chemical used in the production of various products, including plastics, fibers, and polyesters. Here, a potent microbial platform for 1,4-BDO production from glucose via the L-glutamate-derived CoA-independent pathway was developed using Corynebacterium glutamicum as the host strain. Introducing a CoA-independent 1,4-BDO biosynthesis pathway and removing a metabolic flux bypass, the resulting initial strain produced 2.20 g/L of 1,4-BDO from glucose. Upon prolonged cultivation, C. glutamicum showed an endogenous capacity to degrade 1,4-BDO. Transcriptomic analysis was conducted to identify genes potentially involved in this pathway. Among them, fadH, aldH, and adhA were verified as the key enzymes for 1,4-BDO degradation. The deletion of three dehydrogenase genes from the initial strain removed the product degradation and achieved an improved 1,4-BDO titer of 3.14 g/L. Finally, fed-batch fermentation of the final strain was conducted, resulting in 13.4 g/L of 1,4-BDO production. This work demonstrates the first successful development of C. glutamicum as a potent platform strain for the biosynthesis of 1,4-BDO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00579-9.Keywords: 1,4-Butanediol; Corynebacterium glutamicum; Metabolic engineering; RNA-seq analysis

Background

1,4-Butanediol (1,4-BDO) is a crucial commodity chemical used as a precursor in the synthesis of tetrahydrofuran, polybutylene terephthalate, and γ-butyrolactone [1]. Owing to its broad applicability, 1,4-BDO is extensively used in the plastics, textiles, and biomedical industries [2–4]. Despite growing demand, 1,4-BDO is primarily produced from petroleum-derived feedstocks, which raises concerns about environmental impacts, resource depletion, and cost efficiency [5].

To address these issues, microbial factories for 1,4-BDO production have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional chemical synthesis. Previous studies have constructed 1,4-BDO biosynthetic pathways in Escherichia coli using various renewable carbon sources, including glucose, xylose, and glycerol [6–8]. Among these strategies, a CoA-independent pathway extended from L-glutamate catabolism was recently established for 1,4-BDO production in E. coli [9]. While the conventional CoA-dependent pathway often suffers from the low catalytic efficiency of key enzymes, the CoA-independent alternative benefits from thermodynamically favorable, irreversible reduction steps, thereby enhancing flux toward the product. In addition, the absence of CoA intermediates relieves the concerns about productivity loss associated with hydrolysis and lactonization, further supporting the advantages of the glutamate-derived CoA-independent pathway.

In this study, Corynebacterium glutamicum was engineered as a microbial chassis for the production of 1,4-BDO. C. glutamicum is a well-characterized GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) organism, widely used in industrial-scale production of amino acids, including L-glutamate [10–12]. Given its capacity to produce L-glutamate, C. glutamicum serves as a metabolically suitable host for introducing glutamate-derived biosynthetic pathways. To establish a 1,4-BDO biosynthesis platform, a CoA-independent pathway was introduced, and endogenous genes diverting metabolic flux away from the product were deleted. Subsequently, it was revealed that C. glutamicum possesses an intrinsic pathway for 1,4-BDO degradation upon flask cultivation. Transcriptomic analysis identified three candidate genes involved in the degradation pathway, and targeted deletion of those genes improved product accumulation. These results, achieved through a combination of metabolic engineering and systems biology approaches, demonstrate the feasibility of employing C. glutamicum as a platform strain for L-glutamate-based 1,4-BDO biosynthesis.

Methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, primers, and reagents

All strains, plasmids, primers, and gene fragments in this study are listed in Supplementary Information, Tables S1, S2, and S3, respectively. E. coli Mach1-T1R (Invitrogen) was used for gene cloning purposes. C. glutamicum ATCC 13,032 (American Type and Culture Collection) was used as a host strain for 1,4-BDO production. Oligonucleotides for gene cloning and RNA-seq were synthesized by Bionics Korea, and gene fragments for the construction of the recombinant plasmids were synthesized by Twist Bioscience. Plasmid DNA was prepared using the Exprep™ Plasmid SV mini kit (GeneAll). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were purified using the DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research) or the Expin™ Gel SV kit (GeneAll). All restriction enzymes, Phusion DNA polymerase, and Quick ligase were purchased from New England Biolabs for gene cloning. All experiments were conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Media and cultivation conditions

For E. coli cultivation, Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and Bacto agar were purchased from BD Bioscience. E. coli was routinely cultured in LB medium at 37 °C and 250 rpm. Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth, purchased from KisanBio, was used for the routine cultivation of C. glutamicum. For 1,4-BDO production experiments, C. glutamicum strains were grown in a cultivation medium (1 g/L MgSO4, 2 g/L KH2PO4, 5 g/L yeast extract, 25 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 20 mg/L FeSO4, 20 mg/L MnSO4, 20 mg/L nicotine amide, 20 mg/L calcium pantothenate, 20 mg/L thiamine, 100 µg/L biotin, and 6 g/L CaCO3) with 5% or 10% (w/v) glucose as a carbon source. The pH was adjusted to 7.2 with 1 M NaOH. C. glutamicum strains were cultivated at 30 °C, 200 rpm. Kanamycin (50 µg/mL for E. coli, 25 µg/mL for C. glutamicum) and spectinomycin (50 µg/mL for E. coli, 100 µg/mL for C. glutamicum) were used when required.

Construction of strains and plasmids

Genome manipulation of C. glutamicum was performed with the pK18mobsacB-mediated two-step homologous recombination method [13]. DNA cassettes for homologous recombination and overexpression were prepared by gene synthesis and amplified with PCR. pK18mobsacB was digested with SalⅠ to produce a linear vector fragment and subjected to a Gibson Assembly with each DNA cassette. Constructed suicide plasmids were electroporated into C. glutamicum, and transformed cells were screened on LB agar plates containing 25 µg/mL kanamycin to screen the first crossover event. For the second crossover event, colonies with single crossover were cultivated in a liquid LB medium and screened on LB agar plates containing 10% sucrose for positive selection.

The construction of plasmids for gene overexpression was performed with pCDF-pHM1519mut, an E. coli-C. glutamicum shuttle vector. pCDF-pHM1519mut was digested with ClaⅠ and AgeⅠ to produce a linear vector. fadH, aldH, and adhA under the tuf promoter were synthesized and amplified with PCR. Each purified PCR product and linearized vector were then subjected to the Gibson Assembly reaction. The constructed plasmids were transformed into C. glutamicum ATCC 13,032 and screened on LB agar plates containing 100 µg/mL spectinomycin.

Transcriptome analysis

RNA-seq experiments were performed in biological duplicates. The seed culture was inoculated into a 500-mL baffled flask containing 50 mL of cultivation medium for 1,4-BDO production with 10% (w/v) glucose. Harvested samples were treated with RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent (Qiagen) to protect RNA from degradation. Total RNA was extracted and purified using the Quick-RNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (ZymoResearch). The ribosomal RNAs were depleted with RiboRid, as previously described [14]. RNA-seq libraries were constructed from the rRNA-removed RNA using the KAPA Stranded RNA-seq Library Preparation Kit (Kapa Biosystems). All the libraries were validated using X-Mark LabChip with the DNA NGS 3 K reagent kit (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, US). The genomes were resequenced using the Illumina Miniseq System and mapped onto the reference genome (RefSeq NC_003450.3) using breseq version 0.33.0 [15]. The output sequence was used as the reference genome for the RNA-seq analysis. The sequencing reads were generated by using the Illumina NextSeq 500 System and mapped onto the resequenced reference genome using bowtie2 version 2.4.4 with option (‘--mp’, ‘4’, ‘--sensitive-local’) [16]. SAM files generated from bowtie2 converted to BAM files using Samtools version 1.13 [17] to count reads per gene through htseq-count [18] and analyze differential gene expression with DESeq2 [19]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with log2 fold change (LFC) >1 and adjusted P value (Padj) < 0.05 were considered statistically meaningful.

In silico enzyme analysis

To assess enzymatic performance, catalytic parameters (kcat and Km) were predicted using CatPred [20], based on three-dimensional protein structures generated via the CHAI-1 folding framework [21]. For the docking simulations, target enzymes were queried against the Protein Data Bank (PDB) using BLASTp to find homologous proteins with available crystal structures. Homologs exhibiting high sequence identity and conserved catalytic motifs were selected for structural alignments and motif analyses using ChimeraX [22]. Conserved substrate binding motifs were then used to define spatial constraints for docking simulations in CHAI-1. Final docking models were refined through molecular dynamics–based energy minimization using OpenMM [23] to ensure structural stability and realistic enzyme-ligand interactions.

HPLC analysis for quantification of glucose and 1,4-BDO

Glucose and 1,4-BDO in the samples were quantified using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system with RID-20 A refractive index detector (Nexera Lite, Shimadzu). For the quantification of glucose, a Hi-plex H (6.5 × 300 mm, 8 μm, Agilent) column was used at 25 ℃. A 0.5 mM H2SO4 buffer was used as the mobile phase with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. For 1,4-BDO analysis, sample separation was performed with Poroshell 120 EC-C18 (4.6 × 150 mm, 4 μm, Agilent) column at 40 ℃. The mobile phase was 0.1 M phosphate buffer, and the flow rate was 0.8 mL/min.

Fed-batch fermentation

The 5-L fed-batch fermentation was conducted with an initial volume of 1 L in a 5-L fermenter (Marado-PDA, BIOCNS). Fermentation media consists of 2.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 2.5 g/L KH2PO4, 5 g/L yeast extract, 25 g/L peptone, 25 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 100 g/L FeSO4·7H2O, 25 mg/L nicotine amide, and 25 mg/L calcium pantothenate with 5% (w/v) glucose was used. Feeding was performed with the same media, except that glucose was concentrated at 50% (w/v). The glucose concentration level of the fermenter broth was carefully maintained below 25 g/L. The pH was maintained at 7.2 with 1 M HCl and 1 M NaOH, and the dissolved oxygen (DO) level was kept below 10% after 12 h of fermentation.

Results and discussion

Design and optimization of a CoA-independent 1,4-BDO pathway in C. glutamicum

To construct a CoA-independent pathway for L-glutamate-derived 1,4-BDO biosynthesis in C. glutamicum ATCC 13,032, a series of heterologous genes were integrated into the genome. Based on the previous studies, a mutant glutamate decarboxylase (EcGadB*) from E. coli [24] was introduced to enable the conversion of glutamate to γ-aminobutyrate (GABA) at neutral pH. The GABA aminotransferase (GabT) of C. glutamicum converts GABA into succinic semialdehyde (SSA) at neutral pH, regulated by GabR, a PucR-type transcriptional regulator [25, 26]. To ensure overexpression independent of regulation, an additional copy of endogenous gabT was integrated into the C. glutamicum genome. An alcohol dehydrogenase (YqhD) from E. coli and carboxylic acid reductase (CAR) from Mycobacterium abscessus were expressed to catalyze the sequential reduction of SSA to 1,4-BDO. EcYqhD was selected due to its broad substrate specificity and widespread use in previous studies for diol production [9, 27, 28]. MaCAR was chosen based on a previous report demonstrating its high reductase activity toward 4-HB [29]. To fully activate CAR, phosphopantetheine transferase (Sfp) from Bacillus subtilis was co-expressed [30].

To prevent depletion of this key intermediate via native degradation routes, three genes encoding succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenases (gabD1, gabD2, gabD3) were deleted. In addition, a gene encoding isocitrate lyase (aceA) was knocked out to block the glyoxylate shunt and enhance carbon flux from isocitrate toward α-ketoglutarate. Finally, the glutamate exporter gene (NCgl1221) was deleted to inhibit extracellular glutamate secretion [31], thus improving precursor availability for the 1,4-BDO pathway (Fig. 1a). The resulting strain, designated CgB01, however, did not produce detectable amounts of 1,4-BDO under shake-flask cultivation with 5% (w/v) glucose, indicating the necessity for further pathway optimization (Fig. 1b).

Glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), which converts glutamate to GABA, typically exhibits optimal activity under acidic conditions [32]. Although EcGadB* (E89Q/Δ452–466) has been engineered to conserve its activity at neutral pH, this may be suboptimal in C. glutamicum. A recent study evaluated the GAD activities from different sources in C. glutamicum and reported that GAD from Lactobacillus senmaizukei improved GABA production compared to EcGAD* [33]. Therefore, EcGAD* was replaced with LsGAD to enhance the metabolic flux toward GABA. The resulting strain, designated CgB02, produced 0.43 g/L of 1,4-BDO after 24 h of cultivation, demonstrating that selecting an optimal GAD in C. glutamicum is critical for 1,4-BDO biosynthesis.

Aldehydes are highly reactive electrophilic carbonyl compounds that can cause cytotoxic effects when accumulated in microbial cells. They can covalently bind to nucleophilic biomolecules such as proteins, DNA, and membrane lipids, thereby impairing cellular functions [34–36]. In strain CgB02, deletion of succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase genes may result in the accumulation of aldehyde intermediate, necessitating rapid downstream conversion to the final product to reduce toxicity and maintain metabolic flux. To accelerate SSA conversion, an additional copy of downstream genes yqhD and CAR was integrated into the genome. The insertion sequence ISCg2 was selected as the integration site, based on a previous study that showed its deletion improved the expression of recombinant proteins [37]. The resulting strain, CgB03, achieved a 1,4-BDO titer of 0.63 g/L after 24 h of cultivation, which is about a 46% increase from the CgB02 strain. This result underscores the importance of minimizing toxic aldehyde intermediates to boost 1,4-BDO productivity.

Transcriptomic and in silico analysis for identifying 1,4-BDO degradation candidate genes

Following the 1,4-BDO production in strain CgB03, extended cultivation experiments were conducted to enhance the production titer further. Unexpectedly, a substantial decrease in 1,4-BDO concentration was observed during prolonged cultivation. When CgB03 was cultivated in media containing 10% (w/v) glucose, it produced up to 2.20 g/L of 1,4-BDO after 48 h, but only 0.43 g/L remained at 72 h (Fig. 2a). Given that there are no reports on the spontaneous degradation of 1,4-BDO, we hypothesized that its degradation results from cellular metabolism. A recent study reported the consumption of 1,3-propanediol by C. glutamicum, which supports the possibility that 1,4-BDO may also be metabolized [38].

Since the introduced enzymes involved in the 1,4-BDO pathway could catalyze reverse reactions, it was unclear whether the product degradation was affected solely by these enzymes or also involved endogenous enzymes. To determine whether the 1,4-BDO degradation pathway is a native characteristic of C. glutamicum, the 1,4-BDO degradation capabilities of CgB03 and the wild type were compared. Both strains were cultivated in the media supplemented with 10 g/L of extracellular 1,4-BDO for 96 h (Fig. 2a). Surprisingly, both strains exhibited 1,4-BDO degradation, with the CgB03 strain showing an earlier and faster degradation. While the rapid degradation in CgB03 could result from reverse reactions of the heterologous pathway, the degradation observed in the wild type suggested that C. glutamicum possesses an intrinsic mechanism to catabolize 1,4-BDO.

To investigate an endogenous degradation pathway of 1,4-BDO in C. glutamicum, RNA-seq analysis was performed for both CgB03 and wild-type strains. Since 1,4-BDO degradation was not observed at early cultivation but observed in both strains at 72 h, transcriptomes at 72 h were compared to those at 16 h for each strain to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Although the microbial 1,4-BDO degradation pathway is not fully elucidated, a putative utilization pathway in Pseudomonas putida KT2440 has been recently proposed based on transcriptomic analysis [39]. According to the proposed mechanism, 1,4-BDO undergoes sequential oxidation to 4-hydroxybutyrate (4-HB) via alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenases and is further metabolized via direct oxidation or CoA-activation (Fig. 2b). Assuming that 1,4-BDO degradation in C. glutamicum may be initiated in a similar mechanism to that of P. putida, a comparative analysis of dehydrogenase genes (Padj < 0.05) was performed based on the changes in expression level (Fig. 2c).

Among the dehydrogenase genes that were differentially expressed, three genes involved in the alcohol degradation pathways—NCgl0313 (fadH) [40], NCgl2698 (aldH) [41], and NCgl2709 (adhA) [42]—showed notable increased expression in CgB03 over time (Fig. 2c). FadH and AdhA catalyze the oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes, while AldH further oxidizes aldehydes to carboxylic acids. These genes were selected as candidate genes for 1,4-BDO degradation based on a prior study in Pseudomonas putida. Catalytic activity prediction with CatPred [20] suggested positive activities of FadH and AdhA toward 1,4-BDO, and of AldH toward 4-hydroxybutanal (4-HBA) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Additionally, structural modeling and docking simulations showed that all three enzymes can bind their respective substrates, further supporting their roles in 1,4-BDO degradation (Supplementary Fig. S2). Interestingly, while adhA and aldH were upregulated in both strains, fadH expression was only elevated in CgB03. FadH may be another factor contributing to the faster degradation in the CgB03 strain, in addition to a reverse reaction of the heterologous pathway. Furthermore, NCgl0283 (phdD), a gene encoding an acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, was also upregulated in the presence of extracellular 1,4-BDO. phdD may participate in CoA-associated downstream degradation steps, considering the proposed 1,4-BDO degradation pathway from P. putida. Although this study is primarily focused on the upstream degradation steps to reduce product degradation, further investigation of phdD would contribute to a deeper understanding of 1,4-BDO metabolism in C. glutamicum. Consequently, fadH, aldH, and adhA were selected as candidate genes for 1,4-BDO degradation and subjected to further validation.

Construction of a sustainable 1,4-BDO-producing C. glutmaicum strain

To validate the functional roles of the three candidate genes in 1,4-BDO degradation, plasmids harboring fadH, aldH, or adhA were constructed and transformed into CgB03, resulting in strains CgB04, CgB05, and CgB06, respectively. After 72 h of cultivation, all three overexpression strains exhibited accelerated 1,4-BDO degradation compared to the CgB03 strain (Fig. 3a). CgB04 exhibited an overall decrease in 1,4-BDO concentration, likely due to the oxidative reaction by FadH. It has been reported that FadH, a mycothiol-dependent formaldehyde dehydrogenase, also shows mycothiol-independent dehydrogenase activity with a preference for short-chain primary alcohols, suggesting potential activity against diols with a terminal hydroxyl group [40]. Notably, 1,4-BDO was barely detectable in CgB05, indicating that AldH plays a dominant role in 1,4-BDO degradation among the three enzymes. CgB06 also showed a lower 1,4-BDO titer compared to the control strain at both 24 and 48 h, indicating the catabolic effect of AdhA. These findings are consistent with a recent study, where the deletion of AldH and AdhA blocked the consumption of 1,3-propanediol and improved its production [38]. These enzymes may exhibit broad substrate specificity toward diols with various chain lengths and should be considered in future strain designs for diol production. Based on these findings, fadH, aldH, or adhA were confirmed to be responsible for the degradation of 1,4-BDO in C. glutamicum.

To construct a strain with reduced 1,4-BDO degradation, the three candidate genes were sequentially knocked out from the CgB03 genome, resulting in strain CgB07. After 48 h of flask cultivation, CgB07 exhibited a 1.28-fold increase in 1,4-BDO titer compared to the parental strain without a substantial decrease in 1,4-BDO concentration. Instead, the titer steadily increased, achieving 3.14 g/L after 72 h of cultivation (Fig. 3b). Despite the triple knockout of dehydrogenase genes, the growth rate and the final biomass of CgB03 and CgB07 exhibited negligible differences (Supplementary Fig. S3). CgB07 exhibited a specific growth rate of 0.26 h⁻¹ and a biomass yield of 0.16 g dcw/g glucose, which were comparable to those of CgB03 (0.25 h⁻¹ and 0.16 g dcw/g glucose, respectively), indicating that improved 1,4-BDO production was due to the reduced product degradation rather than altered cell growth or metabolic fitness. These results demonstrate that disruption of the native degradation pathway alleviated product loss, thereby enabling more efficient 1,4-BDO production.

Fed-batch fermentation for 1,4-BDO production

Confirming that the product degradation was resolved in the CgB07 strain, a 5-L fed-batch fermentation was performed for the scale-up of 1,4-BDO biosynthesis (Fig. 4). As the cells transitioned from exponential to stationary phase, 1,4-BDO production steadily increased. After conducting 85 h of microaerobic fermentation, the CgB07 strain achieved a 1,4-BDO titer of 13.4 g/L, corresponding to a yield of 0.08 g/g glucose and a productivity of 0.16 g/L/h. These values represent improvements over a previous study in E. coli employing a similar CoA-independent pathway, which reported a yield of 0.07 g/g glucose and a productivity of 0.03 g/L/h [9]. However, compared to other E. coli-based systems optimized for 1,4-BDO production, the current performance remains relatively modest, indicating the need for further metabolic engineering to enhance efficiency [1]. A gradual decrease in optical density was observed after 24 h of fermentation, potentially due to the toxicity of accumulated 1,4-BDO as well as increased fermentation volume resulting from feeding and pH control. It is expected that future efforts to mitigate the decrease in cell mass, such as the identification and overexpression of a 1,4-BDO exporter, could lead to further improvements in 1,4-BDO production. A recent study on 1,5-pentanediol production in C. glutamicum demonstrated that screening and engineering of CAR and aldehyde reductase significantly improved titers in fed-batch fermentation. Similarly, the identification and optimization of downstream enzymes suitable for 1,4-BDO biosynthesis may lead to increased production yields in further studies [43]. Surprisingly, acetate accumulation was not detectable throughout fed-batch fermentation. This result is likely due to a combined effect of engineered pathways: (1) the deletion of endogenous dehydrogenase genes involved in acetate production [44, 45], and (2) the inherent low acetate formation of the CoA-independent 1,4-BDO biosynthetic route observed in the previous study [9]. Together, this work demonstrates the potential of C. glutamicum as an effective platform strain for 1,4-BDO biosynthesis.

Conclusion

In this work, we have successfully engineered C. glutamicum, a potent amino acid-producing strain, to convert L-glutamate into 1,4-BDO. By integrating a CoA-independent biosynthetic pathway with the deletion of endogenous bypass genes, the engineered strain was able to produce 1,4-BDO from glucose. Optimization of enzymatic steps, particularly through the replacement of GAD and acceleration of the downstream reductive pathway, significantly improved product formation. Transcriptomic analysis further identified an intrinsic metabolism pathway for 1,4-BDO in C. glutamicum, and targeted deletion of three dehydrogenase genes (fadH, adhA, aldH) enhanced 1,4-BDO accumulation. The final engineered strain achieved 13.4 g/L of 1,4-BDO in fed-batch fermentation, with minimal byproduct formation and no product degradation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to establish a de novo 1,4-BDO biosynthesis pathway in C. glutamicum. Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of C. glutamicum for 1,4-BDO biosynthesis and highlight its broader potential in microbial chassis design for diols and related compounds.
