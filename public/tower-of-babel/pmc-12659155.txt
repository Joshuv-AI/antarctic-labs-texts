Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling unveils anthocyanin regulation in chemically induced flower color variation of Impatiens hybrida ‘Solarscape’
Authors: Yan Tian, Xiao-Yan Luo, Xuan-Yan Li, Jia-Qi He, Xin-Yi Chen, Lu-Qiu Zhao, Hai-Quan Huang, Mei-Juan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Impatiens plants are one of the world’s top three flower bed floriferous plants with important ornamental and horticultural values, vivid floral colors are more likely to attract pollinators, and one of the important ornamental traits of flowers. This study was conducted to determine whether the flower color of Impatiens can be altered after induction, with the aim of clarifying the molecular basis underlying its variation and to offer a vital reference for developing novel Impatiens varieties. Compared with diploids, the flower color of colchicine-induced Impatiens showed obvious changes, the flowers changed from pinkish purple to orange.ResultsIntegrated metabolomics and transcriptomics were comprehensively utilized to reveal the metabolic pathways of anthocyanin biosynthesis in non-mutant purple flowers and mutant orange. The floral hue discrepancies between Ih-WT and Ih-MU in Impatiens hybrida ‘Solarscape’ exhibit marked variations in luminance (L*), the red-green axis (a*), the yellow-blue axis (b*), and color intensity (Chroma c*). Specifically, the L* value of Ih-WT is significantly higher than that of Ih-MU, and the a*, b*, and Chroma c* values of Ih-WT are significantly greater than those of Ih-MU. Metabolomics identified 93 differential metabolites, most of which were Cyanidin and Pelargonidin-like metabolites, and the accumulation of Cyanidin and Pelargonidin was the principal factor underlying the petal color transition to orange in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. Analysis of the transcriptome identified 1888 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including key genes for anthocyanin synthesis (IhC4H, IhUFGT, IhDFR, and IhANS) and regulators (IhMYB308, IhNAC56, and IhMYC2) with high levels of expression of Ih-MU in orange flowers, indicating that they play a role in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis. These genes may be pivotal for the biosynthesis of orange anthocyanins. Co-expression analysis of differentially expressed genes and the relative levels of differentially expressed anthocyanins revealed that each anthocyanin is strongly associated with multiple genes, indicating that the anthocyanin accumulation process is governed by multiple genes. The expression levels of these genes exhibited a statistically significant positive correlation with the relative concentrations of Pelargonidin-3-O-sophoroside, Cyanidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside, and Cyanidin-3-O-sophoroside.ConclusionsThis study showed substantial alterations in color and anthocyanin synthesis in chemically mutagenized I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ flowers, and these findings could provide some insight into the relationship between Impatiens and other flowers could offer a theoretical foundation for the breeding improvement of Impatiens and other flowers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07647-8.Keywords: Impatiens hybrida ‘Solarscape’, Floral color variation, Metabolomics, Transcriptomics, Anthocyanin biosynthesis

Introduction

Impatiens is the largest genus in the Balsaminaceae and extensively acknowledged for its outstanding ornamental value in horticultural applications [1]. Presently, most studies on Balsaminaceae have focused on taxonomic and cytological studies [2, 3]. In the studies of gene regulation and molecular mechanisms, the research encompasses the spur development [4] and flower development [5], as well as the effects of different factors on flower color [6] and the mechanism of color leaf formation in colorful anemones [7], among others, with the studies on flower color focusing on the complex interactions in the biosynthetic and accumulation pathways of anthocyanins and their precursors. Prior investigations have demonstrated that structural genes such as C4H, FLS, PAL, and ANS significantly regulate flower color in Impatiens uliginosa, and the C4H gene positively regulates red flower color in I. uliginosa [8]. Among them, I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ is more widely used in gardens because of its long flowering period, large flowers and various colors (including rose, pink and purple, etc.). Chemical mutagenesis is a well-established breeding technique in which plant material is treated with chemical mutagens to induce genetic mutations that result in morphological variation. These variants are subsequently screened and bred to develop new varieties according to breeding objectives [9]. Related studies have shown that tetraploid I. walleriana produced larger flowers through chemical induction [10] and that chemically induced tetraploid I. walleriana had deepened flower color and increased total anthocyanin content [11].

Flowers are reproductive organs of angiosperms, and flower color is pivotal for attracting pollinators, thereby ensuring reproductive success in plants. It also serves to enhance the visual appeal of vegetation, consequently augmenting biodiversity and the aesthetic value of urban landscapes [12]– [13]. For ornamental plants, flower color stands as one of the most aesthetically and commercially significant traits, impacting both the visual appeal of the plant and its market value in a direct manner [14]. The flower color of plants is susceptible to regulation by a variety of factors, such as the type, content and percentage of pigments, cell pH, ion content and external conditions (temperature and light) and other factors, with the type, content and percentage of each pigment being the most influential [15]. Anthocyanins belong to flavonoids secondary metabolites, encompassing a variety of compounds such as Procyanidins, Delphinidins, Pelargonidins, Peonidins, Malvidins, and Petunidins [16], and these pigments are the primary causes [17].

Anthocyanin biosynthesis and metabolism are co-regulated by structural genes, transcription factors, and environmental factors [18]. This process has been thoroughly investigated in model species like Arabidopsis thaliana [19] and petunia [20]. The first step in anthocyanin biosynthesis begins with phenylalanine, which is converted to cinnamic acid by phenylalanine deaminase (PAL). Subsequently, hydroxylation of cinnamic acid catalyzed by cinnamic acid 4-hydroxylase (C4H) produces coumaric acid, which is further converted by 4-coumaroyl-coenzyme A ligase (4CL) to coumaroyl-coenzyme A. These reactions constitute the phenylalanine pathway, the products of which are precursors for the biosynthesis of compounds such as flavonoids and lignans [21]. The following pathway shifts to the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. Naringenin is formed under the catalytic action of chalcone synthase (CHS) and chalcone isomerase (CHI) and is subsequently transformed into dihydroflavonol through the action of flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H) and flavanone 3’-hydroxylase (F3’H). Afterwards, dihydroflavonols undergo catalysis mediated by three distinct enzymes, dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR), anthocyanin synthase (ANS) and flavonoid glycosyltransferase (UFGT), to form anthocyanins.The expression levels of these nine genes exhibit variation among different plant species, organs and developmental stages, particularly the downstream genes, especially DFR, ANS and UFGT, positively correlating accompanied by anthocyanin biosynthesis [22, 23]. Anthocyanin biosynthesis in plants is regulated by various transcription factors, and in Arabidopsis thaliana, MYB75, MYB90, and BHL modulate the expression of the DFR and ANS genes, which influences anthocyanin biosynthesis [24]. In Hydrangea macrophylla ‘forever summer’ HmWDR68 may specifically regulate the formation of blue sterile flowers in hydrangea by modulating the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related genes to enhance delphinidin-3-O-glucoside synthesis [25]. Two transcription factors, OvMYB and OvbHLH, are significantly up regulated at activated ANS in purple flowers of Orychophragmus violaceus, which regulates the production of anthocyanins in petals to convert white flowers to purple flowers [26]. In Rosa hybrid, MYB transcription factors may positively regulate the expression of key biosynthetic genes such as CHS, F3’H, and DFR to influence anthocyanin synthesis [27]. Two R2R3-MYB TFs, LvMYB5 and LvMYB1, were identified in lily to regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis in flowers [28].

Based on comprehensive research on the current flower market and the aesthetic attributes of Impatiens species, we found that the research on the flower color variation of Impatiens plants at home and abroad is relatively limited, and our group treated I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ by chemical mutagenesis in the early stage. I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ was treated by chemical mutagenesis, and I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ showed a unique flower color variation from purplish-red flowers to orange flowers after chemical mutagenesis, which makes it a significant scholarly importance within the investigation of flower color variation. To elucidate the potential causes of petal color variation, an integrated approach was used in this study, including pigment identification and quantitative analysis, joint analysis of metabolomics and transcriptomics, and verification of gene expression levels by qRT-PCR. This study is not only important for the directed breeding of plants but also provides information and a theoretical basis for improving the ornamental quality of Impatiens and advance comprehension of the mechanism underlying flower color formation in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’.

Materials and methods

Plant MatZC

Plant material consisted of purplish-red (wild-type, Ih-WT) and orange (mutant, Ih-MU) Impatiens hybrida ‘Solarscape’. The orange mutant was generated by treating imbibed seeds of the purplish-red line with 60 mg L⁻¹ trifluralin for 12 h, as described previously. Plants were cultivated in the greenhouse of the Southwest Forestry University Arboretum. At full anthesis, petals were collected from both color variants (Fig. 1) in three independent biological replicates, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses.

Measurement of chromatic values

Fresh petals were collected and measured three times employing the CIE standard D65 illuminant and the CIE 10° standard observer function. Following the completion of measurements, the CIELAB color model was employed to utilize the chromatic divergence of the petal hue [29]. Within this framework, lightness is represented by L* (spanning from 0 to 100), chroma is indicated by c* [a*2+b*2) 1/2], red and green are quantified by a* (with positive and negative values, respectively), and yellow and blue are denoted by b* (with positive and negative values, respectively).

Metabolome analysis

The Metabolomics sequencing was performed by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., based in Wuhan, China. Utilizing liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), metabolites were accurately separated, detected, and quantified. To assess data reproducibility and stability, total ion current (TIC) and relative standard deviation (RSD) plots were constructed. Differential metabolites underwent clustering analysis, and their associated pathways were annotated via the KEGG database. This comprehensive approach facilitated a thorough exploration of the metabolomics data, revealing the key biochemical and signaling pathways involved. Differential metabolites were identified using the criteria of |log2 fold change| >1 and P-value ≤ 0.05.

RNA Extraction

For RNA sample preparation, 1 µg of RNA was utilized per sample. The concentration and integrity of the RNA were assessed using the RNA detection kit from the RNABioteke50 assay kit (Bioteke Corporation, Beijing, China).

RNA-seq sequencing and analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on the petals of Ih-MU and Ih-WT during their full bloom, producing paired-end reads of 125 bp and 150 bp in length. This process was carried out by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., based in Wuhan, China. After the initial sequencing data were obtained, the fastp software (version 0.23.4) was utilized to eliminate adapter sequences and filter out low-quality reads (with Q values ≤ 20) as well as those containing more than 10% N bases, yielding high-quality reads for subsequent analysis[59]. Gene expression levels were assessed using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was based on raw gene read counts, with P values and fold changes computed using the edgeR software. Genes with P values ≤ 0.05 and absolute log2 fold changes exceeding 1 were classified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [60]. Differentially expressed transcription factors were identified using the KEGG database and PlantTFDB.

Expression and analysis of IhANS, IhC4H, IhDFR, IhUFGT, IhMYB308, IhMYC2 and IhNAC56. genes

Six structural genes related to anthocyanins and seven transcription factor genes were identified and validated via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Total RNA was extracted using the UPure Plant RNA Plus Kit from TIANGEN (Beijing, China). The reverse transcription process utilized the Evo M-MLV Mix Kit by AIKERUI (Changsha, China). Primers were designed with Primer 3 (https://primer3.ut.ee/) and synthesized by Bioneer Corporation (Shanghai, China). The qRT-PCR reaction followed the protocol of the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AIKERUI, Changsha, China), which included initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, and 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. Relative gene expression was determined using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. The qRT-PCR data were analyzed with SPSS software through independent samples t-tests to compare gene expression levels between Ih-WT and Ih-MU and to assess significance.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical evaluations were performed utilizing Excel 2019, SPSS 26.0, and TBtools 1.0 software. A range of analytical techniques, such as analysis of variance (ANOVA), Duncan’s multiple range test, Pearson correlation coefficient, and principal component analysis (PCA), were applied to determine statistical significance, with a threshold of P < 0.05 for significance.

Metabolomics analysis

Based on the pigment content measurements, we hypothesized that it may be the anthocyanin content that is critical for the variation in flower color of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ and that the difference in anthocyanins between Ih-MU and Ih-WT was very significant during the full bloom period, therefore, the present study used anthocyanin-targeted metabolomics approach to analyze the I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ Ih-WT and Ih-MU petal samples.

A total of 93 compounds were identified in this study, which can be categorized into eight groups including 24 Cyanidin, 13 Delphinidin, 14 Malvidin, 2 Flavonoids, 19 Pelargonidin, 7 Peonidin, 8 Petunidin, and 6 Procyanidin. Based on the differential metabolite results, the compounds on the KEGG pathway were found to be enriched on the anthocyanin metabolic pathway (Fig. 3). Pelargonidin-3-O-sophoroside was not detected in Ih-WT, and the expression of other metabolites was low, the highest being Malvidin-3-O-(6’’-O-feruloyl)xyloside at 165.16 µg·g-1, which accounted for 41.6% of the total anthocyanin content, but in Ih-MU Pelargonidin-3-O-sophoroside was extremely high at 491.21 µg·g-1, accounting for 46.75% of the total anthocyanin content, followed by (Cyanidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside at 101.68 µg·g-1, accounting for only The content was 101.68 µg·g-1, which was only 9.68% of the total anthocyanin content. In addition to this, Pelargonidin-3-O-5-O-(6-O-coumaroyl)-diglucoside was not detected in Ih-MU and was present in Ih-WT at 15.005 µg·g-1, which only accounted for 3.78% of the total anthocyanin content. Finally, in this study, the data of 93 metabolite contents were used at UV (unit variance scaling), and the heat map was plotted by R software Complex Heatmap package, through the figure this study found that the eight classes of differential metabolites of Ih-WT and Ih-MU were clearly separated (Fig. 4), and it can be seen that the Cyanidin class of metabolites was the most by Class and followed by Pelargonidin, with naringenin being the least. In general, it appeared that Cyanidin and Pelargonidin were higher in Ih-MU and lower in Ih-WT, in contrast to Delphinidin, Malvidin and Petunidin which were higher in Ih-WT and lower in Ih-MU.

Filtering and analyzing transcriptome assembly data

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying anthocyanin biosynthesis in the flowers of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’, its petals were subjected to transcriptome sequencing, and in general, the sequencing data quality for I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ was sufficiently high to be utilized. Following the filtering of raw data, assessment of sequencing error rates, and evaluation of GC content distribution, a total of 87,719,666 clean reads were obtained from the initial 88,750,518 raw reads (Tab S1). The percentage of Q20 bases in the petal samples of Ih-WT and Ih-MU ranged from 98.90% to 99.09%, the percentage of Q30 bases ranged from 96.57% to 97.2% so far, and the GC content ranged from 47.44% to 47.72%, with an error rate less than or equal to 0.02% in each case. The transcriptome splicing and assembly generated 59,339 transcripts, characterized by an average length of 1,251 bp and an N50 length of 1,730 bp. A total of 33,271 unigenes were identified, with an average length of 1,459 bp. The splicing analysis further revealed an N50 length of 1,817 bp for these unigenes (Tab S2).

Functional annotation analysis of genes

In this study, all clean reads were functionally annotated in databases such as KEGG, NR, Swiss-Prot, KOG, GO, Trembl, Pfam, etc. using the BLASTX program (Tab S3) and investigating the function of unigenes (Fig S1). Based on the above databases, a total of 33,271 unigenes were functionally annotated. According to the NR database annotation results, the genetic sequences of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ exhibited the maximum similarity (88.87%) with those of Impatiens glandulifera (Fig S2). A total of 27,189 genes were categorized according to the GO database annotations, which were categorized into three primary classes: BP (Biological Process), CC (Cellular Component), and MF (Molecular Function), which are important for understanding the function of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. The KOG database annotation revealed that the categories with the largest number of genes were R (General function prediction only), T (Signal transduction mechanisms), and O (Posttranslational modification, protein turnover, chaperones’ functions), containing 3727, 2157, and 2085 genes, respectively. The least were N (Cell motility) and W (Extracellular structure) with only 15 and 56 genes (Fig. 7B). The evidence indicates a significant correlation between these functional genes and the variation in petal coloration observed in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. Based on the KEGG database annotation, among the 135 metabolic pathways identified, phenylpropane biosynthesis (KO00940), flavonoid biosynthesis (KO00941), anthocyanin biosynthesis (KO00942), and isoflavonoid biosynthesis (KO00943) were found to be closely associated with anthocyanin production and were significantly abundant in petal tissues.

Combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis

This study integrated the analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics data to elucidate the pigment biosynthesis pathways in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ flowers. The results showed that I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ was found to contain a significant quantity of flavonoids and anthocyanins. Among them, the content of flavonoids (catalyzed by CHS, C4H, and F3’H), and anthocyanins (catalyzed by ANS and DFR) were higher in Ih-MU than in Ih-WT. To explain this finding, the transcriptomes of Ih-WT and Ih-MU were compared. Genes involved in flavonoids, especially the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, were analyzed to excavate the key genes responsible for the change of purplish-red to orange color in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. A total of 11 candidate genes related to the biosynthesis pathway were identified, with 10 up-regulated and 1 down-regulated unigenes, including CHS, C4H, F3’5’H, DFR, ANS, UFGT and other genes were significantly associated with 20 TFs, which included MYB, NAC, bHLH, WRKY, ERF families. TFs such as MYB308, NAC56, MYC2, ERF113, WRKY44, and WRKY65 were screened. These DEGs were largely associated with I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ color variation. The petal color is primarily influenced by the types and concentrations of phytochromes. In this study, seven key genes associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis—CHS, C4H, F3’H, F3’5’H, ANS, UFGT, and DFR—exhibited significant upregulation, particularly in the expression levels of C4H, ANS, DFR, and UFGT. Beyond the structural genes, transcription factors that are directly or indirectly involved in anthocyanin metabolism were also identified among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on gene annotation results. The expressions of transcription factors MYB308, NAC56, MYC2, ERF113, WRKY44, and WRKY65 were all up-regulated, and the expression of MYB308, NAC56, and MYC2 was higher, which was consistent with the high anthocyanin content in the Ih-MU mutant. Taken together, CHS, C4H, F3’H, F3’5’H, ANS, UFGT, and DFR may coordinate the up-regulation of anthocyanins in Ih-MU together with MYB308, NAC56, and MYC2. The results suggest that MYB308, NAC56, MYC2 may play an important role in petal color change, petal color, enhancement of C4H, ANS, DFR, UFGT, MYB308, NAC56, MYC2 may contribute to I. hybrida ‘Solarscape ' flower color change from purplish-red to orange.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

This study identified the anthocyanin-like biosynthetic pathway as a core regulatory site for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs), based on the results of comprehensive transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses and network analysis. To further validate the reliability of the RNA-seq data, this study systematically mapped the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway and screened four key candidate genes (IhC4H, IhDFR, IhANS, IhUFGT) with very significant differential expressions. Meanwhile, a group of key transcription factors (IhMYB308, IhNAC56, IhMYC2) that may synergistically regulate petal color changes were identified in this study. These seven genes (IhC4H, IhDFR, IhANS, and IhUFGT with IhMYB308, IhNAC56, and IhMYC2) were heat mapped against Ih-WT and Ih-MU, and their correlations were analyzed and visualized to construct a network interactions analysis map (Fig. 12A and B).

The qRT-PCR assays revealed that the transcript abundance of all seven candidate genes was significantly higher in Ih-MU than in Ih-WT (Fig. 13), indicating their involvement in anthocyanin regulation. Specifically, IhC4H, IhDFR, IhANS, IhUFGT, IhMYB308, IhNAC56 and IhMYC2 were up-regulated more than two-fold in Ih-MU, corroborating the reliability of the RNA-seq results and supporting the conclusion that these genes contribute substantially to petal coloration in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’.

Discussion

Influence of petal coloration in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ based on the content and percentage of pigments in petals

In the research on ornamental flowering plants, the CIELAB color model can be utilized to assess the lightness, hue, chroma, and color indices of petals that exhibit diverse floral colors. In this context, we observed distinct differences in petal color variation between two different flower colors of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. In this study, the L* value of Ih-MU was lower than that of Ih-WT, and Ih-WT exhibited significant increases in a*, b*, and c* values compared to Ih-MU. The color differences were consistent with the observed floral phenotypes of the plants. We inferred that the chromaticity values and color changes associated with flower color variation following chemical mutagenesis are in accordance with the phenotypic evidence of the plants. Flower color is a very important ornamental characteristic of plants, depending mainly on the content of a certain type of pigment or on the different proportions of multiple pigments. The main types of pigments are flavonoids, anthocyanins and carotenoids [15]. Anthocyanins, as important secondary metabolites, are widely found in flowers, fruits, and leaves and are involved in many physiological and biochemical reactions in plants [33]. This is study consistent with the finding that petals of Gloriosa Superba ‘Rothschildiana’ undergo a color shift from yellow to red during development [34]. In Zingiber mioga, it was found that anthocyanins may act synergistically with proanthocyanidins and other flavonoids, which together influence the color formation of the flower buds during the developmental and maturation stages [35]. The results showed that both Ih-MU and Ih-WT showed significant trends of pigmentation changes at the bloom stage than at the bud stage, suggesting that flower color changes at the bloom stage are regulated by pigmentation. Based on the results, we inferred that the pigment content and species of flower color at each stage were related to the developmental stage of the plant.

Anthocyanins confer a wide range of colors in flowers, from orange to red and from purple to blue [36]. In a study of the pigment content percentage of purplish-red and orange flowers of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ both were found to be high in total anthocyanins and the difference in total anthocyanins was significantly high, which is the same as the pink and orange flowers of Ranunculus asiaticus with the same percentage of pigment content [37]. Related studies have reported that accumulation of different pigments leads to differences in flower color, such as accumulation of Cyanidin, Pelargonidin, and Delphinidin which usually change pink to reddish purple [38], orange to red [39], and purple to blue [40]. In our study, the anthocyanin content of Ih-MU at full bloom was significantly higher than that of Ih-WT, and for anthocyanins the up-regulation of cornflower and geraniolin-like metabolites and the down-regulation of mallow pigment-like metabolites may be the key metabolic basis for the change in the petal color from purplish-red to orange in Ih-WT. I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ has a flower color variation from purplish-red to orange, which we hypothesize is caused by a higher concentration of anthocyanins in the petals. In this study, we will further investigate the anthocyanin-targeted metabolomic and transcriptomic data of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ petals in full bloom and delve deeper into the interactions between anthocyanins and petal color variation.

Interpretation of the mechanism of flower color variation in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ based on targeted metabolomics and transcriptomics data

Flower color variation is not only related to pigment biosynthesis and accumulation, but also to their catabolism and gene expression regulation [41, 42], along with synergistic pigment and flavonoid-metal complexation [43]. In our study, I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ is regulated by different modes of metabolite aggregation for floral color variation, and both samples exhibited anthocyanin biosynthesis metabolism pathway metabolites that were distinctly separated and aggregated into two major groups, suggesting that between purplish-red and orange anthocyanins suggesting the existence of opposite accumulation patterns between purplish-red and orange anthocyanins. In this study, using a metabolomics approach targeting anthocyanins, the analysis of 64 metabolites that were differentially accumulated between the purplish-red flowers of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ and the orange flowers of the mutant showed that these differential metabolites were mainly concentrated in the three classes of anthocyanin derivatives, cornflower, geraniol and mallow pigments, with cornflower pigments used as the major anthocyanins in this stage and geraniol and mallow pigments as the secondary anthocyanins. The key to the composition of red color in roses is the ratio between cornflower and geraniol [44], then the change in petal color from purplish-red to orange in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ may also be related to the ratio of cornflower to geraniol. Further screening of the differential metabolites revealed that geraniol-3-O-sophoroside, cornflowerin-3-(6-O-p-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside, and cornflowerin-3-O-sophoroside were very significantly different among the differential pigments, and they were likely pigments to regulate petal coloration. Cyanidin-3-O-sophoroside is the main pigment that regulates the purple coloration of asparagus pericarp [45], and it was not detected in Ih-WT. And it is also the accumulation of Cyanidin-3-O-sophoroside in the leaves of Cymbidium ensifolium that shows purple color [46]. We reasoned that the up-regulation of Cyanidin and Pelargonidin-like metabolites and the down-regulation of mallow pigment-like metabolites, and that the two anthocyanins Pelargonidin-3-O-sophoroside and Cyanidin-3-O-sophoroside are likely pigments regulating petal coloration, may be responsible for the change in petal coloration in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’, petal color change from purplish-red to orange.

Plant transcriptomics analysis is frequently employed to pinpoint key genes that exhibit differential expression across various developmental stages, as it offers insights into the quantitative variations in gene expression [47]. In the case of Viola Cornuta L., the transition of flower color from white to purple is linked to the differential expression of genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis [48]. Similarly, in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’, changes in flower color have been closely associated with the function of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which are thought to be vital for metabolic processes. Structural genes that have been identified as contributing to flower pigmentation in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ include CHS, F3H, F3’H, DFR, and ANS [49]. By combining Dems and DEGs, our study found that the genes regulating flower color changes in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ are similar to those in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway in chrysanthemum. It has been shown that the C4H gene has the highest expression in red and pink flowers, suggesting that the C4H gene regulates red flower color in I. uliginosa’ [8]. This is like the results of our study in which the C4H gene was significantly up-regulated in Ih-MU, which may be involved in the regulation of making the petals take on a purplish-red color. UFGT, a key enzyme in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, was expressed at significantly higher levels in the purple flowers of Syringa oblata than in the white flowers, suggesting that UFGT may play an important role in Syringa oblata [50]. Moreover, in our study, UFGT was identified as a new potential structural gene that may regulate anthocyanin synthesis in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’, and its expression level may be related to the variation of flower color in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. DFR catalyzes the biosynthesis of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins by reducing dihydroflavanols to anthocyanidin glycosides, which are important steps in the biosynthesis of anthocyanidins and proanthocyanidins [51], and competition with FLS can alter metabolic fluxes and change flower color [52, 53]. DFR is significantly expressed in both materials, and our study hypothesized that DFR is significantly expressed in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ whether there is a similar mechanism to regulate flower color in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. In our results ANS as an enzyme directly involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis was expressed at significantly higher levels in Ih-MU. According to Hao et al. [54], ANS shifted the petal color from red to beige and zinc yellow in red kale-type oilseed rape, and we speculate that this may be due to the high expression of ANS and the significant up-regulation of other genes (e.g., DFR, UFGT), which increase anthocyanin accumulation by enhancing the efficiency of precursor transformation or reducing catabolism. This suggests that the expression levels of four key genes, ANS, C4H, UFGT, and DFR, were significantly up-regulated when the color of the petals changed from purplish-red to orange, which is consistent with the elevated anthocyanin content in the Ih-MU mutant compared with that in Ih-WT.

Explanation of the mechanism of flower color variation in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ based on the relationship between transcription factors and structural genes

As Li et al. [55] have pointed out, transcription factors can influence flower color by modulating the expression of structural genes in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway. In tobacco, the TTG2 and ARF8 transcription factors work in concert to regulate flower color intensity by controlling the expression of ANS and DFR, both of which are integral to anthocyanin production and thereby positively impact flower coloration. Our findings demonstrated that the expression levels of MYB308, NAC56, and MYC2 were significantly upregulated in Ih-MU. These transcription factors may play a role in altering floral color from purplish red to orange by regulating the expression of structural genes like ANS and DFR. According to Wu et al. [56], the regulation of UFGT by MYB308 in Nelumbo nucifera may also play a role in upstream regulation, potentially balancing anthocyanin levels. In sweet potato, the NAC56 gene may be crucial for regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis through the formation of regulatory complexes that modulate or enhance anthocyanin synthesis [57]. Barley grains, which turn blue due to anthocyanin accumulation in the dextran layer leading to pigmentation, were turned blue by restoring the function of MYC2 in non-blue barley grains to produce anthocyanin accumulation, confirming the key role of MYC2 transcription factors in the blue trait [58]. The results of the analysis suggest that the interactions between four key candidate genes (C4H, DFR, ANS, UFGT) and a set of key transcription factors (NAC56, MYC2, MYB308) that may synergistically regulate the petal color change play a crucial role in regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’.

Conclusion

In this study, we combined metabolomics and transcriptomics data to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying flower color formation in I.hybrida ‘Solarscape’. Metabolomics analysis revealed that the major differentially expressed metabolites were concentrated in three classes of anthocyanin derivatives: cyanidin, pelargonidin, and malvidin. In Ih-MU, Malvidin-type metabolites were significantly downregulated, while Cyanidin and Pelargonidin-type metabolites were significantly upregulated. Among these, three anthocyanin derivatives (Pelargonidin-3-O-sophoroside, Cyanidin-3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside, and Cyanidin-3-O-sophoroside) were significantly upregulated, while Pelargonidin-3-O-sophoroside was completely absent in Ih-MT. These pigment changes may be the cause of anthocyanin production variation. Therefore, this study further performed transcriptomic sequencing on I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’ and the orange mutant I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’, identified transcription factors regulating anthocyanin synthesis structural genes (such as IhMYB308, IhNAC56, and IhMYC2), and identified candidate genes playing a key role in the formation of anthocyanin color variation in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’, further exploring the regulatory mechanisms related to the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway in I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. Since our study only measured anthocyanin-targeted metabolomics, we did not investigate the effects of other metabolites (e.g., carotenoids) on flower color, nor did we study other phenotypic changes and physiological functional changes in chemically mutated plants of I. hybrida ‘Solarscape’. In the future, we will further investigate the effects of other metabolites on petal coloration, validate gene functions using transgenic technology, and study their upstream-related transcription factors to further refine our understanding of the mechanisms underlying flower color variation in I. hybrida ‘Solarscap’.
