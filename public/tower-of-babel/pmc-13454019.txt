Preoperative hematological parameters do not predict lesion severity or recurrence in cervical intraepithelial neoplasia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Neçirvan Çağdaş Çaltek, Mahmut Yassa, Ayşe Bacaksız, Hale Özer Çaltek, Deniz Aras, Yiğit Aksoy, İlkbal Temel Yüksel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the predictive value of preoperative hematological parameters for determining lesion grade and recurrence risk in patients diagnosed with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) by biopsy.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 700 women who underwent a loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) following colposcopy at a tertiary referral center. Preoperative complete blood counts obtained within one week before surgery were used to calculate the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI). These indices were compared according to LEEP histopathology and evaluated for recurrence over a two-year follow-up period.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in hematologic parameters (NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, SIRI) between low- and high-grade lesions (all p > 0.05). Similarly, among patients with biopsy-proven CIN2, preoperative indices did not differ across final LEEP results. During a 24-month follow-up, histologic recurrence occurred in 6.7% of patients. Recurrence was associated with HPV persistence (p < 0.001), whereas the univariable association with lower MLR values (p = 0.049) did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. In multivariable analysis, only persistent HPV infection remained an independent predictor of recurrence (OR = 2.98, p = 0.014). ROC analysis showed weak and clinically limited discriminative power for MLR (AUC = 0.61).ConclusionPreoperative hematologic parameters showed no clinically meaningful predictive value for lesion severity or recurrence and should not be used for clinical risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05318-0.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a preventable malignancy that progresses slowly over time, mainly due to the well-established role of its etiologic agent [1]. Human papillomavirus (HPV) is recognized as the leading cause of cervical cancer. HPV infection can lead to the development of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), a precancerous lesion that may progress to cancer if left untreated. Women who undergo proper surveillance and treatment have a markedly reduced risk of developing invasive cancer; however, recurrent cervical dysplasia substantially increases this risk [1, 2]. Histopathologically, CIN is classified into three grades: CIN1 (mild dysplasia), CIN2 (moderate dysplasia), and CIN3 (severe dysplasia or carcinoma in situ) [3]. In contrast, for CIN2 and CIN3 lesions, excisional treatment is recommended to remove the lesion [4]. The loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) operates as both a diagnostic and therapeutic intervention for the management of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia by excising abnormal cervical tissue [4, 5]. Treatment failure—defined as the recurrence of CIN2 or higher-grade lesions (CIN2+) has been reported in 4% to 18% of cases [6, 7], with most recurrences occurring within the first two years after the initial procedure [6–8]. Persistent high-risk HPV infection is a major determinant of recurrence risk [9, 10]; therefore, HPV testing plays a key role in posttreatment follow-up [11]. In addition to viral persistence, factors such as positive surgical margins, age, and immune suppression have also been implicated in CIN recurrence [12].

Several studies have shown that peripheral blood cell counts—particularly neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet levels reflect systemic immune responses [13]. Systemic inflammation has been linked to the initiation and progression of various malignancies [14, 15]. Inflammatory indices derived from these parameters, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII; platelet × neutrophil/lymphocyte), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI; neutrophil × monocyte/lymphocyte), have been recognized as promising biomarkers among different cancer types. Elevated levels of these markers have been correlated with poor prognosis in several studies [15, 16]. In patients diagnosed with CIN, NLR and MLR have been suggested as useful indicators for predicting lesion grade and prognosis prior to LEEP [5, 17].

Patients with HPV positivity or abnormal cytology findings undergo colposcopy. Based on the colposcopy and biopsy results, a decision is made to perform LEEP for appropriate cases [11]. Especially in resource-constrained countries, this situation creates difficulties in accessing treatment and disrupts treatment continuity.

In recent years, several studies have investigated the use of hematologic parameters for diagnostic and prognostic purposes in patients with CIN. However, the existing literature is heterogeneous, with many studies based on relatively small retrospective cohorts and variable definitions of recurrence, leading to inconsistent conclusions regarding the clinical value of inflammatory hematologic markers. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the predictive value of preoperative hematologic parameters for identifying high-risk disease in patients scheduled for excisional treatment, using a larger cohort and a clinically meaningful definition of recurrence, and to assess their ability to identify patients at risk of recurrence during follow-up.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at a university hospital in Türkiye accredited by the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) between May 2020 and December 2024. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Reporting Recommendations for Tumor Marker Prognostic Studies (REMARK) guidelines [18, 19].

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital (Approval Number: E-96317027-514.10-267056456).

Study population

The diagnosis and treatment of all patients were supervised by experienced gynecologic oncologists. The study included patients who underwent colposcopic evaluation at our institution and were subsequently indicated for LEEP based on histopathological findings.

Patients were excluded if they had a previous history of malignancy, known immunodeficiency, hematologic disorders, pregnancy, major organ failure, active infection, or incomplete clinical data.

Because recurrence of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia most commonly occurs within 2 years after treatment [6–8], the follow-up period for recurrence assessment was set at 24 months post-LEEP. Recurrence was defined as histologically confirmed CIN2 or higher (CIN2+) detected during follow-up through cytologic examination, colposcopic biopsy, or clinical evaluation.

Patients who failed to attend regular follow-up visits, those with positive surgical margins or high-risk endocervical curettage findings who did not undergo repeat conization and therefore did not achieve complete excision, were excluded from recurrence analyses. Data collection and variables

At our institution, all LEEP procedures are performed under anesthesia. Therefore, preoperative hematologic values were obtained from complete blood counts collected within 1 week of surgery. Patients with active infections are not deemed eligible for anesthesia; thus, hematologic measurements were assumed to reflect infection-free and clinically stable conditions. Demographic characteristics, pathology reports, and laboratory data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. All conization specimens obtained after LEEP were reviewed by experienced pathologists, and histopathological diagnoses were established according to World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria [20]. Patients were subsequently grouped based on final pathology results.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Multiple ROC curve analyses were additionally performed in R Statistical Software (v4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025) using the pROC and ggplot2 packages. Continuous variables were tested for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test and by examining skewness and kurtosis. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] according to distribution, and categorical variables as numbers (percentages). Between-group comparisons used the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise Mann–Whitney comparisons was applied. Trends across CIN grades were examined using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test. Because CIN grade is an ordinal diagnostic variable, correlational analyses were not applied. Instead, monotonic changes across CIN1, CIN2, and CIN3 were evaluated using the Jonckheere–Terpstra trend test, which is the appropriate method for ordered categories.

Recurrence analyses included only patients with complete 24-month follow-up data; therefore, 432 of the 700 eligible patients were included in the recurrence cohort (n = 432). Smoking status was missing for 183 cases (available n = 249) and was therefore excluded from multivariable analyses to avoid excessive case-wise deletion; missingness was assumed to be random and not imputed. Baseline LEEP histopathology was excluded from the multivariable models due to extremely limited variability in the follow-up cohort (> 90% ≥CIN2), which caused quasi-complete separation and model non-convergence.

Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analyses and/or clinical relevance were considered for multivariable modeling. The monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) and HPV persistence were retained as primary covariates. After confirming the absence of multicollinearity (VIF < 5), multivariable logistic regression was carried out using a hierarchical block-entry (forced-entry) strategy. Model 1 included HPV persistence and MLR; Model 2a additionally included age and parity; Model 2b added the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR); and Model 2c further incorporated the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC; 95% CI).

The ROC direction was specified as “smaller test result indicates positive test” to reflect the inverse relationship between MLR and recurrence; this affected classification only, not regression coefficients. The optimal MLR cut-off (0.215) was derived by the Youden index. To reduce optimism bias, internal bootstrap validation (B = 1000) was performed; however, given the limited number of recurrence events, the results should be interpreted with caution. Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated net clinical benefit for MLR alone and the combined model (MLR + HPV persistence) using R (v4.3.x).

Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the interval between the initial LEEP and the earliest histologically confirmed recurrence (≥ CIN2) or the last negative visit. Kaplan–Meier curves were compared by the log-rank test; patients without recurrence at 24 months were right-censored. Owing to the small number of events, no multivariable Cox analysis was performed.

All tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. An a priori power calculation (CIN2 + = 409 vs. ≤ CIN1 = 291) indicated ≈ 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect a small effect (AUC ≈ 0.56). Post hoc power for the recurrence cohort (n = 432) was ≈ 77%, confirming adequate power to detect small-to-moderate associations.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 39.7 ± 9.9 years, with a median parity of 2. Data on smoking status were available for 303 patients, and among these, 62.4% were current smokers. Cytologic findings showed HSIL in 18.7% and LSIL in 20.3% of cases, whereas 18.2% had negative cytology results. High-risk HPV infection was detected in 86.9% of the cohort, predominantly HPV-16 (33.4%), followed by non-16/18 types (39.2%) and multiple high-risk infections (23.4%). On cervical biopsy, CIN2 and CIN3 were identified in 37.9% and 40.4% of patients, respectively. Corresponding LEEP specimens confirmed CIN2 in 20.9% and CIN3 in 35.6% of cases.

Hematologic and inflammatory parameters were compared between patients with CIN1 or below and those with CIN2 or above (Table 2). No statistically significant differences were observed in any of the evaluated indices, including PLT, MPV, PCT, NEU, LYM, MON, NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, and SIRI (all p > 0.05). Median and mean values were broadly similar across groups, with overlapping interquartile ranges for all parameters. Although slight numerical increases were noted in several indices among CIN2 + lesions, these differences were not statistically or clinically meaningful. Overall, systemic hematologic and inflammatory markers did not differ significantly by lesion grade, implying limited discriminatory capacity to distinguish low- and high-grade lesions.

Hematological parameters of patients with colposcopic biopsy-confirmed CIN2 were compared according to the LEEP histopathological results (Normal, CIN1, CIN2, CIN3; Table 3). When patients with biopsy-proven CIN2 were stratified by final LEEP histopathology, significant differences were observed in neutrophil count (p = 0.045), NLR (p = 0.020), and SII (p = 0.011), whereas all other parameters showed comparable distributions among groups (all p > 0.05). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise tests indicated that these differences were limited to the lower-grade categories, specifically between the Normal and CIN1 groups (NEU adj p = 0.042; NLR adj p = 0.011; SII adj p = 0.007). No significant variation was observed among higher-grade lesions (CIN2 and CIN3). Jonckheere–Terpstra testing confirmed the absence of any significant monotonic or progressive trend across lesion grades for the evaluated hematologic and inflammatory indices (all p > 0.05).

Follow-up analyses were conducted during a planned 24-month surveillance period in patients with available longitudinal data (n = 432). HPV persistence was observed in 131 patients (30.6%), whereas 297 (69.4%) remained HPV negative throughout follow-up. The distribution of HPV genotypes at follow-up showed a predominance of multiple high-risk (HR) infections (74.3%), followed by non-16/18 HR types (16.7%) and HPV16 (9%). Multiple infections were defined as concurrent detection of ≥ 2 h genotypes (e.g., 16 + 18, 16 + other, or 18 + other). Histologic LEEP recurrence occurred in 29 patients (6.7%). For patients with recurrence, the time from the index LEEP procedure to histologic confirmation (progression time) was recorded in months, with a median of 10.6. Detailed follow-up outcomes are summarized in Table 4.

Subsequently, hematologic indices were examined for their association with recurrence within the same follow-up cohort. Among patients who completed postoperative follow-up (n = 432; 29 [6.7%] with recurrence), hematologic and inflammatory indices were compared between those with and without LEEP recurrence (Table 5). Median (IQR) values were broadly similar across groups. Of the evaluated markers, only the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) showed a borderline difference, being slightly lower in the recurrence group (p = 0.049). Although MLR showed a borderline association with recurrence (p = 0.049), this finding did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment and was further explored in subsequent regression models (Table 6; Fig. 2). No other indices—including NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, PLT, MPV, PCT, neutrophil, lymphocyte, or monocyte counts—were associated with recurrence (all p > 0.05).

In univariable analyses, HPV persistence and MLR were both associated with recurrence (Table 5). When these parameters were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model (Model 1), persistent HPV infection remained an independent predictor of recurrence (adjusted OR = 2.98, 95% CI 1.24–7.14, p = 0.014), whereas continuous MLR demonstrated a non-significant inverse association when scaled per 0.10 increase (adjusted OR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.40–1.25, p = 0.246). Thus, although lower preoperative MLR showed a crude association with recurrence, this finding was not maintained after multivariable adjustment. These results are summarized in Table 5, which presents both univariable and adjusted comparisons of clinical and hematologic parameters by LEEP recurrence status. The model showed acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.12) and modest discrimination (AUC = 0.71, 95% CI 0.602–0.809). Bootstrap internal validation (B = 1000) estimated a mean optimism of 0.03, yielding an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.67, although the number of events was limited and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Expanding the model sequentially with age and parity (Model 2a), PLR (Model 2b), and SIRI (Model 2c) did not materially improve fit or discrimination (AUC range: 0.69–0.71; Table 6; Fig. 2). Across all models, HPV persistence consistently remained the sole independent predictor, while hematologic indices showed only borderline or non-significant effects. These models should be interpreted as exploratory, given the limited number of recurrence events.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis for MLR alone yielded limited discriminative ability (AUC = 0.61, 95% CI 0.50–0.71, p = 0.040), with a data-derived cut-off of 0.215 (Youden index). The classification direction was set to “smaller test result indicates positive test,” affecting only ROC classification. At this threshold, sensitivity and specificity were 55% and 63%, respectively. Because this cut-off was internally derived, it should be interpreted as exploratory and not suitable for clinical decision-making without external validation.

Complementary decision curve analysis demonstrated that MLR alone yielded no net clinical benefit compared with treat-all or treat-none strategies across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. The combined model (HPV persistence + MLR) provided a small but consistent net benefit across thresholds of approximately 10–20% (Supplementary Figure S4).

When stratified by the optimal MLR cut-off of 0.215, patients in the low-MLR group had a significantly higher recurrence rate than those with high MLR values (10.1% [95% CI 6.2–15.8] vs. 3.8% [95% CI 1.8–7.1]; p = 0.011), while CIN2 + recurrence rates were comparable between groups (23.5% vs. 24.0%; p = 1.00). Overall, these findings suggest a crude association between lower preoperative MLR and recurrence, which should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Table S3).

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were then performed to evaluate the impact of HPV persistence and preoperative hematologic indices on recurrence-free survival (RFS). Patients with persistent HPV infection (n = 131, 30.6%) had significantly shorter RFS than those who achieved viral clearance (log-rank χ² = 13.08, p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S2). Similarly, patients with low MLR (< 0.215) had shorter RFS than those with high MLR (log-rank χ² = 6.78, p = 0.009; Supplementary Figure S3). At 24 months, estimated RFS rates were 87.0% vs. 96.3% for persistent vs. cleared HPV and 89.9% vs. 96.2% for low- vs. high-MLR groups, indicating that HPV persistence was associated with reduced RFS after LEEP, while the association with MLR should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of events.

Exploratory analyses of early (< 12 months) recurrence did not add discriminative value beyond the main models; full results are provided in Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

Cervical cancer remains the fourth most common malignancy among women worldwide [21]. The majority of cases and cancer-related deaths are reported from low- and middle-income countries where access to healthcare and screening programs remains limited [22]. While widespread screening and HPV vaccination programs in developed countries have reduced incidence rates, late diagnosis due to inadequate screening is still a major challenge in low-resource regions [23]. As a result, identifying low-cost biomarkers to aid screening and follow-up has become an important area of research. Cervical cancer evolves through a well-defined sequence of precancerous lesions, and disease management is based on cytologic and HPV testing followed by colposcopy, with excisional treatment recommended for high-grade lesions [11]. The standard excisional approach for treatment is large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ) [24]. Previous studies have reported diagnostic differences between colposcopy-directed biopsies and LEEP specimens, with LEEP showing greater diagnostic validity [25–27]. Such discrepancies may sometimes lead to overtreatment, while in other cases, high-grade lesions may be missed. The present study aimed to investigate whether routine hematologic indices, “low-cost, widely available biomarkers,” could predict lesion severity before LEEP and identify patients at increased risk of recurrence.

Chronic inflammation has been proposed as a potential contributor to cervical carcinogenesis [27], and hematologic markers have been widely studied in this context. Li et al. reported that NLR and MLR were independent prognostic factors associated with recurrence in patients with stage IIB cervical cancer [28]. In the study by Trinh et al., NLR values correlated with progression-free and overall survival, and higher NLR levels after treatment were associated with poorer progression-free and overall survival [29]. In our study, NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, and SIRI did not show meaningful predictive value for estimating high-grade lesions among patients who underwent LEEP after biopsy under colposcopic guidance. In a study conducted by Qin et al., 324 patients who underwent colposcopy were divided into two groups: those with and without cervical lesions, and their NLR, PLR, and SII levels were compared. The group without cervical lesions had lower NLR, PLR, and SII values than those with lesions; however, the ROC curve discrimination was low. Therefore, the authors did not recommend using these biomarkers for diagnostic purposes [30]. In the study by Vida et al., patients who underwent conization were retrospectively analyzed, as in our study. A difference in NLR values was observed in the cervical cancer group; however, no significant difference was found among the CIN subgroups [17]. Similarly, Taş et al. concluded that NLR and PLR were not useful in predicting precancerous cervical pathology, though they differed between cancer and preinvasive lesions [31]. The evaluation of hematological parameters is a noninvasive, low-cost biomarker that reflects systemic inflammatory status. Several recent studies have demonstrated that these parameters may influence prognosis. In this study, we investigated whether hematological parameters could predict histopathological differences, particularly in patients who underwent LEEP after colposcopy-guided biopsy. To avoid overtreatment and prevent missing high-risk lesions, NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, and SIRI values were analyzed; however, no significant differences were found. We concluded that these parameters were ineffective in predicting the grade of precancerous lesions before LEEP.

CIN2 represents a precancerous lesion that, if left untreated, can progress to cervical cancer. Therefore, LEEP is generally recommended as the standard treatment. However, recent evidence has challenged this approach, as spontaneous regression of CIN2 occurs in approximately 50–57% of cases within two years [32, 33]. In patients diagnosed with CIN2, due to the high rate of spontaneous regression, performing LEEP in young women who desire fertility may lead to overtreatment and increase the risk of preterm birth in subsequent pregnancies [34]. A large Danish population-based study emphasized that active surveillance is a clinically acceptable alternative to immediate excision in women with CIN2 who have low-grade cytology or normal findings [32]. Given the known discordance between biopsy and LEEP results, a substantial proportion of patients with biopsy-proven CIN2 are not confirmed to have CIN2 + upon excision. This raises the question of whether some patients may be overtreated. One of the important findings of our study is the discordance between biopsy and final LEEP histopathology in patients diagnosed with CIN2 on biopsy. Among 237 women diagnosed with CIN2, final LEEP pathology was reported as normal or CIN1 in 104 cases (43.9%). In a substantial proportion of patients, CIN2 + was not confirmed after excision, indicating a potential for overtreatment. The management of CIN2 is still a matter of clinical debate, and our results show that hematologic parameters cannot preoperatively distinguish patients with CIN2 lesions. This limitation further limits their usefulness in supporting conservative management decisions. Although hematologic inflammatory markers were not useful in clarifying this distinction, this observation emphasizes the need for careful risk stratification and individualized management in patients with CIN2.

Women diagnosed with CIN most commonly experience recurrence within the first two years after treatment [6–8]. Persistent high-risk HPV (hr-HPV) positivity is one of the most frequent causes of recurrence [35]. Ding et al. reported a CIN2 + recurrence rate of 6.1% and confirmed a strong association between hr-HPV persistence and recurrence [36]. In our cohort, the recurrence rate within two years was 6.7%, consistent with the literature, and persistent hr-HPV infection was significantly correlated with recurrence. Peripheral blood parameters, such as neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts, have been shown to contribute to tumor invasion and progression [37]; thus, hematologic indices have been investigated as possible prognostic factors for recurrence after CIN treatment. Several studies have reported an association between elevated NLR and recurrence risk. In our study, no significant differences were found in NLR, PLR, SII, or SIRI between recurrent and non-recurrent groups. In a study by Farzaneh et al., the authors reported that an NLR cutoff of 1.9 could be effective for predicting recurrence [38]. Compared with our study, the work by Farzaneh et al. included a smaller sample size and did not exclude patients with positive surgical margins after excision. Since margin positivity is a well-established risk factor for recurrence, the inclusion of these patients may have influenced the prognostic effect of NLR reported in their study. Chun et al. reported that an NLR cutoff of 2.1 was an independent prognostic factor for recurrence after surgical excision in patients with CIN [39]. Origoni et al. likewise emphasized that NLR is a strong and independent prognostic indicator for recurrence [40]. In these studies, the analyses were conducted with only 230 and 428 patients, and recurrence was defined as the reappearance of any CIN lesion, regardless of grade. In contrast, in our study, recurrence was defined more strictly and as a clinically more meaningful endpoint, namely CIN2 + requiring repeat excisional treatment. These differences in recurrence definitions have likely contributed to the divergent findings observed across studies. These data suggest that larger prospective studies are needed to clarify the prognostic impact of hematologic indices in cervical intraepithelial neoplasia. In the study conducted by Szabo et al., the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) was examined, and it was concluded that this parameter may be effective in predicting high-grade lesions and distinguishing invasive disease [5]. In our study, the borderline association observed for MLR in the univariate analyses likely represents a weak or incidental finding, as this relationship did not remain significant in multivariate analyses that included HPV persistence and other clinical variables. Only persistent HPV infection remained an independent predictor of recurrence. Although the predictive power of MLR for recurrence was limited, the ROC results indicated that its prognostic value was weak. Additionally, exploratory analyses conducted for early (< 12 months) recurrence yielded similarly nonsignificant results, supporting the robustness of the main model (Supplementary Figure S1). In terms of clinical utility, MLR alone did not provide additional benefit; however, when combined with HPV persistence, it demonstrated a small but consistent net benefit within the 10–20% probability threshold range. These results indicate that systemic inflammatory indices may be considered complementary tools rather than independent predictors. Moreover, the MLR cutoff value of 0.215 was derived exploratorily within the same cohort and should not be used as a clinical decision threshold without external validation. The effect of systemic inflammatory response on cervical lesions appears to be limited. Our study indicates that recurrence risk is chiefly associated with HPV persistence and that hematologic parameters alone are not suitable for clinical decision-making; however, when combined with HPV persistence and clinical variables, they may play a complementary role in triage or risk stratification algorithms. Given the limited number of recurrence events, the multivariable models may be prone to overfitting, and the findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Biomarker research in gynecological oncology has increasingly shifted toward molecular and tumor microenvironment–based approaches, such as TCGA classification and liquid biopsy [41, 42]. These approaches reflect tumor biology more directly and may help with risk assessment, although they are not yet widely used in routine practice. In this setting, markers like mismatch repair status, tumor mutational burden, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are being studied for their potential role in guiding prognosis and treatment [43]. By comparison, the inflammatory indices evaluated in our study are easy to obtain but showed limited predictive value, suggesting that they are unlikely to be useful on their own in clinical decision-making.

Despite several limitations, our study also has notable strengths. First, it includes a large cohort, which strengthens the robustness and transferability of the findings. Second, all diagnostic and therapeutic procedures were performed in an ESGO-accredited tertiary referral center, guaranteeing high diagnostic reliability and minimal variability in clinical decision-making. Third, defining recurrence strictly as CIN2 + requiring repeat excisional treatment provided a clinically meaningful and strong endpoint, thereby increasing the validity of the recurrence analysis.

Conclusion

Hematologic parameters showed no meaningful predictive value for distinguishing cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grades and were not independently associated with recurrence. No statistically significant results were obtained, even in the analysis limited to the CIN2 patient group. Although MLR showed a borderline association in univariable analysis, this finding was not maintained after adjustment and demonstrated limited discriminative ability. Persistent HPV infection remained the only independent predictor of recurrence. These findings indicate that routine preoperative inflammatory hematologic markers do not improve clinical risk stratification beyond HPV status.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The hematologic parameters in our study were based solely on pre-procedure laboratory measurements. Postoperative hemogram values during follow-up were unavailable. Therefore, time-dependent changes in hematologic parameters before and after LEEP could not be evaluated. Furthermore, reliance on a single preoperative measurement does not capture the dynamic nature of systemic inflammation, which may limit the biological interpretability of these markers. Prospective studies with serial measurements are needed to clarify whether temporal variations among these parameters have clinical implications.

Smoking status was not included in multivariable analyses due to missing data, which may introduce residual confounding; however, this does not appear to affect the dominant role of HPV persistence. Finally, the retrospective, single-center design limits generalizability, and larger, multicenter, prospective studies are justified to confirm these outcomes.
