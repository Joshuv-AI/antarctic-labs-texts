Dramatic Improvement of Treatment-Refractory Prurigo Nodularis Following Initiation of Semaglutide: A Case Report
Authors: Anne-Claire Fougerousse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis characterized by intensely pruritic hyperkeratotic nodules affecting the trunk and extremities. Despite an expanding therapeutic armamentarium including dupilumab and nemolizumab, a subset of patients remains inadequately controlled. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), widely used in the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity, have provided emerging evidence supporting immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects in several chronic inflammatory dermatoses. We report the case of a 64-year-old man with severe, treatment-refractory PN who had failed multiple lines of therapy over four years, including potent topical corticosteroids, dupilumab, and methotrexate. Following initiation of semaglutide for obesity, and with no change to his background PN treatment, a dramatic and progressive clinical improvement was observed. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of PN improvement under GLP-1 RA therapy. The potential mechanisms underlying this benefit include modulation of low-grade chronic inflammation, direct effects on GLP-1 receptor-expressing immune cells, possible modulation of Th2 immune response, and anti-nociceptive action on pruritus via central and peripheral neuro-immune pathways. This observation highlights the potential of GLP-1 RAs as adjunctive therapy in refractory inflammatory dermatoses and warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Prurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis characterized by hyperkeratotic plaques, papules, or nodules affecting the trunk and extremities, associated with intense pruritus lasting at least 6 weeks.1 Its prevalence is estimated at 0.19% in the United States; it predominantly affects women and is associated with comorbidities including atopic dermatitis, type 2 diabetes, and chronic hepatic or renal conditions.2 A recent UK study demonstrated an association with overweight (OR 1.32; 95% CI [1.16–1.50]) and obesity (OR 1.86; 95% CI [1.64–2.17]).3

The pathophysiology of PN remains incompletely understood. It involves neuronal dysregulation and type 2 cutaneous inflammation, perpetuated by an itch–scratch cycle that drives the formation and persistence of skin lesions.4 Current treatment relies on topical agents (corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, capsaicin), antihistamines, phototherapy, and immunosuppressants (methotrexate, ciclosporin).1 Two biologics — dupilumab (anti-IL-4/IL-13 antibody) and nemolizumab (anti-IL-31R antibody) — have received regulatory approval for this indication. Despite this therapeutic armamentarium, some patients remain inadequately controlled, highlighting the need for alternative approaches.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have revolutionized the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity. Beyond their cardiometabolic effects, emerging data suggest immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties, with demonstrated benefit in inflammatory dermatoses such as psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa.5 Cases of improvement in atopic dermatitis6 and chronic spontaneous urticaria7 under GLP-1 RA therapy have also been reported. We report here, to our knowledge, the first case of dramatic improvement of refractory PN following initiation of semaglutide.

Case Report

A 64-year-old man was referred for a pruritic dermatosis that had been evolving for 2 months despite the application of potent topical corticosteroids and levocetirizine 10 mg/day. His medical history included childhood atopic eczema, obesity (BMI 36.29 kg/m2), benign prostatic hyperplasia, and hyperthyroidism. His regular medications included dutasteride–tamsulosin and levothyroxine, both taken for several years. Clinical examination revealed approximately 50 excoriated prurigo lesions (Figures 1 and 2); the pruritus Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score was 9/10. The patient reported significant sleep disturbance related to pruritus, with a Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) of 22/30. Initial workup — including complete blood count fasting blood glucose, glycated haemoglobin, hepatic transaminases (AST, ALT), hsCRP, creatinine, lipid profile, ferritin, hepatitis B, C and HIV serology, anti-skin antibody testing, and serum protein electrophoresis — was unremarkable. Initial management consisted of dupilumab 300 mg every 14 days following a loading dose. After 4 months, the pruritus VAS score was 6/10 with persistent sleep disturbances, and a 50% reduction in the number of PN lesions was observed. A further 6 months of dupilumab therapy produced no additional clinical improvement. The patient was subsequently treated with methotrexate 22.5 mg/week; after 4 months, PN lesions had improved by approximately 50%, but pruritus remained unchanged. The patient self-discontinued treatment, resulting in a severe relapse (>100 PN lesions; pruritus VAS 9/10). Methotrexate was reintroduced at 25 mg/week for 5 months with incomplete control, after which dupilumab 300 mg every 14 days was added. After 6 months of combination therapy, partial symptomatic improvement was noted (pruritus VAS 5/10; 50 active PN lesions).

After 3 years of follow-up for severe, multi-treatment-refractory PN, semaglutide was initiated for the management of obesity, with no modification to the ongoing PN treatment regimen. After 5 months of semaglutide, the patient had lost 12 kg; the pruritus VAS score decreased to 3/10, and only 15 active PN nodules were identified on examination. At 12 months, the pruritus VAS was 1/10 without active PN lesion, and the patient had lost an additional 5 kg (total weight loss: 17 kg; BMI 30.9 kg/m2) (Figures 3 and 4). Discussion

We report the first case, to our knowledge, of dramatic improvement of treatment-refractory PN following initiation of a GLP-1 RA. The temporal association between semaglutide initiation and clinical improvement — in the absence of any modification to the background PN treatment — suggests a potentially causal role of semaglutide in the observed benefit.

Several mechanistic hypotheses may explain this finding. First, GLP-1 RAs exert indirect anti-inflammatory effects by reducing low-grade chronic inflammation, notably through downregulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1α, cytokines implicated in the inflammatory milieu of PN.4 At the molecular level, this anti-inflammatory activity is mediated in part through the cAMP/PKA/CREB signalling cascade: GLP-1R activation stimulates adenylate cyclase, leading to increased intracellular cAMP and PKA activation, which in turn phosphorylates the transcription factor CREB. CREB activation suppresses NF-κB-driven transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators and may promote IL-10 induction, thereby attenuating inflammatory cytokine production.8 Second, GLP-1 receptors are expressed on multiple immune cells — including eosinophils, regulatory T lymphocytes, macrophages, and possibly mast cells — providing a plausible direct immunomodulatory pathway.4 Third, modulation of the Th2 immune response has been demonstrated with GLP-1 RAs in the context of asthma, though cutaneous evidence remains lacking.4 Preliminary data in atopic dermatitis — a condition sharing type 2 inflammatory features with PN — suggest that GLP-1R activation on eosinophils may lead to downregulation of IL-4 and IL-13, cytokines that are also implicated in PN pathophysiology.8 Fourth, GLP-1 RAs may exert antinociceptive effects on pruritus through modulation of neuro-immune signaling at both central and peripheral levels, a particularly relevant mechanism given the prominent neurogenic component of PN.5 Finally, the contribution of weight loss per se cannot be excluded, given the established epidemiological association between obesity and PN severity. However, the immunomodulatory effects of GLP-1 RAs in inflammatory skin diseases appear to extend beyond the secondary consequences of weight reduction. In psoriasis cohort data, improvements in disease activity scores remained statistically significant even after adjusting for percentage weight loss, and clinical responses have been reported in non-obese patients, collectively suggesting a direct immunometabolic mechanism of action.8 Published data increasingly support the role of GLP-1 RAs in chronic inflammatory dermatoses. Improvements have been reported in psoriasis,9,10 hidradenitis suppurativa;11 with current evidence suggesting use of GLP-1 RAs as adjunctive therapy in obese patients with cardiometabolic comorbidities although randomized controlled trials are lacking. Data about atopic dermatitis,6 and chronic spontaneous urticaria7 remain limited. Altogether, these data suggest a broad immunomodulatory effect of this drug class beyond its metabolic actions, and position GLP-1 RAs as a potentially valuable component of an interdisciplinary therapeutic strategy in patients with refractory inflammatory dermatoses and coexisting metabolic comorbidities.8

Beyond its cutaneous effects, semaglutide initiation was associated with a marked improvement in quality of life in our patient, consistent with emerging psychosocial data on GLP-1 RA therapy in chronic inflammatory dermatoses. In hidradenitis suppurativa and psoriasis cohort studies, improvements in DLQI scores and mood — assessed by the Beck Depression Inventory — remained significant after adjustment for weight loss and skin disease activity, suggesting a direct effect of GLP-1 RAs on psychological well-being beyond skin clearance alone.8 Several mechanisms may contribute, including central neuromodulatory effects via GLP-1 receptor expression in brain regions involved in mood regulation, attenuation of systemic low-grade inflammation, and alleviation of stigmatizing symptoms.

Conclusion

The rapid and sustained improvement of refractory PN following introduction of semaglutide in our patient may represent a coincidence; however, the convergence of weight loss, immunomodulatory, and antinociceptive mechanisms associated with GLP-1 RAs offers a compelling biological rationale. This observation underscores the importance of prospectively collecting such data in order to evaluate the potential of GLP-1 RAs as adjunctive therapy in patients with inflammatory dermatoses refractory to conventional and biologic treatments.
