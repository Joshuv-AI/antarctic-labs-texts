Lactiplantibacillus plantarum from Thai fermented pork with inulin ameliorates metabolic disturbances through proteomic mechanisms
Authors: Onrapak Reamtong, Watanalai Panbangred, Mayura Janhom, Pattaneeya Prangthip
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the molecular mechanisms by which Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strain P4, isolated from Thai fermented pork (Nham), affects high-fat diet-induced metabolic alterations in rats, alone and combined with inulin. Male Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 32, initial weight 204.12 ± 4.80 g) were randomized into four groups (n = 8/group): normal diet (N), high-fat diet (HF), high-fat diet with L. plantarum P4 (HFLac, 10^9 CFU/g), and high-fat diet with both L. plantarum P4 and inulin (HFLacIn, 10^9 CFU/g + 0.1% w/w inulin). After 4 weeks, we analyzed lipid profiles, liver and kidney function markers, inflammatory parameters, and conducted proteomic analysis of liver tissue using high-resolution mass spectrometry. The HF group exhibited distinct protein alterations compared to the N group, indicating dysregulated fatty acid metabolism. The synbiotic HFLacIn intervention improved multiple parameters: (1) Enhanced lipid profiles with increased HDL-C (15.8%) and reduced LDL-C (9.5%) and VLDL (15.9%); (2) Improved liver function with reduced ALP (3.4%); and (3) Decreased inflammatory markers TNF-α (13.4%) and CRP (31.4%, p = 0.03). Proteomic analysis revealed upregulation of key proteins in the HFLacIn group: long-chain-fatty-acid-CoA ligase 1 (1.25-fold, p = 0.033) for enhanced fatty acid activation, hemopexin (1.04-fold, p = 0.039) for improved oxidative stress protection, and modified inflammation-related proteins. The marked upregulation of histone H2B type 1-A (2.82-fold, p = 0.002) suggests epigenetic modifications contributing to metabolic improvements. These findings provide molecular evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of probiotics from traditional fermented foods, especially when combined with prebiotics, in managing metabolic disorders.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are becoming a critical global health challenge, causing 74% of deaths globally each year. Cardiovascular diseases account for the majority of these deaths, with 17.9 million people affected annually. Diet is recognized as one of the most significant modifiable risk factors for NCDs, directly affecting blood pressure, blood glucose, blood lipids, and body weight1.

The consumption of high-fat diets (HFDs) has been increasingly recognized as a significant modifiable risk factor for various diseases. HFDs can trigger chronic inflammation and oxidative stress through multiple pathways2, leading to dyslipidemia and pathological alterations in cardiovascular function3. Recent genetic studies have also highlighted the complex interplay between dietary habits and genetic factors in obesity development, with certain genetic variants being associated with increased consumption of sugar and saturated fats4.

While conventional treatments for hyperlipidemia rely on lifestyle modifications and pharmaceutical interventions, probiotics have emerged as an adjunct therapeutic strategy. Traditional fermented foods, particularly Thai fermented pork (Nham), a traditional Thai fermented pork sausage5, serve as rich sources of beneficial lactic acid bacteria (LAB). Previously, several LAB strains from Nham, including Lactococcus lactis (Nisin+), Pediococcus pentosaceus (Ped+), and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strain P4, were screened, isolated, characterized and systematically tested for stability6. In preliminary stability studies prior to this investigation, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strain P4 (L. plantarum strain P4) demonstrated the highest stability among those 3 tested strains, maintaining viable populations above 10^9 CFU/mL throughout a 7-day storage period at 4 °C.

The human gut microbiota represents a complex ecological system, with an estimated 500–1000 bacterial species coexisting at any given time. While earlier estimates suggested a 10:1 ratio of bacterial to human cells, refined calculations indicate approximately 1.3 bacterial cells for every human cell, comprising a highly diverse ecological community that maintains unique characteristics within each individual7. Recent studies have demonstrated that probiotic strains, particularly L. plantarum, can counteract oxidative stress through multiple mechanisms, including enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, free radical scavenging, and regulation of antioxidant gene expression at the transcriptional level8.

Our previous research demonstrated that commercial probiotic Lactobacillus acidophilus (L. acidophilus) significantly improved lipid profiles and reduced inflammatory markers in high-fat diet-fed rats, with reductions of 18.56% in CRP, 35.71% in TNF-α, and 25.6% in LDL-C levels8. These findings were particularly significant given that HFD-induced metabolic disturbances have been shown to cause distinct pathological alterations in various rat models3.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of L. plantarum strain P4, both alone and in combination with the prebiotic inulin, on high-fat diet-induced metabolic disturbances in rats. The selection of inulin as a prebiotic component is based on its well-documented benefits and FDA approval as a prebiotic supplement that improves the nutritional value of food products. Inulin have demonstrated to reduce body weight gain and improve lipid profiles, while synbiotic combinations of probiotics with prebiotics have shown enhanced therapeutic effects compared to either component alone9. We hypothesize that L. plantarum strain P4 from Thai fermented pork would demonstrate unique molecular mechanisms in ameliorating high-fat diet-induced metabolic alterations through pathways distinct from previously studied commercial probiotic strains. Our current work differs significantly from previous studies by using a novel strain derived specifically from Thai fermented food and employing comprehensive proteomic analysis via high-resolution mass spectrometry to elucidate specific hepatic molecular mechanisms. These contribute to understanding traditional fermented food-derived probiotics for metabolic disorder management.

Methods

Ethical approval this animal study was approved by the Faculty of Tropical Medicine Animal Care and Use Committee, Mahidol University (Approval No. FTM-ACUC 020/2020).

Preparation of probiotics and prebiotics

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strain P4 (formerly Lactobacillus plantarum, following the taxonomic reclassification by Zheng et al., 202010) was isolated from Thai fermented pork (Nham). The bacteria were isolated and identified to species level using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The strain was cultured in de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth at 37 °C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions. Preliminary stability studies confirmed viability at 4 °C for up to 7 days with less than 0.5 log CFU/g reduction. The probiotic preparation was standardized to 10^9 colony-forming units (CFU) per gram using spectrophotometric measurements and stored in opaque tubes at 4 °C for up to 3 days before use. The prebiotic inulin from chicory (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS: 9005-80-5) was used in powder form. The product specifications included ≤ 0.05% free glucose (enzymatic assay) and < 15% water content (Karl Fischer), with solubility of 100 mg/mL in water. The inulin was stored at room temperature according to manufacturer specifications and handled under sterile conditions during the experiment.

Animal study design

For sample size calculation, we used data from our previous study where TNF-α concentrations differed between HF and treatment groups by 18.56–56.59% (with synbiotic combination showing the highest reduction). Power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1) with α = 0.05, power = 90%, expected effect size (δ) = 0.4 (based on anticipated 40–50% treatment difference), and standard deviations (σ1 = 0.5, σ2 = 0.4). This analysis determined a minimum required sample size of 8 rats per group (total n = 32 for 4 groups). Thirty-two male Sprague-Dawley rats (5 weeks old, with body weight of 133.21 ± 1.96 g [mean ± SD]) were obtained from the Nomura Siam International, Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). The animals were acclimatized for 7 days with a standard chow diet and housed in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. Environmental conditions were monitored daily using calibrated thermometers and hygrometers. After the acclimatization period, rats with body weight (mean ± SD = 204.12 ± 4.80 g) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 per group) using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel. Experimental groups in this study were: (1) Normal Diet (N): Control group receiving standard chow diet; (2) High-Fat Diet (HF): Group receiving high-fat diet; (3) High-Fat Diet + L. plantarum strain P4 (HFLac): Group receiving high-fat diet and L. plantarum strain P4; (4) High-Fat Diet + L. plantarum strain P4 + Inulin (HFLacIn): Group receiving high-fat diet, L. plantarum strain P4, and inulin.

The experimental diets were formulated according to a modified protocol11. All diets contained a base composition of 20% casein, 15% corn starch, 3.5% mineral mixture, 1% vitamin mixture, 0.3% DL-methionine, and 0.2% choline bitartrate. The normal diet (N) contained 50% sucrose and 5% fiber, while the high-fat diets (HF, HFLac, and HFLacIn) contained 33% sucrose, 17% lard, and varying amounts of fiber (5% for HF and HFLac, 4.9% for HFLacIn). The HFLacIn group’s diet was additionally supplemented with 0.1% inulin.

Daily oral gavage was performed at 10:00 AM. For probiotic preparation, L. plantarum strain P4 cultures were grown in MRS broth, centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and washed twice with 0.9% sodium chloride to remove residual media. Groups N and HF received 0.9% sodium chloride (1 mL/kg body weight), while the HFLac and HFLacIn groups received the washed L. plantarum strain P4 suspension (10^9 CFU/mL) in 0.9% sodium chloride (1 mL/kg body weight). For the HFLacIn group, the standard fiber content in the diet was reduced to 4.9%, with 0.1% of the total diet supplemented with inulin.

Physiological and biochemical measurements

Food intake and water consumption were monitored daily, and body weight was measured weekly. After the 12-week experimental period, rats were fasted for 12–16 h and euthanized using CO₂ according to institutional protocols. Blood samples (approximately 12 mL) were collected from the vena cava in EDTA-coated tubes for whole blood and in serum separator tubes for plasma separation. Biochemical parameters, including lipid profiles (total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, LDL-C) and liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP), were analyzed using the Cobas 6800/8800 System (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). Liver samples were weighed, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for proteomic analysis.

Immunological and inflammatory parameters

Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until analysis. White blood cell counts were measured using the Cobas 6800/8800 System (Roche Group, Switzerland). C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were analyzed using a rat-specific ELISA kit (RAB0097, Sigma-Aldrich) with a standard curve range of 0.246–60.246 ng/mL and sensitivity of 200 pg/mL. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) was analyzed using a rat-specific ELISA kit (RAB0480, Sigma-Aldrich) with a standard curve range of 82.3–20000.3 pg/mL and sensitivity of 25 pg/mL. Both assays demonstrated intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation of < 10% and < 12%, respectively. All samples were analyzed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using the supplied 96-well plates (12 strips × 8 wells), with absorbance measured at 450 nm.

Proteomic analysis

Rat liver samples from the same experimental group were pooled, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground using a mortar and pestle. Lysis was performed by adding a buffer containing 1%SDS, 1% Triton X-100, and 1% NaCl (Merck, Germany). The samples were homogenized using an ultrasonicator (Sonics & Materials, USA). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the Quick Start Bradford Protein Assay (Bio-Rad, USA). For protein separation, 30 µg of protein from each lysate was loaded onto a 12% SDS-PAGE gel (Bio-Rad). Following electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie G-250 solution (Bio-Rad). Each lane was sectioned into 13 fragments, and the proteins were subjected to tryptic in-gel digestion. The resulting peptides were separated using an Ultimate 3000 Nano-LC system (Dionex, Surrey, UK) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 2% (v/v) acetonitrile with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in HPLC-grade water and (B) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in HPLC- grade acetonitrile. Peptides were analyzed using a micrOTOF-QII mass spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) controlled by Hystar software. Mass spectrometry (MS) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data were collected within m/z ranges of 400–2000 and 50–1500, respectively.

Proteomic data analysis

Mass spectrometry (MS) data were converted into Mascot generic files (.mgf) using Mascot v.2.3.0 software (Matrix Science, London, UK). Protein identification was conducted using the SwissProt database, with the following parameters: taxonomy set to Rattus norvegicus, enzyme specified as trypsin, allowance for one missed cleavage site, a precursor mass tolerance of 1.2 Da, and a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.6 Da. Oxidation of methionine and carbamidomethylation of cysteine were included as variable and fixed modifications, respectively. Proteins identified with at least 95% confidence were considered for further analysis. Semi-quantitative analysis of protein abundance was performed using the exponentially modified protein abundance index (emPAI). Proteins were classified according to their gene ontology (GO) terms using String software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Due to non-normal distribution in several parameters, nonparametric tests were employed for all analyses. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to determine differences among groups, followed by Mann-Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons when the Kruskal-Wallis test indicated significant differences. To control for multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.0083 (0.05/6 possible pairwise comparisons). All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 28.0). Results are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR, 25th-75th percentiles). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for the Kruskal-Wallis test and p < 0.0083 for post-hoc comparisons.

Results

Effects on body weight and organ weights

Figure 1 shows body weights were significantly different between groups (p = 0.002) at week 4. The HF and HFLacIn groups exhibited significantly higher body weights compared to the normal diet group (HF: 439.0 g [422.0–448.0.0.0], HFLacIn: 430.0 g [424.5–433.0] vs. N: 404.0 g [390.0–411.5.0.5]). The HFLac group (408.0 g [395.0–410.0.0.0]) was not significantly different from the normal group. As shown in Table 1, liver weights differed significantly across groups (p < 0.001), with high-fat feeding inducing marked hepatomegaly (HF: 20.34 g [19.39–21.51] vs. N: 15.00 g [14.86–15.14]). The HFLac group significantly attenuated this effect (HFLac: 17.42 g [16.49–17.83]), while the HFLacIn group showed intermediate effects (HFLacIn: 19.97 g [17.29–20.60]). Both kidney weights (right and left) were significantly lower in HF and HFLac groups compared to the N group (p < 0.001). Spleen weights showed significant differences between groups (p = 0.001), with lower values in HF and HFLac groups compared to the N group. Heart weights were significantly different (p = 0.008), with HFLacIn group showing higher values compared to N and HFLac groups. Brain weights remained consistent across groups (p = 0.346).

Effects on lipid profiles

Figure 2 illustrates the serum lipid profiles and glucose levels, demonstrating the effects of dietary interventions across experimental groups. Total cholesterol levels tend to decrease in the HFLac and HFLacIn groups compared to the HF group (HFLac: 96.0 mg/dL [71.5–111.5.5.5] HFLacIn: 101.5 mg/dL [97.5–106.0] vs. HF: 113.5 mg/dL [97.0–119.0.0.0] respectively). High-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels showed the highest values in the N group 28.5 mg/dL [26.5–29.0], with both HFLac and HFLacIn groups showing intermediate levels (21.5 mg/dL [19.0–25.0], and 22.0 mg/dL [19.5–23.0], respectively) compared to the HF group (19.0 mg/dL [17.5–22.0]). Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol showed differences between treatment groups, with the HFLacIn group showing lower levels compared to the HF group (31.5 mg/dL [24.5–41.5], vs. 28.5 mg/dL [22.0–34.5.0.5]), representing a 9.5% reduction. Notably, very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) levels were substantially reduced in the HFLacIn group (18.5 mg/dL [15.5–19.0]) compared to the HF group (22.0 mg/dL [19.5–24.5]), representing a 15.9% reduction (p = 0.01). These findings suggest potential modulatory effects of probiotic and synbiotic supplementation on lipid metabolism under high-fat diet conditions.

Effects on liver and kidney function markers

Figure 3 presents the effects of dietary interventions on liver and kidney function markers. The HF and HFLac groups showed elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels (159.50 U/L [151.5–180.0] and 158.50 U/L [154.0–161.0.0.0]) compared to the N group (147.00 U/L[141.0–150.0.0.0]), with the HFLacIn group displaying intermediate values (154.00 U/L[148.0–159.5.0.5]). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels showed no significant differences between groups.

Kidney function markers demonstrated distinct patterns. Creatinine levels were higher in all high-fat diet groups (HF: 0.240 mg/dL [0.215–0.25], HFLac: 0.225 mg/dL [0.215–0.24], HFLacIn: 0.225 mg/dL [0.22–0.24]) compared to the N group (0.195 mg/dL [0.17–0.21]). Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels remained consistent across all experimental groups.

Effects on immune system and inflammation

Immune system and inflammation parameters revealed variations across dietary intervention groups (Table 2). Total white blood cell (WBC) count showed no significant differences between groups (p = 0.122). Neutrophil counts were significantly elevated in all high-fat diet groups compared to the N group (p < 0.05). While lymphocyte counts showed overall differences between groups (p = 0.015), post-hoc analyses revealed no significant differences after Bonferroni correction.

These findings suggest potential immunomodulatory effects of probiotic interventions under high-fat dietary conditions.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strain P4 (L. plantarum strain P4), isolated from Thai fermented pork (Nham), particularly in combination with inulin, can beneficially modulate lipid metabolism and reduce inflammation in high-fat diet-fed rats. This aligns with recent study revealing that L. plantarum strain P4 from diverse fermented food sources can contribute to both functional food development and potential health applications12.

Previous study demonstrated that synbiotic supplementation (Lactiplantibacillus pentosus GSSK2 + isomalto-oligosaccharides) for 12 weeks significantly ameliorated morphometric parameters, improved glucose clearance, enhanced lipid biomarkers, and modulated inflammatory pathways in high-fat diet-induced metabolic syndrome13, highlighting the protective potential of synbiotic combinations over individual components. L. plantarum strain P4 was selected based on our preliminary assessment of its superior growth characteristics, reaching stable populations of 10^9 CFU/g, and its established safety profile as a food-derived probiotic14. Inulin was specifically chosen as the prebiotic component due to its documented ability to enhance probiotic colonization and metabolic activities15. Our findings extend our previous work with commercial Lactobacillus acidophilus (L. acidophilus)9 in several important ways, suggesting distinct advantages of naturally isolated strains.

Molecular mechanisms and pathway integration

To ensure clarity in interpreting these proteomic findings, it is important to emphasize that all protein changes discussed demonstrated statistical significance (p < 0.05) as illustrated in Fig. 5 and detailed in Table 3, where -LOG(P-value) > 1.3 corresponds to p < 0.0516. The fold changes mentioned for key proteins represent statistically significant alterations that provide mechanistic insight into the observed physiological changes in lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses.

Our proteomic analysis revealed significant alterations in liver protein expression.

In the HF group, we observed significant upregulation of mitochondrial 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase (2.8-fold increase) and delta(3,5)-Delta(2,4)-dienoyl-CoA isomerase (2.3-fold increase), representing adaptive responses to increased dietary fat load. These enzymes serve as key control points in fatty acid utilization pathways, with 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase catalyzing the final step of fatty acid β-oxidation by cleaving acetyl-CoA units from long-chain fatty acids17, and delta(3,5)-Delta(2,4)-dienoyl-CoA isomerase facilitating the β-oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids18. The simultaneous downregulation of 70 kDa heat shock protein (2.9-fold decrease) in the HF group suggests compromised cellular stress response capacity, which aligns with recent findings on TNF-α regulation through specific protein pathways19 and documented health effects in animal models20, showing their crucial role in stress tolerance and protein homeostasis21. This protein alteration likely contributes to the pro-inflammatory state observed in high-fat diet conditions.

In contrast, the HFLacIn group exhibited a distinct proteomic profile characterized by significant upregulation of hemopexin (1.04-fold increase), a heme-binding protein with antioxidant properties, histone H2B type 1-A (2.82-fold increase), a DNA-packaging protein involved in gene regulation, and long-chain-fatty-acid-CoA ligase 1(1.25-fold increase), an enzyme essential for fatty acid activation, alongside downregulation of glutathione S-transferase alpha-1(2.57-fold decrease), a detoxification enzyme. The upregulation of hemopexin is particularly noteworthy as it indicates enhanced cellular protection against oxidative stress through heme sequestration. These findings are supported by recent research demonstrating stress resistance mechanisms of L. plantarum22 and its unique genomic features23. The molecular basis for these antioxidant effects has been increasingly understood, with recent studies showing L. plantarum strains can regulate cell redox homeostasis through both enzymatic activities and transcriptional control of antioxidant genes8.While our study demonstrates beneficial effects of the L. plantarum P4 and inulin combination, we did not directly assess the metabolic interactions between them. The observed benefits could result from several mechanisms. Previous studies mentioned direct utilization of inulin by L. plantarum strains24. Other possibilities include metabolism of inulin by gut bacteria to produce SCFAs, or separate but complementary effects of the probiotic and prebiotic.

The variable responses in lipid profiles across treatment groups likely reflect the complex interplay between these differentially expressed proteins, dietary fat intake, probiotic metabolism, and host lipid regulatory mechanisms. This interpretation aligns with recent studies on the metabolic effects of L. plantarum25 and the broader understanding of probiotic effects on lipid metabolism, where systematic review evidence demonstrates that probiotics exert effects on lipid parameters26.

These proteomic alterations suggest that L. plantarum strain P4, particularly in combination with inulin, counteracts high-fat diet-induced metabolic disturbances by enhancing oxidative stress protection mechanisms and modulating inflammatory pathways, rather than primarily affecting the fatty acid oxidation machinery that was altered in the HF group.

Clinical implications and translation potential

The intervention with L. plantarum strain P4 and inulin showed distinct mechanistic effects documented through comprehensive proteomic analysis. The synergistic effects between L. plantarum strain P4 and inulin warrant particular attention, supported by recent studies showing that specific strains of L. plantarum can more efficiently utilize inulin compared to other probiotic species27.

Our findings align with recent synbiotic research demonstrating enhanced therapeutic effects in metabolic disorders. Zhang et al. reported that probiotics combined with inulin improved NAFLD by activating AMPK signaling and enhancing intestinal barrier function28. Similarly, Mu et al. showed in a meta-analysis that probiotics/synbiotics significantly improved glucose metabolism and cholesterol levels in gestational diabetes. These studies collectively support our observation that synbiotics can modulate multiple metabolic pathways simultaneously29. These findings have important clinical implications, as recent meta-analysis evidence shows that probiotic interventions can significantly improve metabolic parameters and reduce inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein in human populations30.

At the physiological level, L. plantarum strain P4 demonstrated several significant advantages over commercial probiotic strains. The anti-inflammatory effects were particularly noteworthy, with reductions in TNF-alpha (13.4%) and CRP (31.4%, p = 0.03) occurring through distinct molecular pathways as revealed by our proteomic analysis. These findings align with recent clinical evidence showing L. plantarum strains can significantly reduce total cholesterol (34.6%) and LDL-C (28.4%) in human subjects through mechanisms including bile salt hydrolase activity31. Previous studies have examined L. plantarum strains with inulin in high-fat diet models, documenting beneficial effects of L. plantarum LS/07 on cholesterol, short-chain fatty acids, and β-glucuronidase activity32. While our findings align with these observations, our study offers important distinctions. We investigated L. plantarum strain P4 isolated from Thai fermented pork (Nham), evaluated the synergistic potential of combined synbiotic treatment, and most significantly, provided mechanistic insights through comprehensive proteomic analysis. This analysis revealed specific alterations in hemopexin, histone H2B type 1-A, long-chain-fatty-acid-CoA ligase 1, and glutathione S-transferase alpha-1 expression. These findings extend current knowledge by elucidating the protein-level changes underlying the observed physiological improvements, contributing new understanding of strain-specific probiotic effects on lipid metabolism and inflammatory pathways.

The relationship between physiological parameters and proteomic findings provides a coherent framework for interpreting our results. The decreased total cholesterol and LDL levels in the HFLacIn group correlate with significant upregulation of fatty acid metabolism enzymes, particularly long-chain-fatty-acid-CoA ligase 1 (p = 0.033), which facilitates fatty acid activation for subsequent metabolic pathways. Similarly, the upregulation of hemopexin (p = 0.039), which provides antioxidant protection through heme sequestration, may explain the observed hepatoprotective effects.

The absence of significant differences in ALT and AST levels across treatment groups indicates that while the high-fat diet affects lipid metabolism and inflammatory processes, it did not progress to substantial liver injury during the experimental period. This maintenance of normal transaminase levels despite metabolic stress may reflect a compensatory hepatoprotective effect, potentially supported by the upregulation of stress-response proteins identified in our proteomic analysis. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that certain probiotic interventions can maintain liver integrity even under metabolic stress conditions33.

Integration with host metabolism

The observed improvements in metabolic parameters suggest complex interactions between the probiotic strain and host metabolism. Recent reviews have highlighted how probiotics and prebiotics can modulate both intestinal microbiota and immune system function in metabolic syndrome, though the full complexity of these interactions is still being elucidated34. These effects appear to be mediated through multiple complementary pathways.

Proteomic analysis has emerged as a powerful tool for understanding probiotic mechanisms, revealing how Lactiplantibacillus strains can differentially express proteins in response to environmental conditions, affecting various metabolic pathways35. In our study, this approach revealed significant alterations in key metabolic proteins, demonstrating specific adaptations in fatty acid metabolism pathways that likely contribute to the observed physiological improvements.

Methodological considerations and future directions

A key limitation is that we did not specifically test ability of L. plantarum strain P4 to directly utilize inulin as a carbon source prior to the in vivo study, which may affect interpretation of the synbiotic mechanisms observed. Other important limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings.

First, while our rat model closely mimics human metabolic responses, species-specific differences in microbiota composition and metabolism may affect the translational potential of these results. Recent meta-analyses have shown that while 86% of animal study results show concordance with clinical findings, only 5% of therapeutic interventions successfully achieve regulatory approval36, highlighting the importance of careful study design and robust methodology in translational research. This consideration aligns with recent microbiome research7. They emphasize that despite advances in understanding the connection of human microbiome to various diseases, moving from correlation to causation remains challenging when developing microbiome-based therapies.

Second, our 4-week intervention period, though sufficient to demonstrate significant effects, may not fully reflect long-term outcomes or adaptive responses. Recent meta-analyses of long-term probiotic interventions (≥ 8 weeks) have shown that while beneficial effects can be achieved relatively quickly, the magnitude of these effects may not necessarily increase with extended treatment duration37.

Third, the absence of histological analyses of liver, adipose tissue, and colon represents a limitation in providing complete physiological context for our biochemical and proteomic findings. However, our previous work with this model demonstrated no significant histological differences at this 4-week timepoint9. Our comprehensive proteomic approach provides more sensitive detection of early metabolic adaptations before they manifest as histopathological changes. Future studies should extend intervention duration to 8–12 weeks, incorporate tissue histology to correlate biochemical changes with structural effects, evaluate strain-specific inulin utilization, and test this synbiotic combination in human clinical trials with metabolic syndrome patients to validate translational potential.

Conclusion

Our key findings as shown in Fig. 7 provide evidence that L. plantarum strain P4, particularly in combination with inulin, demonstrates therapeutic potential through distinct molecular mechanisms, though the extent of physiological effects varied across measured parameters. The synbiotic combination showed statistically significant improvements in inflammatory markers, specifically CRP reduction (31.4%, p = 0.03) and VLDL reduction (15.9%, p = 0.01), with trends toward lipid profile improvement (15.8% increase in HDL-C, 9.5% reduction in LDL-C) and hepatic enzyme activity attenuation (3.4% decrease in ALP). Most importantly, our proteomic analysis provides robust molecular evidence supporting the biological activity through three key pathways: (1) Enhanced lipid metabolism, evidenced by upregulation of long-chain-fatty-acid-CoA ligase 1 (1.25-fold, p = 0.033); (2) Improved oxidative stress protection, demonstrated by upregulation of hemopexin (1.04-fold, p = 0.039); and (3) Modified inflammatory response proteins. The marked upregulation of histone H2B type 1-A (2.82-fold, p = 0.002) suggests epigenetic modifications that may contribute to the observed metabolic improvements. These molecular insights highlight the potential therapeutic value of naturally isolated probiotics from traditional fermented foods like Thai Nham, warranting further investigation in human studies to optimize synbiotic formulations for metabolic disorder management.
