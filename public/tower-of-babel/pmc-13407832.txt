Fatigue in Sjögren’s Disease - Integrating Biomedical and Psychosocial Aspects: A Narrative Review
Authors: Xinyao Zhou, Jinzhou Chen, Pascal Cathebras, Martin Killian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fatigue is a prevalent and disabling symptom in Sjögren’s disease (SjD), yet its pathogenesis remains poorly understood and inadequately managed. This narrative review addresses a critical gap in knowledge by conceptualizing SjD-related fatigue as a bidimensional phenomenon driven by both biomedical and psychosocial mechanisms, and framed within the model of persistent somatic symptoms (PSS). In this narrative review, findings from immunology, neuroendocrinology, psychology, and clinical research were synthesized to examine predisposing, triggering, and maintaining factors contributing to SjD-related fatigue. Particular attention was given to studies exploring immune dysregulation, neuroimmune signaling, autonomic imbalance, and psychological traits. Findings indicate that biomedical contributors—such as pro-inflammatory cytokine (e.g., interleukin [IL]-1β, IL-36α), neuroendocrine dysfunction, dysbiosis, and genetic predispositions—interact with psychosocial vulnerabilities, including early-life adversity, personality profiles, maladaptive coping strategies, and mood disorders. These interactions foster self-reinforcing loops of symptom persistence. Notably, current treatments targeting isolated domains yield limited success. Nonetheless, integrated strategies—ranging from biologics and cytokine modulators to exercise, cognitive behavioral therapy, and neuro-modulatory interventions—have demonstrated promise, particularly when aligned with the PSS framework. This narrative review underscores the need for a paradigm shift in fatigue management, advocating for multidimensional assessment and personalized, multimodal interventions. By illuminating the duality of SjD-related fatigue, this narrative review provides a roadmap for more effective diagnosis, research, and patient-centered care.

Introduction

Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease marked by high clinical heterogeneity [1]. Fatigue, along with unusual dryness and pain, is among the most prevalent (60–70%) and disabling symptoms [2] and is consistently reported as one of the key determinants of impaired quality of life [3]. The paradox between the high frequency of fatigue across all clinical subtypes and the limited effectiveness of current treatments—often failing to meet patient or physician’s expectations—underscores a pressing unmet clinical need [4]. Developing more effective therapeutic interventions requires a re-examination of the complexity and specificity of fatigue in SjD.

Fatigue occurs across a broad spectrum of conditions—such as post-operative states [5], post-stroke fatigue [6], heart failure [7], inflammatory bowel diseases [8], systemic autoimmune diseases, including SjD, rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus [9], fibromyalgia or chronic fatigue syndrome—exhibiting both shared and disease-specific features [10]. Addressing this symptom in SjD requires a broader conceptual framework. The concept of persistent somatic symptoms (PSS) provides such a perspective, encompassing predisposing, triggering, and maintaining factors [11, 12]. Exploring the links between SjD-related fatigue and PSS may open new pathways for understanding and managing the disease burden. Notably, the potential efficacy of non-pharmacologic interventions [13] warrants greater attention [14, 15].

Moreover, as a chronic autoimmune disease, fatigue related to SjD is likely to have a dual nature, arising from both biomedical mechanisms, especially immune dysregulation, and psychosocial influences [16]. The relative contribution of each factor remains unclear in SjD, and could vary from one patient to another. Although some studies have identified associations between fatigue and biomedical markers such as decreased white blood cell count or elevated immunoglobulin G (IgG), many patients continue to experience fatigue even after these abnormalities have been resolved [10]. A similar dissociation is observed with dry eye symptoms, and also in patients with rheumatoid arthritis whose disease activity is well-controlled, yet fatigue can persist [17]. These observations underscore the limitations of current paradigms in symptom recognition and management.

Thus, there is a pressing need to review and integrate the biomedical and psychosocial contributors to fatigue in SjD. This includes evaluating their respective roles, interactions, and the potential for reinforcing feedback loops. Biomedical Mechanisms

Predisposing Factors

Genetic Factors

The earliest study focusing on the relationship between genetics and SjD-related fatigue was conducted by Norheim et al. and dates back to 2014 [18]. The aim of this study was to investigate single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) variations in patients with SjD with high and low fatigue states (193 patients and 70 SNPs in 11 genes analyzed after quality control) using logistic regression. These authors detected one SNP (rs10276819) in the SLC25A40 gene and one SNP (rs3786654) in the PKN1 gene, both associated with high fatigue. SLC25A40 encodes a mitochondrial protein in the brain and periphery [19], while PKN1 is ubiquitously expressed and has several functions, including cell signaling, apoptosis, and negative regulation of the pro-inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB [20]. Genetic variation in these two genes may affect cell signaling and biological pathways, ultimately leading to fatigue. In 2016, the same research team discovered that a number (n = 251) of cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites from 48 patients were differently methylated in patients with SjD with high (Fatigue Visual Analogue Scale [f-VAS] > 72) and low (f-VAS < 50) fatigue levels, with the most pronounced hypomethylation in the high fatigue group observed in the SBF2-antisense RNA1 gene and the most distinct hypermethylation observed in the lymphotoxin α gene [21]. A number of genes linked to these CpG sites are involved in innate and adaptive immune responses, cytokine regulation, cellular stress reactions, and neuronal activity in the brain. These findings provide additional support for an epigenetic contribution to the regulation of fatigue in chronic inflammatory disorders. Current evidence is insufficient to determine the precise effect of DNA methylation on gene expression—i.e., whether it promotes upregulation or downregulation—which may differ depending on the genomic region, sequence context, and interactions with other epigenetic processes such as histone modifications. Nevertheless, the results highlight specific genes and pathways that could play a role in the regulation of fatigue. In the 2020s, 27 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in patients with SjD with fatigue in a bioinformatic analysis study, with receptor transporter protein 4 (RTP4) identified as being highly and specifically expressed in patients with SjD with severe fatigue [22]. These 27 DEGs are mainly localized in the mitochondria, endosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, and cytoplasm, and are involved in antiviral immune responses and type I interferon (IFN) pathways. RTP4 was again found to be associated with fatigue in a meta-analysis study by Norheim et al. that included 682 patients (from Norway, Sweden, UK and USA) and covered 4,966,157 genetic markers. The study specifically demonstrated that the major allele of the indel rs60344347 at the RTP4/MASP1 locus was significantly associated with higher fatigue in Scandinavian patients with SjD [23]. The RTP4 gene produces a protein that regulates the cell surface expression of opioid receptors located in descending pain-modulatory pathways of the central nervous system (CNS), as well as in the limbic system and cerebral cortex. Since pain has a well-established impact on fatigue, this observation may provide a potential molecular basis for their link and help explain why fatigue in non-inflammatory disorders, such as chronic pain syndromes, is invariably accompanied by pain [24]. Interestingly, transcriptome data on peripheral CD19 + B cells were available for a small part of the cohort (n = 16 Swedish patients with SjD) and 20 matched healthy controls; a positive correlation was reported between RTP4 expression and the type I IFN score in B cells from patients with SjD. Moreover, in a study of 251 patients including a Greek (n = 199) and a Dutch (n = 62) cohort [25], patients bearing the TT genotype of the B-cell activating factor (BAFF) rs9514828 gene variant had a significantly reduced risk of SjD-associated fatigue compared to their counterparts. The TT genotype of the rs9514828 BAFF polymorphism has been linked to greater disease activity, elevated serum autoantibody levels, increased circulating BAFF, and a higher risk of lymphomagenesis in SjD [26–28]. Consequently, the reduced frequency of the TT rs9514828 genotype among fatigued patients with SjD may indicate a lack of systemic involvement and B cell activation markers in this subgroup.

Protein Factors

Sixteen serum proteins were found to be differentially expressed between fatigued patients with SjD (n = 22) and non-fatigued patients with SjD (n = 23) in a multiplexed proteomic analysis, of which 14 proteins were found to be upregulated and two proteins to be downregulated in the fatigued patients [29]. The top upregulated differentially expressed proteins were neuroactive synaptosomal-associated protein 25 (SNAP-25), alpha-enolase (ENO1), and ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase isozyme L1 (UCHL1). Furthermore, the proinflammatory mediator interleukin (IL)-36α and several complement factors, including C4a/C4b and C3a, were also found to be upregulated in the fatigued patients with SjD compared to the non-fatigued ones, while epidermal growth factor (EGF) and formimidoyl transferase-cyclodeaminase (FTCD) were downregulated. SNAP-25, ENO1, and UCHL1 all have functions in the brain. The SNAP-25 protein is critical in neurotransmitter release [30] and has already been described to be associated to disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease and fibromyalgia [31–34]. Interestingly, autoantibodies against ENO1 have been described in Hashimoto’s encephalopathy, Behcet’s disease, Crohn’s disease, and rheumatoid arthritis [35–39]. Antibodies against citrullinated ENO1 (Anti-CEP-1) peptides were also observed in SjD [40], raising the question whether such autoantibodies are associated with fatigue. EGF, after binding to its receptor, regulates epithelial cell proliferation and survival and therefore is thought to have protective effects; it also correlates with progression of xerostomia [41, 42]. FTCD is a metabolic enzyme that is primarily active in the liver and kidney, but has been described to have additional neurological effects [43]. Another study with the aim to investigate molecules in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that participate in the pathophysiology of SjD-related fatigue enrolled a total of 72 Caucasian patients and assessed their level of fatigue using the f-VAS scale [44]. Ten patients with the lowest f-VAS scores (3–29) and ten with the highest f-VAS scores (76–91) were selected for the proteomic analysis. The authors of this study reported 15 top discriminatory proteins [44], of which two (hemopexin, and pigment epithelium-derived factor) have previously been identified in the CSF of patients with chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) [45]. Some of the identified proteins are associated with severe depression (hemopexin [46], apolipoprotein A4 [47, 48], pigment epithelium-derived factor [49, 50], and secretogranin-3 [51]). Several of them play roles in downregulation of inflammation and cellular stress defense (hemopexin [44], pigment epithelium-derived factor [52, 53], clusterin [54], osteopontin [55], and selenium-binding protein 1 [56]), which strengthen the hypothesis that fatigue is a complex phenomenon and not always directly associated with pro-inflammatory factors [57, 58]. This result is in line with the result by the same team that heat shock protein 90, which is produced when the innate immune system is activated to protect cells, is strongly associated with increased fatigue in patients with SjD [59].

These observations are scarce, without common findings between studies, and their observational designs make it impossible to distinguish between the possible causal factors leading to fatigue from the possible countermeasures the body is developing against fatigue.

Endogenous Factors

The inverse relationship between the levels of female sex hormones and general physical activity and skeletal muscle fatigue was reported in a mouse study which examined the role of ovarian hormones and skeletal muscle estrogen receptor α (ERα) in regulating physical activity and muscle function in female mice [60]. In ovariectomized mice completing the study (n = 25), combined estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4) treatment (n = 5) increased voluntary running and improved fatigue resistance in soleus muscle, with effects largely driven by E2, compared to the ovariectomy control (n = 6) and those treated with P4 alone (n = 7). In contrast, P4 alone did not enhance activity or muscle performance. In a second experiment, whole-body ERα-knockout mice showed greater muscle fatigability, impaired recovery, and reduced plantar flexor muscle contractility compared with wild-type controls, regardless of physical activity. Long-term wheel running only partially mitigated these deficits. These findings indicate that E2, particularly through ERα in skeletal muscle, is essential for sustaining physical activity and protecting muscle from fatigue, while the absence of ERα leads to muscle weakness and poor recovery even with exercise. These findings suggest a potential inverse relationship of estrogen with SjD-related fatigue; however, further dedicated studies in an autoimmune setting are warranted.

Exogenous Factors

Multiple exogenous biomedical factors possibly contribute to fatigue in SjD, but dedicated studies are lacking. Adherence to the Mediterranean diet was found to be beneficial across various domains in SjD and associated with lower likelihood of having SjD [61]. Nonetheless, no dedicated SjD-related fatigue data has emerged yet. Smoking was found to be significantly correlated with fatigue [62], while smoking cessation studies have reported mixed outcomes [63, 64], again with non SjD-related fatigue data. Although emerging data [65] show protective effects of alcohol on autoimmune diseases (AD) at low–moderate alcohol consumption, SjD-related fatigue has not yet been studied in this context. The exposure of women to silicone was found to be associated with a higher risk of having SjD and with the potential to exacerbate systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in susceptible individuals, including fatigue symptoms [66]; however, data on SjD-related fatigue is still lacking.

Triggering Factors

Acute Disease Flare

Acute disease flare is putatively one of the main triggering factors for SjD-related fatigue, and inflammation is the most studied underlying mechanism. With the production of type I IFN and other pro-inflammatory cytokines induced by sickness behavior, energy expenditure is reserved for the immune system—the origin of fatigue. This process is aimed at preventing further disease spread [67], and sickness behavior is considered to be a survival-enhancing strategy and is highly conserved during evolution [68].

Human studies [69, 70] have shown that IL-1β rapidly increases to activate the immune system confronted with tissue injury or infection, with fatigue involved as an important component [68]. IL-1 blockers may improve fatigue, as has been reported in studies on rheumatoid arthritis (RA), type 2 diabetes, and SjD [71–73]. In the SjD study, 26 SjD patients were assigned to randomly receive either anakinra (an IL-1 receptor antagonist [IL-1Ra]) or placebo for 4 weeks. Of the 12 treated patients, six reported a 50% reduction in the f-VAS score compared to one out of the 13 control patients (p = 0.03), although no significant difference was achieved between baseline and after treatment in either group. Another human study [74] involving 48 SjD patients strengthened the correlation between IL-1Ra and SjD-related fatigue, with the authors reporting that depression, pain, and IL-1Ra were significantly associated with fatigue in simple univariate linear regression; putting f-VAS score as the dependent variable, these authors again reported that depression, pain, and IL-1Ra were significant independent variables (R2 = 0.37; p < 0.001). Intriguingly, in a study comparing 159 patients with SjD with 28 healthy non-fatigued controls, four pro-inflammatory cytokines were inversely correlated to the Profile of Fatigue Questionnaire: interferon-gamma inducible protein (IP-10) (p = 0.019), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (p = 0.046), lymphotoxin-alpha (LT-α) (p = 0.034), and IFN-gamma (IFN-γ) (p = 0.022) [75]. These results were subsequently confirmed by the same team in an additional cohort of 120 patients with SjD and 30 age-matched healthy non-fatigued controls [76]. Only two of these cytokines, TNF-α (p = 0.021) and LT-α ( p = 0.043), were inversely related to patient-reported levels of fatigue [76]. The findings of these two studies did not support the simple concept of higher levels of inflammation leading to worse fatigue. The postulated reasons could be that an inappropriate or exaggerated anti-inflammatory response by persistent immune challenge turns adaptive behavioral response and sickness behavior into a persistent and pathological response causing chronic fatigue [76] or the multifaceted etiology of SjD-related fatigue, including the biomedical (Fig. 2) and psychosocial aspects discussed in this review. Infections and Vaccinations

Historically, SjD has been associated with viral infections [77], such as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), human T-lymphotropic virus (HTLV-1), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), amonmg others. There is strong evidence showing that EBV infection causes frailty and fatigue. A recent Mendelian randomization study [78] in a European population concluded that there is a causal link between EBV infection and frailty and fatigue, due to chronic inflammation.

Fatigue is a frequently reported adverse event of vaccines, especially in the immunocompromised patients with autoimmune diseases who have received COVID-19 vaccine [79–82]. Also, post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) and post-COVID-19 syndrome (PCS) have been observed (active n = 982; control n = 576) with non-specific and dominated patterns of reported symptoms such as fatigue, exercise intolerance, and cognitive complaints [83], although SjD-related fatigue has not yet been specifically studied in this context. Nonetheless, post-vaccination symptoms are usually transient, and they should not become an obstacle to patients with autoimmune diseases accepting vaccination [84, 85]. Future studies should focus on specific post-vaccination side-effects in patients with SjD, with fatigue as primary outcomes.

Maintaining Factors

Sleep Disturbances

Patients with SjD have been shown to have a significantly higher level of sleep deficit and difficulty in falling asleep [86]. In this same study, fatigue was a significantly more frequent complaint in patients with SjD. The relationship between SjD-related fatigue and sleep could be a bidirectional one, with sleep disturbance leading to fatigue and vice versa [87, 88]. Inflammation has been shown to affect the synthesis of neuroendocrine mediators such as monoamines, melatonin, prolactin, and growth hormone, all of which affect sleep [89]. Although this study focused on general inflammation rather than patients with SjD or autoimmunity, the changes in neuroendocrine mediators due to either kind of inflammation could be similar, in which case further explorations are warranted. Sleep quality is also related to the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and cortisol production, the latter being circulated according to circadian pattern to regulate sleep [90]. The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) is part of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). In one study, sleep deficit was found to be prevalent among patients with SjD and to be correlated with a high symptom burden, orthostatic symptoms, and fatigue [91]; these results were interpreted as SNS disorders.

Fibromyalgia

Sjögren’s disease shares common symptoms with fibromyalgia (FM), such as fatigue and pain [92]. Approximately 12–31% of patients with SjD have FM as comorbidity [93–96]. A 2016 Greek study [92] reported that FM, along with depression and neuroticism, played a major role in SjD-related fatigue, based on assessment of 106 patients with SjD, 32 patients with fatigue (25% with associated FM), and 74 patients without fatigue (only 4.1% with associated FM; p = 0.003). Multivariate analysis revealed FM as an independent contributor to fatigue in patients with SjD, along with neuroticism and depression (odds ratio [OR] 5.5, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.1–27.7; OR 6.9, 95% CI 1.7–28.0; OR 3.0, 95% CI 0.8–11.0, respectively) [92]. Similar results were elicited in a Spanish study [97] 1 year later in which patients with SjD with FM showed significantly higher levels of fatigue than those without FM, as well as significantly higher EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Patient Reported Index (ESSPRI) and EULAR Sjögren's Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI) scores, indicating that patients with SjD with FM could develop more severe systemic clinical symptoms than those without and, consequently, indication that FM is a contributor to SjD severity and SjD-related fatigue. This indication was strengthened by the results of a French study that enrolled 134 patients with SjD [98], which reported that FM criteria-positive (American College of Rheumatology [ACR] 2016 criteria) patients with SjD had higher ESSPRI scores, than FM criteria-negative ones, although the study mainly focused on comparing the characteristics of the different criteria used to assess FM in patients with SjD. An earlier Korean study [95] (n = 100) also reported that a higher ESSPRI score was observed in patients with SjD with FM (p < 0.0001), along with a significantly higher prevalence of FM in patients with SjD with moderate-to-severe depression than those with mild-to-none (OR 10.62; p = 0.0009). The emerging data relied on the fact that patients with SjD with FM had higher degrees of SjD symptoms, which indicates that FM could contribute to the exacerbation of SjD symptoms, including fatigue, accompanied by higher prevalence of depression. Nonetheless, the presence of fatigue in patients with SjD has been found not necessarily to be related to the presence of overall disease activity, specific autoantibodies, or laboratory markers [99–101]. This latter observation is also in line with those in RA and spondyloarthritis (SpA). FM is a contributing factor to poor response to rheumatic treatment, particularly in RA and SpA, and disease-modifying treatments are relatively ineffective in alleviating SjD-related FM [102, 103].

Here, we hypothesize that FM, which has been associated to psychopathological changes, particularly depression [104], could act as a maintaining factor of SjD-related fatigue and could contribute to the poor clinical outcomes of other rheumatic diseases when treatment consists of immune-targeting treatment alone. It illustrates the interplay between biomedical and psychosocial aspects of SjD-related fatigue, blurring the lines between disease activity and symptom expression. The overlapping symptomatic features of FM and SjD may partially account for the high co-occurrence of FM in SjD [105, 106], as well as for the often-reported dissociation between symptom severity and objective disease activity across different patient subgroups [107]. Together, these phenomena underscore the intricate and bidirectional interactions among biomedical and psychosocial systems in autoimmunity.

Psychosocial Aspects

Predisposing Factors

Childhood Experiences and Personality Traits

To explore the still debated importance of psychological and psychopathological factors (Fig. 3), adverse childhood experiences, and socio-demographic characteristics in the development of certain types of rheumatic disease, a research team in France conducted a study in 165 French women with FM, RA, SpA, and SjD [108]. All enrolled patients completed an inventory of personality traits, a psychopathology diagnosis questionnaire, a fatigue/pain questionnaire, and questions about adverse childhood experiences and socio-demographic characteristics, leading to the analysis of 20 psychological and social variables. Using random forest and logistic regression machine learning algorithms, the authors identified mistreatment during childhood (OR 18.92, 95% CI 1.61–4.52; p = 0.000), agreeableness (OR 6.11, 95% CI 0.61–3.09; p = 0.002), openness (OR 3.00, 95% CI 0.27–2.02; p = 0.014), and neuroticism (OR 3.78, 95% CI 0.50–2.26; p = 0.002) askey personality traits that were significantly associated with the diagnosis of FM compared to a diagnosis of inflammatory rheumatic disease (either RA, SpA, or SjD) [108]. As aforementioned, approximately 12–31% of patients with SjD have FM as comorbidity, which is higher than the prevalence of FM in the general population, suggesting common features between these conditions. In line with this percentage, in 2016, Karageorgas et al. [92] found neuroticism to be independently associated with SjD-related fatigue. High levels of neuroticism in patients with SjD induces an emotional instability and difficulties in social adaptation, and these have been associated with heightened sympathetic nervous system reactivity, leading to exaggerated and sustained physiological responses to external stressors [111]. Affected patients with SjD hypothetically tend to experience negative emotions and may therefore become more vulnerable and prone to maladaptive coping behavior, negative self-esteem, and depression [112], possibly leading to mental fatigue and disease exacerbation [113].

Stress, Negative Life Events, and Unsatisfactory Coping Strategies

Negative stressful life events were found to be more common prior to disease onset in patients with SjD compared to healthy controls [67], and have been more generally proposed as a risk factor for developing an autoimmune disease [114]. In a 2001 Swedish study [113], patients were asked to solve theoretical situations in which typical SjD-related stressors were presented. The study reported that the most significant stressor was mental fatigue, generally associated with depression, helplessness, and a tendency to isolation, all of which hypothetically have an underlying correlation with personality traits. The existence of social support was found to be of paramount importance for the development of adaptive coping strategies, and a good education level, satisfaction with family relationships, and positive childhood experiences were correlated to better clinical outcomes. These researchers also observed that a significantly increasing number of negative stressful life events were observed in the year before the onset of SjD in the study. In another study, more negative life events, including, among others, adverse close-family member relationships, were the greatest source of distress in patients with SjD along the entire life course [114], which can imply the persistence of unsatisfactory coping strategies in patients with SjD [115].

Triggering and Maintaining Factors

Depression

A large number of clinical studies and reviews have observed and demonstrated the strong connection between depression and SjD-related fatigue [16, 112, 116–120]. Chronic diseases as well as the systemic manifestations of SjD with chronic physical symptoms are risk factors for depression [121]. Also, depression was found to be closely related to both physical and mental SjD-related fatigue [116]. Depressed patients with SjD have been reported to show higher disease activity, were more often unemployed, had generally lower levels of education, and reported more severe pain than their counterparts without depression [122]. Patients with depression commonly experience anhedonia and become uninterested in taking part in activities, irrespective of the task or the amount of effort the task requires [10], which results in impaired physical activity and “activity avoidance”.

A German study [123] observed a strong correlation between depression and the activity of rheumatic diseases, including SjD. In the patient cohort with rheumatic diseases (including RA, SLE and SjD; total n = 445; SjD n = 135), depression (Beck Depression Inventory-II [BDI-II] score ≥ 14) was found in 36.3% (49/135) of patients with SjD, which was the highest rate in comparison to those with RA and SLE (21.9%, n = 36/164 and 28.8%, n = 42/146, respectively, p = 0.002). Also, the patients with depression exhibited a significantly shorter mean duration of illness (103 vs. 140 months; p < 0.001) and higher levels of fatigue (Global Fatigue Index [GFI], 32.8 vs. 20.8; p < 0.001), and they had greater functional impairment (Funktionsfragebogen Hannover [FFbH] score, 68.4% vs. 81.2%; p < 0.001) and lower levels of physical activity (1975 weekly MET-min vs. 2873 MET-min; p = 0.016). Based on these results, it can be speculated that depression could accelerate the development of disease and cause fatigue.

Cognitive Patterns—Learnt Helplessness, Negative Attribution Style, and Physical Activity Cognitions

Learnt helplessness refers to a perceived lack of control over the outcome (usually negative) of a situation. It has been linked to depression and negative attribution style, which is defined as the tendency of individuals to attribute negative events or outcomes to internal, stable, and global factors. This means that patients with learnt helplessness may blame themselves (internal), believe the cause is permanent or unchangeable (stable), and think it will affect many areas of their lives (global). This cognitive pattern can contribute to feelings of helplessness, pessimism, and low/negative self-esteem [124]. It hypothetically originates from a negative childhood experience, which may contribute to the formation of personality traits, leading to stressful, unsatisfactory coping strategies, as discussed earlier in this article. In one study, learnt helplessness, as measured by the 5-item helplessness subscale derived from the original 15 item "Rheumatology Attitudes Index", was reported to be correlated with depression and fatigue in patients with SjD [101].

Specifically, cognitions about physical activity can act as a barrier for being physically active. This notion was strengthened by the results of a study by Wouters et al. [125] in which 300 SjD patients were compared to 100 demographically matched people for assessment of fatigue (Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory [MFI-20]) and physical activity (The International Physical Activity Questionnaire-short form [IPAQ-short]), with the physical activity cognitions “activity avoidance” and “somatic focus” of the Tampa scale of kinesiophobia (TSK) assessed only in the SjD group. These authors reported that patients with SjD had higher scores on the MFI (p < 0.001) and lower scores on the moderate and vigorous intensity activity scales (p < = 0.001), with lower physical activity and higher activity avoidance associated with more severe fatigue in the SjD group. These results indicate that the lack of physical activity and activity avoidance cognition play a role as maintaining factors of SjD-related fatigue and thereby suggest that cognitive behavior therapy could be a beneficial approach in the treatment of SjD fatigue.

Negative Psychological Profiles

The application of psychological profile was first applied by detectives in cases of rape and homicide as an effective tool to guide interrogations of a suspect and the criminal investigation [126]. Creating a psychological profile involves gathering information from multiple sources and analyzing it through a lens of psychological theory. The key components include personality traits, behavioral patterns, cognitive abilities, motivations and goals, and background and life experiences. A hierarchical cluster analysis in a 2015 study [127] identified four psychological profiles encompassing six factors (social support, negative thinking, positive thinking, emotional expressivity, avoidance, and alexithymia) in patients with SjD; these four profiles were, subsequently labeled functional, alexithymic, self-reliant, and dysfunctional profiles. In this study, a total of 300 patients with SjD (93% female) completed all of the questionnaires assessing fatigue (MFI), physical activity cognitions (Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia [TAMPA-SK]), illness cognitions, cognitive regulation, emotion processing and regulation (Toronto Alexithymia Scale 20, Emotion Regulation Questionnaire [ERQ]; Berkeley Expressivity Questionnaire), coping strategies (Brief-Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced [Brief-COPE]), and social support. The authors reported that enrolled patients with SjD within the four psychological profiles had significantly higher fatigue levels than the control group, and that those with a dysfunctional or alexithymic profile reported more fatigue than those with a self-reliant profile. The patients with the self-reliant profile reported low scores of social support and emotional expressivity next to low scores of negative thinking and avoidance [127] . More reserved and solitary behavior in this profile, which indicates cognitive behavior maladjustment, reflects an introverted personality. Different studies may have used different names for the psychological profiles of similar connotations [128, 129], reflecting an overall adaptive coping strategy; one or two profiles that were apparently maladaptive were close to the dysfunctional and alexithymic ones. Therefore, based on the results of the study, psychological profiles are considered to act as maintaining factors contributing to SjD-related fatigue, and could be treated by psychological interventions.

Mechanisms Underlying Biomedical and Psychosocial Aspects

Cytokines in Peripheral Serum and Central Nervous System

Adaptive immunity is evidently involved in the pathogenesis of SjD based on observations of autoreactive B and T cells, with the production of the disease-related autoantibodies anti-Ro/SAA and anti-La/SSB, formation of ectopic lymphoid structures, and hypergammaglobulinemia. Innate immunity also plays a role, as determined by observations of high type I IFN system activation [130, 131]. IFN and other pro-inflammatory cytokines cause sickness behaviors, including fatigue [132]. Heat shock proteins (HSP) are also produced when the innate immune system is activated. Plasma concentrations of HSP90α were found to be significantly higher in patients with high fatigue [59]. However, high levels of these cytokines are not always necessarily associated with worse fatigue, as mentioned previously in this review, and a small proportion of patients do not experience fatigue despite having active SjD [133, 134]. Hence SjD-related inflammation in not self-sufficient alone to explain fatigue in SjD, in line with the notion that fatigue necessarily results from multiple factors acting at different levels.

IL-1 Family

A compelling theoretical framework for understanding fatigue in SjD and other infectious or autoimmune inflammatory disorders is the “sickness behavior” model. Within this context, fatigue is conceptualized as a conserved physiological adaptation to illness, characterized by symptoms such as lethargy, anhedonia, diminished motivation, hypoactivity, social withdrawal, and depressive states [132]. Evidence from both animal models and clinical studies support the involvement of the innate immune system in the pathophysiology of fatigue. Notably, IL-1 signaling has been identified as a critical mediator, with neuronal IL-1 receptors in the CNS playing a key role in transmitting sickness behavior signals [69, 132]. In humans, chronic immune activation in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases is often accompanied by increased IL-1β activity [70, 135]. IL-1β appears to serve as a terminal signaling molecule in fatigue pathways within the brain, influencing ion channel function, particularly potassium and calcium influx [136]. Peripheral IL-1β can cross the blood–brain barrier to affect central neuronal circuits, and is also synthesized locally within the brain by activated microglia [137] (Fig. 4b). While IL-1 signaling has been documented as one of the mechanisms underlying depression [111, 138], the activation of microglia was found in mice to lead to a prostaglandin-dependent reduction of the excitability of striatal neurons, subsequently causing a negative effect [139]. In addition, the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-36α has been best characterized in models of psoriasis [141], and over the past years, the pathological effects of the IL-36 family of cytokines have been extended to a variety of inflammatory diseases [142, 143]. IL-36 has been found to be significantly upregulated in patients with SjD experiencing fatigue when compared to their non-fatigued counterparts [29]. Dysregulation of IL-36α has also been implicated in a range of neurological, cognitive, and psychiatric conditions, suggesting a broader role for this cytokine in neuroimmune and neuropsychological dysfunction [24].

Interferon-gamma

Recent research has highlighted the involvement of type II INF, particularly IFN-γ, in a specific subgroup of patients with SjD who present with prominent symptoms, such as widespread pain, persistent fatigue, and depressive features [144]. IFN-γ is recognized as a potent inducer of the enzyme indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) across a variety of cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, tumor cells, macrophages derived from monocytes, mesenchymal stromal cells, and dendritic cells [144]. The enzymatic activity of IDO1 leads to the catabolism of tryptophan into metabolites that are collectively referred to as kynurenines (Fig. 4a). These kynurenine pathway metabolites are known to exert immunomodulatory effects, primarily by promoting regulatory T cell (TReg) responses and dampening excessive inflammatory activity [145]. It has been hypothesized that activation of the IDO1 pathway may contribute to clinical features, such as heightened pain sensitivity (hyperalgesia), chronic pain, and depressive symptoms. Supporting this hypothesis, elevated kynurenine-to-tryptophan ratios—indicative of enhanced IDO1 activity—have been observed in other chronic pain conditions, suggesting that this pathway may also underlie fatigue in SjD [146, 147]. The results of a more recent clinical study [148] have strengthened this hypothesis. In this study, 44 SjD patients defined as the “severe fatigue” group (ESSPRI fatigue ≥ 5) and 37 SjD patients (ESSPRI fatigue score < 5), along with 30 age-and-sex-matched healthy controls were compared. Serum tryptophan levels in the severe fatigue group were observed to be significantly lower while those of kynurenine were higher in the other groups, while serum IFN-γ, IDO1, and quinolinic acid levels were found to be mostly higher in the less fatigued group. The kynurenine-to-tryptophan ratios were distinctly higher in the severe fatigue group than in both the healthy controls and the less fatigued group (p < 0.001), and this ratio showed a strong degree of positive correlation (r = 0.624, p < 0.001) with severity of fatigue in SjD. Whether this immunoregulatory profile is directly linked to the severity of fatigue, acting via different pathways (e.g., by reducing the amount of substrate to produce serotonin [149]), or is an ineffective regulating mechanism (induced by IFN-γ signaling to contain its immunostimulatory effects), needs to be further investigated.

Activation of the Autonomic Afferent Fibers

With respect to biomedical triggering factors, such as disease flare, infections, and vaccination, previous studies on evidence-related mechanisms showed that peripheral inflammation led to general fatigue through autonomic afferent fibers (Fig. 4c). Visceral afferents, which monitor the condition of tissues of the body, are activated due to peripheral inflammation, forming the interoceptive stimuli [150]. The interoceptive system in primates comprises autonomic afferent fibers that project to lamina I neurons and the nucleus tractus solitarius, then relay in the parabrachial nucleus and ventromedial and mediodorsal nucleus of the primate thalamus, and finally terminate in the limbic sensory (insula) and motor (cingulate) cortices [151, 152], according to which the feeling of fatigue would take place in the insula, whereas the lack of self-motivated behavior would be dependent on the anterior cingulate cortex.

Dysregulation of the ANS

The dysregulation of ANS is a potential contributor to fatigue [153], but also linked to the development of psychological disturbances such as depression in patients with SjD [154, 155] (Fig. 4e). In one study [153], a British cohort of 317 patients with SjD was compared to the same number of age-and-sex-matched healthy controls in terms of symptoms of autonomic dysfunction using a validated instrument—the Composite Autonomic Symptom Scale (COMPASS). The authors reported that COMPASS scores were significantly higher in patients with SjD than in controls (median 35.5, interquartile range [IQR] 20.9–46.0] vs. median 14.8, IQR: 4.4–30.2; p < 0.0001), with a significantly higher percentage of patients with SjD having autonomic dysfunction (COMPASS score > 32.5) than the healthy controls (55% vs. 20%, respectively; p < 0.0001). It was also discovered that the COMPASS total score correlated independently with the ESSPRI and ESSDAI, which implies that dysregulation of ANS may contribute to the overall burden of symptoms and systemic disease activity. Another more recent small-scale Italian study [156] strengthened these results by comparing 17 patients with SjD with 16 healthy control, using supine and 75° head-up tilt: COMPASS scores (p < 0.0001) and all ESSPRI subdomains (fatigue, p = 0.005; pain, p = 0.0057; dryness, p < 0.0001) were higher in patients with SjD compared to the healthy controls. Other measures reflecting the autonomic nervous systems’ dysregulation were also evaluated, and significant differences were reported between patients with SjD and controls, including lower sequence of cardiac baroreflex sensitivity index, higher markers of cardiac vagal modulation (p = 0.021), lower sympatho-vagal balance (p = 0.007), and greater muscle sympathetic nerve activity (p = 0.038) [156]. In another study, depression was more severe in ANS-dysregulated patients with SjD (n = 324, COMPASS score > 32.5; total n = 627) assessed by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), compared to SjD patients whose COMPASS score was < 32.5 (p < 0.001) [118]. The authors of this study also reported that dryness (EULAR Sjögren's syndrome [EULAR-SS] score), fatigue (Profile of Fatigue and Discomfort–Somatic Fatigue [PROF-SF] score), pain (ESSPRI pain scale), orthostatic intolerance (Objective Grading System [OGS] score), quality of life (EQ-5 Dimension-Visual Analogue Scale [EQ-5D-VAS score]), and sleepiness (Epworth Sleepiness Scale [ESS] score) were more severe in ANS-dysregulated patients with SjD, which hypothetically indicates a shared pathophysiology between ANS, SjD-related fatigue, and SjD-related depression.

Interestingly, a case series report [157] showed dysautonomia preceded the diagnosis of SjD in patients by 2–10 years (5 out of 6 cases). The use of prednisone, hydroxychloroquine, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), rituximab, or their combinations partially improved symptoms of dysautonomia in these patients, with only one case reaching total remission.

Neuroendocrine Disorders—HPA and Serotonin

HPA Axis

Patients with SjD often exhibit elevated levels of neuroticism and reduced extraversion, a personality trait that aligns with the widely accepted stress-vulnerability framework in autoimmune disease research [158]. Individuals with low distress tolerance are particularly susceptible to immune dysregulation under conditions of chronic psychological stress. Prolonged or maladaptive stress responses activate both the HPA axis and the sympathetic–adrenal–medullary (SAM) system, contributing to inflammatory processes implicated in autoimmune pathogenesis [159].

Basal cortisol levels—regulated by the HPA axis—are considered biomarkers of chronic stress and are known to affect both cognitive function and emotional regulation [160]. HPA axis dysregulation—manifesting, for example, as elevated early-morning cortisol levels when they should be low—has been linked to persistent morning fatigue in patients with SjD [161]. Individuals showing high levels of neuroticism may also display impaired cell-mediated immunity, increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines, and blunted cortisol reactivity, all of which exacerbate both physical and psychological symptoms in SjD [162].

SjD-related fatigue may be a consequence of altered HPA function, as fatigue and lack of motivation are well-recognized features of hypoadrenalism (Fig. 4d). Lower basal adrenocorticotrophin and cortisol levels were reported in patients with SjD, in association with a blunted pituitary and adrenal response to corticotrophin-releasing hormone compared to their healthy counterparts [163], which suggests that the defect is at the pituitary or hypothalamic level. The underlying mechanisms behind this altered HPA function may be that pro-inflammatory cytokines stimulate the production of corticotropin-releasing hormone by the hypothalamus, and also of the adrenocorticotropic hormone by the pituitary gland, which subsequently increases cortisol production by the adrenal glands, as a negative feedback loop to control pro-inflammatory responses. However, with persistent inflammation, the response of the HPA axis might be blunted and ineffective [10].

Serotonin

In addition to the HPA axis, alterations in the serotonergic (5-hydroxytryptamine [5-HT]) system may also play a role in the pathogenesis of fatigue. Disruptions in monoamine neurotransmitters—including serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine—not only impair mood regulation but also disturb energy homeostasis and motivation, thereby contributing to the development of both fatigue and depressive symptoms [161].

Elevated tryptophan levels and their downstream effects have garnered attention due to their aforementioned modulation by pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, but also IL-2 and TNF-α), which upregulate IDO. IDO enzymatically degrades tryptophan along the kynurenine pathway, thereby reducing the amount of substrate available for serotonin synthesis (Fig. 4a). This immune-mediated serotonin depletion may help explain the high comorbidity of fatigue and depression in SjD, as central serotonin deficiency is a well-established feature of major depressive disorders [164]. A proposed integrative model posits a U-shaped association between synaptic serotonin concentrations and fatigue, suggesting that both excessively low and excessively high serotonin activity may be detrimental to energy regulation [165]. Although current evidence on the involvement of the serotonin system in SjD remains limited, the information currently available represents a promising area for future investigation given its potential to unify immune, endocrine, and neuropsychiatric aspects of disease pathology.

In summary, the predisposing, triggering, and maintaining factors of fatigue in SjD reveal a complex interplay between biomedical and psychosocial domains. Notably, several cross-domain mediators and pathways have emerged as potential therapeutic targets. However, the multifactorial and multidimensional nature of fatigue necessitates an approach that integrates physiological and psychosocial considerations, particularly within the neuro–endocrine–immune network, to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Therapeutic Landscape

Evaluation Tools and Stratification

The specific instruments to measured SjD-related fatigue include the Profile of Fatigue and Discomfort-Sicca Symptoms Inventory (PROFAD-SSI) [166], which has the advantage of being able to distinguish fatigue in physical and mental types, and the EULAR ESSPRI, which is quick and simple to administer and score, but does not capture the multidimensional nature of fatigue. Other non-disease specific multi-item instruments consist of Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy Scale–Fatigue (FACIT-Fatigue) [167], which has been used in many rheumatologic conditions with a range of fatigue concepts, Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) [168], more used in SLE, Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI) [169], Fatigue Impact Scale (FIS) [170], Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFS) [171], or Medical Outcomes Study Short-Form (SF-36 VT) [172]. Additionally, beyond conventional fatigue scales, functional performance measurements commonly employed in physical rehabilitation—as well as broader quality-of-life assessments—may be appropriately incorporated into this field, particularly in studies and observational analyses involving exercise-based or other non-pharmacological interventions.

Interestingly, fatigue is currently a key component in recent attempts to classify and clusterize patients with SjD, with the aim to decipher the heterogenous pathophysiology of the different subgroups of patients with SjD. In this context, it was observed that patients with SjD with different sorts and severity of symptoms, including fatigue level, had significantly different disease characteristics and clinical responses to treatments [133]. In the British cohort (UK Primary Sjögren's Syndrome Registry [UKPSSR], n = 608), patients with SjD were classified based solely on their reported SjD symptoms—pain, fatigue, dryness, anxiety, and depression—into four subgroups: low symptom burden, high symptom burden, dryness dominant with fatigue, and pain dominant with fatigue. Interestingly, despite using only the patients’ reported outcomes to clusterize the cohort, many peripheral cytokines and SjD-specific autoantibodies were significantly different between the clusters, and the responses to treatments were also different. Recently, two multi-dimensional SjD-dedicated scores, namely CRESS (Composite of Relevant Endpoints for Sjögren's Syndrome) and STAR (Sjögren’s Tool for Assessing Response) [173, 174], were built to improve the evaluation of novel therapeutics entering clinical trials in SjD, and they both include evaluation of fatigue (via the ESSPRI scale).

Pharmacological Interventions

Conventional Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs

Evidence on the efficacy of hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) remains mixed. In the JOQUER trial [175] (n = 120), which used fatigue as one of the main outcomes, no significant difference was demonstrated between HCQ and placebo. However, subgroup analyses from the UKPSSR cohort suggested potential benefits with HCQ in the high symptom burden (HSB) subgroup (reduction in ESSDAI and ESSPRI scores) [133]. Meta-analyses indicate short-term relief in subjective fatigue and arthralgia in early-stage SjD, although long-term efficacy data are lacking [176]. A recent randomized study (n = 86) [177] assessed the efficacy of methotrexate (MTX) and HCQ in improving fatigue symptoms in patients with SjD. Patients were treated with either MTX (n = 27), HCQ (n = 29), or a combination of MTX + HCQ (n = 30) for 6 months. The results showed that MTX monotherapy or MTX combined with HCQ significantly improved fatigue scores (FSS, FACIT-F; p < 0.01) at 6 months compared to HCQ group. The MTX group showed highest relief with respect to SjD fatigue, outperforming the HCQ and MTX + HCQ group.

Biological/target synthetic dMARDs

Biologic therapies (or biologics) represent a promising avenue in the management of SjD-related fatigue, particularly through modulation of B-cell activity and cytokine-driven inflammation [178]. B-cell targeted agents aim to reduce aberrant B-cell activation and autoantibody production, while cytokine-directed strategies seek to interrupt pro-inflammatory signaling cascades implicated in SjD. Although clinical trials have yielded mixed results, emerging evidence suggest that certain patient subgroups may experience meaningful benefit, underscoring the need for precision medicine approaches in this heterogeneous disease.

Rituximab (RTX), a CD20 monoclonal antibody, has been tested in multiple trials in SjD. One early randomized double-blind placebo-controlled study [179] (n = 17) reported significant improvement in fatigue VAS scores as the primary outcome. Similarly, another early randomized controlled trial (RCT) [180] (total n = 30, 20 with rituximab, 10 with placebo), with a 48-week follow-up, also reported significant improvement in Multidimensional Fatigue Index (MFI) scores as the secondary endpoint, compared to placebo at weeks 12 and 24, but without significant differences for the other time points. In the French TEARS trial (n = 120) [181], with a 24-week follow-up, a higher percentage of patients in the RTX group achieved a ≥ 30 mm improvement in VAS fatigue score at week 6 and 16 compared to placebo (34.7% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.001; 27.2% vs. 8.9%, p = 0.012, respectively), and fatigue VAS was significantly improved in the RTX group from baseline to weeks 6, 16, and 24. However, in the British TRACTISS trial [182] (n = 133), the primary endpoint (30% reduction in either fatigue or oral dryness at week 48) was not met. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis, including four RCTs [179, 181–183] with a total of 294 SjD-patients included overall, showed no significant difference between the RTX group and the control group in terms of VAS fatigue score at week 24. Nonetheless, subgroup analysis of the UKPSSR cohort [133] revealed an improvement with RTX in the dryness–fatigue dominant subgroup, compared to the other three subgroups, supporting the possible existence of potential precision medicine approach for future clinical management, trial design, and therapeutic development.

Belimumab, a BAFF inhibitor, was evaluated in a 1-year open-label study [184] that enrolled 30 patients with SjD who fulfilled American European Consensus group (AECG) criteria [185]. While the reduction in mean fatigue VAS score of the 30 patients was not statistically significant between baseline and week 28 (6.9 ± 1.8 to 6.0 ± 2.2; p = 0.0606), 23% of patients (7 out of 30) reported ≥ 30% improvement in fatigue VAS score.

Epratuzumab, a humanized anti-CD22 antibody, was evaluated in an open-labeled phase I/II study, with 40% of the 16 patients with SjD who received epratuzumab reporting at least 20% improvement in fatigue VAS score. Also, the level of improvement was significantly different compared to baseline at week 18 and week 32 [186].

Ianalumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the BAFF receptor, showed promising results in a single-dose trial in patients with SjD (total n = 27: 3 mg/kg, n = 6; 10 mg/kg, n = 12, and placebo, n = 9). Patients with SjD who had received the 10 mg/kg dose had sustained improvement of MFI scores compared to those receiving the placebo until week 24; this improvement continued until the end of the study for the MFI domains of general fatigue, physical fatigue, and reduced activity. In comparison, those who had received the 3 mg/kg dose only had transient response with the scores returning to baseline at week 24 [187]. However, a more recent study [188] with a larger sample size (n = 190) did not report differences in FACIT-F scores from baseline to week 24 between patients with SjD receiving 300 mg ianalumab every 4 weeks for 24 weeks and patients with SjD receiving placebo (26.7 ± 11.2 to 35.3 ± 10.6; 24.0 ± 9.7 to 33.0 ± 10.6, respectively; p = 0.87), although the primary endpoint was met: ESSDAI score was significantly decreased in all the ianalumab groups (5 mg, 50 mg, 300 mg every 4 weeks for 24 weeks) with the maximal change in ESSDAI score observed in the 300 mg group.

Two RCTs using infliximab and etanercept showed no significant improvement in fatigue in SjD patients [189, 190], likely due to the non-central role of TNF in SjD pathogenesis [191].

An IL-1Ra, anakinra, was evaluated in a phase II trial (n = 26) [73]. The authors reported ≥ 50% fatigue VAS reduction in 50% of the treated patients; however the Fatigue VAS score and FSS score were not different (p = 0.163) when compared to placebo, possibly due to the limited sample size of the patient cohort, warranting larger trials. Meanwhile, anakinra could be interesting in dry eye syndrome, as evidenced in experimental models [192].

RSLV-132, a ribonuclease targeting pro-inflammatory circulating RNAs, demonstrated superior fatigue improvement in a phase II RCT (n = 30) compared to placebo (ESSPRI, FACIT-F, and PROF; p < 0.05) [193]. This RNA-centric approach shows promise but requires validation in larger cohorts.

Other ongoing novel targeted approaches include nipocalimab [194], an anti-neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) monoclonal antibody; dazodalibep [195], a CD40 ligand antagonist; remibrutinib [196], a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) [197]; and tofacitinib [198, 199] and baricitinib [200], both Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi). All of these molecules have shown preliminary, potential but limited efficacy in SjD, without emphasis on fatigue as yet. We refer to a recent review [178] on the pathophysiology and pharmacological treatment of SjD for readers who wish to acquire more specific information.

Other Agents

The use of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) for managing fatigue in SjD is not recommended due to a lack of consistent therapeutic efficacy [201].

Low-dose naltrexone (LDN), a compound structurally and functionally analogous to the opioid antagonist naloxone yet with a prolonged half-life and enhanced oral bioavailability, is being repurposed as an analgesic and anti-inflammatory agent, despite its conventional use in treating opioid addiction [202, 203]. Case reports on the use of low-dose naltrexone (LDN) [204, 205] describe rapid fatigue relief and reduced inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]) with LDN (4.5 mg/day), potentially via modulation of the Toll-like Receptor (TLR) pathway [203, 206]. Prospective studies are needed to correctly evaluate efficacy and safety.

The previously discussed analysis [22] that reported 27 DEGs in patients with SjD with fatigue features utilized bioinformatics to analyze transcriptomic data from the GEO dataset GSE66795, which included patients with SjD with varying fatigue levels and healthy controls. Key methods included differential expression analysis (using GEO2R) to identify common and fatigue-specific genes, functional enrichment analysis (Gene Ontology [GO] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG] resource) to explore biological pathways, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction (via the STRING and Cytoscape bioinformatics tools) to identify hub genes, transcription factor–gene interaction analysis (using the NetworkAnalyst visual analytics platform), and drug–gene interaction screening (via Enrichr web-based tool and DSigDB database) to pinpoint potential therapeutic compounds. As a result, this study revealed that in addition to chloroquine and testosterone, several compounds—including chlorophyllin, the sulfonylurea hypoglycemic agent acetohexamide, and the antihistamine terfenadine—may have potential fatigue-alleviating effects in SjD. However, chloroquine and testosterone lack robust clinical support and are therefore not strongly endorsed. Acetohexamide has been withdrawn from the USA due to its high risk of severe hypoglycemia, and terfenadine is unsuitable for long-term therapy due to its antihistamine properties and resulting depressive effects on the CNS, leaving chlorophyllin holding some promise for reducing SjD-related fatigue. Interestingly, barley contains approximately 507 to 662 mg chlorophyll per 100 g barley grass, which has been used as herbal medicine in China and been applied for boosting energy and immunity [207], possibly due to its numerous functional components including lutonarin (7-O-GIO) and saponarin (7-O-GIV) with antioxidant potential (3.7 and 2.1 mg/100 g, respectively) [208]. Furthermore, an early observational study [209] on a mostly raw vegetarian diet including dehydrated barley grass juice product to treat FM (n = 20) reported significant improvements in all of the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ) sub-scales from 0 to 7 months, including the fatigue dimension. More trials are warranted to evaluate this kind of herb on SjD-related fatigue relief.

Non-pharmacological Interventions

Vagus Nerve Stimulation and Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation

Recent research has explored the potential of non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS) as a novel immunomodulatory and neuromodulatory intervention aimed at ameliorating fatigue and associated symptoms in patients with SjD. Two British clinical trials by Tarn et al., both investigating gammaCore, a handheld, non-invasive vagus nerve stimulator (electroCore, Rockaway, NJ, USA) that delivers transcutaneous cervical nVNS, offered promising insights into the clinical efficacy of nVNS in improving SjD-related fatigue. The first study [210] (n = 15, all female patients) reported a significant reduction in physical fatigue (using the PRO-F scale) across the three time points—baseline, day 7, and day 28 (p < 0.0003 and 0.02, respectively), while trends of improvement were also observed in the Abnormal Fatigue VAS and ESSPRI fatigue subscale. Meanwhile, twelve out of 15 patients reported an improvement in their fatigue scores, with seven reporting a ≥ 30% reduction within 28 days. These early results were strengthened by a second, larger RCT [211] (n = 20 in the active nVNS groups and n = 20 in the sham nVNS group) with a longer period of intervention (twice daily for 54 days). PRO-F-physical, PRO-F-mental, and Fatigue-VAS (fVAS) scores were significantly reduced at day 56 in the active group only (p = 0.02, 0.02, and 0.04, respectively). Meanwhile, backwards digit span (assessed using the Cognitive Failure Questionnaire [CFQ]) was also improved in the active nVNS group compared to the sham one (p = 0.03), and a negative correlation of frontal region alpha reactivity and fatigue scores was identified in the active nVNS group (p < 0.01). These results suggest a dual action of nVNS on neurocognitive function and immune regulation, and highlight a complex but targeted mechanism involving the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Future large-scale trials with extended follow-up are essential to confirm the durability of response and to further elucidate the neuroimmune circuitry involved.

A double-blind, sham-controlled pilot study [212] investigated the effects of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) on fatigue in 36 women with SjD. Participants received five daily sessions of active or sham tDCS (2 mA, 20 min). The active group showed a significant reduction in fatigue, as measured by the FSS and ESSPRI ( mean group difference − 0.85; 95% CI – 1.57 to – 0.13, effect sizes 0.80 vs. mean group difference – 1.40, 95 % CI – 2.33 to – 0.48, effect sizes 1.04, respectively). While no significant group differences were found in mood, sleep, or anxiety, within-group analysis in the active arm showed small improvements in depression and sleep. Pain and dryness also improved but did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the limited power of the study. No serious adverse events occurred, supporting the safety and feasibility of tDCS. These findings suggest that tDCS may represent another safe, non-pharmacological option to alleviate fatigue in SjD. Larger and longer studies are needed to confirm efficacy and explore combinatory strategies with interventions like exercise.

Exercise

Exercise interventions have emerged as a cornerstone of fatigue management regardless of its etiology. A systematic review and meta-analysis [14] identified significant benefits of exercise in reducing SjD-related fatigue and pain and improving quality of life and aerobic capacity. Notably, resistance exercise was particularly effective across multiple outcomes, including disease activity, with strong statistical significance. A separate RCT [213] further confirmed that resistance training in patients with SjD over 16 weeks significantly improved fatigue, emotional well-being, functional capacity, and patient-perceived disease activity, with excellent tolerability and safety. Aerobic exercise also demonstrated significant efficacy in patients with SjD. A supervised walking program [214] was able to significantly improve VO2max and fatigue (FACIT-F) in a cohort of women (total n = 45, active n = 23, control n = 22) with SjD compared to controls (p = 0.016, and p = 0.030, respectively). Improvements in depression and patients’ global assessments were also observed, without any reported disease flare or adverse event. Another study with a small sample size (total n = 21, active n = 11, control n = 10) utilizing Nordic walking for 12 weeks [215] found significant improvements in aerobic capacity (VO2max), fatigue (fVAS), ratings of perceived exertion, and depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS]) compared to controls (p = 0.03, p = 0.03, p = 0.02, respectively), reinforcing the interest of moderate-to-high intensity aerobic exercise as a therapeutic modality. These findings are consistent with broader recommendations from the 2023 EULAR guidelines, which underscores physical activity as an effective and safe intervention for fatigue in various inflammatory rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases [216]. Nonetheless, heterogeneity in disease and small sample sizes in current studies emphasize the need for further larger trials in SjD to establish long-term efficacy and optimize intervention protocols in stratified patients, as older, fragile, or disabled patients are unable to benefit from exercises.

Cognitive-Behavioral Interventions

As previously noted, physical activity-related cognitions such as “activity avoidance” and “somatic focus” have also been identified as significant psychological contributors to SjD-related fatigue [125], and low physical activity levels combined with high avoidance cognitions have been found to be strongly associated with severe general and physical fatigue. In this same study, somatic focus, was specifically/only linked to mental fatigue. The existence of activity avoidance and somatic focus highlights the need for cognitive-behavioral interventions [217], and acceptance-based therapies, including mindfulness training [218–220], could teach SjD patients to experience, allow, and accept symptoms and to focus their attention on the thoughts and actions that are beneficial to themselves.

Traditional Oriental Medicine

Systematic reviews suggest that yoga can improve fatigue, physical function, and psychological well-being in rheumatic conditions [221, 222], while tai-chi has been shown to enhance aerobic capacity, reduce fatigue, and maintain safety in patients with RA [223, 224]. Moreover, acupuncture has demonstrated immunomodulatory effects in SjD, including the regulation of IL-17 and TNF-α levels, as well as the production of aquaporins [225]. Although fatigue has not always been the primary endpoint of these studies, these findings indicate its potential as an adjunctive option. Interestingly, two recent RCTs on acupuncture in SjD demonstrated contrary results. The earlier study [15] with 120 patients with SjD reported no significant differences between the active acupuncture and sham acupuncture groups, in either primary or secondary endpoints, possibly due to the underlying efficacy of sham acupuncture [226–228], non-stratification recruitment, and heterogeneity of the enrolled patients with SjD. The more recent study [229] with 46 patients with SjD randomized (27 out of 46 who completed the study) and similar but fewer acupoints selected, reported significant differences in total ESSPRI and ESSPRI dryness scores in the acupuncture group compared to the control group. Also, the acupuncture group exhibited improvement over time in the ESSPRI fatigue domain, ESSPRI pain domain, and in unstimulated saliva flow. Acupuncture as a synergistic approach to improve symptoms of SjD, including fatigue, requires more evidence-based trials, with proper stratification of the participants and randomization methodology.

In summary, structured non-pharmacological strategies have shown interesting benefits for fatigue and related outcomes in SjD. The benefits of such interventions were also demonstrated in studies dedicated to other disorders. Exercise was confirmed to be beneficial in chronic fatigue syndrome in a Cochrane systematic review [230]. Meanwhile, cognitive-behavioral intervention was observed to provide significant improvement in alleviating fatigue in idiopathic chronic fatigue (n = 100) [231]. Moreover, in a clinical study, cognitive-behavioral therapy also had a favorable influence in the physical functioning of FM patients when included in a standard medical regimen of FM [232]. Additional approaches, such as yoga, tai-chi, and acupuncture, highlight translational potential for SjD and merit targeted trials, as fatigue has seldom been the primary endpoint in the existing studies. Overall, these interventions are well tolerated, widely applicable when individualized, and can complement pharmacological therapies with possible synergistic effects, thus offering a more comprehensive framework for addressing both the biomedical and psychosocial aspects of fatigue in SjD.

Conclusion and Future Directions

Fatigue in SjD displays a distinctly dual nature, encompassing both biomedical and psychosocial dimensions. As a result, interventions that target only one domain often lead to limited clinical improvement, highlighting a persistent therapeutic challenge. To better conceptualize this complexity, we believe that the framework of Persistent Somatic Symptoms (PSS) is an interesting concept for clinicians, as it was derived from observations across various chronic diseases in which symptoms persist despite biomedical parameters being under control [11, 12]. This conceptual lens also highlights the need for treatment strategies that can simultaneously engage both domains rather than addressing them in isolation.

Moving beyond biomarker-specific therapies, integrative strategies that address both pharmacological and non-pharmacological dimensions appear to offer the greatest benefit. In addition, empirically supported mind–body practices such as yoga, tai chi, and acupuncture are increasingly recognized as adjunctive approaches that may help disrupt the self-reinforcing cycle linking fatigue with comorbid symptoms, including sleep disturbance, chronic pain, sicca exacerbations, and mood disorders. Together, these modalities—aligned with the PSS framework—illustrate the potential of integrative care to optimize fatigue management in SjD.

Despite these promising directions, significant gaps remain to be addressed in future research. Three major challenges emerge. First, there is a need for high-resolution mechanistic analysis to deconstruct the dual nature of fatigue and identify its fundamental “core code”—a task requiring sustained, interdisciplinary collaboration. Second, it is essential to quantify the relative contributions of biomedical versus psychosocial influences in individual patients with SjD-related fatigue, although this task is complicated by their dynamic and context-dependent interactions. Third, existing integrative approaches—particularly non-pharmacological therapies—should be further refined in terms of timing, personalization, and delivery strategy. Given their generally favorable tolerance profiles, these interventions may be combined with targeted pharmacological therapies on a case-to-case basis. By addressing both axes simultaneously, such strategies may more effectively meet the expectations of patients and clinicians in real-world fatigue management [13].
