Abstract
(First-order) transductions are a basic notion capturing graph modifications that can be described in first-order logic. In this work, we propose an efficient algorithmic method to approximately reverse the application of a transduction, assuming the source graph is sparse. Precisely, for any graph class 𝒞 \mathscr{C} that has structurally bounded expansion (i.e., can be transduced from a class of bounded expansion), we give an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 4 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{4}) -time algorithm that given a graph G ∈ 𝒞 G\in\mathscr{C} , computes a vertex-colored graph H H such that G G can be recovered from H H using a first-order interpretation and H H belongs to a graph class 𝒟 \mathscr{D} of bounded expansion. This answers an open problem raised by Gajarský et al. [ACM TOCL, ’20]. In fact, for our procedure to work we only need to assume that 𝒞 \mathscr{C} is monadically stable (i.e., does not transduce the class of all half-graphs) and has inherently linear neighborhood complexity (i.e., the neighborhood complexity is linear in all graph classes transducible from 𝒞 \mathscr{C} ). This renders the conclusion that the graph classes satisfying these two properties coincide with classes of structurally bounded expansion.

Our contribution.

We answer the question of Gajarský et al.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a monadically stable graph class with inherently linear neighborhood complexity.
Then there exists a graph class
\mathscr{D}
of bounded expansion transducible from
\mathscr{C}

G\in\mathscr{C}

n

computes in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
a colored graph
H\in\mathscr{D}
such that
G=I(H)
.

Let us explain the so-far undefined terms used in

1

\mathscr{C}
is

\mathscr{C}
does not transduce the class of all half-graphs. (A

are adjacent iff

\mathscr{C}

if there exists a constant

G\in\mathscr{C}
and a nonempty vertex subset

\mathscr{C}

has linear neighborhood complexity. It follows from the results of Pilipczuk, Siebertz, and Toruńczyk

31

that every class of structurally bounded expansion has inherently linear neighborhood complexity. Thus, the prerequisites of

hold for every class of structurally bounded expansion, but the result implies more: Since the conclusion entails that the original class
\mathscr{C}
is transducible from a bounded expansion class

, in fact the prerequisites — monadic stability and inherently linear neighborhood complexity — are

to having structurally bounded expansion. That is,

The following conditions are equivalent for a graph class

•

\mathscr{C}

•

•

Here, merge-width is a family of graph parameters introduced recently by Dreier and Toruńczyk in
[

Technical overview

For technical reasons it will be convenient to work with bipartite graphs.
This comes at no loss of generality, since given any graph
G
, one can compute a bipartite graph
G^{\prime}
such that if we can efficiently sparsify
G^{\prime}
, then we can also sparsify the original graph
G
. The construction of
G^{\prime}
from
G
essentially boils down to copying every vertex and, for a pair of adjacent vertices
u,v
, making the first copy of
u
adjacent to the second copy of
v
and vice versa.
Consequently, for the rest of the section we will work with a monadically stable class
\mathscr{C}
of bipartite graphs of inherently linear neighborhood complexity. Moreover, we can assume that
\mathscr{C}
is hereditary and closed under bipartite complements, as both properties remain preserved if we close
\mathscr{C}
under taking induced subgraphs and bipartite complements.

Description of the outcome.

Our goal is to efficiently compute, for any
G\in\mathscr{C}
, a sparse graph
H
such that
G=I(H)
for some fixed interpretation
I
depending only on
\mathscr{C}
. The graph
H
that we will compute will have a special form, consisting of two parts: it will contain all the vertices of the original graph
G
, but without any edges connecting them, and a rooted tree
T
to which the vertices of
G
will be suitably connected.
Crucially, the tree
T
will be of bounded height (by a bound depending only on
\mathscr{C}
).
In what follows we will refer to the vertices of
T
as
nodes
.
We will show that each
v\in V(G)
is adjacent only to a bounded number of nodes from
T
(by a bound
d
depending only on
\mathscr{C}
). This will guarantee that
H
is sparse — in particular, it is
d
-degenerate (i.e. every subgraph has a vertex of degree at most
d
). Consequently, the graph class
\mathscr{D}
consisting of all graphs
H
computed by our sparsification procedure, is
d
-degenerate. Being degenerate is a notion of sparseness that is considerably weaker than being of bounded expansion; however, we will be able to show that
\mathscr{D}
in fact has bounded expansion.

G
from
H
.

We now describe how one can recover the original graph
G
from
H
. The construction of

and

will guarantee that if

are any two vertices of
G
in
H
, then there always exists a node

such that both
u
and

are adjacent to

, but no child

of

has this property. To determine whether

and

are adjacent in

, we simply count the number of nodes on the path from

to the root of

. If this number is even, then they are adjacent, and otherwise they are non-adjacent (see also

). This property, together with the fact that

is of bounded height, allow us to recover the adjacency of

from

by a first-order formula. We remark that while for any

there may exist several distinct nodes of

with the property described above, the construction of

Construction of

and

.

The key ingredient behind the construction of

and

is the following simple consequence of our main technical statement,
Lemma

.

Given any
G=(A,B,E)\in\mathscr{C}
without isolated vertices, we can efficiently compute a collection of induced subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
of
G
(here

is not bounded) satisfying the following conditions.

1.

Each

is in at least one and in at most

graphs from
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
, where

is an integer that depends only on
\mathscr{C}
.

2.

Every edge of

is contained in at least one
G_{i}
.

3.

Depending on the direction we apply
Lemma

12
, one of the following will be enforced:

(a)

All graphs
G_{i}
contain a vertex from
A\cap V(G_{i})
that is adjacent to all vertices from
B\cap V(G_{i})
.

(b)

All graphs
G_{i}
contain a vertex from
B\cap V(G_{i})
that is adjacent to all vertices from
A\cap V(G_{i})
.

We will refer to this collection of induced subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
of
G
as a
left branching
or a

of
G
, depending on whether we enforce (
3a
) or (
3b
).

From
Lemma

12
presented earlier,
one can obtain a left branching of

by setting

m\coloneqq|\mathcal{F}|
,

G_{i}\coloneqq G[N(P_{i}),P_{i}]
,
and choosing the dominating vertex of (
3a
) to be
\ell_{i}
.
To obtain a right branching, we simply exchange the roles of
A
and
B
.

Let us now describe the tree
T
.
As part of the construction, each node

will be assigned a graph
G(p)
that is either an induced subgraph of
G
(if
p
is at even depth),
or the bipartite complement of an induced subgraph of
G
(if
p
is at odd depth).
To construct

, we start with creating a root node

and we set
G(r)\coloneqq G
.
Then we remove all isolated vertices from
G(r)
and compute a left branching
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
of the resulting graph.
For each branch
G_{i}
, we create a new child
p_{i}
of
r
, and set
G(p_{i})
to be the bipartite complement of
G_{i}
.
We then recurse into each child
p_{i}
of
r
, and proceed analogously for
G(p_{i})
, again removing all isolated vertices of each
G(p_{i})
, but this time using a
right
branching of the resulting graph. For each branch, we again create a node and again assign the bipartite complement of the branch to this child. To summarize, the general construction looks as follows: For any node
p
and graph
G(p)
, we remove from
G(p)
all isolated vertices (if there are any) and compute a left or right branching
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
of
G(p)
, depending on whether
p
is at even or odd depth. Then for each branch
G_{i}
we create a new child
p_{i}
of
p
, and set
G(p_{i})
to be the bipartite complement of
G_{i}
and proceed recursively into each
p_{i}
. The recursion stops when
G(p)
is an edgeless graph.

For any
G\in\mathscr{C}
, we define the
ladder index
of
G
as the maximum order of a half-graph that is contained in
G
as an induced subgraph. Since
\mathscr{C}
is monadically stable, there is a finite bound
t
, depending only on
\mathscr{C}
, on the ladder index of graphs from
\mathscr{C}
. The ladder index is a convenient parameter to measure the progress of our algorithm: the alternation between induced subgraphs of
G
and complements of induced subgraphs of
G
in our choice of

, as well as between using left branchings and right branchings, leads to the decrease of the ladder index in every second step of the construction. To be more precise – it is not difficult to show that if
p
and
p^{\prime\prime}
are nodes of
T
such that
p
is the grandparent of
p^{\prime\prime}
in
T
, then the ladder index of
G(p^{\prime\prime})
is smaller than the ladder index of
G(p)
.
As the ladder index of any graph from
\mathscr{C}
is bounded by
t
, the height of

is hence bounded by

.

Once the tree
T
is computed from
G
, we finish the construction of

by
adding the vertex set of
G
to

(but without any edges),
and for every

connecting

to all nodes

of
T
with
v\in V(G(p))
.
The bound on the depth of

, and on the branches per node that each vertex is contained in, ensure that each
v\in V(G)
has bounded degree.

Proving that
\mathscr{D}
has bounded expansion.

The proof that
\mathscr{D}
is of bounded expansion can be split into three parts.
First, one argues that
\mathscr{D}
is weakly sparse, that is, that there exists a number
h
such that no
H\in\mathscr{D}
contains
K_{h,h}
as a subgraph. This is an easy consequence of the fact that
\mathscr{D}
is
d
-degenerate for some
d
depending only on
\mathscr{C}
, as then we can set
h\coloneqq d
.

Second, one argues that
\mathscr{D}
is transducible from
\mathscr{C}
. This is technically the most challenging part of the proof, but ultimately follows from the fact we can not only compute a (left or right) branching of the input graph efficiently, but the branching computed by our algorithm is also definable by a first-order formula in a suitable sense.

The third step uses the previous two steps and combines them with a result of Dvořák
[
13
]
to argue that
\mathscr{D}
has bounded expansion. This result states that if
\mathscr{D}
is a weakly sparse graph class, then to show that
\mathscr{D}
has bounded expansion it is enough to prove that for every
r\geqslant 1
, graphs from
\mathscr{D}
do not contain arbitrarily dense graphs as induced
({\leqslant}r)
-subdivisions.
We use this as follows.
As
\mathscr{D}
is transducible from
\mathscr{C}
, we know that it also has inherently linear neighborhood complexity. Now we proceed by contradiction and assume that for some fixed
r\geqslant 1
the graphs from
\mathscr{D}
contain arbitrarily dense graphs as induced
({\leqslant}r)
-subdivisions. From this and the assumption that
\mathscr{D}
is weakly sparse, we can argue that there is a transduction
\mathsf{T}
that from
\mathscr{D}
transduces a graph class that contains arbitrarily dense graphs as
1
-subdivisions. It is easy to see that such a graph class cannot have linear neighborhood complexity, which leads to a contradiction.

Main lemma.

It remains to discuss the idea behind the proof of the main lemma,
Lemma

12
.
We will rely on the following consequence of Haussler’s packing lemma (a combinatorial result about packings in set systems): Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of bipartite graphs with linear neighborhood complexity. Then there exists
k
such that if
G=(A,B,E)
is a bipartite graph from
\mathscr{C}
that has no twins in
B
, then there are two vertices
u,v
in
A
that are
k
-near-twins. Here, by
u,v
being
k
-near-twins
we mean that their adjacency towards
B
agrees on all but at most
k
vertices. We remark that the number
k
used in this statement is different from the value
k^{*}
claimed in the main lemma, but we will see that
k^{*}
is closely related to
k
.

We now describe the algorithm to compute
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
n\coloneqq|A|
.
We set
G_{0}\coloneqq G
and proceed in
n
rounds as follows. To obtain
G_{i}
from
G_{i-1}
, we first eliminate all twins on the right side of
G_{i-1}
(by keeping exactly one vertex from each twin class and deleting the rest) to obtain graph
G_{i-1}^{\prime}
. Then we find a pair
u,v
of
k
-near-twins of
G_{i-1}^{\prime}
and remove one of them to obtain
G_{i}
. We also remember the removed vertex as
a_{i}
. By proceeding in this way, we eventually eliminate the whole graph and obtain an ordering
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of
A
.
We then define the partition
\mathcal{F}
claimed in the main lemma as follows: For each
i\in[n]
, let
S_{i}
be the set of vertices
v
from
B
such that
i
is the largest index for which
a_{i}
is a neighbor of
v
. We then define
\mathcal{F}
to be the set of all nonempty sets
S_{i}
, and choose
a_{i}
as the leader of
S_{i}
.

To show that
\mathcal{F}
has the desired properties, we proceed as follows. It is easy to see that each part of
\mathcal{F}
is contained in the neighborhood of its leader, so we only need to show that each vertex from
A
has neighbors in at most
k^{*}
parts of
\mathcal{F}
. We define a bipartite graph
G(A,\mathcal{F})
with sides
A
and
\mathcal{F}
in which there is an edge
vP
for
v\in A
and
P\in\mathcal{F}
if
v
has a neighbor in
P
. To finish the proof of the main lemma, we will show that every vertex from
A
has at most
k^{*}
neighbors in
G(A,\mathcal{F})
. To this end, we establish the following two properties of
G(A,\mathcal{F})
:

1.

The ladder index of
G(A,\mathcal{F})
is bounded by a constant depending only on
\mathscr{C}
. Note that this is nontrivial: while
G
cannot contain a large half-graph as an induced subgraph because
\mathscr{C}
is monadically stable, it is not immediately clear that
G(A,\mathcal{F})
also should not contain a large half-graph.

2.

For every
a_{i}
with
i<n
, there exists
a_{j}
with
j>i
that is a
k
-near-twin of
a_{i}
in
G(A,\mathcal{F})
.

Then we consider a graph
K
with vertex set
V(G)
and edges between all pairs of vertices that are
k
-near-twins in
G(A,\mathcal{F})
. By property (ii) above there is a path from each
a_{i}
to
a_{n}
in
K
. In particular, all vertices from
A
are in the same connected component of
K
. We then invoke the result from
[
19
]
that states if
G
is a graph of ladder index at most
t
and
K
is a
k
-near-twin graph of
G
, then any two vertices that are in the same connected component of
K
are
k^{\prime}
-near-twins of
G
, for some
k^{\prime}
depending on
t
and
k
.
By the construction of
\mathcal{F}
we see that
a_{n}
has degree at most
1
in
G(A,\mathcal{F})
, and therefore all vertices in
A
have degree at most
k^{*}\coloneqq k^{\prime}+1
in
G(A,\mathcal{F})
. This finishes the proof outline of the main lemma.

We remark that the presentation of the algorithm that computes the partition
\mathcal{F}
differs from the presentation given in Section
4
. The presentation given here is more concise, while the presentation in Section
4
is more suitable for the arguments used in the proofs. However, it is not difficult to see that the two formulations of the algorithm are equivalent.

We use standard graph terminology and notation, some of which we will now briefly review. For a graph

We define

Let

We call the original vertices of

the

-subdivision and the internal vertices of the paths the

Our main technical lemmas will work with bipartite graphs.
For a bipartite graph
G=(A,B,E)
we define
L(G)=A
and
R(G)=B
. For

and

, we define

.

3.2
Logic

Logic and graphs.

We assume familiarity with first-order logic
\mathsf{FO}
.

We will work with graphs, modelled as structures over a signature consisting of one binary predicate
\mathsf{adj}
, indicating adjacency between vertices. The vertices of our graphs will often be marked with (possibly overlapping) colors, each modelled by an additional unary predicate. We then speak of
colored
graphs and if a colored

is obtained from

by adding some unary predicates, then
G^{\prime}
is a
unary coloring
of
G
.
Graphs that are explicitly bipartite are equipped with additional unary predicates
L
and
R
to distinguish their two sides.

Transductions and interpretations.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be a graph. A
k
-copy
of
G
is the structure
G^{+}
over signature
\{\mathsf{adj},\mathsf{cp}\}
with universe
V(G)\times[k]
in which the binary relations
\mathsf{adj}_{G^{+}}
and
\mathsf{cp}_{G^{+}}
are realized as follows: We have that
(u,i)(v,j)\in\mathsf{adj}_{G^{+}}
if and only if
i=j
and
uv\in E(G)
and we have
(u,i)(v,j)\in\mathsf{cp}_{G^{+}}
if and only if
u=v
. Informally,
G^{+}
consists of
k
disjoint copies of
G
in which we can determine whether two vertices correspond to the same vertex of the original graph by predicate
\mathsf{cp}
.

A first-order
transduction
from graphs to graphs is determined by a number
k
, a finite set
\Lambda
of unary predicates and a first-order formula
\psi(x,y)
over the signature
\{\mathsf{adj},\mathsf{cp}\}\cup\Lambda
. If
\mathsf{T}=(k,\Lambda,\psi)
is a transduction and
G
and
H
are graphs, we say that
\mathsf{T}

transduces

H
from
G
, denoted by
H\in\mathsf{T}(G)
, if
H
can be obtained from
G
via the following steps:

1.

First, a
k
-copy
G^{+}
of
G
is created.

2.

Then we mark vertices of
G^{+}
with unary predicates from
\Lambda
arbitrarily, thus obtaining a unary coloring
G^{*}
of
G^{+}
.

3.

We define graph
\psi(G^{*})
as the graph on vertex set
V(G^{*})
in which there is an edge between
u,v
if and only if
G^{*}\models\psi(u,v)
.

4.

Finally, as the resulting graph
H
we take an arbitrary induced subgraph of
\psi(G^{*})
.

If
\mathscr{C}
is a class of graphs, we set
\mathsf{T}(\mathscr{C})\coloneqq\bigcup_{G\in\mathscr{C}}\mathsf{T}(G)
. We then say that a graph class
\mathscr{D}
is
transducible
from
\mathscr{C}
if
\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathsf{T}(\mathscr{C})
for some transduction
\mathsf{T}
.

Transductions can be composed – if
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{T}
are two transductions, we denote their composition by
\mathsf{S}\circ\mathsf{T}
, which is again a transduction. We refer to
[
17
,
30
]
for a more detailed overview of transductions and their properties.

A first-order
interpretation
from graphs to graphs consists of two formulas
\psi(x,y)
and
\nu(x)
. If
I=(\psi,\nu)
is an interpretation and
G
is a graph, we define graph
I(G)
as follows. The vertex set of
I(G)
is the set
\{v\in V(G)\ |\ G\models\nu(v)\}
and the edge set of
I(G)
is defined as
\{uv\in V(G)\times V(G)\ |\ G\models\psi(u,v)\}
.

3.3
Graph classes

A

is just a set of graphs, typically infinite. We need the following standard definitions.

Definition 4
.

We say that a graph class

is

if there exists

such that no graph

contains

as a subgraph.

Definition 5
.

We say that a graph

. We say that a class

Definition 6
.

A

We note that a half-graph of order

Remark 7

We will often work with half-graphs in the context of bipartite graphs. It is easy to see that if a bipartite graph

contains

with vertices

.

We say that a class

We say that two vertices

The following claim is a simple consequence of Haussler’s Packing Lemma

]

By translating the Packing Lemma from the language of set systems (used by Haussler) to the language of bipartite graphs
(as done e.g. in

Finally, the notion of

For the rest of the section we fix a monadically stable graph class

4.1

Let

Assume now that

Proof.

Requirement (ii) is satisfied by definition.

We now describe the algorithm that computes the tree

To each node

1.

Appendix A
Sparsification of monadically stable classes

A.1
Additional preliminaries

Almost nowhere denseness.

We first recall the notion of almost nowhere dense graph classes. For this, we need some standard definitions about shallow minors.

Recall that a graph
H
is a
depth-
r
minor
of a graph
G
if
H
can be obtained from a subgraph of
G
by contracting mutually disjoint connected subgraphs of radius at most
r
. We define the following parameter:

\displaystyle\nabla_{r}(G)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{\|H\|}{|H|}\colon\textrm{$H$ is a depth-$r$ minor of $G$}\right\}.

As proved by Dvořák
[
12
]
, the parameters
\nabla_{r}(\cdot)
are bounded subpolynomially in nowhere dense classes, in the following sense.

Theorem 36

(Dvořák
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a nowhere dense class of graphs. Then for any graph
G\in\mathscr{C}
and any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\nabla_{r}(G)\leqslant\mathcal{O}_{r,\varepsilon}(|G|^{\varepsilon}).

The notion of almost nowhere denseness takes the conclusion of
Theorem

36
as the definition:

Definition 37
.

A class of graphs
\mathscr{C}
is almost nowhere dense if for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon>0
, and a graph
G\in\mathscr{C}
, we have

\nabla_{r}(G)\leqslant\mathcal{O}_{r,\varepsilon}(|G|^{\varepsilon}).

By
Theorem

36
, every nowhere dense class is almost nowhere dense. The reverse implication holds for hereditary classes, but not in full generality. It is known (see e.g.
[
27
,
33
]
) that many graph parameters — the maximum edge density among depth-
r
topological minors, weak
r
-coloring numbers, strong
r
-coloring numbers, and
r
-admissibility — are all tied to the parameters
\nabla_{r}(\cdot)
by polynomial inequalities. Hence, replacing the parameters
\nabla_{r}(\cdot)
with any of these families in the definition of almost nowhere denseness yields exactly the same notion. We omit the details, as we will not use these connections here.

Decompositions.

We now recall the notion of
decompositions
of graph classes proposed by Braunfeld, Nešetřil, Ossona de Mendez, and Siebertz
[
4
]
. The concept is inspired by the classic notion of low treedepth colorings from the field of Sparsity (see e.g.
[
27
,
33
]
).

Definition 38

(
[
4
, Definition 14]
)
.

Let
\Pi
be a property of graph classes. We say that a graph class
\mathscr{C}
admits quasibounded-size
\Pi
-decompositions if there are graph classes
\mathscr{D}_{1},\mathscr{D}_{2},\mathscr{D}_{3},\ldots
, all enjoying
\Pi
, so that the following holds: For every graph
G\in\mathscr{C}
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a vertex coloring
\lambda\colon V(G)\to C
, for some set
C
consisting of
\mathcal{O}_{t,\varepsilon}(|G|^{\varepsilon})
colors, such that

G[\lambda^{-1}(X)]\in\mathscr{D}_{t}\qquad\textrm{for each $X\subseteq C$ with $|X|\leqslant t$.}

We note that the original formulation of Braunfeld et al. speaks about partitions of the vertex set instead of vertex colorings, but this is equivalent to the formulation above.

We will rely on the following observation of Braunfeld et al. that almost nowhere denseness persists under quasibounded-size decompositions.

Lemma 39

(
[
4
, Lemma 34]
)
.

Suppose a graph class
\mathscr{C}
admits quasibounded-size almost nowhere dense decompositions. Then
\mathscr{C}
is itself also almost nowhere dense.

Monadic dependence and nowhere denseness.

Recall that a graph class
\mathscr{C}
is
monadically dependent
if the class of all graphs is not transducible from
\mathscr{C}
; and a graph class
\mathscr{D}
is
weakly sparse
if there is
h\in\mathbb{N}
such that no member of
\mathscr{D}
contains
K_{h,h}
as a subgraph. As noted e.g. in
[
29
, Corollary 2.3]
, the following statement is implied by the results of Adler and Adler
[
1
]
and of Dvořák
[
13
]
.

Theorem 40

(follows from
[
1
,
13
]
)
.

Every monadically dependent graph class that is weakly sparse is actually nowhere dense.

Note that since every monadically stable graph class is also monadically dependent, the conclusion of
Theorem

40
also applies to weakly sparse monadically stable classes: they are, in fact, nowhere dense.

A.2
Sparsification of monadically stable graph classes to almost nowhere dense classes

We assume the reader’s familiarity with the proof of

To replace the assumption about linear neighborhood complexity, we will use the result of Dreier, Eleftheriadis, Mählmann, McCarty, Pilipczuk, and Toruńczyk

The analogue of

Having defined the coloring
\lambda
for each graph
S(G)\in\mathscr{D}
, we verify that these colorings witness that
\mathscr{D}
admits quasibounded-size nowhere dense decompositions. For this, we prove the following claim.

Claim 45
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
For every
G\in\mathscr{C}
, if we consider the coloring
\lambda\colon V(S(G))\to C
constructed above, then for every subset of colors
X\subseteq C
with
|X|\leqslant t
we have the following:

•

S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]
is
t
-degenerate, and

•

S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]\in\mathsf{S}^{t}(G)
, where
\mathsf{S}^{t}
is a transduction depending only on
\mathscr{C}
and
t
.

Proof.

To see that
S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]
is
t
-degenerate, note that every vertex
u\in\lambda^{-1}(X)\cap V(G)
has degree at most
t
in
S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]
, for all its neighbors in
V(T(G))\cap\lambda^{-1}(X)
have pairwise different colors under
\sigma
, hence also under
\lambda
. Therefore, every subgraph of
S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]
either contains a vertex of
\lambda^{-1}(X)\cap V(G)
, which has degree at most
t
, or is a subtree of
T(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]
, and hence contains a vertex of degree at most
1
.

For the second assertion, let
X^{\prime}
be the closure of
X
under taking nonempty prefixes. That is, for each color
c\in X
(which, recall, is a word over
D\times D^{\prime}
of length at most
h
), add all the nonempty prefixes of
c
to
X^{\prime}
. Thus,
X^{\prime}\supseteq X
and
|X^{\prime}|\leqslant ht
. As
S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X)]
is an induced subgraph of
S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X^{\prime})]
, it suffices to prove that
S(G)[\lambda^{-1}(X^{\prime})]\in\mathsf{S}^{t}(G)
, for some fixed transduction
\mathsf{S}^{t}
depending only on
\mathscr{C}
and
t
. To argue this, we may repeat the reasoning from the proof of
Lemma

30
, but with the following amendment: in the definition of
S_{d}(G)
, we additionally remove all the vertices that do not belong to
\lambda^{-1}(X^{\prime})
. (We may assume here without loss of generality that
V(G)\subseteq\lambda^{-1}(X^{\prime})
, thus this only removes some the nodes of
V(T(G))\setminus\lambda^{-1}(X^{\prime})
.) Since
X^{\prime}
is closed under taking nonempty prefixes by definition, we see that
V(S_{d}(G))\cap V(T(G))
is a prefix
T(G)
, that is, a set of nodes of
T(G)
closed under taking ancestors. With this observation, the transduction
\mathsf{S}^{t}_{d}
, used to transduce
S_{d}(G)
from
S_{d-1}(G)
, can be constructed in a similar way, except for the following adjustment.
To define the formula
\psi(x,y,z)
, instead of using, for each node
p
of
T(G)
, the unary coloring
G^{+}(p)
, we consider the unary colorings
G^{+}_{c}(p)
for all the colors
c\in X^{\prime\prime}
, where
X^{\prime\prime}\subseteq D\times D^{\prime}
is the set of all the colors featured in the elements of
X
(thus
|X^{\prime\prime}|\leqslant ht
). Also, instead of considering all the colors
c\in C
that can be possibly used by
\lambda
, we restrict attention only to the colors
c\in X^{\prime\prime}
.
Then, from the formulas
\mathsf{branch}_{c}(x,y)
for
c\in X^{\prime\prime}
we may piece together
a formula
\psi(x,y,z)
that works as in the proof of
Lemma

30
, but is applied to a unary coloring of
G(p)
with a bounded number of unary predicates, and expresses the condition that
y
is the leader of the branch containing
z
only under the assumption that this branch is of color belonging to
X^{\prime\prime}
. With such a formula
\psi
in place, in the same manner as in the proof of
Lemma

30
we may obtain a transduction
\mathsf{S}^{t}_{d}
that constructs, for each
p\in T_{d}\cap\lambda^{-1}(X^{\prime})
, the children of
p
of colors belonging to
X^{\prime}
, together with all the edges connecting them to the vertices of
G
.

As in the proof of
Lemma

30
, the final transduction
\mathsf{S}^{t}
is obtained by composing all the transductions
\mathsf{S}^{t}_{d}
, for
d
ranging from
1
to
h
, and a transduction that clears all the edges of
G
.

\lrcorner

Given
45
, we define
\mathscr{E}_{t}
to be the class of all
t
-degenerate graphs belonging to
\mathsf{S}^{t}(\mathscr{C})
. Thus,
\mathscr{E}_{t}
is in particular weakly sparse. Since
\mathscr{E}_{t}
is transducible from a monadically dependent (even monadically stable) class
\mathscr{C}
, it follows that
\mathscr{E}_{t}
is also monadically dependent. Hence, from
Theorem

40
we conclude that
\mathscr{E}_{t}
is actually nowhere dense. Therefore, by
45
, the classes
\mathscr{E}_{1},\mathscr{E}_{2},\mathscr{E}_{3},\ldots
and the colorings
\lambda
witness that
\mathscr{D}
admits quasibounded-size nowhere dense decompositions, as claimed. This finishes the proof of
Theorem

41
.
