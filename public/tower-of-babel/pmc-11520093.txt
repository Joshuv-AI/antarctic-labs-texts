The role of moderate to vigorous physical activity level and number of treatments/medications in mediating the effect of body mass index on diabetic nephropathy: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Shasha Hu, Yuling Chen, Mingjie He, Jun Wen, Aimin Zhong, Dandan Zhan, Zhibin Ye
Published: 2024

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is defined by the presence of persistent proteinuria and/or impaired renal function. Approximately 537 million people were living with diabetes in 2021 [1]. As the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2], 40% diabetes patients would develop CKD [3]. Moreover, CKD significantly increases mortality of cardiovascular [4]. Consequently, finding risk factors and appropriate intervention is increasingly catching the concern by researchers. Several observational and intervention studies, including type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D), indicated that glucose levels is one of the risk factor for the progression of DN [5]. Meanwhile, a population-based cohort study showed that Body mass index (BMI) also was a potential risk factor that increased risk of DN [6]. Another study also revealed that weight control and proper sleep duration are essential to preventing DN [7]. However, it remains unclear that which mediator factors play the key role in effects of BMI on DN. Furthermore, a trial showed that lifestyle intervention with diabetes support and education might also be useful for individuals with T1D [8]

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis is an epidemiological method for exploring the causal correlation between risk factors on disease, based on genetic variants. Genetic variants are assigned randomly at conception, allowing MR analysis to decrease the bias from potential unmeasured confounders and reverse causation [9,10]. Nevertheless, traditional observational study tools that estimate the causality of risk factors on diseases are more likely be interfered by measurement error, unmeasurable confounding factors. Thus, MR is increasing being used to identify causal effect of factors on diseases. Over the past decade, MR has gained popularity for generating more robust estimates of the causal effects of various risk factors on DN, aligning closely with findings from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) when both are accessible [11,12]. Recently, a MR analysis identified that coffee intake was a new risk factor for DN [13]. A comprehensive multivariable MR study showed that both adult BMI and pediatric BMI were associated with DN [14]. Furthermore, a MR study provided that systolic blood pressure (SBP), and T2D, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), as potential mediators in the context of obesity on kidney health revealed that obesity with renal health is largely independent of blood pressure and T2D [15]. However, the MR analyses for effect of BMI on DN which mediated by key risk factors remains unclear.

The purpose of this study initially was to evaluate the genetic relationship between BMI and DN. Then, we would identify the potential mediators factor, including the number of treatments/medications taken and coffee intake were used as mediators, using two-step MR and mediator analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design

The main design of this study design was depicted in Figure 1(A). MR studies must satisfy the following three assumptions: (a) there exists a robust correlation between instrumental variables (IVs) and exposure, (b) IVs are independent of confounding factors associated with both exposure and outcome, and (c) IVs influence the outcome solely through exposure and not via alternative biological pathways. In our study, we performed univariate MR (UVMR) (Figure 1(B)) and multivariate MR (MVMR) (Figure 1(C)) to explore the causal association between different lifestyles (moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, years of schooling, BMI, nap during day, number of treatments/medications taken and coffee intake) and DN.

(A) Overall design of the MR analysis framework in this study. A flow chart depicts how the MR analysis was conducted step by step in this study. (B) Univariate MR analyses to analyze causal relationship between exposure and outcome (DN). (C) Multivariate MR analyses to analyze causal relationship between exposure and outcome (DN). (D) IVW tested for potential causal effects of BMI, mediators (moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, years of schooling, nap during day, number of treatments/medications taken, coffee intake) and DN.

Screening of instrumental variables

Associations of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with the phenotypes were extracted from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The GWAS data dataset of different lifestyles and DN was shown in Table 1. Thereafter, ‘Two Sample MR’ package (version 0.5.6) was conducted to screen of SNPs [16]. The independent SNPs that were strongly relevant to exposure factors were selected with a significance level of p < 5 × 10−8, linkage disequilibrium (LD) distances ≥10,000 kb, and R2≤0.001 in univariate and multivariate MR analyses. In addition, to ensure the robustness of our analysis and mitigate potential bias arising from weak instrumental variables, we calculated the F-statistic (F = R2 * (n−2)/1−R2) as a measure of instrument strength. Subsequently, we screened SNPs with an F-value exceeding 10 for inclusion in our subsequent analytical steps (Supplementary Table 1).

MR analysis

MR analyses were performed based on five methods, including MR Egger [17], Inverse variance weighted (IVW) [18], Simple mode, Weighted mode [19], and Weighted median [20]. The IVM method was identified as the most effective in detecting causation in MR analysis. The MR results were depicted by using scatter plots, forest plots, and funnel plots.

Sensitivity analysis was performed to demonstrate the robust of the MR results, using the heterogeneity test, the horizontal pleiotropy test, and the Leave-One-Out (LOO) method. No heterogeneity was detected with a Q-value above 0.05. Additionally, a p-value exceeding 0.05 indicated the absence of horizontal pleiotropy in the test for horizontal pleiotropy. The leave-one-out (LOO) method was utilized to identify potential outliers in the effect of each instrumental variable (IV). In addition, in order to exclude the potential for reverse causality, the MR-steiger test was performed using the ‘Steiger filtering’ function within the R package ‘TwoSampleMR’ (version 0.6.5) (16).

Mediated MR analysis

We displayed the proportion of variance in BMI and all mediators explained by their IVs, along with the F-statistic for the regression of BMI and all mediators on their IVs, to demonstrate the robustness of the association between IVs and phenotypes. In instances of reverse causality, where the outcome impacts the mediators and the instrumental variables influence the mediators through the outcome, the calculation of the percentage of the BMI-DN effect explained by a set of mediators may be biased. Hence, we employed the IVW method to examine potential causal relationships among BMI, mediators (moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, years of schooling, nap during the day, number of treatments/medications taken, coffee intake), and DN. (Figure 1(D)).

The mediated MR analysis, based on two-step MR, was used to evaluate the proportion effect of mediated factors (moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, years of schooling, nap during day, number of treatments/medications taken, coffee intake) between BMI and DN [21,22]. The total effect (C) was divided into direct (C’) and indirect effects (A*B) (Figure 1(D)) [23]. The proportion of mediator effect was calculated by A*BA*B+C′.

Functional enrichment analysis

In order to clarify the biological functions involved in the genes that were associated with genetic variants, the genetic variants were first associated with the genes through GWAS. After having identified the list of genes that were significantly associated with the genetic variants, these genes were then enriched using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.0), with respect to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations [24]. This process revealed the biological functions in which these genes were primarily involved, as well as the metabolic pathways or signaling pathways in which they participated.

Statistical analysis

UVMR and MVMR were performed using the R package named ‘TwoSampleMR’ (version 0.5.6) [25]. All analyses were based on R software (version 4.3.3) (https://www.r-project.org/). The causalrelationship’s statistical significance was determined using a threshold of p < 0.05. Confidence intervals for mediating effects were calculated by the R boot package (version 1.3–28.1) [26].

Results

Effects of different lifestyles on DN in univariate MR analysis

The MR results were presented in the Figure 2. Here, we generally referred to the IVW results, which revealed that these six lifestyles were associated with DN occurrence. MR results revealed that years of schooling (OR = 0.782; p < 0.05; CI = 0.612 − 0.988), along with moderate to vigorous physical activity levels (OR = 0.261; p < 0.005; CI = 0.106 − 0.640) were protective factors for DN, while body mass index (OR = 1.675; p < 0.001; CI = 1.427 − 1.964), coffee intake (OR = 2.218; p < 0.05; CI = 1.073 − 4.583), nap during day (OR = 2.740; p < 0.05; CI = 1.304 − 5.758) and number of treatments/medications taken (OR = 2.991; p < 0.05; CI = 1.012 − 8.841) were risk factors. The results of the scatter plot (Figure 3) and forest plot (Figure 4) provided further support for our causal inference. The funnel plot showed that the sample distribution of the exposure factor was random, suggesting that it conformed to the Mendel second law (Figure 5). The result of heterogeneity tests disclosed heterogeneity in DN for number of treatments/medications taken (Q_Pval = 0.001; I2=82.971), coffee intake (Q_Pval = 0.026; I2=33.271), years of schooling (Q_Pval = 0.037; I2=12.955) and moderate to vigorous physical activity levels (Q_Pval = 0.003; I2=53.621) (Table 2). In the presence of heterogeneity, the IVW method was employed for MR analysis, mitigating its impact through the application of appropriate weighting schemes. There was no effect of level pleiotropy using MR-Egger intercept test (Table 3). Moreover, according to LOO analysis, none of the six exposure factors exhibited a point without significant bias, proving the reliability of data and the fact that no single SNP had a stronger effect than the others (Figure 6). Finally, the results of the MR-Steiger test conclusively demonstrated that all outcomes were ‘TRUE’, ruling out the possibility of reverse causality (Table 4).

Effects of different lifestyles on DN

A multivariate MR analysis was then employed to examine the causal link between the six exposure factors and DN. The findings demonstrated that BMI (OR = 1.514; p < 0.001; CI = 1.199 − 1.911) was the direct risk factor linked to DN when six variables simultaneously occurred (Figure 7). Furthermore, BMI was employed as the exposure factor, whereas moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, years of education, daytime naps, the number of treatments or drugs taken, and coffee consumption were used as possible mediators. We investigated mediators that were supported by MR for both the effect of BMI on them (step one) and their effect on DN (step two): six different lifestyles. The MR results revealed that BMI were causally associated with moderate to vigorous physical activity level (OR = 0.955; p < 0.001; CI = 0.936 − 0.974) and number of treatments/medications taken (OR = 1.161; p < 0.001; CI = 1.141 − 1.801) (Figure 8). The causal inference was further supported by the results of scatter plot (Supplementary Figure 1) and forest plot (Supplementary Figure 2). The funnel plot indicated that the distribution of the exposure factor in the samples was random, implying adherence to Mendel’s second law (Supplementary Figure 3). Heterogeneity tests disclosed heterogeneity (Q_Pval <0.05) (Table 5). However, there was no heterogeneity based on IVW analysis (p < 0.05). In the MR-Egger intercept test, there was no significant evidence of horizontal pleiotropy observed for moderate to vigorous physical activity level (p = 0.08) and number of treatments/medications taken (p = 0.19) (Table 6). Moreover, according to LOO analysis, none of the six exposure factors had a point without significant bias, proving the reliability of data and the fact that no single SNP had a stronger effect than the others (Supplementary Figure 4). Therefore, we thought that BMI only had a causal relationship with moderate to vigorous physical activity level, along with number of treatments/medications taken.

Functional enrichment analysis results

To comprehend the potential functional and pathway relationships between mediators and BMI, we conducted GO and KEGG analyses, linking genetic variants to genes via a Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS). The GO analysis results indicated that, regarding BMI, the enrichment observed was in telencephalon development, axonogenesis, and etc. (Figure 9(A)). for moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, there was enrichment in the melanosome, pigment granule, and etc.(Figure 9(B)) For number of treatments and medications, the enrichment pertained to negative regulation of hemostasis, negative regulation of cell activation, and etc. (Figure 9(C)). On the other hand, KEGG results showed that for BMI, it was mainly enriched in longevity regulating pathway, prolactin signaling pathway and other pathways (Figure 9(D)). For moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, the main pathways enriched were salmonella infection, renin secretion, and etc. (Figure 9(E)). For number of treatments and medications, mainly enriched in ubiquitin mediated proteolysis, arginine biosynthesis pathway, and etc. (Figure 9(F)).

Discussion

Identifying the risk factors for diabetic nephropathy (DN) is crucial due to its status as the primary attributable cause of chronic kidney disease. BMI is influencing a variety of health outcomes like diabetes, cardiovascular disease and DN [27–29]. However, the Mendelian randomization (MR) study for effect of BMI on DN which mediated by key risk factors is insufficient. We used univariate and multivariate MR analyses to estimate the causal association between different lifestyles (including moderate to vigorous physical activity levels, years of schooling, BMI, nap during day, number of treatments/medications taken and coffee intake) and DN. We found these six lifestyles were causally associated with DN occurrence. Besides, MR analysis also suggested that BMI had a significant causal effect on the occurrence of DN, in which BMI was caused by the mediating effect of number of treatments/medications and moderate to vigorous physical activity level.

In a retrospective cohort study involving patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes (T2D), higher levels of moderate to vigorous physical activity are linked to reduced risks of diabetic nephropathy (DN) [30]. In a T2D population-weighted cohort study, patients with higher levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity had lower mortality [31]. Furthermore, studies have shown that extended periods of sleep could elevate the risk of diabetic nephropathy (DN), findings that align with our own results [7]. Meanwhile, A previous MR study also indicated a positive association between coffee consumption and the risk of diabetic nephropathy (DN) in individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) [13]. An MR study indicated that BMI has a causal effect on DN, suggesting that genetically determined BMI is associated with an elevated risk of diabetic nephropathy (DN) [6]. Epidemiological studies have shown socioeconomic disparities in diabetes care, with individuals having lower education levels and the lowest monthly household income demonstrating poorer metabolic control and higher prevalence of DN [32]. Consistent with prior research, our study utilized a larger sample size and increased genetic variants, revealing that elevated BMI is associated with an elevated risk of DN. Our results also indicated that the number of treatments/medications and levels of moderate to vigorous physical activity play crucial roles in mediating the causal effect of BMI on DN. Functional enrichment results show that for treatments/medications, the pathway is enriched mainly in calcium signaling pathway. Previous study identified that the inhibition of calcium signaling pathway exerted reno-protective effects by protecting podocyte [33]. Furthermore, our results do not provide support for a causal relationship between BMI and years of schooling, nap duration during the day, or coffee intake with DN. In contrast, our results do not support causal effects for the observed association between BMI and years of schooling, nap during day, coffee intake with DN. The mechanisms underlying these associations are poorly understood. Lay, A. have found that the diabetic environment consists of increased adiposity, hyperglycemia, and inflammation all play important roles in promoting glomerular dysfunction [34]. A recent study had demonstrated SMAD3 within the inflammation/fibrosis pathway as an important modulator in the pathogenesis of renal disease induced by obesity [35]. Previous study had showed that obesity might increase chronic inflammation. In obesity, the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 inflammasome contributes to obesity-related inflammation and insulin resistance. Weight loss in obese individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) has been linked to a decrease in NLRP3 levels, leading to reduced inflammation and improved insulin sensitivity [36]. Interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18, produced by adipose tissue macrophages can also cause insulin resistance and promote the progression of DN [37,38]. Furthermore, previous study demonstrated that obesity induces renal lipotoxicity, leading to structural and functional damage to mesangial cells, podocytes, and proximal tubules in the kidney due to the accumulation of ectopic lipids in renal tissues [39]. While a combination of exercise and caloric restriction delays renal failure by postponing renal fibrotic changes in a study [40]. These may explain the adverse role of BMI in the progression of DN and reno-protective effect of physical activity. Future MR studies with adequate power may provide further insights into the potential mechanisms linking BMI to DN. These findings may support the adverse effect of BMI and are consistent with our results partially. Although an observational study indicated that a high BMI (≥25 kg/m2) might protect against deterioration of renal function in patients with T2D complicated by CKD stages 3 or 4, this finding lacks high reliability due to potential unmeasured confounders or reverse causal biases [27]. Instead, the two-sample MR analysis we employed is a technique that can compensate for the limitations of conventional epidemiological studies. Consequently, our MR results that causal relationship involving BMI and DN etiology is more reliable.

However, our study had several limitations. First, only European descent was recruited in our study, and the reliability of the causal associations should be verified in other non-European populations. In future research, we will strive to establish collaborative partnerships with other groups to obtain more data from diverse regions, ethnicities, and cultural backgrounds, in order to further validate our findings. Second, this study did not account for the potential impact of time-varying exposure factors on the results, and there may be measurement errors due to the self-reported nature of some factors, such as physical activity. In future research, we plan to introduce time series analysis methods to capture trends in time-varying exposures such as physical activity and medication use. We will adjust the current statistical models to incorporate these time-varying factors. Additionally, we aim to use more precise measurement tools to collect data, reduce measurement errors, and validate our findings. Third, in this study, heterogeneity was observed in four groups, with the number of treatments/medications showing highly significant heterogeneity. This could be due to substantial differences in the types, dosages, and treatment durations used in different studies, leading to variability in treatment effects. However, in the LOO analysis, none of the six exposure factors showed biased points, demonstrating the reliability of our data. In the future, we can explore the sources of heterogeneity through methods such as subgroup analysis, which will help us better understand the complexity and diversity of how different variables influence DN. We are confident that by implementing these measures, the experimental results of this study will become more robust and reliable.

Overall, we used MR analysis to confirmed that BMI had a significant causal effect on the occurrence of DN, in which BMI was caused by number of treatments-medications and the mediating effect of moderate to vigorous physical activity level. Thus, by reducing BMI, individuals can significantly decrease the risk of diabetic nephropathy. But more studies are warranted to confirm our findings, and the validity of the causal relationships should be verified with other non-European populations.
