A WeChat mini-program-based intervention for orofacial myofunctional therapy in adults with obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome: a randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: Yuqing Ren, Ning Ding, Mao Mao, Xinxue Qi, Ye Zhu, Mei Su, Zhifei Yin, Xiao Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrofacial myofunctional therapy (OMT) has been recommended as a safe, effective, and low-cost option for treatment of obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS). This study aims to evaluate the therapeutic effects of a WeChat mini-program-based OMT in adults with OSAHS compared to traditional home-based OMT. We hypothesize that patients trained with a WeChat mini-program-based OMT will show greater improvement in sleep quality, health-related quality of life, and exercise adherence than patients with traditional home-based rehabilitation therapy.MethodsThis study is a single-center, prospective, assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu Province Hospital). Patients in both groups received routine health education, with the intervention group additionally using a WeChat mini-program and the control group receiving traditional home-based OMT. The primary outcome measure is apnea–hypopnea index (AHI). Secondary outcome measures include lowest oxygen saturation (LSpO2), the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Short-Form Health Survey-36 (SF-36), and exercise adherence. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline and 12 weeks post-intervention.DiscussionThis study attempts to evaluate the efficacy and safety of telerehabilitation among OSAHS patients in China, representing our efforts in the field of telemedicine. This RCT will provide valuable evidence for remoted home-based long-term management strategies for OSAHS patients.Ethics and disseminationThis study has received ethics approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Ref. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is characterized by recurrent partial or complete upper airway collapse during sleep, resulting in apnea/hypopnea episodes [1]. The clinical presentation typically features recurrent snoring, intermittent hypoxemia, daytime sleepiness, and decreased memory [2]. OSAHS has been established as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Epidemiological evidence consistently demonstrates its significant association with elevated morbidity and mortality in hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke, and arrhythmia [3]. Recent epidemiological study has demonstrated that approximately 176 million individuals in China are affected by OSAHS, imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens [4].

The current first-line therapeutic options for OSAHS include continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), oral appliance, and operative treatment [1, 5]. As the gold-standard treatment for moderate-to-severe OSAHS, CPAP maintains upper airway patency during sleep by delivering constant airflow [6]. However, its clinical utility is often limited by suboptimal adherence attributable to device-related issues such as nasal congestion, aerophagia, mask leakage, and operational difficulties [7]. Additionally, oral appliances are small and portable, making them ideal for patients with mild-to-moderate OSAHS or those intolerant to CPAP [8]. However, oral appliances can cause adverse effects such as drooling and bite disturbances, and their long-term efficacy is unsatisfactory, with effectiveness diminishing over time [9, 10]. Surgical treatment is suitable for patients with clinical symptoms who cannot tolerate conventional conservative treatments like CPAP, or those with severe craniofacial deformities [11]. Nevertheless, some patients may experience recurrence post-surgery, with a success rate ranging from 38.71% to 59.26% [12]. Therefore, the benefits and risks must be carefully weighed when considering surgery.

Recently, orofacial myofunctional therapy (OMT) has emerged as a promising alternative treatment for OSAHS, characterized by favorable cost-effectiveness and safety profiles compared to conventional therapies [13–15]. This neuromuscular re-education approach employs systematic exercises targeting oropharyngeal musculature to enhance muscle tone and pharyngeal patency, directly addressing OSAHS pathophysiology [16]. Increasing clinical evidence have confirmed OMT’s efficacy in significantly reducing apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), daytime sleepiness, and nocturnal snoring in OSAHS patients [15, 17–19]. The foundational OMT protocol was established through an 8-year research program culminating in a randomized controlled trial by Guimarães et al. [18]. Their methodology incorporates isometric and isotonic exercises targeting the tongue, soft palate, and lateral pharyngeal wall, with specific focus on functional patterns including suction, swallowing, mastication, respiration, and articulation [18]. The therapeutic mechanisms potentially involve (1) reduction of upper airway edema and collapsibility; (2) optimization of tongue positioning; (3) correction of soft palate and uvular laxity; and (4) enhancement of facial muscle strength and endurance—collectively contributing to mandibular and hyoid elevation for oral closure maintenance [14, 15]. Despite these benefits, OMT implementation faces substantial adherence challenges, with most studies reporting compliance rates as low as 10%, primarily due to its reliance on prolonged patient self-management [14, 20]. This limitation underscores the need for developing intelligent platforms to improve OMT adherence in adult OSAHS populations.

WeChat, China’s predominant social media platform with over 800 million active users [21], provides an ideal medium for healthcare interventions. Its integrated mini-programs offer app-like functionality without requiring separate downloads, thereby conserving device storage while maintaining accessibility and convenience [22]. Compared with traditional face-to-face treatment, home-based rehabilitation demonstrates distinct advantages including temporal flexibility, reduced spatial requirements, and lower healthcare expenditures [23]. Current standard home-based OMT protocols typically incorporate healthcare provider education, instructional guidance, WeChat group communication, and telephone follow-up reminders [15, 24]. However, these conventional approaches suffer from insufficient professional supervision, leading to suboptimal standardization and adherence rates. WeChat mini-program-based OMT may address these limitations [20].

Our interdisciplinary research team developed a WeChat mini-program for OMT visualization in adult OSAHS patients, collaborating with software developers during the study’s initial phase. The mini-program comprises three core components: a training reminder system, instructional OMT training videos, and a post-training documentation feature. Through a dedicated backend management platform created by the development team, researchers can monitor and analyze participant data. All collected data are encrypted and stored in a secure database to maintain patient confidentiality and prevent unauthorized access.

Objectives {7}

The principal objective of this study is to utilize this WeChat mini-program visualization system to implement remote home-based OMT for OSAHS patients, and to evaluate its effects compared to traditional home-based rehabilitation therapy on sleep monitoring parameters, daytime sleepiness, sleep quality, and quality of life. Additionally, the secondary objective is to assess adherence to therapy for OSAHS in comparison to traditional home-based rehabilitation therapy. We hypothesize that 12 weeks of WeChat mini-program-based OMT will demonstrate statistically superior improvements in polysomnographic parameters and quality of life compared with traditional home-based rehabilitation therapy.

Trial design {8}

This single-center prospective assessor-blinded RCT will enroll adult participants with primary snoring and mild-to-moderate OSAHS, allocated through block randomization (1:1 ratio) to either experimental or control groups. The control group will receive health education and home-based OMT guidance, utilizing training logs for self-recorded home-based rehabilitation treatment. The intervention group, while also receiving routine health education, will additionally participate in a WeChat mini-program-based OMT intervention with integrated monitoring capabilities. Both groups will undergo follow-up assessments at the 6 th and 12 th week timepoints. Each OMT training session is designed to be completed within 8 min, comprising three daily training periods (totaling approximately 20 min), implemented five times weekly over a 12-week intervention period. Comprehensive polysomnography and functional assessments will be performed at baseline and 3 months post-intervention. The study protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [25]. The flow chart is presented in Fig. 1.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This research will be conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu Province Hospital, Grade III, Class A Hospital). The study is conducted by a research team comprising seven members, including two physicians from Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine Department, one rehabilitation therapist, one speech and language therapist (SLT), two master’s students, and one data statistician.

Eligibility criteria {10}

Inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18–75 years, male or female; (2) recently diagnosed with simple snore and mild-to-moderate OSAHS [1]; (3) owning a smartphone and having access to WeChat and the internet; (4) not participating in other concurrent clinical trials; (5) informed consent and voluntary participation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) having a body mass index (BMI) of 40 kg/m2 or greater; (2) previous brain injury; (3) severe substance addiction; (4) regular use of hypnotic medications; (5) unstable coronary heart disease; (6) heart failure; (7) inability to cooperate with the study due to cognitive impairment; (8) tonsillar enlargement of degree III; (9) hypothyroidism; (10) neuromuscular disease; (11) autoimmune diseases; (12) craniofacial malformations; (13) severe obstructive nasal disease; (14) receiving continuous positive airway pressure; (15) receiving other clinical trials or treatments within the last 3 months.

Protocol discontinuation criteria encompass voluntary withdrawal through written revocation of informed consent or clinician-determined exclusion due to (1) emergent comorbidities contraindicating continued participation (ICD-10 coded pathologies), (2) loss to follow-up exceeding 30 days, or (3) investigator-initiated termination for safety concerns. Per intention-to-treat (ITT) principles, no participant replacement will occur post-randomization, with our a priori sample size calculation incorporating a 20% attrition rate. Treatment cessation poses no immediate health risks given the non-pharmacological nature of OMT, with all participants receiving standardized clinical care following discontinuation.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

The coordinating center will assist in obtaining consent for the research team to approach potential participants. The clinical coordinator will assess whether each patient meets the inclusion criteria and inquire about their willingness to participate in the study. Following this informed discussion, participants must provide their consent by signing a written informed consent form, thereby affirming their voluntary agreement to take part in the study.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

The participant data collected may be reused in subsequent studies which is included in the consent given to participants. While this study does not involve biological specimens from the participants, therefore no further consent will be obtained from the participants.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Current treatments of the control group in the experimental design involve health education and traditional home-based OMT. Although OMT is not currently a first-line treatment for OSAHS, as an emerging therapeutic approach, multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated its efficacy in treating OSAHS patients. For instance, in a study conducted by Guimarães et al., 31 patients with moderate OSAHS were randomized to either a structured OMT program (30 min daily for 3 months; n = 16) or a control group performing seated nasal breathing exercises (n = 15). Post-intervention, the OMT group showed a 39% reduction in the AHI along with significant improvements in sleep quality, whereas the control group exhibited no clinically meaningful changes [18]. These results suggest that OMT may serve as a viable adjunct or alternative therapy for OSAHS. However, further large-scale trials are needed to establish standardized protocols and evaluate long-term outcomes.

Intervention description {11a}

Drawing on prior researches [15, 18, 19], the treatment protocol for OMT in this study consists of approximately 20 min per day (less than 8 min of each training session, 3 sessions per day), with a schedule of 5 days per week for a total of 12 weeks. Before the experiment commenced, patients underwent OMT conducted by a skilled SLT from the Rehabilitation Medicine Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University until they achieved proficiency in correct posture and movements. A complete training session of OMT in this study includes the following 9 training movements (Fig. 2): (1) Push the tip of the tongue against the hard palate and slide the tongue backward (20 times). (2) Suck the tongue upward against the palate, pressing the entire tongue against the palate (20 times). (3) Force the back of the tongue against the floor of the mouth while keeping the tip of the tongue in contact with the inferior incisive teeth (20 times). (4) Push the tongue forward as far as you can and the tip of the tongue against the tongue depressor for 5 s, then put it back (10 times). (5) Push the tongue to the left and the tip of the tongue against the corner of the mouth for 5 s, then put it back; push the tongue to the right and the tip of the tongue against the corner of the mouth for 5 s, then put it back (10 times). (6) Push the tongue forward as far as you can, force the tip of the tongue against the tongue depressor and move upward for 5 s, then put it back (10 times). (7) Push the tongue forward as far as you can, force the tip of the tongue against the tongue depressor and move downward for 5 s, then put it back (10 times). (8) Recruitment of the buccinator muscle against the finger that is introduced in the oral cavity, pressing the buccinator muscle outward for 5 s (10 times each side). (9) Elevation of the soft palate and uvula while intermittently saying the vowel “A” (20 times).

Nine training movements of OMT

The control group

The control group will receive health education and face-to-face home-based OMT guidance in the outpatient clinic and will use training logs to self-record their home-based rehabilitation treatment. Health education will cover the following aspects: (1) Introduction to the etiology and pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, effects on the body, and promotion of good sleep hygiene and living habits to enhance understanding of OSAHS and improve treatment compliance [2]. (2) Recommendations for weight loss for overweight or obese patients [26]. (3) Guidance on smoking and alcohol cessation, and advice on avoiding excessive fatigue [27]. (4) Recommendations for adopting a lateral sleeping position to alleviate OSAHS symptoms [28].

The intervention group

The intervention group will receive the WeChat mini-program-based OMT intervention in addition to standard health education, with a prescribed daily training duration of 20 min. During outpatient visits, participants will obtain face-to-face OMT instruction along with comprehensive guidance on mini-program operation. Medical staff will conduct remote supervision of patient exercises through a one-to-many online monitoring system, utilizing collected training data to verify the safety and efficacy of home-based OMT implementation. Through iterative consultations with the software development team (Nanjing Kuan Cheng Technology Co., Ltd., China), the research team finalized both the structural framework and substantive content of the WeChat mini-program based on the aforementioned treatment protocol. This WeChat mini-program consists of three modules: a training reminder system, OMT training videos, and a clock-in system. The training reminder system sends two text messages daily for reminding patients to complete daily training tasks over 12 weeks. To ensure the accuracy of exercise performance by each patient, this study produced OMT training videos in advance and registered their copyright (2021-I-00260870). During each exercise session, standardized OMT exercises are demonstrated in the training videos, accompanied by textual and verbal explanations to guide patients through the exercises. The clock-in system allows patients to record their subjective fatigue levels by rating of perceived exertion scale (RPE) upon completing each training session by entering their name and the date to log their completion. The WeChat mini-program is ultimately named “Orofacial Myofunctional Treatment Platform for OSAHS.”

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

OMT exhibits minimal adverse effects, with only transient upper airway dilator muscle soreness or mild discomfort reported following training sessions. These self-limiting symptoms resolve spontaneously within 24 h without requiring medical intervention. Per trial protocol, such expected reactions will not be classified as significant adverse events (AEs) nor warrant participant withdrawal from the study.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Adherence to the intervention will be facilitated through the following measures: (1) All researchers will undergo standardized training in accordance with the research manual before the study commences (applied equally to both groups). (2) Participants will receive face-to-face OMT guidance during their initial treatment in the outpatient clinic. (3) WeChat consultations with research psychologists using pre-scripted responses (blinded interface prevents group identification). (4) Telephone follow-ups conducted by blinded personnel using identical scripts across groups. All adherence support measures are implemented uniformly between groups, with operational workflows physically separating intervention delivery (OMT) from adherence monitoring (WeChat/telephone).

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11 d}

To ensure the accuracy and comparability of trial results, throughout their participation in the study, other clinical trials or treatments within the last 3 months will be prohibited.

Provisions for post‐trial care {30}

Patients and their families will receive complimentary consultation and follow-up care during and after the research study.

Outcomes {12}

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic characteristics collected at enrollment, including age, gender, education, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and neck circumference.

Primary outcome

Apnea–hypopnea index

The AHI measured by polysomnography (SOMNOscreen plus PSG +, CPAPeuropa, Germany) will serve as the primary metric for assessing respiratory event frequency during sleep. Measured at baseline (T0) and 12 weeks post-intervention (T1), we will compare absolute AHI values between groups using independent samples t-test. According to the technical specifications of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, it is the most commonly used measurement to assess the severity of sleep apnea. The AHI quantifies the total number of apnea and hypopnea episodes during sleep, divided by the total hours of sleep. It is expressed as the number of apneic events per hour. A higher AHI indicates more frequent episodes, signifying more severe OSA and an increased risk of adverse effects. An AHI of fewer than 5 events per hour is classified as simple snoring, not meeting the diagnostic criteria for OSAHS, although patients may still show noticeable clinical symptoms. An AHI of 5 to 15 indicates mild OSAHS, while an AHI between 15 and 30 signifies moderate OSAHS. An AHI of 30 or more is categorized as severe OSAHS [1]. Polysomnography (PSG), the gold standard for OSAHS diagnosis, captures comprehensive data on a patient’s sleep architecture, including sleep duration, sleep stages, continuous nocturnal respiration, presence and frequency of sleep apnea episodes, apnea duration, arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2), electroencephalography (EEG), electrocardiography (ECG), heart rate, muscle activity, and additional physiological parameters. Following manual adjustment, PSG results yield critical indices, such as AHI and LSpO2 [5, 29].

Secondary outcomes

Lowest oxygen saturation

LSpO2 is also the important index monitoring by PSG in OSAHS patients. PSG reports also include metrics related to the degree of hypoxemia. Several studies suggest that metrics of sleep-disordered breathing severity referencing hypoxemia may better predict adverse outcomes than strict use of AHI [30, 31]. LSpO2 measured via PSG will be analyzed for mean changes from baseline to 12 weeks using paired t-tests for within-group comparisons and ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values for between-group comparisons.

Epworth sleepiness scale

ESS is widely regarded as the primary tool in clinical practice for assessing subjective sleepiness, noted for its robust reliability and validity. The ESS quantifies a subject’s perceived daytime sleepiness by evaluating their propensity to doze off or fall asleep during eight common daytime activities over the preceding 2 weeks. Scores range from 0 to 24, with higher scores denoting increased sleepiness. A score exceeding 10 is indicative of significant subjective sleepiness [32]. ESS will be evaluated through responder analysis, comparing the proportion of participants achieving ≥ 2-point reduction from baseline between groups using chi-square tests at T0 and T1.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

PSQI, developed by Buysse et al., is a widely employed instrument for assessing sleep quality over a 1-month period. It comprises seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction, across a total of 19 self-rated items. The composite score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores reflecting poorer sleep quality. A PSQI score of 8 or above is indicative of a sleep disorder. This scale is extensively utilized to evaluate sleep quality in diverse populations globally, demonstrating strong reliability and validity [33]. Between-group differences in median scores at 12 weeks will be tested with Mann–Whitney U.

Short-Form Health Survey-36

SF-36 is the most widely utilized health survey, comprising two principal components: the physical component summary (PCS) and the mental component summary (MCS). It encompasses eight dimensions: physical functioning (PF), role limitations due to physical health problems (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health perceptions (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role limitations due to emotional problems (RE), and mental health (MH). Higher total scores denote a better quality of life. Studies have demonstrated that the Chinese version of the SF-36 exhibits excellent reliability and validity [34, 35]. Mean changes from baseline to 12 weeks will be analyzed using linear mixed models with group-time interactions.

Adherence evaluation

Adherent patients will be defined as those who complete ≥ 80% of the scheduled training sessions per week [36]. The compliance rates of the two groups will be calculated separately, and a chi-square test will be conducted.

Sample size {14}

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power version 3.1.9.2 power and sample size program of the University of Kiel, Germany, based on the primary outcome measure of AHI. Based on the study by Guimarães et al. [18], we assumed a mean AHI value of 17.4 events per hour with a standard deviation of 15.9 events per hour in the intervention group at T1, and a mean AHI value of 39.3 events per hour with a standard deviation of 21.0 events per hour in the control group at T1. Considering the intervention in our study is self-administered by patients, we assumed a 25% reduction in effect size. Therefore, we recalculated the sample size assuming the effect size is 75% of the original. Based on a type I error probability of 5% and a statistical power of 80%, we estimated that a sample of 32 participants, allocated in a 1:1 ratio between two groups, would be required. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the study aims to recruit a total of 40 participants, ensuring around 20 participants per group.

Recruitment {15}

Participants will be recruited from outpatients receiving treatment at the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, Respiratory Outpatient Clinic, First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu Province Hospital).

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The qualified patients expressing interest in participating and signing the informed consent form will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control groups. Random allocation will be conducted following the principle of simple randomization with a random number table ranging from 1 to 40, along with their corresponding group assignments (A or B) generated by the data statistician using SAS software.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Groups are predefined as group A for the intervention group and group B for the control group. An independent statistician pre-generated all 40 allocation sequences (group A/B) using SAS prior to trial commencement. These assignments were sealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes (1–40) by a third-party administrator. Upon confirming a participant’s eligibility, the physician contacts the administrator (not the statistician) to obtain the next consecutively numbered envelope. The administrator documents the assignment in a secured log inaccessible to study personnel.

Implementation {16c}

A respiratory physician will screen patients against inclusion/exclusion criteria. Upon confirming eligibility, the physician contacts the third-party administrator (unaffiliated with recruitment or analysis) to obtain the next consecutively numbered opaque envelope. The administrator verifies the envelope number against the pre-generated allocation list, documents the assignment in a secured electronic log (with timestamp and physician ID), and communicates only the group assignment (A/B) to the treating therapist. The statistician who generated the original randomization sequence has no role in this real-time allocation process.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

To ensure methodological rigor, blinding procedures were implemented as follows. The outcome assessor (respiratory physician) remained fully blinded to group allocation throughout the trial. Statisticians analyzed de-identified datasets labeled as “group X” and “group Y” without access to clinical grouping information until final database lock and manuscript drafting. The Data Monitoring Committee (DMC), while unblinded to monitor intervention-specific safety signals, operated under strict confidentiality agreements prohibiting disclosure of allocation details. Patients were partially blinded through concealment of alternative treatment modalities, whereas therapists administering interventions necessarily remained unblinded for protocol fidelity. Final unblinding occurred only after statistical analyses and interpretation were completed, with group labels (intervention/control) revealed solely for reporting purposes.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

There are no reasons for which unblinding would be required.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

All outcome measures—including demographic characteristics, PSG parameters, and questionnaire responses—will be assessed at two time points: baseline (0 th week) and study completion (12 th week).

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow‐up {18b}

Both study groups will undergo a follow-up assessment at 6 weeks. During this follow-up, research staff will contact participants by telephone to verify OMT adherence, and document any concurrent therapies. All instances of loss to follow-up will be systematically investigated and recorded.

Data management {19}

Trained research staff will prospectively document all study data with accuracy and completeness in the case report forms (CRFs). This includes comprehensive recording of demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, physical examination findings, PSG parameters, and questionnaire responses. All the CRFs will be completed by one investigator and independently verified by a second researcher. Study data will be securely archived and maintained for at least 3 years after trial completion.

Confidentiality {27}

Access to all trial data will be strictly limited to the principal investigator and authorized study personnel to ensure confidentiality during and after the trial period.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation, and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

Not applicable, as no biological specimens will be collected in this trial.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Statistical analyses will be conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and R 4.2.1, with analytical methods pre-specified as follows. For the primary outcome (AHI), absolute values at 12 weeks will be compared between groups using independent samples t-test, after verifying normality via Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.10) and homogeneity of variance with Levene’s test; non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test will supplementarily analyze data violating parametric assumptions. Secondary outcomes will employ tailored approaches: LSpO2 mean changes from baseline will be assessed within groups via paired t-tests and between groups through ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values; ESS responder rates (≥ 2-point reduction) will be compared using chi-square tests with Yates’ correction (or Fisher’s exact test for sparse data); PSQI score differences will be evaluated via Mann–Whitney U test with Hodges-Lehmann effect size estimation; SF-36 trajectories will be modeled using linear mixed effects models incorporating group-time interactions and participant-level random intercepts. Adherence rates (≥ 80% weekly completion) will undergo chi-square testing with Bonferroni-adjusted weekly comparisons. All analyses will report effect sizes (mean differences/risk ratios) with 95% confidence intervals, handle missing data via multiple imputation (≤ 15% missingness) or complete-case analysis, and maintain α = 0.05 two-tailed significance thresholds.

Interim analyses {21b}

There are no interim analyses planned.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

For longitudinal assessment, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test will be implemented to evaluate within-group and between-group differences for all variables measured at baseline and 3-month follow-up. Furthermore, Pearson correlation coefficients will be calculated to examine potential associations between ΔAHI (change in apnea–hypopnea index) and changes in covariates including body mass index (BMI) and neck circumference.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non‐adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

All randomized participants will be analyzed under the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, regardless of protocol adherence. A per-protocol (PP) population (participants completing ≥ 80% interventions and follow-up) will be secondarily analyzed for sensitivity assessment. Missing data exceeding 5% in primary outcomes will be handled via multiple imputation under missing-at-random assumptions, with complete-case analysis as supplementary. Protocol deviations (e.g., < 4 weekly sessions) will be documented but retained in ITT analyses to preserve randomization integrity.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant‐level data, and statistical code {31c}

The full study protocol will be made freely accessible through PubMed Central (PMC) immediately upon manuscript acceptance. Protocol amendments and statistical analysis plans will be archived as supplementary files linked to the PMC record.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5 d}

The coordinating center is composed of the study’s principal investigator, clinical research coordinators, data managers, statisticians, and regulatory specialists. This team oversees daily operations, including participant recruitment, data collection, protocol adherence, safety monitoring, and communication with ethics committees or regulatory authorities. The trial steering committee is an independent oversight body comprising external experts including a non-affiliated chair, clinical expert, statistician, and ethics representative. The team provides strategic guidance, reviews interim data for safety or efficacy concerns, adjudicates major protocol deviations, and ensures the study remains aligned with ethical and scientific objectives.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role, and reporting structure {21a}

The Data Monitoring Committee will include professionals from various disciplines including respiratory, rehabilitation, and statistics, ensuring a comprehensive oversight of trial proceedings. It will safeguard participant safety and will maintain the integrity of trial data. To achieve this, the committee will monitor the safety and efficacy of the data throughout the trial duration, review adverse events, assess trial progress to ensure sustained scientific validity and data integrity, and assess the validity and rigor of final data.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Adverse events (AEs) will be proactively monitored through daily patient self-report diaries and weekly clinician-administered structured interviews during follow-up visits. Participants will be systematically queried about intervention-specific risks, including temporomandibular joint pain, dry mouth, headache, daytime hypersomnolence (ESS > 10), and unintended weight changes (> 5% baseline). All AEs will be graded for severity using CTCAE v5.0 (grade 1 = mild, self-limited; 2 = moderate, requiring intervention; 3 = severe, disabling), coded via MedDRA v25.0 terminology, and assessed for causality (related/unrelated) by a blinded adjudication committee. Serious AEs (SAEs) will be reported to the ethics committee within 24 h, with cumulative safety data reviewed quarterly by the unblinded Data Monitoring Committee (DMC).

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

The trial steering committee, quality control team, and data monitoring and ethics committee will convene yearly to scrutinize several key areas: adherence to the research trial’s protocol, accuracy and integrity of the data, compliance with ethical guidelines, effective implementation of safety reporting and monitoring protocols for participants, prompt identification and resolution of potential safety concerns, and overall compliance with applicable national and international regulations, guidelines, and standards.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

In the event of any changes to the protocol, we will take the following steps: (1) inform the funder; (2) provide investigators and the ethics committee with a copy of the amended protocol; (3) fully document any deviations using a breach report form; and (4) update the protocol in the clinical trial registry. Any modifications or changes must be reported to this ethics committee for re-review and approval prior to implementation before being uploaded to the clinical trial registry.

Dissemination plans {31a}

The outcomes of the trial, whether positive or negative, will be documented and published in internationally recognized, peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

This RCT evaluates the efficacy and safety of a WeChat mini-program-based OMT intervention versus conventional home-based OMT in adults with OSAHS. The study hypothesizes that the intervention group will demonstrate (1) significant improvements in AHI and LSpO2; (2) marked reduction in OSAHS-related symptoms; and (3) enhanced health outcomes including diminished daytime sleepiness (ESS), improved sleep quality (PSQI), and better quality of life (SF-36). Furthermore, the WeChat-mediated telerehabilitation approach is projected to yield superior treatment adherence compared to traditional home-based OMT protocols.

Standardized OMT protocols remain absent in current clinical practice [18, 19, 37–39]. Through comprehensive review of recent domestic and international literature, this study synthesized existing OMT approaches. The foundational protocol established by Guimarães et al. [18]—currently regarded as the most comprehensive regimen—targets upper airway dilator muscle function but suffers from exercise complexity and time-intensive requirements, resulting in poor adherence. Furthermore, this protocol demands professional supervision to maintain procedural standardization. To address these limitations, Ieto et al. [19] developed a simplified 8-min protocol demonstrating significant snoring improvement, thereby enhancing OMT’s feasibility for home rehabilitation. More recently, O’Connor-Reina et al. [15, 20] implemented a mobile health (mHealth) application for severe OSAHS patients, reporting superior AHI reduction and adherence compared to non-digital interventions. The enhanced adherence may be attributed to easier access to guidance from healthcare professionals, as well as the acoustic and visual feedback on patient performance provided by the app. However, this study was limited by its restricted sample size and exclusive focus on severe OSAHS cases. The current study introduces a WeChat mini-program specifically designed to provide standardized remote guidance and automated reminders for Chinese OSAHS patients, aiming to optimize home-based rehabilitation efficacy while reducing healthcare burdens. This investigation holds significant implications for advancing telerehabilitation strategies in OSAHS management.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, although blinding protocols were implemented for AHI through PSG to mitigate detection bias, those self-reported outcomes including ESS, PSQI, SF-36, and treatment adherence metrics remain susceptible to participant awareness of treatment allocation. Nevertheless, these instruments remain essential for capturing patient-centric outcomes that directly reflect subjective experiences of daytime somnolence, sleep-related quality of life, and therapeutic engagement. Secondly, our WeChat mini-program exhibits restricted functionality and needs further improvement, including the integration of periodic health education updates and facilitating online communication between patients and healthcare providers. Last but not least, this study does not include severe OSAHS. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that OMT may help reposition the tongue, improve nasal breathing, and reduce mouth leaks in patients using CPAP, especially for patients who are intolerant to CPAP [40]. Consequently, suggestions for future research include exploring the effects of OMT for patients with severe OSAHS, comparing the differences of effects on different severity subgroups of patients with OSAHS, and assessing the combined effects of WeChat mini-program-based OMT with CPAP in adults with OSAHS. In summary, this trial is designed to establish WeChat-mediated home-based OMT as a safe, effective, and clinically practical intervention for OSAHS management.
