Endodontic consultation and referral practices among Finnish general dental practitioners – a questionnaire-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Reetta Kuosmanen, Marja-Liisa Laitala, Vuokko Anttonen, Tarja Tanner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo study the consultation and referral practices of Finnish dentists treating endodontic patients and to investigate the associations between dentists’ backgrounds and their practices.MethodsThis cross-sectional questionnaire study was conducted during endodontic training sessions held in seven cities in Finland. After the lecture, a structured Webropol questionnaire was used to collect the background information and endodontic consultation and referral practices of the attending dentists (n = 96). These practices included reasons for consultations and referrals, choice of consulted professional, and number of endodontic consultations and referrals. The associations of explanatory variables (gender, years of practice, participation in endodontic education, case difficulty, occurred complication) with the choice of consulted professional (endodontist/experienced colleague) and with the number of endodontic referrals done (0/1-5times/>5times) were analyzed with binary or ordinal logistic regression, respectively.ResultsA total of 96 dentists responded to the questionnaire. Most respondents were female (74%), under 45 years of age (77%), general dental practitioners (93%), and were working in the public sector (94%) with less than 10 years of working experience (66%). The majority (73%) of the dentists worked in two out of seven counties. In the context of endodontic cases, 34% of the dentists had never referred a patient. An experienced colleague was consulted rather than an endodontist by 59% of the dentists. However, 55% of the respondents had consulted an endodontist at least once. In the logistic regression, years of practice more than 10 years [6.06 (1.83–20.06), p = 0.003] and participation in endodontic education [4.19 (1.29–13.59), p = 0.02] associated with the likelihood of consulting an endodontist instead of an experienced colleague. In the ordinal regression, years of practice more than 10 years [3.66 (1.32–10.18), p = 0.01] associated with the likelihood of numerous referrals.ConclusionsThere remains a continued need for consultations and referrals to endodontists among Finnish dentists. Our findings indicate that clinical experience and postgraduate education increase the likelihood of consulting or referring to an endodontist, rather than relying solely on advice from experienced colleague.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07583-x.Keywords: Endodontics, Referral and consultation, Endodontic education, Continuing education, General dental practitioners, General dental practice, Finland

Introduction

When using periapical healing as criteria, success rates of root canal treatments (RCT) performed by general dental practitioners (GDP) vary significantly after canal filling [1]. There is evidence from systematic reviews that RCTs have the highest estimate of success, as defined by periapical healing or periapical healing and clinical normalcy, when performed by endodontists [2, 3]. Even RCTs carried out by undergraduate students in educational clinics under specialist supervision have demonstrated higher success rates than those performed by GDPs [1]. Continuous education, which refers to post-graduate updating education courses, has been shown to improve the technical quality of root canal fillings [4, 5] and the survival rate of root canal treated teeth performed by GDPs, but not the success rate [4, 6]. Therefore, it is important that GDPs have the possibility to refer the most difficult cases to endodontic specialists in order to improve treatment outcomes.

Among GDPs, between 60% [7] and 94% [8] reported referring endodontic patients to specialists and referral practices seem to be country-specific. A recent Norwegian study found that 28% of Norwegian dentists did not have an opportunity to refer their patients to an endodontist at all [9]. This is somewhat similar with the situation in Finland, especially in small municipalities with long distances to specialist dental services and due to high fees of specialist care [10]. Similar reasons have been identified in international publications, along with long waiting times for a specialist consultation [8, 11, 12]. Previous studies have shown that longer work experience and female gender of dentists are associated with a higher likelihood of referring patients for endodontic treatment [10, 13]. In contrast, having a postgraduate qualification has been found to reduce endodontic referrals [8]. Case complexity is known to increase the likelihood of referral [13], and root canal obstruction [7, 14–17] as well as inability to locate canals [18, 19] have been identified as common reasons for referrals. To our knowledge, no studies have investigated GDPs’ consultation practices when it comes to endodontic patients. Furthermore, there is limited literature available on dentists’ consultation and referral practices of endodontic patients in Finland. According to Forss et al. [10], Finnish dentists report a need to refer an average of nine patients to an endodontist for endodontic treatment per year yet only 22% of those patients were actually referred. The purpose of this study was to investigate Finnish dentists’ consultation and referral practices with regard to endodontic patients as well as to study the associations between the dentists’ background factors and these practices.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study has been written according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [20]. The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with Finnish legislation, ethical approval was not requested, as it is not required for non-interventional studies that do not involve personal or sensitive data (https://www.finlex.fi/api/media/statute-foreign-language-translation/688701/mainPdf/main.pdf?timestamp=1999-04-08T21%3A00%3A00.000Z). Participation in the study was voluntary.

Research arrangements and participants

This questionnaire-based study was conducted during training sessions focusing on launching an updated version of the Finnish Current Care Guidelines for Endodontic Treatment [21]. Seven training sessions were held during autumn 2022 for all the dentists working in oral health care provided by the cities of Tampere, Seinäjoki, Oulu, Rovaniemi and Jyväskylä, and for dentists attending continuous education events in Savonlinna and Helsinki. Five specialists in endodontology gave the lectures at the training sessions. After the lectures, the participating dentists were invited to respond to a structured Webropol questionnaire based on published literature on the topic and discussions with experienced specialists in endodontics (Supplemental Fig. 1, Additional File 1). Responding to the anonymous questionnaire was voluntary. Informed consent was not needed because no personal data was collected. The inclusion criteria were participation in one training session and completion of the questionnaire. All the answers of the participating dentists were included and no record was kept of the total number of dentists who attended the training sessions. Therefore, no exclusion criteria existed. Purposive and self-selection sampling were used to recruit dentists to respond to the questionnaire. The final sample size was formed by the number of dentists willing to answer the questionnaire in the training sessions.

The explanatory and outcome variables

The questionnaire was unvalidated and modified based on a previously published questionnaire study on Finnish dentists’ management methods for a deep carious lesion or a pulp exposed during carious tissue removal [22]. The content was determined through discussions with experienced specialists in endodontics and the e-form was pre-tested by two researchers. The questionnaire aims to cover the key factors that can be considered to affect endodontic referral and consultation practices. It consisted of 14 questions about the participating dentists’ demographic factors, professional factors, consultation and referral practices and reasons for consultations and referrals. The questionnaire captured the entire professional career of each dentist rather than focusing on a specific time frame. It was mandatory to respond to all questions. All the variables collected are presented in Supplemental Fig. 1 (Additional File 1).

The variables were categorized as follows. ”The choice of consulted professional” (an experienced colleague or an endodontist); “the number of endodontic consultations made for an endodontist” (≥ 1 time or never); and “the number of endodontic referrals made for an endodontist/an experienced colleague/another specialized dentist” (> 5 times, 1–5 times or never) were treated as outcome variables.

Some of the explanatory variables were excluded as follows: “Age” and “years of practice” showed multicollinearity (r = 0.65, p < 0.001, Pearson’s correlation). “Years of practice“ was considered more relevant. “Multiple factors affecting case difficulty” and “one factor affecting case difficulty” appeared to have been understood as opposite alternatives (r=-0.44, p < .001, Pearson’s correlation). “Multiple factors affecting case difficulty” was chosen. “Participating in continuous education” was regarded as more exact than “reading scientific articles”. The response rates in “specialization”, “type of clinical work”, “sector of practice”, “patient wanted a second opinion” and “sum score obtained from the American Association of Endodontists’ Endodontic Case Difficulty Assessment Form (ECDAF)” were too low for reliable conclusions. The distribution in terms of the location was too skewed for reliable conclusions either. As a result, the following explanatory variables were included: gender, years of practice (< 10 years, ≥ 10 years), participation in endodontic continuous education during the last 12 months (yes, no), multiple factors affecting case difficulty (yes, no) and complication during RCT (yes, no). Five explanatory variables were considered an adequate number in relation to the sample size. All explanatory and outcome variables were dichotomized except the number of referrals, which categorized in three classes.

Statistics

Variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check for multicollinearity. No multicollinearity was found between the explanatory variables included in the analyses. VIF ranged from 1.033 to 1.107.

Binary logistic regression was used for the outcome variables of “the choice of consulted professional” and “the number of consultations”. Ordinal regression was used for the outcome variable of “the number of referrals”. All explanatory variables were included in multivariable models. Odds ratios (OR) and confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated. Missing data were not imputed. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The data was analyzed with SPSS statistical analysis software (IBM Corp. Released 2023. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

Results

Background information

A total of 96 dentists responded to the questionnaire. Most respondents were female (74%), under 45 years of age (77%), GDPs (93%), did clinical work on a full-time basis (92%), and were working in the public sector (94%) with less than 10 years of working experience (66%). The majority of the dentists worked in the county of Pirkanmaa (45%) and in Central Finland (28%) whereas the rest of the participants worked in South Ostrobothnia (13%), South Savo (6%), North Ostrobothnia (4%), the county of Uusimaa (3%) and in North Karelia (1%).

Consultation and referral practices

The reasons for consultations or referrals are presented in Table 1. A total of 71 (74%) dentists reported having consulted or referred an endodontic patient for the following reasons: patient wanted a second opinion, multiple factors affecting case difficulty, one factor affecting case difficulty, sum score obtained from ECDAF, complication during RCT. Of these dentists, 22 provided multiple reasons. All together 98 reasons were named.

The most common complication mentioned in the open comments was a fractured instrument in the root canal (58%). Other complications were perforation (11%), obturation (5%), unreachable apex (5%), underfilled root canals (5%), RCT not responding to treatment and requiring an apicoectomy (5%), a large restoration or crown (5%), and showing sodium hypochlorite leakage beyond the apex (5%).

One third of the respondents (34%) reported that they had never referred an endodontic patient to an endodontist, to another specialized dentist or to an experienced colleague (Supplemental Table 1, Additional File 2). Roughly half (44%) of the dentists reported that they had referred a patient from one to five times and one-fifth (22%) reported that they had referred a patient over five times. In the context of endodontic cases, a higher proportion of the dentists had consulted an experienced colleague (60%) than an endodontist. However, 56% of the respondents had consulted an endodontist at least once.

Associations between the explanatory variables are presented in Supplemental Table 2 (Additional File 3). Associations of explanatory and outcome variables are presented in Supplemental Table 1 (Additional File 2).

Practice of more than 10 years associated with both a higher likelihood of consulting an endodontist rather than an experienced colleague (Table 2), and referring a patient more often (Table 3). In addition, participation in endodontic continuous education during the past 12 months associated with a higher likelihood of consulting an endodontist rather than an experienced colleague (Table 2). No significant associations were found between the explanatory variables and the number of consultations (Table 4).

Discussion

This study showed Finnish GDPs’ relatively low consulting and referring rates of endodontic patients (Supplemental Table 1, Supplemental Fig. 1). Experience and participation in endodontic continuous education associated with consulting an endodontist rather than an experienced colleague (Table 2). More experienced dentists also referred an endodontic patient more frequently (Table 3). A patient was most likely to be referred if there were multiple or one factor affecting case difficulty or due to a complication in RCT (Table 1).

When compared to the latest labor market study of Finnish dentists [23], the sample population could be considered representative in relation to gender (female; 74% vs. 72%) and the proportion of clinical work (full-time basis; 92% vs. 90%). GDPs were slightly overrepresented (93% vs. 82%). Less experienced dentists (working less than 10 years; 66% vs. 34%) and working in the public sector (94% vs. 52%) were noticeably overrepresented. Practice location was clustered in Pirkanmaa (45% vs. 9%) and Central Finland (28% vs. 5%) areas differing from the nationwide distribution of dentists’ practice locations. Thus, the sample largely represents public-sector and early-career dentists, which should be considered when generalizing the results of the study.

In the previous studies from Belgium and the Northern Ireland the distribution of GDPs reporting to refer endodontic patients to endodontists varied from 60% [7] to 94% [8]. Consistent with these studies, 66% of the dentists in the present study reported referring endodontic patients (Supplemental Table 1). However, it seems that the referral rates are relatively low in Finland compared to other countries. Of the respondents, 44% reported that they had ever referred an endodontic patient from one to five times, and only 22% had ever referred over five times (Supplemental Table 1). Among Dutch GDPs, 55% referred 1–5 endodontic cases per month [7] whereas in Northern Ireland 82% of GDPs referred less than once a month [8]. Long distances to specialist dental services and high cost of such services in Finland might partly explain the low referral rates as they have been perceived by Finnish dentists as barriers to referral, particularly in rural or remote areas [10].

Here, consulting an endodontist was fairly uncommon (Supplemental Table 1). One reason could be the distinct deficiency in providing special dental care in Finland [10]. To cover the need for specialist care 35% of the dentists should be specialists but only 15.4% of all dentists under 65 years old were specialized and only 1.3% were specialized in endodontics and cariology in Finland in 2023 [24, 25]. Furthermore, the specialists are distributed unequally in Finland and the majority of them work in the biggest cities, where dental schools are also located [26].

More experienced dentists consulted more often an endodontist rather than an experienced colleague (Table 2). Given that years of practice did not increase the frequency of consultations to an endodontist, the emphasis of the time factor cannot explain this association alone (Table 4). GDPs’ feelings of anxiety, stress and frustration associated with RCTs [27], technically difficult and time-consuming RCTs encountered during ones’ careers as well as possible history of malpractice claims might explain the matter. Malpractice claims are rather common in endodontics [28–31] and GDPs have been reported to be concerned about the risk of litigation resulting from their RCT [32]. It might be that as dentists gain more experience, they become better at recognizing various factors, which may lead to a need to consult with a specialist. It has been reported that less experienced dentists may still have problems to distinguish the difficult cases [33] and that time they may even lack the competence needed to perform complex endodontic procedures [34]. It is possible that less experienced dentists might consult more frequently overall, with most of their consultations directed toward a more experienced colleague (Supplemental Table 1, Table 2).

According to the present study, as experience accumulates, the number of referrals does not decrease (Table 3). This is in line with the findings of Caplan et al. [35]. GDPs with more than 10 years of experience in dentistry and at a certain Health Maintenance Organization were more likely to refer difficult cases than less experienced dentist. Likewise, Ghotane et al. [36] found that more experienced dentists were less likely to undertake more endodontic treatment in a primary dental care setting even if they had received more training or had specialist support.

Attending endodontic continuous education more recently associated with consulting an endodontist rather than an experienced colleague (Table 2). It might be that more careful and conscientious dentists who consult more often are more likely to seek updating education courses. On the other hand, participants in updating education become more acquainted with endodontology and learn to recognize difficult cases. No such association between participation in continuous education and the number of referrals was found (Table 3), indicating that post-graduation education may also expand the range of endodontic procedures that the dentists concerned is able to carry out. Post-graduate qualification has been reported to increase confidence in performing endodontic treatments and to reduce the number of referrals of patients with an endodontic problem [8, 36]. Furthermore, in this study, dentists who consulted endodontists more frequently per se, also reported consulting someone or referring patients less frequently due to complications in RCTs (Supplemental Table 2), even though this finding was not statistically significant. Updating endodontic skills, therefore, seem to highlight the importance of treatment planning and decision making. It could even help address the deficiency of specialist dental care and reduce the occurrence of complications.

In the present study, patient referrals or consultations were most frequently made when there were one or several factors affecting case difficulty, followed by complication in RCT (Table 1). Overall patients’ requests for second opinions were rare and it seems that patients trust their dentists. Root canal obstruction such as calcified canals or broken instruments [7, 14–17] as well as inability to locate canals [18, 19] have been identified as common reasons for referrals. In keeping with previous studies, fractured instrument was the most common complication mentioned in this study and on the other hand these reasons can be regarded as factors affecting case difficulty too.

Gender did not affect the choice of the consulted professional, the number of consultations or referrals, or participation in endodontic continuous education (Tables 1, 2 and 3). However, women reported consulting on or referring an endodontic patient due to a complication in RCT more often than men (Supplemental Table 2). In literature, women have been reported to be more likely to refer patients with an endodontic problem than men [8], to have lower confidence and feel less secure in performing endodontic treatments [36, 37] and to have lower confidence in their knowledge of RCT procedures [32]. The gender difference could result from female physicians engaging in more patient-centered communication [38], communicating with their patients with a more participatory decision-making style [39] and displaying empathy more effectively than men [40].

The study has several limitations. First, the question concerning the number of consultations and referrals included all the consultations and referrals done during the respondents’ entire career. The fact that more experienced dentists have had more time to consult on and refer patients during their career complicates the interpretation and comparability of the results and can be considered as a limitation of the study. This could have been avoided by pre-testing the questionnaire by several dentists for proper content validation. Also, some of the questions may subject to memory bias, representing one of the study’s limitations. Furthermore, the results might be biased by the uneven distribution of the respondents’ years of experience: (< 10 years) 66%, (≥ 10 years) 34% as well as age: (< 45 years) 77%, (≥ 45 years) 23%, practice setting: (public sector) 94%, (elsewhere) 6%, and location of workplace: (Pirkanmaa) 45%, (Central Finland) 28%, (elsewhere) 27%. Second, the CIs of the significant findings in Tables 2 and 3 were wide, which is due to limited size of the study population. This adds to the uncertainty around the results and reduces the representativeness of the sample population. Sample size calculation was not applicable since all the respondents were included in the study. Moreover, not all the respondents reported having consulted or referred an endodontic patient for any of the five reasons provided in the questionnaire, which we acknowledge as a weakness of the study. Answering the questionnaire took place only once in the training session via a web link to the questionnaire given during the lecture. No reminder emails were sent afterwards. This partly explains the small study population.

The study raises awareness of the consultation and referral behavior of Finnish GDPs, a topic for which the existing evidence is limited both nationwide and internationally. In terms of consultation practices, it is, to our knowledge, the first of its kind. Although the generalizability of the findings should be approached with caution, the participants represented a wide range of geographical locations. It is also worth noting that most of the participants were working in the public sector. The cities providing oral health care urge their dentists to participate in continuous education lessons, meaning that the majority of the participants were not necessarily particularly interested in endodontics and that the study population was not skewed. These can be regarded as strengths of the study.

In the future, Finnish stakeholders should invest in continuous education and make sure that dentists have an opportunity to consult an endodontist. Teledentistry has been proven to increase consultations, decrease unnecessary referrals [41] and to be a valid tool for examinations and decision-making [42]. Developing digital services for endodontic consultations could be a topic for future studies in order to provide dentists with the possibility to seek consultations even from small and distant municipalities.

Conclusions

There remains a continued need for consultations and referrals to specialists in endodontics among Finnish dentists. Our findings indicate that clinical experience and postgraduate education increase the likelihood of consulting or referring to a specialist in endodontics, rather than relying solely on advice from experienced colleague.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. A statement from the medical ethics committee, and informed consent were not requested as they were not needed according to the Finnish legislation (https://www.finlex.fi/api/media/statute-foreign-language-translation/688701/mainPdf/main.pdf?timestamp=1999-04-08T21%3A00%3A00.000Z). Under the Finnish Medical Research Act (488/1999), ethical review is not required for non-interventional studies that do not include personal or sensitive data.

In this study, the questions of the questionnaire did not involve any intervention affecting the respondents’ physical or psychological integrity and no personal or sensitive data were collected. Participation in the study was voluntary. All phases of data collection, including responding to the questionnaire, were anonymous. No identifying information was collected from the respondents, making individual identification impossible, and so the responses could not be connected with participants at any stages of analysis. Therefore, no ethical approval or informed consent was required under applicable national laws.
