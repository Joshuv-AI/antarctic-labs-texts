The Sunflower-Free Process Thanks: The first author’s research is supported in part by Simons Foundation Grant #848648.
Patrick Bennett, Amanda Priestley

Abstract
. An r r -sunflower is a collection of r r sets such that the intersection of any two sets in the collection is identical. We analyze a random process which constructs a w w -uniform r r -sunflower free family starting with an empty family and at each step adding a set chosen uniformly at random from all choices that could be added without creating an r r -sunflower with the previously chosen sets. To analyze this process, we extend results of the first author and Bohman [ BB16 ] , who analyzed a general random process which adds one object at a time chosen uniformly at random from all objects that can be added without creating certain forbidden subsets.

1.
Introduction

An
r
-sunflower
, or a
sunflower with
r
petals
, is a collection of
r
sets such that the intersection of any two sets in the collection is identical. When
r
is clear we suppress it and just say
sunflower
. We will refer to this common intersection as the
kernel
of the sunflower. In particular, any collection of
r
disjoint sets (resp.
r
identical sets) forms an
r
-sunflower with an empty kernel (resp. a kernel equal to the identical sets). Let
f(w,r)
denote the least integer so that any
w
-uniform family of
f(w,r)
sets contains a sunflower with
r
petals.
Most will be familiar with the famous
sunflower conjecture
of Erdős and Rado
[
ER60
]
which hypothesizes that,
f(w,r)<C^{w}
for some constant
C\coloneqq C(r)
, and they proved the bounds

(r-1)^{w}<f(w,r)\leq w!(r-1)^{w}+1.

Their lower bound follows from the following construction. Let

\{X_{1},\dots X_{w}\}
be a collection of
w
pairwise disjoint sets, each of size
r-1
. The family
\mathcal{F}
defined as

\mathcal{F}\coloneqq X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\dots\times X_{w}\,.

This family appears to be the most common construction referenced when discussing known examples of large sunflower-free families
[
ALWZ21
,
Kos00
]
. While there are quite a few meticulous constructions of Abbott and coauthors for special cases of
w
and
r
, (see, e.g.;
[
AG69a
,
AHS72
,
AH74
,
AE92
]
) until the present work, to our knowledge there has been no other analysis of a general algorithm for constructing a
w
-uniform
r
-sunflower-free family.

In this work, we consider the following random process which we will call the
r
-sunflower-free process
, which generates a
w
-uniform
r
-
sunflower-free
hypergraph. Going forward, let
\mathcal{K}_{n,w}
denote the complete
w
-uniform hypergraph containing all possible
\binom{n}{w}
sets. Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{n,w}
, and in addition, let
\mathcal{H}_{0}\coloneqq\emptyset
. In step
i
of the process for
i\geq 1
, an edge
e
is chosen from
\mathcal{L}_{i-1}
each with uniform probability, and added to
\mathcal{H}_{i-1}
to form
\mathcal{H}_{i}
so long as adding
e
to
\mathcal{H}_{i-1}
does not construct an
r
sunflower in
\mathcal{H}_{i}
. Whether or not the edge is included,
e
is deleted from
\mathcal{L}_{i-1}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{i}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{i-1}\setminus e
. The algorithm continues until the final step
t
such that
\mathcal{L}_{t}=\emptyset
. An equivalent version of this algorithm just chooses
e
uniformly at random from all edges that would be accepted. This algorithm was introduced in an old paper of Abbott and Exoo
[
AE92
]
, and a
modified version (including an additional backtracking step) was used to construct the largest known example of a
3
-uniform
4
-sunflower-free family. The algorithm was used only as a method of constructing small concrete examples by way of computer, and has yet to be analyzed in its own right.

Based on the known constructions for good lower bounds on
f(w,r)
, it seems likely that very large sunflower-free collections must be “structured,” in the sense that these constructions tend to exploit specific properties of set systems. In this paper we will not come close to improving the best known lower bound, but rather we will address the following question:

Question 1.1
.

What does a “typical, non-structured” sunflower-free collection look like? How large can it be?

One simple way to interpret this question is to generate a collection of
w
-sets contained in a universe of
n
points, taking each
w
-set independently with probability
p
, where
p
is small enough that our random collection is unlikely to contain an
r
-sunflower. In particular,
following the notation of
[
FK16
]
, let
\mathbb{H}_{n,p,w}
denote a random hypergraph where each of the possible
\binom{n}{w}
edges is chosen independently with probability
p
. Then we have the following.

Proposition 1.2
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
w=n^{\alpha}
for some fixed
\alpha\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. In addition, let

N:=\binom{n}{w},\qquad D\coloneqq\sum\limits_{s=0}^{w-1}\frac{w!(n-w)!}{(r-1)!s![(w-s)!]^{r}[(n-rw+(r-1)s]!},

where
N
is the number of edges, and
D
denotes the number of sunflowers containing a fixed edge. Then,

\Pr\left(\ \mathbb{H}_{n,p,w}\mbox{ contains an }r\mbox{ sunflower }\right)\to\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }p=o\left({ND}\right)^{-\frac{1}{r}}\\
1&\mbox{ if }p=\omega\left({ND}\right)^{-\frac{1}{r}}\end{cases}

Note that the case where
w=n^{\alpha}
for
\alpha<1/2
is less interesting, since then two random edges probably do not intersect at all, and so a random set of
r
edges is likely a sunflower (matching) already. Even for
\alpha=1/2
there is a constant probability that two random edges intersect, and so we probably have a matching on
r
edges when the total number of edges is a large constant.

If one wants a larger random collection than the one given by the above proposition, one can use the alterations method. To apply this method we would again take a random collection of density
p
, but allow
p
to be large enough so that our random collection probably does contain some (not too many)
r
-sunflowers. We then remove some sets from our collection to get rid of any
r
-sunflowers. In this work we will analyze a process that produces an even larger collection than the alterations method.

On the other hand, one might consider a hypergraph,
\mathbb{H}_{n,w,m}
chosen uniformly from the set of all
w
-uniform hypergraphs on
n
vertices, with
m
edges. In this case, using the celebrated result of Frankston, Kahn, Narayanan, and Park
[
FKNP21
]
, one can say the following.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
r=r(n)\to\infty
. If
m\geq Ke^{2w-t}rn^{t}\log r
, for some absolute constant
K
, then with high probability
\mathbb{H}_{n,m,w}
contains a sunflower with
r
petals and a kernel of size
t
.

Note that in the above proposition, we must let
r
grow with the number of points in the universe,
n
. This is only point in the paper in which we consider this regime of
r
, and in what follows (and what came before) we think of
r\in\mathbb{N}
as being fixed.

For our main result, however, we will analyze the sunflower-free process in detail by adopting the perspective that a sunflower-free collection of sets can be viewed as an independent set in an appropriately defined hypergraph.
An
independent set
in
\mathscr{H}
is a subset of the vertices containing no edge of
\mathscr{H}
. For the the sunflower-free problem on a universe
U
of
n
points, the hypergraph of interest is denoted
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
.
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
has vertex set
\mathscr{V}_{n,w}=\binom{U}{w}
, the collection of all
sets of size
w
in
U
.
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
has edge set
\mathscr{E}_{w,r}
, which is the collection of all sets of
r
-sunflowers (i.e. sets of
r
many sets of size
w
that form a sunflower).
Going forward, we will call
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
the
sunflower hypergraph
. Thus, an independent set in
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
is just a
w
-uniform
r
-sunflower-free collection.

The random greedy independent set process, or just the
independent process
, for a hypergraph of
\mathscr{H}
, is defined as follows. We use similar notation to that used in
[
BB16
]
for ease of translation. We begin with
\mathscr{H}(0)\coloneqq\mathscr{H}
, and in the
(i+1)^{th}
step, a vertex
v
from the set of
open vertices

V(i)
, (where
V(0)\coloneqq V(\mathscr{H})
) is chosen to be added to the independent set
I(i)
where
I(0)\coloneqq\emptyset
. After
v
is added to
I(i)
to form
I(i+1)
, the following updates are performed to obtain
\mathscr{H}(i).

(1)

The
closed vertices
are removed from
V(i)
to form
V(i+1)
. That is, the vertex that was chosen to be added to the independent set in step
i+1
,
v_{i+1}
, is removed, along with every vertex
u\in V(i)
such that
\{v,u\}
is an edge of
\mathscr{H}(i)
.

(2)

The edge set of
\mathscr{H}(i)
is updated by removing the vertex
v_{i+1}
from every edge containing
v_{i+1}
and at least two other vertices, and every edge containing the an edge of the form
\{v_{i+1},u\}\in\mathscr{H}(i)

is also removed.

(3)

In general, by convention, for any edge
e^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}(i)
such that there exists an edge
e\in\mathscr{H}(i)
with
e\subseteq e^{\prime}
,
e^{\prime}
is also removed.

The independent process has been analyzed for many specific hypergraphs and in general for “nice” hypergraphs. The most well-known example is the triangle-free process, first analyzed by Bohman
[
Boh09
]
and then in greater detail by Bohman and Keevash
[
BK10
]
and independently by Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[
FPGM20
]
. This process is a randomized graph algorithm which gave the best known lower bound on the Ramsey numbers
R(3,t)
until it was very recently improved by Campos, Jenssen, Michelen and Sahasrabudhe
[
CJMS25
]
(though the new lower bound still uses a similar randomized construction together with new ideas). More generally, the
H
-free process was analyzed by Bohman and Keevash
[
BK21
]
and gives the best known lower bound for
R(s,t)
for fixed
s\geq 5
(until recently it was also the case for
s=4
, but the lower bound on
R(4,t)
has now been improved by Mattheus and Verstraete
[
MV24
]
using completely different ideas). To encode the
H
-free process as an instance of the independent process, we define the following hypergraph
\mathscr{H}_{H}
. The
H
-free process is then just the independent process on
\mathscr{H}_{H}
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
H
be a fixed graph. Then
\mathscr{H}_{H}
is the hypergraph with vertex set
E(K_{n})
, where each hyperedge of
\mathscr{H}_{H}
is the edge set of an isomorphic copy of
H
.

In a note of the first author and Bohman
[
BB16
]
a general lower bound was given on the size of an independent set,
I
, given by the random greedy independent set process under certain assumptions about the codegrees in the hypergraph
\mathscr{H}
. Unfortunately there are some interesting hypergraphs, including
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
, which do not satisfy the assumptions in
[
BB16
]
. In this paper we modify the analysis to tolerate relaxed assumptions. In particular, this will allow us to analyze the sunflower-free process.

The method we will use is the
differential equation method
, which is a collection of techniques and tools useful for analyzing random processes which select one object at each step. For these processes, the selection is generally random, but may depend on previous steps. Typically (as will be the case for us), we are forbidden from making certain selections based on the result of previous steps, but the distribution at any given step is uniform over all non-forbidden possibilities. The method is strongly associated with Wormald, who played a crucial role in developing it and finding many applications
[
Wor99
]
. The unfamiliar reader can also refer to a gentle introduction to the method by the first author and Dudek
[
BD22
]
. The differential equation method is the approach used in the papers
[
Boh09
,
BK10
,
FPGM20
,
BK21
]
mentioned previously.

Recall that
V(i)
denotes the set of
open
vertices in step
i
, with
|V(0)|=N
. We say that
\mathscr{H}
is
D
-regular
if each vertex
v\in V(\mathscr{H})
is in exactly
D
edges. We let
d_{A}
denote the
degree of a subset

A\subseteq N
of the vertices, meaning the number of hyperedges in
\mathscr{H}
containing
A
. Furthermore let
\Delta_{\ell}(\mathscr{H})
denote the
maximum degree of an
\ell
-subset of the vertices.
That is,

\Delta_{\ell}(\mathscr{H})=\max\{d_{A}:A\subseteq N,|A|=\ell\}\,.

We let the
(r-1)
-codegree
of a pair of distinct vertices
v,v^{\prime}
be the number of subsets
S\subseteq V
of size
r-1
such that
S\cap\{v,v^{\prime}\}=\emptyset
, and
\{v\}\cup S
and
\{v^{\prime}\}\cup S
are both edges of
\mathscr{H}
.

Finally, we let
\Gamma(\mathscr{H})
denote the maximum
(r-1)
-codegree of over all pairs of vertices
v,v^{\prime}
.

As one of our main theorems is a modification of the theorem of the first author and Bohman
[
BB16
]
, we restate it here for convenience.

Theorem
1.5

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
BB16
]
)
.

Let
r\geq 3
and
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. Let
\mathcal{\mathscr{H}}
be a
r
-uniform,
D
-regular hypergraph on
N
vertices such that
D>N^{\varepsilon}
. If

\Delta_{\ell}(\mathscr{H})<D^{\frac{r-\ell}{r-1}-\varepsilon}\quad\text{ for }\ell=2,\ldots,r-1

and
\Gamma(\mathscr{H})<D^{1-\varepsilon}
then the random greedy independent set algorithm produces an independent set
I
in
\mathscr{H}
with

|I|=\Omega\left(N\cdot\left(\frac{\log N}{D}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\right)

with probability
1-\exp\left\{-N^{\Omega(1)}\right\}
.

Remark 1.6
.

Note that under relaxed assumptions (assuming only that the maximum degree is
D
), one can prove the existence of an independent set of size
\Omega(ND^{-\frac{1}{r-1}})
using either the alterations method or the Lovász Local Lemma. Theorem
1.5
gives a logarithmic improvement.

In this work, we relax the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
in several ways. For example, we allow that the hypergraph
\mathscr{H}
no longer be
r
-uniform, allowing some smaller edges.
We also allow for some variation in the degrees of vertices in
\mathscr{H}
, only requiring them to be
(1+o(1))D
. We allow the set degrees
\Delta_{\ell}(\mathscr{H})
to be larger.
And perhaps most importantly, due to our interest in the sunflower hypergraph, we are in need of a theorem which significantly relaxes the assumption on the
(r-1)
-codegree of
\mathscr{H}
. We will allow the
(r-1)
-codegrees to be larger, and also we will allow there to be some pairs of vertices for which we do not assume anything at all about the
(r-1)
-codegree. This is important, since we will later show that when looking at the sunflower hypergraph
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
in the regime where
w=n^{\alpha}
and
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<1
, the
(r-1)
-codegree of two vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathscr{V}_{w,r}
corresponding to hyperedges
e_{\mathcal{K}},e_{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{n,w}
is maximized when
e_{\mathcal{K}}
and
e_{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}
have large intersection. In fact, when
|e_{\mathcal{K}}\cap e_{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}|=w-1
the
(r-1)
-codegree is nearly
D
. We will see that in this regime, most sunflowers in
\mathcal{K}_{n,w}
will have relatively small kernels, so the sets forming a sunflower typically barely intersect and mostly avoid each other. Therefore if
e_{\mathcal{K}}
and
e_{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}
have a large intersection, there will be many sets in
\mathcal{K}_{n,w}
that mostly avoid both of them, and could easily form a sunflower with either one.

We will need to assume this phenomenon (pairs with high
(r-1)
-codegree) to be relatively rare. Toward this end, let us define the set of
bad pairs
as follows. The definition uses a parameter
\phi
which can be thought of as small.

Definition 1.7

(Bad Pairs)
.

Vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V\left(\mathscr{H}\right)
form a bad pair if their
(r-1)
-codegree is at least
\phi D
. For a fixed vertex
v
, the set of vertices
v^{\prime}
which form a bad pair with
v
will be denoted by
B(v)
.

Roughly speaking, bad pairs are highly correlated. Supposing that
v,v^{\prime}
is a bad pair and
v^{\prime}
is chosen to go in our independent set, then
v
becomes significantly more likely to end up in the independent set. Indeed, there are many
(r-1)
-sets
S
such that
\{v\}\cup S
and
\{v^{\prime}\}\cup S
are both hyperedges. When we choose
v^{\prime}
, due to step (
2
) in our description of the process, we remove
v^{\prime}
from
\{v^{\prime}\}\cup S
, leaving just
S
. But since
S\subseteq\{v\}\cup S
, in step (
3
) we delete the hyperedge
\{v\}\cup S
. Since there are many such sets
S
, the degree of
v
drops by a lot in the step where we pick
v^{\prime}
. This means
v
becomes less likely to ever be closed, meaning that it is more likely to eventually be chosen. We will discuss more specific examples of this phenomenon in Sections
1.1.1
and
1.1.2
.

To state our next theorem we will need the following definitions. For a (possibly non-uniform) hypergraph
\mathscr{H}
, we say that
\mathscr{H}
is
r
-bounded
if every edge has size at most
r
. We let
\mathscr{H}^{(s)}
denote the
s
-uniform hypergraph consisting of all edges of size
s
from
\mathscr{H}
.

Definition 1.8

(Degrees of Sets)
.

For a set of vertices
A
, let
d_{A\uparrow b}
be the number of edges of size
b
containing
A
in
\mathscr{H}
. If
A=\{v\}
is a singleton then we just write
d_{v\uparrow b}
.

Definition 1.9

(Bad Degree of Sets)
.

For a set of vertices
A
, let
d^{\prime\prime}_{A\uparrow b}
be the number of edges of size
b\geq|A|+2
containing
A
and a pair of bad vertices
v,v^{\prime}\notin A
.

Finally, we say that a sequence of events
\mathcal{E}_{N}
occurs
asymptotically almost surely
or
a.a.s.
if
\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{N}]\rightarrow 1
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. With these definitions in mind, we can state our main theorem as follows.

Theorem
1.10
.

Fix an integer
r\geq 3
, and positive functions
\phi=\phi(N)\leq\log^{-300r}N
and
D=D(N)\geq\phi^{-r}
. Suppose
\mathscr{H}
is an
r
-bounded hypergraph on
N
vertices such that

(1)

for every vertex
v
, we have
d_{v\uparrow r}\in[(1-\phi)D,(1+\phi)D]
,

(2)

for
2\leq\ell<k\leq r
we have
\Delta_{\ell}(\mathscr{H}^{(k)})\leq\phi D^{\frac{k-\ell}{r-1}}
,

(3)

for
2\leq k\leq r-1
we have
\Delta_{1}(\mathscr{H}^{(k)})\leq\phi D^{\frac{k-1}{r-1}}
,

(4)

for all
v\in V(\mathscr{H})
we have
|B(v)|\leq\phi D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}
, and

(5)

for all
A\subseteq V(\mathscr{H})
with
|A|\geq 1
and
|A|+2\leq b\leq r
we have
d^{\prime\prime}_{A\uparrow b}(\mathscr{H})\leq\phi D^{\frac{b-|A|}{r-1}}
.

Then the random greedy independent set algorithm produces an independent set
I
in
\mathscr{H}
with

|I|=\Omega\left(N\cdot\left(\frac{\log 1/\phi}{D}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\right)

asymptotically almost surely.

Remark 1.11
.

The hypotheses of Theorem
1.10
imply that
\mathscr{H}
is not too dense. Specifically, counting
|\mathscr{H}|
in two ways we have

(1-\phi)\frac{ND}{r}\leq|E(\mathscr{H})|\leq\frac{1}{\binom{r}{2}}\binom{N}{2}\phi D^{\frac{r-2}{r-1}}.

This implies that we have

(1.1)

\begin{split}N=\Omega\left(D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{-1}\right),\qquad D=O\left(\phi^{r-1}N^{r-1}\right)\,.\end{split}

A similar result was recently obtained by Guo and Warnke
[
GW25
]
, so we briefly discuss the similarities and differences. Instead of analyzing the random greedy process, Guo and Warnke
[
GW25
]
consider a semi-random “nibble” version. In other words, they consider a random process in which one “step” involves choosing many potential vertices to go into the independent set, and possibly discarding some of them which would cause a problem. The main theorem in
[
GW25
]
requires only an upper bound on vertex degrees, whereas our Theorem
1.10
requires almost-regularity. Their main theorem requires the hypergraph to be
r
-uniform, while our Theorem
1.10
does not. Their main theorem also has conditions very similar to our Condition (
2
), as well as a stronger (compared to Theorem (
1.10
)) assumption on the
(r-1)
-codegrees. Roughly speaking, in the language of our paper, the main theorem in
[
GW25
]
assumes there are no bad pairs of vertices. For that reason, the result of Guo and Warnke could not be directly applied to the hypergraph
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
to obtain a sunflower-free collection.

As mentioned, one of the central motivations behind introducing some of the conditions in Theorem
1.10
, is in order to apply it to the sunflower-free process. In doing so, we obtain the following. (Note that here
r
is held constant while
w
grows with
n
.)

Theorem
1.12
.

Fix
r\geq 3
and let
w=n^{\alpha}
for some fixed
\alpha\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. Let
N=\binom{n}{w}
and
D
be defined as in (
A.1
). The sunflower-free process run on a universe of size
n
produces a
w
-uniform
r
-sunflower-free family of size

\Omega\left((w^{2}/n)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}ND^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\right)

asymptotically almost surely.

Let us define the following parameter for the sunflower-free process,

\kappa(n,w)\coloneqq\left(\frac{w^{2}}{nD}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\,.

Theorem
1.12
then says that the sunflower-free process is able to construct a hypergraph with a
\kappa(n,w)
fraction of the total number of possible edges, asymptotically almost surely, before terminating. Theorem
1.12
gives an improvement by the factor
(w^{2}/n)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}
over the alterations method or Lovász Local Lemma (see the remark after Theorem
1.5
).
Of course Theorem
1.12
only gives a lower bound on the final size of the sunflower-free family, so one might hope that the truth is significantly larger. However, the bound in Theorem
1.12
follows from the bound in Theorem
1.10
, and it is known that there exist instances (e.g. when
\mathscr{H}
is a random hypergraph) where the largest independent set in
\mathscr{H}
matches the lower bound up to a constant factor. Furthermore, there exist hypergraphs
\mathscr{H}
that do have much larger independent sets, but for which it is known that the random greedy algorithm terminates long before finding one. For example, Mantel’s theorem gives us that the largest independent set in
\mathscr{H}_{K_{3}}
(see Definition
1.4
) has size
\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor
. But the independent process on
\mathscr{H}_{K_{3}}
, i.e. the triangle-free process, terminates after only
O(n^{3/2}\log^{1/2}n)
steps a.a.s.
[
BK10
,
FPGM20
]
(note that this upper bound number of steps matches the lower bound given by Theorem
1.10
). Bohman and Keevash
[
BK21
]
conjecture that in general, the lower bound they proved for the
H
-free process (which matches what we get from Theorem
1.10
) is correct up to a constant factor. This conjecture has
been verified in a few more cases, including the
K_{4}
-free process
[
War14b
,
Wol10
]

and the
C_{\ell}
-free process for all
\ell\geq 4

[
Pic11
,
Pic14b
,
War14a
]
. In light of all this, it seems more likely that the lower bound in Theorem
1.12
is not far from the truth. If that is the case, then the family produced by the sunflower-free process is small compared to other known constructions. Indeed, in some of the best known constructions, the authors make use of the fact that the precise answer is known for very small cases of
r
and
w
(e.g.,
[
AG69b
]
) and then use structural properties of set systems to iteratively combine small sunflower-free families on disjoint ground sets, to form larger ones
[
AE92
,
AHS72
]
. However, it’s the case that the sunflower-free family given by Theorem
1.12
is still relatively large for a “random-looking” sunflower-free family. To be more precise, by “random-looking,” we mean that our sunflower-free family has
pseudorandom
properties, i.e. it shares some properties with a uniformly random family of
w
-sets of the same density. In this paper we do not go out of our way to prove these pseudorandom properties, but some are established as part of the proof of Theorem
1.10
. For example, the proof implies that in our final
r
-sunflower-free family, certain substructure counts are pseudorandom (approximately the same as they would be in a uniform random family of the same density). In
[
BB16
]
, the first author and Bohman proved that under the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
, a wide variety of substructure counts in the final independent set are pseudorandom. This ought to also be the case in the setting of Theorem
1.10
, and the authors plan to pursue some preliminary work they have already done on such a result. It is worth noting that Guo and Warnke
[
GW25
]
proved that their semi-random process produces an independent set with pseudorandom properties.

When considering the bound in Theorem
1.12
, we know due to the sunflower lemma of Erdős and Rado, that any family containing at least
w!(r-1)^{w}
sets must contain a sunflower. This bound was improved in the breakthrough result of Alweiss et al.
[
ALWZ21
]
, which was quickly followed by improvements by Rao
[
Rao20
]
, and Bell et al.
[
BCW21
]
to give that
(\alpha r\log(w)))^{w}
sets are sufficient for guaranteeing the existence of a sunflower, for some absolute constant
\alpha
. What this implies in our setting is that, when
n
is sufficiently large such that
\binom{n}{w}
is much larger than
(\alpha r\log(w)))^{w}
, adding additional points to the universe won’t help the algorithm to construct a larger
r
-sunflower-free family. In particular, for fixed
w
it makes sense for
\kappa(n,w)
to increase as a function of
n
up to some threshold
{n^{\ast}}
, after which, it should tend to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, characterizing this threshold behavior as a function of
n
, is an interesting question in its own right.

1.1.
Discussion of the conditions in Theorem
1.10

Here we discuss why we need something resembling the assumptions of Theorem
1.10
, and specify a couple of additional examples that satisfy those conditions, but do not satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.5
.

Our analysis of the process is fundamentally motivated by tracking the number of open vertices, and so we must also keep track of how many vertices become closed. The mechanism that causes a vertex
v
to become closed is an edge
e
that contains
v
where all other vertices in
e
are in the independent set
I
. This does not happen all at once: in the beginning none of the vertices of
e
are in
I
, and as the process runs we put vertices of
e
into
I
. To keep track of this phenomenon, for each vertex
v
we will need to estimate
d_{\ell}(v)
, which is the number of edges containing
\ell
open vertices including
v
, with the rest of the vertices in the edge belonging to
I
. So we will keep track of a large system of random variables including both the number of open vertices and these variables
d_{\ell}(v)
.
For each of these random variables, we will show that it is a.a.s. within a relatively small error of some deterministic function, called the
trajectory
of that random variable. (Technically, we will only do this for
almost
all the variables
d_{\ell}(v)
, allowing for the possibility that a few of them deviate significantly from their trajectories)

The differential equation method naturally lends itself to treating all vertices the same, and so it is hard to imagine analyzing the process without something like Condition (
1
). For the differential equation method, we also need a
boundedness hypothesis
for the variables we track, meaning that we need a bound on how large the change can be over one step of the process. Condition (
2
) is reminiscent of the “spreadness” condition in
[
ALWZ21
]
which was an integral ingredient in achieving an improved upper bound on the theorem of Erdős and Rado. A hypergraph
\mathscr{H}
is
\kappa
-spread if
\Delta_{\ell}(\mathscr{H})\leq\kappa^{\ell}|\mathscr{H}|
for each
\ell
. Roughly speaking, spreadness translates to the assumption that the degree of a subset of vertices decreases by a factor of
\kappa
as the set increases in size by a single element. In our case, Condition (
2
) helps with the boundedness hypothesis for our variables
d_{\ell}(v)
. For example, in one step the maximum possible increase in
d_{r-1}(v)
is
\Delta_{2}(\mathscr{H}^{(r)})
. It is worth noting that, in the absence of the imposition of a spread-like condition, the independent process can behave very differently. Indeed, Picollelli
[
Pic14a
]
studied the
H
-free process where
H=K_{4}^{-}
is formed by removing one edge from
K_{4}
. This process is of course equivalent to the independent process on the hypergraph
\mathscr{H}_{K_{4}^{-}}
, which does not satisfy Condition (
2
). While Picollelli was still able to analyze the process, the trajectories of all the random variables significantly deviated from what we will show is true of the independent process under the assumptions of Theorem
1.10
. Condition (
3
) is there to guarantee that even though we allow for some smaller edges, they will have a negligible effect, in the sense that most vertex closures should be from edges that originally had size
r
. For the applications we have in mind (e.g. the sum-free process discussed in the next section), we view the smaller edges as degenerate cases which should not affect much. Condition (
4
) is also related to the boundedness hypothesis. For example, when we choose
v_{i}
to go into our independent set, for an arbitrary vertex
v
,
d_{r}(v)
could decrease by the
(r-1)
-codegree of
v
with
v_{i}
due to Step (
3
). We actually do allow some pairs of vertices to have large
(r-1)
-codegree, but we must put some limit on how many such pairs there are. If
u,v
have high
(r-1)
-codegree and
u
is chosen to go in the independent set, then we are forced to give up on tracking
d_{\ell}(v)
, which might have drastically changed in that one step. From that point on we only use a crude upper bound on
d_{\ell}(v)
. We manage to analyze the process so long as we can still keep track of
d_{\ell}(v)
for almost all open
v
. Condition (
4
) allows us to bound the number of
v
for which we can no longer track
d_{\ell}(v)
. Condition (
5
) avoids a certain problem in tracking
d_{\ell}(v)
. Namely, suppose that many edges contained
v
as well as a bad pair of vertices. As the process runs, we would like to keep track of how these edges contribute to
d_{\ell}(v)
for each
\ell\geq 2
. These contributions occur due to these edges having vertices chosen to go in the independent set, but if we choose a member of a bad pair, the other member starts to behave unpredictably. Our analysis can handle only a few such contributions to
d_{\ell}(v)
, and Condition (
5
) gives us that these contributions will not become pathological.

1.1.1.
The sum-free process

Another random process which can also be characterized as finding a large independent set in a hypergraph is that of the
sum-free process
. The sum-free process on
\mathbb{Z}_{2n}
iteratively constructs a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2n}
, starting with
S=\emptyset
, and in each step adds a uniform random element of
\mathbb{Z}_{2n}
so long as
S
maintains the property that there exists no choice of
a,b,c\in S
, such that
a+b=c
is satisfied. In this case, this is equivalent to the random greedy independent set process run on the hypergraph
\mathscr{H}
with vertex set
\mathscr{V}=\mathbb{Z}_{2n}
, and edge set
\mathscr{E}
which is the family of all solutions
\{a,b,c\}
where
a+b=c
(or the set of all
Schur triples)
. Of course there are a few “triples” where not all of
a,b,c
are distinct, and we avoid those solutions as well.
In
[
Ben20
]
, the first author showed the following.

Theorem
1.13

(Theorem 3 of
[
Ben20
]
)
.

Asymptotically almost surely, the sum-free process run on
\mathbb{Z}_{2n}
produces a set
S
of size

|S|\geq\frac{1}{6}n^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n.

The proof of this theorem similarly required modifying the proof of Theorem
1.5
in order to tolerate a modest number of vertices with large codegree. In fact, we can observe that our new Theorem
1.10
implies Theorem
1.13
(except “1/6” is replaced by some unspecified constant).

To apply Theorem
1.10
to the sum-free process, we can let
r=3
and
N=2n-1
(we do not include the vertex
0
since 0+0=0). We let
D=3n
and say
\phi=n^{-1/3}
. To check Condition (
1
), note that every vertex
v
is in
2n+O(1)
solutions to
v+b=c
(only counting cases where
v,b,c
are distinct) and
n+O(1)
solutions to
a+b=v
, and so
d_{v\uparrow 3}=3n+O(1)
. For Condition (
2
), note that the only relevant case is
\ell=2,k=3
, and
\Delta_{2}(\mathscr{H}^{(3)})=O(1)
. For Condition (
3
) the relevant case is
k=2
and since edges of size 2 occur when we have
a+b=c
and
a,b,c
are not all distinct, we have
\Delta_{1}(\mathscr{H}^{(2)})=O(1)
. For Condition (
4
), consider a bad pair
v,v^{\prime}
, so there are at least
\phi D=\Omega(n^{2/3})
pairs
x,y
such that both
xyv
and
xyv^{\prime}
are Schur triples. For
xyv
to be a Schur triple we need
v\in\{x+y,x-y,y-x\}
and likewise we need
v^{\prime}\in\{x+y,x-y,y-x\}
. Furthermore we assume
v\neq v^{\prime}
for a bad pair. In most cases, e.g. when
v=x+y
and
v^{\prime}=x-y
, there are only
O(1)
solutions for
x,y
. The only case where we could possibly get more solutions is when
v=x-y,v^{\prime}=y-x
which of course can only happen if
v^{\prime}=-v
. So if
v^{\prime}\neq-v
then they have 2-codegree
O(1)
. Each
v
is in exactly one bad pair
v,-v
, and
|B(v)|=1
, verifying Condition (
4
). Finally, for Condition (
5
) the relevant case is
|A|=1,b=3
. Since bad pairs are all of the form
x,-x
, any fixed vertex
v\neq 0
can be in
O(1)
Schur triples consisting of
v
and a bad pair, verifying Condition (
5
).

As we showed above, the bad pairs in this hypergraph
\mathscr{H}
are precisely the pairs
v,-v
. As we discussed earlier, bad pairs are highly correlated. We can see this phenomenon illustrated in the sum-free process. Indeed, there are
O(n)
bad pairs, so if we run the process for
O(n^{1/2}\log^{1/2}n)
steps, one would guess (based on the heuristic assumption that our independent set resembles a uniform random set) that our independent set contains
O(\log n)
bad pairs
v,-v
. To the contrary, in
[
Ben20
]
the first author proved that the number of bad pairs in the independent set grows like a power of
n
.

1.1.2.
The independent process on a blowup

Let
\mathscr{H}
be a
D
-regualar hypergraph on
N
vertices, and assume it satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
. The
b
-blowup
of
\mathscr{H}
is a hypergraph
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
whose vertex set is
\{(v,i),v\in V(\mathscr{H}),1\leq i\leq b\}
, so we duplicate each vertex of
\mathscr{H}

b
times. For each edge
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
in
\mathscr{H}
there are
b^{r}
edges
\{(v_{1},i_{1}),\ldots,(v_{r},i_{r})\}
in
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
(each
i_{j}
ranges from
1
to
b
). Thus,
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
is
r
-uniform on
N^{\prime}=bN
vertices, and
D^{\prime}
-regular where
D^{\prime}=b^{r-1}D
.

Let
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
be the 2-blowup of
\mathscr{H}
(
b=2
here for simplicity). For
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
, we see that the bad pairs consist of duplicates
(v,1),(v,2)
of the same vertex from
\mathscr{H}
. In fact, the
(r-1)
-codegree of
(v,1),(v,2)
is exactly
D^{\prime}
. If the independent process chooses
(v,1)
to go into the independent set, this immediately removes all edges containing
(v,2)
. Thus
(v,2)
will remain open until it is inevitably also chosen to be in the independent set. Likewise, if
(v,1)
gets closed then
(v,2)
gets closed simultaneously. Thus, the fate of
(v,1)
(i.e. whether or not it is in our final independent set) is exactly the same as
(v,2)
. This is the most extreme possible case illustrating the correlation between a bad pair of vertices. We also mention this example because even if
\mathscr{H}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
,
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
does not. However
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}
still satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1.10
.

Notation and conventions

In this paper we use standard asymptotic notation

. We use the notation

. I.e.,
\frac{a}{b}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
We also use expressions with the symbol “

b\geq 0

2.
The good event

Like in
[
BB16
]
, our random greedy independent set should behave asymptotically like a binomial random set (except for the fact that a binomial random set may not be an independent set in
\mathcal{H}
).
Recall that we use the convention that, if we arrive at a step in the process
i
such that
\mathcal{H}_{i}
that has edges
e,e^{\prime}
such that
e\subseteq e^{\prime}
then we remove the larger edge
e^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{H}
. This choice ends up having no impact on the process as the presence of
e
ensures that we never have
e^{\prime}\subseteq I(j)
. Furthermore, we use the notation
\Delta X\coloneqq X(i+1)-X(i)
to denote the one step change in the random variable
X
. As every expectation taken in this section is conditional on the first
i
steps of the algorithm, we suppress the conditioning. That is, we simply write
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
instead of
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]
where
\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots
is the natural filtration generated by the algorithm.

We begin by providing intuition for the analysis to come. We will make some unjustified (and even not-quite-true) statements in this heuristic discussion, resuming full rigor in the next subsection (
2.1
). Our analysis of the process will necessarily fall apart before our independent set contains very many vertices (compared to the total number of vertices
N
). The analysis falls apart for good reason: almost all the vertices will be closed by that point. The fate of an individual vertex is far more likely to be that it becomes closed than that it is chosen to be in the independent set. Thus, we are motivated to heuristically model the way vertices become closed. Of course,
v
becomes closed when it is in an edge
e
with
e\setminus\{v\}\subseteq I(i)
. We heuristically use a binomial random set to model
I(i)
.
Let
X_{i}
be our binomial random set, where each vertex is in
X_{i}
, independently, with probability

p=p_{i}\coloneqq\frac{i}{N}\,.

This probability is chosen so that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|]=i=|I(i)|
.
Of course, depending on one’s purpose, using
X_{i}
to model
I(i)
may be a bad idea: in particular
X_{i}
might contain edges of
\mathscr{H}
, while
I(i)
of course does not. However, since the event that there exists an edge with
all but one vertex
in
X_{i}
does not seem correlated with the event that an edge has
all vertices
in
X_{i}
, using this model to predict the closure of vertices is a bit more believable.

Let
v
be a fixed vertex. The expected number of witnesses to the vertex
v
being closed, that is, the expected number of edges
e\in\mathcal{H}
such that
v\in e
and
e\backslash\{v\}\subseteq S_{i}
, is

Dp^{r-1}=D\left(\frac{i}{N}\right)^{r-1}\,.

To simulate our random greedy process by a continuous time process, we parameterize a Poisson random variable by this expectation. Thus we introduce “scaled time”, letting
t_{i}^{r-1}\coloneqq D\left(\frac{i}{N}\right)^{r-1}
, so that

(2.1)

t_{i}\coloneqq\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\cdot i}{N}\,.

Going forward we let
t=t_{i}
(suppressing the subscript) unless the step
i
is noted otherwise. We will define a family of random variables that all depend on
i
, and claim that a.a.s. each such variable
Z=Z(i)
stays close to an appropriate deterministic function
z=z(t)
. Justifying this claim will involve investigating how
Z
changes over time, and for our approximation to hold we should have

(2.2)

\mathbb{E}[\Delta Z(i)]\approx\Delta z(t)\approx z^{\prime}(t)\Delta t=z^{\prime}(t)(t_{i+1}-t_{i})=\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}z^{\prime}(t)

In continuizing the process, we think of the number of times a vertex
v
is closed as being distributed according to the Poisson distribution. Thus, the probability that this quantity is zero for a fixed vertex (i.e. the vertex is open) is given by

(2.3)

q=q(t)\coloneqq e^{-t^{r-1}}

which allows us to think of
q
as the probability a vertex is open in this continuous time setting.

As our main goal is to prove dynamic concentration of
|V(i)|
, which is the number of vertices that remain in the hypergraph in step
i
, it will be necessary to track the following variables: For every vertex
v\in V(i)
and
\ell=2,\ldots,r
, we define
d_{\ell}(v)=d_{\ell}(v,i)
to be the number of edges of cardinality
\ell
in
\mathcal{H}(i)
that contain
v
.
Treating
q
as the probability that a vertex is in
V(i)
(rather than selected to belong to the independent set) we should have
|V(i)|\approx

q(t)N
. Furthermore, if we heuristically assume that the distribution of the number of open vertices is binomially distributed with parameter
q
,
d_{\ell}(v)\coloneqq d_{\ell}(v,i)
should follow the trajectory

(2.4)

s_{\ell}(t)\coloneqq D\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}q^{\ell-1}p^{r-\ell}=\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}t^{r-\ell}q^{\ell-1}.

In what is to come, we separate the accumulated positive contributions to
d_{\ell}(v)
,
d^{+}_{\ell}(v)
, from the negative contributions,
d^{-}_{\ell}(v)
. We write
d_{r}(v)=D-d_{r}^{-}(v)
, and for
\ell<r
we write
d_{\ell}(v)\coloneqq d_{\ell}(v,i)=d_{\ell}(v,0)+d_{\ell}^{+}(v,i)-d_{\ell}^{-}(v,i)
, where
d_{\ell}^{+}(v),d_{\ell}^{-}(v)
are non-negative random variables which we use to track the number of edges of cardinality
\ell
containing
v
that are created and destroyed, respectively, through the first
i
steps of the process. We define

(2.5)

s_{\ell}^{+}(t)\coloneqq D^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\ell s_{\ell+1}(\tau)}{q(\tau)}d\tau\quad s_{\ell}^{-}(t)\coloneqq D^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(\ell-1)s_{\ell}(\tau)s_{2}(\tau)}{q(\tau)}d\tau\,,

and claim that we should have
d_{\ell}^{\pm}\approx s_{\ell}^{\pm}
. Note that
s_{\ell}
satisfies the differential equation

s_{\ell}^{\prime}=\frac{\ell s_{\ell+1}-(\ell-1)s_{\ell}s_{2}}{q}=\left(s_{\ell}^{+}\right)^{\prime}-\left(s_{\ell}^{-}\right)^{\prime}

and so
s_{\ell}=s_{\ell}^{+}-s_{\ell}^{-}
, (and thus we expect
d_{\ell}\approx s_{\ell}
).
We claim that the choice of
s_{\ell}^{\pm}
is natural in light of the main contributions to
d_{\ell}
. Indeed, for each edge in
d_{\ell+1}(v)
there are
\ell
vertices that could be put into our independent set which would cause this edge to now be in
d_{\ell}(v)
, each one occurring with probability
1/|V(i)|
. Thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta d_{\ell}^{+}\right]\approx\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\cdot\ell d_{\ell+1}\approx\frac{1}{Nq}\cdot\ell s_{\ell+1}=\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}\,,

which is what we would expect (see (
2.2
)). For negative contributions we will see that the main way for edges in
d_{\ell}(v)
to be destroyed is for their vertices (other than
v
) to get closed. Each such edge has
\ell-1
vertices other than
v
and the number of ways to close each
v^{\prime}
is
d_{2}(v^{\prime})\approx s_{2}
, each occurring with probability
1/|V(i)|
. Thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta d_{\ell}^{-}\right]\approx\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\cdot(\ell-1)d_{\ell}d_{2}\approx\frac{1}{Nq}\cdot(\ell-1)s_{\ell}s_{2}=\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}(s_{\ell}^{-})^{\prime}.

Using (

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
2\leq a<b\leq r
, and recall that
D_{a\uparrow b}\coloneqq D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b)\lambda}.
Then,

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\leq\tau\text{ and }A\in\binom{V(i)}{a}\text{ such that }d_{A\uparrow b}(i)\geq D_{a\uparrow b}\right)=o(1)\,.

Proof.

We define variables
Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)

Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}d_{A\uparrow b}(i)-\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{b-a+1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}\cdot i&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\text{ holds }\\
Y_{A\uparrow b}(i-1)&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

We will bound

by appealing to the following lemma due to Freedman
[
Fre75

Lemma 3.2

(Freedman)
.

Let

be a supermartingale, with
\Delta Y(i)\leq C
for all

, and

W(i)\coloneqq\sum_{k\leq i}\operatorname{Var}\left[\Delta Y(k)\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right].

Then

\mathbb{P}[\exists i:W(i)\leq w,Y(i)-Y(0)\geq d]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2(w+Cd)}\right)\,.

To show that
Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)
is a supermartingale, it suffices to observe

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta d_{A\uparrow b}(i)\right]=\frac{(b+1-a)d_{A\uparrow b+1}}{|V(i)|}\leq\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{b-a+1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}.

For our application of Freedman’s theorem (
3.2
), we can set
C=D_{a+1\uparrow b+1}=D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b-2)\lambda}
. We remind the reader that all expectations are conditioned on the history of the process (even though we omit this conditioning in our notation), and furthermore we will start using this convention on variance as well. With that in mind, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}ar\left[\Delta Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)\right]=\mathbb{V}ar\left[\Delta d_{A\uparrow b}(i)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta d_{A\uparrow b}(i))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta d_{A\uparrow b}(i)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{2b-2a+1}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}.

Using (
2.12
) we have
W({i_{\max}})\leq i_{\max}\cdot\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{2b-2a+1}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\leq D^{\frac{2b-2a}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\log(1/\phi),
so we can take
w
to be the latter expression. Setting
d=D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}
and noting that
Y_{A\uparrow b}(0)\leq\Delta_{a}(\mathscr{H}^{(b)})\leq\phi D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}
by Condition (

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P\left[\exists i\leq i_{\max}:Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)-\phi D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}\geq d\right]&\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2(w+Cd)}\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left(-\frac{D^{\frac{2(b-a)}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-2)\lambda}}{2\left(D^{\frac{2b-2a}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\log(1/\phi)+D^{\frac{2(b-a)}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\right)}\right)\\
&<\exp\left(-\phi^{-\lambda/2}\right)=o(N^{-r}),\end{split}

where on the last line we used that

and

. The probability above is small enough to beat a union bound over all

choices for the set
A
, and so a.a.s. the above event does not happen for any

. In other words,
Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)-\phi D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}<d
for all

. Thus we have

d_{A\uparrow b}(i)\leq\phi D^{\frac{b-a}{r-1}}+d+\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{b-a+1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}\cdot i_{\max}<D_{a\uparrow b}.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Recall that

We define variables

containing some vertex

\displaystyle\begin{split}P\left[\exists i\leq i_{\max}:Y_{\ell}(v,i)\geq d\right]&\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2(w+Cd)}\right)\\

\phi^{1-(2r-2)\lambda}

2f_{2}-f_{V}^{\prime}=-\Omega(1)
by Lemma
C.1
part (1). Therefore
Z_{V}
is a supermartingale.

To check that
\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{--}\cr$Z$\cr}}}_{V}
is a submartingale, one can run through the derivation of (
4.5
) mutatis mutandis, using
\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{--}\cr$Z$\cr}}}_{V}
instead of
Z_{V}
and lower bounds instead of upper bounds, to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{--}\cr$Z$\cr}}}_{V}\right]\geq-D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}(2f_{2}-f_{V})+O\left(D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2)\lambda}\right)\geq 0.

4.2.
Showing
Z_{\ell}^{+}
is a supermartingale

In several ways this will be similar to what we just did for
Z_{V}
. In particular, in the definition (
4.1
), we have a case where
\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}=0
and so we can restrict our attention to the other case. Going forward it will be important to recall that
\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}\coloneqq\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(i)=Z_{\ell}^{+}(i+1)-Z_{\ell}^{+}(i)
, i.e., we are considering the change in
Z_{\ell}^{+}
from step
i
to
i+1
. In doing so, we assume we are in the good event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, and when we estimate
\mathbb{E}[\Delta d_{\ell}^{+}]
we ignore any possible contribution coming from the possibility that the
i^{th}
vertex to be chosen,
v_{i}
, is such that
v_{i}\in B(v)
(since in that case we would have
v\in M(i+1)
and thus
\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}=0
). We have

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)\right]=\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)}x_{u}

where
x_{u}
is the change in
Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)
if
v_{i}=u
. By definition (
4.1
),
x_{u}=0
if
u\in B(v)\cup\{v\}
since then
v\in M(i+1)\cup I(i+1)
. We write (explanation follows)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}\left(d_{\{v,u\}\uparrow\ell+1}(i)-\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}-\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}f_{\ell}^{\prime}+O\left(D^{\frac{2}{r-1}}N^{-2}((s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime\prime}+D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}^{\prime\prime})\right)\right)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell d_{\ell+1}(v)}{|V(i)|}-\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}-\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}f_{\ell}^{\prime}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{2-(2r-2\ell-1)\lambda}+D^{\frac{\ell+1}{r-1}}N^{-2}\phi^{-\lambda}\right)

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(s_{\ell+1}+2D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell+1})}{Nq-N\phi^{\delta}f_{V}}-\frac{\ell s_{\ell+1}}{Nq}-\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}f_{\ell}^{\prime}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{1-\lambda}\right)

(4.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}\cdot\left(2\ell q^{-1}f_{\ell+1}+\ell\binom{r-1}{\ell}t^{r-\ell-1}q^{\ell-2}f_{V}-f_{\ell}^{\prime}\right)+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{2\delta-5\lambda}\right).

To justify line (
4.7
), first we estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}d_{\{v,u\}\uparrow\ell+1}(i)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus\{v\}}d_{\{v,u\}\uparrow\ell+1}(i)+O\left(\frac{|B(v)|D_{2\uparrow\ell+1}}{|V(i)|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell d_{\ell+1}(v)}{|V(i)|}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{2-(2r-2\ell-1)\lambda}\right).

To finish justifying (
4.7
), we use Lemma
C.1
to bound the error terms:

(4.10)

\frac{|B(v)\cup\{v\}|}{|V(i)|}\left(\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}+\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}f_{\ell}^{\prime}\right)+O\left(D^{\frac{2}{r-1}}N^{-2}((s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime\prime}+D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}^{\prime\prime})\right)=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell+1}{r-1}}N^{-2}\phi^{-\lambda}\right).

To justify line (
4.8
), we use our bounds on
d_{\ell+1}(v),|V(i)|
and recall by (
2.5
) that
(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}=D^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\ell s_{\ell+1}/q
. The big-O term on line (
4.8
) is just simplifying the previous big-O term using (
1.1
). To justify line (
4.9
) we will use the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

For any real numbers
x,y,\varepsilon_{x},\varepsilon_{y}
, if we have
x,y\neq 0
and
\left|\frac{\varepsilon_{x}}{x}\right|,\left|\frac{\varepsilon_{y}}{y}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then

\frac{x+\varepsilon_{x}}{y+\varepsilon_{y}}-\frac{x}{y}=\frac{\varepsilon_{x}}{y}-\frac{x\varepsilon_{y}}{y^{2}}+O\left(\frac{y\varepsilon_{x}\varepsilon_{y}+x\varepsilon_{y}^{2}}{y^{3}}\right)

With this lemma, we turn to (
4.8
) and see that

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(s_{\ell+1}+2D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell+1})}{Nq-N\phi^{\delta}f_{V}}-\frac{\ell s_{\ell+1}}{Nq}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ell D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell+1}}{Nq}+\frac{\ell s_{\ell+1}N\phi^{\delta}f_{V}}{N^{2}q^{2}}+O\left(\frac{N^{2}qD^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell+1}\phi^{\delta}f_{V}+s_{\ell+1}N^{2}\phi^{2\delta}f_{V}^{2}}{N^{3}q^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ell D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell+1}}{Nq}+\frac{\ell s_{\ell+1}N\phi^{\delta}f_{V}}{N^{2}q^{2}}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{2\delta-5\lambda}\right)

where on the second line we have used Lemma
C.1
. This justifies (
4.9
).

Now observe that (
4.9
) is negative since
\phi^{\delta}
is much larger than
\phi^{2\delta-5\lambda}
and

2\ell q^{-1}f_{\ell+1}+\ell\binom{r-1}{\ell}t^{r-\ell-1}q^{\ell-2}f_{V}-f_{\ell}^{\prime}=-\Omega(1)

by Lemma
C.1
parts (2) and (3). Therefore
Z_{\ell}^{+}
is a supermartingale. Similarly,
\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{--}\cr$Z$\cr}}}_{\ell}^{+}
is a submartingale.

4.3.
Showing
Z_{\ell}^{-}
is a supermartingale

The main term in the expected change of
d_{\ell}^{-}(v)
comes from the selection of vertices
y
for which there exists a vertex
x
such that
\{y,x\}\in\mathscr{H}(i)
, and there is an edge
e
counted by
d_{\ell}(v)
such that
x\in e
. However, here we must also account for the convention of removing redundant edges. For a fixed edge
e
counted by
d_{\ell}(v)
, the selection of any vertex in the following set results in the removal of
e
from this count:

\{y\in V(i):\exists A\subset e\text{ such that }A\neq\{v\}\text{ but }A\cup\{y\}\in\mathscr{H}(i)\}

(Note that this is essentially restating the convention that we remove edges that contain other edges). Together, the sums

\sum_{x\in e\backslash\{v\}}d_{2}(x)+\sum_{A\subseteq e,|A|\geq 2}d_{A\uparrow|A|+1}

count each
y
with the property that the choice of
y
causes the removal of
e
from the count
d_{\ell}(v)
at least once and at most
O(1)
many times. The number of
y
that are counted more than once in the first sum is at most
\binom{\ell-1}{2}C_{2,2\rightarrow 1}
.

Let
y_{u}
be the change in
Z_{\ell}^{-}(v)
if
v_{i}=u
. By definition (
4.1
), we have

y_{u}=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }u\in B(v)\cup\{v\}\\
\Delta\Big[d_{\ell}^{-}(v|u)-s_{\ell}^{-}-D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}\Big]&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\Delta d_{\ell}^{-}(v|u)
above is interpreted as what the change in
d_{\ell}^{-}(v)
would be if we chose
v_{i}=u
. With this in mind we have

\Delta d_{\ell}^{-}(v|u)=d_{\{u,v\}\uparrow\ell}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}c_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}(v,u).

Indeed, the first term accounts for all the edges containing both
u
and
v
, and the sum accounts for the edges in
d_{\ell}(v)
which get removed due to the fact that they would contain smaller edges after choosing
v_{i}=u
.
Given this, we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Z_{\ell}^{-}(v)\right]
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)}y_{u}=\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}\Delta\Big[d_{\ell}^{-}(v|u)-s_{\ell}^{-}-D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}\left[d_{\{u,v\}\uparrow\ell}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}c_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}(v,u)-\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}\left((s_{\ell}^{-})^{\prime}+D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}^{\prime}\right)+O\left(\frac{D^{\frac{2}{r-1}}}{N^{2}}\left((s_{\ell}^{-})^{\prime\prime}+\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}\right)\right)\right]

(4.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|V(i)|}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}\left[d_{\{u,v\}\uparrow\ell}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}c_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}(v,u)-\frac{(\ell-1)s_{\ell}\cdot s_{2}}{Nq}-\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}f_{\ell}^{\prime}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell+1}{r-1}}N^{-2}\phi^{-\lambda}\right)\right],

where on the last line we used (
2.5
) to get
(s_{\ell}^{-})^{\prime}
and Lemma
C.1
to simplify the big-O term. Now

(4.12)

\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}d_{\{u,v\}\uparrow\ell}=O(d_{\ell}(v))=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\right)=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi\right).

The last expression follows from our assumption that
D>\phi^{-r}
, since
D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}<D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\frac{r}{r-1}}<D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi
.
since each edge of
d_{\ell}(v)
is counted for at most
\ell-1
vertices
u
. Also, as we will explain,

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}c_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}(v,u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus\{v\}}c_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}(v,u)+O\left(D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi\cdot D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-4)\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in d_{\ell}(v)}\sum_{w\in e\setminus\{v\}}d_{2}(w)+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2\ell+2)\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in d_{\ell}(v)\setminus d_{\ell}^{\prime}(v)}\sum_{w\in e\setminus\{v\}}d_{2}(w)+{O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(6r-2\ell-4)\lambda}\right)}

\displaystyle\subseteq(\ell-1)\left(s_{\ell}\pm 2D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}+O\left(D^{\prime}_{\ell}\right)\right)\left(s_{2}\pm 2D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{2}\right)+{O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(6r-2\ell-4)\lambda}\right)}

(4.13)

\displaystyle\subseteq(\ell-1)\left(s_{\ell}\pm 2D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}\right)\left(s_{2}\pm 2D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{2}\right)+{O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(6r-2\ell-4)\lambda}\right)}

Indeed, on the second line we used Conditions
4
and
2.17
. To justify the third line, note that for
k=1
we have

\sum_{u\in V(i)\setminus\{v\}}c_{\ell,2\rightarrow 1}(v,u)=\sum_{e\in d_{\ell}(v)}\sum_{w\in e\setminus\{v\}}d_{2}(w)

and to take care of
k\geq 2
we note that

\sum_{k=2}^{\ell-1}c_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}(v,u)=O\left(d_{\ell}(v)\sum_{k=2}^{\ell-1}D_{k\uparrow k+1}\right)=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2\ell+2)\lambda}\right).

To justify the fourth line, note that the dropped terms contribute
O(D^{\prime}_{\ell}\cdot D_{1\uparrow 2})=O(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(6r-2\ell-4)\lambda}).
The fifth line follows from our bounds on
d_{\ell}(v)
and
d_{2}(w)
, and the last line is from our bound on
D^{\prime}_{\ell}
.

Picking back up from (
4.11
) and using (
4.12
), (
4.13
), and (
2.14
) we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Z_{\ell}^{-}(v)\right]
is at most (explanation follows)

\displaystyle\frac{(\ell-1)\left(s_{\ell}+2D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}\right)\left(s_{2}+2D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{2}\right)+{O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(6r-2\ell-4)\lambda}\right)}}{Nq-N\phi^{\delta}f_{V}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{|V(i)\setminus B(v)\setminus\{v\}|}{|V(i)|}\left[\frac{(\ell-1)s_{\ell}\cdot s_{2}}{Nq}+\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}f_{\ell}^{\prime}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell+1}{r-1}}N^{-2}\phi^{-\lambda}\right)\right]

(4.14)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(\ell-1)\left[D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}s_{2}f_{\ell}+D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}s_{\ell}f_{2}\right]}{Nq}+\frac{(\ell-1)\phi^{\delta}s_{\ell}s_{2}f_{V}}{Nq^{2}}-\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}^{\prime}}{N}+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{2\delta-5\lambda}\right)

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}}{N}\bigg[2(\ell-1)\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}t^{r-\ell}q^{\ell-2}f_{2}+2(\ell-1)(r-1)t^{r-2}f_{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+(\ell-1)(r-1)\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}t^{2r-\ell-2}q^{\ell-2}f_{V}-f_{\ell}^{\prime}\bigg]+O\left(D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}N^{-1}\phi^{2\delta-5\lambda}\right).

Indeed, to get line (
4.14
) we use Lemma
4.1
, Lemma
C.1
, Condition (
4
) and line (
1.1
). Line (
4.15
) is just algebra. Now observe that (
4.15
) is negative since
\phi^{\delta}
is much larger than
\phi^{2\delta-5\lambda}
and the quantity in square brackets is

2(\ell-1)\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}t^{r-\ell}q^{\ell-2}f_{2}+2(\ell-1)(r-1)t^{r-2}f_{\ell}+(\ell-1)(r-1)\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}t^{2r-\ell-2}q^{\ell-2}f_{V}-f_{\ell}^{\prime}=-\Omega(1)

by Lemma
C.1
parts (4), (5) and (6). Therefore
Z_{\ell}^{-}
is a supermartingale. Similarly,
\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raisebox{0.86108pt}{--}\cr$Z$\cr}}}_{\ell}^{-}
is a submartingale.

4.4.
Applying martingale variation inequalities

We complete the proof by applying martingale variation inequalities to prove that
Z_{V}
and
Z_{\ell}^{\pm}
remain negative a.a.s. We will apply the following lemmas (which both follow from Hoeffding
[
Hoe63
]
):

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X_{i}
be a supermartingale such that

|\Delta X|\leq c_{i}
for all
i
. Then

\mathbb{P}(X_{m}-X_{0}>d)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq m}c_{i}^{2}}\right)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X_{i}
be a supermartingale such that
-B\leq\Delta X\leq b
for all
i
, for some
b<\frac{B}{10}
. Then for any
d<bm

we have

\mathbb{P}(X_{m}-X_{0}>d)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{3mbB}\right)

We apply Lemma
4.2
to the supermartingale
Z_{V}(i)
. Note that if the vertex
v
is inserted to the independent set at step
i
then
\Delta V(i)=-1-d_{2}(v).
Now we have

\displaystyle|\Delta Z_{V}|

\displaystyle\leq|\Delta V(i)|+|\Delta Nq|+|\Delta N\phi^{\delta}f_{V}|=O\left(D_{1\uparrow 2}+D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}q^{\prime}+D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{V}^{\prime}\right)=O\left(D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-4)\lambda}\right)\,,

where the last equality holds by Lemma
C.1
.

As we have that
Z_{V}(0)=-N\phi^{\delta}
, by definition, the probability that
Z_{V}
is positive at time step
\tau
is at most

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\frac{\left(N\phi^{\delta}\right)^{2}}{ND^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\log^{\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\left[D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-4)\lambda}\right]^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(ND^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{2\delta+(4r-8)\lambda}\log^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\phi^{-1+2\delta+(4r-8)\lambda}\log^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\right)\right\}=o(1),

where on the second line we used
N=\Omega\left(D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\phi^{-1}\right)
from (
1.1
).

For our upper bound on
d_{\ell}^{+}(v)
we apply
4.3
to the supermartingale
Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)
. Note that we have either
\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)=0
due to the supermartingale beng frozen, or else we can upper bound
\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)
as follows:

(4.16)

\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)=\Delta d^{+}_{\ell}(v)-\Delta s^{+}_{\ell}-\Delta D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}f_{\ell}\leq D_{2\uparrow\ell+1}=O(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-4)\lambda}).

Note that above, we used the fact that
s^{+}_{\ell},f_{\ell}
are increasing.
For a lower bound on
\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)
, using
\Delta d^{+}_{\ell}(v)\geq 0
and Taylor’s theorem, we obtain

(4.17)

\Delta Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)\geq-O\left(\left[\left(s_{\ell}^{+}\right)^{\prime}+D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}f_{\ell}^{\prime}\right]\frac{D^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{N}\right)\geq-O\left(\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}\right)

We have
Z_{\ell}^{+}(0)=-D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}
. For our present application of Lemma
4.3
we let
B=O\left(\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}\right),b=D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-4)\lambda}
(from (
4.16
), (
4.17
)),
d=D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}
and
m=i_{\max}
. The assumptions of Lemma
4.3
hold since
\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}=o\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-4)\lambda}\right)
using that (
1.1
) and
r\geq 3
, and
D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}=o\left(\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}\cdot i_{\max}\right)
. So the probability that
Z_{\ell}^{+}(v)
is positive at step
\tau
is at most

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-{\Omega}\left(\frac{\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}\right)^{2}}{ND^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\log^{\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\cdot\frac{1}{N}D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\cdot D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-4)\lambda}}\right)\right\}=\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\phi^{-1+2\delta+(2r-4)\lambda}\log^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\right)\right\},

which is small enough to beat a union bound over
O(N)
choices for
v
and
\ell
. The lower bound on
d_{\ell}^{+}(v)
follows similarly. For our upper bound on
d_{\ell}^{-}(v)
we apply Lemma
4.3
to the supermartingale
Z_{\ell}^{-}
. Note that

(4.18)

-O\left(\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}\right)<\Delta Z_{\ell}^{-}(v)<O\left(\sum_{1\leq k\leq\ell-1}C_{\ell,k+1\rightarrow k}\right)=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-8)\lambda}\right)

where the lower bound argument follows similarly to (
4.17
), and the upper bound accounts for the number of edges, which, after adding one of their vertices to the independent set, is contained in an edge of size
\ell
containing
v
. We have
Z_{\ell}^{-}(0)=-D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}
. The rest of this application of Lemma
4.3
is very similar to the previous one. We let
B=O\left(\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}\right),b=D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-8)\lambda}
,
d=D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}
and
m=i_{\max}
. The assumptions of Lemma
4.3
hold since
\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}=o\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-8)\lambda}\right)
by (
1.1
) and the fact that
r\geq 3
, and
D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}=o\left(\frac{D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}}{N}\cdot i_{\max}\right)
. So the probability that
Z_{\ell}^{-}(v)
is positive at step
\tau
is at most

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-{\Omega}\left(\frac{\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{\delta}\right)^{2}}{ND^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\log^{\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\cdot\frac{1}{N}D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\cdot D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-8)\lambda}}\right)\right\}=\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\phi^{-1+2\delta+(4r-8)\lambda}\log^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi)\right)\right\},

which is small enough to beat a union bound over
O(N)
choices for
v
and
\ell
. The lower bound on
d_{\ell}^{-}(v)
follows similarly.

5.
Concluding remarks

There are quite a few natural next directions to follow. Here we consider the case in which
w
is small enough relative to
n
such that the matching sunflowers, and those with relatively small kernels, are the dominant type. Thus, it is of interest to see what happens when this is not the case. In fact, when
\frac{n}{w}<r
any existing sunflower must have a non-empty kernel, so perhaps the regime where
w\in(\frac{n}{r},n-1]
would be a natural next step.
In addition, the algorithm described in
[
AE92
]
includes an additional backtracking step. The authors use a basic learning algorithm to help determine which edges should be removed in the backtracking step, and also use it to bias the algorithm towards choosing specific sets. Thus, it would be interesting if one could give a proof related to this more involved algorithm.

The sunflower-free process

We use this section to show that the Sunflower Hypergraph satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.10

\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
is the hypergraph whose vertex set
\mathscr{V}_{n,w}

\mathscr{E}_{w,r}
is an
r

\mathcal{K}_{n,w}

\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
is

In the equation displayed above,

represents the size of the kernel. Since
\mathscr{H}_{n,w}
is uniform and regular, Conditions (
1
) and (
3

\phi>0

Since we assume

In this regime, we will see that the “matching sunflowers”, or the sunflowers with empty kernels, contribute the most to the degree.
We now estimate
N

Claim
1
.

We have

(A.3)

\begin{split}N&=\operatorname{exp}\left\{w+w\log\left(\frac{n}{w}\right)-\frac{w^{2}}{2n}+o\left(\frac{w^{2}}{n}\right)\right\},\\

Proof of Claim
1
.

Recall Stirling’s approximation, which says

(A.4)

n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}.

Thus we have

(A.5)

\begin{split}N&=\binom{n}{w}=\frac{n!}{w!(n-w)!}=\frac{\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}}{\left(\frac{w}{e}\right)^{w}\left(\frac{n-w}{e}\right)^{n-w}}\exp\{O(\log n)\}\\
&=\operatorname{exp}\left\{n\log n-w\log w-(n-w)\log(n-w)+O(\log n)\right\}\\
&=\operatorname{exp}\left\{n\log n-w\log w-(n-w)\left[\log n-\frac{w}{n}-\frac{w^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{w^{3}}{n^{3}}\right)\right]+O(\log n)\right\}\\
&=\operatorname{exp}\left\{w+w\log\left(\frac{n}{w}\right)-\frac{w^{2}}{2n}+o\left(\frac{w^{2}}{n}\right)\right\},\end{split}

where on the third line we used the fact that when

we have
\log(x+y)=\log x+\frac{y}{x}-\frac{y^{2}}{2x^{2}}+O\left(\frac{y^{3}}{x^{3}}\right).
This follows from the Taylor series
\log(1+t)=t-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}+\cdots
.
Thus our estimate of

is justified. Now we estimate

Let

\begin{split}R(s):=&\frac{D_{s+1}}{D_{s}}=\frac{\binom{w}{s+1}\frac{(n-w)_{(r-1)(w-s-1)}}{[(w-s-1)!]^{r-1}}}{\binom{w}{s}\frac{(n-w)_{(r-1)(w-s)}}{[(w-s)!]^{r-1}}}\\
&=\frac{(w-s)^{r}}{(s+1)\bigg(n-w-(r-1)(w-s)\bigg)_{r-1}}\end{split}

where we use
(x)_{n}\coloneqq x(x-1)(x-2)\dots(x-n+1)

We claim that

\max\{D_{s}:0\leq s\leq w-1\}=\begin{cases}D_{0}&\text{ if }\alpha\leq\frac{r-1}{r},\\
D_{s^{*}}\text{ for some }s^{*}\approx n^{r\alpha-r+1}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We now estimate the largest possible

\displaystyle D_{s^{*}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w!(n-w)!}{(r-1)!s^{*}![(w-s^{*})!]^{r}[(n-rw+(r-1)s^{*}]!}

\displaystyle\qquad-(n-rw+(r-1)s^{*})\log[n-rw+(r-1)s^{*}]+O(\log n)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg\{w\log w+(n-w)\left[\log n-\frac{w}{n}-\frac{w^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{w^{3}}{n^{3}}\right)\right]-r(w-s^{*})\left[\log w-O\left(\frac{s^{*}}{w}\right)\right]

\end{split}

Therefore, applying
[

K
, then
\mathbb{H}_{n,m,w}
contains a sunflower with kernel size
t
w.h.p.. In particular notice that this value is
Ke^{2w-t}r\log r
for a matching of size
r
.
∎

Appendix C
Analytic considerations

In this section we discuss our deterministic functions
q,s_{\ell}^{\pm},f_{V},f_{\ell}
and the choice of constants.

Before our next lemma, recall from (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and (
2.12
) that

q(t)=e^{-t^{r-1}},\qquad s_{\ell}(t)=\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}t^{r-\ell}q^{\ell-1},\qquad t_{max}=\zeta\log^{\frac{1}{r-1}}(1/\phi).

Our error functions will have the form

(C.1)

\begin{gathered}f_{\ell}=\left(1+t^{r-\ell+2}\right)q^{\ell}\exp\left(\alpha t+\beta t^{r-1}\right)\\
f_{V}=\left(1+t^{2}\right)q^{2}\exp\left(\alpha t+\beta t^{r-1}\right).\end{gathered}

Lemma C.1
.

For any fixed
r\geq 2
,
\delta=1/10
and
\lambda=1/100r
there exist positive constants
\zeta,\alpha,\beta
satisfying the following for all
t\in[0,t_{max}]
.

(1)

f_{V}^{\prime}>3f_{2}

(2)

f_{\ell}^{\prime}>5\ell q^{-1}f_{\ell+1}

(3)

f_{\ell}^{\prime}>2\ell\binom{r-1}{\ell}t^{r-\ell-1}q^{\ell-2}f_{V}

(4)

f_{\ell}^{\prime}>7(\ell-1)\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}t^{r-\ell}q^{\ell-2}f_{2}

(5)

f_{\ell}^{\prime}>6(\ell-1)(r-1)t^{r-2}f_{\ell}

(6)

f_{\ell}^{\prime}>3(\ell-1)(r-1)\binom{r-1}{\ell-1}t^{2r-\ell-2}q^{\ell-2}f_{V}

(7)

\phi^{\delta}f_{V}=o(q)

(8)

q-\phi^{\delta}f_{V}\geq\phi^{\lambda}

(9)

q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime}=O\left(1\right)

(10)

s_{\ell},(s_{\ell}^{\pm})^{\prime},(s_{\ell}^{\pm})^{\prime\prime}=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\right)

(11)

f_{\ell},f_{\ell}^{\prime},f_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}=O(\phi^{-\lambda})

(12)

f_{V},f_{V}^{\prime},f_{V}^{\prime\prime}=O(\phi^{-\lambda})

Proof.

First we note that we can choose
\alpha,\beta
to satisfy (1)–(6). Indeed, these bounds are precisely the same as the variation equations in the first author and Bohman
[
BB16
]
(see equations (13)–(18) in that paper). Finally we will choose
\zeta>0
to be sufficiently small. Since we choose
\zeta
last, it can depend on
r,\alpha,\beta,\delta,\lambda
. Now we justify (7)–(12). Recall from (
2.3
) that
q\coloneqq e^{-t^{r-1}}
, thus

(7)

\frac{\phi^{\delta}f_{V}}{q}=\phi^{\delta}(1+t^{2})\exp(\alpha t+(\beta-1)t^{r-1})=O\left(\phi^{\delta}\log(1/\phi)\phi^{-(\alpha+\beta-1)\zeta^{r-1}}\right)=o(1),

where the final bound follows since we choose some small
\zeta>0
after having chosen the other constants. For the rest of the proof we will use similar reasoning without comment.

(8)

This follows from (7) and the fact that
q(t)\geq q(t_{\max})=\phi^{\zeta^{r-1}}.

(9)

q^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime\prime}
both have the following form: a polynomial in
t
times
q
. For any polynomial
P(t)
, the continuous and differentiable function
P(t)q(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, which suffices to conclude that
P(t)q(t)=O(1)
for
t\in[0,\infty)
.

(10)

s_{\ell}(t)
can be written as
D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}P(t)[q(t)]^{\ell-1}
for a polynomial
P
, and so by the same reasoning as the previous bound we get
s_{\ell}(t)=O\left(D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\right)
. Likewise by (
2.5
) we have

(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}=\frac{D^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}\ell s_{\ell+1}}{q}=D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}P(t)[q(t)]^{\ell-1}

for some polynomial
P(t)
and so we get the bound on
(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime}
. Similarly
(s_{\ell}^{-})^{\prime},(s_{\ell}^{+})^{\prime\prime},(s_{\ell}^{-})^{\prime\prime}
all have the same form and so their bounds follow as well.

(11)

f_{\ell},f_{\ell}^{\prime},f_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
all have the following form:
P(t)\exp(\alpha t+(\beta-\ell)t^{r-1})
, where
P(t)
is some polynomial in
t
. Thus,
P(t)
will be
polylog(\phi)
for all
t\leq t_{max}
, and we have

P(t)\exp(\alpha t+(\beta-\ell)t^{r-1})=O\left(polylog(\phi)\phi^{-(\alpha+\beta)\zeta^{r-1})}\right)=O(\phi^{-\lambda})

(12)

This follows similarly to the last bound.

∎

Appendix D
Deferred proofs of crude bounds

First, we show that Condition (
2.16
) holds until step
i_{\max}
a.a.s. for
a=1
. Note that the case
b=r
follows from Condition
1
and the fact that we never create new edges of size
r
. So we assume
b\leq r-1
.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
2\leq b\leq r-1
, and recall that
D_{1\uparrow b}\coloneqq D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-2b)\lambda}
. Then,

(D.1)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\leq\tau\text{ and }v\in V(i)\text{ such that }d_{b}(v,i)\geq D_{1\uparrow b}\right)=o(1)\,.

Proof.

We define variables
Y_{b}(v,i)
as follows.

Y_{b}(v,i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}d_{b}(v,i)-\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{b}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}\cdot i&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\text{ holds }\\
Y_{b}(v,i-1)&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

To show that
Y_{b}(v,i)
is a supermartingale, it suffices to observe

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta d_{b}(v,i)\right]\leq\frac{bd_{b+1}(v,i)}{|V(i)|}\leq\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{b}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}.

For our application of Freedman’s theorem (
3.2
), we can set
C=D_{2\uparrow b+1}=D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2b-2)\lambda}
. Now

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}ar\left[\Delta Y_{b}(v,i)\right]=\mathbb{V}ar\left[\Delta d_{b}(v,i)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta d_{b}(v,i))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta d_{b}(v,i)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{2b-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}

Using (
2.12
) we have
W({i_{\max}})\leq i_{\max}\cdot\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{2b-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\leq D^{\frac{2b-2}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\log(1/\phi)
, so we can take
w
to be the latter expression. Setting
d=\frac{1}{2}D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-2b)\lambda}
and noting that
Y_{b}(v,0)\leq\Delta_{1}(\mathscr{H}^{(b)})\leq\phi D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}
by Condition (
3
), Freedman’s theorem gives us that

(D.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P\left[\exists i\leq i_{\max}:Y_{A\uparrow b}(i)-\phi D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}\geq d\right]&\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2(w+Cd)}\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{D^{\frac{2(b-1)}{r-1}}\phi^{-(4r-4b)\lambda}}{D^{\frac{2b-2}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4b-3)\lambda}\log(1/\phi)+D^{\frac{2b-2}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-4b-2)\lambda}}\right)\right)\\
&<\exp\left(-\phi^{-\lambda/2}\right)=o(N^{-1}).\end{split}

If the unlikely event above does not happen, i.e. if
Y_{b}(v,i)-\phi D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}<d
for all
i\leq i_{\max}
, then

d_{b}(v,i)\leq\phi D^{\frac{b-1}{r-1}}+d+\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{b}{r-1}}\phi^{-(2r-2b-1)\lambda}\cdot i_{\max}<D_{1\uparrow b}.

Now (
D.1
) follows from the union bound over at most
N
choices for
v
.

∎

We next show that condition (
2.17
) also holds to step
i_{\max}
a.a.s.

Lemma D.2
.

Let
2\leq a,a^{\prime}\leq r
and
1\leq k<a,a^{\prime}
be fixed. Then, recalling that
C_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\coloneqq 2^{r}D^{\frac{a+a^{\prime}-k-2}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-2k-2)\lambda}
, we have that

(D.3)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\leq\tau\right.\mbox{ and a non-bad pair }v,v^{\prime}\mbox{ such that }\left.c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\geq C_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\right)=o(1)\,.

Proof.

We first note that the definition of the good event implies Lemma
D.2
except in the case
a=a^{\prime}=k+1
. To see this, suppose
a^{\prime}>k+1
and let
v,v^{\prime}
be any two vertices. Then we have

c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime}\right)\leq D_{1\uparrow a}\cdot\binom{a-1}{k}\cdot D_{k+1\uparrow a^{\prime}}\leq 2^{r}\cdot D^{\frac{a+a^{\prime}-k-2}{r-1}}\phi^{1-\left(4r-2a-2a^{\prime}\right)\lambda},

which gives the desired bound.
So we restrict our attention to the case
a=a^{\prime}=k+1
. The case of
k=r-1
holds by the assumption since
v,v^{\prime}
is not a bad pair, and thus their
(r-1)
-codegree is less than
\phi D
. Now we assume
k\leq r-2
.

We define variables
Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)
as follows.

Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\coloneqq\begin{cases}c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)-\frac{3\cdot 2^{r}}{N}D^{\frac{k+1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-1)\lambda}\cdot i&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\text{ holds }\\
Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}(v,v^{\prime},i-1)&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

We justify that
Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)
is a supermartingale. Note that
c_{k+1,k+1\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)
can increase in size only when the algorithm chooses a vertex contained in the intersection of a pair of edges from
c_{k+2,k+2\rightarrow k+1}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)
, or when the algorithm chooses the vertex not contained in the intersection of a pair of edges counted by
c_{k+2,k+1\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)
or
c_{k+1,k+2\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)
. Thus

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\right]\leq\frac{C_{k+2,k+2\rightarrow k+1}+C_{k+2,k+1\rightarrow k}+C_{k+1,k+2\rightarrow k}}{|V(i)|}&\leq\frac{2D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-4)\lambda}+D^{\frac{k-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-2)\lambda}}{N\phi^{\lambda}}\\
&\leq\frac{D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-2)\lambda}\left(2\phi^{2\lambda}+1\right)}{N\phi^{\lambda}}\\
&\leq\frac{3\cdot 2^{r}}{N}D^{\frac{k+1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-1)\lambda}\,.\end{split}

Where, to get from the second to the third line we use that
\phi^{2\lambda}=o(1)
, and so
\left(2\phi^{2\lambda}+1\right)\leq 3
.
For our application of Freedman’s theorem (
3.2
), we can set
C=2D_{2\uparrow k+2}+D_{2\uparrow k+1}\leq 3D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-4)\lambda}
. Now

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}ar\left[\Delta Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\right]=\mathbb{V}ar\left[\Delta c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9\cdot 2^{r}}{N}D^{\frac{2k+1}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4k-5)\lambda}

Thus we have
W({i_{\max}})\leq i_{\max}\cdot\frac{9\cdot 2^{r}}{N}D^{\frac{2k+1}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4k-5)\lambda}=O\left(D^{\frac{2k}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4k-5)\lambda}\log(1/\phi)\right)
so we can take
w
to be the latter expression. We will set
d=D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(4r-2k-2)\lambda}
. Observe that
Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}(v,v^{\prime},0)\leq\phi D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}
by Condition (
3
). Freedman’s theorem gives us that

(D.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P\left[\exists i\leq i_{\max}:Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)-\phi D^{\frac{k-1}{r-1}}\geq d\right]&\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2(w+Cd)}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{D^{\frac{2k}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(8r-4k-4)\lambda}}{D^{\frac{2k}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4k-5)\lambda}\log(1/\phi)+D^{\frac{2k}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(6r-4k-6)\lambda}}\right)\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left(-\phi^{-(2r+2)\lambda}\right)=o(N^{-2}).\end{split}

If the unlikely event above does not happen, i.e. if
Y_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)-\phi D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}<d
for all
i\leq i_{\max}
, then

c_{a,a^{\prime}\rightarrow k}\left(v,v^{\prime},i\right)\leq\phi D^{\frac{k}{r-1}}+d+\frac{3\cdot 2^{r}}{N}D^{\frac{k+1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2k-1)\lambda}\cdot i_{\max}<C_{k+1,k+1\rightarrow k}.

Now (
D.3
) follows from the union bound over at most
N^{2}
choices for
v,v^{\prime}
.

Lemma D.3
.

Recall that
D^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\coloneqq D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2\ell)\lambda}
. Then,

(D.5)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\leq\tau\text{ and }v\in V(i)\text{ such that }d^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(v,i)\geq D^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\right)=o(1)\,.

Proof.

We define variables
Y_{\ell}(v,i)
as follows.

Y_{\ell}(v,i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}d^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(v,i)-\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2\ell-1)\lambda}\cdot i&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\text{ holds }\\
Y_{\ell}(v,i-1)&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

To justify that
Y_{\ell}(v,i)
is a supermartingale, we observe

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta d^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(v,i)\right]=\frac{(\ell-2)d^{\prime\prime}_{\ell+1}}{|V(i)|}\leq\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{\ell}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2\ell-1)\lambda}.

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta d^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(v,i)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{2\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4\ell-3)\lambda}

Thus we have
W({i_{\max}})\leq i_{\max}\cdot\frac{r}{N}D^{\frac{2\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4\ell-3)\lambda}<D^{\frac{2\ell-2}{r-1}}\phi^{2-(4r-4\ell-3)\lambda}\log(1/\phi)
so we can take
w
to be the latter expression. We set
d=\frac{1}{2}D^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\phi^{1-(2r-2\ell)}
. We claim that
Y_{\ell}(v,0)\leq\phi D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}

\ell=r

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P\left[\exists i\leq i_{\max}:Y_{\ell}(v,i)-\phi D^{\frac{\ell-1}{r-1}}\geq d\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{2(w+Cd)}\right)\\
