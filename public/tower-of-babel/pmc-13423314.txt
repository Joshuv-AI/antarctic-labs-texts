Functional dyspepsia redefined: evidence supporting inflammatory dyspepsia – a systematic review
Authors: Lauren Phang, Alice Kim, Chunlong Mu, Kamran Rostami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:With growing evidence linking food antigens to inflammation and symptoms, our objective was to review the evidence for the impact of dietary factors on functional dyspepsia (FD) pathogenesis.Background: FD is a common gastrointestinal disorder characterised by postprandial fullness, early satiety, epigastric pain, and burning.Methods: A systematic search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Cochrane databases was conducted for studies published between January 2000 and December 2024. Eligible studies included adults (≥18 years) with FD diagnosed using the Rome criteria. Data were synthesised narratively, and the risk of bias was assessed using ROBIS, RoB 2.0, and ROBINS-I tools.Results: Fourteen studies met inclusion criteria, evaluating seven key dietary categories: FODMAPs/wheat, prebiotics/probiotics, capsaicin, peppermint–caraway and cinnamon oils, herbal therapies, medications, and ginger-based interventions. High-FODMAP foods, gluten, and capsaicin were linked to symptom exacerbation, with some studies noting impaired epithelial barrier function, immune activation, or microbiota alterations. Interventions with anti-inflammatory or microbiota-modulating effects, including probiotics, peppermint–caraway oil, herbal formulations, and ginger-based supplements, were found to mitigate symptoms.Conclusion: Our findings show that specific dietary components can be implicated in inducing inflammation and symptoms in FD. Stress and anxiety might be another triggering factor for FD; hence, we see the utility of antidepressants in some cases. Since the symptoms are related to inflammation in most cases, we propose using ‘inflammatory dyspepsia’ to represent the true presentation for those patients with inflammation detected in their gastrointestinal mucosa. Large-scale randomised trials are needed to confirm findings and refine dietary guidelines.

Introduction

Functional dyspepsia (FD) is a chronic disorder of sensation and movement in the upper digestive tract that does not show visible structural abnormalities on endoscopy. The latest Rome IV criteria described FD by the presence of one or more persistent symptoms, including postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain, or burning (1). Although FD is a common and disruptive disorder, it carries some stigma due to its classification as a "functional" condition.

Some studies suggest that FD patients show increased duodenal eosinophils, mast cells, and intraepithelial cytotoxic T cells (2). This low-grade inflammation can increase visceral hypersensitivity and alter motility, contributing to symptoms like early fullness, bloating, or upper abdominal pain (2, 3).

While the immune pathways behind food sensitivities in FD have not been studied sufficiently, they appear to involve immune cell activation similar to those found in non-IgE-mediated food allergies and eosinophilic esophagitis (2). Potential involvement of the immune system along with evidence for food-antigen-induced symptoms in FD would put this condition in the category of organic disorders and is contrary to the theory of being a functional disease. While FD is not classically an ‘inflammatory disease’, subtle inflammation - especially in the duodenum - appears to be linked to its development in some patients (4-8). We recognize that antidepressant medications can alleviate symptoms, which might suggest a spectrum in which stress-induced factors combined with food antigens contribute to dysbiosis.

This systematic review aimed to review the evidence for how dietary factors contribute to FD pathogenesis. The findings gained from this review will help to clarify the relationship between diet and FD, offering new avenues for treatment.

A total of 18,424 records were retrieved from six databases (2000–2024) and exported into EndNote for initial duplicate removal. Remaining records were screened in Rayyan, where additional automated and manual deduplication was completed. This process resulted in 11,839 records for title and abstract screening. Full texts were assessed according to predefined criteria (adult participants, Rome-defined FD, and dietary exposures or interventions). After exclusions for non-dietary focus, insufficient diagnostic criteria, and ineligible study design, 14 studies were included in the final synthesis.

Research ethics

This study is not a clinical trial, so it did not require participant consent/ethical approval.

Data extraction and analysis

A single reviewer (LP) extracted data from 14 articles into a summary table categorised by key dietary components. Due to the heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures, results were synthesised through narrative analysis, systematically evaluating each study according to predefined categories.

Study quality

Fourteen articles were sourced from various countries, including the United States, Canada, Iran, China, Australia, India, Austria, and Korea. Among these, seven were RCTs, four were systematic reviews/meta-analyses, one was a case-control study, one was a prospective cohort study, and one was a retrospective cohort study.

The ROBIS (Risk Of Bias In Systematic Reviews) was used to assess systematic reviews. The RoB 2.0 (Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomised Trials) was used to assess RCTs. The ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions) was used for cohort and non-randomised intervention studies. Three studies had a low risk of bias, eight studies had a moderate risk of bias, and three studies had a serious risk of bias. See Appendix I for risk of bias evaluations.

Results

Dietary factors were divided into seven categories: high-FODMAP intake, probiotics and prebiotics, capsaicin/spicy foods, peppermint–caraway or cinnamon oil, herbal formulations, ginger-based combinations, and medications with dietary relevance.

FODMAPS/Wheat

In a 2022 RCT, Goyal et al. (2022) randomised 105 FD patients to a low-FODMAP diet (LFD) or traditional dietary advice (TDA) (Table 1) (9). Both diets improved symptoms at 4 weeks, with greater benefit in LFD patients with postprandial distress syndrome or bloating (p=0.04). A 2018 case–control study by Du et al. (2018) compared gluten intake in 101 FD patients and 31 controls (10) (Table 1). Higher gluten consumption correlated with early satiety, and biopsies showed reduced claudin-1 and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes, indicating impaired barrier function and immune activation (10). Together, these findings indicate that specific dietary components- particularly FODMAPs and gluten- may provoke symptoms through barrier dysfunction and immune activation in so-called FD.

Prebiotics and probiotics

Two meta-analyses evaluated probiotics in FD. Zhang et al. (2020) pooled five RCTs (n=409) and reported modest symptom improvement with probiotics/prebiotics (RR 1.15, 95% CI 1.01–1.30) (Table 2) (11), while Agah et al. (2020) analysed six trials (n=422) and found a non-significant trend toward benefit (RR 1.35, 95% CI 0.99–1.84) alongside evidence of microbiota modulation (Table 2) (12). Both reviews reported good safety profiles but emphasised the need for higher-quality trials. These findings suggest probiotics may offer modest symptom relief in FD, though current evidence remains limited and inconsistent.

Spicy foods/Capsaicin

In a 2011 randomised, placebo-controlled trial, Fuhrer et al. (2011) found that FD patients given oral capsaicin exhibited markedly higher symptom scores than with placebo, demonstrating pronounced chemical sensitivity consistent with visceral hypersensitivity (Table 3) (13). Complementing this, Lee et al. (2016) conducted a prospective cohort study that showed that higher spicy food intake correlated with greater stomach fullness and retching, particularly in younger and female patients, supporting a role for capsaicin-rich foods in symptom exacerbation (Table 3) (14). These findings indicate that spicy foods, and capsaicin specifically, can worsen FD symptoms, likely through TRPV1-mediated nociceptive and inflammatory pathways.

Oils (peppermint–caraway and cinnamon oil)

A 2019 meta-analysis by Li et al. (2019) pooled five RCTs (n = 578) and found that peppermint–caraway oil combinations (POCO) significantly improved global FD symptoms and epigastric pain, although overall evidence quality was low (Table 4) (15). Peppermint oil’s actions - including smooth-muscle relaxation, modulation of visceral sensitivity, and reduced psychosocial distress (16), align with gut–brain mechanisms implicated in FD (17).

In contrast, a 2021 double-blind RCT of cinnamon oil found no benefit over placebo despite symptom improvement in both groups (Table 4) (18). These findings suggest that some herbal formulations, particularly POCO, may offer symptomatic relief in FD, whereas others such as cinnamon oil show no advantage beyond placebo.

Traditional and herbal therapies

A 2024 trial by Wang et al. found that daily consumption of a herbal porridge (Hou Gu Mi Xi) for two months led to higher symptom remission at 90 days (45.7% vs 20.7%; p=0.036) and improved multiple quality-of-life domains (Table 5) (19). In contrast, a large 2024 multicentre RCT of STW 5-II showed greater, but non-significant, improvement in gastrointestinal symptom scores compared with placebo (p=0.07) (Table 5) (20). These findings suggest that certain herbal formulations may offer symptomatic and quality-of-life benefits in FD, though mechanisms potentially involving anti-inflammatory bioactive compounds remain insufficiently defined.

Ginger-based interventions

A 2015 double-blind RCT by Giacosa et al. found that a ginger–artichoke extract significantly improved nausea, fullness, pain, and bloating compared with placebo after 14 days (Table 6) (21). Complementing this, a 2020 retrospective cohort study by Di Pierro et al. reported broad symptom improvement within one to two weeks in FD patients taking a perilla–ginger supplement, with only mild, self-limiting adverse effects (Table 6) (22).

Ginger supplements may serve as an effective natural option for FD, particularly for patients seeking non-pharmaceutical treatments. Given its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (23), ginger may reduce oxidative stress–related mucosal inflammation, indirectly enhancing motility leading to symptom relief, aligning with the concept of FD being an inflammation-linked disorder.

Medications

A 2019 Cochrane review of 38 trials found that prokinetics significantly reduced global FD symptoms (RR 0.81; NNT=7), though with substantial heterogeneity and overall low-quality evidence (Table 7) (24). Complementing pharmacologic options, a large 2015 multicentre RCT showed that amitriptyline provided greater symptom relief than placebo or escitalopram over 10 weeks, despite no effects on gastric emptying or satiety, with modest quality-of-life improvements across antidepressant groups (Table 7) (25). These findings indicate that prokinetics and low-dose tricyclic antidepressants may offer clinical benefit in FD, supporting a stress/anxiety component to FD.

Proposed mechanism of ‘inflammatory dyspepsia’. Dysbiosis and exposure to dietary antigens can promote mucosal immune activation, characterised by infiltration of lymphocytes, eosinophils, neutrophils, and mast cells, leading to inflammation of the gastric and duodenal mucosa.

Discussion

Our study aimed to explore the potential links between food antigens and FD symptoms. Across studies, dietary factors that aggravated FD symptoms (gluten, high-FODMAP, spicy foods) were associated with evidence or plausible mechanisms leading to mucosal inflammation or epithelial barrier dysfunction (26). Dietary factors (probiotics, peppermint-caraway oil, and herbal formulations) improved symptoms (11, 12, 15, 19) though robust trials are still required to investigate anti-inflammatory or microbiota-modulation associated with these products. Collectively, these findings reinforce that FD may represent a diet-triggered inflammatory condition rather than a purely functional disorder, prompting our suggestion to rename FD to inflammatory dyspepsia at least in those patients who present with mucosal inflammation. Symptom improvement with antidepressant therapy in some patients suggests that FD may involve a broader spectrum and more complex underlying etiology.

We identified that nutrients rich in FODMAPs and gluten are involved in FD pathogenesis (26, 27). These food antigens seem to trigger abnormal immune stimulation in gastric and duodenal mucosa, characterised by mucosal barrier disruption and altered immune responses (28, 29). Du et al. found decreased expression of the tight junction protein claudin-1 in FD patients, leading to increased intestinal permeability, particularly in the first and second part of the duodenum (28). Additionally, FD patients showed an increase in intraepithelial lymphocytes (especially CD3-positive cells), with some patients exhibiting lymphocytic duodenitis, characterised by increased lymphocyte counts without flat villi (26, 28). Burns et al. provide further evidence by examining the genetic contribution of T cell receptor (TCR) variations, particularly T Cell Receptor Alpha Variable 26-2 (TRAV26-2), in driving immune responses to gluten in FD patients (29). They identified that FD patients showed an increase in duodenal effector Th17 cells following gluten exposure, and this response positively correlated with TRAV26-2 gene expression (29). This suggests that specific TCR variants may influence gluten-related immune responses in FD, regardless of self-reported wheat sensitivity (29).

Together, these findings propose an immune mechanism in FD, combining increased intestinal permeability and immune activation with genetic variations in TCRs that may modulate gluten-related immune responses (26). It is likely that an inflammatory dyspepsia appears to arise from an immune-mediated response to dietary antigens, such as gluten and possible FODMAPs, prompting our proposal to consider re-naming FD to ‘inflammatory dyspepsia’.

Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) is a sensory heat/pain receptor activated by capsaicin (14). Clinical and dietary studies show that acute capsaicin ingestion provokes symptoms in FD patients such as stomach fullness, discomfort and retching potentially through binding to TRPV1 (14, 30). However, mechanistic studies suggest that capsaicin may have therapeutic potential to reduce FD symptoms by targeting gastrointestinal motility, desensitizing C nociceptive fibers, counteracting inflammation, and regulating gut microbiota (31). These somewhat conflicting results give rise to the possibility that while capsaicin may initially trigger FD symptoms due to TRPV1 activation, repeated or prolonged exposure may lead to desensitisation and eventually symptom relief. Further studies are required to elucidate any temporal effect.

While pharmacological treatments for FD remain inconclusive, prokinetic antagonists (32), H2-receptor antagonists (32) and probiotics (33) are often used as off-label symptomatic treatments. Relative to healthy controls, microbiota analysis revealed that FD patients had higher Bacteroidetes and lower Proteobacteria levels, with an absence of Acidobacteria (34). Interestingly, FD patients undergoing probiotic therapy shifted their gastric fluid microbiota to resemble that of healthy controls (34). Given the presence of dysbiosis in FD (35, 36), probiotics may be effective in treating FD by restoring normal gastro-intestinal microflora composition. The strategy should be based on individual needs through stool assessments.

Limitations

Inconsistent outcome reporting across studies limits the ability to make direct comparisons. Additionally, the risk of bias in many included studies may further constrain the interpretation of findings. Nevertheless, our review employed a standardised, systematic approach to searching extensively for literature in databases using the PRISMA framework.

Conclusion

This systematic review indicates that multiple dietary factors – including gluten, FODMAPs, prebiotics/probiotics, spicy foods, oils, herbal remedies, medications, and ginger – may influence FD symptoms. Emerging evidence suggests that FD may not be purely functional and shows an element of stress induction associated with inflammation in many individuals. Therefore, we propose using the term inflammatory dyspepsia for individuals presenting with microscopic inflammation. We also propose taking biopsies from the upper gastrointestinal tract in patients presenting with dyspepsia during gastroscopy despite normal-looking gastroduodenal mucosa. While many studies suggest the promising potential of diet-based therapies for managing FD, further rigorous randomised trials are needed to confirm these findings and inspire hope in patients.
