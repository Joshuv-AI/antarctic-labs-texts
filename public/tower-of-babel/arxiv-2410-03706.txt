Abstract




Abstract

The goal of this work is to serve as a foundation for deep studies of the topology of state, action, and policy spaces in reinforcement learning. By studying these spaces from a mathematical perspective, we expect to gain more insight into how to build better algorithms to solve decision problems. Therefore, we focus on presenting the connection between the Banach fixed point theorem and the convergence of reinforcement learning algorithms, and we illustrate how the insights gained from this can practically help in designing more efficient algorithms. Before doing so, however, we first introduce relevant concepts such as metric spaces, normed spaces and Banach spaces for better understanding, before expressing the entire reinforcement learning problem in terms of Markov decision processes. This allows us to properly introduce the Banach contraction principle in a language suitable for reinforcement learning, and to write the Bellman equations in terms of operators on Banach spaces to show why reinforcement learning algorithms converge. Finally, we show how the insights gained from the mathematical study of convergence are helpful in reasoning about the best ways to make reinforcement learning algorithms more efficient.

Keywords :
Markov Decision Process; Reinforcement Learning; Contraction mapping; Fixed Point; Banach Space; Bellman Operators; Q-Learning.

Introduction

There is not much work on the foundations of Reinforcement Learning (RL). Especially from a topological point of view. This work is, to the best of our knowledge, the first attempt to unify the basic concepts of Reinforcement Learning in a coherent and well-ordered way, specifying all the underlying mathematical concepts.

Some researchers have instead been interested in related questions, trying to explore RL concepts from a mathematical point of view, with the aim of improving the effectiveness of RL algorithms. For example:

In the paper

]

Their characterization of the value function space as a general polytope provides an understanding of the relationships between policies and value functions. This perspective enhances the theoretical understanding of RL dynamics. The authors of
[
5

also explored the concept of Lipschitz continuity in model-based RL, emphasizing the importance of learning Lipschitz continuous models for efficient value function estimation. This establishes some error bounds and demonstrates the benefits of controlling the Lipschitz constant in RL setting. The work done in
[

The aforementioned works can be difficult to understand or even less precise if the foundations are not well clarified. Therefore, the main goal of our work is to serve as a first stone in studying and organising the basic concepts, while presenting Reinforcement Learning in a simple and concise way, to help future researchers to quickly dive into the foundations of RL and to study more deeply, in particular the topology of state, action, and policy spaces. By studying these spaces from a mathematical perspective, we expect to gain more insight into how to build better algorithms to solve decision problems.

At the end of this work, we even illustrate the latter assumption by showing that, with the mathematical investigation we have done, we are able to make very strong and general conjectures which merit to be taken seriously into account. This work is organized then as follows :

In chapter

Chapter 2
Metric spaces and the Banach fixed point

In this chapter, we present the fundamental concepts of metric spaces and explore their completeness, a crucial property in understanding the behavior of sequences within these spaces. We also study the Banach fixed point theorem, a result which establishes the existence and uniqueness of fixed points for mappings in complete metric spaces. We elucidate the significance of this theorem in providing a theoretical foundation for iterative algorithms and solution techniques in various mathematical and computational contexts. Furthermore, we culminate our discussion by illustrating the practical relevance of the Banach Fixed Point Theorem through a concrete example, demonstrating its applicability in solving real-world problems and providing insight into its computational implications.

2.1
Complete metric spaces

Metrics and metric spaces provide a structured framework for precisely measuring the relationship between elements within a set. This facilitates deeper insights into their interconnections and dependencies, enabling pattern recognition, association identification, and informed decision-making across diverse domains. In this section, we delve into complete metric spaces, which offer a comprehensive framework for understanding the entirety of a set’s elements and their interrelations.

2.1.1 Definition

(Metric and metric space)
.

A metric on a set
M
is a function
d:M\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that for
x,y,z\in M
:

(
i
)

[Non-negativity]
d(x,y)\geq 0
and
d(x,x)=0

(
ii
)

[Identity of indiscernibles]
d(x,y)=0\implies x=y

(
iii
)

[Symmetry]
d(x,y)=d(y,x)

(
iv
)

[Triangle Inequality]
d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)

The pair
(M,d)
is called a metric space.

Cauchy sequences play a fundamental role in the study of completeness within metric spaces. A sequence is considered Cauchy if, for any arbitrarily small positive distance, there exists a point in the sequence beyond which all subsequent points are within that distance from each other. Completeness, on the other hand, refers to the property of a metric space wherein every Cauchy sequence converges to a limit within the space itself. Together, Cauchy sequences and completeness provide essential tools for understanding the convergence behavior and structural integrity of metric spaces.

2.1.6 Definition

(Cauchy sequences)
.

A sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in a metric space
\Big(X,d\Big)

Cauchy sequence

\varepsilon>0

such that whenever
\min\{m,n\}>N_{0}

d(x_{m},x_{n})<\varepsilon.

While every convergent sequence is a Cauchy sequence, the reverse of this is only true in ‘complete metric spaces’.

2.1.7 Definition

(Completeness)
.

The metric space
\Big(X,d\Big)

X

2.2
Contraction mapping and fixed-point

2.2.1 Definition
.

Let
X
be an nonempty set and
f:X\to X
be a mapping on that set :

-

A point
x
is said to be a
fixed point
of
f
if
f(x)=x
.

-

We will write
Fix(f)=\{x\in X:f(x)=x\}
the set of fixed points of
f
on
X
.

2.2.2 Proposition
.

Let
X
be a nonempty set and
f:X\to X
a mapping defined on it. If
x\in X
is a unique fixed point of
f^{n}
with
f^{n}=\underset{n-times}{\underbrace{f\circ f\circ\cdots\circ f}}
for any
n>1
, then it is the unique fixed point of
f
and reversely :

Fix(f^{n})=\{x\}\iff Fix(f)=\{x\}

Proof.

Let
x
be a fixed point for
f^{n}
in
X
. Then we can write :

f(x)=f\left(f^{n}(x)\right)=f^{n+1}(x)=f^{n}\left(f(x)\right)\implies~~f(x)~~\text{is a fixed point of}~~f^{n}

Since
x
is the only one fixed point for
f^{n}
, then
f(x)=x
, i.e.
x
is also a fixed point for
f
. Let’s now show that the aforementioned fixed point is unique also for
f
:

Let’s take
y\neq x
another fixed point of
f
, and compute

y=f(y)=f^{2}(y)=\cdots=f^{n}(y)
or
f^{n}(y)=y\iff y=x
. Then, we’ve clearly seen that
x
is the unique fixed point for
f
.
∎

Before stating the Banach Contraction Principle, let’s define all the important notions related to that specific theorem.

2.2.3 Definition
.

[
13
,
14
,
26
,
29
]

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
f:X\to X
a mapping on
X
.
f
is said to be
Lipschitz
continuous if there exist
\alpha>0
such that :

d\left(f(x),f(y)\right)\leq\alpha\cdot d(x,y)~~~\forall x,y\in X

(2.2.1)

-

If
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
f
is said to be a
contraction
.

-

If
\alpha=1
then
f
is said
non-expansive
.

-

If
d\left(f(x),f(y)\right)<d(x,y),~\forall x\neq y
then
f
is contractive.

2.2.4 Proposition
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
f:X\to X
a contraction mapping with
\alpha\in(0,1)
. If
f
has a fixed point, that point is unique.

Proof.

Suppose we have two fixed points
x
and
y
for
f
with always
x\neq y
. Because
f
is a contraction, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
we can write :

0\neq d(x,y)=d\left(f(x),f(y)\right)\leq\alpha\cdot d(x,y),

which is impossible (contradictory). So, the fixed point is unique.
∎

As we have now a formal definition of fixed point, contractions and some obvious implications, let’s now state the Banach Contraction Principle.

2.2.5 Theorem

(Banach Contraction Principle : BCP
[
37
]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space,
f:X\to X
a contraction. Then
f
has a unique fixed point
x^{*}
and for each
x\in X
,
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(x)=x^{*}
. Moreover,

d\left(f^{n}(x),x^{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{\alpha^{n}}{1-\alpha}d\left(x,f(x)\right).

.

Proof.

Let us construct a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
, starting on
x_{0}
and using the following recurrence :

x_{n}=f(x_{n-1})~~\forall n\in\mathbb{N}~~~\text{i.e.}~~~x_{n}=f^{n}(x_{0})

We show that
\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy-Sequence. In fact, since
f
is a contraction, we should have :

\displaystyle d(x_{m+1},x_{m})

\displaystyle=d\left(f(x_{m}),f(x_{m-1})\right)\leq\alpha\cdot d(x_{m},x_{m-1})

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot d\left(f(x_{m-1}),f(x_{m-2})\right)\leq\alpha^{2}\cdot d(x_{m-1},x_{m-2})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{m-1}\cdot d\left(f(x_{1}),f(x_{0})\right)\leq\alpha^{m}\cdot d(x_{1},x_{0})

(2.2.2)

Now, for any given
m
and
n
, positive and with
n<m
, the triangle inequality applied recursively gives :

\displaystyle d(x_{m},x_{n})

\displaystyle\leq d(x_{m},x_{m-1})+d(x_{m-1},x_{m-2})+\cdots+d(x_{n-1},x_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\alpha^{m-1}+\alpha^{m-2}+\cdots+\alpha^{n}\right)\cdot d(x_{1},x_{0})~~~\text{from \ref{eq : demons1}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{n}\left(\alpha^{m-n-1}+\alpha^{m-n-2}+\cdots+1\right)\cdot d(x_{1},x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{n}}{1-\alpha}\cdot d(x_{1},x_{0})

(2.2.3)

As we can now see from
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\alpha^{n}=0
and
d(x_{1},x_{0})
is fixed, we can say
d(x_{m},x_{n})\to 0
as
m,n\to\infty
. So,
\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence and since
X
is complete, there exits only one
x^{*}\in X
such that
x_{n}\to x^{*}
. So, by continuity of contractions, we can write :

x^{*}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n+1}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=f\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\right)=f\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(x_{0})\right)=f(x^{*})

And the proposition above (Proposition
2.2.2
), we have a way to find this unique fixed point independently on the starting point.

And to show a good estimate of how we are approaching the limit we can rewrite the Expression
2.2.3
like this :

d\left(f^{n}(x),x^{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{\alpha^{n}}{1-\alpha}d\left(x,f(x)\right)~~~\equiv~~~d\left(x_{n},x^{*}\right)\leq\dfrac{\alpha^{n}}{1-\alpha}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)

∎

2.2.6 Remark
.

The beauty of the Banach Contraction Principle is that it only require completeness for the metric space and contraction property on the mapping. Also, we can get closer and closer to the fixed point, starting from any point
x\equiv x_{0}\in X
by just applying recursively the mapping on that specific point.

2.3
Application of the Banach Contraction Principle - An example

The Banach Fixed Point principle or Banach Contraction Principle (BCP) is very important in many fields, in mathematics as well as in its applications
[

,
24
,
38
,

]
. We here introduce a problem whose solution is guaranteed by the

Theorem, the uniqueness of solutions of a first order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). We show that its solution can be obtained using the BCP.

Let’s say we have the following initial value problem (which is here an ODE with initial conditions) :

y^{\prime}=F(x,y)~~~;~~~y(x_{0})=y_{0}~~~\text{with}~~y^{\prime}=\dfrac{dy}{dx}

(2.3.1)

This problem is in fact equivalent to the following :

\displaystyle y(x)-y(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}F\Big(t,y(t)\Big)dt~~\text{as we have $y_{0}=y(x_{0})$}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow y(x)

\displaystyle=y_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}F\Big(t,y(t)\Big)dt

(2.3.2)

So, the two problems
2.3.1
and
2.3.2
are exactly the same. And from this last Expression
2.3.2
we can define a sequence given by :

y_{n+1}(x)=y_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}F\Big(t,y_{n}(t)\Big)dt~~~\text{with $y_{0}=y_{n}(x_{0})~~\forall~n$.}

(2.3.3)

The problem can be seen as demonstrating the

which states the following :

(Picard-Lindelof Theorem
[

]

on closed intervals

R=\{(x,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x-x_{0}|\leq h~~\text{and}~~|w-y_{0}|\leq h.c\}~~\text{with $h,c\in\mathbb{R}$ and $w=y(x)$}

Following the assumptions made for this Proposition, we know that
0<h\leq a,~~hc\leq b
, i.e.
R\subset S
.

Let
X
be the set of all real-valued continuous functions
y=y(x)
on
[x_{0}-h,x_{0}+h]
. Then
X
is a closed subset of the normed space
\mathcal{C}\left([x_{0}-h,x_{0}+h]\right)
with the sup norm
||\cdot||_{\infty}
.

Let’s now define an operator (a functional) like this (inspired by the second formulation of our problem, given in
2.3.2
) :

\displaystyle T:X\to

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle g(x)\mapsto

\displaystyle h(x)=Tg=y_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}F\Big(t,g(t)\Big)dt

(2.3.4)

Since

d\Big(h(x),y_{0}\Big)=\underset{x}{\sup}{\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}F\Big(t,g(t)\Big)dt\right|}=\left|\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}F\Big(t,g(t)\Big)dt\right|\right|_{\infty}\leq c(x-x_{0})\leq c\cdot h\leq b,

we can say that the operator
T
in
2.3.4
is well defined.

Now, we have to prove that this operator is a contraction mapping. For that, let’s take
g,g_{1}\in X
. We’ll have :

\displaystyle d\left(Tg,Tg_{1}\right)=d(h,h_{1})=

\displaystyle~~\left|\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\Big(F\left(t,g(t)\right)-F\left(t,g_{1}(t)\right)\Big)dt\right|\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~~\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\left|\left|\Big(F\left(t,g(t)\right)-F\left(t,g_{1}(t)\right)\Big)\right|\right|_{\infty}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~~L\cdot\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\left|g(t)-g_{1}(t)\right|dt\text{~~~using the second assumption.}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~~Lh\cdot d\left(g,g_{1}\right)=k\cdot d\left(g,g_{1}\right).

(2.3.5)

In
2.3.5
, from the third assumption, we know that
k<1
, then the operator
T
is a contraction.

Hence now
T
is a contraction mapping and X is a closed subset of a metric space,
X
is also a complete metric space and by the Banach Fixed Point principle,
T
has one unique fixed point
g
which is the exactly the unique solution to the given ODE.
∎

Overview on Reinforcement Learning

3.1
Markov Decision Processes in the Reinforcement Learning setting

We here present a formal definition of the Markov Decision Process. But before that, we discuss about Markov Chain because it allows us to present the fact that, there are situations in which the previous observation contains all the information about the past, in such a way that we don’t need to look at the whole history.

3.1.1 Definition

(Markov chain
[
22
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
(a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for example) represent the state space. The discrete-time dynamic system
\{S_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{S}
is a Markov chain if :

\mathbb{P}(S_{t+1}=s|S_{t},S_{t-1},\cdots,S_{0})=\mathbb{P}(S_{t+1}=s|S_{t}),

(3.1.1)

i.e. everything we need to predict (in probability) the future is contained in the current state (this is what we call Markov property). Given an initial state
S_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
, a
Markov chain
is defined by the following transition probability :

p(s^{\prime}|s)=Pr(S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}|S_{t}=s).

(3.1.2)

We can now clearly define the Markov Decision Process and related concepts, important for Reinforcement Learning.

3.1.2 Definition

(Markov Decision Process
[
22
,
32
]
)
.

Formally, an Markov Decision Process (MDP) is a 5-uplet
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma\rangle
where :

•

\mathcal{S}
represents the
state space
. It can be finite, countable infinite or continuous.

•

\mathcal{A}
represents the
action space
which could also be finite, countable infinite or continuous.

•

p(s,a,s^{\prime})
is the
transition probability
(called environment dynamics) defined by :

p(s,a,s^{\prime})\equiv p(s^{\prime}|s,a)=Pr(S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a),

(3.1.3)

with
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
This represents the probability of observing a next state
s^{\prime}
when the action
a
is taken in the state
s
.

•

r(s,a,s^{\prime})
is the
reward function
called also a
reinforcement
obtained when taking the action
a
, in the state
s
and the next state observed is
s^{\prime}
. So :

r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}

•

The transition probability
p
and the reward function
r
defines the
environment model
.

•

\gamma\in[0,1)
is the
discount factor
used to define (let’s say for discrete time here for illustration) the expected cumulative reward :

G=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\cdot r(S_{t},A_{t},S_{t+1}).

If we have a problem which can be modeled as an MDP, the goal is generally to maximize the quantity
G
by choosing at each time and for each state the action
A_{t}
. That choice is defined by a function
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta\mathcal{A}
called
policy
. Where
\Delta\mathcal{A}
represents a probability distribution under actions.

3.1.3 Definition

(Policy or decision rule)
.

Policy is a mapping of states to actions, which can be :

•

Deterministic : in the same conditions, the same action is always chosen.

•

Stochastic : at each time, actions are only chosen with a certain probability. In this case the policy is a mapping from the state space to a probability distribution over actions.

The policy can also be varying as the time evolve (Non-stationary policy) or constant (Stationary policy).

We have given the foundational definitions, let us now reformulate everything in the Reinforcement Learning setting :

1.

The agent-environment interface

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are indeed a simple way to frame the problem of learning from interaction to achieve a goal. We are here define some important terms in the field of Reinforcement Learning :

•

The element that has to learn and make decisions is called
agent
.

•

Everything with which the agent interacts, including any entity outside the agent, is called the
environment
.

•

The agent and the environment are in constant interaction, the former selecting actions, the latter responding to those actions and presenting new situations to the agent. These new situations are called
states
of the environment.

•

The agent also receives
rewards
(special numerical values) from the environment and tries to maximise these over time through its choice of actions.

•

We call
action
the way the agent chooses to interact with its environment. Or simply, the interaction of the agent with its environment.

Let us now call
S_{t}
,
A_{t}
and
R_{t}
respectively the state, the action and the reward at a specific time-step
t
.The MDP and the agent will now produce the following kind of sequences :

S_{0},A_{0},R_{1}~~,~~S_{1},A_{1},R_{2}~~,~~S_{2},A_{2},R_{3}~~,...

The set of rewards, actions and states will be respectively written as
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{S}
. We’ll also call
\mathcal{A}(s)
the actions which are feasible in a specific state
S_{t}=s
.

The Figure
3.1
shows schematically all the above concepts and how they are related.

Figure 3.1:

]

2.

Model of the environment

Let us now suppose that we are able to have access to all the dynamic of our environment. Then, we can generally define a function which gives the probability of transiting in a certain state
S_{t}
and receive a certain reward
R_{t}
if we were previously in the state
S_{t-1}
and made the action
A_{t-1}
. That kind of function, defined for the whole action and state spaces is called the
model of the environment
. It can be generally defined as follows :

p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\equiv Pr\Big(\{S_{t}=s^{\prime},R_{t}=r|S_{t-1}=s,A_{t-1}=a\}\Big)~\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}~,r\in\mathcal{R};~a\in\mathcal{A}(s)

(3.1.4)

Most of the time, it is assumed that, the next values depend only on the immediate previous values (
Markov property
)
[
22
]
. The Markov Property can be formalized as follows :

p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)=Pr\Big(\{S_{t},R_{t}|S_{t-1},A_{t-1},S_{t-2},A_{t-2},...\}\Big)=Pr\Big(\{S_{t}=s^{\prime},R_{t}=r|S_{t-1}=s,A_{t-1}=a\}\Big)

This function defines the dynamic of a Markov decision process.

3.

Other important definitions from the model of the environment

MDPs are powerful tools for modeling and solving problems where decision making involves uncertainty and sequential actions. We are here giving other formal definitions which shows how we can derive well-known concepts from the general Definition given by the Expression
3.1.4

[
36
]
.

(a)

Second form of the environment model

This definition is the commonly used but is not as general as the one we gave in Relation
3.1.4
. The reward is not explicitly considered :

p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\equiv Pr\left\{S_{t}=s^{\prime}|S_{t-1}=s,A_{t-1}=a\right\}=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)

(3.1.5)

(b)

Definition of the reward

Here we define the reward without considering the next state of the environment:

r(s,a)\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t}|S_{t-1}=s,A_{t-1}=a\right]=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left(r\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\right)

(3.1.6)

(c)

Reward depending on next state

Here we consider the next state in our definition of reward. That captures the most similar to our usual view of the reward function in real life:

r(s,a,s^{\prime})\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t}|S_{t-1}=s,A_{t-1}=a,S_{t}=s^{\prime}\right]=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left(r\cdot\dfrac{p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)}{p(s^{\prime}|s,a)}\right)

(3.1.7)

4.

Formalization of the concepts of goals, rewards and returns

In Reinforcement Learning (RL), the purpose or goal of the agent is formalized in terms of a special signal called a “reward” as explained previously, which is sent to the agent from the environment. In other words, given the reward at any given time as just a real number, the “goal” of the agent is to maximize the total amount of rewards it receives. This is not a maximization of the immediate reward, but of the cumulative reward in the long run. With this in mind, if we call
R_{t}
the reward we get at time
t
, we can generally define a function that we’ll call
return
at that time like this :

G_{t}=R_{t+1}+\gamma\cdot R_{t+2}+\gamma^{2}\cdot R_{t+3}+\cdots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\cdot R_{t+k+1}~~~\text{with}~~\gamma\in[0,1)

(3.1.8)

In this Expression
3.1.8
, the
discount factor

\gamma
can also be taken equal to one but this will lead to the divergence of the return function in infinite cases. This definition is mathematically consistent, and it is also consistent in real-life. In fact :

•

Mathematically
: Taking
\gamma
in the interval
[0,1)
ensures the convergence of the series if only the reward function is bounded.

•

In real life
: This definition of the return function is consistent with the concept that immediate rewards are more appreciated than rewards deferred into the future.

5.

Policies and Value Functions

Almost all RL algorithms involve estimating
value functions
, which are functions of states (or of state-action pairs) that estimate how good it is for an agent to be in a particular state (or to perform a particular action in a particular state).

The reward that an agent can expect to receive in the future depends on which action will be taken. Accordingly, value functions are defined with respect to particular ways of acting, called
policies
. Formally, a policy is a mapping from states to action space. In general, it quantifies the probability of selecting a certain action in a specific state. So, the policy can be defined as follows:

\pi\left(a|s\right)\equiv\pi\left(A_{t}=a|S_{t}=s\right)=Pr\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)|s\right\},

(3.1.9)

where
\mathcal{A}(s)
represents the set of possible actions when we are in the state
s
.

The value function now, for a state
s
, under a policy
\pi
, denoted
v_{\pi}(s)
, is the expected payoff of starting in that state and then following that policy. For MDPs we can formally say that the value function is given by :

v_{\pi}(s)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G_{t}|S_{t}=s\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\cdot R_{t+k+1}|S_{t}=s\right]

(3.1.10)

We can similarly define the value function of taking an action
a
while following the same policy, starting in the state
s
, which is called the
Q-value
:

q_{\pi}(s,a)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G_{t}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\cdot R_{t+k+1}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]

(3.1.11)

The equations
3.1.10
and
3.1.11
correspond respectively to the
state-value function
and the
action-value function
. Also, the state value function can be seen as the average of all the values that can be achieved by acting from a given state, i.e., we can see the state value function as a weighted average of the action-value functions that we can obtain starting from that state. So, formally :

v_{\pi}(s)\equiv\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)}q_{\pi}(s,a)\cdot\pi(a|s)

(3.1.12)

Now the following holds between the value of
s
and the value of its possible successor states for any policy
\pi
and any state
s
:

\displaystyle v_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G_{t}|S_{t}=s\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\cdot G_{t+1}|S_{t}=s\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}\sum_{r}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\Big[r+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G_{t+1}|S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}\right]\Big]\right)

(3.1.13)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow v_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\left(\sum_{s^{\prime},r}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\Big[r+\gamma\cdot v_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\Big]\right)

(3.1.14)

using then the equation
3.1.6

\displaystyle\Rightarrow v_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)

(3.1.15)

The Relation
3.1.13
is explained by the definition of the reward function given by the equation
3.1.6
, and also by the fact that we normally have to do a weighted summation under the action set
\mathcal{A}
to account for all actions.
The equation
3.1.15
is then called
Bellman equation
for the value function
v_{\pi}
.

We can define in the same way the Bellman equation corresponding to the action-value function as follows
[
18
]
:

q_{\pi}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot q_{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)

(3.1.16)

From now on we can notice that the MDP framework is a considerable abstraction of the problem of goal-directed learning from interaction.

3.2
Optimal Policies and Optimal Value Functions

Here we present the optimal value functions and discuss how they can be used to find the optimal policy
[
36
]
. In fact, the value function defines a partial ordering over policies. More precisely, given two policies
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, we can say,

\pi\geq\pi^{\prime}\iff v_{\pi}(s)\geq v_{\pi^{\prime}}(s)~~~~~\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(3.2.1)

but we are not always able to compare two value functions. So, the policy
\pi
is defined to be better than or equal to
\pi^{\prime}
if its expected return is greater than or equal to that of
\pi^{\prime}
for all states.

And now, if
\pi^{*}
is the optimal policy we have :

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}v_{*}(s)&\equiv&\underset{\pi}{\max}~v_{\pi}(s)\\
q_{*}(s,a)&\equiv&\underset{\pi}{\max}~q_{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\cdot v_{*}(S_{t+1})|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]\end{array}\right.

3.2.1
Bellman optimality equation

Since we take the optimal action at each time (following the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
), we can write
v_{*}(s)=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)}{\max}~q_{\pi^{*}}(s,a)
, and this will be the best achievable expected reward at each time. So :

\displaystyle v_{*}(s)

\displaystyle=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)}{\max}~q_{\pi^{*}}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}~\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[G_{t}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}~\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\cdot G_{t+1}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}~\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\cdot v_{*}(S_{t+1})|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}~\sum_{s^{\prime},r}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\cdot\left[r+\gamma\cdot v_{*}(s^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{*}(s^{\prime})\right]

(3.2.4)

The Expression (
3.2.4
) follows the definition of the model of the environment given in point
2
on page
2
. We can then show the consistency with the connection given in Expression (
3.1.12
) by deducing
q_{*}(s,a)
as follows :

\displaystyle q_{*}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~q_{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a,~~S_{t+1}=s^{\prime},A_{t+1}=a^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime},r}p(s^{\prime},r|s,a)\cdot\left[r+\gamma\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~{q_{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow q_{*}(s,a)

\displaystyle=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~{q_{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow q_{*}(s,a)

\displaystyle=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot{v_{*}(s^{\prime})}

(3.2.5)

Equations (
3.2.4
) and (
3.2.5
) are called
Bellman optimality equations
.
For a finite MDP, the Bellman optimality equation has a unique solution (in the case of infinite MDPs, the Bellman optimality equation may not have a unique solution because infinite MDPs can have multiple optimal policies that lead to the same value function).

And having the optimal value function
v_{*}
, one can determine an optimal policy (because the value function defines a partial order on the policy space).

Finally, even if we have a complete and accurate model of the environment (the dynamics), it is usually not possible to compute an optimal policy simply by solving the Bellman equation. Therefore, approximate methods are developed and used
[
33
]
.

3.3
Solving Markov Decision Processes

Solving an MDP means try to find an optimal value function
v_{*}
or
q_{*}
, which corresponds to find the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
.

There are many ways of doing that, and algorithms were developed for specific challenges. The most used classification consists in distinguishing
model-based
and
model-free algorithms
, direct or indirect.

Generally, model-based algorithms concern what is called
Dynamic Programming
(DP). Model-based algorithms assume that the model of the environment is well known and can be used to find value functions and policies using the Bellman equations.

Model-free algorithms now, is considered as true-RL algorithms. They don’t require the availability of a perfect model (we suppose that, the environment is not totally known). This method rely on interaction with the environment. That interaction is done by simulating the policy and generating samples of state transitions and
rewards. Then, the samples are used to estimate value functions and even to improve the used policy.

In model-free algorithms, since no model of the MDP is known, the agent must explore the MDP to obtain information. This introduces a
exploration-exploitation trade-off
that must be balanced to obtain an optimal policy.

We can also talk about model-based reinforcement learning algorithms in which the agent does not possess, at the beginning, a model of the environment, but estimates
it after a certain amount of time. Once a reasonable model of the environment has been induced, the agent can then apply dynamic programming algorithms to compute the optimal policy.

3.3.2
Model-free algorithms : Monte Carlo

Dynamic Programming (DP) assumes that the agent has a perfect access to the model of the environment (dynamics of the environment). But most of the time, we don’t have access to that. Additionally, sometimes the state and/or action spaces are very huge in such a way that DP algorithm can be inefficient.

Monte Carlo refers to the method of using experience to estimate value functions by averaging sample returns. Statistically it is proven to converge to the true estimate of the returns
[
10
]
. It uses sequences of state-action-reward samples generated by the agent by interacting with the environment.

There are always two important steps to be taken into account, the evaluation step and the improvement step. The idea of Monte Carlo (MC) policy evaluation for example is, following a certain policy
\pi
, to generate a sample of state-action-reward-state or state-action-reward-state-action starting from a certain state. After generating a large amount for many episodes (because here we are talking about processes which have a terminating state, i.e. they can end in a finite number of steps), the expected return is obtained by averaging the returns from a large amount of complete sample episodes. This gives the corresponding value for that state, given that specific policy. As the number increases, the average will converges to the true value of
v_{\pi}

[
10
]
.

The previous text just gives a general idea on how MC works. But, there are many implementations of this idea :

•

First-visit MC policy evaluation (using value function or action-value function)

•

Every-visit MC policy evaluation (using value function or action-value function)

•

Incremental MC policy evaluation (using value function or action-value function)

•

Monte Carlo Control (Monte Carlo Policy Improvement).

The update is done by observing whether it is the first time to visit the state in each episode and then update the return and the number of visits. 3.3.3
Model-free algorithms : Temporal Difference

MC is limited to episodic problems. Temporal Difference (TD) methods generalize MC by dealing with both episodic and continuing (no natural ending or terminating state for the considered task) or infinite RL problems.

So, MC has two main drawbacks : it only takes into account episodic problems and we have to wait for the end of an episode to do any update on the estimated values. This is called Temporal Difference because the agent update its estimates based on the immediate reward and the estimated value for the next state without waiting for the end, even if these values will change another time during the episode. The main idea is a continuous updating of values, a guess from guesses.

Let’s give the most important implementations which really show how TD works :

SARSA stands literally for State-Action-Reward-State-Action. ]

In general also, we can use

Chapter 4
Bellman Operators and Convergence of RL algorithms

In this chapter, we will express reinforcement in terms of operators so that we have a simple way to prove why reinforcement learning algorithms work well and find the optimal policy. To do this, we will first write the Banach contraction principle on normed spaces in a way that makes it easy to use in our next proofs, then we will discuss the Bellman operators, and finally we will talk about the core methods for finding optimality introduced in the previous chapter (but now using the language introduced in this chapter).

4.1
Refinement of the Banach Contraction Principle

We need to state the Banach Contraction Principle presented in the first chapter in such a way that everything in this part will be expressed in the same way, using the same language.

4.1.1
Contraction mapping and fixed-point

Let us now refine everything in term of norms in such a way that we will have a consistent notation until the end of this work. In fact, as we can see in this work, all the concepts related to reinforcement learning are defined in term of norms. So,

4.1.2 Definition
.

Let
(X,||\cdot||)
be a normed space. A mapping
\mathcal{T}:X\rightarrow X
is a
\gamma-
contraction mapping if, there exists a
\gamma\in[0,1)
such that, for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
we have

||\mathcal{T}x_{1}-\mathcal{T}x_{2}||\leq\gamma\cdot||x_{1}-x_{2}||

From this, each contraction is Lipchitz and thus continuous
[
20
]
. It implies that :

\text{If}~~x_{n}\underset{||\cdot||}{\longrightarrow}x~~~\text{then}~~~\mathcal{T}x_{n}\underset{||\cdot||}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{T}x,

where
\underset{||\cdot||}{\longrightarrow}
denotes the convergence using that norm
||\cdot||
.
Now, by definition, an element
x^{*}\in X
is called fixed point for
\mathcal{T}
if we have :

\mathcal{T}x^{*}=x^{*}

4.1.3 Proposition
.

Let
(X,||\cdot||)
be a Banach space and
\mathcal{T}:X\rightarrow X
a
\gamma-\text{contraction mapping}
. Then :

1.

\mathcal{T}
has a unique fixed point
x^{*}\in X
.

2.

For every starting point
x_{0}\in X
, the sequence recursively defined by
x_{n+1}=\mathcal{T}x_{n}
converges to
x^{*}
in a geometric fashion.

Proof.

For the first part, see the proof of the Banach fixed point Theorem
2.2.5
. Let’s now only justify the fact that the convergence is in geometric fashion :

\mathcal{T}||x_{n-1}-x^{*}||=||\mathcal{T}x_{n-1}-\mathcal{T}x^{*}||=||\mathcal{T}x_{n-1}-x^{*}||=||x_{n}-x^{*}||,

and as we are working with a contraction mapping, we also have
||\mathcal{T}x_{n-1}-\mathcal{T}x^{*}||\leq\gamma\cdot||x_{n-1}-x^{*}||
, then we can conclude directly
||x_{n}-x^{*}||\leq\gamma\cdot||x_{n-1}-x^{*}||
. Which implies that we get, by iteratively applying the mapping on the second member of this previous inequality,
||x_{n}-x^{*}||\leq\gamma^{n}\cdot||x_{0}-x^{*}||
thus

\lim_{n\to\infty}||x_{n}-x^{*}||\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\gamma^{n}\cdot||x_{0}-x^{*}||\right)=0\Rightarrow\lim_{n\to\infty}||x_{n}-x^{*}||=0

∎

As we may have noticed, the Proposition
4.1.3
, is the Banach Contraction Principle as given by the Theorem
2.2.5
. We restrain it on Banach spaces because this is sufficient for the RL setting. In fact, in many instances encountered in Reinforcement Learning (RL), the vector space
X
is frequently identified as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or the space of real bounded functions, and the most relevant norms are mostly
||\cdot||_{2}
and
||\cdot||_{\infty}
. So, stating the theorem on Banach Spaces is sufficient in this setting.

4.2
Bellman Optimality Operators

Operators are maps in space of functions. Here we will be defining them with respect to the associated RL equations. Let’s first define the whole mathematical framework that we’ll be using here.

c

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma\rangle
be a Markov Decision Process (MDP),
\mathcal{V}_{s}\equiv\mathcal{V}
the space of bounded real-valued functions over
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{Q}
the space of bounded real-valued functions over
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
. With that we will call :

•

\mathcal{V}
: the space of all state-value functions,

•

\mathcal{Q}
: the space of all action-value functions,

•

\left(\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}\right):\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
the Bellman Expectation Operator for the state value function,

•

\left(\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{Q}\right):Q\rightarrow Q
: the Bellman Expectation Operator for the action-value function,

•

\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{v}\right):\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
the Bellman Optimality Operator for the state value function,

•

\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{Q}\right):Q\rightarrow Q
: the Bellman Optimality for the action-value function

•

\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
the policy (a conditional probability defined by
\pi(a|s)=Pr\{a|s\}
) with
\Delta\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
denoting a probability distribution of actions.

Inspired by the definition given in the Expression
2.1.3
and the Example
2.1.14
we know that, with
||f(s)||_{\infty}\equiv\underset{s}{\max}|f(s)|
, the tuple
(\mathcal{V},||.||_{\infty})
is a Banach Space.

Also, with
||f(s,a)||_{\infty}\equiv\underset{s,a}{\max}|f(s,a)|
we can also see that
(\mathcal{Q},||.||_{\infty})
is a Banach Space. Those two supremum norms are different but we will be writing them in the same way because, the real formula will depend on the space we are working with.

4.2.1 Definition
.

Let’s recall the fact that, the Bellman Optimality equation for the value function (Relation
3.2.4
) is given by the following expression :

v_{*}(s)=\underset{a}{\max}\Big(r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot v_{*}(s^{\prime})\Big)

By analogy, we will define, point-wise, the
Bellman Optimality Operator for state value function

\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{v}\right):\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
by :

\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{v}f\right)(s)\equiv\underset{a}{\max}\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot f(s^{\prime})\right]~~~\forall f\in\mathcal{V}

(4.2.1)

4.2.2
Properties of this operator

The convention we’ll take here is to write
\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{v}\right)
just
\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}\right)
, knowing that the space will be written
\mathcal{V}
by default. Now, what is very interesting is that, the operator, defined in Relation

has these properties
[
2
]
:

•

\mathcal{T}^{*}
is a
\gamma-
contraction with respect to the supremum norm
||\cdot||_{\infty}
on
\mathcal{V}
:

||\mathcal{T}^{*}u-\mathcal{T}^{*}v||_{\infty}\leq\gamma\cdot||u-v||_{\infty}~~\forall~u,v\in\mathcal{V}~~~\text{with $\gamma\in[0,1)$}

•

\mathcal{T}^{*}
is monotonic :

\forall~u,v\in\mathcal{V}~\text{s.t}~u\leq v,~\text{in any state,}~~\mathcal{T}^{*}u\leq\mathcal{T}^{*}v

Proof.

First, let us rewrite simply the Bellman Optimality Operator in Relation
4.2.1
like this :

\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{v}f\right)(s)\equiv\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}f\right)(s)\equiv\underset{a}{\max}\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}f(s^{\prime})\Big]

(4.2.2)

Now, let us prove the two properties of the Bellman Optimality Operator
\mathcal{T}^{*}
:

1.

Using the definition of a contraction mapping and Bellman Optimality Operator we expand :

\left|\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)\right|=\left|\underset{a}{\max}\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})\right]-\underset{b}{\max}\left[r(s,b)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,b}v(s^{\prime})\right]\right|

Since for
f,g
real-valued functions,
|\underset{x}{\max}f(x)-\underset{x}{\max}g(x)|\leq\underset{x}{\max}|f(x)-g(x)|
, it follows that :

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underset{a}{\max}\big|\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})\right]-\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\right]\big|

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}\big|\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})-\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\big|

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}\left(\gamma\cdot\big|\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}\left(u(s^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime})\right)\big|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\underset{s^{\prime}}{\max}\big|\gamma\cdot\left(u(s^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime})\right)\big|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\underset{s}{\max}\big|\left(u(s)-v(s)\right)\big|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left|\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u-v||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\underset{s}{\max}\left|\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u-v||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow||\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u-v||_{\infty}

(4.2.3)

2.

We can now prove the monotonicity very simply :

Assume that for all
s

v(s)\leq u(s)

r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\leq r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})
.

Let us evaluate
\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)
and see what happens :

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)~

\displaystyle=\underset{a}{\max}\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\right]-\underset{a}{\max}\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underset{a}{\max}\Big(\underset{\leq~~0}{\underbrace{\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\right]-\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})\right]}}\Big)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)-\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)~

\displaystyle\leq~~0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{*}v(s)~~

\displaystyle\leq~~\mathcal{T}^{*}u(s)~~~\forall~s

(4.2.4)

∎

Now, using the

as refined by the Proposition

on
(\mathcal{V},||\cdot||_{\infty})
has the following behaviors :

•

\mathcal{T}^{*}
has a unique fixed point
f^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
.

•

For any starting point (function)
f_{0}
, we can find the fixed point
f^{*}
by looking for the convergence of the sequence defined by
f_{n+1}=\mathcal{T}f_{n}
.

Also, the fixed point that we will find will be the optimal value function, since it’ll be the maximum (in fact, this sequence is monotonically increasing by our definition of the problem).

This fixed point
f^{*}
is the overall optimal value function because here the equation use exactly the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
.

We can, in the same way, define the operator for action-value functions.

4.2.3 Definition
.

As we know, the Bellman Optimality equation for Q-values (Relation
3.2.5
) is given by this expression :

q_{*}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~{q_{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}

Now, giving the same MDP
\mathcal{M}
, let
\mathcal{Q}
be the space of bounded real-valued functions over
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.

We can also define, point-wise, the
Bellman Optimality Operator for the Q-Value

\mathcal{T}^{*}_{Q}:Q\rightarrow Q
as follows :

\left(\mathcal{T}^{*}_{Q}f\right)(s,a)\equiv r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left[p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(s^{\prime})}{\max}f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]~~~\forall f\in\mathcal{Q}

(4.2.5)

The same properties can be stated for this Bellman optimality operator for the Q-value, using the same kind of proofs.

The Bellman optimality operators as defined here are particular cases of what we will refer to as
Bellman Expectation Operators
. In this context, we considered the scenario of selecting the action that maximises the expected reward, without consideration of alternative policies. We now consider the policy in more general terms. This does not lead only to the optimal value function, but also to the value function corresponding to the chosen policy.

4.3
Bellman Expectation Operators

We will start with the state-value Bellman Expectation Operator. We know that, the Bellman Expectation equation is given by the Relation
3.1.15
:

v_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)

We can define by analogy the Bellman Expectation Operator by :

\left(\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}f\right)(s)=\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)f(s^{\prime})\right)~~\forall f\in\mathcal{V}

(4.3.1)

4.3.1 Proposition
.

We claim that, the operator
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}
, as defined by the Expression
4.3.1
is :

1.

A
\gamma-
contraction mapping.

2.

A monotonic mapping.

Proof.

Let consider two value functions
u
and
v
in
\mathcal{V}
.

1.

Let us compute the absolute value between the transformations of those two functions and see what happens :

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)u(s^{\prime})-\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v(s^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\left|\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)u(s^{\prime})-\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v(s^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\left|\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{s^{\prime}}{\max}\left|u(s^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left|\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{s}{\max}\left|u(s)-v(s)\right|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\underset{s}{\max}\left|\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u(s)-v(s)||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow||\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u(s)-v(s)||_{\infty}

(4.3.2)

This proves that the Bellman Expectation Operator for the state value function is a contraction mapping.

2.

Let us now prove the monotonicity :

Assume that
u(s)\leq v(s)
in all states, then we will get :

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}v(s)

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}u(s^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}v(s^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}\underset{u(s)\leq v(s)~\forall s}{\underbrace{\left(u(s^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime})\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}|s,a}\left(u(s^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime})\right)\right)\leq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)-\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}v(s)

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}u(s)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}v(s)~~~\text{i.e. $\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}$ is a monotonic mapping}

(4.3.3)

∎

From the properties we’ve just proven, using the Banach Contraction Principle as refined in the Proposition
4.1.3
, we can conclude that :

•

\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{v}
has a unique fixed point
f^{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}
.

•

For any starting point
f_{0}
, we can find the fixed point
f^{\pi}
by looking for the convergence of the sequence defined by
f_{n+1}^{\pi}=\mathcal{T}f_{n}^{\pi}
.

This fixed point
f^{\pi}
is the optimal value function with respect to the policy
\pi
we are following.

4.3.2 Remark
.

For the action value function, the Bellman Expectation Operator
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{Q}
and the Bellman Optimality Operator
\mathcal{T}^{*}_{Q}
as defined by the Expression
4.2.5
have exactly the same properties.

Note also that, the Bellman Expectation Operator, by analogy with the formula
3.1.16
is given by :

\left(\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{Q}f\right)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)

(4.3.4)

Here we have presented in a condensed way, the Bellman Operators. Now, we are able to say precisely how the Reinforcement Learning algorithms work really. This is why, in the following section, we are talking about the policy evaluation and policy iteration methods but now in the operator setting and knowing all the properties of those operators, it shows us why the RL algorithm come to work.

4.4
Policy evaluation and iteration in operators setting

From these properties (of the Bellman Operator), we can now deduce two ways of searching for the state-value function and the best policy. There are the well-known foundational
policy evaluation
and
policy improvement
(but this last one will be illustrated through policy iteration) algorithms (as presented in the previous chapter) :

1.

Policy evaluation :

The policy evaluation, is a method used to find the true state-value function associated to an MDP while following a specific policy. It’s based on the properties of the Bellman operator. Starting from a random distribution of value functions, until reaching the true one as follows :

•

Start with
v_{0}
a random initial value function

•

Update the value function using :
v_{k+1}\leftarrow T^{\pi}v_{k}

By the Banach Fixed-point Principle, it will converge to
v_{\pi}
as
k\rightarrow\infty

2.

Policy iteration :

The policy iteration algorithm is used to find the best policy, by updating concurrently the policy (policy improvement) as well as the associated value-functions. It is done in the following way :

•

Start with a random policy
\pi_{0}

•

Concurrent iterations :

*

Policy evaluation :
v_{k+1}\leftarrow T^{\pi_{i}}v_{k}

*

Policy improvement : Greedily improve the policy using :
\pi_{i+1}=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)}{argmax~}q_{\pi_{i}}(s,a)

Again, using the Banach Fixed-point Principle, it will converge to the best policy
\pi
and the associated value function
v_{\pi}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

This is sufficient to justify how Reinforcement Learning algorithms work because it explains how the two foundational methods work.

Chapter Conclusion

In this chapter we have talked about Bellman operators. We first presented the Bellman optimality operators (for both the state and the state-action value functions), before presenting a general formulation via the Bellman expectation operators (again for both the state and the state-action value functions). We have shown that these operators are
\gamma-
contraction and monotonic mappings, i.e. they have only one fixed point and that fixed point is the optimum. This is very important because it justifies why and how RL algorithms work from a fundamental point of view. In the next chapter we will now discuss some limitations of the Bellman operators in their classical expression and show some alternatives that seem interesting, even if they require further investments.

Chapter 5
Contributions, implementations and analysis

In this chapter we discuss some limitations of the classical Bellman operators as introduced in the previous chapter. We present how we have to make a trade-off between optimality and efficiency, and finally we show some practical results that we have obtained through experiments, which suggest that we need to think more about some refinements that should be made either to the Bellman operators or to the associated value functions.

5.1
Alternatives to the Bellman Operator

As we have seen, value-based reinforcement learning algorithms solve decision making problems through iterative application of a convergent operator, and an initial value function is recursively improved. Many other research works have examined alternatives to the Bellman operator
[
4
,
6
,
8
,
11
,
23
,
42
]
, but there are two which seem to be more promising, this is the reason why we will take inspiration from them. The first one is the
consistent Bellman Operator

[
8
]
, and the second one is the family of
Robust Stochastic Operators

[
23
]
. These two operators appear to be more interesting than those suggested before
[
23
]
, but for the second,
we’ll suggest a refinement of it which will remain good (we postulate) even without the stochasticity, improving the stability
.

5.1.1
Motivation
[
8
,
23
]

The motivation for new expressions of the Bellman Operator is simple. While Q-learning based methods continue to be successfully used in RL to determine the optimal policy, there is always a need to improve the convergence speed, accuracy and robustness of these algorithms. Now considering the fact that most of the time there are intrinsic error approximation (for example in the usual case of using discrete MDP to approximate a continuous system), the optimal state-action value function obtained through the Bellman
operator does not always describe the value of stationary policies. And most importantly, when the differences between the
optimal state-action value function and the suboptimal state-action value functions are small, this can lead to errors in identifying the true optimal actions. This is why, even if the classical Bellman Operators can solve problems in a perfect discrete setting, we need to refine it to be general because we know that for many real-world problems of our interest, discrete-time systems come from approximating continuous systems. So, there is always an intrinsic error, thus a need to integrate the corrector in the Operator.

Let us now study the effectiveness of the two aforementioned operators and find some insights on the general method of determining optimal policies in RL.

5.1.2
The Consistent Bellman Operator

This operator, is primarily defined by the authors of
[
8
]
for the action-value function. Here is the definition :

\mathcal{T}_{c}f(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot\left[\mathbb{I}_{\{s\neq s^{\prime}\}}\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})+\mathbb{I}_{\{s=s^{\prime}\}}f(s,a)\right]~~~\text{with $f\in\mathcal{Q}$}

(5.1.1)

Knowing that
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function, we claim also that, the following properties holds for the consistent Bellman Operator given by the Expression
5.1.1
:

1.

\mathcal{T}_{c}
is a contraction mapping,

2.

\mathcal{T}_{c}
is monotonic.

Proof.

First, let’s do some refinements :

•

We will replace for simplicity

\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\cdot(f)\text{~~~~by~~~~}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}(f)

•

We will refine the action-value function as follows :

f_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})&\text{if}&s\neq s^{\prime}\\
f(s,a)&\text{if}&s=s^{\prime}\\
\end{array}~~~\text{for}~~~f\in\mathcal{Q}\right.

(5.1.2)

Now we can rewrite the consistent operator in
5.1.1
as follows :

\mathcal{T}_{c}f(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\max_{a^{\prime}}~f_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]~~~\text{with $f\in\mathcal{Q}$}

(5.1.3)

We can now start the proof :

1.

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{Q}
, we will have :

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}_{c}u(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{c}v(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~u_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)-\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\underset{b^{\prime}}{\max}~v_{s}(s^{\prime},b^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~u_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~v_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\left|\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(u_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(u_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{s^{\prime},a^{\prime}}{\max}~\left|u_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\underset{s,a}{\max}~\left|u_{s}(s,a)-v_{s}(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot||u_{s}(s,a)-v_{s}(s,a)||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left|\mathcal{T}_{c}u(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{c}v(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u_{s}(s,a)-v_{s}(s,a)||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow||\mathcal{T}_{c}u(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{c}v(s,a)||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||u_{s}(s,a)-v_{s}(s,a)||_{\infty}

(5.1.4)

2.

Now, for us to prove the monotonicity, we have to consider two state-action value functions
u
and
v
such that
u(s,a)\leq v(s,a)~~\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
. From the definition we gave through the Expression
5.1.2
, this also means that
u_{s}(s,a)\leq v_{s}(s,a)
. Based now on the proof we just provided for the monotonicity, we have :

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{c}u(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{c}v(s,a)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{a^{\prime}}{\max}~\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\underset{u_{s}\leq v_{s}}{\underbrace{\left(u_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v_{s}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{c}u(s,a)-\mathcal{T}_{c}v(s,a)

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{c}u(s,a)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{T}_{c}v(s,a)~~~\forall~u,v\in\mathcal{Q}

(5.1.5)

∎

From this proof, we can conclude that
\mathcal{T}_{c}
has only one fixed point and that fixed point is optimal for this setup (that is the setup in which we are using the consistent Bellman equation instead of the classical one).

The question that remains is how this fixed point relates to the one we have found using the classical Bellman operator.

The most important thing is that, we are sure to converge to a unique fixed-point. As we don’t yet have the mathematical tool to say something about the relation between that fixed point and the one which will be found using the classical Bellman Operator, we can just wait and see how the operator will behave in practice in comparison to the Bellman Operator.

5.1.3
Modified Robust Stochastic Operator

Here, the proposed operator take inspirations in both the articles
[
23
]
and
[
8
]
but mostly that former article. This is more general (expectation operator) and different from the Robust Stochastic Operator suggested in
[
23
]
.

We know that, as an RL algorithm get better, if we express
v_{\pi}(s)
by :

v_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot q_{\pi}(s,a),

the difference that we will call
advantage learning
, given by

A(s,a)=q_{\pi}(s,a)-v_{\pi}(s),

always gives an idea on how well is our policy (which should be learning how to often choose better actions) as well as the value function. The quantity
\left|A(s,a)\right|
should get smaller as we are learning the good policy.

Now, let’s say we modify the
Bellman Expectation Operator for the action-value function
as defined by the Relation
4.3.4
in such a way that we integrate the idea of the
advantage learning
directly in the Bellman Operator instead of waiting applying it in the learning process as for some policy gradient methods
[
15
]
. Now, if we call that new operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
, it will be defined for all
f\in\mathcal{Q}
as follows :

\left(\mathcal{T}_{a}f\right)=r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)+\beta\cdot\underset{A(s,a)\text{:~advantage learning}}{\underbrace{\left[f(s,a)-\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)f(s,a)\right]}}

(5.1.6)

We can now study the properties of the operator defined by this Expression
5.1.6
. The coefficient
\beta
is any real now but we will define it properly at the end of this theoretical investigation.

5.1.4 Proposition
.

The operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
as defined by the Relation
5.1.6
is not a contraction mapping.

Proof.

Let
u(s,a)\equiv u
and
v(s,a)\equiv v
be two elements of
\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}_{a}u-\mathcal{T}_{a}v\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\left(u(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right)+\beta\left[(u-v)-\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)(u-v)\right]\right|

\displaystyle>\beta\cdot\Big|u(s,a)-v(s,a)\Big|~~~\text{for some couples $(u,v)$~ and ~ $\beta$}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow||\mathcal{T}_{a}u-\mathcal{T}_{a}v||_{\infty}

\displaystyle>\beta\cdot||u-v||_{\infty}

(5.1.7)

So, as we can see, whatever
\beta
will be, there exist situations where the operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
is not a contration.
∎

Since the operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
is not a contraction, we cannot assess its convergence. But, even if we are not sure about the convergence, we can still say something about its behavior. For that, inspired by the definitions given in
[
23
,
8
]
, let’s define two important properties that we will call the properties of a
well-behaving operator
. There are the optimality preservation and the gap increasing properties.

Note
: in the following definitions, we’ll change a little bit our conventions by writing value functions in capital letters. This will help us to write properly operators as indices.

5.1.5 Definition

(Optimality preservation)
.

Let call
\mathcal{T}_{a}
an alternative to the Bellman operator and
\mathcal{T}_{b}
the classical Bellman operator. We will say that
\mathcal{T}_{a}
preserves the optimality property if :

Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}<V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}\Rightarrow Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}<V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}~~~~\text{when $k\to\infty$}

Q_{k,\mathcal{T}}
represents the action value function that we find at the iteration
k
, using the operator
\mathcal{T}
and
V_{k,\mathcal{T}}
the associated state value function.

5.1.6 Definition

(Gap increasing)
.

Using the same notation as previously, we will say that an alternative operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
to the Bellman one
\mathcal{T}_{b}
induces the gap increasing property if, for each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and each feasible action in that state
a\in\mathcal{A}(s)
:

\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}\right]\right|\leq\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}\right]\right|

Knowing that at each state,
V_{k,\mathcal{T}}
is an average of all
Q_{k,\mathcal{T}}
that we can get by making a certain action from that state.

The
optimality preservation
and the
gap increasing
properties represent respectively :

•

How well the operator is likely to behave while looking for the fixed point and,

•

How is the model able to differentiate the values of suboptimal actions with those for optimal actions.

5.1.7 Proposition
.

We claim that, the operator defined by the Expression
5.1.6
, even if it is not a contraction, is both optimality preserving and gap increasing.

Proof.

Let’s prove those properties in the same order :

1.

Optimality preservation
: We know that
Q_{k,\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{T}Q_{k-1}
and
V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}=\displaystyle\sum_{a}\pi(a|s).Bell(Q_{k-1})
. Now, using the Definition
5.1.5
, we assume that
Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}<V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}
and we will deduce the relation between
Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}
and
V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}
. To make the notation easy to manage, we will do this replacement :

r(s,a)+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot Q_{k-1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)=Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)

Now we can start (knowing that we are working with
k\to\infty
) :

\displaystyle Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}

\displaystyle<V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{b}Q_{k-1}

\displaystyle<V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)

without a loss of generality, let
K=\min_{a}{\Big(Q_{k-1}(s,a)-V_{k-1}(s)\Big)}
. Then

\displaystyle Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot K

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot K\cdot 1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot K

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot K\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot K

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot K

we know that
K\leq\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}(s,a)-V_{k-1}(s)\right)~~\forall~(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, and when
k\to\infty
as we are getting better policy, we have almost surely that
\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)\leq\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot K

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot K

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\underset{Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}}{\underbrace{Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\Big[Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}(s,a)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}

\displaystyle<V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}~~~~~~~~~\text{as $k\to\infty$}

(5.1.8)

2.

Gap increasing
: Using the Definition
5.1.6
, we have now to show that :

\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}\right]\right|\leq\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}\right]\right|

In fact, we know that, using the Bellman Operator
\mathcal{T}_{b}
, if we are improving the policy at the same time, the state value function
V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}
will converge to the action value function
Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}
as the policy get better. So, for optimal actions we will have :

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}\right]=0,

i.e. also using
\mathcal{T}_{a}
, we can say surely for optimal actions :

\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}\right]\right|=0~~~~\leq~~~~\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}\right]\right|

For other actions, the inequality also holds because on convergence
\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)<0
almost surely and from the proof of optimality preservation we have seen that :

Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right)<\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot Bell\left(Q_{k-1}\right)+\beta\cdot\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)\cdot\left(Q_{k-1}-V_{k-1}\right),

i.e. also
\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{b}}\right]\right|\leq\left|\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[Q_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}-V_{k,\mathcal{T}_{a}}\right]\right|
for any
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.

∎

So, the operator that we suggested through the Expression
5.1.6
is a
well behaving operator
, even if we just assume the convergence (modulo the value of
\beta
).

Let us now give some comments about the possibility of finding a fixed point for this operator. But, before doing so, we can first say something about the continuity and the boundedness of that operator because without that, we cannot talk about fixed points.

5.1.8 Proposition
.

Let
r(s,a)
, the reward function, be bounded and continuous. The operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
as defined by the Expression
5.1.6
, will also be
bounded
and
continuous
.

Proof.

We can split our operator in three parts for simplicity :

\left(\mathcal{T}_{a}f\right)=\underset{\text{part 1}}{\underbrace{r(s,a)}}+\underset{\text{part 2}}{\underbrace{\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)}}+\underset{A(s,a)\text{:~advantage learning :: part 3}}{\underbrace{\beta\cdot\left[f(s,a)-\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)f(s,a)\right]}}

1.

Part 1
: the reward function is assumed to be continuous and bounded.

2.

Part 2
: If we take that part as an operator by itself, it will be bounded (because it is a contraction) and also linear. And being a bounded linear operator implies that it is also continuous. So, if we call this new operator
\mathcal{T}_{small}
written as follows :

\Big(\mathcal{T}_{small}f\Big)(s,a)=\gamma\cdot\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)=\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right),

We can firstly prove the linearity, assuming that
u,v\in\mathcal{Q}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We have to show that

\mathcal{T}_{small}\Big(\alpha\cdot u+\beta\cdot v\Big)=\alpha\cdot\mathcal{T}_{small}~u+\beta\cdot\mathcal{T}_{small}~v

And, that is true because the operator is just formed with average operations, which are in fact linear. So,
\mathcal{T}_{small}
is linear.

Let’s now prove the contraction. For that, using again
u
and
v
defined previously, we have :

\displaystyle\Big|\mathcal{T}_{small}~u-\mathcal{T}_{small}~v\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot\left(u(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big|\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot\left(u(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\cdot\Big|\left(u(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{s^{\prime},a^{\prime}}{\max}\Big|\left(u(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\Big|

\displaystyle=\gamma\cdot\underset{s,a}{\max}\Big|\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\Big|\mathcal{T}_{small}~u-\mathcal{T}_{small}~v\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{s,a}{\max}\Big|\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\underset{s,a}{\max}\Big|\mathcal{T}_{small}~u-\mathcal{T}_{small}~v\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot\underset{s,a}{\max}\Big|\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow||\mathcal{T}_{small}~u-\mathcal{T}_{small}~v||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\cdot||\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)||_{\infty}

(5.1.9)

Thus, the second part is also continuous and bounded.

3.

Part 3
: We know that, if
f
is continuous and bounded, then
f(s,a)-\sum_{a}\pi(a|s)f(s,a)
will also be continuous and bounded. We just need to ensure that
\beta
has good properties, ensuring convergence.

Finally, knowing that the summation of continuous and bounded operators is also continuous and bounded, we have proved the assertion, and
\mathcal{T}_{a}
is continuous and bounded (the notion of good behavior for
\beta
is just to be precised).
∎

From the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{a}
is continuous and bounded (modulo the behavior of
\beta
), we are sure that it will not diverge if we set well the values of
\beta
. And, looking carefully on that operator, we can now say something about
\beta
.

We will always need an operator which is well behaving (improving speed and optimality) but also close enough to the classical Bellman Operator as we are evolving. That is the only way we can ensure that we will get closer enough to the
same fixed point
while maintaining some nice properties of this alternative operator.

If now we consider a family of operators based on
\mathcal{T}_{a}
by varying
\beta
, and the case of a
\beta
which changes per iteration in one operator during the learning process, to ensure convergence, they should fulfill the following conditions :

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i,j}&<&\infty\\
&&\\
\{\beta_{i,j}\}&\to&0~~~\text{with $j$ fixed.}\end{array}\right.~~\text{$i$ : the operator,~~ $j$ : iteration}

(5.1.10)

For an operator which has a varying
\beta
across training, as it will be applied iteratively, we can directly notice that after many iterations, we will have some terms like this

\sum\beta_{i,j}\cdot\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)_{j}\equiv\sum\beta_{i,j}\cdot A_{j},

and as the difference
\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)_{j}
is bounded, we can replace it by its bound. Let’s call the bound of
\left(u(s,a)-v(s,a)\right)_{j}\equiv A_{j}
simply
A
. Then :

\sum\beta_{i,j}\cdot A_{j}\leq A\cdot\sum_{j}\beta_{i,j}<\infty\iff\sum_{j}\beta_{i,j}<\infty

So, for one operator, with varying
\beta
across iterations, the sum of values should be finite.

Also, with the conditions we gave, it allows us to construct directly a sequence of operators converging to the classical Bellman Operator. So, that’s why we need :

\{\beta_{i,j}\}\to 0~~~\text{with $j$ fixed.}

5.1.9 Example
.

One example of a family of operators with the form given by the Expression
5.1.6
and fulfilling the conditions on
\beta
given by
5.1.10
, can be :

1.

Operator 1 :
\beta
is a sequence given by
\{\beta_{1,j}\}
with
\beta_{1,j}=\dfrac{1}{j^{2}}

2.

Operator 2 :
\beta
is a sequence given by
\{\beta_{2,j}\}
with
\beta_{2,j}=\dfrac{1}{j^{3}}

3.

Operator
k
:
\beta
is a sequence given by
\{\beta_{k,j}\}
with
\beta_{k,j}=\dfrac{1}{j^{k+1}}
.

As last comment on this part we can observe : during our diverse proofs, we just require to the mappings to be contractions and
monotonic
in order to establish the existence of an
optimal
fixed point. The use of specific definitions of value functions was not really important. This is why we can conjecture that :

5.1.10 Conjecture
.

Any monotonic contraction mapping integreting a certain notion of policy is a suitable candidate as operator in Reinforcement Learning (we just have to refine accordingly the definitions of the associated value functions for them to be suitable to the RL framework).

Chapter Conclusion

In this last chapter, we have introduced the Consistent Bellman Operator as the first alternative to the classical Bellman Operator presented in the previous chapter, and we showed that it fulfills exactly the same properties as the classical one (uniqueness of the optimal fixed point), although this raises the question of the relation between a fixed point found by the Consistent Bellman and the one we can find by the classical Bellman. Secondly, we presented our version of the robust stochastic operator. We modified the one proposed in the literature in such a way that it no longer needs stochasticity, but still preserves some good properties (gap increasing and optimality preservation), even if it loses the properties of a classical Bellman operator. Finally, with results from a practical implementation, we clearly show that our proposed operator must be taken seriously, since it unexpectedly outperforms the classical Bellman operator on some difficult tasks, as hypothesised.

Chapter 6
Conclusion

This work serves as a foundational effort in the deep and precise mathematical study of Reinforcement Learning concepts. The aim is to facilitate breakthrough discoveries and subsequent investigations, and to enable even pure mathematicians to contribute to this subject by suggesting insightful adjustments necessary for improving Reinforcement Learning algorithms. This has been achieved through the various chapters in this work as follows:

After the general introduction, in Chapter
2
, we primarily discussed contraction mappings in a metric space and the Banach Fixed Point Theorem. We also demonstrated how the Banach Fixed Point Theorem can be used to prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions to first-order Ordinary Differential Equations with initial conditions.

In Chapter
3
, we expressed the Reinforcement Learning problem in terms of a Markov Decision Process. This allowed us to introduce different expressions of the value function, both in terms of state and state-action pairs. Subsequently, we presented the concepts of Bellman Equations, which served as the first step in introducing the Bellman Operators.

Chapter
4
was dedicated to Bellman Operators. We have demonstrated that these operators are
\gamma
-contraction and monotonic mappings, implying that they have a unique fixed point that is the optimum. This justification sheds light on why and how Reinforcement Learning algorithms work from a fundamental perspective.

At the end, in chapter
5
, we presented the Consistent Bellman Operator as the first alternative to the classical Bellman Operator. After that, we showed that it fulfills exactly the same properties as the classical Bellman Operator (uniqueness of the optimal fixed point). However, this raised questions about the relationship between a fixed point found through the Consistent Bellman Operator and one found through the classical Operator. We then introduced a modification of the Robust Stochastic Operator, which is deterministic but defined in a more general and precise way. This demonstrates that stochasticity is no longer necessary to achieve better results than the classical Bellman Operator. Finally, using an implementation in Python and OpenAI Gymnasium environments, we clearly demonstrated that our proposed operator merits serious consideration, as it outperforms the classical Bellman Operator in many of the considered tasks.

Based on the foundations laid by this work, the perspectives are numerous. The mathematical properties of value function spaces have been clearly presented (value functions can indeed be properly studied in Banach spaces), but future researchers can explore in depth the state space and the action space as well as the policy space. They can also extend the analysis of the efficiency of our proposed operator. The most relevant attempts we have seen during our research are in
[
12
]
, where the geometric and topological properties of value functions for Markov decision processes are explored, and in
[
21
]
, where a unified formalism for defining state similarity metrics in Reinforcement Learning is introduced, hierarchies among metrics are established, and their implications for Reinforcement Learning algorithms are studied. We expect that these can be well understood and extended with the help of the investigations done in this work.

This work enables researchers, even those not currently involved in the field, to quickly and meaningfully engage with Reinforcement Learning, as the most important foundations are mathematically clarified.
