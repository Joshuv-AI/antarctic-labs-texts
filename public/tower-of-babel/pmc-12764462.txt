Predicting critical crack propagation length in sustainable additive-enhanced concrete using explainable machine learning
Authors: Manish Kewalramani, Arsalan Mahmoodzadeh, Raouf Hassan, Mohamed Hechmi El Ouni, Abdullah Alqahtani, Abed Alanazi, Shtwai Alsubai, Sivaprakasam Palani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting the critical crack propagation length (CCPL) of sustainable additive-enhanced concrete (SAEC) is a significant challenge in structural durability analysis and fracture mechanics. Experimental and numerical techniques often face limitations of complexity, cost, and computational inefficiency. To overcome these limitations, this paper presents a comprehensive machine learning framework that integrates ensemble, kernel-based, and deep learning models. A high-quality experimental dataset of 800 SAEC samples, incorporating nine key features and controlled curing, mixing, and fracture testing, was prepared. Model performance was evaluated using different statistical indices under both hold-out and k-fold cross-validation. Among all the machine learning models, the novel Neural Tangent Kernel Gaussian Process (NTK–GP) achieved the best predictive performance with R2 = 0.95‒0.96, RMSE = 0.74‒0.90 mm, MAPE = 0.09‒0.14, and VAF = 0.95‒0.96. The NTK–GP’s hybrid architecture, which unites the flexibility of neural representations with Bayesian uncertainty quantification, enabled accurate, smooth, and stable predictions even under nonlinear, high-dimensional data. Statistical significance tests, such as the Friedman and Nemenyi tests, confirmed that the NTK-GP is statistically comparable to several state-of-the-art models. Explainable AI analysis using SHAP revealed that fiber type (FT) and fiber volume content (FVC) are the most influential features, accounting for over 65% of the model’s variance in CCPL. SHAP interaction and dependency plots showed strong combined influences between FT and FVC, especially with steel and basalt fibers at higher volumes. This supports the idea that these fibers bridge cracks and dissipate energy. Bootstrap-based 95% confidence intervals were applied for uncertainty quantification, confirming the predictive reliability by showing consistent coverage across the dataset. This study pioneers the use of NTK–GP for fracture mechanics. It demonstrates that integrating explainable machine learning with uncertainty-aware regression provides a data-efficient, robust, and interpretable alternative to experimental and numerical methods. The proposed framework not only enhances CCPL prediction accuracy and computational efficiency but also contributes to the broader goal of designing sustainable, fracture-resistant concrete materials through intelligent and data-driven modeling.Keywords: Sustainable additive-enhanced concrete, Critical crack propagation length, Machine learning, Statistical analysis, Uncertainty quantificationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Since the application of fracture mechanics to concrete, much work has focused on determining concrete’s fracture toughness to better assess crack propagation. Early work calculated fracture toughness by using a concrete sample’s peak load and initial crack length in the linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM) formula, yielding a size-dependent fracture toughness. Concrete, being quasi-brittle, undergoes a stable crack-propagation phase before total failure. The critical crack length (CCL), which includes the initial crack length and the critical crack propagation length (CCPL), should be used to determine concrete’s fracture toughness. Because of the importance of the CCPL, it has been the focus of extensive research in recent years1,2.

The CCPL is essential for determining how tough concrete is when it breaks and for assessing the safety of concrete structures with cracks. Because of its importance, the CCPL of concrete has been the subject of extensive study in recent years. Early research used dye penetration to measure crack growth3. This method involved applying colored dye to pre-cracked concrete samples. The crack length was then determined from the area of the stained, cracked surface. Horii and Ichinomiya4, along with Xu and Reinhardt, studied concrete crack growth using a laser speckle technique. They got displacement data from laser speckle images taken before and after deformation using a special optical approach. Crack length was determined by examining the continuity of the displacement data. The photoelastic coating technique, as detailed in studies5–7, allows for a fair reproduction of specimen surface deformation in the bonded coating.

CPL can be found through the coating’s fringe pattern using the strain-optic law. Also, the acoustic emission (AE) technique8–10can locate microcracks in concrete using surface-mounted AE sensors. The CPL is then determined by examining AE events or energy. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) allows us to observe the specimen surface and indirectly measure crack growth11–13. Digital image correlation (DIC) is another way to measure CPL in concrete14–16. By comparing spackle patterns before and after deformation, we can get the displacement and strain fields of the specimen surface. If the crack tip is well-defined, crack growth length can be accurately determined. While these methods can measure crack growth in concrete, they have limitations that prevent their widespread use. First, the experiments are complex, costly, time-consuming to prepare, and difficult to run on a standard testing machine. Second, many methods are sensitive to light, noise, and vibration in the lab. Third, some methods damage the concrete specimen17.

As computer science advances, numerical simulation methods such as the finite element method (FEM)18–20, the extended finite element method (XFEM)21,22, and the boundary element method23 are now used to predict how cracks propagate. XFEM has become a favored method because it avoids the need to redo meshing by using enrichment functions to define cracks. Still, XFEM requires crack-propagation criteria, and applying them to three-dimensional problems can be tricky. To handle more challenging problems, Bourdin et al.24 proposed a variable-based approach based on Griffith’s theory for simulating brittle fracture, where the solution is obtained by minimizing a global energy function. Also, Miehe et al.25–27put forward phase-field models in an engineering style similar to that of damage problems, which greatly helped fracture phase-field modeling. Today, the fracture phase-field method is widely used and effective for a range of fracture problems28–30. Yet, obtaining an exact solution from the fracture phase-field model requires a fine mesh and small time steps, which makes simulations costly31–33. To make calculations faster, some researchers have been working on high-performance computing plans33–38. To mitigate the impact of fine discretization, a mesh-adaptation method has been developed to help reduce CPU time more effectively34–37. Numerical solvers can be slow, so some improved numerical methods are being used in simulations38–40. Ensuring that numerical methods remain both computationally efficient and numerically stable is a challenging task, as achieving higher efficiency is constrained by the computational cost of fine discretization and large-scale matrix operations31,33,37,39.

While traditional methods provide valuable information on crack propagation, they are often limited by their inherent limitations. These problems (such as high costs, long preparation times, sensitivity to test conditions, and the risk of sample damage) highlight the need for alternative and potentially more efficient approaches. With the continuous evolution of computational tools, machine learning models are emerging as promising alternatives or complements to traditional methods. With the rapid development of machine learning techniques, a growing number of scholars have begun exploring data-driven approaches to predict the performance of various structures41–46. These methods have helped with complex simulations47–53. Unlike conventional techniques, these models can analyze large, multidimensional datasets to predict complex behaviors, such as crack propagation in concrete, thereby avoiding the need for time-consuming experiments and costly simulations. Machine learning approaches offer relatively fast, scalable, and data-efficient solutions for predicting CCPL. Once trained, these models can make accurate predictions with modest computational effort, without the need for detailed mesh refinements or complex experimental setups. Furthermore, machine learning frameworks can incorporate uncertainty quantification and provide probabilistic predictions that account for variations in material properties and experimental conditions. These advances have the potential to significantly reduce the time and cost of traditional methods, while providing high generalizability and predictive reliability in real-world applications.

Motlagh et al.49 used different physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to develop deep learning phase-field models for brittle fracture, thereby advancing data-driven solutions for the brittle phase-field method. Deep learning has also accelerated the analysis of material strength and fracture in cracked materials54–58. Xu et al.58 used a deep neural network to predict the load-bearing capacity of brittle materials with microcracks. This offers a way to quickly predict and assess other mechanical and physical properties of microcracked materials, which could help design better composites. Wei et al.59 combined neural networks with a discrete simulation model to predict material fracture under different loads, thereby improving prediction accuracy compared to data-driven models. Many researchers have used machine learning models to speed up computations51,52,59,60. Sepasdar et al.61 made a deep learning framework for composite fracture failure, using two U-net neural networks to predict failure patterns. It predicts the final crack state, but doesn’t show how it propagates, so it needs many training examples. To fix this, neural networks can learn how fracture propagates48,62. Dinh et al.48 and Trung et al.62 used the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models for the brittle fracture phase-field evolution, accurately predicting a specific case. But mesh refinement in fracture simulation produces high-dimensional data, increasing training time and the scale of machine learning models63. Zhang et al.64 proposed an innovative approach to improve the prediction of axial compressive capacity of coal-filled concrete steel tubular columns (CFFCS). This approach combined genetic programming-enhanced code calibration and Bayesian-optimized machine learning. Their study improved on existing design codes and identified CatBoost as the best predictor, thereby providing a solution for accurately modeling the impact of coal aggregate replacement rates on sustainable building materials. Zhang et al.65 proposed an AI-assisted analysis framework combining machine learning and multi-objective optimization (MOO) for the design of sustainable glass powder concrete (GPC) that balances cost, carbon emissions, and mechanical performance. By optimizing concrete mix design through Bayesian fitting and Pareto solutions, this framework provided practical recommendations for engineers to promote circular and low-carbon economy principles in the construction sector. In phase-field methods for microstructure evolution, this issue has been addressed using dimensionality reduction (e.g., manifold learning)50,66–68, which maps high-fidelity data to a lower-dimensional latent space. This isn’t often used to speed up the fracture phase-field method because dimensionality reduction can introduce errors when processing fracture phase-field features. This approach also isn’t good at extrapolating from known cases to quickly predict crack propagation63. This study addresses limitations in earlier methods, including experimental, numerical, and data-based approaches, for assessing the CCPL of Sustainable Additive-Enhanced Concrete (SAEC). Past work on fracture has advanced techniques such as DIC, AE, SEM, FEM, XFEM, and phase-field models. Still, each of these methods faces issues with cost, difficulty, expansion, and computer use. Even current data methods, like deep learning and PINNs, are often constrained by the need for large amounts of data, generalization problems, and poor handling of uncertainty when applied to reinforced concrete. To fix these ongoing limits, this paper gives a machine learning system that combines models like Support Vector Regression (SVR), Nu-Support Vector Regression (NuSVR), Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), Multilayer Perceptron Regression (MLPR), Extra Tree Regression (ETR), Decision Tree Regression (DTR), Random Forest Regression (RFR), Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and a new Neural Tangent Kernel Gaussian Process (NTK–GP). Each was made, tuned, and tested to see how well it could find the links between mixture design, fiber details, curing, and how SAEC breaks. This data-driven approach, unlike standard fracture studies or numerical tests, is safe, inexpensive, and well-suited to computers, so there is no need for long experiments or fine meshes. By using kernel-based learning, ensemble methods, and deep neural networks, the study achieves strong predictive performance while remaining clear and consistent with physics. The NTK–GP provides a blend of theory and computational work that combines neural network power with Gaussian process statistics, yielding both accurate predictions and uncertainty estimates—a key feature missing from prior studies.

This research has a hyperparameter tuning plan using grid search and cross-validation to ensure fair, repeatable model testing. Adding uncertainty quantification and explainable AI tools, such as SHAP, makes the results more reliable and more transparent. These make it easier to spot the key variables in the predicted CCPL, providing solid information that aligns with fracture ideas. Past numerical methods often incur excessive computational costs and mesh problems. The models here learn from test data, yielding reasonably fast CCPL estimates with modest computational effort. By using regression methods that account for uncertainty, the study ensures that CCPL values include confidence intervals, providing engineers with a valuable tool for making safe choices.

In short, this paper presents a new machine learning system that links fracture studies with modern data-driven methods. It aims to address some limitations of past approaches and provides predictions that are competitive with existing data-driven models by integrating kernel theory, ensemble learning, and hybrid Gaussian methods. This system provides a precise, reliable, and computationally practical way to understand how cracks grow in SAEC, marking a meaningful step forward in both fracture research and innovative material design.

Scope and significance of the research

This research seeks to improve the ability to predict CCPL in SAEC. The study applies different machine learning methods chosen for their strengths in modeling nonlinear relationships, data use, and predictive power. These models (kernel-based regression, ensemble learning, deep neural networks, and Gaussian process frameworks) are applied to a detailed experimental dataset comprising various concrete mixes, mechanical properties, and curing conditions. Accurately predicting CCPL is key to increasing concrete’s durability and reliability in real-world applications, particularly in structures operating in harsh environments and under high stress.

Scope of the research

The main aim is to study and compare how well several machine learning models predict CCPL across different inputs, such as concrete mix, fiber type, and curing conditions. The research covers the application of several machine learning models, including SVR, NuSVR, GPR, GBR, MLPR, DTR, RFR, XGBoost, ETR, and NTK–GP. These models were selected for their ability to handle complex, high-dimensional, and noisy datasets, a common challenge in predicting CCPL in concrete. The NTK–GP model stands out for mixing deep learning-style feature extraction with Bayesian reasoning, making it fit for modeling material behaviors accurately. The study adds uncertainty quantification methods to assess the confidence of model predictions, which are vital for making engineering decisions. Unlike point estimates, uncertainty allows for understanding the variability and reliability of forecasts.

Methodology

In this study, we employed several machine learning methods, including kernel-based regression, ensemble approaches, and deep learning models. Each algorithm was selected for its ability to identify nonlinear relationships, its adaptability to limited data, and its particular advantages in predicting CCPL of SAEC.

NuSVR and SVR

Kernel regressors project the input space into a higher dimension, allowing nonlinear patterns to be handled as linear. SVR uses an ε-insensitive loss function to tolerate minor deviations from target values83, while NuSVR’s ν parameter balances the number of support vectors and the error tolerance84. They both effectively modeled the nonlinear links between input features and the target.

GPR

GPR is a flexible, non-parametric approach that uses a Gaussian process prior over functions. The model’s prediction includes a mean and a confidence interval, which is useful when assessing uncertainty in complex material behavior85. By employing a specific kernel, GPR determines the relationship between input variables. This allows the model to smoothly interpret experimental data and learn the nonlinear relationships within CCPL data.

ANN

An ANN, inspired by biological neural networks, serves as a competent tool for function approximation, adept at identifying complex, non-linear relationships between input variables86. Owing to its flexible design, it can capture and generalize intricate connections in concrete mixture design and mechanical behavior. Still, this method calls for careful regularization and thorough training to avoid overfitting, especially when working with medium-sized datasets.

GBR

GBR gains predictive power by using a series of simple decision trees. Each tree is trained to fix the errors made by the previous trees87. This iterative process produces predictions that are both reliable and consistent. When used with CCPL data, this method handles complex relationships between inputs and its structure balances bias and overfitting, which is helpful when dealing with noisy or small datasets.

DTR

DTR works by dividing data based on its features until prediction errors are acceptably low88. DTR is computationally easy to interpret, but it tends to overfit, especially in systems such as the CCPL of SAEC. Overfitting occurs when the model reacts too strongly to small changes in the data, or when key variables are omitted. This can lead to inaccurate predictions when the model encounters new data.

RFR

RFR extends the single-tree method by training multiple individual trees, each trained on a different random subset of rows and columns89. Averaging the predictions from these trees reduces the variance and noise that a single tree might amplify. This ensemble approach creates a more stable model, better suited for managing intricate relationships in UHPC data.

XGBoost

XGBoost, an improved form of gradient boosting, aims to improve performance by making it more computationally efficient and more accurate. It fixes mistakes by fitting more trees to the errors and adds rules to keep the model simple90. Using shrinkage controls how much each tree contributes, and developing tree nodes simultaneously reduces processing time. In CCPL prediction, XGBoost balances variance and accuracy. It works well even if some features are left out, and it gives accurate estimates.

NTK-GP

NTK–GP is a framework linking extensive neural networks to Gaussian processes. It uses the neural tangent kernel (NTK). NTK is a kernel that shows how small changes in network settings affect the model’s output. This kernel is used to model complex nonlinear relationships and estimate uncertainty91. Basically, when a fully-connected neural network starts with random weights and becomes infinitely vast, its training behavior using gradient descent can be approximated by a Gaussian Process. This process draws on the network’s structure and activation function. The NTK controls how input points relate in the function space and remains constant during training under certain conditions. Although Eqs. (1) and (2) describe NTK in the infinite-width range, in practice, NTK was computed using a multilayer perceptron (MLP) of finite size but large enough to best approximate this regime. The network used to estimate NTK consisted of four hidden layers of width [1024, 1024, 1024, 1024], which is consistent with previous work showing that widths ≥ 1000 per layer produce a nearly constant experimental NTK during training and are well aligned with the theoretical kernel. All weights were initialized using the He normal distribution (variance 2/fan-in) and the biases were initialized to zero, maintaining the necessary scaling assumptions for convergence to the infinite-width NTK. To obtain the NTK kernel matrix, the network’s parameter Jacobians were computed and integrated over the training samples. Since the inversion of the NTK Gram matrix can be numerically impractical, a Tikhonov regularization was applied by adding a small perturbation term εI to the diameter. In this study, we used ε = 10⁻⁶ to ensure a stable Cholesky factorization without affecting the model’s accuracy. The resulting stable kernel matrix was then used in analytical calculations of the posterior predictor mean and covariance of the GP. This method produces an empirical NTK matrix of finite width that is close to the theoretical NTK matrix of infinite width while remaining numerically stable for regression.

NTK–GP models blend the adaptability of neural networks with the strong theory and clarity of Gaussian processes. They work well at understanding smooth, complex, and nonlinear links without overfitting to small data sets. This is a common problem in materials science and structural engineering. This research used the NTK–GP model to estimate the CCPL based on the material’s composition, strength, and curing method. Unlike standard kernels like RBF or Matérn, the NTK adapts to the data, learning relationships similar to those in deep learning setups without requiring repeated adjustments. This mix of neural network-style representation and Bayesian reasoning makes NTK–GP especially useful for modeling how materials act, predicting fractures, and in other areas where being able to understand and trust the model is as necessary as getting accurate results.

We used Jupyter Notebook with Python 3.7 (Anaconda Navigator) for all machine learning model development and assessment. Our setup included an Intel Core i7-10750H processor (2.60 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. This system could manage training several models at once, SHAP computations, and cross-validation smoothly. Each machine learning model’s hyperparameters were tuned using a grid search over the training data. This method tests various hyperparameter settings to find the best combination for model performance. Cross-validation was used to score each attempt. Grid Search provides a clear, repeatable tuning history, showing that all configurations were considered and that results were compared in a structured way. Dataset preparation

Materials and mix design

In this study, 780 samples were generated through a comprehensive series of controlled laboratory experiments. All materials were carefully selected and characterized, and batch certificates were collected and recorded. The type and brand of cement, aggregate particle size distribution and absorption, water source, fiber type, and any additional cementitious materials or additives were determined, measured, and recorded. A precise formulation and a standard mixing protocol were developed to ensure homogeneity in all batches. Fibers were added gradually during mixing to achieve uniform dispersion and to prevent agglomeration. The measured values for each batch were recorded to two significant figures.

The geometry of the specimens and the characteristics of the notches were carefully defined and recorded. Prism beams (Fig. 1) were used for three-point bending tests; the nominal dimensions (length 400 mm × width 100 mm × height 100 mm) were recorded. The depth and position of the initial groove tip were checked (generally 20–30% of the specimen depth with the sharp tip), and the method of sharpening the groove was recorded. The molds and supports were labeled, and the nominal bore/depth ratio for each specimen geometry was recorded in the test report.

Detailed laboratory arrangement illustrating materials, equipment, and specimen preparation steps for SAEC specimens.

The molds were prepared (cleaned and greased or coated with a release agent), placed on a flat surface, and the necessary inserts or guides were installed to ensure uniform notch placement. Casting was performed according to a documented dosing and mixing procedure; the specimens were filled in uniform layers and compacted using a standard method (specified protocol and vibration time). When adding fibers, the vibration intensity and duration were carefully controlled to prevent fiber separation. The casting date and time, as well as environmental conditions (temperature and relative humidity), were recorded for each batch.

After the specified initial curing period, the specimens were removed from the mold, and the groove was inspected and, if necessary, reworked to the exact depth using a water-cooled saw or notching machine. The final dimensions of the specimens (width, depth, length) and the notch depth were measured and recorded, and the radius of curvature of the notch end or the sharpening method was photographed.

All specimens were cured according to the selected controlled method, including immersion in lime water, storage at standard humidity conditions (20 ± 2 °C and relative humidity > 95%), or curing under a controlled atmosphere. The curing method, temperature, relative humidity, and start and end date and time were carefully recorded for each batch.

Mechanical testing and measurement of CCPL

The specimens were allowed to adapt to the laboratory conditions for a prescribed duration before assessment. They were then subjected to visual analysis of the specimens. The specimen photographs and notched area photographs were taken. Photographs were taken with scale bars for reference during measurements. The mass and measurements of the specimens were recorded and logged again, and weights were retaken for defects to the specimen surface. If notches were sharpened right before testing, it was documented, and a photograph was taken. All pre-session metadata were combined into the specimen file, including operators, date/time, and history, before storage/record.

Testing equipment and sensors were prepared, and the apparatus was calibrated before the start of the experimental campaign. Load cells and calibration certificates were verified, along with the corresponding dates, and documented. For fracture measurement, a high-precision load cell was used to record load versus time, and the machine crosshead displacement was captured to capture overall deformation. A local clip gauge or LVDT was utilized to measure the crack mouth opening displacement. High-resolution cameras and a Digital Image Correlation (DIC) system were strategically placed to observe the notch and the crack tip during loading. The camera mounts were securely attached, and calibration images were obtained to convert pixel measurements to millimeters. We selected sampling frequencies to ensure that the crack events are accurately captured (10 Hz for the quasi-static tests and up to 100 Hz for the more rapid events). The clocks of the sensors and cameras were also synchronized for precise timing.

The three-point bending setup was assembled with specific span measurements and roller radii, all of which were noted. Each sample was positioned with the notch centered at the midpoint, perpendicular to the load. Alignment shims and a spirit level were used to ensure correct placement (as seen in Fig. 2). Tests were run under displacement control to capture post-peak fracture behavior and avoid unstable failures. A constant displacement rate, usually between 0.02 and 0.1 mm/min for quasi-static fracture testing, was kept, and the rate for each test was documented. To ensure thorough sensor contact with the surface, a light initial load—roughly 5–10% of the estimated peak load—was applied, and then the local sensors were zeroed. Before using any load, preliminary data and images were gathered to establish a baseline. Data acquisition went on until a predetermined stopping condition was met, such as displacement after reaching the peak, cessation of crack growth, or specimen fracture. All stopping conditions were recorded.

Three-point bending test.

Calipers and photos were used to measure the initial notch length (a0) right before testing. During loading, image analysis was the primary method for tracking the crack tip. We examined image sequences or DIC outputs to locate the crack tip in each frame, and we converted pixel coordinates to millimeters using a calibration scale. In DIC, the crack tip was the location where the strain gradient or displacement changed most. For standard imaging, we used edge detection and then manually checked the results. The CCPL was the crack length at which unstable fracture began (the point at which the load dropped quickly after the peak). The critical crack length (ac) was determined from load, CMOD, and time data and confirmed by image measurements. We defined the CCPL as Δa = ac–a0 (in mm), where positive values indicate crack growth up to the critical point.

For time-resolved examination, a(t) data came from tracking the crack tip frame by frame, and we figured out growth rates (da/dt) right away. We guessed at our uncertainty in crack length by looking at the pixel resolution (in millimeters) and used that to estimate Δa and da/dt when needed. These crack length numbers were what we mainly wanted to predict and understand using machine learning.

Overall data analysis

The total number of physical tests performed was 780, corresponding to 290 mix designs with 2 to 3 replicates per mix, and CCPL measurements were distributed across all batches to ensure a representative dataset for modeling crack propagation behavior in SAEC. Therefore, a database including 780 samples was achieved with nine features including water-to-cement ratio (w/c), maximum aggregate size (MAS), silica fume content (SFC), fly-ash content (FAC), superplasticizer content (SPC), fiber volume content (FVC), curing time (CT), fiber type (FT), and nano silica content (NSC). To find outliers, a step-by-step statistical method based on the Interquartile Range (IQR) was used. The first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles for each item were found, and the IQR was calculated as IQR = Q3 − Q1. Any items outside of [Q1 − 1.5 × IQR, Q3 + 1.5 × IQR] were labeled as outliers and taken out. Among the data points, 20 outliers were identified and the remaining 760 samples were used for machine learning analysis. Data normalization

To ensure that each input parameter contributed to training the machine learning algorithm, we first normalized the new experimental data. The data set included features with different numerical ranges and units, such as geometric proportions (dimensionless), mechanical strengths, and blending proportions expressed as mass percentages. Without normalization, the features with the highest magnitude could have dominated the model parameter selection and hidden the contributions from smaller-scale features that were still important for accurate predictions. Normalization aims to scale all variables to a common number system while keeping their original distribution and interdependencies. After looking at different scaling techniques, min–max normalization method was used because it is simple, direct, and appropriate for algorithms that are sensitive to data scaling. The transformation for any feature (x) was done using Eq. 3. The minimum and maximum observed function values are xmin and xmax, respectively. This process scales each observation to a range between 0 and 1. This way all predictors give the same input and stay in the same range while learning. If outliers are likely, we find them before scaling to lessen any warping of the rescaled data.

Results analysis

This section gives an overview of the machine-learning setup designed to estimate how long cracks will grow in enhanced concrete. All models were trained using input data that had been normalized using min–max, and data was split into training and testing sets at a ratio of 80:20. K-fold cross-validation was also used to test each model. The NTK-GP model had the most stable and quickest convergence. As shown in Fig. 7, training and validation losses in the NTK-GP model quickly fell during the first few training cycles before leveling off, showing good learning and no overfitting. The following sections compare model performance using different validation methods, performance metrics, and explainable machine learning via SHAP values to rank input variables by importance to crack growth.

The NTK-GP model did so well because it can approximate very large neural networks using the NTK, which is good at capturing complicated, smooth, and nonlinear relationships between the input variables. The model includes probabilistic regularization through its kernel structure, which helps it balance bias and variance and deal with noise in the experimental data. The NTK-GP training curve (Fig. 7) shows it converges quickly and becomes stable, which confirms it learns well and avoids overfitting. Because of this, NTK-GP is a dependable way to map the complex relationships between the material’s composition, curing conditions, and how cracks spread in sustainable concretes.

The MLPR model was the second best, with an R2 of 0.93 and RMSE of 0.97 mm. This shows it is also good at capturing nonlinear interactions between features, like those between FT, CT, and additive amount. As an adaptable nonlinear function approximator, the MLPR could model the multidimensional parameter space after being properly tuned and regularized. Differently from NTK-GP, its performance changes more depending on the hyperparameter settings, and it may need careful control to avoid overfitting using methods like dropout or weight decay. Still, the MLPR’s good results show that it can still be applied well to new data if trained under controlled conditions.

The GBR model was third overall, with an R2 of 0.92 and RMSE of 0.99 mm. This shows it can model nonlinear and interaction effects by fitting residuals in stages. Because it is an ensemble model based on sequential tree, it can progressively correct mistakes from preceding weak learners. This leads to high accuracy with little overfitting if the regularization parameters are properly tuned. Its strong performance across all measures suggests that the data has structural nonlinearity that gradient boosting can capture well.

The RFR and ETR models also did well, both with R2 values of 0.91 and RMSEs of 1.06 and 1.08 mm, respectively. These ensemble algorithms work by combining multiple decision trees trained on subsets of data, which helps reduce variance and allow for better generalization. While RFR and ETR had good prediction abilities, their slightly lower performance compared to GBR and MLPR might be because their decision boundaries are more varied, which can cause some prediction imprecision. Even so, their interpretability and resistance to overfitting make them dependable tools for analyzing feature relevance.

The GPR model had an R2 of 0.91 and RMSE of 0.98 mm, similar to RFR and GBR. This result confirms that Gaussian Process models are good for capturing smooth nonlinear trends and uncertainty in experimental datasets. The slightly lower performance compared to NTK-GP likely comes from the limits of stationary kernel functions, which may not capture heteroscedastic noise or non-stationary dependencies between input variables well enough. Still, the GPR is helpful for measuring uncertainty and making probabilistic inferences.

On the other hand, DTR had much weaker prediction ability (R2 = 0.80, RMSE = 1.61 mm). This shows the high variance and piecewise-constant nature of single-tree models. Because there is no ensemble averaging, it tends to overfit local data and not be able to generalize well to new data. So, while DTR models are straightforward and computationally lightweight, they are not good as standalone predictors for complex and nonlinear material behavior like crack spread in composite concretes.

Interestingly, XGBoost, which often competes well in regression tasks, only had moderate accuracy in this study (R2 = 0.79, RMSE = 1.73 mm). This underperformance likely happened because of non-ideal hyperparameter tuning, excessive tree depth, or not enough regularization during training. It is also possible that the complex nonlinear interactions in the data favor kernel- and network-based methods over boosting algorithms, especially when the data dimensionality and interaction depth go beyond what the XGBoost configuration used here can handle. Changes to the learning rate, early stopping, and feature weighting might improve its prediction ability in the future.

The SVR model had the weakest results, with R2 = 0.72 and RMSE = 1.93 mm. This is because the SVR is very sensitive to kernel selection and hyperparameter settings (C, ε, and γ), and it does not scale well to higher-dimensional or heteroscedastic data. In this case, the nonlinear relationships and mixed variables in the data likely went beyond what the selected SVR kernel could represent, leading to underfitting.

The scatter plots in Fig. 8 support these findings. The best models (NTK-GP, MLPR, GBR, and RFR) show data points clustered tightly around the 1:1 line, meaning there is good calibration and little systematic bias. In contrast, the weaker models (SVR, XGBoost, DTR) show significant dispersion, especially at higher measured CCPL values, which indicates underfitting or not enough feature representation. A slight regression-to-mean pattern can be seen for some models at the higher end of the CCPL range, which is common when data is sparse in extreme-value regions. In general, the statistical results and visual assessments confirm that the NTK-GP model has the best balance of accuracy, stability, and generalization of all the algorithms tested. Its strong probabilistic design and ability to model smooth nonlinear functions make it well-suited for predicting fracture-related properties of sustainable concretes. The consistent performance of MLPR, GBR, and RFR also supports the reliability of ensemble and neural-based methods in handling the complex feature dependencies that control crack spread. On the other hand, models with more basic architectures like DTR, SVR, and untuned XGBoost could not fully capture the nonlinear and interactive relationships in the data. Practically, these results show that predicting CCPL in SAEC accurately requires models that can represent nonlinear interactions between mix composition, curing conditions, and fiber properties. Kernel-based and deep learning models, especially NTK-GP, not only give better prediction results but also can be combined with explainable machine learning techniques like SHAP. These techniques can show how key variables affect crack spread behavior.

Statistical significance testing

When algorithms were compared across experimental tasks using RMSE values from five cross-validation folds, rank-based nonparametric methods were selected. This choice avoids the assumptions about normal distribution and equal variance that parametric tests require. It also lowers the chance of Type I error inflation from many pairwise comparisons. We performed a Friedman test, followed by a Nemenyi post-hoc comparison, and the Nemenyi results appear in the CD diagram92 in Fig. 10. The Friedman test showed a difference in performance among the algorithms ( = 34.818, p = 2.9 × 10⁻5), justifying the pairwise comparison.

SHAP analysis

To understand the NTK-GP model’s predictions for CCPL in SAEC, SHAP analysis was done. SHAP, based on cooperative game theory, measures each input feature’s contribution to the predicted result. This method gives both overall and specific interpretability by breaking down each prediction into individual feature contributions, keeping consistency and fairness across all samples. By relating feature effects to physical parameters, SHAP interpretation gives insights into the model’s decision-making and confirms its reliability.

Figure 11 shows SHAP force plots for three samples (A, B, and C), showing how the NTK-GP model makes its CCPL predictions. Each plot starts at a base value (the average model output before considering feature effects). Red bars are features that raise the predicted CCPL, and blue bars lower it. The final predicted value comes from adding all SHAP contributions to the base value, giving a detailed local explanation of how individual feature combinations shape the model’s output. For Sample A (predicted CCPL = 5.83), CT = 28 days and moderate SFC = 3.02% greatly raise the prediction, while NSC = 2.77% and a low w/c ratio of 0.52 add positive influence. These are partly countered by FT = Steel and higher MAS = 12, which have small downward effects. The result is a moderately high CCPL compared to the base level, showing the benefit of good curing and proper SFC.

Force plot for a local interpretation of three sample points using the NTK-GP model.

For Sample B (predicted CCPL = 6.62), the model starts from a slightly higher base value, but several features lower the output. A higher w/c ratio (0.58), low NSC (0.18), and FT = Glass all lower the predicted CCPL, suggesting weaker densification and interfacial bonding. Although moderate MAS = 10 and FAC = 3.3% partially counter these, their influence isn’t enough to offset the main downward contributions.

In Sample C (predicted CCPL = 17.67), almost all key variables push the prediction up. A high SPC (20.69 ml/kg), high FAC (16.47%), and balanced SFC (3.16%) strongly raise the predicted CCPL, showing the combined effect of cementitious and polymeric components. The absence of NSC (0.51%) and FVC (0.0) has small negative impacts, but the overall trend is very positive. The “FT = Not used” setup also helps slightly, possibly because of better matrix homogeneity in fiber-free conditions.

Figure 12 shows the global SHAP (mean |SHAP|) feature-importance ranking for the NTK-GP model when it predicts the CCPL of SAEC. The bar chart gives a clear order: FT is the biggest factor, followed by FVC. The rest—MAS, SFC, NSC, w/c, CT, SPC, and FAC have much lower mean absolute SHAP values.

Total sensitivity index of input parameters on the NTK-GP model.

The big role of FT and FVC makes sense from a materials and fracture-mechanics point of view and fits with what we already know about fiber-reinforced concretes. Fibers directly control post-crack bridging, energy loss, and how well cracks resist growing. A model that tries to predict the CCPL will naturally focus on what influences bridging ability and toughness. The large mean |SHAP| for FT means the model’s predictions change a lot when the fiber category changes. The large mean |SHAP| for FVC shows a strong continuous link to fiber amount. In other words, the NTK-GP learned that choosing the right fiber and amount are the main ways to lower CCPL in this dataset.

FT and FVC may seem extra important because of numerical and representational factors. FT is a category, shown with one-hot vectors. Switching between categories causes a sudden, often large, change in input space that can lead to big absolute SHAP values. Also, FVC usually has a strong, steady impact on crack resistance and, based on its range and how much it varies in the dataset, will have bigger average absolute impacts on the model output than things that don’t vary as much. So, Fig. 12 shows a mix of (a) real mechanical importance and (b) representation/scale issues (category jumps for FT, and how much the input varies for continuous inputs). Knowing this is key when using mean |SHAP| to compare things.

The low mean |SHAP| values for MAS, SFC, NSC, w/c, CT, SPC, and FAC don’t mean those aren’t important at all. Instead, they have small average absolute impacts across the dataset, given the ranges sampled and how much fiber influences things. For instance, MAS and w/c change the crack path, how well things interlock, and the matrix strength. Silica-based additives (SFC, NSC, FAC) change how dense and micro structured the matrix is. Their smaller SHAP values likely mean that, within the ranges and mixes tested, their added effect on CCPL is smaller than changing the FT or FVC. It’s also possible that some of their effects are conditional, for example, only mattering for certain FTs or CTs which lowers their overall average contribution but still makes them matter in certain parts of the input space.

The quick meaning of Fig. 12 for practical engineering is that ways to lower CCPL should focus on fiber choice and amount. Changing FT or raising FVC causes the biggest drops in predicted CCPL in this dataset. Though, when trying to get top results for specific service conditions (like, high heat, long curing), the smaller-average inputs may still matter locally or together. So, designers should mix the general SHAP insights with the dependence and interaction steps above before making material choices.

To ensure that the effect of the FT categorical variable was not artificially amplified by its one-hot encoding, another grouped-SHAP analysis was performed in which all FT indicator variables were aggregated into a single composite feature. The grouped-SHAP results (Fig. 13) show that although FT is still an influential predictor, its mean value (|SHAP|) is significantly lower than the dominant value of the ungrouped FT_NotUsed. This confirms that the ungrouped representation has somewhat amplified the significance due to sparse binary activation. After grouping, FT is ranked among the top contributors alongside quantitative composite variables such as FVC, MAS, NSC, and SFC, allowing for a more relevant attribution of the effects of this feature. Other FT subgroups (steel, polypropylene, basalt, glass) maintain non-zero SHAP contributions, reflecting material-specific side effects after accounting for the overall presence of fibers. These results indicate that the grouping of one-hot encoded features allows for a more accurate and consistent interpretation of the relationship of features in predicting CCPL.

Grouped SHAP feature importance analysis for the NTK-GP model.

FT has the biggest and most regular areas of large SHAP values (clear red bands for some groups and blue for others), and these match the highest values of the model output f(x) in the top panel. This means FT often strongly influences the prediction, either up or down, based on the type. FVC has obvious rows of SHAP values across many instances, showing that the model uses FVC to change CCPL predictions. The other inputs (MAS, SFC, NSC, w/c, CT, SPC, FAC) have smaller and more scattered SHAP values in the heatmap. They make small changes for some instances but don’t have the consistent, large that FT and FVC do.

The global SHAP dot plot shown in Fig. 15 adds detail by showing the direction and value dependence of each variable’s impact. FT has groups of SHAP values that are tightly clustered by category. Some categories always give positive SHAP values (increasing predicted CCPL), while others always give negative values. This is typical for how categorical variables are handled and means that changing fiber category changes the predicted CCPL by a good amount. FVC has a wider range of SHAP values that are linked to variable size. This range shows that values of FVC can either increase or decrease predicted CCPL based on the situation, meaning a conditional or complex relationship rather than a simple linear effect. The other numerical variables (MAS, SFC, NSC, w/c, CT, SPC, FAC) have points close to zero SHAP, with some outliers. This means they don’t have much influence on average and only matter for some instances or when they work with the main fiber variables.

Global SHAP values based on the NTK-GP model.

From a physical and mechanistic view, fibers manage crack-bridging ability and energy loss after cracking, which directly affects how far a crack spreads before breaking. So, it makes sense that FT and FVC control the model’s output. The smaller average impacts of matrix and aggregate modifiers (SFC, NSC, FAC, MAS, w/c) don’t mean they aren’t important. Instead, within the tested amounts, they have smaller or conditional impacts, such as changing toughness only when fiber properties are weak, or changing how cracks move in certain aggregate/fiber combinations.

There are also numerical and representational reasons for the SHAP values seen. FT is handled categorically, so category changes cause sudden jumps in input space that create large SHAP values. FVC’s impact will scale with its range and with the model’s sensitivity. Variables with bigger ranges and nonlinear impacts will get larger mean |SHAP| values. So, the importance of FT and FVC shows their true mechanistic importance and how they are shown in the training data and the model.

The figures also show important variations and relationships. The heatmap has groups of instances where FT and FVC together create large positive SHAPs that match high f(x) values. This suggests that they work together in some areas of the data, like how certain FTs at certain amounts strongly increase predicted CCPL. Also, there are columns where FT creates strong negative SHAPs while other variables stay small, showing categories that lower CCPL. The spread of MAS, SFC, and NSC SHAPs, sometimes positive, sometimes negative, means that their impacts depend on the context and could depend on FT or CT.

It is noteworthy that the influence of FT on the CCPL predictions is significant, as expected. The results show a consistent trend in which steel fibers lead to the highest predicted CCPL values, followed by basalt fibers, while glass and polypropylene fibers show relatively less influence. The SHAP dependence plots confirm the pivotal role of fiber type in determining the crack resistance in SAEC. Furthermore, the interaction between FT and other mixture components such as w/c ratio, SFC, SPC, and FAC is evident. The w/c ratio shows a significant effect on steel fibers with higher SHAP values at lower w/c ratios, indicating that lower w/c improves crack resistance when steel fibers are used. SFC also interacts with FT (especially basalt fibers), where higher SFC values have a positive effect on crack propagation resistance. However, it is clear that other properties such as w/c, FAC, SFC and CT have smaller effects across different FTs, as indicated by their more dispersed SHAP values. The different impact of these parameters on CCPL may be due to interactions with the dominant fiber properties. Despite their lower absolute impact in some cases, these minor properties still play a role in the model in complex ways, especially under specific mixing conditions.

Uncertainty quantification

Uncertainty quantification changes machine learning outputs from simple estimates into data that can be used while being aware of the risks. Figure 17 illustrates the NTK–GP model’s predicted CCPL on the test dataset, along with the associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each point prediction using a bootstrap method. These intervals turn each single prediction into a small claim about where the real CCPL value might be. This is important when decisions need to consider the chance of rare but important errors. The plot shows three main things. First, the predicted and real values match up well overall. For most test samples, the red dots (predicted values) are very close to the blue dots (measured values), and the green intervals are small. This means the model correctly predicts the main CCPL tendency across the test set and reports low uncertainty in those areas. Second, the width of the CIs changes with the signal size, which suggests uncertainty changes with the data. The green CIs get wider for samples with bigger or more sudden CCPL peaks and smaller for more stable mid-range values. This pattern fits with the idea that the model knows predictions for extreme or high-variance samples are less certain than those for stable areas. This is good because it separates confident and uncertain predictions for each sample, making the uncertainty estimates useful instead of the same for all. Third, some predictions differ from the measured values in certain places. While most intervals include the data, some samples show big differences or wider intervals than normal. These points probably come from input space areas with less training data (epistemic uncertainty), from noisy or strange experimental data, or from cases where the NTK–GP model can’t capture some nonlinear material behavior.

NTK-GP predictions with bootstrapped 95% CIs.

From a quantitative perspective, several diagnostics should be computed to confirm these visual impressions. Empirical coverage should be measured to find the percentage of test points where the real CCPL falls in the 95% CIs. A well-adjusted model should have coverage close to 0.95. Higher coverage means the intervals are too wide, while lower coverage means they are too narrow. The interval width should also be reported, since narrow, well-covered intervals are favored. Also, standard error measures like RMSE, MAE, and bias should be calculated across the test set and sorted by CCPL size to find systematic underprediction or overprediction. Analyzing residuals by plotting prediction errors against expected CCPL or key inputs can show if there’s heteroscedasticity, skewness, or patterns that point to unmodeled things. Calibration plots that compare empirical versus nominal quantiles can check if the model’s uncertainty estimates match reality. Influence analysis can point out test samples with big residuals or wide intervals that need more study.

The results show that the NTK–GP model gives useful confidence estimates for individual CCPL predictions. Most intervals are tight and include the real values, meaning the model’s uncertainty estimates are usually good. But, the model is less sure and less right at big CCPL peaks, where intervals are wider and misses sometimes happen. This is normal in data-driven modeling, since extreme responses are less common in training data and more variable. For safety, the model’s awareness of bigger uncertainty in these areas is good and helpful.

Localized differences should be carefully handled. They might mean there are missing features, experimental problems, or extrapolations outside the training data. These samples should be marked for more study, and more data should be collected in those areas. To make the model better, empirical coverage and interval width should be measured, intervals should be recalibrated if coverage is bad, and other uncertainty estimation methods like conformal prediction or heteroscedastic Gaussian processes should be tested. Getting more training data, studying feature-importance, and comparing different uncertainty quantification methods will help understand the performance better.

In conclusion, the NTK–GP predictions for CCPL of SAEC in Fig. 17 look good. The model’s predictions match measured values well across most test samples, and the 95% CIs are small where the model is sure and wider where the signal changes more. Some samples show big differences or wide uncertainty, which means the model might not work as well in those areas. Overall, the figure shows that uncertainty quantification turns model predictions into useful risk assessments for each sample. Still, before using the model for safety, one should measure coverage, check calibration, and study the local differences to make sure predictive confidence matches real-world reliability.

Key limitations and suggestions

This work offers an investigation into the CCPL in SAEC, using machine learning models and explainability analyses. While models like NTK–GP showed strong prediction, some methodological and practical limits need attention in the future. One limit is the dataset’s size, range, and sampling. The cleaned database has 760 samples with different mix designs, CTs, and FTs, but some combinations, especially those with high CCPL or rare fiber–additive reactions, are not well represented. This can cause errors and underestimation in some areas. Future studies should grow the dataset with experiments in under-sampled areas, using active learning or experimental design. If tests are costly, data growth through simulations or transfer learning can help fix this problem.

Experimental noise and pre-processing also create problems. Fracture parameter measurements from DIC or CMOD have uncertainties that grow with crack length. Pre-processing steps, like removing outliers and normalization, can make this noise worse. It is suggested to measure error spread and add this uncertainty to model training using noise-aware loss functions or Gaussian process models. Using strong scaling methods and keeping raw values with normalized data would improve openness and reproduction.

The residual plots provided valuable information about the model behavior during cross-validation and highlight significant performance issues related to anisotropy noise and poor fit at high CCPL values. As noted, the models generally performed well but struggled to accurately predict extreme CCPL values due to the limited number of high-value samples and unmodeled nonlinearities. To address these challenges, we propose to integrate adaptive learning and active data augmentation techniques to improve model robustness and prediction accuracy, especially in severe crack propagation scenarios. Adaptive learning and active data augmentation can significantly reduce anisotropy errors by allowing models to focus more on regions of higher prediction uncertainty, typically at high CCPL values. More specifically, adaptive learning algorithms, such as instance weighting or dynamic loss functions, can adjust the model’s emphasis on underrepresented data points, especially in the region of high CCPL values. By increasing the weight of these high-value instances during training, the model is forced to give more weight to these extreme scenarios, thereby helping to reduce the bias and error observed in the upper part of the CCPL spectrum. This can be achieved through methods such as significance sampling or active learning, in which the model identifies and prioritizes the data points that are experiencing the most problems, especially outliers in the high CCPL range. In addition, active data augmentation techniques can be used to artificially generate more diverse instances in areas where the model performs poorly, especially for high CCPL values. This could include augmenting the dataset with the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to generate synthetic samples of high CCPL data points or applying domain-specific transformations that simulate potential material variations (such as different FTs or polymerization conditions). This approach not only increases the number of high CCPL data points, but also improves the model’s ability to generalize to extreme unobserved values.

The uncertainty quantification used here, based on bootstrap CIs for the NTK–GP model, gives insights but cannot separate epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty well. Future work should include calibration and coverage analyses, and test methods like conformal prediction, deep ensembles, or Gaussian processes to get reliable uncertainty estimates.

The models were tested using internal hold-out and K-fold cross-validation, but not with external datasets from different labs or conditions. Future efforts should include external tests or domain adaptation studies to see how well the model works across different materials and conditions. Also, defining where the model can be used will help people know when it is reliable.

Another promising avenue for future research involves integrating the trained NTK-GP model into a multi-objective optimization framework to simultaneously optimize various design parameters of durable admixture concrete (DAC). Multi-objective optimization, which involves balancing multiple competing objectives, is particularly relevant for concrete mix design and structural integrity analysis. By integrating NTK-GP, engineers can iteratively optimize DAC formulations based on performance and durability objectives. This could include optimizing fracture toughness (by minimizing CCPL), cost-effectiveness, environmental impact, and other parameters such as compressive strength or durability under harsh environmental conditions. Thanks to its ability to handle complex and nonlinear relationships while maintaining high computational efficiency, the NTK-GP model can be integrated with existing multi-objective optimization algorithms, such as genetic algorithms (GA) or particle swarm optimization (PSO). This integration allows for the use of the model’s near–real-time predictive capabilities, facilitating timely decision-making and reducing the need for costly experimental tests.

Conclusions

This work looked into how well machine learning models predict CCPL in enhanced concretes. It used models like NTK–GP, ensemble methods, and neural networks. The models were trained and tested using hold-out and K-fold cross-validation methods, with SHAP analyses and uncertainty measures for clarity and reliability. The NTK–GP model had the best prediction accuracy, achieving the highest R2 and lowest RMSE. It captured nonlinear links between mix parameters, curing, and fiber traits, showing kernel-based learning’s potential for modeling fracture behavior in concrete. Other algorithms, like MLPR and ensemble tree-based models (GBR and RFR), also did well, suggesting that different algorithms can model CCPL when tuned correctly.

The SHAP analysis found FT and FVC as key factors affecting CCPL, consistent with fracture mechanics theory. These features manage the post-cracking bridging effect and fracture energy dissipation. Other factors, like w/c ratio, MAS, and CT, had less but still noticeable influence on CCPL prediction. Uncertainty measures using bootstrap CIs showed that NTK–GP predictions were accurate across most feature space regions, though higher uncertainties appeared in extreme CCPL ranges with sparse data.

The Friedman test revealed significant performance differences between the models, and Nemenyi post hoc analysis determined which differences were statistically significant. Although NTK-GP achieved the highest average rank, followed by ETR, GBR, RFR, and GPR, the critical difference indicated that NTK-GP was not statistically superior to this middle group. Only the lowest-ranked methods including DTR, XGBoost, and SVR performed significantly worse than NTK-GP.

The combined NTK-GP model offered an ideal compromise between expressivity and statistical accuracy. By coupling a GP to the NTK, it inherited the richness of deep nonlinear network modeling while preserving analytical uncertainty a posteriori. This combination addresses two fundamental limitations of previous deep learning (DL)/ physics-informed neural networks (PINN) fracture models for SAEC: (i) improved generalization in small-to-medium experimental datasets through kernel-based induced bias, and (ii) accurate and calibrated uncertainty estimates, which promote safer and more reliable engineering decisions. Furthermore, interpretability analyses such as SHAP can be applied to the mean NTK-GP predictor to produce feature ratios consistent with physical expectations (e.g., fiber presence and volume as key factors).

From a design perspective, the combined results of uncertainty quantification and explainable artificial intelligence can be directly integrated into reliability processes: the upper and lower CCPL confidence intervals (or conservative quantiles) are used to define safety margins, propagate predictive uncertainty to limit state or serviceability studies, and identify areas of high uncertainty that require additional testing or conservative partial factors. Together, these steps define a repeatable and transparent method for translating machine learning-derived CCPL predictors into safer, mechanically sound, and sustainable structural design decisions.

Practically, fiber traits, especially FT and FVC, are most effective for reducing CCPL in enhanced concretes. Still, due to experimental variability and limited data, predictions should be interpreted with confidence bounds and safety margins for design or evaluation. Future studies should expand the dataset to include more compositional and environmental variations and improve uncertainty calibration. Also, integrating physical principles into the learning framework and external validation across labs are needed to confirm model consistency. Creating user-friendly, computationally practical predictive tools will help with practical use in engineering.

The suggested NTK-GP-based framework can be extended to other sustainable concrete systems, such as steel fiber reinforced concrete, geopolymer, and recycled aggregate concrete, by adapting the feature set to take into account specific material properties. Furthermore, by integrating durability indices and environmental loading conditions, the model provides a more comprehensive and explainable approach to failure prediction, consistent with sustainability objectives. This integrated framework allows for the prediction of failure behavior of different concrete systems, while considering the environmental and economic impacts of different formulations.
