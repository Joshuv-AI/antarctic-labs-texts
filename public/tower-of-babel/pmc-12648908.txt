Domain and Language adaptive pre-training of BERT models for Korean-English bilingual clinical text analysis
Authors: Eunbeen Jo, Eunbi Cho, Yebin Lee, Sanghoun Song, Hyung Joon Joo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop bilingual Korean-English medical language models through domain- and language-adaptive pre-training and evaluate their performance in clinical text analysis tasks, specifically semantic similarity and multi-label classification.MethodsA bilingual corpus comprising Korean (medical textbooks and online health articles) and English (medical textbooks, health-related articles, and MIMIC-IV EHRs) clinical texts were constructed. Three BERT-based foundation models (Korea Medical [KM-BERT], English Biomedical [BioBERT], and multilingual general domain [M-BERT]) underwent additional pre-training using a newly created bilingual WordPiece vocabulary (45,000 tokens). Model performance was assessed intrinsically on the medical semantic textual similarity (MedSTS) benchmark and extrinsically through multi-label classification of chest computed tomography (CT) reports from tertiary hospitals. Macro F1 scores and Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used as primary evaluation metrics.ResultsAfter bilingual pre-training, the Korean semantic similarity performance of bi-BioBERT improved significantly from a Pearson correlation coefficient ranging 0.190–0.871. In the multi-label classification of chest CT reports, all bilingual models outperformed their respective foundation models; bi-KM-BERT achieved the highest Macro F1 score in both internal (0.9460 vs. 0.8902 for KM-BERT) and external validation (0.9288 vs. 0.8495 for KM-BERT). However, bi-KM-BERT and bi-M-BERT experienced semantic performance declines in Korean tasks, indicating catastrophic forgetting, and gradient-based token-importance heatmaps confirmed that the bilingual models captured critical cross-lingual medical contexts more effectively.ConclusionThe findings underscore that careful bilingual vocabulary curation and targeted domain-adaptive pre-training enhance natural language processing (NLP) performance in multilingual clinical environments, even with modest training resources. Continual-learning strategies should be explored to mitigate minor forgetting effects. Domain- and language-adaptive pre-training of bilingual medical corpora improves NLP model performance in multilingual clinical settings, thereby providing a scalable strategy for enhancing clinical text analysis capabilities in resource-limited bilingual contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03262-7.

Introduction

Background and significance

Hospitals manage vast amounts of electronic medical record (EMR) data that include structured and unstructured text [1–3]. Approximately 80% of the data within EMR systems is in unstructured formats, such as free-text notes and images [4]. Although structured data is designed to store predefined data points, such as laboratory test values, essential clinical information is often recorded as free-text format within clinical notes [4, 5]. Integrating structured and unstructured data yields superior performance in medical applications, such as clinical code prediction, compared to using a single data type alone [6, 7]. Applying natural language processing (NLP) methods is essential for extracting and structuring critical clinical information from free-text recorded in clinical notes [8].

In multilingual clinical settings, clinical notes often contain a mixture of English and the local language, posing challenges for processing with a single-language model [9]. In Korea, clinical notes are commonly written with a mixture of Korean and English [10, 11]. Furthermore, the complexity of medical terminology and abbreviations in clinical texts can pose significant challenges in analyzing clinical notes using NLP techniques [7, 12]. To address bilingual or multilingual clinical NLP effectively, domain-adaptive or language-adaptive approaches can be used [9]. Although fine-tuning pre-trained models enables solving various tasks, additional pre-training tailored to domain-specific data, known as domain adaptation, has been shown to further enhance downstream task performance within the target domain [13, 14].

Some multilingual language models underperform when handling medical queries in non-English languages, partly because general-domain multilingual corpora seldom capture domain-specific knowledge [15]. Non-English medical corpora can help mitigate this gap [9]. Qiu et al. [15] constructed a multilingual medical corpus (MMedC), a large-scale multilingual medical corpus covering six major languages, namely English, Chinese, Japanese, French, Russian, and Spanish, and showed that domain-adaptive pre-training significantly enhances performance in multilingual NLP tasks.

Although recent large language models (LLMs), particularly generative pre-trained transformers (GPT)-based, have achieved notable performance in biomedical NLP tasks, they pose challenges such as limited data-sharing potential owing to patient confidentiality and the risk of generating inaccuracies (hallucinations) [16, 17]. Moreover, Brown et al. [17] demonstrated that traditional machine learning models outperformed GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 in clinical prediction tasks using electronic health records (EHRs) [17]. Jo et al. [18] also reported that a fine-tuned bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)-based model significantly outperformed ChatGPT in recommending appropriate outpatient specialties. BERT-based models have consistently showed strong performance in diverse clinical NLP applications [19], exceeding that of generative LLMs in nongenerative tasks such as information extraction or classification [16, 20].

Objective and scope

This study proposes the construction of a bilingual Korean-English medical corpus and further pre-training foundation models (KM-BERT, BioBERT, and M-BERT) using a newly built bilingual medical vocabulary. The performances of the proposed models were evaluated through a sentence similarity assessment using the MedSTS benchmark and a multi-label classification task based on real-world chest CT reports, thereby highlighting their practical implications for bilingual clinical text analysis.

Related work

Adaptive pre-training for clinical NLP

Pre-trained language models (PLMs) adapt their pre-training knowledge to downstream tasks by updating parameters with task-specific data during fine-tuning [21, 22]. The performance of pre-trained language models to perform sub-tasks is best when the target domain is similar to the domain of the pre-training corpus [23]. Most pre-trained models are trained on general domain data such as web text or news articles [23, 24], resulting in a domain gap when applied to clinical natural language processing tasks that involve complex specialized terminology and unique document structures [23]. Domain adaptation techniques address this domain gap through domain-adaptive pre-training using domain-specific corpora to adapt existing language models to new target domains [21, 25].

Bonfigli et al. [21] compared general-domain models, such as the encoder-based BERT and the decoder-based GPT-2, with their biomedical domain-adapted counterparts, BioBERT and BioGPT, on natural language inference and named entity recognition tasks. Their results demonstrated that BioBERT, which was continuously pre-trained on biomedical corpora from BERT, outperformed BioGPT, which was pre-trained from scratch on biomedical text, in both tasks. However, most existing studies have focused on English [26], and publicly available clinical report datasets are largely limited to high-resource languages [27]. Consequently, domain adaptation for clinical NLP in non-English languages is closely associated with the development of new domain-specific datasets [26].

Clinical NLP in non-English contexts

Clinical NLP identifies large volumes of clinical information contained in EHRs and clinical records, enabling the generation of structured data and enabling analysis of vast amounts of clinical data [28]. Deep learning is an active area of research in clinical NLP and is used in various clinical NLP tasks such as clinical concept extraction [29, 30], event detection [31], and text classification [32]. In the medical domain, NLP in languages other than English is a challenge [30], and research has been conducted to address this challenge. Solarte-Pabón et al. [30] proposed a deep learning-based approach for extracting breast cancer information from clinical narratives written in Spanish. Based on the BERT and RoBERTa architecture, transfer learning was performed using Spanish and multilingual pre-trained models. The RoBERTa Biomedical model, which was pre-trained on Spanish biomedical domain text, outperformed models pre-trained on general Spanish or multilingual domain texts. Gérardin et al. [33] utilized two methods to extract medical concepts from French clinical records: applying a French model and translating French text into English and then applying an English model. In French, there are no models specialized for the biomedical domain, despite the use of general domain model, the native French method showed higher performance than the English translation method. Chen et al. [34] developed a model to predict emergency department patient disposition and unscheduled revisits using EHRs in Taiwan and pre-trained language models. This model is based on BlueBERT, a pre-trained language model specialized for the biomedical field. Medical records in Taiwan are written in a mixture of Mandarin and English. To ensure linguistic consistency in the text data and improve the prediction performance of BlueBERT, which is mainly designed for processing English data, Chinese characters were translated into English words.

Previous studies show that pre-trained language models have been used in various ways for clinical NLP in languages other than English. Some languages have local language models specialized for the medical field, but in some cases, only language models pre-trained in general domains can be used. Furthermore, translating local language data and applying it to English-centric medical language models can result in information loss due to translation errors [33]. Therefore, clinical NLP in languages other than English remains a significant challenge, requiring language models and approaches that are suitable for local clinical environments.

NLP for Korean clinical record analysis

To analyze Korean clinical records mixed with Korean and English, Kim et al. [19] performed pre-training on a BERT-based model using outpatient records to enhance the context comprehension ability of the model for bilingual clinical texts. The study suggested that the ability of the model to understand the clinical context is related to the replacement of Korean tokens that are not in the model vocabulary with unknown ([UNK]) tokens, and emphasized the importance of using multilingual models rather than relying on single-language models when processing bilingual clinical records. Kim et al. [11] used a multilingual pre-trained language model to identify alcohol-related information in unstructured clinical records. They mentioned that the reason the performance of Korean sentences is lower than that of English sentences or bilingual sentences may be due to tokenization, as Korean is an agglutinative language that does not separate words with spaces.

Pre-trained language models are additionally trained or fine-tuned to analyze clinical texts in a bilingual environment, however, this approach relies on the vocabulary of the original language model, which may still be unable to learn the characteristics of Korean. Language models that can be used for analyzing Korean clinical texts include single-language and multilingual language models, and for effective analysis, it is necessary to consider not only the linguistic characteristics of Korean but also the specificity of the medical domain.

Methods

Study design

Figure 1 presents the overall research framework. To develop bilingual medical language models, a bilingual corpus of Korean and English clinical medical texts was constructed, from which a WordPiece-based vocabulary was generated for bilingual tokenization [35]. Three foundation models (KM-BERT, BioBERT, and M-BERT) were further pre-trained using masked language modeling (MLM) with the newly built vocabulary. The resulting bilingual models (bi-KM-BERT, bi-BioMERT, and bi-M-BERT) were then evaluated intrinsically using the MedSTS dataset and extrinsically using chest computed tomography (CT) reports from three tertiary hospitals.

Three BERT-based foundation models were selected. For language adaptation, KM-BERT [36], pre-trained on Korean medical textbooks, health news articles, and medical research papers, was selected alongside BioBERT [14], which was pre-trained on English biomedical abstracts from PubMed and full-text articles from PubMed Central. For domain adaptation, M-BERT [37, 38], a multilingual model pre-trained on Wikipedia data in 104 languages, was included. KM-BERT had a vocabulary size of 26,986, of which 21,599 tokens (80.04%) were associated with Korean subwords, and 393 tokens (1.46%) with English subwords. The remainder includes numerals, punctuation marks, BERT special tokens, such as [CLS] and [SEP], and other symbols. Although KM-BERT was pre-trained primarily on Korean medical texts, it retained its basic tokenization capacity for English texts. BioBERT has a vocabulary size of 28,996, consisting of 26,015 English tokens (89.72%) and 10 Korean tokens (0.03%), indicating a limited ability to process Korean input. M-BERT had a vocabulary size of 119,547, with 53,013 English tokens (44.34%) and 3,176 Korean tokens (2.66%).

To enhance the ability of the models to process Korean-English medical texts, the constructed bilingual corpus and newly generated vocabulary were used to further pre-train each of the three foundation models. This additional pre-training stage aimed to align the token representations with the target medical domain and bilingual linguistic structure. Consequently, three bilingual medical language models were derived: bi-KM-BERT, bi-BioBERT, and bi-M-BERT, each initialized from a different foundation model architecture (KM-BERT, BioBERT, and multilingual BERT) and adapted using the same corpus and vocabulary.

The performances of the developed models were evaluated in two stages. First, we conducted an intrinsic evaluation using the medical semantic textual similarity (MedSTS) [39] benchmark, which measures sentence-level semantic understanding in clinical contexts. Subsequently, an extrinsic evaluation was performed through internal and external validation using real-world chest computed tomography (CT) reports. These evaluations were designed to assess the effectiveness of the models in handling practical clinical narratives and their generalizability across different medical text sources.

Construction of Korean–English medical corpora

To develop a bilingual medical language model capable of processing both Korean and English medical domains, bilingual medical corpora were constructed (Fig. 2). The Korean dataset comprised 777,485 sentences extracted from medical textbooks spanning 28 specialized medical fields (such as internal medicine, general surgery, rehabilitation medicine, radiology, emergency medicine, and otorhinolaryngology) provided by the AI Hub, an artificial intelligence training data platform [40]. In addition, 10,020,505 sentences collected from online health information news articles published between February 2004 and January 2024 through the Korean search portal, NAVER. The collected news corpus was preprocessed using regular expressions to remove non-linguistic elements such as journalist names, copyright marks, news agency identifiers, contact information, and image attributions.

The English dataset included 1,333,716 sentences from medical textbooks covering 35 specialized medical fields (such as internal medicine, cardiology, hematology/oncology, family medicine, public health, and microbiology) published between 2013 and 2024. These textbooks were acquired as PDFs from online sources and subsequently converted into plain text using the PyMuPDF library in Python. Furthermore, 2,386,425 sentences were extracted from the health information articles published on the Healthline website. The dataset al.so incorporated EHRs from the MIMIC-IV (version 2.2) database [41], specifically containing 7,033,140 sentences from discharge summaries and radiology reports.

The collected corpora consisted of well-structured sentences, enabling segmentation based on punctuation marks, such as periods, question marks, and exclamation points. Because of the frequent occurrence of decimals and abbreviations in medical texts, punctuation following numeric values or abbreviations (such as ‘Dr.’) was carefully excluded from the sentence boundary detection.

Bilingual medical vocabulary construction

Tokenization plays a crucial role in enabling language models to learn the semantic structure of input text, and vocabulary size can influence overall model performance [42]. To effectively train the newly constructed bilingual medical corpora, a vocabulary was generated using the WordPiece algorithm [35], a subword tokenization method originally used in BERT [42]. A vocabulary size of 45,000 tokens was established, along with a minimum token frequency threshold of 2. Special tokens ([PAD], [UNK], [CLS], [SEP], [MASK]) were also included. In addition, the tokenizer preserved the original letter cases to accurately represent medical abbreviations. Within a vocabulary of 45,000 tokens, 15,212 (33.80%) corresponded to English subwords and 24,497 (54.44%) to Korean subwords, thereby reflecting the linguistic composition of the underlying bilingual corpus.

Development of bilingual medical language model

Three foundation models, KM-BERT (specialized for Korean medical texts), BioBERT (trained on biomedical literature from PubMed abstracts and PubMed Central articles), and M-BERT (a multilingual model pre-trained on Wikipedia data from 104 languages) were selected for further domain and language adaptation. Before additional pre-training, each foundation model was initialized with pre-trained weights. To incorporate the newly developed WordPiece vocabulary, the embedding layers were resized from their original vocabulary sizes to a vocabulary size of 45,000. Additional pre-training was conducted using MLM as the primary pre-training objective, with an MLM masking probability of 15%, enabling the models to predict randomly masked tokens within the input sequences. For this additional training, a newly constructed vocabulary generated using the WordPiece algorithm was used. The models were trained for five epochs using a training-validation split of 9:1, batch size of 32, maximum sequence length of 256 tokens, and an AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-5. A total of 486,729,187 tokens were used during the training. Through the additional pre-training, the embedding weights were updated to reflect the newly constructed vocabulary, enabling the models to learn representations of subword units for languages and domains not covered in the original pre-training.

Assessing sentence-level semantic similarity in medical sentences

The MedSTS dataset [39] was used to measure the semantic similarity of medical sentences to evaluate whether the developed bilingual model effectively learned the medical context. The MedSTS consists of sentence pairs annotated by two medical experts who assign similarity scores on a scale of 0 to 5. To comparatively evaluate the model performance across both languages, the original English MedSTS dataset and the Korean version translated by Kim et al. [36] were adopted. The model performance was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to measure the semantic similarity between the model predictions and expert-assigned scores from the MedSTS dataset [36, 43]. In addition, the performance of the bilingual model was compared with that of the original foundation model.

Fine-tuning using real-world clinical notes

Bilingual medical language models were fine-tuned based on real-world clinical notes collected from three tertiary hospitals to develop a multi-label classification model for identifying cancer, pneumonia, and pulmonary embolisms. The performance of the developed bilingual model was compared with that of the original foundation models. Chest CT reports recorded between 2021 and 2023 were composed in a free-text format, incorporating both Korean and English medical terminology. Based on hospital local codes, 20 types of chest CT-related reports were extracted, and these local codes were categorized into seven concepts of the systematized nomenclature of medicine—clinical terms (SNOMED CT) [44], an international standard terminology system. The following are included: CT of chest, CT of chest without contrast, high resolution CT of chest without contrast, CT arteriography of coronary artery with contrast, CT angiography of chest with contrast, CT venography of pulmonary vein with contrast, and CT of esophagus. Based on the various clinical applications of chest CT, such as cancer staging and prognosis prediction [45–47], diagnosis and exclusion of pulmonary embolism and pneumonia [48, 49], and evaluation of cardiovascular findings [50], the dataset was constructed to include multiple types of examinations. A total of 53,497 reports from two hospitals were used for model development and internal validation, whereas an independent set of 12,227 reports from a third hospital was used for external validation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (IRB No. 2024AN0261). Given the retrospective design of the study and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Chest CT reports were labeled for cancer, pneumonia, and pulmonary embolism by trained clinical informatics specialists following a standardized multistep protocol. Briefly, a comprehensive lexicon for each target condition was developed based on the diagnosis and clinical guidelines. Each report was screened and analyzed using a negation-detection procedure. All candidate annotations were independently reviewed by two reviewers and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus adjudication. The final expert-validated labels were used for downstream model training and evaluation. Table 1 presents the distribution of the label combinations in the internal and external validation datasets. Model performance was evaluated using confusion matrix–based metrics, including precision, recall, and the F1-score. Precision and recall were derived from the counts of true positive, false positive, and false negative predictions, and the F1-score was calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall to provide a balanced assessment of classification performance [51].

Fine-tuning was conducted for up to 20 epochs with a batch size of 32, using binary cross-entropy as the loss function and the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2e-5. To prevent overfitting, early stopping was applied when no further improvement in the Macro-F1 score on an internal validation set was observed. The final model was selected based on the highest validation performance.

Results

Additional pre-training of the foundation model

The foundation models underwent additional training for five epochs using MLM to further enhance the domain-specific language representation. The training was conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. Each epoch required approximately three days to complete. The training loss gradually decreased (Fig. 3), with the final loss values after five epochs recorded as 2.302 for bi-KM-BERT, 2.386 for bi-BioBERT, and 2.381 for bi-M-BERT. In total, 486,729,187 tokens were used for the training. Notably, the validation loss was consistently lower than the training loss across all epochs and models. During training, each base model employed dropout (rate = 0.1) to mitigate overfitting by randomly deactivating units, whereas dropout was not applied during validation [52, 53]. This stochastic regularization makes the training objective effectively harder [52, 54], resulting in higher training-loss estimates relative to validation.

Semantic similarity evaluation on medsts dataset

The performances of the foundation models and additionally trained bilingual models were evaluated on the medical sentence similarity task in both Korean and English (Table 2). Supplementary Table S1 shows examples of scores predicted by each model for the Korean and English versions of MedSTS. Supplementary Tables S2 and S3 provide model-specific tokenization results for examples in the Korean and English versions, respectively. In the Korean evaluation, the foundation model, KM-BERT, achieved the highest performance, with a Pearson correlation of 0.896, whereas BioBERT, which contains only a limited number of Korean tokens in its vocabulary, showed the lowest performance, with a correlation of 0.190. The bilingual model bi-BioBERT, derived from BioBERT through additional pre-training, demonstrated significantly improved performance on the Korean version (0.871), indicating successful adaptation. In contrast, bi-KM-BERT and bi-M-BERT exhibited slightly lower performances than their respective foundation models on Korean.

This performance degradation may reflect catastrophic forgetting, a phenomenon in which artificial neural networks often forget previously acquired knowledge when trained on new data or tasks [55]. This pattern is also consistent with findings on multilingual language modeling. Chang et al. (2023) [56] demonstrated that adding multilingual data improves language modeling for low-resource languages but consistently degrades performance for high-resource languages. Similarly, Rust et al. (2020) [57] showed that when training BERT models on the same monolingual data, using a language-specific tokenizer outperforms using a multilingual tokenizer.

Conversely, in the English evaluation, the bi-KM-BERT and bi-M-BERT outperformed their respective foundation models, achieving Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.903 and 0.899, respectively. However, bi-BioBERT showed a slightly lower performance (0.889) than its original foundation model.

Performance of the fine-tuned model on clinical reports

Performance evaluation of the multi-label classification model for chest CT reports was conducted using the Macro F1 score to address the issue of label imbalance. Table 3 lists the results of the internal validation, in which all the bilingual models outperformed their corresponding foundation models. Among them, bi-KM-BERT achieved the highest Macro F1 score of 0.9460, followed by bi-BioBERT with a score of 0.9451 and bi-M-BERT with a score of 0.9444. In comparison, the foundation models, KM-BERT, BioBERT, and M-BERT recorded Macro F1 scores of 0.8902, 0.9418, and 0.9428, respectively. The improved performance of each bilingual model relative to its foundation model indicates that additional training may have contributed to more effective processing of Korean-English mixed medical texts.

Gradient-based token-wise importance heatmaps were generated to visualize how the bilingual model focuses on semantically meaningful tokens of mixed-language clinical text [58]. Tokenization statistics of the internal dataset (n = 53,497) are summarized in Table 5. The KM-BERT tokenizer generated the highest average token count per report (250.78), whereas the bilingual tokenizer showed the lowest (110.22). BioBERT, which has limited Korean vocabulary coverage, produced the highest proportion of [UNK] tokens (10.39%), while bilingual model tokenizers achieved the lowest rate (0.0001%). Figure 4 presents representative heatmaps across the bilingual models, illustrating the cross-lingual semantic capture of the model and highlighting differences in token importance. Using a representative chest CT report annotated positive for cancer, containing clinical terms such as “metastatic,” “nodules,” and “lungs,” we observed distinct attention patterns across models. As shown in Fig. 4(A), the fine-tuned KM-BERT model highlighted some of the tokens corresponding to “metastatic” with the highest brightness, indicating that it regarded these tokens as especially important in making its prediction. In the case of M-BERT (Fig. 4(B)), although tokens related to medical terminology were not prominently highlighted, the token corresponding to “numerous” appeared with the highest brightness. Due to the limited number of Korean tokens in the vocabulary of BioBERT, most tokens were replaced with [UNK] tokens, and the token corresponding to “significant” appeared with the highest brightness (Fig. 4(C)). This indicates that the token “significant” contributed more significantly to the prediction made by the model than the relatively less emphasized tokens “numerous” and “metastatic.” Owing to the differences in token segmentation, the heatmap lengths varied among the foundation models. In contrast, bilingual models using shared vocabulary produced uniform heatmap dimensions (Figs. 4(D)–(F)). All bilingual models highlighted the token “metastatic,” whereas bi-KM-BERT and bi-BioBERT used a broader color scale range in their heatmaps compared to bi-M-BERT. Notably, bi-KM-BERT not only emphasized core medical terms but also assigned pronounced importance to contextual tokens such as “both,” showing its enhanced ability to capture nuanced surrounding contextual expressions.

Discussion

In this study, bilingual medical models were developed by conducting additional training on a foundation model using vocabulary generated from bilingual medical corpora, rather than relying on the native vocabulary of the foundation model. This paper presents a methodology for developing bilingual medical language models through domain- and language-adaptive pre-training. The key findings are summarized as follows: First, additional pre-training enhanced the Korean language processing performance of the BioBERT foundation model, which originally had a few Korean tokens. The improved BioBERT results on Korean highlight the importance of robust token coverage. Second, despite KM-BERT being primarily Korean-centric, its built-in capacity to recognize some English tokens, coupled with additional bilingual vocabulary training, led bi-KM-BERT to achieve the best results in the fine-tuning task. Third, when applied to real-world chest CT reports, bilingual models achieved improved detection of cancer, pneumonia, and pulmonary embolism. This underscores the importance of incorporating language- and domain-adaptive pre-training strategies when developing clinical NLP systems for multilingual healthcare settings.

In the present study, bilingual pre-training induced small declines in baseline semantic performance: Pearson’s correlation of bi-KM-BERT on the Korean MedSTS decreased from 0.896 to 0.872 (-2.7%), that of bi-M-BERT decreased from 0.861 to 0.859 (-0.2%), whereas the English correlation of bi-BioBERT decreased from 0.908 to 0.889 (-1.9%). These reductions are consistent with catastrophic forgetting, in which neural models lose previously acquired knowledge when exposed to a new data distribution. Similar 1–5% decreases in performance have been reported in continual-pre-training experiments with both BERT and RoBERTa following in-domain adaptation [13]. To mitigate such forgetting, several continual-learning strategies, including elastic weight consolidation [59], rehearsal methods [60], knowledge distillation [61], and learning-rate scheduling, have been proposed [62]. Integrating these techniques into bilingual medical pre-training pipelines may help retain foundational language representations, while still enabling effective domain adaptation.

Next, we observed that the inclusion of both Korean and English subword tokens by bi-KM-BERT within the vocabulary improved performance in bilingual clinical tasks. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating that domain-specific pre-training on Korean medical corpora can yield cross-lingual benefits, provided that the tokenizer of the model sufficiently covers English tokens [36]. Similarly, Cho et al. [63] determined that general-purpose models such as Ko-BERT and BERT-base surpassed KM-BERT and Clincal-BERT in certain tasks, owing to broader token vocabularies that better accommodate mixed-language clinical notes. These studies underscore that vocabulary composition is a critical determinant of bilingual performance, and that modest English token inclusion within the tokenizer of KM-BERT can materially enhance downstream bilingual NLP efficacy.

Compared with large-scale multilingual corpora approaches, such as the MMedC of Qui et al., which leveraged over 25.5 billion tokens across six languages to improve auto-regressive LLM performance [15], our results reveal that targeted domain- and language-adaptive pre-training on a more modest, bilingual Korean-English corpus can achieve comparable gains with substantially fewer computational resources. In a comprehensive evaluation of BERT variants on mixed Korean-English clinical notes, models exhibiting fewer [UNK] token substitutions consistently outperformed those with higher [UNK] rates, underscoring the critical influence of vocabulary composition on knowledge inference [19]. When considered with prior evidence that meticulous tokenization adjustments can recover 3–5% of performance in Korean word-sense disambiguation tasks [64], these results indicate that careful curation of bilingual vocabularies, even at relatively small scales, provides a pragmatic pathway to robust multilingual clinical NLP without the overhead of massive multilingual pre-training.

This study had several limitations. First, the developed models were fine-tuned and evaluated using only a single type of clinical note, which may have limited their generalizability across diverse clinical text formats. Second, although the vocabulary was constructed from bilingual medical corpora, it may not fully capture the complexity of real-world clinical languages. Due to challenges in data collection and access, scientific journal articles were not incorporated into the corpus, which could have further enriched the medical knowledge representation. Third, in the English medical domain, only BioBERT, the most widely used domain-specific model [65], was used, and no comparison with other biomedical transformer variants (such as ClinicalBERT and PubMedBERT) was conducted. Finally, this study focused exclusively on BERT-based architectures. In future research, we plan to develop models capable of processing longer contextual sequences, which were constrained in this study due to limited computational resources. Additionally, alternative model paradigms, including encoder-decoder frameworks and emerging generative LLMs, will be explored to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study showed that targeted domain- and language-adaptive pre-training through the construction of a bilingual Korean-English medical corpus, creation of a shared vocabulary, and further pre-training of BERT-based foundation models yield improvements in both intrinsic semantic similarity and extrinsic multi-label classification tasks. The enhanced performance of the bilingual models highlights the critical role of vocabulary composition and domain adaptation in multilingual clinical NLP, particularly in low-resource languages. Future research should apply this framework to diverse clinical document types and investigate its integration with generative language models to further advance robust and scalable NLP solutions in multilingual healthcare environments.
