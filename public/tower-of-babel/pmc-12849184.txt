Effect of an active teaching method on writing drug prescriptions applied to nursing students: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Fillipi André dos Santos Silva, Jonas Sâmi Albuquerque de Oliveira, Marília Souto de Araújo, Raphael Raniere de Oliveira Costa, Almária Mariz Batista, Sâmara Luíza Barroso de Araújo Alves, Maria Ruth Cândido Espinola, Soraya Maria de Medeiros
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Brazil, as well as in several other countries, e.g. United Kingdom, Ireland, Netherlands, Australia, Canada and New Zealand, nurses are legally authorised to prescribe medications, yet this practice remains under-explored in Primary Health Care. Active teaching methods can be employed to enhance drug prescriptions writing skills in undergraduate Nursing Education.AimThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of an active teaching method and the situational motivation for learning drug prescriptions writing in Primary Health Care, applied to Brazilian undergraduate Nursing students.MethodsA quasi-experimental study with a single-group pretest/posttest design was conducted from September to November 2024. The study was conducted in the Nursing Department of a Brazilian public university with a convenience sample of 54 undergraduate Nursing students. The intervention followed the “Good Drug Prescriptions Writing Practices Teaching Method”, by which students produced drug prescriptions, and their quality was assessed. Situational motivation for learning was also evaluated.ResultsThe intervention significantly improved the quality of drug prescriptions (p < 0.001), with immediate post-training effects sustained over time, indicating durability. For situational motivation, the intervention had a positive (p = 0.029) - though nonuniform - impact on overall motivation (p < 0.001).ConclusionsImplementing this intervention in Nursing Education fostered competency in drug prescriptions writing, directly contributing to strengthening public health and the consolidation of safe, evidence-based practices in healthcare systems.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Brazilian Registry of Clinical Trials (ReBEC) in the https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-5mwfczh under ID code (RBR-5mwfczh) aproved in 11 november of 2024, retrospectively registered.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04251-x.Keywords: Education in nursing, Teaching, Drug prescriptions, Primary health care, Advanced practice nursing, Patient safety

Introduction

Medication prescribing by nurses is an essential component of Advanced Nursing Practice (ANP) and represents a transformation in care that advances the profession [1]. ANP involves highly qualified nurses performing extended and specialized roles beyond basic nursing competencies [2, 3]. Introducing prescribing competencies during undergraduate nursing education is crucial, as it prepares students to engage safely in medication management by understanding the clinical, ethical, and legal dimensions of prescribing in primary health care.

Safe and effective prescribing is a critical step within the medication process, an interprofessional workflow involving multiple professionals and stages [4]. This process begins with prescribing (prescriber), continues with dispensing (pharmacy team), and concludes with preparation and administration (nursing team) [4, 5]. Prescribing is a key point in this process, as it integrates medication specifications, therapeutic decisions, and essential patient and prescriber information [6].

Studies in primary and secondary care units have identified deficiencies in recording information related to medication, prescribers, and patients [7–11]. A WHO systematic review found prescribing to have the highest prevalence of safety incidents within the medication process [12]. While patient safety research often focuses on hospitals, errors also occur in primary care, contributing significantly to the global burden of disease and frequent hospitalizations [13].

In Brazil, medication prescribing by nurses is legally permitted for advanced practice nurses in primary health care, particularly within national programs and the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS, Brazilian acronym) protocols [14]. This authority, while expanding access to care, requires solid technical, ethical, and scientific training [2, 3]. Consequently, integrating prescribing education into undergraduate nursing programs is essential to ensure safe, evidence-based practice aligned with national protocols [14, 15]. Developing these competencies early enables students to transition into professional practice with confidence and safety.

Active learning methodologies, including peer learning, are effective for teaching prescribing skills, as they encourage knowledge sharing, discussion, and collaborative problem-solving [16]. At the Multicampus School of Medical Sciences (EMCM, Brazilian acronym)) of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte (UFRN, Brazilian acronym), Brazil, faculty developed the “Good Practices in Medication Prescribing” teaching method, focusing on undergraduate and graduate students to prepare future prescribers [17, 18].

Learning is intrinsically linked to motivation [19]. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posits that motivation depends on the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness, all essential for learning [19, 20]. SDT differentiates intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, along a continuum (external, introjected, identified, integrated), and amotivation [20]. Motivation influences how students engage with, process, and retain complex clinical skills, such as prescribing [19, 20]. Evidence shows that learning activities requiring higher-order cognitive effort are most effective when students perceive autonomy, competence, and task relevance [19, 20]. Active teaching methods aim to enhance these psychological needs, evaluating situational motivation critical for understanding both skill acquisition and the learning experience [16, 21, 22].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an active teaching method and situational motivation for learning medication prescribing in primary health care among Brazilian undergraduate nursing students.

Methods

Design

This was a study using a single-group, quasi-experimental pretest/posttest design. To ensure transparent reporting, this study followed the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND Statement) guidelines [23].

Study setting and participants

The sample, consisting of a convenience probability sample, included 54 undergraduate Nursing students from a federal public university located in northeastern Brazil. Participants were regularly enrolled Nursing students who were present for the application of both the study instruments and the intervention, and who agreed to the research objectives by signing the Free and Informed Consent Form (FICF). Exclusion criteria were students under 18 years of age; participants involved in other projects, including extension programs, research activities, tutoring programs, or related initiatives conducted concurrently with this study; those who did not participate in all phases of the research; and scientific initiation scholarship holders or collaborators who contributed to the development of this study.

Data collection

The study was conducted from September to November 2024 by a research team comprising two Nursing professors with Doctoral Degrees, one Nursing professor with a Master’s Degree, and an undergraduate Nursing student holding a scientific initiation scholarship. Study development

An extension course entitled “Writing Drug Prescriptions in Nursing for Public Health Programs” was offered as an engaging strategy to recruit undergraduate students. The course was advertised through a virtual announcement on the university’s Integrated Academic Activity Management System (SIGAA, Brazilian acronym), which included an invitation to participate in both the course and the research.

Students who agreed to participate in the course first completed an initial sociodemographic and academic characterization instrument, along with questions designed to assess the quality of drug prescriptions by undergraduate Nursing students.

Subsequently, a clinical case was projected using the classroom’s multimedia resources, prompting students to issue a drug prescriptions based on the knowledge they had acquired up to that point in the activity. Following collection of the drug prescriptions, a third instrument - the Situational Motivation Scale (SIMS) - was administered to measure the students’ motivation for participating in the skills training [24, 25].

Before the skills training, a dialogic exhibition was conducted using study materials that had been sent via SIGAA at least three days in advance. The materials addressed drug prescriptions in nursing with a focus on the Brazilian legal framework; the concept/definition of drug prescriptions; current patient safety scenarios in the medication process; and quality assessment criteria for prescriptions [18].

The intervention involved implementation of the method entitled “Teaching Good Practices for Drug Prescriptions Writing” published by Batista & Costa [18], based on the Theory of Meaningful Learning and Peer Learning Theory [21, 22, 26] and in the QualiPresc instrument [17]. This method is structured into eight steps for skills training on drug prescriptions writing, namely: 1) group organization and presentation of the trigger case; 2) individual initial prescription; 3) group discussion and development of a collective prescription; 4) individual feedback; 5) collective feedback; 6) answer key; 7) repetition of the dynamics with a new trigger case; and 8) session closure [18].

Regarding the skills training, the first stage involved organizing groups and presenting the trigger case. The larger group, consisting of all enrolled Nursing students, was divided into smaller subgroups arranged in a circle of chairs. Each student received two printed materials, an individual document represented by a “simulated prescription form” and an additional collective document.

In the second stage, students completed an individual initial prescription exercise. Undergraduate Nursing students were instructed to read the projected clinical case and then prepare an individual prescription within 5 minutes.

The third stage involved paired discussions and the development of a collective prescription. Students were directed to share their individual prescriptions from the previous stage and discuss them within their groups to collaboratively create a new group prescription based on information they deemed relevant to the projected case, within a 10-minute time limit.

During the fourth stage, an individual group feedback was provided. Instructors collected the prescriptions produced by each subgroup and subsequently delivered immediate, individualized feedback to each subgroup within 5 minutes.

In the fifth stage, collective feedback was provided to all groups. The instructors delivered comprehensive feedback to all participants and groups during this session, highlighting both positive aspects and areas for improvement, within a 5-minute timeframe.

The sixth stage involved faculty members using multimedia resources to project the answer key, demonstrating how the prescription should be correctly formulated. Following this presentation, the skills training session was finished within 5 minutes.

For the seventh stage, the exercise was repeated by using a new trigger case. During this phase, participants created individual prescriptions based on a pre-designed new case for further skills practice. Building upon their prior learning experience from the first session, this second iteration was completed more rapidly when compared to the initial session. Feedback was provided during this stage, presenting a response pattern for the participants’ review.

The eighth and final stage concluded the overall session. During this phase, the instructors wrapped up the day’s teaching-learning session while providing recommendations for practice and guidance for both individual and group study.

The prescriptions produced during the seventh stage of the method (based on the newly projected case) were considered the immediate posttest, being collected and subsequently evaluated. In addition, the SIMS (Situational Motivation Scale) was administered after collecting the drug prescriptions at this stage to measure and later assess how motivation was influenced by the skills training.

Regarding delayed posttests, clinical cases were administered 15 and 45 days after the intervention, respectively. The drug prescriptions produced during these sessions were collected and evaluated.

Data collection instruments

The sociodemographic and academic characterization instrument was developed by the study authors to collect relevant participant profile information, considering essential variables for study analysis. These variables included personal characteristics such as gender, marital status, race, and age, along with academic aspects like academic performance index (IRA, Brazilian acronym) and current semester. Moreover, the instrument addressed factors related to experience and knowledge about the drug prescriptions by nurses, including work history, prior knowledge about prescribing by nurses, experience with drug prescriptions, familiarity with the current legislation and regulations, as well as mastery of pharmacological concepts.

QualiPresc - an instrument developed by Batista; Gama; Souza [17] to assess the quality of drug prescriptions in Primary Health Care settings. This tool contains 13 quality indicators that gather information on the user, the prescriber and the prescribed medications, as well as 1 composite indicator reflecting overall prescription quality, under the safety and effectiveness dimensions, incorporating both Brazilian recommendations and standards from recognized international organizations.

Situational Motivation Scale (SIMS) - this 7-point Likert-type instrument consists of 16 items divided into 4 categories: Intrinsic Motivation (“Because I think this activity is interesting”) - items 1, 5, 9 and 13; Identified Regulation (“Because it is for my own good”) - items 2, 6, 10 and 14; External Regulation (“Because it is possible for me to do it”) - items 3, 7, 11 and 15; and Amotivation (“There may be good reasons to do this activity, but I do not see any”) - items 4, 8, 12, and 16 [24, 25].

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel 365 spreadsheet and subsequently exported to the free and open-source statistical software JAMOVI. The Rj module was installed in JAMOVI, enabling the integration of R scripts directly within the software environment. This functionality allowed data analysis using specific R commands.

The data from the forms were processed using both descriptive and inferential statistics. For inferential statistics and respective tests, a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) and a 5% significance level were considered. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was used, given the sample size of this study. Descriptive data and p-value results from statistical tests were incorporated into data distribution tables.

The Friedman test was used to compare the means of the QualiPresc composite indicator across different data collection time points. To identify specific differences between time points, the Durbin-Conover post hoc method was applied, with significance values adjusted for multiple comparisons. Additionally, the Cochran’s Q test was employed to assess differences in the proportions of correct responses among the QualiPresc quality indicators, given the dichotomous nature of the analyzed variables. For post hoc comparisons between items, the Bonferroni adjustment was used to ensure strict control of type I error in multiple analyses.

Regarding situational motivation, the Wilcoxon Test was used to compare each item of the scale individually, accounting for potential asymmetry in data distribution. Furthermore, the paired t-test was applied to compare the means of the four dimensions of the scale, as well as the global dimension, between the assessed time points. To examine intraindividual consistency within the sample, the Pearson’s correlation (pretest and posttest) was used for the global dimension. This approach allowed for the identification of statistically significant differences in situational motivation levels over time, providing a comprehensive view of the participants’ motivational dynamics.

To identify and understand how the dimensions of the Situational Motivation Scale (SIMS) were related, the Spearman’s correlation was used, followed by a descriptive comparison of these values.

Ethical approval and trial registration

This study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee (REC) of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte (UFRN) under approval number 7,080,533 on September 16, 2024. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study, after they had received detailed information about the study objectives, procedures, and their voluntary right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Following REC/UFRN approval, the study was registered in the Brazilian Registry of Clinical Trials (ReBEC) in the https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-5mwfczh under ID code (RBR-5mwfczh) aproved in 11 november of 2024, retrospectively registered.

Results

Sociodemographic and academic characteristics of the sample

The study included 54 students, predominantly female (72.2%), with an average age of approximately 23 years. The majority were of mixed race (48.1%), students (98.1%), and single (94.4%). Regarding academic data, the mean Academic Performance Index (IRA) was 8.14 out of 10 points, and students were enrolled in the fifth semester of the undergraduate Nursing program. In this sample, 92.6% of the participants reported knowing that nurses drug prescriptions; 87% reported having no experience in prescribing medications during the current semester and at the time of data collection; 96.3% reported not knowing about legal regulations regarding prescribing medication; and 94.4% reported having knowledge of pharmacology or being enrolled in Nursing Applied Pharmacology courses.

Discussion

An integrated analysis of the results demonstrated that the intervention effectively promoted significant improvements in the overall quality of drug prescriptions written by undergraduate Nursing students. These findings align with educational intervention literature, which emphasizes the efficacy of active learning methods for complex clinical practices like prescribing medication [27]. Studies indicate that interventions incorporating immediate feedback and peer learning—as employed in this study—are particularly valuable for developing practical skills and critical reflection [28, 29].

Furthermore, the observed decline over time is consistent with theories of learning retention, which highlight the need for periodic review and deliberate practice to consolidate knowledge [30]. The identified requirement for periodic reinforcement underscores the importance of implementing continuous educational strategies—such as regular refresher sessions and clinical practice support—to sustain the beneficial effects of educational interventions in Nursing training [31].

Regarding applications of the “Teaching Good Practices in Drug Prescriptions Writing“method, the authors previously published a pilot study describing the development and implementation of this approach with undergraduate medical students [32], which revealed persistent difficulties in prescribing certain elements, including allergy documentation, medication instructions, nonpharmacological recommendations, and length of treatment - even when operational definitions were provided within clinical scenarios.

Sabatino et al. [31] conducted a quasi-experimental single-group pretest and posttest study to evaluate the impact of a 14-week online educational module on 26 Nursing students. The pharmacist-led intervention focused on developing drug prescriptions skills, allowing students to issue or adjust prescriptions. While overall performance improved by 17%, with particular gains in technical aspects, error rates remained above 30% - a concerning finding for graduate Nursing Education. Significant improvements were noted for dosage instructions and patient/medication information.

The increased global motivation, combined with improvements in specific items, suggests the intervention created a more favorable learning environment where participants felt more motivated and engaged. However, the effects were not uniform across all participants, indicating variability in individual responsiveness to the training approach.

In a quasi-experimental single-group pre-post study, Sanches et al. [33] examined the implications of university students’ motivation regarding the return to practical activities in a Skills and Simulation Laboratory after the COVID-19 pandemic (skills training). The authors conducted an analysis of intraindividual consistency of the SIMS dimensions, finding that the intervention was not effective in significantly altering motivational levels. This study did not include a correlational analysis of the SIMS global dimension.

In a randomized clinical trial with a pretest/posttest design, Rodrigues [19] evaluated the effect of virtual reality (active methodologies) on medical students’ motivation in the context of cardiology education. Two assessments were conducted, an intergroup and an intragroup. The intergroup assessment identified low motivation levels at pre-intervention that remained nonsignificant in posttests (immediate and delayed). For the intragroup assessment, although the global dimension was included, no statistically significant differences were found.

From the perspective of analyzing interactions between dimensions, Honório et al. [34] conducted a motivational profile study of university students regarding life. The descriptive profile showed students were more self-determined, with higher values for Identified Regulation and Intrinsic Motivation, aligning with the findings of the current study. Unlike our results, the correlation between SIMS dimensions analyzed by these authors showed no statistical significance.

The attitudes that drove students to engage in undergraduate course activities were found to be based on a more internalized sense of personal achievement, characteristic of Identified Regulation, as well as feelings of pleasure and satisfaction in performing them, associated with Intrinsic Motivation. This behavior proved to be less linked to external rewards or potential penalties resulting from participation in activities [34].

In this context, both the teaching methodology and the assessment approach adopted by instructors influence how students learn, as there is a relationship between motivation and learning approach, where the predominant type of motivation is related to different learning strategies [35].

These findings reinforce the usefulness of the scale as a robust tool for evaluating health education interventions, since the SIMS instrument has proven to be reliable in other studies [36, 37].

The sample profile revealed a female predominance (72.2% of participants), consistent with studies highlighting the historical feminization of nursing as a profession [9, 38]. These findings may also reflect local demographic composition and inclusion policies, such as affirmative action measures implemented in Brazilian universities in recent years [39, 40].

The lack of practical experience reported by 87% of the participants underscores a misalignment between theoretical knowledge and educational practice, likely stemming from structural limitations in training, including the absence of institutional protocols and insufficient availability of experienced preceptors [41]. This gap is particularly relevant, as medication prescribed by nurses extends beyond technical competence; it serves as a tool to expand access to care and to strengthen nurses’ roles in Primary Health Care [42]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating structured opportunities for clinical practice into nursing education, ensuring that students develop the competencies required for safe and evidence-based prescribing.

The integration of theoretical and practical learning environments also represents a significant determinant of meaningful learning. Although simulation and skills laboratories provide valuable opportunities for applied training, practice settings often lack standardized institutional protocols for drug prescriptions by nurses. The absence of such protocols may weaken the learning process and hinder the development of clinical reasoning and professional autonomy [31].

Additionally, it is essential to discuss specific challenges that influence the teaching and learning process of drug prescriptions in Nursing Education. One of the main issues concerns the legal aspects related to the prescription of specific drug classes, such as antimicrobials, which in Brazil are regulated by Resolution RDC No. 20/2011 of ANVISA and are conditioned by Ministry of Health protocols that restrict nurses’ prescription to specific public health programs [14, 15]. Thus, the question of which antibiotics a nurse cannot prescribe pertains to all those that are not included in the protocols, routines, or public health programs established and approved by the institutions.

Furthermore, 96.3% of the students reported limited knowledge of legal regulations governing drug prescriptions, corroborating Cabral et al. [43], who identified similar gaps among Nursing students in Primary Health Care settings, particularly in Family Health Units. These findings suggest that Nursing Education must integrate not only technical aspects but also the legal and ethical foundations of prescribing to ensure safer professional practice [44].

On the other hand, the high percentage of students with pharmacological knowledge or enrolled in related courses (94.4%) suggests that undergraduate programs adequately cover the theoretical foundations required for clinical practice. However, it remains crucial to integrate this knowledge with practical application in real or simulated settings [45]. This calls for pedagogical strategies that foster contextualized learning, such as skills training, clinical simulations, and case-based studies, since the ability to educate patients on dosage, route, and potential adverse effects requires a deeper and more consolidated understanding of pharmacology, which should be reinforced throughout the curriculum. This reinforces the need for longitudinal pharmacology education, integrated with practical experiences, so that theoretical learning translates effectively into safe clinical practice [45, 46].

The findings of this research are highly relevant for Nursing Education, as they demonstrate that the active learning method used in this study can significantly improve the quality of drug prescriptions and ensure knowledge retention over time. These results highlight the need to use innovative pedagogical approaches that stimulate autonomy, clinical reasoning, and safety in professional practice.

Regarding Primary Health Care (PHC), these results indicate a potential positive impact on patient safety and care quality, since prescriptions that are more accurate reduce errors and promote treatments that are more effective. The maintenance of gains after the intervention suggests this training could be incorporated into professional qualification policies, ensuring that nurses practice more safely and in alignment with institutional guidelines.

The integration of these findings into Advanced Nursing Practice (ANP) reinforces the need to invest in specialized training and continuing education for nurses. One of the pillars of ANP is the development of specialized clinical skills associated with evidence-based decision-making. In the context of PHC, ANP plays a fundamental role in expanding access to care, in reducing service overload, and in promoting more effective healthcare. The improvement in the nurses’ ability to drug prescriptions with greater quality and safety aligns directly with ANP guidelines, which aim to optimize care by ensuring that nurses are prepared to take on expanded roles in PHC.

Limitations

The lack of a control group prevents direct comparison with other methods, making it difficult to establish causality. Additionally, the exploratory nature of this study aims to assess the feasibility of the method and identify adjustments for future research, rather than provide definitive conclusions on its effectiveness.

Recommendations for further research

Within the limits of this study, new research paths can be outlined with recommendations for improvement. One of them involves applying the teaching method in a more robust research design, preferably for Randomized Clinical Trials, in addition to implementing it to teach drug prescriptions to nurses in other contexts.

Furthermore, another research possibility would be to associate technology and healthcare by investigating the impact of technological tools, such as electronic prescribing software and applications, on the safety of drug prescriptions by nurses in public health programs and routines implemented in healthcare institutions

Implications for policy and practice

Regarding the implications of this study, it provides relevant insights for enhancing academic curricula by encouraging the adoption of innovative and effective active teaching methods, which can be replicated in the same or other institutions and contexts. It is recommended that the teaching method used in this study be integrated as a pedagogical practice in mandatory courses of undergraduate nursing programs, ensuring that all students are exposed to the method during their training.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that the teaching of drug prescriptions should be systematically articulated within the Nursing curriculum, encompassing the expansion of Pharmacology, Ethics, and Deontology disciplines, as well as the inclusion of prescription competencies within the scope of the Nursing Process. This integration ensures that students develop not only technical competence but also ethical, legal, and clinical judgment skills essential for safe and autonomous professional practice [44, 47].

Furthermore, by aligning with international patient safety guidelines and investing in the qualification of nursing professionals, this study directly contributes to strengthening public health and consolidating safe, evidence-based practices. Specifically, the intervention addressed one of the core dimensions of patient safety, the safety of drug prescription, by enhancing the accuracy and completeness of prescriptions written by nursing students. This initiative may not only improve the technical quality of prescriptions but also foster the development of safer, more competent professionals prepared to work autonomously and collaboratively within healthcare systems.

Conclusions

Regarding the quality of drug prescriptions, the results indicated that the educational intervention was effective in promoting significant improvements (p < 0.001). Concerning situational motivation, the findings demonstrated that the intervention had a significant positive impact on the participants’ overall situational motivation (p = 0.029), reflecting meaningful improvements, though the benefits were perceived differently (p < 0.001).

In conclusion, the implementation of this active teaching intervention in Nursing Education demonstrated improvements in students’ competence in writing drug prescriptions and contributed to the development of safer and more evidence-based prescribing practices within the study context. Although these findings suggest potential benefits for educational initiatives aimed at strengthening public health and promoting medication safety, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the quasi-experimental design and the use of a convenience sample, which limit their generalizability.
