Takayasu Arteritis in a 55‐Year‐Old Woman With Prior Mycobacterium leprae Infection: A Possible Postinfectious Association
Authors: Ujjawal Bhattarai, Abhisek Jha, Ishan Shrestha, Naphibanroi Lamar, Mandakini Parajuli, Sanjay Bhujel, Dipen Majhi, Kritika Paudel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTakayasu arteritis (TAK) is a rare large‐vessel vasculitis affecting the aorta and its major branches. Although autoimmune mechanisms are central, prior mycobacterial infections have been hypothesized to contribute to the disease onset. A 55‐year‐old South Asian woman with a history of vitiligo and treated multibacillary leprosy presented with right upper limb claudication and absent radial pulse. Angiography demonstrated thickening and narrowing of the right subclavian artery. Based on the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, TAK was diagnosed with a cumulative score of eight. Initial immunotherapy with intravenous prednisolone and methotrexate, followed by oral leflunomide, failed to achieve clinical improvement, following which endovascular stenting was planned. This case emphasizes the potential role of prior mycobacterial infections in TAK pathogenesis and highlights the need for early recognition, comprehensive vascular imaging, and individualized management in atypical or postinfectious presentations.Keywords: large‐vessel vasculitis, leprosy, mycobacterium, sub‐clavian artery stenosis, Takayasu arteritis

Introduction

Takayasu arteritis (TAK) is a chronic idiopathic large vessel vasculitis with granulomatous inflammation of the aorta and its main branches [1]. It commonly leads to vessel wall inflammation, stenosis, occlusion, or aneurysmal dilation ultimately resulting in significant end‐organ ischemia along with pulseless limb syndrome. Although TAK is an autoimmune condition, an association of microorganisms with TAK has been extensively investigated and so far, based on epidemiological and laboratory data, the most likely pathogen that appears to be implicated in the pathogenesis of TAK is Mycobacterium tuberculosis [2].

TAK typically presents with nonspecific constitutional symptoms such as fever, weight loss, and malaise. Depending on the location of arteries involved, it may manifest as missing pulses, vascular tenderness, and ischemic symptoms such as limb claudication, dizziness, angina, focal neurological deficits, Raynaud's phenomenon, and renovascular hypertension [3].

The diagnosis of Takayasu arteritis is a combination of clinical findings and accurate vascular imaging. The 2022 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification criteria for Takayasu arteritis assign points as follows: female sex (+1); angina (+2); limb claudication (+2); arterial bruit (+2); reduced upper extremity pulse (+2); reduced pulse or tenderness of a carotid artery (+2); blood pressure difference between arms of ≥ 20 mmHg (+1); number of affected arterial territories (+1 to +3); paired artery involvement (+1); and abdominal aorta plus renal or mesenteric involvement (+3). A cumulative score of ≥ 5 points classifies a patient as having TAK [4].

The management of TAK is primarily immunosuppression. The objective of the treatment is to use either steroids or nonsteroidal immunosuppressants to control inflammation, prevent further complications, and stop the progression of the disease. High‐dose glucocorticoids are widely accepted as first‐line treatment to induce remission [5].

Here, we present the case of a 55‐year‐old woman with a history of vitiligo and prior treated multibacillary leprosy who presented with persistent ischemic symptoms and was diagnosed with TAK. This case highlights the complexities of diagnosing TAK in older, postinfectious patients and the challenges encountered when initial immunosuppressive therapy fails.

Case History/Examination

A 55‐year‐old South Asian woman with a past history significant for multibacillary leprosy—diagnosed 2 years earlier and treated with a 12‐month course of multidrug therapy, vitiligo, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (left ventricular ejection fraction: 30%–35%), and an active smoking history of 15 pack‐years was referred to the rheumatology outpatient department for evaluation of Raynaud's phenomenon affecting the fingers of both upper extremities. Upon assessment, the patient reported pain, stiffness, and a sensation of heaviness in both upper extremities, with predominant involvement of the right metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints. Additionally, she described the recurrent appearance of 2–3 nonpainful bullae on the right thumb and palmar surface, which subsequently ulcerated and healed spontaneously. She also experienced weakness and fatigability of the right upper extremity, even on mild exertion.

On clinical evaluation, mild atrophy of the musculature in the right upper limb was observed, accompanied by reduced muscle tone. The patient's fingers exhibited distal tapering, consistent with acro‐osteolysis or digital resorption. Vascular assessment revealed an absent pulse in the right radial artery and a diminished pulse in the right brachial artery, whereasa femoral pulses were bilaterally palpable and symmetric along with intact left upper limb pulses. Blood pressure measurement on the left arm was recorded at 130/70 mmHg; however, it was unobtainable on the right arm. Neural examination demonstrated tenderness and palpability of the right ulnar nerve. Auscultation detected a faint bruit over the right subclavian region, suggestive of arterial stenosis, while no bruits were appreciated over carotid or abdominal arteries. Fundoscopic evaluation revealed no abnormalities.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Laboratory investigations revealed a normal serum C‐reactive protein (CRP) level, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and a negative rheumatoid factor (RF), antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti‐dsDNA, anti‐Smith, and anti‐Ro/La antibodies. Anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide (Anti‐CCP) was measured at 3.1 U/mL (reference range: < 17.0 U/mL). Notably, the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio was elevated at 85/15% (normal range: 40–70/20%–45%). Additionally, urinalysis showed no proteinuria or hematuria, and liver and renal function tests were within normal limits.

These laboratory findings helped guide the differential diagnosis. Giant cell arteritis (GCA) was considered less likely given the patient's age, vascular distribution, and normal inflammatory markers, as GCA typically presents with markedly elevated ESR and CRP. Similarly, systemic auto‐immune vasculitides including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) were effectively ruled out based on negative autoantibody panels, unremarkable urinalysis, and normal renal and liver function tests.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) angiography of the neck and thorax demonstrated a long‐segment circumferential symmetric thickening with significant luminal narrowing involving nearly the entire right subclavian artery from its origin, with complete occlusion observed in its mid‐portion—consistent with large vessel vasculitis, most commonly suggestive of TAK (Figure 1). The absence of classic “string‐of‐beads” appearance of alternating stenoses and dilatations on imaging excluded fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD). Histopathological confirmation was not obtained, owing to procedural risk of vascular biopsy, precluding the definitive assessment of an infectious etiology or rare vascular disorders. Based on clinical, laboratory, and radiological evaluation, a diagnosis of TAK with cumulative score of 8 was made in accordance with the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification criteria for TAK. The patient was initially treated with a combination immunotherapy regimen consisting of intravenous prednisolone (1 g/day) and intravenous methotrexate (15 mg/day) for 2 weeks.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) angiography image demonstrating symmetric thickening, narrowing, and irregularity of the right subclavian artery.

Results and Conclusions (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

On 2 weeks of follow‐up, there was no clinical response. In light of this, leflunomide (20 mg/day) was introduced to target disease modification and further modulate the immune response. Despite over a month of immunotherapy, the patient failed to show adequate clinical improvement with persistent right upper limb weakness and claudication along with unresolved bullae formation. Given the persistent upper limb ischemia and critical subclavian stenosis, endovascular stenting was planned. However, at the time of manuscript submission, the procedure had not yet been performed.

Discussion

TAK is a chronic, idiopathic large‐vessel vasculitis involving the aorta and its major branches, leading to progressive vascular wall thickening, stenosis, occlusion, and aneurysmal dilatation that results in end‐organ ischemic features such as limb claudication, angina, Raynaud's phenomenon, and the classical manifestation of pulseless limb syndrome [1, 2, 3].

Link Between TAK and Mycobacterial Infections

TAK and Mycobacterium tuberculosis share similar histopathological and immunological features, and tuberculosis (TB) has been associated with more severe constitutional symptoms and structural aortic damage in TAK patients. Mycobacterial infections, including TB, may contribute to TAK through persistent antigenic stimulation and molecular mimicry. For instance, Phan et al. reported a 20‐year‐old female patient with TAK with a history of pulmonary tuberculosis infection, whose vasculitis improved following the initiation of anti‐tubercular therapy in conjunction with corticosteroid‐based immunosuppression, suggesting the hypothesis of mycobacterial contribution to disease pathogenesis [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. In the present case, the temporal association with recently treated Mycobacterium leprae infection raises a similar hypothesis; however, direct causation cannot be established from a single case. Alternative explanations, including coincidence or shared genetic susceptibility in endemic regions, remain plausible. This case underscores the need for larger, systematic studies to investigate potential immunological links, identify risk factors, and clarify the role of prior mycobacterial exposure in the development and clinical course of TAK.

Diagnostic Challenges in Atypical or Postinfectious TAK

The diagnosis of TAK relies on a combination of key clinical features and vascular imaging. Although the gold standard for diagnosis of TAK remains angiography, doppler ultrasonography and Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) has shown comparable results [12]. In our case, 55‐year old lady (+1) presented with limb claudication (+2), arterial bruit (+2), reduced upper extremity pulse (+2), blood pressure difference between arms of ≥ 20 mmHg (+1), number of affected arterial territories (+1) on Contrast enhanced Computed Tomography (CECT) angiography. Based on the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification, we diagnosed our patient as having TAK with a cumulative score of eight (a score of five or more is diagnostic of the disease). These criteria demonstrate a sensitivity of 93.8% (95% CI 88.6%–97.1%) and a specificity of 99.2% (95% CI 96.7%–100.0%) reinforcing the clinical diagnosis of TAK despite the absence of histopathological confirmation [4]. Given the patient's age, symptomatology, vascular distribution, and normal inflammatory markers, GCA was considered unlikely [13]. Systemic vasculitis such as SLE and PAN were also less probable, based on negative autoantibody panels, unremarkable urinalysis, and normal renal and liver function tests. The 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria for SLE require a positive ANA test and a combination of clinical and lab features with a score of 10 or more need for classification, which our patient did not meet [14, 15]. Similarly, lack of characteristic “string of pearls” appearance on CT imaging rules out FMD [16]. However, as histopathological confirmation could not be obtained due to high procedural risks, the confirmative assessment of either vasculitis or a potentially infectious trigger could not be ascertained—representing the major limitation of this study.

Therapeutic Challenges and Treatment Resistance

Standard management of TAK entails initial therapy of high‐dose oral glucocorticoids, with nonglucocorticoid immunosuppressive agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF‐alpha) inhibitors recommended when initial therapy fails to achieve clinical remission [17]. In this case, the patient demonstrated poor response to initial therapy of intravenous prednisolone and methotrexate. This was followed by subsequent administration of oral leflunomide, which is in line with the 2021 American College of Rheumatology/Vasculitis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Giant Cell Arteritis and Takayasu Arteritis [17]. However, continued ischemic symptoms and radiological progression despite immunotherapy warranted the need for surgical intervention in the form of endovascular stenting to achieve clinical response as suggested by the 2021 American College of Rheumatology/Vasculitis Foundation Guideline [17]. Vascular intervention—either open or endovascular—is typically reserved for symptomatic or refractory TAK, with optimal disease control before and after the procedure being essential for favorable outcomes [18]. Endovascular therapy has demonstrated high technical success, with stent grafts showing superior patency and fewer reinterventions than bare metal stents, suggesting an evolving role in TAK management despite limited long‐term data [19]. This is further supported by a large retrospective JACC study, which found that most vascular lesions in TAK can be safely and effectively treated with stent‐based percutaneous interventions, especially covered stents, achieving high early and late success rates when combined with long‐term immunosuppression [20].

What Makes This Case Unique?

Several aspects of this case make it clinically note‐worthy. First, the occurrence of TAK following treated Mycobacterium Leprae infection is exceptionally rare. Although mycobacterial associations with TAK have been reported, the vast majority of cases highlight a link with Mycobacterium tuberculosis , whereas post‐leprosy TAK remains scarcely documented in the literature [2]. Second, the atypical late onset of TAK in a 55‐year‐old woman may suggest a potential role of prior leprosy as an external trigger. Moreover, pre‐existing peripheral nerve damage secondary to leprosy likely obscured early ischemic symptoms and may have exacerbated tissue injury by diminishing protective sensory feedback, thereby contributing to delays in both symptom recognition and diagnosis. Such overlapping clinical features can pose a diagnostic challenge, particularly in resource‐limited and endemic settings. Lastly, the patient's poor clinical response raises the hypothesis that TAK patients with concurrent or prior mycobacterial infections may experience more severe outcome or diminished treatment response, highlighting an area of future research.

Collectively, these features demonstrate the uniqueness of this case and underscore the importance of future studies to explore potential associations between prior mycobacterial infections and TAK, particularly in atypical or late‐onset presentations, to better inform early recognition, risk stratification, and management strategies.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering prior or concurrent mycobacterial infections when evaluating patients with TAK, particularly in atypical or late‐onset presentations. Although a direct causal link cannot be established, the temporal association with treated Mycobacterium leprae raises a plausible role for postinfectious immune mechanisms in disease pathogenesis. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with unusual clinical features or treatment‐resistant TAK. Further systematic studies are needed to clarify the immunopathological connections between mycobacterial exposure and TAK, which may enhance early recognition, risk stratification, and individualized management.
