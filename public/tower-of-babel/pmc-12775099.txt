Measuring and decomposing inequalities in intrinsic capacity among older adults in china: from an urban-rural divide perspective
Authors: Tian Zheng, Li Chenyang, Liu Shangjun, Xiao Shuqin, Dai Jiaqi, Zhang Yanyan, Jing Liwei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing population of older adults in China has drawn attention to the significant disparities in health resources and overall health status exist between urban and rural older adults. Intrinsic capacity (IC), a key indicator of comprehensive health levels in older adults, plays a crucial role in achieving healthy aging. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the inequality in IC among Chinese older adults from an urban-rural divide perspective, identify the factors influencing these inequalities, and decompose the sources of inequality.MethodsOn the basis of data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), 7,695 adults aged 60 years and above were included. Concentration curves and concentration index (CI) were used to measure economic-related inequality in IC. Using the Dahlgren–Whitehead model of social determinants of health, generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were applied to analyze factors influencing IC among urban and rural older adults. Wagstaff’s decomposition method was further employed to decompose the CI.ResultsA pro-rich inequality in IC (CI > 0) was observed among both urban and rural older adults, with a higher degree of inequality in urban areas that continued to widen over time. In 2013, age was the largest contributor to inequality in rural areas (contribution to CI: 27.55%), while social activity was the main contributor in urban areas (contribution to CI: 26.02%). By 2015, social activity had become the primary contributor in both rural (contribution to CI: 22.69%) and urban (contribution to CI: 28.91%) areas. Multivariate analysis showed that increased age, higher Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL)/Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scores, and the presence of chronic diseases were associated with lower IC, whereas longer sleep duration, more social activities, higher education levels, and improved green coverage in built-up areas were associated with higher IC. Having more children was positively associated with IC only among urban older adults, while being married and engaging in exercise were positively associated with IC only among rural older adults.ConclusionChinese older adults showed inequality in IC, with more pronounced inequality in urban areas. Social activity and age are major contributing factors. Interventions such as promoting social participation, optimizing environmental resources, and implementing tailored urban-rural health policies are recommended to mitigate IC inequality and advance health equity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03284-1.

Introduction

China is undergoing the world’s largest and fastest population aging process [1]. By 2035, the number of individuals aged 60 years and above in China is projected to reach 400 million, accounting for 30% of the total population [2]. As population aging accelerates in China, notable disparities have emerged between urban and rural older adults in terms of healthcare accessibility, social security levels, and overall health status [3]. In comparison with their rural counterparts, older adults in urban areas may benefit from more concentrated and high-quality medical resources and more comprehensive healthcare reimbursement policies, and they may exhibit stronger cognitive function, better daily living activity capacities, and higher health literacy [4–8]. Against this backdrop, promoting health equity among older adults and comprehensively achieving healthy aging—based on the critical regional structural features of urban-rural duality—has become a core strategic task in China’s national agenda. The 14th Five-Year National Health Plan explicitly identifies achieving healthy aging as a key objective and emphasizes the need to continuously narrow the health disparities between urban and rural areas [9].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has highlighted that maintaining and promoting the intrinsic capacity (IC) required for a healthy life is key to achieving healthy aging in older adults [10]. In this context, IC refers to the composite of all physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw upon throughout their life course, encompassing five key domains: locomotor, sensory, vitality, cognitive, and psychological. Thus, IC can provide a comprehensive reflection of an older adult’s physical and mental functioning, offering a crucial research framework for systematic and scientific assessment of their health status [10]. Previous studies have often assessed urban-rural health disparities among older adults on the basis of single health indicators such as diabetes prevalence, life expectancy, or self-rated health; consequently, these studies could not conduct systematic evaluations using comprehensive health metrics [11–13]. Existing research has demonstrated that IC, as a comprehensive health assessment indicator, is crucial for maintaining older adults’ ADL, improving their quality of life, and delaying functional decline [14–16].

Since the concept of IC was introduced, related research has primarily focused on three aspects: First, in terms of the measurement and assessment of IC, current studies often rely on the ICOPE tool recommended by the WHO, integrating scales across various functional domains such as cognition, mobility, and psychology to derive comprehensive scores [17]– [18]. Second, regarding the predictive value of IC for health outcomes, research has found that declines in IC are associated with various adverse outcomes, including frailty, limitations in activities of daily living, increased risk of falls, and higher mortality [19]– [20]. Third, concerning the influencing factors of IC, existing studies have identified advanced age, female gender, low education level, and economic hardship as factors influencing IC [21]. It is worth noting that existing research has shown that the distribution of IC is closely related to economic level. Salinas-Rodríguez found that older adults with higher wealth levels maintain better IC [22]; Tan’s study indicated that individuals with low socioeconomic status have a higher risk of impaired IC [23]. However, most existing studies focus on exploring the influencing factors of IC from the perspectives of general sociodemographic factors and lifestyle, lacking systematic exploration based on theoretical frameworks. This may result in the investigated influencing factors lacking scientific rigor and comprehensiveness, making it difficult to provide in-depth guidance for the formulation of intervention strategies [24]– [25]. Furthermore, although the association between IC and economic level has been confirmed, there is still a relative lack of research quantifying the inequality in the distribution of IC among populations with different economic levels. Against the backdrop of China’s unique urban-rural dichotomy, inequalities in IC among urban and rural older adults may exacerbate structural health inequalities, affecting the quality of life and social well-being of the elderly population. Most existing literature merely treats the urban-rural attribute as a control variable [24]– [25], failing to delve into the equity of the urban-rural distribution of IC. There is also scarce research that systematically decomposes the specific sources of inequality in IC among urban and rural older adults under the guidance of scientific theories. Exploring the factors influencing IC among urban and rural older adults, systematically assessing the current state of inequalities in IC across these areas, and elucidating the mechanisms underlying these inequalities are essential for advancing the national strategy on healthy aging, bridging the urban-rural health gap, and promoting social equity.

The Dahlgren–Whitehead model of social determinants of health is a socio-ecological model designed to provide a systematic analytical framework for conceptualizing the factors influencing health and health inequities [26]. This model illustrates the determinants of health and health equity across five dimensions: individual characteristics, individual lifestyle factors, social and community networks, living and working conditions, and broader socioeconomic, cultural, and environmental conditions [26]. Recent studies have used this model to identify health disparities among patients with interstitial lung disease [27] and care inequities in dementia patients [28], and to study the factors influencing health equity among new urban immigrants in mainland China [29]. This model has also been utilized to analyze influencing factors related to sleep disorders [30], unhealthy dietary habits [31], and participation in disease screening [32], yielding systematic and reliable research conclusions. The Dahlgren–Whitehead model of social determinants of health can provide robust support for comprehensively exploring the social determinants of health, identifying the root causes of social health inequalities, and promoting health equity.

Based on the above background, this study aims to utilize the Dahlgren–Whitehead model from an urban–rural dichotomy perspective to thoroughly analyze the influencing factors of IC among Chinese older adults and its associated equity issues. The specific objectives are threefold: (i) to scientifically and comprehensively explore the factors influencing IC based on the Dahlgren–Whitehead model; (ii) to measure economic status-related inequality in IC among Chinese older adults from an urban–rural perspective; and (iii) to systematically decompose the sources of IC inequality between urban and rural older adults using the same conceptual framework. The findings will provide an evidence-based foundation for understanding the urban-rural structural disparities in the process of healthy aging, promoting the balanced allocation of health resources, and advancing health equity among older adults in both urban and rural areas.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study were sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [33], implemented by the National School of Development at Peking University. CHARLS is a nationally representative, prospective cohort study targeting Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. Sample selection was performed using a multistage, stratified probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling technique. The sampling process was stratified by municipal district/county-level administrative units and sorted according to per capita Gross Domestic Product (GDP). CHARLS conducted five waves of surveys across 150 counties and 450 communities within 28 provinces of China in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 [34]. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires. The questionnaire content encompassed an extensive set of parameters, including sociodemographic characteristics, health status, healthcare service utilization, medical insurance, cognitive function and depression status, work, retirement and asset status, family structure, and income [34].

This study received ethical approval from the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Review Committee (Approval No.: IRB00001052-11015). Before data collection for CHARLS, the research objectives were explained in detail to each respondent, and their informed consent was obtained. All analytical data were anonymized to protect participant privacy.

Screening of research participants

The data for this study were derived from the 2013 and 2015 survey waves of CHARLS. These specific waves were selected to minimize variable loss during the follow-up period. Since the measurement variables for the mobility and vitality dimensions of the IC (probably referring to a specific construct like IC or instrumental capacity) were missing in the 2018 and 2020 surveys, these waves were not included in the current analysis. This study was conducted using data from older adults aged ≥ 60 years. We excluded 132 individuals with missing age records, 7,238 individuals aged < 60 years, and 3,547 individuals lost to follow-up in the 2015 wave. Ultimately, 7,695 older adults were included. Measurements

Intrinsic capacity

The measurement of IC was conducted in accordance with the paradigm established by Yuan [35] and Yue [36] using CHARLS data. These studies developed the IC measurement method by integrating the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) guideline [37], the systematic review by Lopez-Ortiz et al. [38], and the cohort study by Zhao [39]. Specifically, the IC score ranged from 0 to 10 points, and it was a composite measure of the following five dimensions:

Locomotion: Locomotion was evaluated using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [40], which includes assessments of walking speed, balance ability, and the five times chair stand test. This assessment was based on scores derived from the participant’s 4-m walk time, standing times at three different distances, and five times chair-stand time, and this dimension was scored as 0, 1, or 2 points.Sensory: This dimension evaluated vision and hearing. Vision assessments included evaluation of distant and near vision; participants reporting “excellent,” “very good,” or “good” distant or near vision scored 1 point; those reporting “fair” vision scored 0.5 points; and those reporting “poor” vision, requiring glasses, or blindness scored 0 points. Hearing was scored using the same rules as vision.Vitality: This dimension was assessed using two indicators: lung capacity and grip strength. Scores of 0 or 1 point were assigned on the basis of the maximum measured values and predefined thresholds for each indicator.Psychological: This dimension was assessed using the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) [41]. This 10-item scale has a total score range of 0–30; the dimension was scored as 0, 1, or 2 points based on established cut-off values.Cognitive: This dimension was assessed using the Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS) [42]– [43], adapted from the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS). The CHARLS cognitive assessment tool evaluated intellectual level (calculation ability, time orientation, and visuospatial ability) and episodic memory (immediate recall and delayed recall). A score of 0 points was assigned if performance in any sub-dimension fell below 1 standard deviation; otherwise, 1 point was assigned. The cognitive dimension score was the sum of these two sub-dimension scores (range: 0–2 points).

Covariates

On the basis of the Dahlgren–Whitehead model of social determinants of health [12], covariates were selected from the CHARLS database across the following five levels: Innate Individual Traits, Individual Lifestyle Factors, Social and Community Networks, Living and Working Conditions, General Socioeconomic Conditions, and Cultural and Environmental Conditions. Existing studies have confirmed that environmental factors such as wastewater treatment, green space area, and domestic waste treatment impact health [44–46]. Since the CHARLS database lacks relevant variables at the General Socioeconomic and Cultural and Environmental Conditions level, this study supplemented the data using information from the 2013 Urban Construction Statistical Yearbook and 2015 Urban Construction Statistical Yearbook [39, 47], which is compiled by the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the People’s Republic of China (MOHURD), to incorporate key variables at this level.

The covariates included at each level are shown in Fig. 2 and detailed as follows:

(1) Innate Individual Traits: Sex, age, Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) score, Activities of Daily Living (ADL) score, presence of chronic disease.

(2) Individual Lifestyle Factors: Alcohol consumption, smoking, sleep duration, exercise.

(3) Social and Community Networks: Marital status, family size, number of living children, social activity level.

(4) Living and Working Conditions: Per capita household consumption expenditure, education level, health insurance coverage, pension coverage.

(5) General Socioeconomic, Cultural and Environmental Conditions: Wastewater treatment rate, built-up area green coverage rate, domestic waste treatment rate.

More detailed descriptions of the relevant covariates are provided in the eMethods within the Supplementary Content.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis for this study was performed using R version 4.3.1 and STATA version 18.0. Continuous variables showing a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (X ± SD), and inter-group comparisons of these variables were conducted using the independent-sample t-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage (n (%)), with inter-group comparisons performed using the χ2 test. On the basis of the Dahlgren–Whitehead model, generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were employed to analyze factors influencing IC among urban and rural older adults. A GEE effectively accounts for the correlation within repeated measures data from the same individuals and provides robust parameter estimates, making it suitable for analyzing influencing factors in longitudinal data [48]. This method focuses on estimating the “population-average effect” of independent variables, i.e., the average effect of explanatory variables on the overall study population [49]. Furthermore, GEE is robust to misspecification of the working correlation matrix and can provide valid standard errors and reliable statistical inference, especially in scenarios with large sample sizes [50]. This study employed the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) method to impute missing values. All tests were two-sided, with the significance level set at P < 0.05.

The World Bank recommends using concentration curves and concentration index (CI) to measure health inequality [51]. To quantify the degree of inequality in IC relative to economic status among Chinese older adults, this study utilized the concentration curve and CI [51]. The concentration curve depicts the relationship between the cumulative percentage of IC (y-axis) and the cumulative percentage of the population ranked by economic status (x-axis). Per capita household consumption expenditure was used to measure the economic status of the older adults. Research indicates that in comparison with income measures, per capita household consumption expenditure is less susceptible to errors due to concealment or measurement bias and better reflects disposable long-term economic resources [52]–[53].

The CI was defined as twice the area between the concentration curve and the diagonal line of absolute equality (the line of perfect equity), and it ranged from − 1 to 1. CI = 0 indicates absolute equality in the distribution of IC across the population. CI < 0 indicates that individuals with lower household per capita consumption expenditure had better IC, implying pro-poor inequality in IC. CI > 0 indicates that individuals with higher household per capita consumption expenditure have better IC, indicating pro-rich inequality in IC. Results

Baseline characteristics

Characteristics of the older adults at the 2013 baseline survey and comparisons of urban-rural differences are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 68.11 years (SD = 6.73); 3,818 (49.6%) participants were male, and 3,877 (50.4%) were female; 6,140 (79.8%) participants were married, while 1,555 (20.2%) had other marital statuses; 1,473 (19.1%) participants had education level above primary school, and 6,222 (80.9%) had education level of primary school or below. Statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed between urban-dwelling and rural-dwelling older adults in terms of education level, family size, number of living children, per capita household consumption expenditure, pension coverage, smoking status, number of social activities participated in, chronic disease prevalence, IADL score, ADL score, and built-up area green coverage rate. Multivariate GEE analysis of factors influencing IC in older adults in urban and rural areas

Using the total IC score of older adults as the dependent variable and the Dahlgren–Whitehead model of social determinants of health, independent variables were selected from five levels: Innate Individual Traits, Individual Lifestyle Factors, Social and Community Networks, Living and Working Conditions, and General Socioeconomic, Cultural and Environmental Conditions. Multivariate GEE analysis was performed separately to identify factors influencing IC among urban and rural older adults. The results are presented in Tables 2 and 3. The results of the GEE analyses from Tables 2 and 3 are presented in the corresponding forest plots in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively.

The GEE analysis revealed common factors influencing IC among both urban and rural older adults. Increasing age (urban: β = −0.032; rural: β = −0.036), higher IADL score (urban: β = −0.235; rural: β = −0.237), higher ADL score (urban: β = −0.116; rural: β = −0.122), and presence of chronic disease (urban: β = −0.162; rural: β = −0.254) were associated with decreased IC scores in both urban and rural older adults. Conversely, longer sleep duration (urban: β = 0.053; rural: β = 0.093), increased participation in social activities (urban: β = 0.239; rural: β = 0.223), education level at primary school or above (urban: β = 0.208; rural: β = 0.248), and higher built-up area green coverage rate (urban: β = 0.022; rural: β = 0.021) were associated with increased IC scores in both groups (P < 0.05).

Additionally, the analysis revealed specific influencing factors on IC scores among urban and rural older adults. A larger number of children (β = 0.040) was associated with an increase in IC scores only among urban older adults. Being married (β = 0.186) and engaging in physical exercise (β = 0.094) were associated with improved IC scores only among rural older adults (p < 0.05).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically measure the inequality in IC among older adults in China from an urban-rural comparative perspective, and to apply the Dahlgren–Whitehead Model to analyze its underlying causes. The results reveal a “pro-rich inequality” in IC among older adults in both urban and rural China, with the degree of inequality consistently higher in urban areas than in rural ones, and such inequality widening over time in both settings. Further analysis indicates that factors influencing IC differ between urban and rural elderly populations. The primary factors driving inequality in IC among urban and rural seniors also vary. These findings reveal how the social determinants of health exert differential effects on the IC of older adults in urban and rural areas. They also indicate that, against the backdrop of China’s rapid social transformation, the mechanisms leading to IC inequality among the elderly in urban and rural areas may differ. A deeper understanding of this urban-rural heterogeneity is of great significance for the targeted implementation of healthy aging strategies and for promoting health equity among older adults in both urban and rural settings.

This study employs the CI and its decomposition method to measure economic-related inequality in IC among older adults in urban and rural China. As a bivariate analytical method, the CI directly reveals the economic gradient in health inequality by ranking the health variable according to economic status. Its key advantage lies in its decomposability using the Wagstaff decomposition approach, which allows for precise quantification of the specific contribution of various social determinants to the observed urban-rural inequality in IC. In contrast, the relative deprivation index, such as the Kakwani index, also commonly used in health inequality research, is a univariate analytical method. This approach does not rely on external economic ranking but instead focuses on the overall distributional differences of the health variable within the group, aiming to capture the sense of perceived relative deprivation arising from individual comparisons with others [55–57]. Consequently, although both methods can be used to quantify inequality, the CI was selected for this study as it is better aligned with our objective of systematically disentangling the sources of urban-rural IC inequality. This is due to its ability to directly assess health equity related to economic status and its valuable property of decomposability. A cautious interpretation of the magnitude of the CI is essential for understanding the real-world implications of the research findings. The observed CI values in this study are relatively low in absolute terms, suggesting that the degree of socioeconomic inequality in IC among older adults in urban and rural China is generally mild. However, given that IC is a bounded multidimensional composite index, even minor deviations in numerical values may reflect meaningful health disparities. Notably, from 2013 to 2015, the CI values in both urban and rural areas showed an upward trend, indicating a potential widening of inequality. Furthermore, considering China’s large aging population, even a mild degree of pro-rich inequality may affect a substantial number of older adults at the macro level. Therefore, from a public health perspective, this phenomenon remains of clear practical significance and warrants continued monitoring and attention.

This study finds that the inequality in IC among urban older adults is higher than that among their rural counterparts, which may be closely related to the “unequal allocation” and “imbalanced utilization” of health resources within urban areas. With the ongoing urbanization process, the large-scale migration of rural labor to cities has not only reshaped the urban-rural demographic structure but also exacerbated the unequal distribution of health resources within urban areas [3]. Pan’s research points out that in urban areas with concentrated labor inflows, local governments, constrained by fiscal limitations, often prioritize public services and medical resources for local residents with household registration, while non-local migrant workers find it difficult to access health resources tied to hukou status. Although migrant workers contribute to local finances and social security funds through taxes and insurance premiums, thereby enhancing the health resources available to local residents, they themselves are often excluded from these benefits due to household registration restrictions [58]. This leads to an inequitable distribution of health resources within the urban population, widening the IC disparities among urban older adults. Furthermore, Zhao’s study indicates that urban residents generally prefer seeking care directly at higher-level hospitals, resulting in overwhelmed tertiary hospitals and underutilized primary community medical institutions in urban areas [59]. Although urban areas possess more abundant medical resources compared to rural areas, this imbalance in resource utilization concentrates high-quality medical resources among groups with higher socioeconomic status and greater payment capacity. This exacerbates inequalities in health service utilization among urban older adults and amplifies disparities in IC across different economic strata within cities [60]. In contrast, although the total volume of medical and health resources in rural areas is relatively insufficient compared to urban areas, the smaller differences in income and occupation among rural residents lead to a more balanced internal distribution of health resources. This, to some extent, mitigates the exacerbation of IC inequality within rural areas [61]. Therefore, to alleviate intra-urban health inequities, policymaking should focus on dismantling the restrictions imposed by the household registration system on access to public services, and progressively ensure that migrant populations enjoy the same basic public health services as local residents. Meanwhile, it is imperative to rectify the imbalance in healthcare resource utilization by improving the tiered diagnosis and treatment system and enhancing the capacity of primary medical institutions, thereby ensuring that quality resources more equitably benefit urban elderly populations across different economic strata [60]– [61].

Previous studies have indicated that older adults with higher levels of wealth tend to have better IC, which is consistent with the findings of this study [62]– [63]. Notably, this study reveals that the degree of pro-rich inequality in IC among older adults has been continuously widening, both in rural and urban areas. This phenomenon corroborates the Cumulative (Dis)advantage Theory and the Fundamental Cause Theory. The Cumulative (Dis)advantage Theory suggests that differences in resources among individuals amplify over time, creating a cycle where“the advantaged become more advantaged, and the disadvantaged become more disadvantaged.” Specifically, older adults with greater wealth can continuously access high-quality medical and service resources, forming a virtuous cycle that helps maintain their IC; in contrast, those with less wealth face deteriorating health disadvantages due to limited resources [6]. The Fundamental Cause Theory further emphasizes that individuals with higher incomes can leverage their financial resources to adapt to changing health environments and access emerging medical resources, thereby sustaining their health advantages. In contrast, low-income groups, which lack such resources, struggle to cope with evolving health challenges and find it difficult to reverse their health disadvantages [64]. Additionally, disparities in health literacy driven by income levels are a significant factor contributing to the persistent expansion of pro-rich inequality in IC among older adults. Older adults with higher incomes generally possess better health literacy, making them more likely to invest proactively in health, as well as adopt beneficial lifestyle practices such as engaging in household chores, volunteering, and continuous learning. In contrast, older adults with lower incomes often face constraints due to limited financial resources and insufficient health literacy, making it difficult for them to adopt healthy lifestyles or engage in proactive health management. As a result, the gap in IC between the two groups continues to widen [65]– [66]. Based on this, narrowing the IC disparity among urban older adults requires not only improving the social security system for the elderly and alleviating the economic burden of low-income groups through targeted medical insurance policies to prevent economic disadvantages from translating into health disadvantages [60], but also striving to implement personalized health interventions. Approaches like face-to-face education, pharmacist consultations, and the use of illustrated materials can strengthen the ability of older adults with low economic status to understand and apply health information, thereby improving their health literacy [67].

In 2013, the primary contributing factors to inequality in IC among rural and urban older adults were age and social activities, respectively. This suggests that the formation mechanisms of IC inequality may differ between urban and rural older adults. This discrepancy may be related to differences in production and lifestyle patterns between urban and rural areas. Older adults in rural China often rely on traditional agricultural production for subsistence, which requires continued engagement in physical labor [68]. Age-related physiological decline, such as reduced muscle strength and cognitive deterioration, significantly diminishes the labor capacity of rural older adults, limiting their ability to sustain basic livelihoods or afford essential health maintenance resources such as medical expenses. These factors exacerbate health inequalities and widen disparities in IC among individuals [69]– [70]. In contrast, urban older adults have largely moved away from physical labor-based production models, making social engagement a relatively more influential factor for their health [68]. Studies have indicated that social activity directly affects multiple domains of IC, including locomotor, sensory, cognitive, and psychological functions, and serves as an important pathway for maintaining IC among urban older adults [71]. Thus, disparities in social activity levels among urban older adults are a major source of inequality in IC. Notably, in 2015, social activity became the foremost contributing factor to inequality in IC among rural older adults. This shift is likely closely associated with the social transformation occurring in rural China. Between 2013 and 2015, China prioritized agricultural modernization and introduced a series of policy documents, such as the Opinions of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and the State Council on Accelerating the Development of Modern Agriculture and Further Enhancing Rural Development Vitality [72–74]. The consequent agricultural modernization gradually reduced older adults’ reliance on heavy physical labor, increased their leisure time, and elevated the importance of social activity in influencing their health [74-75]. Meanwhile, during this period, China’s primary healthcare service system underwent continuous improvement, with expanded coverage of basic public health services such as health management for older adults and prevention and control of chronic diseases. These enhancements enabled more rural older adults to participate in social activities by supporting their physical health, thereby increasing the influence of social activity on IC [76]– [77].

Older adults in both urban and rural areas with advanced age, higher IADL and ADL scores, and chronic diseases exhibit poorer IC. This finding indicates that advanced age, functional limitations and disease burden are closely associated with diminished IC. The WHO states that an individual’s IC declines with age and may eventually be lost [10]. In older adults, aging is accompanied by a gradual reduction in the dopaminergic tone in the striatal-hippocampal circuit, which impairs the efficient transmission of neural information and compromises cognitive function [78]. Age-related decline also leads to reductions in functional fitness, such as cardiorespiratory endurance and balance, thereby diminishing motor function [79]. ADL and IADL are important indicators for assessing functional disability among older adults. Higher ADL and IADL scores indicate a greater number of daily activities that cannot be performed independently, reflecting a more severe degree of disability [80]. Impairments in ADL and IADL can reduce cognitive function and quality of life, decrease social participation, weaken social support received by older adults, and ultimately lead to an overall decline in IC [70]. The presence of chronic diseases can accelerate the decline in physiological functions such as hormonal regulation, metabolism, and cardiorespiratory function. Additionally, chronic conditions may exacerbate the negative effects of aging on cognitive and motor functions, further contributing to a decrease in IC [81]. Furthermore, older adults in both urban and rural areas who sleep longer, engage more actively in social activities, possess higher educational attainment, and reside in built-up areas with greater green space coverage demonstrate better IC. This finding suggests that interventions targeting these modifiable personal factors, behaviors factors and environmental factors may help maintain older adults’ IC. Adequate sleep facilitates bodily repair, memory consolidation, and neural plasticity, while also clearing metabolic waste from the brain to protect neurocognitive function and thereby slowing the decline in physiological reserves and enhancing IC [82]. Social activities have been proven to effectively improve cognitive, psychological, sensory, and motor functions, consequently boosting IC levels among older adults [83]. Older adults with higher educational attainment typically possess better cognitive and problem-solving abilities, as well as a stronger self-awareness regarding their personal health. Consequently, they are more capable of adopting and maintaining a healthy lifestyle, which helps preserve a higher level of IC [84]. Notably, although educational attainment is difficult to significantly improve in the short term, targeted health education and behavioral interventions can partially mitigate the decline in IC associated with this factor [85]. Public green spaces have been shown to alleviate depressive symptoms and improve sleep quality in older adults, thereby positively influencing the maintenance of IC [86]. To delay the decline in IC among older adults in China, primary healthcare institutions should develop functional promotion programs targeting advanced-age populations, strengthen the prevention and management of chronic diseases, and establish regular screening and personalized treatment mechanisms. Simultaneously, health materials that are easily understandable for older adults with low education levels should be developed, along with targeted behavioral interventions and campaigns to raise awareness about sleep health, complemented by professional counseling. Communities should provide early assessment and rehabilitation training for ADL and IADL, expand social activities suitable for older adults with varying health statuses, and reduce isolation risks by promoting social participation to enhance cognitive and psychological capacities. Additionally, urban and rural planning and management should prioritize increasing green coverage in built-up areas and improving accessibility to public recreational green spaces, creating a natural environment conducive to the physical and mental well-being of older adults.

Notably, urban older adults with more children tend to have better IC, which may be related to differences in family structures and migration patterns between urban and rural China. Previous studies have indicated that single-child families are more common in urban Chinese households [87]. Therefore, for urban older adults, having multiple children represents a relatively scarce family resource. However, multiple children can share responsibilities for elderly care and provide support across multiple dimensions, such as social facilitation, life adaptation, material security, and self-identity, thereby collectively slowing the decline in IC [88]– [89]. As a result, for urban older adults, having more children generally implies access to richer support resources. Although rural older adults tend to have more children than their urban counterparts [87], a large proportion of these children migrate to urban areas for employment, resulting in a low rate of long-term co-residence. Consequently, the practical contributions of rural children in terms of daily care and emotional support are often limited [90]. Rural older adults who are married and regularly engage in physical exercise demonstrate better IC, underscoring the importance of close interpersonal relationships and consistent physical activity in maintaining IC within rural settings. Marital status typically implies that rural older adults receive daily companionship and care from their spouses. This form of family support is particularly crucial in the context of out-migration of adult children, and serves as a key factor in maintaining the physical and mental health of these older adults [91]– [92]. In contrast, urban older adults often have access to diverse cultural and recreational facilities, convenient community health services, and a wide range of social activity options [93]. For rural older adults, physical exercise is a low-cost and easily accessible means of health promotion, making it one of the most important ways to maintain physical and mental function. Regular physical activity can help sustain physical function and may also compensate for the relative lack of cultural, recreational, and social resources, thereby exerting a more pronounced positive impact on IC [94]. Therefore, for urban older adults, policies should encourage multi-child families to strengthen collaborative support systems and promote mutual-aid models of elderly care. For rural older adults, emphasis should be placed on addressing the psychological and social support needs of widowed individuals. Additionally, efforts should be made to construct age-friendly public activity spaces and promote suitable physical exercise programs to enhance the health outcomes of the rural older population.

By integrating the classic theoretical model of social determinants with cutting-edge quantitative decomposition methods for health inequalities, this study, within a systematic analytical framework, for the first time quantifies and dissects the current status, trends, and multi-level drivers of economic inequalities in IC among older adults in China, while also revealing their urban-rural heterogeneity. This provides a new scientific basis for developing more targeted healthy aging interventions and resource allocation policies. However, this study has several limitations. First, all participants in this study were Chinese, which limited the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Second, this study was based on data from the CHARLS 2013 and 2015 waves. Due to the absence of indicators for the locomotor and vitality dimensions of IC in the 2018 and 2020 survey waves, the data from later waves were not included in the analysis to ensure measurement consistency and model robustness. While this safeguards internal validity, it may limit the generalizability of our findings. Profound changes in living conditions, healthcare, and social security over the past decade in China may have altered the distribution of health resources and the mechanisms of IC. Consequently, this study provides a crucial historical baseline for early patterns of IC inequality, but its findings require cautious interpretation in the current context. Future research could collect more recent longitudinal data and conduct cross-cultural comparisons among older populations of different racial and cultural backgrounds to enhance the contemporary relevance and generalizability of the findings.Third, due to the unavailability of detailed geographic codes in the public CHARLS database, this study could not employ high-precision spatial modeling techniques like Geographically Weighted Regression to examine the spatial heterogeneity of IC. Future research that obtains data containing precise geographic codes should further explore the spatial distribution patterns of IC among older adults in China, so as to provide a basis for the formulation of targeted policies. Fourth, the Dahlgren-Whitehead model, systematic in identifying influencing factors, is fundamentally a static framework. This limits our ability to capture their dynamic interplay and cross-level pathways. For example, how macro-level environmental policies affect individuals’ lifestyles or psychological states, and thereby their IC, remains under-examined. Future research should integrate dynamic perspectives like life course theory and employ methods such as structural equation modeling to empirically test these causal pathways. This will provide a precise foundation for developing targeted interventions.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that the distribution of IC among older adults in both urban and rural China exhibits a “pro-rich inequality,” with the degree of this inequality persistently widening in both regions and remaining consistently higher in urban than in rural areas. The most significant contributing factors to inequality in IC among urban and rural older adults were age and social activity. Using the Dahlgren–Whitehead model of social determinants of health, this study systematically revealed the mechanisms influencing IC inequality among urban and rural older adults. These findings provide a scientific basis for formulating targeted healthy aging policies and promoting urban-rural health equity, while also offering direction and foundation for further related research.
