Accelerating High-Fidelity Fixed Point Schemes with On-the-fly Reduced Order Modeling
Philippe-André Luneau, Jean Deteix

Abstract
A general method for accelerating fixed point schemes for problems related to partial differential equations is presented in this article. The speedup is obtained by training a reduced-order model on-the-fly, removing the need to do an offline training phase and any dependence to a precomputed reduced basis (e.g. a fixed geometry or mesh). The surrogate model can adapt itself along the iterations because of an error criterion based on error propagation, ensuring the high fidelity of the converged result. Convergence results are given for a general class of fixed point problems with complex dependence structures between multiple auxiliary linear systems. The proposed algorithm is applied to the solution of a system of coupled partial differential equations. The speedups obtained are significant, and the output of the method can be considered high-fidelity when compared to the reference solution.

Introduction

Acceleration of iterative schemes by reusing previously computed data is not a new idea, and has been studied during the last decades in multiple contexts. This article presents a methodology to accelerate fixed-point schemes arising from the determination of solutions to partial differential equations (PDEs) using reduced-order models (ROMs) in the framework of inexact fixed point iterations
(
Alfeld 1982
;
Birken 2015
)
.

In optimization, especially in structural optimization, the concept of approximate reanalysis, consisting of using previous designs to reduce the computational cost of solving the state equation has been introduced by Kirsch in the eighties
(
Kirsch 1982
)
. This has led to the development of the method of combined approximations
(
Kirsch 1999
)
, which has been shown to be equivalent to a particular case of the preconditioned conjugate gradient method
(
Kirsch et al. 2002
)
. These methods were generalized to topology optimization (TO) problems by Amir and Sigmund
(
Amir et al. 2009
;
Amir et al. 2010
;
Amir et al. 2012
)
. More recently, data-driven techniques have been used to learn a ROM on-the-fly during the optimization procedure, e.g. orthogonalization of previous iterates with Gram-Schmidt
(
Gogu 2015
)
and hyperreduction techniques like principal orthogonal decomposition (POD) for steady
(
Xiao et al. 2020
;
Xiao et al. 2022
)
and unsteady
(
Qian 2022
)
TO problems. Theoretical framework and proof of convergence have been established for on-the-fly ROM combined with trust-region methods
(
Yano et al. 2021
;
Wen and Zahr 2023
)
and line search methods
(
Grundvig and Heinkenschloss 2024
)
.

In the field of fluid-structure interaction (FSI), iterative or fixed point-like strategies are popular to solve the coupling between the solid and fluid equations, leading to large computational cost. Offline ROMs have been used recently in combination with quadratic POD approximations
(
Tiba et al. 2024
)
, but an on-the-fly ROM framework in combination with fixed point and quasi-Newton methods has been used in the literature
(
Vierendeels et al. 2007
;
Degroote et al. 2009
;
Delaissé et al. 2022
)
.

Algebraic solvers have also benefited from advances in reduced-order modeling, for instance linear solvers have used Krylov subspace recycling for a long time
(
Soodhalter et al. 2020
)
, and more recently combined it with POD-based approaches
(
Carlberg et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
to augment the Krylov subspace in meaningful ways.
Elman and Su 2020
uses low-rank approximation to accelerate the GMRES method for the solution of Navier-Stokes equation in an all-at-once space-time paradigm. Nonlinear solvers for complex unsteady nonlinear mechanics problems have leveraged ROM-based subspace augmentation as well
(
Kerfriden et al. 2011
)
. Other works have studied the acceleration of Picard iterations, for instance by using partial solving
(
Senecal and Ji 2017
)
or relaxation schemes
(
Birken et al. 2015
)
.

In this work, a general framework for on-the-fly adaptive ROM construction for fixed point methods will be presented. The main idea will be to monitor accurately how the approximation error is propagated throughout the iterative procedure and to refine the ROM when needed. To the knowledge of the authors, such an approach has not been used in the literature so far; a simple a posteriori proxy for the error is often used instead to quantify the precision of the ROM (e.g. the relative residual), which could lead to an inconsistent method. Moreover, since a lot of iterative processes can be reframed in the language of fixed point iterations (e.g. optimization, FSI, autonomous dynamical systems), the global methodology presented could be adaptable to a large variety of problems in applied mathematics. The general setting will allow for a fixed point process with dependences to an arbitrary number of linear equations at each iteration.

First, general theory about inexact fixed point iterations and reduced-order modelling will be presented (
Section

2
). Then, the main algorithm and its theoretical guarantees will be given (
Section

3
), when applied to the solution of an arbitrary number of coupled linear systems. Multiple convergence criteria will be given for such large coupled nonlinear systems. Finally, the algorithm will be applied to a problem in thermo-fluidics (
Section

4
), where coupled PDEs are solved in an alternate manner, which can be formulated as a fixed point scheme.

Preliminaries

A quick overview of the theory of fixed point approximations with exact and inexact iterative methods will be presented, followed by an introduction to fundamental concepts of reduced-order modeling using POD.

2.1
Fixed Point Iterations

Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a selfmap. The definition of Lipschitz continuity is recalled.

Definition 1
.

G
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous if for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there is a constant
L>0
such that

\|G(\mathbf{x})-G(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

In the case where
L<1
,
G
is said to be a
contraction
. The well-known theorem from
Banach 1922
(§2, Thm. 6) ensures that a contractive mapping has a unique fixed point and that the sequence generated by the so-called
Picard iterates
, starting from a point
\mathbf{x}^{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=G(\mathbf{x}^{k}),

converges to the fixed point. In the case where
L\geq 1
, a function might still admit a unique fixed point but some other requirement has to be satisfied, for instance, strong pseudocontractivity
(
Chidume 1994
;
Inchan 2009
;
Jorquera Álvarez 2023
)
. Iterative schemes can be designed to approximate fixed point in these situations, like the
Krasnoselskij iterates

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=G_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}^{k})=(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}^{k}+\lambda G(\mathbf{x}^{k})

(1)

or the
Mann iterates

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=G_{\lambda^{k}}(\mathbf{x}^{k})=(1-\lambda^{k})\mathbf{x}^{k}+\lambda^{k}G(\mathbf{x}^{k}),

which both converges to the unique fixed point under conditions relating the pseudocontraction constants and the relaxation factor
\lambda
(resp.
\lambda^{k}
)
(
Berinde 2007
)
. Under the right hypotheses, the averaged operators
G_{\lambda}
and
G_{\lambda^{k}}
are contractions as well. Those iterative schemes arise especially in the context of operator splitting
(
Liang et al. 2016
)
. One could also obtain a constant step gradient descent scheme from (
1
) by taking
G(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}-\nabla J(\mathbf{x})
, which indeed satisfies the convergence hypotheses in the case of
J
strongly convex and
\nabla J
Lipschitz continuous
(
Jung 2017
;
Wensing and Slotine 2020
)
. Those techniques are mentioned here because the error propagation analysis performed in the next section could be directly applied to those iterative schemes instead of the Picard scheme, thus making the methodology presented in the following section applicable to a wide range of problems.

In the case of nonlinear problems where the evaluation of
G
is obtained itself by an iterative process, or in the case where a data-based surrogate model is used for
G
, the evaluation at a point
G(\mathbf{x})
will be inexact. Let
G_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an approximation of
G
used at the
k
-th iteration of the iterative process. The inexact Picard iterations
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}
are defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}=G_{k}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}),

starting from an initial datum
\mathbf{x}^{0}
.

Hypothesis 1
.

At the
k
-th iteration of the inexact fixed point algorithm, the approximation error satisfies

\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-G_{k}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})\|\leq\delta^{k}.

for some
\delta^{k}>0
depending on
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}
.

Early work by Alfeld studies the convergence of inexact fixed point algorithms. The main result from
(
Alfeld 1982
, §2, Thm. 3)
is that a necessary and sufficient condition for the convergence of the inexact fixed point scheme to the fixed point of
G
is that
\delta^{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
(
Birken 2015
, §2, Thm. 4)
specialized this result to alternate solution of systems (like FSI), which will be used later not as a stopping criteria as it was intended, but as a ROM quality indicator (§
4
). The result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
,
H
be
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-selfmaps with respective Lipschitz constants
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
s.t.
L_{1}L_{2}<1
. Assuming the sequences
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=H(G(\mathbf{x}^{k}))
and
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}=H_{k}(G_{k}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}))
both converge to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
respectively, and
1
is satisfied for sequences
\delta_{1}^{k}\leq\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}^{k}\leq\delta_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{*}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\leq\frac{L_{2}\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{1-L_{1}L_{2}}.

In particular, when
H_{k}=H
for all
k
(
\delta_{2}=0
),

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{*}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\leq\frac{L_{2}}{1-L_{1}L_{2}}\delta_{1}.

If by construction of
G_{k}
, it is possible to establish an upper bound to
\delta_{1}^{k}
, then it can be ensured at each iteration that

\displaystyle\delta_{1}^{k}\leq\delta_{1}\leq\frac{1-L_{1}L_{2}}{L_{2}}\varepsilon,

(2)

for
\varepsilon
the tolerance of the algorithm. We can hope that asymptotically, the error will satisfy the bound when the stopping criterion is attained (e.g.
\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}\|\leq\varepsilon
). In the next section, a strategy to design a non-asymptotic criterion based on error propagation in the inexact sequence will be proposed, based on the analyses of Alfeld and Birken.

Recent literature has studied similar results for inexact Mann-like iterative schemes
(
Liu 1995
;
Xu 1998
;
Liang et al. 2016
;
Bravo et al. 2019
)
and inexact Newton’s method
(
Argyros 1999
)
.

2.2
Projection-based ROMs and Full-field Approximations

Consider the problem

\displaystyle\Lambda(\mu)u=f(\mu)

for
\Lambda
some linear partial differential operator depending on a parameter
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, with suitable boundary conditions to ensure existence and uniqueness.

By using standard discretization techniques like the finite element method (FEM)
(
Ern and Guermond 2021
)
, if
\Lambda
is elliptic, this problem can be approximated by the solution of a finite-dimensional linear system

\displaystyle A(\mu)u_{h}=F(\mu)

(3)

with a symmetric positive definite matrix
A(\mu)
of dimension
N
.

Assume that the residual map of the discretized PDE

\displaystyle r(\mu,u)=F(\mu)-A(\mu)u

is smooth enough w.r.t. to
\mu
at a solution
(\mu^{*},u^{*})
(i.e.
r(\mu^{*},u^{*})=0
) such that a continuous solution operator
\mu\mapsto S(\mu)=u
exists, by the implicit function theorem
(
Hinze et al. 2009
)
.
Intuitively, this means that solutions associated to parameters sufficiently close to each other will behave similarly. Leaning into this paradigm, a data-driven approach to approximate solutions based on precomputed snapshots (solutions at other values of
\mu
) can be considered.

The goal of projection-based ROMs (PROMs) is to find a linear subspace
\mathcal{V}
on which to project
Equation

3
while minimizing some notion of projection error. This linear subspace should be spanned by a basis of size much smaller than the ambient space to reduce the cost as much as possible. A maximal size for the reduced basis
N_{b}
is first chosen, i.e.
\mathrm{dim}\mathcal{V}\leq N_{b}
. A snapshot matrix
\Phi
of size
N\times N_{b}
is built. Every column is the solution
u_{h}^{(i)}
to
Equation

3
for some parameter value
\mu^{(i)}
(
i=1,\dots,N_{b}
). Ideally, the subspace
\mathcal{V}
should be taken as the one that minimizes the high-dimensional embedding error of the projected snapshots,

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{dim}\mathcal{V}=M\\
M\leq N_{b}\end{subarray}}\|\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{V}}(\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}(u_{h}^{(i)}))-u_{h}^{(i)}\|,

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathcal{V}
is the low-dimensional projection and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the high-dimensional embedding.

Two common basis reduction methods are based on the Gram-Schmidt (GS) process and singular value decomposition (SVD). First, the snapshot matrix is centered by subtracting the mean snapshot to each column,
\overline{U}=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}u_{h}^{(i)}
. This is equivalent to right-multiplying
\Phi
by the centering matrix
C_{N_{b}}=I_{N_{b}}-\frac{1}{N_{b}}J_{N_{b}}
. The projection matrix
W
can be obtained either by the Gram-Schmidt algorithm (orthogonalizing the columns of
\Phi C_{N_{b}}
) or by singular value decomposition (SVD),

\displaystyle\Phi C_{N_{b}}=W^{\top}\Sigma U.

In the case of simple orthogonalization, the basis is always of size
N_{b}
. In the case of the SVD, an energy criterion
(
Quarteroni et al. 2016
)
can be used to determine the fraction of the variance captured by a basis of size
M<N_{b}
composed of the
M
first columns of
W
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}<1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rb}}^{2},

where
\sigma_{i}
are the diagonal elements of
\Sigma
, or the singular values of
\Phi
. Those
M
columns will be grouped in a matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}=V^{\top}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{V}}=V
. To find an approximation to
u_{h}
for a new value of
\mu
, the following low-order projected linear system has to be solved

\displaystyle V^{\top}A(\mu)Vv=V^{\top}F(\mu)-V^{\top}K(\mu)\overline{U},

(4)

where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and the POD approximation to
u_{h}
is given by
u_{\mathrm{rb}}=Vv+\overline{U}
.

The error in euclidean norm is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\norm{u_h - u_\mathrm{rb}}\leq\norm{A(\mu)^{-1}}\norm{r(\mu,u_\mathrm{rb})}.

(5)

3
Methodology

First, an adaptive error estimator for inexact fixed point iterations will be derived. This estimator can be computed on-the-fly. Then, the setting of coupled linear systems solved through Picard iterations will be presented, and the quantities needed to compute the aforementioned error estimator will be derived in this context.

3.1
Error Propagation

The main idea in this work will be to ensure that the distance between a fixed point sequence and an inexact fixed point sequence using a ROM approximation stays under a desired tolerance. Incidentally, picking this tolerance to be at most equal to the tolerance
\varepsilon
of the fixed point algorithm (in the following, this tolerance will be set to
\varepsilon
as well) will yield a solution very close to the one that would be obtained by using only high-fidelity data. First, results from
Alfeld 1982
and
Birken 2015
are adapted to measure the distance between the exact and inexact sequence, starting from a common point, after an arbitrary number of iterations.

Lemma 2
.

Assuming
G
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, starting from a common point
\mathbf{x}^{j}=\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{j}
, the distance between the
k+1
-th inexact iterate and the
k+1
-th exact iterate (
k\geq j
) is upper bounded by

\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^{k+1}\|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-j}L^{i}\delta^{k-i}.

(6)

Proof.

The error is split into two parts,

\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^{k+1}\|\leq\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-G(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|+\|G_{k}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})\|.

The first term is related to the sensitivity of
G
w.r.t the error on the previous point. By the Lipschitz continuity,

\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-G(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|\leq L\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}\|.

The second term can be bounded by
1
:

\|G_{k}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})\|\leq\delta^{k}.

The error bound depends on the error at the previous step:

\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^{k+1}\|\leq L\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}\|+\delta^{k}.

By applying recursively the splitting above
k-j
times, until reaching
\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{j}-\mathbf{x}^{j}\|=0
, the desired upper bound is obtained.
∎

Depending on the choice of inexact fixed point operator
G_{k}
(its nature left voluntarily ambiguous for now), the quantity
\delta^{k}
could be evaluated numerically along the iterative process and the propagated error could be quantified explicitly. The Lipschitz constant
L
can be easily approximated numerically by considering the ratio of distances between successive iterations,

L\approx\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^{k}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k-1}\|}.

The next important thing is not only to measure the propagated error, but to be able to control it in a way that it stays below the given tolerance at all times. If the mapping
G
is non-expansive, it can be easily shown that using the exact fixed point operator instead of the inexact fixed point operator will ensure the error between the next iterates of the inexact and exact sequences is below the threshold.

Lemma 3
.

Assuming
G
is nonexpansive (
L\leq 1
), if
\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}\|\leq\varepsilon
, then

\displaystyle\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-\mathbf{x}^{k+1}\|\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-\mathbf{x}^{k+1}\|=\|G(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k})-G(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|\leq L\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{k}-\mathbf{x}^{k}\|\leq L\varepsilon,

and
L\leq 1
.
∎

With these two properties in hand, an algorithm can be designed to ensure that the error will stay below the threshold. First, it will be assumed that a certain number of iterations (samples),
N_{b}
, have to be collected first to build the inexact fixed point operators. From there, the algorithm can be outlined:

1.

Until the maximal sample size
N_{b}
is attained or the inexact operator error
\delta^{k}
is under the tolerance, the exact fixed point is used.

2.

As long as the measured propagation error is under the tolerance, the inexact fixed point is used.

3.

When the propagation error gets above the threshold, the current step is rejected, and the previous (valid) iterate is used with the exact fixed point operator. The propagation error is set to
L
times the previous error. The inexact fixed point operator is updated using the high-fidelity data from this data point.

4.

Steps 2 and 3 are repeated until convergence.

A last validation step can be executed at the end of the algorithm to ensure that the convergence is true as well in terms of the exact fixed point. If it is not the case, a looping strategy can be applied to reach true convergence. The pseudocode of the algorithm is available at

and

Corollary 1
.

Assuming
G
is a contraction, the sequence generated by
Algorithm

will stay at a distance at most
\varepsilon
from the sequence generated by the exact fixed point scheme.

It is important to notice that the algorithm guarantees precision, but not acceleration. If the costs of computing and evaluating

3.2
ROM-based Inexact Operator

The algorithm presented in the previous subsection is very general. However, the explicit construction of the inexact operators
G_{k}
must be detailed. Most of the time, the costly part of the evaluation of the operator
G
will be related to the solution of some PDE. Depending on what is the problem at hand,
G_{k}
might be obtained in multiple ways (e.g. a shallow or physics-informed neural network, a response surface, or any other surrogate requiring few training data and with fast training time). In this case a ROM using POD is considered, and the previous iterations will be used as snapshots, as in
Vierendeels et al. 2007
and
Xiao et al. 2020
;
Xiao et al. 2022
. This can be seen as a non-parametric approach to the ROM methodology, because there is no parameter
\mu
changing along the iterations, but since the fixed point iterations are convergent, the continuity argument mentioned in
Section

2.2
still holds.

3.2.1
Picard Iterations Depending on the Solutions of Multiple Linear Systems

The case where the fixed point operator
G
depends on a variable
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
but also on the solution of multiple linear systems, themselves dependent on
\mathbf{x}
. Let
A_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{R})
,
F_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
(
i=1,\dots,p
) be the matrices and right-hand sides respectively of
p
linear systems. It is assumed that those systems might have dependences to the solution of other linear systems
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
. In this context, it is assumed that the there exists an order in which all the systems can be solved sequentially; otherwise, a nested nonlinear solver would be required, which will not be accounted for in the following analysis. The most general dependance scheme that can be considered is obtained by ordering the systems by the order in which they are solved, and making each system dependent of the solutions to every previous systems, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{i}

\displaystyle:=A_{i}^{-1}F_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,p,

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{i-1}),

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle=F_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{i-1}).

The dependences of
\mathbf{y}_{i}
will not be written in the following to simplify the expressions, but are assumed to follow the rule established above. To represent that the computation of a single fixed-point iteration is dependent on the solution of those systems, a function

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

is introduced, and the fixed point operator is defined as

G(\mathbf{x})=\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p}).

The function
\varphi
can be seen as a multipoint iterative function without memory
(
Petković et al. 2014
)
with more complex dependence between the auxiliary quantities. A few examples are frequently encountered when
p=1
.
For instance, consider a nonlinear equation of the form

\displaystyle A_{1}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}=F_{1}(\mathbf{x}),

which could be solved by the following fixed point scheme

\displaystyle A_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{k})\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=F_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{k}).

The corresponding Picard iterates are characterized by the function

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})=\mathbf{y}_{1}.

Another example can be obtained when thinking about constrained optimization,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in U}J(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad A_{1}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}_{1}=F_{1}(\mathbf{x}).

The fixed point scheme can be obtained by the projected gradient descent method, and the corresponding function is

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})=\Pi_{U}(\mathbf{x}-\eta\nabla J(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})),

where
\Pi_{U}
is the orthogonal projection onto
U
and
\eta
is the step size. An example with
p=2
will be presented in
Section

4
. A sufficient condition for the convergence of such iterative process is given.

Theorem 4
.

Assuming
\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous in each of its argument with Lipschitz constants
L_{i}
(
i=0,\dots,p
), and that the solution maps
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{i-1})\mapsto\mathbf{y}_{i}
are Lipschitz continuous with respect to each of their arguments, with constants
K_{i,j}
(
i=1,\dots,p
,
j=0,\dots,i-1
). Assume

\displaystyle L_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}L_{j}\sum_{\sigma\in d_{0,j}}\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma}-1}K_{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m+1}}<1,

where
d_{i,j}
is the set of strictly decreasing integer sequences starting at
j
and ending at
i
. Then,
G
is a contraction.

Proof.

An upper estimate to the Lipschitz constant of
G
is computed. For an arbitrary pair of points
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{G(\mathbf{x})-G(\mathbf{x}')}=\absolutevalue{\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_1,\dots,\mathbf{y}_p) - \varphi(\mathbf{x}',\mathbf{y}_1',\dots,\mathbf{y}'_p)}\leq L_{0}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|+\sum_{j=1}^{p}L_{j}\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{j}\|

(7)

where
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{1},\dots)

and,

\displaystyle\norm{\mathbf{y}_j-\mathbf{y}'_j}\leq K_{j,0}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}K_{j,\ell}\norm{\mathbf{y}_\ell-\mathbf{y}'_\ell}.

(8)

The statement of the theorem will be proven using strong induction. Assume that for
\ell=1,\dots,j-1
,

\displaystyle\norm{\mathbf{y}_\ell-\mathbf{y}'_\ell}\leq\sum_{\sigma\in d_{0,\ell}}\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma}-1}K_{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m+1}}\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}.

(9)

Since all elements of
d_{0,\ell}
start with
\sigma_{1}=\ell
, when multiplying (
9
) by
K_{j,\ell}
, the product can be written as

\displaystyle\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma'}-1}K_{\sigma_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m+1}^{\prime}}\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}

for some
\sigma^{\prime}\in d_{0,j}
with
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}=j
and
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}=\ell
. By summing over
d_{0,\ell}
as in (
9
), all strictly decreasing integer sequences of the form
(j,\ell,\dots,0)
are considered. Then, by summing over
\ell
as in (
8
), all sequences of the form
(j,j-1,\dots,0)
,
(j,j-2,\dots,0)
down to
(j,1,0)
are considered. When adding the first term in (
8
), they are precisely all the sequences of
d_{0,j}
, meaning

\displaystyle\norm{\mathbf{y}_j-\mathbf{y}'_j}\leq\sum_{\sigma\in d_{0,j}}\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma}-1}K_{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m+1}}\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}.

(10)

The base case is trivially satisfied, because if
j=1
, (
8
) gives

\displaystyle\norm{\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}'_{1}}\leq K_{1,0}\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'},

and
(1,0)
is the only strictly decreasing integer sequence starting at
1
and ending at
0
. The case
j=2
gives

\displaystyle\norm{\mathbf{y}_{2}-\mathbf{y}'_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq K_{2,0}\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}+K_{2,1}\norm{\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}'_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq(K_{2,0}+K_{2,1}K_{1,0})\norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'}

and
(2,0)
and
(2,1,0)
are all the desired sequences.

Combining (
10
) and (
7
) gives an upper bound to the Lipschitz constant of
G
,

\displaystyle L\leq L_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}L_{j}\sum_{\sigma\in d_{0,j}}\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma}-1}K_{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m+1}}.

Requiring this to be strictly less than
1
makes
G
a contraction.

∎

Remark 1
.

|\sigma|
is always upper bounded by
j-i+1
, and
d_{i,j}
has cardinality
2^{j-i-1}
for
j>i
(
|d_{i,i}|=1
), since it follows the sequence
a_{j+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}a_{k}
with
a_{0}=1
,
(
OEIS Foundation Inc. 2025
, A166444,)
. This means the computation of the constant requires an number of operation that is growing exponentially with
p
.

Remark 2
.

The constants
K_{i,j}
can be seen as defining a dependence structure matrix
(\mathcal{K})_{ij}=K_{i,j}
between the equations; a nonzero value indicates a coupling, and a zero value indicates no direct coupling. If
\mathcal{K}
has strictly positive elements on its subdiagonal and zeros elsewhere (
K_{i,j}>0\iff j=i-1
), then the problem is said to be linearly structured; any equation depends only on the solution of the previous equation (
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{i-1}(\mathbf{x}))
).

Remark 3
.

In the case where the problem is the Picard method to solve a system of multiple coupled equations, the function
\varphi
is

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p})=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p}),

meaning
L_{0}=0
,
L_{i}=1
(
i>0
).
\varphi
will be called a Picard solver. The case where the first equation depends only on the last equation

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p})=\mathbf{y}_{p}

then
L_{p}=1
,
L_{i}=0
(
0\leq i<p)
.
\varphi
will be called a weak Picard solver.

An upper bound to the Lipschitz constant sufficient for the operator
G
to be a contraction can be obtained.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that the Lipschitz constants satisfy
K_{i,j}\leq\kappa
and
L_{i}\leq\lambda
. If any of the following condition holds:

1.

\lambda(\kappa+1)^{p}<1
,

2.

\varphi
is a Picard solver and
\kappa<2^{\frac{1}{p}}-1
,

3.

\mathcal{K}
is linearly structured and

\lambda{\left(1+\frac{\kappa^{p+1}-\kappa}{\kappa-1}\right)}<1
,

4.

\varphi
is a Picard solver,
\mathcal{K}
is linearly structured,
\kappa<1
and
\kappa^{p+1}-2\kappa+1>0
(or
\kappa<\frac{1}{2}
),

5.

\varphi
is a weak Picard solver,
\mathcal{K}
is linearly structured and

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{p}K_{i,i-1}<1,

then
G
is a contraction.

Proof.

For the first identity, the coefficient from
Theorem

4
can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle L<\lambda{\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}{\binom{j-1}{\ell}}\kappa^{\ell+1}\right)}

(11)

because the number of paths of length
\ell+1
between
j
and
0
corresponds exactly to
\binom{j-1}{\ell}
. By the binomial theorem and the geometric sum identity,

\lambda\quantity(1+\sum_{j=1}^p \kappa(\kappa+1)^{j-1})=\lambda\quantity(1+ \kappa\frac{(\kappa+1)^p-1}{\kappa})=\lambda(\kappa+1)^{p}

the formula is obtained.

By assuming the hypotheses of statement 2 on
L_{i}
, the expression of
Theorem

4
becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\sigma\in d_{0,j}}\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma}-1}K_{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m-1}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}{\binom{j-1}{\ell}}\kappa^{\ell+1}=(\kappa+1)^{p}-1<1

by using the same strategy as for the first statement. Isolating
\kappa
yields the second expression.

For the third expression, this means that the only term left in (
11
) is

\displaystyle\lambda\quantity(1+\sum_{j=1}^p\kappa^j)=\lambda\quantity(1+\kappa\frac{\kappa^p - 1}{\kappa-1})<1.

(12)

The fourth expression is obtained by setting
\lambda=1
in (
12
) and removing the first unit term (because
\varphi
is a Picard solver), then writing it in polynomial form.
\kappa<\frac{1}{2}
is sufficient because it ensures that for any
p
, the polynomial is positive. The last statement, is obtained by considering only
j=p
in the summation (because
\varphi
is a weak Picard solver and this term comes from
L_{0}
) then simplifying.
∎

The fifth statement is well-known, for instance from
Traub 1964
(§2, Thm. 4), but can still be derived from the general bound of
Theorem

4
, which can be applied in the case where the dependence structure is more complex.

As an example, consider the system of equations

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(D_{1}\nabla\mathbf{y}_{1})=f_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(D_{2}\nabla\mathbf{y}_{2})=f_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})

on a smooth bounded domain with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. It can be solved in an iterative fashion using the Picard solver
\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})
with the scheme

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(D_{1}\nabla\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}),

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(D_{2}\nabla\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}),

where
\mathbf{x}^{k}=(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})
. Since
p=2
, the problem is necessarily linearly structured. We can show using the Poincaré and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities and the mean value theorem that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}_{1}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{y}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq\underbrace{C_{P}^{2}\frac{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}f_{1}(\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\|_{\infty}}{\min_{\Omega}D_{1}}}_{K_{1,0}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{(H^{1}_{0})^{2}},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})-\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{1})\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq\underbrace{C_{P}^{2}\frac{\|\partial_{\mathbf{y}_{2}}f_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\xi(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime}))\|_{\infty}}{\min_{\Omega}D_{2}}}_{K_{2,0}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{(H^{1}_{0})^{2}},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})-\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime})\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq\underbrace{C_{P}^{2}\frac{\|\partial_{\mathbf{y}_{1}}f_{2}(\xi(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime}),\mathbf{x}_{2})\|_{\infty}}{\min_{\Omega}D_{2}}}_{K_{2,1}}\|\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}.

where
C_{P}
is the Poincaré constant for
\Omega
, and
\xi(a,b)=\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Thus,

\displaystyle K_{i,j}\leq\kappa:=\frac{C_{P}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\|\partial_{\mathbf{y}_{i}}f_{j}\|_{\infty}}{\min(\min_{\Omega}D_{1},\min_{\Omega}D_{2})},

assuming
f_{i}
are differentiable and Lipschitz continuous in each of their arguments. Since this is a linearly structured Picard solver, it corresponds to the fourth statement. If this constant is smaller than
\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}=\phi-1\approx 0.619
(
\phi
is the golden ratio), then the fixed point problem has a unique solution.

3.2.2
ROM Approximation

Assume the
i
-th equation is solved using a PROM, meaning
\mathbf{y}_{i}\approx\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
,
where
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
is obtained by solving
Equation

4
with data
A_{i}
and
F_{i}
. Notice that the parameter used for the dimensionality reduction is
\mu_{i}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{i-1})
, and the snapshots
\mu_{i}^{k}=(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{k}),\dots,\mathbf{y}_{i-1}(\mathbf{x}^{k}))
are obtained from data at previous iterations. This will define an inexact fixed point operator

\displaystyle G_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{p}).

Notice that because of the dependance to previous solutions, all solutions
\mathbf{y}_{j}
with
j>i
are actually perturbed as well. The perturbed solutions are defined recursively in the following way:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{i+1}(\mathbf{x},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i+2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{i+2}(\mathbf{x},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i+1}),

To measure the total error committed by using
G_{k}
instead of
G
, the error must be propagated through the successive perturbed solutions.

Theorem 5
.

Under the same hypotheses on
\varphi
and
\mathbf{y}_{j}
(
j=1,\dots,p
) as in
Theorem

4
, if the approximation
\mathbf{y}_{i}\approx\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
is made to obtain
G_{k}
, the sequence

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{i,p}=\left(L_{i}+\sum_{j={i+1}}^{p}L_{j}\sum_{\sigma\in d_{i,j}}\prod_{m=1}^{\absolutevalue{\sigma}-1}K_{\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m+1}}\right)\|A_{i}^{-1}(\mu^{k})\|\|r_{i}(\mu^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i})\|

is an upper bound to the approximation error in the sense of
1
.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to that of the previous theorem, but starting from the inequalities

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{G(\mathbf{x})-G_k(\mathbf{x})}=\absolutevalue{\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_1,\dots,\mathbf{y}_p) - \varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_1,\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_i,\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_p)}\leq\sum_{j=i}^{p}L_{j}\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\|

and, for
j>i
,

\displaystyle\norm{\mathbf{y}_j-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_j}\leq\sum_{\ell=i}^{j-1}K_{j,\ell}\norm{\mathbf{y}_\ell-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_\ell},

and by expressing every error in terms of
\norm{\mathbf{y}_i-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_i}
. Since this last approximation is made with a ROM, it follows (
5
).

∎

It is interesting to point out a few very common cases:

1.

In the case
p=1
,
i=1
(necessarily) and

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{1,1}=L_{1}\|A_{1}^{-1}(\mu_{1}^{k})\|\|r_{1}(\mu_{1}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|.

2.

In the case
p=2
and
i=1
,

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{1,2}=(L_{1}+L_{2}K_{2,1})\|A_{1}^{-1}(\mu_{1}^{k})\|\|r_{1}(\mu_{1}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|.

3.

In the case
p=2
and
i=2
,

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{2,2}=L_{2}\|A_{2}^{-1}(\mu_{2}^{k})\|\|r_{2}(\mu_{2}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{2})\|.

The case where multiple systems are approximated by ROMs can be simply derived.

Corollary 3
.

Assume the same regularity conditions as in the previous theorems. In the case where multiple systems are approximated with a ROM, say
\mathbf{y}_{i}\approx\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\dots,p\}
, then the sequence

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{\mathcal{I},p}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\delta^{k}_{i,p}

is an upper bound to the approximation error in the sense of
1
.

Proof.

The proof is made by induction on
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{I}}
. Assume that, for some
m
such that
1\leq m<p
,

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\},p}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta^{k}_{i_{j},p}.

In the case where the index set is enlarged to
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m+1}\}
, the approximation error is

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m+1}\},p}=\|\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p})-\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{p})\|,

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{i-1})&i\in\mathcal{I},\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{i-1})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Consider the term

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{p})

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{i-1})&i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i_{m+1}\},\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{i-1})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This is equivalent to a fixed point step where the
i_{m+1}
-th system is solved with the FOM instead of the ROM (every occurence of
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i_{m+1}}
becomes
\mathbf{y}_{i_{m+1}}
). The triangle inequality yields the following:

\displaystyle\|\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p})-\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{p})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p})-\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{p})\|

\displaystyle+\|\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{p})-\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{p})\|.

The first term is simply
\delta^{k}_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\},p}
. Since the only difference in the second error term is the approximation of a single linear system,
Theorem

5
can be applied. By applying the induction hypothesis, the previous inequality becomes:

\displaystyle\|\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{p})-\varphi(\mathbf{x},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{p})\|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{k}_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\},p}+\delta_{i_{m+1},p}=\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}\delta_{i_{j},p}.

The base case (
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1}\}
) is trivially satisfied by
Theorem

5
. By induction, the property will hold for any
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,p\}
.

∎

Remark 4
.

Because of the definition of the
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
, the errors
\delta^{k}
have to be evaluated at
\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{k}=(\mathbf{x}^{k},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots)
, meaning that the parameter contains both FOM and ROM solutions when they were respectively computed.

Again, a common case is worth pointing out. In the case where
p=2
and both equations are solved approximately, the upper bound becomes

\displaystyle\delta^{k}_{\{1,2\},2}=(L_{1}+L_{2}K_{2,1})\|A_{1}^{-1}(\mu_{1}^{k})\|\|r_{1}(\mu_{1}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|+L_{2}\|A_{2}^{-1}(\widehat{\mu}_{2}^{k})\|\|r_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{2}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{2})\|,

where
\widehat{\mu}_{2}^{k}=(\mathbf{x}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{k}))
.

4
Application

The algorithm will be applied to the solution of coupled quasi-linear PDEs, for instance the Stokes problem coupled with an advection-diffusion problem. This specializes the theory of nested inexact fixed points from
Birken 2015
to the special case of PROMs.

4.1
Alternate Solution of Coupled Equations

In a general case, we could be interested to solve two quasi-linear partial differential equations

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathbf{y}_{1}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})\mathbf{y}_{2}

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}),

where
\Lambda_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\Lambda_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
are elliptic linear differential operators. Using a fixed point scheme, the solutions would be obtained in an alternating manner, given
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{0}
and
\mathbf{y}_{2}^{0}
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1})\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}).

A linearization step
(
Thomée 2006
)
can be applied under suitable regularity assumptions to reduce the complexity of the problem, by requiring to solve only linear systems at each step:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}).

Let the discretized linear equations be

\displaystyle A_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=F_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}),

\displaystyle A_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=F_{2}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}).

obtained, for instance, by FEM (from now on, by abuse of notation, the solutions
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k}
will be considered as discretized).

Hypothesis 2
.

The matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are invertible and their inverses are uniformly bounded,

\|(A_{1}(\cdot,\cdot))^{-1}\|,\|(A_{2}(\cdot,\cdot))^{-1}\|<M,

for some real constant
M>0
.

Hypothesis 3
.

The solution operator
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1})\mapsto\mathbf{y}_{2}
is
K_{2,1}
-Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
\mathbf{y_{1}}
uniformly over
\mathbf{x}
,

\|{\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}\|\leq K_{2,1}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}\|,\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}.

Following the notation of the previous section, note that this is equivalent to
\mathbf{x}^{k}=(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})
,
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}=\mathbf{y}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{k})
and
\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}=\mathbf{y}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{k}))
. The fixed point iteration to obtain the simultaneous solution to both equations is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2})=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}).

Remark 5
.

The function
\varphi
depends on the choice of fixed point scheme chosen to solve the problem, and not on the problem itself.

The function
\varphi
is entrywise Lipschitz continuous with constants
L_{0}=0
and
L_{1}=L_{2}=1
. As a consequence of
Theorem

5
, if a ROM is used on the first equation (special case 2), the approximation error is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\delta^{k}=(1+K_{2,1})M\|r_{1}(\mu^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|.

If a ROM is used on the second equation (special case 3), then

\displaystyle\delta^{k}=M\|r_{2}(\mu^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{2})\|.

Finally, if the ROM is used on both equations,

\displaystyle\delta^{k}=(1+K_{2,1})M\|r_{1}(\mu_{1}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|+M\|r_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{2}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{2})\|.

Remark 6
.

The norm used to compute any quantity in this section is the euclidean norm on the finite element vector of degrees of freedoms (DoFs). The distance mentioned in
Corollary

1
will be the metric induced by this norm.

The approximate solution operators
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(\cdot)
(
i=1,2
) are updated when the error in
Algorithm

1
gets above the tolerance. The new snapshot is added to the snapshot matrix in a “first in, first out” (FIFO) pattern to keep
N_{b}
snapshots in total, as previously done in the on-the-fly ROM literature
(
Gogu 2015
;
Xiao et al. 2020
;
Xiao et al. 2022
)
.

4.2
Constants Estimation

The error estimation depends on multiple constants related to the regularity of the data. These constants can be hard to determine analytically for complex nonlinear problems. However, they can be estimated from iterations of the fixed point scheme. Most of these estimations are unfortunately
underestimations
, meaning that the resulting quantity will not be a true upper bound to the error, but as numerical results will show in the next section, these quantities are precise enough for the algorithm to be precise and fast.

M
can be estimated from the latest snapshots:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}\|\leq M\|F_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\|\implies M\approx\frac{\|\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}\|}{\|F_{1}(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k})\|}.

K_{2,1}
can be estimated from successive iterations:

\|\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}\|\leq K_{2,1}\|\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k}\|\implies K_{2,1}\approx\frac{\|\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}\|}{\|\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k}\|}.

The Lipschitz constant of the map
\mathbf{x}_{2}\mapsto\mathbf{y}_{1}((\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}))
, noted
K_{1,2}
, will be needed as well in the following part. Similarly,

\|\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k}\|\leq K_{1,2}\|\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}-\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k-1}\|\implies K_{1,2}\approx L^{\prime}_{2}\frac{\|\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{1}^{k}\|}{\|\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}-\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k-1}\|},

where
L_{2}^{\prime}
satisfies
\|\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}\|\approx L^{\prime}_{2}\|\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k}-\mathbf{y}_{2}^{k-1}\|
.

4.4
Comparison of ROM Quality Measures

The methodology presented in
Section

3
introduces a way to characterize the quality of the ROM at the current iterate. Other methods can be used as proxies to assess the precision of the ROM, for instance

-

the residual of the ROM
\|r_{1}(\mu_{1}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|

(
Xiao et al. 2020
)
;

-

the error bound from
Theorem

5
;

-

the asymptotic error behaviour (
2
) combined with the error bound in
Theorem

5
,

\displaystyle\|r_{1}(\mu_{1}^{k},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\|\leq\frac{1-K_{2,1}K_{1,2}}{K_{2,1}(1+K_{2,1})M}\varepsilon.

(15)

When
N_{b}
is smaller, a good quality indicator is crucial to detect automatically when to refine the ROM, because when the data is sparse, the error can become larger. To compare the accuracy of those error criteria, a small sample size (
N_{b}=5
) and a large basis energy threshold (
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rb}}=10^{-4}
) are used.

Remark 7
.

The tolerance for a residual-only criterion is an additional parameter that must be chosen to ensure the precision of the solution. It is not trivial to determine which value must be picked for a given problem, and this choice is clearly problem dependant, although
Xiao et al. 2022
introduces automatic update heuristics during the optimization process. The method presented in this work has the advantage to eliminate completely this parameter choice by giving a constructive and meaningful a posteriori error criterion, which can be set according to the tolerance of the iterative method itself.

For the problem studied above with a U-ROM, a tolerance of
10^{-8}
(the same value as the tolerance of the algorithm) is used. The experiments with and without using an outer validation loop were compared (
Table

4
). Where data is ommited, it means that the validation loop detected covergence after the first cycle. For the problem at hand, whether using simply the upper bound of
Theorem

5
, the asymptotic error (
15
) or the full error propagation (
Algorithm

1
), a similar number of iterations and FOM solves are needed to achieve convergence, although the asymptotic criterion required a few more. However, the propagation approach was able to capture very accurately the true error in the solution obtained when compared to the two others, that underestimated it. Finally, the pure ROM residual criterion performed extremely poorly, by converging prematurely, and then by requiring a very large number of validation cycles before finally converging, which resulted in a longer computation time. The evolution of all those ROM quality metrics along the iterations are shown in
Figure

5
.

.

One could infer using
Theorem

that if the constant in front on the residual in
\delta_{i,p}^{k}
is smaller than
1
, the residual alone could be a good quality metric for the ROM.

5
Conclusion

This article presents an accelerated inexact fixed point algorithm with an on-the-fly quality measurement based on error propagation. This method is combined with PROMs to accelerate on-the-fly the solution to PDE-adjacent problems. General convergence results for fixed point iterations depending on the solution of mutiple auxiliary equations are given. Error estimates are given in the case where those auxiliary equations are solved inexactly using a PROM, to integrate to the proposed algorithm. Results on the incompressible Stokes problem coupled with the energy equation indicate that the methodology can give speedups of up to 30% when solved in parallel, without the need to sacrifice precision, and at no additional offline cost. The method is stable to variations in hyperparameters like the reduced basis maximal size, and gives an accurate estimate of the actual error w.r.t. a regular Picard scheme.

There are multiple possible follow-ups to this work. First of all, since the methodology here was inspired by on-the-fly ROM applied to optimization, it would be interesting to apply directly the methodology to a convex optimization problem, or to generalize
Corollary

1
to more general cases of nonlinear and nonconvex optimization (where the contraction principle does not necessarily apply). Moreover, this methodology could be applied to solve nonlinear unsteady problems, like the Navier-Stokes equations coupled with the heat convection-diffusion equation. For instance, an accelerated fixed point could be used at each timestep to solve the equations. Because of warm starting (the initial guess for the fixed point is the final solution at the previous timestep), less fixed point iterations might be needed to reach convergence, thus potentially reducing the efficiency of the proposed method. However, one could hope that reusing snapshots from previous time steps might not introduce too much error in the process, and the sampling phase might be skipped altogether, which could balance the aforementioned shortcoming. Finally, fixed point methods that are already accelerated by other means, like projection-based coupling schemes
(
Deteix et al. 2014
)
or Aitken acceleration
(
Irons and Tuck 1969
;
Vierendeels et al. 2007
)
, could be further improved using these ideas. There is also theoretical interest to situate this methodology in a larger class of numerical algorithms like Anderson acceleration and Reduced-Rank Extrapolation
(
Fang and Saad 2009
;
Sidi 2016
;
Pollock and Rebholz 2025
)
or multipoint methods
(
Traub 1964
;
Petković et al. 2014
)
.
