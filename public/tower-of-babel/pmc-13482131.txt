Novel multiple-image encryption with ultrafast light springs
Authors: Di Wang, Aimin Yan, Hongbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research proposed a novel multiple-image encryption scheme using ultrafast light springs. The encryption process begins with the random phase encoding of input images, followed by multiplication with distinct orbital angular momentum (OAM) modes. The modulated signals are then processed using a 4-f ultrashort pulse shaper. The dynamic optical amplitude and phase control of the signal was achieved via a liquid crystal–based spatial light modulator (SLM). The resulting light springs, characterized by helical phase and intensity profiles, are generated through the superposition of multiple laser frequency components. The integration of a double random phase encoding (DRPE) with tailored pulse-shaping operations has further converted the encrypted images to ultrashort pulse springs. This approach extends the temporal characteristics of OAM multiplexing encryption, significantly enhancing the system’s encryption capability, key space, and security. The performance of the proposed system is evaluated using multiple security metrics including key sensitive analysis, entropy analysis, correlation analysis, and resistance to attacks. The systematic evaluation demonstrated robustness of this encryption technique and highlighted its potential applications for optical image encryption and real-time secure communication.

Introduction

Optical encryption technology has emerged as a powerful solution for parallel high-dimensional information processing, offering unparalleled advantages in speed and security1–3. Optical encryption has been benefited from the earlier work of Refregier and Javidi in the 1990s, who demonstrated that double random phase encoding (DRPE) could transform original images into stationary white noise for information encryption4. Subsequently, numerous derivative techniques have been proposed including Fresnel transform5, fractional Fourier transform6, Hartley transform7, Gyrator transform8, and wavelet domains9. Meanwhile, previous research has further explored the use of other imaging system for optical encryption including digital holography10, optical scanning holography11,12, ptychography13, and ghost imaging14, which have strengthened the scientific merits of the optical encryption. Although several dynamic encryption schemes based on spatial light modulators (SLM) have been developed to realize video or sequential image encryption15–18, many conventional optical cryptosystems are still proposed for single-image processing in a single frame, which limits the encryption capacity and efficiency for multi-image parallel tasks required in manufacturing, banking, and medical applications. This limitation therefore motivates our development of a highly efficient multiple-image encryption framework.

Our multiple-image encryption system is motivated by the rich degrees of freedom associated with orbital angular momentum (OAM) of light therefore suitable for optical encryption19,20. OAM modes offer physically unlimited orthogonal states for information multiplexing characterized by helical wavefronts exp(ilθ), where l and θ denote topological charge and azimuthal angle respectively. This unique property has enabled successful applications in optical communications21,22 and optical trapping23. With OAM technique, researchers have developed multiplexing strategies including wavelength24, position25, space26, angular27, phase mask28, and multiplexed holography29,30. Earlier work has shown the promise for using OAM in multi-image encryption31–34. Furthermore, the doughnut-shaped intensity profile and helical phase structure of vortex beams provide additional degrees of freedom for mode division multiplexing. Recent innovations include Cheng’s elliptical-channel OAM multiplexing for enhanced data storage30, Zhang’s multi-dimensional holography for capacity expansion31, and Yuan’s frequency-shift augmented encryption scheme32. Recently, significant breakthroughs have been achieved in the experimental verification of OAM-based structured light encryption: as the core carrier of OAM technology, vector vortex beams and their arrays are widely used for high-security information encoding, with multiple experimental implementations demonstrating robust performance35 and further confirming OAM’s potential in multi-dimensional multiplexing encryption. Specifically, arrayed vector vortex beams have been experimentally validated for real-time audio encryption via topological charge multiplexing and intensity-based decoding36. Parallelly, single-shot intensity recording based on structured light’s binary polarization states enables experimental color image encryption, offering a cost-effective, speckle-free scheme compatible with partially coherent sources37 that supports the practical application of OAM-based encryption. These developments underscore OAM’s potential for high-security, ultra-large capacity encryption systems. Our research is further motivated by the unique spatial-temporally correlated optical encryption leveraging the emerging feature of the special OAM structural light. For this, the spatio-temporally correlated Light Springs (LS) has been used, which encode frequency correlations through superposed Laguerre-Gaussian (LG) modes38,39. As a novel class of ultrafast OAM-carrying beams, LS exhibits matched helical structures in both intensity and phase profiles. This distinctive characteristic has enabled breakthroughs in frequency Raman amplification39, super-resolution imaging40, and information coding41. Yang et al. demonstrated that the precise control of LS intensity distribution and azimuth angle could substantially increase information capacity41, while ultrafast pulse shaping techniques offer additional coding dimensions42–45. Despite these advances, previous research has not explored the use of LS for large-capacity multiple-image optical encryption, which is a critical gap to be filled by our research.

In this study, we present a novel multiple-image encryption technique that synergistically integrates ultrafast LS with DRPE system. Our approach makes three key contributions. First, we have implementation a novel encryption scheme of leveraging LS parameters (combined with DRPE and pulse shaping) to enhance encryption robustness. Second, we have developed a multi-functional ultrashort pulse shaper capable of simultaneous pulse shaping and image encryption. Third, we have used spatio-temporal light spring to expand the multiplexing encryption capabilities. Simulation validation confirms our system’s ability to achieve parallel encryption of multiple images with exceptional security, noise resistance, and resilience against attacks.

4-f pulse shaper with DRPE

The conventional optical setup of DRPE4 employs a 4-f system comprising two lenses, L1 and L2, with identical focal length f, as illustrated in Fig. 2. In this process, the original image g(x, y) is encoded to a stationary white noise G(x,y) using of two statistically independent random phase keys RPM1 and RPM2, positioned on the input and Fourier planes, respectively. Mathematically, RPM1 and RPM2 are expressed as exp[i2πP1(x,y)] and exp[i2πP2(u,v)], where P1(x,y) and P2(u,v) are random functions uniformly distributed over the interval [0, 1]. However, utrashort pulsed light springs impose specific constraints on the DRPE method. Due to the broadband nature of the ultrafast pulses, the two Fourier lenses in the DRPE system introduce significant dispersion, distorting the spatiotemporal wave packets carrying OAM. Thus, applying DRPE requires simultaneous consideration of ultrafast optical pulse shaping and OAM encryption. To address this challenge, we have used a novel Fourier-transform pulse shaper with the DRPE system to enable the encryption of images with light springs.

Figure 3a illustrates the proposed Fourier-transform pulse shaping and DRPE setup. The system consists of diffraction gratings G1 and G2, parabolic mirrors L1 and L2, and random phase masks RPM1 and RPM2. RPM2, generated by a SLM controlled by a personal computer (PC), is positioned into N sub-regions to modulate the amplitude and phase of N LG modes with the distinct OAM values l. The plaintext images are modulated by the input of LS and RPM1, which is denoted as Ein (r, θ; t). Grating G1 is placed at the front focal plane of L1, while G2 is located at the rear focal plane of L2. The encrypted output pulses, Eout (r, θ; t), are obtained from G2.

If we remove the two random phase masks RPM1 and RPM2 in Fig. 3a, the proposed Fourier-transform pulse shaping encryption setup can generate ideal LS beam. The spatio-temporal 3D intensity profiles and 2D slice images at different time of LS are shown in Fig. 1. We can easily see that it is a spring-like intensity distribution. And the value of Δl can be deduced from the number of bright spots in 2D slice images, as shown in Fig. 1a Δl = 1 and Fig. 1(b) Δl = 2. The measurement of the topological charges of the LS beam can be conducted using diffraction method. The schematic setup for the detection of their diffraction patterns is shown in Fig. 3(b). The generated LS pulses exiting grating G2 in Fig. 3a are focused onto grating G3 using spherical mirror L3. A cylindrical lens (CL) with focal length f is placed behind the third grating G3. A CCD camera is placed in the focal plane of this cylindrical lens, which is marked as the observed plane, to measure the diffraction patterns of the LS. Figure 1c and Fig. 1d show the diffraction patterns of the LS after cylindrical lens corresponding to the intensity profiles shown in Fig. 1a and Fig. 1b. A multiple-lobe structures can be observed in the diffraction patterns. For each component of the LS with topological charge l = n, the diffraction pattern has n + 1 lobes. Furthermore, n gaps are found in the diffraction profile, and each gap is caused by a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2{\uppi\:}$$\end{document} phase winding. From the measured results shown in Fig. 1c, it can be observed that the simulated LS consists of three vortex beams with topological charges of 1, 2, and 3. And Fig. 1(d) shows that the LS consists of three vortex beams with topological charges of 1, 3, and 5. With this diffraction property, each component of the LS with a different topological charge can be detected directly and rapidly using a single diffraction grating and cylindrical lens.

The process of cryptosystem

The overall encryption and decryption process is organized in below. The encryption process consists of two key steps:

The first step involves in the generation of controllable OAM modes, where multiple independent OAM modes are generated to modulate the plaintext images, denoted as Ein (r, θ; t).The second step involves the use of the ultrafast pulse shaping and DRPE system, where the ultrashort pulse shaping, DRPE, and coherent superposition are employed to produce the encrypted ultrashort LS and convert plaintext images into ciphertexts.

The decryption process is shown in Fig. 5. The encrypted LS Een (r, θ; t) is first spectrally dispersed by a grating G2, each angularly separated frequency component of Een is then focused by an off-axis parabolic mirror L2 onto a specific lateral position in the focal plane of the mirror. The spectrum information corresponding to the ciphertext can be obtained. The space light modulator SLM1 loaded globally the conjugate of RPM2 (denoted RPM2*) to eliminate the phase distortion introduced by the encryption process. Then, a switching mask M(u,v), which is matched with SLM1 and partitioned into N sub-regions to modulate the corresponding LG modes with topological charge l, is used to select the effective spectral region corresponding to the topological number l. After effective spectrum filtering, the spatial domain field is obtained by the inverse Fourier transform of the parabolic lens L1 and grating G1. The decrypted images from G1 are imaged onto a CCD camera after multiplying the conjugate of RPM1 (denoted as RPM1*) and amplitude modulation index to eliminate the influence of modal amplitude term Bj(r, t), which can be load by a space light modulator SLM2. Numerical simulations

Key sensitivity analysis

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed LS-based DRPE method, we conducted numerical simulations using MATLAB (R2024b). The simulations employed a 256 × 256 pixel of original image size, where RPM1 matches this dimension. The SLM had a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. The LG modes followed the configuration in Fig. 1(b), with N = 3 modes characterized by topological charges lj ={1, 3, 5} and Δl = 2. Figures 6(a1)-6(c1) display the three plaintext images: ‘Baboon’, ‘Cameraman’, and ‘Guang’. The encryption process generated two random phase keys in Fig. 6(a2) and Fig. 6(b2), and produced the multiplexed encrypted image shown in Fig. 6(c2). Successful decryption with all correct keys yielded the reconstructed images in Figs. 6(a3)-6(c3). The ciphertext in Fig. 6(c2) exhibits a pixel distribution statistically equivalent to stationary white noise, confirming the encryption’s effectiveness as no discernible information remains visible.

These results confirm that the main information of each target image can be effectively recovered and the visual quality of the decrypted images is acceptable. It should be emphasized that the slight residual imprints and the relatively low CC value for the Cameramen image are mainly caused by the inherent cross-talk among different channels in multi-image multiplexing and the numerical errors in calculation. The presented results represent the optimal recovery performance that has been achieved through extensive tests and parameter adjustments. Moreover, compared with the limited conventional DRPE method5, the LS parameters (including the OAM mode index l, frequency ω, topological charge gap Δl, spectral width Δω, and temporal pitch τp) serve as additional physical keys that effectively expand the key space. Any tiny deviation in these highly sensitive parameters will lead to complete decryption failure, which ensures the high security and information capacity of the proposed system.

Strength of encryption key space

The key space of the encryption system has been rigorously evaluated. To resist brute-force attacks, a robust encryption system requires a key space exceeding 2100. In this scheme, the key composition includes not only the random phase masks RPM1 and RPM2, but also the physical parameters of light springs, such as topological charge l, spectral width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:\omega\:$$\end{document}, and coil pitch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{p}$$\end{document}. For an image with 256 × 256 pixels, each pixel value in this mask can range from 1 to 255. This provides a key space of 255256 × 256=25565536, which is far larger than the security threshold of 2100. Combined with additional physical keys including topological charge gap Δl, spectral width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:\omega\:$$\end{document}, and temporal pitch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{p}$$\end{document}, the total key space is further enlarged to ensure high security against brute-force attacks.

Attack analysis

Encryption systems often face the risk of being attacked by others, so we have done a security analysis. Existing research has shown that traditional DRPE system is based on the principle of Fourier transform algorithm and its linearity opens avenues of attacks, such as known-plaintext attack (KPA)46, chosen-plaintext attack (CPA)47, and ciphertext-only attacks (COA)48. KPA is where an adversary has a quantity of plaintexts (that cannot be specifically chosen) and the corresponding ciphertexts. CPA is similar to a KPA with the added advantage (for the adversary) to choose plaintexts. In the proposed cryptosystem, although DRPE is a linear operation, the light-springs-related parameters including orbital angular momentum l, topological charge gap Δl, spectral width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:\omega\:$$\end{document}, and temporal pitch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{p}$$\end{document} are generated from the dynamic ultrafast pulse shaping system, which involves multiple nonlinear operations and are indirectly dictated by the secret key value. Besides, it is more challenging for an attacker to decipher the cryptosystem because of the existence of spatio-temporal coupling light springs encryption process. That confirms that LS-based DRPE is resistant to the CPA and the KPA.

Conclusions

This study has successfully developed and validated a novel multiple-image encryption framework integrating ultrafast light springs with DRPE architecture. The core innovation of the research is the integration of the orbital angular momentum mode multiplexing, dynamic ultrafast pulse shaping via SLM, and DRPE-based spatial encoding. This approach enables the transformation of plaintext images into light springs-characterized by helical phase-intensity profiles-through superposition of modulated ultrashort pulse components. The integration of tailored pulse-shaping operations with spatial-phase encoding establishes a physically realizable encryption mechanism, with the SLM-based 4-f pulse shaper providing dynamic control over optical amplitudes and phases. Simulation results confirms that the generated light springs effectively obscure plaintext information while maintaining structural stability during transmission. Numerical simulations and security analyses verify the effectiveness of the proposed multi-image encryption method and its strong ability to counteract common attacks. Given its high security threshold, scalable key space, and compatibility with optical communication infrastructures, this light spring-based encryption paradigm shows significant promise for real-time secure image transmission. While the proposed framework demonstrates promising performance for ultrafast LS in the field of multiple-image encryption, more possibilities of generation LS and encryption methods remain to be discovered, such as fractional topological charges, optical frequency comb and asymmetric cryptosystems. In the future, we will explore to establish a physical experimental platform to validate the cryptosystem’s performance and security under rigorous attack scenarios.
