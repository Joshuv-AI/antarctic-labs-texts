Sampling theorems for inverse problems on Riemannian manifolds
G. S. Alberti 1, E. De Vito 1, B. Gariboldi 2, G. Gigante 2

Abstract
. We consider inverse problems consisting of the reconstruction of an unknown signal f f from noisy measurements y = F ​ f + noise y=Ff+\text{noise} , where F ​ f Ff is a function on a Riemannian manifold without boundary ℳ \mathcal{M} . We consider the case when only pointwise samples are available, namely y j = ( F ​ f ) ​ ( x j ) + η j y_{j}=(Ff)(x_{j})+\eta_{j} , where { x j } j = 1 n ⊆ ℳ \{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M} is a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family. We derive sampling theorems providing explicit bounds on the reconstruction error depending on n n , the smoothness of f f and the properties of F F . As a corollary, we state a sampling theorem for convolutions on the two-dimensional sphere, and discuss four relevant examples related to terrestrial and celestial measurements.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Sampling and approximation

The classical Shannon sampling theorem, a cornerstone of signal processing and harmonic analysis, asserts that a bandlimited function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with spectral support contained in an interval
[-\Omega,\Omega]
is entirely determined by its values on the discrete set
\{x_{j}=j/(2\Omega)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and can be exactly reconstructed via the Shannon-Whittaker interpolation formula
[
71
,
54
,
60
]
. This fundamental result highlights the intrinsic link between the bandwidth
\Omega
— the measure of frequency content — and the density
\frac{1}{2\Omega}
of the samples required for perfect reconstruction.
In higher dimensions, analogous results hold for functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
whose Fourier transforms are supported in compact sets: such functions can be reconstructed from their values on suitable lattices, provided the sampling rate exceeds the Nyquist threshold determined by the volume of the spectral support
[
42
,
9
]
.

On compact domains such as the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the sphere
S^{d}
, or a compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
without boundary, where the frequency domain is discrete, the situation becomes even more structured: bandlimited functions
f
become finite-dimensional, and exact recovery from a finite number of measurements
\{f(x_{j})\}_{j}
is possible, provided the samples
\{x_{j}\}_{j}
are suitably taken
[
48
,
29
,
46
,
45
]
. This can be formalized with the concept of Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family, whose construction is guaranteed whenever the sampling space can be divided into regions, each with a prefixed volume and small diameter
[
49
,
47
,
22
,
21
]
. This type of partition, on the other hand, exists in every connected compact orientable Riemannian manifold without boundary
[
27
,
19
]
, and is explicit in simple settings, such as for the two-dimensional sphere
[
56
]
. As with the Shannon theorem, the bandwidth of
f
is explicitly linked with the density of the sampling points
\{x_{j}\}_{j}
. All these results have an “approximation” counterpart in the cases where
f
is not bandlimited but smooth, an assumption that is quantified by the decay of the generalized Fourier coefficients of
f
.

1.2.
Inverse problems

Inverse problems appear when an unknown signal
f
belonging to a space
X
has to be recovered from indirect measurements of the form
Ff
, where
F\colon X\to Y
is the so-called forward map, and
Y
is the measurement space
[
10
,
36
,
59
]
. Inverse problems are typically ill-posed: whenever measurements are noisy, so that only an approximation
y
of
Ff
is available, where
\|y-Ff\|_{Y}
is small, the reconstruction of an approximation of
f
is problematic, because the inverse of
F
is not continuous. In the linear setting, this is often a consequence of the compactness of
F
. For example, this is the case whenever
F
is given as an integral operator with a suitable kernel, such as a convolution operator or the Radon transform
[
51
,
57
]
. The classical method to address ill-posedness is regularization
[
20
,
38
]
.

Even if
X
and
Y
are usually infinite-dimensional function spaces, which is due to the fact that the models often involve integral or differential operators, the available measurements are always finite dimensional
[
37
,
43
,
3
,
39
,
8
,
4
]
.
In many settings, assuming that
Y
is a space of functions defined on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, the finite measurements can be written as noisy pointwise samples of
Ff
on a suitable family of points
\{x_{j}\}_{j}\subset\mathcal{M}
, namely,

(1)

y_{j}=(Ff)(x_{j})+\eta_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

where
\{\eta_{j}\}_{j}
represents measurement noise. This problem is often studied in a random design setting, in which the points
x_{j}
are sampled i.i.d. with respect to a fixed probability distribution on
\mathcal{M}

[
17
]
. Several results exist in this context, regarding approaches based on statistical inverse learning (mostly for linear problems)
[
12
,
58
,
16
,
15
,
32
,
1
]

and approaches studying the statistical consistency for nonlinear problems
[
2
,
28
,
52
,
53
]
. Most of these works are based on smoothness/Gaussian priors on
f
; for sparsity-based priors, several works have investigated the use of compressed sensing techniques to further reduce the number of measurements in inverse problems, see e.g.
[
33
,
7
,
18
,
44
,
5
,
6
]
. In the context of a deterministic design, we refer the reader to
[
64
,
65
,
50
]
, where the authors study the case of Fourier Integral Operators
F
.

1.3.
Our contribution: sampling for inverse problems

In this work, we address the inverse problem consisting of the recovery of
f
from the measurements
\{y_{j}\}_{j}
given in (
1
) by applying the techniques based on sampling and approximation discussed in
\lx@sectionsign
1.1
. In contrast to the approaches based on random design points mentioned above, here the points
\{x_{j}\}_{j}
are deterministic and chosen to form a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family, which as we mentioned before can be achieved e. g. in any orientable compact connected Riemannian manifold without boundary. We consider abstract inverse problems under suitable smoothness assumptions on the forward map
F
. We prove recovery estimates and explicitly quantify the error as a function of the noise level and of the number of samples.

Furthermore, we leverage the flexibility of considering general Riemannian manifolds beyond the Euclidean setting and consider convolution operators
F
on compact two-point homogeneous spaces, which allows us to write
F
as Fourier multiplier (with respect to the basis given by the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
). In this setting, the inverse problem consists in the reconstruction of a function
f
from noisy pointwise samples of
(h*f)(x_{j})
, where
h
is a given filter.
As a key application, we consider the convolution on the two-dimensional sphere
[
68
,
30
,
41
,
40
,
69
]
, in the spirit of the results obtained in
[
34
]
with pointwise samples. We obtain explicit error estimates, and quantify how the smoothness of
f
and the degree of ill-posedness of
F
(namely, the decay of the generalized Fourier coefficients of
h
) affect the reconstruction error and the number of required samples. These estimates are then applied to four examples of deconvolution problems on the sphere
[
62
,
61
]
.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
we anticipate our results specialized for the convolution problem on the sphere, in order to better illustrate the abstract approach presented in the rest of the paper. These results are applied to four different examples in Section
3
. In Section
4
we review some basic facts about sampling and approximation for functions on manifolds, based on Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund families. In Section
5
we apply these results to abstract inverse problems. Finally, in Section
6
we apply the abstract derivation to convolutions on compact two-point homogeneous spaces.

2.
Convolution on the sphere

In this section, motivated by several applications, we anticipate our results specialized for the convolution operator on the sphere of the Euclidean space.

2.1.
The abstract result for the sphere
S^{d}

We briefly introduce the notation and setting.
Let
S^{d}
be the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
endowed with the
Riemann measure
\mu
normalized as
\mu(S^{d})=1
.
The orthogonal group
SO(d+1)
acts transitively on
S^{d}
and its
action is denoted by
gx
where
g\in SO(d+1)
and
x\in S^{d}
.
Letting
o\in S^{d}
denote the north pole, the stability subgroup at
o
is

\{g\in SO(d+1):go=o\}=SO(d),

so that we identify
S^{d}
with the quotient space
SO(d+1)/SO(d)

by means of the map

SO(d+1)\ni g\mapsto go\in S^{d}.

For a fixed filter function
h\in L^{1}(S^{d},\mu)
, the convolution operator

F\colon L^{2}(S^{d})\to L^{2}(S^{d})
by
h
,
Ff=h*f
, is defined as

(Ff)(x)=\int_{SO(d+1)}h(g^{-1}x)f(go)dg\,,

where
dg
is the Haar measure of
SO(d+1)
normalized to
1
.
It is well known that the convolution operator has a nice structure in terms of spherical
harmonics. Indeed, let
\Delta
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
S^{d}
,
regarded as a self-adjoint positive operator on
L^{2}(S^{d})
. Its
(distinct) eigenvalues are

\lambda_{m}^{2}=m(m+d-1)\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{H}_{m}
be the space of the
eigenfunctions of
\Delta
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{m}^{2}
. The space

\mathcal{H}_{m}
is finite dimensional, and we denote its dimension by

\dim\mathcal{H}_{m}=\delta_{m}=\binom{d+m}{d}-\binom{d+m-2}{d}\asymp m^{d-1}

and the corresponding orthogonal projection by
\Pi_{m}
.
Finally, let

\mathcal{Q}_{m}=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{m}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}

be the space of diffusion polynomials of bandwith

\lambda_{m}
.

Assume now that the filter function
h\in L^{2}(S^{d})\subset L^{1}(S^{d})
. By setting

(2)

b_{m}=\frac{1}{\delta_{m}}(\Pi_{m}h)(o)\qquad m\in\mathbb{N},

the convolution operator
F
with filter
h
reads as

(3)

Ff=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}\Pi_{m}f\qquad f\in L^{2}(S^{d}),

i.e.

F
is a Fourier multiplier. Note that, for any bounded sequence

\{b_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, Eq. (
3
) defines a bounded operator,
which in general is not a convolution operator. For example,
if
b_{m}=1
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, then
F
is the identity
operator. We stress that our results hold true also for this larger class of operators.

We need to recall the definition of Sobolev
spaces
H^{\sigma}(S^{d})
on the sphere. For
\sigma\geq 0
, we have that

H^{\sigma}(S^{d})=\left\{f\in L^{2}(S^{d}):\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}(1+{\lambda}_{m}^{2})^{\sigma}\lVert\Pi_{m}f\rVert^{2}<+\infty\right\}

is a Hilbert space with respect to the norm

\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\sigma}(S^{d})}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}(1+{\lambda}_{m}^{2})^{\sigma}\lVert\Pi_{m}f\rVert^{2}.

The following proposition provides some smoothing properties of the convolution operator; it follows from the general case treated in Proposition
24
.

Proposition 1
.

Choose
h\in L^{2}(S^{d})
, set
b_{m}
as in (
2
) and assume that

(4)

|b_{m}|\leq c(1+{\lambda}^{2}_{m})^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}}

for some
c,\gamma\geq 0
. Fix
\omega\geq 0
and
\sigma=\omega+\gamma
, then

F
is a bounded operator from
H^{\omega}(S^{d})
into
H^{\sigma}(S^{d})
,
with norm bounded by
c
.

The next result shows the existence of a nice discretization family of
points. It is a particular case of Theorems
12
and
13
.

Proposition 2
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<1
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a
finite family
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
of distinct points of
S^{d}
such that

(5)

(1-\varepsilon)\|p\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{L_{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|p(x_{j})|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|p\|_{2}^{2},\qquad p\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}

and
L_{m}=\lceil C_{1}({\lambda}_{m}^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}\rceil\sim m^{d}
for
some constant
C_{1}>0
.

It is possible to provide an explicit construction of the
discretization family
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
; we describe the case
d=2
in
Section
2.2
.
The points
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
are called a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund
family. The map

p\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}\longmapsto\{p(x_{j})\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}\in\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}

is
\varepsilon
-close to an isometry, which allows for the stable recovery of
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}
from its pointwise samples
\{p(x_{j})\}_{j}
.

We now state our main result for the sphere, which is a particular case of Theorem
25
and Corollary
27
. The focus is the reconstruction of
f^{\dagger}
, where the measurement data are the noisy values of the function
Ff^{\dagger}=h*f^{\dagger}
sampled at the discretization points
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
, namely,

y_{j}=(h*f^{\dagger})(x_{j})+\eta_{j},

where the noise
\eta_{j}
is bounded by
\beta
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
h\in L^{2}(S^{d})
such that
b_{0}\neq 0
and Eq. (
4
)
holds true for some
\gamma\geq 0
. Fix
f^{\dagger}\in H^{\omega}(S^{d})
with
\omega\geq 0
, and assume that for some
\zeta\geq 0
,

\sigma=\omega+\gamma>d/2+\zeta
. Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
be the discretization family of points given by
Proposition
2
and let
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
be the corresponding
family of noisy outputs such that

|y_{j}-Ff^{\dagger}(x_{j})|\leq\beta\quad j=1,\ldots,L_{m}

where
\beta\geq 0
is the noise level. Choose
p_{m}^{\beta}\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}
as

(6)

p_{m}^{\beta}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}}\frac{1}{L_{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|y_{j}-Fp(x_{j})|^{2},

then

\displaystyle\|Ff^{\dagger}-Fp_{m}^{\beta}\rVert_{H^{\zeta}(S^{d})}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1+\kappa}{\sigma-\zeta-\frac{d}{2}}}\,\|Ff^{\dagger}\rVert_{H^{\sigma}(S^{d})}(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{-\frac{\sigma-\zeta}{2}+\frac{d}{4}}+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}

where
\kappa=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}
. If furthermore
\zeta\geq\gamma
and for some constant
c_{0}>0
,

(7)

|b_{m}|\geq c_{0}(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{-\zeta/2}\text{ for all }m\geq 0,

then we have

\|f^{\dagger}-p_{m}^{\beta}\|_{2}\leq c_{0}^{-1}\|Ff^{\dagger}-Fp_{m}^{\beta}\|_{H^{\zeta}(S^{2})}.

The function
p_{m}^{\beta}
is a solution of a least squares problem having the space of diffusion polynomials (with a given bandwith)
\mathcal{Q}_{m}
as hypothesis space (as in regularization by projection
[
38
]
), and depends only on the measured noisy data
y_{j}
. Note that

\mathcal{Q}_{m}
has dimension
\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\delta_{\ell}\asymp m^{d}
and the number of
discretization points is
L_{m}\asymp m^{d}
. Our result shows that, for a sufficiently small noise level
\beta
,
Fp_{m}^{\beta}

is a good approximation (in the
H^{\zeta}
-norm) of the true value
h*f^{\dagger}
, and that, under hypothesis (
7
)
on
h
,
p_{m}^{\beta}
is a good approximation (in the
L^{2}
norm) of
f^{\dagger}
.

In order to highlight the dependence on the discretization parameter
m
and the noise level
\beta
, let us write the recovery estimate without the explicit constants, by using that
\lambda_{m}^{2}\asymp m^{2}
for
m\geq 1
:

\|f^{\dagger}-p_{m}^{\beta}\|_{2}\lesssim m^{\zeta-\sigma+\frac{d}{2}}+\beta m^{\zeta}.

For a fixed noise level
\beta
, this leads us to choose
m
in such a way that the two terms have the same order, namely
m^{\zeta-\sigma+\frac{d}{2}}\asymp\beta m^{\zeta}
, so that

(8)

m=\left\lceil\beta^{-\frac{1}{\omega+\gamma-\frac{d}{2}}}\right\rceil,

where we used that
\sigma=\omega+\gamma
. Plugging this into the bound above yields

(9)

\|f^{\dagger}-p_{m}^{\beta}\|_{2}\lesssim\beta^{1-\frac{\zeta}{\omega+\gamma-\frac{d}{2}}}.

As expected, since
\omega+\gamma-\frac{d}{2}>\zeta
by assumption, as
\beta\to 0
, we have
p_{m}^{\beta}\to f^{\dagger}
, provided that the number of measurements increases accordingly to (
8
). Furthermore, it is possible to understand the role of the parameters involved in these estimates:

•

the parameter
\omega
such that
f^{\dagger}\in H^{\omega}(S^{2})
quantifies the smoothness assumption on
f^{\dagger}
;

•

the parameters
\gamma
and
\zeta
quantify the smoothness of
h
through the decay of its generalized Fourier coefficients in (
4
) and (
7
), and will usually be of the same order.

Therefore, according to (
8
), fewer samples are needed as
\gamma
and
\omega
increase (in other words, as the problem becomes more ill-posed and as
f^{\dagger}
becomes smoother). Similarly, in light of (
9
), a smoother
f^{\dagger}
(larger
\omega
) yields a smaller reconstruction error, but a more ill-posed problem (larger
\gamma
and
\zeta
) yields a larger error.

In
[
34
]
, results similar to ours were obtained for the two-dimensional sphere, but in a setting with statistical noise and a different choice of approximating polynomial. In particular, exact knowledge of the multiplier associated with the operator is required, and the sampling points must form a spherical design—a construction that is more challenging than that of the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund
family provided by Proposition
2
.

2.2.
The particular case of the two-dimensional sphere
S^{2}

We provide an example of a discretization family for the two-dimensional sphere. It can be shown (see
[
22
, Theorem 5.1]
) that if one can divide the manifold (in this case the sphere
S^{2}
) into
N
regions with the same measure and bounded diameter, then every choice of points, one in each region, gives a discretization family, as in Proposition
2
. For the
2
-dimensional sphere, we describe an explicit partition
[
56
]
.
Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
N=L_{m}
be the size of the
discretization family. Let

•

\theta_{0}=\arccos(1-50/N)
;

•

s
be the largest odd integer smaller than or equal to
\sqrt{\pi N}/2
;

•

\Delta\theta=(\pi-2\theta_{0})/s
;

•

\theta_{k}^{\prime}=\theta_{0}+k\Delta\theta
for
k=1,2,\ldots,s
,
\theta^{\prime}_{s+1}=\pi
;

•

and
y_{k}=N(\cos\theta_{k-1}^{\prime}-\cos\theta_{k}^{\prime})/2
, for
k=1,2,\ldots,s
.

It can be proved that there exists a symmetric sequence of integers
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}
such that

(1)

\sum_{i=1}^{s}{\ell}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}y_{i}=N-50
.

(2)

|y_{1}-{\ell}_{1}|=|y_{s}-{\ell}_{s}|\leq 1/2
and
|y_{i}-{\ell}_{i}|\leq 1
for
i=2,\ldots s-1
.

(3)

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(y_{i}-{\ell}_{i})\right|\leq 1/2
, for
k=1,\ldots,s
.

Finally set
{\ell}_{0}={\ell}_{s+1}=25
.
It is shown in
[
56
]
that for
N\geq 50
, the regions of the sphere described in spherical coordinates as

D^{N}_{k,j}=[\theta_{k-1},\theta_{k}]\times[2\pi(j-1)/{\ell_{k}},2\pi j/{\ell}_{k}]
, for
k=0,\ldots,s+1
,

j=1,\ldots,{\ell}_{k}
, where

\theta_{k}=\arccos(1-(2/N)\sum_{i=0}^{k}{\ell}_{i})
for

k=0,\ldots,s+1
, and
\theta_{-1}=0
, form a partition of the sphere
of regions with equal area
1/N
, each contained in a spherical cap of radius bounded above by
c_{4}N^{-1/2}
, for some
c_{4}>0
. It can be easily shown that all regions contain a spherical cap of radius
c_{3}N^{-1/2}
, for some
c_{3}>0
.

Therefore we can give the following constructive version of Proposition
2
(see also Theorem
12
).

Proposition 4
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<1
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For every possible choice of
x_{k,j}\in D_{k,j}^{L_{m}}
, the family
\{x_{k,j}\}_{k=0,j=1}^{s+1,\ell_{k}}
is such that

(10)

(1-\varepsilon)\|p\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{L_{m}}\sum_{k=0}^{s+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{k}}|p(x_{k,j})|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|p\|_{2}^{2},\qquad p\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}

whenever
L_{m}=\lceil C_{1}m(m+1)\rceil
for
some constant
C_{1}>0
.

Four examples

3.1.
Sea surface temperature anomalies.

This experiment

[
62
,
61
]
simulates the global map of temperature
anomalies of the sea surface produced by NASA’s Aqua satellite in
January 2011, by sampling it in
N=6745
locations. Since temperatures
typically have smooth variations, one may assume the anomaly function

f^{\dagger}
to be an element of
H^{\omega}(S^{2})
for some

\omega>1
. The inversion problem here reduces to
F
being the
identity operator, that is
b_{m}=1
for every
m

(as already noted, our
result holds true for any Fourier multiplier of the form (
3
)).

We may therefore set
\gamma=0
, and apply Theorem
3
with
\zeta=0
to obtain

\displaystyle\|p_{m}^{\beta}-f^{\dagger}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\omega}(S^{2})}\frac{1}{(\omega-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{1}{(1+m(m+1))^{\frac{\omega-1}{2}}}+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta.

3.2.
Wildfires and Deforestation.

This example deals with global density maps of trees and
wildfires across the globe for the year 2016. In both experiments, the data used in
[
62
,
61
]
consists of tree and fire counts recorded by
NASA’s Aqua and Terra satellites. In
[
62
,
61
]
, it is assumed that the spatial density
f^{\dagger}
of trees (respectively fires) is a function in
H^{\omega}(S^{2})
with
\omega>1
, where we model the Earth surface by
S^{2}
. In both cases, the inverse problem consists in recovering
f^{\dagger}
from a finite number of pointwise evaluations of
Ff^{\dagger}=h\ast f^{\dagger}
, where
h
is the characteristic function of a small region around the North Pole. Here we propose to replace this choice of
h
with the characteristic function of a spherical cap of angle
\vartheta_{0}\leq\pi/2
, so that
h(x)=\chi_{[0,\vartheta_{0}]}(\rho(o,x))
where
\rho(o,x)
is the angular distance of
x
from the North Pole.
With this choice, by Eq. (
25
) below and Rodrigues’ formula (see
[
67
, (4.3.1)]
), if
m\geq 1
,

b_{m}=\frac{1}{m}P^{1,1}_{m-1}(\cos\vartheta_{0})\left(\sin\frac{\vartheta_{0}}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\cos\frac{\vartheta_{0}}{2}\right)^{2}.

Let us now prove the upper bound (
4
) with
\gamma=3/2
.
Kogbetliantz inequality
[
24
, eq. (1.3)]
applied to our setting reads as

\left|P_{m-1}^{1,1}(\cos\vartheta_{0})(\sin\vartheta_{0})^{\frac{3}{2}}\right|\leq\frac{4\Gamma\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)(m+1)\Gamma(m)}

for all
\vartheta_{0}\leq\pi/2
and for all
m\geq 1
. It follows that for
m\geq 1

\displaystyle|b_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{(\sin\vartheta_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{4m}P^{1,1}_{m-1}(\cos\vartheta_{0})\left(\sin\vartheta_{0}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{(\sin\vartheta_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{m}\frac{\Gamma\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)(m+1)\Gamma(m)}\right|\leq\frac{3^{\frac{3}{4}}}{2}\frac{(\sin\vartheta_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(1+m(m+1))^{\frac{3}{4}}}.

Since

4.
Sampling

In this section, we revisit some fairly recent results regarding the approximation of smooth functions from a finite number of pointwise evaluations. This collection of results begins with the work of K. Gröchenig
[
29
]
, concerning the approximation of a function in a properly defined Sobolev space with a certain “polynomial” obtained by least squares from the samples of the function on the nodes of a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family.
One of the main features of this work is that the simplicity of the arguments allows for a great generality. For example, the ambient space is just a topological space with a probability measure, and the “polynomials” are simply the finite linear combinations of the functions of any orthonormal system in
L^{2}
.

Soon after, Gröchenig’s results were revisited in more explicit situations: for example, W. Lu and H. Wang
[
46
]
studied the case of the sphere, and J. Li, Y. Ling, J. Geng and H. Wang
[
45
]
the case of a compact Riemannian manifold. In these cases, the orthonormal system is given by the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator and the Sobolev spaces are defined as usual. In these situations, it is possible to improve Gröchenig’s results, obtaining asymptotically optimal estimates on
the decay of the approximation error.

4.1.
Sampling with Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund families

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a space with a topology (usually a compact space) and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{M}
. Let
L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
denote the Hilbert space of (equivalence classes of) square-integrable functions, equipped with

\langle f,g\rangle_{2}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\,\overline{g}\,d\mu,\qquad\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|f|^{2}\,d\mu.

Futhermore, we assume that

(i)

\mathcal{S}=\{\psi_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
, such that each
\psi_{k}
is continuous and bounded on
\mathcal{M}
, and
\psi_{0}\equiv 1
. We set
H(\mathcal{S})=\overline{\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{L}=\{\Lambda_{k}:\Lambda_{k}\geq 0\}
is a non-decreasing sequence with
\Lambda_{k}\to+\infty
.

Let the Sobolev space associated to
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{L}
be defined as

H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})=\left\{f\in H(\mathcal{S}):\|f\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}<+\infty\right\},\qquad\sigma\geq 0,

where the norm is given by

(12)

\|f\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left|\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}\right|^{2}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Notice that
H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
is a Hilbert space, with the scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}\overline{\langle g,\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{\sigma}.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{r}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
be the set of “polynomials” of degree
r
on
\mathcal{M}
, that is

\mathcal{Q}_{r}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})=\operatorname{span}\{\psi_{k}:\Lambda_{k}\leq r\}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathcal{M}).

Definition 5
.

A doubly-indexed set
\{x_{m,j}:m\in\mathbb{N},\,j=1,\ldots,L_{m}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
,
a set of positive weights
\{\tau_{m,j}:m\in\mathbb{N},\,j=1,\ldots,L_{m}\}
, and a non-negative, increasing sequence
\{r_{m}\}
of “degrees” diverging to
+\infty
, such that

(13)

A\|q\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|q(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{m,j}\leq B\|q\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L}),

with constants
A,\,B>0
independent of
m
, is called a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family
\mathcal{X}

for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{L}
. We set
\kappa=B/A
.

We define the series

C_{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}|\psi_{k}(x)|^{2}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{-\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and the
remainders

\phi_{\sigma}(r,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{k:\Lambda_{k}>r}|\psi_{k}(x)|^{2}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{-\sigma}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

When
C_{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})<+\infty
, then
\phi_{\sigma}(r,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})<+\infty
too. If
\phi_{\sigma}(r,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})\to 0
as
r\to+\infty
, then
C_{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})<+\infty
.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family
for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{L}
, with nodes
\{x_{m,j}\}
, weights
\{\tau_{m,j}\}
and degrees
\{r_{m}\}
. For any finite sequence of complex numbers
y_{m}=\{y_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
, we set

(14)

T^{\dagger}y_{m}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\,\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|y_{m,j}-q(x_{m,j})|^{2}\,\tau_{m,j}.

The following result shows that this minimization problem has indeed a unique minimizer, and provides a useful bound on its norm.

Lemma 7
.

For any finite sequence of complex numbers
y_{m}=\{y_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
, problem (
14
) admits a unique minimizer
T^{\dagger}y_{m}\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
, and

\|T^{\dagger}y_{m}\|_{2}\leq A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|y_{m,j}|^{2}\tau_{m,j}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Let us equip
\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}
with the weighted norm

\|a\|_{\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|a_{j}|^{2}\tau_{m,j}.

Let
T\colon\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})\to\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}
be the sampling operator defined by

Tq=\left\{q(x_{m,j})\right\}_{j}.

By (
13
),

(15)

A^{\frac{1}{2}}\|q\|_{2}\leq\|Tq\|_{\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}}\leq B^{\frac{1}{2}}\|q\|_{2},

and
T
is therefore injective. Let
\mathcal{W}=\operatorname{Im}T
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}
be the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}
onto
{\mathcal{W}}
, then
T
is an invertible operator from
\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
onto
\mathcal{W}
with inverse
S\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
. The bound (
15
) implies that

A^{\frac{1}{2}}\|Sy\|_{2}\leq\|y\|_{\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}}\qquad\forall y\in\mathcal{W},

so that
\|S\|\leq A^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
Furthermore, since

\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\|Tq-y_{m}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\|Tq-\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}y_{m}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{L_{m}}}=S\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}y_{m},

it follows that the pseudoinverse of
T
(see
[
55
]
) is
T^{\dagger}=S\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}
and
\|T^{\dagger}\|\leq\|S\|\|\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}\|\leq A^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

∎

The following theorem holds (see

[
29
, Theorem 3.3]
). It concerns the reconstruction of a smooth function
f^{\dagger}\in H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
from its pointwise samples
\left\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\right\}_{j}
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family
for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{L}
, with nodes
\{x_{m,j}\}
, weights
\{\tau_{m,j}\}
and degrees
\{r_{m}\}
.
Let
f^{\dagger}\in H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
be continuous for some
\zeta\geq 0
, and define

q_{m}=T^{\dagger}\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})-q(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{m,j}.

Then for every
\sigma\geq\zeta
and
m\in\mathbb{N}

\|f^{\dagger}-q_{m}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L}).

For the sake of completeness, and given that this version is slightly more general and with a somewhat refined constant than the original result in
[
29
]
, we report here the elementary proof.

Proof.

Let us call
P_{r_{m}}
the orthogonal projection of
H(\mathcal{S})
onto
Q_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
. Given that if
f^{\dagger}\in H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
, then
f^{\dagger}-P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}
remains orthogonal to
Q_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
in
H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
,

\|f^{\dagger}-q_{m}\|^{2}_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}=\|f^{\dagger}-P_{r_{m}}f\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}^{2}+\|P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}-q_{m}\|^{2}_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}.

Now,

\displaystyle\|f^{\dagger}-P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{\Lambda_{k}>r_{m}}\langle f^{\dagger},\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}\psi_{k}\right\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}=\left\|\sum_{\Lambda_{k}>r_{m}}\langle f^{\dagger},\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\psi_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{\Lambda_{k}>r_{m}}\langle f^{\dagger},\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}(1+\Lambda_{k}^{2})^{-\frac{\sigma-\zeta}{2}}\psi_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L}).

On the other hand, using the notation of the proof of Lemma
7
, since
P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}\in Q_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
,

\displaystyle\|P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}-q_{m}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}

\displaystyle\leq(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\|P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}-q_{m}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\|T^{\dagger}\{P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}-T^{\dagger}\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq A^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})-f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{m,j}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq B^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\sup_{x}|P_{r_{m}}f^{\dagger}(x)-f^{\dagger}(x)|

\displaystyle=B^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\sup_{x}\left|\sum_{\Lambda_{k}>r_{m}}\langle f^{\dagger},\psi_{k}\rangle_{2}\psi_{k}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq B^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\leq B^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L}),

where we have used that
\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}\tau_{m,j}\leq B

(simply set
q\equiv 1
in (
13
)).
∎

Notice that normally
\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}\tau_{m,j}=1\leq B
, in which case the factor
\kappa
could be replaced by
A^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

In the original theorem in
[
29
]
,
\zeta
is set equal to
0
and
\mathcal{S}
is assumed to be a complete orthonormal system, so that
H(\mathcal{S})=L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
. Also, in that paper,
r_{m}
is set to be equal to
m
.

In a real-life context, evaluations
f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})
may be subject to certain measurement errors. The following result takes this possibility into account.

Corollary 9
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{L}
, with nodes
\{x_{m,j}\}
, weights
\{\tau_{m,j}\}
and degrees
\{r_{m}\}
.
Let
f^{\dagger}
be a continuous function in
H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
,
\zeta\geq 0
, and let
\{y_{m,j}\}
be the corresponding
family of noisy samples such that

|y_{m,j}-f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})|\leq\beta,\qquad j=1,\ldots,L_{m},

where
\beta\geq 0
is the noise level. Set

q_{m}^{\beta}=T^{\dagger}\{y_{m,j}\}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|y_{m,j}-q(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{m,j}.

Then for every
\sigma\geq\zeta
and
m\in\mathbb{N}

\|f^{\dagger}-q_{m}^{\beta}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
7
and Theorem
8
we have

\displaystyle\|f^{\dagger}-T^{\dagger}\{y_{m,j}\}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\dagger}-T^{\dagger}\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}+\|T^{\dagger}\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}-T^{\dagger}\{y_{m,j}\}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\dagger}-T^{\dagger}\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}+(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\|T^{\dagger}\{f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}-T^{\dagger}\{y_{m,j}\}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})+A^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{L_{m}}|f^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})-y_{m,j}|^{2}\tau_{m,j}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})+B^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\beta(1+r_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}},

where we have used that
\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}\tau_{m,j}\leq B
.
∎

The following result gives a lower estimate for the function
\phi_{\sigma}(r_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
.

Proposition 10
.

Assume that for a doubly indexed collection of nodes
\{x_{m,j}\}_{m=1,j=1}^{+\infty,L_{m}}
, for corresponding weights
\{\tau_{m,j}\}
, for an increasing sequence of degrees
\{r_{m}\}_{m=1}^{+\infty}
diverging to
\infty
, and for
\sigma\geq 0
and
\zeta\geq 0
,
there exists a function
\Psi_{\sigma,\zeta}(r_{m})
such that
for every polynomial
f
(finite linear combination of the
\psi_{k}
’s), if we define

q_{m}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|f(x_{m,j})-q(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{m,j},

there holds

\|f-q_{m}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\leq\|f\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\Psi_{\sigma,\zeta}(r_{m}).

Then, if we let
k_{m}
be the smallest value
k
for which
\Lambda_{k}>r_{m}
, we have

\Psi_{\sigma,\zeta}(r_{m})\geq(1+\Lambda_{k_{m}}^{2})^{-\frac{\sigma-\zeta}{2}}.

Proof.

Set
f=\psi_{k_{m}}
. Then
f
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
, and so

\displaystyle(1+\Lambda_{k_{m}}^{2})^{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\leq\|f\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}^{2}+\|-q_{m}\|^{2}_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}=\|f-q_{m}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}(\Psi_{\sigma,\zeta}(r_{m}))^{2}=(1+\Lambda_{k_{m}}^{2})^{\sigma}(\Psi_{\sigma,\zeta}(r_{m}))^{2},

as claimed.
∎

Assume now that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator and
\mathcal{L}
the set of the square roots of the corresponding eigenvalues. It has been proved in
[
45
]
(see also
[
46
]
) that in this case, under the hypotheses of Theorem
8
with
\zeta=0
,

\|f^{\dagger}-q_{m}\|_{2}\leq C(1+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}})r_{m}^{-\sigma}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})},

which is asymptotically sharp in view of Proposition
10
. In our context, we require the possibility for
\mathcal{S}
to be an orthonormal system but not necessarily a basis.

It is unclear whether the above result from
[
45
]
can be extended to a proper subset of a basis of eigenfunctions of the Laplace–Beltrami operator. For this reason, we shall employ a slight refinement of Gröchenig’s result (Theorem

), which yields a better constant, though it generally does not achieve the optimal asymptotic decay.

4.2.
The construction of Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund families in Riemannian manifolds

As a first and remarkable example, let us suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a connected compact orientable
d
-dimensional Riemannian
manifold without boundary and with normalized Riemannian measure
d\mu
, such that

\mu\left(\mathcal{M}\right)=1
. Let us consider the distinct eigenvalues
\{0=\lambda_{0}^{2}<\lambda_{1}^{2}<\lambda_{2}^{2}<\ldots\}
of the
Laplace-Beltrami operator and let
\mathcal{B}=\left\{\varphi_{m}^{\ell}\right\}_{m=0,\ell=1}^{+\infty,\delta_{m}}
be a corresponding orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions, so that
\Delta\varphi_{m}^{\ell}=\lambda_{m}^{2}\varphi_{m}^{\ell}
. Set
\mathcal{E}=\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m=0,\ell=1}^{+\infty,\delta_{m}}
.

In this case, the space of diffusion polynomials of bandwith
r\geq 0
is

\mathcal{Q}_{r}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})=\operatorname*{span}\left\{\varphi_{m}^{\ell}:\lambda_{m}\leq r\right\}.

By Weyl’s estimates on the spectrum of an elliptic operator
[
35
, Theorem 17.5.3 and Corollary 17.5.8]
,

(16)

\dim\left(\mathcal{Q}_{r}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})\right)\sim r^{d}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{m:\lambda_{m}\leq r}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta_{m}}|\varphi_{m}^{\ell}(x)|^{2}\leq Wr^{d}

for some
W>0
.

We begin with a definition.

Definition 11
.

Let
0<a\leq 1\leq b
and
0<c_{1}<c_{2}
. We say that a collection
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of measurable subsets of
\mathcal{M}
is a partition of
\mathcal{M}
with parameters
a
,
b
,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}

if the following conditions hold:

•

\cup_{j=1}^{N}R_{j}=\mathcal{M}
and
\mu(R_{i}\cap R_{j})=0
for every
1\leq i<j\leq N
;

•

a/N\leq\mu(R_{j})\leq b/N
for every
j=1,\cdots N
;

•

and each
R_{j}
is contained in a geodesic ball of radius
c_{2}N^{-1/d}
and contains a geodesic ball of radius
c_{1}N^{-1/d}
.

Maggioni, Mhaskar and Filbir
[
21
,
22
,
47
]
have proven that any choice of
N
points, each one in a region
R_{j}
as above, produces a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family with weights equal to the measures of the regions.

Theorem 12

(
[
22
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

Let
0<a\leq 1\leq b
and
0<c_{1}<c_{2}
.
Then, there exist positive constants
C
and
D
such that for all
N\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
N\geq Cr^{d}
, for all partitions
\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with parameters
a
,
b
,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
as in Definition
11
, for all
x_{j}\in R_{j}
, for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
it holds

\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|q(x)\right|^{2}d\mu(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu(R_{j})\left|q(x_{j})\right|^{2}\right|\leq DrN^{-1/d}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|q(x)\right|^{2}d\mu(x).

In particular, for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
N\geq\max\{C,D^{d}/\varepsilon^{d}\}r^{d}
, then

\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|q(x)\right|^{2}d\mu(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu(R_{j})\left|q(x_{j})\right|^{2}\right|\leq\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|q(x)\right|^{2}d\mu(x),

that is

(1-\varepsilon)\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|q(x)\right|^{2}d\mu(x)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu(R_{j})\left|q(x_{j})\right|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|q(x)\right|^{2}d\mu(x).

It remains to show that partitions as required by the previous theorem do exist. Indeed the following theorem holds (see
[
19
,
26
,
27
]
).

Theorem 13
.

There exist two constants
0<c_{3}<c_{4}
such that for all constants
a
and
b
with
0<a\leq 1\leq b
, for every
N\geq 1
and for every choice of weights
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{j}=1
and
a/N\leq\tau_{j}\leq b/N
,
there is a partition of
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, with parameters
a
,
b
,
c_{3}(a^{2}/b)^{1/d}
and
c_{4}b^{1/d}
such that
\mu(R_{j})=\tau_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
.

Sampling in inverse problems

In this section, we apply the previous results to inverse problems. More precisely, we consider the problem consisting of the recovery of a signal

from sampled measurements of
Ff^{\dagger}
, where

We make the following assumptions. The first assumption concerns the measurement space

for the inverse problem, which will be a Sobolev space on
\mathcal{M}
.

Assumption 14
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a topological space and

\mathcal{M}

, and

.
Let
\mathcal{E}=\{\lambda_{k}:\lambda_{k}\geq 0\}

.
Assume that there exists a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family

for

and
\mathcal{E}
, with nodes
\{x_{m,j}:m\in\mathbb{N},\,j=1,\ldots,L_{m}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
,

\{\tau_{m,j}\}
and degrees
\{r_{m}\}
, such that

A\|q\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|q(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{n,j}\leq B\|q\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E}),

with constants

independent of

.
Let

, for some
\sigma\geq 0
.

In the second assumption, we fix a Galerkin nested sequence of subspaces of

, the space where the unknown

belongs to.

Assumption 15
.

Let

be a Banach space and
assume that

admits a multi-resolution structure, that is a family

of subspaces of

such that

(i)

(ii)

,

(iii)

The last assumption quantifies the smoothing effect of

.

Assumption 16
.

Let

be linear and bounded, and assume that

(i)

F(G_{m})\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{r_{m}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
,

(ii)

,

(iii)

\dim\operatorname{Im}F=+\infty
.

We now build a new orthonormal system
\mathcal{S}
of

and a new non-decreasing sequence
\mathcal{L}=\{\Lambda_{k}:\Lambda_{k}\geq 0\}
with
\Lambda_{k}\to+\infty
, adapted to the operator

. Let us begin with
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\{\psi_{k}:0\leq k\leq d_{0}\}
, an orthonormal basis for
F(G_{0})
, with
\psi_{0}\equiv 1
. Being

, for each
k
between
0
and
d_{0}
we set

.
Let us now complete (if necessary)

to an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{\psi_{k}:0\leq k\leq d_{1}\}
for
F(G_{1})
. Being

, for each

between
d_{0}+1
and

(if any) we set

.
Proceeding similarly, for any

to an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{S}_{m+1}=\{\psi_{k}:0\leq k\leq d_{m+1}\}

.
Being

for each

between
d_{m}+1
and

(if any) we set

.
We call

.

In the following result, we investigate the recovery of

from a finite number of noisy sampled measurements.

Corollary 17
.

Let us suppose that Assumptions
14
,
15
,
16
hold and
let

. Let

be such that

is continuous and let

Convolutions on compact two-point homogeneous spaces

Here, we apply the results of Section

6.1.
Two-point homogeneous spaces

A
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
with distance
\rho
is
said to be a two-point homogeneus space if given four points
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\rho(x_{1},y_{1})=\rho(x_{2},y_{2})
, then there exists an isometry
g
of
\mathcal{M}
such that

gx_{1}=x_{2}
and
gy_{1}=y_{2}
. Wang in
[
70
]
has completely
characterized compact connected two-point homogeneous spaces. More precisely

\mathcal{M}
is isometric to one of the following compact rank

1
symmetric spaces:

i)

the Euclidean sphere
S^{d}=SO(d+1)/SO(d)\times\{1\}
,

d\geqslant 1
;

ii)

the real projective space
P^{n}(\mathbb{R})=O(n+1)/O(n)\times O(1)
,
n\geqslant 2
;

iii)

the complex projective space
P^{n}(\mathbb{C})=U(n+1)/U(n)\times U(1)
,
n\geqslant 2
;

iv)

the quaternionic projective space
P^{n}(\mathbb{H})=Sp(n+1)/Sp(n)\times Sp(1)
,
n\geqslant 2
;

v)

the octonionic projective plane
P^{2}(\mathbb{O})
.

In the following we will assume that
\mathcal{M}
is one of the above
symmetric spaces and that
d
is its real dimension. In particular the real
dimension of
P^{n}(\mathbb{K})
is
d=nd_{0}
, where
d_{0}=1,2,4,8

according to the real dimension of
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
,

\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{O}
respectively. In the case of
S^{d}
it will be
convenient to set
d_{0}=d
. See
[
25
, pp. 176-178]
, see also

[
31
]
,
[
63
]
and
[
72
]
. In the following, to keep the notation simple, we will use

(17)

a=\frac{d-2}{2},\ \quad\ b=\frac{d_{0}-2}{2}.

Let
\mu
be the Riemannian measure on
\mathcal{M}
normalized so that

\mu(\mathcal{M})=1
. Let us assume that
\rho
is normalized so that
\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})=\pi
and let
B_{r}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{M}:\rho(x,y)<r\}
.

If
o
is a fixed point in
\mathcal{M}
, then
\mathcal{M}
can be identified
with the homogeneous space
G/K
, where
G
is the group of isometries of

\mathcal{M}
and
K
is the stabilizer of
o
. We will also identify
functions
f(x)
on
\mathcal{M}
with right
K
-invariant functions
\widetilde{f}(g)
on

G
by setting
\widetilde{f}(g)=f(x)
when
go=x
. The measure
\mu
is invariant under the action of
G
;
in other words, for every
g\in G
,

\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(gx)d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)d\mu(x).

Let
dg
and
dk
denote left Haar measures on
G
and
K
, respectively. These measures are normalized in such a way that
\int_{G}dg=1
and
\int_{K}dk=1
. By
[
23
, Theorem 2.51]
, we have

(18)

\int_{G}f(go)\,dg=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)d\mu(x),\qquad f\in L^{1}(\mathcal{M}).

6.2.
Zonal functions and convolutions

Definition 18
.

A function
f
on
\mathcal{M}
is a zonal function if for every

x\in\mathcal{M}
and every
k\in K
we have
f(kx)=f(x)
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
f
be a zonal function. Then
f(x)
depends only on

\rho(x,o)
. Furthermore, defining
f_{0}
so that
f(x)=f_{0}(\rho(x,o))
we have

(19)

\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)d\mu(x)=\int_{0}^{\pi}f_{0}(r)A(r)dr,

where

A(r)=c(a,b)\left(\sin\Big(\frac{r}{2}\Big)\right)^{2a+1}\left(\cos\Big(\frac{r}{2}\Big)\right)^{2b+1}

and

c(a,b)=\left(\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(\sin\Big(\frac{r}{2}\Big)\right)^{2a+1}\left(\cos\Big(\frac{r}{2}\Big)\right)^{2b+1}dr\right)^{-1}=\frac{\Gamma(a+b+2)}{\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(b+1)}.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
be such that
\rho(x,o)=\rho(y,o)
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is two-point homogeneous there exists
g\in G
such that
gx=y
and
go=o
. Thus,
g\in K
and
f(y)=f(gx)=f(x)
. Equation (
19
) follows from (4.17) in
[
25
]
.
∎

Definition 20
.

If
f
and
h
are two integrable functions on
\mathcal{M}
, then we define the convolution of
h
and
f
by

h\ast f(x)=\int_{G}h(g^{-1}x)f(go)dg.

The following lemma shows that this convolution with filter
h
coincides with the convolution with a zonal function
\widetilde{h}
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
f,h\in L^{1}(\mathcal{M})
. Write

(20)

\widetilde{h}(x):=\int_{K}h(kx)\,dk=\widetilde{h}_{0}(\rho(x,o)).

Then
\widetilde{h}
is zonal and

h*f=\widetilde{h}*f.

Furthermore

\widetilde{h}*f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\widetilde{h}_{0}(\rho(x,y))f(y)d\mu(y).

Proof.

Take
x\in\mathcal{M}
and write
x=uo
with some
u\in G
. Recall that
dg
is also a right Haar measure on
G
. Observe that for any
k\in K
, by the change of variable
w=k^{-1}g^{-1}u
,

h\ast f(x)=\int_{G}h(g^{-1}uo)f(go)dg=\int_{G}h(kwo)f(uw^{-1}k^{-1}o)dw=\int_{G}h(kwo)f(uw^{-1}o)dw.

Integration in
k\in K
gives

h\ast f(x)=\int_{K}\int_{G}h(kwo)f(uw^{-1}o)dwdk=\int_{G}f(uw^{-1}o)\int_{K}h(kwo)dkdw.

Notice that the function

\widetilde{h}
is zonal on
\mathcal{M}
.
It follows that

h\ast f(x)=\int_{G}f(uw^{-1}o)\widetilde{h}(wo)dw=\int_{G}\widetilde{h}(g^{-1}uo)f(go)dg=\widetilde{h}\ast f(x).

In other words, the convolution depends only on
\widetilde{h}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}\ast f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\widetilde{h}(g^{-1}x)f(go)dg=\int_{G}\widetilde{h}_{0}(\rho(g^{-1}x,o))f(go)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\widetilde{h}_{0}(\rho(x,go))f(go)dg=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\widetilde{h}_{0}(\rho(x,y))f(y)d\mu(y),

where we used (
18
) in the last identity.
∎

Let
\Delta
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
, let

It is well known that

Take

\lambda^{2}_{m}=m(m+a+b+1)

\displaystyle=(2m+a+b+1)\frac{\Gamma(b+1)}{\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(a+b+2)}\frac{\Gamma(m+a+b+1)}{\Gamma(m+b+1)}\frac{\Gamma(m+a+1)}{\Gamma(m+1)}\sim m^{2a+1}

Proof.

\displaystyle Z_{o}^{m}\ast f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}Z_{o}^{m}(g^{-1}x)f(go)dg=\int_{G}Z_{go}^{m}(x)f(go)dg

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta_{m}}\overline{Y_{m}^{\ell}(go)}Y_{m}^{\ell}(x)f(go)dg=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta_{m}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(y)\overline{Y_{m}^{\ell}(y)}d\mu(y)\right)Y_{m}^{\ell}(x)=\Pi_{m}f(x),

as claimed.

Concerning (
ix
), inequality (
24
) is trivially satisfied for
a=b=-1/2
. In the remaining cases,
a\geq b\geq-1/2
and
a\geq 0
. Furthermore, either
a+b
or
a
are nonnegative integers. If
a+b
is a nonnegative integer, then

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(m+a+b+1)}{\Gamma(m+1)}\frac{\Gamma(m+a+1)}{\Gamma(m+b+1)}

\displaystyle=(m+a+b)\ldots(m+1)(m+a)\ldots(m+b+1)

\displaystyle\leq m^{a+b}m^{a-b}(1+a+b)\ldots(2)(1+a)\ldots(1+b+1)

\displaystyle=m^{2a}\frac{\Gamma(a+b+2)\Gamma(a+2)}{\Gamma(b+2)}.

Similarly, if
a
is a nonnegative integer, then

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(m+a+b+1)}{\Gamma(m+b+1)}\frac{\Gamma(m+a+1)}{\Gamma(m+1)}

\displaystyle=(m+a+b)\ldots(m+b+1)(m+a)\ldots(m+1)

\displaystyle\leq m^{a}m^{a}(1+a+b)\ldots(1+b+1)(1+a)\ldots(2)

\displaystyle=m^{2a}\frac{\Gamma(a+b+2)\Gamma(a+2)}{\Gamma(b+2)}.

Thus, in any case,

\displaystyle\delta_{m}

\displaystyle\leq(2m+a+b+1)\frac{\Gamma(b+1)}{\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(a+b+2)}m^{2a}\frac{\Gamma(a+b+2)\Gamma(a+2)}{\Gamma(b+2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a+1}{b+1}\left(2m+a+b+1\right)m^{2a}.

∎

Let
f,h
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
. By Lemma
23
(
vii
) we have the following expansion of the zonal function
\widetilde{h}
given in (

\widetilde{h}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}Z^{m}_{o},

where

\displaystyle b_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta_{m}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\widetilde{h}(y)Z_{o}^{m}(y)d\mu(y)=\frac{1}{\delta_{m}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\int_{K}h(ky)Z_{o}^{m}(y)dkd\mu(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta_{m}}\int_{K}\int_{\mathcal{M}}h(x)Z_{o}^{m}(k^{-1}x)d\mu(x)dk=\frac{1}{\delta_{m}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}h(x)Z_{o}^{m}(x)d\mu(x).

Also, by (

23
), we have

(25)

b_{m}=\frac{1}{\delta_{m}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\widetilde{h}(y)Z_{o}^{m}(y)d\mu(y)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\widetilde{h}_{0}(r)\frac{P_{m}^{a,b}(\cos r)}{P_{m}^{a,b}(1)}A(r)dr.

Furthermore, by Lemma

(

\displaystyle=\widetilde{h}\ast f=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}Z^{m}_{o}\ast f=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}\Pi_{m}f.

This means that the convolution with a function

Let us now set
\mathcal{B}=\{Y^{\ell}_{m}\}_{m=0,\ell=1}^{+\infty,\delta_{m}}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{\sqrt{m(m+a+b+1)}\}_{m=0,\ell=1}^{+\infty,\delta_{m}}

Proposition 24
.

Take
\omega,\gamma\geq 0
. Let
h\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\widetilde{h}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}Z^{m}_{o}
, with

|b_{m}|\leq c\big(1+\lambda_{m}^{2}\big)^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}}

for some
c>0
, and
b_{0}\neq 0
. Let
\sigma=\omega+\gamma
and consider the linear bounded operator
given by

. Then
F
maps continuously

into
H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
, with norm bounded by

.

Proof.

By the definition of the norm in
H^{\sigma}
(see (
12
)) and by Lemma
23
, part (
viii
), we have

\displaystyle\|Ff\|^{2}_{H^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{h}\ast f\|^{2}_{H^{\sigma}}=\left\|\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}\Pi_{m}f\right\|^{2}_{H^{\sigma}}=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}^{2}\|\Pi_{m}f\|_{2}^{2}(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq c^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\|\Pi_{m}f\|_{2}^{2}(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\sigma-\gamma}=c^{2}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\omega}}.\qed

6.3.
Sampling convolutions

The focus of the following result is the reconstruction of
Ff^{\dagger}
from some noisy sampled measurements, where
F
is a convolution operator.

Theorem 25
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
d
-dimensional compact two-point homogeneous space as described above,
d\geq 2
. Assume that the nodes
\{x_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
, the weights
\{\tau_{m,j}\}_{j=1}^{L_{m}}
and the sequence
r_{m}=\lambda_{m}
are a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family for
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{E}
with constants
1-\varepsilon
and
1+\varepsilon
, with
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
h\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
be such that
\widetilde{h}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}b_{m}Z^{m}_{o}(x)
, with
|b_{m}|\leq c(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{-\gamma/2}
for some
c>0
,
\gamma\geq 0
, and
b_{m}\neq 0
for infinitely many values of
m
, including
m=0
. Let
\omega\geq 0
,
\sigma=\omega+\gamma
and consider the linear bounded operator
F\colon H^{\omega}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})\to H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})

given by
Ff=h\ast f
. Let
\zeta\geq 0
, assume
\sigma-\zeta>d/2
and take
f^{\dagger}\in H^{\omega}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
. Let
\{y_{m,j}\}
be a
family of noisy outputs corresponding to
\{Ff^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})\}
,

|y_{m,j}-Ff^{\dagger}(x_{m,j})|\leq\beta\quad j=1,\ldots,L_{m}

where
\beta\geq 0
is the noise level. For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
let

p_{m}^{\beta}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{m}}|y_{m,j}-Fp(x_{m,j})|^{2}\tau_{m,j}.

Then

\displaystyle\|Fp_{m}^{\beta}-Ff^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|Ff^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d(1+\kappa)}{d_{0}(\sigma-\zeta-d/2)}}\frac{c}{(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{(\sigma-\zeta-d/2)/2}}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\omega}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}.

Recall that
\kappa=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}
, and

d/d_{0}=1
for the sphere, while
d/d_{0}=n
for the projective space
P^{n}(\mathbb{K})
.

The existence of a Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund family, as in the hypotheses of Theorem
25
, is guaranteed by Theorems
12
and
13
.

Proof.

We want to apply Corollary
17
. Assumption
14
is satisfied by hypothesis. In particular, notice that the space
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
coincides with
the space
G_{m}:=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{m}\mathcal{H}_{s}

and Assumption
15
follows.
Finally, since
F(G_{m})\subseteq G_{m}
,
1\in F(G_{0})
(since
G_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}=\operatorname{span}\{1\}
and
b_{0}\neq 0
), and
\dim\operatorname{Im}F=+\infty
(since
b_{m}\neq 0
for infinitely many values of
m
), then also Assumption
16
holds.

We may therefore apply Corollary
17
, with
\mathcal{S}=\{Y_{m}^{\ell}:m=0,\ldots,+\infty\text{ s.t. }b_{m}\neq 0,\ell=1,\ldots,\delta_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{L}=\{\sqrt{m(m+a+b+1)}:m=0,\ldots,+\infty\text{ s.t. }b_{m}\neq 0,\ \ell=1,\ldots,\delta_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
and we obtain

\|Ff^{\dagger}-Fp_{m}^{\beta}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\leq\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|Ff^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}.

Notice that
\|Ff^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})}=\|Ff^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}<+\infty
by Proposition
24
and that, since
\sigma>d/2+\zeta\geq d/2
, then
Ff^{\dagger}\in H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
is continuous. Also,

\displaystyle\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{n>m,\ b_{n}\neq 0}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta_{n}}|Y_{n}^{\ell}(x)|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n}^{2})^{-(\sigma-\zeta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{n>m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta_{n}}|Y_{n}^{\ell}(x)|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n}^{2})^{-(\sigma-\zeta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E}).

Since
Z_{x}^{n}(x)=\delta_{n}
by Lemma
23
(
v
), and since by (
24
)

\delta_{n}\leq\frac{a+1}{b+1}\left(2n+a+b+1\right)(1+\lambda_{n}^{2})^{a},

by Lemma
23
(
i
) and (
17
) it follows that

\displaystyle\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x}\sum_{n>m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\delta_{n}}|Y_{n}^{\ell}(x)|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n}^{2})^{-(\sigma-\zeta)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x}\sum_{n>m}Z_{x}^{n}(x)(1+\lambda_{n}^{2})^{-(\sigma-\zeta)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a+1}{b+1}\sum_{n>m}(2n+a+b+1)(1+\lambda_{n}^{2})^{a+\zeta-\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a+1}{b+1}\int_{m}^{+\infty}(2x+a+b+1)(1+x(x+a+b+1))^{a+\zeta-\sigma}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{a+1}{b+1}\int_{1+m(m+a+b+1)}^{+\infty}y^{a+\zeta-\sigma}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{a+1}{b+1}\frac{1}{\sigma-\zeta-a-1}\frac{1}{(1+m(m+a+b+1))^{\sigma-\zeta-a-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d_{0}}\frac{1}{\sigma-\zeta-d/2}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\sigma-\zeta-\frac{d}{2}}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 26
.

It is clear from the above proof that the term
\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
in the thesis of the theorem can be replaced by
\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
. Of course, when several terms of the sequence
b_{m}
vanish, then
\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
may be asymptotically smaller than
\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})
.

In the following result, we investigate the reconstruction of
f^{\dagger}
.

Corollary 27
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
25
, assume furthermore that
\zeta\geq\gamma
and that, for some constant
c_{0}>0
,

|b_{m}|\geq c_{0}(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{-\zeta/2},\text{ for all }m\geq 0.

Then

\displaystyle\|f^{\dagger}-p_{m}^{\beta}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}^{-1}\|Ff^{\dagger}-Fp_{m}^{\beta}\|_{H^{\zeta}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}^{-1}\sqrt{1+\kappa}\|Ff^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\sigma}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}\phi_{\sigma-\zeta}(\lambda_{m},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{c_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{d(1+\kappa)}{d_{0}(\sigma-\zeta-d/2)}}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{(\sigma-\zeta-d/2)/2}}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\omega}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{E})}+\sqrt{\kappa}\beta(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{2}}.

Proof.

Note that, since
b_{m}\neq 0
for every
m
, we have that
F
is injective, with inverse given by

F^{-1}f=\sum_{m}b_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{m}f.

Thus

\|F^{-1}f^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{m}|b_{m}|^{-2}\|\Pi_{m}f^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}\leq c_{0}^{-2}\sum_{m}(1+\lambda_{m}^{2})^{\zeta}\|\Pi_{m}f^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}=c_{0}^{-2}\|f^{\dagger}\|_{H^{\zeta}}^{2}.

Therefore,

\|f^{\dagger}-p_{m}^{\beta}\|_{2}\leq c_{0}^{-1}\|Ff^{\dagger}-Fp_{m}^{\beta}\|_{H^{\zeta}}.

Applying this to the estimates in Theorem
25
, the thesis follows.
∎

The following proposition provides an alternative description of

Proposition 28
.

Let

(ii)

|b_{m}|\leq\frac{\|(-\Delta)^{K}h\|_{2}}{\delta_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}(m(m+a+b+1))^{K}}\leq\frac{2^{K}\|(-\Delta)^{K}h\|_{2}}{\delta_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+m(m+a+b+1))^{K}}.
