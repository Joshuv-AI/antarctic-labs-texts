A body shape index and sarcopenia risk in middle-aged and older adults: evidence from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Haibin Shang, Zhen Deng, Youzhi Lian, Youli Ai, Guozhong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe A body shape index (ABSI) has been associated with various diseases, and existing research suggests a link with sarcopenia. However, systematic evaluations focusing on Asian populations—particularly middle-aged and older adults in China—remain insufficient, and the specific association mechanisms and predictive value have yet to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to examine this relationship utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database.MethodsA total of 1873 participants aged ≥ 45 years with complete data from the 2015 CHARLS were included. Eligibility criteria required the availability of: (1) parameters necessary for ABSI calculation, namely, waist circumference (WC), height, and weight; (2) sarcopenia assessment components (muscle mass, grip strength (GS), and physical performance); and (3) all relevant covariates. Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to AWGS2019 guidelines, defined as low muscle mass (appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM)/height2 < 7.0 kg/m2 for male, < 5.7 kg/m2 for female) in combination with either low GS (< 28 kg for male, < 18 kg for female) or impaired physical performance (gait speed < 1 m/s or chair stand time > 12 s). Three progressively adjusted weighted logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for demographic variables, including age, sex, and marital status), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables, such as physical activity, chronic diseases, and depression). Predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and a K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm was applied to determine variable importance.ResultsThe cross-sectional analysis demonstrated significant differences in ABSI between individuals with sarcopenia (n = 329) and those without (n = 1544), with mean ABSI values of 4.59 and 3.71, respectively. The prevalence of sarcopenia was approximately 17.57% in the study population. All three models consistently indicated a significant association between ABSI and sarcopenia: Model 1 (odds ratio = 5.85, 95% confidence interval: 4.73–7.33), Model 2 (odds ratio = 5.34, 95% confidence interval: 4.23–6.83), and Model 3 (odds ratio = 5.22, 95% confidence interval: 4.10–6.74). The ROC analysis revealed that the fully adjusted model yielded an area under the curve of 0.871, compared to 0.760 for the ABSI-only model. In the KNN model, ABSI ranked highest in feature importance.ConclusionsABSI is strongly associated with sarcopenia prevalence and functions as an independent indicator, suggesting its potential applicability in sarcopenia screening strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03513-3.

Introduction

Rosenberg initially introduced the concept of sarcopenia [1], a severe geriatric syndrome characterized by aging-related reductions in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and functional capacity [2]. This condition results in diminished physical performance and mobility, elevates the likelihood of falls, fractures, and disability, and is linked to hospitalization, loss of independence, and increased mortality. Furthermore, it imposes a substantial economic burden on patients and national healthcare systems [3, 4]. According to the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia report, the prevalence of sarcopenia ranges from 5.5% to 25.7%, with rates of 5.1–21.0% among males and 4.1–16.3% among females [5]. Evidence indicates that factors such as physical inactivity, inadequate nutrition, tobacco use, extreme sleep duration, and diabetes are associated with an elevated risk of sarcopenia [6]. Given its high prevalence and the multifactorial nature of its risk factors, identifying additional risk factors is of considerable importance for the prevention and management of this condition.

A body shape index (ABSI) is an anthropometric parameter standardized by waist circumference (WC) relative to height and body mass index (BMI), capable of predicting the risk of morbidity and mortality [7–9], with predictive performance markedly superior to that of WC and BMI. As a waist-girth-based anthropometric measure, ABSI is particularly suitable for identifying sarcopenic obesity. It has been demonstrated that ABSI assists in determining the risk of sarcopenia among overweight or obese individuals [10]. Furthermore, ABSI independently predicts the fat-free mass index (FFMI) in females and serves as a significant predictor of sarcopenia [11]. Given that the association between ABSI and sarcopenia risk remains inconclusive, ABSI measurement needs to be both simple and practicable. Establishing a definitive relationship between ABSI and sarcopenia would enhance predictive capacity and offer valuable reference for clinical research and therapeutic strategies in patients with sarcopenia. Despite mounting evidence linking body composition indices to sarcopenia, the predictive utility of ABSI remains underexplored in Asian populations. Regarding population specificity, the combined effect of ABSI and sarcopenia on mortality requires validation in Asian cohorts [12]. Furthermore, the clinical implications of bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) are unclear, and existing studies often fail to account for confounding factors, such as urban–rural disparities and occupational activity levels [13]. This study aims to investigate the association between ABSI and sarcopenia risk, compare its predictive performance with conventional anthropometric measures, and identify clinically actionable thresholds. By addressing these objectives, we seek to advance early sarcopenia screening paradigms in resource-limited settings.

Materials and methods

Data extraction

Data from the 2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were utilized to investigate the association between ABSI and sarcopenia risk among Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. CHARLS is a nationally representative, large-scale survey of middle-aged and older adults in China, collecting questionnaire-based information from individuals and households (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/). Participants were recruited through a standardized multistage probability sampling procedure. First, 150 county-level units were randomly selected from 28 provinces, followed by the random selection of villages or urban communities within these units. Subsequently, eligible households were systematically sampled, and all age-qualified members (≥ 45 years) were invited to participate. Written informed consent (two copies per participant) was obtained by trained interviewers during home visits before conducting face-to-face interviews and physical assessments. Ethical approval for CHARLS was granted by the Institutional Review Board at Peking University, and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Initially, 21,805 participants from the 2015 wave were included. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age < 45 years or with missing age data; (2) incomplete data on ABSI calculation parameters (WC, height, or weight) and incomplete sarcopenia assessment data (missing muscle mass, grip strength (GS), or physical performance measurements); and (3) missing data on key covariates or missing outcome data (undetermined sarcopenia status). Definition of outcome and exposure factor

This study followed the AWGS2019 guidelines and implemented a standardized protocol for the assessment of sarcopenia. Physical function was evaluated by measuring gait speed using a standard 5-m measuring tape and an electronic stopwatch, as well as by conducting chair stand tests with armless chairs and an electronic stopwatch. Muscle strength was assessed with a Yuejian TMWL-1000 GS meter [14]. ASM was determined using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 720), and both ASM and appendicular skeletal muscle mass index were calculated according to established formulas [15]. Quality control procedures included daily equipment calibration, operator training, environmental monitoring, and dual data entry verification to ensure diagnostic reliability and comparability. In this context, sarcopenia was defined as reduced muscle strength or impaired physical function. Specifically, the squared ratio of ASM to height (ASM/height2, kg/m2) was employed to quantify muscle mass [5].

Sex was coded as 1 for males and 2 for females; age was recorded in years, height in centimeters, and weight in kilograms. A deficit in muscle mass was diagnosed when ASM/height2 was less than 5.08 kg/m2 for females and less than 6.88 kg/m2 for males [16]. GS measurements were utilized to evaluate overall muscle strength, with thresholds for insufficient GS defined as less than 18 kg for females and less than 28 kg for males [5]. Physical function was further assessed using both the chair stand test and the walking speed test. Low physical function was defined as a gait speed of less than 1 m/s or completion of five chair stand repetitions in more than 12 s [5]. Therefore, participants presenting with both low muscle mass and reduced muscle strength, or with both low muscle mass and impaired physical function, were classified as having sarcopenia; all remaining individuals were categorized as non-sarcopenic.

The ABSI was introduced as a novel anthropometric index of body mass, adjusted for WC while incorporating both height and weight, to more accurately reflect the degree of central abdominal obesity [10, 17]. In the cross-sectional statistical analysis, ABSI values were categorized into four groups according to quartile calculations: Q1 (≤ 3.47), Q2 (3.47–3.78), Q3 (3.78–4.18), and Q4 (> 4.18). By integrating WC, height, and weight, ABSI minimizes the collinearity inherent in traditional anthropometric indicators. Multi-model validation demonstrated that its independent predictive performance for sarcopenia exceeded that of BMI and WC, enabling the straightforward and accurate identification of populations at early risk. The linear association between ABSI and the logit was verified using the Box–Tidwell test, confirming the effect size that directly interprets unit change.

Definition of covariates

To evaluate the influence of potential confounding factors, several key covariates were included in this study, namely, sex, age [3, 19], history of falls, hip fractures [20], balance problems, smoking status [21], engagement in agricultural work [22], physical exercise [21], healthy lifestyle, depression [23], chronic disease [24], sleep duration [25], alcohol consumption [26], self-rated health [27], life satisfaction [28], marital status [29], and type of medical insurance [30] gait speed [31], chair standing test (sec, physical function) [32], GS, appendicular skeletal muscle mass, muscle mass, height, weight, BMI [33] and WC [34]. Statistical analysis

The stats package (v 4.2.2) (https://www.R-project.org/) was employed to analyze differences in exposure factors and covariates between sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups using the t test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables (P < 0.05). The tableone package (v 0.13.2) [35] was used to generate the cross-sectional table. To further elucidate the relationship between ABSI and sarcopenia, weighted multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted with the stats package (v 4.2.2). Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, where the OR represented the ratio of the odds of the outcome occurring in the exposed group to that in the non-exposed group. These ORs were derived from a weighted multiple logistic regression model with three stepwise adjustments for confounding factors, all fitted using the R stats package. In model construction, ABSI was treated as a continuous variable and included in all three weighted multiple logistic regression models (Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3). Model 1 examined ABSI without adjustment for any covariates, enabling preliminary exploration of the degree of association between ABSI and sarcopenia in the absence of confounding factors. Model 2 was adjusted for age, marital status, and sex to evaluate whether the association changed after controlling for demographic confounders, thereby isolating their potential influence and producing results that more closely approximated the true relationship. Model 3 further incorporated fall history, hip fractures, balance problems, smoking status, engagement in agricultural work, physical exercise, healthy lifestyle, depression, chronic disease, sleep duration, alcohol consumption, self-rated health, life satisfaction, and medical insurance type into the adjustments made in Model 2. This comprehensive adjustment sought to minimize interference from all potential confounders, thereby presenting the most accurate and reliable association between ABSI and sarcopenia and providing robust evidence for the study’s conclusions. To assess the clinical value of the exposure factor identified in Model 1, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed with the R package pROC (v 1.18.0) [36] to evaluate the predictive ability of ABSI for sarcopenia. The critical value of ABSI for ROC analysis was 4.655184. Furthermore, to verify that ABSI functions as a stronger predictive factor than BMI or WC, ROC curves for BMI and WC in predicting sarcopenia were also generated. Finally, to confirm the stability of the association between ABSI and sarcopenia under the influence of different covariates, a stratified association analysis was performed based on Model 3.

Subsequently, the ROC curve for Model 3 was generated using the pROC package (v 1.0.12) [37] to evaluate the capacity of ABSI to predict sarcopenia. In cases, where model underfitting occurred, regularized logistic regression was applied to optimize model performance, after which the ROC curve was plotted for performance assessment. The KNN model was then constructed using the software package (v 6.0–93) [38]. Covariate rankings obtained through cross-validation and variable permutation importance measures in the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) model were compared with the principal logistic regression findings to ensure consistency. In addition, the predictive value of ABSI relative to other covariates in stratified analyses for the occurrence of myasthenia gravis and sarcopenia was examined. Based on Model 3, and utilizing the rms package (v 6.5–0) [39], restricted cubic splines (RCS) curves were plotted with the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles as knots to investigate the association between ABSI and sarcopenia. The selection of these knots allowed for a comprehensive depiction of variable trends across the entire numerical range, particularly capturing variations at both extremes and the midpoint of the distribution. Critical inflection points for linearity were identified through segmented regression to fit a piecewise linear relationship between ABSI and sarcopenia.

R software (v 4.2.2) was employed for all statistical analyses, and a p value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Association between ABSI and sarcopenia

The cross-sectional analysis indicated that among the 1873 participants, 329 were classified as having sarcopenia and 1544 as non-sarcopenic. Significant differences (P < 0.05) between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups were observed in ABSI, age, fall history, physical exercise, depression, sleep duration, self-rated health, marital status, life satisfaction, type of medical insurance, gait speed, chair standing test (Sec, physical function), GS, appendicular skeletal muscle mass, muscle mass, height, weight, BMI and WC (Table 1). According to the quartile grouping, the corresponding BMI grouping in the Q4 group of ABSI data is as follows: in the highest ABSI quartile, most participants fell into the underweight (n = 131) or normal weight (n = 335) categories, with only 2 cases classified as overweight and 1 case as obese (Table 2). Subsequently, three weighted multivariate logistic regression models were applied to examine the association between ABSI and sarcopenia (Table 3). Model 1 (OR 5.85, 95%CI 4.73–7.33), Model 2 (OR 5.34, 95%CI 4.23–6.83), and Model 3 (OR 5.22, 95%CI 4.10–6.74) all demonstrated a statistically significant association between ABSI and sarcopenia (P < 0.001). The strength of the association remained consistent with the progressive inclusion of covariates from Model 1 to Model 3. Stratified correlation analysis further confirmed that the relationship between ABSI and sarcopenia was robust across varying covariate conditions (OR: 5.22, 95%CI 4.10–6.74, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that ABSI functions as an independent correlation factor for sarcopenia. Strong correlation between ABSI and sarcopenia

The area under the curve (AUC) value of the ROC curve for Model 1 was 0.760, indicating good predictive capability (Fig. 3). The AUC values for BMI and WC were 0.856 and 0.875, respectively, both exceeding 0.7, thereby indicating strong predictive performance (Figs. 4, 5). The AUC value for ABSI was 0.871 in the ROC curve, suggesting high accuracy in predicting sarcopenia (Fig. 6). However, the ROC analysis revealed that the AUC value for the training set was 0.871, whereas the value for cross-validation was 0.5, indicating model overfitting. Consequently, regularized logistic regression was applied to optimize the model, resulting in an AUC value of 0.869 after regularization (Fig. 7). Construction of the KNN model identified ABSI as the most influential factor in relation to sarcopenia, followed by age (Fig. 8). Furthermore, a nonlinear association between ABSI and sarcopenia was observed. Specifically, ABSI acted as a protective factor (OR < 1, P < 0.05) when ABSI ≤ 4.7, and as a risk factor (OR > 1, P < 0.05) when ABSI > 4.7 (Fig. 9). Discussion

Studies utilizing the CHARLS database have reported a relatively high prevalence of sarcopenia among older adults in China, with rural residents being more likely to develop sarcopenia than their urban counterparts [40]. Previous research has indicated that the coexistence of elevated ABSI values and sarcopenia is associated with a markedly increased risk of mortality [41]. For instance, individuals presenting with both low GS and high ABSI have demonstrated higher mortality rates [42]. ABSI has been demonstrated to independently identify elevated cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk associated with skeletal muscle loss, irrespective of BMI [43]. The central obesity reflected by ABSI may synergistically accelerate muscle wasting through the combined effects of aging, physical inactivity, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [44, 45].

By compensating for the limitations of BMI, ABSI enables more effective risk stratification and facilitates the personalization of risk screening [46]. This study found a significant association between ABSI and sarcopenia risk in the Chinese middle-aged and elderly population, with ABSI > 4.7 identified as an independent risk factor. ROC curve analysis further validated the predictive efficacy of ABSI (AUC = 0.871), demonstrating its superiority over traditional indicators, such as BMI and WC. These findings suggest that ABSI can serve as a simple and cost-effective early screening tool, particularly valuable in resource-limited settings, to identify high-risk individuals and facilitate timely interventions for delaying sarcopenia progression.

Based on the ABSI calculation formula established by Chinese scholar Wang Hongli et al. in 2020 for assessing physical characteristics of the Chinese population [47], this study validated its clinical value in sarcopenia assessment among middle-aged and elderly populations.

ABSI has been shown to offer notable advantages over traditional indicators, such as BMI and WC, in the identification of sarcopenia. These advantages are primarily attributed to its unique formula, which comprehensively integrates three essential parameters: weight, height, and WC [48]. The innovative calculation incorporates dual corrections through: (1) the BMI2/3 term, which addresses the overestimation of muscle mass in obesity, and (2) the height1/2 component, which mitigates height-related bias inherent in isolated WC measurements [18]. These features render ABSI particularly valuable for the early detection of sarcopenic obesity associated with visceral adiposity [49] and for the evaluation of individuals with a normal BMI but central obesity [10, 50]. The ABSI cutoff identified in this study is consistent with the findings from Qiao et al. [12] and Keun et al. [51] supporting its cross-cultural applicability. Furthermore, this threshold provides a clear screening criterion for clinical practice. Although certain limitations may arise in extremely cachectic populations, the mathematical refinement of ABSI constitutes a substantial advancement over conventional anthropometric measures, enabling clinicians to achieve greater precision in muscle mass assessment and metabolic risk stratification [52–54].

The study results indicate that the mean ABSI in patients with sarcopenia (4.59) was significantly greater than that in the non-sarcopenia group (3.71). This difference remained stable after controlling for multidimensional confounding factors, including demographics, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics (adjusted OR 5.22, 95% CI 4.10–6.74 in Model 3). This finding not only confirms ABSI’s effectiveness as a strong indicator of sarcopenia risk in the Chinese middle-aged and elderly population, but also underscores its value as an independent predictor.

A systematic approach to covariate selection was employed in this study, based on established determinants of sarcopenia risk, including sex, age, history of falls, and clinically validated confounders, such as aging, adverse lifestyle factors, depression, and comorbidities [3, 21, 55]. Using multivariable regression models with sequential covariate adjustments (Model 1 to Model 3), a nonlinear association between ABSI and sarcopenia risk was identified: ABSI was associated with a protective effect at lower ranges but became a significant risk factor (P < 0.05) when exceeding the critical threshold of 4.7 (Model 3, fully adjusted). Cross-sectional analysis indicated that sarcopenia differed significantly with respect to age, physical exercise, sleep duration, and marital status. A longitudinal study reported a relatively high prevalence and mortality of sarcopenia among older adults in China [56]. These findings were consistent with these results, as more cases were observed among individuals aged over 75 years. Previous research has shown that exercise can delay the progression and reduce the rate of sarcopenia [57]. However, stratified analysis in this study revealed that physical exercise acted as a protective factor against sarcopenia. Jiang et al. used nationally representative data from CHARLS to study 7703 individuals aged over 45 years and found that sarcopenic obesity (or possible sarcopenic obesity) was positively associated with the occurrence of CVD [58]. Zhong et al. used CHARLS (2015) to study 14,258 individuals aged 40 years and above and found that depression contributed to the development of sarcopenia. Timely recognition and management of depression may represent an important strategy for preventing sarcopenia [59]. Chen et al. used CHARLS to examine 1819 participants aged over 60 years without sarcopenia and determined that BMI played a mediating role between sarcopenia and the TyG index in the Chinese older adult population [60]. Du et al. analyzed data from 2391 participants aged over 60 years in CHARLS (2015) and found that possible sarcopenia was associated with an elevated risk of cognitive decline in the Chinese population [61].

ABSI has been found to independently predict FFMI in females and serves as a significant predictor of sarcopenic obesity [62]. An analysis based on data from U.S. and Korean adults demonstrated a rapid increase in the odds of sarcopenia with higher log-transformed A-body size index cut-points [63]. In this study, the RCS curve similarly indicated that ABSI became a risk factor for sarcopenia when exceeding a threshold of 4.7. Another investigation reported that FFMI was significantly higher in both males and females with lower ABSI compared to those in the higher ABSI group, suggesting its potential utility in assessing sarcopenia risk among overweight or obese individuals [10].

Previous studies have reported a negative correlation between the ABSI and the FFMI, identifying ABSI as an independent predictor of muscle depletion syndrome [64]. This study systematically examined the role of ABSI as an independent risk factor through stepwise adjustment across three statistical models. Using ROC curve analysis and evaluation via the KNN algorithm, its predictive capacity and relative importance among covariates were further assessed. This research addresses a key gap in the existing literature regarding the independent association between ABSI and muscle depletion syndrome, particularly with respect to methodological rigor.

Methodologically, the study adopted the internationally recognized AWGS 2019 criteria to diagnose sarcopenia and incorporated a nationally representative CHARLS sample (representing Chinese adults aged 45 and above, with a mean age of 62.3 years), significantly enhancing the reliability and generalizability of the findings. In practical terms, the ABSI cutoff value (4.7) established in this study demonstrated excellent predictive performance (AUC = 0.871), showing clear advantages over traditional screening methods. Since ABSI requires only basic anthropometric measurements, its cost-effective and simple operation makes it particularly suitable for resource-limited primary healthcare settings, holding particular value in rural applications. This effectively addresses the gap in early management tools for sarcopenia across different healthcare systems. The comprehensive analytical framework applied herein not only confirms the clinical relevance of ABSI but also offers novel insights and practical tools for the early diagnosis and targeted intervention of muscle-related disorders.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design cannot establish causality between ABSI and sarcopenia. Second, the exclusion of numerous individuals with incomplete data during sample screening may affect the representativeness of the results, particularly highlighting the under-representation of rural elderly populations. Third, the applicability of ABSI in individuals with extreme body types remains unclear. Fourth, although multiple covariates were adjusted for in the analysis, potential confounding factors such as dietary nutrition were not included, which could influence the strength of the association analysis. Fifth, the BIA method used to diagnose sarcopenia may yield biased muscle mass measurements in obese populations, necessitating further calibration with imaging methods. Finally, the machine learning models employed exhibited overfitting, indicating that algorithm optimization remains a key challenge for future research. Future studies should: conduct multicenter longitudinal research to clarify causal relationships; expand sample sizes to include more population subgroups for validating the generalizability of ABSI; and develop screening tools combining ABSI with other simple indicators. These efforts will help establish the clinical utility of ABSI for early sarcopenia screening and prevention, facilitating its inclusion in international sarcopenia diagnostic consensus guidelines.

Conclusion

ABSI was found to be significantly associated with sarcopenia and identified as a risk factor, thereby providing an evidential basis for both the understanding and prevention of the condition. It is anticipated that ABSI could be applied for the early identification of populations at high risk for sarcopenia, enabling the timely implementation of preventive and therapeutic measures aimed at improving disease outcomes.
