Traumatic pneumatoceles in adulthood: A case highlighting the limitations of supine chest radiography in trauma
Authors: Aditya Devrath Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic pneumatoceles are uncommon pulmonary pseudocysts that may occur following blunt thoracic trauma and can be entirely occult on chest radiography, potentially mimicking other cavitary lung pathologies. We present the case of a 52-year-old male transferred to a tertiary trauma center following a motorcycle accident. On presentation, the patient was hypoxic with extensive right-sided chest wall injuries. Initial supine chest radiography demonstrated multiple right-sided rib fractures, patchy airspace opacities, a veiling opacity, and a suspected pneumothorax, raising concern for a large right hemopneumothorax. Subsequent computed tomography (CT) imaging clarified the injury, revealing only a small right hemopneumothorax alongside pulmonary contusions and numerous diffuse, thin-walled subcentimeter cystic cavitations throughout the right lower lobe, with a solitary left lower lobe lesion, consistent with traumatic pneumatoceles not appreciated on the radiograph. With the extent of injury clarified on CT, the patient was initially managed conservatively, later requiring intercostal catheter insertion and operative rib fixation due to worsening hemothorax and increasing oxygen requirements. Follow-up CT imaging at 4 weeks demonstrated complete resolution of the pneumatoceles. This case highlights a key radiological pitfall in the assessment of traumatic pneumatoceles on supine chest radiography, the characteristic CT appearances and the importance of accurate radiological diagnosis in guiding safe management.Keywords: Traumatic pneumatocele, Pulmonary pseudocyst, Blunt chest trauma, CT chest

Introduction

Traumatic pneumatoceles are a lesser-known complication of blunt or penetrating chest trauma, reported to occur in fewer than 3% of patients with pulmonary parenchymal injuries [1]. These pseudocysts are most frequently described in the literature in children and young adults, a distribution primarily attributed to the greater elasticity and pliability of the younger chest wall which allows a greater transmission of traumatic force to the underlying lung parenchyma [2]. The precise mechanism of formation remains debated, though a 2-step process is most frequently proposed: an initial phase of rapid lung compression causing barotrauma with parenchymal rupture, followed by elastic recoil generating sudden negative intrathoracic pressure that produces lacerations which retract into pseudo cystic cavities filled with air, blood or fluid until pressure equilibrium is reached with the surrounding tissue [3]. In penetrating trauma or airway injury, a 1-way check-valve mechanism in the smaller airways has also been proposed, permitting air entry into distal airspaces while impeding its escape, with the resulting tension forming cyst-like structures [2].

Unlike simple pulmonary contusions, traumatic pneumatoceles lack an epithelial lining, distinguishing them as true pseudocysts. Instead, they may present as a solitary lesion or as multiple diffuse cavities, often in a distribution pattern sparing the lung apices [1]. Pneumatoceles are frequently asymptomatic, though some patients develop nonspecific symptoms attributable to the underlying chest wall trauma, including chest pain, dyspnea and hemoptysis [3]. Diagnosis is primarily radiological: plain radiography has a reported sensitivity of only 24% for detecting traumatic pneumatoceles, further reduced when imaging is performed supine or when lesions are smaller than 2 cm, whereas computed tomography (CT) imaging offers a substantially higher sensitivity reported at 96%, thus remaining the diagnostic gold standard [1]. Distinguishing traumatic pneumatoceles from other post-traumatic and nontraumatic thoracic pathology is clinically important, as it directly influences management and the potential for iatrogenic complications from unnecessary intervention.

We present a case of an adult with an unusually extensive burden of traumatic pneumatoceles following blunt chest trauma, in which supine chest radiographs provided an initial assessment which may have prompted unnecessary intervention, if subsequent gold-standard CT imaging was not undertaken to define the extent of injuries.

Case report

A 52-year-old male was brought to our tertiary trauma center after primary retrieval from regional Australia, following a motorcycle accident at approximately 40 km/h in which the rider lost control on an S-bend, passed through a roadside barrier, and fell down an embankment. There was no reported loss of consciousness. Pre-hospital management included spine stabilization, analgesia, oxygen supplementation and intravenous crystalloid fluid.

On arrival, the patient was alert and oriented (Glasgow Coma Scale 15). He was tachypneic and hypoxic, with an oxygen saturation level of 80% on room air improving to 90% on 10 L of oxygen via a nonrebreather mask, in the setting of visible right-sided chest wall injuries. The patient was mildly hypotensive with a systolic blood pressure of 100 mmHg. Other observations were within normal range. Physical examination revealed diffuse tenderness and crepitus over the right chest wall, maximal at the lower ribs, with no other significant findings. Point of care ultrasound scan showed no acute intra-abdominal injury, with bilateral pleural sliding, albeit reduced on the right.

Supine chest radiograph in the trauma bay demonstrated a veiling opacity throughout the right hemithorax, a small suspected right pneumothorax, patchy right airspace opacities, and multiple displaced right-sided rib fractures (Fig. 1). No discrete cystic lesions were identified. Given the appearance of a probable large right hemopneumothorax with clinical hypoxia, intercostal catheter (ICC) insertion was initially considered prior to further imaging. However, given the patient’s hemodynamic stability, the decision was made to further define his injuries prior to any further intervention.

Supine AP chest radiographs performed on admission demonstrating a veiling opacity over the right hemithorax with loss of normal peripheral lung markings, in keeping with an underlying effusion. A right sided pneumothorax is visible with multiple displaced right sided rib fractures. No discrete cystic lesion is visible. The left lung appears clear.

CT imaging excluded intracranial, spinal and intra-abdominal injuries. Chest CT better characterized the findings suggested on initial radiography: a small right hemopneumothorax, right-sided rib fractures involving ribs 1-10 with a radiological flail segment, and diffuse ground-glass opacification with consolidation of the right upper and bilateral lower lobes in keeping with pulmonary contusions in the setting of trauma (Fig. 2). The unique finding not appreciated on the initial radiograph, was innumerable, diffuse, subcentimeter thin-walled cystic cavitations throughout the right lower lobe (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), with a single similar lesion in the posterior left lower lobe (Fig. 5). These features were consistent with multiple traumatic pneumatoceles rather than the isolated large hemopneumothorax as suggested on the initial supine radiograph.

Axial (left) and coronal (right) CT chest demonstrating a discrete, thin-walled, air-containing pneumatocele in the posterior right lower lobe (arrows), situated within a region of surrounding ground-glass opacification in keeping with pulmonary contusions. An air-fluid level is present within the lesion on the axial image, consistent with intracavitary hemorrhage.

Axial CT chest showing diffuse subcentimeter cystic cavitations (circled) throughout the right lower lobe, embedded within extensive surrounding consolidation.

Axial CT chest showing a solitary, thin-walled pneumatocele in the posterior left lower lobe (arrow)—a contralateral finding likely representing a contrecoup injury.

With the small size of the hemopneumothorax confirmed on CT, and in the absence of any feature suggesting the newly identified pneumatoceles required urgent intervention, the decision was made to manage the patient conservatively with oxygen supplementation and analgesia rather than proceed with ICC insertion. However, serial imaging over the following days showed progression of the right hemothorax with an increasing oxygen requirement, and a right-sided ICC was subsequently placed under general anesthesia to allow careful, controlled insertion given the multiple rib fractures and adjacent parenchymal pneumatoceles. The patient also underwent an open reduction and internal fixation of the displaced rib fractures.

He was discharged 11 days postadmission, symptomatically improved and off supplemental oxygen. At 4-week outpatient follow-up, the patient had returned to his baseline function with no recurrent symptoms. Repeat CT imaging showed resolution of the right pneumothorax and pneumatoceles, with a persistent but progressively reducing right pleural effusion compared to prior imaging (Fig. 6).

Follow-up axial (left) and coronal (right) CT chest at 4 weeks demonstrating substantial resolution of the previously seen right lower lobe pneumatoceles and consolidation, with persistence of small right sided pleural effusion. Green arrows highlighting area of previous large posterior right lower lobe pneumatocele, now resolved showing normal lung parenchyma.

Discussion

Clinical presentation of traumatic pneumatoceles depends on the extent of associated injuries, with the most common symptoms including chest pain, tachypnea, cough, hemoptysis and leucocytosis occurring within 12-36 hours of the initial trauma [4]. Our patient developed most of these features with the exception of hemoptysis, however, did require significant oxygen supplementation. It is important to note however, that his other traumatic injuries which included multiple displaced rib fractures, right sided hemopneumothorax and pulmonary contusions were also likely contributing to this clinical picture independent of the pneumatoceles themselves.

Diagnosis of traumatic pneumatoceles is primarily radiological. Chest radiography remains the first-line imaging modality in acute thoracic trauma but has consistently shown poor sensitivity for this specific pathology. A study by Ulutas et al. [5] showed that in 1 cohort of 52 patients with traumatic pulmonary pseudocysts, only 19.2% were identified on chest radiograph, with the remainder detected exclusively on subsequent CT imaging [5]. Sensitivity is further reduced when imaging is performed supine, or when lesions are smaller than 2 cm [1]. In our patient, the supine chest radiograph showed only a veiling opacity attributable to the accompanying hemothorax, with no discrete cavitary lesion identified. These findings illustrate both the limited sensitivity of chest radiography for subcentimeter pneumatoceles and the compounding effect of an overlying pleural collection.

On CT imaging, traumatic pneumatoceles typically appear as round or oval, thin-walled cavitary lesions surrounded by ground-glass opacification representing adjacent pulmonary contusion. Lesions may be entirely air-filled or may contain an air-fluid level when intracavitary hemorrhage has occurred [6]. In our case, the lesions were characteristically thin-walled and well circumscribed, present within dense right sided consolidation, a combination that likely contributed to them being entirely occult on the initial radiograph where the surrounding opacification obscured any discrete cavity margin. This illustrates a key radiological pitfall that in high velocity trauma cases, a pulmonary contusion or effusion can mask the presence of underlying pneumatoceles, and thus a normal or nonspecific chest radiograph should not be taken as reassurance against their presence given a compatible mechanism.

The differential diagnosis for a cystic or cavitary pulmonary lesion following trauma is broad and includes pre-existing congenital cysts, postinfectious pneumatoceles, cavitating lung carcinoma and pulmonary abscess, all of which necessitate careful review of any available prior imaging to distinguish from an acutely trauma-related lesion [7]. Unfortunately, no prior comparative imaging was available for our patient, highlighting the importance of correlating clinical history with radiological findings for accurate interpretation. Pulmonary bullae may also mimic pneumatoceles radiologically, though these are typically chronic findings associated with underlying chronic obstructive pulmonary disease rather than acute trauma and another use for comparative imaging in this case [8].

Management of traumatic pneumatoceles is primarily supportive, with a period of observation often sufficient for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic cases. Radiological resolution is variable between patients, typically occurring over weeks to months, though some lesions persist longer and require ongoing follow-up [9]. Intervention is generally reserved for cases involving respiratory compromise, rapid enlargement (tension pneumatocele), or secondary complications such as hemorrhage or infection. Rupture of a pneumatocele can result in a subsequent pneumothorax or, less commonly, hemothorax if surrounding vasculature is involved [4]. Our patient was initially managed conservatively, however, subsequent clinical deterioration and radiological progression of the hemothorax prompted escalation to an ICC insertion. Given the multiple adjacent parenchymal pneumatoceles, ICC placement required a deliberately cautious, imaging-assisted technique under general anesthesia to avoid iatrogenic injury. The inadvertent placement of a catheter into a pneumatocele rather than the pleural space carries the significant risk of inducing a bronchopleural fistula, with subsequent risks of persistent air leak, empyema and further respiratory compromise [10]. Internal fixation of the displaced rib fractures likely also contributed to clinical improvement by restoring chest wall stability, consistent with the recognized association between flail chest and delayed resolution of underlying parenchymal injury [11].

This case is notable for 2 features that are atypical to the current reported literature. Firstly, traumatic pneumatoceles predominate in children and young adults, attributed to greater chest wall compliance in this patient cohort and thus a presentation in a 52-year-old adult is comparatively uncommon and warrants recognition, as clinicians assessing older trauma patients may be less inclined to consider this diagnosis [2]. Secondly, the disease burden in this case with innumerable subcentimeter pneumatoceles throughout the right lower lobe and an additional contralateral lesion is substantially greater than the solitary or few lesions typically described in adult case reports, suggesting either an unusually severe shearing mechanism or that there is a lower threshold for multifocal cyst formation than is currently appreciated in this age group [12]. The mechanism of injury is a plausible contributing factor. Despite a relatively moderate impact speed (40 km/h), the trajectory of the collision with the patient passing through a barrier and descending an embankment suggests additional acceleration-deceleration shearing forces beyond simple direct impact.

This case study has several limitations. As a single case report, the findings described cannot be generalized, and thus causal relationships between management decisions and outcomes cannot be established. The lack of comparative imaging whilst not diagnostically required in the context of trauma, limits the ability to exclude a pre-existing congenital or infective cystic lesion as a differential diagnosis. Prehospital information was obtained largely via collateral history and retrieval documentation rather than a direct account, which may have affected the overall accuracy. Lastly, follow-up imaging was performed at 4 weeks; while this confirmed resolution of the pneumatoceles, radiological resolution timelines reported in the literature can extend to several months, and longer-term outcomes beyond this window were not captured [9].

Conclusion

This case highlights an important radiological pitfall in the assessment of blunt chest trauma. Traumatic pneumatoceles may be entirely occult on supine chest radiography, particularly when obscured by an overlying effusion or pulmonary contusion, and their absence on initial imaging should not be taken as reassurance in a patient with a compatible mechanism of injury. CT imaging remains the diagnostic gold standard, offering superior sensitivity and the ability to characterize lesion morphology and extent, both of which are critical in guiding safe subsequent intervention for associated injuries. The case illustrates that even an extensive burden of pneumatoceles can resolve completely with conservative management, provided that concurrent thoracic injuries are appropriately identified and managed.
