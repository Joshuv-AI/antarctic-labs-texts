Introduction

In the physical literature, a Brownian motion process
B
is often referred to as
free diffusion
, while the
solution of the SDE

\displaystyle\dot{X}

\displaystyle=\sigma(X)\dot{B}

(1.1)

is called a
heterogeneous diffusion
, with
\sigma
representing the diffusion coefficient.

From a mathematical perspective, this corresponds to a one-dimensional SDE with multiplicative noise.
However, from the physical point of view, equation (
1.1
)
does not completely specify the process
X
. Instead, it serves as a “pre-equation”
that must be complemented by an interpretation rule in order to define an actual stochastic process
(see
van Kampen 1981
).

The interpretation rule determines the choice of stochastic integral.

In the classical Itô framework, the SDE (
1.1
) is interpreted as the stochastic integral equation

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle:=x+\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k}\sigma(X_{t_{k}^{n}\wedge t})(B_{t_{k+1}^{n}\wedge t}-B_{t_{k}^{n}\wedge t})

(1.2)

where
\{t^{n}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes a sequence of partitions of the half-line
[0,\infty)
with
mesh size tending uniformly to zero, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{k}|t^{n}_{k+1}-t^{n}_{k}|=0
,
The limit in (
1.2
) is taken in the ucp (uniform convergence on compacts in probability) sense,
see, for example, Chapter II in
Protter 2004
.
It is the characteristic feature of the Itô stochastic integral, that the integrand
\sigma(X)

is always evaluated at the left endpoint of each partition interval.

Different interpretations of multiplicative noise can be introduced by means of an

interpretation parameter

\lambda\in[0,1]
.

The corresponding
\lambda
-stochastic integral is defined as the ucp-limit

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\circ_{\lambda}\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k}(\lambda\sigma(X_{t_{k+1}^{n}\wedge t})-(1-\lambda)\sigma(X_{t_{k}^{n}\wedge t}))(B_{t_{k+1}^{n}\wedge t}-B_{t_{k}^{n}\wedge t}),

(1.3)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\circ_{\lambda}\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\lambda[\sigma(X),B]_{t},

(1.4)

where
[\sigma(X),B]
denotes the bracket process

\displaystyle{}[\sigma(X_{t}),B]_{t}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k}(\sigma(X_{t_{k+1}^{n}\wedge t})-\sigma(X_{t_{k}^{n}\wedge t}))(B_{t_{k+1}^{n}\wedge t}-B_{t_{k}^{n}\wedge t}),

(1.5)

provided, the limit exists.
If
\sigma
is sufficiently smooth, for instance,
\sigma\in C^{2}
, the bracket process can be expressed
explicitly as a Lebesgue integral,

\displaystyle{}[\sigma(X),B]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\sigma^{\prime}(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s.

(1.6)

In this case, the interpretation parameter
\lambda
contributes
an additional
noise-induced drift
term
\lambda\sigma(X)\sigma^{\prime}(X)
,
so that the
\lambda
-interpretation of (
1.1
) coincides with the Itô SDE

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\sigma^{\prime}(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s.

(1.7)

The best-known special case is the Stratonovich interpretation with
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
; see Section V.5 of
Protter 2004
.
Another important case is
\lambda=1
, referred to in the physics literature as
the Hänggi–Klimontovich or kinetic interpretation; see
Sokolov 2010

This paper focuses on a particular heterogeneous diffusion with the irregular diffusion coefficient

\sigma(x)=|x|^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)

by
Girsanov 1962
as an example of an SDE without the uniqueness property. More recently, it was
examined in
Cherstvy et al. 2013
in the context of the Stratonovich SDE

\displaystyle\dot{X}=|X|^{\alpha}\circ\,\dot{B},

(1.8)

corresponding to the interpretation parameter
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
In that work, the authors approached the problem at a formal, physical level
of rigour and derived the probability density function for a symmetric solution.
Further, in Chapter 3.3 of
Heidernätsch 2015
and in
Kazakevičius and Ruseckas 2016

explicit densities of non-negative
solutions for general
\lambda

The Stratonovich heterogeneous diffusion (

using stochastic calculus in
Pavlyukevich and Shevchenko 2020
and
Pavlyukevich and Shevchenko 2025
for
\alpha\in(-1,1)

It was shown that the equation is underdetermined and admits infinitely

Markov solutions that spend zero time at
0

as nonlinear transformations of a skew Brownian motion.
Specifically, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and any
\theta\in[-1,1]

\displaystyle X_{t}=|(1-\alpha)Z^{\theta}_{t}|^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\sign(Z^{\theta}_{t})

For

Our analysis of the SDE (

The intuition is as follows. Consider the nonlinear, one-to-one mapping

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|x|^{1-\alpha}\operatorname{sign}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.14)

with the inverse

\displaystyle H^{-1}_{\alpha}(z)=((1-\alpha)|z|)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\operatorname{sign}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{R},

(1.15)

which appeared earlier in (

X

1.11

Z:=H_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha}(x)+\frac{\delta-1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{Z_{s}}+B_{t},\quad t\in[0,\tau_{0}),

(1.16)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha,\lambda}=\frac{1-2\alpha(1-\lambda)}{1-\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}.

For

If the initial point is negative, then the process

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}_{t}=|H_{\alpha}(x)|+\frac{\delta-1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\widetilde{Z}_{t}}+\widetilde{B}_{t},\quad t\in[0,\tau_{0}),

Since we consider solutions on the time interval

Summarizing this intuition, we see that the solution

In this paper, we focus on solutions that spend zero time at zero and are strong Markov

2
Bessel processes

In this section, we review the fundamental properties of Bessel processes,
with a focus on their infinitesimal generators and martingale characterizations.

By
C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})
we denote the space of real valued continuous bounded functions defined on
\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,+\infty]
,
i.e.,
f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})
is
bounded and continuous on
(0,+\infty)
and the limits

\displaystyle f(0+)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f(x),

(2.1)

\displaystyle f(+\infty)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{x\uparrow+\infty}f(x),

exist and are finite.
Equipped with the supremum norm, the space
C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})
is a Banach space.

A Bessel process of dimension
\delta\in(0,\infty)

is the conservative diffusion process
Z^{\delta}
with values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
whose generator is

\displaystyle L^{\delta}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{\delta-1}{2x}f^{\prime}(x)

(2.2)

on the domain

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\delta}=\{f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,\infty),\mathbb{R})\colon\ \lim_{x\downarrow 0}x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x)=0\ \text{and}\ L^{\delta}f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})\}.

(2.3)

For the Bessel
Z^{\delta}
starting at
z\in[0,\infty)
,
we will use notation
\mathrm{BES}^{\delta}(z)
.

The description of the domain of the operator
L^{\delta}

follows from the general theory of Markov processes as presented in
(
Dynkin 1965
, Chapter 16,§3)
or

(
Mandl 1968
, Chapter II)
.

Indeed, following
Revuz and Yor 2005
, p. 446, for
\delta\in(0,\infty)
, let us introduce a strictly increasing
continuous function
S_{\delta}\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
(the scale function),

\displaystyle S(x)=S_{\delta}(x)=\begin{cases}x^{2-\delta},&\delta\in(0,2),\\
2\ln x,&\delta=2,\\
-x^{2-\delta},&\delta\in(2,\infty),\end{cases}

(2.4)

and a positive continuous function
m_{\delta}\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
(the speed measure density)

\displaystyle m_{\delta}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{2-\delta}x^{\delta-1},&\delta\in(0,2),\\
x,&\delta=2\\
\frac{2}{\delta-2}x^{\delta-1},&\delta\in(2,\infty),\end{cases}

(2.5)

and let

\displaystyle M(x)=M_{\delta}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}m_{\delta}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(2.6)

Then for sufficiently smooth functions
f
, the operator (
2.2
) can be written as

\displaystyle L^{\delta}f=\frac{1}{2}D_{M}D_{S}^{+}f,

(2.7)

where
D_{M}
and
D_{S}^{+}
denote the (right-hand-side) derivatives with respect to the functions
M
and
S
, i.e.

\displaystyle D_{S}^{+}f(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{y\downarrow x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{S_{\delta}(y)-S_{\delta}(x)}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2-\delta}x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x),\quad\delta\in(0,2),\\
\frac{x}{2}f^{\prime}(x),\quad\delta=2,\\
\frac{1}{\delta-2}x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x),\quad\delta\in(2,\infty),\\
\end{cases}

(2.8)

\displaystyle D_{M}f(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{M_{\delta}(y)-M_{\delta}(x)}=\begin{cases}\frac{2-\delta}{2x^{\delta-1}}f^{\prime}(x),\quad\delta\in(0,2),\\
\frac{1}{x}f^{\prime}(x),\quad\delta=2,\\
\frac{\delta-2}{2x^{\delta-1}}f^{\prime}(x),\quad\delta\in(2,\infty).\end{cases}

The domain of
D_{M}D_{S}^{+}f
consists of functions

f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})
such that

f
is continuously differentiable with respect to
S_{\delta}
and

D_{S}f
is continuously differentiable with respect to
M
, and
D_{M}D_{S}^{+}f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})
,
which leads to the definition (
2.3
), see Section 4 in
Blei 2012
for details.

Note that for
\delta\in[2,\infty)
, the condition
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x)=0

Alternatively, we can write
L^{\delta}
as

\displaystyle L^{\delta}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{w_{\delta}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(w_{\delta}(x)f^{\prime}(x))

with

\displaystyle w_{\delta}(x)=x^{\delta-1}.

(2.10)

The operator
(L^{\delta},\mathscr{D}_{\delta})
defines the
a Feller transition function with the following density.

Proposition 2.1

(A.2 in
Göing-Jaeschke and Yor 2003

)
.

The transition probability of a Bessel process of dimension
\delta\in(0,\infty)
is

\displaystyle p^{\delta}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=t^{-1}x^{-\nu}y^{\nu+1}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2t}}I_{\nu}(xy/t),

\displaystyle p^{\delta}(t,0,y)

\displaystyle=2^{-\nu}t^{-\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu+1)^{-1}y^{2\nu+1}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2t}},\quad\nu=\frac{\delta}{2}-1,

where

The value

We now present a martingale characterization of the Bessel process, which will allow us to

By
C_{b}^{2}([0,+\infty),\mathbb{R})
we understand a set of real valued functions defined on

,

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\delta}:=\{f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}((0,+\infty),\mathbb{R})\colon f^{\prime}(0+)=0\text{ and }L^{\delta}f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathbb{R})\}.

(2.12)

Then for any
\delta\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\delta}=\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}_{\delta}.

(2.13)

Moreover, for
f\in\mathscr{D}_{\delta}
we have
f^{\prime\prime}(0+)=\frac{2L^{\delta}f(0+)}{\delta}
.

Proof.

1. Let
f\in\mathscr{D}_{\delta}
.
Let us show that
f^{\prime}(0+)=0
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0+)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
g:=L^{\delta}f
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow 0}g(x)=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{x^{\delta-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x))=g(0+)\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.14)

Hence, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
x_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\infty)

such that

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{2x^{\delta-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x))-g(0+)\Big|<\varepsilon\quad\text{ for every }0<x\leq x_{\varepsilon}.

(2.15)

Hence,

\displaystyle 2(g(0+)-\varepsilon)x^{\delta-1}<\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x))<2(g(0+)+\varepsilon)x^{\delta-1}.

(2.16)

Since
\delta\in(0,\infty)
we can integrate these inequalities on the interval

(2.19)

2. Let

3
Skew Bessel processes

In the previous section, we studied Bessel processes taking values on the positive half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

A natural question is whether a Bessel process can be extended to the negative half-line in
such a way that the resulting process is strong Markov, spends zero time at
0
, and behaves like

\pm\mathrm{BES}

away from zero. This can be achieved using
skew Bessel processes
.

Since a Bessel process cannot reach the origin from a starting point
z\in(0,\infty)
for
\delta\in[2,\infty)
,
or is gets trapped at the origin for
\delta\in(-\infty,0]
,
a non-trivial skew Bessel process can only be constructed for dimensions
\delta\in(0,2)
.

For completeness, we first consider the trivial cases.

Let
\delta\in[2,\infty)
and
\theta\in[-1,1]
.
We define the skew Bessel process
Z^{\delta,\theta}(z)
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{for }z\in(0,\infty)\quad Z^{\delta,\theta}(z)=\mathrm{BES}^{\delta}(z),

(3.1)

\displaystyle\text{for }z\in(-\infty,0)\quad Z^{\delta,\theta}(z)=-\mathrm{BES}^{\delta}(|z|).

For
z=0
, we randomize the sign of the process and set

\displaystyle\mathrm{Law}(Z^{\delta,\theta}(0))=\frac{1+\theta}{2}\mathrm{Law}(Z^{\delta}(0))+\frac{1-\theta}{2}\mathrm{Law}(-Z^{\delta}(0)).

(3.2)

Let
\delta\in(-\infty,0]
. Then for any
\theta\in[-1,1]
and any
z\in(0,\infty)
we set
Z^{\delta,\theta}(z)=Z^{\delta}(z)

for any
z\in(-\infty,0)
we set
Z^{\delta,\theta}(z)=-Z^{\delta}(|z|)
, and

Z^{\delta,\theta}(0)\equiv 0
.
These processes equal to zero for
t\in[\tau_{0},\infty)
.

In the nontrivial case
\delta\in(0,2)
, let us first consider the skewness parameter
\theta\in(-1,1)
.

We define the strictly increasing scale function

\displaystyle S_{\delta,\theta}(x)=\frac{2}{\operatorname{sign}(x)+\theta}|x|^{2-\delta}=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{1+\theta}x^{2-\delta},&x\in[0,\infty),\\
-\frac{2}{1-\theta}|x|^{2-\delta},&x\in(-\infty,0),\end{cases}

(3.3)

and the speed measure
M_{\delta,\theta}
with the
density

\displaystyle m_{\delta,\theta}(x)=\frac{1-\theta}{2-\delta}|x|^{\delta-1}\mathbb{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)+\frac{1+\theta}{2-\delta}x^{\delta-1}\mathbb{I}_{(0,\infty)}(x).

(3.4)

We say that

C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}},\mathbb{R})
is a space of real valued bounded functions that are continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and such that the limits

\displaystyle f(+\infty)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{x\uparrow+\infty}f(x),\quad f(-\infty):=\lim_{x\downarrow-\infty}f(x)

(3.5)

exist and are finite.
Consider the operator

\displaystyle L^{\delta,\theta}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}D_{M_{\delta,\theta}}D^{+}_{S_{\delta,\theta}}f(x)

(3.6)

on the domain

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\delta,\theta}=\Big\{f

\displaystyle\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}},\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\mathbb{R})\colon(1-\theta)\lim_{x\uparrow 0}|x|^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x)=(1+\theta)\lim_{x\downarrow 0}x^{\delta-1}f^{\prime}(x),\ L^{\delta}f\in C_{b}(\bar{\mathbb{R}},\mathbb{R}),\Big\}

(3.7)

It is clear that on the domain
\mathscr{D}_{\delta,\theta}
, the operator

takes the form
(
2.2
).

Then, the operator
(L^{\delta},\mathscr{D}_{\delta,\theta})
generates a
strongly continuous (Feller) semigroup. The corresponding process is the
skew Bessel process.

For
\theta=1
, we define the skew Bessel process as follows:

\displaystyle Z^{\delta,1}(z)=Z^{\delta}(z),\quad\text{for }z\in[0,\infty)

(3.8)

and for
z\in(-\infty,0)

\displaystyle Z^{\delta,1}_{t}(z)=\begin{cases}-Z^{\delta}(|z|),\quad t\in[0,\tau_{0}),\\
Z^{\delta}_{t-\tau_{0}}(0),\quad t\in[\tau_{0},\infty).\end{cases}

(3.9)

For
\theta=-1
, the construction is analogous.

It is clear, that

is a
\delta
-dimensonal Bessel process for any
\theta\in[-1,1]

Proposition 3.1

5
Bessel and skew Bessel processes as solutions of SDEs

The Bessel and skew Bessel process in Section
2
turn out to be solutions of the SDEs
with unbounded or singular drift.

This connection is well know for the Bessel process, see
(
Revuz and Yor 2005
, Chapter XI)
,
but it is a bit more subtle for the skew Bessel processes.

Two notions of a local time will be used in this section: the semi-martingale local times and a local time
with respect to a speed measure
m(\mathrm{d}a)
.

For a continuous semimartingale
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
, the field of (symmetric) local times
L(X)=(L^{a}_{t}(X))_{a\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,\infty)}

is the a.s. unique random field for which the occupation times formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\langle X\rangle_{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(a)L^{a}_{t}(X)\,\mathrm{d}a

(5.1)

holds true a.s. for each measurable non-negative function
\varphi
, see

(
Revuz and Yor 2005
, Chapter IV)
. The symmetric semimartingale local times can be calculated as the a.s. limit

\displaystyle L^{a}_{t}(X)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}_{[a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon]}(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\langle X\rangle_{s}.

(5.2)

The mapping
a\mapsto L_{t}^{a}(X)
is a.s. right-continuous. It is a.s. continuous is
X
is a martingale.

On the other hand, a real valued Markov diffusion
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
with a speed measure
m(\mathrm{d}a)
given,
a random field
\ell(X)=(\ell^{a}_{t}(X))_{a\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,\infty)}
is said to be a local
time with respect to
m
if the following holds a.s.:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(a)\ell^{a}_{t}(X)\,m(\mathrm{d}a).

(5.3)

A local time
\ell(X)
is unique up to null sets of the measure
m
.

The following results for the Bessel processes are generally known.

.

X=(H^{-1}_{\alpha}(Z^{\delta,\theta}_{t}(z)))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a weak solution of (
1.11
)
started at
x
. The semimartingale local time of
X
satisfies the following balance equation at zero:

\displaystyle(1-\theta)\lim_{a\downarrow 0}a^{-2\alpha\lambda}L^{a}_{t}(X)=(1+\theta)\lim_{a\uparrow 0}|a|^{-2\alpha\lambda}L^{a}_{t}(X).

(6.1)

Proof.

Let
\beta=(\beta_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
be a standard Brownian motion started at
\beta_{0}=S_{\delta,\theta}(z)
.
By Theorem
4.2
, the process
Z^{\delta,\theta}:=(R_{\delta,\theta}(\beta_{r_{t}}))_{t\in[0,\infty)}

is a skew Bessel process of dimension
\delta
and skewness
\theta
, where
r
is the random time change
defined in (
4.9
).
Consider the function

\displaystyle f(x):=H^{-1}_{\alpha}(R_{\delta,\theta}(x))=\Big[\Big(\frac{1-\alpha}{(1-\theta)^{\frac{1}{2-\delta}}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\mathbb{I}_{[0,\infty)}(x)-\Big(\frac{1-\alpha}{(1+\theta)^{\frac{1}{2-\delta}}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\mathbb{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)\Big]|x|^{\frac{1}{(2-\delta)(1-\alpha)}}.

(6.2)

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2-\delta)(1-\alpha)}=\frac{1}{1-2\alpha\lambda}\in(1,\infty),

(6.3)

the derivative
f^{\prime}
is absolutely continuous,
f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{AC}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
,
so that Krylov’s generalized Itô formula
yields

\displaystyle f(\beta_{t})=f(\beta_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s.

(6.4)

By the time change formula
(
Øksendal 2003
, Theorem 8.5.7)
, the process

\displaystyle B_{t}:=\int_{0}^{r_{t}}R^{\prime}_{\delta,\theta}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{s},\quad t\in[0,\infty),

(6.5)

is a Brownian motion adapted to the filtration
(\mathscr{F}_{r_{t}})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
.
Therefore by the time change formula we get the equality

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r_{t}}f^{\prime}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\beta_{r_{s}})r^{\prime}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{r_{s}}

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(\beta_{r_{s}})(R^{\prime}_{\delta,\theta}(\beta_{r_{s}}))^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}|f(\beta_{r_{s}})|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

The change of variables in the Lebesgue integral yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r_{t}}f^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{r_{s}})r^{\prime}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

(6.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{r_{s}})(R^{\prime}_{\delta,\theta}(\beta_{r_{s}}))^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda\int_{0}^{t}|f(\beta_{r_{s}})|^{2\alpha-1}\operatorname{sign}{\beta_{r_{s}}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

In other words, the processes
X_{t}=f(\beta_{r_{t}})=H^{-1}_{\alpha}(Z^{\delta,\theta}_{t})
,
t\in[0,\infty)
, and
B

defined in
(
6.5
) solve the SDE

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}|X_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\alpha\lambda\int_{0}^{t}|X_{s}|^{2\alpha-1}\operatorname{sign}(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s.

(6.8)

The semimartingale local time of
X
is obtained from the local time of
\beta
:

\displaystyle L^{X}_{t}(x)=\frac{H^{\prime}_{\alpha}(x)}{S_{\delta,\theta}^{\prime}(H_{\alpha}(x))}L^{\beta}_{r_{t}}(S_{\delta,\theta}(H_{\alpha}(x))).

(6.9)

Observing that the local time
x\mapsto L^{\beta}_{t}(x)
of
\beta
is continuous, we get the balance equation
(
6.1
)
for
L^{X}
at zero.
∎

Now we consider the cases
\theta=\pm 1
. By symmetry, it is sufficient to consider the case
\theta=1
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
be such that

\delta=\delta_{\alpha,\lambda}\in(0,\infty)
. Let
x\in[0,\infty)
and
let
Z^{\delta}
be a Bessel process started at
z=H_{\alpha}(x)\in[0,\infty)
.
Then, the process
X=(H^{-1}_{\alpha}(Z^{\delta}_{t}(z)))_{t\geq 0}
is a non-negative weak solution of (
1.11
)
started at
x
.

Proof.

1. Let
\delta\in[2,\infty)
and
Z^{\delta}
be a Bessel process of dimension
\delta
that satisfies the SDE
(
5.4
).
Note that in this case
\alpha\in[1/2,1)
, so that
H_{\alpha}^{-1}\in C^{2}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
.
Applying the Itô formula
to
H_{\alpha}^{-1}(Z^{\delta})
yields the result.

2. For
\delta\in(0,2)
we recall Theorem
4.1
. Let
\beta
be a Brownian motion started at
S_{\delta}(z)
. Then

Z^{\delta}=R_{\delta}(|\beta_{r_{t}}|)
is a Bessel process of dimension
\delta
.

Let
f(x)=H^{-1}_{\alpha}(R_{\delta}(|x|))

and consider the process
X=(f(\beta_{r(t)}))_{t\geq 0}
.
Since
f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{AC}([0,\infty))
we conclude that

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{r(t)}f^{\prime}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{r(t)}f^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

(6.10)

by the Krylov generalized Itô formula.
The process

\displaystyle B_{t}=\int_{0}^{r(t)}R^{\prime}_{\delta}(|\beta_{s}|)\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{s}

(6.11)

is a Brownian motion
adapted to the filtration
(\mathscr{F}_{r_{t}})
. Repeating the formulae (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) yields the result.
∎

The last two lemmas allow constructing weak solutions of (
1.11
)
by transforming a Bessel or a skew Bessel process with the help of a non-linear mapping
H_{\alpha}^{-1}
.
We now show that all solutions of (
1.11
)
are of that form. We first deal with just non-negative solutions and then extend it

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\delta=\delta_{\alpha,\lambda}\in(0,\infty)
,
and let
(X,B)
be a weak solution of (
1.11
) started at
x\in[0,\infty)

such that
X
is non-negative, time homogeneous strong Markov process spending zero time at
0
.
Then, the process
Z=(H_{\alpha}(X_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
is a Bessel process of dimension
\delta
started at
z=H_{\alpha}(x)
.

Proof.

Let
f\in C^{2}_{c}((0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
, and set

h(x):=f(H_{\alpha}(x))
, so that
h\in C^{2}_{c}((0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover,
h
satisfies for
x\in[0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(H_{\alpha}(x))x^{-\alpha},

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=f^{\prime\prime}(H_{\alpha}(x))x^{-2\alpha}-\alpha f^{\prime}(H_{\alpha}(x))x^{-\alpha-1}.

The Itô formula yields

\displaystyle f(Z_{t})=h(X_{t})

\displaystyle=h(x)+\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(X_{s})X_{s}^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(X_{s}^{2\alpha}h^{\prime\prime}(X_{s})+2\lambda\alpha X_{s}^{2\alpha-1}h^{\prime}(X_{s})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s

(6.12)

\displaystyle=f(z)+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})X_{s}^{-\alpha}X_{s}^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}^{2\alpha}\Big(f^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})X_{s}^{-2\alpha}-\alpha f^{\prime}(Z_{s})X_{s}^{-\alpha-1}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\alpha X_{s}^{2\alpha-1}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})X_{s}^{-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=f(z)+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})+(2\lambda-1)\alpha X_{s}^{\alpha-1}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=f(z)+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})+\frac{(2\lambda-1)\alpha}{(1-\alpha)H(X_{s})}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=f(z)+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})+\frac{(2\lambda-1)\alpha}{(1-\alpha)}\frac{f^{\prime}(Z_{s})}{2Z_{s}}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=f(z)+\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})+(\delta-1)\frac{f^{\prime}(Z_{s})}{2Z_{s}}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Therefore the process

\displaystyle M^{f}_{t}=f(Z_{t})-f(z)-\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})+\frac{\delta-1}{2Z_{s}}f^{\prime}(Z_{s})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s

(6.13)

is a continuous martingale. Since the process
Z
spends zero time at
0
,
the result follows from Theorem
2.3
.
∎

In order to obtain a similar result for two-sided weak solutions of (
1.11
) we have to
identify the scale function of the process
H_{\alpha}(X)
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
Y
be a continuous time homogeneous strong Markov process started at
y\in\mathbb{R}

such that
|Y|
is a Bessel process of dimension
\delta\in(0,2)
started at
|y|
. Then, there is
\theta\in[-1,1]

such that
S_{\delta,\theta}
is a scale function of
Y
, i.e.,
S_{\delta,\theta}(Y)
is a local martingale.

Proof.

Following the proof of
(
Pavlyukevich and Shevchenko 2020
, Proposition 7.1)
,
for
a<b

we define the first exit time

\displaystyle\tau_{(a,b)}=\inf\left\{t\in[0,\infty)\colon Y_{t}\not\in(a,b)\right\}.

(6.14)

First we show that the probability

\displaystyle p_{+}(\varepsilon)=\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}}=\varepsilon)

(6.15)

does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
Let
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=\varepsilon^{\prime})p_{+}(\varepsilon)+\mathbf{P}_{-\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=\varepsilon^{\prime})(1-p_{+}(\varepsilon)).

(6.16)

Since
|Y|
is a Bessel process of dimension
\delta\in(0,2)
,
the function
S_{\delta}(x)=x^{2-\delta}
,
x\in[0,\infty)
, is its scale function. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{-\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},0)}}=-\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(0,\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=\varepsilon^{\prime})=\frac{S_{\delta}(\varepsilon)-S_{\delta}(0)}{S_{\delta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})-S_{\delta}(0)}=\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta},

(6.17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{-\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},0)}}=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(0,\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=0)=\frac{S_{\delta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})-S_{\delta}(\varepsilon)}{S_{\delta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})-S_{\delta}(0)}=1-\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(0,\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=\varepsilon^{\prime})+\mathbf{P}_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(0,\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=0)p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})

(6.18)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}+\Big(1-\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}\Big)p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime}),

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{-\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime})}}=\varepsilon^{\prime})=\mathbf{P}_{-\varepsilon}(Y_{\tau_{(-\varepsilon^{\prime},0)}}=0)p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})=\Big(1-\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}\Big)p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime}).

(6.19)

Finally, we get that

\displaystyle p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})=\Big[\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}+\Big(1-\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}\Big)p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\Big]p_{+}(\varepsilon)+\Big[\Big(1-\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\Big)^{2-\delta}\Big)p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\Big](1-p_{+}(\varepsilon)),

(6.20)

so that

\displaystyle p_{+}(\varepsilon^{\prime})=p_{+}(\varepsilon)=:p_{+}\in[0,1].

(6.21)

Let
\theta=2p_{+}-1

and
a<0<b
. Without loss of generality, let
|a|<b
. First assume that
x=0
. Since
Y
is a strong Markov process, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(\tau_{b}<\tau_{a})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b)=p_{+}+(1-p_{+})\mathbf{P}_{-b}(Y_{\tau(a,0)}=0)\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b)

(6.22)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b)=p_{+}+(1-p_{+})\frac{S_{\delta}(|a|)-S_{\delta}(b)}{S_{\delta}(|a|)-S_{\delta}(0)}\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b),

so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b)=\frac{S_{\delta}(|a|)p_{+}}{S_{\delta}(b)p_{-}+S_{\delta}(|a|)p_{+}}=\frac{S_{\delta,\theta}(0)-S_{\delta,\theta}(a)}{S_{\delta,\theta}(b)-S_{\delta,\theta}(a)}

(6.23)

with
S_{\delta,\theta}
defined in (
3.3
).
For
x\in(0,b)
we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(\tau_{b}<\tau_{a})=\mathbf{P}_{x}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{x}(Y_{\tau(b,0)}=b)+(1-\mathbf{P}_{x}(Y_{\tau(b,0)}=b))\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b)

(6.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{\delta}(x)-S_{\delta}(0)}{S_{\delta}(b)-S_{\delta}(0)}+\frac{S_{\delta}(b)-S_{\delta}(x)}{S_{\delta}(b)-S_{\delta}(0)}\mathbf{P}_{0}(Y_{\tau(a,b)}=b),

so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(\tau_{b}<\tau_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{\delta,\theta}(x)-S_{\delta,\theta}(a)}{S_{\delta,\theta}(b)-S_{\delta,\theta}(a)}.

(6.25)

Analogously,
for
x\in(a,0)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(\tau_{b}<\tau_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{\delta,\theta}(x)-S_{\delta,\theta}(a)}{S_{\delta,\theta}(b)-S_{\delta,\theta}(a)}.

(6.26)

It is clear, that the same equalities hold true for
a=-b
,

0<a<x<b
and
a<x<b<0
.
Therefore,
S_{\delta,\theta}
is a scale function of
Y
.
∎
