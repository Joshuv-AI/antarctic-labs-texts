BloomAPR: A Bloom’s Taxonomy-based Framework for Assessing the Capabilities of LLM-Powered APR Solutions CCS: Software and its engineering Software maintenance tools CCS: Software and its engineering Empirical software validation
Yinghang Ma, Jiho Shin, Leuson Da Silva, Zhen Ming (Jack) Jiang, Song Wang, Foutse Khomh, Shin Hwei Tan

Abstract
. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have accelerated the development of AI-driven automated program repair (APR) solutions. However, these solutions are typically evaluated using static benchmarks such as Defects4J and SWE-bench, which suffer from two key limitations: (1) the risk of data contamination, potentially inflating evaluation results due to overlap with LLM training data, and (2) limited ability to assess the APR capabilities in dynamic and diverse contexts. In this paper, we introduced BloomAPR , a novel dynamic evaluation framework grounded in Bloom’s Taxonomy. Our framework offers a structured approach to assess the cognitive capabilities of LLM-powered APR solutions across progressively complex reasoning levels. Using Defects4J as a case study, we evaluated two state-of-the-art LLM-powered APR solutions, ChatRepair and CigaR, under three different LLMs: GPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama-3.1, and StarCoder-2. Our findings show that while these solutions exhibit basic reasoning skills and effectively memorize bug-fixing patterns (fixing up to 81.57% of bugs at the Remember layer), their performance increases with synthetically generated bugs (up to 60.66% increase at the Understand layer). However, they perform worse on minor syntactic changes (fixing up to 43.32% at the Apply layer), and they struggle to repair similar bugs when injected into real-world projects (solving only 13.46% to 41.34% bugs at the Analyze layer). These results underscore the urgent need for evolving benchmarks and provide a foundation for more trustworthy evaluation of LLM-powered software engineering solutions.

The contributions of this paper are:

•

This paper introduces

Our framework not only mitigates data-contamination and robustness issues common to static benchmarks, but also addresses consistency limitations and faithfulness concerns in current dynamic benchmarks. To our knowledge, this is the first work to transform existing static benchmarks into structured dynamic evaluations grounded in Bloom’s Taxonomy.

•

While we reuse existing perturbation and memorization checks where applicable (e.g., at the

•

Prior empirical studies on the impact of data contamination in software engineering tasks (e.g.,

2.
Background and Related Work

In this section, we discuss the background and existing research related to our work.

2.1.
Automated Program Repair (APR)

APR solutions automatically identify and fix software bugs without human intervention.
Traditional APR solutions often rely on generate-and-validate approaches, where candidate patches are generated and tested against a suite of test cases to ensure correctness
(
Liu et al. 2019
;
Wen et al. 2018
;
Yuan and Banzhaf 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2018
)
.
Learning-based APR techniques utilize various information sources (e.g., historical bug-fix data) to predict potential patches, thereby improving the efficiency and accuracy of repairs
(
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. [n. d.]
;
Ye et al. 2022b
;
Ye et al. 2022a
)
.
Large Language Model (LLM)-based APR solutions utilize LLMs’ advanced capabilities to identify and fix bugs in software code automatically.
Unlike traditional generate-and-validate approaches, LLM-powered APR solutions leverage the extensive pre-trained knowledge of LLMs to generate potential patches directly from the context of the entire program
(
Bouzenia et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Ahmed and Devanbu 2023
;
Li et al. [n. d.]
)
. Consistent with prior works
(
Xia et al. 2023
;
Xia and Zhang 2024
;
Hidvégi et al. 2024
;
Xia and Zhang 2022
)
, we evaluated the effectiveness of APR agents using well-known metrics like Exact Match (EM), SYntactic Equivalence (SYE), and Plausible Patches (PP).

2.2.
Data Contamination

Data contamination occurs when information from the test set is unintentionally included in the training set, leading to inflated performance metrics and compromised model evaluation.
In the context of LLMs, data contamination can significantly affect the evaluation fairness, making it difficult to assess the model’s true capabilities accurately.
Prior studies
(
Renzullo et al. 2025
;
Just et al. 2014b
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Silva and Monperrus 2024
)
have witnessed such influence and proposed various ways to mitigate the problem.
To avoid data contamination, Jiang et. al. constructed HumanEval-Java
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
by manually converting HumanEval Python programs to Java and injecting bugs into the correct Java programs.
Meanwhile, RepairBench
(
Silva and Monperrus 2024
)
, which includes GitBug-Java
(
Silva et al. [n. d.]
)
, featuring recent bugs from 2023, beyond the training cut-off date of most LLMs—and
Defects4J
, is proposed as a framework for the standardized evaluation of frontier APR solutions.
However, due to their static nature, i.e., having a fixed set of evaluation tasks, the problems will persist as models evolve and re-train over time
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Roberts et al. 2023
;
Deng et al. 2024
)
.

To assess the impact of data contamination and the ability of APRs to generalize, we not only reused some existing approaches but also introduced novel evaluation techniques. In particular, we reused existing membership inference techniques
(
Zhang 2025
;
Carlini et al. 2021
)
at the
Remember
layer to examine the overlap of the training dataset and the target benchmark. However, this result alone does not reveal the impact of memorization on APR effectiveness. Therefore, we re-ran and re-evaluated APR agents on the original benchmark and evaluated the results to assess their impact. The name perturbation technique at the
Apply
layer is similar to
(
Zhang et al. [n. d.]
)
. However, as we want to assess the effectiveness of APR under semantic variations, we evaluated the impact of two perturbation techniques: rephrasing-based renaming and hash-based renaming. The controlled bug injections and synthetic bug generation across diverse project contexts at the
Understand
and
Analyze
layers are novel and are intended to assess the APR performance under logical and context variations, respectively.

2.3.
Dynamic Benchmarking

Dynamic benchmarking has emerged as a promising approach for evaluating LLMs, mitigating the limitations of static benchmarks that are prone to data contamination and fail to keep pace with rapidly advancing generation capabilities
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Castillo-Bolado et al. 2024
;
Kurtic et al. 2024
)
.
Key features of dynamic benchmarking include: (1) adaptive evaluation, tailoring more complex tasks as models advance over time
(
Zhu et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
; (2) mitigation of data contamination by generating novel, out-of-distribution scenarios
(
Chen et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
; and (3) fine-grained capability assessment that dissects specific cognitive abilities such as reasoning and problem-solving
(
Zhu et al. 2024b
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
.
For instance, DyVal
(
Zhu et al. 2024a
)
utilizes directed acyclic graphs (DAG) to create structured reasoning tasks with controllable complexity, while DyVal2 employs meta-probing agents inspired by psychometric theories to transform existing problems into new challenges targeting specific cognitive skills.
Similarly, DARG
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
constructs and perturbs reasoning graphs to generate diverse test samples, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of LLMs’ generalization and robustness.

While dynamic benchmarking has gained traction in evaluating LLMs, it remains underexplored in APR tasks.
Recent studies have introduced dynamic benchmarking frameworks in ASE that apply semantic-preserving mutations to code benchmarks, creating syntactically new yet semantically identical programs
(
Chen et al. 2025
;
Guan et al. 2025
;
Orvalho and Kwiatkowska 2025
)
.
These approaches aim to mitigate data contamination and better assess the robustness of code understanding and reasoning models.
However, the integration of such dynamic benchmarking methodologies for the APR task remains unexplored.
To address these challenges, we propose the
BloomAPR
framework, which incorporates dynamic benchmarking to robustly and reliably evaluate LLMs for the APR task by adapting to their evolving capabilities at various cognitive layers and mitigating data contamination.

3.
Our Evaluation Framework

In this section, we describe our evaluation framework,
BloomAPR
, which systemically assesses the capabilities of LLM-powered APR solutions. As illustrated in Figure
1
, our framework is conceptually divided into six layers,
corresponding to the Bloom’s Taxonomy:
Remember
,
Understand
,
Apply
,
Analyze
,
Evaluate
, and
Create
.

Our framework leverages high-quality data from publicly available static bug-fixing benchmarks to facilitate cross-comparison and performance tuning. Depending on the objectives of each layer, variations of each bug instance from the adopted benchmarks are generated using techniques such as program transformation, data synthesis, and bug injection. Bloom’s taxonomy is hierarchical, meaning each layer builds on and assumes the competencies of those below it. To demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of
BloomAPR
, we developed our research prototype based on version
2.1.0
of the
Defects4J
benchmark. In our design, the
Remember
layer evaluates whether SUEs can retrieve or recognize known fixes or patterns from a bug-fixing benchmark. For each bug instance in the original
Defects4J
, the subsequent layers deliberately increase difficulty and novelty in a controlled manner:

•

Logical variations
: the
Understand
layer tests whether SUEs can grasp the underlying bug logic through simple synthetically LLM-generated bug instances that preserve bug semantics while avoiding lexical overlap.

•

Lexical variations
: the
Analyze
layer probes brittleness by introducing surface-level synthetic variations via behavior-preserving transformations (e.g., variable or function renaming).

•

Context variations
: the
Apply
layer further increases complexity by placing bugs into diverse real-world project contexts with external dependencies.

In this initial prototype, we did not address the top two advanced reasoning layers:
Evaluate
and
Create
. The
Evaluate
layer focuses on patch assessment and selection, which is often treated as a related but distinct research problem (e.g.,
(
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Le-Cong et al. 2023
;
Le et al. 2019
)
). The
Create
layer targets the generation of new bug types and fixes, a capability that current APR tools cannot yet achieve.
The remainder of this section explains each layer in detail.
To aid explanation, we use a running example to illustrate how an existing bug instance from
Defects4J
, is transformed across different layers.

3.1.
Remember

Objective
: The goal of the evaluation at the
Remember
layer is to check whether the LLM-powered APR solutions under evaluation (SUEs) can recall or recognize facts about the target bug-fixing benchmark (
Defects4J
).

Approach
: As existing studies show that LLMs may memorize bug benchmarks
(
Ramos et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. [n. d.]
)
, it is very likely that many existing bug benchmarks have already been included as part of the training datasets.
Hence, at the
Remember
layer of
BloomAPR
, we only included 217 bug instances (single-function-level bugs), which are added only from version 2.1.0 of the
Defects4J
, and intentionally removed bugs from the previous version (v1.0.0). The APR solution under evaluation (SUE) will be executed against these 217 bug instances
to check whether these solutions can retrieve known bug fixes or recognize bug-fixing patterns as a result of their training process.

Evaluation Schemes
: These three metrics will be tracked:

•

% bugs with
plausible patches (PP)
: PPs refer to bug fixes that pass all the given tests. % of bugs with PP is an optimistic measure, as some of the plausible patches may fail on additional test cases that are not included. Hence, it provides an upper bound in terms of how well the evaluated solutions can recognize known bug-fixing patterns.

•

% bugs with
exact matches (EM)
patches:
EMs refer to bug fixes that exactly match the provided oracle. While there may be multiple valid ways to fix a bug, a high percentage of EMs is a strong indicator of the SUEs’ effectiveness. For each PP and its corresponding oracle code snippets, we removed comments and whitespace, then compared the resulting string sequences directly. If they were equal, the patch was classified as an EM.

•

% bugs with
SYntactic Equivalence (SYE)
patches:
SYEs refer to patches that are syntactically equivalent to the oracle code but may differ in formatting or minor syntactic details. These patches do not alter the program’s behavior and are considered valid fixes due to their structural similarity. For each PP and its corresponding oracle code snippets, we removed comments and whitespace, then tokenized the resulting code snippets and replaced variable names with generic placeholders. If the resulting sequences of tokens were identical, the patch was classified as an SYE.

JacksonCore-5

_

3.2.
Understand

Objective
: The goal of the evaluation at the
Understand
layer is to assess whether SUEs can understand the underlying bug logic by breaking down textually completely different code segments and identifying the known bug patterns embedded within.

Approach
: We leveraged LLMs to first analyze each bug instance from
Defects4J
and then to generate novel synthetic bugs with corresponding test cases. These synthetic cases were created based on the LLM’s analysis of each bug (e.g., identifying similar error messages or the same root causes). This type of evaluation differs from the
Apply
layer, since the datasets here are generated entirely by LLMs rather than injected into real-world projects. As a result, the bug instances and their surrounding code context are textually distinct from both the original
Defects4J
programs and the injected real-world variants, while still capturing the same failure logic. For our prototype, we picked a different LLM, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, as our choice of LLMs to analyze the bugs and to generate synthetic datasets. We pick Claude 3.5 Sonnet due to its high quality and also to avoid potential bias and data
contamination with our case study experiments in Section
4
. We have generated two groups of synthetic datasets:

•

Synthetic Bugs with the same Root Causes
: For each bug instance in
Defects4J
, we supplied Claude with the same bug metadata, but prompted the model to identify the root causes. Then, based on the root causes of the bug, we prompted Claude 3.5 Sonnet again to generate five sets of buggy code, bug fixes, and their corresponding test cases.
The
Understand
row in Figure
2
shows an example of a synthetically generated bug with the same root cause (a.k.a., a number format parsing failure) as the
JacksonCore-5
issue.

•

Synthetic Bugs with Similar Buggy behavior
: For each bug instance in
Defects4J
, we prompted Claude to generate five sets of buggy code, bug fixes, and their corresponding test cases by feeding the model with this bug’s buggy functions, fixed code snippets, and error messages. Due to space constraints, please check our replication package for an example.

These synthetically generated bugs, along with their corresponding fixes and test cases, were manually checked and corrected to ensure validity.

Evaluation Schemes
: To systematically evaluate the effectiveness of the SUEs, we have established the following classification of the SUE behavior while fixing a particular bug instance in
Defects4J
under different versions synthetically created by Claude.

•

FIX_{0}
indicates that none of the injected bugs were fixed, suggesting the SUE cannot handle this bug type.

•

FIX_{1}
indicates that only one of the injected bugs was fixed, reflecting limited capability.

•

FIX_{+}
indicates that more than one, but not all, bugs were fixed, suggesting constrained capability.

•

FIX_{A}
indicates that all injected bugs were fixed, demonstrating high proficiency in handling this bug type.

This classification allows for a nuanced assessment of patch efficacy, facilitating a clearer understanding of SUE’s scope and reliability. To calculate the percentage of bugs resolved, we only aggregated
FIX_{+}
and
FIX_{A}
, which reflect consistent performance in fixing a particular bug type, and used this sum for comparison across layers.
We also employed McNemar’s Test
(
McNemar 1947
)
to check the statistical significance of the results. Similar to the
Remember
layer, % of bugs with PPs/SYEs/EMs will be tracked.

3.3.
Apply

Objective
: The goal of the evaluation at the
Apply
layer is to
determine whether the SUEs can understand the logic of the code and subsequently apply the right bug-fixing patterns to bugs that they have previously seen but been perturbed with lexical variations.

Approach
: Various behavior-preserving code transformations can be applied here, so that the textual context of the code is altered while keeping the runtime behavior the same. As shown in previous studies, LLM-powered SE solutions can be brittle even in response to small code perturbations
(
Wu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. [n. d.]
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
. We only applied the simple code transformation actions (variable and function renaming) here to check whether SUEs even possess the basic ability to understand the logic behind the code. Altering
variable names
aims to check whether the SUEs understand the internal logical flow of the code within a function, and altering
function names
aims to check whether the SUEs understand the function-level dependencies. These two types of renaming schemes were applied:

(1)

Rephrasing-based renaming
: Depending on the context of the code, alternative function and variable names are given. The rephrased variable/function names can be misleading or contextually different from the original names. Hence, the SUEs must understand the bug in context and reconcile potentially tricky naming cues to fix the bugs.

(2)

Hash-based renaming
: Instead of texts that can be read and parsed for meaning, renaming function/variable names with cryptic hash values forces the SUEs to comprehend the underlying logic behind the code, as these names are no longer meaningful and cannot provide any clues regarding the purpose or the concrete implementation of the buggy code. This tests whether the SUEs understand the logic of the bug only through the structure and semantics of the code.

The
Apply
row in Figure
2
shows an example of an injected bug from a real-world GitHub project
JSON-java

(
Leary et al. 2025
)
. Similar to the original
JacksonCore-5
issue, this bug also involves number parsing errors related to scientific notation and can be triggered by the same test case (“
1e0
”).

We first used the Eclipse AST package
(
Foundation 2025
)
to locate which buggy function and their internal variables should be renamed. Then, to generate names for the rephrasing-based renaming scheme, we fed the buggy functions to GPT-4o and prompted the model to provide alternative function names and all the internal variables within as outputs. To generate names under the hash-based renaming scheme, we wrote scripts to calculate the SHA-256 hashes for the target function names and their internal variable names, respectively. To ensure the uniqueness of the generated hash values, we concatenated these names along with the current timestamp before calculating their hash values.

Finally, we replaced these names in the original buggy functions with the generated alternative names by again parsing through the AST trees of these buggy functions. For the renamed functions, we also did another pass to ensure all the invocations of these functions from the feature code and the testing code in this project are updated.

For our research prototype, we selected the same 217 bug instances from
Defects4J
v2.1.0 to apply our code transformation actions. Among these cases, we managed to rename all the internal variable names of the buggy functions. Evaluation Schemes
: Similar to the previous two layers, we tracked the % of bugs with PPs/SYEs/EMs. In addition, for each bug instance, the SUE either solves it (value of 1) or fails (value of 0). Consequently, each round of evaluation can be represented as a binary vector, indicating whether each bug was solved under a particular setup (e.g., function renaming via hash values). to check whether the SUE results differ statistically between the

3.4.
Analyze

Objective
: The goal of the evaluation at the
Analyze
layer is to check whether SUEs can
analyze

new project contexts
with different build systems while adopting learned fixing strategies, dependencies, and usage scenarios.

Approach
: At the
Apply
layer, we kept the code context the same by performing the code transformation actions on buggy code in the same project as the original benchmark. Here, we intended to evaluate the SUE’s performance by checking whether it can recognize the buggy patterns and apply the corresponding fixes for bugs from completely different project contexts. This is more challenging as, in addition to understanding the structure and the logic of the corresponding code snippets, the SUEs also need to take the coding context (e.g., external dependencies, deployment environment, and usage scenarios) into consideration. To ensure the freshness of our evaluation datasets, we intended to inject new bugs, which have the same characteristics as our benchmark datasets, into real-world projects. Our steps are listed below:

•

Step 1 - Code Search
: For each bug instance in the target benchmark, we searched for open source projects that contain similar code snippets as the fixed versions from the benchmark. In other words, we wanted to first search for the correct version of the code from the real-world project and then inject bugs afterwards.
We leveraged Microsoft Copilot
(
Microsoft 2025
)
as our code search engine, as it can understand both natural language and code within a broader context. The search results contain not only code snippets with the exact textual matches (a.k.a., type-1 clones) but also functions with small variations (a.k.a., type-2 or type-3 clones). Unfortunately, Copilot hallucinates sometimes by returning phantom projects or non-existent code snippets. We wrote a script to explicitly filter them and output up to five candidate code snippets for each bug instance from the original
Defects4J
benchmark. Since some bug instances do not have any matches with the real-world projects (e.g.,
Cli-5
), and some have less than five matches (e.g., only two matches for the
Cli-11
bug), we ended up with a total of
261
code snippets from
37
real-world GitHub projects.

•

Step 2 - Bug Injection
: We used LLMs to inject bugs through few-shot prompting. For our research prototype, all of the obtained code snippets from the previous step correspond to the fixed version of the selected bug instances. We used GPT-4o to inject bugs into these code snippets by supplying the corresponding bugs and their bug fixes from the
Defects4J
bug instances.

•

Step 3 - Test Case Generation
: Similar to the previous step, for each injected bug instance, we used LLMs to generate the corresponding test cases through few-shot prompting. We then reused the existing test harness from
36
out of the
37
selected real-world GitHub projects, inserting the newly generated test cases into them. The one remaining project, BitStream
(
Wiggins 2025
)
, does not have any test cases. Therefore, we must develop its test harness before injecting the newly generated tests.

•

Step 4 - Verification and Refinement
:
The resulting buggy and bug-fixed versions were compiled and tested to ensure the validity of the bug/bug-fixing pairs and the effectiveness of the generated test cases. Whenever compilation errors or test execution issues arose (e.g., the test failing to reveal the bug or still failing on the fixed version), the error messages and the current implementations were fed back to GPT-4o for further refinement. This process was repeated until no further compilation or testing issues persisted.

Evaluation Schemes
: Same as the
Understand
layer, we tracked
FIX_{0}
,
FIX_{1}
,
FIX_{+}
, and
FIX_{A}
and used only
FIX_{+}
and
FIX_{A}
to calculate the % of bugs with PPs/EMs/SYEs. In addition, we also employed McNemar’s test to check whether the evaluation results differ statistically from the previous layers.

5.
Discussions and Result Implications

5.1.
Assessment Summary

Our case study shows that while current LLM-powered APR solutions demonstrate some reasoning ability when addressing synthetic bugs, they largely rely on memorized patterns to generate patches. SUEs often output the same fixed code without considering the specific context. For instance, both
ChatRepair_{GPT}
and
CigaR_{GPT}
repeatedly produced identical fixes for the
Compress-7
bug, even when function names were renamed in the
Apply
layer. Although both SUEs employ iterative refinement using sophisticated workflows and feedback, such as compilation and test results, these mechanisms proved ineffective, as they continued to use the original function names. This highlights two key limitations of current LLM-powered APR solutions: (1) insufficient understanding of code structure and syntax, and (2) difficulty adapting to complex, real-world project contexts. Novel techniques are needed to enhance the code comprehension and context awareness capabilities, either at the model or the agent level.

5.2.
Patch Assessment in the Context of FM-powered APR Solutions

Since ChatRepair and CigaR only generate patches that pass the pre-existing test cases, they cannot accurately assess the correctness of their own outputs. Moreover, our manual analysis at the
Understand
layer reveals that many PPs generated by these APR solutions are in fact invalid. Unfortunately, none of the existing patch assessment and selection works (e.g.,
(
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Le-Cong et al. 2023
;
Le et al. 2019
)
) have focused on patches generated from FM-based solutions, whose characteristics can differ substantially from those of rule-based or deep-learning-based APR solutions. Further research is needed in this area in order to ensure FM-powered APR solutions are safe for practical use.

5.3.
Evaluating the Capabilities of APR Solutions beyond
Defects4J
, ChatRepair, and CigaR

Our research prototype was demonstrated on the
Defects4J
benchmark, but the framework applies to other APR benchmarks as well. For instance, we executed SWE-agent
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, a popular LLM-powered agentic solution, a transformed version of SWE-bench
(
Jimenez et al. [n. d.]
)
, which is a Python-based APR benchmark. Our experimental results also showed a significant performance drop (up to 92.61%) between the
Remember
and
Understand
layers, indicating that the limitations observed in our case study generalize beyond CigaR and ChatRepair. We selected SWE-agent instead of the two studied SUEs because many current LLM-powered solutions are tightly coupled with specific benchmarks, making it difficult to adapt tools like CigaR and ChatRepair to work with SWE-bench.

5.4.
Benchmark Adaption Strategies

For each bug in the original benchmark, our framework generates multiple variations to support robust evaluation. New strategies can be continuously added to better assess LLM-powered APR capabilities. For example, the current
Understand
layer focuses on evaluating SUEs’ ability to comprehend code structure and syntax. In
BloomAPR
, only renaming schemes have been applied to
Defects4J
so far. To enrich this layer, changes to code comments can also be introduced. Our initial experiments show that adding or removing textual comments significantly impacts the SUE performance. Similarly, code refactoring or injecting code smells can also be incorporated to alter or further complicate the project context at the
Apply
and/or
Analyze
layers.

5.5.
Using Bloom’s Taxonomy to Evaluate LLM-powered Software Engineering Solutions

In this paper, we emphasize the urgent need to update static benchmarks, which make up the majority of current SE benchmarks, into dynamically evolving ones to more faithfully assess and improve the capabilities of LLM-powered SE solutions. Instead of focusing solely on eliminating overlap with LLM training data, adapting static benchmarks using Bloom’s Taxonomy offers a promising path for comprehensive evaluation. While our framework is demonstrated on the APR benchmark
Defects4J
, it is also applicable to many other SE use cases, such as test case generation (e.g., TestGenEval
(
Jain et al. 2024
)
) and vulnerability detection (e.g., Vul4J
(
Bui et al. [n. d.]
)
, Big-Vul
(
Fan et al. [n. d.]
)
).

6.
Threats to Validity

Internal Validity
:
While evaluating the capabilities of LLM-powered APR solutions, we identified two primary sources of potential confounding factors:
(1)
Data Contamination
: we established an upper-bound baseline by evaluating SUEs on the original
Defects4J
benchmark, which likely overlapped with the training datasets from the underlying LLMs due to its long-standing public availability. We then evaluated SUEs on dynamically generated bugs that are textually and contextually distinct but semantically similar to the original bugs. By comparing evaluation results across these bug datasets, we measured the potential impact of data contamination.
(2)
Bug Characteristics
: To ensure fair comparison across different
BloomAPR
layers, all dynamically generated bugs were crafted to preserve key bug characteristics (e.g., root causes, bug fixing patterns, and triggering test failures). This consistency enables reliable cross-layer analysis and interpretation of evaluation results.

External Validity
:

Choice of LLMs and Agents
: This paper presents a framework for transforming static SE benchmarks into dynamic benchmarks structured by Bloom’s Taxonomy. We demonstrated its effectiveness by evaluating two state-of-the-art LLM-powered APR agents (ChatRepair and CigaR) using our prototype,
BloomAPR
, a Bloom’s Taxonomy-based dynamic benchmark derived from the Java-based
Defects4J
dataset. There are many LLM-powered APR agents (e.g., OpenHand
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, SWE-agent
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, and AutoCodeRover
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
) introduced recently. However, these agents’ implementations are often tightly coupled with a specific benchmark (e.g., SWE-Bench)
(
Kapoor et al. 2024
)
, causing difficulties in cross-comparison with other APR agents causing difficulties in cross-comparison with other APR agents which are designed for other benchmarks or evaluation criteria. To demonstrate the generalizability of our framework, we also applied
BloomAPR
to another benchmark (SWE-Bench) using a different APR agent (SWE-agent) and observed similar results (Section
5.3
). However, even with the current number of agents and LLMs studied, it took us over three months and cost over $14,000, despite extensive pipeline optimization and parallelization. We emphasize that our goal is not exhaustive benchmarking, but to demonstrate the generalizability of
BloomAPR
.

Generalizability of Empirical Findings
: Since our evaluation focuses specifically on Java-based function-level bugs, the findings may not generalize to other programming languages (e.g., JavaScript or Haskell) or bug types (e.g., performance or security bugs). Additionally, results from this case study may not extend to non-LLM-powered APR solutions (e.g., rule-based systems) or LLM-powered agents operating in other SE tasks (e.g., code review or requirements analysis).

Construct Validity
:

Evaluation Metrics
Our study adopts PP, SYE, and EM as the primary evaluation metrics, consistent with prior works in this area
(
Liu et al. 2019
;
Wen et al. 2018
;
Yuan and Banzhaf 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. [n. d.]
;
Ye et al. 2022b
;
Ye et al. 2022a
;
Bouzenia et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Ahmed and Devanbu 2023
;
Li et al. [n. d.]

. While these metrics help estimate patch quality, they have limitations: passing test cases does not guarantee correctness, and multiple valid fixes may exist for the same bug. The goal here is to evaluate whether APR agents can comprehend code logic and apply correct bug-fixing patterns for familiar bugs.

7.
Conclusion

LLM-powered APR solutions have shown promising results in recent years, yet their evaluation still relies heavily on static benchmarks such as
Defects4J
and SWE-bench. These benchmarks suffer from critical limitations, including the risk of data contamination and a lack of contextual diversity needed to robustly assess reasoning capabilities. In this paper, we propose
BloomAPR
, a novel dynamic evaluation framework grounded in Bloom’s Taxonomy, which enables structured assessment across six levels of cognitive complexity. Applying
BloomAPR
to evaluate ChatRepair and CigaR across multiple LLMs, we found that while these solutions perform well on synthetically generated bugs, they struggle with realistic, context-rich scenarios. Their heavy reliance on memorized patterns results in substantial performance drops under minor code perturbations and project context shifts.

Our study highlights key limitations of current LLM-powered APR solutions and the benchmarks used to assess them. It calls for broader adoption of dynamic, Bloom-based evaluation methods to better understand and enhance the robustness, generalizability, and reasoning capabilities of AI-driven software engineering tools.
