Health policy and health system experiences of forcibly displaced populations: a scoping review of qualitative evidence
Authors: Sileshi Demelash Sasie, Zenebech Mamo Argaw, Melkamu Asrat Alava, Fantu Mamo Aragaw, Sisay Temesgen Dema, Lensa Fekadu, Hailemichael Wasye Misganaw, Tigist Ali Gebeyehu, Neima Zeynu, Tadele Dana Darebo

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesForcibly displaced populations face persistent barriers to accessing equitable healthcare, yet qualitative evidence on how policy and health system factors shape these experiences remains fragmented, particularly across underrepresented domains.DesignThis scoping review synthesised qualitative and mixed-methods evidence on healthcare experiences among refugees, asylum seekers and internally displaced persons.Data sourcesPubMed, Web of Science and Scopus were searched for studies published between January 2010 and April 2025, alongside grey literature from Google Scholar and institutional repositories.Eligibility criteriaEligible studies reported primary qualitative data across four domains: mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative and end-of-life care.Data extraction and synthesisData were charted using a structured framework and synthesised thematically, guided by the Policy Triangle Framework developed by Walt and Gilson, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research and Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy.ResultsOf 2522 records identified, 42 studies met inclusion criteria. Across settings, participants reported restrictive entitlement policies, documentation barriers, limited interpreter services and fragmented referral systems. Weak coordination between humanitarian and national systems and limited participation in governance constrained access. These factors resulted in delayed care, unmet needs, communication challenges and exclusion from decision-making. Findings clustered into six domains: entitlement frameworks, service integration, community-based and home-based care, workforce governance, participatory mechanisms and system alignment. Disability inclusion and palliative care were underrepresented in low- and middle-income settings.ConclusionsInequities in healthcare experiences among displaced populations are shaped by policy design, governance structures and implementation processes. Strengthening entitlement coherence, service integration and participatory governance is critical to improving equity and responsiveness across underrepresented domains.

Background

Worldwide, forced displacement, including refugees, asylum seekers and internally displaced persons (IDPs), continues to produce substantial and largely avoidable health burdens.1 2 Although these populations are shaped by different legal and institutional arrangements, they share common experiences of disruption that place their health at sustained risk. Refugees are individuals who have crossed international borders and obtained protection on the basis of a well-founded fear of persecution under the 1951 Refugee Convention and related instruments.3 4 Asylum seekers, by contrast, have sought such protection but remain awaiting a final determination, often for extended periods marked by legal uncertainty and restricted access to health and social services.3 5 6 IDPs flee comparable threats, including conflict, violence or disaster, but remain within their countries of origin, where protection and assistance depend largely on domestic systems and the non-binding Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement.4 7 8

Across these categories, displaced populations experience a disproportionate burden of disease and adversity. More than 100 million people are currently forcibly displaced worldwide as a result of conflict, persecution and disasters.911 Health risks span a well-documented triple burden of communicable diseases, non-communicable diseases and mental health conditions, frequently at levels exceeding those observed in host communities.10 12 Depression affects approximately one in four displaced persons, while reported prevalence estimates for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and anxiety disorders are consistently elevated, although highly variable across contexts, with PTSD ranging from 3% to 88% and depression from 5% to 80%.1315 These clinical vulnerabilities are compounded by structural conditions such as overcrowded living environments, inadequate sanitation and limited access to healthcare. Children and women face additional, intersecting challenges, including disrupted education, constrained maternal care and heightened exposure to gender-based risks.16 17 The COVID-19 pandemic further intensified these inequities by increasing infection risks, economic precarity and barriers to vaccination and care, while amplifying mental health stressors linked to isolation, uncertainty and fear of deportation.16 18

Despite the scale and persistence of these challenges, access to healthcare for displaced populations remains severely constrained. Legal, administrative and financial barriers intersect with language differences, stigma and discrimination to limit service use across settings.16 18 19 Many refugees and asylum seekers are unable to obtain even basic care because of documentation requirements, fear of engagement with authorities or exclusion from national health systems.16 18 Mental health services are particularly under-resourced, with utilisation remaining low despite high levels of need. Where services are available, interventions often focus narrowly on trauma and PTSD, overlooking depression, anxiety and preventive care.15 20 The rapid expansion of digital health tools during the COVID-19 pandemic introduced additional obstacles, including unequal access to technology, limited digital literacy and persistent language barriers. Misinformation, lack of trust in health systems and vaccine hesitancy further undermined public health responses.9 Taken together, these dynamics point to an urgent need for integrated, accessible and culturally responsive health services capable of addressing the full range of health needs among displaced populations.10 13 15 16

In response, a variety of policy initiatives and programmatic approaches have emerged, informed by international frameworks from WHO and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) that emphasise rights-based access to healthcare and the inclusion of refugees within national health systems.21 22 At the national level, host countries have established primary healthcare services in reception settings, implemented targeted mental health interventions and developed community-based programmes serving both camp-based and urban populations.5 23 Innovative strategies have included brief psychological interventions, community health worker models and digital platforms designed to expand access to information and care, particularly during the pandemic.5 24 In protracted displacement contexts, some countries have piloted integrated refugee–host health services, reflecting growing recognition of the need for approaches that address legal, economic and social determinants of health alongside clinical care.22

Nevertheless, important gaps remain. Many initiatives continue to be fragmented, short-term and dependent on external funding, with limited coordination across health, social and humanitarian sectors.21 25 26 Persistent barriers related to language, documentation and cost continue to restrict access, while mental health and women’s health services remain especially under-resourced.5 23 27 Opportunities for meaningful community participation in programme design are often limited, and digital health interventions frequently fail to account for the digital divide or to involve displaced populations in their development.24 Although existing research underscores the importance of integrated, sustainable and participatory approaches, such as cultural competence training, robust interpreter services and simplified enrolment processes, it remains fragmented across health domains and only weakly connected to questions of governance, institutional design and implementation.22 24 27

This study responds to these gaps by mapping and organising global qualitative literature across mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care to examine how health system experiences of forcibly displaced populations are described. These domains were selected because they remain systematically under-represented in refugee health governance, despite recurring qualitative attention to unmet needs. By bringing together evidence across these areas, the review clarifies how institutional arrangements, governance dynamics and implementation processes are discussed in the literature, and highlights patterns and gaps relevant to health equity for forcibly displaced populations.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a scoping review design to map qualitative literature relevant to policy and health system experiences of forcibly displaced populations. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies were prioritised to capture lived experiences, perceptions and contextual dynamics that are not readily accessible through quantitative approaches alone, particularly in relation to policy, governance and health system interactions.28 The review followed Joanna Briggs Institute methodology,29 informed by the framework of Arksey and O’Malley.30 Reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.31 The unit of interest in this study was the health system context within which interventions and services are described, rather than individual interventions themselves.

To organise and interpret the mapped evidence, the Policy Triangle Framework developed by Walt and Gilson32 was used alongside the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and Proctor’s taxonomy of implementation outcomes33 as analytic lenses. These frameworks supported structured categorisation of how studies described policy context, content, processes, actors and system-level considerations. Four focal domains—mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care—were selected a priori based on their persistent under-representation in refugee health policy frameworks and their relevance to long-term health system integration. This approach enabled the review to map qualitative evidence across these domains and to situate reported findings within broader discussions of governance, institutional arrangements and implementation-related considerations as described in the literature.

Information sources and search strategy

Consistent with scoping review methodology, a comprehensive, multisource search strategy was developed to map the breadth of peer-reviewed and grey literature relevant to the review objectives. The time frame (January 2010 to April 2025) was selected to capture the evolution of global refugee and health policy over the past two decades, including the growing emphasis on health system integration, equity and inclusion. This period encompasses the post-2010 phase marked by major global policy shifts, including the formal recognition and subsequent reinforcement of universal health coverage as a central global health priority, alongside increasing international commitments to addressing the health needs of refugees and migrants within broader health systems frameworks.21 It also aligns with the emergence of key global and regional policy instruments and compacts on migration and refugee protection, which have strengthened commitments to equitable access to health services and integration into national health systems.34 Peer-reviewed studies were identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus and the Web of Science Core Collection. Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary terms (eg, Medical Subject Headings) with free-text keywords across four conceptual domains: forcibly displaced populations, focal health domains, qualitative research approaches and policy or implementation-related concepts. Boolean operators and syntax were adapted to the indexing structure of each database to support broad coverage while maintaining relevance.

Grey literature was systematically explored to capture policy-oriented and practice-oriented evidence not routinely indexed in academic databases. Sources included the WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing, the World Bank Documents Portal, publications from the UNHCR, resources from the International Organisation for Migration, UNICEF reports and Médecins Sans Frontières archives. While database searches included refugees, asylum seekers and IDPs, the grey literature search focused primarily on refugee populations, reflecting the scope and structure of institutional outputs in this area. Targeted Google site searches (eg, site:unhcr.org refugee health qualitative filetype:pdf) were conducted to identify relevant reports, guidelines, frameworks and policy documents and to refine retrieval by document type and file format.

Additional supplementary searches were undertaken to enhance coverage. Google Scholar was searched in a structured manner, with an initial set of results screened to identify relevant qualitative literature. Citation tracking was conducted in both directions: backward citation tracking involved screening reference lists of included studies, while forward citation tracking used the Google Scholar ‘cited by’ function to identify subsequently published relevant work. In line with scoping review objectives, searches aimed to comprehensively map available evidence rather than to achieve exhaustive retrieval. Full search strategies, including Boolean strings, applied filters and retrieval counts for each database and repository, are provided in online supplemental table S1 to support transparency and reproducibility. The electronic database searches were conducted between April 2025 and June 2025, with the final search completed in June 2025. Grey literature searches were conducted during the same period.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote for deduplication prior to screening. Study selection was conducted in two sequential stages in accordance with scoping review methodology. First, titles and abstracts were screened against predefined eligibility criteria to identify potentially relevant studies. Second, records meeting these criteria were advanced to full-text review. Two reviewers (SDS and FMA) independently conducted both stages of screening using a structured and standardised approach.

For each study assessed at full text, inclusion or exclusion decisions were documented, together with the reason for exclusion where applicable. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. To enhance transparency, a complete record of all full-text articles assessed, including inclusion status and reasons for exclusion, was maintained and is provided in online supplemental table S2.

To promote consistency in screening decisions, a calibration exercise was undertaken prior to full screening. A random sample of ten records was independently reviewed by both reviewers at the title and abstract stage. Agreement was reached on nine of ten records, corresponding to a raw agreement rate of 90% and a Cohen’s kappa statistic of 0.82, indicating substantial inter-rater agreement.35 Following calibration, the remaining records were screened independently by the two reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and unresolved cases were adjudicated by a third reviewer (ZMA). Following calibration, the remaining records were screened independently. Any unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer (ZMA).

Data extraction

A structured data extraction framework was developed and piloted on five studies prior to finalisation (online supplemental table S3). Extraction fields included bibliographic details, country or setting, study type and design, population characteristics, focal domain, intervention characteristics (CFIR constructs), implementation outcomes (Proctor’s taxonomy), study-reported findings, limitations and author recommendations. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Data were recorded using a standardised extraction form in Microsoft Excel.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis followed a two-stage thematic approach consistent with scoping review methodology and oriented toward mapping policy-level and system-level concepts. In the first stage, inductive coding was applied to identify recurring themes related to system-level barriers, enabling conditions and governance features as described in the included studies. In the second stage, these themes were organised deductively using established analytical frameworks to support structured categorisation.

The Policy Triangle Framework developed by Walt and Gilson was used to organise how studies described policy context, content, processes and actors. The CFIR was applied as an analytic lens to categorise reported organisational-level and system-level considerations, including relationships between inner and outer settings and the adaptability of intervention approaches. Proctor’s taxonomy was used to organise how implementation-related concepts such as acceptability, feasibility, sustainability and equity were discussed in relation to policy and system contexts.

Domain-specific and cross-domain syntheses were defined a priori to organise evidence across mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care. Analytical outputs were presented as narrative summaries and structured evidence matrices to support comparison across domains and identification of recurring patterns and gaps in the literature.

Patient and public involvement

This study is a scoping review of existing published literature and did not involve direct patient or public participation in its design, conduct, analysis or interpretation. No patients or members of the public were consulted to develop research questions, outcome measures or dissemination strategies. The findings are derived solely from previously collected and published data.

Results

Study selection summary

A total of 2522 records were identified, of which 325 duplicates were removed, leaving 2197 records for screening; 2136 records were excluded at title and abstract stage, 61 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, 19 were excluded after full-text review and 42 studies were included in the final analysis (figure 1).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram of study selection process for the scoping review on health policy and health system experiences of forcibly displaced populations.

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 42 qualitative studies published between 2000 and 2025 were included in the review. The studies spanned a range of geographic regions and displacement contexts, including camp-based, urban and resettlement settings, across high-, middle- and low-income countries. Qualitative methods reported in the studies included in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, ethnographic observation and participatory approaches.

The included studies addressed four focal domains: mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care. Across these domains, studies described health system and policy contexts, service organisation and care experiences in refugee and other forcibly displaced populations. An overview of study characteristics by domain is provided in online supplemental table S4.

Multi-level governance diagnostic of policy barriers and enablers

The qualitative evidence mapped in this review described recurring system-level governance patterns across the domains of context, content, process and actors. Included studies reported governance-related issues at multiple levels of the health system, including national, subnational, facility and community settings. Across the literature, regulatory arrangements, financing structures and coordination mechanisms were commonly discussed in relation to healthcare experiences of forcibly displaced populations.

When organised using the Policy Triangle domains, studies described how governance conditions varied across system levels and care settings, with attention to how policies were formulated, implemented and experienced by different actors. These reported patterns are summarised in table 1.

Cross-domain policy analysis of refugee health

Policy architecture: cross-domain mapping using the Policy Triangle

Across the 42 qualitative studies, recurring policy-related and system-related features were described across four domains: mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care. These features were organised using the Policy Triangle to map how studies discussed policy context, content, processes and actors in relation to healthcare experiences of forcibly displaced populations.

Within this mapping, studies most frequently described issues related to policy context and processes, including entitlement arrangements, referral structures and coordination between services and sectors. Descriptions related to policy content and actors commonly focused on service design features, delivery approaches and the roles of community-based and non-state actors. The distribution of these descriptions across policy domains varied by care area and setting.

Domain-specific findings

Mental health

Thirteen studies reported consistent barriers to mental health service access for refugees, including language inaccessibility, stigma, limited cultural safety and low awareness of available services.3644 Stigma, particularly acute in community contexts, discouraged help-seeking even where services were nominally present.39 41 Social support networks including faith-based practices, community groups and peer connections acted as protective factors,36 38 40 41 43 although these were frequently undermined by trauma, poverty and family separation.36 42 45 Providers highlighted low confidence and limited consultation time to manage complex refugee mental health needs.45 Youth-specific barriers included homelessness and food insecurity, which served as structural determinants of poor mental health outcomes.40 45

Disability inclusion

Eleven studies documented compounded disadvantage among refugees with disabilities, including elevated risks of gender-based violence, poor mental health outcomes and persistent barriers to both primary and specialist care.4655 Service fragmentation where disability, health and protection systems operated in silos left gaps in early developmental screening, assistive technology provision and rehabilitation services.50 52 53 Stigma, lack of provider training and intersectional discrimination (eg, women with disabilities) further restricted participation and use of services.48 51 Interventions such as assistive technology access programmes50 and mindfulness-based caregiver support47 demonstrated promising approaches to reducing these barriers.

Women’s health

Eight studies found that refugee women face persistent barriers in maternal and perinatal care, inadequate access to family planning and underutilisation of preventive services due to communication gaps, low health literacy and cultural mismatches with predominantly male or culturally unfamiliar providers.5661 Social isolation linked to resettlement stress, disrupted family networks and limited access to information further restricted care.57 58 62

Palliative care

Seventeen studies highlighted late or absent referral to palliative care, particularly for refugees with uncertain legal status or lacking insurance coverage.6368 Reported barriers included financial constraints, ambiguous eligibility criteria, the absence of home-based and community-based service options and misalignment between biomedical models and cultural or family expectations.63 65 66 69 Informal caregivers described emotional distress, dignity concerns and lack of respite when providing end-of-life care.70 71 In camp and fragile settings, feasibility was demonstrated for integrating palliative care into primary and community health services through participatory appraisal methods and NGO-government partnerships.70 72

Discussion

This policy analysis demonstrates that structural exclusion arising from restrictive entitlements, insurance exclusions and misalignment between humanitarian and national health systems remains the central barrier to equitable refugee health. Across diverse settings, these structural constraints interact with governance gaps, service delivery limitations and chronic under-resourcing to shape access to care. Consequently, refugees face systematic barriers to essential services across mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care. These inequities are further compounded by broader social and economic conditions, including poverty, housing insecurity, food scarcity and discrimination, which are insufficiently addressed within health sector policies. Legal and financing frameworks rarely ensure sustained inclusion of refugees within national systems, reinforcing fragmented service provision and continued reliance on short-term humanitarian funding. Although these challenges are global, their manifestation varies by context, reflecting differences in system capacity, policy design and implementation conditions.

Critical service delivery gaps persist even in settings with innovative initiatives. Key enabling mechanisms mandated interpreter and cultural mediator services, integrated screening for mental health, disability and gender-based violence and adequately resourced community-based and home-based care are rarely institutionalised. Their absence sustains inequities and limits uptake. Language barriers and the lack of culturally adapted care remain widespread.7375 For mental health, insufficient interpreter provision compounds stigma and low awareness; for women’s health, the absence of female providers and tailored education limits service utilisation. In palliative care, the lack of home-based models often results in complete exclusion from end-of-life support.73 76 77

Governance gaps further weaken system responsiveness. Refugees, women and persons with disabilities are frequently excluded from decision-making, despite evidence that participatory approaches enhance adoption, relevance and equity. Refugee-led organisations are underused, diminishing accountability and trust. This exclusion is particularly problematic in disability and palliative care, where needs are less visible in policy agendas. While some mental health and women’s health programmes involve community engagement through peer navigators or health workers, such mechanisms are inconsistently supported and rarely embedded in governance structures.73 76 77

Chronic under-resourcing compounds these systemic issues. Mental healthcare is often narrowly focused on acute conditions such as PTSD, neglecting depression, anxiety and preventive services.78 79 These challenges are especially pronounced in protracted displacement settings such as Somalia and South Sudan, where health systems face severe workforce shortages and infrastructure limitations, often driven by prolonged conflict, instability and weak system capacity. In South Sudan, strained health infrastructure and limited system capacity further restrict access to essential services, particularly for displaced populations.80 In Somalia, health workforce density remains critically low (fewer than 0.11 health workers per 1000 population), substantially limiting service delivery capacity,81 while broader evidence highlights persistent shortages of skilled personnel and weak infrastructure across the system.82 In Ethiopia and Uganda, resource constraints and competing priorities often limit the integration of specialised services within primary healthcare. In Uganda, although refugees are formally integrated into the national health system, service delivery remains constrained by limited funding, workforce shortages and pressure on primary care services, restricting access to higher-level care.83 In Ethiopia, similar constraints are observed, where refugee and host health services rely heavily on primary healthcare platforms with limited availability of specialised services due to system capacity and resource limitations.84

Disability inclusion is constrained by fragmented delivery and insufficient access to assistive technologies. Refugee women face compounded barriers in maternal and reproductive health, particularly in the absence of gender-congruent providers and culturally responsive services. These deficiencies are reinforced by weak integration, fragmented referral pathways and poor coordination between government and non-government actors, as well as limited community participation in programme design.8587

Comparative evidence underscores the persistence of these challenges across diverse policy environments. In the UK, broader entitlement to care contrasts with Germany’s initial restriction to emergency services, yet in both settings, procedural, linguistic and cultural barriers reduce utilisation and increase reliance on NGOs.85 In Austria, high satisfaction with available services coexists with unmet needs due to waiting times, scheduling conflicts and language obstacles.85 Similar patterns are observed in non-European contexts. In Ethiopia and Uganda, despite policy commitments to integrate refugees into national health systems, implementation remains constrained by limited resources, workforce shortages and pressure on primary healthcare services, restricting access to more specialised care.88 In South Sudan, ongoing conflict and system fragility further limit access, with displaced populations heavily reliant on humanitarian actors for essential health services.80 In the Middle East and North Africa region, including countries such as Jordan and Lebanon, formal entitlements often coexist with financial, administrative and system capacity barriers that restrict effective access for refugees.89 These examples illustrate that these inequities are shaped by both the scope of entitlements and by operational and cultural factors that undermine accessibility.8587 The available qualitative evidence remains disproportionately concentrated in high-income settings, underscoring the need for more primary research from low- and middle-income displacement contexts where the majority of forcibly displaced populations reside.

The findings converge around a coherent set of policy-relevant domains, including interpreter and cultural mediation services, integrated screening, community-based care models, culturally responsive workforce development, participatory governance mechanisms and alignment between humanitarian and national health frameworks.73 76 77 Framed through the Policy Triangle, these domains map onto context (entitlement structures), content (service protocols), process (coordination mechanisms) and actors (refugee representation and institutional leadership). Because stigma, trust, entitlement negotiation and cultural mismatch are central to refugee health system performance, reliance on qualitative evidence enabled analysis of governance and implementation processes not visible in quantitative indicators.28 90

Taken together, this synthesis of qualitative evidence on health policy and system experiences of forcibly displaced populations shows that persistent gaps in mental health, disability inclusion, women’s health and palliative care are shaped primarily by policy architecture, governance arrangements and implementation processes within national health systems. These findings indicate that displacement-related health challenges are not confined to humanitarian response, but reflect broader public health system performance in relation to equity, integration and resilience. This framing provides the basis for the policy implications outlined below.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a system-level policy analysis of refugee health, grounded in a synthesis of 42 qualitative studies across diverse geographic and service delivery contexts. A major strength lies in the explicit integration of three complementary analytical frameworks: the Policy Triangle Framework developed by Walt and Gilson, the CFIR and Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy, enabling a structured examination of governance arrangements, institutional determinants and implementation processes across multiple health domains. The exclusive focus on qualitative evidence allows in-depth analysis of lived experiences, service delivery dynamics and implementation constraints that are not captured by quantitative indicators alone.

This study has several limitations. First, it relies on published qualitative literature, which may under-represent informal practices, unpublished innovations or context-specific interventions not documented in academic sources. Second, despite a comprehensive multi-database and grey literature search, not all relevant studies may have been included. Third, variation in methodological quality across included studies may affect the consistency of reported findings. Fourth, the absence of primary data collection or stakeholder validation constrains contextual specificity. Fifth, the lack of quantitative metrics limits assessment of effect magnitude. Finally, the scope of included domains reflects the analytical focus of the review and does not capture all clinical areas of refugee health. Despite a comprehensive search strategy, not all relevant studies may have been included.

Policy implications

Synthesis of the qualitative evidence identifies a set of policy-relevant directions with potential to strengthen refugee health systems across diverse institutional contexts. Consistent with findings on restrictive entitlement policies and administrative barriers, reforms that reduce entitlement-related constraints and improve alignment between humanitarian and national health policies were associated with improved access to services. Reflecting evidence of fragmented referral systems and weak service integration, service delivery arrangements benefit from the institutionalisation of interpreter and cultural mediation services, integration of mental health, disability and gender-based violence screening within primary care and expansion of community-based and home-based care models.

In response to findings highlighting limited participation of displaced populations in governance processes, governance was perceived as more equitable and responsive in settings where refugees, women and persons with disabilities participated meaningfully in decision-making, with refugee-led organisations emerging as one mechanism for enhancing representation and accountability. Aligned with evidence on workforce and communication barriers, health workforce capacity was strengthened where training incorporated trauma-informed, gender-sensitive and disability-inclusive approaches. Consistent with findings linking social determinants to access and continuity challenges, policies that integrate health services with housing, food security and legal protection were associated with improved continuity of care and reduced access barriers.

These policy implications are grounded in recurring patterns across the qualitative evidence base. Their relevance and applicability are likely to vary by legal, institutional and resource contexts, and require context-specific adaptation, participatory engagement and ongoing evaluation.

Conclusion

This analysis brings together qualitative evidence from a wide range of refugee and displacement settings to clarify how structural conditions, service delivery arrangements and governance practices shape access to healthcare and equity. Across contexts, restrictive entitlement regimes, fragmented policy frameworks and sustained underinvestment in key areas particularly mental health, women’s health, disability inclusion and palliative care emerge as recurring constraints. At the same time, the review highlights institutional arrangements and governance practices that are consistently associated with better access, improved continuity of care and closer alignment between health systems and the needs of forcibly displaced populations.

The findings offer policymakers, public health actors and donors a grounded basis for understanding where policy coherence, investment choices and governance reforms are most likely to influence system performance. Although the evidence synthesised here is qualitative and does not support uniform or prescriptive solutions, it provides a cross-context view of policy levers that have repeatedly been linked to more integrated, culturally responsive and equitable care. The relevance of these insights depends on how they are adapted to specific legal, institutional and resource environments and on whether displaced populations themselves are meaningfully engaged in shaping the policies and systems that affect their health.
