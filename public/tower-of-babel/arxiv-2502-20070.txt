Partial Orders for Precise and Efficient Dynamic Deadlock Prediction See corrigendum after appendix.
Bas van den Heuvel, Martin Sulzmann, Peter Thiemann

Abstract
Deadlocks are a major source of bugs in concurrent programs. They are hard to predict, because they may only occur under specific scheduling conditions. Dynamic analysis attempts to identify potential deadlocks by examining a single execution trace of the program. A standard approach involves monitoring sequences of lock acquisitions in each thread, with the goal of identifying deadlock patterns. A deadlock pattern is characterized by a cyclic chain of lock acquisitions, where each lock is held by one thread while being requested by the next. However, it is well known that not all deadlock patterns identified in this way correspond to true deadlocks, as they may be impossible to manifest under any schedule. We tackle this deficiency by proposing a new method based on partial orders to eliminate false positives: lock acquisitions must be unordered under a given partial order, and not preceded by other deadlock patterns. We prove soundness (no falsely predicted deadlocks) for the novel TRW partial order, and completeness (no deadlocks missed) for a slightly weakened variant of TRW. Both partial orders can be computed efficiently and report the same deadlocks for an extensive benchmark suite.

Introduction

To fully leverage today’s multicore architectures, programs must be
designed for concurrency.
Yet, concurrent programming introduces
significant challenges, as developers must use synchronization
primitives, such as locks, to ensure correct behavior.
Incorrect
use of locks can lead to critical issues, including resource
deadlocks.
Resource deadlock occurs when two or more threads are
stuck waiting for each other’s locks, resulting in a standstill that
halts program execution.
These deadlocks are particularly difficult to
diagnose, as they may only appear intermittently, often after hundreds
of successful runs.

Program analysis can help programmers detect potential resource
deadlocks.
In particular, dynamic analysis aims at predicting a program’s behavior
under different schedules by analyzing a trace of events (including
acquires and releases of locks)
generated during a
program run.
A common approach to predicting deadlocks is to search a trace for
deadlock patterns
,
that involve series of lock acquire events with a cyclic dependency.
Deadlock patterns are
not sufficient to characterize resource deadlocks, meaning that they may lead
to false positives: predicted deadlocks that cannot manifest under any schedule.

Prior work.

To eliminate false positives,
prior work
[
13
,
24
,
12
,
22
,
24
]

attempts to find a schedule which exposes the deadlock, either symbolically via
SMT solving or by program re-execution.
Such methods can be very costly, as we might need to exhaustively explore
all alternative schedulings of the execution.
To improve efficiency, recent works
[
6
,
26
]

employ alternative solving methods to remove false positives.
However, these methods may still be costly (the time complexity of
[
6
]
is quadratic in trace length) and leave deadlocks unpredicted (
[
26
]
reports only so-called “sync-preserving” deadlocks).

Our novel approach.

We introduce a new approach that eliminates false-positive deadlock patterns, inspired by partial-order methods known from data-race prediction.
Particularly, our work is novel in that it requires only an analysis of the ordering of trace events, in contrast to prior works that incorporate partial orders (such as
[
13
,
6
,
26
]
) but need further steps akin to trace exploration.

To be precise, our approach refines the notion of deadlock pattern from the
literature by introducing the following two conditions:

Partially-ordered acquires

A deadlock pattern is a false positive if some of its acquires are ordered.
Hence, we only consider deadlock patterns where all acquires are concurrent (i.e., pairwise unordered).

Partially-ordered deadlock patterns

A deadlock pattern is a false positive if it is blocked by an earlier deadlock.
We identify such situations by defining a partial order on deadlock
patterns, and only consider the “earliest” deadlock patterns.

This way, given an appropriate partial order, our method instantly removes false positives without requiring extra steps or limiting the search to a subclass of deadlocks.

A new partial order for sound and efficient deadlock prediction.

Thus, the challenge lies in finding an appropriate partial order among acquires
that fits the purpose of deadlock prediction (the partial order among deadlock patterns is fairly straightforward).
Existing partial orders employed in the context
of data race prediction
[
16
,
23
,
14
,
8
]

are not suitable, because when applied to deadlock detection we may end up with false positives
and
false negatives.

In this paper, we introduce the novel TRW partial order.
We are able to show that under TRW all deadlock patterns correspond to true deadlocks.
However, we may encounter false negatives.
Earlier complexity results
[
26
]
suggest that it is impossible to find
an
efficient
deadlock-prediction method that is
sound
(no false positives) and
complete
(no false negatives).
Hence, we also consider PWR
[
25
]
,
and show that under PWR deadlock patterns are complete, although we may face some false positives.
Our experiments show that PWR and TRW are efficient and report the same set of deadlock patterns
for an extensive benchmark suite.

2.2
False Positives

The deadlock-pattern approach suffers from false positives.
Consider trace
T_{2}
in
Figure

1
.
This trace gives rise
to the same lock dependencies as
T_{1}
in
Figure

1
. Alas, the resulting deadlock pattern is a
false positive
,
because it does not correspond to a true deadlock: there is no (correct) reordering of
T_{2}
that exhibits the deadlock.
For example, the reordered prefix
[e_{1},e_{6},e_{7}]
is deadlocked on the enabled events
e_{2}
and
e_{8}
enabled, but it is incorrect:
the read event
e_{6}
no longer observes the same write event
e_{5}
it does in
T_{2}
(i.e., the
last write
of
e_{6}
is different).
Hence,
e_{6}
may read a different value than before, possibly affecting the program flow such that the events
e_{7}
–
e_{10}
may never happen.

A similar observation applies to lockset-based data-race prediction methods
where Condition
[DP-Guard]
is used to check if two conflicting
memory operations are in a data race.
Two operations are
conflicting
if they refer to the same memory address and at least one of them
is a write.

We conclude that Conditions
[DP-Cycle]
and
[DP-Guard]
are not sufficient to guarantee a deadlock: additional conditions are necessary to rule out false positives.
A popular method in the area of data-race prediction is to derive a partial order on
events from the program trace.
Ideally, this partial order captures the inter-event dependencies imposed be the trace and its semantics, making it possible to reason about correctly reordered traces without exploring all possible interleavings.
This way, e.g., a partial order may order a read after its last write, or order the events within the same thread.
Unordered events are then considered concurrent: they may appear in different orders among correct reorderings.
Hence, a data race is signaled if two conflicting memory operations are concurrent.
A sufficiently strong partial order then eliminates falsely signaled data races.

2.3
Partial-order Methods for Deadlock Prediction

We apply the partial-order idea to the deadlock-prediction setting and refine the definition of a deadlock pattern

\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
with the following additional condition:

[DP-P]

Events
e_{i}
are pairwise concurrent under partial order P.

Which partial order P to use?
Answering this question is a non-trivial task, shown by
review of a number of existing partial orders that have been applied
in the data-race setting.

Happens-Before is too strict.

We first consider the Happens-Before relation (HB)
[
16
]
.
Under HB, lock releases and acquisitions on the same lock
are ordered as they appear in the trace.
For trace
T_{2}
in
Figure

1
, we
then find that
e_{4}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny HB}}}e_{5}
.
Since HB is closed under trace order within the same thread (program order) and transitivity,
we find that
e_{2}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny HB}}}e_{6}
: these lock acquires are not concurrent.
Thus, Condition
[DP-HB]
eliminates the (false-positive) deadlock pattern
\{e_{2},e_{6}\}
.
However, Condition
[DP-HB]
does not hold
for any
deadlock pattern,
because
of the strict textual order among lock acquisitions and releases (critical sections).
We conclude that Condition
[DP-HB]
reports no false positives but too many false negatives.
Clearly, this means that DP is not useful in practice: we need a weaker partial order
that does not strictly order critical sections.

Weak-Causally-Precedes is not strict enough.

The Weak-Causally-Precedes relation (WCP)
[
14
]

considers two critical sections as unordered, unless they contain conflicting memory operations.
In case of trace
T_{1}
in
Figure

1
, we find that
e_{2}
and
e_{6}
are not ordered.
Thus, Condition
[DP-WCP]
(correctly) predicts that
\{e_{2},e_{6}\}
codifies a deadlock.
Unfortunately, we now encounter false negatives
and still some false positives, as shown next.

Trace
T_{2}
in
Figure

1
shows that we encounter false positives.
Events
e_{5}
and
e_{6}
refer to conflicting memory operations, but they are not part of a critical section, so there is no order among the events in threads
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
.
Hence, Condition
[DP-WCP]
applies to deadlock pattern
\{e_{2},e_{8}\}
.
This is a false positive, because any reordering that exhibits the deadlock
will violate the last-write dependency among
e_{5}
and
e_{6}
.

The case of false negative is explained by trace
T_{3}
in
Figure

2
.
The critical sections for lock
l_{2}
in threads
\tau_{2}
and
\tau_{3}

contain some conflicting write operations on
x
, and therefore we find that
e_{7}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny WCP}}}e_{9}
.
WCP composes (to the left and right) with HB.
Hence,
e_{2}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny WCP}}}e_{10}
by composition of
e_{7}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny WCP}}}e_{9}

with
e_{2}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny HB}}}e_{7}
and
e_{9}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny HB}}}e_{10}
.
But then Condition
[DP-WCP]
(wrongly) rules out
\{e_{2},e_{10}\}
: a false negative.

We conclude that neither HB nor WCP are suitable for the purpose of deadlock prediction.
Similar observations apply to other partial orders such as SDP
[
8
]

and DC
[
20
]
.

Total-Read-Write order and deadlock-pattern order for soundness.

Our idea is to adapt WCP to a new Total-Read-Write partial order (TRW), in the following non-trivial way.
Under TRW, if two critical sections contain TRW-ordered events,
the release of the critical section that appears earlier in the trace is ordered before
the TRW-ordered event in the later critical section.
Though similar, there are two main differences between TRW and WCP.
First, TRW does not compose with HB.
This eliminates false negatives, as seen in trace
T_{3}
in
Figure

2
.
Second, under TRW, any pair of conflicting memory operations is ordered based on their order in the trace.
This eliminates false positives, as seen in trace
T_{4}
in
Figure

3
.

We can show that the additional Condition
[DP-TRW]
guarantees
soundness (no false positives)
if there is no
earlier
deadlock reported.
To explain this issue, consider trace
T_{4}
in
Figure

2
.
The deadlock pattern
\{e_{5},e_{13}\}
does not codify a true deadlock, because there is
an earlier deadlock pattern
\{e_{2},e_{10}\}
.
Hence, there is no correct reordering of trace
T_{3}

where events
e_{5}
and
e_{13}
are enabled.

To understand the issue in more detail,
we examine the corresponding cyclic lock-dependency chains in
Figure

2
.
The deadlock pattern
\{e_{2},e_{10}\}
is supported by dependencies
D_{1}
and
D_{3}
.
The deadlock pattern
\{e_{5},e_{13}\}
is supported by
D_{2}
and
D_{4}
.
The lockset of dependency
D_{1}
is a subset of the lockset of
D_{3}

and the lockset of dependency
D_{2}
is a subset of the lockset of
D_{4}
.
In both cases, the locks held are acquired by the
same event
as indicated
by the (dark and light gray) highlighting.

Hence, the cyclic lock-dependency chain between
D_{2}
and
D_{4}
is not reachable,
as it is blocked by the earlier cyclic lock-dependency chain between

D_{1}
and
D_{3}
.
To rule out such cases, we impose the following condition
that imposes an order among deadlock patterns:

[DP-Block]

A deadlock pattern is not ordered after any other deadlock patterns.

In
Section

4

[DP-Guard/-Cycle/-TRW/-Block]

are sufficient to establish soundness (no false positives).

Comparison against the state of the art.

TRW and the ordering among deadlock patterns can be computed efficiently, as our experiments confirm.

We also compare experimentally against

SPDOffline

[
26
]
, which represents the state of the art when it comes to efficient and sound
deadlock prediction methods.
For an extensive benchmark suite, we show that
our method covers the same deadlocks as
SPDOffline
.

The similar performance and precision (absence of false positives) between
SPDOffline
and our method does
not
imply that they are equivalent.
We propose an entirely new direction
to achieving efficient and sound deadlock prediction.
Generally speaking,
SPDOffline
and our approach are incomparable when it comes
to precision and underlying methods, as we explain
via the following examples.

Consider
T_{5}
in
Figure

3
.
Deadlock pattern
\{e_{2},e_{8}\}
is a true positive and reported by
SPDOffline
.
However, under
[DP-TRW]
deadlock pattern
\{e_{2},e_{8}\}
is ruled out
for the following reason.
We find that
e_{3}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}}e_{7}
, because these events refer to conflicting memory operations.
Both events are protected by a common lock and therefore
{e_{5}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}}e_{7}}
.
By program order and transitivity, we conclude that

On the other hand,

In case of

SPDOffline

Initially, the closure starts with

Hence, there are connections between

The major difference is that we

In contrast,

Experimenting with other partial orders.

We use

A trace

is a list of events reflecting a single execution of a

We write

3.2
Well Formedness and Correct Reorderings

Traces must be well formed, following the standard sequential consistency conditions for concurrent objects
[

Definition 2

(Well Formedness)
.

A trace
T
is

if all the following conditions are satisfied:

[WF-Acq]

For every
a=(t,\textit{acq}(l)),a^{\prime}=(s,\textit{acq}(l))\in T
where
a\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a^{\prime}
, there exists
r=(t,\textit{rel}(l))\in T
such that
a\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a^{\prime}
.
We say that
a
and
r

match
.

[WF-Rel]

For every
r=(t,\textit{rel}(l))\in T
, there exists
a=(t,\textit{acq}(l))\in T
such that
a\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r
and there is no
r^{\prime}=(s,\textit{rel}(l))\in T
with
a\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r^{\prime}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r
.
We say that
a
and
r

match
.

[WF-Req]

For every
a=(t,\textit{acq}(l))\in T
, there exists
q=(t,\textit{req}(l))\in T
such that
q\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a
, and for every
q=(t,\textit{req}(l))\in T
and
e=(t,op)\in T
such that
q\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
, if there is no
f=(t,op^{\prime})
such that
q\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
then
e=(t,\textit{acq}(l))
.
We say that
q

requests

e
.

Condition
[WF-Acq]
states that a previously acquired lock can only be acquired after it has been released.
Similarly, Condition
[WF-Rel]
states that a lock can only be released after is has been acquired but not yet released.
Note that these conditions require matching acquires and releases to occur in the same thread.
Condition
[WF-Req]
states that all lock acquires must have been requested in the same thread immediately before.
We often omit requests from example traces, but we assume that they precede each acquire implicitly.
At the end of the trace, acquired locks do not have to be released and requests do not have to be fulfilled.
All traces in
Figures

1
,
2
and
3
are well formed, with implicit requests; we discuss an example with explicit requests in the next subsection.

A trace represents only one possible interleaving of events: there can be as many interleavings as there are permutations of the original trace.
However, not all such reorderings are feasible in the sense of being reproducible by executing the program with a different schedule.
In addition to well formedness, a reordering must guarantee that (a) program order and (b) last writes are maintained.
A reordering maintains program order if, in any thread, the order of events is unchanged.
For guarantee (b), note that every read observes some write on the same variable: the last preceding such write.
Last writes are then maintained if the write observed by any read is unchanged.
Guarantee (b) is particularly important to ensure that the control flow of the traced program is unaffected by the reordering, e.g., when the read is used in a conditional statement.

Reorderings do not have to run to completion, so we consider reordered
prefixes
of traces.

Definition 3

(Correctly Reordered Prefix)
.

The
projection
of
T
onto thread
t

\proj(t,T)

T
to events
e
with
\thd(e)=t
.
That is,

if and only if
e\in T
and
\thd(e)=t
, and

e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{proj(t,T)}}f
implies
e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f
.

Take

.
We say that

is the

of

T

f
appears before
e

That is,

f\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
, and
there is no

such that
f\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}g\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
.

Trace

is a

of

[CRP-WF]

is well formed and
\evts(T^{\prime})\subseteq\evts(T)
.

[CRP-PO]

For every

,
\proj(t,T^{\prime})
prefixes
\proj(t,T)
.

[CRP-LW]

For every

3.3
Resource Deadlocks

We consider a standard definition of (resource) deadlock, where multiple threads are simultaneously requesting to acquire a lock, but every such lock is held by another thread: none of the requests can be fulfilled, and so the trace is stuck.
Our goal is to predict that a trace can be rescheduled to deadlock, and so we identify traces with
predictable
deadlocks in terms of correctly reordered prefixes with deadlocks.
We first identify these deadlocks using
deadlock patterns
.

Deadlock patterns codify cycles of requests that cannot be fulfilled because the requested lock is held by the next request.
Hence, we first define what it means for an event to hold a lock, in terms of
critical sections
of events bordered by lock acquires and releases.

Definition 4

(Critical Sections)
.

Given
a=(t,\textit{acq}(l))\in T
, we define the
critical section acquired by
a
as
\@CS_{T}(a)\subseteq\evts(T)
, where
e\in\@CS_{T}(a)
if all the following conditions hold:

[CS-PO]

e=a
or
a\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PO}}^{T}}e
.

[CS-Held]

There is no
r=\textit{rel}(l)\in T
that matches
a
with
a\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PO}}^{T}}r\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PO}}^{T}}e
.

The set of
acquires held
by
e\in T
is defined as
\@AH_{T}(e)=\{a\in T\mid e\in\@CS_{T}(a)\}
.
Accordingly, the set of
locks held
is defined as
\@LH_{T}(e)=\{l\mid\exists\textit{acq}(l)\in\@AH_{T}(e)\}
.

Thus, an event is in a critical section if it is or appears after the acquire in the same thread (Condition
[CS-PO]
), as long as the critical section has not yet been closed (Condition
[CS-Held]
).
Note that Condition
[CS-Held]
considers a lock held if it is never released at the end of the trace.

Definition 5

(Deadlock Patterns (DPs))
.

Given trace
T
, let
A=\{(a_{1},q_{1}),\ldots,(a_{n},q_{n})\}\subseteq\evts(T)\times\evts(T)
for
n\geq 2
, where every
(a_{i},q_{i})\in A
is an acquire-request tuple, and
\thd(q_{i})\neq\thd(q_{j})
for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
We define the following properties for
A

[DP-Cycle]

For every
1\leq i\leq n
,
q_{i}=\textit{req}(l)\in\@CS_{T}(a_{i})
and
a_{(i\mathbin{\%}n)+1}=\textit{acq}(l)
.

[DP-Guard]

\@LH_{T}(a_{i})\cap\@LH_{T}(a_{j})=\emptyset
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
.

Condition
[DP-Cycle]
identifies
A
as a
cycle in
T

If
A
is a cycle in
T
that satisfies Condition
[DP-Guard]

A
is a
deadlock pattern (DP)
in
T

Thus, we consider sets of acquire-request pairs, where the request holds the acquire.
Condition
[DP-Cycle]
ensures that the set forms a cycle of requests for locks held by other requests.
DPs satisfy an additional condition to eliminate false alarms: Condition
[DP-Guard]
ensures that no two requests hold a common lock, which would make it impossible to schedule the deadlock.

4
Soundness: Partial Orders to Eliminate False Positives

As discussed in
Section

2
, DPs as defined in
Section

3.3
alone are imprecise: they often signal false alarms.
To be precise, we identified two situations in which DPs falsely identify deadlocks.
To rule out such situations, we introduce two new conditions on DPs that use partial orders, eliminating all false positives and allowing us to prove soundness (
Theorem

1
).

4.1
Partially-ordered Requests

A DP is a predictable deadlock in
T
when there is a
T^{\prime}\in\crp(T)
in which its requests are the last events in their respective threads.
Because two requests never directly depend on each other, this implies that the requests can occur in
T^{\prime}
in any order, i.e., they are concurrent.
Hence, the first situation occurs when some requests are not concurrent, making it impossible to find such a
T^{\prime}
.
We rule out this situation by giving a partial order that is appropriate for determining the concurrency of the requests in a DP.

It turns out that our novel Total Read-Write order (TRW), as defined below, is up to the task (cf.
Section

4.3
).
TRW is derived from PWR
[
25
]
by imposing that all reads and writes on the same variable are ordered as in the source trace.
Moreover, TRW is similar to WCP
[
14
]
, which soundly predicts deadlocks in absence of data races but is not sound in general (cf.
Section

8
for details).

Definition 7

(Total Read-Write Order (TRW))
.

We say events
e
and
f
are
conflicting
, denoted
e\mathrel{\bowtie}f
, if
e
and
f
are reads/writes on the same variable and at least one is a write.

Given trace
T
, we define the
Total-Read-Write order (TRW)
as a relation
\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}
on
\evts(T)
, where
e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}f
if either of the following conditions holds:

[TRW-PO]

e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PO}}^{T}}f
.

[TRW-Conf]

e\mathrel{\bowtie}f
and
e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f
.

[TRW-Rel]

There are acquires
a_{1},a_{2}\in T
on the same lock such that (i)
e
is the release matching
a_{1}
, (ii)
a_{1}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a_{2}
, (iii)
f\in\@CS_{T}(a_{2})
, and (iv)
a_{1}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}f
.

[TRW-Tr]

There exists
g\in T
such that
e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}g\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}f
.

We define
\TRWcrp(T)=\{T^{\prime}\in crp(T)\mid\forall f\in T^{\prime},e\in T\colon e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}f\implies e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T^{\prime}}}f\}
.

A DP
A
in well-formed trace
T
satisfies Condition
[DP-TRW]
if neither
q\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}q^{\prime}
nor
q^{\prime}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}q
(denoted
q\mathrel{{||}_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}q^{\prime}
) for every distinct
(a,q),(a^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in A
.

Crucially, Condition
[TRW-Conf]
orders all reads/writes on the same variable by source-trace order;
note that the reads/writes have to be
conflicting
, so one event must be a write meaning that reads are not ordered directly.
Condition
[TRW-Rel]
orders (the events within) critical sections on the same lock, if they contain TRW-ordered events;
note that events that precede the later TRW-ordered event
f
are not affected, such that
f
correctly does not affect ordering in correctly ordered prefixes from which
f
is omitted (we revisit this point in context of completeness in
Section

5
).
Conditions
[TRW-PO/-Tr]
close TRW under program order and transitivity, respectively.

To illustrate, we reconsider trace
T_{2}
in

We consider variant

This trace contains DP

: we have
e_{2}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T^{\prime}_{2}}}e_{6}
(
[TRW-PO/-Tr]
),
e_{6}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T^{\prime}_{2}}}e_{7}
(

4.2
Partially-ordered Deadlock Patterns

In the second situation, the candidate deadlock cannot be reached because of an “earlier” deadlock.
We make this formal by means of a partial order on cycles (sets of acquire-request pairs that satisfy Condition
[DP-Cycle]
), and rule out false positives by reporting only the earliest DPs.

Definition 8
.

Given trace
T

The ordering crucially requires that every acquire of a request in the earlier cycle is held by a request in the later cycle.
This way, we ensure that the earlier cycle is unavoidable when trying to achieve the later.

To illustrate, we reconsider trace

5
Completeness: A Partial Order to Eliminate False Negatives

In the previous section, we were able to leverage the Total Read-Write order (TRW) to non-trivially eliminate all DPs that do not correspond to predictable deadlocks.
On the other hand, TRW is slightly too strong to catch all predictable deadlocks: some deadlocks require reordering some reads and writes, which TRW does not permit.
In other words, a DP that does not satisfy Condition
[DP-TRW]
can still be a predictable deadlock.
Although such false negatives rarely occur in practice (cf.
Section

7
), there is no theoretical guarantee that all predictable deadlocks are caught.
The question is then whether there is a variant of Condition
[DP-TRW]
that rules out enough false positives to be practically relevant, while guaranteeing that no predictable deadlock is missed.

5.1
PWR: Weakening TRW

Recall that TRW is derived from Program-Write-Release order (PWR)
[
25
]
.
PWR is subtly weaker than TRW, meaning that it orders events less strictly: TRW orders all conflicting reads and writes, whereas PWR only orders reads and their last writes.
Interestingly, this subtle difference is enough for PWR itself to be a suitable candidate for a complete characterization of predictable deadlocks.
We define PWR and, accordingly, Condition
[DP-PWR]
.

Definition 9

(Program-Write-Release Order (PWR))
.

Given trace
T
, we define the Program-Write-Release order (PWR) as a relation
\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T}}
on
\evts(T)
, where

if either of the following conditions hold:

[PWR-PO]

e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PO}}^{T}}f
.

[PWR-LW]

e
is the last write of read
f
w.r.t.
T
.

[PWR-Rel]

There are acquires
a_{1},a_{2}\in T
on the same lock such that (i)
e
is the release matching
a_{1}
, (ii)
a_{1}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a_{2}
, (iii)
f\in\@CS_{T}(a_{2})
, and (iv)
a_{1}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T}}f
.

[PWR-Tr]

There exists
g\in T
such that
e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T}}g\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T}}f
.

A DP

in well-formed trace

satisfies Condition
[DP-PWR]
if neither
q\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T}}q^{\prime}
nor
q^{\prime}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T}}q
(denoted

Thus, PWR is defined exactly as TRW (
Definition

As mentioned before in

Recall that in trace

in

Indeed, Condition
[DP-PWR]

.
On the other hand, suppose we simplified Condition
[PWR-Rel]
to order

instead of only
e\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}}f
.
Then
e_{3}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T^{\prime\prime}_{2}}}e_{5}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T^{\prime\prime}_{2}}}e_{7}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny PWR}}^{T^{\prime\prime}_{2}}}e_{8}

5.2
Lock Requests

As hinted at in
Section

3

To illustrate, suppose we were to define deadlock patterns in terms of lock acquires, and predictable deadlocks by correctly reordered prefixes in which the acquires of a DP are the next event in their thread.
Consider trace

As witnessed by trace

However, we have

5.3
Completeness

Note that our completeness result does not require a trace to be well nested or bounded, as our soundness result does.

Theorem 2

(Completeness)
.

If DP
A
in well-formed trace
T
is a predictable deadlock, then
A
satisfies Conditions
[DP-PWR/-Block]
.

Proof (sketch).

By assuming that
A
is a predictable deadlock, there is
T^{\prime}\in\crp(T)
such that every
q_{i}
in
A
is the last event in its thread.
We prove both conditions by contradiction.
Condition
[DP-PWR]
is straightforward: requests at the end of threads can be trivially reordered, contradicting any PWR ordering among them.
Condition
[DP-Block]
follows by showing that a preceding cycle would lead to a cyclic trace order in
T^{\prime}
.
∎

Our experiments (
Section

7
) show that the result above is not trivial, as PWR does not report any false positives, even though it is theoretically unsound.
As such, our methodology allows a choice between near-sound completeness (PWR) and near-complete soundness (TRW).
Ideally, we will develop a partial order that is both sound and complete, lying somewhere between TRW and PWR, but this seems to be a quest for a “holy grail”.

6.2
Computing and Verifying Deadlock Patterns

Function
undeadTRWBlock
(
1
)
in
Algorithm

2

is the main entry point of our algoritm.
We first call
computeTRWLockDeps
(in
Algorithm

1
) to compute
the set of abstract lock dependencies (
2
discussed in
Section

6.1
).

Next, we enumerate in
{\mathcal{D}}
all cyclic chains of abstract lock dependencies (referred to in
[
26
]
as “abstract lock dependencies”) that
satisfy Conditions
[DP-Cycle/-Guard]
(
3
).
We refer to
[
27
]
for details on the computation of
{\mathcal{D}}
.
Elements in
{\mathcal{D}}
are sequences of the form
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})
,
where each
F_{i}
is an abstract lock dependency, i.e., a list of triples
E_{j}=(j,V,\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\})
.
For every such
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})
, we then look for a concrete
instance
(E_{1},\ldots,E_{n})
, where every
E_{i}\in F_{i}
,
that satisfies Conditions
[DP-TRW/-Block]
.

Function
checkTRW
looks for an instance
that satisfies Condition
[DP-TRW]
(
13
).
We systematically enumerate instances by starting
with the first element in
F_{i}
(
14
).
We use array access notation to obtain the
k_{i}
th element
combined with pattern matching to retrieve the vector clock
V_{i}

of that element (
16
).
If the vector clocks are pairwise incomparable,
we have found an instance that satisfies Condition
[DP-TRW]
(
17
).
Otherwise, we need to select a new instance.

To avoid naive enumeration of all instances, we exploit the property that for

{F=[E_{1},\ldots,E_{k}]}
we find that

V_{j}<V_{j+1}
where
E_{j}=(\rule[0.0pt]{4.26773pt}{0.4pt},V_{j},\rule[0.0pt]{4.26773pt}{0.4pt})
for every
E_{j}\in F
.
This follows from the fact that the elements of
F
are stored in
the order in which they are generated
(cf.
13
in
Algorithm

1
).
Hence, if we find that
V_{i}<V_{j}
,
we can immediately conclude that, for any
V^{\prime}

where
(\rule[0.0pt]{4.26773pt}{0.4pt},V^{\prime},\rule[0.0pt]{4.26773pt}{0.4pt})=F_{i}[k]
for
k<k_{i}
,
we have
V^{\prime}<V_{j}
.
It follows that it suffices to check for “later” candidates
V^{\prime}

for which
V^{\prime}<V_{j}
does
not
hold.
This is achieved by synchronizing all candidates (
19
)
and calling function
next
(
20
).

The resulting instances, that satisfy Condition
[DP-TRW]
, are stored in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
What remains is to select only those instances that satisfy Condition
[DP-Block]
.
Function
lessThan
checks whether two instances are ordered
following the description in
Definition

8
.
For convenience, we omit the helper function
cycLk

that retrieves the acquire held that is part
of the cyclic dependency, as stated in Condition
[DP-Cycle]
.
The resulting instances stored in
{\mathcal{B}}
represent
deadlocks patterns satisfying Conditions
[DP-Cycle/-Guard/TRW/-Block]
.

For example, for trace
T_{10}
in
Figure

6
,
we find

.
The first instance to be checked
is the pair of elements
(2,V_{2},\{e_{1}\})
and
(8,V_{8},\{e_{7}\})
, but it is rejected because
V_{2}<V_{8}
.
The next pair of elements considered is

(12,V_{12},\{e_{11}\})
and
(8,V_{8},\{e_{7}\})
.
This pair is successful: we find that

The resulting instance is

6.3
Time Complexity

We first consider the time complexity of
Algorithm

1
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the number of threads and

\mathcal{C}
be the number of critical sections (acquire-release pairs).
Then, each call to function
syncCS
takes time
O(\mathcal{T}\cdot\mathcal{C})
,
because the number of acquires held is treated as a constant,
there are
O(\mathcal{C})
entries to consider, and
for each entry the vector clock operations involved take time
O(\mathcal{T})
.
Hence, function
computeTRWLockDeps
takes time
O(\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T}\cdot\mathcal{C})
where
\mathcal{N}
is the length of the trace,
assuming that access and manipulation of state variables (
\textit{Th}(t),...
)
takes time
O(1)
and ignoring initialization of state variables.
Instead of a global history
\mathcal{H}(l)
of size
O(\mathcal{C})
,
our actual implementation follows WCP
[
15
]

and PWR
[
25
]
in using thread-local histories.
This way, for realistic examples
the number of entries in thread-local histories can be treated like a constant.
We therefore argue that the time complexity of
syncCS
can be simplified to
O(\mathcal{T})
, and hence that
Algorithm

1
takes time
O(\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T})
.

What remains is to consider the time complexity of
Algorithm

2
.
As shown by Tunç et al.
[
26
]
,
the number of cyclic chains of abstract lock dependencies
can be exponential in terms of the number of threads and acquires.
We follow
[
26
]
in writing
O(\mathit{Cyc})
to represent this number
as well as the time complexity of computing set
{\mathcal{D}}
(
3
).
Next, we consider the complexity of
checkTRW
(
6
).
We treat the length of the sequence
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})
as a constant and
always strictly move forward through the list of candidates in
F_{i}
.
In each step, vector clock operations take time
O(\mathcal{T})
.
Hence, function
checkTRW
takes time
O(\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T})
.
This shows that
5
,
6
and
7

take time
O(\mathit{Cyc}\cdot\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T})
.
Function
lessThan
takes time
O(\mathit{Cyc})
.
Thus, we find that
Algorithm

2
takes time

O(\mathit{Cyc}+\mathit{Cyc}\cdot\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T}+\mathit{Cyc}^{2})
.

Overall, our method takes time
O(\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T}+\mathit{Cyc}+\mathit{Cyc}\cdot\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathcal{T}+\mathit{Cyc}^{2})
.
In practice,
O(\mathit{Cyc})
is small and
O(\mathcal{T})
can be interpreted as a constant, such that the complexity reduces to
O(\mathcal{N})
.
This is confirmed by our experiments (
Section

7
).

Reducing the number of concrete lock dependencies.

As also observed in

the number of abstract lock dependencies remains small.
Recall trace

In further loop iterations, further entries
will be added to the abstract lock dependency
\mathcal{M}{\langle\tau_{1},l_{2},\{l_{1}\}\rangle}

The number of entries (i.e., concrete lock dependency) increases,
whereas the number of abstract lock dependencies remains the same.

In our approach, we can aggressively remove ‘duplicates’ of concrete lock dependencies.
We consider two entries as duplicates if no inter-thread synchronization took place between
processing the respective acquires.
Such an optimization is not possible for

We believe that this is caused by a difference in handling lock-request versus lock-acquire events:

8
Related Work

Lockset-based dynamic resource-deadlock analysis.

The idea of identifying deadlocks via circularity in the lock-order dependency
of threads dates back to the work by Havelund
[
10
]
and
Harrow
[
9
]
.
In subsequent work,
Bensalem and Havelund
[
4
]
introduce lock-order graphs
that capture the lock-order dependency for threads,
where deadlock analysis reduces to checking for cycles.
Extensions of lock-order graphs to avoid false positives if a circular lock-order
dependency takes place within a single thread or is protected by a common lock
are discussed by
Agarwal et al.
[
2
]
.

Instead of lock-order graphs,
Joshi et al.
[
12
]

introduce lock dependencies on a per-thread basis.
The advantage compared to lock-order graphs is that common locks or singular threads
can be easily detected.
Several works
[
22
,
27
,
5
]

improve on this idea, e.g., by using an efficient representation
for lock dependencies and/or ignoring impossible cyclic chains
due to fork-join dependencies.
Our experiments show that considering fork-join dependencies
only avoids some false positives, but a significant number remains.

Sound dynamic resource-deadlock analysis.

Lockset-based analysis methods are prone to false positives.
One way to eliminate false positives is to re-run the trace
(and/or the program)
to verify that a deadlock exists (e.g.
[
24
,
12
,
22
]
).

Kalhauge and Palsberg
[
13
]
rely on an SMT-solver for exhaustive trace
exploration to eliminate false positives among deadlock patterns, but
the use of SMT-solving may severely impact performance
[
26
]
.
Also, false positives may arise if
a request is guarded by a lock in another thread (cf., e.g., the example
in
Figure

5
, adapted from Tunç et al.
[
26
]
).
Our soundness result (
Theorem

1
) explicitly excludes such traces.

Section

7
contains an extensive discussion of the relation
to
SPDOffline

[
26
]
.

SeqCheck
[
6
]

is similar to
SPDOffline
, also employing a closure construction
that relies on a partial order to eliminate infeasible deadlock patterns.
The SeqCheck partial order does not impose last-write dependencies
but a weaker form of observation order,
so further checks are required during the closure construction.
According to Tunç et al.
[
26
]
,
SeqCheck infers almost the same deadlocks as
SPDOffline
,
but the running time is significantly higher.
We were not able to include SeqCheck in our own measurements, because our requests to access the artifact were not answered.

Ang and Mathur
[
3
]
discuss a novel approach to predictive
monitoring and its application to race and deadlock
detection, based on Mazurkiewicz’s trace equivalence rather than
classical partial-order techniques.
The idea is to match critical
patterns like conflicting events or deadlock patterns against a trace
while assuming commutativity of all events except those that are
marked dependent (like those in the same thread, operating on the same
lock, etcetera).
As standard trace equivalence is unsuitable for predicting
deadlocks, they introduce two refinements:
strong trace prefixes and
strong reads-from prefixes.
With these refinements, they obtain
algorithms for detecting sync-preserving data races and
deadlocks.
Their experimental results for deadlocks match those of
previous work
[
26
,
19
]
, but with
substantial slowdown
[
3
, Table 3]
.

Partial-order methods for dynamic data race prediction.

Starting with Lamport’s Happens-Before relation
[
16
]
,
the literature offers a wide range of partial orders
[
23
,
14
,
17
,
8
]
.
As discussed in detail in
Section

2
,
none of these partial orders is suitable for sound deadlock prediction,
motivating the introduction of the new TRW partial order.
Like in the case of data-race prediction, establishing soundness is a non-trivial task.
Our soundness proof shares some similarities with the WCP soundness proof
in that we argue that either (a) requests in a deadlock pattern can be placed
next to each other, or (b) there must be a blocking cycle.
The details differ and are specific to TRW and the deadlock-prediction setting.

9
Conclusions

This paper considered the application of partial-order methods
to eliminate false positives often reported by traditional lockset-based deadlock predictors.
Inspired by dynamic data-race prediction,
key ingredients for our refined deadlock
patterns are concurrency of lock acquisitions and absence of earlier blocking deadlock patterns.
We established soundness under
our novel TRW partial order and
completeness under the slightly weaker PWR.

We implemented our approach in an offline version of
the UNDEAD deadlock predictor.
Our experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and
precision of our approach, based on a sizeable set of
pre-recorded program traces.
Avenues for future work are improving the efficiency of the implementation
for large numbers of threads,
as well as exploring ways to allow for
unbounded critical sections without introducing false positives.

In base case and inductive case, the idea is the same: we identify the problematic pair of acquires on the same lock, and show that their respective ordering is impossible due to another pair of acquires on another lock that cannot be ordered.
In the base case, which we detail, the size of
A
is two.
We show that this new pair of acquires must also precede the two requests in
A

B
to contradiction Condition
[DP-Block]
.
In the inductive case, the new pair of acquires may involve a third request in
A

1

A
, so we ought to eventually encounter a “new” request we have seen before).

To find the pair of problematic acquires, we apply induction on the distance between the problematic acquires and requests in
A
(IH
2
).
In the inductive case, assume that both acquires have matching releases that precede requests in
A

Since this acquire is closer to a request in
A
, the thesis follows from IH
2
.
If one of the acquires has a matching release that succeeds a request in
A
, we proceed as in the base case.

In the base case, the acquires are as close to requests as possible, so there are no further problematics acquires.
It must then be that one of the acquires has a matching release that must succeed a request in
A
; otherwise, there could not be an issue in ordering the acquires (remember that we ruled out write-read issues).

Thus, we have acquires

on the same lock

that, for a prefix of

cannot occur before
q_{1}
(w.l.o.g., assume

).
Hence, by

2
and
3

, and
b^{\prime}_{1}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}q_{2}
.
Note that
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
, for otherwise we would already have

r^{\prime}_{1}
matches
b^{\prime}_{1}
: reordering would be unnecessary and hence there is no issue.

By TRW boundedness,

.
It follows that

and hence
l_{1}\in\@LH_{T}(q_{1})
.
Then, by Condition
[DP-Guard]
,

so
b^{\prime}_{1}\notin\@AH_{T}(q_{2})
.
Hence,

.

This means that
q_{2}
being the last in its thread cannot prevent

from being placed.

Hence, there must be acquire
b_{2}
that precedes
b^{\prime}_{1}
(

by
Lemma

2
) that cannot be moved to precede some
b^{\prime}_{2}
on the same lock
l_{2}
, because the release
r_{2}
matching
b_{2}
succeeds
q_{2}
(

3

Assuming

b^{\prime}_{2}\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}q_{1}\not\mathrel{<_{\mbox{\tiny TRW}}^{T}}r^{\prime}_{2}

r^{\prime}_{2}
matches
b^{\prime}_{2}

and

.

and

and

If there are more than two requests in

Since every acquire encountered is assumed to contribute to unorderability, it must then be that we would uncover a pair of unorderable acquires that we have seen before.
This way, we reach the cycle of requires for us to build
