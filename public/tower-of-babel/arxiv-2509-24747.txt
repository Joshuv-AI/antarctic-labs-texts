When risk defies order: On the limits of fractional stochastic dominance
Christian Laudagé, Felix-Benedikt Liebrich

Abstract
. Motivated by recent work on monotone additive statistics and questions regarding optimal risk sharing for return-based risk measures, we investigate the existence, structure, and applications of Meyer risk measures . Those are monetary risk measures consistent with fractional stochastic orders suggested by Meyer 1977a , Meyer 1977b as refinement of second-order stochastic dominance (SSD). These so-called v v -SD orders are based on a threshold utility function v v . The test utilities defining the associated order are those at least as risk averse in absolute terms as v v . The generality of v v allows to subsume SSD and other examples from the literature. The structure of risk measures respecting the v v -SD order is clarified by two types of representations. The existence of nontrivial examples is more subtle: for many choices of v v outside the exponential (CARA) class, they do not exist. Additional properties like convexity or positive homogeneity further restrict admissible examples, even within the CARA class. We present impossibility theorems that demonstrate a deeper link between the axiomatic structure of monetary risk measures and SSD than previously acknowledged. The study concludes with two applications: portfolio optimisation under a Meyer risk measure as objective, and risk assessment of financial time series data. Keywords: Fractional stochastic order, monetary risk measure, return risk measure, risk aversion, second-order stochastic dominance.



1.
Introduction

How should one decide between two random prospects? This question is central to decision theory, risk management, and financial investment.
A classical approach is comparing the prospects in second-order stochastic dominance (SSD) pioneered by
Hadar and Russell 1969
,
Hanoch and Levy 1969
, checking if there is consensual preference for one payoff over the other among all expected utility (EU) agents who share a probabilistic view of the future; prefer more to less (nondecreasing utility); and are risk averse (concave utility).

It has been observed, e.g., by
Leshno and Levy 2002
that SSD-based decisions can be hijacked by unrealistically risk-averse EU agents.
Even when the preference between two payoffs is intuitively clear, SSD may not provide a ranking.
They illustrate this with the choice between two payoff lotteries: the first delivers $1 with certainty; the second offers 1 million dollars with 99% chance, and nothing otherwise.
While virtually all decision makers would prefer the second lottery over the first, this preference is not reflected by an SSD ordering.
Another drawback of using SSD comparisons is that they do not always yield a decision—it is an incomplete order of random prospects.

Two alternatives exist. The first one, suggested by
Leshno and Levy 2002
and revisited later by
Tzeng et al. 2013
,
Tsetlin et al. 2015
,
Müller et al. 2017
, and
Mao et al. 2021
, modifies the set of relevant utility functions, excludes agents whose risk aversion is deemed extreme, and adds locally risk-loving EU agents. These contributions have been preceded by
Meyer 1977a
,
Meyer 1977b
, where the risk aversion of the decision-making EU collective is controlled directly. This approach is crucial for the present paper and has been revisited, e.g., by
Huang et al. 2020
,
Liu and Meyer 2025
.
Several of these contributions, e.g.,
Müller et al. 2017
, lead to fractional-degree interpolation between classical first- and second order stochastic dominance. Building on this observation,
Huang et al. 2020
call their framework
fractional degree stochastic dominance
.

A related perspective, establishing a direct link to fractional integration, is used in

, where a stochastic order grounded in the so-called Riemann-Liouville fractional integral is proposed.
For simplicity, we summarise stochastic orders inspired by these approaches under the umbrella term
fractional stochastic orders

The shortfall that stochastic orders may not yield clear decisions among arbitrary prospects remains.
A second approach is to summarise prospects with a single value, such as a risk measure—see
Artzner et al. 1999
and
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Chapter 4)
—and choose the one with lower risk.
Though coarser than SSD consensus, one can always reach a decision.
Moreover, the weaknesses of the EU model underpinning SSD are well known.
Risk measures are compatible with different decision frameworks and can, e.g., be Choquet integrals.
Not least, risk measures typically comply with the preference of more to less by definition, and many practically relevant examples
respect
SSD by weakly ordering payoffs that are already weakly ordered in SSD. Such an example is the Expected Shortfall (ES) class parametrised by its confidence level, which has been axiomatised by
Wang and Zitikis 2021
.
For further aspects regarding its use in practice, see
Embrechts et al. 2014
,
Koch-Medina and Munari 2016
.

Another related example is portfolio choice in
mean-risk models
studied in
Herdegen and Khan 2021
,
Herdegen and Khan 2024
, where the variance in the classical mean-variance model is replaced by a risk measure.
Such a choice often results in decisions consistent with an underlying stochastic order; see
Ogryczak and Ruszczyński 1999
,
Gotoh and Konno 2000
. We also refer to
Levy 1992
for further discussions on the interplay between SSD and portfolio selection.

This manuscript joins both outlined alternatives and studies risk measures that are consistent with respect to
fractional stochastic orders
that may differ from SSD.
This raises the question
how
exactly the SSD order should be modified.
To this end, inspired by
Leshno and Levy 2002
, we illustrate why it is reasonable from a risk management perspective to exclude overly risk-loving agents from the consensus collective.
Consider two payoff lotteries. Lottery 1 offers a 25% chance to win $1 million and nothing otherwise.
Lottery 2 offers a 75% chance to win $400,000 and a 25% chance to lose $100,000.
While the probability of winning is higher in lottery 2, the potential gain is smaller, and it carries a downside risk. By contrast, losses are excluded in lottery 1.
It is therefore reasonable to prefer lottery 1 over lottery 2. Moreover, a decision maker choosing lottery 2 should be required to hold sufficient risk capital to hedge against potential losses.

To formalise these considerations, fix a twice differentiable, strictly increasing
threshold utility function

v
.
Following
Meyer 1977b
, we modify the SSD order to the
v
-SD order
, where the relevant test utilities
u
are strictly increasing, twice differentiable, and at least as risk averse as
v
:

-\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{u^{\prime}}\geq-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v^{\prime}}.

If
v
belongs to the exponential utility class from above, its risk aversion is constant and we recover the stochastic orders recently studied by
Huang et al. 2020
as interpolations between classical integer-degree stochastic orders.
Focusing on the
v
-SD order also aligns with the recent study
Mu et al. 2024
on monotone additive statistics and the return-based risk measures in
Bellini et al. 2018
.
Note that this approach differs markedly from controls of marginal utilities as in
Leshno and Levy 2002
,
Müller et al. 2017
.
For a comparison of various fractional stochastic orders, see
Mao and Wang 2022
.

Regarding risk measures, their purpose is often not only qualitative—allowing to select the lower-risk prospect—but also quantitative.
Going back to
Artzner et al. 1999
and the suggested axiom of
cash-additivity
, their value often represents the capital required to reduce the risk of a financial prospect to an acceptable level.
Consequently, adding capital to a payoff should reduce risk by the same amount, while withdrawing risk capital leads to an equivalent risk increase.
An abundance of such
monetary
risk measures respect SSD ordering (see
Mao and Wang 2020
or Section
3.1
).

A priori
, the tensions that may arise between cash-additivity and consistency with a general
v
-SD order are unclear though, and we aim to fill this gap.
To honour his initial contribution, we call a monetary risk measure that respects some
v
-SD order a
Meyer risk measure
.

More precisely, we develop comprehensive answers to the following questions:

(1)

Why should one care about Meyer risk measures?

(2)

Do nontrivial Meyer risk measures exist, and if yes, what do they look like?

(3)

Which properties do Meyer risk measures have, and which common risk measure properties
can
they have?

(4)

How do they behave in real-world applications?

Section
3
addresses question (1) and motivates the study from various angles of the literature.
The case of constant threshold risk aversion in
Huang et al. 2020
is shown to play a crucial role.
However, Sections
3.3
and
3.4
suggest strong reasons like local risk-lovingness to keep
v
general.

Question (2) is answered in Section
4
.
Under unbounded risk aversion in the limit, the only Meyer risk measure tends to be the—unrealistically conservative—worst-case risk measure. This helps to explain the special role the CARA threshold utilities of
Huang et al. 2020
play.
In their case, constructive representation results in the spirit of
Mao and Wang 2020
are readily available and deliver nontrivial examples of Meyer risk measures even when
v
exhibits nonconstant risk aversion.
In a more abstract fashion, we show that any
v
-SD-consistent risk measure—existent or not—is the lower envelope of a family of simple
base risk measures
of shape

\rho_{Z,v}(X):=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}\mid Z\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}X+m\}.

Here,
X
and
Z
are bounded random payoffs, and
\rho_{Z,v}(X)
is the minimal capital to add to
X
to make it at least as desirable as
Z
when compared in
v
-SD order (denoted
\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}
).

While these base risk measures appear innocuous, they pose problems that lead to research question (3), which we address in Sections
4.2
and
5
.
First, base risk measures are only invariably
v
-SD-consistent themselves if
v
is CARA.
Conversely, if
v
displays monotonically increasing or decreasing absolute risk aversion in the limit, most of them are not
v
-SD-consistent.
Paradoxically, this means that
v
-SD-consistent risk measures are most naturally represented in terms of simpler risk measures that are not
v
-SD-consistent.
Secondly, risk measures are often additionally assumed to be positively homogeneous (like Expected Shortfall and Value-at-Risk) or to express a positive attitude to diversification.
Apart from the worst-case risk measure, positive homogeneity and
v
-SD-consistency often prove to be irreconcilable.
Similarly, properties like convexity and star-shapedness require the risk to grow at maximal speed when leverage is increased.
Hence, many convex risk measures like the ES from above are not
v
-SD-consistent for nontrivial
v
.
In conclusion, our findings indicate that the axiomatic framework for monetary risk measures is more profoundly and intricately linked to SSD than acknowledged before.

Finally, we approach question (4) in Section
6
by means of two applications.
The first one is inspired by the risk minimisation problem with an SSD-consistent risk measure in
(
Mao and Wang 2020
, Section 5)
. In our case, the objective evaluates payoffs with an SSD-consistent return risk measure, obtained as a transformation of a Meyer risk measure consistent with an exponential threshold utility. We prove existence and uniqueness of the optimal solution, which, in contrast to the original problem in
Mao and Wang 2020
, turns out as a product of different model ingredients.
In the second example, we test the performance of Meyer risk measures based on exponential threshold utilities for real-world time series data. These considerations illustrate, how the decision making depends on the CARA-risk aversion parameter of the investor.

For the reader’s convenience, all proofs are collected in appendices.

2.
Preliminaries and notation

Throughout this paper,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
denotes a fixed underlying atomless probability space.
The space of all real-valued (bounded) random variables on
\Omega
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}
(
\mathcal{X}
, respectively).
Unless specified otherwise, all (in)equalities between random variables hold almost surely (a.s.) under
\mathbb{P}
.

Positive and negative part of a random variable
X
are denoted by
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
, respectively.
The subspace of
\mathcal{L}
of all random variables with finite expectation, i.e., for which positive and negative part have finite expectation, is
\mathcal{L}^{1}
.

Throughout, we distinguish between increasing and nondecreasing scalar functions.

2.1.
Risk measures

A
(monetary) risk measure
is a functional
\rho\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

antitonicity:

\rho(X)\geq\rho(Y)
holds for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
with
X\leq Y
; and

cash-additivity:

\rho(X+c)=\rho(X)-c
holds for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

For a thorough introduction to monetary risk measures, see
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Chapter 4)
.
While risk measures have been studied on spaces different from
\mathcal{X}
containing unbounded random variables, the present setting is convenient and sufficient for our purposes.

The quantity
\rho(X)
is interpreted as the capital that needs to be held in combination with the financial position
X
to limit risk to an acceptable level. This can be seen from the representation

\rho(X)=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}\mid X+m\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\}
,

where the
acceptance set

\mathcal{A}_{\rho}:=\{X\in\mathcal{X}\mid\rho(X)\leq 0\}

collects all payoffs that are already adequately capitalised and do not require additional capital injections.
Financial positions
X\in\mathcal{X}
model payoffs, i.e., net gains instead of net losses.
Antitonicity reflects that an a.s. larger payoff should not require more risk capital to reach acceptability.
Cash-additivity means that an additional sure payoff reduces the required capital by precisely that amount.

The interpretation of risk measures as capital requirements makes also other properties economically meaningful.
A monetary risk measure
\rho
is called:

(a)

law invariant
if payoffs with the same distribution under
\mathbb{P}
are mapped to the same value;

(b)

positively homogeneous
if
\rho(tX)=t\rho(X)
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
t>0
;

(c)

convex
if
\rho(\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y)\leq\lambda\rho(X)+(1-\lambda)\rho(Y)
for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
;

(d)

normalised
if
\rho(0)=0
;

(e)

star-shaped
if it is normalised and
\rho(\lambda X)\geq\lambda\rho(X)
holds for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\geq 1
.

Positive homogeneity reflects the absence of liquidity risk: the risk associated with a larger exposure to a given payoff increases proportionally with that exposure.
Convexity means that diversification does not increase the capital requirement and expresses favour for strategies that simultaneously merge and downsize positions. Convexity is a stronger property than star-shapedness, which promotes downsizing, but does not necessarily encourage merging.
This further indicates that—unlike positive homogeneity—star-shapedness does not rule out the occurrence of liquidity risk. A comprehensive treatment of star-shaped risk measures is
Castagnoli et al. 2022
.

The following are examples of risk measures, with
Expected Shortfall
and the
worst-case risk measure
being particularly important for the subsequent discussion.
For
X\in\mathcal{L}
define
M(X)
as the smallest constant a.s. upper bound, i.e.,

(2.1)

M(X):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid X\leq x\},

where as usual
\inf\varnothing=\infty
. The
worst-case risk measure

\rho^{\mathrm{w}}(X):=M(-X)
,
X\in\mathcal{X}
,

carries this name because
\rho^{\mathrm{w}}(X)\geq\rho(X)-\rho(0)
holds for all risk measures
\rho
and
X\in\mathcal{X}
.
It has properties (a)–(e).
Analogously, the
best-case risk measure

\rho^{\mathrm{b}}(X):=-M(X)
,
X\in\mathcal{X}
,

expresses the most optimistic risk assessment—
\rho^{\mathrm{b}}\leq\rho-\rho(0)
holds for all risk measures
\rho
—and has properties (a)–(b) and (d)–(e).

Now, let
q_{X}
denote a quantile function of
X\in\mathcal{L}
.
The
Expected Shortfall
(ES) at level
p\in[0,1]
, which satisfies properties (a)–(e), is the map

\displaystyle\es_{p}(X):=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{1-p}\int_{0}^{1-p}q_{X}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,&p\in[0,1),\\[-1.72218pt]
\rho^{\mathrm{w}}(X),&p=1,\end{cases}\qquad X\in\mathcal{X}.

2.2.
Stochastic orders

In this section and throughout the remainder of the paper,
let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a nonempty open interval, and
\mathcal{U}(I)
be the set of all increasing and twice differentiable functions on
I
.
We call its elements utility functions.
The Arrow-Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion of
u\in\mathcal{U}(I)
is

R^{A}_{u}(x):=-u^{\prime\prime}(x)/u^{\prime}(x),\quad x\in I.

A key concept of the present paper is the
second-order stochastic dominance (SSD) relation
or
increasing concave order
between random variables.
Consider
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}
whose negative parts have finite expectation:
\mathbb{E}[X^{-}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{-}]<\infty
.
We say that
Y
dominates
X
in second-order stochastic dominance (
X\leq_{\textnormal{SSD}}Y
) if for all
u\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
with
R_{u}^{A}\geq 0
such that the expectations of
u(X)
and
u(Y)
are finite, we have
\mathbb{E}[u(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]
.
2
2

2

For
X\in\mathcal{L}
, the condition
\mathbb{E}[X^{-}]<\infty
is necessary for the existence of some
u\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
such that
u(X)\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
.
Without, SSD is vacuous, as we could have
X\leq_{\textnormal{SSD}}Y
and
Y\leq_{\textnormal{SSD}}X
for all
Y\in\mathcal{L}
.

If
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
, it is known that the above definition is tantamount to requiring that each
risk-averse expected utility (EU) decision maker weakly prefers
Y
to
X
.
Formally, this means that for all nondecreasing and concave, but not necessarily differentiable utility functions
u
on
\mathbb{R}
delivering finite expectations of
u(X)
and
u(Y)
, inequality
\mathbb{E}[u(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]
holds.

In this paper, we go beyond the classical SSD relation and focus on a more general concept:
v
-SD orders
, a broad class of fractional stochastic orders.
These refine SSD and enable interpolation toward the adjacent first-order stochastic dominance.
The most relevant theoretical references are
Huang et al. 2020
,
Meyer 1977b
,
Mao and Wang 2022
.
3
3

3

Importantly,

v
-SD orders differ from alternative stochastic orders that constrain ratios of marginal test utilities, as in
Leshno and Levy 2002
and
Müller et al. 2017
.
See
De Vecchi and Scherer 2024
for the connection of such orders to risk measures.

To define these orders, fix a
threshold utility function

v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
; let

\mathcal{U}_{v}(I):=\{u\in\mathcal{U}(I)\mid R_{u}^{A}\geq R_{v}^{A}\}

be the class of test utility functions representing EU agents at least as risk averse as
v
; and define
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{v}(I)
to be the set of all
X\in\mathcal{L}
that are
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
I
-valued and for which
v(X)
has finite expectation. Whenever
I
is unambiguous from the definition of
v
, we write
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{v}
for short.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a threshold utility function
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
and
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{v}
,
Y
dominates
X
in the
v
-SD order
(
X\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}Y
) if
\mathbb{E}[u(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]
holds for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{v}(I)
such that
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{u}
.

In contrast to SSD, the above definition does not require the threshold utility
v
or the test utilities
u\in\mathcal{U}_{v}(I)
to be risk averse.
Equivalently, no sign constraint is imposed on
R_{v}^{A}
.
In particular,
v
may be (locally) convex, allowing it to represent (locally) risk-loving behaviour.
In case
v(x)=x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, Definition
2.1
recovers the SSD-order.
Concluding, the concept of
v
-SD orders generalises the classical SSD order.

(
Müller and Stoyan 2002
, Theorem 8.1.3)
states a parsimonious equivalent characterisation of
\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}
. For
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{v}
,

(2.2)

X\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}Y\quad\iff\quad v(X)\leq_{\textnormal{SSD}}v(Y).

This equivalence also appears as
(
Mao and Wang 2022
, Proposition 1)
.

Two kinds of threshold utility functions are fundamental for the present study.
The first class are
constant absolute risk aversion (CARA)
utilities defined on
I=\mathbb{R}
. They are represented by the parametrised family
(\mathfrak{e}_{c})_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
of
exponential utilities
on
\mathbb{R}
. The function
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
is given by

(2.3)

\mathfrak{e}_{c}(x):=\begin{cases}e^{cx}&\text{if }c>0,\\[-3.44444pt]
x&\text{if }c=0,\\[-3.44444pt]
-e^{cx}&\text{if }c<0.\end{cases}

In this case, the constraint
R^{A}_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}}(x)=-c
is a constant.
If
c<0
, all test utilities
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}}(\mathbb{R})
are strictly concave (equivalently, strictly risk averse), i.e., the respective order is weaker than the classical SSD-relation.
In case
c=0
, the
\mathfrak{e}_{0}
-SD order recovers the classical SSD order.

If
c>0
, the set of test utilities
\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}}(\mathbb{R})
also contains (locally) risk-loving utilities, and the
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD-order is stronger than the SSD-order.
These orders have been put forward, studied in detail, and generalised to higher orders in
Huang et al. 2020
as a means to interpolate between first- and second-degree (or more general integer degree) stochastic orders.

Another case of interest are
constant relative risk aversion (CRRA)
utilities defined on
I=(0,\infty)
. For a parameter
a\geq 0
, they are defined by

(2.4)

\mathfrak{p}_{a}(x)=\begin{cases}\log(x)&\quad\text{if }a=0,\\[-2.58334pt]
\frac{x^{a}}{a}&\quad\text{if }a\neq 0.\end{cases}

Their relative risk aversion is given by
1-a
, while
R^{A}_{\mathfrak{p}_{a}}(x)=\frac{1-a}{x}
holds for all
x\in I
.
Consequently,
\mathfrak{p}_{a}
is strictly risk averse and with decreasing absolute risk aversion if
a<1
, resulting in the fact that the
\mathfrak{p}_{a}
-SD-order is weaker than the SSD order on
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\mathfrak{p}_{a}}\cap\mathcal{X}
.
More precisely, it holds that

(2.5)

\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{p}_{a}}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{p}_{b}}(I),\quad 0\leq a<b.

We are now ready to define
v
-SD consistency of a functional on random variables.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
be a threshold utility function and suppose
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a nonempty set of random variables.
A functional
\varphi\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
v
-SD consistent
if, for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}^{1}_{v}
,

X\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}Y
implies
\varphi(X)\geq\varphi(Y).

A
Meyer risk measure
is a risk measure that is
v
-SD-consistent for some open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and a threshold utility function
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
.
When the threshold utility function
v
is specified explicitly, we speak of a
v
-Meyer risk measure
.

When functional
\varphi
represents an economic quantity of interest—such as a risk measure—we assume, unless stated otherwise, that
X\in\mathcal{D}
represents a future payoff.
Moreover, Meyer risk measures exist, even though the most immediate example is a trivial one.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
, the worst-case risk measure
\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
is
v
-Meyer.

The discussion below (
2.3
) shows the following sequence of implications for functionals
\varphi\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and constant parameters
a\leq 0<b
:

(2.6)

\mathfrak{e}_{a}\text{-SD-consistency}\quad\implies\quad\text{SSD-consistency}\quad\implies\quad\mathfrak{e}_{b}\text{-SD-consistency}.

Likewise, if
\mathcal{D}
is a suitable domain of positive random variables, (
2.5
) yields the implications

(2.7)

\mathfrak{p}_{a}\text{-SD-consistency}\quad\implies\quad\mathfrak{p}_{0}\text{-SD-consistency}\quad\implies\quad\text{SSD-consistency}.

This is relevant for the upcoming motivation via so-called return risk measures in Section
3.4
.

3.
Why
v
-SD consistency matters in risk analysis

In this section, we provide context for our analysis of
v
-SD consistent risk measures.
While the literature on the interaction between law-invariant risk measures and stochastic orders is a key motivation, the
monotone additive statistics
of
Mu et al. 2024
also naturally lead to Meyer risk measures, for which exponential utilities play a central role.
In Section
3.3
, we discuss the benefits of keeping
v
flexible
a priori
. Finally, we note the natural occurrence of Meyer risk measures when measuring the risk of returns, as in the approach of
Bellini et al. 2018
.

3.1.
Classical results on consistency

The literature contains numerous results about consistency of functionals designed to assess risk or utility with stochastic orders.
These are not only theoretically significant, but also underpin the study of optimal investment decisions that minimise risk (see Section
6.1
below) and enable finding optimal risk sharing schemes (see Section
3.4
).

Crucially, it is often not consistency itself that is presumed, but law invariance of the risk measure in question.
If an agent uses a lower-semicontinuous, convex and law-invariant risk measure as objective—as in the risk sharing study of
Filipović and Svindland 2008
—the conjunction of these properties implies SSD-consistency.
This implication has, e.g., been observed for risk measures with domain
\mathcal{X}
— the case of interest to the present paper—in
(
Cerreia-Vioglio et al. 2011
, Theorem 5.1)
, and for domain
\mathcal{L}^{1}
in
(
Dana 2005
, Theorem 4.1)
and
(
Bäuerle and Müller 2006
, Theorem 4.4)
.
Not least,
(
Bellini et al. 2021
, Theorem 3.6)
extend the result to risk measures defined on general spaces of random variables.
4
4

4

This result is even more general, because it only requires quasiconvex functionals and does not impose monotonicity. The latter necessitates to use the
concave order
rather than SSD.

Given the prominence and applications of these results, several logical questions arise. Which risk measures are consistent with stochastic orders beyond SSD?
What specific properties are required to achieve this consistency?
And how can one construct new types of such consistent risk measures?
In this study, we address all these questions for the broad class of

v
-SD orders, which includes SSD as a special case, but extends significantly beyond it and leads to stronger or weaker consistency concepts, as already illustrated by the implications in (
2.6
) and (
2.7
).
As a byproduct of this level of generality, one may hope to uncover unifying principles.

This motivation is also supported by the existing literature.

Yamai and Yoshiba 2002
define risk measures consistent with higher-order stochastic dominance relations.
The case of the so-called 3-convex order (3CO) is treated in
Bellini and Caperdoni 2007
.
Their Theorem 14—extending
(
Hürlimann 2004
, Theorem 6.3)
—demonstrates that, under a mild condition on the underlying probability space, only a trivial 3CO-consistent distortion risk measure exists, the expectation itself. This negative result shares the spirit of some of our main results here.
Hence, an additional question that requires attention and care is if non-trivial Meyer risk measures exist at all.
As we shall see momentarily, the answer to the latter question is affirmative.

3.2.
Monotone additive statistics

In

3.3.
Why not only CARA threshold utilities?

The discussion in Section
3.2
hints at the fundamental importance of the
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD orders of
Huang et al. 2020
.
This raises the question why we do not limit our attention only to CARA threshold utilities.
Already at this stage, numerous arguments can be put forward to support the general approach via
v
-SD orders.

Firstly, the natural domain of definition of
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
is
\mathbb{R}
.
The definition of
v
-SD orders also admits smaller domains of definition

, which will become important in Section

Secondly, the risk aversion defined by
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
is always constant. If

are necessarily strictly risk averse on all levels of wealth.
If

\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}}(\mathbb{R})
contains a locally risk-loving test utility, then
\mathcal{U}_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}}(\mathbb{R})
fails to represent a consensual aversion to very large losses.

Thirdly—and related to the second point—a flexible
v
encodes more nuanced information about risk aversion that the CARA class fails to capture.
As examples serve

and

, which we introduce next. For the sake of illustration, their risk aversion profiles for various parameter choices are depicted in Figure

3.4.
Risk sharing with return risk measures

Another motivation for this work arises from the study of
return risk measures
(RRMs), first introduced in
Bellini et al. 2018
. RRMs axiomatise the conventional use of logarithmic payoff returns as inputs to classical monetary risk measures, as is commonly done in time series analysis; see, for instance,
McNeil and Frey 2000
,
Candia and Herrera 2024
.
In recent years, there has been a growing body of research exploring the theoretical properties of RRMs.

Laeven et al. 2024
derive a range of representation results for RRMs, while
Aygün et al. 2024
propose a broader, axiomatic framework for their analysis. More recently,
Laudagé et al. 2025
proposed an extension of RRMs based on achieving a specified acceptability criterion by securitisation involving multiple eligible assets.

In Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
below, we show that SSD-consistency of RRMs is equivalent to
\mathfrak{e}_{1}
-SD or
\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
-SD consistency of a corresponding monetary risk measure, directly linking RRMs to Meyer risk measures.

A key distinction between RRMs and risk measures is that the former are defined on the cone

\mathcal{C}:=\{X\in\mathcal{X}\mid X>0~\text{a.s.}\}

of strictly positive elements of
\mathcal{X}
, rather than the full space.
We interpret the elements of
\mathcal{C}
as future payoffs.
For later, we also define the cone of random variables bounded away from 0,

\mathcal{E}=\{e^{Y}\mid Y\in\mathcal{X}\},

which is the norm interior of the positive cone of
\mathcal{X}
.

Given a risk measure
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
and a payoff
X\in\mathcal{E}
(i.e.,
\log(X)\in\mathcal{X}
), consider

(3.3)

\eta(X):=(\exp\circ\rho)(\log(X)).

This expression captures the core idea behind RRMs: logarithmic values are first passed through a classical risk measure
\rho
, and the subsequent application of the exponential function restores the monetary scale of
X
, thereby inducing properties that reflect those of
\rho
itself. Specifically, antitonicity of
\rho
carries over to
\eta
: for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{E}
such that
X\leq Y
, we have
\eta(X)\geq\eta(Y)
.
Similarly, cash-additivity of
\rho
becomes
-1
-positive homogeneity of
\eta
, i.e., scaling a payoff reduces the RRM value by the inverse factor—for all
t>0
and
X\in\mathcal{E}
,
\eta(tX)=t^{-1}\eta(X)
.

These properties are used to define RRMs axiomatically on the set
\mathcal{C}
, without reference to any specific monetary risk measure.
A functional
\eta\colon\mathcal{C}\to(0,\infty)
is an RRM if it is:

antitone:

\eta(X)\geq\eta(Y)
when
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
and
X\leq Y
; and

-1
-positively homogeneous:

For all
t>0
and
X\in\mathcal{C}
,
\eta(tX)=t^{-1}\eta(X)
.

A key difference from the original RRM definition in
Bellini et al. 2018
is that elements of
\mathcal{C}
represent payoffs,
not losses
.
We address this by developing a loss-based alternative.
For comparability, the domain of a loss-based RRM
\kappa
remains
\mathcal{C}
, but elements
L\in\mathcal{C}
now represent pure losses, with associated payoffs
-L
that are negative a.s.
A loss-based RRM
\kappa\colon\mathcal{C}\to(0,\infty)
is defined by:

isotonicity:

\kappa(L)\geq\kappa(L^{\prime})
when
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
and
L\geq L^{\prime}
; and

positive homogeneity

For all
t>0
and
L\in\mathcal{C}
,
\kappa(tL)=t\kappa(L)
.

The counterpart to (
3.3
) is

(3.4)

\kappa(L)=(\exp\circ\rho)(-\log(L)),\quad L\in\mathcal{E},

where, again,
\rho
is a (monetary) risk measure.

Moreover, every RRM
\eta
admits a representation as in (
3.3
) on
\mathcal{E}
via a risk measure
\rho_{\eta}
on
\mathcal{X}
. The latter is given by

(3.5)

\rho_{\eta}(Y)=(\log\circ\eta)(\exp(Y)).

Likewise, any loss-based RRM
\kappa
has a representation using in (
3.4
) the risk measure

(3.6)

\rho_{\kappa}(Y)=(\log\circ\kappa)(\exp(-Y)),\quad Y\in\mathcal{E}.

Turning to the consistency of RRMs with stochastic orders, we note that
Definition
2.2
interprets the functional’s argument as a payoff, not a loss.
It thus applies directly to RRMs
\eta
, but must be adapted for loss-based RRMs
\kappa

Definition 3.2
.

Let
I=(-\infty,0)
and let
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
be a threshold utility.
We call
\kappa

v
-SD-consistent if, for all

with
-L,-L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{v}

-L\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}-L^{\prime}\quad\implies\quad\kappa(L)\geq\kappa(L^{\prime}).

The threshold utility
v
is defined on
(-\infty,0)
to retain the focus on payoffs. For
\kappa

v
-SD-consistency means that an increase of the payoff in
v
-SD-order does not lead to an increase in measured risk.

A question so far unexplored in the literature on RRMs—partly due to interpretational challenges beyond the scope of this paper—is that of
risk sharing
.
Consider a finite collective
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,n\}
of agents
i
, each assessing the risk of payoffs in
\mathcal{C}
using a potentially agent-specific RRM
\eta_{i}
.
The setup is similar when the focus shifts to sharing pure losses.

An aggregate payoff
X\in\mathcal{C}
is allocated among agents via a

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
, a vector with the property
X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}=X
.
Agent
i
receives the random share
X_{i}
.
Notably, fesibility means that all agents profit.
5
5

5

If we consider loss-based RRMs instead of payoff-based ones, the set of feasible allocations reflects a weak form of the
indemnity principle
common in the theory of optimal insurance contracts, preventing overinsurance.

Optimal risk sharing
aims to find
Pareto-optimal
allocations
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
of
X
:
If
\mathbf{X}
is another feasible allocation of
X
satisfying
\eta_{i}(X_{i})\leq\eta_{i}(X_{i}^{\star})
for all

, then this inequality must be an equality for all
i
.
An alternative feasible allocation can only reduce an agent’s risk share by increasing another’s.

To our knowledge, risk sharing based on RRMs has not been studied so far. The literature on risk sharing under classical monetary risk measures is well developed though (see
Barrieu and El Karoui 2005
,
Filipović and Svindland 2008
,
Liebrich 2024
, and the references therein).
As mentioned in Section

, many studies focus on law-invariant and SSD-consistent functionals, for which the search for optimal allocations can be restricted to the subclass of

.
An allocation
\mathbf{X}
of

if there exist nondecreasing scalar functions

such that

for each

Without loss, one can assume that
f_{1}(x)+\dots+f_{n}(x)=x
holds for all
x>0
.
Such allocations not only prevent unfair situations in which a greater aggregate payoff

leads to a lesser share for some of the agents.
If the aggregate results from agents in
\mathcal{I}
pooling their payoffs, comonotonicity removes incentives to underreport earnings.
6
6

6

This corresponds to the
no-sabotage principle
from the theory of optimal insurance contracts.

To potentially apply these classical techniques, one may study risk sharing for SSD-consistent RRMs. The next two results characterise SSD-consistency of payoff-based RRMs

4.
Constructing Meyer risk measures

In this section, we focus on monetary risk measures that respect fractional stochastic orders. As noted in Section
3.2
, the monotone additive statistics of
Mu et al. 2024
include many Meyer risk measures, but with two key limitations. Firstly, the assumption of additivity on independent pairs is very restrictive. Secondly, the threshold utilities
v
in Proposition
3.1
show upper bounded risk aversion, excluding unbounded aversion to severe and increasing losses.

To comprehensively represent all possible Meyer risk measures, we generalise the approach of
Mao and Wang 2020
to SSD-consistent risk measures on
\mathcal{X}
.
Their main result states that every SSD-consistent monetary risk measure is the lower envelope of a family of law-invariant convex risk measures, which are automatically SSD-consistent.
This representation can be refined to a minmax representation involving shifted Expected Shortfalls (see Proposition
4.1
(b) below).
We will demonstrate that this approach also generalises to
v
-SD-orders instead of SSD.
This will be facilitated by the assumption that
v
be defined on all of
\mathbb{R}
; see Remark
B.1
for possible relaxations.

4.1.
Two representations and an impossibility

First, we address the case of CARA (exponential utilities), which the reader can infer from Sections
3.2
and
3.4
to be particularly important.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let threshold utility
v
be CARA, i.e.,
v=\mathfrak{e}_{c}
for some

.

(a)

If

\rho

\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\tfrac{1}{|c|}\log\Big(\tfrac{\es_{p}(-e^{cX})}{g(p)}\Big).

(b)

\rho

\displaystyle\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\;\;\tfrac{1}{|c|}\log\left(\frac{\es_{p}(-e^{cX})}{g(p)}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{m\in\mathbb{R}\mid Y\leq_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}\textnormal{-SD}}X+m\right\},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\text{respectively}\quad\mathrel{\phantom{=}\smash{\sup_{p\in[0,1]}}}\;\;\tfrac{1}{c}\log\left(\frac{g(p)}{\es_{p}(e^{cX})}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{m\in\mathbb{R}\mid Y\leq_{\mathfrak{e}_{c}\textnormal{-SD}}X+m\right\}.

The right-hand sides in (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) have the same structure as the SSD-consistent risk measure on the right-hand side of (
4.4
) and thus suggest that an equally simple and fundamental class of risk measures for general
v
-SD orders is given by the following definition:

Definition 4.2
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
be a threshold utility. For
Z\in\mathcal{X}
, the
base risk measure
under
v
is the functional

(4.7)

\rho_{Z,v}(X):=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}\mid Z\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}X+m\},\quad X\in\mathcal{X}.

The next lemma shows that all expressions in (
4.4
)–(
4.6
) are base risk measures, though only (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) are necessarily convex.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given a threshold utility
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
, each base risk measure under
v
is a risk measure.
Moreover, each base risk measure under
v
is convex if and only if
v
is concave.

A second point to note is that the inner supremum in (
4.2
) is known as adjusted ES in
Burzoni et al. 2022
. If the function
g
comes from the ES-profile of a random variable as in (
4.4
), it becomes a
benchmark-adjusted ES
, a risk measure well-suited for portfolio risk management.
By (
4.4
), this benchmark-adjusted ES compares two indices in SSD. In this sense, (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) extend the concept: the
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD-order compares the indices using a narrower (
c<0
) or broader (
c>0
) class of test utilities. Risk aversion parameter
c
specifies which utility functions the investor deems relevant.

Next, we extend Proposition
4.1
to the case of general
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
and show that base risk measures are the fundamental building blocks of all Meyer risk measures.

Proposition 4.4
.

Given a threshold utility
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
, any
v
-Meyer risk measure
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
can be represented as the lower envelope of base risk measures. More precisely,

(4.8)

\rho(X)=\inf_{Y\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}}\rho_{Y,v}(X),\quad X\in\mathcal{X}.

Together, Propositions
4.1
and
4.4
improve the understanding of Meyer risk measures significantly.
Suppose the threshold risk aversion
R_{v}^{A}
is bounded above by constant
-c
. Using Proposition
4.1
, we can construct an
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-Meyer—and thus
v
-Meyer—risk measure, ensuring the existence of nontrivial examples. Conversely, if
R_{v}^{A}
is bounded below by constant
-a
and
\rho
is a
v
-Meyer risk measure,
\rho
is also
\mathfrak{e}_{a}
-SD-consistent. Concluding, we can either represent
\rho
in terms of exponentials (Proposition
4.1
), avoiding explicit recurrence to
v
, or in terms of
v
-base risk measures (Proposition
4.4
).

In the preceding point, it is crucial though to
presume
the existence of such a
\rho
, because Proposition
4.4
is not an equivalence and
does not
claim that each risk measure of shape (
4.8
) is
v
-Meyer.
While this would follow if each base risk measure were
v
-Meyer, Propositions
4.6
and
4.9
show that
v
-SD-consistency cannot be expected. Additionally, Proposition
4.1
is inapplicable if
R_{v}^{A}
is unbounded above as no
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD-order will be weaker than the
v
-SD order.
It is precisely in this situation that the following widely applicable theorem—the first of numerous impossibility results in the present paper—precludes existence of nontrivial
v
-SD-consistent risk measures.

Theorem 4.5
.

The only normalised
v
-Meyer risk measure is
\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
if threshold utility
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

(4.9)

\liminf_{x\to\infty}R_{v}^{A}(x)=\infty\quad\textnormal{or}\quad\liminf_{x\to-\infty}R_{v}^{A}(x)=\infty.

The proof of Theorem
4.5
confirms a central insight: cash-additivity and
v
-SD-consistency are in fundamental tension, as speculated already in the introduction.
Moreover, condition (
4.9
) is mild, does not preclude lower bounded threshold risk aversion, and is violated by pathological and economically unsound threshold utilities like
R_{v}^{A}(x)=x^{2}(\sin(x)+1)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We conclude by highlighting a consequence of Proposition
4.1
for the shape of SSD-consistent RRMs, a simple example of which can be constructed as follows.
Fix
c=-1
and
Y\in\mathcal{E}
and define a nondecreasing function
g\colon[0,1]\rightarrow(0,\infty)
by

g(p)=\es_{p}(-e^{c(-\log(Y))})=\es_{p}(-Y).

We may set
\mathcal{G}=\{g\}
and define
\rho
via (
4.1
) to obtain the loss-based RRM
\kappa
with (
3.4
), i.e.,
\kappa(L)=(\exp\circ\rho)\big(-\log(L)\big),\ L\in\mathcal{E}
.
Consequently, for all
L\in\mathcal{E}
,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\kappa(L)=(\exp\circ\rho)(-\log(L))=\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\tfrac{\es_{p}(-L)}{\es_{p}(-Y)}.

By Proposition
4.1
,
\rho
is
\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
-SD-consistent; by Proposition
3.4
,
\kappa
is an SSD-consistent RRM. Analogously, we can construct an SSD-consistent RRM
\eta
via (
3.3
).

4.2.
The trouble with base risk measures

By Lemma
4.3
, base risk measures are indeed risk measures, but not always convex.
This subsection will demonstrate that they are not generally
v
-SD-consistent either.
The only exception is constant threshold absolute risk aversion
R^{A}_{v}
, i.e.,
v
can be assumed to be one of the exponential utilities
(\mathfrak{e}_{c})_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The following statements are equivalent for a threshold utility
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
.

(a)

X\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}Y
implies for arbitrary
m\in\mathbb{R}
that
X+m\leq_{v\textnormal{-SD}}Y+m
.

(b)

For each
Z\in\mathcal{X}
, the base risk measure
\rho_{Z,v}
is
v
-SD-consistent.

(c)

R^{A}_{v}
is constant.

One might think that the lack of
v
-SD-consistency in some base risk measures does not matter if most of them have this property. However, Theorem
4.9
will show that the latter is not true for threshold utility functions complying with the following assumption.

Assumption 4.7
.

There is
a\in\mathbb{R}
and an interval
J\in\{(a,\infty),(-\infty,a)\}
such that
v|_{J}
is three times continuously differentiable and absolute risk aversion
R_{v}^{A}
is increasing or decreasing throughout
J
.

Apart from the continuous differentiability assumption, Assumption
4.7
means
v
exhibits strictly increasing or decreasing absolute risk aversion (IARA or DARA) for all sufficiently large holdings or losses. The DARA assumption is economically sound, as argued by
Arrow 1971
. Moreover, the described class of utilities is rich: SAHARA and logistic utilities from Section
3.3
both satisfy Assumption
4.7
, as shown in Figure
2
. Another S-shaped example follows:

Example 4.8
.

For fixed parameters
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
, define

(4.11)

v(x)=\begin{cases}x^{\alpha},&x\geq 0,\\[-3.44444pt]
-(-x)^{\beta},&x<0,\end{cases}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

This
v

à la
Tversky and Kahneman 1992
is not twice differentiable and thus not in
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
. However, a slight adjustment at wealth levels near 0 makes it three times continuously differentiable and fit Assumption
4.7
.
In that case, formula (
4.11
) can be assumed to hold for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
|x|\geq\varepsilon
.

Generally, any utility function that is CRRA for large holdings satisfies Assumption
4.7
.

Our interest in Assumption
4.7
is due to the following main result.
Given a threshold utility
v
, each
Z\in\mathcal{X}
defines a “location family” of base risk measures
\rho_{Z+a,v}
parametrised by
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
In case of constant
R_{v}^{A}
, this operation is a mere additive shift of the base risk measure
\rho_{Z,v}
, i.e.,
\rho_{Z+a,v}=\rho_{Z,v}+a
for each
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and each element of the family is
v
-SD-consistent.
If
v
complies with Assumption
4.7
, we shall see that these families contain base risk measures lacking
v
-SD-consistency.

Theorem 4.9
.

If
v\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{R})
complies with Assumption
4.7
and
Z\in\mathcal{X}
is not
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. constant, then there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\rho_{Z+a,v}
is not
v
-Meyer.

By Theorem
4.9
, there is no reason to expect risk measures of shape (
4.8
) to be
v
-Meyer. The only proven guarantee for their existence, discussed below Proposition
4.4
, requires an upper bound on threshold risk aversion.
The existence of
v
-Meyer risk measures which are
not
simultaneously
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-Meyer for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
remains open, though our results tentatively suggest that none exist.

5.
Impossibility of positive homogeneity and convexity

In this section, we further examine Meyer risk measures.
As Section
4
has shown, there is tension between their
v
-SD-consistency and cash-additivity.
We shall see that these tensions intensify if we additionally ask for properties like positive homogeneity, convexity, or star-shapedness.
In each of the results, we additionally need to impose conditions on the threshold utility
v
in question.

5.1.
Further properties of threshold utilities

We first discuss two such additional assumptions for
v

Assumption 5.1

\liminf_{x\uparrow\sup I}v^{\prime}(x)=0\quad\text{or}\quad\limsup_{x\downarrow\inf I}v^{\prime}(x)=\infty.

5.2.
Impossibility of positive homogeneity

The first impossibility theorem considers the additional property of positive homogeneity.
Under Assumption
5.1
, it turns out that the only positively homogeneous

v
-Meyer risk measure is the worst-case risk measure—an unrealistically conservative risk assessment which respects all
v
-SD orders.

Theorem 5.4
.

If threshold utility
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
satisfies Assumption
5.1
, the only positively homogeneous
v
-Meyer risk measure is
\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
.

Theorem
5.4
has theoretical merits and practical relevance.
In terms of theoretical significance, a comparison to the existing literature is essential.

(
Huang et al. 2020
, p. 4632)
observes that the
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD order (
c>0
) is invariant under additive translations, but not changes of scale. While reminiscent of Theorem
5.4
, our result addresses risk measures consistent with the stochastic order rather than the stochastic order itself. Additionally, threshold utilities
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
with parameter
c>0
do not comply with Assumption
5.1
and are not covered by Theorem
5.4
.

Another instructive comparison can be drawn between Theorem
5.4
and one of the main results from
Mao and Wang 2022
, Theorem 3.
The first statement in said theorem asserts that, for
c\geq 0
, a positively homogeneous risk measure is
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-consistent if and only if it is SSD-consistent.
This implies that many
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD-consistent, positively homogeneous risk measures exist.
Conversely, Theorem
5.4
shows that for many threshold utilities,
v
-SD-consistency, cash-additivity and positive homogeneity do not go together at all unless one deals with the worst-case risk measure.
This asymmetry is fully in line with Table
1
, which records that
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
complies with Assumption
5.1
if and only if
c<0
.
Hence, we also see that Assumption
5.1
cannot be dropped without risking Theorem
5.4
to fail.
Another clarifying observation is the second statement from
(
Mao and Wang 2022
, Theorem 3)
, asserting that every SSD-consistent risk measure is
\mathfrak{p}_{a}
-Meyer for suitable parameters
a
. These threshold utilities are defined on
(0,\infty)
rather than the whole real line.

In this way, Theorem
5.4
complements the results of
Mao and Wang 2022
and deepens the understanding of the relationship between risk measures and stochastic orders.

Theorem
5.4
also has practical relevance for assessing log-return risk, as discussed in Section
3.4
. Following
Bellini et al. 2018
, consider a loss-based RRM
\kappa
on the cone
\mathcal{E}
of losses bounded away from 0, defined via (
3.4
).
This construction starts from the associated risk measure
\rho_{\kappa}
, often assumed to be positively homogeneous.
Proposition
3.4
(a) and Theorem
5.4
together show:

Corollary 5.5
.

A loss-based RRM
\kappa
with positively homogeneous
\rho_{\kappa}
is SSD-consistent on
\mathcal{E}
if and only if
\kappa|_{\mathcal{E}}=M|_{\mathcal{E}}
. Here,
M
is defined by (
2.1
).

In other words, only a trivial
\kappa
can respect the traditional and widely accepted notion of risk aversion in the sense of SSD.
Proposition
3.3
(a) shows that this conclusion applies only to loss-based RRMs, not to payoff-based ones.
In sum, caution is needed when the risk of log-returns is measured.

5.3.
Impossibility of star-shapedness

We now turn to the class of law-invariant star-shaped risk measures studied by
Castagnoli et al. 2022
, which encompasses all
positively homogeneous and normalised convex risk measures.
A key concept is the associated
asymptotic functional

\rho^{\infty}
, a standard object from convex analysis:

\rho^{\infty}(X):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{\rho(nX)}{n},\quad X\in\mathcal{X},

where the preceding limit is necessarily isotone.
By construction,
\rho^{\infty}
is itself a positively homogeneous risk measure with
\rho\leq\rho^{\infty}
.

Theorem
5.6
asserts that, if
v
complies with Assumption
5.3
and
\rho
is star-shaped, each
v
-Meyer risk measure
\rho
satisfies
\rho^{\infty}=\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
.
The value of this necessary—but not sufficient—condition is that it excludes many common star-shaped risk measures from being
v
-Meyer.

Theorem 5.6
.

If threshold utility
v\in\mathcal{U}(I)
satisfies Assumption
5.3
, a law-invariant star-shaped risk measure
\rho
is
v
-Meyer only if
\rho^{\infty}=\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
.

Note that Theorem
5.6
applies to every normalised, convex, law-invariant risk measure.
However, also in that case the condition
\rho^{\infty}=\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
is not sufficient.
Indeed, consider for
c>0
the risk measure
\rho_{c}:=-K_{\mathfrak{e}_{-c}}
—which satisfies
\rho_{c}^{\infty}=\rho^{\mathrm{w}}
—and set
v=\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
. Then
\rho_{c}
is
v
-Meyer if and only if
c\geq 1
.

6.
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-Meyer risk measures in practice

In a nutshell, the results in the preceding sections show that assuming a risk measure is
v
-SD-consistent shrinks the class of admissible functionals immensely, and often excludes other typically assumed properties.
Theorem
5.4
and Theorem
5.6

illustrate the latter tension for positive homogeneity and star-shapedness.
Moreover, the discussion in Section
4
implies that exponential threshold utilities hold particular prominence, even in case of a general threshold utility
v
.

To conclude our investigation, we explore Meyer risk measures for CARA threshold utilities more thoroughly in two applications.
In Section
6.1
, they provide objectives in a risk minimisation context inspired by the results for SSD-consistent risk measures in
(
Mao and Wang 2020
, Section 5)
. Second, we apply
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-Meyer risk measures to time series data in Section
6.2
.

6.1.
Risk minimisation via SSD-consistent (loss-based) RRMs

We develop the optimal solution of a risk minimisation problem for an SSD-consistent loss-based RRM, which can equivalently be expressed as minimisation problem for an
\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
-SD-consistent risk measure. To our knowledge, this particular problem has not been considered in the literature so far.

Our goal is to solve the optimisation problem

(6.1)

\displaystyle\text{minimise }\kappa(L)\text{ over all }L\in\mathcal{E}\text{ subject to }\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]\geq x_{0},

involving the components
\kappa
,
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
x_{0}
, which we specify in detail first. The probability measure
{\mathbb{Q}}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.
Throughout this subsection,
U
is a fixed uniformly distributed random variable such that
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=q_{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}}(U)
; see
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Lemma A.32)
.

Next,
\kappa
is an SSD-consistent loss-based RRM. The risk
\kappa(L)
represents the capital relative to which the loss
L
is deemed acceptable, meaning that its negative log-value has nonpositive risk under the associated classical risk measure.
By Proposition
4.1
(a), it can be represented on
\mathcal{E}
using a family
\mathcal{G}
of nondecreasing functions
g\colon[0,1]\rightarrow(0,\infty)
via

(6.2)

\displaystyle\kappa(L)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\frac{\es_{p}(-L)}{g(p)}.

In order to be able to solve problem (
6.1
),
we impose:

Assumption 6.1
.

Every
g\in\mathcal{G}
is continuous; the associated function

\hat{g}(p):=(1-p)g(p)
,
p\in[0,1]

is concave; and the left-derivative
\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}
of
\hat{g}
is negative, bounded below, and bounded away from 0.

A typical choice for
g
is an ES-profile, i.e.,
g(p)=\es_{p}(-L^{\prime})
for a fixed
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
, which yields the SSD-consistent RRM from (
4.10
).

Thirdly, constant
x_{0}>0
is best understood in the context of an agent taking short positions in a frictionless market with pricing measure
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
A short position leading to loss
L
at maturity (or payoff
-L
) allows the agent to receive capital
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]
at the initial time point.
In (
6.1
), this benefit is constrained not to undercut
x_{0}
.
While larger short positions provide more initial capital, they should also have higher risk, as measured by
\kappa
.
In view of the interpretation of RRMs, the agent solving (
6.1
) determines the minimal own capital relative to which obtaining
x_{0}
with a short position poses acceptable risk. Note also that constraint
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]\geq x_{0}
must be binding for a solution to (
6.1
) because
\kappa
is isotone.

Last, we introduce two Borel measures
\varphi
and
\mu
on
[0,1]
. Following the construction in the proof of
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Lemma 4.69)
, the map
t\mapsto\varphi([0,t])
is assumed to be the right-continuous version of
t\mapsto q_{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}}(t)
and
\mu
is defined via density
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi}(t)=1-t
.

The solution of (
6.1
) is split in two results. The first solves the case of a singleton set
\mathcal{G}=\{g\}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

The following assertions hold for
\mathcal{G}=\{g\}
and

\kappa_{g}^{\star}:=\frac{x_{0}}{\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)}.

(a)

\es_{p}(\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U))=g(p)
for all
p\in[0,1]
.

(b)

Problem (
6.1
) has optimal value
\kappa_{g}^{\star}
and solution

L^{\star}=-\kappa_{g}^{\star}\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U)
.

(c)

L^{\star}
is the a.s. unique solution if
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}
is continuously distributed.

Proposition
6.2
leads to the solution of problem (
6.1
) for a non-singleton set
\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

The following assertions hold for a general set
\mathcal{G}
and

\kappa^{\star}:=\frac{x_{0}}{\sup\limits_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)}.

(a)

The optimal value of problem (
6.1
) is
\kappa^{\star}
.

(b)

If there exists

(6.3)

g^{\star}\in\argmax_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u),

a solution to (
6.1
) is given by
L^{\star}:=-\kappa^{\star}(g^{\star})^{\prime}_{-}(U)
.

(c)

If a solution to (
6.1
) exists and
\kappa(\cdot)=\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\kappa_{g}(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{E}
, then
g^{\star}
as in (
6.3
) exists.

(d)

In the situation of
(c)
, if
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}
has a continuous distribution, any solution to (
6.1
) is of shape
-\kappa^{\star}(g^{\star})^{\prime}_{-}(U)
for some
g^{\star}\in\mathcal{G}
as in (
6.3
).

Using the transformation
L=e^{-Y}
,
problem (
6.1
) can be reformulated as

(6.4)

\displaystyle\text{minimise}\ \rho_{\kappa}(Y)\ \text{over all}\ Y\in\mathcal{X}\ \text{subject to}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-Y}]\geq e^{-y_{0}}.

Here,
y_{0}=-\log(x_{0})
and
\rho_{\kappa}
is the
\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
-SD-consistent risk measure for the set

7.
Conclusion and outlook

We study
Meyer risk measures
, defined by their consistency with a stochastic order from the
v
-SD class,
v
being a threshold utility function. This generalises SSD-consistent risk measures and allows to grapple with risk sharing problems for the recently introduced return risk measures. Among the possible choices for
v
, the family
(\mathfrak{e}_{c})_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
of exponential utilities, displaying constant absolute risk aversion, is crucial. With this choice, formulae for general risk measures are readily available, demonstrating the abundance of nontrivial examples. However, this is not the case for general threshold utilities
v
. While
v
-SD-consistent risk measures can be represented as the lower envelope of a class of so-called base risk measures, the latter are typically not
v
-SD consistent if risk aversion of
v
is not constant.
These observations reveal a clear distinction between Meyer risk measures and the subclass of SSD-consistent ones, which becomes more pronounced when additional properties are imposed.
Notably, only the worst-case risk measure achieves both positive homogeneity and
v
-SD consistency. Similarly, the classes of convex or star-shaped Meyer risk measures are quite limited.

Finally, we apply Meyer risk measures in practice.
We use an
\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
-Meyer risk measure—corresponding to an SSD-consistent RRM—as the objective in a risk minimisation problem. Unlike SSD-consistent risk objectives, which yield an optimal solution with an additive structure, our problem yields a multiplicative structure.
We then empirically evaluate the performance of
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD-consistent risk measures using real-world financial data.

Open questions persist and present opportunities for future research. Firstly, in our study we always fix a threshold utility function
v
and inspect if
\rho
is
v
-Meyer. The inverse route, identifying for a given risk measure
\rho
all threshold utilities
v
for which it is consistent, remains open.
Secondly, the risk minimisation problem (
6.4
), studied for
\mathfrak{e}_{-1}
-Meyer risk measures, could be extended to the more general class of
\mathfrak{e}_{c}
-SD-consistent risk measures with
c\neq-1
.
Thirdly, while
v
-SD orders exclude EU agents with risk aversion incomparable to that of
v
, they still allow arbitrarily high risk aversion.
As noted by
Meyer 1977a
and
Liu and Meyer 2025
, it may be economically desirable to exclude excessively risk-averse investors by imposing a
maximal
relative or absolute risk aversion via a second threshold utility.
Can risk measures respect such refined stochastic orders?
Finally, investigating the risk-sharing problem for RRMs is beyond the scope of this paper.

Proof of Proposition
6.2
.

Part (a) follows directly from
(
Mao and Wang 2020
, Lemma 5.1)
by noticing that
\es_{p}(\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U))=\frac{1}{1-p}\int_{p}^{1}q_{\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U)}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
.

Turning to (b), there are by assumption constants
a,b>0
such that

\kappa_{g}^{\star}a\leq L^{\star}\leq\kappa_{g}^{\star}b
.
In particular,
L^{\star}\in\mathcal{E}
.
By positive homogeneity of the ES and part (a),

\displaystyle\kappa_{g}(L^{\star})=\kappa_{g}^{\star}\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\frac{\es_{p}(\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U))}{g(p)}=\kappa_{g}^{\star}.

For
L\in\mathcal{E}
, the argumentation in the proof of
(
Mao and Wang 2020
, Proposition 5.2)
shows that

(D.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\es_{u}(-L)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)=\int_{0}^{1}q_{L}(u)q_{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L].

The estimate is due to Hardy-Littlewood inequality, see
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Theorem A.28)
. Consequently, if
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]\geq x_{0}
,

(D.2)

\displaystyle\kappa_{g}(L)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\frac{\es_{p}(-L)}{g(p)}\geq\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\es_{u}(-L)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)}{\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)}\geq\kappa_{g}^{\star}=\kappa_{g}(L^{\star}).

This shows optimality of
L^{\star}
, provided we can show that
L^{\star}
satisfies the budget constraint
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]=x_{0}
.
As
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=q_{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}}(U)
and
-\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}
is increasing,
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}
and
g^{\prime}_{-}(U)
are comonotone and hence, the Hardy-Littlewood inequality in (
D.1
) holds with equality for
L=-\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U)
. We then obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L^{\star}]=\kappa_{g}^{\star}\int_{0}^{1}\es_{u}(\hat{g}^{\prime}_{-}(U))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)=\kappa_{g}^{\star}\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)=x_{0}.

To prove part (c)—uniqueness of the solution
L^{\star}
—let
L^{0}
be any solution of (
6.1
), i.e.,
\kappa_{g}(L^{0})=\kappa_{g}^{\star}
.
Using this in (
D.2
), we infer that

\kappa_{g}(L^{0})=\kappa_{g}^{\star}\geq\tfrac{\es_{p}(-L^{0})}{g(p)},\quad p\in[0,1]

and that

(D.3)

\int_{0}^{1}\big(\kappa_{g}^{\star}g(p)-\es_{p}(-L^{0})\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(p)=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\tfrac{\int_{0}^{1}\es_{u}(-L^{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)}{\int_{0}^{1}g(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)}g(p)-\es_{p}(-L^{0})\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(p)=0.

Continuity of the distribution of
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}
implies that
q_{\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}}
is strictly increasing and that the density of the absolutely continuous part of
\mu
is positive a.e. Since the integrand
\kappa_{g}^{\star}g(\cdot)-\es_{\cdot}(-L^{0})
is continuous, (
D.3
) delivers
\es_{p}(-L^{0})=\kappa_{g}^{\star}g(p)=\es_{p}(-L^{\star})
for all
p\in[0,1]
.
By
(
Mao and Wang 2020
, Lemma 5.1)
,
L^{0}
and
L^{\star}
are equidistributed.
Another consequence of optimality of
L^{0}
is that (
D.1
) is an equality.
By
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Theorem A.28)
,
L^{0}
is comonotone with
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}
—and therefore with
L^{\star}
. Hence,
L^{0}=L^{\star}
holds a.s.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

To prove statement (a), set
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\{L\in\mathcal{E}\mid\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]\geq x_{0}\}
. Then

\inf_{L\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}\kappa(L)=\inf_{L\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G^{\star}}}\kappa_{g}(L)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G^{\star}}}\inf_{L\in\mathcal{E}_{0}}\kappa_{g}(L)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G^{\star}}}\kappa_{g}^{\star}=\kappa^{\star}.

For (b), suppose we can find
g^{\star}
as described in (
6.3
) and let
L^{\star}:-\kappa^{\star}(g^{\star})^{\prime}_{-}(U)
.
Then

\kappa_{g^{\star}}(L^{\star})=\kappa_{g^{\star}}^{\star}=\kappa^{\star}
.

Under the assumptions of (c), assume
L^{0}
is a solution to (
6.1
). Select
g^{\star}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\kappa(L^{0})=\kappa_{g^{\star}}(L^{0})
. Hence,

(D.4)

\displaystyle\kappa^{\star}=\kappa(L^{0})=\kappa_{g^{\star}}(L^{0})=\frac{x_{0}}{\int_{0}^{1}g^{\star}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(u)},

which shows that
g^{\star}
satisfies (
6.3
).

Finally, for (d), assume that
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}

Y

6.1
).
From the preceding argument, we obtain a

such that (

) holds. The remainder then follows directly from Proposition
6.2
.
