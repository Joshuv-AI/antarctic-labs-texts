A LSTM-based model predictive control method for unlocking the potential of building energy flexibility in Malaysian commercial buildings
Authors: Quan Wen, Mazran Ismail, Muhammad Hafeez Abdul Nasir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Buildings are regarded as promising energy flexibility resources due to their significant energy consumption and better integration into the electricity grid. To fully exploit the potential of building flexibility in tropical climates, optimized operational strategies need to be developed in which cost savings and thermal comfort should be considered. Model predictive control (MPC) is widely acknowledged as one of the effective methods for developing optimal strategies. However, the practical implementation of traditional MPC is often hindered by substantial computational burdens. This study proposes a Long Short-Term Memory based model predictive control (LSTM-LBMPC) method employing LSTM neural networks to learn and imitate MPC behavior from a dataset containing optimal control trajectories. This method eliminates the need for online optimization, significantly reducing dependency on computational resources. The simulation experiment was performed on a Malaysian commercial office building with a variable air volume (VAV) cooling system under tropical climate conditions. The results indicate that, compared to the baseline control strategy, traditional MPC and LSTM-LBMPC reduced energy costs by 13.89% and 12.75%, respectively, and peak electrical loads by 30.20% and 27.8%, respectively, without compromising thermal comfort. Especially, compared to traditional MPC, LSTM-LBMPC can significantly reduce the computational cost by as much as 99.8%, with only a small trade-off in performance.

Introduction

The global energy landscape is undergoing unprecedented transformation as renewable energy sources become increasingly prevalent in electricity grids worldwide1. This transition, while essential for addressing climate change, introduces significant challenges in maintaining grid stability due to the intermittent nature of solar and wind power generation2. Buildings, which consume approximately 40% of global energy and 30% of electricity, are increasingly recognized as critical resources for providing grid flexibility through their inherent thermal storage capacity and controllable loads.

Managing supply and demand in the electricity grid is becoming more challenging due to the increasing penetration of variable renewable energy sources3. As significant end-use consumers, and through better grid integration, buildings are expected to play an expanding role in the future smart grid4. The concept of building energy flexibility encompasses the ability to modulate energy consumption patterns in response to local climate conditions, user requirements, grid constraints, and electricity pricing signals5. Unlike temperate climates where buildings can utilize both heating and cooling for flexibility, tropical climates present unique challenges and opportunities.

In Malaysia, the energy sector faces unique challenges characteristic of tropical developing economies6. Buildings account for approximately 13–15% of total national energy consumption, with commercial buildings’ HVAC systems consuming 60–70% of building energy due to year-round cooling demands necessitated by the tropical climate’s high temperatures and humidity levels7. The Malaysian government’s National Energy Policy 2022-2040 emphasizes building energy efficiency and demand response capabilities as critical components for achieving carbon neutrality goals by 2050, creating significant opportunities for advanced building energy management technologies.

The consistent cooling demands throughout the year provide stable baseline loads that can be strategically managed, while the thermal mass of buildings can be leveraged for load shifting strategies during peak electricity pricing periods8. However, the tropical climate conditions in Malaysia present additional challenges compared to temperate climates where most existing research has been conducted9. Year-round cooling demands, high humidity levels, intense solar radiation, and limited natural ventilation potential create different control requirements and opportunities.

Predictive control allows buildings to better harness available energy flexibility from the building passive thermal mass10. However, due to the heterogeneous nature of the building stock, developing computationally tractable control-oriented models, which adequately represent the complex and nonlinear thermal-dynamics of individual buildings, is proving to be a major hurdle11. Model predictive control (MPC) is widely acknowledged as one of the most effective methods for developing optimal strategies12,13. MPC can achieve significant energy and cost savings by considering forecasts and exploiting thermal storage effects in buildings14. The approach formulates building control as an optimization problem that minimizes energy costs while maintaining thermal comfort constraints over a prediction horizon15.

However, the practical implementation of traditional MPC in real buildings faces significant computational challenges, particularly for large-scale deployment across building portfolios16. Specifically, MPC demands intensive software and hardware resources due to its reliance on real-time iterative optimization at each control interval17. For a typical commercial building with multiple thermal zones, the optimization problem involves hundreds of decision variables and constraints, requiring sophisticated solvers and substantial computational power18. This computational burden becomes even more pronounced when considering the complexity of control tasks related to energy flexibility and the demand for large-scale implementation of MPC within building automation systems19.

Furthermore, the Malaysian electricity pricing structure, with its specific peak and off-peak hour definitions and demand charges, requires tailored optimization approaches that may not be directly applicable from research conducted in other climatic and economic contexts20. The tropical climate conditions in Malaysia present additional modeling and control challenges, as the year-round cooling demands and high humidity levels create different control requirements compared to temperate climates where most existing MPC research has been conducted.

Therefore, this study proposes a learning-based model predictive control approach using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks, termed LSTM-LBMPC, to address these computational limitations while maintaining the effectiveness of traditional MPC. LSTM networks are particularly well-suited for this application due to their ability to capture long-term temporal dependencies in building energy systems, which is crucial for effective energy flexibility utilization21. The proposed method employs LSTM neural networks to learn and imitate MPC behavior from a comprehensive dataset containing optimal control trajectories generated under various operating conditions representative of Malaysian tropical climate22.

This learning-based approach eliminates the need for online optimization during real-time control, significantly reducing computational requirements while preserving the performance benefits of MPC. The method enables direct output of optimal control strategies in real-time, making it suitable for implementation on resource-constrained building automation systems commonly found in Malaysian commercial buildings.

The main contributions of this work are: (1) Developing a data-driven framework that employs LSTM techniques to rapidly construct LBMPC controllers for demand response directly from optimal control trajectories, thereby eliminating online optimization requirements to facilitate real-time implementation in buildings under tropical climate conditions, with the methodology being generalizable to other buildings through building-specific training data generation; (2) Establishing a high-fidelity co-simulation virtual testbed to enable a fair comparison of control strategies and demonstrate the practical effectiveness of the proposed method in a multi-zone Malaysian commercial building; (3) Assessing the factors that influence the control performance of LSTM-LBMPC controllers and the benefits of demand response in tropical climates, offering valuable insights for practical application.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section “Methodology” outlines the methodological framework, which includes the control-oriented building thermal model, the MPC formulation, the LSTM architecture for generating LBMPC controllers, and the high-fidelity co-simulation virtual testbed. Section “Results” presents the results of model identification, LSTM-LBMPC controller learning, and closed-loop simulation experiments. Section “Discussion” discusses the factors that affect the learning accuracy of the LSTM-LBMPC controller and analyzes how weather and model accuracy impact the benefits of demand response in tropical climates.

Methodology

Framework overview

Figure 1 shows the framework of this study23. The core of this method lies in generating the dataset for optimal control sequences and training LSTM models to imitate MPC behavior. Initially, the building model that is easy to identify and optimize forms the foundation of the entire framework. The resistance-capacitance (RC) model is selected to capture the thermal dynamics of buildings. Subsequently, the MPC optimization problem is formulated based on the RC model, incorporating Malaysian-specific considerations such as TNB electricity pricing structures and tropical climate comfort requirements.Figure 1Framework diagram of the LSTM-LBMPC methodology showing offline and online phases with main components.

Framework diagram of the LSTM-LBMPC methodology showing offline and online phases with main components.

The framework operates in two distinct phases24. In the offline phase, a comprehensive dataset is generated by solving the MPC optimization problem under various scenarios representative of Malaysian tropical climate conditions. The dataset generation process produces 52,560 training samples, which includes 35,040 baseline samples from annual simulation at 15-minute intervals plus 17,520 augmented samples from disturbance scenarios, forecast uncertainties, and operational variations to ensure robust model training25. The LSTM network is then trained on this dataset to learn the mapping between system states, weather forecasts, and optimal control actions. In the online phase, the trained LSTM network directly outputs control decisions without requiring computationally expensive optimization, making it suitable for real-time implementation on resource-constrained building automation systems.

While this study demonstrates the framework using a specific Malaysian commercial building, the methodology is generalizable to other buildings by following the same systematic approach: (1) developing a control-oriented thermal model for the target building, (2) generating building-specific optimal control trajectories under representative operating conditions, and (3) training LSTM networks on the building-specific dataset. The framework’s transferability depends on the availability of building-specific training data and the similarity of operating conditions, making it particularly suitable for buildings within similar climate zones and with comparable HVAC systems.

Control-oriented building thermal model

The resistance–capacitance (RC) model is selected as the control-oriented building thermal model due to its favorable characteristics for identification and optimization20. The RC model strikes a balance between model accuracy and computational simplicity, making it well-suited for MPC applications26. The selection of a second-order RC model is based on extensive literature analysis showing that second-order models provide the optimal trade-off between computational complexity and thermal dynamics representation accuracy for MPC applications27. While first-order models are too simplistic to capture building thermal mass effects, higher-order models (third-order and above) provide marginal accuracy improvements at significantly increased computational cost and parameter identification complexity. Second-order RC models have been demonstrated to achieve over 90% accuracy in representing building thermal dynamics while maintaining parameter identifiability and computational tractability for real-time MPC implementation. For this study, a second-order RC model is adopted to represent each thermal zone, which has been proven adequate for capturing the essential thermal dynamics while maintaining computational tractability.

Simulation study limitations and model uncertainties

While the RC model provides a computationally efficient representation of building thermal dynamics, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of simulation-based studies. The simplified RC model, though validated against high-fidelity EnergyPlus simulations, represents an approximation of complex building thermal behavior. In practical implementation, several factors not fully captured in simulation may impact system performance: (1) sensor measurement uncertainties and calibration drift, (2) weather forecast inaccuracies affecting predictive control performance, (3) occupancy pattern variations beyond typical schedules, (4) IoT system reliability issues and communication delays, and (5) HVAC equipment performance degradation over time. The model-plant mismatch between the RC model used for control design and the actual building thermal dynamics represents a fundamental challenge in translating simulation results to real-world deployment. These limitations are partially addressed through comprehensive robustness testing (“Robustness and sensitivity analysis”) and sensitivity analysis, but complete validation requires real-world implementation studies.

Tropical climate modeling considerations

The tropical climate in Malaysia presents unique modeling challenges compared to temperate climates. The year-round high temperature and humidity conditions require special attention to humidity control and the impact of intense solar radiation29. The model incorporates several Malaysia-specific considerations:

High humidity effects: The model accounts for the latent cooling load, which is significant in tropical climates where humidity control is crucial for thermal comfort. The coupling between sensible and latent cooling loads is modeled through additional terms that capture the relationship between zone humidity and cooling energy consumption.

Intense solar radiation: Malaysia experiences high solar irradiance throughout the year, with peak values often exceeding 800 W/m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2}$$\end{document} on building surfaces30. The model incorporates detailed solar heat gain calculations that account for building orientation, window properties, and shading effects.

Limited natural ventilation potential: Due to high ambient temperatures and humidity levels, natural ventilation provides limited cooling potential. The model reflects this constraint by emphasizing mechanical cooling systems while accounting for the minimal contribution of natural ventilation to thermal comfort.

Model identification and validation

The RC model parameters are identified using a least-squares estimation approach based on comprehensive building operation data generated through detailed EnergyPlus simulations. To clarify the data sources and methodology, the “historical building operation data” refers to a systematically generated dataset that encompasses multiple operational scenarios rather than actual measured building data.

Building Operation Data Composition: The building operation dataset includes: (1) Zone air temperatures for all five thermal zones measured at 15-min intervals, (2) HVAC system supply air temperatures and mass flow rates, (3) Total and zone-level electrical power consumption, (4) Internal heat gains from occupancy, lighting, and equipment following ASHRAE 90.1 schedules, (5) Solar heat gains calculated for all building surfaces with detailed shading analysis, (6) Ambient weather conditions including dry-bulb temperature, wet-bulb temperature, relative humidity, and solar radiation intensity.

Data Generation and Processing: The identification process uses Typical Meteorological Year (TMY3) data for Malaysia, which provides representative weather conditions for Kuala Lumpur. A detailed EnergyPlus building model serves as the ground truth for generating the training dataset, ensuring consistency and accuracy of the thermal dynamics representation. The EnergyPlus model incorporates detailed building geometry, construction materials, HVAC system specifications, and operational schedules representative of Malaysian commercial buildings.

Parameter Identification Methodology: The least-squares estimation minimizes the sum of squared errors between RC model predictions and EnergyPlus simulation results across all operational conditions. The identification dataset covers a full year of operation (8760 hours) with 15-minute intervals, encompassing both dry season (March-September) and rainy season (October-February) conditions typical of Malaysian tropical climate. Multiple operational scenarios are included: normal operation, pre-cooling periods, peak demand periods, and various internal load conditions to ensure robust parameter identification across diverse operating conditions.

The model accuracy is evaluated using coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document}) and coefficient of variation of root mean square error (CV(RMSE)), with target performance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document} > 0.90 and CV(RMSE) < 15% according to established building energy modeling standards31.

MPC formulation

Detailed objective function formulation

The objective function terms are formulated with specific mathematical expressions and units to ensure dimensional consistency and practical applicability. The peak demand term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{peak}(k)$$\end{document} requires careful consideration due to the temporal mismatch between MPC control horizon (24 hours) and TNB’s monthly demand charge billing cycle. In Malaysia’s TNB commercial tariff structure, demand charges are calculated based on the maximum 15-minute average power demand recorded during the monthly billing period, charged at 45.10 MYR/kW/month. To address this temporal scale discrepancy, the peak demand term is formulated as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_{peak}(k) = \alpha \cdot \max (0, P_{total}(k) - P_{baseline}) \quad [\text {kW}] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{baseline}$$\end{document} represents the historical monthly peak demand baseline [kW], and α is a scaling factor (0.00154 MYR/kWh) that converts the instantaneous demand reduction benefit into equivalent energy cost units for objective function consistency.

The inclusion of demand charges in the short-term MPC objective function is justified through the cumulative effect of receding horizon optimization. While individual MPC solutions optimize over 24-h horizons, the continuous application of demand-aware control throughout the month systematically reduces peak demand occurrences. The scaling factor α is derived from the monthly demand charge rate (45.10 MYR/kW/month) divided by average monthly operating hours (720 hours), providing an equivalent hourly demand cost representation. This approach has been validated in commercial building applications where consistent short-term demand management leads to measurable monthly demand charge reductions.

The MPC formulation incorporates several Malaysian-specific factors:

Prediction horizon and control interval

The selection of 15-min control intervals is based on comprehensive analysis of multiple technical factors specific to Malaysian commercial building HVAC systems and tropical climate conditions. The temporal resolution choice considers: (1) HVAC system dynamics where VAV systems with chilled water cooling have thermal time constants typically ranging from 10–30 min, making 15-min intervals optimal for capturing system response while avoiding excessive computational burden, (2) building thermal mass characteristics where the fast-responding zone air temperature (time constant 5–15 min) and slow-responding thermal mass (time constant 2-6 hours) create a dual-timescale system that is effectively controlled with 15-minute intervals, (3) Malaysian smart meter infrastructure that provides energy consumption data at 15-min intervals, enabling seamless integration with existing building management systems, (4) electricity pricing structure where TNB’s time-of-use tariffs change hourly, and 15-min intervals provide sufficient granularity for effective load shifting while maintaining computational tractability, and (5) occupancy pattern dynamics in Malaysian commercial buildings where significant changes in internal loads typically occur over 15–30 min periods, making finer temporal resolution unnecessary.

Sensitivity analysis of different temporal resolutions reveals that 5-minute intervals provide only 2.3% improvement in control performance while increasing computational cost by 300%, whereas 30-min intervals result in 8.7% performance degradation due to insufficient responsiveness to rapid load changes. The 15-min interval represents the optimal balance between control accuracy and computational efficiency for tropical commercial building applications with VAV systems. This choice aligns with established best practices in building MPC literature for similar HVAC system configurations and climate conditions33.

The MPC formulation uses a prediction horizon of 24 hours (N = 96) with a control interval of 15 min, aligning with Malaysian smart meter standards and providing sufficient look-ahead capability for effective load shifting34. The receding horizon approach updates the optimization problem at each control interval with the latest state measurements and weather forecasts.

LSTM architecture for LBMPC generation

Network structure design

The LSTM neural network architecture is specifically designed to capture the temporal dependencies inherent in building energy systems and optimal control sequences. The network consists of multiple LSTM layers followed by fully connected layers to map from system states and forecasts to optimal control actions.

The proposed LSTM architecture consists of five distinct layers designed for optimal performance. The input layer accepts sequences of length 96 (24 hours) with 15 features, providing comprehensive temporal information for decision making. The first LSTM layer contains 64 units with return_sequences=True and dropout=0.2 to capture long-term dependencies while preventing overfitting35. The second LSTM layer uses 32 units with return_sequences=False and dropout=0.2 to consolidate temporal information into a fixed-size representation. The first dense layer incorporates 16 units with ReLU activation for non-linear feature transformation. Finally, the output layer consists of 5 units with linear activation corresponding to the control actions required for building operation.

The sequence length of 96 time steps (24 hours) allows the network to capture daily patterns and utilize historical information for making informed control decisions. The dropout layers are included to prevent overfitting and improve generalization to unseen conditions.

LSTM hyperparameter optimization

The LSTM hyperparameters are systematically optimized through a combination of literature-based initial selection and empirical fine-tuning specific to building control applications38. The optimization process follows a structured approach based on established best practices in deep learning for time series prediction and building energy systems.

Network architecture parameters are determined through systematic grid search and validation. The sequence length of 96 time steps (24 h with 15-min intervals) is selected based on building thermal dynamics literature showing that 24-h historical information provides optimal balance between temporal context and computational efficiency for MPC applications. The first LSTM layer with 64 units is chosen based on the complexity of building thermal dynamics, following recommendations from building energy prediction studies that suggest 50-100 units for multi-zone systems. The second LSTM layer with 32 units provides dimensionality reduction while maintaining essential temporal information, consistent with hierarchical feature learning principles in deep learning literature.

Training hyperparameters are optimized through systematic experimentation with validation-based selection. The learning rate of 0.001 with cosine annealing schedule is adopted from successful building energy applications, providing stable convergence while enabling fine-tuning in later epochs. The batch size of 32 is selected as a compromise between gradient estimation quality and computational memory constraints, following standard practices in time series deep learning. Dropout rates of 0.2 in both LSTM layers are chosen based on building control literature recommendations for preventing overfitting while maintaining model capacity.

The optimization process involves three stages: (1) literature-based initial parameter selection using recommendations from building energy deep learning studies, (2) grid search over key hyperparameters including learning rate (0.0001, 0.001, 0.01), LSTM units (32, 64, 128), and dropout rates (0.1, 0.2, 0.3), and (3) fine-tuning based on validation performance using early stopping with patience of 10 epochs. The final hyperparameter selection achieves validation loss convergence within 50 epochs while maintaining robust generalization performance on unseen test data.

The hyperparameter choices are validated through comprehensive performance evaluation including control accuracy (mean absolute error < 0.5°C), energy efficiency (within 2% of optimal MPC), and computational efficiency (inference time < 10ms per control decision)39. This systematic approach ensures that the selected hyperparameters are both theoretically sound and practically effective for real-time building control applications.

The output layer uses linear activation to allow continuous control values, which are subsequently clipped to respect system constraints during implementation.

Temporal data splitting and validation strategy

The evaluation methodology employs a temporal data splitting strategy that addresses the unique challenges of time series validation in building control applications. Rather than using random data splitting, which would violate temporal dependencies, the dataset is divided chronologically to ensure realistic evaluation conditions.

Temporal Data Partitioning: The 52,560 training samples are temporally partitioned as follows: the first 70% of chronological data (January 1 - September 12) serves as the training set, the next 15% (September 13 - October 27) serves as the validation set for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping, and the final 15% (October 28 - December 31) serves as the testing set for unbiased performance evaluation. This temporal splitting ensures that the model is evaluated on genuinely unseen future conditions, preventing data leakage that could occur with random splitting approaches.

Closed-loop Evaluation Methodology: The closed-loop simulation evaluation uses the same annual weather dataset (TMY3 for Malaysia) as the training data generation, but employs the temporally separated testing portion for performance assessment. This approach is justified for several reasons: (1) it ensures consistent environmental conditions across all control methods for fair comparison, (2) it follows established practices in building control literature where model validation typically uses the same building and climate conditions as training, (3) it enables direct comparison of learned policies against the optimal MPC solutions from which they were derived, and (4) it provides a controlled evaluation environment that isolates the impact of learning algorithms from external variability.

Generalization Assessment: To evaluate generalization capability beyond the training year, additional robustness testing is conducted using: (1) weather data with systematic perturbations (±10% temperature, ±15% solar radiation) to simulate year-to-year climate variations, (2) modified occupancy patterns representing different operational schedules and usage intensities, (3) equipment degradation scenarios simulating aging HVAC systems, and (4) extreme weather events not present in the original TMY3 dataset. These tests demonstrate that LSTM-LBMPC maintains acceptable performance (within 8% of training year performance) under moderate variations, though performance degrades more significantly under extreme deviations from training conditions.

Validation Limitations and Future Work: We acknowledge that using the same annual weather dataset for both training and evaluation represents a limitation in demonstrating true generalization capability. While the temporal data splitting prevents direct overfitting, the learned policies are optimized for the specific climate patterns, occupancy schedules, and operational characteristics present in the TMY3 dataset. Future research should validate the approach using multiple years of actual weather data, different building types, and varied operational patterns to fully establish generalization performance. The current evaluation provides a controlled assessment of learning algorithm effectiveness while highlighting the need for comprehensive multi-year validation studies.

CART-LBMPC implementation details

The Classification and Regression Trees (CART) based learning-based model predictive control (CART-LBMPC) serves as a critical baseline for evaluating the performance advantages of the proposed LSTM approach. CART-LBMPC employs decision tree algorithms to learn optimal control policies from the same training dataset used for LSTM-LBMPC, enabling direct comparison of different machine learning approaches for building control applications.

CART Architecture and Structure: The CART-LBMPC system uses a multi-output regression tree structure where each tree predicts the five control outputs (VAV airflow rates for five zones). The decision tree structure consists of internal nodes that perform binary splits based on input features, leaf nodes that provide control action predictions, and a hierarchical branching structure that captures non-linear relationships between system states and optimal control decisions. The tree depth is optimized through cross-validation to balance model complexity and generalization performance.

Feature Engineering and Input Processing: CART-LBMPC uses the same 15 input features as LSTM-LBMPC but processes them differently due to the non-sequential nature of decision trees. Unlike LSTM networks that naturally handle temporal sequences, CART requires explicit feature engineering to capture temporal dependencies. The input features are augmented with lagged variables (previous 4 time steps) to provide historical context, statistical features including moving averages and standard deviations over 24-h windows, and temporal difference features capturing rate of change in key variables such as zone temperatures and outdoor conditions.

Tree Construction and Splitting Criteria: The CART algorithm employs the mean squared error (MSE) reduction as the splitting criterion for regression tasks. At each internal node, the algorithm evaluates all possible binary splits for each input feature and selects the split that maximizes the reduction in MSE across the resulting child nodes. The splitting process continues recursively until stopping criteria are met: minimum samples per leaf (50 samples), maximum tree depth (12 levels), or minimum MSE improvement threshold (0.001).

Hyperparameter Optimization: The CART-LBMPC hyperparameters are systematically optimized through grid search with 5-fold cross-validation. Key hyperparameters include: maximum tree depth (tested values: 6, 8, 10, 12, 15), minimum samples per split (tested values: 20, 50, 100, 200), minimum samples per leaf (tested values: 10, 25, 50, 100), and maximum features considered per split (tested values: ‘sqrt’, ‘log2’, ‘all’). The optimal configuration achieves maximum tree depth of 12, minimum samples per split of 50, minimum samples per leaf of 25, and considers all features for splitting decisions.

Training Strategy and Validation: CART-LBMPC training follows the same data split as LSTM-LBMPC (70% training, 15% validation, 15% testing) to ensure fair comparison. The training process involves: (1) recursive binary partitioning of the training dataset based on optimal splits, (2) pruning using cost-complexity pruning to prevent overfitting, (3) validation-based model selection to choose optimal tree complexity, and (4) ensemble methods using Random Forest with 100 trees to improve robustness and reduce overfitting. The final model selection is based on validation performance across multiple metrics including control accuracy, energy efficiency, and computational requirements.

Fundamental Differences from LSTM Approach: CART-LBMPC differs fundamentally from LSTM-LBMPC in several key aspects: (1) temporal modeling where CART relies on explicit feature engineering for temporal dependencies while LSTM naturally captures sequential patterns, (2) non-linear representation where CART uses piecewise constant functions through binary splits while LSTM employs continuous non-linear transformations, (3) memory mechanism where CART has no inherent memory and requires lagged features while LSTM maintains internal memory states, (4) scalability where CART performance degrades with increasing temporal horizon length while LSTM maintains consistent performance, and (5) interpretability where CART provides explicit decision rules while LSTM operates as a black-box model.

The CART-LBMPC implementation enables comprehensive evaluation of different machine learning paradigms for building control, providing insights into the relative advantages of sequential neural networks versus tree-based methods for temporal control applications.

All control methods are implemented within the same co-simulation framework to ensure fair comparison under identical weather conditions, occupancy patterns, and electricity pricing. Black-box controller robustness challenges

The LSTM-LBMPC method, functioning as a black-box controller, faces unique robustness challenges compared to traditional model-based controllers. The lack of explicit physical model representation within the neural network makes it difficult to predict system behavior under conditions not well-represented in the training data. To address these challenges, several mitigation strategies are implemented:

Sensor uncertainty handling: The LSTM network is trained with artificially corrupted sensor data to improve robustness to measurement noise. Sensor redundancy is incorporated where multiple temperature sensors provide cross-validation. Outlier detection algorithms identify and filter erroneous sensor readings before feeding data to the LSTM controller. When sensor failures occur, the system gracefully degrades by using virtual sensors based on neighboring zone measurements and historical patterns.

Weather forecast uncertainty mitigation: The training dataset includes scenarios with various levels of weather forecast errors to improve controller robustness. When weather forecasts are unavailable or unreliable, the system falls back to historical weather patterns and seasonal averages. Adaptive learning mechanisms allow the controller to adjust its behavior based on observed forecast accuracy over time.

Communication and IoT reliability: The LSTM controller is designed to operate with cached parameters during communication disruptions. Local backup controllers maintain basic thermal comfort when the main LSTM system is unavailable. Data buffering mechanisms handle temporary communication delays up to 10 min without significant performance degradation. Periodic model updates ensure the controller adapts to changing system characteristics even with intermittent connectivity.

Model-plant mismatch compensation: While the RC model used for training data generation represents a simplified building model, several techniques help bridge the gap to real building behavior: (1) the training dataset includes multiple building configurations and parameter variations to improve generalization, (2) online adaptation mechanisms allow the LSTM controller to adjust to observed building responses, and (3) hybrid control approaches combine LSTM predictions with simple feedback controllers to maintain stability under model uncertainties.

Discussion

Model-plant mismatch and simulation-to-reality gap

A critical consideration in the practical implementation of LSTM-LBMPC is the inherent mismatch between the simplified RC model used for control design and the complex thermal dynamics of real buildings. This model-plant mismatch represents a fundamental challenge in translating simulation-based control strategies to real-world applications.

The RC model, while computationally efficient and suitable for optimization, makes several simplifying assumptions that may not hold in practice: (1) linear thermal dynamics, whereas real buildings exhibit non-linear behavior due to varying heat transfer coefficients, (2) constant thermal parameters, while actual building properties change with weather conditions, occupancy, and equipment aging, (3) perfect sensor measurements, whereas real sensors have noise, drift, and calibration errors, and (4) ideal actuator response, while real HVAC systems have delays, hysteresis, and capacity constraints.

The impact of model-plant mismatch on LSTM-LBMPC performance can be significant because the neural network learns optimal control strategies based on the simplified RC model dynamics. When deployed in real buildings, the controller may exhibit suboptimal performance or even instability if the actual building behavior deviates significantly from the training model. This challenge is particularly acute for black-box controllers like LSTM-LBMPC, which lack the explicit model structure that allows traditional MPC to adapt to model uncertainties.

To mitigate these challenges, several strategies are recommended for practical deployment: (1) comprehensive system identification using real building data to calibrate RC model parameters, (2) robust training data generation that includes parameter uncertainties and disturbances representative of real operating conditions, (3) hybrid control architectures that combine LSTM predictions with model-based feedback controllers for stability assurance, (4) continuous model validation and updating based on observed building performance, and (5) gradual deployment with extensive monitoring to identify and correct performance deviations.

While this study demonstrates promising simulation results, the authors acknowledge that real-world validation is essential to fully establish the practical effectiveness of the proposed method. The simulation-based evaluation provides valuable insights into the method’s potential, but cannot fully capture the complexity and unpredictability of real building operations.

The quality and diversity of training data significantly influence LSTM-LBMPC performance. Analysis of different training dataset sizes reveals that:

Equipment Failure Integration in Training Data: Equipment failures are systematically incorporated into the training dataset through degraded system modeling and fault scenario simulation. Four categories of equipment failures are considered: (1) HVAC equipment failures including chiller capacity reduction (10–50%), AHU fan efficiency degradation (15–30%), and VAV damper positioning errors (± 10% of commanded position), (2) sensor failures including temperature sensor drift (±2°C bias), humidity sensor calibration errors (± 5% RH), and power meter communication loss (replaced with estimated values), (3) actuator failures including valve sticking (fixed at current position), damper positioning errors (±15% deviation), and pump speed control issues (±20% of commanded speed), and (4) control system failures including BMS communication delays (30 s to 5 min) and partial sensor data loss (10–30% of sensors unavailable).

Each failure scenario is modeled by modifying the building thermal dynamics and HVAC system equations to reflect degraded performance characteristics. Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated an LSTM-based model predictive control (LSTM-LBMPC) method for unlocking building energy flexibility potential in Malaysian commercial buildings under tropical climate conditions. The proposed method addresses the computational limitations of traditional MPC while maintaining effective control performance through offline learning of optimal control strategies.

Key findings

The main findings of this research demonstrate the effectiveness of the LSTM-LBMPC approach across multiple performance dimensions. In terms of energy performance, the LSTM-LBMPC method achieved a significant 12.75% reduction in energy costs and a 27.8% reduction in peak load compared to baseline control methods. The performance gap with traditional MPC was limited to only 1.36%, which validates the efficacy of the learning-based approach. Furthermore, the system consistently delivered energy savings throughout the year, with an average of 11.2% savings during the dry season and 9.8% during the rainy season. Additionally, the method successfully shifted 18% of cooling energy from peak to off-peak periods.

In terms of computational efficiency, the LSTM-LBMPC approach achieved a remarkable 99.8% reduction in computational time compared to traditional MPC, reducing the time from 1,458 seconds per day to just 3.3 seconds per day. The system also demonstrated real-time inference capability with an average decision time of 0.8 milliseconds. It requires minimal hardware, with only 45 MB of memory consumption and less than 5% CPU utilization, and features a scalable architecture suitable for buildings with 5 to over 50 thermal zones.

The adaptation to tropical climate conditions was also successful. The LSTM-LBMPC method effectively managed the year-round cooling demands typical of Malaysian tropical climates, handling high humidity and intense solar radiation efficiently. It showed robust performance across seasonal variations, such as the dry and rainy seasons, and integrated Malaysian-specific factors, including TNB pricing, cultural holidays, and operational practices.

Finally, the superiority of the method was validated through several aspects. LSTM networks outperformed CART-based approaches in temporal pattern recognition, offering superior control consistency and robustness against sensor failures and communication disruptions. The method also adapted better to changing weather patterns and occupancy variations. Enhanced feature importance analysis revealed optimal input selection strategies, further underscoring the method’s effectiveness.

Technical contributions

This study makes several pivotal technical contributions to the domain of building energy control. Firstly, it pioneers the comprehensive application of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks for offline learning in building Model Predictive Control (MPC). This approach demonstrates superior temporal dependency modeling capabilities when compared to traditional machine learning methods, facilitated by the development of a specialized LSTM architecture tailored for building control applications. The effectiveness of this learning-based MPC is validated under realistic operating conditions, showcasing its practical viability.

In addressing the challenges posed by tropical climates, the study validates the effectiveness of learning-based MPC under such conditions, thereby filling a significant research gap. It develops control strategies specifically designed to manage high humidity and intense solar radiation, and introduces a methodology for addressing year-round cooling demands without the complications of a heating season. Additionally, a framework is established to incorporate monsoon season variations into control optimization, enhancing the adaptability of the system.

The research further contributes by developing a localization framework tailored to Malaysian conditions. This includes the adaptation of control strategies to the Malaysian electricity pricing structure (TNB tariff), integration with Malaysian building standards (MS 1525:2019), and consideration of cultural factors such as prayer times and public holidays. An economic analysis framework is also provided, based on the characteristics of Malaysian commercial buildings.

Methodological innovations are a cornerstone of this study, with the introduction of a high-fidelity co-simulation platform that allows for a fair comparison of control strategies. A comprehensive robustness testing framework is developed to validate real-world deployment, alongside a feature importance analysis methodology for optimal sensor selection. Furthermore, the study proposes a prediction horizon optimization approach specifically for applications in tropical climates, enhancing the precision and efficiency of the control strategies.

Regarding the framework’s applicability, it is important to clarify that while the LSTM-LBMPC methodology is generalizable, it is not automatically applicable to different buildings without adaptation. This represents a significant advancement over traditional MPC by eliminating real-time optimization requirements while maintaining the need for building-specific calibration.

Research limitations and future work

Despite the significant achievements of this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. The current research is limited to a single building case study, restricting validation to one representative commercial building type. The evaluation is primarily simulation-based, with real-world deployment validation yet to be completed. Additionally, the analysis is based on typical meteorological year data, limiting extreme weather testing, and focuses on a single HVAC system type, specifically VAV systems with chilled water cooling.

More fundamentally, this study faces the inherent limitations of simulation-based research in building control. The high-fidelity co-simulation environment, while comprehensive, cannot fully replicate the complexity and unpredictability of real building operations. Critical factors that may significantly impact practical performance include: (1) sensor measurement uncertainties and calibration drift that are difficult to model accurately, (2) weather forecast errors and their propagation through the predictive control system, (3) occupancy pattern variations beyond typical schedules, including cultural and behavioral factors specific to Malaysian commercial buildings, (4) IoT system reliability issues, communication delays, and cybersecurity concerns, (5) HVAC equipment performance degradation, maintenance issues, and non-ideal actuator responses, and (6) the fundamental model-plant mismatch between the simplified RC model used for control design and the complex thermal dynamics of real buildings.

The black-box nature of the LSTM controller presents additional challenges for practical deployment, as it lacks the transparency and interpretability of traditional model-based controllers. This makes it difficult to diagnose performance issues, ensure safety constraints, and gain acceptance from building operators. The method’s dependence on high-quality training data also means that performance may degrade when deployed in buildings with characteristics significantly different from the training scenarios.

Furthermore, the economic analysis, while based on realistic Malaysian electricity tariffs and building characteristics, relies on simulation-derived energy savings that may not be fully achievable in practice due to the factors mentioned above. The payback period calculations should therefore be considered as optimistic estimates that require validation through real-world deployment studies.

Future research should address these limitations by expanding validation to diverse building types, including hospitals, hotels, and retail establishments. Real-world deployment through pilot projects in Malaysian commercial buildings is essential. Testing under climate change scenarios and extreme events will enhance resilience. System integration with renewable energy systems and energy storage should be explored, alongside the investigation of advanced algorithms such as transformer networks. Portfolio optimization methods for coordinated control of multiple buildings are also recommended.

Addressing the simulation-to-reality gap requires comprehensive real-world validation studies that systematically quantify the impact of practical implementation challenges. Priority should be given to: (1) pilot deployment in actual Malaysian commercial buildings with extensive monitoring of sensor accuracy, communication reliability, and control performance, (2) development of uncertainty quantification frameworks that explicitly model sensor noise, weather forecast errors, and model-plant mismatch, (3) creation of adaptive control strategies that can automatically adjust to changing building characteristics and operating conditions, (4) investigation of hybrid control architectures that combine the efficiency of LSTM-LBMPC with the robustness and interpretability of traditional controllers, and (5) development of comprehensive commissioning and validation protocols for learning-based building control systems.

Long-term research should focus on developing more robust and interpretable learning-based control methods that can handle the uncertainties and complexities of real building operations. This includes investigating physics-informed neural networks that incorporate building physics knowledge, developing online learning capabilities that allow controllers to adapt to changing conditions, and creating comprehensive digital twin platforms that can bridge the gap between simulation and reality.
