De novo haplotype-resolved genome assembly of the endemic kiwifruit Actinidia hubeiensis
Authors: Zhi Wang, Guangming Hu, Yonghong Zhang, Lei Gao, Xuan Luo, Qin Xiao, Lixia Ye, Qiong Huang, Tao Xiao, Lei Zhang, Quan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Actinidia, which encompasses the widely cultivated kiwifruit, is characterized by its rich species diversity. Wild Actinidia species serve as invaluable germplasm reservoirs for crop improvement. As an important kiwifruit species, Actinidia hubeiensis represents a unique taxonomic group endemic to Hubei Province, contributing valuable genetic diversity to the genus Actinidia. Here, we present a haplotype-resolved genome assembly for A. hubeiensis. The two haplotype assemblies (Hap1 and Hap2) spanned 658.03 Mb (N50 = 23.16 Mb) and 597.19 Mb (N50 = 20.89 Mb), encoding 35,741 and 36,647 high-confidence protein-coding genes, respectively. Based on comprehensive assessments, both haplotypes demonstrated high completeness (BUSCO completeness > 99%), excellent continuity (LAI up to 21.67), low base-error rates (QV > 40), and nearly complete read mapping rates (> 98%). This genome assembly provides crucial genomic resources for the genus, enriching our understanding of kiwifruit biodiversity and offering new insights into the genetic background and evolutionary characteristics of this distinctive species.

Background & Summary

Wild plant germplasm resources serve as invaluable reservoirs of genetic diversity, playing a crucial role in crop improvement and evolutionary studies1–3. Kiwifruit, as an important horticultural crop, has garnered widespread attention, and genomic and molecular biology research on this plant has been increasing steadily. Although multiple kiwifruit genome sequences have been reported in recent years4–7, genomic resources for strictly geographically endemic wild Actinidia species remain nearly absent. Such endemics are inherently limited in number across the genus, and no reference-quality genome assemblies for any qualifying taxon have been published to date. Here, we present a haplotype-resolved assembly of Actinidia hubeiensis, an endangered Hubei-endemic species, to fill this critical gap.

A. hubeiensis, a species endemic to Hubei Province (the cradle of the global kiwifruit industry8), was first collected in Yichang in 1978, over forty years ago, and was formally described by Sun and Huang9. Since its discovery, it has been maintained in extremely limited quantities through ex situ conservation at the National Germplasm Resources Repository of Kiwifruit (Wuhan, China). Preliminary studies on this species suggested that it might serve as a case of interspecific hybridization within the Actinidia genus10. Furthermore, it appeared to be closely linked with the origin or domestication of a specific haplotype in the promoter region of the AcSWEET9b gene, which regulates fruit sweetness in commercial red-fleshed cultivars11. Remarkably, natural populations of A. hubeiensis have been sporadically rediscovered in recent years. However, these wild populations are extremely rare and under severe threats from deforestation and habitat loss12, which has driven many other regionally endemic Actinidia species toward extinction. Indeed, 63.2% of Actinidiaceae species are assessed as threatened, which represents the second highest among Chinese angiosperm families13, with ≥ 10 Yangtze Basin endemics listed as Critically Endangered (CR) or Endangered (EN)12. Given its critically endangered status and potential values for genetic breeding, there is an urgent need to study A. hubeiensis to support effective conservation strategies and improve our understanding of genomic diversity in Actinidia.

In this study, we de novo assembled a haplotype-resolved genome of wild A. hubeiensis, yielding an assembled size of 658.03 Mb with an N50 of 23.16 Mb and 597.19 Mb with an N50 of 20.89 Mb for both haplotypes, respectively. Of these, 45.41% (298.81 Mb) of the Hap1 assembly and 41.20% (246.03 Mb) of the Hap2 assembly were composed of repeat elements. In addition, we annotated a total of 35,741 and 36,647 protein-coding genes for Hap1 and Hap2. The genome assembly of A. hubeiensis provides new insights into the genetic background and evolutionary characteristics of this distinctive species, further enriching the crucial genomic resources of Actinidia and enhancing the understanding of kiwifruit biodiversity.

Methods & Results

Materials collection, sequencing technologies and ploidy estimation

The mature plant used in this study was cultivated in the kiwifruit germplasm conservation nursery of Shiyan city (32.5° N, 110.8° E) in Hubei province since 2025 (Fig. 1). Fresh leaves collected from this individual were frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen for subsequent use. High-quality total DNA was extracted with modified CTAB14 to prepare a next-generation library, a PCR-free SMRTbell library and a high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) library. The SMRTbell library was sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and raw subreads generated from PacBio single-molecule sequencing were saved in BAM files with sequencing adapters and low-quality reads removed during this process. Quality assessment and filtering of HiFi reads were performed using the SMRT Link v12.0 (https://www.pacb.com/support/software-downloads/) for downstream analyses with recommended parameters. In addition, tissues from fruits, stems and leaves were also sampled for RNA extraction and library construction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Both Hi-C sequencing and RNA-seq were performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. The raw data of NGS were filtered using fastp v1.0.115 with default parameters for downstream analysis.

Sample location and morphological characteristics of A. hubeiensis in this study. (a) Sample location. (b) Leaves and fruits of A. hubeiensis in natural environment.

In addition, we used flow cytometry to estimate the ploidy of A. hubeiensis. The detailed procedure followed Li et al.16 with minor modifications. Fresh leaves from the sample were chopped together with leaf tissue of an internal standard (a diploid Actinidia chinensis var. chinensis cultivar, ‘Hongyang’, 2n = 2x = 58)17 in 0.5 mL ice-cold Otto I buffer (High-Resolution Kit, Partec, Germany). The homogenate was filtered through a 30 μm nylon mesh, followed by the addition of 1 mL Otto II buffer containing 4 μg/mL DAPI. The mixture incubated at 4 °C for 5 min, which was used to estimate the ploidy levels on a CyFlow Ploidy Analyser (Partec, Germany) automatically.

Genome survey

Flow cytometry analysis suggested that A. hubeiensis was diploid (Fig. 2a). In addition, a total of 37.83 Gb of 150 bp paired-end data were generated for genome survey (Table 1). K-mer (21-mer) frequency distribution was analyzed using Meryl18 and GenomeScope v2.019, which estimated a haploid genome size of approximately 615 Mb for A. hubeiensis, with a high level of heterozygosity (~6%) (Fig. 2b).

Chromosome-level haplotype-resolved genome assembly

Approximately 26 Gb of PacBio HiFi long reads with an N50 of 17.5 Kb were assembled using HiCanu20 with default parameters (Table 1). The primary assembly had a total size of 1.29 Gb and consisted of 809 contigs, with a contig N50 of 16.39 Mb and a contig N90 of 6.80 Mb (Table 2), consistent with the diploid genome size estimated from the genome survey (2n = 2x). In addition, the L50 and L90 values of assembly were 30 and 74, respectively.

Using a chromosome-level scaffolding strategy via HapHiC v1.0.721 with 100 Gb clean data according to the software’s default instructions, 97.57% (1.26 Gb) of the assembled sequences were successfully anchored onto 58 pseudochromosomes (Table 2). These pseudochromosomes were further phased into two haplotype-resolved assemblies, designated Hap1 and Hap2 (Fig. 3). To standardize chromosome numbers and orientations of the A. hubeiensis genome, both haplotypes were re-assigned using the most recently published A. chinensis genome22 as a reference by RagTag v2.1.023 with default parameters. In addition, we performed synteny analysis with JCVI v1.6.524 (cscore = 0.99), which confirmed strong collinearity between the two species, further supporting the validity of our chromosome-level assignment (Fig. 4). The Hap1 assembly had a total size of 658.03 Mb with an N50 of 23.16 Mb, while Hap2 spanned 597.19 Mb with an N50 of 20.89 Mb (Table 3). Both haplotypes were organized into 29 pseudochromosomes, with the largest chromosome reaching 30.22 Mb in Hap1 and 27.57 Mb in Hap2, and the shortest measuring 16.31 Mb in Hap1 and 14.35 Mb in Hap2. The numbers of gaps was limited (20 in Hap1 and 24 in Hap2), and the GC content was highly consistent between haplotypes (35.33% and 35.42%, respectively) (Table 3; Fig. 5).

Comparison between two haplotypes

Structural variation analysis was performed to characterize genome sequence differences between the two assemblies using Minimap2 v2.30-r128725 with asm5 and the --eqx options, SYRI v1.7.126 (-F B --nosnp) and visualized by plotsr v1.1.027 with parameters -H 10 -W 7 -S 0.5 to set the figure height, width, and scaling factor, respectively. A total of 1,118 syntenic regions were identified, spanning approximately 507.8 Mb in Hap1 and 464.1 Mb in Hap2. The analysis also revealed 145 inversions (~44.5 Mb) and 1,004 translocations (~3.4 Mb) between the two assemblies. In addition, we identified 671 duplications in Hap1 (~2.8 Mb) and 516 duplications in Hap2 (~1.4 Mb) (Fig. 6).

Gene structure prediction and functional annotation

Transposable elements (TEs) were annotated using the Extensive de novo TE Annotator (EDTA) v2.2.228 with the --anno 1 option. The results showed that repetitive elements occupied a substantial proportion of both haplotype-resolved assemblies. In Hap1, a total of 298.81 Mb of repetitive sequences were identified, accounting for 45.41% of the assembly, while Hap2 contained 246.03 Mb, representing 41.20% of the genome (Table 4; Fig. 5). In these repeat elements, LTR retrotransposons were the dominant repeat class in both haplotypes, comprising 186.58 Mb (28.35%) and 143.46 Mb (24.02%) for Hap1 and Hap2, respectively, indicating that LTR elements are the major contributors to genome size variations between the two haplotypes, followed by TIR, LINEs and SINEs, which together accounted for a much smaller proportion of the assemblies.

The library of TE annotations was subsequently used to generate a soft-masked genome through RepeatMasker v4.1.229 using --xsmall option. Gene structure prediction was performed with Braker330 which integrates evidence from ab initio prediction, homology-based proteins, and RNA-seq data of fruits, leaves and stems. For transcriptome data, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the soft-masked genome assembly using HISAT2 (v2.2.4)31 with the --dta option. Only complete protein-coding genes encoding at least 50 amino acids were retained for further functional annotation, which was performed using (i) InterProScan v5.76-107.032 with the -appl CDD, Pfam option; (ii) Diamond v2.1.14.16833 against both the Swiss-Prot and UniProt databases separately, with an E-value cutoff of 1e-5; (iii) EggNOG-mapper v234 using thresholds of E-value ≤ 1e-5, score ≥ 60, and percent identity ≥ 40%. According to this pipeline, we annotated a total of 35,741 and 36,647 high-confidence protein-coding genes for Hap1 and Hap2 (Table 3; Fig. 5), respectively. Of the 35,741 genes in Hap1, 29,917 (83.7%) showed significant matches in at least one public database, whereas 34,900 of the 36,647 genes in Hap2 (95.2%) were successfully annotated (Table 5). InterProScan searches identified conserved domains in 10,768 genes with CDD domains and 25,035 genes with PFAM domains in Hap1, and in 13,871 (CDD) and 29,976 (PFAM) genes in Hap2, respectively. A total of 29,714 and 34,374 genes in Hap1 and Hap2 were annotated via EggNOG-mapper. In addition, DIAMOND searches against Swiss-Prot and UniProt annotated 23,705/23,738 genes in Hap1 and 28,108/28,141 genes in Hap2, respectively.

Technical Validation

The completeness of genome assembly and gene annotation was evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) v6.0.0 with default parameters using the eudicots_odb12 database45. The results showed that both haplotypes demonstrated high-quality genome assembly and annotation, with completeness exceeding 99% in both the genome and gene set assessments (Fig. 7). Fragmentation was minimal (below 0.3%), and the proportion of missing genes was below 1.1%. All the above results indicated a high level of completeness for both the genome assembly and the gene annotation.

BUSCO assessment results of genome and annotation for both haplotypes of A. hubeiensis.

The assembly continuity was further assessed using the LTR Assembly Index (LAI)46, which was estimated with LTRharvest47 and LTR_FINDER_parallel v1.33548 and LTR_retriever v3.0.449 using the default parameters. The LAI values reached 21.67 for Hap1 and 17.26 for Hap2 (Table 3), indicating that the Hap1 assembly meets the standard of a gold-quality reference genome, while Hap2 also shows high assembly continuity. We further calculated the base-level accuracy of the genome with quality values (QV) using Merqury v1.350 based on short-read data. The QV scores were 41.23 for Hap1 and 40.74 for Hap2, reflecting a low base-level error rate in both haplotype assemblies (Table 3).

In addition, we realigned the NGS data and HiFi reads to the genome sequence using BWA v0.7.17-r11883051 and Minimap2 v2.30-r128725, respectively, both with default parameters. The mapping rates of short reads reached 98.17% and 98.19% for Hap1 and Hap2, while HiFi reads showed mapping rates of 99.83% and 99.85%, respectively (Table 1). In summary, these results including the high BUSCO completeness, elevated LAI values, high QV scores, and excellent read-mapping rates suggested our genome assembly is of high quality and high confidence.
