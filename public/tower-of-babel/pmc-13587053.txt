A rapid visual detection system for nitrogen-fixation activity in soybean root nodules
Authors: Min Zhou, Dan-Xun Zhang, Zhi-An Liu, Yuan Wang, Zi-Ting Ou, Xiang-Bin Zhong, Chang-Fu Tian, Jian Jiao, Zhi-Chang Chen
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Nitrogenase, the key enzyme for biological nitrogen fixation, has attracted significant attention due to its important role in improving nitrogen use efficiency in crops and reducing our dependence on chemical fertilizers. In legume root nodules, nitrogenase from endosymbiotic rhizobia catalyzes the reduction of nitrogen gas to ammonia, providing nitrogen to the host plant and directly influencing crop yield and quality [1,2]. The most commonly used techniques for determining nitrogenase activity in root nodules are the acetylene reduction assay [3,4] and the 15N tracer method [5]. Although these methods are highly sensitive and accurate, they are expensive, time-consuming, and require specialized equipment, limiting their suitability for large-scale studies. Therefore, there is a growing need for a rapid, sensitive, visually intuitive system for assessing nitrogenase activity in root nodules.

Here, we propose a reporter gene-based biosensing strategy for the rapid visual assessment of symbiotic nitrogen-fixation activity. In this approach, green fluorescent protein (GFP), red fluorescent protein (RFP), β-glucuronidase (GUS), and bacterial luciferase (LUX) are employed as visually detectable reporter molecules to monitor the expression of nitrogen fixation–related genes in rhizobia. Because the transcriptional activity of these genes is closely associated with nitrogenase enzymatic activity, reporter-derived signals can serve as indirect but intuitive proxies for nitrogen-fixation activity within root nodules.

We first constructed four transgenic strains of Sinorhizobium fredii CCBAU45436, each containing the reporter gene GFP, RFP, GUS or LUX (luxCDABE) driven by the NPTⅡ (aminoglycoside 3’ phosphotransferase II) promoter [6], using the triparental mating method [7] (Supplementary Methods). These four transgenic strains successfully expressed their respective reporter genes when grown in culture medium (Fig. 1A) and were designated as SF-GFP, SF-RFP, SF-GUS, and SF-LUX, respectively. Upon inoculating soybean (Glycine max) nodules with these strains, all reporter genes were successfully expressed under symbiotic conditions (Fig. 1B and C), with their expression levels in nodules increasing over time (Fig. 1B–D). Although the overexpression of exogenous genes, particularly the LUX system, is generally recognized as an energetically and oxygen-demanding process, soybean plants and nodules inoculated with the recombinant rhizobia exhibited no significant phenotypic differences compared to the wild-type controls (Fig. S1). The fluorescence signals from nodules inoculated with SF-GFP and SF-RFP were accompanied by strong background autofluorescence, while GUS detection in nodules inoculated with SF-GUS requires in vitro staining, making it unsuitable for real-time monitoring. The LUX signals from nodules inoculated with SF-LUX were free from these limitations (Fig. 1B–D). However, the LUX signals did not significantly change from 14 to 22 days post inoculation (dpi), during which nitrogen-fixation activity progressively increased (Fig. 1D). This suggests that the correlation of the NPTII promoter-driven LUX gene to nitrogen-fixation activity remained insufficient.

To identify promoters that exhibit high correlation to nitrogen-fixation activity in nodules, we compared the transcriptomes of bacteroids under high-vs. low-nitrogen treatment of their host plants, as well as between bacteroids from the soybean vtl1-1 mutant [8], which lose their nitrogen-fixing ability due to an iron deficiency, and bacteroids from wild-type soybean (Fig. S2). Given that high-nitrogen treatment and using the vtl1-1 mutant as the host both significantly reduce nitrogen-fixation activity, we looked for overlapping downregulated genes from the two transcriptomic datasets, identifying 55 common genes (Fig. S2). From these genes, we selected 15 candidate genes based on the relative strength of their downregulation (Table S1).

We subsequently constructed 15 transgenic S. fredii strains, each carrying the LUX gene driven by one of the 15 candidate promoters. These strains were inoculated onto wild-type and vtl1-1 soybean plants to assess the correlation of LUX expression with nitrogenase activity. At 21 dpi, 13 strains showed reduced LUX signals in nodules on vtl1-1 plants (Fig. 1E), indicating that these promoters correlated to nitrogen-fixation activity. From these, we selected three strains that exhibited the strongest LUX signal responses and highest overall signal intensities for further investigation: SF-pc31030, SF-pb51400, and SF-pc16350.

We inoculated the soybean mutants sultr2;1ab and sultr3;5ab, which are defective in nitrogen fixation, with these three recombinant strains [9] and obtained comparable results (Fig. S3). We then subjected soybean nodules inoculated with these three strains to high-nitrogen treatment for 0, 1, and 2 days and observed the LUX signal. LUX signal intensity decreased in response to high-nitrogen treatment, with the response being strongest for SF-pc16350, intermediate for SF-pc31030, and weakest for SF-pb51400 (Fig. 2A). We then evaluated the correlation between the LUX signal intensity of these strains and nitrogenase activity, as measured by an acetylene reduction assay using randomly selected nodule samples from 14 to 21 dpi. We detected a strong, significant correlation between LUX signal intensity and nitrogenase activity only for strain SF-pc16350 (Pearson's R2 = 0.6369, P = 2.4653E−10) (Fig. 2B). This indicates that the promoter of the candidate gene in this strain is highly correlated with nitrogenase activity, making it suitable for use as a biosensor for the real-time dynamic monitoring of nitrogen-fixation activity in nodules. Legocki et al. [10] developed a luxAB-based reporter system in Bradyrhizobium japonicum driven by the nifD/H promoter to monitor the expression of symbiotic genes in soybean root nodules. However, that system required the application of exogenous aldehyde substrate and nodule excision. In this study, we used the full luxCDABE operon, including the genes for aldehyde synthesis, allowing in situ, real-time imaging of intact nodules. This non-invasive, time-course-capable approach represents a clear advance over the earlier luxAB system.

In summary, we developed a useful tool for real-time, non-invasive monitoring of nitrogenase activity in soybean nodules, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods. Moreover, comparative genomic analysis revealed that S. fredii CCBAU45436 is phylogenetically closely related to rhizobial strains HH103, USDA192, SMH12, USDA193, and USDA205 [11], all of which are soybean-nodulating strains that were reported to infect other legume species, suggesting that the method developed in this study could be adapted to other legume–rhizobium systems. Further optimization is required to enhance the utility of this approach across a broader range of species and to improve the sensitivity of the LUX system.
