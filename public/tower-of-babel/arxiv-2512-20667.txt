Best approximation results for fuzzy-number-valued continuous functions Thanks: This research received no funding
Juan J. Font, Sergio Macario

Abstract
. In this paper we study the best approximation of a fixed fuzzy-number-valued continuous function to a subset of fuzzy-number-valued continuous functions. We also introduce a method to measure the distance between a fuzzy-number-valued continuous function and a real-valued one. Then we prove the existence of the best approximation of a fuzzy-number-valued continuous function to the space of real-valued continuous functions by using the well-known Michael Selection Theorem.

1.
Introduction

Approximation Theory originated from the necessity of approximating real-valued continuous
functions by simpler class of functions, such as trigonometric or algebraic polynomials and has attracted
the interest of many mathematicians for over a century. Among the most recognized results in this branch of Functional Analysis, we can mention the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem, Korovkin type results and the approximation of functions using neural networks.

More recently, all the above results have been also addressed in the context of fuzzy functions (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
8
]
).

Another fundamental problem in Approximation Theory
is the study of best approximation in spaces of continuous functions, which has a long story with famous results by Chebyshev, Haar, Young, Remez, de la Vallée-Poussin who established the existence of best approximations, as well as characterized and estimated them. Here we could mention the problem of the uniform approximation of a scalar-valued
function continuous on a compact set by a set of other functions continuous on this compact set (see, e.g.,
[
17
]
,
[
16
]
or
[
4
]
).

The existence of the best approximation of a set-valued continuous function by vector-valued ones is another important topic in Approximation Theory and has been studied by several authors (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
5
]
or
[
10
]
).

In this paper we address these two problems of best approximation type in the context of fuzzy-number-valued continuous functions.

First, we study the best approximation of a fixed fuzzy-number-valued continuous function to a subset of fuzzy-number-valued continuous functions.

Secondly, we introduce a novel method to measure the distance between a fuzzy-number-valued function and a real-valued one. Then we prove the existence of the best approximation of a fuzzy-number-valued continuous function to the space of real-valued continuous functions by using the well-known Michael Selection Theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
F({\mathbb{R}})
denote the family of all fuzzy subsets on the real
numbers
{\mathbb{R}}
(see
[
7
]
). For
\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
and a fuzzy set
u
, its
\lambda
-level set
is defined as

[u]^{\lambda}:=\left\{\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,u(x)\geq\lambda\,\right\},\quad\lambda\in]0,1],

and
[u]^{0}
stands for the closure of
\left\{\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,u(x)>0\,\right\}
.

The set of elements
u
of

F({\mathbb{R}})
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

u
is normal, i.e., there exists an
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
u(x_{0})=1
;

(2)

u
is convex, i.e.,
u(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq\min\left\{u(x),u(y)\right\}
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
;

(3)

u
is upper-semicontinuous;

(4)

[u]^{0}
is a compact set in
{\mathbb{R}}
,

is called the fuzzy number space
\mathbb{E}^{1}
and contains the reals.
If
u\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
, then it is known that
\lambda
-level set

[u]^{\lambda}
of
u
is a compact interval for each

\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
. We will write
[u]^{\lambda}=\mathopen{[}u^{-}(\lambda),u^{+}(\lambda)\mathclose{]}
.

The following characterization of fuzzy numbers, which was proved by Goetschel and Voxman (
[
9
]
), is essential in the sequel:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
and
[u]^{\lambda}=\mathopen{[}u^{-}(\lambda),u^{+}(\lambda)\mathclose{]}
,
\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
.
Then the pair of functions
u^{-}(\lambda)
and

u^{+}(\lambda)
has the following properties:

(1)

u^{+}(\lambda)
is a bounded left continuous nonincreasing function
on
\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
;

(2)

u^{-}(\lambda)
is a bounded left continuous nondecreasing function
on
\mathopen{(}0,1\mathclose{]}
;

(3)

u^{-}(1)\leq u^{+}(1)
;

(4)

u^{-}(\lambda)
and
u^{+}(\lambda)
are right continuous at

\lambda=0
.

Conversely, if two functions
\gamma(\lambda)
and

\nu(\lambda)
satisfy the above conditions (i)-(iv), then there
exists a unique
u\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
such that

[u]^{\lambda}=\mathopen{[}\gamma(\lambda),\nu(\lambda)\mathclose{]}

for each
\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
.

As usual, given
u,v\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the sum
u+v:=\mathopen{[}u^{-}(\lambda),u^{+}(\lambda)\mathclose{]}+\mathopen{[}v^{-}(\lambda),v^{+}(\lambda)\mathclose{]}
and the product
ku:=k\mathopen{[}u^{-}(\lambda),u^{+}(\lambda)\mathclose{]}
.
It is well-known that
\mathbb{E}^{1}
endowed with these two natural operations is not a vector space, not even
(\mathbb{E}^{1},+)
is a group.

The fuzzy number space
\mathbb{E}^{1}
can be endowed with several metrics (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
) but perhaps the most used is the following:

Definition 2.2
.

[
9
,
7
]

For
u,v\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
,

d_{\infty}(u,v):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}}\max\left\{|u^{-}(\lambda)-v^{-}(\lambda)|,|u^{+}(\lambda)-v^{+}(\lambda)|\right\}.

It is called the supremum
metric on
\mathbb{E}^{1}
, and with this metric,
\mathbb{E}^{1}
is a complete metric
space. Indeed,
{\mathbb{R}}
endowed with the Euclidean topology can be topologically identified
with the closed subspace
\tilde{R}=\left\{\,\tilde{x}\,:\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,\right\}
of

(\mathbb{E}^{1},d_{\infty})
where

\tilde{x}^{+}(\lambda)=\tilde{x}^{-}(\lambda)=x
for all

\lambda\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
. We will always
consider
\mathbb{E}^{1}
equipped with the supremum metric.

Proposition 2.3
.

(
[
8
, Proposition 2.3]
) The metric space
(\mathbb{E}^{1},d_{\infty})
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

d_{\infty}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{\infty}(u_{i},v_{i})
where
u_{i},v_{i}\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
for
i=1,...,m
.

(2)

d_{\infty}(ku,kv)=kd_{\infty}(u,v)
where
u,v\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
and
k>0
.

(3)

d_{\infty}(ku,\mu u)=\mid k-\mu\mid d_{\infty}(u,0),
where
u\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
,
k\geq 0
and
\mu\geq 0
.

(4)

d_{\infty}(ku,\mu v)\leq\mid k-\mu\mid d_{\infty}(u,0)+\mu d_{\infty}(u,v),
where
u,v\in\mathbb{E}^{1}
,
k\geq 0
and
\mu\geq 0
.

In the space of continuous functions defined between the compact Hausdorff space
K
and the metric space
(\mathbb{E}^{1},d_{\infty})
, i.e.,
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
, we will consider the following metric:

D(f,g)=\sup_{t\in K}d_{\infty}(f(t),g(t)),

which induces the uniform convergence topology on
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
.

Let us next introduce a basic tool for our purposes.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
W
be a nonempty subset of
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. We define

Conv(W):=\{\varphi\in C(K,[0,1]):\varphi f+(1-\varphi)g\in W\hskip 5.69046ptfor\hskip 5.69046ptall\hskip 5.69046ptf,g\in W\}.

Proposition 2.5
.

(
[
8
, Proposition 3.2]
) Let
W
be a nonempty subset of
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. Then we have:

(1)

\phi\in Conv(W)
implies that
1-\phi\in Conv(W)
.

(2)

If
\phi,\varphi\in Conv(W)
, then
\phi\cdot\varphi\in Conv(W)
.

Definition 2.6
.

It is said that
M\subset C(K,[0,1])
separates the points of
K
if given
s,t\in K
, there exists
\phi\in M
such that
\phi(s)\not=\phi(t)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

(
[
8
, Lemma 3.6]
) Let
W\subseteq C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. If
Conv(W)
separates the points of
K
, then, given
x_{0}\in K
and an open neighborhood
N
of
x_{0}
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
contained in
N
such that for all
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
, there is
\varphi\in Conv(W)
such that

(1)

\varphi(t)>1-\delta,
for all
t\in U
;

(2)

\varphi(t)<\delta,
for all
t\notin N
.

3.
Best approximation for subspaces of
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})

Definition 3.1
.

Let
A
be a subspace of
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
and let
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. We define

d(f,A):=\inf_{g\in A}\{\sup_{x\in K}d_{\infty}(f(x),g(x))\}=\inf_{g\in A}\{D(f,g)\}

d_{x}(f,A):=\inf_{g\in A}\{d_{\infty}(f(x),g(x))\}

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
W
be a subspace of
C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
and assume that
Conv(W)
separates points.
For each
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
, we have

d(f,W)=d_{x}(f,W)

for some
x\in K
.

Proof.

We shall first show that
d(f,W)=\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)
. It is apparent that
d(f,W)\geq\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)
since
d(f,W)\geq d_{x}(f,W)
for each
x\in K
.
Let us prove that
d(f,W)\leq\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)
.

To this end, fix
\varepsilon>0
. Given
x^{\prime}\in K
, we can find
f_{x^{\prime}}\in W
such that
d_{\infty}(f(x^{\prime}),f_{x^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}))<\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+\varepsilon
.
Let us define the following open neighborhood of
x^{\prime}
:

N(x^{\prime}):=\{t\in K:d_{\infty}(f(t),f_{x^{\prime}}(t))<\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+\varepsilon\}.

Let
U(x^{\prime})
be an open neighborhood of
x^{\prime}
satisfying the properties in Lemma
2.7
.

Since
K
is compact, there exist finitely many
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
in
K
such that
K\subset U(x_{1})\cup\ldots\cup U(x_{m})
.
Choose
\delta>0
such that
\delta<min(1,\frac{\varepsilon}{km})
, where

k:=\max\{D(f,0),D(f,f_{x_{1}}),...D(f,f_{x_{m}})\}.

By Lemma
2.7
, we know that there exist
\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{m}\in Conv(W)
such that for all
i=1,\ldots,m
,

(i)

\phi_{i}(t)>1-\delta,
for all
t\in U(x_{i})
;

(ii)

0\leq\phi_{i}(t)<\delta,\quad if\kern 5.0ptt\notin N(x_{i})
.

Let us define the functions

\psi_{1}:=\phi_{1}
,

\psi_{2}:=(1-\phi_{1})\phi_{2}
,

\vdots

\psi_{m}:=(1-\phi_{1})(1-\phi_{2})\cdots(1-\phi_{m-1})\phi_{m}
.

By Proposition
2.5
, we know that
\psi_{i}\in Conv(W)
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
.
Next we claim that

\psi_{1}+\ldots+\psi_{j}=1-(1-\phi_{1})(1-\phi_{2})\cdots(1-\phi_{j}),

j=1,\ldots,m.

Indeed, it is clear that

\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}=\phi_{1}+(1-\phi_{1})\phi_{2}=1-(1-\phi_{1})\cdot(1-\phi_{2}).

We proceed by induction. Assume that the result is true for a certain
j\in\{4,...,m-1\}
and let us check

\psi_{1}+\ldots+\psi_{j}+\psi_{j+1}=1-(1-\phi_{1})(1-\phi_{2})\cdots(1-\phi_{j})(1-\phi_{j+1}).

Namely,

\psi_{1}+\ldots+\psi_{j}+\psi_{j+1}=

=1-(1-\phi_{1})(1-\phi_{2})\cdots(1-\phi_{j})+(1-\phi_{1})(1-\phi_{2})\cdots(1-\phi_{j})\phi_{j+1}=

=1-(1-\phi_{1})(1-\phi_{2})\cdots(1-\phi_{j})(1-\phi_{j+1}),

as was to be checked.

Fix
x_{0}\in K
. Then there is some
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
x_{0}\in U(x_{i_{0}})
. Hence
\phi_{i_{0}}(x_{0})>1-\delta
and consequently,

1\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\psi_{i}(x_{0})=1-(1-\phi_{i_{0}}(x_{0}))\prod_{i\not=i_{0}}(1-\phi_{i}(x_{0}))>1-\delta.

Furthermore, we clearly infer

(3.1)

\psi_{i}(t)<\delta\hskip 7.22743pt{\rm for}\hskip 2.8903pt{\rm all}\hskip 2.8903ptt\notin N(x_{i_{0}}),\hskip 2.8903pti=1,\ldots,m.

Let

(3.2)

h:=\psi_{1}f_{x_{1}}+\psi_{2}f_{x_{2}}+\ldots+\psi_{m}f_{x_{m}}.

It seems apparent that

h=\phi_{1}f_{x_{1}}+(1-\phi_{1})[\phi_{2}f_{x_{2}}+(1-\phi_{2})[\phi_{3}f_{x_{3}}+\cdots+(1-\phi_{m-1})[\phi_{m}f_{x_{m}}\cdots]].

Therefore,
h\in W
since
\phi_{i}\in Conv(W)
for
i=1,...,m
(see Definition
2.4
).

From Proposition
2.3
, we know that, given
x\in K
,

d_{\infty}(f(x),h(x))\leq d_{\infty}\left(f(x),\sum^{m}_{i=1}\psi_{i}(x)f(x)\right)+d_{\infty}\left(\sum^{m}_{i=1}\psi_{i}(x)f(x),h(x)\right)\leq

\leq\left|1-\sum^{m}_{i=1}\psi_{i}(x)\right|d_{\infty}\left(f(x),0)\right)+\sum^{m}_{i=1}\psi_{i}(x)d_{\infty}\left(f(x),f_{x_{i}}(x)\right).

On the one hand,
\left|1-\sum^{m}_{i=1}\psi_{i}(x)\right|d_{\infty}\left(f(x),0)\right)\leq\delta D(f,0)\leq\epsilon
.

On the other hand, let

I_{x}=\{1\leq i\leq m:x\in N(x_{i})\}

and

J_{x}=\{1\leq i\leq m:x\notin N(x_{i})\}.

Then, for all
i\in I_{x}
, we have

\psi_{i}(x)d_{\infty}(f(x),f_{x_{i}}(x))\leq\psi_{i}(x)(\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+\varepsilon)\leq\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+\varepsilon

and, for all
i\in J
, the inequality (
3.1
) yields

\psi_{i}(x)d_{\infty}(f(x),f_{x_{i}}(x))\leq\delta d_{\infty}(f(x),f_{x_{i}}(x))\leq\delta D(f,f_{x_{i}})\leq\delta k.

From the above two paragraphs, we can infer

d_{\infty}(f(x),h(x))\leq\epsilon+\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+\varepsilon+\delta km\leq\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+3\varepsilon

and, since
x\in K
is arbitrary,

D(f,h)\leq\sup_{x\in K}d_{x}(f,W)+3\varepsilon.

As a consequence, we deduce that

d(f,W)=\inf_{g\in W}\{D(f,g)\}\leq\sup_{x\in K}d(f_{x},W_{x}).

Finally, we can define a continuous function
\gamma:K\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\gamma(x):=\inf_{g\in W}\{d_{\infty}(f(x),g(x))\}.

Since
K
is compact, we know that
\gamma
attains its supremum at some
x^{\prime}\in K
. Hence, we can write

d(f,W)=d_{x^{\prime}}(f,W).

∎

4.
Best approximation with respect to real-valued continuous functions

In Approximation Theory, a natural question is when we can approximate a set-valued function by continuous real-valued functions. In the classical setting, Cellina’s Theorem (
[
2
]
) is the fundamental result (see also
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
). In this section we introduce a method to measure the distance between a fuzzy-number-valued function and a real-valued one. Then we will prove the existence of the best approximation of a fuzzy-number-valued continuous functions to the space of real-valued continuous functions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
and let
F\in C(K)
. The distance between
f
and
F
can be defined as

D(f,F):=\sup_{x\in K}\left\{\sup\{|F(x)-t|:t\in[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]\}\right\}.

Remark 4.2
.

Indeed we are evaluating the distance between
f
and
F
at the core of each fuzzy number
f(x)
. We can define a similar distance at each level
\lambda\in[0,1]
as follows

D_{\lambda}(f,F):=\sup_{x\in K}\left\{\sup\{|F(x)-t|:t\in I_{\lambda}:=[f(x)^{-}(\lambda),f(x)^{+}(\lambda)]\}\right\}.

and take
D(f,F)=\inf_{\lambda\in[0,1]}D_{\lambda}(f,F)
.
But, being the intervals
I_{\lambda}
a nonincreasing family, it implies that the distances form a nondecreasing family as well, that is,

D_{\lambda}(f,F)\geq D_{\eta}(f,F)\quad\text{if $0\leq\lambda\leq\eta\leq 1$}.

Then
D(f,F)=D_{1}(f,F)
which coincides with Definition
4.1
.
To avoid the case when the core reduces to a real number, we could define

D(f,F)=\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}D_{\lambda}(f,F)=D_{0}(f,F)
, which means we are evaluating the distance at the support of each
f(x)
.
In that case, the following results could be adapted easily to that situation.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. We can define the distance between
f
and
C(K)
as

D(f,C(K)):=\inf_{F\in C(K)}D(f,F).

Definition 4.4
.

Let
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
and
x\in K
. We can define

rad(x,f):=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\sup\{|\alpha-\beta|\ :\ \beta\in[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]\}\right\}

It is clear that
rad(x,f)
turns out to be the radius of the interval
[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. We define the
radius
of
f
as

rad(f):=\sup_{x\in K}rad(x,f)

Remark 4.6
.

From these definitions we infer easily that

D(f,F)\geq rad(f)

for all
F\in C(K)
. Hence

D(f,C(K))\geq rad(f).

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
f\in C(K,\mathbb{E}^{1})
. Then there exists a function
F_{0}\in C(K)
such that

D(f,C(K))=D(f,F_{0}).

Proof.

Let us define a map
G:K\longrightarrow 2^{\mathbb{R}}
such that, for each
x\in K
,

G(x):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}:[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]\subseteq[\alpha-rad(f),\alpha+rad(f)]\}

Let us first check that
G(x)\neq\emptyset
for each
x\in K
. We know that

rad(x,f):=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\beta\in[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]}|\alpha-\beta|\leq rad(f).

Since
rad(x,f)
turns out to be the radius of the interval
[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]
, it is clear that the center of this interval belongs to
G(x)
.

It is apparent that
G(x)
is closed for each
x\in K
since the intervals which appear in its definition are closed.

Now we take
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
in
G(x)
and
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0
with
k_{1}+k_{2}=1
. Then, given
\alpha\in[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]
,

|\alpha-(k_{1}\alpha_{1}+k_{2}\alpha_{2})|=|\alpha(k_{1}+k_{2})-(k_{1}\alpha_{1}+k_{2}\alpha_{2})|\leq

\leq k_{1}|\alpha-\alpha_{1}|+k_{2}|\alpha-\alpha_{2}|\leq rad(f),

which shows that
G(x)
is convex for each
x\in K
.

Next, we shall prove that the map
G
is lower semicontinuous, that is, we have to check that the set

\mathcal{O}:=\{x\in K:G(x)\cap O\neq\emptyset\}

is open in
K
for every open set
O\subset\mathbb{R}
. To this end, fix
x_{0}\in\mathcal{O}
and take
\alpha_{0}\in G(x_{0})\cap O
for a certain open set
O
.
Let
\delta_{0}>0
such that
(\alpha_{0}-\delta_{0},\alpha_{0}+\delta_{0})\subset O
.
From the continuity of
f
and from the fact that

[f(x_{0})^{-}(1),f(x_{0})^{+}(1)]\subset[\alpha_{0}-rad(f),\alpha_{0}+rad(f)],

we infer that, given
\epsilon>0
, as we have

[\alpha_{0}-rad(f),\alpha_{0}+rad(f)]\subset(\alpha_{0}-rad(f)-\epsilon,\alpha_{0}+rad(f)+\epsilon),

there exist an open neighborhood,
Q(\epsilon)
, of
x_{0}
such that

[f(x)^{-}(1),f(x)^{+}(1)]\subset(\alpha_{0}-(rad(f)+\epsilon),\alpha_{0}+rad(f)+\epsilon)

for all
x\in Q(\epsilon)
. Our goal is to prove that
Q(\epsilon)\subseteq\mathcal{O}
for some
\epsilon>0
to get the openness of
\mathcal{O}
.

Fix
x_{1}\in Q(\epsilon)
. Since
G(x_{1})\neq\emptyset
, there exists
\alpha_{1}\in G(x_{1})
. That is,

[f(x_{1})^{-}(1),f(x_{1})^{+}(1)]\subset[\alpha_{1}-rad(f),\alpha_{1}+rad(f)].

Besides, we know that

[f(x_{1})^{-}(1),f(x_{1})^{+}(1)]\subset(\alpha_{0}-(rad(f)+\epsilon),\alpha_{0}+rad(f)+\epsilon).

Taking
\epsilon>0
as small as necessary, we can find
\delta^{\prime}<\delta_{0}
such that

\displaystyle[\alpha_{0}-(rad(f)+\epsilon),\alpha_{0}+rad(f)+\epsilon]\cap[\alpha_{1}-rad(f),\alpha_{1}+rad(f)]

\displaystyle\subset[(\alpha_{0}+\delta^{\prime})-rad(f),(\alpha_{0}+\delta^{\prime})+rad(f)]

and consequently

[f(x_{1})^{-}(1),f(x_{1})^{+}(1)]\subset[(\alpha_{0}+\delta^{\prime})-rad(f),(\alpha_{0}+\delta^{\prime})+rad(f)],

which implies that
\alpha_{0}+\delta^{\prime}\in G(x_{1})\cap O
. Hence
x_{1}\in\mathcal{O}
, as desired.

Gathering the information we have obtained so far , we know that
G
is a lower semicontinuous mapping defined between
K
and the closed convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Hence, by the Michael Selection Theorem (
[
14
]
), we infer that there exists
F_{0}\in C(K)
such that
F_{0}(x)\in G(x)
for all
x\in K
.

As a consequence of the above paragraph, we can deduce that

D(f,F_{0})\leq rad(f),

which combined with the comments before this theorem yields

D(f,F_{0})=rad(f)=D(f,C(K)).

∎

Conclusions

In this paper we address two problems of best approximation type in the context of fuzzy-number-valued continuous functions: (1) the problem of the uniform approximation of a fuzzy-number-valued
function continuous on a compact set by a set of other functions continuous on this compact set; and (2) the existence of the best approximation of a fuzzy-number-valued continuous function to the space of real-valued continuous functions. We obtain positive results in both cases.
