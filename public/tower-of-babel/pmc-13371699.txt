Healthcare under fire: a retrospective analysis of a national ngo response during the 2024 conflict in Lebanon
Authors: Rana El Haidari, Adnan Husseini, Kassem Baydoun, Rima Badran, Samir Mansour, Abbas Hoballah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 2024 conflict in Lebanon triggered widespread internal displacement and placed significant strain on the national health system. This study analyzes the emergency healthcare response of the Health Society during Lebanon’s 2024 conflict using a health systems resilience framework and SWOT analysis to inform evidence-based interventions for conflict-affected populations and guide future policy and research.MethodsFrom September to December 2024, a large Lebanese NGO (“Health Society”) scaled up its response by operating 91 facilities, including 48 newly established emergency sites and 43 existing centers. In parallel, 36 mobile medical teams comprising 984 healthcare professionals (254 physicians) were deployed to maintain service continuity. Service utilization data were aggregated and analyzed descriptively.ResultsA total of 588,000 beneficiaries received essential services. Beirut recorded the highest service volume, including 145,523 medication dispensations and 71,276 consultations. Mount Lebanon also showed substantial utilization, while Baalbeck-Hermel accounted for the highest number of psychological consultations (1,130) and surgical procedures (2,076). Environmental risk assessments conducted in 158 displacement centers identified hazards in 30.4% of sites, most commonly unsafe indoor coal use (17.1%). Preventive efforts included vaccination campaigns in 33 displacement schools, reaching 520 children.ConclusionThe findings highlight the value of combining fixed facilities, mobile outreach, mental health integration, and community-based risk assessments to sustain healthcare access during conflict. While challenges related to geographic inequities, surge staffing, and financial sustainability persist, the response demonstrates how emergency interventions can strengthen health system resilience in crisis settings.

Introduction

Since October, 2023, Israeli attacks on Lebanon have resulted in more than 4000 deaths, injured more than 17 000 individuals, and forcibly displaced almost 1 million people [1–3]. Lebanon, a small Middle Eastern country, has endured devastating attacks linked to the conflict in Gaza [1]. The intensification of this conflict since September 16, 2024, has precipitated a significant wave of internal displacement across Lebanon [4]. The ongoing escalation of Israeli airstrikes has profoundly affected several Lebanese governorates, including South Lebanon, Nabatieh, Bekaa, Baalbeck-Hermel, and Mount Lebanon [5]. These hostilities have directly impacted an estimated 1.5 million individuals [6]. In response, national authorities have established over 1,000 shelters, accommodating more than 185,000 displaced persons, with women and children constituting half of this population [6]. The widespread use of nearly 60% of public schools as shelters has led to repeated delays in the commencement of the new academic year [6].

The two-month conflict, culminating in a ceasefire agreement with Israel on November 27, marked Lebanon’s deadliest violence in decades and severely damaged its economy [5]. With an average of over three child fatalities per day, the recent two-month conflict represents the most severe period of violence Lebanon has faced in decades [3]. As of September 2024, nearly 30 Primary Healthcare Coordination Centers (PHCCs) ceased operations due to prevailing security risks. In parallel, the World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted a critical demand for mental health and psychosocial support services among over 1.5 million refugees and internally displaced persons (IDPs) [7].

In Lebanon, Health Society, established in 1984, is one of the largest non-governmental health organizations and serves as a key supportive healthcare provider within the national health sector. It delivers primary healthcare and mental health services across various Lebanese governorates through a comprehensive network linked to the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health.

Operating approximately 71 medical facilities, including medical centers, pharmacies, and mobile medical units, the organization provides essential services ranging from routine primary care and emergency medical response to specialized mental health interventions, maternal and child health programs, and chronic disease management. Health Society is a community-based healthcare provider focused on improving health and health-related quality of life by enhancing the overall physical and mental well-being of the general Lebanese population. As part of Lebanon’s National Primary Healthcare Network, coordinated by the Ministry of Public Health, Health Society plays a significant role in emergency preparedness, civil defense services (rescue and evacuation), and public health campaigns addressing issues such as infectious disease control, tobacco cessation, and substance abuse prevention. It therefore functions as a key non-state actor in Lebanon’s broader health sector response to both chronic healthcare needs and acute humanitarian crises.

Armed conflict and large-scale population displacement represent major public health emergencies that disrupt health service delivery, strain fragile systems, and increase vulnerability to communicable diseases, mental health disorders, and chronic illness complications [8–10]. In fragile contexts such as Lebanon, where the health system is already under economic and political strain, rapid emergency scale-up requires not only operational response but also systematic evaluation to assess resilience, equity, and sustainability [11]. This study applies a SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) analytical framework to critically examine the Health Society’s emergency health response during displacement escalation. Rather than serving as a descriptive program report, the analysis aims to assess institutional adaptability, system-level gaps, and strategic opportunities for strengthening health system resilience in crisis settings.

This paper details the strategic adaptations and comprehensive interventions implemented by the “Health Society” to safeguard the health and well-being of displaced populations during this acute phase of conflict. This study critically examines the emergency healthcare response of a national NGO, Health Society, during Lebanon’s 2024 conflict, applying a health systems resilience framework through a structured SWOT analysis. The findings of this study will provide insight for humanitarian interventions and serve as input for government and other stakeholders in designing evidence-based health interventions for conflict-affected areas. It will also serve as baseline evidence for further investigation in the area.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a retrospective analysis of health services data provided by the “Health Society”, a prominent non-governmental organization in Lebanon. Data were collected over a two-month period, from September 23, 2024, to November 27, 2024. The study population comprised Lebanese citizens residing in various Lebanese governorates (South Lebanon, Nabatieh, Bekaa, Baalbeck-Hermel, and Mount Lebanon) who had been internally displaced to safer urban regions during the specified period. Data were extracted from the “Health Society” database.

Procedures

The protracted crisis in Lebanon, exacerbated by the outbreak of renewed conflict and its expansion across the country on September 23, 2024, presented unprecedented challenges to public health and humanitarian aid. Amidst widespread displacement and a rapidly deteriorating security situation, non-governmental organizations (NGOs) became critical actors in bridging significant gaps in essential service provision. “Health Society” deeply rooted in Lebanese communities and with a long-standing commitment to health and social welfare, rapidly adapted its operations to address the urgent and evolving needs of displaced citizens. Our immediate focus pivoted towards providing integrated health, psychological, and social services, emphasizing the continuity of care for those most affected by the hostilities (Fig. 1).

Adapting health service delivery to a crisis footing

In response to the mass displacement, “Health Society” significantly scaled up and reconfigured its health service delivery mechanisms. Core to this response was the establishment of 48 new health emergency points in critical displacement areas, augmenting the continued operation of 43 main centers where security conditions permitted. This expansion resulted in a network of 91 operational health facilities, strategically positioned to maximize accessibility for displaced individuals across Lebanon. To address the pervasive geographical dispersal of displaced persons, “Health Society” deployed 36 dedicated mobile medical teams, covering public shelters in the following Lebanese governorates Beirut (20 teams), Mount Lebanon (10 teams), Tyre (4 teams), and Nabatieh (2 teams). These teams, along with fixed and newly established centers, were staffed by a surge in medical personnel, reaching a total of 254 doctors committed to providing continuous medical consultation and emergency care. Furthermore, a fleet of mobile clinics was established to operate on multiple routes, specifically designed to reach scattered displaced populations and ensure vital health services, including the provision of necessary medications, could be delivered directly to those in need. The integrated health service delivery model implemented by Health Society, structured around a people-centered care approach. The model integrates key service components delivered at the primary care level, including medication provision, mental health support, medical consultations, and clinic and hospital referrals, ensuring continuity across different levels of care. It also highlights the interconnection between prevention, consultation, and treatment, emphasizing that services are delivered in a coordinated and comprehensive manner rather than as isolated interventions. This framework guided the organization and implementation of health services during the conflict response (Fig. 1).

Prioritizing mental health and psychosocial support

Recognizing the profound psychological toll of conflict and displacement, “Health Society” placed a strong emphasis on providing comprehensive mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS). Dedicated psychological teams were rapidly mobilized for intervention in displacement centers, implementing a diverse array of specialized activities tailored to the specific needs and age groups of the affected populations. This included direct psychological support and crisis intervention in severe cases, such as suicide attempts, highlighting the critical nature of immediate and skilled MHPSS. Interventions were designed with flexibility, leveraging diverse and specialized activities to cater to the varied experiences of trauma and stress. Crucially, the “Health Society” initiated robust coordination efforts with the Ministry of Public Health and various professional associations and unions mentioned in Fig. 2. This collaboration aimed to effectively utilize existing human resources and qualified personnel within the national mental health landscape, ensuring a broader and more coordinated MHPSS response. To maintain oversight and quality, dedicated coordinators were assigned to each operational area, tasked with monitoring field teams and submitting regular reports on activities and needs.

Fostering community health and resilience

Beyond direct clinical care, the “Health Society” implemented a robust community health component to mitigate public health risks and promote resilience among displaced populations. A systematic health and environmental assessment were conducted across 158 displacement centers in Beirut and Mount Lebanon governorates, utilizing a standardized screening form. This assessment thoroughly evaluated critical public health determinants, including water sources, hygiene practices, food safety, the adequacy of health facilities, and waste management, alongside identifying specific risks pertinent to different age groups. Data from these surveys informed targeted interventions. Practical preventive measures were implemented to prevent the spread of epidemics, ranging from direct guidance sessions on hygiene and sanitation to the distribution of essential medicines and informative brochures. Furthermore, a comprehensive survey was undertaken to identify available human resources among the displaced, facilitating their potential integration into response efforts where appropriate. All environmental health assessments and preventive interventions such as vaccination campaigns, and preventive measures were conducted according to the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health recommendations and relevant local authorities to ensure alignment with national emergency response frameworks and public health standards. Specific attention was given to vulnerable groups, a detailed survey identified pregnant women in hosting centers, assessing their health needs and facilitating linkages to the nearest primary healthcare centers for essential antenatal and postnatal services, including periodic checkups, tests, and imaging. Finally, a meticulous division of age groups within displacement centers allowed for the identification of children at risk of missing basic vaccinations within the National Immunization Program. Close coordination with the Ministry of Public Health ensured these children were connected to nearby primary healthcare centers for catch-up vaccinations, and “Health Society” conducted outreach visits to displacement centers to identify children who had missed essential vaccinations and, ensured that these children received the appropriate immunizations (Figs 1 and 2).

SWOT analysis

The SWOT framework was employed as a strategic health systems evaluation tool to analyze internal organizational capacities (strengths and weaknesses) and external contextual factors (opportunities and threats). This framework allows structured assessment of emergency responsiveness, sustainability, equity of service distribution, and system resilience in humanitarian settings.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Research Department at Health Society (Reference No: 170125-18). The study involved a retrospective secondary analysis of routinely collected, de-identified, and aggregated service data. No individual-level identifiers were accessed, and no direct contact with beneficiaries occurred. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to applicable data protection and confidentiality standards.

Vaccination campaigns

In collaboration with the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health, vaccination campaigns were implemented at 33 schools that were being used as displacement centers for internally displaced persons. A total of 520 children were vaccinated with the Measles, Mumps, and Rubella (MMR) vaccine, Oral Poliovirus Vaccine (OPV), and influenza vaccine (Fig. 6).

Environmental health and community resilience

A total of 158 schools in the Beirut and Mount Lebanon governorates were visited to assess health and environmental risks within displacement centers. A comprehensive inspection was conducted, focusing on water sources, hygiene, food safety, health facilities, and waste management to identify existing hazards. The assessment of displacement centers revealed that the majority of locations (69.6%) had no identified environmental risks. However, several hazards were present in a minority of sites. The most prevalent risk was the unsafe use of coal and fire indoors, found in 17.1% of the centers. Less frequent risks included exposed electrical wiring (5.7%), broken doors and windows (2.5%), low and unsecured windows (2.5%), and the use of gas in rooms and corridors (2.5%) (Fig. 7).

SWOT analysis

Weaknesses

Despite the impressive breadth of interventions, several weaknesses were observed in the distribution and sustainability of services. The concentration of psychological consultations and surgical interventions in Baalbeck-Hermel suggests geographical disparities in access to specialized care. Furthermore, the rapid scale-up of operations was highly dependent on an emergency surge in personnel, raising concerns regarding the sustainability of human resources and potential staff fatigue or burnout. Infrastructure assessments in displacement centers revealed that 30.4% of sites faced environmental hazards, including unsafe indoor use of coal (17.1%), exposed electrical wiring, and structural vulnerabilities such as broken doors and windows. These risks not only compromise safety but also challenge the provision of consistent, high-quality care. The limited capacity for long-term care provision and psychosocial support across all regions points to a systemic gap in resilient health systems planning.

Opportunities

The response presents significant opportunities for health system strengthening and community resilience. The integration of displaced individuals into the response workforce, based on a human resource mapping initiative, fosters community engagement and offers a scalable workforce solution. Collaboration with MOPH and involvement in national campaigns, such as the November 2024 immunization drive, reflect a model of effective public-private partnerships. The structured environmental and health needs assessments offer a replicable framework for evidence-based interventions in future emergencies. Additionally, the data collected on pregnant women and under-immunized children facilitated targeted outreach and service linkage—an approach that could be institutionalized into national emergency preparedness strategies.

Threats

Several external and contextual threats may hinder the continuity and expansion of essential health services. Overcrowding in displacement centers, combined with identified environmental hazards, increases the risk of epidemic outbreaks, particularly in the absence of sustained water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions. The reliance on short-term mobilization of staff and external funding raises concerns about the durability of services post-crisis. Additionally, while coordination mechanisms were activated, gaps in crisis governance, logistical management, and inter-agency collaboration during peak emergencies remain potential threats to the efficiency and equity of health interventions during future shocks.

Strengths

The Health Society demonstrated robust adaptability and rapid response capacity during the escalation of displacement due to conflict. A major strength lies in the extensive scaling of health service delivery: the establishment of 48 new health emergency points and the continued operation of 43 existing centers created a nationwide network of 91 accessible health facilities. This expansion was strategically backed by the deployment of 36 mobile medical teams, particularly targeting governorates with large displaced populations such as Beirut and Mount Lebanon. The integration of 254 doctors and establishment of mobile clinics ensured continuity of care, including emergency services and medication provision. Mental health was proactively addressed through the deployment of specialized psychological teams, crisis interventions, and coordinated efforts with the Ministry of Public Health (MOPH) and professional associations. Community-level public health actions—such as health-environmental risk assessments in 158 displacement centers and catch-up immunization campaigns—further reinforced the comprehensiveness and impact of the response, reaching over 588,000 beneficiaries nationwide.

Discussion

The current study documented the rapid and expansive reconfiguration of essential healthcare delivery in response to the 2024 conflict-induced displacement in Lebanon. While limited to one NGO’s service data, the findings provide insight into how non-governmental actors contributed to maintaining service continuity within a coordinated national emergency response. A network of 91 operational health facilities (43 existing (old) health care centers and 48 newly established centers), supported by 36 mobile medical teams and 254 doctors, delivered continuity of care including emergency services, medication provision, and mental health support to over 588,000 beneficiaries.

Lebanon has endured recurrent cycles of armed conflict that have profoundly affected its population’s health and wellbeing. The 1975–1990 civil war caused large-scale displacement, destruction of health infrastructure, and long-term psychosocial trauma among survivors [12]. The July 2006 war further strained the fragile healthcare system, with over one million people displaced and health facilities damaged or destroyed [13]. More recently, consequences from the Syrian conflict since 2011 have compounded Lebanon’s chronic political and economic fragility, increasing the burden on already overstretched primary health care services [14].

NGOs are often the backbone of health service delivery in wars, rapidly mobilizing community networks to keep primary care, emergency care, chronic disease management, maternal-child services, vaccination, and mental health support running when state capacity and infrastructure are disrupted [15]. In the Lebanese context, NGOs benefit from long-term trust, pre-existing PHCC, mobile medical units, and coordination with municipalities bridge referral gaps, sustain medication and vaccine supply, and adapt services to shifting displacement and security conditions [16, 17].

The findings show that the organization was able to rapidly expand services during a crisis, demonstrating strong adaptability within a fragile health system. However, differences in service availability between regions and the reliance on short-term staff recruitment reveal challenges in maintaining sustainable care. These issues reflect common difficulties in humanitarian settings, where emergency expansion is not always followed by long-term system integration. Strengthening permanent surge capacity and integrating mental health services into routine primary care could improve long-term resilience beyond the immediate crisis response [18].

In the other hand, it is important to distinguish between organizational resilience and health system resilience [19]. The present analysis primarily reflects the adaptive capacity of one NGO, including rapid service scale-up, deployment of mobile teams, and continuity of care. However, health system resilience also depends on broader factors such as preparedness planning, governance structures, coordination mechanisms, and resource allocation under conditions of scarcity [20]. While this study illustrates organizational contributions within a coordinated national response, it does not represent a comprehensive evaluation of Lebanon’s overall health system resilience.

This study represents the first scientific article to document the Lebanese war experience of 2024 from a health systems perspective, offering valuable evidence on how essential healthcare services were adapted to meet the needs of conflict-affected IDPs. Its strengths lie in the large scale of beneficiaries reached (> 588,000), the breadth of interventions delivered including fixed health centers, mobile medical teams, vaccination campaigns, mental health and psychosocial support, and environmental risk assessments and the close coordination with MoPH, which ensured alignment with national strategies. These features underscore the comprehensiveness and policy relevance of the response, while also providing a replicable model for similar fragile contexts. However, several limitations should be noted. This study is limited by its retrospective design and reliance on the administrative database of a single non-governmental organization, without inclusion of comparative data from other humanitarian actors or Ministry-led services. As such, the findings should not be generalized to reflect overall national health system performance and must be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the analysis is based on routinely collected service data during a conflict setting. Given the emergency context and the priority placed on rapid service delivery, detailed demographic information on internally displaced persons was not systematically captured. Key variables, including age, sex, socioeconomic status, and place of origin, were therefore unavailable. The absence of these data restricts comprehensive characterization of the beneficiary population and limits the ability to conduct subgroup analyses or evaluate variations in service utilization patterns.

The geographic focus on relatively secure and accessible regions may have led to underrepresentation of remote or highly insecure areas, potentially limiting generalizability. In addition, reliance on aggregated facility-level data restricted the ability to assess individual health outcomes or long-term patient trajectories. The short temporal scope of analysis captured the immediate emergency response but did not allow evaluation of sustained impacts, such as continuity of chronic disease care or follow-up for mental health needs. Finally, the absence of pre-conflict baseline or control data limited opportunities for comparative analyses.

The emergency response was primarily funded by the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health, with additional financial support from the Republic of Iraq. In addition, the response was supported by a large multidisciplinary team of healthcare professionals and other specialists who contributed on a voluntary basis. This volunteer surge capacity was instrumental in maintaining service continuity during the acute crisis phase.

Implications for public health

Future conflict responses should move from ad hoc service delivery toward a coordinated, conflict-ready PHC model. Priority actions include needs-based geographic microplanning with pre-positioned mobile capacity; a standardized surge staffing roster with task-sharing protocols; strengthened referral and continuity pathways for NCDs and maternal-child services; implementation of a minimum routine dataset (including age/sex/displacement status) to guide equitable deployment; resilient essential medicine supply chains with buffer stocks; and phased financing that shifts from emergency-only funding to stabilization and integration under Ministry stewardship. These measures offer practical guidance to improve equity, continuity, and sustainability of services in future escalations.

Conclusion

This study illustrates how NGO-level adaptive capacity contributed to maintaining service continuity within Lebanon’s coordinated emergency health response. By combining facility expansion, mobile outreach, mental health integration, and community risk assessment, the response model advances the humanitarian–systemic connection. Lessons from this experience offer actionable insights for strengthening health systems resilience in fragile contexts, both within Lebanon and across similar global settings.
