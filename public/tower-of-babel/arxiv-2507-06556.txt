1.
Introduction

Random geometric graphs arise naturally in modeling complex systems with latent spatial or geometric structures
[
42
,
33
]
, such as wireless communication networks
[
37
]
, biological networks
[
24
]
, and social graphs
[
25
]
. In a random geometric graph, each node is assigned a random position in a geometric space, and edges are formed based on pairwise geometric proximity, inducing intricate dependencies between edges. These dependencies distinguish random geometric graphs from classical random graph models like Erdős–Rényi graphs, where edges are included independently.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
n
independent random vectors uniformly distributed on the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. The
random geometric graph

\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
is constructed by adding edges between two vertices
i
and
j
if their corresponding vectors satisfy
\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle\geq\tau
. The threshold parameter
\tau
is chosen such that for
i\not=j
,

\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}(\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle\geq\tau)=p
.
This implies the edge density of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
is the same as the Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
.

A compelling aspect of this model is that the graph structure emerges from a geometric representation of the vertices. This makes it particularly suitable for modeling applications in data science, where network nodes are naturally described by feature vectors in a
d
-dimensional space, and edges reflect similarity in features
[
43
]
. Additionally, the geometric random graph exhibits the
triangle-closing property

[
13
]
, the phenomenon that “a friend of a friend is also likely to be a friend.” This arises from the inherent dependency among edges: if two nodes are each close to a common neighbor in the geometric space, then they are more likely to be close to each other as well, making the formation of triangles more probable. Such edge correlations are absent in classical Erdős–Rényi graphs, but are crucial for capturing clustering behaviors observed in real-world networks.

In this paper, our primary objective is to investigate the limiting spectral distribution and the second eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix associated with
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
, and to determine under what conditions it resembles the spectral properties of
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
. Understanding the spectral properties of random geometric graphs, particularly in high dimensions and sparse regimes, is crucial for advancing both theoretical and applied research in network science, high-dimensional statistics, and random matrix theory.

Testing high-dimensional geometry

Recent advances in statistics and learning theory highlight the growing importance of high-dimensional feature spaces. In this context, the random geometric graph model
\mathcal{G}(n,p,d)
has become a canonical model.
[
14
]
first showed that that for fixed
n
and
d\to\infty
,
\mathcal{G}(n,p,d)
and
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
become indistinguishable in total variation distance. When
n,d\to\infty
, with constant
p
,
[
11
]
identified a sharp phase transition at
d\asymp n^{3}
in the total variation distance between the two models. In the sparse regime
p=\frac{\alpha}{n}
for a constant
\alpha>0
, the same authors proved showed that the total variation distance between the two distributions goes to
1
if
d=o(\log^{3}(n))
and they also conjectured a a critical window at
d\asymp\log^{3}(n)
. This conjecture was nearly solved by
[
31
]
, who showed that indistinguishability holds when
d=\Omega(\log^{36}(n))
.

Subsequent works have extended these testing thresholds to varied settings.

[
3
]
used Edgeworth‐style expansions to analyze random toroidal graphs.
[
5
,
6
]
investigated low‐degree polynomial complexity and the sandwiching coupling for comparing random geometric and Erdős–Rényi graphs.
[
10
]
determined thresholds for anisotropic latent geometries, and
[
30
,
36
]
studied the recovery of the latent inner product
\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle
, locating a threshold at
d\asymp np\log(1/p)
.

While the total variation distance gives one angle of comparison, another perspective comes from spectral graph theory. Since Wigner’s semicircle law, it has been known that for the Erdős–Rényi model
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, the empirical eigenvalue distribution of its adjacency matrix converges to the semicircle law
[
46
]
. This raises the question:

Can one distinguish
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
from
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
by their limiting spectral distributions?

We answer this in the negative under a mild dimension condition: as soon as
d\gg np\log^{2}(1/p)
, the two models share the same global spectrum for all
p\in(0,1/2)
. We treat the case when
np\to\infty
and
np=c
differently since
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
has different limiting spectral distributions
[
28
,
46
]
. The case when
np\to 0
is trivial since
(1-o(1))n
many vertices are isolated and
1-o(1)
portion of the eigenvalues are
0
.

Our results (Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
) thus reveal a universality phenomenon: despite their very different constructions,
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
and
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
become spectrally indistinguishable above a critical dimension.
Based on the entropic and spectral thresholds
d\asymp np\log(1/p)
pointed out in
[
6
,
30
,
36
]
, we conjecture that the true threshold for semicircle convergence is in fact
d\gg np\log(1/p)
, removing just a single logarithmic factor from our current condition.

Nonlinear random matrices

A nonlinear random matrix is obtained by a nonlinear function applied entrywise to a classical random matrix ensemble.
In recent years, a rich theory has emerged around such models, including kernel random matrices
[
8
,
12
,
15
,
17
,
39
,
40
]
, conjugate kernels and neural tangent kernels arising from neural networks
[
34
,
41
,
19
,
22
]
. Semicircle laws have been obtained
[
35
,
16
,
48
]
for a broad class of nonlinear random matrices.

Closest to the random geometric graph setting is the random inner-product kernel matrices studied in
[
35
]
. Let
\mathbf{A}
be an
n\times n
random matrix such that
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=f(\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle)\mathbf{1}\{i\not=j\}
, where
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
are i.i.d. random vectors uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
f
is a function independent of
d
. It was shown in
[
35
]
that when
d=\omega(n)
, the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathbf{A}
is the semicircle law. Taking
f(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x\geq\tau\}
recovers the adjacency matrix of a
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
for fixed
p\in(0,1)
. However, in the sparse regime
p\to 0
,
f
is a function depending on
d
, and existing proofs break down.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
are the first to describe the limiting spectral distributions of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
in the simultaneous high‐dimensional (
d\to\infty
) and sparse (
p\to 0
) regimes. This model unites two of the most challenging aspects in modern random matrix analysis, nonlinearity and sparsity, which require novel technical tools. Beyond the growing‐dimension setting, the spectrum of fixed‐dimensional random geometric graphs has also been explored in

[
2
,
23
]
.

Spectral gap of random graphs

The adjacency matrix in the Erdős–Rényi graph
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
has long been an important object in both random graph theory and random matrix theory. In particular, once the average degree satisfies
np=\Omega(\log n)
, it is known that, with high probability, the second-largest eigenvalue (in absolute value) is of order
O(\sqrt{np})

[
20
,
29
,
7
]
. These results show that sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs exhibit nearly optimal spectral expansion, comparable with random regular graphs
[
21
]
.

In recent years, this line of work has been extended to high‐dimensional random geometric graphs
[
32
,
5
,
6
]
, motivated by connections to high‐dimensional expanders and data‐science models. In parallel,
[
3
]
shows that by changing to the
\ell_{\infty}
-metric on the
d
-dimensional torus, the spectral properties of the random geometric graphs are different from the spherical case.
[
30
]
analyzed a Gaussian‐mixture block‐model variant of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
, showing consistency of spectral clustering based on the adjacency matrix.

A natural question is whether, despite the edge dependencies induced by geometry,
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
still achieves the optimal
O(\sqrt{np})
bound once
d
exceeds an appropriate threshold.
Our Theorem
2.3
shows that for
d=\Omega(np)
, the second eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
is of order
O(\log^{4}(n)\sqrt{np})
, which is optimal up to a
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
factor.
We conjecture that, above a critical dimension, the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
indeed satisfies the optimal
O(\sqrt{np})
bound. Achieving such a bound with the moment method would likely require a novel combinatorial counting argument that goes beyond the classical high‐trace method developed in
[
47
]
.

2.
Main results

2.1.
Semicircle law for
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)

Our first main result establishes a semicircle law for random geometric graphs.

Theorem 2.1

(Semicircle law)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
. Assume
p\in(0,p_{0}]
for any constant
p_{0}<1/2
,
np\to\infty
and
d=\omega(np\log^{2}(1/p))
. Then the empirical spectral distribution of
\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
converges weakly to the semicircle law.

When
p
is constant, Theorem
2.1
recovers the semicircle law for random inner-product kernel matrices from
[
35
]
in the regime
d=\omega(n)
. The special case
p=1/2
can also be proved using the results from
[
5
]
. More broadly, it exhibits a universality phenomenon for
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
: even as the graph becomes sparse, the semicircle law persists under the relatively mild condition
d=\omega(np\log^{2}(1/p))
, quantifying the precise trade‐off between sparsity and the ambient dimension.

2.2.
Limiting spectral distribution for
\mathcal{G}(n,d,\frac{\alpha}{n})

We next turn to the very sparse regime
p=\frac{\alpha}{n}
, for fixed
\alpha>0
. In this case, the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathcal{G}(n,\frac{\alpha}{n})
no longer follows the semicircle law. Instead, as shown in
[
9
,
28
,
18
]
, the empirical spectral distribution of the rescaled adjacency matrix
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\mathbf{A}
converges weakly to a deterministic limit
\nu_{\alpha}
. Its odd moments vanish, and for even
k=2m
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{2m}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\ell+1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\alpha^{\ell-m}.

(1)

Moreover, as
\alpha\to\infty
,
\nu_{\alpha}
converges back to the semicircle law, although no closed‐form density is known for
\nu_{\alpha}
.
Our next theorem shows that the random geometric graph
\mathcal{G}(n,d,\frac{\alpha}{n})
shares this same limiting spectral distribution
\nu_{\alpha}
whenever the dimension grows mildly.

Theorem 2.2

(Bounded expected degree)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph
G
sampled from
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
. Assume
p=\frac{\alpha}{n}
and
d=\omega(\log^{2}(n))
. Then the empirical spectral distribution of
\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}
converges weakly to
\nu_{\alpha}
.

We present two numerical simulations for Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. Figure

shows a semicircle law behavior of the empirical spectral distributions for both
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
and
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
and Figure

shows when
p=\frac{c}{n}
, the empirical spectral distributions for both
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
and
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
are close even when
d
is small.

2.3.
Second largest eigenvalue

Applying the trace estimates based on ear decomposition and the block-cut tree in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we also obtain the following bound on the second largest eigenvalue (in absolute value)

\lambda(\mathbf{A}):=\max\{|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|,|\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})|\}.

Theorem 2.3

(Second largest eigenvalue)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph
G
sampled from
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
. For any constant
\gamma\geq 1
, assume
\frac{C_{0}\gamma^{4}\log^{4}(n)}{n}\leq p\leq e^{-4}
for an absolute constant
C_{0}>0
. Then there is an absolute constant
C
such that with probability
1-n^{-\gamma}
,

\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbf{A})\leq C\left(\gamma^{4}(\log n)^{4}\sqrt{np}+\tau np\right).

(2)

Remark 2.4

(Removing technical conditions in
[
32
]
)
.

A second eigenvalue bound appears in
[
32
, Theorem 1.7]
as a corollary of
[
32
, Theorem 1.10]
, but only under two restrictive assumptions: (i)
\tau\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{np}}
, equivalently
d=O(np\log^{2}(1/p))
and (ii)
np=\omega(d^{3}\log^{4}(n))
, which confine their result to a low-dimensional regime (see also
[
31
, Eq. (6) and Theorem 3.1]
).
These conditions limit its applicability when one studies expansion properties of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)

[
1
]
. By contrast, we remove these technical constraints so that (
2
) holds for all ranges of
d
.

When
d=\Omega(np)
, Theorem
2.3
implies
\lambda(\mathbf{A})=O(\log^{4}(n)\sqrt{np})
, showing that in high–dimensional regimes, the expansion of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
matches that of the Erdős-Rényi graph
[
20
,
29
]
, up to a
\mathrm{poly}\log(n)
factor. The generalized Alon-Boppana bound in
[
27
, Theorem 8]
implies that
\lambda(\mathbf{A})=\Omega(\sqrt{np})
with high probability. Thus Theorem
2.3
is tight up to a
\mathrm{poly}\log(n)
factor.

2.4.
Technical overview

Our proof relies on the moment method from random matrix theory
[
4
,
47
]
, applied both to derive the limiting spectral distribution and to bound the top eigenvalues.

To obtain the limiting spectral distribution for a Wigner-type matrix
\mathbf{M}
with independent (up to symmetry) entries
[
49
]
, one analyzes the normalized trace
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}^{k})
for each fixed
k
. After a proper scaling, the dominant contributions come from closed walks on rooted planar trees, each edge of which is traversed exactly twice. Counting these “double‐edge” walks yields the Catalan numbers and hence the
k
-th moment of the semicircle law. Crucially, independence forces any walk containing an edge visited only once to have zero expectation. To obtain a sharp spectral norm bound, one lets
k=O(\log n)
and evaluates
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M}^{k})
by a finer subgraph‐count estimate, and then applies Markov’s inequality
[
47
]
.

In contrast, the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
exhibits edge dependencies that invalidate the “zero‐contribution” argument for edges traversed only once.
To apply the moment method, we work with the centered adjacency matrix
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}
and study its normalized trace:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{n^{k/2+1}(p(1-p))^{k/2}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k-1}i_{k}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k}i_{1}}].

(3)

Each term corresponds to a closed walk on the complete graph. To overcome the edge dependencies, our analysis proceeds in two stages:

(1)

Tree-like closed walks:
When the underlying walk graph is a tree (each edge traversed exactly twice), we exploit conditional independence: by peeling off leaves one at a time, we evaluate the expectation recursively. This recovers the Catalan‐number moments of the semicircle law.

(2)

Non‐tree closed walks:
For walks whose underlying graph contains cycles or edges traversed more than twice, we develop a novel graph‐decomposition algorithm combining block‐cut trees and ear decompositions (see Section
3
for a brief introduction) to evaluate the expectation of a closed-walk graph recursively. Conditioned on the random vectors associated with the endpoints of each ear, this peeling procedure allows us to show that each ear contributes negligibly in the limit. Together with refined subgraph probability estimates from
[
30
]
, we obtain the semicircle law down to
d=\omega(np\log^{2}(1/p))
. A similar strategy works for the proof of Theorem
2.2
in the constant expected degree regime.

Earlier works also applied the trace method to
\mathcal{G}(n,d,p)
.
[
32
]
used a 2-core contraction argument to bound
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{k})
by conditioning on vertices with degree
\geq 3
.
[
30
]
expanded the threshold function in Gegenbauer polynomials and used their orthogonality to evaluate the closed-walk subgraphs, also with a contraction argument. However, their estimates fall short of proving the moment convergence to the semicircle.
Different from their arguments, we evaluate conditional expectations recursively by peeling off ears one by one, which helps us to obtain a tighter bound for the contribution of non-tree closed walks.

For the second eigenvalue bound, we apply our new estimates to all closed-walk subgraphs to bound
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A})^{k}]
for
k=O(\log n)
. This allows us to remove the technical conditions in
[
32
]
and achieve a spectral gap bound valid across all regimes of
d
.
We expect that our decomposition and peeling techniques will extend to other sparse random geometric graph models and to more general nonlinear random matrices.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Random matrix theory

Definition 3.1

(Empirical spectral distribution)
.

Given an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix
A
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A)\leq\lambda_{2}(A)\leq...\leq\lambda_{n}(A)
, the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) for
A
is

\mu_{A}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(A)}(x).

The semicircular distribution with density supported on
[-2,2]
is

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}
, whose
k
-th moment
m_{k}
satisfies
m_{k}=\begin{cases}0&k\text{ is odd}\\
C_{\frac{k}{2}}&k\text{ is even}\end{cases}
, where
C_{k}=\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{k+1}
is the Catalan number
[
45
]
.

We will show that the empirical spectral distribution of a random matrix converges to a limiting distribution due to the following lemma:

Lemma 3.2

(Lemma B.3 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
m_{k}:=\int x^{k}d\mu
be the
k
-th moment of a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
. Under the following Carleman’s condition

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m_{2k}^{1/2k}}=\infty,

a distribution is uniquely defined by
m_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

The moment method states that, if a distribution
\mu
is uniquely defined by its moment, then for a given sequence of distribution
\mu_{n}
for which
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n}}(x^{k})\to\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(x^{k})
for all
k\geq 1
integer, then
\mu_{n}
converges weakly, in the sense of distributions, to
\mu
.

Ear decomposition

Our proof for the semicircle law will rely on the ear decomposition
[
38
,
44
]
of 2-edge connected graphs to evaluate the contribution of each subgraph in the trace expansion. We give a brief introduction below.

Definition 3.3

(
k
-edge-connected)
.

A graph is
k
-edge-connected if the removal of any
(k-1)

Definition 3.4

(Ear decomposition)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, undirected, 2-edge-connected graph. An
ear decomposition
of

is a sequence of subgraphs

(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{s})
satisfying the following conditions:

•

R_{1}

•

For each

R_{i}
is a path (called an

) whose internal vertices are disjoint from
\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}V(R_{j})
, and whose endpoints lie in
\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}V(R_{j})
.

•

The union of all edges in the sequence gives the edge set of
G
, i.e.,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}E(R_{i})=E(G).

3.3.
Block-cut tree

Another ingredient in our proof is the
block-cut tree
. Any connected graph can be decomposed into a tree of 2-edge-connected components, which is called the
block-cut tree
of the graph
[
26
]
. We now introduce some definitions for
block-cut trees
.

Definition 3.6

(bridges)
.

We define
bridges
as edges whose removal increases the number of connected components.

Definition 3.7

(Block-cut tree)
.

We decompose the graph
G
into its 2-edge-connected components, which are connected by bridges. We express
G
as

\displaystyle G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}G_{C_{i}}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}F_{j},

(4)

where each
G_{C_{i}}
denotes a
2-edge-connected component
, and each
F_{j}
represents a
1-edge-connected component
spanned by bridges. We call each
F_{j}
a
bridge component
, and it is a subtree of
G
. This gives a
block-cut tree
of
G
.

Proof of Theorem

4.1.
Trace expansion

Denote

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}.

We will show Theorem
2.1
by applying the moment method to
\mathbf{Q}
.
We start with the trace expansion,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{n^{k/2+1}(p(1-p))^{k/2}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k-1}i_{k}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k}i_{1}}],

(5)

where the sum is over all sequences
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
with
i_{j}\not=j_{j-1},2\leq j\leq k
and
i_{k}\not=i_{1}
. The sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k},i_{1})
corresponds to a closed walk of length
k+1
on a complete graph with
n
vertices. For each closed walk
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k},i_{1})
, we can associate a graph
G_{I}=(V,E)
.
We classify closed walks into two categories:

•

\mathcal{C}_{1}
: Closed walks in which each edge is traversed exactly twice, and the underlying graph is a tree.

•

\mathcal{C}_{2}
: All other closed walks not included in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

We write (
5
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)^{k}=S_{1}+S_{2},

(6)

where
S_{i}
is the contribution from all
G_{I}
in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.
We estimate each
S_{i}
in the following subsections.

4.2.
Estimating
S_{1}

By our definition,
S_{1}=0
if
k
is odd since closed walks on the tree must be of even length. Now we assume
k=2m
, then for each closed walk
I\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
G_{I}=(V,E)
is a tree and we must have
|V|=m+1
and
|E|=m
. With (
5
),

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{m+1}(p(1-p))^{m}}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},...i_{2m})\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1},i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2},i_{3}}...\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2m},i_{1}}]

(7)

Let
\mathcal{T}_{m}
be the set of all rooted planar trees with
m+1
vertices, then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{m}|=\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{m+1},

(8)

which is the
m
-th Catalan number
[
45
]
. For each unlabeled rooted planar tree
T\in\mathcal{T}_{m}
, there are
n(n-1)\cdots(n-m)=(1+o(1))n^{m+1}
many ways to label
T
with distinct vertex labels from
[n]
. Each rooted planar tree
T
with
m+1
distinct vertex labels in
[n]
corresponds to a closed walk
(i_{1},\dots,i_{2m})
, with the vertex sequence in the depth-first search on
T
.
We apply a canonical labeling with
\{1,\dots,m+1\}
to vertices in
T
such that whenever the
k
-th distinct vertex
v
is visited by the depth-first search on
T
for the first time,
v
is assigned with a label
k\in[m+1]
.

Since each edge in
T
is visited exactly twice, we can rewrite
S_{1}
as

\displaystyle S_{1}=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-m)}{n^{m+1}(p(1-p))^{m}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{m}}\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2},

(9)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{e}=\mathbf{Q}_{ij}
if
e=\{i,j\}
, which represents the entry of
\mathbf{Q}
index by an edge
e\in T
.
Let
T
be a tree with
m
edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
and
m+1
vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{m+1}
. Since each
v\in V(T)
corresponds to a latent vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}

(10)

as the expectation taken over all vectors corresponding to the vertices
v\in V(T)
. We denote the collection of such vectors by
\mathcal{V}
.

We aim to compute (
10
)
by recursively peeling off the leaves of the tree. At each step, we choose one of the leaf edges
e^{*}=(v_{1}^{*},v_{2}^{*})
, where
v_{1}^{*}
is a leaf node and
v_{2}^{*}
is an internal node. We condition on the random vector
\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}
associated with node
v_{2}^{*}
to make the leaf edge independent from the rest of the tree.
By independence, we can write:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}\}}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{2}^{*}\}}\prod_{e\in E(T)\setminus\{e^{*}\}}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{*}}\mathbf{Q}_{e^{*}}^{2}\right].

(11)

Since for each edge,
\mathbf{Q}_{e}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}=p(1-p)^{2}+(1-p)(-p)^{2}=p(1-p),

(12)

we can iteratively peel off one leaf at a time, multiplying the expectation by
p(1-p)
at each step. Repeating this for all
m
edges in the tree, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}=[p(1-p)]^{m}.

(13)

From equations (
9
) and (
8
), this gives:

\displaystyle S_{1}=\begin{cases}(1+o(1))\dfrac{\binom{2m}{m}}{m+1}&\text{if }k=2m,\\
0&\text{if }k\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(14)

4.3.
Estimating
S_{2}

Note that in the analysis of
S_{1}
, no assumption on
d
is used. However, estimating
S_{2}
involves closed walks where certain edges are traversed only once, and their contributions depend on the vector dimension
d
. To establish that closed walks in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
have vanishing contributions to the moment, we combine
block-cut tree decomposition
and the
ear decomposition
and algorithmically evaluate the moment.

4.3.1.
A graph decomposition algorithm

Recall that we want to evaluate

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k/2+1}(p(1-p))^{k/2}}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1},i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2},i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k},i_{1}}].

(15)

For a closed walk of length
k
,
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
, we denote by
G_{I}=(V,E)
the simple graph spanned by
I
, with
v=|V|
and
e=|E|
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{2}
denote the collection of distinct unlabeled graphs obtained from all possible closed walks in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. For each
G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, there are

n(n-1)\cdots(n-v+1)=O(n^{v})

ways to label it. It suffices to evaluate the contribution of a fixed
G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G)}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)},

(16)

where
m(e)
denotes the multiplicity of each edge
e\in E(G)
, and the expectation is taken over the random vectors
\mathbf{v}
corresponding to each vertex
v\in V(G)
as we did in Section
4.2
. We proceed with the following steps.

Step 1: A block-cut tree of
G

Recall the definition of a block-cut tree from Definition
3.7
.
We decompose the graph
G
into its 2-edge-connected components, which are connected by bridges. Since
G
is spanned by a closed walk
I
, every bridge must be traversed at least twice. We express
G
as

\displaystyle G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}G_{C_{i}}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}F_{j},

(17)

where each
G_{C_{i}}
denotes a
2-edge-connected component
, and each
F_{j}
is a
bridge component
. This gives a
block-cut tree
of
G
. Note that when
r=0
,
G
is a tree.

Denote by
v_{F_{j}}
and
e_{F_{j}}
the number of vertices and edges in
F_{j}
, respectively, and by
v_{C_{i}}
and
e_{C_{i}}
the number of vertices and edges in the 2-edge-connected component
G_{C_{i}}
, respectively.

Let
J(G)
be the set of all junction vertices in
G
defined in Definition
3.8
.
We condition on all junction vertices to make the contribution from each 2-edge-connected component and each bridge independent. We can bound (
16
) by

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G)}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

(18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\in J(G)}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G_{C_{i}})\setminus J(G)}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|\prod_{j=1}^{q}\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(F_{j})\setminus J(G)}\prod_{e\in E(F_{j})}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|.

(19)

Step 2: Evaluating the expectation of a leaf component

Denote
T(G)
the block-cut tree of
G
. A leaf of
T(G)
has two possibilities:

(1)

It corresponds to a leaf in
G
, belonging to certain
F_{j}
.

(2)

It corresponds to 2-edge-connected component
G_{C_{i}}
that only contains one junction vertex.

We call this
F_{j}
or
G_{C_{i}}
an leaf component of
G
. We treat the two cases separately.

Case 1:
F_{j}
is a leaf component

Since each edge in the bridge path
F_{j}
is traversed at least twice and
F_{j}
contains a leaf vertex in
G
, by (
13
), and the fact
|Q_{e}|\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(F_{j})\setminus J(G)}\prod_{e\in E(F_{j})}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(F_{j})\setminus J(G)}\prod_{e\in E(F_{j})}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{2}=[p(1-p)]^{v_{F_{j}}-1},

(20)

where the last identity holds by taking the conditional expectation sequentially as in (
13
).

Case 2:
G_{C_{i}}
is a leaf component

We now deal with the conditional expectation from a 2-edge-connected component
G_{C_{i}}
, where
G_{C_{i}}
contains only one junction vertex.

Our analysis relies on the
ear decomposition
defined in Definition
3.4
. By Lemma
3.5
, we can find an ear decomposition whose initial cycle
R_{1}
contains the junction vertex in
G_{C_{i}}
.
Let
(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{s})
be the ear decomposition of
G_{C_{i}}
, where
s=s(C_{i})
is the number of ears. Let
e_{R_{j}}=|E(R_{j})|
. By the properties of the ear decomposition, we have

\displaystyle e_{C_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s(C_{i})}e_{R_{j}}.

(21)

We have the following inequality from the ear decomposition:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G_{C_{i}})\setminus J(G)}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G_{C_{i}})\setminus J(G)}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(R_{j})\setminus J(G)}\prod_{e\in E(R_{j})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|.

(22)

Next, we handle the contribution from each ear recursively. There are two subcases:

Case (1):
s=1
. In this case,
G_{C_{i}}
is a cycle of length
e_{C_{i}}
. Note that
G_{C_{i}}
contains only one junction vertex, with Lemma
A.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G_{C_{i}})\setminus J(G)}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

(23)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}(\alpha_{m(e)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}+\beta_{m(e)})\right|,

(24)

where
\alpha_{m(e)},\beta_{m(e)}
are constants
with
\alpha_{m(e)}\in[0,1]
. We can expand the expectation in (
24
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}[\mathbf{Q}_{e}\alpha_{m(e)}+\beta_{m(e)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\mathbf{Q}_{e}\alpha_{m(e)}+\sum_{S\subsetneq E(G_{C_{i}})}\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in S}\mathbf{Q}_{e}\alpha_{m(e)}\prod_{e\notin S}\beta_{m(e)}

(25)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\mathbf{Q}_{e}\alpha_{m(e)},

(26)

where the second term on the right-hand side vanishes because each subset
S\subsetneq E(G_{C_{i}})
forms a forest and the expectation is zero due to the conditional independence of edges.

On the other hand, by a binomial expansion and the conditional independence of edges,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\mathbf{Q}_{e}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}(\mathbf{A}_{e}-p)\right|

(27)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\mathbf{A}_{e}-p^{e_{C_{i}}}\right|\leq Cp^{e_{C_{i}}-1}(C\tau)^{e_{C_{i}}-2}\sqrt{\log(1/p)}

(28)

for an absolute constant
C>0
, where the last inequality is due to the cycle probability estimate from (
86
).

Combining all (
24
), (
26
), and (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G_{C_{i}})\setminus J(G)}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|\leq Cp^{e_{C_{i}}-1}(C\tau)^{e_{C_{i}}-2}\sqrt{\log(1/p)}.

(29)

Case (2):
s\geq 2
. In this case,
R_{2},\dots,R_{s}
are paths. Note that the last ear
R_{s}
only intersects with the previously constructed subgraph
\bigcup_{j=1}^{s-1}R_{j}
through its two endpoints. Let
J(R_{j})
be the set of the two endpoints in
R_{j}
. There are two cases depending on
e_{R_{j}}
:

•

If
e_{R_{j}}\geq 3
, using the the expansion as in (
24
) (
26
), and (
28
), the contribution of
R_{j}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(R_{j})\setminus J(R_{j})}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(R_{j})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(R_{j})\setminus J(R_{j})}\prod_{e\in E(R_{j})}\mathbf{A}_{e}-p^{e_{R_{j}}}\right|

(30)

\displaystyle\leq Cp^{e_{R_{j}}-1}(C\tau)^{e_{R_{j}}-2}\sqrt{\log(1/p)},

(31)

where in the last inequality, we use the path probability estimate from Lemma
87
.

•

If
e_{R_{j}}=2
, let
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
be the path formed by
R_{j}
with
e_{1}=(v_{1},v_{2}),e_{2}=(v_{2},v_{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(R_{j})\setminus J(R_{j})}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(R_{j})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{v_{2}}[\mathbf{Q}_{v_{1}v_{2}}^{m(e_{1})}\mathbf{Q}_{v_{2}v_{3}}^{m(e_{2})}]\right|\leq|\mathbb{E}_{v_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{v_{1}v_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{v_{2}v_{3}}|\leq\mathbb{E}_{v_{2}}|\mathbf{Q}_{v_{1}v_{2}}|\leq 2p,

(32)

where in the first inequality we use (
24
). Let
t_{C_{i}}
be the number of length-2 ears in
G_{C_{i}}
.
We can apply the estimate (
31
) and (
32
) sequentially by peeling off
R_{s},\dots,R_{2}
, and finally
R_{1}
to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G_{C_{i}})\setminus J(G)}\!\!\prod_{e\in E(G_{C_{i}})}\!\!\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

(33)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{j=2}^{s_{C_{i}}}\left(p^{e_{R_{j}}-1}(C\tau)^{e_{R_{j}}-2}C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\mathbf{1}_{e_{R_{j}>2}}+2p\mathbf{1}_{e_{R_{j}}=2}\right)p^{e_{R_{1}}-1}(C\tau)^{e_{R_{1}}-2}C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{t_{C_{i}}}p^{e_{C_{i}}-s_{C_{i}}}(C\tau)^{e_{C_{i}}-2s_{C_{i}}}C^{s_{C_{i}}-t_{C_{i}}}\log^{(s_{C_{i}}-t_{C_{i}})/2}(1/p),

(35)

where
s_{C_{i}}
is the number of ears in
G_{C_{i}}
.
Compared to (
28
), we can see (
35
) holds for both Cases (1) and (2).

Step 3: Peeling off the block-cut tree

In Step 2, we have shown how to evaluate the contribution from the first leaf component in the block-cut tree of
G
.
Now, we decompose each graph
G
by starting with any leaf component in its block-cut tree.
After evaluating its conditional expectation, we sequentially remove leaf components one by one until the block-cut tree is empty.

Using the conditional independence, combining (
20
) and (
35
), we can bound (
19
) by

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{v\in V(G)}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(2^{t_{C_{i}}}p^{e_{C_{i}}-s_{C_{i}}}(C\tau)^{e_{C_{i}}-2s_{C_{i}}}C^{s_{C_{i}}-t_{C_{i}}}\log^{(s_{C_{i}}-t_{C_{i}})/2}(1/p)\right)\prod_{j=1}^{q}p^{v_{F_{j}}-1}

(37)

\displaystyle=2^{\sum_{i=1}^{r}t_{C_{i}}}p^{e(G)-\sum_{i=1}^{r}s_{C_{i}}}(C\tau)^{\sum_{i=1}^{r}(e_{C_{i}}-2s_{C_{i}})}C^{\sum_{i=1}^{r}(s_{C_{i}}-t_{C_{i}})}\log^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(s_{C_{i}}-t_{C_{i}})}(1/p).

(38)

Denote
t(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}t_{C_{i}}
the total number of length-2 ears in
G
.
Since the number of all ears in the block-cut tree is the excess of
G
denoted by

g(G):=e(G)-v(G)+1,

we have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}s_{C_{i}}=g(G)=e(G)-v(G)+1
.
We also denote
c(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{C_{i}}
to be the number of total edges in all 2-edge-connected components of
G
. Then we can simplify (
38
) as

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{t(G)}p^{v(G)-1}(C\tau)^{c(G)-2g(G)}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g(G)-t(G)},

(39)

which holds for any
G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

4.3.2.
Bounding
S_{2}

Recall

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k/2+1}(p(1-p))^{k/2}}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1},i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2},i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k},i_{1}}]

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}}\Bigl[\frac{n^{v(G)}}{n^{k/2+1}(p(1-p))^{k/2}}\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\Bigl].

(41)

For each
G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, from (
39
), since
p\in[0,p_{0}]
,

\displaystyle\frac{n^{v(G)}}{n^{k/2+1}(p(1-p))^{k/2}}\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|\leq 2^{k/2+t}(np)^{v-1-k/2}(C\tau)^{c-2g}(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)})^{g-t}.

(42)

Here,
t
,
v
,
c
, and
g
stand for
t(G)
,
v(G)
,
c(G)
, and
g(G)
, respectively.

We now consider two cases:

Case 1:
G
is a tree.

In this case, since
G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
does not contribute to
S_{1}
,
G
contains only bridges, and at least one bridge is visited more than twice. This implies

v<1+\frac{k}{2},\quad\text{ and }t=c=g=0.

Therefore, since
np\to\infty
, (
42
) is bounded by

2^{k/2}(np)^{-1}=o(1).

Case 2:
G
is not a tree.

In this case, we have
g\geq 1
.
Since
\tau\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/p)}{d}}
for some absolute constant
C^{\prime}>0
from Lemma
A.3
, we can find a constant
C>0
and bound (
42
) by

\displaystyle 2^{k/2+t}(np)^{v-1-k/2}(C\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/p)}{d}})^{c-2g}(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)})^{g-t}

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{k/2+t}C^{c-g-t}\frac{(np)^{v-1-k/2}\log(1/p)^{c/2-g/2-t/2}}{d^{c/2-g}}.

(44)

Since the number of edges in
G
equals the sum of the number of edges in all bridges and the number of edges in all
2-edge-connected components
, and since each edge in a bridge must be visited at least twice, we have

e\leq c+\frac{k-c}{2}=\frac{k}{2}+\frac{c}{2}.

Thus

v-1-\frac{k}{2}\leq\frac{c}{2}-e+v-1=\frac{c}{2}-g

Thus we can bound (
42
) by

\displaystyle 2^{k/2+t}C^{c-g-t}\Bigl(\frac{np\log^{2}(1/p)}{d}\Bigl)^{c/2-g}(\log(1/p))^{3g/2-t/2-c/2}.

(45)

Since the total number of edges in all
2-edge-connected components
equals twice the number of length-2 ears plus the number of edges in all other ears of length
\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle c\geq 2t+3(g-t)=3g-t.

(46)

Moreover, by our construction of the ear decomposition, each 2-edge-connected component
G_{C_{i}}
has at least a cycle ear
R_{1}
of length at least
3
, so we have
g\geq t+1
, hence
c\geq 2g+1
.
Under the assumption
d=\omega(np\log^{2}(1/p))
, we obtain (
45
) is
o(1)
for any fixed
k
.

Combining Cases 1 and 2, since
|\mathcal{G}_{2}|
is finite when
k
is fixed, we conclude
S_{2}=o(1)
.

Proof of Theorem

.

Combining all the estimates above, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=S_{1}+S_{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{m+1}\binom{2m}{m}&\text{if }k=2m,\\
0&\text{if }k\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(47)

This implies the empirical spectral distribution of
\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
converges weakly to the semicircle law.

Define
\mathbf{B}
to be the
n\times n
matrix obtained by subtracting
p
from each entry of
\mathbf{A}
, including the diagonal entries. Then
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})=1
. By Lemma
77
,

\displaystyle\|F^{\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}}-F^{\frac{\mathbf{B}}{np(1-p)}}\|\leq\frac{1}{n},

(48)

and thus
\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
and
\frac{\mathbf{B}}{np(1-p)}
have the same limiting spectral distribution. Note that the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
differs from
\mathbf{B}
only in the diagonal entries. Moreover, adding
p\mathbb{I}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix, shifts the eigenvalues by at most
p/\sqrt{np(1-p)}=o(1)
. Therefore
\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
and
\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}
have the same limiting spectral distribution. This finishes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Now we aim to show that for
\mathcal{G}(n,p,d)
, when
np=\alpha
for some fixed constant
\alpha>0
, the normalized adjacency matrix attains the same limiting spectral distribution as
G(n,\frac{\alpha}{n})
. Again, we start with the trace expansion.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k-1}i_{k}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k}i_{1}}],

(49)

We classify closed walks into two categories:

•

\mathcal{C}_{1}
: Closed walks in which the resulting simple graph is a tree.

•

\mathcal{C}_{2}
: All other closed walks not included in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

Our goal is to demonstrate that when
n\to\infty
, walks belonging to
\mathcal{C}_{1}
contribute to (
49
), whereas those in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
have vanishing contributions.

5.1.
Estimating
S_{1}

By our definition,
S_{1}=0
when
k
is odd, since closed walks on a tree must be of even length. Note that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
here differs from the definition in Section
4
; here we also consider walks that may traverse some edges more than twice.

Now assume
k=2m
. For each closed walk
I\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, the graph
G_{I}=(V,E)
is a tree, and we must have
|V|=|E|+1\leq m+1
and
|E|\leq m
. Using (
49
), we can write:

\displaystyle S_{1}=\frac{1}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{2m})\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2m}i_{1}}].

(50)

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
denote the set of all rooted planar trees with
\ell+1
vertices. Then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{\ell}|=\frac{1}{\ell+1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell},

(51)

which is the
\ell
-th Catalan number. For each unlabeled rooted planar tree
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
, there are
n(n-1)\cdots(n-\ell)=(1+o(1))n^{\ell+1}
ways to assign distinct vertex labels from
[n]
. Each such labeled tree corresponds to a closed walk
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
, where the vertex sequence follows a depth-first search traversal of
T
.
We apply a canonical labeling using
\{1,\dots,\ell+1\}
such that whenever the
k
-th distinct vertex
v
is first visited in the depth-first search of
T
, it is assigned label
k\in[\ell+1]
.

We can now rewrite
S_{1}
as

\displaystyle S_{1}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-\ell)}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right]

(52)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{e}=\mathbf{Q}_{ij}
if
e=\{i,j\}
.
By Lemma
A.2
, we have that for
m(e)\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}]=(1+o(1))p.

(53)

Therefore, for a tree
T
with
\ell
edges, for any fixed
\ell
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}=[(1+o(1))p]^{\ell}=(1+o(1))p^{\ell}.

(54)

With (
54
) and (
51
), we can rewrite (
52
) into

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{n^{\ell+1}}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell+1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\cdot(1+o(1))p^{\ell}

(55)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{(np)^{\ell}}{\alpha^{m}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell+1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}

(56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\alpha^{m-\ell}}\cdot\frac{1}{\ell+1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}+o(1).

(57)

This matches the moments of the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathcal{G}(n,\frac{\alpha}{n})
, as shown in (
1
).

5.2.
Bounding
S_{2}

We apply the graph decomposition algorithm in Section
4.3.1
and replace
np
with
\alpha
to bound
S_{2}
.

\displaystyle|S_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}_{i_{1},i_{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{i_{2},i_{3}}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{i_{k},i_{1}}]|

(58)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}}\frac{n^{v(G)}}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|.

(59)

For each
G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, from (
39
),

\displaystyle\frac{n^{v(G)}}{n\alpha^{k/2}}\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\Bigl|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{-k/2}2^{t(G)}(np)^{v(G)-1}(C\tau)^{c(G)-2g(G)}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g(G)-t(G)}

(60)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{v-1-k/2}2^{t}\left(C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/p)}{d}}\right)^{c-2g}\left(C^{\prime}\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g-t}

(61)

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
, where we use the upper bound on
\tau
in Lemma
A.3
.
Since we have
c-2g\geq g-t
, when
d=\omega(\log^{2}(n))
, we obtain (
61
) is
o(1)
for any fixed
k
. Since
|\mathcal{G}_{2}|
is finite when
k
is fixed, we conclude
S_{2}=o(1)
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{k}=\begin{cases}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\alpha^{l-m}\cdot\frac{1}{l+1}\binom{2l}{l}&k=2m\\
0&k=2m+1\end{cases}

(62)

Since
\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}
and
\frac{\mathbf{A}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}
have the same limiting spectral distribution due to (
48
), this finishes the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

6.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

In this section, we apply our graph decomposition method to bound the second eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
. Recall from (
39
), for each fixed graph
G
spanned by a closed walk of length
k
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\prod_{e\in E(G)}\mathbf{Q}_{e}^{m(e)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{t(G)}p^{v(G)-1}(C\tau)^{c(G)-2g(G)}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g(G)-t(G)}

(63)

for some constant
C>0
,
where

•

v(G)
is the number of vertices in
G
;

•

t(G)
is the number of length-2 ears in
G
;

•

g(G)=e(G)-v(G)+1
is the excess number of
G
, which is also equal to the total number of ears in the ear decomposition of
G
;

•

c(G)
is the number of total edges in all 2-edge connected components.

Let
b(G)
be the number of single edges in
G
. We will use the following counting lemma from
[
32
]
.

Lemma 6.1

(Claim 3.10 in
[
32
]
)
.

The number of closed walks in the trace expansion whose associated graph
G
satisfies
e(G)=e,b(G)=b,g(G)=g
is at most

n^{e+1-g}k^{2(k-b+g)}.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
k
be even. From (
63
) and Lemma
6.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c=0}^{k}\sum_{b=0}^{c}\sum_{g=0}^{\frac{c}{2}}\sum_{e=c}^{\frac{k+b}{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{g}n^{e+1-g}k^{2(k-b+g)}2^{t}p^{e-g}(C\tau)^{c-2g}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g-t}

(64)

\displaystyle\leq k\sum_{c=0}^{k}\sum_{b=0}^{c}\sum_{g=0}^{\frac{c}{2}}\sum_{e=c}^{\frac{k+b}{2}}n^{e+1-g}k^{2(k-b+g)}p^{e-g}(C\tau)^{c-2g}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g},

(65)

where we use the assumption
p\leq e^{-4}
so that
\frac{2}{\sqrt{\log(1/p)}}\leq 1
. Since
np\geq 1
, summing over
e
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq nk^{2}\sum_{c=0}^{k}\sum_{b=0}^{c}\sum_{g=0}^{\frac{c}{2}}(np)^{\frac{k+b}{2}-g}k^{2(k-b+g)}(C\tau)^{c-2g}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{g}

(66)

\displaystyle\leq nk^{2}\sum_{c=0}^{k}\sum_{b=0}^{c}\max\{(C\tau)^{c}k^{2(k-b)}(np)^{\frac{k+b}{2}},(np)^{\frac{k+b-c}{2}}k^{2(k-b)+c}(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)})^{c/2}\}.

(67)

Assuming
np\geq k^{4}
and
k\geq\log n
, we further have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq nk^{3}\sum_{c=0}^{k}\max\left\{(C\tau)^{c}k^{2(k-c)}(np)^{\frac{k+c}{2}},(np)^{\frac{k}{2}}k^{2k-c}\left(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)}\right)^{c}\right\}

(68)

\displaystyle\leq nk^{4}\max\left\{k^{2k}(np)^{\frac{k}{2}},(C\tau np)^{k},(np)^{\frac{k}{2}}k^{k}(C\sqrt{\log(1/p)})^{k}\right\}

(69)

\displaystyle=nk^{4}\max\left\{k^{2k}(C^{2}np)^{\frac{k}{2}},(C\tau np)^{k}\right\}.

(70)

Take
k=4\lfloor\gamma\log n\rfloor
for any
\gamma\geq 1
.
Then by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\geq 3\max\{(4\gamma\log n)^{4}\sqrt{np},C\tau np\}\right)\leq nk^{4}3^{-k}\leq n^{-\gamma}.

(71)

Therefore when
p\geq C_{0}\gamma^{4}\log^{4}(n)
and
p\leq e^{-4}
, with probability

,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Q}\|\leq C_{1}\left((\gamma\log n)^{4}\sqrt{np}+\tau np\right)

(72)

for an absolute constant
C_{1}
.

Moreover, by the Courant min-max theorem,

\displaystyle\lambda(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{A}-p\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\|

(73)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}\|+\|p\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\|

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{A}\|+p

(75)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left((\gamma\log n)^{4}\sqrt{np}+\tau np\right).

(76)

This finishes the proof.
∎
