A Structure-Preserving Numerical Scheme for Optimal Control and Design of Mixing in Incompressible Flows
Weiwei Hu 1, Ziqian Li 2 3, Yubiao Zhang 3, Enrique Zuazua 2 4 5

Abstract
. We develop a structure-preserving computational framework for optimal mixing control in incompressible flows. Our approach exactly conserves the continuous system’s key invariants (mass and L 2 L^{2} -energy), while also maintaining discrete state–adjoint duality at every time step. These properties are achieved by integrating a centered finite-volume discretization in space with a time-symmetric Crank–Nicolson integrator for both the forward advection and its adjoint, all inside a gradient-based optimization loop. The result is a numerical solver that is faithful to the continuous optimality conditions and efficiently computes mixing-enhancing controls. In our numerical tests, the optimized time-dependent stirring produces a nearly exponential decay of a chosen mix-norm, achieving orders-of-magnitude faster mixing than any single steady flow. To our knowledge, this work provides the first evidence that enforcing physical structure at the discrete level can lead to both exact conservation and highly effective mixing outcomes in optimal flow design.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Problem statement

Understanding mass transport and fluid mixing by advective flows poses fundamental yet long-standing challenges in both analysis and applications. Examples where such problems arise when studying the oceanic circulation
[
59
]
, the spreading of environmental pollutants
[
62
]
and chemical stimuli
[
56
]
, as well as microfluidic mixing
[
53
]
, to name but a few. Developing efficient and operational protocols for fluid transport and mixing has been widely investigated in both academic and industrial communities
[
37
]
.

This paper contributes to this field by developing a structure-preserving numerical scheme for optimal control in mixing, or, in other words, for the optimal design of optimal mixers to maximize the mixing rate.

We consider passive scalar fields
\theta=\theta(t,x)
, advected by incompressible velocity fields
v=v(t,x)
, in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with, typically,
d=2,3
), whose dynamics is described by the model (with
T>0
)

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta+\text{div}\,(\mathbf{v}\theta)=0\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega;~~\theta|_{t=0}=\theta^{0}.

Assume that the velocity field
\mathbf{v}
satisfies the incompressibility and no-penetration boundary conditions in
\Omega
, i.e.,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{div}\,\mathbf{v}=0\text{ in }\Omega\text{; }~\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

where
\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}
denotes the outward normal vector of the domain
\Omega
.
Under (
1.2
), the conservative form (
1.1
) is equivalent to
the nonconservative form

\partial_{t}\theta+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\theta=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

and we shall use these two formulations interchangeably.

For each
\theta^{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d}))
verifying (
1.2
), equation (
1.1
) has a unique solution in
C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
(see Lemma
7.1
).

Roughly, mixing refers to the process under which the solution
\theta
approaches its space-like average as time
t
increases.
For simplicity and without loss of generality (given the conservation of the solution’s space-like mean as time evolves), it is sufficient to consider initial data with zero mean.

In the sequel, we assume

(1.3)

\displaystyle\theta^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}\theta^{0}(x)\,dx=0,

and study how
\theta(t)
converges to
0
as a measure of mixing efficiency. Under these assumptions, the solution
\theta(t,x)
remains uniformly bounded, which in turn simplifies the analysis and allows the optimal control problem to be posed and treated rigorously within an appropriate functional framework.

To quantify mixing, we adopt negative Sobolev norms, which are widely used in the literature and initiated by Mathew, Mezić, and Petzold in
[
45
]
(with a negative Sobolev index
-1/2
). In fact, any norm that quantifies the weak convergence in
L^{2}
can be used as a mix-norm (e.g.
[
40
,
43
]
). Specifically, we use the homogeneous Sobolev norm
\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
, the dual norm of zero-mean functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, which, when restricted to the subspace
L^{2}(\Omega)
, takes the following form:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}=\sup\Big\{\int_{\Omega}f\phi dx~:~\int_{\Omega}\phi dx=0,~\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dx\leq 1\Big\},~\forall\,f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

A smaller value
\|\theta\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}
corresponds to better mixing, as it corresponds to a transfer of energy from low to high frequencies.

Other measures, such as the geometric mixing scale, the measure of the interface of the mixtures, as well as entropy, are employed to quantify the mixing process and the related control problems (e.g.
[
11
,
18
,
58
,
57
,
1
,
61
,
16
,
24
]
). A more detailed review can be found in
[
55
]
. But, in the present work, we focus on the
\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
-norm above since it is better suited for an optimal control treatment.

Admissible incompressible velocity fields live in an infinite-dimensional function space. In many practical settings, however, particularly in actuator-constrained mixer design, the flow cannot be chosen freely: the mixing velocity must be realized as an optimal (typically time-dependent) combination of a finite set of prescribed stirring profiles. This reduced-actuation viewpoint is standard in the fluid mechanics literature; see, for instance, work on optimized stirring flows
[
40
]
and related finite-mode mixing constructions such as random cellular flows
[
48
]
.

Thus, in the sequel, we assume the velocity field
\mathbf{v}
to be generated by a finite set of basis flows. More precisely, define the finite-dimensional space of velocity fields

(1.5)

\displaystyle U:=\text{span}\,\{\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}\},

where
\{\mathbf{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
are orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and satisfy (
1.2
) for
i=1,...,m.

Such a finite-dimensional restriction of the velocity field is standard in optimal control of mixing studies, reflecting practical actuator limitations (see, e.g.,
[
63
]
).

We consider the class of velocity fields
\mathbf{v}
in
L^{2}((0,T);U)
taking the form

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}(t)\mathbf{b}_{i},~t\in(0,T)

for some coefficients
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
in
L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Our goal is to derive efficient numerical solvers to compute the optimal mixer within this class.

Although formulated as an optimal control problem, the control
\mathbf{v}(\cdot)\in L^{2}((0,T);U)
can be interpreted as the design of an efficient incompressible stirring field within a prescribed actuation subspace
U
.

There are multiple possible choices for the basis functions
\mathbf{b}_{i}
. In 2D cases, a typical example, often employed in the analysis of mixing, consists of cellular flows
[
26
,
17
,
33
]
in square domains:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}-i\pi\sin(i\pi x_{1})\cos(i\pi x_{2})\\[3.00003pt]
i\pi\cos(i\pi x_{1})\sin(i\pi x_{2})\end{bmatrix}^{T},~i=1,2,\ldots,m.

Another example is Doswell frontogenesis
[
20
,
46
]
in circular domains, whose first basis mode can be written as

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}-x_{2}g(r(x_{1},x_{2}))\\[3.00003pt]
x_{1}g(r(x_{1},x_{2}))\end{bmatrix}^{T},

where

g(r(x_{1},x_{2})):=\frac{1}{r(x_{1},x_{2})}\ \overline{v}\ {\text{sech}}^{2}{(r(x_{1},x_{2}))}\tanh{(r(x_{1},x_{2}))},

with
r(x_{1},x_{2}):=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}
and
\overline{v}
a constant parameter (
\overline{v}:=2.59807
in
[
20
,
46
]
). We construct a multi-scale version of this Doswell flow basis as the second basis
\mathbf{b}_{2}
for our experiments (see Section
5.2
for details).

These two prototypical families of basis flows (cellular flows and Doswell frontogenesis flows) will serve as illustrative test cases in the numerical experiments of Section
5
. However, both the numerical scheme and the accompanying analysis developed in this paper are fully general and are not tied to these particular examples.

We now introduce the following optimal control problem, aiming to achieve effective mixing

(1.9)

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)}J(\mathbf{v}):=\min_{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|\theta(T;\mathbf{v})\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,dt\bigg],

subject to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) and (
1.6
), where
\gamma>0
is a regularization parameter.

Note that in this setting, the initial datum
\theta^{0}
has been fixed. Therefore, the optimal mixer depends on the initial datum. We could also consider the problem of building the optimal mixer for all possible initial data. This would lead to a more complex min-max problem:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)}\,\max_{||\theta^{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|\theta(T;\mathbf{v})\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,dt\bigg].

The latter corresponds to an optimal functional mixer, in the sense of being the best possible, for all possible configurations of the initial data. Rather different and more complex from an analytical perspective, we will focus on the first problem, (
1.9
), in which the initial datum is assumed to be given and fixed. We refer to
[
50
]
for an analysis of the problem of the optimal observers for wave and heat processes, in which the analog of (
1.10
) is considered, and the significant differences to the case where the initial data are fixed are discussed.

To better interpret the negative Sobolev norm, we observe that the functional
J
admits the following equivalent representation (see Appendix
7.4
for the proof):

\displaystyle\text{(OP)}\quad J(\mathbf{v})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\theta(T;\mathbf{v}),\eta(\theta(T;\mathbf{v}))\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(1.11)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,dt,~\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U),\hskip 21.68121pt

where
\eta=\eta(\theta(T;\mathbf{v}))\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is the solution of the elliptic equation (see Appendix
7.3
for the well-posedness)

(1.12)

\displaystyle-\Delta\eta=\theta(T;\mathbf{v})\text{ in }\Omega;~~\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}}\eta=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega;~~\int_{\Omega}\eta dx=0.

A control
\mathbf{v}^{*}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)
is said to be optimal for (OP) if it is a minimizer, namely, when

J(\mathbf{v}^{*})=\min_{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)}J(\mathbf{v}).

The proof of existence of an optimal control for (OP) is straightforward, and is presented in Appendix
7.2
, for the sake of completeness.

To solve the problem (OP) numerically, we aim to develop a numerical scheme based on the gradient of
J
which can be characterized as (see Appendix
7.5
for the proof):

(1.13)

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(\mathbf{v})=P_{U}(\gamma\mathbf{v}+\theta\nabla\rho),\text{ in }L^{2}((0,T);U),~\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U),

where
P_{U}
is the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
onto
U
, and
\rho\in C([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))
satisfies the adjoint equation

(1.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\text{div}\,(\mathbf{v}\rho)=0\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega;~~\rho|_{t=T}=\eta\text{ in }\Omega,

with the Lagrange multiplier
\eta\in H^{1}(\Omega)
given by (
1.12
). This gradient is well-defined thanks to the uniform boundedness of
\theta(t,x)
.

An optimal control
\mathbf{v}^{*}
to problem (OP) satisfies the optimality condition
J^{\prime}(\mathbf{v}^{*})=0
. Together with the state equation (
1.1
) and the adjoint equation (
1.14
), this condition yields the first-order necessary optimality conditions (FONCs) (in agreement with Pontryagin’s maximum principle) for problem (OP).

1.2.
Computational challenges and motivations.

A primary challenge in numerical simulations of scalar transport and mixing is the need to preserve the
global conservation
properties of the scalar field under incompressible flows. Beyond this, several coupled difficulties arise:

•

Mixing-driven multiscale formation.
The mixing dynamics generate increasingly fine-scale structures and sharp gradients in the scalar distribution.

•

Frequency transfer and norm imbalance.
As the mix-norm
\|\theta\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}
decreases, by duality
\|\theta\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
generally increases, reflecting the transfer of energy from low to high frequencies.

•

Resolution requirements.
The appearance of thin filaments during the evolution forces the computational mesh to be sufficiently refined to resolve these small spatial scales.

•

Algorithmic cost of the optimal control loop.
Introducing an optimal control problem adds substantial cost: one must solve the state equation forward in time together with the adjoint equation backward in time, coupled through a nonlinear optimality condition. A direct enforcement of these conditions typically yields prohibitively expensive computations.

•

Structure- and conservation-preserving discretization.
The numerical method must also maintain the conservation laws and the structural consistency inherent in both the state and adjoint systems (
1.1
) and (
1.14
).

Specifically, the following conservation laws need to be taken into consideration:

•

L^{p}
-norm conservation.

L^{p}
-norms are conserved along the dynamics generated by (
1.1
). More precisely, when
\theta^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\|\theta(t)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\|\theta^{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\text{ for each }t\geq 0,~p\in[1,+\infty],

as a consequence of incompressibility and no-penetration conditions.
In particular, the total mass

M(t):=\int_{\Omega}\theta(t)\,dx,~t\geq 0

and the energy

E(t):=\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2}(t)\,dx,~t\geq 0.

remain constant in time.

•

State–adjoint consistency.
The numerical scheme must preserve the duality relation between the state and adjoint equations (see (
7.4
)) so that

(1.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta(t)\,\rho(t)\,dx\equiv\int_{\Omega}\theta(T)\,\rho(T)\,dx,~t\in[0,T].

This identity is fundamental for accurate gradient computation in PDE-constrained optimization. If the discrete analog (
1.15
) is violated, the discrete adjoint becomes inconsistent with the discrete forward solver. This inconsistency produces an inexact reduced gradient, which can stall convergence in gradient-based optimization.

However, structure-preserving numerical schemes specifically tailored to mixing-control problems remain scarce.
The majority of PDE-constrained mixing studies employ conventional spatial discretizations, for instance, discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods (e.g.
[
34
,
63
]
) without explicitly targeting geometric or conservation properties inherent to the continuous system. To the best of our knowledge, no existing work systematically enforces state–adjoint consistency within the optimization loop, a property crucial for ensuring numerical fidelity and stability in optimal control solutions. This gap motivates the development of structure-preserving numerical methods specifically adapted to fluid mixing control.

1.3.
Main results and contributions

Our main results are as follows:

•

Numerical scheme.

We develop a solver (stated in Algorithm
2.1
) for the problem (OP) based on a conservative finite-volume spatial discretization and a second-order, time-symmetric Crank–Nicolson time integrator applied to both the state equation (
1.1
) and the adjoint equation (
1.14
). The gradient (
1.13
) is enforced by a conjugate gradient method with line search for step selection. We show the convergence of discrete solutions of state equation in Appendix
7.8
and the convergence of the discrete cost functional in Appendix
7.9
.

•

Structure-preserving properties.

The fully discrete scheme preserves the key invariants and dualities of the problem (OP), (see Theorem
3.1
).

–

Discrete mass conservation
(see (
3.2
)): the flux-difference finite-volume method with no-penetration boundaries preserves discrete mass at every time step.

–

Discrete
L^{2}
-energy conservation
(see (
3.3
)): the semi-discrete advection operator is skew-symmetric, and the Crank–Nicolson time integrator is an isometry in the discrete inner product, hence the discrete energy is invariant.

–

State–adjoint consistency
(see (
3.4
)): the Crank–Nicolson time integrator is applied to both the state and adjoint equations, to preserve the discrete analog of the pairing (
1.15
).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first mixing-control scheme that simultaneously enforces all three properties in a single fully discrete framework.

•

Equivalence between the Optimize-Then-Discretize and Discretize-Then-Optimize approaches.
The numerical scheme developed in this paper is designed by the Optimize-Then-Discretize approach. We construct a discrete gradient of
J
from the continuous gradient (
1.13
) by discretizing a coupled system consisting of the state and adjoint equations, together with (
1.12
). Theorem
4.1
states that this discrete gradient can also be established by the Discretize-Then-Optimize approach, where only the functional
J
and the state equation (
1.1
) are discretized. Thus, our numerical scheme owes the advantages of both approaches.

•

Numerical experiments.

Our numerical experiments provide consistent evidence that the proposed discretization delivers the intended structure-preserving behavior and robust optimization performance. Across the tested configurations, the fully discrete scheme preserves the targeted invariants to machine precision and maintains a tight state–adjoint consistency throughout the time-stepping. In addition, the computed optimal controls produce a marked faster decay of the chosen mix-norm than standard steady benchmarks, with the time-dependent optimized strategies exhibiting nearly exponential decay over significant time horizons.

•

Practical implications for optimal mixing

From an actuator-design perspective, these results point to a clear practical lesson: within a realistic low-dimensional actuation space, the decisive factor is not the sophistication of individual steady stirring modes, but the ability to combine them time-dependently. Each steady mode in isolation tends to lead, at best, to polynomial decay of the mix-norm. By contrast, an optimized time-dependent combination of the same limited set of modes can systematically break these barriers and yield near-exponential mixing rates. This highlights time modulation as a key design lever for effective mixing under actuation constraints.

Structure-preserving schemes.
In computational mathematics, there is broad recognition that numerical schemes should respect the intrinsic structures and invariants of physical systems. Schemes that exactly conserve quantities like mass, energy, or symplectic form can prevent unphysical drift and improve long-term stability.

For pure transport equations such as advection, numerous structure-preserving methods have been developed to maintain fundamental conservation laws. The finite-volume method (FVM) discretizes the integral form of a conservation law over control volumes, which guarantees strict local conservation of mass by construction
[
38
]
. High-order DG methods provide conservation together with sharp resolution of advective features; classical analyses include the unified DG framework
[
4
,
12
]
and hp-DG theory for advection–diffusion–reaction
[
30
]
.

In PDE-constrained optimal control problems, state–adjoint consistency reflects the standard duality relation between the state and adjoint variables; see, for example,
[
41
,
19
]
. The numerical scheme must preserve this consistency at the discrete level.

Adjoint consistency means that the discrete adjoint is truly the “transpose” (with respect to the chosen inner product) of the discrete forward operator, so that duality pairings are preserved, and the computed gradient is exact for the discrete problem
[
39
]
.

Time-integration schemes that are time-symmetric (self-adjoint), such as Crank–Nicolson or the implicit midpoint rule, are particularly attractive in this regard. Related consistency-preserving discretizations can be found, for instance, in
[
15
,
54
]
.

2.
Numerical scheme

This section is devoted to the numerical scheme (stated in Algorithm
2.1
) for solving problem (OP), which preserves several structural properties (presented in Theorem
3.1
). This scheme is designed according to the following four-component workflow: (i) discretization of the state equation (
1.1
), (ii) computation of the Lagrange multiplier (
1.12
), (iii) discretization of the adjoint equation (
1.14
), and (iv) computation of the derivative of the functional with respect to the velocity field, which, combined with the conjugate gradient method, is used to update this field and optimize its design. These four components are introduced in detail in the following subsections.

2.1.
Full discretization of the state equation

We construct a numerical method for the state equation (
1.1
) in two stages. First, we develop a finite-volume method (FVM) for spatial discretization. Second, we apply the Crank–Nicolson time discretization to obtain a fully discrete numerical method for equation (
1.1
). The fully discrete quantities depend on both the spatial mesh size
h
and the time step
\Delta t
.
To keep the notation light, we write
\theta_{h}
for the fully discrete density, with the dependence on
\Delta t
understood implicitly. The same convention applies to all other discrete variables.

(1) Finite-volume space discretization.

The FVM is based on enforcing the conservation law on each
control volume
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a partition of the physical domain
\Omega
into cells
K
. Integrating the transport equation over an arbitrary cell
K
and applying the divergence theorem yields the local balance relation

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{K}\theta(t)\,dx\;+\;\int_{\partial K}(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K})\,\theta(t)\,d\sigma\;=\;0,

where
\mathbf{n}_{K}
denotes the unit outward normal on
\partial K
.
This identity states that the rate of change of the
cell mass

\int_{K}\theta
is exactly balanced by the net advective flux through the cell boundary. The spatial discretization then proceeds by representing
\theta
with cell averages

\theta_{K}(t):=\fint_{K}\theta(x)dx,~K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}

and replacing the boundary integral in (
2.1
) by a sum of numerical fluxes across the faces of
K
. This leads to a conservative semi-discrete ODE system for the cell averages.

Spatial mesh and its faces.

Let
h>0
.
Consider an admissible mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of the mesh size
h
for the physical domain
\Omega
, that is, a finite family of open, convex polygonal control volumes
\{K\}_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
forming a conforming partition of
\Omega
. So that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle c_{1}h^{d}\leq|K|~\text{and}~|\partial K|\leq c_{2}h^{d-1}~\text{for each}~K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}

(see
[
25
, Definition 24.1]
for further details on admissible meshes).
Let
|\mathcal{T}_{h}|
be the total number of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We define the sets of faces of a control volume
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{K}:=\big\{\Gamma\subset\partial K~:~\Gamma\text{ is a face of }K\big\}~~\text{and}~~\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\cup_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathcal{F}_{K}.

In what follows, we will repeatedly use these definitions for the discrete velocity and the corresponding numerical flux.

Discrete unknowns.
According to (
2.1
), a simple way to approximate the solution of (
1.1
) is to discretize it as a piecewise constant function over the elements of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. In line with this approach, we introduce the following state spaces for solutions of (
1.1
) and for their discrete approximations:

(2.3)

\displaystyle X:=L^{2}(\Omega)~\text{and}~X_{h}:=\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|},

where
X_{h}
is equipped with the inner product

(2.4)

\displaystyle\big\langle(a_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},(b_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}a_{K}b_{K}|K|.

The space
X_{h}
is regarded as a discretization of
X
in the sense that each function in
X
can be approximated by a vector in
X_{h}
.

To approximate the solution
\theta(t)
of (
1.1
), we represent it by an element of
X_{h}
, namely

\displaystyle\theta_{h}(t):=(\theta_{K}(t))_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},

which will be computed via a discretized transport equation. In particular, the discretization
(\theta_{K}^{0})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h}
of the initial data
\theta^{0}
is given by

(2.5)

\displaystyle\theta_{K}^{0}:=\fint_{K}\theta^{0}(x)dx,~K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

Numerical flux.

We introduce the discretization of the flux (i.e., the boundary integral in (
2.1
)). By the no-penetration condition
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, the boundary integral in (
2.1
) for a control volume
K
is the sum of the fluxes across its interior faces, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K})\theta(t)d\sigma=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\int_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K})\theta(t)d\sigma.

At the same time,
\theta(t)
is approximated by a vector
\theta_{h}(t)
whose coordinates are correspondingly assigned to control volumes. Thus, we discretize the above boundary integral in the following way:

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}(\mathbf{v}(t)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K})\theta(t)d\sigma=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}F_{K,\Gamma}(\mathbf{v}(t),\theta_{h}(t)),

where for each
\mathbf{u}\in U
and
\mathbf{y}_{h}:=(y_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h}
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle F_{K,\Gamma}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{h}):=\frac{y_{K}+y_{L(K,\Gamma)}}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma,

and
L(K,\Gamma)
is the unique control volume in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
sharing the same face
\Gamma
with
K
. Here, we used the central flux for the approximation of the boundary integral.
Furthermore, we note that when two control volumes
K
and
L
share the same face
\Gamma
, the numerical fluxes from one to the other cancel each other, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{K,\Gamma}+F_{L,\Gamma}=0.

This property is crucial for ensuring mass conservation of the numerical scheme.

Semi-discrete state equation.

For each
\mathbf{u}\in U
, we introduce the following numerical
form of
\text{div}\,(\mathbf{u}y)
as an operator from
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|}
to
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|}
: for each
\mathbf{y}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}_{h}|}
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{y}_{h}):=\bigg(\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}F_{K,\Gamma}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{h})\bigg)_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}},

where
F_{K,\Gamma}
is given by (
2.6
). Note that the velocity fields are taken from
L^{2}((0,T);U)
with
U
given by (
1.5
). The state equation (
1.1
) is discretized as the following semi-discrete ODE with the discrete unknown
\theta_{h}
:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\theta_{h}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}(t)D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\theta_{h}(t)=0,~t\in(0,T);~~\theta_{h}(0)=(\theta^{0}_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h},

where the velocity coefficients
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
and the discrete initial data
(\theta^{0}_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
are given by (
2.5
).

Remark 2.1

(Exact flux conditions for structure preservation)
.

In the statements below, “exact conservation” means exact at the algebraic (machine-precision) level and holds under the following discrete conditions:
(i)
single-valued interior fluxes:
for every interior face
\Gamma=\partial K\cap\partial L
,

F_{K,\Gamma}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{h})=-F_{L,\Gamma}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{h})
;
(ii)
discrete incompressibility:

D_{\mathbf{u}}1=0
(equivalently, zero net flux in each cell);
(iii)
exact face flux evaluation:
the face integrals used to build
F_{K,\Gamma}
are evaluated exactly (or enforced so that (i)-(ii) hold exactly).
If face integrals are approximated by quadrature or
\mathbf{u}
is only approximately divergence-free, then (i)-(ii) hold up to a residual and the invariants below are preserved up to the corresponding quadrature/discrete-divergence error.

(2) Crank–Nicolson time discretization.

Let
N_{t}
be a positive integer. Consider the following time partition

\displaystyle t_{n}:=n\Delta t~(n=0,1,\ldots,N_{t})\text{ with }\Delta t:=T/N_{t}.

Applying the Crank–Nicolson (trapezoidal) rule to the semi-discrete equation (
2.8
), we obtain the following fully discrete scheme for the state equation (
1.1
) with unknowns
\{\theta^{(n)}_{h}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{~}l}\dfrac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}-\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{\Delta t}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(n)}D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\dfrac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2}=&0,~n=0,\ldots,N_{t}-1,\\
\theta^{(0)}_{h}=&(\theta^{0}_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h},\end{array}\right.

where the discrete initial data
(\theta^{0}_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
is given by (
2.5
), and the discrete velocity coefficients
\{(v_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Under standard mesh-regularity and consistency assumptions, this cell-centered finite-volume discretization combined with a Crank-Nicolson time integrator yields a convergent approximation of equation (
1.1
) (see Appendix
7.8
). Moreover, for sufficiently regular solutions, the underlying cell-centered finite-volume discretization is first-order accurate in the mesh size
h
(see
[
25
, Remark 6.2]
), while the Crank–Nicolson integrator is second-order accurate in the time step
\Delta t
(see
[
47
, Eq. (2.85)]
).

It is worth noting that the Crank–Nicolson finite-volume scheme (
2.1
) applied to the state equation (
1.1
) is
symmetric
(time-reversible): (
2.1
) remains unchanged if we interchange
\theta_{h}^{(n+1)}
and
\theta_{h}^{(n)}
and replace
\Delta t
by
-\Delta t
.
This observation motivates our choice to discretize the adjoint equation (
1.14
) in the same manner, as introduced in Subsection
2.3
below.

Remark 2.2

(Why not an upwind form)
.

Since our goal is a
structure-preserving
discretization for a PDE-constrained mixing-control problem, we deliberately avoid upwind fluxes and instead employ a centered finite-volume flux coupled with Crank–Nicolson time stepping. The main reasons are as follows.

(i)
L^{2}
conservation.
With a centered FVM discretization combined with the Crank–Nicolson scheme, our method preserves both the discrete energy and the state–adjoint pairing at each time step. In contrast, upwind fluxes introduce numerical diffusion, destroy skew-adjointness, and lead to
L^{2}
-energy decay; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Sec. 4.4.2.2]
.

(ii)
Scheme complexity.
For an upwind spatial discretization, the algebraic discrete adjoint requires a different non-symmetric flux and special boundary treatment, so the forward and adjoint solvers cannot share a single simple scheme. In our approach, we use the same centered Crank–Nicolson discretization for both state and adjoint equations, retaining the conservative structure needed for accurate gradient computation while keeping the overall algorithmic design considerably simpler.

Remark 2.3

(Spurious oscillations)
.

Centered advection schemes are non-dissipative and can, in principle, generate Gibbs-like oscillations near sharp gradients. In our simulations, however, we did not observe problematic oscillations. The initial scalar profiles we consider in the numerical experiments are resolved on a fine grid, which minimizes any overshoot. Moreover, the “mix-norm” is relatively insensitive to small-scale wiggles in
\theta
, since it emphasizes large-scale concentration differences. Thus, any minor oscillations at the grid scale do not noticeably pollute the mix-norm or its decay rate in our results.

2.2.
Computation of the Lagrange multiplier

In this subsection, we aim to solve (
1.12
) numerically by the FVM discretizations introduced in Section
2.1
and adapted from
[
25
, Section 10]
. For this purpose, we integrate (
1.12
) over each control volume to get the following integral form

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial K}\nabla\eta\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma=\int_{K}\theta(T;\mathbf{v})dx,~\forall\,K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

Write
X_{h}^{0}
for the following subspace of
X_{h}
:

(2.11)

\displaystyle X_{h}^{0}:=\Big\{(f_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h}~:~\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}f_{K}|K|=0\Big\}.

The aforementioned integral form is then discretized as the following linear equations with unknowns
\eta_{h}=(\eta_{h,K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
in
X_{h}^{0}
:

(2.12)

\displaystyle L_{h}\eta_{h}:=\left(-\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\frac{|\Gamma|}{d_{K,L(K,\Gamma)}}\Big(\eta_{h,L(K,\Gamma)}-\eta_{h,K}\Big)\right)_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}\text{ in }X_{h}^{0},

where
d_{K,L}
denotes the distance between the centers of
K
and
L
, and
\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}
is the solution to equation (
2.1
) at time step
N_{t}
. The operator
L_{h}
is invertible and self-adjoint over
X_{h}^{0}
(see Lemma
7.2
in Appendix
7.6
).
By solving the linear equations (
2.12
) by a numerical solver (e.g. conjugate-gradient (CG) method),
\eta_{h}
is obtained.

2.3.
Backward discretization of the adjoint equation.

The adjoint equation (
1.14
) has the same form as the state equation
(
1.1
), except that the initial data is imposed at time
t=T
. Meanwhile, the Crank–Nicolson rule used for the state equation (
1.1
) is symmetric, as mentioned above.
Thus, we can apply the same numerical scheme to the adjoint equation (
1.14
). More precisely, by exchanging
\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}
and
\theta^{(n)}_{h}
and replacing
\Delta t
by
-\Delta t
in (
2.1
), we obtain the following fully discrete equation for the adjoint equation (
1.14
) with unknowns
\{\rho^{(n)}_{h}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{~}l}\dfrac{\rho^{(n)}_{h}-\rho^{(n+1)}_{h}}{-\Delta t}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(n)}D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\dfrac{\rho^{(n)}_{h}+\rho^{(n+1)}_{h}}{2}=&0,~n=N_{t}-1,\ldots,1,0,\\
\vskip 5.0pt\rho^{(N_{t})}_{h}=&\eta_{h}\in X_{h},\end{array}\right.

where
\eta_{h}
is obtained from (
2.12
).
This equation is essentially the same as (
2.1
), except for the different notations for unknowns and the initial data.
Starting from the terminal condition
\rho^{(N_{t})}
, we solve (
2.3
) step-by-step for
n=N_{t}-1,\dots,1,0
.

Remark 2.4

(Algebraic adjoint interpretation)
.

Let the forward Crank–Nicolson update be written as
\theta_{h}^{(n+1)}=A_{n}\theta_{h}^{(n)}
, where

A_{n}=(I_{h}+D^{(n)})^{-1}(I_{h}-D^{(n)}),\qquad D^{(n)}:=D_{0.5\,\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(n)}\mathbf{b}_{i}}.

Under the antisymmetry of
D^{(n)}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X_{h}}
(Lemma
3.2
),

A_{n}
is an isometry on
X_{h}
, hence
A_{n}^{\ast}=A_{n}^{-1}
.
Therefore, applying the same CN scheme backward in time coincides with using the algebraic adjoint (transpose) of the forward timestep map.

2.4.
Numerical scheme for problem (OP)

Recall that each admissible velocity field
\mathbf{v}
is of the finite-dimensional form (
1.6
). It is therefore equivalent, and convenient, to formulate problem (OP) directly in terms of the coefficient functions relative to the basis
\{\mathbf{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. To this end, we introduce the coefficient-space functional

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}\big((v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\big)\;:=\;J\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}(t)\,\mathbf{b}_{i}\right),\qquad(v_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in L^{2}\big((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m}\big),

and compute the gradient of
\widetilde{J}
with respect to the velocity coefficients. It follows
from (
1.13
) that for each
\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)
,

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{J}((v_{i})_{i=1}^{m})=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\gamma\mathbf{v}+\theta\nabla\rho,\mathbf{b}_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\vdots\\
\langle\gamma\mathbf{v}+\theta\nabla\rho,\mathbf{b}_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma v_{1}+\langle\theta,\text{div}(\mathbf{b}_{1}\rho)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma v_{m}+\langle\theta,\text{div}(\mathbf{b}_{m}\rho)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{pmatrix}.

This allows us to introduce the discrete version of the gradient as follows: for discrete velocity coefficients
\{(v_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

(2.15)

\displaystyle(\nabla\widetilde{J})_{h}\Big(\{(v_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\Big)=

\displaystyle\left\{\Delta t\begin{pmatrix}\gamma v_{1}^{(n)}+\big\langle\frac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2},D_{\mathbf{b}_{1}}\rho_{h}^{(n)}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma v_{m}^{(n)}+\big\langle\frac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2},D_{\mathbf{b}_{m}}\rho_{h}^{(n)}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}\end{pmatrix}\right\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1},

where
D
is given by (
2.7
).

It is worth noting that
\theta
and
\rho
are discretized in (
2.15
) using slightly different time conventions: the state
\theta
enters through the midpoint average
(\theta_{h}^{(n+1)}+\theta_{h}^{(n)})/2
, which is naturally associated with the time interval
(t_{n},t_{n+1})
, whereas the adjoint
\rho
appears with its nodal value
\rho_{h}^{(n)}
at time
t_{n}
. This choice ensures that the numerical gradient defined above coincides with the gradient of the discrete functional
\widetilde{J}_{h}
introduced in (
2.16
), as stated in Theorem
4.1
.

In parallel with the discrete state and adjoint equations, we now introduce the discrete cost functional that defines the fully discrete optimization problem

(2.16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h}):=\frac{1}{2}\langle\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\eta_{h}\rangle_{X_{h}}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|v_{i}^{(n)}|^{2},~\mathbf{v}_{h}:=\{(v_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},

where
\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}
and
\eta_{h}
are computed from equations (
2.1
) and (
2.12
), respectively.

The convergence of the discrete cost functional
\widetilde{J}_{h}
toward
J
is proved in Appendix
7.9
.
In the fully discrete setting, the admissible set is finite-dimensional and closed, so the existence of a global minimizer follows from standard compactness/continuity arguments.
This provides a rigorous justification for minimizing
\widetilde{J}_{h}
as a reliable route to approximating optimal continuous mixers.

Next, to compute the optimal discrete velocity coefficients

\{(v_{i}^{(n),*})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}
for problem (OP), we rely on the following components:
(i) a conjugate gradient (CG) method,
(ii) a line-search strategy detailed in our recent work
[
63
, Algorithm 3.9]
, and
(iii) the discrete gradient
(\nabla\widetilde{J})_{h}
given by (
2.15
), which is evaluated from the discrete state and adjoint systems (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), together with the discrete approximation of
\eta
in (
2.12
).

Once the optimal coefficients are obtained, the corresponding optimal velocity field
\mathbf{v}^{*}
for (OP) is reconstructed via

\displaystyle v^{*}(t)\approx\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\chi_{[nT/N_{t},\,(n+1)T/N_{t})}(t)~v_{i}^{(n),*}\mathbf{b}_{i},~t\in(0,T).

With the above-mentioned elements, the complete framework of the numerical scheme for problem (OP) is summarized in Algorithm
2.1
, which preserves good structures, introduced in Section
3
.

3.
Structure-preserving properties of the numerical scheme

This section is devoted to the structural properties preserved by the numerical scheme introduced in Section
2
.

We first define the discrete mass
M_{h}
and discrete
L^{2}
-energy
E_{h}
as follows:
Given

\xi_{h}=(\xi_{h,K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h}
, we set

(3.1)

\displaystyle M_{h}(\xi_{h}):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\xi_{h,K}\,|K|,\qquad E_{h}(\xi_{h}):=\|\xi_{h}\|_{X_{h}}^{2}.

The following holds.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that the fluxes satisfy the exact conditions in Remark
2.1
.
Let
\theta^{(n)}_{h}
and
\rho^{(n)}_{h}
be the solutions at time level
t_{n}
(
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N_{t}\}
) to equations (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) with arbitrary initial data
\theta_{h}^{(0)},\rho_{h}^{(N_{t})}\in X_{h}
.

Then, for each time step

, the following properties hold:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\text{(Mass conservation)}\quad M_{h}(\theta_{h}^{(n)})=M_{h}(\theta_{h}^{(0)})\text{ and }M_{h}(\rho_{h}^{(n)})=M_{h}(\rho_{h}^{(N_{t})})

(3.3)

\displaystyle\text{(Energy conservation)}\quad E_{h}(\theta_{h}^{(n)})=E_{h}(\theta_{h}^{(0)})\text{ and }E_{h}(\rho_{h}^{(n)})=E_{h}(\rho_{h}^{(N_{t})})

(3.4)

\displaystyle\text{(State--adjoint consistency)}\quad\big\langle\theta^{(n)}_{h},\rho^{(n)}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=\big\langle\theta^{(N_{t})}_{h},\rho^{(N_{t})}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
requires the following lemma, which presents a basic property of the discrete divergence
D_{\mathbf{u}}
in (
2.7
) (see Appendix
7.7
for the proof).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X_{h}
be given by (
2.3
),
\mathbf{u}\in U
and
D_{\mathbf{u}}
be as in (
2.7
).
Assume that the fluxes satisfy the exact conditions in Remark
2.1
.
Then,
D_{\mathbf{u}}
is antisymmetric over
X_{h}
(equipped with the inner product in (
2.4
)).
Furthermore,
I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}}
is invertible over
X_{h}
, where
I_{h}
is the identity operator over
X_{h}
.

We are now in the position to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N_{t}\}
be fixed.
The proof is organized in the following three steps.

Step 1. Proof of (
3.4
)

Let
I_{h}
be the identity operator over
X_{h}
. Write

\displaystyle D^{(k)}:=D_{0.5\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(k)}\mathbf{b}_{i}}=0.5\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(k)}D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}},~k=0,\ldots,N_{t}-1.

From (
2.1
), we know that for each
k\in\{0,\ldots,N_{t}-1\}
,

\displaystyle\big(I_{h}+D^{(k)}\big)\theta_{h}^{(k+1)}=\big(I_{h}-D^{(k)}\big)\theta_{h}^{(k)}.

Since each operator

is invertible and antisymmetric over
X_{h}
(by Lemma

with
\mathbf{u}
replaced by
0.5\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(k)}\mathbf{b}_{i}
), we rewrite the above equality as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\theta_{h}^{(k+1)}=\big(I_{h}+D^{(k)}\big)^{-1}\big(I_{h}-D^{(k)}\big)\theta_{h}^{(k)},~k=0,\ldots,N_{t}-1.

Because each

is antisymmetric (see Lemma

), the above implies that for each

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{h}^{(k+1)},\rho_{h}^{(k+1)}\rangle_{X_{h}}=\Big\langle\theta_{h}^{(k)},\big(I_{h}-D^{(k)}\big)^{-1}\big(I_{h}+D^{(k)}\big)\rho_{h}^{(k+1)}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}.

Meanwhile,
\{\rho^{(k)}_{h}\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
satisfies the same discrete transport equation as
\{\theta^{(k)}_{h}\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
(see (
2.1
) and (
2.3
)). Then, by replacing
\theta
with
\rho
in (
3.5
), we determine from the last equality that

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{h}^{(k+1)},\rho_{h}^{(k+1)}\rangle_{X_{h}}=\Big\langle\theta_{h}^{(k)},\rho_{h}^{(k)}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}},~k=0,\ldots,N_{t}-1.

This gives (
3.4
).

Step 2. Proof of (
3.3
)

By Step 1, we know that (
3.4
) holds for any solutions to equations (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) (with any initial data). Meanwhile, equations (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) are the same except for their initial data. Thus, we can take
\rho_{h}^{(n)}=\theta_{h}^{(k)}
(
k=0,1,\cdots,N_{t}
) in (
3.4
). This, together with (
3.1
), leads to (
3.3
) directly.

Step 3. Proof of (
3.2
)

Note that the constant function
y_{K}^{(k)}\equiv 1
is an exact discrete solution since
D_{\mathbf{u}}1=0
by Remark
2.1
.
Meanwhile, equations (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) are the same except their initial data. Thus, we take
\rho_{h}^{(k)}\equiv 1
(resp.
\theta_{h}^{(k)}\equiv 1
) in (
3.4
) to obtain that when
k=0,1,\cdots,N_{t}
,

\displaystyle\langle\theta_{h}^{(k)},1\rangle_{X_{h}}=\langle\theta_{h}^{(0)},1\rangle_{X_{h}}~(\text{resp. }\langle\rho_{h}^{(k)},1\rangle_{X_{h}}=\langle\rho_{h}^{(N_{t})},1\rangle_{X_{h}}).

The last equalities, along with (
2.4
), yield (
3.2
).

In summary, the proof of Theorem
3.1
is completed.
∎

4.
Commutativity of Optimization and Discretization

The numerical scheme in Section
2.4
is designed by the Optimize-Then-Discretize approach. This means that the discrete gradient (
2.15
) of the function
J
for problem (OP) is obtained from the original gradient (
1.13
) by discretizing a coupled system of the state and adjoint equations. The following Theorem
4.1
states that this discrete gradient can also be established only from the discretizations of the functional
J
and the state equation. In other words, this theorem presents the equivalence between the Optimize-Then-Discretize and Discretize-Then-Optimize approaches for the problem (OP).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(\nabla\widetilde{J})_{h}
and
\widetilde{J}_{h}
be given by (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), respectively. Then, it holds that
(\nabla\widetilde{J})_{h}\equiv\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}
over
\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}
.

Proof.

We will compute
\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}
at any fixed velocity coefficients
v_{h}=\{(v_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}
. For this purpose, we take a variational direction
u_{h}=\{(u_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}
,
and define new velocity coefficients in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}
as follows:

(4.1)

\displaystyle v_{h}^{\varepsilon}:=v_{h}+\varepsilon u_{h},~\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}.

Write
\{\delta\theta_{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
for the solution to the following variational equation:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{~}l}\dfrac{\delta\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}-\delta\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{\Delta t}+&\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(n)}D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\dfrac{\delta\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\delta\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2}\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{(n)}D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\dfrac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2},~n=0,\ldots,N_{t}-1,\\
\delta\theta^{(0)}_{h}&=0\text{ in }X_{h},\end{array}\right.

Write
\eta_{h}
(resp.
\delta\eta_{h}
) for the solutions to equation (
2.12
) with
\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}
(resp.
\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}
).
Then, by the definition of
\widetilde{J}_{h}
in (
2.16
), we obtain

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\widetilde{J}_{h}(v_{h}^{\varepsilon})-\widetilde{J}_{h}(v_{h})\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\delta\eta_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\eta_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}+\gamma\Delta t\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}}.

Meanwhile, we determine from (
2.12
) and Lemma
7.2
in Appendix
7.6
that

\displaystyle\big\langle\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\delta\eta_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=

\displaystyle\big\langle\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},L_{h}^{-1}\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=\big\langle(L_{h}^{-1})^{*}\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle L_{h}^{-1}\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=\big\langle\eta_{h},\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}.

This, along with (
4.5
) and (
4.1
), yields

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(v_{h})u_{h}=\big\langle\delta\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\eta_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}+\gamma\Delta t\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}}.

Combined with equations (
4
) and (
2.3
) (as well as Lemma
3.2
with
\mathbf{u}
replaced by
\mathbf{b}_{i}
), the last equality yields

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(v_{h})u_{h}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\Big\langle-\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{(n)}D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\frac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2},\rho_{h}^{(n)}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}+\gamma\Delta t\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{(n)}\Big\langle\frac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2},D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\rho_{h}^{(n)}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}+\gamma\Delta t\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}}.

Since
u_{h}=\{(u_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}
was arbitrarily taken from
\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}
, the above implies

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(v_{h})=\bigg\{\Big(\Delta t\Big\langle\frac{\theta^{(n+1)}_{h}+\theta^{(n)}_{h}}{2},D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\rho_{h}^{(n)}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}\Big)_{i=1}^{m}\bigg\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}+\gamma\Delta tv_{h}.

From this and (
2.15
), we conclude that

\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}=(\nabla\widetilde{J})_{h}
over
\mathbb{R}^{m\times N_{t}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

5.2.
Doswell frontogenesis basis

We next consider a more complex flow configuration to demonstrate the flexibility of our framework and to verify that the structure-preserving properties hold on a non-Cartesian mesh. In this section, the domain

Since the finite-volume formulation integrates fluxes across cell interfaces, it naturally extends to the curvilinear control volumes defined by the polar grid. By treating the polar mesh as a logical graph of adjacent control volumes, the matrix structure of the discrete operators remains identical to the Cartesian case.

6.
Conclusions and outlook

We proposed a structure-preserving numerical scheme for optimal control of mixing in incompressible transport. The central innovation is the integration of a conservative-centered finite-volume discretization and time-symmetric Crank–Nicolson stepping within an adjoint-based optimization loop in a way that preserves, at the fully discrete level, (i) mass and
L^{2}
invariants and (ii) exact state–adjoint consistency. This yields a reduced-gradient method whose discrete gradient is consistent with the discrete objective (optimize–then–discretize and discretize–then–optimize agree), which is a key requirement for reliable PDE-constrained optimization.

Numerically, the proposed scheme preserves the invariants to machine precision and yields a stable, reliable adjoint-based optimization method. Across the tested geometries and initializations, the resulting optimal controls deliver a significantly faster decay of the mix-norm than steady single-mode mixers, demonstrating both substantial mixing enhancement and robust optimization performance.

From a practical mixing viewpoint, the experiments point to a clear takeaway: with a small, actuator-limited set of stirring modes, any steady actuation remains a weak mixer (at best, polynomial decay), while an optimized time-dependent scheduling of the same modes can produce mix-norm decay consistent with near-exponential rates over the tested horizons.

Several extensions are natural and relevant for future work. First, higher-order spatial discretizations that retain skew-adjointness and time symmetry would improve performance in strongly filamented regimes without compromising gradient fidelity. Second, incorporating realistic actuation constraints (amplitude bounds, enstrophy/energy budgets, switching costs) would quantify performance–cost trade-offs and improve physical relevance. Third, extending the framework to advection–diffusion and Navier–Stokes settings, including three-dimensional configurations, would test the robustness of the observed control mechanisms and enable large-scale studies aligned with practical mixing applications.

\displaystyle\langle\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T),z\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T),\rho(T;z)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(0),\rho(0;z)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle-\text{div}(\mathbf{w}\theta_{\varepsilon}),\rho(t;z)\big\rangle_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime},H^{1}(\Omega)}dt=\int_{0}^{T}\theta_{\varepsilon}\nabla\rho(t;z)\cdot\mathbf{w}(t)dt.

Here, we used the no-penetration boundary condition of
\mathbf{w}
(as well as a density argument on
\theta_{\varepsilon}
) in the last step. Then, we obtain from (
7.13
) and (
7.15
) that the sequence
\{\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T)\}_{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}}
is bounded in
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
, and that

(7.16)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\langle\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T),\eta\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{0}^{T}\theta\nabla\rho(t;\eta)\cdot\mathbf{w}(t)dt.

Furthermore, since each function
\delta\eta_{\varepsilon}=\eta(\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T))
satisfies (
1.12
) (as well as (
7.7
)), the boundedness of the sequence
\{\delta\eta_{\varepsilon}(T)\}_{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}}
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
follows from that of
\{\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T)\}_{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}}
in
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
.

Finally, since
\mathbf{w}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)
was arbitrarily taken, we deduce from (
7.5
) and (
7.16
) (as well as the boundedness of
\{\delta\theta_{\varepsilon}(T)\}_{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}}
and
\{\delta\eta_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}}
) that
J^{\prime}(\mathbf{v})
exists in the Gâteaux sense. Furthermore, it holds that for each
\mathbf{w}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)
,

\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{w}\rangle_{L^{2}((0,T);U)}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle\theta\nabla\rho(t;\eta),\mathbf{w}(t)\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}dt+\gamma\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle_{L^{2}((0,T);U)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle\gamma\mathbf{v}(t)+\theta\nabla\rho(t;\eta),\mathbf{w}(t)\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}dt.

This leads to (
1.13
) and completes the proof.

Properties of the discrete Laplacian
L_{h}

Lemma 7.2
.

The operator
L_{h}
in (
2.12
) is self-adjoint and invertible over the finite dimension space
X_{h}^{0}
(given by (
2.11
)).

Proof.

We just give the sketch of the proof. By the definition of
L_{h}
in (
2.12
), one can directly check that it maps
X_{h}^{0}
to
X_{h}^{0}
and is self-adjoint. The invertibility of
L_{h}
can be proved by similar arguments as the proof of
[
25
, Lemma 10.1]
(there another kind of meshes is applied), which is equivalent to the injectivity of
L_{h}
. Indeed, we let
L_{h}\eta_{h}=0
for some
\eta_{h}\in X_{h}^{0}

2.12

\displaystyle 0=\langle L_{h}\eta_{h},\eta_{h}\rangle_{X_{h}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\frac{|\Gamma|}{d_{K,L(K,\Gamma)}}(\eta_{h,L(K,\Gamma)}-\eta_{h,K})^{2},

which implies
\eta_{h}\in X_{h}^{0}
is constant and so is zero due to its zero-mean. Therefore,
L_{h}
is invertible over

. This completes the proof.
∎

7.7.
Proof of Lemma
3.2

Proof.

First, we aim to show that
D_{\mathbf{u}}
is antisymmetric over
X_{h}
. For this purpose, take two arbitrary vectors
\mathbf{y}_{h}:=(y_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
and
\mathbf{z}_{h}:=(z_{K})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}
from
X_{h}
. By the definition of
D_{\mathbf{u}}
in (
2.7
) as well as (
2.4
) and (
2.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{z}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}F_{K,\Gamma}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}_{h})\right)z_{K}

(7.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\big(z_{K}y_{K}+z_{K}y_{L(K,\Gamma)}\big)\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}z_{K}y_{K}\left(\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}z_{K}y_{L(K,\Gamma)}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L(K,\Gamma)}d\sigma.

Meanwhile, since
\mathbf{u}\in U
, we see from (
1.5
) that
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
and div
\,\mathbf{u}=0
in
\Omega
, which yields that for each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,

(7.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K}}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma=\int_{\partial K}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}d\sigma=\int_{K}\text{div}\,\mathbf{u}\,dx=0.

This, together with (
7.7
), implies

(7.19)

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{z}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}z_{K}y_{L(K,\Gamma)}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L(K,\Gamma)}d\sigma.

At the same time, for each control volume
L\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and each face
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{L}
with
\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega
, there is a unique
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
so that

\displaystyle\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K}~\text{and}~L=L(K,\Gamma)~(\text{i.e., }K=L(L,\Gamma)),

which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{K},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}z_{K}y_{L(K,\Gamma)}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L(K,\Gamma)}d\sigma=\sum_{L\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{L},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}z_{L(L,\Gamma)}y_{L}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L}d\sigma.

This, together with (
7.19
) and (
7.18
) (with
K
replaced by
L
), implies

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{z}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{L\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{L},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}z_{L(L,\Gamma)}y_{L}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L}d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{L\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{L},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\big(z_{L}y_{L}+z_{L(L,\Gamma)}y_{L}\big)\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L}d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{L\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}y_{L}\left(\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{L},\,\Gamma\not\subset\partial\Omega}\frac{z_{L}+z_{L(L,\Gamma)}}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{L}d\sigma\right).

Then, by the definition of
D_{\mathbf{u}}
in (
2.7
) as well as (
2.6
) and (
2.4
), we determine

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{z}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=-\sum_{L\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}y_{L}F_{L,\Gamma}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z}_{h})=-\big\langle\mathbf{y}_{h},D_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{z}_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}.

Since
\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{z}_{h}
are arbitrary in
X_{h}
, the above leads to the desired conclusion.

Next, we verify the invertibility of the linear operator
I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}}
on
X_{h}
. Since
X_{h}
is finite-dimensional, it suffices to show that
0
is not an eigenvalue of
I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}}
.

Arguing by contradiction, assume that
0
is an eigenvalue. Then there exists an eigenvector
\mathbf{y}_{h}\in X_{h}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}})\mathbf{y}_{h}=0\qquad\text{in }X_{h}.

Using that
D_{\mathbf{u}}
is antisymmetric, we obtain

\langle\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h}\rangle_{X_{h}}=\langle\mathbf{y}_{h},\mathbf{y}_{h}\rangle_{X_{h}}+\langle\mathbf{y}_{h},D_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{y}_{h}\rangle_{X_{h}}\\
=\langle\mathbf{y}_{h},(I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}})\mathbf{y}_{h}\rangle_{X_{h}}=0,

which implies
\mathbf{y}_{h}=0
in
X_{h}
, a contradiction. Hence,
0
is not an eigenvalue of
I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}}
, and therefore
I_{h}+D_{\mathbf{u}}
is invertible on
X_{h}
. This completes the proof.
∎

7.8.
Convergence of the numerical scheme

This subsection presents the convergence of the Crank-Nicolson cell-centered finite-volume scheme (
2.1
) to the original equation (
1.1
).

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
T>0
and
\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}((0,T);U)
.
Let
h>0
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be an admissible mesh as in Section
2.1
. Let
\Delta t:=T/N_{t}
with
N_{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and let
\{(\theta_{K}^{(n)})_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}\subset X_{h}
be the solution of equation (
2.1
) where

(7.20)

\displaystyle v_{i}^{(n)}:=\fint_{n\Delta t}^{(n+1)\Delta t}\langle\mathbf{v}(s),\mathbf{b}_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}ds,~i=1,\ldots,m,~n=0,\ldots,N_{t}-1.

Then, when
h\to 0^{+}
and
\Delta t\to 0^{+}
, the following piecewise-constant time–space reconstruction

(7.21)

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}(t,x):=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\chi_{[n\Delta t,\,(n+1)\Delta t)}(t)\,\chi_{K}(x)\,\theta^{(n)}_{K},~t\in[0,T],~x\in\Omega

converges to
\theta(T;\mathbf{v})
strongly in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
Furthermore, it holds that

(7.22)

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}(T,\cdot)\to\theta(T;\mathbf{v})\text{ strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega)\text{ as }h,\Delta t\to 0,

Proof.

The strong convergence of
\{\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}\}
in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
will be done by the weak convergence, plus the convergence of the norms, of this sequence.
First, we know from (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle\|\theta_{h}^{(n)}\|_{X_{h}}=\|\theta_{h}^{(0)}\|_{X_{h}},~n=0,\dots,N_{t}.

By this and (
7.21
), the sequence
\{\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}\}
satisfies that for each
t\in[0,T]
,

(7.23)

\displaystyle\|\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}(t)\|_{X_{h}}=\|\theta_{h}^{(0)}\|_{X_{h}}\rightarrow\|\theta^{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\text{ as }h\rightarrow 0^{+}.

In particular, it is bounded in the space
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

Next, we claim that

(7.24)

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}\rightharpoonup\theta(T;\mathbf{v})\text{ weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)\text{ as }h,\Delta t\rightarrow 0^{+}.

To this end, arbitrarily take a subsequence with a weak limit in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, denoted by
\theta^{*}
. Arbitrarily take a function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\Omega)
and define

(7.25)

\displaystyle\varphi_{h}^{(n)}:=\bigg(\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\varphi(n\Delta t,x)dx\bigg)_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in X_{h},~n=0,\dots,N_{t}.

From the anti-symmetry of
D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}
(see Lemma
3.2
), we rewrite equation (
2.1
) in the following discrete weak form:

\displaystyle-\big\langle\theta_{h}^{(0)},\varphi_{h}^{(0)}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}-1}\Delta t\Big\langle\theta_{h}^{(n)},\frac{\varphi_{h}^{(n)}-\varphi_{h}^{(n-1)}}{\Delta t}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}

(7.26)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(n)}\Big\langle\frac{\theta_{h}^{(n)}+\theta_{h}^{(n+1)}}{2},D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\varphi_{h}^{(n)}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}.

At the same time, by computations as well as (
2.2
), there is a constant
C>0
(independent of
h
) so that

(7.27)

\displaystyle\sup_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Big|D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}f_{h}-\fint_{K}\text{div}(\mathbf{b}_{i}f)dx\Big|\leq Ch\|f\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})},~\forall\,f\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}),

where
f_{h}
is defined by the way as in (
7.25
) with
\varphi
replaced by
f
.
This, together with (
7.8
), (
7.21
) and (
1.6
), yields

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\theta^{0}(x)\varphi(0,x)dx=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta^{*}\big(\partial_{t}\varphi+\text{div}(\mathbf{v}\varphi)\big)dxdt.

Since
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\Omega)
was arbitrarily taken, the above implies that
\theta^{*}
is the weak solution to equation (
1.1
), which is
\theta(\cdot;\mathbf{v})
. Note that
\theta^{*}
is any accumulation point of the bounded sequence
\{\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}\}
in the weak topology of
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
Therefore, (
7.24
) is true.

Now, because the space
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
is uniformly convex, by
[
10
, Proposition 3.32]
, the desired strong convergence of
\{\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}\}
in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
follows from (
7.24
) and
(
7.23
).

Finally, we show (
7.22
) by a similar idea. For this purpose, let
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and define
\psi_{h}
in a similar way as in (
7.25
) with
\varphi
replaced by
\psi
.
From the anti-symmetry of
D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}
(see Lemma
3.2
), we rewrite equation (
2.1
) in another discrete weak form:

\displaystyle\big\langle\theta_{h}^{(N_{t})},\psi_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}-\big\langle\theta_{h}^{(0)},\psi_{h}\big\rangle_{X_{h}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N_{t}-1}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{(n)}\Big\langle\frac{\theta_{h}^{(n)}+\theta_{h}^{(n+1)}}{2},D_{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\psi_{h}\Big\rangle_{X_{h}}.

Because of the strong convergence of
\{\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}\}
in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, the above, together with (
7.21
), (
7.27
) and (
1.6
), yields that any weak accumulation point
\theta^{*}_{T}
of the sequence
\{\bar{\theta}_{h,\Delta t}(T,\cdot)\}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta^{*}_{T}\psi dx-\int_{\Omega}\theta^{0}\psi dx=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta(t;\mathbf{v})\text{div}(\mathbf{v}\psi)dxdt.

Since
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
was arbitrarily chosen, the last equality implies that
\theta^{*}_{T}
must be equal to
\theta(T;\mathbf{v})
. This, along with (
7.23
) (with
t=T
) and
[
10
, Proposition 3.32]
, yields (
7.22
).
This completes the proof.

∎
