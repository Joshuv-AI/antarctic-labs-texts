Anisotropy-driven reshaping and diagnostic limits of high-charge perfect optical vortices
Authors: Yukti Pandey, Raj Kumar, Nandan S Bisht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerfect optical vortices (POVs) are annular beams designed such that their radii are, in principle, independent of topological charge, making them attractive for structured illumination and optical manipulation. We present a systematic experimental and numerical study of POVs generated using a phase-only spatial light modulator and investigate how controlled angular anisotropy reshapes their structure across a wide range of charges. Three distinct regimes are identified. In the absence of anisotropy, charge-invariant annuli persist only for moderate charges, while higher charges exhibit multi-ring, Bessel-like structure accompanied by a gradual increase in effective radius. At moderate anisotropy, these multi-ring profiles consolidate into single opened annuli with tunable azimuthal asymmetry. In this regime, speckle cross-correlation remains structured even for widely separated charges that exhibit weak correlations under isotropic encoding. At extreme anisotropy, annular intensity profiles converge across all charges, forming pseudo-POVs that remain annular in intensity but exhibit pronounced redistribution of OAM-dependent signatures and a collapse of charge-dependent cross-correlation signatures within the sensitivity of intensity-only measurements. Non-interferometric speckle analysis supports these findings. Autocorrelation rules out trivial ellipticity as the origin of radius convergence, while cross-correlation reveals sensitivity to charge ordering at low anisotropy and an effective loss of charge distinguishability within intensity-only diagnostics at extreme anisotropy. Together, these results clarify the robustness limits of POVs under angular deformation and establish angular anisotropy as a practical control parameter for engineering and diagnosing annular vortex beams.Subject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Structured light fields carrying orbital angular momentum (OAM) play a central role in modern optics, underpinning applications in optical manipulation, microscopy, metrology, and communications1–5. Since the formal identification of OAM with helical wavefronts by Allen et al.6,7, building on earlier descriptions of phase singularities by Nye and Berry8, a wide variety of structured beams have been developed and experimentally realized using devices such as spiral phase plates and diffractive holograms9,10, enabling the exploitation of the coupling between spatial phase structure and physical functionality.

Beyond specific beam families, a broader and more fundamental issue in structured light optics is the robustness and fidelity of structured wavefields under realistic perturbations2,10. In many settings, visual appearance alone does not reliably reflect the underlying phase or modal content of a field7. Aberrations and asymmetries can preserve certain intensity features while redistributing or obscuring the information encoded in phase and modal structure11,12. Understanding when such decoupling occurs is therefore essential for assessing the validity of idealized beam concepts and for enabling reliable mode recognition in practical systems13. This motivates the use of controlled perturbations that deliberately break rotational symmetry in order to probe when the visual intensity morphology of a vortex beam ceases to reliably reflect its underlying OAM content.

Perfect optical vortices (POVs) provide a particularly clean platform to examine these questions. In contrast to conventional Laguerre–Gaussian modes, POVs are designed such that their annular intensity radius is, in principle, independent of topological charge, enabling access to high-order vortex states without a concomitant increase in beam size14,15. POVs have been generated using axicons, diffractive optical elements, and phase-only spatial light modulator (SLM) holography14,16,17, and their charge-invariant geometry has been exploited in optical trapping, sorting, and structured illumination18–20. However, as experiments move toward higher charges and tighter control, practical departures from the ideal POV picture become increasingly evident15.

Related structured vortex beams incorporating power-exponent-phase profiles, multiramp fractional phases, and Airy-derivative structures have been studied extensively in connection with beam generation, propagation, topological-structure evolution, and optical manipulation21–25. In addition, Anisotropic vortex structures have also been reported in superpositions of Bessel and Bessel–Gaussian beams, where the observed asymmetry arises from the interference of multiple field components26,27. In contrast, the present work does not introduce a new beam family but instead examines how controlled angular anisotropy reshapes POVs and probes the limits of their structural and modal robustness across a wide charge range.

In realistic SLM-based implementations, phase encoding is discrete and pixelated and typically relies on blazed gratings to isolate diffraction orders17,28,29. These constraints become increasingly severe at large charge, where rapid azimuthal phase variation can challenge finite sampling and phase quantization, leading to deviations from ideal POV behavior15. Generalized POV structures, including elliptic vortices spanning different topological charges, have been investigated30,31, while practical realizations at large charge can become increasingly sensitive to system constraints. Recent investigations have further reported interference effects in high-order POVs32, nonlinear self-focusing33, and broader developments in vector and structured vortex beams34, together with recent advances in vortex-beam engineering and integration35, collectively underscoring the continuing development of vortex-beam technologies under realistic conditions.

A relatively unexplored aspect in this context is the use of angular anisotropy as a controlled perturbation for probing the robustness of POVs. In particular, anisotropic phase deformation provides a systematic way to test when the characteristic annular intensity morphology of a POV remains visually intact while the underlying OAM content and vortex phase structure are redistributed or degraded. Many prior studies implicitly assume isotropic phase encoding, despite the fact that real optical systems routinely introduce asymmetries through alignment errors, aberrations, or intentional beam shaping. Here we deliberately depart from the isotropic construction and introduce a controlled angular anisotropy that effectively warps the azimuthal coordinate. This anisotropy acts as a deterministic and continuous control parameter for reshaping vortex fields without changing the wavelength, aperture, or nominal charge. The central question we address is how such angular anisotropy reorganizes the intensity and OAM content of POVs and where ideal POV properties cease to provide a reliable description. Unlike prior studies that treat asymmetry as an imperfection, we use angular anisotropy as a controlled degree of freedom to probe the limits of vortex robustness.

Addressing this question requires diagnostics that remain sensitive to phase structure even when the far-field intensity appears annular. While interferometric and phase-sensitive measurement techniques can directly reveal phase singularities, they typically require stable references and careful alignment13,36. In contrast, diffuser-generated speckle offers a non-interferometric, intensity-only alternative whose statistical correlations encode information about symmetry and spatial scale37,38, as well as modal overlap and OAM-dependent structure39,40. Although speckle correlations have been widely employed for OAM mode sorting and transmission through scattering media39,40, their use as a diagnostic tool to identify pseudo-POVs and anisotropy-driven redistribution of OAM has not been systematically explored.

In this work, we present a combined experimental, numerical, and statistical study of POVs under controlled angular anisotropy. By correlating direct intensity measurements with speckle autocorrelation and cross-correlation analysis, we identify distinct anisotropy regimes in which annular intensity, charge-dependent phase signatures, and mode distinguishability decouple. These results clarify the robustness limits of POVs under angular deformation and demonstrate that angular anisotropy and speckle-based diagnostics provide practical tools for probing and recognizing vortex integrity in realistic optical systems.

The specific form of Eq. (6) is chosen as a minimal single-parameter deformation that enables systematic control of angular anisotropy while preserving a single-valued phase winding for integer ℓ. This formulation provides a controlled means of breaking rotational symmetry without altering the nominal topological charge, thereby allowing us to investigate how the morphology and modal structure of POVs evolve under progressively stronger angular distortions.

Physically, this mapping can be viewed as a simplified phenomenological representation of transverse anisotropic distortions in structured optical fields. Such distortions are related to known effects including elliptic deformation of vortex beams and distortion-induced OAM mode mixing11,12,30,31. While the present formulation does not model these effects in a system-specific manner, it provides a controlled framework for isolating and studying their impact on vortex structure.

At sufficiently large m, the angular coordinate approaches a step-like distribution, so that the field is no longer an eigenfunction of the rotation operator in the original (X, Y) plane. In this case, the vortex field cannot be represented by a single OAM eigenmode. Instead, the field can be decomposed into a superposition of multiple integer OAM modes.

For an ideal vortex beam, the spectrum consists of a single peak at the programmed charge, whereas symmetry breaking leads to a redistribution of power among neighboring modes. This modal redistribution provides a useful indicator of deviations from rotational symmetry in the phase structure.

To further probe the consequences of angular anisotropy beyond direct intensity measurements, we employ a statistical optics approach based on speckle analysis. A diffuser placed at the POV plane imposes a random phase, producing speckle patterns whose ensemble statistics encode information about the incident field44. While direct intensity measurements capture only the amplitude distribution of the field, the speckle pattern arises from interference of the scattered wavefront and is therefore sensitive to both the amplitude and phase structure of the incident field. As a result, speckle-based analysis provides access to structural features of the underlying vortex field that may not be fully captured in the measured intensity distribution alone.

Because the speckle field inherits the spatial-frequency content of the incident optical field, the PSD exhibits an annular structure whose characteristic spatial frequency reflects the dominant scale of the originating POV beam. The loss of distinguishability in the CC response indicates regimes in which the vortex field no longer retains a unique charge-dependent signature, despite preserving an annular intensity distribution.

Simulation

Numerical simulations were performed to isolate the consequences of the imposed anisotropic phase mapping and to distinguish them from experimental artefacts such as alignment errors, optical aberrations, and finite-aperture effects. To separate these contributions clearly, three complementary numerical representations were considered. First, the anisotropic phase remapping itself was examined through wrapped vortex phase masks of the form , shown in Supplementary Fig. S3 for representative topological charges and anisotropy parameters. In the large-m limit, most of the transverse plane approaches nearly uniform phase while the phase variation becomes confined to narrow regions around y=0. This behavior demonstrates that the imposed remapping breaks rotational symmetry and fundamentally alters the angular phase structure of the field.

To understand how this modified phase structure influences the intrinsic field distribution, we evaluated the direct Fourier transform of the anisotropic Bessel–Gaussian complex field prior to holographic order filtering. Representative intensity distributions for ℓ =40 are shown in Fig. 3a, while the complete set of results across all investigated charges and anisotropy parameters is provided in Supplementary Fig. S4. In the isotropic limit (m=1), the transformed field exhibits a well-defined circular annular intensity distribution characteristic of the Fourier transform of a Bessel–Gaussian vortex. As anisotropy is introduced (m=12.5), the circular symmetry of the Fourier-plane intensity distribution is strongly broken. The spatial-frequency components become redistributed along preferred directions, producing elongated, eye-like interference structures. These features arise because the anisotropic phase mapping modifies the local azimuthal phase gradient around the beam, leading to a redistribution of spatial-frequency content and the emergence of complex interference fringes. At strong anisotropy (m=100), these extended interference structures begin to collapse toward the center of the Fourier plane. In addition to this collapse, the radial extent of the interference features is significantly reduced compared to the moderate anisotropy case (m=12.5), indicating a compression of the dominant spatial-frequency components toward lower spatial frequencies. The intensity distribution becomes more symmetric again, but the spatial-frequency energy becomes increasingly concentrated near the origin. In the extreme anisotropy regime (m=10000), this localization becomes even more pronounced, and the field is dominated by a compact central structure with only weak residual ring-like features. These results demonstrate that strong angular anisotropy significantly reorganizes the intrinsic Fourier-plane structure of the field.

The holographic phase pattern was constructed according to Eq. (8), combining the anisotropic vortex phase, the radial phase term that determines the POV ring radius, and a linear blazed grating for diffraction-order separation. The numerical parameters were chosen to match the experimental configuration, with a wavelength of 632.8 nm, an SLM pixel pitch of 3.74~μm, and a computational grid of 4096× 4096 pixels. The incident field at the SLM plane was modeled as a Gaussian beam, and the total phase was quantized to 8-bit resolution to reproduce phase-only modulation. Propagation to the Fourier plane of a lens with focal length 30 cm was modeled using scalar Fourier optics within the paraxial approximation, implemented via a fast Fourier transform (FFT). The first diffraction order was isolated by cropping a fixed spatial window centered at the predicted diffraction position, with the window size chosen to fully capture the order while avoiding overlap with neighboring orders. In the isotropic case (m=1), the beam forms a closed annular ring for all charges, consistent with conventional POV behavior. At low charge the ring is nearly ideal, whereas for larger ℓ weak charge-dependent structure appears due to the radial oscillations inherited from the underlying Bessel–Gaussian construction. With increasing anisotropy, the intensity patterns evolve systematically. At moderate anisotropy (m=5.5 and m=12.5), the annulus develops pronounced azimuthal distortions and opening-like discontinuities. These features arise because the anisotropic azimuth defined in Eq. (6) introduces a non-uniform azimuthal phase gradient around the ring, breaking rotational symmetry and producing non-uniform intensity along the annulus in the Fourier plane. The resulting intensity distribution exhibits distinct localized maxima along the ring, resulting from the interference of spatial-frequency components induced by the non-uniform angular phase gradient introduced by the anisotropic mapping. At strong anisotropy (m\ge 100), the intensity pattern becomes progressively less sensitive to the programmed charge. For m=100, the ring remains distorted but is more regular than in the moderate regime. For extreme anisotropy (m=2000 and m=10000), the pattern again approaches a nearly closed annulus whose radius and overall appearance vary only weakly with charge. In this regime, the anisotropic phase mapping compresses most of the angular phase variation into narrow sectors, effectively reducing the range of azimuthal phase gradients contributing to the field. As a result, the angular interference responsible for the splitting observed at moderate anisotropy is strongly suppressed. This leads to a recovery of a continuous annular intensity distribution, even though the underlying modal analysis reveals significant redistribution of OAM. Taken together, the simulations reveal a clear distinction between modal content and observed morphology. The direct-field OAM spectra show that increasing angular anisotropy progressively destroys the dominance of the programmed OAM eigenmode and redistributes power into neighboring and lower-order modes. In contrast, the order-isolated holographic intensities show that, after diffraction filtering, the resulting beams can still exhibit a stable annular intensity profile. Strong anisotropy begins to stabilize the observed intensity morphology, while extreme anisotropy leads to a regime in which the beam exhibits a stable annular intensity profile without preserving the original vortex eigenstate, indicating pseudo-POV behavior.

All simulations were implemented in Python using NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, and PIL. Generation of a single order-isolated POV intensity pattern required approximately 10–15 s per parameter set, while full-field simulations including modal decomposition required approximately 30–120 s depending on the topological charge.

Experimental setup

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 5. Panel (a) presents a schematic of the optical layout used to generate anisotropy-modified POVs and speckle patterns, while panel (b) shows a photograph of the corresponding laboratory implementation. A continuous-wave He–Ne laser operating at a wavelength of 632.8 nm served as the light source. The beam was spatially filtered using a pinhole to remove higher-order spatial modes and residual aberrations, and then collimated to produce a uniform, near-planar wavefront. An aperture placed after the collimating lens defined the beam diameter to approximately 8 mm and suppressed residual edge diffraction. A polarizer (P) prepared the beam in the required linear polarization state for efficient phase modulation by the reflective phase-only SLM (Holoeye GAEA 2.1; pixel pitch 3.74 m; resolution 4094× 2464). The resulting s-polarized beam was aligned with the modulation axis of the SLM. The reflected field contained multiple diffraction orders due to the blazed grating encoded in the hologram, and an iris was used to spatially select the +1 diffraction order before the beam entered the Fourier lens . Lens (f = 30 cm) performed the Fourier transform of the SLM plane, producing the POV at its back focal plane. The distances were cm (SLM–) and cm (–POV plane). After order selection, the beam was redirected by a mirror toward either direct POV observation or speckle measurements. For direct POV measurements, the intensity at the Fourier plane of was recorded using a CCD camera (Prosilica GT2750, Allied Vision; pixel pitch 4.54 m). For speckle measurements, a static ground-glass diffuser (GG, 220 grit) was placed at the POV plane, and the scattered field was collected by a second lens (f=30 cm). The distances were cm (GG–) and cm (–CCD). This configuration allowed the speckle field to be recorded in an intermediate Fresnel propagation regime, where the correlations remain sensitive to the spatial structure of the incident vortex beam. Representative intensity patterns are shown as insets in Fig. 5a: inset (i) shows a typical POV intensity for ℓ =2, m=1, while inset (ii) shows the corresponding speckle pattern after inserting the diffuser. The use of the GG diffuser ensured reproducible speckle realizations under identical input conditions, enabling reliable ensemble-averaged correlation analysis. Within the investigated range, we classify the vortices as low-order (), intermediate-order (), and high-order (). For clarity, we refer to m=1 as the isotropic case, m=5.5 and 12.5 as moderate anisotropy, m=100 as strong anisotropy, and m=2000 and 10000 as extreme anisotropy. In the isotropic case (m=1), shown in Fig. 6a (top row) and in the additional examples of Fig. 6b, the beams form closed annular intensity distributions characteristic of POVs. For low and intermediate charges the ring radius remains nearly constant, consistent with the expected charge-invariant radius of ideal POV beams. At larger ℓ, weak additional radial structures appear around the dominant annulus due to the oscillatory radial envelope inherited from the Bessel–Gaussian construction and practical constraints of phase-only holographic encoding. These additional rings introduce mild charge-dependent variations in the observed intensity profile but do not alter the dominant annular morphology.

When angular anisotropy is introduced (m>1), the annular intensity undergoes a systematic, anisotropy-driven deformation. At moderate anisotropy (m=5.5 and m=12.5), the annulus becomes azimuthally distorted and develops two-sided openings together with localized intensity maxima along the ring. These features arise from the anisotropic azimuthal mapping, which introduces a non-uniform azimuthal phase gradient and breaks rotational symmetry. As a result, the intensity along the annulus becomes angularly modulated, producing symmetric bright maxima located at opposite positions along the ring. This behavior is consistent with the simulated POV intensity distributions (Fig. 4), which reproduce the anisotropy-induced azimuthal modulation and opening of the annulus.

As the anisotropy parameter increases further (m ≳ 100), the opened annulus progressively closes again and the intensity reorganizes into a nearly circular ring. For extreme anisotropy (m=2000 and m=10000), the resulting annular profiles appear almost identical for all investigated charges, indicating a visually charge-invariant ring structure.

This convergence toward visually similar annular profiles at extreme anisotropy foreshadows the pseudo-POV regime discussed later, in which the intensity morphology becomes nearly charge invariant even though the underlying vortex phase structure is substantially modified.

While Fig. 7a establishes the convergence of the annular radius with increasing anisotropy, complementary information about azimuthal symmetry is captured by the annular opening metric shown in Fig. 7b. The opening is defined as the largest contiguous azimuthal sector along the dominant annular band where the smoothed angular intensity profile falls below a fixed fraction of the annular peak intensity, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2, where the ring detection, angular profile, and threshold-based gap identification are shown explicitly.

The opening response is strongly charge dependent and exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on anisotropy. The lowest-order beam (ℓ =2) remains nearly closed across the entire anisotropy range, with only small values of the opening metric (G ≲ 0.05). In contrast, ℓ =10 shows a clear increase in opening at intermediate anisotropy, reaching G ≈ 0.4 at m=12.5,before decreasing again as anisotropy increases beyond the moderate regime. For intermediate charges, the opening becomes more pronounced, with typical values in the range G ∼ 0.3–0.6 at moderate anisotropy. At higher charges (), a finite baseline value of G is already present at m=1, and the subsequent evolution with anisotropy occurs relative to this baseline. The metric captures angular intensity continuity and is therefore sensitive to weak azimuthal nonuniformities. In particular, as the ring radius increases with ℓ, the corresponding increase in circumference leads to reduced angular uniformity, making the metric more responsive to local intensity variations. In addition, faint nearby radial side structures arising from the Bessel–Gaussian envelope and phase-only encoding can locally reduce the annular intensity below the threshold over limited angular sectors. As a result, a small nonzero value of G may be obtained even when the ring appears visually closed, representing a baseline level of angular nonuniformity rather than a true geometric opening. In contrast, under anisotropic phase mapping (m>1), the observed openings correspond to a genuine structural deformation of the annulus. Taken together, these observations indicate that the two-sided openings and associated azimuthal intensity modulation observed in Fig. 6 originate from the anisotropy-induced redistribution of the azimuthal phase gradient under the imposed angular remapping. The observed openings therefore represent the intensity signature of a controlled deformation of the vortex phase rather than experimental artefacts.

To assess whether this apparent morphological convergence reflects preserved vortex phase, we employ speckle-based statistical diagnostics. Figure 8 shows representative speckle intensity patterns obtained after transmission of the POV beams through the ground-glass diffuser. Although individual realizations appear random, systematic variations in the speckle texture are observable. For the isotropic case (m=1), speckle fields produced by low-charge beams exhibit relatively homogeneous textures with weak filamentary or snake-like features, while higher-charge beams generate finer and more structured speckle grains. This behaviour is consistent with an increase in the spatial-frequency content of the underlying vortex field at larger ℓ.

Because the statistical properties of fully developed speckle are governed by the spatial-frequency spectrum of the incident optical field, structural modifications of the vortex beam prior to scattering are encoded in the resulting speckle correlations.

The residual mismatch between direct and speckle-derived radii is itself physically informative. It shows that the PSD-based estimate is sensitive not only to the dominant annular feature visible in the order-isolated POV intensity, but also to additional field structure that is not fully captured by a single-radius description. Although the observed POV intensity retains an annular appearance, the corresponding speckle statistics remain sensitive to the underlying spatial-frequency content of the field. In particular, moderate anisotropy modifies the spatial-frequency distribution of the field, while stronger anisotropy leads to increasingly localized spectral content with reduced contribution from higher-order components. These trends are consistent with the behavior observed in the Fourier-plane intensity distributions (Supplementary Fig. S4). As a result, the PSD-based radius reflects an effective spatial scale rather than a strictly geometric annular radius.

While PSD-based analysis captures an effective characteristic scale of the field, it does not directly probe the relative phase structure of different vortex states. To access additional structural information, we therefore analyze the speckle cross-correlation (CC) between fields generated under different vortex conditions.

CC measurements are sensitive to differences in the underlying field structure, including relative phase variations, and therefore provide information beyond what is accessible from intensity-based metrics alone. Having established this ordering rule, we now examine how the CC maps evolve under increasing anisotropy, as summarized in Fig. 11. At m=1, the CC retains clear charge dependence for small charge separations, but the signal becomes weak and poorly defined for widely separated charges because the corresponding high-ℓ POVs contain multi-ring and low-contrast structure. At moderate anisotropy (m=5.5 and 12.5), the CC maps become sharper and more reproducible, even for larger charge differences. In this regime, angular anisotropy suppresses weak outer-ring contributions and stabilizes a dominant annular component in the speckle correlations. Importantly, the CC response remains charge-order sensitive: measurable differences in ring radius, contrast, and lobe intensity persist across different charge pairs. This indicates that the speckle correlations continue to distinguish between vortex states even when the order-isolated POV intensity exhibits similar annular morphology. This intermediate-anisotropy regime therefore represents a practical operating window in which anisotropy improves the robustness of correlation-based discrimination rather than destroying it. The experimental CC maps and the corresponding Fourier-plane simulations exhibit qualitatively similar trends in this regime. Specifically, intermediate anisotropy produces elongated eye-like structures in the intrinsic Fourier-plane intensity distribution of the anisotropically transformed Bessel–Gaussian field (Supplementary Fig. S4), while the corresponding CC maps develop anisotropic elongation and directional structure. This agreement indicates that the speckle correlations remain sensitive to the underlying structure of the anisotropically transformed vortex field. At strong anisotropy (m=100), the CC morphology becomes strongly anisotropic and the charge-dependent annular signatures are increasingly masked by the imposed geometry. For extreme anisotropy (m \ge 2000), the CC maps corresponding to different charge pairs converge toward highly similar patterns. This convergence can be understood as a consequence of the anisotropic azimuth becoming increasingly step-like at large m, such that the scattered-field statistics are governed primarily by the imposed anisotropic phase geometry rather than by the nominal programmed charge. To interpret the strongly anisotropic regime, each highly anisotropic field is correlated with a fixed low-anisotropy reference state (ℓ =1, m=1), as shown in Fig. 11b. In these correlations, the dark half of the CC ring consistently appears below the center, corresponding to the ordering signature associated with a second field that behaves as if it has a lower effective charge in the CC diagnostic than the reference state. Remarkably, this same diagnostic pattern appears for all highly anisotropic beams regardless of their nominal programmed charge.

This observation indicates that, under extreme anisotropy, the speckle cross-correlation exhibits a strongly reduced sensitivity to the programmed vortex charge. Instead, the anisotropically transformed fields produce a similar low-charge-like statistical signature in the CC diagnostic.

Taken together, these observations indicate that, under extreme anisotropy, the beams no longer behave as conventional charge-preserving POVs, despite retaining an annular intensity profile. This motivates a precise operational definition of the pseudo-POV regime. In the present work, a pseudo-POV is a beam that simultaneously exhibits: (i) an annular intensity profile with nearly charge-invariant radius, (ii) loss of charge distinguishability in intensity-based CC diagnostics, and (iii) redistribution of OAM content away from the nominal programmed charge. The first two signatures are directly observed experimentally, while the third is inferred from numerical simulations and supported by the observed loss of distinguishability between different programmed charges in the CC response. In the simulations, this redistribution is observed as a broadening of the OAM spectrum at moderate anisotropy and the emergence of a dominant low-order component under extreme anisotropy.

Thus, although the observed intensity may resemble that of a conventional perfect vortex, the underlying optical field no longer corresponds to a single high-charge OAM eigenstate. Such fields may remain useful in applications that depend primarily on annular intensity morphology, but they no longer preserve the well-defined high-charge OAM content required for OAM-sensitive tasks such as mode-selective communication or OAM-based particle manipulation.

Importantly, while the OAM spectra directly confirm modal redistribution in simulation, the experimental identification of this redistribution is achieved here without interferometric phase measurements, relying instead on intensity-based speckle cross-correlation. Taken together, the experimental measurements and numerical analysis reveal a consistent progression: moderate anisotropy stabilizes the annular intensity morphology and correlation signatures, strong anisotropy begins to suppress charge-dependent features, and extreme anisotropy leads to a pseudo-POV regime in which the beam retains an annular intensity profile but no longer corresponds to a single high-charge OAM eigenstate.

Conclusion

In summary, we have shown that controlled angular anisotropy provides a deterministic and continuous means to reshape POVs across a wide range of topological charges. By tuning a single anisotropy parameter, the multi-ring structures that emerge in high-ℓ POVs under realistic phase-only encoding are progressively consolidated into dominant annular intensity profiles. This evolution is quantitatively captured through the systematic suppression of charge-dependent radius variations and the convergence of annular radii at extreme anisotropy, yielding intensity distributions that become visually indistinguishable across ℓ.

Crucially, we demonstrate that this apparent convergence of annular intensity does not imply preservation of vortex phase or OAM. In the extreme-anisotropy regime, the beams enter a pseudo-POV state: annular in intensity but characterized by a redistribution of OAM content that renders charge-dependent signatures diagnostically indistinguishable. Non-interferometric speckle analysis provides decisive insight into this transition. AC measurements confirm that the observed radius convergence cannot be attributed to trivial ellipticity or geometric distortion, while CC reveals a clear progression from charge-order sensitivity at low anisotropy to a collapsed, charge-indistinguishable response at extreme anisotropy. In this diagnostic sense, annular intensity alone is insufficient to assess vortex integrity.

More broadly, this work clarifies the robustness limits of high-charge POVs under angular deformation and identifies angular anisotropy as a powerful control parameter governing both radial and azimuthal structure. The introduction of a quantitative annular opening metric further reveals a non-monotonic evolution from closed annuli to opened profiles and back to closed, pseudo-POV states as anisotropy increases. The combined ability to engineer annular symmetry and to diagnose hidden redistribution of OAM using intensity-only, non-interferometric statistics establishes a practical framework for mode recognition in structured annular beams.

Such control and diagnostics are expected to be relevant for applications that rely on structured annular illumination, including structured illumination microscopy, mode recognition, and intensity-only classification of OAM-encoded states. In particular, the intrinsic ordering sensitivity of speckle CC, exploited here as a diagnostic and referenced to a low-charge state, provides a practical non-interferometric route for identifying effective charge collapse in annular vortex beams even when their direct intensity profiles appear ideal.
