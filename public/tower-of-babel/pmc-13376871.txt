Determination of the Florfenicol drug solubility in supercritical CO2 and thermodynamic modeling by PR EoS/vdW and density-based correlations
Authors: Gholamhossein Sodeifian, Hamidreza Bagheri, Shima Golshani, Kiana Malekpour Afshar, Adel Noubigh, Mohammadreza Rashidi-Nooshabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the solubility of Florfenicol in supercritical carbon dioxide (SC-CO₂) was investigated for the first time under various conditions: pressures (P) from 120 to 270 bar (120 < P ≤ 270) and temperatures of 308, 318, 328, and 338 K. The equilibrium solubility parameter ranged from 0.22 to 3.65 kg/m3, resulting in mole fractions (y) ranging from 0.45 × 10− 4 to 5.74 × 10− 4. Two different modeling approaches were applied to correlate the drug solubility data. The first approach used an equation of state, specifically the Peng-Robinson equation of state with the van der Waals Type I mixing rule, yielding an average absolute relative deviation (AARD) of %10.48. The second approach comprised 22 density-based correlations categorized into three groups based on their independent variables: (T, ρ), (P, T), and (P, ρ, T). These correlations included those by Bartle et al., Garlapati and Madras (I & II), Chrastil, Andonova and Garlapati, Kumar and Johnston, del Valle and Aguilera, Sung-Shim, Ch and Madras, Adachi and Lu, Alwi-Garlapati, Bian et al., Cherif Si-Moussa, Mendez-Santiago and Teja, Jafari Nejad et al., Sodeifian et al. (Models I & II), Keshmiri et al., Jouyban et al., Khansary et al., Hozhabr et al., and Gordillo et al. These models have between three and six adjustable coefficients. Among the (T, ρ) category, the Adachi and Lu model showed better agreement (AARD = 4.91%), while among the (P, ρ, T) category, the Mendez-Santiago and Teja model showed the best agreement (AARD = 4.19%) with the solubility data of Florfenicol. Finally, the enthalpies of vaporization, total, and solvation were determined for the Florfenicol/SC-CO₂ system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52975-w.Keywords: Peng-Robinson, Florfenicol drug, Supercritical CO2, Solubility, Semi-empirical, OptimizationSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Florfenicol is a fluorinated synthetic derivative of thiamphenicol, primarily used as an antibiotic in veterinary medicine. It is employed for the treatment of respiratory diseases in cattle caused by Histophilus somni, Pasteurella multocida, and Mannheimia haemolytica, as well as for bovine interdigital phlegmon associated with Fusobacterium necrophorum and Prevotella melaninogenica. Additionally, Florfenicol is used in aquaculture1,2.

Drugs are available in various forms, including tablets, capsules, syrups, and ampoules, to prevent or treat diseases and enhance physiological functions. Various approaches have been developed for fabricating drug particles at the nanoscale, especially through bottom-up techniques that build particles from molecular or solution phases rather than breaking down larger particles3,4. These methods can be broadly classified into controlled precipitation or crystallization and evaporation-based techniques. Various solvent precipitation techniques include hydrosol production by anti-solvent precipitation, which is simple and cost-effective for laboratory-scale use but tends to yield mostly amorphous nanoparticles with less precise mixing control. Selecting the drug production method is crucial, especially for new medications developed to treat various diseases, including cancer5,6. High-gravity controlled precipitation can make crystalline particles and has been commercially scaled up; however, it requires a singular apparatus. Spray drying is one way to make particles by atomizing a drug solution and letting the solvent evaporate. Spray drying can give moderate yields; nevertheless, it makes amorphous particles. The aerosol flow reactor method allows for precise control of droplet residence time and temperature, and it could be scaled up; however, it might not be suitable for drugs that are sensitive to heat4–6.

There are numerous parameters to consider when choosing an appropriate manufacturing method, such as the type of drug, the dosage form, the amount needed, the cost, and the availability of equipment. Drug solubility is a crucial factor in determining a drug’s dosage and efficacy. Improved solubility increases absorption and bioavailability, accelerates the onset of action, and minimizes side effects. This is especially important for rapid treatment during outbreaks of emerging diseases, such as COVID-197,8. Drugs with low solubility may not completely dissolve in body fluids, leading to reduced absorption and, as a result, suboptimal therapeutic effects and potential drug-drug interactions9,10. Drugs that dissolve faster are usually absorbed faster and indicate their therapeutic effects more quickly8–11. As particles become smaller, their surface area increases, which speeds up the rate at which they dissolve and interact with solvents. To accurately model the solubility mechanism in supercritical CO2 (SC-CO2), we need to find this connection. This will help us improve extraction efficiency and make better thermodynamic predictions12–14. The use of existing organic solvents in manufacturing processes presents several disadvantages, including detrimental environmental effects and higher processing costs, particularly within the pharmaceutical industry15. Consequently, the global demand for non-toxic, environmentally friendly, non-hazardous, low-cost, and accessible solvents is increasing to supplant existing solvents. Thus, green solvents such as supercritical fluids, ionic liquids, and deep eutectic solvents can serve as sustainable alternatives to conventional solvents across various sectors, including the pharmaceutical industry16–18. Increasing CO₂ concentration contributes to global warming. CO₂ is primarily released from fossil fuels and certain industrial processes. Additionally, increasing CO₂ levels result in greater heat retention in the atmosphere. To mitigate the harmful effects of CO₂ on the environment, it is utilized in various sectors, such as power generation and, after conversion to a supercritical state, in the pharmaceutical industry19.

SCFs have a unique duality in that they can act either as liquids or gases, depending on parameters such as pressure and temperature. SCFs can move through porous materials similar to gases because they have significant kinetic energy, and there is no boundary between the gas and liquid phases20–22. They have a high diffusivity, which is similar to that of gases, a property that makes it easy for them to mix and move quickly through porous media. Additionally, SCFs have densities that are close to those of liquids. This property enables them to dissolve components in the same way that liquids do23. The gas-like properties of SCFs include high diffusivity, low viscosity, and the ability to penetrate porous media. The liquid-like properties include high density, good solvating power, and the ability to adjust these properties for different uses24. SCF extraction presents numerous advantages, such as faster processing, enhanced selectivity, sensitivity to pressure and temperature fluctuations, a concentrated solvent for meticulous solubility control, superior performance, reduced solvent usage, and compatibility with heat-sensitive substances25–27. SCFs are characterized by exceeding their critical properties, i.e., pressure and temperature, which allows them to exhibit features of both gases and liquids: gas-like properties (i.e., high diffusivity, low viscosity) and liquid-like properties (i.e., high density, effective solvating power)28. SCFs exhibit significant compressibility near critical temperatures, leading to slight alterations in mass transport, density, and pressure parameters that can greatly affect their solvation capacity29–31. Because CO2 does not react with other substances in the environment, using it as a solvent in high-pressure processes greatly reduces the environmental problems that come with using hazardous solvents32.

Because CO2 is not dangerous to the environment, using it as a solvent in high-pressure processes significantly reduces the environmental problems associated with using hazardous solvents. Solvents including chlorinated solvents and organic solvents represent a significant environmental hazard and have been shown to harm the ozone layer. Given the remarkable properties of SCFs and their classification as green solvents, they can serve as substitutes for organic and harmful solvents such as chlorofluorocarbons33. SC-CO2 is economical, has high purity, and is readily available at low critical temperature and pressure. Beyond its use as a solvent, SC-CO2 has significant utility in various fields, including essential oil extraction, seed oil extraction, nanoparticle synthesis, impregnation, and polymer fabrication34–38. The supercritical anti-solvent process uses supercritical carbon dioxide as an anti-solvent. This means that it can mix completely with the chosen liquid solvent that contains the solute. The drug dissolves in the liquid solvent but not in carbon dioxide. This occurs because supercritical carbon dioxide diffuses rapidly, which makes the solution supersaturated39,40. This technique produces solute particles that are nano- or micrometer-sized, which enhances solute dissolution, bioavailability, and controlled release. The solubility of many different drugs in SC-CO2 and the results were modeled by different equations of state (EoSs). Such equations can be used to make predictions under conditions where no experimental data are available, making them invaluable for the design of new processes41. Artificial intelligence and fuzzy logic methods have a wide range of applications across all areas of natural sciences and engineering, resulting in more insightful analyses of problems42. Machine learning is a crucial and extensively utilized field in computer science and AI that enables machines to learn and improve from data without explicit programming43–45. It encompasses a range of methods and techniques that can be classified into three primary categories: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning46,47. Deep learning is among the most advanced subfields of machine learning, rooted in the structure and function of artificial neural networks, particularly those with multiple layers (deep neural networks)48–50. These techniques, along with semi-empirical equations, are employed to predict the solubility of various drugs in supercritical fluids, and numerous articles have been published in this domain51–53. Among all the equations of state used to model drug/SC-CO2 binary systems, cubic EoSs are particularly significant due to their simplicity in mathematical calculations. The SAFT (statistical associating fluid theory) family of EoSs has parameters derived from pure density or vapor pressure, necessitating more complex calculations for drug/fluid equilibrium41,54. We reported a comprehensive literature review in Table S1 (Supplementary data file)55–95.

The drug solubility data in SC-CO2 is important because it affects the pressure and temperature of the process, which in turn affects the particle shape and size during the extraction process. Accurate solubility allows for control over particle micronization, which speeds up drug dissolution and makes it more bioavailable96. This is important for the effectiveness of pharmaceuticals in SCFs processes. Understanding the solubility of pharmaceutical compounds in SC-CO2 and its connection to thermodynamic models helps enhance pharmaceutical operations. Diverse theoretical methodologies have been proposed and advanced for predicting the solubility of drugs in SC-CO297. Accurate characterization of key features requires thermodynamic modeling and efficient experimentation with all possible mixtures of solute and solvent molecules. In the present research, two models, cubic equations of state (CEoSs) and density-based correlations, were employed for thermodynamic modeling of the solubility data. The first model was based on the Peng-Robinson (PR) EoS using a mixing rule, i.e., van der Waals (vdW) type I. The density-based models do not need thermodynamic properties, as they use density, pressure, and temperature as required inputs. In the present communication, the Florfenicol solubility in SC-CO2 has been calculated and correlated for the first time at various operating conditions with 22 density-based models, as well as the PR EoS model. Finally, the vaporization, total, and solvation enthalpies were determined.

2 g of Florfenicol was placed inside a 70-mL equilibrium vessel. To increase the interaction between Florfenicol and the SC-CO2, the equilibrium vessel was prepared with sintered filters and glass beads. Using both ensured uniform drug distribution and prevented any undesired channeling. The solubility of Florfenicol was determined by measuring absorbance at λmax = 274.09 using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer equipped with a 1-cm quartz cell. A known mass of Florfenicol was dissolved in DMSO to create a stock solution. Additional standard solutions were produced by diluting the stock solution. The calibration curve was established (R2 = 0.991) to determine the Florfenicol concentration in the vial. It should be mentioned that, each experimental data point was the average of at least three experimental measurements. To confirm equilibrium solubility, the experiments utilized fresh samples and monitored solubility over different durations. At a fixed temperature and pressure, the drug was exposed to supercritical CO2 in a stirred equilibrium cell for periods of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80 min, with solubility measured at each interval. The solubility remained unchanged after 60 min, indicating that equilibrium had been reached (see Fig. S1).

In this investigation, as presented in Table 2; Fig. 4, the mole fraction, y, and equilibrium solubility parameter of Florfenicol drug were measured across a range of temperatures (308 to 338 K) and pressures (120 to 270 bar). Figure 4 shows that at a constant temperature, the Florfenicol drug solubility increases when the operating pressure is increased. At a given temperature, an increase in pressure enhances the solubility of the gas in solvents. Conversely, decreasing pressure reduces the gas solubility rate in liquids. The increase in drug solubility may be attributed to the higher density and the improved capacity of SC-CO₂ to dissolve compounds at elevated pressures. Increasing the SC-CO₂ density enhances the Florfenicol solubility. The SC-CO2 density is directly related to the Florfenicol drug solubility. As the density increases, the number of SC-CO2 molecules in a specific volume increases, thereby increasing the likelihood of interactions between the SC-CO2 molecules and the Florfenicol drug. These interactions facilitate enhanced dissolution of the Florfenicol drug in the SC-CO2. Similar information has been reported in the literature95,100–102,104,151–153. How operating temperature affects the solubility power of SC-CO2 depends on changes in the solvent density and the vapor pressure of the drug. The Florfenicol drug vapor pressure and the SC-CO2 density are two crucial parameters that influence drug solubility. The impact of temperature variations on these two parameters differs. As the temperature rises, the vapor pressure increases while the density decreases. The impact of operating temperature on the solubility of Florfenicol depends on the interaction between the drug and SC-CO2, as well as their individual properties. In fact, these two opposing effects of temperature on the vapor pressure of Florfenicol and the SC-CO2 density lead to the crossover phenomenon. Accordingly, at pressures below the cross-over pressure (P < 150 bar), the influence of density prevails over that of vapor pressure regarding the drug’s solubility, resulting in decreased solubility with rising temperature. Conversely, at pressures above the cross-over pressure (P > 150 bar), the influence of vapor pressure dominates, leading to increased solubility. Substances in a solid state usually become more soluble with increasing temperature, but the opposite is true for substances in a gaseous state. Specifically, higher temperatures lead to higher kinetic energy, resulting in the overcoming of intermolecular interactions between solvents and solutes. Higher temperatures can also increase the solute’s vapor pressure, thereby enhancing its solubility in supercritical fluids. Conversely, increased temperatures may decrease the density of SC-CO2, thereby reducing its solvent capacity. Several other studies have reported similar behavior60,61,92,154–157.

Thermodynamic modeling with PR EoS/vdW-I

Thermodynamic modeling is widely used and valuable for verifying the accuracy and precision of laboratory results158. Modeling in various systems that contain at least two phases can yield important insights into the properties of the system159. Conversely, thermodynamic modeling can also be employed to make predictions in the operational range where experimental data are lacking160,161. The present investigation used PR EoS in combination with vdW-I mixing rule to model the Florfenicol drug experimental data at four different operating temperatures and a pressure range from 120 to 270 bar. The results of modeling at T = 308 K and T = 338 K are given in Fig. 5, and other results are presented in the Supplementary data file (see Figs. S2,S3). In contrast to other intricate models like the SAFT (statistical associating fluid theory) EoS family, the PR EoS facilitates very straightforward application in computational investigations. According to Fig. 5, raising the operating pressure at a constant operating temperature enhances Florfenicol solubility in supercritical carbon dioxide, which is attributed to the increased solvating power of SC-CO₂. The behavior of the PR EoS also reflects this across the entire operating pressure range. This behavior required correction to achieve a more accurate model prediction. For this purpose, the binary interaction coefficient (kij) is utilized. This coefficient is a parameter in the PR EoS. Table 4 gives the values of kij and the AARD values. The use of kij has enhanced the PR EoS; it typically outperforms other cubic EoS, such as the SRK EoS, in modeling solid-supercritical phase behavior56,58,65,68,85,95,162–164. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the PR EoS demonstrates acceptable performance with satisfactory accuracy across nearly the entire operating pressure range.

Density-based correlations

In a subsequent method of drug solubility modeling, the following 22 density-based equations were used: Kumar and Johnston, Garlapati and Madras (I & II), Bartle et al., Andonova and Garlapati, Chrastil, Alwi-Garlapati, Sung-Shim, del Valle and Aguilera, Ch and Madras, Adachi and Lu, Bian et al., Jafari Nejad et al., Cherif Si-Moussa, Khansary et al., Mendez-Santiago and Teja, Hozhabr et al., Sodeifian et al., (models I & II), Jouyban et al., Keshmiri et al. and Gordillo et al. These correlations were divided into three categories i.e. y = f (T, ρ), y = f (P, ρ, T) and y = f (P, T). The details of all semi-empirical correlations are presented in Appendix A. One reason for applying density-based correlations is the simplicity of calculations and the absence of critical properties of the drug and supercritical solvent. Semi-empirical correlations require operational parameters such as temperature, pressure, and density to determine drug solubility. They have some unknown coefficients. The algorithms for these applications are expanding rapidly across various industries, playing a crucial role in fields ranging from machine learning to engineering and design165,166. Optimization algorithms are a category of algorithms used to identify the best possible solution to a specific problem. The primary objective of these algorithms is to determine the optimal solution that either minimizes or maximizes the associated objective function, depending on the nature of the problem167,168. Among these algorithms, search algorithms, genetic algorithms, stochastic algorithms, and multi-objective optimization algorithms are noteworthy165–170. As a general principal, it should be considered that when optimizing the parameters, their values in EoSs or semi-empirical models may be extracted by different methods including various algorithms like nonlinear regression methods [171-172]. In this study, a genetic algorithm was used to obtain the coefficients of semi-empirical correlations. The coefficients and AARD values of 22 density-based equations are tabulated in Table 4. According to this table, the AARD values are between 4.19% and 24.23%. The AL correlation (in category y = f (T, ρ), AARD = 4.91%) and MST correlation (in category y = f (P, ρ, T), AARD = 4.19%) show better performance for modeling the solubility of Florfenicol drug solubility. Furthermore, all the semi-empirical models applied in this study showed satisfactory agreement with the experimental data. Among the correlations with 3–5 adjustable coefficients, except for the Andonova and Garlapati correlations (which have 3 adjustable coefficients), better agreement was observed between the correlations and the solubility data. Also, the graphical outcomes of some density-based equations are presented in Fig. 6 as follows: Sodeifian et al. I (Fig. 6a) and Chrastil (Fig. 6b). The remaining results are presented in the Supplementary data file (see Fig. S4).

The negative sign convention corresponds to a standard enthalpy of mixing framework that reflects the enthalpy required to solvate Florfenicol in supercritical carbon dioxide. Conclusions

This investigation aimed to measure, for the first time, the solubility of the antibiotic Florfenicol in supercritical carbon dioxide within a specific pressure and temperature range. The results show that the measured solubility ranges were: 0.45 × 10− 4 < y (mole fraction) < 5.74 × 10− 4 and 0.22 kg/m³ < S (equilibrium solubility) < 3.65 kg/m³. The maximum solubility of Florfenicol was achieved at P = 270 bar and T = 338 K, with y = 5.74 × 10⁻⁴. To model the obtained solubilities, the Peng-Robinson equation of state (PR EoS) and 22 density-based equations, i.e. Alwi-Garlapati, Garlapati and Madras (I & II), Kumar and Johnston, Chrastil, Andonova and Garlapati, del Valle and Aguilera, Ch and Madras, Sung-Shim, Adachi and Lu, Jafari Nejad et al., Bian et al., Cherif Si-Moussa, Mendez-Santiago and Teja, Hozhabr et al., Khansary et al., Sodeifian et al., (model I & II), Keshmiri et al., Bartle et al., Jouyban et al., and Gordillo et al. were utilized. The PR EoS with AARD = 10.48% and among all density-based equations AL equation with AARD = 4.91% in T, ρ category and MST equation with AARD = 4.19% in P, ρ, T category, prepared a better correlation with the experimental data.
