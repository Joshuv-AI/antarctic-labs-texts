Idiopathic intestinal intussusception causing intestinal obstruction in an adult: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Imen Ben Ismail, Hakim Zenaidi, Marwen Sghaier, Saber Rebii, Ayoub Zoghlami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Unlike the pediatric form, most adult cases are secondary to an identifiable pathological lead point. Idiopathic intussusception, particularly involving the small bowel, is exceedingly rare and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentationWe report the case of a 42-year-old woman with no prior abdominal surgery who presented with a three-day history of abdominal pain, vomiting, and cessation of stool and flatus. Early diagnosis and tailored surgical management can ensure favorable outcomes while avoiding unnecessary bowel resection.

Introduction

Adult intestinal intussusception is an uncommon clinical entity distinguished by the telescoping of one segment of the gastrointestinal tract into an adjacent segment, leading to bowel obstruction and, in some cases, ischemia or perforation. While intussusception is a familiar diagnosis in pediatric populations, accounting for the majority of cases in infancy and early childhood, its occurrence in adults is rare, representing approximately 1–5% of all cases of intussusception and only about 1% of adult bowel obstructions [1].

The pathophysiology in adults differs markedly from that in children. In pediatric patients, the condition is frequently idiopathic and often managed nonoperatively; by contrast, adult intussusception is more commonly associated with an identifiable lead point, such as a benign or malignant neoplasm, Meckel’s diverticulum, or other structural lesions [2]. Idiopathic cases, defined as intussusception without a demonstrable pathological lead point, constitute a minority of adult presentations, with some systematic reviews suggesting idiopathic etiology in less than half of cases, especially in the small bowel [3].

Clinically, adult intussusception often presents with nonspecific and intermittent symptoms, including crampy abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and signs of bowel obstruction, which contribute to diagnostic challenges and frequent delays in diagnosis [4]. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced computed tomography, has emerged as the diagnostic modality of choice, allowing visualization of the characteristic “target” or “sausage” signs and facilitating preoperative recognition of this condition [5].

Given the potential for underlying pathology and complications such as ischemia or perforation, surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment in adults, with operative reduction or resection based on intraoperative findings and the likelihood of a pathological lead point [6]. Adult idiopathic intussusception, especially involving the jejunum without a demonstrable lead lesion, is particularly rare and warrants detailed reporting to expand the clinical understanding of this atypical presentation.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 42-year-old man, with no significant past medical or surgical history, who presented with symptoms of acute intestinal obstruction. He reported diffuse abdominal pain, bilious vomiting, and cessation of both stool and flatus for the past three days. There was no history of previous abdominal surgery, trauma, or similar episodes in the past.

On clinical examination, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination revealed a mildly distended but soft abdomen without tenderness, palpable mass, or peritoneal signs. Bowel sounds were hypoactive, and digital rectal examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations were within normal limits. Abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a classic “target” or “sausage-shaped” configuration consistent with a jejuno-jejunal intussusception located in the left flank (Fig. 1). No pathological lead point, such as a tumor or polyp, was identified. There was no significant upstream small bowel dilatation or evidence of ischemia.

The patient underwent exploratory laparoscopy. Intraoperative findings confirmed a jejuno-jejunal intussusception involving approximately 10 cm of small bowel, with mild proximal distension (Fig. 2). Careful manual reduction of the intussuscepted segment was achieved without difficulty. The bowel appeared viable, and no intraluminal or mural lesion was found to explain the intussusception. Consequently, no resection was performed.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. Oral feeding was resumed on the second postoperative day, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 in good condition. At follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of symptoms.

Discussion

Adult intussusception is a distinct clinical entity that differs fundamentally from the pediatric form in terms of etiology, diagnosis, and management. In adults, intussusception accounts for only a small fraction of bowel obstructions approximately 1% and represents around 5% of all intussusception cases [1, 7]. Unlike in children, where idiopathic presentations predominate, most adult cases are associated with a definable pathological lead point that alters normal peristalsis and predisposes to the invagination of the bowel segment. In large case series, up to 80–90% of adult intussusceptions are reported to involve an identifiable lesion such as a benign or malignant tumor, strictures, or other structural abnormalities. This high prevalence underscores the importance of careful assessment for underlying pathology in adult presentations [8].

Small bowel intussusceptions in adults, especially idiopathic jejuno-jejunal invaginations without an evident lead point, remain rare. Histopathologic lead points are identified less frequently in the small bowel than in the colon, and idiopathic cases in the small intestine constitute a minority of reported presentations. This relative paucity of identifiable causative lesions may be linked to transient, physiological alterations in intestinal motility, but such transient phenomena are more often detected incidentally on imaging and may not present with clinical obstruction. Therefore, the occurrence of idiopathic intussusception causing frank obstruction, as seen in our patient, highlights a clinically significant but uncommon subset of adult intussusception [9].

Clinically, adult intussusception presents with nonspecific symptoms that mimic other causes of bowel obstruction. Common manifestations include intermittent, colicky abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and altered bowel habits, which often contribute to delayed or missed diagnoses when based solely on clinical evaluation [4]. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), has transformed the preoperative recognition of this condition by allowing visualization of characteristic radiologic signs such as the “target” or “sausage” appearance of the invaginated bowel segments. CT also aids in assessing features that suggest a pathological lead point, the extent of bowel involvement, and signs of ischemia or obstruction [5].

Surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment in adults due to the high probability of an underlying pathological lesion and the risk of complications such as bowel ischemia, perforation, and sepsis. Traditionally, surgeons have advocated formal resection of the involved segment with oncologic principles when a neoplasm is suspected; in contrast, intraoperative reduction without resection may be considered when the bowel appears viable and no lead point is identified, particularly in small bowel intussusceptions or in younger patients with benign features [10]. In cases where malignancy is more likely especially in colonic intussusceptions en bloc resection without reduction is generally recommended to avoid tumor dissemination. Nonetheless, advances in imaging and endoscopic techniques have led to increased identification of idiopathic or transient intussusceptions that may safely be managed with a more conservative approach under close clinical and radiologic surveillance [11].

To better contextualize our findings, a structured literature review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases for studies published between January 2018 and February 2026. Only case reports describing adult patients (≥ 18 years) with confirmed idiopathic intussusception were included. Cases with identifiable pathological lead points were excluded. These are summarized in Table 1, which outlines patient demographics, intussusception type and location, clinical presentation, management, and outcomes. The review confirms that idiopathic intussusception in adults most often involves the small bowel, typically the jejunum, and tends to occur in relatively young, otherwise healthy individuals. The majority of patients underwent surgical intervention, with manual reduction being the preferred option when bowel viability was preserved and no lead lesion was detected. Conservative management was reported only in transient, self-limiting forms identified incidentally on imaging.

In the present case, the absence of a detectable lead point both preoperatively on CT and intraoperatively emphasizes the idiopathic nature of the jejuno-jejunal intussusception. The decision to perform manual reduction without resection was guided by the viability of the bowel and the lack of any suspicious lesion, consistent with recommendations that preserve bowel length in selected benign cases. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the lack of recurrence further supports the appropriateness of the chosen management strategy for idiopathic small bowel intussusception.

Conclusion

Idiopathic small bowel intussusception in adults remains an exceptional cause of intestinal obstruction. Because of its nonspecific presentation, diagnosis often relies on high-resolution imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced CT scan. Although the majority of adult cases are associated with an organic lead point, the absence of any underlying lesion should not be overlooked, especially when imaging and intraoperative findings confirm viable bowel and exclude neoplasia. In such situations, cautious manual reduction without resection is both safe and effective. This case highlights the importance of individualized surgical decision-making and contributes to the growing literature describing this rare, idiopathic form of adult intussusception.
