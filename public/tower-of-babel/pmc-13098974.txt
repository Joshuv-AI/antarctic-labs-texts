Effect of attaching ChARM automated respiratory rate counter on actual respiratory rate in children
Authors: Ahad Mahmud Khan, Md Shafiqul Islam, Nabidul Haque Chowdhury, Salahuddin Ahmed, Fatima Tul Jannat, Md Faizul Ahasan, Rizouan Ur Rashid, Rakib Bhuiyan, Mohammad Sarafat Ullah, Ting Shi, Abdullah H Baqui, Steve Cunningham, Eric D McCollum, Harry Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate respiratory rate (RR) measurement is essential for diagnosing and classifying pneumonia in children. The Children’s Automated Respiration Monitor (ChARM) can measure RR, but placing the device on the chest may influence a child’s breathing. This study evaluated whether ChARM affects RR measurement and identification of fast breathing. We conducted a cross-sectional study in multiple health facilities in Bangladesh (2021–2022). Children aged 0–59 months presenting with suspected pneumonia were enrolled. RR was first measured manually by a physician or a health worker and then using ChARM, while chest movements during both measurements were video recorded. A six-member video expert panel (VEP) reviewed all recordings and derived manual RR. Differences in RR values and fast breathing classifications between the two measurement methods were examined. Of 339 enrolled children, both manual and ChARM measurements were obtained for 294; the remainder could not be assessed because of crying or movement. The child’s condition remained same during both measurements in 256 cases. After excluding uninterpretable videos and readings without consensus, VEP-determined RR was available for 217 children. The mean difference in RR between the two measurements was 0.5 breaths per minute (standard error 0.4). RR classification (normal vs. fast breathing) changed in 21 children (9.7%), with moderate agreement between measurements (kappa = 0.749). ChARM did not significantly alter mean RR, though classification changed for a small proportion of children. Because exclusions and non-interpretable videos may have introduced bias, additional studies are required to more definitely evaluate whether device attachment influences RR or fast-breathing classification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44337-026-00595-8.Keywords: Pneumonia, Respiratory rate, Fast breathing, Automated counter

Introduction

Respiratory rate (RR) is a vital physiological parameter for assessing respiratory health in children under five years of age [1]. Accurate measurement of RR is essential for diagnosing respiratory illnesses [2], particularly pneumonia, which remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children aged less than five years worldwide [3–5]. According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) guidelines, childhood pneumonia is primarily identified by the presence of fast breathing, defined using age-specific RR thresholds: ≥ 60 breaths per minute (bpm) for infants aged < 2 months, ≥ 50 bpm for children aged 2–11 months, and ≥ 40 bpm for children aged 12–59 months [6, 7]. Traditionally, this involves manually observing chest movements and counting the RR, a method that is highly susceptible to human error and inter-observer variability. Factors such as provider fatigue, varying experience levels, and environmental distractions further compromise the reliability of manual RR assessments [8].

To address these challenges, a range of automated RR counters has been developed with the aim of improving the accuracy and consistency of RR measurements [1, 9]. Among these, the Children’s Automated Respiration Monitor (ChARM) is a compact battery-operated device developed specifically for use in low-resource settings to assist health workers in identifying fast-breathing pneumonia in young children. This device uses tri-axial accelerometer sensors to detect subtle chest and abdominal movements associated with breathing [10, 11]. These motion signals are processed through embedded algorithms that automatically calculate RR and classify fast breathing according to WHO age-specific thresholds [6, 7]. When secured around the child’s chest with an adjustable belt, ChARM provides a digital readout within approximately one minute [12]. The manufacturer reports a measurement precision of ± 2 bpm under standard conditions [13].

Compared with manual RR counting, which is prone to observer bias, fatigue, and counting errors, especially in restless or crying infants, ChARM offers the advantages of automation, objectivity, and reproducibility. It also frees health workers from the need to observe for a full minute. However, its accuracy can be influenced by motion artifacts, poor belt placement, or irregular breathing patterns, which may limit its reliability in some field conditions. Previous studies from Ethiopia [14] and Nepal [15] have evaluated its usability and acceptability, but evidence on its measurement accuracy and agreement with expert counts remains limited.

In our previous study, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy and agreement of the ChARM in counting RR compared to a VEP reference. ChARM has demonstrated the potential to support frontline health workers in improving pneumonia diagnosis [12]; however, its clinical implementation requires validation against standard methods to ensure reliability. Importantly, the need to strap the device around the child’s chest may cause discomfort, fear, agitation, and crying. These conditions can change the RR and affect the measurement accuracy. Because RR is the key determinant in pneumonia diagnosis, any influence of the device itself on RR could result in misclassification and inappropriate treatment. The current study built upon our earlier publication [12] explored a distinct and novel question: whether attaching the ChARM device around the chest itself has any physiological or behavioural influence on the child’s actual RR.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was part of a broader research initiative focused on developing VEP as a reference standard for RR assessment in children [16]. This study was conducted from December 2021 to December 2022 across multiple tiers of health facilities in Bangladesh, including three Community Clinics (CCs) and the Zakiganj Upazila Health Complex (UHC), a sub-district-level hospital located in Sylhet, and the Institute of Child and Mother Health (ICMH) in Dhaka. ICMH is a national-level specialist institute focused on improving maternal and child health through clinical services, research, and human resource development, and serves as a referral facility offering comprehensive preventive, promotive, and curative care for children and mothers. The CCs, operated by Community Healthcare Providers (CHCPs), represent the most basic level of healthcare service delivery in the country [17]. Six physicians were trained and standardized, and a video expert panel (VEP) was established with them to assess the RR measurements. This study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Study population

The study enrolled infants aged less than two months presenting with any illness, and children aged 2–59 months who had suspected pneumonia i.e., cough and/or breathing difficulties. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age 0–59 months, (ii) presentation to the study facility with the above symptoms, and (iii) provision of written informed consent by a parent or legal guardian. Exclusion criteria were: (i) presence of IMCI-defined danger signs at presentation, and (ii) refusal of a parent or legal guardian to provide informed consent.

Study procedure

Detailed study procedures have been described in a separate publication [16]. Briefly, chest movement videos were recorded using a Canon EOS M50 camera [18]. Recording started once the child appeared clinically settled, defined operationally as the absence of persistent crying, marked agitation, or vigorous body movement. For sleeping children, recording was started while they remained asleep or when they awoke but were not crying or visibly distressed. The physician or CHCP signalled the start and end of the one-minute manual RR count by tapping the microphone. Afterward, the ChARM device was strapped around the child’s chest, the physician or CHCP switched on the device, and video recording was resumed until the device displayed an RR or error. If neither occurred within this timeframe, the attempt was marked as a failed attempt (Fig. 1).

All the videos were securely transferred to a password-protected computer. After the required editing, each video, which included both the video without ChARM and the video with ChARM, was randomly assigned to two members of the VEP for independent RR interpretation. Videos judged as uninterpretable by both members were excluded. When both members provided RR values within a 2-bpm difference, their average was used the final RR. If the disagreement exceeded this threshold or involved interpretability, a third member assessed the video. If no two members produced RR values within a 2-bpm range, a fourth member reviewed the video. When any two members agreed, the average RR was taken as the final RR. Videos with no agreement between the two members were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 2).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated to detect a clinically meaningful difference in RR between paired measurements obtained before and after attachment of ChARM in the same child. Assuming a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, we considered a difference of 2 bpm to be clinically relevant. Based on prior experience with paediatric RR measurements in routine clinical settings, the standard deviation (SD) of the within-child difference in RR was conservatively assumed to be 10 bpm. Using the formula for paired comparisons, a minimum of 196 children with interpretable paired RR measurements was required. Because RR assessment in children is frequently affected by crying, movement, or poor video quality, we anticipated that a proportion of measurements would be uninterpretable or excluded due to lack of consensus among VEP reviewers. To account for an estimated 35–40% loss of analysable observations, the target enrolment size was inflated accordingly. Therefore, approximately 330–340 children were planned for enrolment. A total of 339 children were ultimately enrolled, ensuring adequate power for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

A Bland-Altman plot was generated to assess the level of agreement between two RR measurements derived from chest movement videos reviewed by the VEP, one recorded during measurement using the ChARM device and the other during manual RR counting. The mean and SD of RR values from both methods were calculated, and differences were analysed using the mean and standard error (SE). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The number of fast breathing cases identified by each method, as well as any changes in classification between the two, were recorded. Cohen’s kappa statistic was used to evaluate the level of agreement between the two methods in identifying fast breathing.

Results

Of the 612 children screened, 339 were recruited for the study. The RR was successfully measured both manually and using the ChARM device in 294 children. RR could not be measured in the remaining children due to a lack of cooperation during assessment, such as excessive movement or crying. In some cases, manual measurement was completed, but the child’s condition changed before ChARM measurement could be performed. The child’s condition remained the same during both measurements in 256 of 294 children. Among these, some video recordings were not interpretable, and the VEP could not reach a consensus on the RR count. Ultimately, the VEP reached a consensus on RR from the video recordings of 217 children, who were included in the final analysis (Fig. 3).

Agreement between two RR measurements from VEP-reviewed videos

The Bland–Altman plot shows agreement between the two RR measurements assessed by the VEP (Fig. 4). The mean difference was 0.5 bpm, indicating that RR measured after attaching ChARM tended to be slightly higher than without ChARM. Most data points clustered around the mean difference line, suggesting a good agreement between the two measurements for the majority of children. A few outliers with larger discrepancies were observed, reflecting some inconsistencies. The limits of agreement (− 11.2 to 12.2 bpm) indicated considerable variability between the two methods.

Effect of attaching the ChARM device on respiratory rate counts and classification

The mean difference in VEP-derived manually counted RR from videos between the two measurements (with and without the ChARM device) was 0.5 bpm, which was not statistically significant (p = 0.216). However, subgroup analysis showed a significantly higher mean RR measured with ChARM compared to without ChARM among children aged 0–2 months (mean difference = 3.7 bpm, p = 0.016), those enrolled at ICMH (mean difference = 2.9 bpm, p = 0.003), and children who were asleep during the measurement (mean difference = 1.9 bpm, p = 0.010) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study explored whether attaching the ChARM device influences RR measurements in children and whether this influence leads to changes in RR classification. The findings indicate that, overall, the application of ChARM did not significantly affect RR counts, as the mean difference between measurements with and without the device was minimal and not statistically significant. However, the fast breathing classification used to determine clinical decisions such as the presence of fast breathing changed in 9.7% of children following the attachment of ChARM, with a moderate agreement (kappa = 0.749) between classifications.

These results are consistent with those from Helldén et al., who reported a 5% change in RR classification in a similar study conducted in Ethiopia [19]. Although the classification shift observed in our study was slightly higher, it remained within the comparable range. The marginal increase in RR following ChARM attachment may be attributed to the physical disturbance or discomfort experienced by the child during device application. As ChARM is a contact-based tool that requires attachment to the child’s body, brief discomfort or agitation, particularly in younger or unwell children, cannot be entirely excluded. Moreover, the magnitude of the observed differences was small, and this study did not provide sufficient evidence to conclude that the ChARM attachment itself causes a meaningful change in RR. The substantial within-child variability observed across repeated measurements highlights the inherent difficulty of obtaining exact and reproducible RR counts in sick children, regardless of the measurement method. In this context, continued evaluation and refinement of both contact-based and alternative RR measurement approaches may help improve feasibility and reliability under routine clinical conditions, rather than implying superiority of any single method.

It is also important to consider that natural within-child variability in RR is non-negligible and may have influenced our findings. Although contextual factors within health facilities, such as noise, handling, and child behaviour, can contribute to transient changes in RR, the study protocol itself may also have played a role. In this study, RR was measured once for each condition (manual measurement and after ChARM attachment), which limits the ability to distinguish true effects of device attachment from inherent short-term fluctuations in RR. Repeated or longer-duration measurements before and after attachment would have provided a more robust assessment by averaging out this natural variability. Similar observations have been reported by Lanaspa et al. and Muro et al., both of whom found that although RR measurements were generally reliable, classification errors occurred in approximately 5–10% of cases due to natural variability between repeated measurements [20, 21]. This point should be considered when interpreting the observed classification changes in the present study.

Among children who were asleep during the initial measurement, a relatively larger increase in RR was observed after device attachment. This may be due to partial arousal during attachment, as even minimal handling can transiently increase RR without overt distress. Alternatively, the finding may reflect natural RR variability during sleep–wake transitions rather than a true device effect, and should be interpreted cautiously given the single measurements per condition.

This study had several notable strengths. First, it was conducted across different tiers of healthcare facilities in Bangladesh, including CCs, and higher-level referral centers, allowing for the inclusion of a broad range of clinical presentations, from non-severe to severe cases. Second, RR was assessed using a systematic and standardized process through a trained VEP to provide reliable measurements.

However, this study had some limitations. A key challenge was the inability to assess RR in some children due to non-cooperation, such as crying or excessive movement, which affected both manual and ChARM measurements. In several instances, although RR was successfully recorded during manual measurement, subsequent changes in the child’s condition, such as restlessness or agitation, prevented RR measurement after ChARM attachment. Additionally, VEP could not interpret RR in some video recordings because of poor cooperation or movement artifacts. This reduced the number of cases included in the final analysis. Furthermore, the exact time interval between ChARM attachment and the start of RR measurement was not systematically recorded, limiting our ability to assess whether any transient effects of device attachment persisted. The exclusion of these children and the absence of timing information may have led to selection bias, which may have influenced the study’s reliability and limited the generalizability of its findings.

Conclusions

Although a small number of children experienced changes in the RR classification following ChARM application, the clinical significance of these changes remains unclear and should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, this study does not provide sufficient evidence to conclude that the device itself causes a physiological change in a child’s RR. Moreover, exclusions due to unsuccessful or uninterpretable measurements may have introduced bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Further studies are required to address these issues and determine whether such misclassifications influence clinical decision-making and outcomes in real-world healthcare settings.
