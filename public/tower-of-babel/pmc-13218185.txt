Chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions in primary healthcare settings: a scoping review
Authors: Catherina Elizabeth Naude, Corlia Janse Van Vuuren, Robyn Smith, Anthea Jansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis scoping review intended to map the existing literature on chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions in primary healthcare (PHC) settings, with a particular focus on intervention dosage—frequency, intensity, type and duration—as well as the mode of delivery, which encompasses intervention location, format and provider.DesignA scoping review was conducted following the guidelines outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis.Data sourcesA comprehensive search of 16 databases was conducted on the EBSCOhost platform on 10 February 2022. A supplementary Google search was conducted to identify grey literature up to 19 November 2022.Eligibility criteriaSources published in English between 2010 and 2022 with full-text availability, addressing interventions for chronic stroke survivors delivered by various stakeholders in PHC, community or home-based settings.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened the identified sources to determine eligibility for inclusion. Data were extracted independently, compared between reviewers, analysed and synthesised narratively with descriptive statistics.ResultsIn total 34 sources are included in the review, with most (n=28) originating from high-income countries. Interventions predominantly targeted the body function and structure component of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health framework (n=29) through physical exercise (n=21). However, few interventions addressed the activity (n=17) and participation (n=11) components. Contextual factors (n=9) were seldom considered in intervention design. Intervention dosage varied widely. Most interventions were delivered by the multidisciplinary team (n=9) and were provided either at home (n=16) or in the community (n=13). Individual sessions (n=18) were more commonly offered than group sessions (n=8).ConclusionsThe optimal dosage for chronic stroke interventions remains uncertain, and current interventions, along with their modes of delivery, are often misaligned with PHC settings. Further research is essential to establish best practices in both well-resourced and under-resourced environments to address the current evidence gap and to enable the development of effective rehabilitation protocols that meet the needs of chronic stroke survivors and their families in PHC settings.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a renewed global focus on universal access to healthcare. The WHO defines universal health coverage (UHC) as the ability to access a full range of quality health services wherever and whenever they are needed, without financial hardship being experienced.1 Successful UHC depends on a robust primary healthcare (PHC) system, where the overarching goal is to bring healthcare services closer to communities. This is achieved through person-centred care and community engagement to ensure ‘health for all’.28

The WHO recognises the critical role of rehabilitation in the continuum of care and advocates for its integration into UHC to enhance health outcomes.9 Considering that, today, over 1 billion people are living with disabilities, access to rehabilitation services is essential to ensure optimal health outcomes.10 11 However, people with disabilities are often inadequately served by the current UHC framework.9 10 In response to this need, the WHO established the World Rehabilitation Alliance to strengthen the Rehabilitation 2030 action plan, which is a call for global action to strengthen health systems by emphasising the necessity of rehabilitation services as an integral component of healthcare for people living with disabilities.1 2 12 The WHO urges all healthcare stakeholders to incorporate rehabilitation in UHC by ensuring equitable access, building a robust multidisciplinary rehabilitation workforce and enhancing health information systems through the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework.9

The ICF provides a conceptual framework and common language to define and measure function and disability and integrating both the medical and social models while also considering the individual and their environment’s influence on function.13 The ICF framework can guide the identification of functional goals and can be used as an aid to develop treatment and rehabilitation plans for conditions such as stroke.1315 Stroke is one of the leading causes of disability globally, and the need for rehabilitation to promote recovery is evident.16 17

Despite the potential of multidisciplinary rehabilitation services to promote recovery after a stroke,16 many stroke survivors face challenges in accessing adequate rehabilitation in PHC, which can lead to lower quality of life and worse health outcomes.18 19 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the scarcity of resources, including insufficient rehabilitation facilities and personnel, poses a considerable barrier to implementing stroke rehabilitation guidelines.2022 Despite the increasing emphasis on developing rehabilitation guidelines for stroke survivors in LMICs, these guidelines predominantly focus on acute and subacute stroke survivors, leaving a gap in rehabilitation strategies and interventions for chronic stroke, particularly in the PHC setting.16 21 23 Moreover, these guidelines are usually developed in high-income countries and may not necessarily be applicable in LMICs, which limits their applicability to broader stroke rehabilitation.19 This challenge makes the implementation of stroke rehabilitation guidelines particularly complex, especially for chronic stroke rehabilitation in PHC.

Given recognised economic and resource constraints in LMICs, developing cost-effective, low-resource and appropriate rehabilitation guidelines will be critical to achieving the goals of the WHO Rehabilitation 2023 action plan, and to ultimately ensure UHC.21 24 25

Objective

This scoping review aimed to comprehensively identify, evaluate and synthesise literature on chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions in PHC settings. The review focused, specifically, on intervention dosage, including type (focus and content), duration and frequency, along with the mode of delivery, which encompasses intervention location, provider and format.

Methods

A scoping review protocol was developed following the guidelines outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis.26 27 Reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist (online supplemental appendix A).28

Eligibility criteria

The population included chronic stroke survivors of all ages, their family members and/or caregivers and all stakeholders providing rehabilitation services or interventions to these individuals. The concept comprised chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions or programmes developed and implemented in the context of a PHC and/or community and/or home settings, in both the public and private health sectors. Interventions could be provided/delivered by any member of the multidisciplinary rehabilitation team, community health workers, mid-level rehabilitation workers, caregivers and/or family members. The type of sources of evidence included peer-reviewed articles and grey literature, such as dissertations/theses, articles and reports on websites relating to chronic stroke interventions. To reflect current global trends in rehabilitation services, full-text quantitative, qualitative and systematic review study design types were included, published in English between 2010 and 2022. Included sources were supplemented by a secondary search of reference lists to identify any additional sources meeting the inclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria

Conference abstracts and editorials were excluded, as were all sources that did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. Interventions relating to computerised technology, telehealth, virtual reality and robotics were excluded, as they may not be readily available, feasible or accessible to stroke survivors in a PHC setting in LMICs, mainly due to financial constraints.

Search strategy

The researcher partnered with an information librarian, who assisted in developing the search strategy, identifying search strings and running the search. The iterative literature searches were conducted between February and November 2022. The search aimed to identify both published and unpublished literature. A preliminary search on the topic was done using the EBSCOhost platform. The words in the title and abstract and index terms were analysed to identify keywords for the second search, and the following search terms were compiled (online supplemental appendix B): ti (stroke or CVA or “cerebrovascular accident*” or “cerebrovascular incident*” or “cerebral vascular accident” or post-stroke) and (patient* or survivor* or family or families or child* or spous* or wife* or husband* or care*) and ti (rehab* or interven* or program* or therap*) and (PHC or “primary health*” or “primary care” or community-based or home-based). A comprehensive search was done across 16 databases, including Academic Search Ultimate, Africa-Wide Information, APA PsycArticles, APA PsycInfo, CAB Abstracts, CINAHL with Full Text, Communication and Mass Media Complete, ERIC, GreenFILE, Health Source – Consumer Edition, Health Source: Nursing/Academic Edition, Humanities Source Ultimate, MEDLINE, OpenDissertations, Sociology Source Ultimate, SPORTDiscus with Full-Text. Duplicate articles were removed through the EBSCOhost automatic system deduplication. Another search was done to locate grey literature, and the identified keywords were used to conduct a systematic Google search (online supplemental appendix B). The reference lists of all the included literature were also screened to identify additional sources.

A pilot study was conducted to test the screening procedures, the data extraction process and the completeness of the data extraction tables. During the pilot study, the search terms and Excel scoping review tables were tested with 43 sources. Based on the results, the search terms were refined to ensure a comprehensive literature search. The final scoping review included only sources retrieved using refined search terms, excluding those of the pilot study.

A physiotherapist (AV) and an occupational therapist (LS) were selected as the second and third reviewers, based on their knowledge of the topic, thereby broadening the scope of the review process and enhancing the depth of expertise. Both reviewers were trained by the researcher (CEN) on the scoping review process, procedures and the completion of the study record and data extraction table.

Source selection and screening

Sources identified in published and grey literature were, first, screened manually to remove any duplicates that had not been removed by the automated search system. The systematic process was followed, by screening the identified literature sources by title, then abstract and, finally, by full text. Literature sources were included according to established inclusion and exclusion criteria. All screened literature sources were recorded in a study record table, as were the reasons for excluding sources, for reporting purposes.

Two reviewers independently screened the identified literature sources. Their screening results were compared, and where consensus was lacking on the inclusion or exclusion of a source, they discussed it to reach agreement. Where consensus could not be achieved, a third independent reviewer was consulted to make a final decision on inclusion or exclusion.

Data extraction, charting and analysis

During the full-text review, data relevant to the identified data points were extracted from the included sources and manually recorded in a data extraction table that had been developed by the researcher. Each reviewer charted the data independently, whereafter the charted data were compared. Any discrepancies in the data extracted were discussed and addressed. Published and grey literature sources were excluded if it became evident that they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Data were extracted using a data extraction table (online supplemental appendix C), which was refined through an iterative process to comprehensively chart chronic stroke intervention components, thereby allowing for more focused data synthesis to facilitate the reporting of results. The ICF framework was used to interpret the intervention components.

Quality of the sources of evidence

Although formal critical appraisal is not required for scoping reviews, documenting the quality of sources contributes to methodological rigour and enhances transparency and enables readers to better evaluate the robustness of the review conclusions. The quality of the included sources was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool V.2018 for information professionals and researchers.29 30 However, no sources were excluded based on the outcome of the appraisal, which meant all available evidence was charted.

Data synthesis

The extracted data were summarised and described narratively according to the objectives of the review. Reported results are supplemented by tables and graphs. Descriptive statistics were calculated for pooled data, including means with ranges for continuous data and frequencies with percentages for categorical data.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Results on chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions in a PHC setting will be presented under included sources, study and population characteristics, quality of the sources included and chronic stroke interventions.

Included sources

A total of 709 sources were identified and screened: 692 were from databases and registers, 5 were from systematic internet searches, 4 were from organisations and 8 were from secondary searches of included reference lists. After the full-text review, only 34 sources were included in the scoping review. The main reason for excluding sources was non-compliance with the inclusion criteria (figure 1).

Study and population characteristics

The distribution of included sources across countries ranged in terms of geographical location, country income level (based on the 2025 World Bank country classification31) and UHC index scores (obtained as supplemental data according to the reported study location32). The highest number of sources were from Asia (n=10), while the fewest were from Africa (n=2). One source reported findings for multiple continents, namely Asia, Australia, Europe and North America. Most of the sources (n=28) reported on studies in high-income countries that had a UHC index of 80 or higher. In contrast, only three sources were from LMICs with UHC index scores ranging from 38 to 65. Two sources were from upper-middle-income countries with UHC index scores of 71 and 76, respectively (figure 2).

Global distribution of sources by World Bank Income Classification, UHC Service Coverage Index, ICF-mapped interventions and mode of delivery (n=33). ICF, International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health; UHC, universal health coverage.

Most studies are experimental (n=23), four are qualitative studies (n=4), three are systematic or literature reviews (n=3) and two are descriptive studies (n=2). Two sources did not specify the study design. Nine sources had been published within the last 5 years (n=9). A total of 3316 stroke survivors participated in the interventions across the included sources, of whom 1843 (55.6%) were male. Not all studies reported sufficient data to calculate both average age and time post stroke. Sample sizes and gender distribution reported refer only to intervention groups in experimental studies; control data were excluded. Average ages are reported only when both mean and SD were available (online supplemental appendix D). As a result, the number of participants included in each calculation differs. Based on the experimental designs that reported age, the average age of participants (n=3149) was 61.61 years (95% CI 59.66 to 65.57). In a smaller subset of studies (n=3 029) that reported time since stroke, the mean time post stroke was 1.31 years (95% CI 0.60 to 2.01), of these participants, 59% (95% CI 53% to 65%) were male (see online supplemental table S1.)

Quality of the included sources

The methodological quality of 29 sources was appraised, with most sources (n=23) having acceptable methodological quality (online supplemental table S1, online supplemental appendix E). Quality criteria included the appropriateness of the methodology, completeness of data, adequacy of data analysis and the alignment of findings and conclusions with study data. As stated, no sources were excluded based on methodological quality.

Chronic stroke interventions

Chronic stroke interventions includes aspects relating to intervention dosage and mode of delivery.

Dosage of chronic stroke interventions

Dosage of stroke intervention refers to the type (focus and content of the intervention), the duration (how long) and frequency (how often).

Type of interventions

Focus of the interventions

The focus of chronic stroke interventions, in terms of the ICF components, varied. Most interventions were found to be impairment-driven, relating to the body functions and structure component of the ICF (n=29). Half the interventions (n=17) involved functional activities and tasks and activities of daily living relating to the activity component of the ICF (n=17). Fewer than half of the interventions (n=11) included social and community participation, which relate to the participation component of the ICF. Few authors contextualised their interventions by considering personal factors (n=6) and the environment (n=3). No source covered all ICF components, nine covered three components and one covered four (online supplemental table S1, figures2 3).

Focus of chronic stroke interventions in the context of the ICF framework (n=34). ICF, International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health.

Content of the interventions

Impairments were addressed through physical exercises ranging from upper (n=13) and lower-limb exercises (n=14), balance (n=10) and coordination training (n=1) and techniques such as constraint-induced movement therapy (n=7) and mirror therapy (n=2). The nature of the exercises varied from aerobic exercises (n=8) and strength training (n=11) to progressive resistance exercises (n=2), stretches (n=5), active range of movement (ROM) (n=2) and passive ROM (n=5). Circuit training was used (n=3), and some sources made use of warm-up (n=5) and cool-down activities (n=2). Three sources (n=3) failed to provide details of the type of physical exercises that were used.

A variety of exercises and/or activities, including quizzes, word puzzles, numeracy, memory, executive functioning, crafts, following commands, naming, sound breakdown, fluency of speech, non-speech oro-motor exercises and behavioural speech therapy, were included to address cognitive (n=4) and speech (n=3) impairments. Interventions relating to activity included activities of daily living (n=9), functional (n=8) and task-oriented training (n=4). Interventions relating to participation included instrumental activities of daily living (n=5) and life events, such as group and social activities and leisure (n=8).

Lastly, contextual factors, such as handling principles and overcoming environmental barriers (n=3), uptake of self-management programmes, including personal goal setting (n=4) and psychosocial support (n=4) were rarely considered and addressed in the interventions. Few sources included education (n=9), covering health education, caregiver training to promote independence and education to implement interventions at home.

Overall duration of the interventions

For studies that reported the minimum duration of a chronic stroke intervention (n=26), the average overall minimum duration was 11.9 weeks (SD=11.81). Studies that lacked this information were excluded from the calculation (online supplemental appendix F).

Time/duration of the interventions

Inconsistencies were observed in the reporting of intervention duration across studies, and not all studies reported the duration of their interventions. Studies lacking clear time frames were excluded from the following calculation (online supplemental appendix F). The overall reported average minimum duration per intervention session (n=27) was 94.6 min (SD=96.43).

Frequency of the intervention

Frequency data were not uniformly interpretable and are, therefore, not presented in detail. This is the result of inconsistent reporting of intervention frequency across studies, ranging from daily sessions to sessions several times per week, week or month.

Mode of delivery of chronic stroke interventions

The mode of delivery refers to the intervention location, format and provider (figure 2).

Location

Just more than half the interventions were delivered at home (n=16) or at a local community centre (n=13) while, in some cases, the intervention was offered both at home and at a local community venue, including health and rehabilitation centres, churches and an auditorium (n=4).

Provider

Interventions tended to be delivered by one or more members of the multidisciplinary team (MDT) (n=9) or the MDT member(s) with the caregivers (n=9). The MDT included physical, occupational and/or speech therapists, social workers, nurses, physicians and/or nutritionists. Furthermore, interventions were also delivered by exercise instructors on their own (n=4) or in combination with a physiotherapist and/or an occupational therapist (n=3). Caregivers were the sole intervention providers in only two instances (n=2). Lay workers such as village health volunteers provided the intervention in only one instance (n=1), while peer-delivered intervention was noted in another instance (n=1). Fewer than a quarter of the interventions (n=8) included training of lay persons (such as caregivers and volunteers) to deliver the intervention at home, with a training manual/guide only being provided in four instances (n=4).

Format

Individual sessions (n=18) were more commonly offered than group sessions (n=8). Some interventions included both individual and group sessions (n=7).

Discussion

Data on dosage (focus, type, duration and frequency) and mode of delivery (location, provider and format) of chronic stroke interventions in a PHC setting were extracted and synthesised from 34 sources. Most of the included sources were found to have acceptable methodological quality, and a high number of studies were experimental, which enhances the strength of the evidence presented. However, many of the included studies were pilot studies or had small samples, which makes drawing definitive conclusions on the effective design of chronic stroke interventions in terms of dosage and mode of delivery more difficult, as the interventions only lasted for the duration of the studies. Most of the interventions were delivered in the Global North in high-income countries with established healthcare systems and high levels of health coverage. It is alarming that reported interventions were limited in LMICs, such as South Africa, where health coverage is poorer, despite most stroke survivors being found in these settings.33 A possible explanation for the skewed geographical representation of the sources in this review could be the absence of rehabilitation protocols, inadequate availability of and less established rehabilitation services in LMICs.21 23 The limited sources that could be included affirm that, worldwide, the focus in stroke rehabilitation remains largely on the acute and subacute phases of stroke recovery, which emphasises the gap in knowledge on chronic stroke rehabilitation.34 This gap is also likely to skew the data on dosage and mode of delivery, leaving a gap in knowledge and understanding of chronic stroke intervention in LMIC contexts.

There was considerable variability in the intervention dosage in terms of type, time and frequency. Intervention types were grouped according to the ICF components, because of its common language, to facilitate health and goals set for stroke survivors.14 Types of chronic stroke interventions in PHC were largely impairment-driven, with a strong emphasis on addressing aspects related to body functions and body structures. The interventions were also fragmented and, while many of these interventions relate to physical exercises, no consensus could be drawn on the type, duration or frequency of exercises that need to be presented to chronic stroke survivors in PHC. The heterogeneity observed in intervention dosage may partly reflect differences in how rehabilitation services are organised and resourced across regions and healthcare systems, particularly in the management of long-term disability, highlighting the need for more coordinated and integrated approaches.35 Despite the call for integrated approaches, participation and contextual factors were rarely considered and included in chronic stroke interventions. This is likely because of the physical impairments resulting from limitations in body functions and body structures after a stroke.36 A recent scoping review on occupational therapy stroke rehabilitation protocols echoes these findings, and refers to an emphasis on body functions and body structures, the failure to include contextual factors and the need for research to determine long-term outcomes, including optimal duration and intensity of interventions.37 These findings contrast recommendations that emphasise empowering stroke survivors through lifestyle modification and self-management approaches, which enhance physical activity and support secondary stroke prevention.38 39 Findings further stand in contrast with calls for participation-oriented therapy and social integration, which emphasise the need for interventions for activity, participation and psychosocial components.244042 Fragmented interventions that fail to address all the components of the ICF and empower the stroke survivor may lead to poor social integration, thereby increasing the risk of isolation and reducing the quality of life of chronic stroke survivors, and strengthens the call for integrated services that ensure patient-centred care through a multidisciplinary network that links health and social care.14

Furthermore, the mode of delivery of chronic stroke interventions in PHC often fails to meet the recommendations for integrated services. While home and community-based interventions align with recent guidelines for longer-term stroke rehabilitation in LMICs,36 not all the findings are suited for these settings in PHC. Despite the variety of providers, including professionals and lay persons, the interventions provided were largely fragmented and profession-bound, with a high number of individual interventions. This may be the result of the high number of high-income countries included in the review, the more readily available resources and the better-established rehabilitation services in these countries.23 Individual, profession-bound interventions may not be the most suitable for LMICs, such as South Africa, because of resource constraints, including inadequate rehabilitation staff and services, which may require a different approach to rendering rehabilitation to chronic stroke survivors in PHC.21 23 43 44 Adding to this complexity, stroke survivors in LMICs face barriers such as transportation challenges, financial constraints and a lack of formal caregivers,21 which influence the mode of delivery required. The limited inclusion of task shifting, caregiver-led rehabilitation and family and caregiver training is concerning and stands in contrast to advocacy for the inclusion and training of family members and caregivers in sustained, long-term rehabilitation.36 Task shifting shows promise to overcome the barriers to accessible and continued rehabilitation for chronic stroke survivors in LMICs in PHC, and further research is recommended to strengthen its evidence base.16 21 36 45

This review found under-representation of reported interventions in LMICs, as well as a shortage of guidance and consensus on designing best-evidence chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions for LMICs in PHC settings, despite calls for continued rehabilitation in the chronic phase after a stroke.1436 4648 Evidence-based strategies for designing optimal dosage and modes of delivery for chronic stroke interventions in both resourced and under-resourced settings remain uncertain. In addition, current practices related to mode of delivery are often misaligned with the principles of PHC, and the effectiveness of task shifting should be explored further. More research on chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions for LMICs in PHC is recommended to achieve the objectives of the WHO’s Rehabilitation 2030 Action Plan and to ensure universal rehabilitation access to chronic stroke survivors.

Limitations

This review had a small sample. The evidence base was not updated beyond the original search period, and therefore, the findings may not reflect the most current literature. The inclusion of grey literature may have influenced the evidence base and introduced some uncertainty in the findings. Differences in reported interventions can be attributed to the individual objectives of the included studies and their variable methodologies and study designs. Not all authors used standardised reporting frameworks for intervention studies, which challenged data extraction. The lack of longer-term outcomes, failure to chart impact and feasibility of the interventions, reliance on research-led short-term interventions, a language bias and the limited availability of grey literature were also limitations. LMICs and African studies are, furthermore, under-represented, and the high number of studies that were undertaken in high-income countries could have skewed the results on the type of interventions being provided, especially in the context of LMICs.

Recommendations

Despite limitations, this review represents a valuable contribution, as one of the first to systematically map chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions in PHC and community contexts. Further research is recommended to establish the best evidence of interventions for chronic stroke rehabilitation in PHC regarding dosage and mode of delivery. It is recommended that research is also undertaken in PHC in LMICs to ensure the design of contextually relevant chronic stroke rehabilitation interventions. In addition, the best practice models for PHC should be explored and task shifting and caregiver-led rehabilitation stand central.

Conclusion

The optimal dosage for chronic stroke interventions remains uncertain, and current interventions, along with their modes of delivery, are often misaligned in PHC settings. Further research is essential to establish best practices in both well-resourced and under-resourced environments to address the current evidence gap and enable the development of effective rehabilitation protocols that meet the needs of survivors of chronic stroke and their families in PHC settings.
