Neurosymbolic Decision Trees
Matthias Möller, Arvid Norlander, Pedro Zuidberg Dos Martires, Luc De Raedt

Abstract
NeSy ( NeSy ) AI studies the integration of NN and symbolic reasoning based on logic. Usually, NeSy techniques focus on learning the neural, probabilistic and/or fuzzy parameters of NeSy models. Learning the symbolic or logical structure of such models has, so far, received less attention. We introduce NLDT , as an extension of decision trees together with a novel NeSy structure learning algorithm, which we dub NeuID3. NeuID3 adapts the standard top-down induction of decision tree algorithms and combines it with a neural probabilistic logic representation, inherited from the DeepProbLog family of models. The key advantage of learning NLDT with NeuID3 is the support of both symbolic and subsymbolic data (such as images), and that they can exploit background knowledge during the induction of the tree structure, In our experimental evaluation we demonstrate the benefits of NeSy structure learning over more traditonal approaches such as purely data-driven learning with neural networks.

We first introduce the necessary concepts and notation for propositional logic programming. Then we describe Boolean decision trees and their probabilistic extension in terms of (probabilistic) logic programming

2.2
Probabilistic Boolean Decision Tree

So far we have assumed that all tests in
\cal F
are deterministically true or false.
We now lift this restriction by adopting probabilistic logic instead and use the concept of a probabilistic fact
[
15
,
8
]
. Probabilistic facts generalize (non-probabilistic) facts in that they allow atoms to be true with a probability. In a logic program we write such probabilistic facts as annotated facts
p_{i}\probcoco f_{i}
stating that
f_{i}
is true with probability
p_{i}
. Consequently, a (non-probabilistic) fact is a probabilistic fact annotated with probability
1.0
.
To incorporate this idea in a
BDT
, we render all tests (i.e., internal nodes) probabilistic.
We exemplify this in
Figure 2

Similar to
BDT
, a probabilistic Boolean Decision Tree can be evaluated with probabilistic logic. Formally, a probabilistic logic program consists of a set of rules
R
and a set of probabilistic facts
\mathcal{PF}
of the form
p_{i}\probcoco f_{i}
.
Probabilistic logic programs induce a probability distribution on the set of interpretations of the logic theory, i.e. the set of its possible worlds, cf.
[
8
]
.
Probabilistic logic programs can be viewed as expressive probabilistic graphical models.
Indeed, the probabilistic facts are marginally independent just like the nodes without parents in a Bayesian network, and dependencies are introduced through the deterministic rules
[
7
]
.
For our purposes, it suffices to define the probability
P(q)
of an atom
q
in a logic program
L=R\cup\mathcal{PF}
consisting of rules
R
and probabilistic facts
\mathcal{PF}=\{p_{1}\probcoco f_{1},\dots,p_{n}::f_{n}\}
.

We define the probability of a subset
M\subseteq\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},...,f_{n}\}
containing probabilistic facts as

\displaystyle P(M)

\displaystyle=\prod_{f_{i}\in M}p_{i}\prod_{f_{i}\in\mathcal{PF}\backslash M}(1-p_{i}),

(1)

which we then use to define the probability of a query
q

\displaystyle P(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\cup L\models q}P(M).

(2)

Computing the probability in Eq.

(
WMC
)
[
6
]
. Probabilistic logic programming languages such as ProbLog
[
8
,
15
]
address this problem through knowledge compilation where the logic is compiled into so-called arithmetic circuits
[
6
]
, i.e. hierarchically structured product and sum operations) that allow for inference in polytime in size of the circuit.
Although

is in general a computationally hard problem (#P-complete), the probability of a query to a
PDT
(
PDT

3.1
Probabilistic Neural Decision Trees

We now present a neural extension that allows for the probabilities of specific facts to depend on a specific example. The key idea is simple, and is based on the neural extension introduced in DeepProbLog
[
27
,
26
]
:
instead of using unconditional probabilities on the probabilistic facts, we now use conditional probabilities parametrized using neural nets.
More formally, a (probabilistic) neural fact is an expression of the form
nn(\mathbf{x})::f
,
where
nn
is a neural network receiving input
\mathbf{x}
. It returns the probability that
f
holds given the input
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.
P(f\mid\mathbf{x})
. Neural facts, when evaluated, are interpreted as conditional probabilistic facts.
As such, we can also interpret probabilistic facts as neural facts with an empty conditioning set. Thereby neural facts strictly generalize probabilistic facts (and deterministic ones).

As an example, consider again example
e_{1}
and the

introduced in

burglary
and
earthquake
with neural ones whose probabilities are predicted by the two

and

3.2
Neurosymbolic Background Knowledge

One distinguishing feature of
NeSy
is its ability to incorporate background knowledge.
We allow for this in
NLDT
by extending the set of possible tests with neurosymbolic rules. For ease of exposition, we introduce neurosymbolic background knowledge by means of an example.

Consider a classifier that is given two images of playing cards as input and has to decide which of the two cards has the higher rank.
The task then boils down to correctly classifying each card and comparing their ranks. We can readily encode this problem using the concept of a neural predicate
[
26
]
.

\displaystyle nn(Image,Rank,[1,\ldots,10])::rank(Image,Class).

\displaystyle smaller(Image1,Image2)\leftarrow

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad rank(Image1,Rank1),rank(Image2,Rank2),Rank1<Rank2.

In the first line we have a neural predicate, which encodes a categorical distribution whose events are integer-valued and take values in the
1
to
10
range. The neural predicate hence encodes the probability distribution
P(Rank|Image)
with
Rank\in[1,\cdots,10]
(assuming we limit ourselves to numeral cards).
We then use the neural predicate in the body of the rule in the second line, where we let the neural predicate predict the ranks of the two cards and symbolically compare them. The head of the rule
smaller(Image1,Image2)
is then true if the comparison in the body evaluates to true. As the predictions of the neural predicate are probabilistic, the comparison of the two predicted ranks is probabilistic, which means that the rule is probabilistically true or false as well.
We can then use these probabilistic rules (in combination with neural predicates) as tests in neurosymbolic decision trees. We refer the reader to
[
26
,
27
]
for a detailed account of neural rules and predicates, and their use in background knowledge.

4
Learning Neurosymbolic Decision Trees

Assume that we are given a set of examples each labeled with either

or

As we would like to learn a neurosymbolic model the examples consist of Boolean and subsymbolic features (e.g. images).
In the context of neurosymbolic logic programming, Boolean features correspond to logical facts and subsymbolic features can be used in the conditioning sets of neural facts and neural predicates.

Learning an

This stands in contrast to many other

[
26
]
and NeurASP
[
39
]

\displaystyle CE(N,\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}\underbrace{\Bigl[log\bigl(P\left(\tau\mid N.\kappa,e\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{l_{e}=pos}\Bigr]}_{ce_{pos}(N,e,\tau)}+

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}\underbrace{\Bigl[log\bigl(P\left(\neg\tau\mid N.\kappa,e\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{l_{e}=neg}\Bigr]}_{ce_{neg}(N,e,\tau)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}\left[ce_{pos}(N,e,\tau)+ce_{neg}(N,e,\tau)\right],

where we compute
P\left(N.\kappa\land\tau\mid e\right)
and
P\left(N.\kappa\land\neg\tau\mid e\right)
using oracle calls to DeepProbLog, and where
\mathbbm{1}_{l_{e}=pos}
indicates whether the label of an example is
pos
or not (analogous for
\mathbbm{1}_{l_{e}=neg}
). We also explicitly indicate with a subscript on
\mathcal{E}_{N}
that we only take into consideration the subset of examples that fall into node
N
after applying the filtering steps in Lines
1
and
1
in
Algorithm 1
.

However, in the setting of training (neural) tests in an
NLDT
this overlooks two issues. First, examples only fall fraction-wise into the lower nodes due to probabilistic splitting. Second, with an increasing depth class imbalances arise because the classification accuracy is improving.
As a consequence, the deeper a test is situated in the tree the more imbalanced the dataset becomes.
We remedy these issues by replacing the standard cross-entropy as used in DeepProbLog with a weighted cross-entropy loss
\widetilde{CE}(N)
when training a test in node
N
.

\displaystyle\widetilde{CE}(N,\tau)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}\left[w_{pos}(N,e)\cdot ce_{pos}(N,e,\tau)+w_{neg}(N,e)\cdot ce_{neg}(N,e,\tau)\right],

where the weights
w_{c}(N,e)
(
c\in\{pos,neg\}
) are defined as follows:

\displaystyle w_{c}(N,e)

\displaystyle=P(N.\kappa\mid e)\cdot\frac{1}{\underset{=2}{\underbrace{|\{pos,neg\}|}}\cdot P(c\mid N.\kappa)}

\displaystyle=P(N.\kappa\mid e)\cdot\frac{1}{2\cdot(N.\delta\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{c=pos}+(1-N.\delta)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{c=neg})}

with
P(N.\kappa\mid e)
being the probability of node
N
being reached given example
e
– thereby handling the issue of fractional examples,
and where the factor

1/(2\cdot P(c\mid N.\kappa))
addresses the issue of a class imbalance of two classes. The computation of the class weights follows the implementation of scikit-learn
[
32
]
and is inspired by
[
21
]
.

4.3
Scoring function

Once the neural tests have been learned we need to pick one test to add to the
NLDT
. To this end, we compute for each test (including non-neural ones) a score. The test with the highest score is then selected and added to the
NLDT
.
In order to select tests to be added, deterministic decision trees commonly use the
IG
(
IG
) of a test
\tau
in node
N

[
5
]
:

\displaystyle IG_{N}(\tau)

\displaystyle=H(Class\mid N.\kappa)

\displaystyle\qquad-\underset{\approx P(\tau\mid N.\kappa)}{\underbrace{\cfrac{|\mathcal{E}_{N}(\tau)|}{|\mathcal{E}_{N}|}}}H(Class\mid N.\kappa\land\tau)-\underset{\approx P(\neg\tau\mid N.\kappa)}{\underbrace{\cfrac{|\mathcal{E}_{N}(\neg\tau)|}{|\mathcal{E}_{N}|}}}H(Class\mid N.\kappa\land\neg\tau),

where
H(Class\mid\cdot)

denotes the conditional entropy of the random variable
Class
and is computed as follows:

\displaystyle H(Class\mid\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\{pos,neg\}}P(Class=c\mid\phi)\log_{2}P(Class=c\mid\phi)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi}\log_{2}\delta_{\phi}+(1-\delta_{\phi})\log_{2}(1-\delta_{\phi}),

with
\phi
denoting a propositional formula and which we approximate using
Equation 6
.
Furthermore, the ratios
\nicefrac{{|\mathcal{E}_{N}(\tau)|}}{{|\mathcal{E}_{N}|}}
and
\nicefrac{{|\mathcal{E}_{N}(\neg\tau)|}}{{|\mathcal{E}_{N}|}}
serve as approximations for
P(\tau\mid N.\kappa)
and
P(\neg\tau\mid N.\kappa)
where:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}(\phi)=\{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}\mid N.\kappa\land\phi\models e\}.

For probabilistic (neural) tests, we replace the relative example counts with probabilities of the examples falling into the respective branches:

\displaystyle\widetilde{IG}_{N}(\tau)

\displaystyle=H(Class\mid N.\kappa)

\displaystyle\quad-\cfrac{S_{N}(\tau)}{S_{N}}H(Class\mid N.\kappa\land\tau)-\cfrac{S_{N}(\neg\tau)}{S_{N}}H(Class\mid N.\kappa\land\neg\tau),

with
S_{N}
and
S_{N}(\cdot)
as

\displaystyle S_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}P\Bigl(N.\kappa\mid e\Bigr)

\displaystyle S_{N}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{N}}P\Bigl(N.\kappa\wedge\phi\mid e\Bigr)\qquad(\phi\in\{\tau,\neg\tau\}).

Our estimations
P(\tau\mid N.\kappa,e)\approx\nicefrac{{S_{N}(\tau)}}{{S_{N}}}
and
P(\neg\tau\mid N.\kappa,e)\approx\nicefrac{{S_{N}(\tau)}}{{S_{N}}}
can be derived following
Equation 6
.
Finally, we define
best_test
for picking the best test in node
N
(c.f. Line
1
in
Algorithm 1
) as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{best\_test}}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{T},N)=\argmax_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{IG}_{N}(\tau).

Note that for the sake of notational clarity, we drop the explicit dependency of
\widetilde{IG}_{N}(\tau)
on
\mathcal{E}
.

We identify five main dimensions along which the different approaches to specifying and learning decision trees can be characterized which we also reflect in our
Table 1
.

1.

Whether a relevant subset of the features is selected by the learner (like in decision trees), or whether the learner always uses the full set of features. While the second option avoids the need to find the best features, it also decreases the interpretability of the model and may require more data.

2.

Whether the tests can be symbolic and/or subsymbolic. In the latter case, the test is carried out as (part of) a neural network.

3.

Whether the structure tree is pre-fixed and provided as input to the learner, or whether the structure itself is learned. By pre-fixing the tree, one only has to optimize the parameters over the whole tree and effectively avoids problems like
weaker splits hiding stronger splits

[
1
]
. On the other hand, those approaches usually also fix aspects related to selecting features at each node, the order of selecting tests, and determining the depth of a tree.

4.

Whether the splits can be used in a probabilistic manner. Probabilistic approaches compute the probability that an example falls into a leaf, and allow to aggregate the probabilities over several leaves to compute the class membership, although some approaches
[
17
,
36
]
select the maximum probability path. Deterministic approaches can only use one single path for predicting.

5.

Whether background knowledge in the form of rules can be used to define and derive further features from the inputs for use by the learner.

We observe in
Table 1
that related work can be distinguished into roughly two categories. The first can be called soft decision trees, which are essentially a type of neural net that is structured as a decision tree and where the tests are softened. This is related to the probabilistic splitting that we discussed.
The second category learns (deterministic)
DT
by reformulating the search over symbolic splits and input features as a parameter learning problem.

From this analysis, it follows that
NLDT
are uniquely positioned in the context of recent related work on decision trees through their distinct neurosymbolic approach.

5.2
Neurosymbolic Learning

We briefly discuss how our work relates to other
NeSy
structure learning approaches. Our discussion is led by the work of
Marra et al. [29]
separating
NeSy
learning works into four classes:

(i)

neurally guided structure search,

(ii)

program sketching,

(iii)

structure learning via parameters,

(iv)

and learning structure implicitly

. Interestingly,
NLDT
together with NeuID3 do not fit in any of these categories and, instead, are a new group that alternates between learning structure and parameters.
We now discuss the differences between NDTs and these classes of related work.

Neurally guided structure search

This class describes approaches
[
23
,
11
]
where structure learning is guided by a function approximator that replaces the typical uninformed search usually found in structure learning. For instance,
NN
can be used to score parts in the search space and accelerate the structure learning, or they can even generate symbolic representations that are added to the theory. These approaches usually require large amounts of data
[
29
,
12
]
. In contrast,
NLDT
are
NeSy
programs with learned parameters and structure but still rely on an exhaustive and uninformed search. Furthermore, neurally guided search has mainly been used to find symbolic structures, and to the best of the authors’ knowledge, not yet to learn neurosymbolic models themselves.

Program sketching

These approaches
[
27
]
usually fix parts the program and keep a few parts open that still need to be learned or filled in. This requires the user to already have an understanding of the target program and only learns the parts of the structure. In contrast,
NLDT
are fully learned from scratch.

Structure learning via parameters

Here, works
[
26
,
13
,
35
]
reformulate the structure learning task into an optimization problem solved by parameter learning.
One example is to start from a set of clauses and learning weights for each of them. While learning the weights (parameters), clauses can become irrelevant (and get low weights), but they will never be removed from the theory which results in noisy theories. In contrast,
NLDT
are induced bottom-up and result in clear theories.

Learning structure implicitly

These approaches
[
28
]
do not learn a human-readable structure of a logic program but instead an implicit neural net modeling relations between data. In contrast,
NLDT
learn human-readable structures.

6.2.3
Discussion

We can now answer our experimental question:
How do
NLDT
compare to
NN
?
.
In our experiments
NLDT
outperform the
NN
and the difference in performance increases when Boolean attributes are replaced by images.
This is especially obvious on the UCI
one-hot
and UCI
MNIST
datasets. The drop in the
NLDT
’ performance from UCI
one-hot
to UCI
MNIST
is relatively low compared with the neural counterpart.
A possible explanation is that our top-down induction of
NLDT
searches for subsets of features and learns to process them separately, while a
NN
has to learn simultaneously on all features.

Next, we discuss the stability in inducing
NLDT
on different data distributions. In our UCI experiment, we observe that the standard deviation of our
NLDT
is quite similar between UCI
one-hot
and UCI
MNIST
. Arguably, replacing Boolean values with simple images has little effect on the induction of
NLDT
. This becomes even more obvious when we consider that this holds on the dermatology dataset with
322
training samples and 34 different attributes and supports the stability of inducing our trees. Our Eleusis experiment supports this observation where most standard deviations are below
5\%
.

With regard to training times, the
NN
clearly outperforms the
NLDT
when trained on the CPU of the same machine
4
4

4

tested on a a

6.3
Experiment: Background Knowledge

In all our prior experiments, NeuID3 only induced
NLDT
from sets of tests containing only neural or symbolic facts, and, therefore, we have not considered neural rules. We address this issue with our experimental question:
How does including tests that implement background knowledge affect the induction of
NLDT
?
.

We chose Eleusis as the setting for our experiment and learn each hidden concept from the previous experiment by inducing
NLDT
from four different classes of
\mathcal{T}
:

1.

Our first class
\mathcal{T}^{nf}
only uses two neural facts that depend on either the suits or the ranks of a card as tests:

\displaystyle nn_{r}(ImRank1,ImRank2)

\displaystyle::rel\_rank(ImRank1,ImRank2).

\displaystyle nn_{s}(ImSuit1,ImSuit2)

\displaystyle::rel\_suit(ImSuit1,ImSuit2).

where each probability is computed by a
NN
that receives both images as input.
We provide a further illustration in
Figure 4
.
\mathcal{T}^{nf}
does not depend on the hidden concept that shall be learned.

2.

The class
\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{c}
assigns for each concept
c
a set of tests which we designed with the intent that NeuID3 only has to learn how to combine the tests and to train them to learn
c
. For that purpose, we formulate
NeSy
rules that can express the hidden concept on a symbolic level. For instance, assume the hidden concept alternate_faces:

\displaystyle faces(Card_{0})\not=faces(Card_{1}).

that states that two subsequent cards’ ranks need to alternate between face cards and non-face cards. To learn this concept, we only define one
NeSy
rule as test.

\displaystyle alternate\_attr

\displaystyle(ImRank1,ImRank2)\leftarrow

\displaystyle\phantom{\leftarrow}\quad rank\_attribute(ImRank1,Attr1),

\displaystyle\phantom{\leftarrow}\quad rank\_attribute(ImRank2,Attr2),

\displaystyle\phantom{\leftarrow}\quad Attr1\not=Attr2

where
rank\_attribute
is a neural predicate that has to be learned that maps an image (i.e.
ImRank1
) to an Boolean attribute (i.e.
Attr2
). The set of tests will be:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{alternate\_faces}=\{alternate\_attr\}

Here, NeuID3 has only one test to choose from which simplifies the task to learning the neural predicate
alternate\_attr
.
Another example can be seen in
Figure 3
where the hidden concept is a conjunction of two tests that are in the set of tests.

3.

\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{\cup c}
constructs the set of tests as

\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{\cup c}{}=\bigcup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{c}

with
\mathcal{C}
being the set of hidden concepts.
\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{\cup c}
contains all untrained tests defined in our class
\mathcal{T}^{opt}
and is the most exhaustive set in this experiment. The challenge for NeuID3 is to detect which untrained tests are useful to learn the hidden concept.

4.

For each hidden concept
c
, we define
\mathcal{T}^{bad}_{c}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{bad}_{c}=\Bigl(\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{\cup c}{}\cup\mathcal{T}^{nf}\Bigr)\setminus\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{c}.

More intuitively, we start from
\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{\cup c}
and remove the tests that we designed to learn the specific hidden concept
c
. We intend the remaining tests to either be neural rules that are
bad
for learning
c
or neural facts. This set of tests is used to assess whether NeuID3 would avoid
bad
neural rules and compensate them with neural facts.

Discussion

W.r.t. our second experimental question:
How does including tests that implement background knowledge affect the induction of
NLDT
?
,
we observe that
\mathcal{T}^{opt}
- as the bool that contains background knowledge designed for each rule - receives the best results. From our experiments, we conclude that

providing appropriate background knowledge for inducing a concept leads to a better induction of better
NLDT
.

The positive effect of providing useful background knowledge is also underlined by the fact that the results on
\mathcal{T}^{opt}_{\cup c}
are similar to
\mathcal{T}^{opt}
but outperform
\mathcal{T}^{nf}
.

Another aspect that we identified is that
NLDT
appear to be reliable in not using tests whose background knowledge is counter-productive for learning a hidden concept. In
\mathcal{T}^{bad}
, NeuID3 chose most of the time a neural atom and avoided other tests. Consequently, our tree learner appears to be successful in recognizing when a new relationship has to be learned from scratch, which is not formulated in our background knowledge, yet.

6.4
Experiment: Reuse Neural Tests

In this experiment, we address our third experimental question:
How can we reuse trained neural tests to induce more complex rules?
. For that purpose, we use our insights from the previous experiment. Inducing
NLDT
for the hidden concept hidden_order_simple has shown relatively poor results. However, this hidden concept is a disjunction of the two hidden concepts suit_order and rank_order.

In this experiment, we reuse the neural tests received from suit_order and rank_order to induce
NLDT
where we

(i)

use no trained neural tests,

(ii)

reuse one neural test received from training suit_order or rank_order,

(iii)

or use both trained neural tests.

We induce each
NLDT
with the same seven seeds as in the previous experiment.

Results

Our results are shown in
Table 6
. The results show that including one neural test leads to better results than not using one at all. Using both trained tests leads to the best results. Further, we observe differences in performance depending on which trained neural test we use. Reusing the neural test from rank_order leads to better results and shows similar results as when we reuse both neural tests.

Discussion

We now discuss our experimental question:
How can we reuse trained neural tests to induce more complex rules?
.
The results show that adding trained neural tests can be beneficial for inducing a new hidden concept. Especially noticable is the effect of adding the neural test received from rank_order which receives similar results to using both trained neural tests. This indicates that learning the order of suits is relatively simple compared to learning the order of the ranks. This can be explained by the aspect that our
NLDT
tend to first use the trained neural test and train the untrained ones in lower layers. As discussed in
Section 4
, we have less data samples in nodes in lower layers. Further, learning suit_order means learning an order on 4 elements while learning the ranks_order requires learning an order on 8 elements.

6.5
Summary of the experiments

Our experiments demonstrated that top-down induced
NLDT
outperform
MLP
on tabular datasets with image features. The
MLP
showed little to no learning success, while NeuID3 could successfully induce and train
NLDT
. Arguably, the
MLP
struggled to detect relevant features, while the weak supervision used to induce
NLDT
was sufficient to detect which features are of interest. Furthermore, we demonstrated in our experiments the advantages of including background knowledge in the tests and highlight the advantage of
NLDT
being modular systems where parts can be reused, for instance, in a continual learning setting.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
NLDT
as neurosymbolic soft decision trees and proposed NeuID3 as a
natural extension and upgrade of the popular decision tree learning paradigm. The neurosymbolic component is based on the DeepProbLog
[
27
]
family of systems.
NeuID3 is a neurosymbolic learning system that cannot only learn the parameters of neural networks but also the symbolic structure of such models from scratch. At the same time, NeuID3 is able to deal with both symbolic and subsymbolic features, and it can use background knowledge and weak supervision in the induction process. The experiments show that NeuID3 performs better than purely neural approaches to tackle the same tasks.

Decision tree learning, thanks to its simplicity, has proven to be very effective, efficient and popular. Numerous extensions of decision trees exist and it would be interesting to exploit such extensions also for neurosymbolic decision trees.
