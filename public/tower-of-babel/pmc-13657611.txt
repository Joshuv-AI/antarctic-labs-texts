Cumulative obesity exposure and hepato-pancreato-biliary cancer risk following different weight-loss interventions: retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ivan Kukeev, Yuval Arnon, Elchanan Quint, Sofyan Abu Freih, Anton Osyntsov, Oleg Dukhno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Obesity is a time-dependent carcinogenic exposure associated with an increased risk of hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) cancers. Although weight-loss interventions improve metabolic status, the comparative oncologic impact and the role of cumulative obesity duration remain unclear.Objectives:To compare HPB cancer incidence across lifestyle-based, pharmacologic, and surgical weight-loss strategies and to assess whether differences in cumulative obesity exposure before and after weight reduction may influence cancer risk.Setting:A retrospective cohort study at a tertiary university hospital in Israel.Methods:We analyzed 1131 adults who achieved weight loss between 2010 and 2024 through lifestyle modification, weight-loss medications, or bariatric surgery. For each patient, the duration of obesity before and after weight loss, the magnitude of BMI reduction, and follow-up intervals were recorded. HPB cancers – including hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and gallbladder carcinoma – were identified through national registry linkage. Group differences were evaluated using analysis of variance, Kruskal–Wallis, χ2 tests, and unadjusted risk ratios and odds ratios.Results:Twenty-seven HPB cancers were identified. Incidence did not differ significantly among lifestyle (2.05%), medication-induced (3.24%), and surgery-induced (1.05%) groups (P = 0.334). Bariatric surgery had the lowest numerical incidence, whereas pharmacologic therapy had higher numerical rates; however, these differences were not statistically significant and should be interpreted cautiously. Obesity duration before weight loss differed significantly across groups (P < 0.001), and post-weight-loss duration showed a borderline difference (P = 0.050). BMI reduction was similar across interventions.Conclusions:Although no statistically significant differences in HPB cancer incidence were observed among weight-loss strategies, exploratory numerical differences were noted, with the lowest crude incidence in the bariatric surgery group. Given the limited number of events, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating only. Further adequately powered prospective studies are required before any clinical inference can be made.

Introduction

Obesity is a major global health burden and is strongly associated with an increased risk of several gastrointestinal malignancies, including hepatocellular, pancreatic, and biliary tract cancers[1–3]. Multiple metabolic and inflammatory pathways, including insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, chronic inflammation, and adipokine imbalance, are believed to mediate this elevated risk[4]. Weight reduction improves these metabolic abnormalities, yet the long-term cancer-related effects of different weight-loss strategies remain insufficiently understood. Lifestyle-based weight loss often leads to modest and variable results, while pharmacologic therapies and bariatric surgery produce more rapid and durable metabolic improvements, though their comparative impact on cancer risk is still being established[5,6]. In addition to the method of weight reduction, the cumulative duration of obesity exposure may influence carcinogenic potential. Emerging evidence suggests that prolonged obesity is associated with a higher incidence of several cancers, but the relationship between obesity duration, timing of weight loss, and the risk of hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) malignancies has not been clearly defined[2,4].

This study was designed not to test the superiority of specific weight-loss strategies, but to explore how differences in cumulative obesity exposure – before and after weight reduction – vary across interventions and may influence observed HPB cancer risk. We hypothesized that the timing and durability of metabolic improvement, rather than absolute weight loss alone, are critical modifiers of long-term carcinogenic risk. This study compares HPB cancer incidence among patients who achieved weight loss through natural, pharmacologic, or surgical means and examines whether differences in obesity duration before and after weight reduction may help explain observed trends in cancer risk.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients who achieved clinically meaningful weight reduction between January 2010 and December 2024 at the tertiary Soroka University Medical Center and were categorized into three groups according to their predominant weight-loss strategy: natural (lifestyle-based), pharmacologic (use of structured weight-loss medications, including anti-obesity pharmacotherapy, predominantly GLP-1 receptor agonist–based therapy, mainly liraglutide and semaglutide, with limited exposure to non–GLP-1 medications such as orlistat, bupropion/naltrexone, and metformin), or surgical (standard bariatric procedures). Patients with incomplete weight trajectory data, pre-existing HPB malignancies, or less than 12 months of follow-up after weight reduction were excluded. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, baseline anthropometric data, medication history, and procedural details were extracted from electronic health records and institutional metabolic clinic databases. In addition to demographic and metabolic variables, clinical factors with established associations with HPB carcinogenesis were systematically assessed. This included smoking status, history of pancreatitis, and prior hepatobiliary surgeries (hepatectomy and cholecystectomy). These variables were evaluated as potential confounders, as they may reflect pre-existing hepatobiliary disease, chronic inflammation, or independent carcinogenic exposures that could influence HPB cancer risk irrespective of obesity duration or weight-loss strategy. To assess the primary outcome – cumulative obesity exposure – we recorded, for each patient, the duration of obesity before weight reduction [defined as the interval from the first documented body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2 to the onset of weight loss], the duration of obesity after weight reduction (from the time weight loss was achieved to the last recorded BMI), the interval from intervention to follow-up BMI assessment, and the absolute BMI change. These variables were collected to reflect differences in obesity burden and weight-loss trajectories across the three groups. The secondary outcome was the development of HPB cancers – including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma, gallbladder carcinoma, and cholangiocarcinoma – identified through pathology reports, imaging findings, and linkage with the National Cancer Registry. BMI measurements were obtained from electronic medical records, including both hospital-based measurements and documented measurements from community healthcare settings. In cases where patients did not attend scheduled follow-up visits or BMI measurements were not recorded, data were considered missing.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges and compared across the three weight-loss groups using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using χ2 tests. Group differences in obesity-related exposure – specifically the duration of obesity before weight reduction, duration after weight reduction, and timing of post–weight-loss BMI assessment – were evaluated using the same tests to assess heterogeneity in weight-loss trajectories. The incidence of HPB cancers was compared using odds ratios (ORs), relative risks (RRs), and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Because the overall number of HPB cancer events was low, no multivariable or time-to-event modeling was applied, and the results were interpreted as exploratory. All statistical tests were two-sided. Given the very low absolute number of HPB cancer events and the risk of model overfitting, multivariable regression, propensity-score matching, or time-to-event analyses were intentionally avoided. All analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, aiming to describe clinically meaningful patterns rather than to establish causal associations. Given only 27 total HPB cancer events, adjusted multivariable regression and Cox proportional hazards modeling were considered statistically unstable and at high risk of overfitting; therefore, exploratory sensitivity analyses, including Firth-penalized logistic regression and follow-up-adjusted incidence rate analysis, were used instead. ORs with 95% CIs were calculated using the natural weight-loss group as the reference category. To partially account for differences in follow-up duration between groups, a follow-up–adjusted incidence rate analysis was additionally performed. Estimated person-years were calculated using the median duration of BMI follow-up within each group as a proxy for observation time. Incidence rates were expressed as events per 1000 person-years, and incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CIs were calculated for between-group comparisons. To further explore potential heterogeneity in patient selection across weight-loss strategies, an exploratory sensitivity analysis integrating crude risk estimates, penalized regression, follow-up-adjusted incidence rates, and obesity-duration metrics was performed.

Results

A total of 1131 patients met inclusion criteria, including 635 (56.1%) individuals who achieved conservative weight reduction, 401 (35.5%) who received medical therapy, and 95 (8.1%) who underwent bariatric surgery. Across the entire cohort, hepatobiliary malignancies were rare, with individual cancer types occurring in fewer than 1.3% of patients in any group (Table 1). When comparing the distribution of specific malignancy subtypes across the natural weight-loss, medication-induced, and bariatric surgery groups, no statistically significant differences were observed (all P > 0.05). Within the pharmacologic weight-loss cohort, treatment was predominantly GLP-1 receptor agonist–based, with liraglutide and semaglutide accounting for 79.0% of pharmacologic therapies. Non–GLP-1 therapies, including orlistat, bupropion/naltrexone, and metformin, represented 21.0% of treatments (Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B381). This indicates relative pharmacologic homogeneity within the medication-treated cohort. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma was the most frequently identified cancer, diagnosed in four patients in the natural weight-loss cohort (0.62%) and five patients in the medication cohort (1.21%), with no cases reported after bariatric surgery; this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.375). HCC occurred in four individuals in the natural cohort (0.62%) and four in the medication cohort (0.97%), with no cases in the surgical group (P = 0.555). Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma was detected only among patients using weight-loss medications (3 cases; 0.7%), with no cases in the natural or surgical cohorts (P = 0.067). Distal cholangiocarcinoma was rare across all groups, occurring in 2 patients in the natural cohort (0.31%) and 1 patient in the medication cohort (0.24%), with none identified after surgery (P = 0.854). Gallbladder carcinoma was observed in 3 patients in the natural cohort (0.46%) and 1 patient following bariatric surgery (1.04%), with none in the medication cohort (P = 0.218) (Table 1). When evaluating the overall incidence of any hepatobiliary malignancy (13 cases among natural weight-loss patients [2.05%], 13 among patients receiving weight-loss medications [3.24%], and 1 among bariatric surgery recipients [1.05%]), no statistically significant difference was identified between groups (P = 0.334) (Fig. 1). Analysis of time-related clinical parameters demonstrated several meaningful differences between the weight-loss groups (Table 4). The median number of days from cancer diagnosis to death varied across the cohorts but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.118). Patients who achieved Surgery-Induced Weight Loss had numerically prolonged survival intervals, although the sample size was limited. The number of days from obesity diagnosis to death was similar among the three groups (P = 0.652), indicating that the overall duration of obesity-related disease burden before mortality did not differ substantially by weight-loss method. In contrast, the time interval between obesity onset and the initiation of weight reduction showed a significant difference across interventions (P < 0.001). Individuals in the Lifestyle-Induced and Surgery-Induced Weight Loss groups experienced markedly longer obesity duration prior to initiating weight-loss efforts compared with those who were treated medically. A borderline-significant trend was observed for the duration of obesity after weight loss (P = 0.050), with the Surgery-Induced group demonstrating the shortest post-intervention obesity duration. Median BMI reduction did not differ significantly among the groups (P = 0.839), suggesting comparable degrees of weight reduction regardless of intervention type. The comparative risk analysis did not demonstrate any statistically significant differences between groups (Table 5). Patients in the medication-induced weight-loss group showed a higher, though non-significant, risk of HPB cancer compared with the lifestyle weight-loss group (RR = 1.58, OR = 1.61, 95% CI: 0.74–3.45, P = 0.27). In contrast, bariatric surgery was associated with a numerically reduced risk of HPB malignancy when compared with both lifestyle weight loss (RR = 0.51, OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.06–3.90, P = 0.49) and medication-induced weight loss (RR = 0.32, OR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.04–2.50, P = 0.19). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the relatively low number of HPB cancer events, particularly in the surgical cohort. To account for differences in follow-up duration, a follow-up–adjusted incidence rate analysis was performed using median BMI-measurement duration as a proxy for observation time (Table 6). The estimated incidence rates of HPB cancer were 2.97 per 1000 person-years in the lifestyle-induced weight-loss group, 5.43 per 1000 person-years in the medication-induced group, and 1.73 per 1000 person-years in the surgery-induced group. Compared with lifestyle-induced weight loss, medication-induced weight loss demonstrated a numerically higher incidence rate (IRR = 1.83, 95% CI: 0.85–3.94), whereas surgery-induced weight loss showed a numerically lower incidence rate (IRR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.08–4.45); however, neither comparison reached statistical significance.

Exploratory sensitivity analyses were performed to assess whether observed numerical differences in HPB cancer incidence remained consistent across alternative analytical approaches (Table 7). Crude risk estimates, Firth-penalized logistic regression, and follow-up–adjusted incidence rate analyses demonstrated similar directional findings, with numerically lower HPB cancer incidence in the bariatric surgery cohort and numerically higher incidence in the medication-induced group, although none reached statistical significance. Baseline obesity duration and follow-up differed significantly between groups, supporting the presence of measurable between-group heterogeneity and the possibility of residual selection bias. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated the incidence of HPB malignancies among patients who achieved weight loss through lifestyle modification, pharmacologic therapy, or bariatric surgery, with particular emphasis on cumulative obesity exposure before and after weight reduction. Although no statistically significant differences in HPB cancer incidence were identified between weight-loss strategies, several clinically meaningful patterns emerged that contribute to the growing body of evidence linking obesity duration, metabolic interventions, and cancer risk. Importantly, because only 27 HPB cancer events occurred, this study was not adequately powered to detect modest but potentially clinically relevant between-group differences.

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for multiple HPB malignancies, including HCC, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, gallbladder cancer, and cholangiocarcinoma[1–3]. Carcinogenesis in this context is mediated through complex mechanisms involving chronic systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, adipokine dysregulation, oxidative stress, and progressive hepatic steatosis[4,7,8]. Importantly, contemporary epidemiologic data increasingly conceptualize obesity as a time-dependent carcinogenic exposure rather than a static BMI threshold, with longer duration of obesity independently associated with increased cancer risk regardless of subsequent weight loss[2,9,10]. Our findings align with this paradigm, demonstrating substantial heterogeneity in obesity duration across weight-loss strategies despite comparable BMI reduction.

The numerically lowest incidence of HPB malignancies observed in the bariatric surgery cohort is consistent with data from large prospective and population-based studies demonstrating reduced long-term cancer incidence following surgical weight loss; however, no statistically significant difference was demonstrated in the present study[5,6,11,12]. Bariatric surgery induces rapid and durable metabolic improvements, including enhanced insulin sensitivity, reduced systemic inflammation, and regression of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, effects that often precede maximal weight reduction[13–15]. In our study, patients undergoing bariatric surgery exhibited the shortest duration of obesity after weight loss, supporting the hypothesis that truncation of cumulative metabolic insult may play a central role in modifying long-term cancer risk. The absence of statistical significance may reflect limited statistical power related to the small surgical cohort and rarity of HPB cancer events; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Given the low absolute number of HPB cancer events, Firth-penalized regression was additionally performed to reduce sparse-data bias; results remained non-significant, supporting the robustness of the primary findings despite limited statistical power.

An important consideration is that treatment allocation in this retrospective cohort was non-randomized. Patients undergoing bariatric surgery likely represented a systematically selected subgroup, potentially differing from non-surgical patients in obesity severity, referral pathways, treatment motivation, comorbidity burden, and access to specialized healthcare. Exploratory sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the overall directional pattern remained consistent across crude, penalized, and follow-up-adjusted models; however, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Therefore, observed between-group numerical differences should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating.

Conversely, patients achieving weight loss through pharmacologic therapy demonstrated a numerically higher incidence of HPB malignancies. This observation warrants cautious interpretation. While modern pharmacologic agents, particularly GLP-1 receptor agonists, effectively promote weight loss and improve glycemic control[16], their long-term oncologic impact remains incompletely defined. Large trials and meta-analyses have not demonstrated increased pancreatic or biliary cancer risk associated with GLP-1–based therapies[17–19]; however, prolonged follow-up is required given the long latency of HPB malignancies. Additionally, pharmacologic weight loss may be initiated later in the course of metabolic disease, after prolonged subclinical insulin resistance or hepatic steatosis, and may be less durable than surgical intervention, potentially allowing oncogenic processes to persist. Patients selected for pharmacologic weight-loss therapy may represent a population with more prolonged metabolic disease or delayed intervention, which could confound observed cancer risk independently of the pharmacologic treatment itself. Importantly, the pharmacologic cohort in this study was predominantly composed of GLP-1 receptor agonist–based therapies, which accounted for nearly four-fifths of all pharmacologic treatments. This relative predominance of a single mechanistic class reduces within-group heterogeneity; however, differences in treatment duration, adherence, and metabolic response may still have influenced long-term oncologic outcomes.

Lifestyle-induced weight loss remains a foundational component of obesity management but is often characterized by variable durability. In our cohort, patients in the lifestyle group experienced prolonged obesity both before and after weight reduction and the longest duration of BMI follow-up, reflecting real-world challenges related to maintenance of weight loss. While sustained lifestyle modification has been associated with reduced cancer risk at the population level[20,21], its oncologic benefit may be attenuated when metabolic abnormalities persist or weight regain occurs. HPB malignancies represent a distinct oncologic subset that may be particularly sensitive to metabolic and inflammatory exposures due to the central role of the liver and pancreas in glucose and lipid metabolism. The rising incidence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease–related HCC and the established links between metabolic dysfunction and pancreatic carcinogenesis underscore the relevance of obesity duration in this context[22,23]. HPB malignancies are characterized by long latency periods, which may limit the ability of relatively recent weight-loss interventions – particularly pharmacologic therapies – to influence cancer risk within the available follow-up duration. This may partially explain the absence of statistically significant differences despite consistent directional trends between groups. Importantly, follow-up–adjusted incidence rate analysis yielded similar directional findings, with the lowest estimated incidence rate observed in the surgical cohort and the highest in the medication-induced group, although these differences remained statistically non-significant. This supports the interpretation that observed between-group differences are exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. This further suggests that unequal duration of BMI monitoring alone is unlikely to fully explain the observed between-group differences in crude HPB cancer incidence.

Our findings suggest that the oncologic relevance of weight loss may lie less in the magnitude of BMI reduction and more in the cumulative duration of metabolic exposure before and after weight loss. Despite similar absolute BMI reduction across all three groups, substantial heterogeneity was observed in both the time patients remained obese prior to intervention and the duration of residual obesity following weight loss. These temporal differences may be particularly relevant for HPB malignancies, which are characterized by prolonged latency periods and sustained exposure to insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and hepatic steatosis. From this perspective, bariatric surgery may confer potential oncologic benefit not merely through greater weight loss but through earlier and more durable truncation of obesity-related metabolic insult, whereas delayed or less sustained interventions may allow carcinogenic processes to persist despite comparable short-term weight reduction.

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including potential residual confounding and limited availability of data on alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, fibrosis stage, and genetic predisposition. The pharmacologic cohort encompassed heterogeneous treatment regimens, precluding agent-specific analysis. Most importantly, the low number of cancer events limited statistical power and precluded multivariable or time-to-event modeling.

The relatively low absolute number of HPB cancer events (n = 27) substantially limited statistical power, particularly for subgroup comparisons involving bariatric surgery, and increased the risk of Type II error. Consequently, clinically relevant differences between interventions may have remained undetected despite consistent directional trends.

Nonetheless, a key strength of this study is the incorporation of obesity duration metrics, an aspect rarely accounted for in weight-loss and cancer research. These findings highlight the potential importance of earlier metabolic intervention and sustained obesity remission as components of long-term cancer prevention strategies in high-risk populations. Residual confounding related to the indication for weight-loss strategy selection cannot be excluded. BMI measurements were derived from both hospital and community medical records, which may introduce variability in measurement timing and methodology. In addition, missing BMI data occurred in patients who did not attend follow-up visits or in patients in whom BMI was not recorded, potentially introducing selection bias. Group imbalance, particularly the relatively small bariatric surgery cohort, further limits the precision of effect estimates and contributes to wide CIs. Residual confounding related to treatment selection and unmeasured baseline differences between intervention groups cannot be excluded. In addition, because treatment allocation was non-randomized, systematic differences in baseline patient characteristics between intervention groups likely existed and may have influenced observed cancer risk independently of weight-loss modality. Differences in follow-up duration between intervention groups may have influenced cancer ascertainment; however, follow-up-adjusted incidence rate analysis yielded similar directional findings, partially mitigating this concern.

Conclusion

In this retrospective exploratory cohort, no statistically significant differences in HPB cancer incidence were observed among weight-loss strategies. Although numerical variation in crude incidence was noted, the very small number of events substantially limits statistical inference, and clinically meaningful differences may have gone undetected. Accordingly, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, warranting validation in larger prospective studies with longer follow-up.
