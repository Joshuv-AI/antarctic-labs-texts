Agronomic performance and yield stability of field pea (Pisum sativum L.) genotypes in multi-environment trials
Authors: Chaoqin Hu, Jie Cun, Alaa A Soliman, Feng Yang, Zeinab E Ghareeb, Xingxing Yuan, Tao Yang, Xuejun Wang, Jijun Zhang, Chao Xiang, Long Zhao, Changyan Liu, Gaoling Luo, Bin Zhou, Xu Zhu, Yongsheng Tang, Ruigang Wang, Haitian Yu, Xin Yang, Yubao Wang, Zhengming Dai, Aiqing Zheng, Qiong Li, Xiuzhi Xia, Xin Chen, Yuhua He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe selection of stable, high-yielding pea genotypes is crucial for sustaining production under changing climate conditions. Twenty multi-environment trials (10 locations x 2 years) were conducted to evaluate 11 genotypes during the 2020–2021 and 2021–2022 growing seasons in southern China.ResultsThe Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) pooled analysis revealed that environmental effects accounted for the largest proportion of total variation (59.62%), followed by genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) at 30.11%. The factor analysis revealed that plant height, first pod height, number of nodes on the main stem, number of pods/plant, number of seeds/pod, and number of nodes containing pods were the best multi-traits that significantly contributed to high yields. Cluster analysis identified five superior genotypes - Jingwan 8, Yuwan 6, Yunwan 116, 20205, and Suwan 8, with Yunwan 116 also showing disease resistance. The genotype main effects plus GEI (GGE) biplot identified Kunming and Xinxiang locations as the most effective environments for differentiating the genotypes, and Jingwan 8 as the ideal genotype for high yield and stability, particularly in these locations. Further, Yuwan 6 and 20205 also showed wide adaptability across environments.ConclusionsJingwan 8, Yuwan 6, and 20205 pea genotypes are recommended for large-scale cultivation due to their high yield and broad adaptability.Keywords: Pisum sativum L., Multi-environment trials, Genotype-by-environment interaction, AMMI analysis, GGE biplot, Multi-trait analysis, Yield stability, Climate change adaptation, Genotype selection, Wide adaptability

Background

Pea (Pisum sativum L.) is an annual legume crop, grown in both spring- and autumn-sown systems. It ranks as the fourth most widely cultivated legume worldwide [1]. It can be used as a seed, a vegetable, food, feed, and green manure [2]. It is rich in protein, starch, dietary fibers, vitamins, and high-quality potassium, iron, phosphorus, and other mineral nutrients [3–5]. It is one of the important sources of protein and other nutrients for human beings.

According to FAO statistics, 95 countries produced dry peas, and 82 countries produced fresh peas in 2023. China is the leading global producer and consumer of peas, with a total cultivation area of 2.4 million hectares (dry and fresh). However, for dry peas specifically, China ranks third in area (921,000 hectares) and production (1.499 million tons), following Russia and Canada. Despite this, China’s average dry pea yield (1.63 t/ha) remains below the global average (2.04 t/ha), ranking only 48th worldwide. In addition, the production areas of fresh and dry peas overlap in some areas, which leads to a decrease in dry pea yield. In recent years, China’s imports of dry peas have steadily increased, reaching 2.676 million tons in 2023 (2.9 times higher than in 2015) [6]. Dry pea productivity in China remains constrained by factors such as limited improved varieties, susceptibility to diseases, and suboptimal agronomic practices [7]. Pea cultivation in China dates back more than 2,000 years [8], with key production regions including Yunnan, Sichuan, Gansu, Guizhou, Inner Mongolia, Qinghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shandong, and Hebei. Among these, Yunnan, Sichuan, Gansu, Hebei, and Guizhou have the largest cultivation areas of peas. Based on the sowing season, pea production in China is classified into spring and autumn sowing regions, with the autumn-sown crop covering a larger area. The country’s field pea breeding programs began in the 1960 s, focusing on developing high-yielding varieties with improved resistance to various biotic and abiotic stresses, as well as better adaptation to diverse agroecological conditions [8]. Environmental stresses such as cold, drought, and soil limitations strongly influence pea yield. Moreover, genotype × environment interactions (GEI) further complicate breeding efforts due to China’s diverse agro-climatic zones [9].

Multivariate analyses, such as factor analysis, are widely applied in crop breeding to identify essential traits, reduce data dimensionality, and explore relationships among yield components [10]. Additionally, cluster analysis is commonly used to group similar variables into distinct categories, enabling a clearer understanding of genotype classification and trait associations [11].

In breeding programs, the selection of promising genotypes is primarily based on key traits, with the final seed yield and quality being the most critical. Understanding the relationship between yield and its contributing components is also essential for effective selection [12, 13]. In this study, factor analysis enabled the identification of the yield-contributing traits that significantly influence seed yield.

Factor analysis was used to reduce a large number of variables into a smaller set of factors by eliminating redundant traits from correlated variables [10]. As a result, the traits represented by the first factor are considered common and more relevant for selection, emphasizing not only yield but also other important multi-traits. The genotypes identified through this process can serve as valuable initial material for further selection of high-yielding, promising genotypes in breeding programs.

Multi-environment trials (MET) are extensively employed to assess and quantify the adaptability and stability of genotypes across various environments [14, 15]. The presence of significant genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) in MET data requires stability analysis for meaningful interpretation. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of GEI can help breeders minimize its negative impact and enhance the efficiency of breeding programs. Several methods, such as analysis of variance (ANOVA), additive main effects and multiplicative interaction (AMMI), and genotype + genotype-by-environment (GGE) biplot, can be used to evaluate stability. The graphical GGE biplot provides valuable insights into genotype-by-environment interaction patterns and helps classify environments as either broadly or specifically suited to certain genotypes [16]. This approach allows the identification of superior genotypes that combine high yield and stability, thereby facilitating more effective breeding strategies. In the study area (China), there is limited information on the performance of pea genotypes across different environmental trials. Declining yields and environmental susceptibility have reduced farmer interest in pea cultivation, highlighting the need for high-yielding, resilient varieties. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate eleven pea genotypes across multiple environments (10 locations × 2 years). Biplot graphs were used to illustrate genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI), identify ideal genotypes (those stable with higher yields) under varying environmental conditions, and determine the most suitable genotype for each environment, while also estimating traits related to pea yield and assessing the relationships among different environments.

Experimental design

Eleven field pea genotypes were evaluated during the Autumn sowing season in southern China for two consecutive years (2020–2021 and 2021–2022) in the ten locations, making a total of twenty environments. Each location × year combination was considered a single environment, giving a total of 20 environments. Each genotype was evaluated in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Plots measured 15 m² (10 rows, 3 m long, 50 cm row spacing). In each row, seeds were planted in holes spaced 12 cm apart with two seeds per hole, resulting in a plant density of approximately 33 plants per square meter. Fertilizers included ordinary superphosphate (600 kg ha⁻¹), potassium sulfate (150 kg ha⁻¹), and nitrogen (≤ 150 kg ha⁻¹ as urea), applied according to soil fertility. Weeds were controlled periodically by hand-weeding, irrigation was performed at the seedling and flowering stages, and other management practices were implemented as required. Pea is often sown in mid-October. The harvest took place in mid and late April of the following year. Data collection

Seed yield (SY) was measured on a plot basis and converted to kg ha⁻¹. Yield-related traits, including plant height (PH), First pod height (FPH), were measured as the distance from the soil surface to the node bearing the first (lowest) pod on the main stem, seeds per plant (SPP), seed weight per plant (SWP), and seeds per pod (SPD), were measured from 20 randomly selected plants per plot. Hundred-seed weight (100-SW, g) was recorded from dried seed samples. Powdery mildew and rust diseases were scored visually on a 1–9 scale, where 1 indicated high resistance and 9 indicated high susceptibility, following the standard procedure described by ICARDA (2013) for pea disease assessment [17].

Statistical analysis

Individual and combined ANOVA

The collected data on agronomic traits were subjected to RCBD analyses (ANOVA) for each season and each location using GenStat software, 18th edition. Each location x season combination was considered as an environment, giving a total of 20 environments. Homogeneity of error variances among environments was tested using Levene’s test [18]. Means were compared using the least significant difference test (LSD at 5%) according to Steel and Torrie [19]. Correlation coefficients were calculated using the Pearson correlation coefficient model, and graphical analysis was performed using Past software version 8 [20].

The factor analysis method [20] was used to reduce the large number of correlated variables to a much smaller number of variables, known as factors. Varimax orthogonal rotation was applied for factor loadings after extraction [21]. Communality array, as the amount of variance accounted by the common factors together was estimated by the highest correlation coefficient in each array [22]. Factors with eigenvalues greater than one out of the total number of variables were employed to estimate variance. Then, the proportion of variance in the variable groups accounted by a factor is the sum of squares loading for this factor (its variance) divided by the number of variables. Factor analysis was conducted using SPSS software (v.22). The cluster analysis was carried out using a similarity level measure and Euclidean distance [23]. Cluster analysis with Minitab (v.16). Graphical analyses were prepared using Origin program version 9 (Origin Lab).

Stability analysis

Data from 20 environments (10 locations × 2 seasons) were pooled and estimated for the presence of significant G × E using analysis of variance. Then, additive main effects and multiplicative interaction analysis (AMMI) were described by Gauch et al. [24], were applied for each environment separately, and after combining them. The genotype main effect and G×E interaction (GGE biplot) were performed to visualize the G×E interaction. The stability and G×E analysis were conducted using GenStat software version 18 [25].

Results

Analysis of variance

Combined analysis of variance was performed for pooled data after testing the homogeneity of individual error variances for most studied traits among the different environments. The main effects of variation for AMMI combined analysis of variance for the studied traits of eleven pea genotypes across twenty environments (10 locations × 2 years) are established in Table 3. Results showed that the additive main effects of pea genotypes (G), environments (E), and their multiplicative (G x E) interactions were highly significant for all measured traits. Accordingly, AMMI analysis further separated the G x E interaction sum of squares using principal component analysis. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) and residuals were significant for all studied traits. Other proportions of the total sum of squares exhibited that environments ranged from 30.33 to 73.77% had the major proportion for all traits except for plant height (50.20%) and first pod height (44.02%). Genotypes variation recorded the lower attributes for 8 traits seed yield (10.27%), effective branching (9.13%), number of pods/plant (15.66%), number of seeds/pod (6.27%), pods length (23.04%), number of nodes contain pods (8.47%), powdery mildew (25.18%) and Rust (4.29%). Meanwhile, the other 5 traits (plant height, first pod height, number of nodes on main stem, pod width, 100-seed weight) had lower attributes for genotype x environment variation (19.47, 24.03, 16.42, 27.72, and 30.40%, respectively).

The graph illustrated in Fig. 3 shows that pea seed yield and its contributing traits revealed various trends of associations among themselves. Best multi-traits and selection of promising genotypes

Determination of the best yield-related traits

Previous results revealed the relationships among the studied pea genotypes, determining many traits that are associated with pea seed yield. To identify the most yield-influencing traits among the studied genotypes, factor analysis was used as an effective method for selecting superior genotypes based on multiple yield-related traits.

Factor analysis

Data in Table 5 present results obtained from the factor analysis as ‘Eigen value, percent of variance, and its cumulative percent’ after rotation of the thirteen studied pea traits. Factor analysis presented that only 4 of the 13 original factors had eigenvalues higher than one and were selected as the best factors. These four factors together explain 94.81% (12.32 eigenvalues/13 variables) of the variance among the genotypes. The communality array indicated the amount of variance of a variable accounted for (12.32) by the common factors together.

Genotypes by environmental interaction and stability

Yield is a complex quantitative trait responsive to genotype by environment interaction (GEI). It is the most used parameter by breeders to detect a genotype’s acceptance. In this study, we performed stability analysis on the seed yield. Genotypes by environment interaction (GEI) under different locations was investigated. Finally, a GGE biplot was performed to combine the data of all genotypes against the environment means to detect adaptability under different conditions.

A bi-dimensional cluster from the mean yield of locations and genotypes of each location was generated to assess the contribution of the genotypes on the locations. The horizontal axis groups genotypes based on phenotypic similarity across locations. The differences in color intensity indicated the values of each feature, with blue color being the highest and red being the lowest.

Ideal genotype

A comparison of the genotypes studied with the ideal one is shown in the GGE biplot Fig. 10a, b. The ideal genotype, allocated and plotted in the concentric circle of the biplot graph, exhibited both stable and high-yielding genotypes across environments. This middle concentric circle, pointed with a drawn arrow, helps in comparison between the genotypes and the ideal genotype [16].

The graphs obtained illustrate that Jingwan 8 was the ideal genotype, with high seed yield and the most stable performance across different environments, especially in the locations of Kunming and Xinxiang. In comparison, the nearest genotypes to the ideal genotype were the most stable ones. Thus, Yuwan 6, Suwan 8, 20205, and Yunwan 116, located above the yield average and close to the ideal one, were desirable and stable genotypes. Meanwhile, G1 (Wanwan 1) was below the yield average and far from the ideal genotype, and was an unstable, low-yielding one. Similar results were reported by Castiano et al., Yan and Rajcan, and Yan et al. [30–32], who suggested that ideal common bean and wheat genotypes were determined for different locations. In the present study, Kunming and Xinxiang locations were the most discriminating environments.

As seen from the results, the ideal genotypes Jingwan 8, Yuwan 6, Suwan 8, 20205, and Yunwan 116 exhibited high mean yield performance coupled with high stability, which provides wide adaptability across different environments/sites.

Discussion

Significant differences across the 20 environments indicate strong environmental variation between locations and years. Similar findings were reported by Mohammadi et al. and Mahgoub et al. [26, 37], they noted that differences between genotypes could be attributed to variations in location and year. The significant differences among the genotypes in the present study reflect the variability in their genetic potential for the evaluated traits. Furthermore, the significant differences detected among the sites studied highlight the substantial genotype effect in the additive structure of the data for these traits across locations. These results are consistent with those reported by Mahgoub et al. [37].

Pea breeders face the challenge of environmental influences when attempting to develop a novel genotype. To address this, they conduct multiple trials, as previously noted by Yan [38]. In these efforts, breeders often use AMMI and GGE models to identify suitable targets, along with principal component analysis (PCA) as proposed by Gabriel [39]. The main distinction between the two approaches is that AMMI partitions the genotype–environment (GE) interaction into components (PCA1, PCA2, and PCAn), which are then presented in the ANOVA results (Table 3).

The pooled analysis of AMMI explained large differences within main effects (genotypes and environments) that could be due to variability in genetic makeup and environmental conditions that differ from one location to another and from one year to another.

On the other hand, the combined analysis for environments revealed that the largest proportion of the total variance was explained by the environment item, followed by the G × E interaction, indicating that the large influence of environment accounted for most of the variation in pea yield and genotypes’ performance across all seasons and locations. This season and location variation may be due to climatic changes (Fig. 2) and location conditions (Table 2). The present findings are in support of the reported results by Bocianowski [40], Greveniotis [41] and Yang et al. [42].

Positive correlations were observed between yield and most of the phenotypic traits studied. Correlation analysis indicated that selection for effective branching, the number of nodes containing pods, the number of nodes on the main stem, and the number of pods/plant with tolerance to powdery mildew disease would be accompanied by a high-yielding ability and would be more effective for improving pea seed yield. These results agreed with Bahadur and Devi [43], Ertiro [44], and Xu et al. [45], who suggested that genetic improvement of pea seed yield could be achieved by selecting for effective traits. These results may confirm the importance of each or all studied traits on pea seed yield.

Factor analysis proved more appropriate for detecting and screening effective traits for selecting the best genotypes based on multiple yield traits rather than a single parameter. The first factor had an eigenvalue greater than one, indicating that the evaluated factor values were more reliable than the original variables. The rotated components matrix facilitated the identification of key components, highlighting traits such as plant height, first pod height, number of nodes on the main stem, number of pods per plant, number of seeds per pod, and number of nodes containing pods as the most influential for selection. These traits can serve as valuable criteria for identifying superior genotypes, which may constitute promising initial material for a further breeding program. The present findings align with earlier research demonstrating that factor analysis and principal component analysis can effectively condense complex agronomic data into a smaller set of meaningful selection indices in legumes and other crops. By integrating multiple yield-contributing traits into a selection index, breeders can improve the efficiency of early-generation screening and increase the likelihood of advancing high-performing genotypes. Similar applications in pea have been reported by Anand et al. [46], Haile and Tesfaye [47], and Uhlarik et al. [48], who applied factor analysis and rotated PCA matrices to identify yield-contributing traits and enhance the selection of high-yielding genotypes.

Based on the most related yield traits, cluster analysis classified the best measured genotypes and ranked them as (Jingwan 8, Yuwan 6, Yunwan 116, 20205, and Suwan 8) that recorded the highest seed yield over the grand mean. Hence, these genotypes may contain some desirable traits related to the high productivity of the pea [49]. It is noted that the genotypes Jingwan 8 and Yuwan 6 are similar in most traits (green seed color, normal leaflets, hard pod type, semi-dwarf, purple flower color), while the genotypes 20205 and Suwan 8 are similar in traits, viz., dimpled seed type, normal leaflets, soft pod type, and long vine. These genotypes are expected to be valuable resources for future breeding programs [50]. These findings demonstrate substantial genetic diversity among the studied pea genotypes and provide valuable resources for future breeding programs. Such diversity is highly valuable for selecting superior and promising genotypes aimed at improving seed yield through breeding programs. These findings are consistent with previous research by Jagadeesh et al. [51], who reported significant morphological and quality trait variation via multivariate analysis in field pea breeding lines. These results could explain genotype ranking and selected genotypes for the multiple traits [12, 13, 52].

In this study, relationships between multivariate factors were visualized using a heat-map cluster. The GEI heat map enabled the assessment of genotype adaptability across diverse environments and locations, providing a comprehensive view of the associations among all tested pea genotypes and sites. Cluster analysis revealed consistent performance patterns, with Jingwan 8 and Yuwan 6 exhibiting high yield across nearly all test locations. These findings are aligned with recent GEI analyses in pea by Zhang et al. [53], who employed AMMI, GGE-biplots, and BLUP to identify stable, high-yielding genotypes across multiple environments. The use of heat-map clustering in grass pea ranking for genotype stability, as assessed by the WAASB index, also supports the power of such visual analytics in multi-environment evaluation, as noted by Koka et al., [54]. The examination of genotype × environment interaction (GEI) in pea proves essential for identifying genotypes that are both high-yielding and stable across multiple environments.

GGE biplot analysis is widely recognized as an effective tool for evaluating multi-environment trial data, as demonstrated by Yan et al. and Olanrewaju et al. [55, 56]. This method enables visualization of genotype × environment (G×E) interactions; helps breeders identify genotypes with superior performance and stability. In the present study, GGE biplot analysis was applied to data from multi-environment trials (10 locations x 2 years), encompassing a broad range of climatic conditions such as temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity. Overall, GGE biplot analysis indicated that Nanyang, Kunming, and Chengdu were the most discriminating and informative environments for evaluating genotypes. Nanyang site with sandy loam and medium soil fertility recorded the best location, which might be due to the preceding legume crop, Soybean. The Chongqing site, with its good soil fertility, was not able to record the best location, which may be due to the proximity of the maximum and minimum temperatures. However, the genotypes Yuwan 6, Jingwan 8, 20205, and Yunwan 116 were widely adaptable genotypes and the most desired stable ones. Jingwan 8 was identified as the ideal genotype, combining high yield with broad adaptability across environments. In many of the previous studies conducted in multi-environments, stable and unstable pea genotypes were identified by Yang et al. [42].

Conclusion

The eleven pea cultivars under this investigation exhibited significant differences in response to the 20 measured environments at the ten geographical locations. The differential expressions of yield and yield-related traits were due to environments and GEI effects. Factor analysis identified plant architecture and pod traits as the most informative for selecting high-yielding genotypes. These genotypes provide valuable breeding material for the development of improved pea cultivars. Cluster analysis highlighted Yuwan 6, Jingwan 8, Suwan 8, 20205, and Yunwan 116 as the top-performing genotypes with desirable traits for improving pea productivity. Among them, Jingwan 8 was identified as the ideal genotype, combining high yield and stability. Along with Yuwan 6 and 20205, these cultivars are recommended for wider cultivation, particularly in Kunming and Xinxiang.
