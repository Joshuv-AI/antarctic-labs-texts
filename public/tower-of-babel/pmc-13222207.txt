Foslevodopa/Foscarbidopa Subcutaneous Infusion Safety and Efficacy in Patients with and Without Prior Deep Brain Stimulation
Authors: Tove Henriksen, Juan Carlos Martínez-Castrillo, Mark H B Huisman, Sara Dhanani, Elizabeth Peckham, Jason Aldred, Lars Bergmann, Megha B Shah, Resmi Gupta, Pavnit Kukreja, Juan Carlos Parra, Victor S C Fung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs Parkinson’s disease (PD) advances, limitations of oral medication include pill burden, increasing “Off” time, and “On” time with bothersome dyskinesia. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) can improve motor complications, but disease progression may warrant further intervention later. An approved nonsurgical option, continuous subcutaneous infusion of foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp), has been shown to improve motor fluctuations, though the impact of prior DBS on its efficacy and safety is unknown.MethodsPost hoc analysis of a 52-week open-label phase 3 trial (NCT03781167) evaluated baseline characteristics, safety, and efficacy in patients treated with LDp/CDp with or without prior DBS. Fisher’s exact test, t test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and analysis of covariance were performed.ResultsTwenty-four (9.8%) of 244 patients received prior DBS. Both groups had similar baseline characteristics, although prior patients treated with DBS had significantly more time since diagnosis and more “speech” and “gait” impairment (Movement Disorders Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale [MDS-UPDRS] Parts II/III single items). Both groups showed significant within-group improvements in “Off” time and “On” time without dyskinesia, and the between-group difference for these improvements was not significant, indicating LDp/CDp had efficacy regardless of DBS exposure. The no DBS group had significant improvement in sleep and quality of life, while the prior DBS group had nonsignificant trends in the same direction. The no DBS group had significant improvement in MDS-UPDRS Part II score but worsening in Part III score, as expected with advancing disease; change from baseline in the prior DBS group was not significant. The safety data were similar in both groups, with the only significant difference being a higher percentage of prior patients treated with DBS experiencing severe treatment-emergent adverse events than no DBS (45.8% vs 23.6%).ConclusionWhile limited by small prior DBS patient numbers, this post hoc study suggests generally similar overall baseline, safety, and efficacy profiles in LDp/CDp-treated prior DBS and no DBS.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03781167.Keywords: Deep brain stimulation, Foslevodopa, Foscarbidopa, Parkinson’s disease, Subcutaneous infusion

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Parkinson’s disease (PD) prevalence is rapidly increasing. For patients with persistent PD symptoms despite oral medication, deep brain stimulation (DBS) and other device-aided therapies, including the newly approved continuous infusion of foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp), may help regain symptom control. However, there remains an unmet need for patients who have improved with DBS but still have “Off” time and “On” time with troublesome dyskinesia.Previous reports suggest motor symptom and quality of life benefits from sequential device-aided therapies in PD. We hypothesized that LDp/CDp may offer comparable safety and efficacy in patients with persistent motor fluctuations, regardless of prior experience with DBS. What was learned from the study? This post hoc analysis of data from a 52-week open-label phase 3 clinical trial of patients with advanced PD treated with LDp/CDp demonstrated that patients who had received prior DBS therapy had generally similar baseline traits, safety profiles, and improvements in average normalized “Off” time and “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia as patients without prior DBS therapy.The results suggest that, regardless of having DBS treatment or not, patients experience improvements in motor fluctuation with LDp/CDp, which adds an additional treatment option to potentially provide more comprehensive disease control.

Introduction

The number of people worldwide with Parkinson’s disease (PD) increased twofold to more than six million from 1990 to 2016, and economic costs plus patient and caregiver burdens increase substantially throughout disease progression [1]. As a result of increased sensitivity of the striatum to levodopa blood levels as PD progresses, there is an increase in medication “Off” time, and “On” time may occur with increasingly bothersome dyskinesia [2]. About 80% of patients receiving long-term levodopa treatment have motor fluctuations and 30% to 80% have dyskinesia [3].

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is considered one of the standard-of-care options for advanced PD in patients with inadequately controlled motor complications. It involves surgically implanting electrodes that send high-frequency signals to stimulate certain brain regions, particularly the subthalamic nucleus (STN) or internal globus pallidus (GPi) for most cardinal PD symptoms, with ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus (VIM) stimulation reserved for tremor-dominant cases [4, 5]. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, DBS significantly reduces motor fluctuations and dyskinesia [5]. However, the clinical benefits may diminish as the disease progresses, [6, 7] requiring adjustments to stimulation and supplemental oral therapies to maintain symptom control, as adverse effects and increasing pill burden can occur [6].

Other device-aided therapies can be considered either as an alternative to DBS or post-DBS to improve PD symptom control while also minimizing pill burden, such as continuous subcutaneous apomorphine infusion (SCAI) [4], levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel (LCIG; also called carbidopa-levodopa enteral suspension), and levodopa-entacapone-carbidopa intestinal gel (LECIG) infusion [8]. A study of 58 patients who received combined device-aided therapy (n = 42 DBS followed by LCIG [DBS-LCIG], n = 14 DBS-SCAI, and n = 2 LCIG-DBS) reported improved motor fluctuations in all patients and improved quality of life (QoL) in some [9]. These findings suggest that select patients may benefit from synergistic effects of sequential device-aided therapies targeting different mechanisms of action to improve motor and non-motor symptoms as well as QoL [9]. Nonetheless, DBS, LCIG, and LECIG are surgical interventions and may not be suitable for patients with contraindications to surgery.

A nonsurgical levodopa-based device-aided therapy option independent of prior DBS therapy was recently approved for treating uncontrolled motor fluctuations in patients with advanced PD on optimized oral therapy. Foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp) is a 24-h/day delivery of the levodopa/carbidopa prodrugs via continuous subcutaneous infusion (CSCI) using a portable pump; phosphatase enzyme activity in the body rapidly converts LDp/CDp into pharmacologically active levodopa (LD) and carbidopa (CD) [10, 11]. A 12-week, double-blind, randomized phase 3 trial comparing LDp/CDp to oral LD/CD immediate-release treatment (NCT04380142) and a recent 52-week, open-label, single-arm phase 3 trial (NCT03781167) both demonstrated 24-h/day CSCI of LDp/CDp in patients with PD improved motor fluctuations and displayed a favorable safety profile [10, 11]. However, the effect of LDp/CDp on patients previously treated with DBS therapy experiencing motor fluctuations is not yet established.

The objective of this post hoc analysis was to evaluate the baseline demographics, baseline disease characteristics, safety profile, and efficacy profile of patients treated with LDp/CDp from the 52-week open-label phase 3 clinical trial based on whether they had received prior DBS therapy or not. We hypothesized that patients treated with LDp/CDp with and without prior DBS therapy would have similar overall safety and efficacy profiles, as well as improved motor fluctuation, sleep, and QoL measures.

Methods

Data from the third interim analysis of a single-arm, open-label, 52-week phase 3 clinical trial evaluating the safety and tolerability of LDp/CDp infusion therapy were analyzed post hoc to assess whether patients with PD who had experienced DBS treatment prior to LDp/CDp therapy demonstrated differences in baseline characteristics, safety, or efficacy compared to patients who had not received prior DBS treatment. The study design and primary results have already been published [11]. The third interim analysis dataset was used here because it was almost identical to the final trial results, with only 12 patients of the total population who had not yet fully completed the 52-week follow-up final visit. All 24 prior-DBS-treated patients were already included.

Study Design and Procedures

Full study details can be found in Aldred et al. 2023 [11]. In brief, the 52-week treatment period of the single-arm, open-label trial was divided into a 4-week optimization period followed by a 48-week maintenance period. LDp/CDp study drug solution was continuously infused subcutaneously over 24 h via a single infusion cannula connected to an ambulatory pump that could supply an individualized dose depending on patient need, ranging from approximately 600 to 4250 mg of LD equivalents/24 h through an infusion site (primarily on the abdomen) required to be rotated at a minimum of every 3 days.

Participants

Patients eligible for the 52-week open-label trial were those diagnosed with idiopathic LD-responsive advanced PD who were ≥ 30 years of age and had symptoms that investigators determined were inadequately controlled with their current therapy. Patients were also required to have ≥ 2.5 h of average “Off” time/day for two successive days before enrollment via electronic PD diary entries, as well as demonstrate discernible motor fluctuations (“On” and “Off” states) verified by investigator observation. As long as their PD remained stable and responsive to LD, patients fulfilling all remaining eligibility criteria for this trial who had previously experienced DBS treatment were also allowed to enroll in the trial. Patients were allowed to continue receiving DBS during the study. While the target for DBS implications has importance for clinical outcomes, this study was not designed in a manner that allowed capture of this data.

The clinical trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03781167), and the independent ethics committees (IECs) or institutional review boards (IRBs) at each study site approved the clinical trial protocol, informed consent forms, and recruitment materials before patient enrollment (with the names of all IECs/IRBs providing approval previously published in the Aldred et al. Supplementary Appendix) [11]. The clinical trial was conducted in accordance with its protocol and the International Council for Harmonization guidelines, applicable regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All patients provided written informed consent before initiating any study-specific procedures.

Outcomes

The open-label trial’s primary objective was to investigate the systemic and local safety and tolerability of LDp/CDp in patients with PD for 52 weeks, as assessed by patient-reported treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) as well as changes from baseline in vital signs, clinical laboratory, and electrocardiogram parameters. The trial’s secondary objective was to evaluate LDp/CDp efficacy based on the change from baseline to the final available visit in the PD diary-assessed average daily “On” and “Off” time (normalized to a 16-h waking day); Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS, where higher scores indicate more severe disease impact on motor function and daily life); the 39-item Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire (PDQ-39, where higher numbers indicate a more severe impact of PD on health-related quality of life); and the Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale-2 (PDSS-2, where higher scores indicate more severe sleep disturbance) [11]. Each scale is composed of multiple single items that assess individual effects of PD and which can be compiled to provide an overall evaluation of a patient’s functioning, QoL, or sleep disturbance. Permissions to use the aforementioned scales were obtained as appropriate.

This post hoc analysis evaluated the baseline characteristics, overall summary of safety, and TEAEs in ≥ 10% of participants reported by preferred term during the entire study period in patients treated with LDp/CDp with PD from the 52-week open-label trial who received either prior DBS or no DBS therapy, as well as assessed the baseline and change from baseline to final available visit motor complications (including “Off” time, “On” time without dyskinesia, “On” time with non-troublesome dyskinesia, “On” time with troublesome dyskinesia, and “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia), MDS-UPDRS Parts II–III summary and single items scores, PDSS-2 total score, and PDQ-39 Summary Index total score.

Statistical Analyses

The 52-week open-label trial’s third interim analysis data (cutoff date March 2, 2022) were used in the current post hoc analysis to assess between-group differences in baseline characteristics, safety, and efficacy outcomes of patients treated with LDp/CDp who had either prior DBS or no DBS therapy. The safety analysis set (SAS) was defined as all patients receiving ≥ 1 LDp/CDp infusion any time during the trial; the full analysis set (FAS) was defined as all patients receiving ≥ 1 LDp/CDp infusion that also had ≥ 1 efficacy measure baseline and treatment observation; and Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 24.0 was utilized to code TEAE preferred terms [11]. Baseline frequency and percent values for categorical variables as well as mean and standard deviation (SD) values for continuous variables are presented for the prior DBS and no DBS groups, with categorical values between groups compared using Fisher’s exact test and continuous variables that passed their normality assumptions compared between groups using t test; non-normal continuous variables between groups were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Overall safety outcomes categorical values between groups were analyzed for comparison via Fisher’s exact test. For the efficacy outcomes, least squares (LS) mean and standard error (SE) change from baseline to final available visit values as well as their LS mean of difference were compared between groups using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model (where change equals DBS minus group baseline) and two-sided P values; with a statistical significance level of P < 0.05 used in the modeling phase. SAS® software version 9.2 or later (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was utilized to conduct statistical analysis calculations.

Safety Outcomes

The overall safety profile observed in the 52-week open-label trial has been previously reported by Aldred et al. with the systemic LDp/CDp safety profile considered generally aligned with that expected of LD/CD-based therapies, as well as the local infusion site event profile overall aligned with what has been previously published for subcutaneous infusion treatments [11]. In the overall trial, investigators judged the severity of most TEAEs to be mild to moderate and nonserious, and reported that these TEAEs resolved with treatment or without [11]. The safety summary in this analysis indicates that the safety profiles were largely similar across the two groups, with the only significant difference being a higher prevalence of severe TEAEs in the prior DBS group than the no DBS group (45.8% vs 23.6%, Table 2). The discontinuation rates for the study (33.3% vs 39.5%) and for LDp/CDp treatment (33.3% vs 45.0%) were relatively balanced between the prior DBS and no DBS groups and not significantly different (Table 2). Both the prior DBS and no DBS groups reported infusion site events as the four most frequent TEAEs that occurred in patients during the study, including infusion site: erythema, cellulitis, and nodule as the three most frequent TEAEs in both groups; followed by infusion site abscess in the prior DBS group vs infusion site edema in the no DBS group (Table 2). The systemic TEAEs of hallucination and fall were the next most frequent TEAEs observed in both groups (Table 2). Based on the summary of safety as well as overall similarity in the order of TEAE frequency reported in both groups, the safety profiles of the prior DBS and no DBS groups were considered similar.

Efficacy Outcomes

Motor Fluctuations

Both the prior DBS group and the no DBS group had significant within-group improvements in average normalized change from baseline to final available visit LS mean (SEs) “Off” time (− 2.21 [0.70], − 2.42 [0.24]) and “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia (2.68 [0.74], 2.70 [0.25]) that were primarily driven by improvement in “On” time without dyskinesia (2.39 [0.90], 3.13 [0.30]) (Fig. 1a). DBS exposure did not have a statistically significant effect on these improvements, indicated by the between-group differences all having P values > 0.05 (Fig. 1a). While the imbalance of the sample size between the two groups limits the conclusions that can be made from the statistical comparisons, both the prior DBS and no DBS groups had similar improvements in average normalized “On” time without troublesome dyskinesia and “Off” time upon treatment with LDp/CDp infusion therapy.

Sleep, QoL, and Functioning

Patients treated with LDp/CDp with no DBS therapy demonstrated a clinically meaningful and statistically significant within-group change from baseline improvement of − 6.06 (0.66) in PDSS-2 total score and of − 5.81 (0.99) in PDQ-39 summary index score (Fig. 1b), with each change from baseline exceeding the reported minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold of − 3.44 for PDSS-2 [12] and of − 4.72 for PDQ-39 [13]. Patients treated with LDp/CDp who received prior DBS therapy had nonsignificant trends in the same direction, with a PDSS-2 total score and a PDQ-39 summary index score within-group change from baseline to final available visit LS mean (SE) of − 4.36 (1.94) and − 1.23 (2.89), respectively (Fig. 1b). The between-group differences for change in PDSS-2 total score and PDQ-39 summary index score were not statistically significant.

Patients treated with LDp/CDp who received prior DBS therapy did not have statistically significant changes from baseline in MDS-UPDRS Part II (Fig. 1c) or Part III (Fig. 1d) total score or any of the single item scores. However, there were nonsignificant score increases for MDS-UPDRS Part II items “speech” and “walking & balance” (Fig. 1c), and all Part III items in this group (Fig. 1d). Patients treated with LDp/CDp with no DBS therapy demonstrated statistically significant within-group improvements from baseline in the MDS-UPDRS Part II summary score (− 2.30 [0.43]) and all Part II single items (“speech”, “freezing”, “tremor”, and “walking and balance”, Fig. 1c); however, this group had statistically significant within-group increases from baseline in MDS-UPDRS Part III summary score (3.57 [0.84]) and the Part III single items “speech problems”, “gait”, “postural stability”, and “posture” (Fig. 1d). The MCID thresholds for change from baseline in MDS-UPDRS Part II summary score (− 3.05) [14] and Part III summary score (− 3.25) [15] were not met. There was a significant between-group difference for MDS-UPDRS Part II summary and single item “speech” scores, with the no DBS group demonstrating improvement (Fig. 1c). However, the sample size of the prior DBS group limits the power of the statistical comparisons that can be made.

Discussion

Summary

In this post hoc analysis of 244 patients with advanced PD from a 52-week open-label phase 3 clinical trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of LDp/CDp therapy, approximately 10% of patients had received DBS treatment prior to LDp/CDp trial participation. The groups had similar baseline demographics and disease characteristics, although the prior DBS group had been diagnosed with PD for an average of 3 years longer and had more severe speech and gait impairment at baseline. This was not surprising, given that DBS can improve some PD motor symptoms while also impairing speech and gait [5, 16, 17]. The prior DBS and no DBS groups had similar safety profiles, with the only significant difference being a higher prevalence of severe TEAEs in the prior DBS group. Both groups experienced statistically significant improvements in “Off” time and “On” time without dyskinesia. Additionally, the no DBS group had significantly improved sleep (PDSS-2 score) and quality of life (PDQ-39 score); the smaller prior DBS group showed trends in the same direction that were not significant. In the no DBS group, scores improved for Part II of the MDS-UPDRS but worsened for Part III, in line with expectations for advancing PD; changes from baseline in the prior DBS group were not significant. Because motor fluctuations improved significantly within each group and because these improvements did not differ significantly between the two groups, we conclude that LDp/CDp treatment had similar efficacy regardless of prior DBS treatment.

Relevance with the Literature

Previous studies have shown that the magnitude of initial motor complication improvement mediated by DBS in patients with PD diminishes over time with disease progression. As reviewed by Limousin and Foltynie [6], 15 studies which measured UPDRS Part III motor scores before and after STN-DBS surgery in the off-medication/on-stimulation state demonstrated significant improvements from preoperative baseline at 1 year, 5 years, and ≥ 8 years post-surgery; however, a decrease over time in the magnitude of improvement was observed in the subsequent follow-up motor scores. In addition, while the 15-study mean levodopa equivalent daily dosage (LEDD) decreased by 52% vs preoperative baseline at 1 year post-surgery and remained reduced throughout the follow-up period, the LEDD value also increased over time from year 1 to ≥ 8 years after STN-DBS [6], indicating a need for increasing additional oral therapy over time. Other studies evaluating long-term STN-DBS efficacy in patients with advanced PD have also reported this waning magnitude of improvement over time, including 37.2% improvement at 3 years waning to 35.1% improvement at 5-year follow-up in the off-medication/on-stimulation UPDRS Part III total score (vs the same time point off-medication/off-stimulation states) [18]; 46.7% significant improvement at 6 months waning to a 42.4% increase at 6 years and a 14.8% decrease from baseline at 12-year follow-up in the on-medication/on-stimulation worst UPDRS Part III scores [7]. These studies and others also report a significant reduction in patient LEDDs from preoperative baseline to the first follow-up after STN-DBS surgery, with the LEDD values increasing at subsequent follow-ups, despite all LEDDs remaining significantly improved at last follow-up vs preoperative baseline [7, 18–20].

While STN-DBS is known to improve motor symptoms responsive to levodopa and thus permits the post-surgery antiparkinsonian medication burden to be reduced, the reported percent reductions in those dopaminergic therapies have been variable [21]. Therefore, options other than levodopa or dopamine agonist monotherapies are often needed to manage post-DBS parkinsonian symptoms such as motor fluctuation, and switching or combining device-aided therapies may be warranted [22]. In the systematic literature review component of their study, Boura et al. identified six articles in which a total of 50 patients experienced initial DBS combined with either SCAI or LCIG treatment and reported considerable reductions in daily “Off” time and motor fluctuations in five of the studies, as well as significant improvement in motor symptoms via “On” state UPDRS Part III assessment in the other study with the combined device-aided therapies [9]. In the case series component of their study, Boura et al. reported that six patients with initial DBS combined with either LCIG or SCAI infusion experienced substantial improvements in “Off” time, motor fluctuations, and QoL [9]. Georgiev et al. reported that unsatisfactory attenuation of motor fluctuations and dyskinesias improved after LCIG was added in six patients with initial DBS [22]. More recently, Pürner et al. reviewed 16 previous device-aided therapy combination studies and noted that most of those studies reported relevant clinical improvement in motor complications as well as in the main symptoms of PD [23]. And, in their own study of 116 patients with 148 total device-aided therapy modifications, one of the largest case collections of device-aided therapy combinations presented, Pürner et al. reported significant improvements in motor symptoms (via MDS-UPDRS Part III), motor complications (via MDS-UPDRS Part IV), and “Off” time (via MDS-UPDRS Part IV item 4.3) [23]. Of note, the sequence of the combined device-aided therapies could also be varied. For example: (1) Boura et al. reported that two patients initially receiving LCIG were then later treated with DBS as a strategy to spare levodopa, which substantially reduced total daily “Off” time as well as levodopa dose [9]; (2) the interim results from the PD-DUAL multicenter retrospective chart review study in patients with advanced PD who had prior or current DBS treatment and subsequently received LCIG therapy reported a median oral/transdermal LEDD reduction of 92% 6 months after starting LCIG based on 34 patient records evaluated [24]; and (3) a prospective study where 20 patients on optimized medical treatment were first treated with SCAI as a bridging therapy until they received STN-DBS reported that both the SCAI and the subsequent DBS significantly reduced mean daily “Off” time, UPDRS Part IV, and QoL [25]. Thus, DBS treatment can occur before, after, or concurrently with infusion therapy [9, 24, 25].

As summarized above, the current study results suggest that patients treated with LDp/CDp with or without prior DBS therapy showed generally similar safety outcomes and improvements in motor fluctuations complications. The long-term benefits of DBS on axial symptoms (such as speech, gait, and walking and balance) remain unclear. A 2023 systematic review of 44 publications representing 866 total patients reported that most studies indicated speech and voice parameters deteriorated after DBS treatment [5]. Conversely, another study followed 25 STN-DBS-treated patients with PD for a median of 5 years post-surgery and found their postoperative speech intelligibility was not significantly reduced compared to their preoperative values [26].

Because LDp/CDp was an investigative therapy at the time of the trial, patients were generally only offered it later in their treatment cycle. Now that LDp/CDp therapy has received regulatory approval in several regions, LDp/CDp treatment may be a first-line treatment option once oral treatments no longer provide sufficient symptom control, and therefore does not necessarily need to follow DBS therapy.

Limitations

Several factors limit the generalizability of the current study. First, the sample size imbalance between the prior DBS (n = 24) and no DBS (n = 220) treatment groups reduced the power of the statistical analysis, and limited the conclusions that could be drawn; thus, further research is warranted in a larger dataset. Also, the parent trial for this post hoc analysis did not capture how long after DBS surgery LDp/CDp was initiated, whether patients had STN or GPi stimulation, nor was it designed to capture adjustments to DBS during long-term CSCI therapy; therefore, there is limited ability to determine the effect that LDp/CDp has on the need for DBS therapy adjustment, which may vary based on DBS target (STN vs GPi). Another limitation is the absence of a control arm for comparison, due to the design of the original single-arm open-label clinical trial. In addition, a substantial number of patients discontinued the 52-week trial prematurely, limiting the interpretation of the current study’s results. However, that concern is potentially mitigated by the fact that discontinuation rates for the overall study as well as for LDp/CDp treatment remained relatively balanced in both groups. Further comparative research may be warranted to enable patients and clinicians to best determine the fit for DBS, LDp/CDp, or potential combination/sequential therapy to guide decision-making on the best treatment strategy to provide optimal outcomes according to a patient’s profile.

Conclusion

Post hoc analysis of data from an open-label 52-week phase 3 trial of patients with advanced PD treated with LDp/CDp indicated that the safety and efficacy profiles were largely similar in patients who had received prior DBS treatment and those who had not. Overall, these data indicate that LDp/CDp infusion could improve motor complications in DBS-naive patients as well as in those who received prior DBS treatment but whose motor fluctuations remained uncontrolled.
