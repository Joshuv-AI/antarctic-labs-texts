Predicting knowledge of cervical cancer screening among reproductive-age women in sub-saharan africa using machine learning algorithms
Authors: Nebebe Demis Baykemagn, Gebrie Getu Alemu, Makda Fekadie Tewelgne, Alemu Teshale Bicha, Tirualem Zeleke Yehuala, Mekuriaw Nibret Aweke, Habtamu Wagnew Abuhay, Miteku Andualem Limenih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sub-Saharan Africa faces twice the incidence and up to fifteen times the fatality rate of cervical cancer compared to developed countries. Screening coverage remains low at 7–25.3%, far below the WHO target of 70%. Increasing women’s knowledge about cervical cancer screening is crucial for improving uptake and reducing this high burden. The study was conducted among 50,584 reproductive-age women across seven Sub-Saharan African countries. A combination of filter and wrapper methods was applied for feature selection. Data preprocessing and management were performed using Stata version 17 and Python (Colab, version 3.10.2). MinMaxScaler, StandardScaler, and RobustScaler were applied to normalize variable ranges. One-hot encoding was used for nominal categories, and ordinal encoding was applied for features with an inherent order. The dataset was split using an 80; 20 (40,467: 10,117) ratio for training and testing. Eight algorithms were selected for model training and development, including Decision Tree, Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting (LightGBM), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), and Gradient Boosting (GB). Hyperparameter tuning was performed for XGBoost and LightGBM. Model performance was evaluated using Accuracy, AUC, F1 Score, Recall, and Precision. The XGBoost algorithm outperformed the other models, achieving an accuracy of 84%, an AUC of 82%, an F1 score of 83%, a recall of 82%, and a precision of 84%. Overall, 56% of reproductive-age women demonstrated good knowledge of cervical cancer screening. HIV status, educational status, media exposure, maternal age, health status, antenatal care attendance, wealth status, occupation, autonomy, place of delivery, distance to health facility, marital status, and fertility were identified as the top predictors of knowledge of cervical cancer screening. Knowledge of cervical cancer screening was low, at 56%, compared with the WHO target of 70%. Strengthen community-based educational programs through mass media, reinforce health education during antenatal care (ANC) follow-up and HIV programs, and implement strategies that promote women’s autonomy, education, and economic empowerment are recommended.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a significant global public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income nations1. In 2022, it caused approximately about 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually2. It ranks as the fourth most common cancer affecting women worldwide and is the second most prevalent cancer among women of reproductive age3, recent evidence shows that cervical cancer screening coverage in Sub-Saharan Africa is low, ranging from 7% to 25.3%4,5, far below the WHO recommended target of 70% coverage needed for effective prevention and control6.

Also, study showed in 11 sub-Saharan African nations indicates that the prevalence of cervical cancer screening among women is only 8.47%7. As this evidence indicated that cervical cancer screening uptake is severely hampered, delaying detection and treatment, and increasing the incidence and fatality rates of the disease8.

Sub-Saharan Africa has twice the incidence of cervical cancer and up to fifteen times the fatality rate compared to developed countries9. If this rate continues, cases and deaths will increase by 14.8% and 17.8%, respectively10. This highlights the critical importance of early identification of precancerous alterations through cervical cancer screening, which not only enables timely prevention of invasive malignancy but also improves life expectancy and empowers women by enhancing their health knowledge11.

Evidence shows that increasing women’s knowledge about cervical cancer screening can play a pivotal role in improving screening uptake12. However, Knowledge about cervical cancer screening among African women, particularly those in Sub-Saharan Africa, remains generally low13. with evidence from a systematic study showing that only 43% have good knowledge14.

Current findings also reveal a strong correlation between knowledge and screening uptake, as women with poor knowledge are 25% less likely to participate in screening programs compared to those with good knowledge14. Consequently, many women undergo screening or seek treatment only after symptoms appear, leading to late-stage diagnoses, less effective treatment, and reduced chances of survival15.

There is limited evidence about the causes of poor knowledge of cervical cancer screening. Therefore, identifying the major predictors of cervical cancer knowledge is crucial for designing effective interventions to improve screening coverage.

This study aims to utilize machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict knowledge of cervical cancer screening among reproductive-age women in Sub-Saharan Africa, leveraging DHS data. The objectives are to develop predictive models, compare the performance of various ML algorithms in accurately predicting cervical cancer screening knowledge, and identify the most influential factors affecting knowledge.

Machine learning offers enhanced accuracy, adaptability, and deeper insights compared to traditional statistical approaches16. It provides accurate evidence for policymakers, researchers, and users to enable early detection and intervention, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality by increasing the productivity of women.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilized 50,584 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) secondary data records obtained from a cross-sectional survey design and analyzed them using supervised machine learning algorithms to develop predictive models. Machine learning techniques were employed for their capacity to efficiently analyze large datasets containing numerous predictor variables.

The study was conducted across seven Sub-Saharan African countries Benin, Cameroon, Gabon, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mauritania, and Tanzania between 2020 and 2023, using data from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS). Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), located south of the Sahara Desert, is home to over 1 billion people and is a diverse region comprising West Africa (including Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, and Côte d’Ivoire), East Africa (including Kenya, Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Uganda), Central Africa (featuring countries like the Democratic Republic of Congo, Cameroon, and Gabon), and Southern Africa (which includes South Africa, Botswana, Namibia, and Angola).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For this study, all women of reproductive age (15–49 years) living in Benin, Cameroon, Gabon, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mauritania, and Tanzania were included, while those with highly incomplete or missing data were excluded.

Study Variables

Dependent variable

Knowledge of cervical cancer screening was categorized based on whether respondents had heard of cervical cancer screening. Women of reproductive age who answered “Yes” were classified as having good knowledge (coded as 1), while those who answered “No” were classified as having poor knowledge (coded as 0).

Independent variables

Education status, media exposure, wealth status, maternal age, marital status, husband’s educational level, type of place of residence, autonomy, antenatal care attendance, distance to health facility, woman’s occupation, HIV status, fertility preference, number of living children, place of delivery, and health status.

Data processing and management

Recent studies indicate that irrelevant variables can reduce a model’s generalization capacity, increase complexity, and potentially lower overall classifier accuracy [17]. To address this, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied to systematically remove irrelevant features, reducing dimensionality while retaining the most predictive variables, thereby improving model efficiency and generalizability. Each dataset was first reviewed to ensure structural consistency and the presence of common identification variables. Outcome variables related to cervical cancer screening were renamed consistently across countries, and datasets with the standardized outcome name were appended to create a unified dataset. The merged dataset was then exported as a CSV file, imported into Stata version 17 for verification, and subsequently loaded into Python (Colab, version 3.10.2) for further preprocessing and management in the machine learning pipelinintegrity of the dataset.

Following data consolidation, data transformation techniques were employed to enhance model convergence and predictive performance. Feature scaling methods, including MinMaxScaler, StandardScaler, and RobustScaler, were applied to normalize variable ranges and mitigate the impact of outliers. Additionally, categorical features were encoded into numeric representations, utilizing label encoding for ordinal variables, one-hot encoding for nominal categories, and ordinal encoding for features with inherent hierarchical order .[Figure 1].

Feature selection was employed to identify the most relevant and informative attributes for classification. This step was undertaken to reduce data dimensionality, improve prediction accuracy, and minimize potential challenges such as overfitting, underfitting, and excessive computational complexity17. For this study, a combination of Filter and Wrapper methods was employed to balance speed and accuracy in selecting the most relevant features for the knowledge of cervical cancer screening see in Fig. 2.

Splitting the data

Previous studies indicates that data splitting offers a trustworthy estimation of the model’s performance on unseen data and aids in the detection of overfitting18. For data splitting, an 80:20(10,137 and 40,547) ratio was used for training and testing, respectively, to identify overfitting by assessing model performance on independent, unseen data and evaluating its generalization ability.

Handling imbalanced data

In this study, the distribution of the outcome variable was 45–55%, which is considered almost balanced. Therefore, no special data balancing techniques were applied, as models such as Decision Trees and Random Forests can easily handle this small difference see Fig. 3.

Model Selection

In this study, several supervised machine learning algorithms were evaluated to identify the model with the best predictive performance. The algorithms included Decision Tree, Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting (LightGBM), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), and Gradient Boosting (GB). These models were selected to cover a range of approaches, including tree-based methods, distance-based methods, and neural network models, allowing the study to capture both simple and complex relationships in the data. Model selection was guided by considerations of predictive accuracy, interpretability, and computational efficiency, with the final model chosen based on superior performance on validation metrics.

Model training

After selecting the candidate machine learning algorithms, the dataset was split into training and testing sets using a stratified approach to preserve the class distribution. Each model was trained on the training set, with hyperparameters optimized through cross-validation to enhance predictive performance and reduce overfitting. For tree-based and ensemble models, the number of trees, depth, and learning rate were tuned, while for K-Nearest Neighbors, the optimal number of neighbors was determined. Artificial Neural Networks were trained using multiple hidden layers with appropriate activation functions, learning rate, and epochs to ensure convergence.

Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameter tuning is an essential step in machine learning model development, as it optimizes the learning process and ensures that each model achieves its best predictive performance17. In this study, hyperparameters were carefully tuned for tree-based ensemble models, specifically Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). Tuning key parameters such as the number of trees, tree depth, learning rate, subsampling rate, and regularization parameters allows these models to reduce overfitting, improve generalization, and maximize accuracy on unseen data [ Table 1].

Model Evaluation

Among the candidate supervised machine learning algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated the best predictive performance on the study dataset. It achieved the highest accuracy (84%) and a strong discriminative ability (AUC = 82%) compared to other models, including Decision Tree, Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors, ANN, LightGBM, AdaBoost, and Gradient Boosting. XGBoost’s superior performance can be attributed to its combination of tree-based boosting, regularization techniques, and ability to capture complex non-linear relationships, making it well-suited for the moderate-sized, low-dimensional dataset used in this study. Individual Characteristics of Knowledge of Cervical Cancer Screening.

A total of 50,584 women participated in the study. The majority were aged 25–34 years (46.87%), and most resided in rural areas (53.20%). More than half of the respondents were married (57.26%), while widowed women represented the smallest proportion (2.73%).

Regarding educational attainment, secondary education was most common among mothers (42.31%), whereas higher education was the least reported (5.28%). Household economic status showed that the largest proportion of women belonged to the rich category (41.16%), and the middle-income group was least represented (20.35%). In line with these socioeconomic indicators, most women were employed (56.86%), while networking occupations were less common (43.14%).

Health-seeking behaviors also reflected positive trends. The majority delivered their most recent child at a health institution (69.64%) and reported frequent use of social media (69.27%). Access to healthcare appeared relatively manageable, with 60.71% of women perceiving distance to health facilities as not a big problem. Similarly, antenatal care (ANC) utilization was high, with 61.64% achieving optimal visits, whereas only 11.94% had no ANC contact.

The educational profile of spouses was largely higher education (60.88%), contrasting with the small proportion who attained secondary school education (4.09%). In terms of reproductive history, multiparous women represented the majority (77.39%), while grand-multiparous women were the least common (2.03%). Contraceptive use was reported by 36.54% of women. Slightly more than half of the respondents had ever been tested for HIV (54.29%), and 45.33% had a health check-up in the past 12 months.

Fertility preferences indicated that most women wished to have more children (65.63%). Autonomy in decision-making was predominantly shared jointly with their husband or partner (72.03%), whereas decisions made solely by the respondent were the least frequent (12.07%). Overall, these findings highlight that most women in the study were of reproductive age, married, with secondary education, employed, and actively engaged in healthcare-seeking behaviors, with shared decision-making being the norm.

Regarding reproductive health, multiparous women represented the largest group (77.39%), whereas grand-multiparous women were the smallest group (2.03%). Use of contraceptives was reported by 36.54% of women. HIV testing history showed that slightly more than half of the respondents had been tested (54.29%). Health check-up in the past 12 months was reported by 45.33% of women.

Fertility preference data indicated that the majority of women desired more children (65.63%). Women’s autonomy in decision-making was predominantly shared jointly with their husband/partner (72.03%), while decision-making solely by the respondent was the least common (12.07%).

Discussion

In this study, the XGBoost algorithm outperformed the other models, achieving an accuracy of 84%, an AUC of 82%, an F1 score of 83%, a recall of 82%, and a precision of 84%. Previous studies have shown that the XGBoost algorithm consistently outperforms other methods, supporting our result19–24.

SHAP values showed that 56% of reproductive-age women have good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is higher than previous studies conducted in Ethiopia (39%25 25%;26, and India (35.7%27.

The possible justification for this could be that our study pooled data from eight Sub-Saharan African countries, where differences in healthcare access, education, and awareness levels may collectively result in a higher overall estimate compared to single-country studies.

SHAP analysis showed that women who had checked their HIV status were positively associated with good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is supported by previous studies28–31. In general, women who are checked for HIV engage with healthcare providers more frequently and receive health education, including advice on cervical cancer screening. Women who know their HIV status, especially those who test positive, are more likely to be aware of this risk and motivated to seek screening because HIV infection raises the chance of persistent HPV infection and, as a result, cervical cancer. Additionally, a lot of healthcare systems combine HIV care and testing services with cervical cancer screening, which increases access and uptake among women who have received an HIV test32.

This finding also showed that women’s educational status positively predicted good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is supported by previous studies28,29,33,34. This may be because educated women have greater access to current health information, particularly on global health issues. They are more likely to obtain information through digital media, health facilities, and public health campaigns. As a result, they tend to have greater knowledge about cervical cancer and its screening. This finding implies that responsible bodies should place greater focus on uneducated women by strengthening targeted awareness and educational programs, which may help increase their knowledge and ultimately contribute to the prevention of cervical cancer in Africa35,36.

SHAP analysis also showed that women who frequently use social media were positively associated with good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is supported by previous studies37–40. The possible explanation for this could be that, nowadays, many health programs share information on serious health issues, including cervical cancer, through various organizations and digital platforms. Women who frequently use social media are more likely to be exposed to such health information, including preventive health messages, which may increase their knowledge about cervical cancer screening41.

The machine learning model also identified frequent health status checking as an important predictor of good knowledge of cervical cancer screening among women, which is consistent with findings from previous studies33,42–44.

This finding may be explained by the fact that frequent visits to health facilities reflect higher health-seeking behavior. Women who visit health facilities more often have increased opportunities to interact with health professionals, receive counseling, and access education about cervical cancer. Moreover, observing severely ill patients during these visits may further raise their awareness and knowledge about cervical cancer and its prevention45.

SHAP showed that women who had more than four ANC follow-ups were a positive predictor of good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is supported by previous studies28,29,31,46. The possible justification for this could be that frequent ANC visits increase exposure to health education, enhance contact with healthcare providers, and strengthen preventive health-seeking behavior. Repeated counseling and access to health information during these visits provide women with better awareness of reproductive health issues, which aligns with evidence from previous studies47.

Women with rich and above wealth status were positively associated with good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is supported by previous studies48–51. A possible explanation is that women with higher wealth status generally have better access to education, health information, and healthcare services, which increases their awareness of preventive health measures, including cervical cancer screening. Wealthier women may also have more exposure to media, digital platforms, and community programs that provide health education. In addition, financial resources can reduce barriers such as transportation costs or fees associated with accessing health facilities, enabling greater interaction with health professionals and uptake of health information. These factors collectively contribute to higher knowledge of cervical cancer screening among women with richer wealth status52,53.

According to this finding, women who deliver in health facilities were positively associated with good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is consistent with previous studies33,40,54. The possible explanation could be that women who deliver in health facilities are more likely to interact with trained healthcare providers and receive routine health education programs that promote health-seeking behavior. They also have a higher chance of being exposed to health campaigns, all of which can improve their health literacy, including knowledge about cervical cancer55.

Also, this evidence showed that married women were positively associated with good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is consistent with previous studies38,56,57. The possible justification is that married women are more likely to access reproductive and maternal health services where cervical cancer screening information is provided, benefit from spousal and family support in health decisions, and are targeted by awareness campaigns through maternal health clinics and community outreach programs58–60.

SHAP findings showed that women who want to have more children were predictive of good knowledge of cervical cancer screening, which is supported by earlier studies30,61,62. One explanation for this could be that women who intend to have more children usually interact with medical professionals more frequently through family planning, antenatal care, and reproductive health services, which offers chances to learn about cervical cancer screening. Furthermore, women are motivated to actively manage their reproductive health by the desire to have more children, which in certain settings encourages them to pay more attention to health information on reproductive issues like cervical cancer63.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of this study include the use of a sizable and diverse dataset, which enhances the findings’ generalizability over a wider geographic area. Furthermore, machine learning models may be used to uncover complex linkages and patterns that standard statistics may overlook. However, the limitations of this study include self-reported information, recall bias, and missing data, which might affect forecast accuracy. Furthermore, because the data is cross-sectional, it is difficult to establish causal relationships. Additionally, rather than being perceived as causes, the results derived from machine learning outputs should be viewed as associations.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, the XGBoost algorithm outperformed the other models, achieving an accuracy of 84%, an AUC of 82%, an F1 score of 83%, a recall of 82%, and a precision of 84%. Overall, 56% of reproductive-age women demonstrated good knowledge of cervical cancer screening. HIV status, educational status, media exposure, maternal age, health status, antenatal care attendance, wealth status, occupation, autonomy, place of delivery, distance to health facility, marital status, and fertility were identified as the top predictors of knowledge of cervical cancer screening.

Strengthen community-based educational programs through mass media, reinforce health education during antenatal care (ANC) follow-up and HIV programs, and implement strategies that promote women’s autonomy, education, and economic empowerment are recommended.

Future researchers could use longitudinal data to predict changes in cervical cancer knowledge over time and incorporate socio-cultural, behavioral, and healthcare system factors to improve predictive accuracy.

Implications of the Study

The findings of this study have important policy implications for cervical cancer prevention in Sub-Saharan Africa. By identifying women with low knowledge of cervical cancer screening, policymakers can design targeted health education campaigns and allocate resources more efficiently to high-need regions and populations. Integration of predictive models into digital health platforms or community outreach programs can provide personalized educational interventions, enhancing screening uptake. Moreover, the study highlights disparities in awareness, which can inform policies aimed at promoting equitable access to information and services. Overall, these insights support data-driven strategies to improve cervical cancer prevention and early detection across the region.
