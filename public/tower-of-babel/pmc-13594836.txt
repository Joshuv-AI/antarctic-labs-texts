Brains, quacks, and boojums: a review of neurotransmitter and neuroendocrine bases of welfare and enrichment in poultry with an emphasis on the Pekin duck
Authors: J.M. Schober, M.M. Bergman, G.S. Fraley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In 1876, Lewis Carroll wrote The Hunting of the Snark in which he described a boojum as a particularly dangerous snark that if you found it, you would slowly disappear. In 1950, Dr. Franek Beach wrote The Snark was a Boojum in which he warned of the dangers of over interpreting physiological data related to behaviors. Today, we understand a great deal about the neurobiology of behavior, yet few have related this field to welfare. The purpose of this review is to provide an overview of this field as related to poultry and to point out some potential boojums. The Pekin duck, a major poultry product, is an important livestock species that requires further study to understand their physiological needs and welfare. Assessing welfare includes measuring physical, biological, and mental states of an animal and how it copes with its environment. There are many ways to improve animal welfare, one way is to provide environmental enrichment. Environmental enrichment varies among poultry species. Pekin ducks are waterfowl, so some believe that they should be allowed access to open water to increase positive natural behaviors and reduce unwanted behaviors. Beyond physical enrichment, another emerging approach is auditory enrichment, which has been extensively studied in zoo animals and is now gaining attention in livestock management. Vocalizations are a way to assess welfare and can be used as auditory enrichment. Birds use vocalizations as an effective way to communicate and can be used in various environmental conditions. This review highlights the established indicators for mental state, such as serotonin and dopamine, to validate methods of measuring affective state applied to environment changes. Neurotransmitter activity levels or turnover may be another physiological variable to determine affective state that is more reliable across age and sex. Similarly, gene expression can be linked to affective state by investigating the rate-limiting enzyme in serotonin and dopamine synthesis. Developing neuroendocrine profiles for poultry can elucidate how environmental changes affect physiology and welfare.

Introduction

Poultry is the second most widely consumed type of meat worldwide, accounting for approximately 30% of total meat production (FAOSTAT, 2021). It encompasses the production of chickens, turkeys, ducks and other domesticated birds. There is an increase in demand for poultry products due to the high protein content, amino acid balance, and valuable nutrients that are produced on relatively small geographic footprint (Bohrer, 2017). Pekin ducks (Anas platyrhynchos) are the primary duck breed raised for meat, though they are also produced for their offal, eggs, and feathers (Huang et al., 2012). Pekin duck production has grown tremendously in the past decades especially since the 1950′s when producers began genetically selecting for desired traits like fast growth rate and high efficiency (Huang et al., 2012). In 2019, approximately 27.5 million ducks were commercially harvested, with two Indiana-based companies, Culver Duck (Middlebury, IN) and Maple Leaf Farms (Leesburg, IN), that account for about 75% of the total U.S. production (USDA, 2019). In 2020, more than 22 million ducks were produced for meat in the U.S. Global Pekin duck production has been growing over the past few decades. By 2021, global duck meat production increased to approximately 6.2 million tons, indicating a growing demand for duck meat worldwide (FAOSTAT, 2021). In 2021, China was the world’s largest poultry producer with the U.S. being the second largest (FAO Meat Market Review, 2022). Pekin ducks play a crucial role in both global and U.S. duck meat production as well as feather production, with Indiana being a core producer for U.S. production.

With the increased production of Pekin ducks, there is also increased desire for practices that help improve their welfare. Pekin ducks are waterfowl, and in their natural environments they have access to open water sources for food, hydration, feather health, temperature regulation, and safety. The presence of open water access in commercial barns for Pekin ducks has been a topic of interest to welfare scientists, consumers, and producers. There are conflicting opinions as to whether or not implementation of open water sources would allow them to perform more natural behaviors (e.g. wet preening) thus improve their overall welfare as well as their production. Several humane welfare certifications require open water access in the Pekin duck industry (e.g. American Humane Society, Global Animal Partnership, A Greener World). Open water sources have been shown to either improve or worsen feather condition, eye health, nostril health, and footpad scores (improve – Jones and Dawkins, 2009; worsen – Schenk et al., 2016). These contradictory findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between open water access and duck welfare. Consequently, there is limited information regarding alternative forms of environmental enrichment that promote natural behaviors while improving welfare of Pekin ducks. Another welfare concern is managing stressful experiences such as transportation. Pekin ducks can be transported multiple times during their life cycle depending on their status of grow-out or breeder birds. This transportation can cause the release of stress hormones that alter physiological functions related to welfare. Our current generation of poultry scientists should balance consumer perception and scientific based research when improving environmental conditions for Pekin ducks and the poultry industry (Karcher et al., 2013).

The concept of animal welfare

The concept of animal welfare was introduced due to ethical concerns from the public about how their food is raised and treated. It has developed into a subject for scientific research that incorporates aspects of physiology, reproduction, genetics, ethology, welfare, among others. Animal welfare is defined as “the physical and mental state of the animal in relation to the conditions in which it lives and dies” (WOAH Terrestrial Code Chapter, 7.1; Broom, 1986, 1991). It encompasses factors such as handling, nutrition, veterinary care, behavior, enrichment, and housing.

There are several ways to assess animal welfare. The first framework created to assess animal welfare was the Five Freedoms of animal welfare. The Five Freedoms framework includes freedom from hunger and thirst, freedom from discomfort, freedom from pain, injury or disease, freedom to express normal behavior, and freedom from fear and distress (Brambell, 1965; FAWC, 1993). Fraser et al. (1997) adapted the Five Freedoms framework and created the three circles model stating that the five freedoms focused on preventing suffering, but welfare perspectives need to extend beyond just to prevent suffering.

The three circles model shifts from a purely harm-avoidance approach to a broader, more integrated perspective of welfare (Fraser et al., 1997; FAWC, 1993). This model involves overlapping circles indicating that each concept can affect the others. The 3 circles are described as follows: (1) basic health and biological functioning – ensuring that animals are free from disease, injury, and malnutrition, focusing on veterinary care, proper nutrition, and general health, (2) affective states – addressing the emotional experiences of animals, including minimizing negative states and promoting positive states, and (3) natural living/behavior – allowing animals to express natural behaviors and live in an environment that resembles their natural habitat as closely as possible (Fraser et al., 1997).

Another adaptation of the Five Freedoms framework presented by Mellor et al. (2020) is the Five Domains framework. This framework’s first four domains include nutrition, environment, physical health, and behavior and the fifth domain is the mental domain. This framework recognizes that animals can experience feelings which range from negative to positive. The Five Freedoms of animal welfare do not attempt to achieve an overall picture of mental state and welfare status, but the principles themselves are everlasting (Webster, 2016). As consumers increase demand for higher welfare standards, the importance of animal welfare frameworks in guiding agricultural practices becomes more evident. Advancing these frameworks not only enhances animal welfare but also aligns with consumer expectations, thereby promoting both ethical decision-making and sustainable production.

General public and consumer concerns over the treatment of animals in food production have grown over the past few decades, and they care more about how the food they eat is raised (Alonso et al., 2020). A study conducted by Yang (2023) showed that consumers’ moral attitudes toward animal products significantly affect their willingness to purchase animal products. For example, Heng et al. (2013) showed that consumers are willing to pay higher prices for eggs produced by non-cage methods. There are certification programs in the U.S. that help ensure the humane treatment of farm animals. These standards are species-specific and grounded on scientific research along with the framework of Key Welfare Indicators (KWI). These certification programs are shifting the methodology used for evaluating animal welfare from the Five Freedoms to the 5 domains, emphasizing the importance of how the animal feels (Certified Humane, American Human Society, Animal Welfare Approved, Global Animal Partnership). With the increasing interest of consumers in animal welfare, it is important to continue to advance our animal welfare assessments to ensure that our animals are experiencing positive welfare. To promote animal welfare, producers should focus on preventing disease, reducing injury, and avoiding stressors. In addition, they should develop systems to reduce abnormal behavior by accommodating natural behavior and providing animals with an environment where they can thrive (Fraser and Weary, 2008).

There are several ways to assess animals in poor, adequate, or good welfare. According to Broom (1986), there are two general types of poor welfare indicators. The first is evidence that the individual fails to cope with its environment, and the second is how the individual attempts to cope. Examples of an animal failing to cope with its environment may involve mortality and a reduction in reproductive success. Examples of attempting to cope with the environment may include an increase in heart rate, glucocorticoid levels in the blood, and abnormal behaviors, such as stereotypic behaviors and/or self-inflicted injury (Broom, 1986).

Negative behaviors and self-inflicted injury behaviors in poultry are seen as feather pecking and picking. Feather pecking is when the bird pecks at the feathers, head, and/or wings of a conspecific. Feather picking is when the bird pecks at itself. In chickens, there are degrees of which feather pecking is described, based on how much damage it causes (Jones et al., 2004; van Staaveren and Harlander, 2020). Stress is a key factor to induce poor welfare, so understanding the physiological mechanisms behind the stress response is essential for assessing how animals cope with environmental challenges.

Neuroendocrinology of stress

A stress response begins when the central nervous system perceives a potential threat to homeostasis (Moberg, 2000). Whether the stress is acute or chronic depends on the intensity and duration of said stressors (Scanes, 2016). The General Adaptation Syndrome, originally developed by Seyle described three phases (reviewed by Tan and Yip, 2018) to the physiological processes associated with adaptation to changing environments: 1) the Alarm stage, what we now know is the sympathetic nervous system, 2) Resistance Stage, which we now know is the S/HPA response, and 3) Exhaustion Stage, which is when an animal does not adapt and becomes injured, sick or dies. The stress response consists of physiological and behavioral coping mechanisms that consist of two branches. The first branch is the sympathomedullary pathway (SAM; also known as the fight-or-flight response), which is also known as the sympathetic nervous system. The SAM system is governed by two signal transducers, epinephrine and norepinephrine released from the adrenal medulla and postganglionic fibers, respectively. This pathway responds to acute stress and increases heart rate and blood pressure (Cannon, 1929). Activation of the sympathetic nervous system can also occur during other types of stimuli; for example, a bird may have a small increase in heart rate when it hears a feeding call of its conspecific during exercise, or reproduction (Thompson et al., 1968). The second branch is the activation, in birds, of the septo-hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (SHPA) axis, which results in glucocorticoid (GC) release (Gross and Siegel, 1973; Sapolsky et al., 2000; Kuenzel and Jurkevich, 2010; Kuenzel et al., 2012; Kang and Kuenzel, 2014; Nagarajan et al., 2017). The SHPA axis is the avian equivalent of the mammalian hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. When the SHPA axis is activated in birds, corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and arginine vasotocin (AVT) are released from the septum and hypothalamus, respectively, each of which stimulates the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from the anterior pituitary gland, which then stimulates the secretion of GCs, which is corticosterone for birds and rodents and cortisol for mammals, from the cortical cells of the adrenal glands (deRoos, 1961; Kuenzel and Jurkevich, 2010). An increase in plasma corticosterone, in conjunction with other welfare measurements, may be used to indicate when and to what degree a bird is experiencing stress. In order to understand the neurobiology of GCs, there needs to be an understanding of the three levels of physiological regulation: homeostasis, allostasis, and rheostasis. Cannon (1929) defined homeostasis as maintaining constant internal conditions (such as blood pressure or blood glucose levels). Homeostasis involves moment to moment regulation of physiological factors to maintain life and includes a great many different systems, including both the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. Allostasis is the adaptive, short-term process that alters internal parameters in response to stressors (Sterling and Eyer, 1988; McEwen and Wingfield, 2003). Allostasis is more of the genetically driven response to an immediate change; the SHPA axis response to a stressor is an excellent example. Lastly, rheostasis is the longer shifts in baseline set points, allowing the animal to maintain stability across changing environments (Mrosovsky, 1990); also described as a resetting of physiological zero. While allostasis and rheostasis are best known for their role in adaptation, it is important to point out that their primary roles are in daily, moment-to-moment regulation of energy homeostasis. The sympathetic system is also involved in non-stressors such as salivation, reproduction, and cardiovascular activity related to energetics; while glucocorticoids are primarily involved with basal metabolic rates. Thus, using these variables as primary measures of welfare or stress can easily lead to misinterpretation. Further, it is critical that poultry scientists accurately interpret data, such as changes in glucocorticoid levels, as an actual allostatic response to stress vs a rheostatic change in baseline secretion.

Septo-Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis. Mammals synthesize and release CRH from neurons that primarily originate in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN). In the avian brain however, CRH is primarily released from the septum, and AVT is primarily released from the PVN of the hypothalamus. Both neurohormones travel through the hypophyseal-portal blood system to the anterior pituitary. During acute stress, CRH is released from the nucleus of the hippocampal commissure (NHpC) in the septum and the PVN in the hypothalamus and AVT is released primarily from the PVN (Kuenzel and Golden, 2006; Aman et al., 2016; Nagarajan et al., 2017). Under food deprivation, the NHpC shows early CRH expression to that stressor where the PVN shows signaling that peaks hours after the onset of stress (Kadhim et al., 2019; Tonissen et al., 2022). Admittedly, the role of the NHpC in regulating the avian stress response I complicated and requires much more study. CRH and AVT have a synergistic effect on ACTH release but the differential regulation of CRH and AVT release themselves is not clear (Cornett et al., 2013). CRH neurons in the NHpC appear to be activated prior to the PVN in birds during a stressor (Nagarajan et al., 2017); whether this involves stimulating AVT in the PVN, or the small population of CRH neurons in the PVN, or direct affects ACTH release in the anterior pituitary still remains to be elucidated.

In the avian anterior pituitary, CRH binds two G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs): CRH receptor type 1 (CRH-R1) and CRH receptor type 2 (CRH-R2) (Cornett et al., 2013). AVT binds four GPCRs: vasotocin receptor type 1 (VT1R), vasotocin receptor type 2 (VTR2), vasotocin receptor type 3 (VTR3), and vasotocin receptor type 4 (VTR4), where VTR2 is the primary receptor affiliated with the stress response (Cornett et al., 2013). These receptors are shown to regulate the release of ACTH from the anterior pituitary (Castro et al., 1986; Jurkevich et al., 2005). CRH and AVT work synergistically to release ACTH. This hormone has roles in regulating blood pressure, blood glucose, and circadian rhythms (Castro et al., 1986). Under severe dehydration, the mammalian PVN has been shown to release antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which is an orthologue to AVT, to increase ACTH and retain water in the kidney. ACTH travels through the blood to bind to melanocortin 2 receptor (MC2R) on receptive cells, including the adrenal cortical cells to release GCs (Kalliecharan, 1981; Ling et al., 2004).

The SHPA in birds like the HPA in mammals is controlled by negative feedback, in which GC release should reduce HPA activity (Sapolsky et al., 2000). When assessing SHPA response in poultry to stress, care should be taken when samples are collected in relation to the onset of the stressor. Research that focuses on GC release during transportation has reported increase, decrease, and no effect on plasma concentrations of corticosterone (Scanes, 2016). This uncertainty may be due to the lack of uniformity in collection time where previous studies may have captured or missed the peak hormone release. Also, careful consideration should be given to the sex of the bird as females and males show differing responses to stress as shown in zebra finches (Katz et al., 2008; Khan and Robert, 2013), cockatoos (Walsh et al., 1985), chickens (Madison et al., 2008), and ducks (Tetel et al., 2022; Tonissen et al., 2022).

Glucocorticoids (GC). GCs play a key role in restoring homeostasis following exposure to stress, they also modulate important physiological responses such as ion transport, glycogenolysis, immune response, and memory, as well as have anti-inflammatory, anti-allergic, and immune-suppressive roles in the body (Coutinho and Chapman, 2011; Finsterwald and Alberini, 2014). GCs are released in a pulsatile manner throughout the day and are necessary for normal bodily function, though any form of stress is an acute inducer of GC secretion (Sarabdjitsingh et al., 2012). Circulating GCs influence protein, glucose, and fat metabolism to facilitate an increase in energy expenditure, particularly in response to stressful events (Timmermans et al., 2019). This process involves elevating blood glucose levels through gluconeogenesis, enhancing protein availability, and increasing the release of free fatty acids. In conclusion, the acute stress response, mediated by the activation of the SAM pathway and the SHPA axis, plays a vital role in an animal’s ability to cope with stress. While GCs are essential for restoring homeostasis and supporting metabolic changes, chronic or excessive stress can have detrimental effects on the animal’s overall health and welfare. Understanding the physiological mechanisms behind stress responses can help improve management strategies to minimize stress and improve animal welfare. An increase in GCs in birds leads to a commonly used indirect measure of stress in poultry, the heterophil to lymphocyte ratio.

Heterophil to Lymphocyte Ratio (HLR). GCs are known to activate birds’ immune system and alter immune function (Scanes, 2016). Leukocytes are white blood cells in the avian immune system that can be divided into three categories with specialized functions (Maxwell, 1993). One category is granulocytes that include heterophils (functional equivalent to neutrophils in mammals), eosinophils, and basophils. Heterophils are responsible for defending against bacteria via phagocytosis and are known to migrate into the blood from lymph tissue following a stress event (Gross and Siegel, 1983). Lymphocytes are from another category of leukocytes that include cells that recognize and destroy pathogens. They are known to migrate into tissues from blood while under stress (Gross and Siegel, 1983). One method to indirectly assess a stress response is to collect blood and measure heterophil to lymphocyte ratio (HLR). HLR has been used to assess poultry stress but in an indirect manner.

Chickens who were fed corticosterone increase heterophils and decrease lymphocytes in the blood, which supports the concept of an overall increase in HLR during stress (Gross and Siegel, 1983; Broom, 2006). A similar study found that HLR was increased during transportation and that “pleasant” human contact before transport can help to reduce HLR during transportation (Alaqil et al., 2013). After administering lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as an immune challenge, HLR and corticosterone were increased showing how an activated immune system redistributes white blood cells (Shini et al., 2008). Throughout the years, HLR has been thought of as being more stable and informative for chronic stress (Bin-Jumah et al., 2020), but several studies have validated the use of HLR during acute stress in poultry (Mack et al., 2013; Mohammed et al., 2021; Prieto and Campo, 2010; Wang et al., 2018), and more evidence suggests that it is best suited for acute responses given its limited sensitivity to stressor intensity and duration (Shini et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2022). HLR is an avian stress indicator that can be measured to understand the immune response under stressful conditions but has also been shown to fluctuate during reproductive activation (Caliva et al., 2026), and in return should be used alongside other physiological and behavioral measures to increase its reliability.

Measuring animal welfare

Welfare Assessments. There are different science-based tools to measure animal welfare and convert those measures into accessible and understandable information (Blokhuis, 2008). For poultry, these tools include the Welfare Quality® Assessment Protocol for Poultry (Welfare Quality, 2009), Animal Welfare Indicators Network turkey welfare assessment protocol (AWIN, 2015), and the body condition scoring system for ducks (Jones and Dawkins, 2010). The Welfare Quality® Assessment Protocol for Poultry includes welfare assessments for broilers from rearing to end of life, and the production stage of laying hens (Welfare Quality, 2009). It focuses on four main principles of animal welfare, including (1) good feeding, (2) good housing, (3) good health and (4) appropriate behavior. These four main principles are divided into 12 criteria, each assessed using standardized protocols and integrated into an overall welfare score (Welfare Quality, 2009). The AWIN turkey welfare assessment protocol is a welfare assessment protocol for growing turkeys in farming systems. It follows the same four main principles of animal welfare as the Welfare Quality® Assessment Protocol for Poultry and is designed to be practical for on-farm assessments (AWIN, 2015). While the Welfare Quality® and AWIN protocols provide comprehensive welfare assessments for chickens and turkeys, respectively, the development of species-specific tools, such as the body condition scoring (BCS) rubric for ducks, allows for more detailed and targeted evaluations of duck welfare.

Jones and Dawkins (2010) developed the first duck BCS system that looks at eye, nostril, and feather condition, along with posture, feather cleanliness, stubby quill, footpad dermatitis, and callous toe. Karcher et al. (2013) and Fraley et al. (2013) improved the scoring system evaluation to include eye, nostril, heel and toe pad, and feather condition, as well as feather cleanliness, and gait at individual bird level. The BCS rubric is on a scale of 0-2 for eyes, feather condition, heel and toe pad condition, and gait with, where 0 is the best or ideal condition and a 1 indicates a worsening condition and a 2 indicates the worst condition for the specific quality. BCS provides information about the behavior of the ducks quickly, as opposed to watching behavior videos after the fact (Fraley et al., 2013; Colton and Fraley, 2014; Schenk et al., 2016; Oluwagbenga et al., 2022, 2023; Schober et al., 2023; Bergman et al., 2024b; Tonissen et al., 2025). If ducks in a barn are experiencing a high score in feather cleanliness, this may mean they are not properly preening themselves. If they are experiencing a high score in feather condition, this may mean that they are experiencing feather picking and/or pecking (Colton and Fraley, 2014). It is critical to understand when ducks display unwanted behaviors or exhibit a decrease in natural behaviors so that producers can quickly determine the cause in order to correct the issue.

Affective States. Another measure of welfare is to consider the animal’s mood and emotions, referred to as their affective state (Jirkof et al., 2019). The basis for animals’ emotions can be tracked to the 1800′s when Charles Darwin wrote about his observations of environmental interaction with the animal’s responses (Darwin, 1872; Briefer, 2012). These observations suggest that animals are attentive and possess the ability to react to positive and negative stimuli. Emotions are a basic system that allows an animal to understand and interact with its environment by choosing actions that are associated with pleasant emotions and avoiding actions associated with unpleasant emotions (Briefer, 2012). The goal of animal welfare assessments is to reduce negative affective states, such as suffering, pain, fear, and hunger, and to promote positive affective states, such as feelings like comfort, contentment, and pleasure (Fraser et al., 1997). Measuring affective states can include physiological measurements like heart rate, glucocorticoid levels, and brain neurotransmitter activity, in combination with behavior measurements. Interpretation of these measurements can be difficult, as they could reflect an emotion that has positive or negative valence (Mendl et al., 2010; Crump et al., 2020). For example, high arousal, which can be explained by high heart rate and/or an increase in glucocorticoid secretion, can be associated with an escape from a predator (negative valence) or an anticipation of a reward (positive valence). While affective states are often assessed through behavioral and physiological measures, research has also focused on neurotransmitter activity in the brain as a key indicator of emotional and welfare states (Braganza and Wilson, 1978; Blanchard et al., 1991; Lucas and McMillen, 2002; Ren-Patterson et al., 2006; Ahmed et al., 2017; Bergman et al., 2024, 2025).

Methods to determine affective state and the level of fear and suffering are currently being developed. Affective state can be described through measuring physiology, behavior, and cognition (Jacobs et al., 2022). Cognitive bias testing is a popular measure for affective state that evaluates how an animal thinks and how that affects their behavior and choices. Cognitive bias testing for starlings explored affective state and optimism within enriched colony cages (Matheson et al., 2008). Laying hens in enriched and conventional cages were subjected to cognitive bias testing where no differences were found in fear level and anticipation behavior (Wichman et al., 2012). This method uses behavior to assess emotional state, which can create unclear results as behavior and emotion are related but there are various factors that could alter the birds’ responses. To understand an animal’s mental state, investigating neuroendocrinology allows researchers to assess quantitative and repeatable measures of welfare.

Neuroendocrinology of Affective State. Neurotransmitters and neuromodulators are the active regulators of all brain function, including an animal’s mental state. Within the brain, there is a balance of excitatory and inhibitory signals that control cognition, emotion, and homeostasis (Tsuboi et al., 2024). These signals are created by neurotransmitters and neuromodulators interacting with their ionotropic or metabotropic receptors. Ionotropic receptors bind to a signal ligand that causes a conformation change in the receptor to immediately open or close an ion channel (Dai, 2023). This signal transduction is usually fast acting and allows ions to flow across a membrane and activate or inhibit an action potential. Metabotropic receptors, also called a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR), have a large intracellular signal cascade that can change gene expression and enzyme activity. These receptors can produce a longer-lasting response with intracellular signaling and transcription factors that have roles in behavior, homeostasis, and even neurodegenerative diseases and psychiatric disorders (Wong et al., 2023), including actions that can alter affective state.

The two most predominate neurotransmitters studied that are associated with affective state in numerous species include serotonin (5-HT) and dopamine (DA) (Schildkraut and Kety, 1967; Thierry et al., 1976; Morgan et al., 1987; Kaneyuki et al., 1991; Olguín et al., 2016; Micheli et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2020; Tsuboi et al., 2024). 5-HT and DA are synthesized in neuronal cell bodies in the midbrain and their axons extend outward into the telencephalon (TEL), diencephalon (DI) and rhombencephalon (RE) (Kuenzel and Masson, 1988; Alenina and Klempin, 2015). After synthesis in the presynaptic neuron, 5-HT and DA are stored in vesicles and released into the synapse. This release is based upon the frequency of action potential arriving at the synaptic bouton (axon terminal). There are multiple fates for neurotransmitters in the synapse, such as binding receptors on the post-synaptic neuron to create a signal cascade that may lead to the alteration of brain signaling and mental state. Also, neurotransmitters can be stored in glial cells to be catabolized or saved for later use. They can be recycled via re-uptake transporters where neurotransmitters re-enter the pre-synaptic neuron to be catabolized or re-released into the synapse. Another fate includes being catabolized by enzymes expressed by the post-synaptic neuron. To correctly understand the activity of these neurotransmitters, 5-HT and DA turnover should be evaluated in addition to their static levels. Neurotransmitter turnover is a ratio of metabolites to respective neurotransmitter levels that provides an understanding of how neurotransmitters are metabolized within the synapse. Thus, turnover is conversely related to neurotransmitter concentration; as the turnover decreases, there is a higher activity of the neurotransmitter in the synapse. Therefore, an increase in turnover reflects a decrease in synaptic activity of 5-HT or DA. Turnovers provide a measure of how these neurotransmitters are utilized to alter cognition or emotional activity and not just measuring neurotransmitters that are stored and inactive. Investigating neurotransmitter turnover as it relates to affective state can provide novel insight into neuroscience and how the brain relates to welfare (Mendl et al., 2010).

Serotonin (5-HT). A major neurotransmitter of mental health in humans and our pets that has piqued pharmacological interest is 5-HT. This is due to 5-HT’s role in homeostasis and mood regulation. Neurons that synthesize 5-HT are in the brainstem raphe nuclei and include the caudal mesencephalon (CM) and rostral mesencephalon (RM) (Alenina and Klempin, 2015; Kanova and Kohout, 2021). In mammals it is well established that the rostral raphe nuclei (the dorsal raphe and median raphe) and the caudal raphe nuclei (the raphe magnus, raphe obscurus, and raphe pallidus) project to the limbic, cortical, hypothalamic areas, and the spinal cord and brainstem, respectively; functionally modulating mood, anxiety, sleep‑wake cycles, and memory and regulating pain, motor control, and autonomic functions. However, these pathways are not as well established in birds, nor are the exact specific structures of the limbic system. These neurons utilize tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), the rate-limiting enzyme, to synthesize 5-HT from the amino acid, tryptophan. TPH has two isoforms, TPH1 and TPH2. TPH1 is primarily expressed in the peripheral tissues, while TPH2 is expressed in the central nervous system. 5-HT interacts with at least seven metabotropic receptors, each of which is a classified family with distinct subtypes and functions (Micheli et al., 2018; Tsuboi et al., 2024). A selective transporter (SERT) and the 5-HT1A receptor are responsible for re-uptake into the pre-synaptic neurons. These are pharmacological targets of selective serotonin re-uptake inhibitors (SSRIs) to allow 5-HT to bind to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron and increase signal transduction on the postsynaptic membrane, that is, synaptic activity. A mechanism that leads to neurological decline is the reduction of available 5-HT within the synaptic clefts or in the presynaptic synthesis. In the raphe nuclei, 5-HT1A receptors are located on the cell bodies and dendrites of 5-HT-producing neurons. When 5-HT is released from these neurons binds to these presynaptic autoreceptors, it triggers negative feedback that inhibits further 5-HT release (Wang et al., 2019). New medications including fluoxetine and dapoxetine are SSRIs that promote 5-HT activity in the synapse by downregulating SERT and 5-HT1A expressions (Goodnick and Goldstein, 1998). Without available 5-HT, mammals have been categorized with depressive moods and increased anxiety (Goodnick and Goldstein, 1998). These affective states can also be indicated by an increase in 5-HT metabolism.

5-HT can be metabolized into 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5HIAA) by the enzyme monoamine oxidase (MAO) (Correia and Vale, 2022). 5-HT activity within a synapse can be measured using turnover equations that ratios neurotransmitter to its metabolites. 5-HT turnover equals the concentration of 5HIAA divided by the concentration of 5-HT (Ahmed and Azmat, 2017; Correia and Vale, 2022). The increase in turnover is due to an increase of 5HIAA or decrease in 5-HT levels, which is related to depression and poor welfare in mammals (Barton et al., 2008). Human depression patients are known to have high 5-HT turnover and when administered SSRIs, 5-HT turnover significantly decreased, thus increasing 5-HT synaptic activity (Barton et al., 2008). Another measure of serotonergic activity is the gene expression of TPH. Two isoforms of this enzyme (TPH1 & TPH2) have been described in the human, mouse, and rat genomes (Walther et al., 2003). TPH1 is responsible for 5-HT synthesis within the gut, pineal gland, spleen, and thymus and TPH2 is primarily in the raphe nuclei in the brainstem (Walther and Bäder, 2003). TPH2 mRNA gene expression is known to be increased in depressive states while 5-HT turnover increases in humans (Lowry et al., 2008; Zhu et al., 2021). An increase in gene expression would suggest that the brain is producing more synthetic enzymes to increase 5-HT synthesis, however the exact relationship to gene expression and NT production remains elusive. Gene expression related to affective state has not been investigated in Pekin ducks extensively. Bergman et al. (2025) did show that TPH1 and TPH2 were expressed in duck brains, with TPH1 being expressed only in hens. Kang et al. (2023) showed that TPH2 is expressed in broiler chicken brains, specifically in the caudal and dorsal raphe nucleus of the brainstem. These previous studies are limited, and conclusions are largely inferred from mammalian studies. Due to the lack of previous literature reporting on the distribution of TPH1 and TPH2 gene expression in birds, studies should report on both isoforms to avoid unintentional misinterpretation of data.

Dopamine (DA). DA plays a significant role in the excitability of neurons and controls emotion, motor function, motivation, anxiety, aggression, and resource guarding. Altered activity of DA is related to many neurophysiological disorders (Tsuboi et al., 2024). When DA activity is inconsistent, an individual can develop adverse neuronal signaling that can lead to pathologies such as Parkinson’s disease, addiction, impulsive behavior, hyperactivity, or inappropriate aggression. The midbrain is an area rich in DA synthesis including structures such as the substantia nigra (SN) and the ventral tegmental area (VTA) (Olguín et al., 2016). Although there are additional sources of DA synthesis in the hypothalamus related to reproductive function and behavior. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) is the synthetic enzyme that produces DA from the amino acid tyrosine (Kumer and Vrana, 1996). Neuronal cell bodies located in those structures extend into the brain along four main axonal pathways. The mesolimbic pathway is associated with reward and addiction (Nestler and Carlezon, 2006; Cao et al., 2010; Chaudhury et al., 2013; Grace, 2016). The mesocortical pathway is involved in cognitive functions such as memory and decision-making (Le Moal and Simon, 1991; Tidey and Miczek, 1996). The tuberoinfundibular pathways project from the hypothalamus to the median eminence to control secretion of hormones such as gonadotropin releasing hormone and prolactin (Demarest and Moore, 1979; Gudelsky, 1981). Finally, the nigrostriatal pathway regulates motor coordination and motor initiation (Demarest and Moore, 1979; Robertson and Robertson, 1989; Timmerman and Abercrombie, 1996; Bergquist et al., 2003). These pathways are essential for dopaminergic activity and may influence animal behaviors such as reward, aggression, and mental health. DA is a key regulator of mood disorders and depression (Diehl and Gershon, 1992). Decreased DA release and receptor antagonists have been shown to produce less mobility and increased feelings of sadness in human patients (Diehl and Gershon, 1992).

The TH gene regulates DA levels within the brain and can be used as a target to improve Parkinson’s Disease, which is the dysregulation and deficiency of DA due to neuronal death in the substantia nigra (Nagatsu et al., 2022). TH may prevent the degradation of DA neurons and increase DA synthesis to supplement the brain (Nagatsu et al., 2022). Mice lacking a functional TH gene fail to develop, suggesting that DA is imperative to natural growth and brain development (Kumer and Vrana, 1996). DA may increase as individuals age after birth/hatch to promote natural behavioral development and to prepare the brain for the onset of puberty, such as an activation of neuroendocrine reproductive signaling (Davison and Kuenzel, 1991; Fraley and Kuenzel, 1993a, 1993b).

DA’s metabotropic receptors are grouped into D1-like receptors that create excitatory signals by increasing cytoplasmic cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) activity and D2-like receptors that can elicit inhibitory signals by decreasing cAMP (Tsuboi et al., 2024). The levels of cAMP mediate activity of protein kinase A signaling, which phosphorylates target proteins (Tsuboi et al., 2024). DA’s interaction with MAO produces the metabolite DOPAC and further interaction with catechol-O-methyl transferase (COMT) produces the metabolite homovanillic acid (HVA), and if the primary interaction is with COMT, then the metabolite 3-MT is produced (Olguín et al., 2016). DA turnover adds the concentrations of HVA, 3-MT, and DOPAC to be divided by the concentration of DA.

Neurotransmitter Link to Aggression. Aggressive behavior is regulated by neurobiology parameters such as gene expression, action potential frequency, and neurotransmitter activity (van Erp and Miczek, 2000). Neurotransmitters, 5-HT and DA, affiliated with affective state may alter states of aggression (van Erp and Miczek, 2000). The act of aggression can be neuronally initiated by various variables including neurotransmitters, enzymes, hormones, cytokines and even growth factors (Nelson and Chiavegatto, 2001). Aggressive behavior is indicative of a negative affective state but is ultimately a survival behavior that allows individuals to defend resources, establish a hierarchy, and to maintain mating opportunities (Yamaguchi and Lin, 2018). Behavior can be altered by the brain with changes to neurotransmitter turnover and by changing receptor expression on postsynaptic membranes (Nelson and Chiavegatto, 2001).

5-HT’s relationship with aggression is unclear and needs further research, but previous literature reports differences in its turnover that relate to increased feather pecking behavior in chickens (van Hierden et al., 2004). 5-HT turnover is an indicator of serotonergic activity in chickens (Dennis et al., 2008; Kops et al., 2013). Chickens with severe feather pecking behavior showed an increased 5-HT turnover, thus decreased synaptic activity, when compared to chickens that show low feather pecking behavior (Kops et al., 2013). In addition, van Gierden et al. (2002, 2004) reported that high feather pecking genetic strains showed an increased 5-HT turnover and low 5-HT activity when compared to low feather pecking strains. The use of 5-HT1A and 5-HT1B antagonists may elicit increased feather pecking behavior compared to chickens administered saline due to decreasing 5-HT receptor binding and therefore, activity (Dennis et al., 2008; de Haas and Eijk, 2018). The reduced serotonergic activity minimizes 5-HT signaling to alter brain function and may lead to abnormal behaviors and negative welfare. This suggests that 5-HT may be an inhibitory signal for aggression and consideration of 5-HT activity should be investigated as a method to reduce feather pecking or resource guarding.

DA’s relationship with aggression has been investigated for decades but our understanding of its role in aggression is still rudimentary. DA may alter behavior by changing signaling within the brain and can be recognized as a mediator of aggression (Hasselager et al., 1972). The mesolimbic pathway for DA is used to investigate the reward and reinforcement of aggressive behaviors. This pathway can shed light on the relationship between how DA functions to elicit aggression and how defending a resource can become rewarding and addictive (Yamaguchi and Lin, 2018). Increased DA activity in the brain was reported to elicit aggression in mice (Hasselager et al., 1972) and rats (van Erp and Miczek, 2000). D1 and D2 receptor antagonists are the current treatment to suppress aggressive behavior in humans with psychotic conditions by reducing DA receptor activity (Yamaguchi and Lin, 2018). In chickens, increased DA concentrations are related to aggression (Cheng et al., 2003). Highly aggressive strains of laying hens with D1 and D2 receptor antagonists have reported decreased feather pecking compared to hens without the antagonist (Kjaer et al., 2004; Dennis and Cheng, 2011). To fully investigate the connection among affective state, neurotransmitters and aggression, research needs to consider multiple factors including neurobiology, genetics, receptor availability, and environmental management. Understanding how the brain initiates and responds to aggression is imperative to mitigating aggressive behavior and providing the animal with a safe environment.

Neurotransmitter Link to Stress. Stress is a physiological response that disrupts homeostasis and activates biological systems to attempt to maintain internal equilibrium (Scanes, 2016). Immobilization stress in rats has been shown to increase 5-HT turnover which is associated with a decrease in 5-HT activity while increasing TPH2 expression tenfold (Morgan et al., 1975; Chamas et al., 1999). During a period of stress, serotonergic activity decreases and gene expression responds by increasing TPH-2 levels to replace the metabolized 5-HT in order to return the individual to homeostasis.

DA from the VTA is related to reward-related behaviors that are important for survival, such as feeding and sex, and can also be associated with addictive-like behaviors associated with drugs or resources in humans (Polter and Kauer, 2014). The dopaminergic system can be enhanced or inhibited within the hypothalamic and mesolimbic pathways to cope and respond to a stressor (Pani et al., 2000; Baik, 2020). These pathways modify behaviors and produce neuronal signaling to cope with stressful events (Baik, 2020). DA can further be converted into other catecholamines, norepinephrine and then epinephrine, in neurons or glands that contain the respective synthetic enzymes. Norepinephrine and epinephrine can then respond to the stressor and stimulate a release of glucocorticoids (GC) indirectly through its effects on the HPA axis, particularly by modulating hypothalamic activity (Belujon and Grace, 2015). The dopaminergic system may increase its activity during acute stressors where plasma corticosterone is elevated (Thierry et al., 1976; Herman et al., 1982; Baik, 2020). While stress can alter behavior as animals cope with a stressor, chronic stress may dysregulate the DA system by reducing receptor affinity, leading to addiction-like behaviors and increased aggression (Pani et al., 2000). Psychological stress may induce an increase in DA turnover in rats to cope with fear and uncertainty within an unfamiliar environment (Kaneyuki et al., 1991).

Stress is a complex individual experience that activates multiple pathways within the body including brain signaling and GC response. Investigating stress and welfare together is important to understand an animal’s mental state. Increasing our understanding of how the brain responds to stress is central to mitigating stressors and developing strategies to minimize stressful situations within the production system.

Impact of transportation stress in poultry

Transportation is a common event in the life cycle of production livestock. Ducks can be transported multiple times during production including from hatchery to developer to breeder farm to processing facility (Tonissen et al., 2022). Pekin ducks are herded onto trucks and transported approximately 60-120 minutes to processing facilities. Stress, whether it is acute or chronic, can cause various physiological responses based on intensity, length, and severity of the stress (Scanes, 2016). Transportation is associated with an unfamiliar environment, confinement, change in temperature and humidity, deprivation of food and water, and unknown motions/vibrations, which prevent an animal from being content and coping with its environment (Fazio, 2003). Transportation has been associated with reduction in body weight, altered behavior, and decreased welfare (Siddharth et al., 2024). This process is a biological stressor associated with standard production practices that have been shown to alter concentrations of corticosterone in chickens and may alter the affective state of poultry (Scanes, 2016).

The release of GCs, corticosterone and cortisol, during stress has been reported often but many studies report conflicting results. Chickens who experienced pre-transportation handling express higher corticosterone levels than only transported chickens (Vošmerová et al., 2010). Alaqil et al. (2013) found that transportation increases corticosterone levels, but pleasant human contact before transportation can reduce stress, as indicated by a decrease in both the heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (HLR) and corticosterone levels. Other research has indicated that corticosterone levels in chickens are unaffected by crating alone and transportation (Kannan et al., 1997; Zhang et al., 2009). Shipping stress for Pekin ducks showed increased corticosterone and cortisol levels in hens when compared to drakes (Tetel et al., 2022). Before and after transportation, Pekin duck hens had significantly increased secretion of cortisol and corticosterone when compared to drakes, but no significant difference due to transportation (Tonissen et al., 2022). In addition, Tonissen et al. (2022) demonstrated increased Fos expression, which is a marker of neuronal activity, in the PVN during and 24-hours after transportation combined with a novel environment suggests that transportation stimulates neuronal activation of neuroendocrine pathways that may be involved with chronic stress, as did the sustained CORT levels 24 hours after transportation shown in that same study. Bergman et al. (2025) investigated crate and transportation stress in adult breeder Pekin ducks by measuring serum corticosterone, HLR, and neurotransmitter synaptic activity with the use of mass spectrometry. Corticosterone concentrations increased in crated hens and in both transported hens and drakes, with a similar increase in the heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (HLR) observed in crated hens and transported birds of both sexes. These findings suggest that hens are more susceptible to the stress of crating, whereas transportation disrupts homeostasis in both hens and drakes. Although DA turnover did not differ between treatments in either sex, only hens exhibited increased 5-HT turnover in the CM and RM, both from control to crate and from crate to transportation. This serotonergic response supports the physiological evidence that hens are more responsive to crating and transportation than drakes and suggests that these stressors may induce a negative affective state during transportation. Multiple physiological measures have now suggested that duck hens are more responsive to stressors than are drakes, and 5-HT activity may yet be another. To increase animal welfare, transportation stress should continue to be investigated to mitigate the negative effects on welfare while taking care to understand the effect of sex on results.

One area in need of further research is the difference in physiological stress response for females and males. In rodents and avian species, females are shown to produce higher responses to stress including higher corticosterone (Kant et al., 1983; Haleem et al., 1988; Madison et al., 2008; Small, 2015). To continue assessing poultry species' responses to stress, research should evaluate the regulation of GC release, neurobiology and how sex and/or age may play a role. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that transportation and handling can alter physiological and neurobiological indicators of stress in poultry, although the magnitude of these responses may depend on factors such as species, sex, and management practices. As interest in improving poultry welfare has grown, considerable attention has been directed toward management strategies that reduce stress and promote positive welfare, including environmental enrichment.

Improving animal welfare

Environmental Enrichment for Poultry. Animal welfare is about reducing negative experiences and promoting positive experiences and one method to obtain positive welfare has been the use of environmental enrichment (EE). Environmental enrichment was defined by Newberry (1995) as “an improvement in the biological functioning of captive animals resulting from modifications to their environment.” This definition was later broadened by van de Weerd and Day (2009) into a framework of successful environmental enrichment, which includes four criteria: increasing species-specific behavior, maintaining or improving health of the animals, improving production efficiency and economics, and practicality of applying it in the production systems. EE can assist animals in coping with their environment by offering novel and interactive resources (Broom, 1991). The benefits of adding enrichments include providing the animal with a sense of control to interact with the enrichment at their own pace while being rewarded afterwards (Jacobs et al., 2022). Also, enrichment allows animals to explore their enclosures while participating in environmental investigations that may reduce stereotypic behaviors and possibly reduce aggression (Jacobs et al., 2022). Mellen and Sevenich MacPhee (2011) stated that the critical elements of effective environmental enrichments are 1) assessing the animal’s natural history, individual history, and enclosure restraints and 2) providing species-appropriate opportunities; the animal should have some choices within its environment. EE is used to enhance animals’ welfare by enhancing natural behavior, reducing stress and aggression, and improving physical health. EE has also been shown to improve learning and memory (Renner and Rosenzweig, 1987). Early studies on EE have shown that EE increases neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine as well as enhances the expression of the gene for serotonin A1 receptor (Por et al., 1982; Rasmuson et al., 1998). EE improves many variables of an animal’s life, including overall welfare, and when animal welfare improves, an increase in production and performance can also occur.

Types of environmental enrichment and their uses for poultry

There are many different types of EE for poultry, each meeting diverse behavioral needs. Pecking blocks stimulate curiosity and reduce boredom and abnormal behaviors (Zimmerman et al., 2011), while foraging substrates encourage natural pecking and scratching (Rodenburg et al., 2013). Perches encourage roosting, improve bone strength, and reduce aggression (Norring et al., 2016), whereas platforms increase vertical space utilization and encourage movement (Campbell et al., 2016). Dust baths allow the birds to engage in natural dust-bathing behavior (Widowski and Duncan, 2000). Decades of research have examined the effects of EE on chicken production and welfare, particularly regarding perches and platforms. Elsayed et al. (2024) found that broilers housed with either elevated platforms, straw bales, or in increased distance between the feeder and the drinker exhibited an increase in preening, wing flapping, and floor pecking compared to broilers in the control group. Matkovic et al. (2019) found that fast-growing broilers housed with perches had fewer incidences of hock burn and footpad dermatitis. Bailie et al. (2018) observed that broilers preferred platform perches over bars or ramps and noted that overall perch availability did not improve leg health. Similarly, de Jong et al. (2019) reported that broilers with perches exhibited more resting behaviors than those without. Yang et al. (2020) reported that fast-growing broilers housed with mesh platforms had fewer incidences of footpad dermatitis. Ghani et al. (2025) reported that broilers housed with hanging bottles or perches had the lowest feed intake compared to broilers in the control group, resulting in an improved feed conversion ratio.

Research on turkey environmental enrichment has shown that various EE modifications can influence behavior and welfare. Dong et al. (2023) concluded that providing turkeys with straw bales and tunnels can improve wing feather quality, while straw bales may also reduce the development of footpad dermatitis. Sherwin et al. (1999) found that suspending various pecking substrates from the ceiling significantly reduced injuries caused by wing, tail and head-pecking, in addition to increasing the latency to sit after 2.5 min of forced standing. Similarly, Martrenchar et al. (2001) reported that metal objects and straw enrichment redirected pecking activity, thereby reducing injurious pecking in young male and female turkeys. Crowe and Forbes (1999) observed that perches and ceiling-suspended objects were more effective at reducing pecking than forage and straw enrichments, though all treatments resulted in less pecking compared to the control. However, Duggen et al. (2014) found that multicolored plastic balls, when used in a commercial setting, were an ineffective form of enrichment for turkeys. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of selecting appropriate enrichment types, as certain materials and structures can effectively reduce injurious pecking while others may offer little benefit.

While extensive research has explored the benefits and limitations of environmental enrichment for chickens, less attention has been given to turkeys, and even less to ducks. Ducks have unique behavioral needs, particularly related to their need for water, as they are waterfowl. They require enrichment strategies that cater to these natural instincts. Understanding how different enrichment types impact duck welfare and production is essential for optimizing their living conditions.

Environmental Enrichment to Prevent Unwanted Behaviors. A major reason for providing poultry with EE is to prevent unwanted behaviors that can negatively impact welfare and production. These unwanted behaviors include feather pecking and feather picking. These behaviors arise in environments where birds experience boredom, stress, or frustration due to limited environmental stimulation. Feather pecking in poultry refers to the pecking at, pulling out, and sometimes eating the feathers of conspecifics. Feather picking is the act of self-mutilation and is most common among psittacines and ducks (Jenkins, 2001; Colton and Fraley, 2014). In chickens and turkeys, feather pecking is categorized into two forms: gentle and severe (Savory, 1995). Severe feather pecking association with DA is described above, but how DA is involved with gentle feather pecking is not known. Are the two types of feather pecking simply the results of a range of DA activity? Gentle feather pecking involves light pecks to the tips of feather, causing minimal damage and often going unnoticed by the recipient. In contrast, severe feather pecking is more harmful, involving forceful pecks that pull-out feathers, which are often eaten. This behavior leads to significant feather loss, particularly on the back, vent, and tail. Victims of severe feather pecking typically respond by either moving away or confronting the pecker (Rodenburg et al., 2013). While ducks may also engage in feather pecking, the distinction between gentle and severe pecking has not generally been made for this species, but regardless of the “type” of feather pecking, the behavior can still cause harm to conspecifics.

Environmental enrichment for ducks

Water-Based Enrichment. Open water, which includes troughs and baths, for Pekin ducks has been a controversial topic for welfare scientists and producers, as it allows the ducks to fully climb into and swim. It is thought to increase natural behaviors like wet preening, head-dunking, and swimming. Researchers have been showing contradictory outcomes with the use of open water sources for commercial duck flocks and their effects on health, production, welfare, and housing environment. Although wet versus dry preening is a concept that does not appear in any ornithological literature, these terms have been coined in welfare science to describe waterfowl natural behaviors (Rice et al., 2014; Jones and Dawkins, 2010; Waitt et al., 2009).

One behavior that is not described anywhere in ornithological literature but does appear in welfare science is wet preening. Wet preening has been described as the act of using the bill to apply water directly to the feathers while nibbling or stroking at the feathers or after tossing water over the body (Jones and Dawkins, 2010; Waitt et al., 2009). Ducks use their uropygial gland, also referred to as the “preening gland,” which is a holocrine gland located at the base of the tail, to preen. The oil secreted from the gland helps maintain and waterproof their feathers, while also providing defense against ectoparasites (Moreno-Rueda, 2017). The uropygial gland size can change the properties of the oil secreted which in turn can affect the bird’s health (Moreno-Rueda, 2016), and the size of the gland can be affected by environmental factors (Chiale et al., 2014). However, all birds have uropygial glands, except for some species of psitticines, and all birds utilize water for preening to some degree (Montalti and Salibián, 2000). Interestingly, Montalti and Salibián (2000) also showed after studying the preening gland of over 100 birds from nearly 50 families that there is no correlation between uropygial gland size and amount of time interacting with water. It is interesting to note that the Northern Gannet, a pelagic species, has the smallest relative uropygial gland size of any bird. Thus, the actual relationship between uropygial gland size to “wet” preening is not clear.

Recently, restrictions have prohibited the use of open water pools for raising ducks in some areas of China (Fraley, personal communications with faculty at Sichuan Agricultural University). Little is known about the effects of not providing an open water pool on ducks’ welfare, or the development of the preening gland. Mi et al. (2020) investigated Sanshui white ducks by randomly dividing them into 2 groups, water pool and no water pool. They saw that the lack of access to an open water pool significantly inhibited the growth of the preening gland based on its weight, size, and quantity of oil secretions, (Mi et al., 2020). The decreased preening gland size may be due to the decrease in wet preening, providing little stimulation to the gland. While Mi et al. (2020) highlighted the impact of restricted access to open water on the preening gland, further studies have explored how water depth and length influence ducks’ water usage and behavior, providing additional insight into their preferences and welfare.

Liste et al. (2012) designed a study to perform a choice test to see which depth of water ducks prefer. The 3 depths of water were: 10 cm (where they could stand but not swim), 20 cm (where they could stand and swim), and 30 cm (where they could not stand but could swim). They found no significant differences among the usage of the 10 cm deep pools and the 20 cm deep pools, but the ducks chose to use the 10 cm pools more than the 30 cm pools. They also found that when the water was dirty, ducks spent less time inside the pools, they spent less time sitting during bathing bouts and drank more from the bell drinkers (Liste et al., 2012). These findings suggest that while ducks prefer shallow water for standing and swimming, water cleanliness also plays a significant role in their overall usage and behavior. These observations highlight the importance of maintaining water quality in duck production environments.

Liste et al. (2012b; 2013) also studied 3 different lengths of troughs; narrow (150 cm long, 15 cm wide, 8 cm deep), intermediate (150 cm long, 20 cm wide, 12 cm deep) and wide (100 cm long, 50 cm wide, 8 cm deep), to assess their effects on Pekin duck health, production, water usage and water quality. They found that the only health difference among the groups was that the footpad condition of the ducks housed with the intermediate trough had lower scores than ducks housed with the wide trough. Additionally, the groups showed no differences in their effects on production (Liste et al., 2012b). They also found that intermediate troughs used twice as much water as narrow troughs and wide troughs. Wide troughs presented the worst values for the percentage of dissolved solids (fecal matter), dissolved oxygen, conductivity, and salinity, and wide and narrow troughs contained significantly higher levels of enterococci and total coliforms, while the wide troughs contained the highest levels of E. coli (Liste et al., 2013). The troughs used for these studies provided the same amount of space for each duck (7mm2); however, the different lengths and widths of the troughs provided different volumes of water available to the ducks. This could account for the differing results of water usage. These results emphasize the importance of trough design in influencing water usage and quality, while also showing that troughs may not adversely or positively affect duck production and welfare.

Jones and Dawkins (2009) observed that open water access, specifically troughs, improves eye, feather cleanliness, and footpad condition. The proportion of ducks with clear and clean eyes was lower in ducks housed with nipple drinkers and was positively correlated with clean feathers and higher growth rate. Feather condition was better in houses with troughs and worse in houses with nipple drinkers. The ducks with the best footpads were from houses with bell drinkers followed by those housed with troughs. Ducks housed with nipple drinkers had the highest incidence of heavily soiled feathers, whereas ducks housed with troughs had the cleanest feathers. The incidence of stubbly quill (broken feathers on the breast) was highest in ducks housed with troughs. O’Driscoll and Broom (2011) also saw that ducks housed with narrow-lip bell drinker were dirtier than those housed with a bath, with those in the trough treatment being intermediate. Ducks were less likely to have dirty nostrils when provided with more open water sources and were less likely to have blocked nostrils in the trough and bath treatments than in the narrow-lip bell drinker (O’Driscoll and Broom, 2011). O’Driscoll and Broom (2012) also showed that ducks housed with an open water source performed more preening behaviors and less unwanted behaviors like feather pecking. However, not all studies of open water systems in commercial duck houses have demonstrated benefits.

Contradictorily, Schenk et al. (2016) found that ducks housed with water troughs showed worse scores for eyes, nostrils, feather quality, feather cleanliness, and footpads compared to ducks housed with nipple lines. The water from the water troughs also showed higher iron, nitrites, pH, and bacterial growth, with the bacterial growth showing higher E. coli, coliform bacteria, Staphylococcus genus, and total number of culturable bacteria. Water lines showed no bacterial growth in culture-based assays. Ducks housed with water troughs used greater volumes of water compared to ducks housed with water lines. Ducks with water troughs also showed a greater percent mortality at all ages compared to ducks with water lines. Lowman et al. (2016) found that the water in troughs had elevated coliform and general aerobic colony forming unit (CFU) counts. Ducks housed with a water trough also had increased incidences of footpad lesions, compared to the ducks housed with nipple lines. This may be due to the increase in moisture percentage in the barns with the troughs. The ducks without the troughs also produced significantly more eggs and used less water than the ducks housed with the troughs (Lowman et al., 2016). These conflicting results may be attributed to differences in study design. Liste et al. (2012) and Jones et al. (2009) reported either no differences or positive effects of open water sources on production and health, while conducting their studies with four ducks per pen. In contrast, Schenk et al. (2016) examined a much larger scale, using 1,000 ducks per pen, and Lowman et al. (2016) focused on breeder Pekin ducks, which have a significantly longer lifespan than grow-out Pekin ducks, and both studies found that open-water sources negatively affect Pekin duck health and production. These variations highlight the importance of considering stocking density and duck type when evaluating the effects of open water access on welfare, production, water usage, and water quality.

Alternate Forms of Water Enrichment. There are also alternate forms of open water, like showers and misters. These still provide ducks with water to promote wet preening behaviors, but do not allow ducks to perform swimming or head-dunking behaviors. Waitt et al. (2009) saw that ducks rested more under the showers than the bath and they used the shower more socially, meaning they spent more time under the showers when two or more ducks were there simultaneously. Campbell et al. (2022) found that applying overhead misting to commercial grower ducks for 1 h daily did not affect the overall expression of preening behaviors. Although one study did show that ducks housed with only nipple lines also showed social behaviors centered around nipples while drinking and preening (Rice et al., 2014). Few studies have looked at the effects of water misters on duck welfare, production, and behavior, but many studies have shown water misters mitigate heat stress in broiler chickens (Timmons and Baughman, 1983; Ryder et al., 2004; Liang et al., 2014). While showers and misters provide an alternative to open water by allowing wet preening behaviors, another option, semi-open water sources, offers ducks the opportunity to engage in both wet preening as well as head dunking behaviors without full-body immersion.

Looking at a type of semi-open water source, the preening cup (PC), Schober et al. (2023) saw that ducks housed with the PC performed wet preening behaviors significantly more than ducks housed with a nipple line, though it did not affect the size of their uropygial glands or their feather cleanliness scores. Schober et al. (2023) did see, however, that ducks housed with the PC showed an increase in feather pecking and a decrease in feather quality. Bergman et al. (2024) saw that when there were about 65 ducks per PC, those ducks had an increase in central DA activity and a decrease in DA turnover. DA is associated with affective states, including aggression. Schober et al. (2023) saw an increase in feather pecking (though not significant) and concluded that the PCs could promote aggression in ducks in a low density (a smaller number of ducks per PC) setting. The increased activity of DA could make the ducks more prone to aggressive behavior and resource guarding, as the heightened reward from interacting with semi-open water may lead to a negative affective state. Following this study, Schober et al. (2025b) investigated PCs as well as a second form of semi-open water source, the Pekino (PEK) and found that the ducks had worse feather quality when housed with the semi-open water sources compared to just the nipple lines. These observations suggest that the ratio of semi-open water sources to ducks in production is important because an imbalance may lead to increased competition for access, potentially triggering aggression and stress. Proper management of semi-open water sources also helps reduce litter moisture and bacterial load, both of which can have harmful effects on duck health. Excess moisture from improperly managed water sources can create damp conditions in the litter, leading to increased bacterial growth and a higher risk of footpad dermatitis, as well as dirtier feathers. By ensuring the water sources are appropriately managed, the risk of these harmful effects can be minimized.

Non-water Enrichment. There are also studies that have examined the effects of non-water physical enrichment on feather pecking and preening behaviors in Pekin ducks. Colton and Fraley (2014) found that ducks housed in a large-scale production barn with whiffle-style balls threaded with 4 zip-ties (EED) at a density of 120 ducks per EED engaged in less feather pecking and feather picking than those without EED. They also saw that ducks with the EED had better feather quality and cleanliness scores compared to the ducks without the EED, which further suggests a lack of unwanted pecking behaviors. They also found that the ducks interacted more frequently with blue/green EED than either red or white EED (Colton and Fraley, 2014). Following this study, Schober et al. (2025b) placed 2 blue/green EED into 4 pens of 30 ducks and saw that the ducks had worse feather quality than ducks with a semi-open water source and ducks with no enrichment. Though feather pecking behavior was not significant, the poor feather quality suggests that these behaviors were present within the pens with the EED. These findings highlight the importance of determining the correct ratio of EE to ducks to reduce feather pecking and picking and improve feather quality in Pekin ducks. Maintaining feather quality is essential in duck production, and enrichment strategies like water and non-water physical enrichment play a key role in achieving this. Beyond physical enrichment, another emerging approach is auditory enrichment, which has been extensively studied in zoos and is now gaining attention in farm animal management.

Environmental enrichment and neurobehavior

Very few poultry studies have investigated the effects of EE on neurotransmitters, but other studies have investigated the effects of EE on neurobehavior. Domestic fowl exhibit levels of cognitive, social, and emotional complexity comparable to those observed in many avian and mammalian species (Marino, 2017; Garnham and Løvlie, 2018; Campbell et al., 2019). In particular, chickens possess a higher degree of cognitive ability than has historically been recognized (Marino, 2017; Garnham and Løvlie, 2018). Simple housing environments, such as floor litter barns or cages, are unlikely to provide sufficient cognitive stimulation or spatial complexity to prepare pullets for navigation in aviary or outdoor laying systems. Tahamtani et al. (2015) reported that hens reared in barren cage environments exhibit long-lasting deficits in spatial task performance compared to those raised in more complex aviary systems. However, Tahamtani et al. (2016) found no immunohistological differences in tyrosine hydroxylase intensity in the hippocampus or caudolateral nidopallium of adult hens reared in cages versus aviaries. This absence of detectable neuronal differences may be attributable to all birds being housed in cages during the first four weeks of life, a critical period for structural brain development. Overall, research in livestock species in regard to EE is needed to ensure the future development of EE that enhances animals’ neurological capacities (for a review see Campbell and Lee, 2021).

Environmental enrichment and neurotransmitters

The effects of EE on neurotransmitter systems have been examined across multiple mammalian species. In pigs, the provision of straw bedding resulted in decreased dopamine (DA) and its metabolites compared to pigs housed in barren pens, suggesting reduced stress levels (Arroyo et al., 2020). Similarly, in rats, EE has been associated with reduced open-field activity and increased serotonin concentrations in the prefrontal cortex (Brenes et al., 2008). Furthermore, Segovia et al. (2008) demonstrated that rats housed with EE had reduced dopamine and acetylcholine levels after being handled compared to rats housed in standard cages that had been handled, indicating that EE may attenuate activity within the mesocortical dopamine system. There are very few studies that have investigated the effects of EE on neurotransmitter systems in ducks. Bergman et al. (2024b) looked at commercial duck barns that housed their ducks with PCs at a density of 1500 ducks/PC and found that the ducks that were actively using the PC showed no differences for DA or 5-HT turnover when compared to ducks not using the PC. Schober et al. (2025b) found no significant differences for neurotransmitter among ducks housed with either a PC, a PEK, or EED in a research setting. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of investigating neurotransmitter systems in poultry to better understand how environmental conditions influence affective states and overall welfare.

Auditory Enrichment. The use of physical enrichment for livestock animals has been around for decades, but there is also another form of enrichment, auditory enrichment. Auditory enrichment is a relatively newer method in the world of livestock animals, but it has been used in zoos for decades. Music has been shown to improve psychological well-being of humans, and research suggests that both humans’ moods and behavior can be influenced by the type of auditory stimulation they are exposed to (Ragneskog et al., 1996; Sousou, 1997; McCraty et al., 1998; Yalch and Spangenberg, 2000). This research in humans has influenced the research of auditory enrichment in animals, and it can be divided into sounds that are specific to that animal’s natural habitat, or other auditory stimulation that is not found in the wild (Wells, 2009). Auditory enrichment has been shown to decrease stereotypic behaviors in elephants, gorillas, and psittacines (Wells and Irwin, 2008; Williams et al., 2017; Robbins and Margulis, 2014).

Other studies have looked at the effects of auditory enrichment on fear and stress in poultry. Dávila et al. (2011) found that Black Castellana chicks exposed to classical music had shorter tonic immobility durations, lower heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, and less fluctuating asymmetry than control chicks. Tonic immobility is a fear response test used as an indicator of fear and stress. A longer duration of tonic immobility has been linked to higher levels of fearfulness (for a review of tonic immobility see Jones, 1986). Another study found that pullets exposed to short-term classical music increased comforting and preening behaviors and also reduced feather pecking, but did not affect tonic immobility (Zhao et al., 2023). Another study has shown that classical music at 65-75 dB significantly lowers corticosterone in chicks (Zhao et al., 2023b). These findings suggest that auditory enrichment, specifically classical music, can have a positive impact on reducing stress and fear-related behaviors in chickens, but a recent study on Pekin ducks found that classical music (specifically Mozart’s String Quartets & Quintets) decreased body weight and increased feather pecking (Schober et al., 2025a). Due to these differing results, further research is needed to determine whether these effects are consistent across poultry species.

Vocalizations as auditory enrichment and welfare indicators

Animal vocalizations play a significant role in communication. Vocalizations are effective as they travel rapidly over long distances and can be used in various environmental conditions. Vocalizations in wild bird species have been studied for over a century. Birds have many species-specific songs and calls. Calls are vocalizations that are produced by all birds, at all ages, during all times of the year for a variety of functions (Ballentine, 2021). Examples of different types of calls include begging signal, contact calls, migratory flight calls, food calls, and alarm calls (Ballentine, 2021). Alarm calls are key antipredator strategies, and different types of alarm calls are characterized by the context in which they are given, and the level or type of predatory threats that exist, but also to some degree by the way they sound (Caro, 2005; Ballentine, 2021). For example, seet calls are high-frequency, narrow-bandwidth calls, typically given by small birds when they detect an avian predator in flight that may be actively hunting for prey. Mobbing alarm calls are typically given when a bird has spotted a terrestrial or perched predator. These can be short, simple “chip” notes or longer broad band sounds that could be described as harsh, rough, or raspy. These calls attract members of the same species (conspecifics) and members of different species (heterospecifics) to elicit a behavior known as mobbing (Caro, 2005; Kalb, 2019). Distress alarm calls are given in the most extreme situation – when an animal has been attacked or captured by a predator. Distress calls tend to be loud, harsh, broad-frequency sounds that could be described as a “scream” (Magrath et al., 2015; Ballentine, 2021). Distress calls are not meant to cause others to dive for cover, but often to attract parents, other family members, or flock mates, or even members of different species. Contact calls allow birds to coordinate movements of a group and recognize preferred social partners. Migratory flight calls are produced by many migratory birds at night during their flights, though the function of these calls is unknown (Ballentine, 2021). Thus, vocalizations in birds provide a great deal of information about their status and welfare to their conspecifics.

Particular states of mood or emotion can be accompanied by vocalizations. The use of vocalizations as a measure of welfare has been used in species such as poultry, porcine, and bovine, as it is non-invasive and can provide information about the animal’s welfare, as long as the meanings of the respective calls are well established (Manteuffel et al., 2004). Many species produce specific vocalizations when experiencing pain or distress. For example, pigs produce high-frequency screams when undergoing painful procedures such as castration (Weary et al., 1998). Similarly, calves vocalize more intensely following branding (Watts and Stookey, 1999). Briefer et al. (2015) showed that goats produce different vocalizations during arousal and valence levels. Birds are also well known to utilize vocalizations to communicate different states to conspecifics.

In poultry, changes in vocalization patterns can indicate stress, such as increased calling rates in chickens subjected to food deprivation (Zimmerman and Koene, 1998). Hens also modify the peak frequency of their food calls depending on the type of reward, and the anticipation of different rewards elicits structurally similar food calls (McGrath et al., 2017). Researchers have also developed different ways to automatically monitor poultry vocalizations in a flock. Lee et al. (2015) developed a non-invasive, automatic monitoring prototype to detect stress calls of laying hens. Similarly, Mao et al. (2022) used deep learning models to develop an automated identification of chicken distress vocalizations. Du et al. (2020) developed a hen vocalization detection method based on machine learning to assess their thermal condition. Vocalizations provide valuable insights into an animal’s emotional and physical state, which offers an accessible, dependable, and ethical way to monitor welfare.

Studies on bird vocalizations have been conducted for over a century on wild bird species. However, this field of study has only recently become of interest to poultry scientists. In order to understand what information vocalizations are conferring to conspecifics, we need to first create a repertoire of vocalizations to characterize each call. This involves analyzing acoustic features such as pitch, duration, intensity, and amplitude modulation, along with considering the behavioral and situational context in which each call occurs. Studies have shown that chickens have a complex vocal repertoire with over 30 different calls, which are each associated with specific behaviors or environmental conditions. Evans et al. (1993) described two distinct types of male chicken alarm calls: short, high-pitched "chuck" calls used in response to overhead predators and longer, lower-pitched calls produced when predators are on the ground. These calls were given in front of hens, which caused the hens to either crouch (aerial alarm call), or develop an erect posture (ground alarm call), suggesting that different alarm calls are produced for different situations and evoke different behaviors from conspecifics (Evans et al., 1993). Evans and Marler (1994) described male chicken food calls as trains of pulsatile calls that are delivered in a regular temporal pattern, and have two major frequency bands, with a fundamental between 0.5 to 1 kHz and the frequency modulation usually appears as an inverted “U” on the spectrogram. Food calls are produced when the chicken finds food, and males are more likely to produce the calls if they are in the presence of a hen (Evans and Marler, 1994). Hens’ food calls are characterized as a rhythmic pattern of pulses that are given no less than 100 ms apart, lasting no more than 900 ms and range from 250 to 1500 Hz. The hens make this food call to attract their chicks to food (Collias and Joos, 1953; Sherry, 1977; Konishi, 1963; Gottleib, 1978). Hens also give a roosting call, which is usually given by the onset of darkness and tells the chicks to retreat to safety (Collias, 1987; Collias and Joos, 1953). The roosting call is a long purring sound that has repeated pulses and frequencies below 2000 Hz, but lacks a rhythmic pattern (Collias, 1987; Collias and Joos, 1953). Studies have also shown that chicks produce distress calls that are characterized as loud, descending low frequencies given at a low rate (Collias and Joos, 1953). Therefore, continued research is crucial to fully understand the complexities of poultry vocalizations and their role in communication, particularly as modern poultry may exhibit changes in vocal behavior due to evolving environments and breeding practices.

Collias and Joos (1953) provided spectral analysis of the following calls of the domestic fowl: (a) sounds made by chicks, including distress calls, pleasure notes, and fear trills; (b) sounds made by broody hens that serve to attract chicks, including clucking, food calls, and the roosting call; and (c) warning signals, including alarm call for aerial predators, alarm call for ground predators, alerting call of a broody hen, fear squawks of a hen held in the hand, threat sounds of cocks, and crowing of a rooster. This study was conducted seven decades ago, and researchers continue to use these repertoires as the foundation for vocalization studies. It is important to understand that the chicken from seven decades ago is not the chicken we produce today (Havenstein et al., 2003; Zuidhof et al., 2014). It may be possible that their vocal repertoire has changed, so more research should be conducted to provide a complete repertoire of chicken vocalizations, including both sexes, as well as different ages.

One study that developed a vocal repertoire of a poultry species was Schober et al. (2024). This repertoire is on Pekin duck vocalizations, and their study showed that Pekin duck vocalizations are affected by stimuli, sex, and social groups. They showed that Pekin ducks produce 16 different vocalizations, and certain types of calls were given under specific conditions. For call diversity, the number of hens present affected the types of calls given (Schober et al., 2024). Many studies have shown that birds produce vocalizations for many different reasons and motivations. Poultry scientists are now using vocalizations as a way to measure fear, stress, health, and motivation to help improve welfare. One method of assessing fear in poultry with the use of vocalizations is the isolation fear test, which evaluates the level of fear and stress birds experience when separated from their social group. The test measures vocalization frequency, with a higher number of vocalizations indicating greater fear. The types of vocalizations the birds produce are typically not recorded, only the frequency. This begs the question of whether chickens are producing different types of vocalizations during the isolation fear test, and not just distress calls.

Physiological responses to vocalizations

Scientists who study wild birds have been studying vocalizations for decades. Knowing how vocalizations affect conspecific physiology is important to understand what each vocalization’s function is. Wild bird literature reports the effects of different vocalizations on conspecific physiology, including heart rate and respiratory rate. Thomspon et al. (1968) showed that when starlings, with an average baseline heart rate of 308 PPM, were played back a distress call, their heart rate significantly increased to a mean of 446 BPM. However, when played back a feeding call, their heart rate significantly increased but only to a mean of 349 BPM. The difference in how these different types of calls differentially affect their physiology suggest that these calls encode different types of information. Starlings have also been shown to have an increase in heart rate when played back synthesized conspecific distress and alarm calls, but not when played back a synthesized sound designed to repel birds (Thompson et al., 1979). It has also been shown that starlings have a greater susceptibility to auditory stimuli, specifically white noise and conspecific distress calls, in the summer compared to the winter (Johnson et al., 1985). Vocalizations have been shown to be critical components of Mallard duck interactions and behaviors (Hicinbothom and Miller, 2018). For example, specific types of maternal vocalizations, or calls, can alter Mallard behaviors (Miller and Gottlieb, 1978; Blaich and Miller, 1988; Hicinbothom and Miller, 2018) and some Mallard calls have even been shown to affect conspecifics’ heart rates and physiology (Evans, 1990).

There has been limited research conducted on poultry vocalizations and how it affects conspecific physiology, though some have investigated how it affects behavior. Evans and Evans (1999) investigated chicken food calls and found that hens who are played back food calls fixated downwards. When chickens were exposed to maternal "cluck" calls during incubation, they exhibited greater social behavior after hatching than chicks that were not exposed to the calls (Gall et al., 2024). More recently, a study conducted by Schober et al. (2025c) found that when Pekin ducks are played back a conspecific honk call (alarm call) their blood pressure significantly increases compared to the no sound control. When hens are played back a conspecific egg laying squiggle, their blood pressure significantly decreases compared to the no sound control, suggesting that the egg laying squiggle is a calming vocalization (Schober et al., 2025c).

These findings demonstrate that vocalizations play a crucial role in avian communication, influencing both behavior and physiological responses in conspecifics. The ability of different calls to elicit distinct changes in heart rate and responsiveness highlights their functional significance in conveying specific types of information. Understanding these vocalization-induced physiological effects can provide deeper insights into the role of acoustic signals in social interactions and stress responses in different avian species. Specifically, if we can understand what calls are distress calls in different poultry species and what each distress call communicates about specific states, we could create an automated tool to analyze the flocks’ vocalizations in real time. This would help producers recognize that their flock is stressed before they see a drop in production and welfare. Also, if we could determine which calls are calming calls, we could potentially play back those calls to our flocks as auditory enrichment. Developing a deeper understanding of vocalizations in poultry could lead to practical applications in production settings, enhancing both welfare and production.

Conclusion and recommendations

When assessing a bird’s affective state, no single factor can be relied upon. A combination of physiological and neurobiological variables along with behavior are required to obtain a holistic view of welfare. The goal to find a single, non-invasive measure of welfare may be hubris with today’s technology, and potentially as misleading as Lewis Carroll’s “Boojum.” The development of AI tools to assess behavior, locomotion, and body condition is ongoing (Michielon et al., 2024; Rao et al., 2026), though the development of AI tools to monitor vocalizations may be of future benefit (for a review see Dawkins, 2025). Animal welfare is an important part of the poultry industry and is increasingly important to consumers. Research is needed to further understand the effects of certain enrichment types on poultry, specifically Pekin ducks. Open-water sources have been shown to have both adverse and beneficial effects on Pekin duck welfare, but more research is needed to determine which source is the best. Vocalizations have also been used as indicators of welfare, but there has been no research done with Pekin ducks prior to studies reported here.

The brain is a major source of information for Pekin duck welfare as neurotransmitters and hormones may elicit psychological and physiological changes. Research needs to further understand the brain and the relationship to welfare as producers require increased production and consumers demand high levels of animal welfare. Open water sources may be an option to improve duck welfare, but more research is needed to assess the true impact on production and welfare. The effects of various management stressors, such as transportation, should continue to be explored to understand the differences in species and sex.
