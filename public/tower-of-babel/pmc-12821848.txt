Inter-rater agreement of respiratory distress observation scale measurement between physicians and nurses in the emergency department
Authors: Nichapha Chongthavonsatit, Kamonchanok Khachintararod, Pongsakorn Atiksawedparit, Thidathit Prachanukool, Suraphong Phongsawad, Tapanee Eaimsamlee, Orathai Piamsiri, Pisitpong Kaewtanyanukul, Jirawat Tuchinda, Jirath Srisomboon, Kei Ouchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDyspnea is an individual’s sensation of discomfort during breathing. For patients with dyspnea who are unable to communicate, the Respiratory Distress Observation Scale (RDOS) was used to rate the severity based on eight parameters observed. In the emergency department, emergency nurses triage the patients with dyspnea and monitor their symptom severities, while emergency physicians evaluate the patients to determine treatment decisions. We aim to study the inter-rater agreement of RDOS measurement between emergency physicians and nurses.MethodBetween March 2024 and January 2025, an observational cross-sectional study was conducted in resuscitation rooms of two university academic hospitals. The participants were emergency physicians and nurses who were the first responders to adult patients presenting to the resuscitation room with dyspnea. The RDOS assessment was done individually by the data record forms within 20 min after arriving in the resuscitation rooms. The primary outcome was inter-rater agreement on RDOS measurement between emergency physicians and nurses.ResultBy 176 patients with dyspnea (N = 176), 44 emergency physicians and 55 nurses were included with no difference in either age or clinical experiences. The overall physicians and nurses reported a fair agreement of RDOS severity (58%) with a Kappa statistic of 0.54 (95% CI: 0.47–0.61, SE 0.075, p < 0.001), with 57% agreement in patients with intact communication and 61% agreement in those with impaired communication. The ratings were internally consistent and homogeneous among each profession. (the overall IIC 0.737 and 0.767, respectively). The inter-rater reliability was poor to moderate across both professions when scoring seven of eight RDOS parameters.ConclusionsEmergency physicians and nurses have fair inter-rater agreement on RDOS measurement. Seven of the eight RDOS parameters revealed poor to moderate inter-rater reliability in both professions. Therefore, implementing RDOS in the ED requires customized training and calibrating the RDOS assessment.

Background

Dyspnea is a common suffering symptom with 5.2% of all emergency department (ED) visits [1] and is significantly leads to the mortality [2]. In the U.S., 1,145,000 to 1,650,000 ED visits because of dyspnea in adult patients were reported [3]. The etiological conditions varied from cancer, lower respiratory tract infection, HIV, heart diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and renal diseases. Among patients with dyspnea who visit the ED, the admission rate is 57%-84%, with inpatient mortality of 6%-8% [1, 4, 5].

Currently, dyspnea assessment has been suggested to evaluate systematically in the ED, enhancing care quality beyond traditional ED assessments [6]. The systematic dyspnea assessment would allow prioritization of symptom management and subsequent treatment with medical interventions in moderate to severe severities [6]. Moreover, delayed management of dyspnea can lead to worsen patient’s conditions, increase hospital length of stay, increase mortality and treatment cost [7]. The assessment of dyspnea remains challenging due to the subjective nature involved with the pathophysiology and psychological and socioeconomic characteristics [8, 9]. The measuring of dyspnea is not only a physiological assessment but must also include the patient’s perception. Therefore, the self-reported numerical rating score (NRS) is a commonly used rating system ranging from 1 (least severe) to 10 (most severe) [8] The NRS is a patient-focused assessment tool that is limited to patients who can communicate, particularly restricted to critically ill patients in the ED. The respiratory distress observation scale (RDOS) has a total score of 16, which is an evaluation of two quantitative parameters (heart rate and respiratory rate) and six qualitative parameters (accessory muscle use, paradoxical breathing pattern, restlessness, grunting at end-expiration, nasal flaring, and look of fear) [10–12]. Previous studies found the relationship between RDOS and NRS. The RDOS score of more than 3 points is associated with moderate to severe dyspnea at 76% sensitivity, 86% specificity, 86% positive predictive value, and 77% negative predictive value [13]. Emerging evidence suggests that RDOS is reliable in the ICU settings [12–14]. However, this reliability has never been demonstrated in the ED settings.

In the ED, emergency nurses triage patients with dyspnea and monitor their symptom severities, while emergency physicians evaluate the patients to determine treatment decisions. Therefore, our study aims to determine the agreement of dyspnea severity measurement using RDOS between physicians and nurses in the ED. Applying RDOS as a screening or monitoring tool by interdisciplinary teams in the ED is challenging due to three reasons. First, rating RDOS can be influenced by personal clinical experiences [15]. Second, RDOS is often used by physicians when deciding on dyspnea management, while the validity of RDOS measurement by ED nurses is limited. Third, previous studies indicated high inter-rater reliability between physician and nurse assessments studied in the ICU or during hospitalization, not in the ED [12–14].

Materials and methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional, multicenter study was conducted from March 2024 to January 2025 at the ED of two academic hospitals of 1600 beds with 50,000 annual ED visits and 400 beds with 17,000 ED visits, respectively. The Human Research Ethics Committee of both institutes approved the study (COA. MURA 2024/83). Our report complied with the instructions for reporting observational study, the STROBE statement.

Participants

Emergency physicians and emergency nurses, who first evaluated 20-year-old or older patients visiting the ED due to dyspnea or an initial respiratory rate (RR) > 20 breaths/minute at the resuscitation room, without traumatic injuries or neuromuscular disorders, were enrolled. All emergency provider participants provided informed consent before enrolling voluntarily. We excluded the emergency providers who refused to participate or complete our case record forms. An ethics committee waives consent requirements for the emergency patients in the ED.

Variables and data collection

Prior to the enrollment phase, the researchers developed an instructional video for teaching dyspnea assessment using the RDOS methodology. This instructional video was created by palliative care experts. All participants were oriented to our study, including the RDOS assessment and additional self-paced video review. Demographic data of the enrolled emergency physicians and emergency nurses (gender, age, and years of clinical experience) and clinical characteristics of patients (gender, age, and the initial cause of dyspnea). For the clinical experiences, the emergency physicians and nurses were limited and biased by the practicing regulation of their own training and clinical practices. Studied since 2010 by Campbell et al., RDOS evaluates eight physical signs, including heart rate, respiratory rate, accessory muscle use for breathing (clavicle rise), paradoxical breathing pattern, restlessness, grunting at end-expiration, nasal flaring, and a look of fear. Each variable is scored from 0 to 2, with total scores ranging from 0 to 16 [10]. The eight parameters of RDOS were independently scored by trained emergency physicians and nurses within 20 min after patients arrived at the ED. Finally, all data were recorded to the case record form before entering the database for analysis. The researcher contacted the study participants directly and completed the case record form with all the missing information within 24 hours.

Sample size estimation

Based on the previous study, the agreement between physicians and nurses on dyspnea ratings, Kappa statistic was 0.71, with a prevalence of diagnosed dyspnea of 40% by physicians [16]. The sample size was determined from the estimation of Kappa statistic equation [17]. Therefore, 151 subjects were determined from the estimation. Assuming a 10% missing data, 168 subjects were required for this study.

Statistical methods

Categorical data was described as numbers and percentages. Continuous data was described as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with minimum and maximum values (min-max), based on normal or non-normal distribution, respectively. The difference between two categorical data was determined by Chi-Square test, and the difference between two continuous data was determined by Student’s t-test for normal distribution and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normal distribution [18].

Mean difference with its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was determined to assess the agreement of RDOS rated by physicians and nurses using Bland-Altman plot. In addition, the total score of RDOS was categorized into three groups following the 2017 study by Campbell et al., in which RDOS 1–3, 4–6, and 7–16 indicated mild, moderate, and severe dyspnea groups, respectively [9]. The agreement among three groups of dyspnea rated by emergency physicians and nurses was determined by estimating the percentage of the agreement (PA) and quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa (k) with standard error (SE). The internal consistency of the RDOS rating was assessed through inter-item correlations (IIC) analysis. Simultaneously, inter-rater reliability between emergency physicians and nurses was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). All statistical analyses were done by STATA software version 18.0 and statistical significance was achieved when p-value < 0.05. The sensitivity analysis was not required.

Results

We enrolled 44 emergency physicians and 55 emergency nurses. The characteristics of emergency physicians and nurses between institutes were described in Table 1. A total of 232 eligible cases, 46 cases were excluded due to exclusion criteria. Therefore, 176 cases (127 cases and 49 cases from center I and II, respectively) were included for final analysis. Baseline characteristics of patients with dyspnea are shown in Table 2. More than half of cases were male (51.7%). The median age was 75 years old. One hundred and twenty-two patients (69.3%) were able to communicate. The most common initial cause of dyspnea in the ED was respiratory infections (39.2%).

The Bland-Altman plot indicates the mean difference between RDOS rated by emergency physicians and nurses was 0.506 with 95% CI: -5.259 to 6.271. Of 176 cases, 14 cases (8.0%) were outside the limits of agreement, see Fig. 1. The inter-rater agreement of RDOS rated by emergency physicians and nurses is shown in Table 3. The overall agreement was 57.95% with a Kappa statistic of 0.54 (95% CI: 0.47–0.61, SE 0.075, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis by patients’ ability to communicate revealed higher agreement in patients with impaired communication than patients with intact communication. Among patients with impaired communication, 61.11% agreement was a moderate level of reliability, with a Kappa statistic of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.52–0.76, SE 0.125, p < 0.001). Whereas, in patients with intact communication, 56.56% agreement was a fair level of reliability, with a Kappa statistic of 0.52 (95% CI: 0.46–0.62, SE 0.089, p < 0.001).

The comparison of each RDOS parameter rated by emergency physicians and nurses is shown in Table 4. The RDOS parameter ratings by emergency physicians or nurses were internally consistent and homogeneous, with the overall IIC 0.737 among emergency physicians and 0.767 among emergency nurses. Inter-rater reliability between emergency physicians and nurses, as measured by ICC, was found to be poor in grunting and nasal flaring (-0.087 (95% CI: -0.463, 0.192) and 0.327 (95% CI: 0.095, 0.500), respectively). Other qualitative metrics and respiration rate show moderate reliability, while heart rate shows good reliability.

Discussion

We conducted a cross-sectional study to determine the agreement of RDOS scoring by emergency physicians and nurses among patients with dyspnea in the ED. Our result indicated fair agreement of the RDOS rating. The agreement of the RDOS rating is higher in patients with impaired communication compared to patients with intact communication ability. In addition, emergency physicians were more likely to rate higher scores for restlessness and look of fear than emergency nurses. This means that standardized training and protocols may be required to improve consistency in RDOS scoring before clinical implementation, particularly when assessing qualitative parameters and patients with varying communication capabilities.

Previous studies in Taiwan, Japan, and Singapore found very high inter-rater agreement, which was different from our result [12–14]. This different finding might be explained by (1) previous studies enrolled patients who were hospitalized as inpatients for more than 24 hours. The attending physicians or ward nurses, who assessed the patients with dyspnea, were able to become familiar with the patients’ conditions over an extended period, whereas emergency physicians and nurses in our study assessed patients immediately upon presentation to the ED. (2) The clinical experiences caring for patients with dyspnea of the emergency physicians and nurses versus inpatient physicians and nurses were different subjective observational parameters, while the equipment-measurable parameters were not [19]. (3) The resuscitation bay in the ED presented a more chaotic and time-pressure environment than inpatient units, which limit all resources (such as time, space, and staff) in ED care [20]. (4) Prior studies were conducted by RDOS raters with expertise and structured training, which could significantly impact reliability [12–14]. In contrast, our study was done by the real-world emergency physicians and nurses, who all had different clinical experiences and relied on instructional self-paced training.

Our results revealed poor to moderate inter-rater reliability between emergency physicians and nurses when scoring seven of eight RDOS parameters, most of which were qualitative in nature. The explanation could be the different clinical experiences, which include the professional training and the personal clinical practices, as well as the surrounding ED contexts. Nurses are trained to be more actively involved in bedside care and continuous monitoring [19]. One systematic review by Douw G et al. reported the overall subjective feeling of “the patient does not feel well,” the nurse has been practically trained to report the breathing patterns and restlessness [21]. Whereas emergency physicians are trained to focus more on medical explanations, diagnoses, and decision-making for the plans of management, which aim to maximize safety for the patients [19]. In addition, our enrolled emergency physicians had less clinical experience than nurses (3 years versus 8 years). These various clinical experiences (e.g., personal perspective, duration of clinical practice) could affect the evaluation of qualitative parameters [21]. For the ED contexts, a prior study indicated a dynamic patient flow and work pressure in ED utilities [20]. Particularly for grunting, which has the lowest inter-rater reliability, the sole auditory parameter obscured by the ED noise environment [22]. This finding is helpful for future studies adjusting or modifying the RDOS implemented in the ED. The RDOS application also requires the ED-specific RDOS evaluation training, which focuses on qualitative parameters.

Subgroup analysis between intact and impaired communicated patients also revealed different levels of agreement. The result indicated the percentage of agreements among impaired communicated patients (61% agreement) was higher than intact communicated patients (57% agreement). In fact, if the patient can self-report, the RDOS is not a substitute [13] and thus is not considered clinical practice for the patients with intact communication. This distinction in RDOS agreement could be related to the characteristics of the patients and the assessors involved in the RDOS scoring. Firstly, the patients with intact communication can express their dyspnea feeling (e.g., restlessness, look of fear) clearer than the impaired communication patients. Secondly, the unfamiliar parameters for the participants and the sensitivity of the RDOS rating scales could reflect the floor effect in statistics. As a result, statistical concerns could be interpreted as agreement, particularly among the patients with impaired communication. Previously, inter-rater agreements of RDOS were studied in a single arm of sedated patients with mechanical ventilators or self-reported patients in the inpatient units [12, 14]. Therefore, the variation in the communication abilities of the patients was not reported. Further study of an adequate sample size is still required on these issues.

Our study has several important implications for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers. For clinicians’ and researchers’ implications, this is the first study that implements RDOS in the ED, which was applied to the patients with both intact and impaired communications in the real world. To improve the application of RDOS in the ED, future studies should assess the RDOS accuracy and validity, calibrate a suitable RDOS cutoff point, and customize training for the emergency providers. For policymakers, emphasis on patient-focused assessment in the ED, such as dyspnea, is not only objective measurement but also includes patient perspective [23]. Implementing RDOS in the ED does not only enhance the quality of physical care specifically but also improves the quality of holistic care through the implementation of a non-complex assessment method. Furthermore, introducing RDOS in the prehospital period could be a further opportunity, as the paramedic team is connected to the ED care provided by emergency physicians and nurses.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, we conducted a prospective observational study in only two tertiary care university hospitals, which limits generalizability. For country-wide generalization, a large-scale multicenter study with an adequate sample size for variation across the country may be required. Secondly, some biases were indicated. The selection bias resulted from participants being overly busy with the overcrowded ED and then refusing to participate or complete the case records. The clustering data regarding repeated emergency physician or nurse assessments in multiple patients produces an underestimated standard error. The measurement bias in data collection from each participant, such as rater order, time discrepancies, and the medical treatments between assessments, could confound the qualitative parameters and outcomes. Thirdly, several aspects of integrating RDOS into the ED need to be explored, including the RDOS training program for emergency providers, the accuracy and validity of RDOS against the patient self-report of dyspnea, and the optimum calibration cutoff point for dyspnea presentation in the ED. This finding was applied just for emergency physicians and nurses. The good agreement and reliability of RDOS measurement among the emergency teams helps in the decision-making for timely non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions, alleviating the patient’s dyspnea.

Conclusions

The inter-rater agreement was fair for RDOS assessed by emergency physicians and nurses. Seven of the eight RDOS parameters across both professions revealed poor to moderate inter-rater reliability. Therefore, implementing RDOS in the ED requires customized training among emergency providers and calibrating the RDOS assessment.
