The Impact of Indian Adapted Mediterranean Diet (IAMD) on Dietary Inflammatory Index in Patients with Coronary Artery Disease: Protocol for a Randomized Controlled Trial

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality in India, accounting for over 26% of all deaths [1]. Compared to Western populations, Indians experience earlier onset, higher incidence, and higher mortality from CVD, with over 50% of CVD-related deaths occurring before the age of 70 years, compared to just 23% in high-income countries [2]. Diet is a significant contributor to cardiometabolic diseases. It was the third most common cause of mortality among risk factors in Global Burden of Disease Study in 2019, and 22% of all deaths were attributed directly to an unhealthy diet [3].

Diet plays a crucial role in coronary artery disease (CAD) by modulating systemic inflammation [4]. Pro-inflammatory dietary patterns—high in refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, and low fruit and vegetable consumption—are highly prevalent in India and contribute to the metabolic and inflammatory burden [5, 6]. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), a validated literature-derived tool, quantifies the inflammatory potential of habitual dietary intake based on its effects on circulating inflammatory biomarkers [7, 8]. Higher DII scores correlate with increased CVD risk and other non-communicable diseases [8, 9]. Importantly, the DII offers an objective surrogate to assess dietary intervention efficacy beyond simple macronutrient composition. Multiple large trials—including PREDIMED, and CORDIOPREV, and a recent metanalysis have demonstrated that adherence to the Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) reduces major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [10–12]. Meta-analyses further confirm its beneficial effects on cardiovascular risk factors, metabolic syndrome and CAD [13, 14]. Several countries have successfully adapted the Mediterranean diet to local food availability and culinary traditions while preserving its core principles [15–19]. We have developed an Indian Adapted Mediterranean Diet (IAMD) based on culturally appropriate, locally available dietary equivalents of conventional Mediterranean Diet constituents [20]. The developed diet had the highest reported DII score in the literature and was feasible to implement among our patients and led to improvement in the DII score of their diets [21].

Here we test the benefits of IAMD as against usual dietary prescription in a randomized control trial among patients with established CAD or at moderate to high risk for CVD.

Methods

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of IAMD on the DII and energy-adjusted DII (E-DII) in patients with established CAD or at moderate to high-risk for CVD (INTERHEART Score ≥ 10) [22]. Specifically, the trial aims to determine whether a structured IAMD-based dietary intervention over six months leads to a greater reduction in DII and E-DII scores compared to standard dietary advice. Secondary objectives include assessing changes in systemic inflammatory biomarkers (C-reactive protein [CRP], interleukin-4 [IL-4], interleukin-6 [IL-6], and interleukin-10 [IL-10]), cardiometabolic risk factors (fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], fasting insulin, lipid profile, uric acid, and serum fatty acid composition), and anthropometric parameters (body mass index, waist circumference and hip circumference). In addition, the study will explore changes in metabolic hormones—adiponectin, leptin, ghrelin, resistin and glucagon like peptide-1 (GLP-1)—as mechanistic endpoints to understand how the IAMD may influence metabolic regulation, appetite control, and inflammation. The study also aims to explore correlations between changes in DII and improvements in biomarkers and metabolic profiles, providing mechanistic insights into the anti-inflammatory potential of culturally contextualized dietary strategies for cardiovascular risk reduction in South Asian populations.

Study design

This is a single-center, open-label, randomized controlled trial with blinded outcome assessment.

Population

Eligible participants for this study include adults aged 30 to 70 years with either established CAD (defined as a prior myocardial infarction or percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting, or angiographic evidence of ≥ 50% diameter stenosis in a major epicardial coronary artery) or individuals classified as moderate to high cardiovascular risk based on an INTERHEART risk score ≥ 10. Participants must be on stable optimal medical therapy for at least one month and willing to comply with the dietary intervention protocol.

Key exclusion criteria include individuals requiring therapeutic dietary modifications for comorbid conditions (e.g., chronic kidney disease, malignancy, thyroid disorders), those with clinical heart failure (New York Heart Association, NYHA Class II or higher), patients with diagnosed eating disorders (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, DSM-5 criteria), or individuals currently enrolled in formal dietary programs.

The enrollment in the trial is complete and the baseline characteristics of the participants that have been enrolled are as in Table 1. The cohort has a mean age of 51.9 (± 8.7) years and 79% of participants are male. Educational attainment is diverse: 9% have completed post-graduation, 29% graduation, 30% high school, 27% primary school and 5% are illiterate. The prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors is high: dyslipidemia (69%), hypertension (59%), diabetes mellitus (45%) and family history of CAD (31%). Tobacco use includes 13% current smokers, 24% former smokers and 11% smokeless tobacco users. A large majority of participants (81%) have previously diagnosed CAD. Majority of participants are receiving statins (86%) and beta blockers (86%). The mean body mass index (BMI) of the cohort is 26.8 kg/m².

Randomization and blinding

Participants who meet eligibility criteria and provide written informed consent are randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention arm (IAMD-based dietary intervention) or the control arm (standard dietary advice). Randomization is performed using a computer-generated allocation sequence through the REDCap Interactive Web Randomization System (IWRS), with stratification based on diabetes status. Due to the nature of dietary interventions, the study has an open-label design, whereby participants and dietitians are aware of the assigned intervention. However, to minimize bias, outcome assessors—including those responsible for dietary analysis, anthropometric measurements, and biomarker assays—will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study duration.

Intervention

Participants in the intervention arm will receive individualized dietary counseling based on IAMD by trained research dietitians. Each participant will receive a personalized 7-day cyclic meal plan tailored to caloric requirements, determined by BMI, ideal body weight (IBW), comorbidities, and dietary preferences. The meal plan is based on over 70 validated North Indian recipes and emphasizes the use of healthy fats (mustard oil, groundnut oil), whole grains, legumes, seasonal fruits and vegetables, nuts, and anti-inflammatory spices such as turmeric and ginger, while minimizing refined carbohydrates, red meat, and processed foods (Detailed IAMD resources are available at https://iamdiet.in/).

Participants will receive a patient education booklet comprising standardized recipes for both summer and winter season; practical tips for eating out and general healthy lifestyle. A token ration kit is delivered which includes multigrain flour, ragi flour, olive oil, almonds, barnyard millet and whole spice mix for tea. To enhance adherence, participants also receive: fridge magnets with motivating messages, pre-scheduled text message reminders, interim telephonic follow-up at 1 month, and in-person reinforcement visits at 3 and 6 months. Adherence will be monitored using food frequency questionnaires, 24-hour dietary recalls, and a validated 16-point adherence scoring tool. Compliance to the diet in terms of ease of following the prescription, use of token ration, adequacy of prescription, accessibility to food items and their affordability will be assessed at these visits using a compliance questionnaire.

Comparator

Participants in the control arm will receive standard dietary advise by trained clinical dieticians, based on routine clinical practice. Dietary recommendations are individualized based on BMI, IBW, dietary habits, cultural practices and medical history. Dietary counseling is reinforced through in-person follow-up visits at 3 and 6 months, in accordance with standard clinical practice at the institute.

Follow up schedule

Participants in both study arms will be followed for a total duration of six months, with structured evaluations at baseline, three months, and six months. Each visit includes anthropometric measurements, dietary intake evaluation using a validated FFQ, 24-hour dietary recalls, and collection of blood samples for biomarker analysis.

Sample size

The sample size estimation for the present study was based on data from the AUSMED Heart Trial, which demonstrated a between-group reduction in DII of approximately 1.56 points at six months, with standard deviations ranging from 2.3 to 3.1 [23]. For the current trial, a conservative effect size of 1.25 and a standard deviation of 2.0 were assumed. Using these parameters, a sample size of 59 participants per group was calculated to achieve 90% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05. Accounting for a 15% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 70 participants per arm (total N = 140).

DII calculation

The information on patients’ dietary intakes will be used to assess the DII® and E-DII. The development of the DII has been described in detail elsewhere [7]. For this study, 34 of the 45 food parameters will be used to calculate an individual’s overall DII score, as information regarding 11 parameters is unavailable in the Indian database (Indian Food Composition Tables, IFCT). First, a z-score of each 34-food parameter for each participant is calculated using the global daily average intake and its standard deviation. Then, individual z-scores will be converted to a centered percentile. Later, this centered percentile is multiplied by its respective food parameter-specific overall inflammatory effect score, and the food parameter-specific DII score is obtained. Finally, the 34-food parameter-specific DII score is summed, and the DII score is obtained for an individual. Energy-adjusted DII (E-DII™ scores) is calculated using the density approach by calculating DII per 1000 kcal consumption [24].The DII score ranges from − 8.87 to 7.98, with lower values indicating a diet with more antiinflammatory potential.

Data management

All study data is being captured using standardized case report forms (CRFs) and entered into a secure, password-protected database using the REDCap electronic data capture system, hosted at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi. The CRF includes multiple modules covering screening eligibility, demographic information, medical and family history, physical examination findings, current medications, laboratory investigations, and study-specific instruments such as the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), adherence tool, and compliance assessment questionnaire.

The FFQ is administered digitally using the CSPro software package, while other assessments—including the Readiness to Change Questionnaire (RCQ), 24-hour dietary recall, and 7-day food diaries—is collected on paper-based forms. Data from REDCap are exported to a central backend database for integration with other study datasets. Based on the participant responses from the RCQ, readiness to change scores are manually computed and entered into a secure Microsoft Excel sheet. Data from FFQ, 24-hour recalls and food diaries are manually entered into DietCal software (version 12.0) to generate individualized dietary intake analyses.

To ensure high-quality data, all research staff involved in data collection were trained before participant recruitment commenced. All equipment will be calibrated regularly, anthropometric measurements were obtained in duplicate, and random cross-checks will be performed for dietary data entered into the Dietcal Software. Information regarding participant withdrawal or loss to follow-up is systematically documented.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, data will be exported from REDCap to STATA version 16.0 (STATA CORP USA) and will be analyzed on intention-to-treat principles. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize baseline demographic, clinical, and dietary characteristics. Continuous variables will be reported as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on distribution, and compared using independent-sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables will be presented as counts with percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. The primary outcome—change in DII and E-DII from baseline to 6 months—will be assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for baseline DII scores and potential covariates such as age, sex, BMI, baseline diet quality, and medication use. Secondary outcomes, including changes in inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10), cardiometabolic risk markers (lipids, glucose, HbA1c, uric acid), cardiometabolic hormones (leptin, adiponectin, ghrelin, resistin, GLP-1) and anthropometric indices, will be analyzed similarly using repeated-measures ANOVA or mixed-effects models where appropriate. Correlation analyses (Pearson or Spearman) will be conducted to explore associations between changes in DII and changes in biomarkers and adherence scores. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation methods if missingness is > 5% and assumed to be at random. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Discussion

Mediterranean Diet has emerged as the dietary intervention with the largest evidence base in primary and secondary prevention of CAD. A large umbrella review of meta-analyses of observational studies and randomized trials including over 12 million participants has correlated adherence to Mediterranean diet to several positive health outcomes in addition to CAD and CVD- lower incidence of diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders and cancers and improved survival [12]. This has led to the adoption of the diet in several countries after matching local culinary practices, cultural choices and food availability.

The importance of the Mediterranean diet which is predominantly plant based with use of legumes and moderate dairy products is immense in the Indian context. The average Indian diet exceeds the recommended energy from carbohydrate content and is deficient in proteins and unsaturated fats [25]. Additionally, there is a high consumption of polished grains, refined flour, and sugars and very low consumption of fruits and vegetables [6]. Recommendation of low-fat diet is common based on the lipid heart hypothesis among cardiologists leading to further rise in carbohydrates in diet. It is in this context that we explored an Indian adapted Mediterranean Diet to improve both the macronutrient and micronutrient content of the diet.

The effort led to the development of a highly anti-inflammatory diet with a reported E-DII of -7.8 to -8.4, the lowest reported in literature [20]. We were able to prove the feasibility of implementation of the diet with 83% of participants adhering to more than 50% of the prescribed diet. This led to a favorable reduction in E-DII of the diet consumed, suggesting improved anti-inflammatory content of the diet. However, the reduction in E-DII was modest for the exceptionally high DII of the diet developed [21]. The learnings from the feasibility study were applied in the present trial. This was achieved by creating appropriate behavior change communication materials and providing more frequent messaging to improve compliance.

DII has emerged as an important objective quantification of the inflammatory potential of the diet consumed. Higher DII scores have been consistently linked with increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality. A landmark meta-analysis of 14 observational studies confirmed a robust association between pro-inflammatory diets, as quantified by the DII, and increased cardiovascular risk and mortality [26]. Individuals in the highest DII category had a 36% higher risk of developing or dying from CVD compared to those in the lowest category (RR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.19–1.57), with consistent findings across multiple endpoints including myocardial infarction and stroke. Importantly, each one-point increase in DII score corresponded to an 8% increase in CVD risk (RR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.04–1.12). There have been similar benefits linked to DII across multiple reviews and over 100 meta-analyses, demonstrating an association between DII scores and chronic disease risk for CVD, cancer, aging, and mortality [27–29]. Thus we believe that if IAMD leads to improvement in DII of diets among our patients, the benefits would extend beyond CVD benefits.

In addition to established inflammatory and metabolic markers, this study will assess changes in key metabolic hormones—adiponectin, leptin, ghrelin, resistin and GLP-1—as exploratory secondary endpoints. These adipokines and gut-derived peptides play a critical role in regulating insulin sensitivity, satiety, energy balance, and low-grade inflammation, all of which are closely linked to the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis and metabolic syndrome [30, 31].

This study holds important public health implications. Given the rapid epidemiological transition occurring in India, scalable, low-cost, non-pharmacologic interventions are urgently needed to reduce burden of cardiometabolic diseases. If successful, the IAMD approach may serve as a pragmatic template for large-scale adoption in South Asian populations in India and abroad that constitute 25% of the world population. Furthermore, as discussed such culturally adapted dietary frameworks may offer synergistic benefits in other chronic conditions linked to systemic inflammation, including diabetes, metabolic syndrome, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and certain cancers [32].

This study has several notable strengths. It is the first randomized clinical trial to evaluate a culturally adapted Mediterranean-style dietary intervention specifically developed for an Indian population with established or elevated risk of CAD. The use of the DII—a validated, literature-derived metric—as the primary endpoint enables objective quantification of the inflammatory potential of dietary intake. The trial also incorporates a comprehensive set of secondary outcomes, including validated inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10), cardiometabolic risk factors, anthropometric parameters, and dietary adherence scores. Moreover, the inclusion of metabolic hormones such as adiponectin, leptin, ghrelin, resistin and GLP-1 provides mechanistic insight into the endocrine effects of dietary change.

The study has some limitations. First, due to the nature of dietary interventions, blinding of participants and dietitians is not feasible, which may introduce performance bias, although outcome assessors remain blinded. Second, dietary intake is assessed through self-reported methods such as food frequency questionnaires and 24-hour recalls, which are subject to recall bias and underreporting. Third, the single-center design may limit generalizability to other regions of India with different dietary patterns and cultural practices. Additionally, while the trial is powered for detecting changes in DII, it is not powered to assess change in clinical endpoints. Lastly, although the intervention includes structured counseling and monitoring, long-term adherence beyond the study period remains uncertain and will require further evaluation in future pragmatic implementation studies.

Conclusion

This trial will be the first to rigorously evaluate the impact of a culturally adapted Indian Adapted Mediterranean Diet on dietary inflammatory potential and cardiometabolic risk in patients with or at moderate to high risk of coronary artery disease. Findings from this trial may inform future dietary guidelines and impact preventive strategies for cardiovascular disease among South Asians living in India and abroad.
