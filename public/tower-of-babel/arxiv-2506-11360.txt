Semi-empirical Pseudopotential Method for Monolayer Transitional-Metal Dichalcogenides Preprint: APS/other condensed matter
Raj Kumar Paudel 1,2, Chung-Yuan Ren 3, Yia-Chung Chang 1,2

Abstract
We present a Semi-empirical Pseudopotential Method for accurately computing the band structures and Bloch states of monolayer transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), including MoS 2 , MoSe 2 , WS 2 , and WSe 2 . Our approach combines local and non-local pseudopotentials, carefully fitted to replicate fully self-consistent density-functional theory results, while using only a minimal set of empirical parameters. By expressing the total potential as a sum of a few separable components, we achieve both accuracy and computational efficiency. The resulting pseudopotentials are transferable to more complex systems such as bilayer, trilayer, and moiré superlattices, offering a reliable foundation for large-scale simulations. When integrated with artificial intelligence techniques, this method provides a powerful tool for the design and exploration of TMDC-based nanodevices for next-generation optoelectronic applications.



I
Introduction

Layered transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) are a class of two-dimensional (2D) materials where covalently bound layers are stacked together by relatively weak van der Waals forces, fundamentally different from their bulk counterparts. The discovery of graphene in 2004
Novoselov et al. 2004
ignited extensive research into 2D materials due to its exceptional properties; however, its lack of a band gap limits its application. This has caused a shift of focus to two-dimensional TMDCs
Mak et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2012
;
Mattheiss 1973
, such as MoS
2
, MoSe
2
, MoTe
2
, WS
2
, WSe
2
, and WTe
2
, offer diverse electronic properties, ranging from semiconducting states to superconductors
Qi et al. 2016
. TMDCs with direct bandgap
Mak et al. 2010
are promising for use in next-generation optoelectronics
Liu et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2012
;
Qiu et al. 2013
, mechanical
Johari and Shenoy 2012
;
Shi and Wang 2018
, spintronics
Zhu et al. 2011
;
Yun et al. 2022
, and electrocatalysis
Li et al. 2018
, systems for energy storage and conversion. These materials also exhibit unique topological properties
Wu et al. 2019
, making them promising for novel electronic and quantum computing applications. Recent advances include the successful fabrication of high performance field effect transistors
Chen et al. 2020
, phototransistors
Li et al. 2014
, and gas sensors
Ko et al. 2016
, which highlight the potential of TMDCs in advanced technological applications and attract significant attention from both the scientific and industrial communities.

First-principles density functional theory (DFT)
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
;
Kohn and Sham 1965
;
Martin 2008
;
Cohen and Louie 2016
;
Ren and Chang 2022
;
Ren et al. 2023
combined with pseudopotential schemes
Herring 1940
;
Phillips and Kleinman 1959
;
Chelikowsky and Cohen 1976
;
Wang and Zunger 1995
;
Wang and Zunger 1996
;
Bester 2008
remains the workhorse to predict electronic properties. However, for 2D materials like TMDCs, conventional approaches using three-dimensional plane waves (PWs) face challenges due to the need for large vacuum spaces in supercell calculations to mimic 3D periodicity. Earlier work of Li et al.
Li and Chang 1994
;
Chang and Li 1996
indicated that a planar-based approach that combines plane waves in the periodic directions (x-y plane) with Gaussian functions in the non-periodic direction (z) is capable of accurate calculations of total energy and work function for isolated slabs. Ren et al.
Ren et al. 2015
;
Ren and Chang 2022
refined the above method using a mixed-basis approach that replaces Gaussian functions in the z direction with B-spline functions
de Boor 1987
;
Bachau et al. 2001
. The use of B-splines is advantageous in accurately representing both fast-varying and slow-varying electronic wavefunctions.
The mixed-basis approach offers advantages in preserving the layer-like geometry and reducing computational overhead in solving the Kohn–Sham equation.

The primary motivation for developing the Semi-empirical Pseudopotential (SEP) Method
Wang and Zunger 1996
;
Paudel et al. 2023
lies in the computational efficiency achieved by avoiding the self-consistent density optimization required in density functional theory. This approach is particularly beneficial for nanoscale structures comprising thousands to hundreds of thousands of atoms. Initially, empirical pseudopotentials defined in reciprocal space
Cohen and Bergstresser 1966
;
Chelikowsky and Cohen 1976
;
Pandey and Phillips 1974
were fitted to match experimentally determined energy levels, allowing accurate predictions of band structures and optical properties with minimal Fourier components. However, these pseudopotentials lacked transferability between different structures. This issue was addressed by developing continuous pseudopotentials in real space, enabling accurate calculations of the electronic, optical, and transport properties in nanostructures using advanced computational methods
Wang and Zunger 1995
;
Fu and Zunger 1997
;
Bester 2008
;
Molina-Sánchez et al. 2012
.

Recent machine learning (ML) has transformed empirical pseudopotentials
Kim and Son 2024
, providing new avenues for improving their transferability and accuracy. Momentum-range-separated, rotation-covariant descriptors capture local symmetry and bond-directional information on anisotropic solids. The “DeepPseudopot” model

Lin et al. 2025
, integrates neural networks for local pseudopotentials with parameterized non-local spin-orbit coupling factors. However, these ML-driven methods have focused only on bulk systems.

In our previous research
Paudel et al. 2023
, SEP was introduced for 2D systems such as graphene and graphene nanoribbons using a mixed basis approach. Using the semi-empirical pseudopotential (SEP) with just a few parameters, we successfully replicated the complete band structure of graphene. The SEP can also be used to model the armchair graphene nanoribbons (aGNRs) by adding the edge-induced correction potential (EICP). The SEP and EICP are applicable for aGNRs of any size, demonstrating the transferability of the SEP for application in large-scale aGNRs. In the present work, we extend the SEP to two‐dimensional TMDC monolayers, where the more complicated crystal structure (with three atoms per unit cell) poses additional challenges.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II presents the SEP formalism and details on our mixed-basis construction. Section III applies the method to layered TMDC materials, comparing the electronic properties obtained using SEP and DFT. Section IV concludes with future prospects on adding spin-orbit coupling and extension to bilayer TMDC systems including morié superlattices.

II.1
Atomic Structure and Basis Functions

We consider monolayer transition‐metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) in their hexagonal (2H) phase. Figure
1
(a) shows the top view of the atomic lattice, where the large (brown) spheres denote the metal atoms (M) and the smaller (yellow) spheres the chalcogen atoms (X). In the side view [Fig.
1
(b)], the atomic planes are aligned with a set of cubic B‐spline basis functions along the
z
–direction.

B‐splines of order
\kappa
(polynomial degree
\kappa-1
) are defined recursively on the knot sequence
\{t_{i}\}

de Boor 1987
;
Bachau et al. 2001
:

B_{i,\kappa}(z)\;=\;\frac{z-t_{i}}{t_{i+\kappa-1}-t_{i}}\,B_{i,\kappa-1}(z)\;+\;\frac{t_{i+\kappa}-z}{t_{i+\kappa}-t_{i+1}}\,B_{i+1,\kappa-1}(z),

(1)

with the zeroth‐order functions

B_{i,1}(z)=\begin{cases}1,&t_{i}\leq z<t_{i+1},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

Following the mixed‐basis pseudopotential approach
Ren et al. 2015
;
Ren and Chang 2022
;
Paudel et al. 2023
, the Bloch states of the monolayer TMDCs considered are expanded within a set of basis functions which are products of B-splines in
z
direction and 2D plane waves in the
xy
plane labeled by the 2D reciprocal lattice vectors
\mathbf{G}
. We denote the basis set as
\{B_{i}(z),\mathbf{G}\}
The effectiveness of B-splines lies in their flexibility through adjustable knot positions, and they are straightforward to evaluate and differentiate.

II.2
Construction of SEP from USPP

Our work focuses on the 2H phase of TMDCs, the most stable and widely studied structure in both monolayer and bulk forms. In 2H phase, each transition metal atom is surrounded by six chalcogen atoms in a trigonal prismatic geometry. A key feature of this structure is the transition of the fundamental bandgap that occurs when moving from indirect in bulk forms to direct in monolayers, making these materials promising for electronic and optoelectronic applications.

Within 2H structure, the metal atoms (Mo or W) occupy the Wyckoff 1c site at coordinates
(0,0,0)
, while the chalcogen atoms (S or Se) are positioned at the 2s sites
(1/3,2/3,\pm u)
. To ensure accurate structural representation, We perform total energy calculations to determine the relaxed internal coordinate
u
via full geometric relaxation.

We conduct systematic structural optimization for the four TMDC compounds MX
2
considered. The primitive lattice vectors are defined as
\mathbf{a}_{1}=\left(\sqrt{3}/2,-1/2\right)a
and
\mathbf{a}_{2}=\left(\sqrt{3}/2,1/2\right)a
. The basis vectors in reciprocal space are
\mathbf{b}_{1}=\frac{2\pi}{a}\left(1,-1/\sqrt{3}\right)
and
\mathbf{b}_{2}=\frac{2\pi}{a}\left(1,1/\sqrt{3}\right)
. These monolayers exhibit space group
P\overline{6}m2
with
D_{6h}
point-group symmetry.

Our SEP methodology starts by solving the Kohn-Sham equation
Kohn and Sham 1965
in the mixed-basis approach
Ren et al. 2015
. This equation comprehensively accounts for kinetic energy contributions alongside potential energy terms arising from both local and non-local pseudopotential components. We extract the local portion of the self-consistent effective potential from the DFT calculation in the mixed basis. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional
Perdew et al. 1996
within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) is used for the electronic structure calculations. We use the conjugate-gradient algorithm implemented previously
Shewchuk et al. 1994
for the eigenvector/eigenvalue search. The geometry optimization is achieved by using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS)
algorithm
Giannozzi et al. 2009
to find the total energy minimum.

To achieve convergent results, we used an energy cutoff of 30 Ry for 2D plane waves and 25 B-splines, distributed over a 4
a_{0}
range (where
a_{0}
is the lattice constant). A Monkhorst-Pack
7\times 7
k-point mesh, including the
\Gamma
-point, is employed to sample the 2D irreducible Brillouin zone (IBZ)
Monkhorst and Pack 1976
. For monolayer TMDC materials transition metals involving Mo and W, we incorporate non-local core corrections
Louie et al. 1982
. The self-consistent calculation continues until the potential variation is less than
10^{-7}
Ry.

In density functional theory (DFT), the electronic states of a solid are obtained by self-consistently solving the single-particle effective Schrödinger equations:

\left\{-{\nabla^{2}}+V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})+\hat{V}_{\text{nloc}}(\mathbf{r})\right\}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r})=\varepsilon_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r}),

(3)

where the
-{\nabla^{2}}
term describes the kinetic energy operator,
V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})
denotes the net effective local potential, and
\hat{V}_{\text{nloc}}(\mathbf{r})
represents the nonlocal pseudopotential.

The net effective potential
V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})
is given by:

V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})=V_{\text{ion}}(\mathbf{r})+V_{\text{hxc}}[\rho(\mathbf{r})],

(4)

where
V_{\text{ion}}(\mathbf{r})
represents the local ionic pseudopotential and
V_{\text{hxc}}[\rho(\mathbf{r})]
accounts for the Hartree and exchange-correlation contributions:

V_{\text{hxc}}[\rho(\mathbf{r})]=\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+V_{\text{xc}}[\rho(\mathbf{r})].

(5)

Here
\rho(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i}|\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
denotes the charge density of electrons in all occupied states.
V_{\text{hxc}}[\rho(\mathbf{r})]
is specific to the system and must be determined self-consistently.

1. Local Ionic Potential

The local ionic potential
V_{ion}(\mathbf{r})
is taken from the ultra-soft pseudopotential (USPP)
Vanderbilt 1990
of the relevant atoms. In the USPP method, the local ionic potential
V_{\text{ion}}(\mathbf{r})
is constructed to accurately reproduce the scattering properties of valence electrons in each atom while ensuring smoothness and transferability between different environments. It can be expressed as:

V_{ion}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\sigma,\mathbf{R}}[V_{c,LR}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r-R})+V_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r-R})],

(6)

where
\sigma
labels the atoms within each unit cell of the crystal.
\mathbf{R}
labels the lattice sites in the crytal.
V_{c,LR}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})
represents the long-range Coulomb potential due to the ionic core that decreases slowly with distance, and
V_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})
is a short-range term that contains the finer details of the core potential close to the nucleus.

The first term in Eq. (
6
) signifies the potential from an auxiliary charge distribution, calculated analytically as:

\displaystyle V_{c,LR}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\rho_{c}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\,d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\int e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}e^{i\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\frac{8\pi}{q^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{c}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\,d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\int e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\frac{8\pi}{q^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{c}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=8\pi\sum_{\mathbf{q}_{\parallel},q_{z}}\int e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\frac{1}{q_{\parallel}^{2}+q_{z}^{2}}\widetilde{\rho}_{c}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{q}_{\parallel}+q_{z}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}),

(7)

where
\widetilde{\rho}_{c}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{Z_{c}}{\mathrm{\Omega}}e^{-q^{2}R_{c}^{2}/4-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\bm{\tau}^{\sigma}}
and
\bm{\tau}^{\sigma}
denotes the position of the
\sigma
atom.
\mathrm{\Omega}
is the sample volume.

In the
(z,\mathbf{G})
representation, the Fourier transform of long-range core potential [the first term of Eq. (
6
)] is expressed as:

V^{\text{LR}}_{c}(z,\mathbf{G})=\frac{8\pi}{\Omega_{c}}\sum_{\sigma,\,g_{z}}\frac{Z_{c}^{\sigma}}{G^{2}+g_{z}^{2}}\,e^{ig_{z}(z-\tau_{z}^{\sigma})}\,e^{-\frac{(G^{2}+g_{z}^{2})R_{c}^{2}}{4}-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{\parallel}^{\sigma}}

(8)

Here,
\mathbf{G}
denotes the in-plane 2D reciprocal lattice vectors of the monolayer TMDC, and
g_{z}
is the out-of-plane component of the reciprocal lattice vector for the supercell used. The volume of the supercell is given by
\Omega_{c}=A_{c}L_{c}
, where
A_{c}
is the in-plane area and
L_{c}
is the length along the out-of-plane direction of the supercell.
\bm{\tau}_{\parallel}^{\sigma}
and
\tau_{z}^{\sigma}
denote the in-plane and out-of-plane components of the position vector
\bm{\tau}^{\sigma}
of atom
\sigma
within the unit cell.

The second term in Eq. (
6
) provides the short-range potential contribution. In the current SEP, we fit the
V_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})
term for the metallic atom (
\sigma=M
) with the following expression

V_{c,SR}^{M}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{5}C_{n}^{M}r^{2n},&r\leq r_{1}^{M},\\[10.0pt]
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{5}C_{n}^{M}r^{2n}e^{-\alpha_{M}r^{2}},&r^{M}_{1}<r\leq r_{2}^{M},\\[10.0pt]
\end{cases}

(9)

and for the chalcogen atom (
\sigma=X
) we use

V_{c,SR}^{X}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{5}C_{n}^{X}r^{2n},&r\leq r_{1}^{X},\\[10.0pt]
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{5}C_{n}^{X}r^{2n},&r_{1}^{X}<r\leq r_{2}^{X},\\[10.0pt]
\sum\limits_{n=0}^{5}C_{n}^{X}r^{2n},&r_{2}^{X}<r\leq r_{3}^{X},\end{cases}

(10)

The best-fit results (solid lines) are shown in Fig.
2
together with the numerical results (dots) used in the Vanderbilt Ultrasoft Pseudopotential (USPP)
Vanderbilt 1990
. The best-fit parameters are listed in Table
1
.

In the
(z,\mathbf{G})

V^{SR}_{c}(z,\mathbf{G})=\sum_{g_{z}}e^{i\mathbf{g}_{z}z}\widetilde{V}_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(|\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{g}_{z}|)e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{\parallel}^{\sigma}},

(11)

with

\widetilde{V}_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(|\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{g}_{z}|)=\int r\,dr\,\sin\left(|\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{g}_{z}|r\right)\frac{V_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(r)}{|\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{g}_{z}|}.

(12)

Here, we have used the identity

e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}=4\pi\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}(-i)^{l}j_{l}(Qr)Y_{lm}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})Y_{lm}^{\ast}(\hat{\mathbf{r}})

(13)

Here,
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
represents the solid angle of the vector
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{g}_{z}
in spherical coordinates.
j_{l}(Qr)
denotes the spherical Bessel function of order
l
. Since
V_{c,SR}^{\sigma}(r)
has spherical symmetry, only the
l=0
term survives. Using the analytic expressions in Eq. (
9
) and Eq. (
10
), the integral in Eq. (
12
) can be evaluated efficiently with the Gaussian quadrature method.

2. Hartree Exchange Correlation (hxc) Potential

To determine
V_{\text{hxc}}[\rho(\mathbf{r})]
, we begin by performing a self-consistent DFT calculation
Ren et al. 2015
. This produces the ground-state electron density
\rho(\mathbf{r})
, from which we derive the effective local potential
V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r})
. Next, we isolate the hxc potential by subtracting the core and atomic contributions. This step is essential because the hxc potential is specific to the system and is not transferable between different systems.

V_{\text{hxc}}(z,\mathbf{G})
, is derived by performing a Fourier transform of the real-space potential using the FFTW library
Frigo and Johnson 2005
. Due to the point-group symmetry of the crystal, the reciprocal lattice vectors
\mathbf{G}
can be grouped into different stars of
\mathbf{G}
, where all
\mathbf{G}
vectors within a star are related by symmetry. Consequently, the hxc potential
V_{\text{hxc}}(z,\mathbf{G})
for different
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}s
in the same star can be related to each other by the structure factor
S(\mathbf{G})
.

The structure factor of the 2D crystal is given by:

S(\mathbf{G})=S^{M}(\mathbf{G})+S^{X}(\mathbf{G}),

(15)

The contributions from the M and X atoms are:

\left\{\begin{aligned} S^{M}(\mathbf{G})&=e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\bm{\tau}^{M}}=1\quad(\text{with }\bm{\tau}^{M}=\mathbf{0}),\\
S^{X}(\mathbf{G})&=e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\bm{\tau}^{X_{1}}}+e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\bm{\tau}^{X_{2}}}=e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{\parallel}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

Here,
\bm{\tau}^{X_{1}}=(\bm{\tau}_{\parallel},z_{1})
and
\bm{\tau}^{X_{2}}=(\bm{\tau}_{\parallel},z_{2})
denote the positions of the two X atoms, which share the same in-plane coordinate
\bm{\tau}_{\parallel}
but have different vertical coordinates
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. For convenience, we set the origin of our coordinate system at the position of the
M
atom,
i.e.

\bm{\tau}^{M}=0
. Therefore,
S^{M}\left(\mathbf{G}\right)=1

To model the hxc potential, we break it down into long-range (LR) and short-range (SR) components in the following way:

V_{\text{hxc}}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right)=V_{\text{hxc}}^{LR}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right)+V_{\text{hxc}}^{SR}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right).

(17)

where the first term denotes the long-range contributions.
s
in the sum runs through different stars of
\mathbf{G}
up to the fourth star. The second term corresponds to the remaining contribution, which is expected to be a slow-varying function of
\mathbf{G}
, and therefore is short-ranged in the real space.

II.2.1
Short-Range Contribution

The short-range component of the exchange-correlation potential,
V_{\text{hxc}}^{\text{SR}}(z,\mathbf{G})
, is determined through the self-consistent DFT calculations. Our analysis reveals two critical features across all four transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs): First, the imaginary component of
V_{\text{hxc}}^{\text{SR}}
becomes negligible for reciprocal vectors beyond the fourth star (
|\mathbf{G}|>G_{4}
). Second, the
z
-dependence exhibits a Gaussian profile centered at the metal atomic plane (
z=0
), confirming the dominant contribution from the transition metal (M) atom.

The observed behavior motivates our proposed functional form:

V_{\text{hxc}}^{\text{SR}}(z,\mathbf{G})=DG^{4}e^{-bG^{2}}e^{-cz^{2}},

(18)

where the prefactor
DG^{4}e^{-bG^{2}}
governs the
\mathbf{G}
-dependence and
e^{-cz^{2}}
describes the spatial decay perpendicular to the monolayer. The factor
G^{4}
ensures minimal impact in small
\mathbf{G}
wave vectors, while the exponentially decaying factor
e^{-bG^{2}}
maintains the softness required for efficient convergence in plane-wave basis. In particular, this formulation naturally vanishes at
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{0}
, preserving the form of the leading term in the long-range potential.

II.2.2
Long-Range Contribution:

For lower
\mathbf{G}
stars (with
s\leq 4
), the long-range component is expressed as:

V^{LR}_{\text{hxc}}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right)=f^{M}(z)S^{M}\left(\mathbf{G}\right)+f^{X}(z)S^{X}\left(\mathbf{G}\right)

(19)

where the shape functions
f^{M}(z)
and
f^{X}(z)
are given by:

\displaystyle f^{M}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,2,3}A_{i}^{M}e^{-\alpha_{i}^{M}\left(z-\tau_{z}^{M}\right)^{2}},

\Delta V^{\text{LR}}_{\text{hxc}}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right)=V_{\text{hxc}}^{\text{DFT}}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right)-V_{\text{hxc}}^{\text{SR}}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right)-V_{\text{hxc}}^{\text{LR}}\left(z,\mathbf{G}\right),

which represents the discrepancy between the small-
\mathbf{G}
components of the potentials obtained from the two methods. To account for these deviations, we fit the difference using the following functional forms:

\displaystyle\Delta f_{LR}^{M}(z)

\displaystyle=p^{M}e^{-\alpha^{M}\left(z-\tau^{M}\right)^{2}}\cos\left(Q(z-\tau^{M})\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\Delta f_{LR}^{X}(z)

\displaystyle=p^{X}\left[e^{-\alpha^{X}\left(z-\tau^{X}\right)^{2}}+e^{-\alpha^{X}\left(z+\tau^{X}\right)^{2}}\right],

where
p^{M}
,
p^{X}
,
\alpha^{M}
,
\alpha^{X}
, and
Q
are the fitting parameters. In these equations,
p^{M}
and
p^{X}
represent scaling factors, while
\alpha^{M}
and
\alpha^{X}
control the width of the Gaussian-like decays. The parameter
Q
captures the oscillatory behavior in the potential difference.
These functions effectively model the deviations observed in the first two stars, enabling a more accurate representation of the long-range potential. The fitted values of the parameters for each material are summarized in Table

3. Nonlocal Pseudopotential

Nonlocal pseudopotentials (NLPPs) are essential for accurately modeling the electronic structures of layered TMDCs, which involve transition metals. It captures angular-dependent electron-ion interactions crucial for d-orbitals. NLPPs improve bonding, hybridization, and electronic structure, enabling better predictions of bandgaps over using only local pseudopotentials.

In the non-relativistic (NR) approximation, the separable formulation of
Kleinman and Bylander
Kleinman and Bylander 1982
(KB) is given by

\widehat{V}_{nl}=\sum_{\sigma lm,nn^{\prime}}E_{lm}^{nn^{\prime}}\left|\beta_{lm}^{n\sigma}\right\rangle\left\langle\beta_{lm}^{n^{\prime}\sigma}\right|,

(24)

Here, projector functions
\beta_{lm}^{n\sigma}
as well as coefficient
E_{lm}^{nn^{\prime}}
characterize the non-local pseudo-potentials for various atomic species (Mo, W, S, Se). The

\beta
functions take the following form:

\beta_{lm}^{n\sigma}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)=A_{l}^{n\sigma}(r)r^{l}Y_{lm}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}).

(25)

We fit the radial part
A_{l}^{n\sigma}(r)
of the beta functions for each orbital labeled by
(l,m)
for different atoms obtained from Refs.
Vanderbilt 1990
;
Garrity et al. 2014
.
For efficient fitting, we break down the fitting functions into two parts, which are separately defined in intervals
(0,R_{s})
and
(R_{s},R_{cut})
, respectively.

A_{l}^{n\sigma}(r)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{5}B^{\sigma}_{p}\,r^{2p}e^{-\alpha r^{2}},&\text{for }r<R_{s},\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{3}C^{\sigma}_{q}\,r^{2q}e^{-\alpha_{q}r^{2}},&\text{for }r\geq R_{s}.\end{cases}

(26)

Here,
B^{\sigma}_{p}
,
C^{\sigma}_{p}
,
\alpha
and
\alpha_{p}
are fitting parameters. The best-fit parameters for atoms M (Mo, W) and X (S, Se) are provided in Table
5
.
These parameters were adjusted by using variational minimization, resulting in a root-mean-square (RMS) fitting error below 1% across all orbital channels.
The comparison of our best-fit
A_{l}^{n\sigma}(r)
functions with those obtained from Ref.
Ren et al. 2015
is shown in Fig.
7
for (a) Mo, (b) W, (c) S, and (d) Se atoms.

III
Electronic Structure Calculations

The all-electron Bloch state of band
\nu
at in-plane wavevector
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
is constructed from a pseudo-wavefunction augmented by a projector term,

\psi_{\nu\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\rho},z)=\bigl(1+\widehat{S}\bigr)\varphi_{\nu\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\rho},z),

(27)

where
\bm{\rho}=(x,y)
lies in the TMDC plane and
z
is the out-of-plane coordinate. The pseudo-wavefunction is expanded in a mixed B-spline–plane-wave basis,

\varphi_{\nu\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\rho},z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}Z_{i\mathbf{G}}^{\nu,\mathbf{k}}\,B_{i}(z)\,e^{i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\bm{\rho}}.

(28)

Here
Z_{i\mathbf{G}}^{\nu,\mathbf{k}}
are the expansion coefficients. The operator
\widehat{S}
restores all-electron character in the core regions via nonlocal projectors. Substituting Eq. (
27
) into the all-electron Schrödinger equation

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{AE}}\psi_{\nu\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\rho},z)=E_{\nu}(\mathbf{k})\psi_{\nu\mathbf{k}}(\bm{\rho},z)

(29)

and projecting onto the basis of Eq. (
28
) yields the generalized eigenvalue problem

\begin{split}\sum_{i^{\prime},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\bigl\langle\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G};B_{i}\bigm|\widehat{H}\bigm|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\bigr\rangle Z_{i^{\prime}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}^{\nu,\mathbf{k}}\\
=E_{\nu}(\mathbf{k})\sum_{i^{\prime},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\bigl\langle\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G};B_{i}\bigm|1+\widehat{S}\bigm|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\bigr\rangle Z_{i^{\prime}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}^{\nu,\mathbf{k}}.\end{split}

(30)

Here,
\widehat{H}=-\nabla^{2}+\widehat{V}_{\rm loc}+\widehat{V}_{\rm nl}
is the pseudo-Hamiltonian comprising kinetic energy, local and nonlocal pseudopotential terms. Diagonalizing this matrix equation at each
\mathbf{k}
yields the band energies
E_{\nu}(\mathbf{k})
.
The matrix elements for the kinetic-energy term are given by

\left\langle\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G};B_{i}\middle|-\nabla^{2}\middle|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G^{\prime}};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle=[K_{i,i^{\prime}}+O_{i,i^{\prime}}|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^{2}]\delta_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}

(31)

with
K_{i,i^{\prime}}=\int dz(d/dz)B_{i}(z)(d/dz)B_{i^{\prime}}(z)
and
O_{i,i^{\prime}}=\int dzB_{i}(z)B_{i^{\prime}}(z)
.
The matrix elements for the local potential term
\widehat{V}_{\rm loc}
are given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G};B_{i}\middle|\widehat{V}_{\rm loc}\middle|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G^{\prime}};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{c}}\int dzB_{i}(z)V^{SEP}_{loc}(z,\mathbf{G-G^{\prime}})B_{i^{\prime}}(z)

where
V^{SEP}_{loc}(z,\mathbf{G-G^{\prime}})
is given in Eq. (23).

The matrix elements for nonlocal pseudo-potential term
\widehat{V}_{\rm nl}
are given by

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|\widehat{V}_{nl}\middle|\mathbf{K}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle

(33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma lm,nn^{\prime}}E_{lm}^{nn^{\prime}}\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|\beta_{lm}^{n\sigma}\right\rangle\left\langle\beta_{lm}^{n^{\prime}\sigma}\middle|\mathbf{K}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle e^{i(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{\tau}_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{c}}\sum_{\sigma l,m\geq 0}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}E_{lm}^{nn^{\prime}}\operatorname{Re}\left[P_{lm}^{in\sigma}(\mathbf{K})P_{lm}^{i^{\prime}n^{\prime}\sigma\ast}(\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\right]e^{i(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{\tau}_{\sigma}}

Here,
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}=\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
.
\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|\beta_{lm}^{n\sigma}\right\rangle
denotes the projection of beta function in the mixed basis, and
e^{i(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{\tau}_{\sigma}}
accounts for the structure factor of the crystal.

In terms of 3D plane waves, indexed by
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{K}+g_{z}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}
(where
g_{z}
denotes the reciprocal lattice vectors along the z-axis for the supercell adopted), the projection of the beta function in the mixed basis is given by

\begin{split}P_{lm}^{in\sigma}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)=&\,i^{l}\sqrt{A_{c}}\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|\beta_{lm}^{n\sigma}\right\rangle\\
=&\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{c}}}\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{z}}\widetilde{B}_{i}\left(g_{z}\right)I_{l}(Q)Y_{lm}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}).\end{split}

(34)

Here,
\widetilde{B}_{i}(g_{z})
is the Fourier transform of
B_{i}(z)
along the z-direction.
Y_{lm}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}})
denotes the spherical harmonics.

\begin{split}I_{l}(Q)&=4\pi\int_{0}^{R_{C}}drr^{l+2}A_{l}(r)j_{l}(Qr)\\
&=4\pi\sum_{i}A_{l}(r_{i})\left(r^{l+2}j_{l+1}(Qr)\right)\bigg|_{r_{i-}}^{r_{i+}}\end{split}

(35)

with

\begin{split}\int_{r_{i-}}^{r_{i+}}drr^{l+2}j_{l}(Qr)&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{r_{i-}}^{r_{i+}}drr^{l+2-1/2}J_{l+1/2}(Qr)\\
&=\left(r^{l+2}j_{l+1}(Qr)\right)|_{r_{i-}}^{r_{i+}}/Q.\end{split}

(36)

r_{i}
denotes the midpoint and
r_{i-}

(r_{i+})
denotes the lower (upper) bound of the
i
-th interval.

We can rewrite Eq. (
33
) as

\begin{split}&\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|{\widehat{V}}_{nl}\middle|\mathbf{K}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{A_{c}}\sum_{\sigma l,m\geq 0}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}^{\ }E_{lm}^{nn^{\prime}}\operatorname{Re}\left[P_{lm}^{in\sigma}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)P_{lm}^{i^{\prime}n^{\prime}\sigma\ast}\left(\mathbf{K^{\prime}}\right)\right]\\
&\quad\times{d_{m}e}^{i(\mathbf{G}^{{}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{\tau}_{\sigma}}\end{split}

(37)

where
d_{0}=1
and
d_{m}=2
for
|m|>0
. We define

Q_{lm}^{in\sigma}(K)=\left|P_{lm}^{in\sigma}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)\right|

and get

P_{lm}^{in\sigma}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)=Q_{lm}^{in\sigma}(K)e^{im\varphi_{K}}=Q_{lm}^{in\sigma}(K)\left(\frac{K_{x}+iK_{y}}{K}\right)^{m}.

The overlap matrix element can be expressed as

\begin{split}\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|\widehat{S}\middle|\mathbf{K}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle&=\sum_{n,n^{\prime},l,|m|}{q_{l}^{nn^{\prime}}Q_{lm}^{in}(K)Q_{lm}^{i^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(K^{\prime})}\\
&\quad\times\left(\frac{{K_{x}K^{\prime}}_{x}+K_{y}{K^{\prime}}_{y}}{KK^{\prime}}\right)^{|m|}d_{m}S^{\sigma}(\Delta\mathbf{G})\end{split}

(38)

where
S^{\sigma}
(
\Delta\mathbf{G})
is the
\sigma
-atom
contribution to the structure factor given in Eq. (
15
). Similarly, for
the nonlocal pseudo-potential, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle\mathbf{K};B_{i}\middle|\widehat{V}_{nl}\middle|\mathbf{K}^{\prime};B_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle=&\sum_{\sigma,n,n^{\prime},l,|m|}{E_{l}^{nn^{\prime}}Q_{lm}^{in}(K)Q_{lm}^{i^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(K^{\prime})}\\
&\times\left(\frac{{K_{x}K^{\prime}}_{x}+K_{y}{K^{\prime}}_{y}}{KK^{\prime}}\right)^{|m|}d_{m}S^{\sigma}(\Delta\mathbf{G})\end{split}

(39)

By using
E_{l}^{nn^{\prime}}
and
q_{l}^{nn^{\prime}}
from Table VI, we can evaluate the matrix elements of
\widehat{V}_{nl}
and
\widehat{S}
.

To exploit the mirror symmetry
z\to-z
of monolayer TMDCs, we construct even and odd combinations of the B-splines:

B_{i}^{\pm}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl[B_{i}(z)\pm B_{N+1-i}(z)\bigr],

(40)

where
N
is the total number of splines. As the Hamiltonian does not couple with even and odd states, the eigen-value problem decouples into two independent ones, reducing the computational cost by approximately a factor of four.

IV
CONCLUSION

We have developed a semi-empirical pseudopotential (SEP) method for monolayer TMDCs that is efficient, accurate for key electronic states, and easy to implement. Our method closely matches density functional theory (DFT) results for the electronic states while being orders of magnitude faster. This efficiency makes our approach ideal for modeling complex TMDC-based optoelectronic devices, where these states play a central role.

Our SEP framework is flexible and can be adapted to different TMDC systems without major modifications. Future improvements include the addition of a spin-orbit correction term to describe the significant spin-orbit splitting in the band structures due to the heavy elements involved. Additionally, we aim to extend the SEP to model moiré superlattices, a crucial area in 2D materials research. While this will require fitting more complex quasi-2D form factors for the moiré potential, it remains a feasible and promising direction. In addition to band structures, our method provides a convenient framework for studying electron-phonon interactions and transport properties in TMDC devices. This work has potential applications in semiconductor technology and nanoelectronics, providing a powerful tool for accelerated device design and material discovery.
