AI-guided design and biomaterial integration for next-generation extracellular vesicle therapeutics: an evidence-weighted translational framework
Authors: Jiaqi Tian, Qiang Li, Ning Li, Dandan Song, Zengfeng Li, Xiaotan Shi, Huiyu Sun, Shuyin Duan, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbractstExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are promising therapeutic and diagnostic platforms, yet their clinical translation remains constrained by population heterogeneity, limited target-site retention, uncertain biodistribution, and variable potency. These persistent limitations have motivated the convergence of artificial intelligence (AI), biomaterials, synthetic biology, and advanced validation systems into what we describe as a next-generation EV framework. The evidence supporting these enabling technologies is, however, uneven. AI methods have demonstrated clear value for retrospective classification and feature discovery but have rarely been prospectively validated for therapeutic design; biomaterial carriers provide measurable gains in local retention and release control yet show inconsistent reporting of EV integrity, dose normalization, and controls; and programmable or trigger-responsive EV systems remain largely preclinical. A similar pattern is evident in the clinical landscape, where trial activity in EV-based interventions continues to grow but most studies remain in early or exploratory phases and no FDA-approved EV therapy is currently available. We therefore present next-generation EVs as an evidence-weighted translational agenda with future progress contingent on validated potency assays, reproducible manufacturing, predictable biodistribution, and a clear separation of demonstrated systems from longer-term concepts.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04831-2.

From natural carriers to engineered precision

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) were first recognized in the early 1980 s during studies of reticulocyte maturation, where vesicular release was initially interpreted mainly as a mechanism for membrane remodeling [1]. Soon, the exosome secretion pathway was further characterized by Harding and colleagues and by Johnstone, who showed that multivesicular bodies can fuse with the plasma membrane and release intraluminal vesicles into the extracellular space [2], and subsequent work established that EVs carry proteins, lipids, metabolites, and nucleic acids capable of altering recipient-cell behavior [3]. In this review, we use EVs as the umbrella term, small EVs (sEVs) when size-based operational definitions are intended, and exosome only when the cited study or clinical product specifically uses that term or when endosomal origin is central to the claim.

The evolution of EV therapeutics can be organized into four successive phases, three of which are now established and a fourth that is designated here as the next-generation framework still emerging (Fig. 1; Table 1). The first-generation work established EV biogenesis, secretion mechanisms, cargo composition, and the distinction between vesicles and co-isolated non-vesicular particles. In contrast, second-generation studies treated EVs as naturally biocompatible carriers or biomarkers, testing relatively simple loading strategies such as passive incubation and sonication in preclinical systems [4, 5]. These earlier generations generated essential biological insight but provided limited control over product identity, potency, or manufacturing comparability.

In response to these limitations, third-generation EV engineering introduced genetic and chemical strategies to modify surface display, cargo loading, and immune recognition [6, 7]. Examples include ligand display, click chemistry, membrane anchoring peptides, parental-cell engineering, and incorporation of protective or targeting motifs such as CD47 signals. These approaches improved experimental controllability, but most remain constrained by heterogeneous vesicle populations, variable loading efficiency, uncertain biodistribution, limited scalability, and incomplete linkage between critical quality attributes (CQAs) and biological potency [8]. The unresolved issues motivate, but do not by themselves prove, the need for a next-generation paradigm.

Building on these foundations, we define next-generation EV therapeutics as engineered EV or EV-like systems that meet most of the following criteria: (i) design is guided by AI, mechanistic modeling, or quantitative structure-function relationships; (ii) targeting, cargo loading, or release behavior is experimentally validated over inferred from in silico predictions alone; (iii) CQAs such as identity, purity, integrity, potency, and stability are prospectively specified; (iv) the platform can be manufactured under a plausible GMP-compatible workflow; and (v) the regulatory pathway is considered as a biologic, device-combination, gene-therapy-related, or other product class depending on the final configuration. Under the definition, many examples discussed below are enabling technologies or proof-of-concept systems. This framework responds to commercialization, AI-engineering, and EV-biomaterial integration challenges identified in recent reviews [9–11].

Within the framework, AI is best regarded as a set of tools for prioritization, pattern discovery, and hypothesis generation. Machine learning (ML) models can help identify EV-associated signatures, nominate targeting motifs, and integrate multi-omics data [12, 13], but their translational value depends on patient-level independence, batch-effect control, clear ground-truth definitions, and external validation. These safeguards are especially important because EV composition and clinical utility are sensitive to isolation, characterization, and subpopulation definitions [14, 15]. High classification accuracy in a retrospective dataset therefore does not necessarily imply improved EV targeting, biodistribution, or therapeutic efficacy in vivo.

Biomaterial platforms, by contrast, address a more pharmacokinetic problem: systemically or locally administered sEVs can be cleared rapidly, diluted away from the target site, or degraded before sufficient uptake occurs [16]. Hydrogels, scaffolds, and hybrid vesicles can extend residence time, tune release kinetics, or provide local presentation cues [17–19]. The same processing steps, however, may alter EV membrane integrity, surface-marker accessibility, uptake route, or immune recognition. Translation therefore requires quantitative release data, dose normalization, EV-only and material-only controls, and post-processing integrity assays.

In stimulus-responsive engineering, most current systems are trigger-responsive, externally actuated, or genetically programmed in producer cells, with only a smaller subset approaching closed-loop behavior. Synthetic biology can encode conditional cargo expression or logic-gated release in producer cells [20–23], whereas exosome-mimetic nanovesicles and synthetic-biology-based bacterial EVs illustrate how engineered vesicle platforms can combine multiple functional components [24, 25]. These designs may reduce off-target exposure, but claims of adaptive behavior should be reserved for systems with experimentally demonstrated sensing, information integration, and output control in relevant biological environments.

Despite technological advances, clinical translation of EV therapeutics remains active but early. As of the registry snapshot taken on 1 Jan 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov listed 176 interventional EV-based trials meeting our inclusion criteria (search method and classification rules are detailed below, and full dataset can be found in Supplementary Material 1). Phase distribution in this dataset comprises 19 Early Phase I, 50 Phase I, 39 Phase I/II, 19 Phase II, 4 Phase II/III, 3 Phase III, 1 Phase IV, and 41 with no conventional phase listed (categorized as “NA”). The Phase III entries comprise androgenetic alopecia (NCT06539273, exosome complex/RNA; cosmetic/aesthetic), acute respiratory distress syndrome (NCT05354141, ExoFlo; disease-modifying), and laser-assisted skin rejuvenation (NCT07281690, UC-MSC exosomes; cosmetic/aesthetic), while the single Phase IV entry evaluates topical lyophilized exosomes with microneedling for facial skin aging (NCT07372001; cosmetic/aesthetic). This Phase IV record reflects registry classification of a post-marketing cosmetic-device study rather than a regulatory approval of an EV therapeutic, and should be described as “registry-listed Phase IV” without implying post-approval therapeutic maturity. No FDA-approved EV therapy is currently available, and IND clearance or permission to initiate a trial should not be described as FDA approval of the product.

The current review therefore evaluates next-generation EVs within an evidence-weighted framework. We first examine AI/ML applications while separating retrospective classification from prospectively validated design. We then analyze biomaterial-enabled EV retention, release, and presentation using quantitative and control-oriented criteria. Subsequent sections discuss responsive and programmable systems, manufacturing and quality control, clinical translation, and convergent validation platforms.

ClinicalTrials.gov search and classification method

To make quantitative claims, assign evidence levels, and construct comparative tables, we summarize here the literature search, inclusion/exclusion logic, evidence grading framework, and registry-search method.

Regarding literature search, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched up to May 2026 for peer-reviewed papers using combinations of the keywords, including extracellular vesicle(s), exosome(s), small EVs, microvesicle(s), with artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, peptide design, hydrogel, scaffold, hybrid vesicle, stimulus-responsive, manufacturing, quality control, biodistribution, potency assay, clinical trial, CRISPR delivery, organoid-on-chip, synthetic biology, digital twin, and biosensing. We prioritized: (i) original studies that report quantitative EV-specific outcomes; (ii) MISEV-compliant or characterization-transparent papers; (iii) recent syntheses where the field has moved quickly; and (iv) clinical or regulatory documents needed for translational claims. We excluded papers in-press preprints without peer review unless cited as commentary, predatory-journal sources flagged by Cabells/DOAJ status checks, non-English papers without an authoritative English translation, and papers whose primary readouts could not be linked to EV-specific identity controls.

To convert narrative claims into testable readiness statements, we graded supporting evidence on a six-level ladder, where E0 is computational or conceptual proposal without EV-specific experimental validation; E1 is in vitro characterization or functional testing; E2 is small-animal efficacy or biodistribution evidence; E3 is large-animal, GLP-like, or disease-relevant advanced preclinical validation; E4 is early human safety or feasibility evidence; and E5 is adequately powered efficacy or registration-level evidence. Technology readiness was scored separately from novelty, meaning that an AI-guided or responsive design remains low readiness if it lacks independent validation, scalable manufacturing, or a mechanism-linked potency assay, even where the design itself is sophisticated.

For clinical-trial census, we queried ClinicalTrials.gov on 1 Jan 2026 using the keyword set { “exosome” OR “extracellular vesicle” OR “small extracellular vesicle” OR “microvesicle” } with the filters Study Type = Interventional regardless of Status. Observational and expanded-access records were excluded a priori to focus the census on interventional development. Each returned record was reviewed manually against the following inclusion rule, the EV/exosome preparation must be a component of the study intervention, not merely a sampled biomarker, and against the following exclusion rules, including duplicate NCT identifier, animal-only trials, withdrawn before enrollment without re-registration, and records describing EV-isolated proteins or RNAs as biomarkers. After de-duplication, 176 unique NCT identifiers remained and constitute the dataset reported as Supplementary Material 1.

Practically, the 176 trials were classified into four mutually exclusive operational categories: (i) disease-modifying therapeutic (141 trials, 80.1%), trials targeting a recognized clinical disease or condition (e.g., COVID‑19, ARDS, stroke, osteoarthritis, diabetic foot ulcer) with exosomes/EVs administered as the primary therapy; (ii) cosmetic/aesthetic (25, 14.2%), trials whose primary outcome is appearance‑related, including alopecia, facial aging, skin rejuvenation, eyelash growth, melasma, and scalp revitalization; (iii) non-therapeutic (8, 4.6%), trials conducted in healthy volunteers (e.g., safety/tolerability of exosome inhalation or injection) or studies using exosome‑rich dietary supplements (e.g., whey‑protein milk‑derived exosomes) without a therapeutic claim; and (iv) others (2, 1.1%), trials where exosomes serve as a procedural adjunct such as in vitro oocyte maturation (NCT07249411) or embryo culture enhancement during IVF (NCT07085312).

AI-Guided EV engineering and validation standards

Predictive models for EV-cell interactions

EV biology is intrinsically heterogeneous, spanning differences in vesicle size, cargo composition, surface markers, and function both within a single preparation and across source cells, culture conditions, isolation methods, and disease states [26]. Moreover, many AI studies analyze EV-enriched preparations but rigorously purified vesicles, so the ground truth may include lipoproteins, protein aggregates, or other nanoparticles. AI/ML can identify patterns in high-dimensional data, but the resulting models are only as reliable as the sampling design, preprocessing pipeline, and biological labels used to train them [27].

Within the constraints, ML applications in EV research have progressed from classification tasks toward more demanding prediction and design workflows (Fig. 2). SVMs, random forests, k-nearest neighbor models, and CNNs have been used to distinguish cancer-derived from control EV-enriched samples using spectroscopic, proteomic, or morphological features [28, 29]. For example, SERS combined with SVM or CNN analysis has reported high accuracy in cell-line, animal, and small clinical plasma-EV datasets; in one recent study, mouse plasma-EV test sets reached 97.5% and 95.8% accuracy with SVM and CNN models, respectively, whereas clinical patient-control test sets showed 91.5% and 95.4% accuracy [30]. SERS cancer-biomarker reviews support the analytical promise of this approach while emphasizing assay design and validation constraints [31]. Such performance metrics are encouraging, but they require interpretation alongside cohort size, patient-level splitting, external validation, sample handling, and instrument drift, because leakage or batch effects can inflate apparent accuracy.

Deep learning architectures extend the analysis beyond conventional feature engineering. CNN-based image analysis and nanoaperture optical-tweezer workflows can capture subtle EV features that are difficult to score manually [32–34]. Related sequence/property models are useful in peptide design, but their success in peptide aggregation or molecular-property prediction should not be treated as evidence that EV biodistribution can already be predicted [35]. At present, most AI-EV studies remain retrospective or analytical. A model that classifies vesicles or predicts an EV-associated signature should not be equated with a validated therapeutic design model unless it has prospectively selected a formulation and shown improved biological outcome over empirical controls.

Beyond classification, predicting tissue tropism and cellular uptake is a central but still incompletely validated goal. EV biodistribution reflects an interplay of vesicle surface composition and recipient-cell receptor profiles, with growing evidence that EV membrane proteins, size distribution, and multi-omic cargo collectively shape uptake and downstream signaling [36, 37]. However, the additional contributions of opsonization, vascular barriers, and mononuclear phagocyte clearance remain less fully resolved in EV-specific reports. Multi-omic models can nominate candidate signatures and prioritize experiments [38, 39], yet robust claims about in vivo targeting require orthogonal biodistribution assays, standardized dose units, and independent replication in relevant animal or human-derived systems.

ML for cargo optimization

Therapeutic cargo loading poses a multidimensional optimization problem involving loading efficiency, cargo stability, endosomal escape, release kinetics, and preservation of vesicle function [40–43]. AI can reduce the experimental search space by prioritizing cargo-vesicle combinations, but the output must be assessed using physical loading measurements, functional potency assays, and appropriate unloaded, free-drug, and process-control comparators.

Among different classes of EV cargoes, RNA cargo design illustrates both the strengths and the limitations of AI-guided EV engineering. RNA localization resources and ML models can help annotate subcellular RNA patterns and predict exosomal miRNA biomarker candidates [44, 45]. Nevertheless, RNA sorting is context-dependent, and reported enrichment does not ensure therapeutic potency, durable target engagement, or lack of off-target immune stimulation. Experimental confirmation remains essential at the sequence, vesicle, and recipient-cell levels [46, 47].

Beyond RNA cargo, protein and small-molecule loading remain less mature areas for AI-guided EV design. A systematic review of engineered exosome cargo assessment emphasizes that loading efficiency, cargo stability, vesicle integrity, and functional potency must be measured together [48]. Programmable RNA loading and bioengineering strategies are beginning to offer more controlled cargo incorporation [49, 50], while macrophage- or plant-derived EV loading studies provide useful empirical benchmarks. For now, computational cargo prioritization should be framed as hypothesis generation unless it is paired with quantitative loading, release, and recipient-cell function assays.

AI-guided targeting peptide design

Targeting peptide design is a useful application area for computational screening because peptide-receptor affinity, protease stability, display geometry, and immunogenicity must be balanced [51]. Structure prediction and generative models can prioritize candidates, but the relevant readouts go beyond binding affinity to include improved uptake, biodistribution, and therapeutic index relative to non-targeted EVs, peptide-free controls, and conventional carriers.

Within the design space, computational search, molecular simulation, and peptide-property models can help prioritize targeting sequences, while their relevance depends on how the peptide is displayed on the EV membrane [52–58]. Notably, exosome-targeting peptides, GPI-anchored nanobodies, and other surface-display methods show that orientation and membrane context matter [53–56]. Computational predictions should therefore be paired with negative controls and off-target binding assessment, because a plausible predicted interface may fail on a curved EV membrane with heterogeneous protein and glycan composition.

More broadly, cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) and peptide-assisted delivery strategies can enhance uptake, endosomal escape, or genome-editing delivery in non-EV systems [59–61]. EV-focused reviews in spinal cord injury and brain infection contexts also discuss peptide or ligand-based targeting strategies [62, 63]. For EV therapeutics, the unresolved question is whether CPP display improves delivery selectively at the diseased tissue without increasing nonspecific uptake by liver, spleen, or immune cells, which requires biodistribution and toxicity readouts.

Combinatorial surface engineering expands the design space further. Graph neural networks and self-driving laboratory workflows may help prioritize ligand combinations [64, 65], but most examples remain enabling methods rather than EV-specific, prospectively validated design systems. Future studies should report training data provenance, independent test sets, calibration, uncertainty estimates, code or model availability, and whether AI-selected candidates outperform empirically selected comparators (Fig. 3).

Methodological boundaries and validation standards

The principal risk in AI-enabled EV research is overinterpreting retrospective pattern recognition as prospective therapeutic design. A robust AI study should report cohort size, preprocessing, external validation, EV characterization, validation design, and reported metric (Table 2). For EV-specific tasks, studies should also define what was labeled as EV or exosome, how co-isolated contaminants were excluded, and whether the model improved an experimental outcome such as loading, target-cell uptake, biodistribution, potency, or therapeutic index.

At present, the strongest evidence for AI in this field is analytical, including classification, biomarker discovery, and prioritization of candidate features. Evidence for AI-selected EV formulations that prospectively outperform empirical designs remains limited. Future work should therefore pair model outputs with blinded validation, independent laboratories, orthogonal EV characterization, and decision thresholds that remain stable across batches and instruments.

Biomaterial platforms for EV retention and controlled release

Hydrogel systems for sustained EV release

Therapeutic EV application is frequently limited by rapid clearance, dilution from the target site, and short local residence time. Biomaterials address these limitations by converting a bolus EV exposure into a local depot, scaffold-bound presentation system, or hybrid vesicle with altered release and trafficking properties. The central translational question is not whether a material can carry EVs, but whether it preserves EV identity and function while providing reproducible release, biodistribution, and dose-response behavior.

Among previous validated materials, hydrogels control EV release through mesh size, crosslink density, degradation rate, electrostatic interactions, and stimulus-responsive chemistry [68–73]. Quantitative reporting should therefore include time-to-50% release, cumulative release fraction, degradation or swelling behavior, EV dose unit, and the method used to distinguish intact EVs from soluble cargo or disrupted membranes. Without the controls, apparent release may reflect diffusion of contaminants or degraded vesicle components.

Natural polymer hydrogels are attractive because they combine biocompatibility with tissue-like biochemical cues, and ECM-derived matrices in particular present binding sites that EVs themselves exploit during retention and presentation [74]. In particular, ECM-derived hydrogels, chitosan-based gels, and thermoresponsive systems have shown sustained EV release and functional effects in wound, bone, cartilage, and cardiac repair models [18, 75–77]. Yet natural materials can introduce batch variability and undefined matrix cues, making material-only, EV-only, inactivated-EV, and contamination controls particularly important.

In practice, EV-laden hydrogels have been tested across diabetic wound healing, bone repair, and cartilage regeneration [18, 78, 79]. These studies support the concept that local retention can amplify EV-mediated regenerative signaling, especially when angiogenesis, immune modulation, and matrix remodeling are needed. The evidence remains predominantly preclinical, and comparisons across studies are difficult because particle number, protein mass, EV source, isolation method, and release assay design are not consistently reported.

Scaffold-based EV immobilization and presentation

Scaffold-based approaches emphasize surface immobilization or matrix-associated presentation. Examples include EV-functionalized Ti6Al4V scaffolds for osseointegration and EV-loaded or EV-associated polymer/collagen scaffolds discussed in hydrogel and scaffold reviews [68, 80]. This mode may better mimic matrix-bound signaling, but it also changes the uptake route and may reduce free diffusion, so functional readouts should distinguish scaffold contact effects from EV-mediated signaling.

Beyond contact-mediated effects, scaffold design must match mechanical and biological requirements of the target tissue. Synthetic-biology-inspired biomaterial design, mechanically reinforced decalcified bone matrix frameworks, and integrated exosome-hyaluronic acid hydrogel devices illustrate different ways to combine structure, biochemical cues, and vesicle-associated signaling [81–83]. These examples support the value of material architecture, and also show why release, degradation, mechanics, and EV integrity should be reported simultaneously.

Hybrid nanovesicles merge natural and synthetic platforms

Hybrid vesicles combine EV membranes or EV-like properties with synthetic liposomes or nanoparticles to improve loading capacity, stability, or manufacturability. Fusion of EVs with functionalized liposomes can enrich vesicles with therapeutic or imaging agents, with PEG-mediated EV-liposome fusion studies showing tunable transfer of lipophilic and hydrophilic probes into the EV fraction [84]. Engineered EV drug-loading studies further show that vesicle-based doxorubicin delivery can improve antitumor delivery compared with free drug in selected models [85]. These gains must be balanced against possible membrane remodeling, altered protein orientation, loss of EV subpopulation identity, and the need for controls that separate fusion products from residual free liposomes or protein aggregates.

To produce optimized hybrids, multiple membrane fusion techniques have been developed to generate hybrid exosome-liposome nanovesicles, each offering distinct advantages depending on the application requirements. The membrane extrusion method involves simultaneous passage of exosomes and liposomes through membrane pores with controllable size under physical pressure, producing mixed vesicles with more uniform particle size compared to incubation or freeze-thaw approaches. For example, hybrid nanovesicles generated by extruding fibroblast-derived exosomes with clodronate-loaded liposomes through 400 and 200 nm polycarbonate films have shown efficacy in treating pulmonary fibrosis [86]. An alternative approach uses DNA-mediated fusion between EVs and mRNA-loaded liposomes, providing a comparatively mild method that avoids the mechanical shear forces associated with conventional techniques [49]. The programmable methodology enables controlled loading at 37 °C without denaturing surface proteins or causing cargo loss, while allowing subsequent release of fused particles through strand displacement reactions.

Once produced, hybrid nanovesicles have been explored in oncology, neurological delivery, and regenerative medicine [87–89]. Some studies report strong tissue accumulation or barrier penetration, such as lung-biased localization after CAR-T cell-derived exosome-liposome fusion [90]. Such results are promising but context-specific, for example, biodistribution depends on route, dose, animal model, labeling method, and whether signal reflects intact vesicles, membrane fragments, or released cargo. These variables should be reported before generalizing hybrid EV performance.

Cell-derived nanovesicles represent an alternative approach to generating EV-like particles with enhanced scalability, produced by physically disrupting cells through serial extrusion using porous membrane filters, microfluidic devices, or ultrasonication. Unlike naturally secreted exosomes harvested from conditioned media, cell-derived nanovesicles are artificially synthesized using physical methods with living cells, yielding 100 to 250 times higher productivity than conventional EV isolation approaches [91]. Despite their artificial origin, these nanovesicles maintain biochemical, structural, and functional properties similar to naturally secreted exosomes while avoiding the scalability constraints that limit clinical translation of native EVs. Cell-derived nanovesicles offer superior advantages over synthetic lipid nanoparticles since they contain no synthetic chemical components but instead carry cocktails of therapeutic biomolecules that can trigger multiple therapeutic mechanisms [92]. The processes involved in their synthesis closely resemble liposome extrusion methods, enabling uniform particle size through sequential passage through polycarbonate membranes with decreasing pore sizes. As the field continues to evolve, the convergence of natural EV biology with synthetic engineering approaches may help produce biomaterial platforms with optimized performance characteristics for specific therapeutic contexts (Fig. 4).

Quantitative comparability and integrity controls

Across biomaterial-enabled EV delivery, the most informative comparison is not hydrogel versus scaffold versus hybrid vesicle considered in isolation, but the match between clinical need and release mechanism. Hydrogels are most suitable for injectable local depots, scaffolds are useful when tissue architecture and contact-mediated presentation matter, and hybrid vesicles are attractive when payload capacity or membrane function must be modified. Each approach introduces distinct failure modes, including burst release, EV disruption, altered uptake, inflammatory material responses, or residual synthetic carrier contamination.

To support such comparisons, minimum reporting should include EV source, separation method, particle/protein ratio, dose unit, release medium, time-to-50% release, cumulative release fraction, post-release EV morphology and markers, potency after release, and controls. These parameters would make the literature more comparable and directly address regulatory concerns about identity, purity, and potency (Table 3).

Stimulus-responsive and programmable EV systems

Endogenous stimuli-responsive systems

Stimulus-responsive EV systems aim to restrict therapeutic release to disease-associated biochemical or externally triggered conditions [96–98]. To avoid overstating current capabilities, we treat trigger-responsive and adaptive systems as separate categories. Trigger-responsive systems to which most current EV-related systems belong release cargo after pH, redox, enzymatic, light, ultrasound, magnetic, or thermal stimulation, whereas adaptive systems would require demonstrated sensing, information integration, and feedback-controlled output.

pH-responsive mechanisms have been the most extensively studied of these endogenous triggers, particularly in cancer therapy, where the characteristically acidic microenvironment of solid tumors produces extracellular pH values of 6.2–6.8, in contrast to the normal physiological pH of 7.4 [99]. The pH differential provides an intrinsic trigger for selective drug release within tumor tissues, and polymeric micelles can be engineered with acid-labile linkages or charge-reversal moieties that respond to the acidic tumor microenvironment, enabling localized cargo release upon endocytosis or in the extracellular tumor milieu [100]. Studies show that ExoSpHL-DOX, a hybrid of breast cancer exosomes fused with pH-sensitive liposomes, achieves pH-triggered doxorubicin release (96.6% at pH 5.0 in 24 h), and it exhibits cytotoxicity comparable to free doxorubicin in 4T1 breast cancer cells, demonstrating its potential as a tumor-targeted delivery system [93]. Furthermore, tumor cell-derived exosomes cultured under low pH and hypoxic conditions mimicking the tumor microenvironment demonstrate stronger tumor-targeting specificity both in vivo and in vitro compared to those derived under normal culture conditions [101]. The current findings underscore how engineering EVs to exploit pathophysiological gradients can substantially enhance therapeutic selectivity.

Redox-responsive systems are a further approach for tumor-targeted EV delivery, exploiting the pronounced gradient in glutathione (GSH) concentrations between the extracellular matrix and the intracellular cytosol, particularly in tumor cells that exhibit elevated GSH levels [102]. Delivery systems that incorporate disulfide bonds or other redox-sensitive linkages into their structural backbone, a strategy widely adopted in synthetic drug delivery, undergo cleavage upon cell internalization where higher GSH concentrations trigger controlled drug liberation [103]. This concept has been adapted for use in stem cell-derived vesicles and immune cell conjugates, achieving intracellular release specifically within target cell populations while maintaining cargo stability during systemic circulation [104]. Enzyme-responsive systems offer an additional layer of specificity by exploiting the aberrant enzyme expression characteristics of diseased tissues. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), highly upregulated in tumor stroma, serve as particularly effective triggers for EV-based delivery systems. Dual-release exosomes incorporating both MMP-cleavable and pH-sensitive linkers demonstrate enhanced tumor targeting and significant inhibition of tumor recurrence compared to non-responsive carriers [105, 106].

External stimuli-triggered release

External stimuli-responsive systems can add temporal control through light, ultrasound, magnetic fields, or temperature modulation [107–109]. Practical constraints should be considered explicitly. NIR light has limited penetration in deep tissues and may require fiber-based or local access, while magnetic systems depend on nanoparticle loading, field strength, and heating safety. In parallel, ultrasound parameters must balance release enhancement against tissue damage, cavitation effects, and EV integrity.

Ultrasound-responsive carriers, in particular, are of interest because ultrasound can provide non-ionizing external actuation with spatial and temporal control. In drug-delivery systems, ultrasound can enhance tissue penetration, disrupt carrier membranes, or trigger release through cavitation, sonoporation, and mechanical stress [110]. EV-focused ultrasound studies indicate that ultrasound can influence EV production, tissue delivery, or release behavior, but the field still needs clearer separation among production enhancement, carrier activation, and true EV cargo release [111]. Hydrogel carriers with bubble-vibration enhancers further illustrate ultrasound-triggered release mechanisms within the broader biomaterials field, although such platforms should not be generalized to EV therapeutics without EV-specific integrity and potency testing [112].

Magnetically responsive systems, by contrast, use magnetic nanoparticles for imaging, localization, or externally induced heating. SPION labeling of EVs supports MRI-compatible and multiscale tracking, enabling assessment of whether labeling preserves EV size distribution and uptake behavior [113]. For drug release and hyperthermia specifically, the evidence base remains stronger in magnetic lipid or nanoparticle systems than in clinically validated EV products [114]. EV applications should therefore distinguish magnetic tracking from magnetic actuation and should report free-particle contamination, labeling stability, EV integrity, heating parameters, and off-target accumulation.

Synthetic biology approaches for programmable behavior

Synthetic biology, an enabling framework established in cellular and molecular engineering, provides a route to programmable EV production, especially when producer cells or bacterial vesicle systems are engineered to respond to defined molecular inputs [20–22]. These systems can implement logic-like behavior at the cellular production stage, but the vesicles themselves usually do not compute after administration unless sensing and output components remain functional in the target environment. Accordingly, adaptive or logic-gated terminology should be limited to experimentally demonstrated input-output behavior.

In current implementations, the design of genetic circuits for therapeutic applications has advanced considerably, with AND-gated systems requiring multiple simultaneous biomarker inputs to trigger activation offering enhanced specificity compared to single-input designs. CAR-T cells engineered with AND-gate logic, for example, can be activated only when all specified biomarkers are present on target cells, providing a safety layer that minimizes off-target effects [115]. Translating these principles to EV-based delivery systems enables the creation of vesicles that release their cargo only upon detection of disease-specific molecular signatures. Synthetic gene circuits responsive to various molecular stimuli, including pH changes, metabolites, hypoxia-induced factors, and disease-specific proteins, have been developed and integrated into mammalian cells that produce engineered EVs [23]. These producer cells can be designed to secrete vesicles containing therapeutic payloads only under specific physiological conditions, creating a layer of regulation at the production stage that complements responsive release mechanisms [116].

Building on the circuit designs, closed-loop therapeutic EV systems remain a forward-looking concept. Implanted or engineered producer cells could, in principle, couple biomarker sensing to EV cargo production or release, and designer-cell work outside the EV field illustrates the feasibility of closed-loop therapeutic control [117]. CRISPR/dCas9 regulatory modules also show how programmable gene-control systems can be built beyond genome editing [118]. Translation to EV therapeutics would require containment strategies, fail-safe circuits, long-term stability, immunogenicity assessment, and regulatory oversight for an adaptive biologic or combination product.

A more tractable variation combines multiple responsive modalities to achieve sequential or hierarchical activation, where both tumor acidity and ultrasound exposure are required before full cargo release. These designs are better described as multi-gated release systems, and their translational value will depend on whether each trigger remains selective in vivo, whether activation can be quantified, and whether added complexity improves therapeutic index compared with simpler EV or nanoparticle controls (Fig. 5).

Manufacturing, quality control, and regulatory readiness

Scalable production platforms

Translation depends on manufacturing platforms that can generate reproducible EV preparations at clinically relevant scale while maintaining predefined CQAs [119]. For engineered, hybrid, or AI-designed EVs, the manufacturing question extends beyond yield to encompass the preservation of identity, purity, integrity, potency, and comparability after each modification step.

Of the scalable culture platforms, hollow fiber bioreactors are widely adopted for large-scale EV production, with notable gains in culture-volume capacity and product yield [120]. They enable the cultivation of producer cells at densities exceeding 108 cells/mL, representing orders of magnitude improvement over conventional flask culture. Recent studies using hollow fiber bioreactors for MSC-derived EV production have demonstrated yields up to 38-fold greater per unit volume compared to small-scale flask systems while maintaining comparable physical identity characteristics including particle diameter, protein content, and surface marker profiles [121]. Furthermore, standardization efforts toward upscaled manufacturing in hollow-fiber bioreactors have shown that dental pulp stem cell-derived EVs retain their pro-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory properties across production scales, a critical consideration for therapeutic applications [122]. The continuous perfusion capabilities of these systems additionally enable extended culture periods and multiple harvest cycles, further amplifying cumulative EV yields.

Vertical-wheel bioreactors represent an alternative 3D culture platform that uses controlled hydrodynamic forces to enhance EV production [123]. The slow rotational motion within these reactors generates flow patterns resembling physiological circulation, simultaneously improving nutrient distribution and mechanically stimulating cells to increase vesicle secretion. Studies using human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived blood vessel organoids in vertical-wheel bioreactors have demonstrated 2–3-fold improvements in EV production compared to static culture conditions, with comprehensive lipidomic analyses confirming that the vesicles maintain characteristic membrane compositions [124]. Similarly, microcarrier-based suspension culture in vertical-wheel systems enables MSC expansion to densities of 2–6 × 105 cells/mL, providing the cellular biomass necessary for industrial-scale EV manufacturing [125]. The integration of such bioreactor platforms with dissolvable microcarriers and human platelet lysate-supplemented media has established complete upstream processes compatible with good manufacturing practice requirements.

Beyond optimizing culture systems, researchers have explored cellular and environmental stimulation strategies to enhance EV secretion rates from producer cells. Hypoxic preconditioning, thermal stress, changes in pH, and mechanical shear forces each modulate cellular EV release through distinct mechanisms. Thermal stress at 37–42 °C for brief periods has been shown to increase EV production depending on cell type, while acidic pH conditions characteristic of tumor microenvironments stimulate enhanced exosome secretion from cancer cells [126, 127]. Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound increases bone marrow MSC exosome secretion through autophagy activation mechanisms without affecting vesicle morphology or marker expression [128]. These stimulation approaches, when integrated with bioreactor platforms, offer synergistic opportunities for maximizing production efficiency. The emerging vision for next-generation manufacturing encompasses closed, automated systems integrating upstream cell expansion, controlled stimulation protocols, and in-line monitoring to achieve the target production for late-phase clinical trials and commercialization.

Isolation and purification technologies

The downstream processing of EVs presents unique challenges distinct from conventional biopharmaceuticals, as the vesicles must be separated from a complex mixture of proteins, nucleic acids, lipoproteins, and cellular debris while preserving their structural integrity and biological activity. Differential ultracentrifugation, long considered the gold standard for EV isolation, suffers from significant limitations including low throughput, potential vesicle damage from high centrifugal forces, and co-isolation of contaminants with overlapping sedimentation properties [129]. These shortcomings have driven the development of alternative purification strategies more amenable to clinical-scale manufacturing.

Tangential flow filtration (TFF) has emerged as the central technology for scalable EV concentration and initial purification. Unlike dead-end filtration, TFF systems apply a constant flow parallel to the filter membrane, enabling continuous processing of large volumes while minimizing membrane fouling. It effectively segregates particles based on molecular weight cutoff, enriching vesicles in the 50–650 nm size range while removing smaller protein contaminants [130]. When combined with subsequent size exclusion chromatography, TFF-based workflows achieve superior purity compared to ultracentrifugation-based methods, with studies demonstrating removal of greater than 97% of protein impurities while maintaining high EV yields [131]. The scalability of TFF is further evidenced by its successful integration into GMP-compliant manufacturing pipelines, where hollow fiber filter cartridges can process liters of conditioned medium efficiently [132].

Complementing TFF, size exclusion chromatography (SEC) provides gentle, non-destructive separation based on hydrodynamic radius, making it useful for preserving EV functionality [133]. Scalable TFF-SEC workflows and commercial SEC resins such as Cytiva’s superSEC illustrate how industrial processing can improve flow properties and fractionation, while multimodal resins such as Capto Core 700 are used as complementary impurity-removal steps instead of as proof that SEC alone is sufficient [134]. Published ultrafiltration-plus-SEC workflows also show that combined methods can improve yield while preserving intact biophysical and functional properties [135]. For therapeutic products, SEC should usually be integrated with orthogonal purity, identity, and potency assays but treated as a complete purification solution.

The most recent innovations in EV purification integrate multiple separation principles within hybrid platforms to maximize both yield and purity. The ExoTFF system exemplifies the approach by combining electrokinetic filtration with size-based TFF, selectively capturing negatively charged EVs on positively charged mesh elements before size-based fractionation [136]. The dual-principle isolation enables purification of EV subpopulations meeting both charge and size criteria, achieving purity levels unattainable through single-principle methods such as SEC alone. Microfluidic platforms additionally offer precise manipulation at the nanoscale, with systems like exosome dean flow fractionation (ExoDFF) enabling label-free separation through purely inertial mechanisms at throughputs approaching 1 L per 16 h [137]. Looking forward, the integration of AI-driven quality control frameworks with these advanced purification technologies may improve the availability of clinical-grade EVs while ensuring batch-to-batch consistency.

Characterization and quality assurance

Comprehensive characterization of EV preparations is a central element of quality assurance, with the field guided by the Minimal Information for Studies of EVs (MISEV) guidelines established by the International Society for EVs. MISEV2023, the most recent iteration of these guidelines, emphasizes rigorous documentation of EV nomenclature, collection procedures, separation techniques, and characterization methodologies to ensure reproducibility and enable meaningful comparison across studies [138]. The framework recommends assessment of EV identity through demonstration of at least three positive protein markers (typically tetraspanins CD9, CD63, CD81 and cytosolic proteins ALIX, TSG101) alongside negative markers indicating absence of cellular contaminants. Single-particle characterization using two complementary techniques such as nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) paired with electron microscopy is required to confirm vesicle morphology and size distribution.

Using Brownian motion tracking of scattered light to infer hydrodynamic diameter, NTA is now a standard technique for determining EV concentration and size distribution. Functionally, NTA instruments can analyze particles ranging from approximately 30–1000 nm, though sensitivity limitations below 70 nm and challenges with polydisperse samples necessitate complementary approaches [139]. Nano-flow cytometry offers higher resolution characterization, with instruments like the Flow NanoAnalyzer capable of detecting single EVs as small as 40 nm and simultaneously assessing surface marker expression at the individual vesicle level [140]. Capture materials and single-EV marker-distribution studies further show why surface-marker accessibility and tetraspanin heterogeneity must be controlled during QC [141, 142]. Advanced systems integrating fluorescence detection enable population-level profiling of EV subsets based on marker combinations, supporting the heterogeneity analyses essential for understanding therapeutic mechanisms [143].

Beyond size and concentration, electron microscopy techniques provide the gold-standard morphological characterization of EVs, with cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) offering particular advantages by preserving native structure in frozen-hydrated states without fixation artifacts. Cryo-EM reveals the considerable morphological heterogeneity within EV populations, distinguishing genuine lipid bilayer vesicles from co-isolated contaminants including lipoproteins and protein aggregates. Recent studies applying cryo-EM to fresh plasma samples have demonstrated that true EVs constitute only a small fraction of submicron particles, underscoring the importance of stringent purity assessment [144]. Atomic force microscopy complements these approaches by enabling measurement of mechanical properties including membrane stiffness and elasticity, parameters increasingly recognized as functionally relevant for EV-cell interactions [145]. Meanwhile, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy provides label-free chemical composition information by amplifying vibrational signals from EV surface molecules, offering rapid characterization without extensive sample preparation [146].

Apart from physical and morphological characterization, clinical-grade EV development requires potency assays that connect measurable attributes to function. Practical examples include macrophage polarization or cytokine suppression assays for immunomodulatory MSC-EVs, endothelial tube formation or scratch-wound assays for pro-angiogenic wound-healing EVs, target-gene knockdown for RNA-loaded EVs, editing efficiency and off-target profiling for CRISPR-loaded EVs, and target-cell killing or antigen presentation for oncology EVs [50, 147]. These assays should be qualified against dose, purity, and release specifications and should be sensitive to batch-to-batch variation (Fig. 6, a detailed summary of all reproduced images and permissions is provided in Supplementary Material 2).

Regulatory classification and adaptive-system challenges

Regulatory classification will depend on the final product architecture. Native or minimally modified human-cell-derived EVs may be reviewed as biologic or advanced therapy-like products, whereas EVs combined with scaffolds, microneedling, hydrogels, or external actuators could be consideredcombination products. Even if the delivery vehicle is vesicular, EVs carrying CRISPR components, mRNA, or engineered producer-cell outputs may raise gene-therapy-like questions even if the delivery vehicle is vesicular. Meanwhile, bottom-up synthetic EVs or EV mimetics may require additional justification of functional equivalence to natural EVs and careful definition of impurities.

AI-designed or learning systems create an additional challenge, where regulators will need to know which model version informed the product, what data were used, how performance was locked before manufacturing, and whether future model updates could change CQAs. For clinical products, adaptive algorithms should be treated as controlled development tools unless a validated change-management plan, audit trail, and bridging strategy are in place.

Clinical translation, safety evidence, and outstanding gaps

Preclinical development requirements

The preclinical development of next-generation EV therapeutics requires comprehensive characterization across pharmacological, toxicological, and pharmacokinetic dimensions to establish the safety and efficacy profiles necessary for investigational new drug applications. Unlike conventional small molecule or antibody therapeutics, EVs present unique challenges for preclinical assessment arising from their complex composition, biological heterogeneity, and cell-derived nature. Investigators must provide comprehensive in vitro and in vivo data demonstrating the pharmacological and toxicological profiles of EVs, with particular attention to the selection of representative animal models that enable meaningful assessment of safety, biodistribution, immunogenicity, and tumorigenicity. A risk-based approach, as articulated for advanced therapy medicinal products by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), should guide the design of nonclinical studies commensurate with the product’s complexity and intended clinical application.

Within the risk-based framework, biodistribution studies constitute a critical component of the preclinical package, elucidating how administered EVs distribute across tissues and organs following various routes of administration. Systemically administered EVs typically accumulate predominantly in the liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, with clearance mediated through mononuclear phagocyte system uptake [151]. However, EVs from different cellular sources exhibit distinct endogenous homing properties and organotropism that can be exploited for therapeutic targeting [150]. Multiple labeling and imaging approaches, including fluorescent, radiolabel, and magnetic methods, can support EV tracking, but each label has limitations related to signal persistence, label transfer, depth, and whether the signal reflects intact vesicles [63]. SPION labeling provides a direct example of MRI-compatible EV visualization and highlights the need to verify that labeling does not substantially alter EV size or uptake behavior [113].

In parallel, immunogenicity assessment takes on particular importance for EV therapeutics, especially those derived from allogeneic sources or incorporating engineered modifications. While EVs generally exhibit low immunogenicity compared to cellular therapies due to their subcellular nature, they may carry pathogen-associated molecular patterns or inflammatory molecules that elicit immune responses upon repeated administration. Studies have demonstrated that repeated administration and surface modification of exosomes do not necessarily increase immunogenicity or toxicity, making them suitable for long-term therapeutic applications, though this must be verified for each specific product [152]. PEGylation and membrane engineering strategies, such as CD47 expression to provide “don’t eat me” signals, can mitigate recognition and clearance by the immune system [47]. Regulatory authorities require extensive long-term toxicology, biodistribution, and off-target effect data before approving clinical investigation, particularly for engineered EVs that may present novel safety considerations.

Clinical safety and efficacy evidence

Published clinical evidence supports short-term feasibility and an encouraging safety profile for several EV preparations, but efficacy remains less certain [153]. Reviews of oncology EV nanosystems and dendritic-cell-derived exosome immunotherapy summarize early cancer experience [154, 155], while more recent MSC-EV trials in severe COVID-19 and complex perianal fistula reported acceptable short-term safety in small or exploratory clinical settings [156, 157]. The results demonstrate safety and feasibility, but do not constitute evidence of therapeutic efficacy, because many trials are small, heterogeneous, open-label, or exploratory.

Therapeutic EV applications can be grouped into immunostimulatory oncology approaches, immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory applications, and regenerative strategies. Some studies report disease stabilization, improved inflammatory markers, oxygenation changes, or wound-related outcomes [155, 158, 159]. However, endpoint definitions, dose units, product characterization, and potency assays vary substantially, limiting cross-trial comparison and preventing confident attribution of outcomes to specific EV attributes.

Taken together, the main lesson from current clinical translation is that favorable safety does not close the identity-potency-efficacy gap. Many published studies did not report validated potency testing before administration, and most relied on characterization panels that are useful for identity but insufficient for predicting clinical response [138]. Next-generation design should therefore start from the clinical failure modes, for example, by addressing uncertain biodistribution, weak dose-response relationships, incomplete potency assays, lack of manufacturing comparability, and unclear release specifications for biomaterial or hybrid products.

Therapeutic indications and ongoing trials

Current therapeutic indications include neurological, respiratory, dermatological, wound-healing, inflammatory, and oncology-related applications. Neurological trials remain early stage, for example, intranasal adipose MSC-exosomes are being evaluated for Alzheimer’s disease (NCT04388982), and iPSC-derived EVs are being assessed for Moyamoya disease (NCT07065409). Preclinical neurodegeneration studies suggest possible anti-inflammatory and synaptic effects of hypoxia-preconditioned MSC-EVs, but these data should not be treated as clinical efficacy evidence [160]. Preclinical evidence supports AB126 (neural stem-cell-derived EVs) as a candidate for ALS-related neuroinflammation [161]. Consistent with the framing used throughout this review, regulatory clearance to initiate clinical investigation under an IND pathway does not constitute FDA approval of the product.

Respiratory EV applications expanded during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Registry activity includes ExoFlo studies for ARDS and COVID-19-associated ARDS more broadly (NCT04493242, NCT05354141), while reviews summarize the broader rationale for MSCs and MSC-derived exosomes in COVID-19-associated inflammatory lung disease [162]. Preclinical pulmonary evidence also includes attenuation of influenza virus-induced acute lung injury by MSC-derived EVs in a pig model [163]. Together, these data support respiratory translation as an active area, but they should be discussed with attention to trial design, comparator, endpoint, and whether observed benefits exceed supportive care or placebo controls.

Dermatological and wound-healing applications include Rion’s Phase II diabetic foot ulcer study (NCT06319287) and Aegle’s AGLE-102 trial for dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (NCT04173650). MSC-EV skin-regeneration and immunomodulatory studies provide a biological rationale for this application area, but cosmetic or aesthetic trials, including skin rejuvenation and hair-related studies, expand safety exposure without necessarily proving disease-modifying activity [164].

Oncology applications continue to evolve from dendritic-cell EV immunotherapy toward plant-derived, hybrid, and engineered EV approaches [165, 166]. A trial evaluating plant-derived exosomes as a curcumin delivery system in colorectal cancer tissue (NCT01294072) illustrates the diversity of sources under clinical study. Most next-generation oncology designs, including AI-selected targeting motifs, responsive release, and synthetic-biology-controlled production, remain preclinical and require rigorous biodistribution, potency, and toxicology packages before human testing (Fig. 7).

Convergent validation technologies

EV-mediated CRISPR gene editing

EV-mediated CRISPR delivery is a compelling but high-risk example of convergent engineering [167, 168]. EVs may reduce some limitations of viral vectors or lipid nanoparticles, yet editing therapeutics require stringent control of cargo loading, target-cell specificity, editing durability, off-target events, innate immune activation, and repeat-dosing safety. Current EV-CRISPR studies demonstrate feasibility in selected preclinical contexts, including oncogenic KRAS targeting and cardiac genome-editing models [169, 170], but they remain far from a standardized therapeutic class.

On the technical side, the optimization of EV-based CRISPR delivery systems has progressed through systematic engineering approaches addressing the fundamental challenges of loading efficiency, cargo protection, endosomal escape, and therapeutic durability [170]. EV-liposome hybrid nanoparticles created through membrane fusion have been validated for functional siRNA delivery, with the hybrid platform restoring the transfection capacity of free EVs while expanding loading capacity beyond what either parental vesicle achieves alone [171]. The authors establish a methodological precedent for adapting the same fusion approach to CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein or plasmid cargo, although direct CRISPR-payload validation in EV-liposome hybrids is still limited. Small EV-mediated delivery of Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes has been optimized for cardiac-specific genome editing applications, with recent studies demonstrating therapeutic potential for correcting cardiomyopathy-associated mutations [170]. Inflammation-inducible CRISPR systems have also been developed, such as the NBS-P65-CRISPR platform expressing Cas9-p65 fusion proteins that activate only under pathological inflammatory conditions, providing an additional layer of safety through conditional genome editing responsive to disease microenvironments [172]. As these technologies mature toward clinical translation, the integration of AI-assisted guide RNA design, single-cell profiling for off-target assessment, and standardized manufacturing protocols will be essential for advancing EV-mediated genome editing across multiple disease indications.

Organoid-on-chip integration and microphysiological systems

Organoid-on-chip and microphysiological systems are near-term validation tools [173, 174]. Their value lies in testing EV uptake, toxicity, vascular transport, immune interaction, and functional potency under human-relevant flow and tissue architecture. These systems can help bridge the gap between simplified cell assays and animal models, especially when AI predicts cell targeting or when biomaterials claim sustained release under physiological conditions.

Within these systems, vascularization deserves particular emphasis because EV biodistribution and clearance are governed by endothelial barriers, shear stress, immune surveillance, and organ-specific vascular beds. Vascularized organoids, vascularized organ-on-chip models, and vascularized organoid-on-chip systems allow EV formulations to be tested under perfusion, endothelial interaction, and tissue-specific uptake conditions [175–178]. They are especially relevant for validating AI-predicted targeting, stimulus-responsive release, and potency under flow.

Microfluidic platforms can also support manufacturing and quality control by enabling scalable isolation, continuous sampling, and integrated analytics [137]. Most published examples, however, remain at proof-of-concept scale, and their translational relevance will depend on throughput, compatibility with GMP materials, closed processing, cleaning validation, and demonstrated comparability with established TFF and SEC workflows. At this stage, microfluidic systems are best positioned as complementary process-analytical tools but not as replacements for the bulk-separation steps that dominate clinical-grade EV manufacturing.

Synthetic biology and bottom-up EV engineering

The emergence of synthetic biology approaches for EV engineering marks a substantive shift from cell-dependent production toward programmable, fully synthetic EV platforms with precisely controlled composition, standardized manufacturing, and enhanced therapeutic functionalities. Bottom-up assembly strategies enable the construction of biomedically relevant synthetic EVs through sequential incorporation of defined lipid compositions, membrane proteins, and therapeutic cargoes, eliminating the batch-to-batch variability inherent in cell culture-derived preparations while enabling quantitative engineering of EV properties. For instance, fully synthetic vesicles (fsEVs) decorated with tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81 and loaded with defined miRNA combinations, a design strategy adapted from synthetic nanocarrier platforms, have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy comparable to natural EVs in wound healing and neovascularization models, with transcriptome profiling revealing synergistic effects between individual EV constituents [179]. Borrowed from synthetic biology, cell-free gene expression systems have emerged as powerful enabling platforms for in vitro protein production; when applied to EV engineering, they enable the incorporation of membrane proteins and bespoke molecular cargoes without the constraints of cellular toxicity or metabolic burden [180].

Complementing fully synthetic platforms, hybrid vesicle engineering strategies combining natural EV properties with synthetic components have produced platforms that address specific limitations of each approach. EV-liposome hybrid vesicles created through freeze-thaw membrane fusion or simple incubation techniques have demonstrated enhanced payload capacity, modified cellular uptake properties, and tunable drug release kinetics [181]. Cell-derived nanovesicles (CDNs) produced through extrusion or microfluidic processing of cells, and protein-functionalized liposomes incorporating EV surface markers, represent complementary approaches to biomimetic synthetic vesicle production that are adapted from cell manipulation and liposome engineering platforms [182]. The integration of synthetic biology circuits within EV-producing cells has enabled the creation of designer exosomes with programmable therapeutic cargo expression, including the development of implantable cellular factories that continuously produce therapeutic EVs in response to physiological stimuli. Microfluidic bottom-up fabrication techniques using ethanol dilution methods, originally developed for lipid nanoparticle–based delivery systems, have demonstrated precise size control and scalability for lipid nanoparticle-based delivery systems, establishing manufacturing approaches applicable to synthetic EV production [183].

More broadly, the distinction between top-down and bottom-up EV engineering approaches has important implications for regulatory classification, quality control, and clinical translation pathways. Top-down strategies involve genetic modification of source cells to engineer natural exosomes with enhanced properties, including surface display of targeting ligands, enrichment of specific cargo molecules, and incorporation of therapeutic proteins or nucleic acids. Bottom-up approaches constructing artificial exosomes de novo, an enabling strategy adapted from synthetic biology and nanomedicine, offer advantages of complete compositional control and standardized manufacturing, but require validation of functional equivalence to natural EVs and establishment of appropriate regulatory frameworks [138]. The MISEV2023 guidelines have explicitly addressed engineered and cell culture-derived EVs within the umbrella definition, acknowledging the evolving landscape of EV engineering while emphasizing the importance of rigorous characterization regardless of production methodology [184]. As AI-assisted design enables computational optimization of synthetic EV properties, the integration of ML models for predicting biodistribution, cellular uptake, and therapeutic efficacy will accelerate the development of customized synthetic platforms addressing unmet medical needs.

Digital twins, computational modeling, and advanced biosensing

Digital twins and advanced computational models are longer-term tools for EV therapeutics [185–188]. They may eventually integrate patient-specific omics, imaging, pharmacokinetics, and EV biomarker data to simulate response, but current applications in EV therapy are largely conceptual. Near-term value is more realistic in trial design, dose-exposure modeling, and biomarker-guided stratification than in automated treatment optimization. Computational modeling can complement experimental validation by making assumptions explicit, testing sensitivity to uncertain parameters, and supporting virtual comparisons of dosing schedules [189, 190]. However, models should be calibrated against experimental biodistribution and potency data, and uncertainty should be reported so that simulations do not overstate predictability.

In parallel with computational approaches, advanced biosensing and analytical platforms are being developed for sensitive EV detection and characterization, including graphene field-effect transistors, SnS quantum-dot-based sensors, and quantum computing-assisted workflows [191–193]. These platforms are positioned as EV-adjacent enabling tools, and claims such as single-particle or ultrasensitive detection should specify the platform, limit of detection, sample preparation, matrix, and validation conditions. In the EV therapeutic context, their most immediate role is likely analytical such as release testing, impurity detection, and biomarker monitoring over direct therapy control (Fig. 8).

Conclusions and future perspectives

The next-generation EV framework is useful only if it moves the field toward clearer criteria rather than serving as another aspirational label. AI, biomaterials, synthetic biology, and advanced validation systems can address persistent bottlenecks in EV therapeutics, but their value depends on experimental validation, standardized characterization, and measurable improvement over simpler platforms.

Near-term progress is most likely where computational design, biomaterial retention, and vascularized validation models are linked to defined CQAs and potency assays. EV-mediated genome editing, bottom-up synthetic EVs, and programmable producer-cell systems are scientifically important, but they require additional evidence on safety, biodistribution, immunogenicity, and manufacturability before they can be framed as clinically ready next-generation therapeutics.

Future work should prioritize international terminology alignment, EV reference materials, orthogonal purity and integrity testing, dose-unit harmonization, validated potency assays, and process comparability. Clinical translation will also require clearer separation between diagnostic EV studies, cosmetic or aesthetic EV applications, early safety studies, and disease-modifying therapeutic trials. If these conditions are met, next-generation EVs can serve as a testable translational framework for precision nanomedicine.
