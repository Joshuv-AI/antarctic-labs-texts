On Nash Equilibria in Play-Once and Terminal Deterministic Graphical Games
Endre Boros, Vladimir Gurvich, Kazuhisa Makino

Abstract
We consider finite n n -person deterministic graphical games and study the existence of pure stationary Nash-equilibrium in such games. We assume that all infinite plays are equivalent and form a unique outcome, while each terminal position is a separate outcome. It is known that for n = 2 n=2 such a game always has a Nash equilibrium, while that may not be true for n > 2 n>2 . A game is called play-once if each player controls a unique position and terminal if any terminal outcome is better than the infinite one for each player. We prove in this paper that play-once games have Nash equilibria. We also show that terminal games have Nash equilibria if they have at most three terminals. Keywords: n n -person deterministic graphical game, play-once game, terminal game, Nash equilibrium, Nash-solvability, pure stationary strategy, digraph, directed cycle.

Introduction

We consider a family of games with perfect information and
without moves of chance, played on finite directed graphs.
Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, the sets
V
and
E

represent positions and moves of the players, respectively.
We are also given a partition

V=V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{n}\cup T

and an initial position
v_{0}\in V\setminus T
, where

V_{i}
is the set of positions controlled by
player
i\in I=\{1,\dots,n\}
and

T
is the set of terminals, that is,
the vertices of out-degree 0.

In the game each player
i\in I
chooses a move from each position
v\in V_{i}
.
Such arcs form a subgraph, called a
situation
, in which there is a unique walk starting at the initial position. That walk may end in a directed cycle, or at a terminal position. Accordingly, the outcome of the game is defined as
\infty
if the walk ended in a cycle, or the terminal position
a\in T
at which the walk ended.
Players have given preferences over the set of outcomes,
A=T\cup\{\infty\}
.
Given a situation, a player
i\in I
may be able to improve the outcome according to its own preferences by choosing different outgoing arcs from the positions
v\in V_{i}
.
A situation is called a
Nash equilibrium
(NE) if none of the players have such an improving deviation from it.

This family of games was introduced by Washburn
[
44
]

in 1990 for the case of
n=2
players and
opposite preferences (zero-sum games).
Furthermore, every non-terminal outcome
\infty
was treated as a draw.
For this reason, these games were referred to as “chess-like”
in
[
4
,
8
,
11
,
30
]
.
Several other publications consider
a natural extension of these games for the case
n\geq 2

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
7
,
10
,
14
,
30
]
.
Washburn
[
44
]
called his

deterministic graphical
(DG)
and we use the same name for the above more general family.

It is known that 2-person DG games have NE
[
7
]
.
This result is based on a more general theory of
Nash-solvability of tight game forms
[
19
,
20
]
,
while the zero-sum case was studied earlier
by Edmonds and Fulkerson
[
15
]
, see also
[
18
,
44
]

and some later works
[

,

,
27
,

,

]
.
The existence of NE (in pure strategies)
for several other classes of games was studied in

[

,

,

,

,

,

.

However, an

;
see examples in

A digraph

is called symmetric

It is also known that

In this paper we focus on two special families of DG games.
In the so called

A terminal DG game has a NE if

Furthermore, it has a terminal NE if in addition some terminal can be reached by a directed path from the initial position.

Theorem 2

A play-once DG game has a NE.

2
Some Concepts, Definitions, and Notation

Graphs modeling DG games
:
We denote by
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
the set of players.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph,
where vertices and directed edges are respectively called
positions
and
moves
.
The vertex set
V
is partitioned by

V=T\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i}
,
where
V_{i}
i s the set of positions controlled
by player
i\in I
,
while
T
is the set of terminals, i.e., positions with no moves from them.
For a player
i\in I
we denote by
E_{i}\subseteq E
the moves
controlled by player

i
, that is,
E_{i}=\{(v,w)\in E\mid v\in V_{i}\}
.
For a position
u\in V\setminus T
we denote by
i(u)\in I
the player who controls that position, that is
u\in V_{i(u)}
.
We have a designated position
v_{0}\in V\setminus T
, called the
initial position
.

Given a subset
F\subseteq E
and a vertex
v\in V

we denote by
N_{F}^{+}(v)
the out-neighborhood of
v

and by
d_{F}^{+}(v)=|N_{F}^{+}(v)|
its cardinality, so-called out-degree.
By definition, we have
d_{E}^{+}(t)=0
for all terminals
t\in T
and
d_{E}^{+}(v)>0
for all positions
v\in V\setminus T
.
For a subset
X\subseteq V
of the positions,
we denote by
G[X]=(X,E[X])
the subgraph of
G
induced by
X
.

Strategies and situations of DG games
:
A
strategy
of a player
i\in I
is a mapping
\sigma_{i}:V_{i}\to E_{i}

that assigns the move
\sigma_{i}(v)\in\{(v,w)\in E_{i}\}

for each position
v\in V_{i}
.
Note that
\sigma_{i}(v)
depends only on the position
v\in V_{i}
, and hence, the strategy is called
positional
or

stationary
in the literature.
We denote by
\Sigma_{i}
the set of strategies of player
i\in I
, and note that

|\Sigma_{i}|=\prod_{v\in V_{i}}d^{+}_{E}(v)
.

For simplicity, we also view
\sigma_{i}
as a subset of the moves of
G
:
\sigma_{i}=\{(v,\sigma_{i}(v))\mid v\in V_{i}\}\subseteq E_{i}
for
i\in I
. In other words, a strategy
\sigma_{i}\subseteq E
satisfies
d^{+}_{\sigma_{i}}(v)=1
for all
v\in V_{i}
and
0
for all
v\in V\setminus V_{i}
.
A
situation
is a collection
\sigma=(\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma_{i}\mid i\in I)
of strategies, one for each player. We can also view a situation as a subset of moves
\sigma\subseteq E
such that
d^{+}_{\sigma}(v)=1
for all positions
v\in V\setminus T
and
0
for all terminals
v\in T
.

Mechanics and outcomes of DG games
:
Each player chooses a strategy.
In the corresponding situation
\sigma
, for any position
v\in V\setminus T
,
there exists a unique walk
P(\sigma,v)
leaving
v
.
Such a walk terminates either in a terminal,
or in a cycle that is then repeated infinitely many times.
We define

g(\sigma,v)~=~\begin{cases}a&\text{ if the path }P(\sigma,v)\text{ is finite that terminates }a\in T,\\
\infty&\text{ if the path }P(\sigma,v)\text{ is infinite.}\end{cases}

We call
P(\sigma)=P(\sigma,v_{0})
the
play
of the situation and denote by
g(\sigma)=g(\sigma,v_{0})
the corresponding outcome, which we call the
outcome
of the situation.
Accordingly, in
DG games

we define the set of outcomes
as the set of terminals and one non-terminal outcome,

A=T\cup\{\infty\}
, and call the play
P(\sigma)

terminal or finite

if
g(\sigma)\in T
, and
infinite
otherwise.

Given a situation
\sigma
, for a player
i\in I
we denote by

\sigma_{-i}=\bigcup_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}\sigma_{j}

the set of moves of
\sigma
controlled by players from
I\setminus\{i\}
.
Thus, for every
i\in I
we have
\sigma=\sigma_{-i}\cup\sigma_{i}
.

Preferences and purpose of DG games
:
Each player
i
has a preference over the outcomes
that is described by a linear order
\succ_{i}
over the set
A
.
We denote by
\succ

the collection
\{\succ_{i}\,\,\,\mid i\in I\}
of preference orders, and denote by
\Gamma=\Gamma(G=(V=T\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i},E),v_{0}\in V\setminus T,\succ)
the corresponding game.

Furthermore, for a subset
X\subseteq V
such that
T\cup\{v_{0}\}\subseteq X
we denote by
\Gamma[X]=(G[X]=(X,E[X]),v_{0},\succ)
the subgame of
\Gamma
induced by the set
X
.
If ambiguity may arise, we write
\Sigma_{i}(\Gamma)
for the set of strategies of player
i\in I
in game
\Gamma
.

Nash equilibria in DG games
:
We say that a situation
\sigma
is a
Nash equilibrium
(or NE, in short), if

g(\sigma)~\succeq_{i}~g(\sigma_{-i}\cup\sigma^{\prime}_{i})

for all players
i\in I
and for all strategies
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\in\Sigma_{i}
. In other words, a situation
\sigma
is a NE if none of the players can strictly improve (according to his/her own preferences) if all the other players stick to their chosen strategies.
We say that a NE
\sigma
is
terminal
if
g(\sigma)\in T
and
non-terminal
otherwise.

Since we consider NE of DG games,
we restrict our attention to linear orders as preferences, rather than orders with ties or payoffs.
Obviously, tie-breaking might destroy NE, but cannot create one.
In contrast, merging outcomes might only create a NE but cannot destroy one.

Backward Induction
:
If the digraph
G
is acyclic then
a special NE can be obtained in a DG game

G,D,\succ)
by the well-known
Backward Induction (BI).
This procedure was introduced in 1953 by Gale and Kuhn
[
Gal53;Kuh53
]
.
More properties of the BI NE can be found in
[
21
,
22
]
.

Some simple proofs and examples

Let us first consider an easy special case.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Gamma=(G=(V,E),v_{0},\succ)
be a DG game in which the set of terminals is not reachable from the initial position. Then
\Gamma
has a non-terminal NE.

Proof.

Let us denote by
S
the set of positions that can be reached from
v_{0}
by a directed path. According to our assumption, we have
S\cap T=\emptyset
.

Let us define a situation
\sigma
by choosing a non-terminating move from each position
v\in S
, and an arbitrary move from positions
v\in V\setminus(S\cup T)
.
Note that a terminal DG game has at least one move from all positions in
V\setminus T
, by definition.

Then the play
P(\sigma)
is not leaving the set
S
, and hence must terminate in a cycle within
S
. Furthermore, all players on this play belong to
S
, and hence none of them can switch to a strategy that would leave
S
. Consequently,
\sigma
is a NE with
g(\sigma)=\infty
, as claimed.
∎

Let us show next a simple proof for the result that we recalled earlier, that is that if a DG game is both terminal and play-once, then it has a NE.
This statement follows from the results of
[
7
]
.
Here we give a simpler direct proof.

Proposition 1
.

A terminal and play-once DG game has an NE.

Proof.

Assume that
\Gamma=(G=(V,E),v_{0},\succ)
is a terminal and play-once DG game in which the a terminal position is reachable from the initial position. Due to Lemma
1
we can assume this, since otherwise
\Gamma
has a non-terminal NE.

We can also assume that all non-terminal positions of
\Gamma
have a non-terminating move, since otherwise we could fix a best terminating move for such a position and reduce the game to a smaller, equivalent one.

Let us then consider a shortest
v_{0}\to T
path
P
(that has the minimum number of arcs) such that the last position
v
on
P
before
T
chooses a best terminal move
(v,a)
for
a\in N^{+}(v)\cap T
according to his preferences. We can then define a situation
\sigma
by using the moves along
P
for positions on
P
, and choosing a non-terminating move for all other non-terminal positions.

We claim that
\sigma
is a terminal NE. Note that a player
u\neq v
on the play
P(\sigma)=P
cannot reach
T
by a single move, since then
P
would not be a shortest path. Thus, all deviations from
\sigma
by players different from
v
on the play yield
\infty
, while a deviation by
v
may yield another terminal that
v
does not prefer to
g(\sigma)
. Hence
\sigma
is a NE, as claimed.
∎

Terminal DG games

For
|T|\leq 2
this result was obtained in
[
7
]
.
Due to Lemma
1
, we can assume
for the rest of our proof that
T
is reachable from
v_{0}
in
\Gamma
.

Our remaining proof is inductive. We consider a potential “smallest” counter-example (smallest number of positions, and within that smallest number of moves) and derive a contradiction from its existence. To arrive to such a proof we need a few technical lemmas.

We say that a move
(u,a)\in E
,
a\in T
is a
\#k
-move
if terminal
a
is the
k
th best outcome of player
i(u)\in I
.
We show that the following properties hold for potential minimal counter-examples:

(NoDE)

No dead end:
T
is reachable from all positions
v\in V\setminus T
.

(NoDummy)

No dummies:
d^{+}(v)\geq 2
for all
v\in V\setminus T
.

(NofT)

No forced termination:
N^{+}(v)\not\subseteq T
holds for all
v\in V\setminus T
.

(No1M)

\Gamma
has no
\#1
-moves.

(No3M)

\Gamma
has no
\#3
-moves.

Lemma 2
.

If
\Gamma
is a minimal counter-example, then the set of terminals is reachable from all positions
v\in V\setminus T
. In particular, it satisfies condition (NoDE).

Proof.

Let us define
Q\subseteq V
as the set of positions of
\Gamma
from which
T
cannot be reached, and assume for a contradiction that
Q\neq\emptyset
. Note that we have
v_{0}\in V\setminus Q
, since we assume that the set of terminals are reachable from the initial position. Then
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma[V\setminus Q]
is a smaller game than
\Gamma
terminal DG game. Thus, it has a terminal NE
\sigma^{\prime}=(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\in\Sigma_{i}(\Gamma^{\prime})\mid i\in I)
by the minimality of
\Gamma
. Let us then extend these strategies to
\Gamma
by defining for all
v\in V_{i}
,
i\in I

\sigma_{i}(v)~=~\begin{cases}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}(v)&\text{ if }v\in V_{i}\setminus Q,\\
w&\text{ for an arbitrary }w\in N^{+}(v)\text{ if }v\in V_{i}\cap Q.\end{cases}

We claim that
\sigma
is a NE of
\Gamma
, contradicting the assumption that
\Gamma
is a counter-example to Theorem
1
.

To see this claim, let us note that
P(\sigma)=P(\sigma^{\prime})
and hence
g(\sigma)=g(\sigma^{\prime})\in T
. Furthermore, a player
i\in I
can improve on
\sigma
only if he can deviate from
P(\sigma)
to a position of
Q
, since all other positions have the same moves as in
\sigma^{\prime}
, which is an equilibrium in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Such a deviation however yields a play that is not final by the definition of
Q
, and hence cannot be a strict improvement for player
i
since all players prefer all terminals to
\infty
in a terminal DG game.
∎

Lemma 3
.

If
\Gamma
is a minimal counter-example, then it satisfies condition (NoDummy).

Proof.

By Lemma
2
we have
d^{+}(v)\geq 1
for all positions
v\in V\setminus T
. Assume for a contradiction that
d^{+}(v)=1
for some
v\in V_{i}
,
i\in I
. (Such a
v
is called a dummy position sometimes since the player controlling it has no real choice in selecting an outgoing move.) Let us denote by
(v,w)\in E
the unique move from
v
, set
F=\{(v,w)\}\cup\{(w,u)\mid u\in N^{+}(w)\}
, and define

V^{\prime}=V\setminus\{w\}\text{ and }E^{\prime}=\left(E\setminus F\right)\cup\{(v,u)\mid u\in N^{+}(w)\}.

Let us also define
v_{0}^{\prime}=v_{0}
if
v_{0}\neq w
, and set
v_{0}^{\prime}=v
if
v_{0}=w
.
Let us then consider the game
\Gamma^{\prime}=(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),v_{0}^{\prime},\prec)
.

Note that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the situations of
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Namely, to a situation
\sigma^{\prime}
of
\Gamma^{\prime}
let us associate
\sigma
defined by
\sigma(v)=w
,
\sigma(w)=\sigma^{\prime}(v)
, and
\sigma(u)=\sigma^{\prime}(u)
for all
u\not\in\{v,w\}
(and vice versa.)

Since
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a smaller terminal DG game than
\Gamma
, it has a terminal NE
\sigma^{\prime}
, because we assumed that
\Gamma
is a minimal counter example. It is easy to see that the unique associated situation
\sigma
of
\Gamma
is a terminal NE of
\Gamma
. This contradicts the assumption that
\Gamma
is a counter-example to Theorem
1
, and hence completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4
.

If
\Gamma
is a minimal counter-example, then it satisfies condition (NofT).

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that for some position
v\in V_{i}\setminus T
,
i\in I
we have
N^{+}(v)\subseteq T
. Let
a\in N^{+}(v)
be the best outcome for player
i
in the set
N^{+}(v)
, and define game
\Gamma^{\prime}
by removing moves
(v,b)
,
b\in N^{+}(v)
,
b\neq a
from
\Gamma
. Since by Lemma
3
we have
d^{+}(v)\geq 2
, game
\Gamma^{\prime}
is strictly smaller than
\Gamma
. Thus, it has a terminal NE
\sigma^{\prime}
.

We claim that
\sigma^{\prime}
is a terminal NE in
\Gamma
, too. This leads to a contradiction, proving our statement.

To see the claim notice that all moves in
\Gamma^{\prime}
are also available in
\Gamma
, and hence
\sigma^{\prime}
is a situation of
\Gamma
, too. Furthermore, the only moves that are available in
\Gamma
and not available in
\Gamma^{\prime}
are the removed
(v,b)
,
b\in N^{+}(v)
,
b\neq a
moves. Thus, only player
i
has more options in
\Gamma
than in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, and hence only he could improve on
\sigma^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
.
Furthermore, player
i
could improve on
\sigma^{\prime}
only by utilizing one of the deleted moves. In that case however he could reach
v
already in game
\Gamma^{\prime}
,
and thus, by our choice of
a\in N^{+}(v)
, he cannot strictly improve on
\sigma^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

If
\Gamma
is a minimal counter-example, then it satisfies condition (No1M).

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that there exists a move
(u,a)
,

such that

is player
i(u)
’s best outcome. Let us now consider the game
\Gamma^{\prime}
obtained from
\Gamma
by deleting all the other moves from

. Note, that by Lemma

, we had more than one move from

, thus,
\Gamma^{\prime}
is strictly smaller than
\Gamma
. Consequently,
\Gamma^{\prime}
has a terminal NE,
\sigma^{\prime}
, since we assumed that
\Gamma
is a minimal counter example. We claim that
\sigma^{\prime}
is a NE also in

, contradicting our assumption that it is a counter-example to Theorem

, and hence proving the statement.

To see our claim, note that only player

has moves in

that are not available in

(from position

is available in both games. Thus, if he can deviate from the play

to

, then he can do the same in

, too, and thus, we must have

, and hence he cannot improve on the outcome which is already his best possible one.
Or otherwise, he cannot reach

, in which case he cannot improve in
\Gamma
either.

If
\Gamma
is a minimal counter-example, then it satisfies condition (No3M).

Assume for a contradiction that there exists a move

,

, where

’s worst terminal outcome (since we have

\Gamma^{\prime}

. Then

. Thus,

, since we assumed that

To see our claim, note that

, and thus, we have

, since

Lemma 7
.

Assume that
\Gamma
satisfies conditions (NofT) and (No1M), and that
\sigma\subseteq E
is a terminal NE with
g(\sigma)=a\in T
. Let us define a new situation
\sigma^{\prime}
by defining for all

\sigma^{\prime}(u)~=~\begin{cases}v\in N^{+}(u)\setminus T&\text{ if }\sigma(u)\in T\setminus\{a\},\\
\sigma(u)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
\sigma^{\prime}
is also a terminal NE of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let us note first that
\sigma^{\prime}
is well defined, due to condition (NofT).

Assume for a contradiction that a player
i\in I
can improve on
\sigma^{\prime}
by switching to situation
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
. Then the play
P(\sigma^{\prime\prime},v_{0})
must pass through at least one of the positions

for which
\sigma^{\prime}(u)\neq\sigma(u)
since otherwise
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
would be an improvement over

, contradicting the assumption that
\sigma
is a NE. This implies that player

could deviate from
\sigma
and reach terminal
\sigma(u)\in T
. Since
\sigma
is a NE by our assumption, it follows that

\sigma(u)\not\succ_{i}a.

(1)

Since we assume that
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
is an improvement on
\sigma^{\prime}
we must have

g(\sigma^{\prime\prime},v_{0})\succ_{i}a=g(\sigma^{\prime},v_{0})=g(\sigma,v_{0}).

(2)

Since
\infty
is not better than

for any of the players, and by the above two relations, it follows that
g(\sigma^{\prime\prime},v_{0})
is player
i

terminal moves are only to

∎

Completion of the proof of Theorem
1
.

Assume for a contradiction that
\Gamma
is a minimal counter-example. Since we assume that
T
is reachable from
v_{0}
, it must have some terminal moves, and by Corollary
1
all such moves are
\#2
-moves. Let
(u,b)\in E(\Gamma)
be such a
\#2
-move. By Lemma
4
condition (NofT) also holds, and thus, the game
\Gamma^{\prime}
defined by
E(\Gamma^{\prime})=E(\Gamma)\setminus\{(u,v)\mid(u,v)\in E(\Gamma),~v\not\in T\}
is strictly smaller than
\Gamma
, and hence it must have a terminal NE
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq E(\Gamma^{\prime})
, since
\Gamma
is assumed to be a minimal counter-example.

Case 1:
g(\sigma^{\prime})=b
.

In this case by Lemma
7
we have another terminal NE
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq E(\Gamma^{\prime})
such that
g(\sigma^{\prime})=g(\sigma^{\prime\prime})=b
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
has no moves to
T\setminus\{b\}
. We claim that
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
is also a terminal NE of
\Gamma
.

To see this claim, note that only player
i(u)
could improve on
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
in
\Gamma
, and only if he could reach his #1 terminal, since
g(\sigma^{\prime\prime})=b
is already his second best outcome and he/she does not prefer
\infty
to

. Since
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
has no moves to

and since
\Gamma
satisfies property (No1M) by Lemma

, such an improvement is impossible.

Case 2:
g(\sigma^{\prime})\neq b
.

We claim that
\sigma^{\prime}
is also a terminal NE of
\Gamma
.

To see this claim, note that only player
i(u)
could improve on
\sigma^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
, and only if he could reach position
u
and utilize one of the deleted moves. In this case however, he can reach position
u
already in
\Gamma^{\prime}
and could deviate to outcome
b
. Since
\sigma^{\prime}
is a NE of
\Gamma^{\prime}
, we must have
g(\sigma^{\prime})\succ_{i(u)}b
due to strict preferences, implying that

is the best terminal outcome of player

. Since condition
\infty
is his/her worst outcome, he cannot improve on

In both cases we could show that

has a terminal NE, contradicting our assumption that it is a counter-example. This contradiction proves our statement.
∎

5
Play-once DG games

In this section we focus on terminal games for which
|V_{i}|=1
for all players
i\in I
. We call such games
play-once
. We prove that such games always have a NE, though some games may have only non-terminal NE, see Figure
2
.

Let us observe first that without loss of generality
we can assume that
N^{+}(v)\not\subseteq T
and that

|N^{+}(v)\cap T|\leq 1\text{ for all positions }v\in V.

(3)

For positions with
|N^{+}(v)\cap T|=1
we denote by
a(v)\in N^{+}(v)\cap T
the terminal to which
v
has a move.

Let us define a partition of the positions. Let us define first

S=\{v\in V\mid N^{+}(v)\cap T\neq\emptyset\text{ and }a(v)\succ_{i(v)}\infty\}

(4)

Let us next define

W=\{v\in V\mid v\text{ is reachable from }v_{0}\text{ by a directed path in }G[V\setminus S]\}

Let us next define
Q\subseteq V\setminus(S\cup W\cup T)
as the set of vertices from which a directed cycle is reachable
in
G[V\setminus(S\cup W\cup T)]
by a directed path (possible empty path).
Finally we set
R=V\setminus(S\cup W\cup T\cup Q)
. Note that the set
R
is acyclic (and could be empty).

Lemma 8
.

A play-once game
G
has a non-terminating NE if and only if
G[W]
, the graph induced by the set
W
, has a cycle.

Proof.

For the if part,
choose as a play such a cycle
C\subseteq W
together with a path from
v_{0}
to
C
.
For all the other vertices we choose non-terminating moves.
It is not difficult to see that this produces a non-terminal NE.

On the other hand, if
W
is acyclic, then any play either terminates in
W
, or goes through a vertex of
S
. In the latter case it cannot be an infinite play by the definition of
S
, since each vertex
v\in S
prefers its terminal move to the infinite play.
∎

Note that by the above definitions if
W\neq\emptyset
, then we have
v_{0}\in W
, while if
W=\emptyset
, then
v_{0}\in S
.

By the above lemma we can assume for the rest of our proof that
W
induces an acyclic graph (or it is empty).

Lemma 9
.

If
G[W]
is acyclic, then all directed cycles in
G[V\setminus Q]
contain a vertex from
S
.

Proof.

Immediate by the above definitions.
∎

Lemma 10
.

If
G[W]
is acyclic, then there exists a terminal NE.

Proof.

Let us denote by
E^{\prime}
the set of moves of the induced subgraph
G[V\setminus Q]
and by
D(S)=\{(u,v)\in E^{\prime}\mid u\in S\}

the set of arcs leaving vertices of
S
.

Let us next consider a minimal subset
F\subseteq D(S)
such that
G^{\prime}=(V\setminus Q,E^{\prime}\setminus F)
is acyclic. By Lemma
9
such a set exists.

Let us next apply backward indiction on the acyclic graph
G^{\prime}
, denote by
\sigma\subseteq E^{\prime}\setminus F
the obtained situation, by
P(\sigma)
the corresponding terminal play, and by
g(\sigma)\in T
the terminal endpoint of
P(\sigma)
.

Let us consider in
G^{\prime}
a maximal directed tree
U
rooted at
g(\sigma)
that contains
P(\sigma)
, and define
\sigma^{\prime}(v)=u
for all moves
(v,u)\in U
. For vertices
w\in V\setminus Q
that does not belong to
U
we set
\sigma^{\prime}(w)=\sigma(w)
. Finally, for vertices
w\in Q
we define
\sigma^{\prime}(w)
such that
(w,\sigma^{\prime}(w))
is a non-terminating move from
w
.

Note that
\sigma^{\prime}
is a situation in
G
and we have
P(\sigma^{\prime})=P(\sigma)
.

We claim that
\sigma^{\prime}
is a terminal NE in
G
. To see this claim let is first observe that for all positions
w\not\in Q
we have

\text{either }g(\sigma^{\prime},w)=g(P(\sigma))\text{ or }g(\sigma^{\prime},w)=g(\sigma,w).

(5)

Since only positions along the play
P(\sigma^{\prime})=P(\sigma)
could improve on
\sigma^{\prime}
, we consider a position
w
along the play
P(\sigma^{\prime})
and a potential improving move
(w,u)\in E
. We break our proof into three cases, as follows:

Case 1:

(w,u)\in E^{\prime}\setminus F
,
is an arbitrary move from
w
not along the play. Since
G^{\prime}
is acyclic, one cannot create a cycle by switching
\sigma^{\prime}(w)
to
u
. Hence, by (
5
) such switching
can earn player
w
either
g(P(\sigma))
or
g(\sigma,u)
. By the properties of backward induction we have
g(\sigma,u)\preceq_{w}g(\sigma,w)=g(P(\sigma))
since the same switch is available for
w
in
G^{\prime}
. Consequently the switch to
u
yields no improvement in both cases.

Case 2:

(w,u)\in F
. By the definition of
F
we must have
w\in S
. By the minimality of
F
there exists a path
u\to w
in
G^{\prime}
, and hence also in
\sigma^{\prime}
by the definition of
\sigma^{\prime}
. Thus, switching
\sigma^{\prime}(w)
to
u
would create a cycle. Since
w\in S
, we have
g(\sigma^{\prime})\succeq_{w}a(w)\succ_{w}\infty
, implying that such a switch is not improving for player
w
.

Case 3:

(w,u)\in E\setminus E^{\prime}
. By the definitions of the partition of the vertices of
G
, we must have
w\in S
and
u\in Q
. Note that a switch of
\sigma^{\prime}(w)
to
u
is not changing
\sigma^{\prime}
inside the set
Q
. Therefore,
since
g(\sigma^{\prime},u)=g(\sigma,u)=\infty
and we have
g(\sigma^{\prime})\succeq_{w}a(w)\succ_{w}\infty
, such a move cannot be improving for player
w
.

Since we could not improve by all possible switches of
\sigma^{\prime}
from a position along its play,
\sigma^{\prime}
is a NE.
∎

Note that Lemma
10
is not an if and only if claim, since we may have a terminal NE even when the induced subgraph
G[W]
is not acyclic.

Proof of Theorem
2
:
The statement follows by Lemmas
8
and
10
.
∎

We conjecture that condition

1

In other words, every terminal DG game has a NE and, moreover
it has a terminal NE whenever
a terminal can be reached by a directed path from the initial position.

Any NE-free DG game has at least 2 players for each of which
