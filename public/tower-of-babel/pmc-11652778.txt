Resolvin D2/GPR 18 axis ameliorates pressure overload-induced heart failure by inhibiting pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization
Authors: Zihui Zheng, Mengmeng Zhao, Yao Xu, Jishou Zhang, Shanshan Peng, Jianfang Liu, Wei Pan, Zheng Yin, Cheng Wei, Juan-Juan Qin, Jun Wan, Menglong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accumulating evidence has revealed that chronic unresolved inflammation can cause significant tissue damage and can be a key mediator of advanced heart failure (HF). Resolvin (Rv) D2, a member of specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators (SPMs), plays a protective role in various diseases by facilitating resolution. However, whether RvD2 participates in the pathogenesis of HF is still unclear. Our study demonstrated that RvD2 treatment mitigated cardiac remodeling and improved cardiac function in HF mice induced by pressure overload. The absence of G protein-coupled receptor 18 (GPR18), an endogenous receptor for RvD2, abolished the beneficial effects of RvD2 on HF. Additionally, RvD2 inhibited inflammatory responses and Ly6Chigh macrophage polarization during both early and late inflammatory stages involved in HF. Further investigation revealed that bone marrow transplantation from Gpr18 deficient mice into WT mice blocked the protective effects of RvD2 in HF mice. Moreover, Gpr18 deficiency impeded RvD2's capacity to downregulate inflammatory responses and Ly6Chigh macrophage polarization. Consistent with experiments in vivo, RvD2 treatment in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) reduced inflammatory responses through its receptor GPR18. Mechanistically, RvD2 suppressed the phosphorylation of STAT1 and NF-κB p65, and the effects of RvD2 were reversed by the application of STAT1 or NF-κB p65 agonists in BMDMs. In conclusion, RvD2/GPR18 axis improved cardiac remodeling and function in pressure overload-induced HF mice by modulating macrophage phenotype via STAT1 and NF-κB p65 pathways. Our findings underscore the anti-inflammatory potential of RvD2/GPR18 axis, suggesting that RvD2/GPR18 axis may be a potential strategy for the treatment of HF.

Heart failure (HF) is the most common cardiovascular reason for hospital admission for people older than 60 years of age (1). Despite the efficacy of many therapies for patients with HF, such as angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, β blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and advanced device therapies, the prevalence of HF is still increasing and the prognosis remains poor (2). Accumulating evidence has revealed that the immune system plays a potentially important role in the pathogenesis of HF (3). The observation in 1990 indicated that circulating levels of tumor necrosis factor are increased in cachectic patients with chronic HF (4). The CANTOS clinical trials, targeted the interleukin-1β immunity pathway with a monoclonal antibody led to improved HF outcomes in patients with myocardial infarction with or without established HF (5). Further investigation in the therapies targeting inflammatory processes may improve the treatment and prognosis of patients with HF.

Although acute inflammation is a vital response to repair tissue damage and protects from infection (6), chronic unresolved inflammation can cause significant tissue damage and can be a key mediator of advanced HF (7, 8). Specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators (SPMs) are small molecular lipids derived from polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), which exert pivotal anti-inflammatory and anti-infective roles through their receptors (6, 9). Evidence has revealed that SPMs are biosynthesized and secreted by activated monocytes/macrophages to limit excessive inflammatory responses during the pathophysiology of HF (10), while the deficiency of SPMs drives pathological and unresolved inflammation, leading to HF (11). Besides, previous studies have found that exogenous supplementation of resolvin (Rv) D1, a member of SPMs, attenuated cardiac dysfunction and remodeling in pressure overload-induced HF mice (12).

RvD2 (7S, 16R, 17S-trihydroxy-4Z, 8E, 10Z, 12E, 14E, 19Z-DHA) is also a member of SPMs, which was first identified and isolated from murine self-resolving exudates during the resolution of self-limited acute inflammation in vivo. As a potent anti-inflammatory lipid mediator, RvD2 has been reported to play a protective role in various diseases by promoting resolution, including ischemic stroke, atherosclerosis, and hypertension (13, 14, 15). However, the role of RvD2 in HF remains unclear.

G protein-coupled receptor 18 (GPR18) was identified as an endogenous receptor of RvD2 by Serhan and colleagues in 2015 (16). Previous studies have shown that GPR18 is highly expressed in various tissues and immune cells, thereby participating in different biological processed (17). As the receptor of RvD2, GPR18 mediates the role of RvD2 in the resolution phase of the inflammatory response. In our previous review, we summarized the role of RvD2/GPR18 axis in different immune cells and various cardiovascular diseases (18). In the present study, we found that RvD2/GPR18 axis inhibited inflammatory responses and pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization via STAT1 and NF-κB p65 pathways, and thus ameliorated pressure overload-induced HF.

Materials and methods

Animals and animal models

Gpr18 knockout (KO) mice and wild-type (WT) mice on the C57BL/6J background were purchased from the Institute of Model Animals, Nanjing University (imported from the Jackson Laboratory). Heterozygous Gpr18-deficient mice were crossed with each other to generate homozygous Gpr18 KO mice, and the WT littermates were used as the negative control. All animals were housed at constant room temperature with a 12:12 h light-dark cycle at the Animal Center of Wuhan University Renmin Hospital and fed standard rodent diet and water. All animals received humane care according to NIH guidelines. All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Policy and Welfare Committee of Wuhan University Renmin Hospital (WDRM20221202A).

KO and WT mice at 8 weeks of age were used in this study. After anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery was performed to induce pressure overload in male mice. TAC or sham surgery was performed as described in a previous study (19).

To explore the role of RvD2 in TAC mice, RvD2 was purchased from Cayman Chemical. RvD2 (2ug/kg) or saline (vehicle) was injected intraperitoneally once a day after the surgery until the mice were sacrificed.

To validate the efficacy of intraperitoneal injection of RvD2, we examined the serum level of RvD2 in mice using an RvD2 ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemical). The serum level of RvD2 was measured according to the instructions of the manufacturer.

Bone marrow transplantation was performed as described in our previous study (20). Briefly, four-week-old mice were lethally irradiated with 8.5Gy X-ray, then received 1 × 107 bone marrow cells from donor mice by tail vein injection. To avoid infection, the mice were fed with water containing sulfamethoxazole (200 mg/ml) and trimethoprim (40 mg/ml) for 2 weeks. WT and Gpr18 deficient mice were used as bone marrow donors to reconstitute lethally irradiated WT mice. Four weeks after transplantation, the mice were administrated with TAC surgery to induce pressure overload.

One or four weeks after surgery, the animals were euthanized with phenobarbital overdose (100mg/kg body weight), followed by collection of the heart tissues for further experiments.

Cell culture and treatment

To investigate the role of RvD2 in macrophages in vitro, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from WT and Gpr18 deficient mice as our previous study described (21). Briefly, the tibias and femurs were obtained from WT and Gpr18 deficient mice after euthanasia. Then, the bone marrow cavity was exposed and washed with RPMI 1640 medium to obtain a cell suspension. After the cell suspension was filtered, erythrocytes were lysed and washed, the target bone marrow cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium (containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin). M-CSF (20 ng/ml) was used for one week to allow BMDMs to differentiate, and the differentiated BMDMs were treated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml) to induce a proinflammatory phenotype, and the sham group was treated with PBS (22). RvD2 (100nM) was administered 0.5 h before stimulation with LPS and IFN-γ. After stimulation for 24h, the cultured cells were used for further analysis. LPS (cat#L2880) was purchased from Sigma. IFN-γ (cat#315-05-20) was obtained from Peprotech.

To investigate the role of STAT1 in RvD2-treated proinflammatory BMDMs, an agonist 2-NP (45μM, Topscience), was administrated 30 min before RvD2 treatment. To investigate the role of NF-κB p65 in RvD2 treated proinflammatory BMDMs, an agonist CU-T12-9 (50μM, Topscience), was administrated 30 min before RvD2 treatment. Both 2-NP and CU-T12-9 was dissolved in DMSO reagent (MCE).

Echocardiography

Four weeks after TAC surgery, echocardiography was conducted to evaluate cardiac structure and function. The parameters included left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd), left ventricular internal diameter at end-systole (LVIDs), ejection fraction (EF), and fractional shortening (FS), which were measured in anesthetized mice noninvasively by transthoracic echocardiography 15 min before euthanasia as described (23). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane using 1.5% isoflurane, and the data were recorded by a MyLab 30 CV ultrasound system (Esaote S.p.A.).

Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) and Masson staining

After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 days, the apical part of the hearts was embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-micron thick sections. Then, the sections were stained with WGA and Masson staining as described previously (24). Based on WGA staining, the cardiomyocyte size was evaluated. The Masson staining was performed to assess the fibrotic area. The sections were then visualized by fluorescence microscope (Olympus DX51) or light microscopy and further analyzed using Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0). Specifically, four fields of view were obtained randomly for each sample. For each field, the sizes of 12 cells were obtained, and the average value from a total of 48 cells was calculated as the final cell size. The collagen area was calculated as the percentage of collagen area to the whole tissue area. All sections were collected and analyzed by two observers unaware of the group assignment or treatment of the animals.

Immunofluorescence

The paraffin sections were generated as described above. The immunofluorescence (IF) was performed as described in our previous study (21). The primary antibodies included F4/80 (Servicebio, cat#GB113373-100) and GPR 18 (Immunoway, cat#YT2005). After being incubated with primary antibodies overnight in a humidified chamber at 4 °C, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h in the dark. The nuclei were stain with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Sections were obtained using a fluorescence microscope and further analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Western blotting

Protein was extracted with RIPA buffer from the left ventricle (LV) of mice and BMDMs. Then, western blotting was performed as previously described (25). Briefly, protein samples were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (FL00010, Millipore). PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature to block nonspecific binding sites and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The protein level of GAPDH was used as an internal standard. The next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the blots were detected and scanned by a two-color infrared imaging system (Odyssey; LICOR). supplemental Table 1 lists all the primary antibodies used. To calculate the phosphorylation level of STAT1 and NF-κB p65, the gray value of the phosphorylated proteins was divided by the grey value of the total proteins. All ratios were presented after being divided by the mean value of the control group (group 1).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted from BMDMs or LV tissue by TRIzol reagent (15,596-026, Invitrogen Corporation), followed by a cDNA synthesis kit (489703001, Roche) reverse transcription of mRNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). The LightCycler 480 system (Roche) was used for RT-qPCR analysis. The amount of target genes was determined and normalized to the amount of GAPDH. Then all the data were divided by the mean value of the control group (group 1) to calculate the relative expression level of target genes. All primers are shown in supplemental Table 2.

Flow cytometry

For LV tissue of mice, flow cytometry was performed as described in our previous study (26). In brief, left ventricular tissue was digested with 0.1% collagenase II and 2.4 U/ml dispase II for 30 min to prepare a single-cell suspension. To examine the BMDMs, cells in culture dishes were digested with 0.25% trypsin to prepare BMDMs suspensions. Then, primary antibodies were used for flow cytometry, including CD45 (BD Biosciences, cat#557659), CD11b (eBioscience, cat#25-0112-82), F4/80 (eBioscience, cat#17-4801-82) and Ly6C (BD Biosciences, cat#563011). After the antibodies were incubated for 30 min in the dark, the cells were analyzed with CytExpert (Beckman). The gating strategy: LV proinflammatory macrophages, CD45+CD11b+F4/80+Ly6Chigh; proinflammatory BMDMs, Ly6Chigh.

Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were randomized and blinded. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software) and reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Shapiro-Wilk test was used to detect the normality of data. When the data conformed to a normal distribution, an unpaired Student's t test was used to assess the differences between the two groups, while one-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni multiple comparisons test was used to analyze the differences among 3 or more groups. When the data conformed to a non-normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied in data with 2 groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA with Dunn's ranked sum multiple comparisons test was applied in data with 3 or more groups. Significance was assumed to be P < 0.05.

Cardiac hypertrophy was induced by TAC surgery as shown by WGA staining (Fig. 1G). The size of cardiomyocytes and the ratio of HW/BW and HW/TL increased significantly in TAC mice (Fig. 1H–J). The RvD2 treatment reversed the increase of heart and cardiomyocytes induced by pressure overload (Fig. 1G–J). In addition, RvD2 reversed the TAC-induced mRNA expression level of genes related to cardiac hypertrophy, RvD2 treatment significantly decreased the expression of natriuretic peptide a (NPPA), natriuretic peptide b (NPPB) and myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7), but increased the level of myosin heavy chain 6 (MYH6) mRNA (Fig. 1K–N).

To validate the cardiac fibrosis induced by pressure overload, Masson staining was conducted. Cardiac fibrosis was found in the heart tissue of TAC mice, which was attenuated by the RvD2 treatment (Fig. 1O–P). Besides, the mRNA expression of collagen 1 (COL1), collagen 1 (COL3), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2), and matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9) increased significantly after TAC surgery. RvD2 reversed this increase (Fig. 1Q).

Taken together, all the data above indicated that RvD2 ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and remodeling in TAC mice.

The level of RvD2 was significantly increased in the serum of mice injected with RvD2, but there was no significant difference between WT mice and Gpr18 deficient mice (Fig. 2B). Our result showed that RvD2 treatment improved the cardiac function in TAC mice, while Gpr18 deficiency blocked the role of RvD2, as shown by LVIDd, LVIDs, EF and FS (Fig. 2C–F). WGA staining revealed that RvD2 reversed the cardiac hypertrophy induced by TAC surgery, and its protective effects were blocked by Gpr18 deficiency (Fig. 2G–H). Consistently, Gpr18 deficiency blocked the impacts of RvD2 on HW/BW and HW/TL, and restored the mRNA level of NPPA, NPPB, MYH7, and MYH6 in TAC mice (Fig. 2I–N). Masson staining showed that RvD2 reversed cardiac fibrosis in TAC mice and Gpr18 deficiency blocked its effects, which was consistent with the changes of COL1, COL3, TGF-β, CTGF, MMP2, and MMP9 mRNA levels (Fig. 2O–Q). RvD2 ameliorated inflammatory responses and inhibited proinflammatory macrophage polarization in TAC mice

As a specialized pro-resolving mediator, RvD2 has been shown to enhance the phagocytic ability of macrophages and reduce inflammatory responses (18). Previous studies have also shown that GPR18 is abundantly expressed in various types of immune cells, especially macrophages (18). Here, we further confirmed the GPR18 expression in macrophages by immunofluorescence staining (supplemental Fig. 2). We first assessed inflammatory responses and proinflammatory macrophage polarization in cardiac tissue at 1 week after TAC surgery, when the inflammatory responses were most pronounced (27). Compared to vehicle treatment, the TAC mice showed increased transcription of inflammatory genes, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which was reversed by RvD2 treatment (Fig. 3A). Besides, TAC surgery promoted the mRNA expression of CD80, CD86 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (proinflammatory macrophage biomarkers), which were significantly reversed by RvD2 treatment (Fig. 3B). Consistently, flow cytometry results suggested that TAC surgery induced proinflammatory macrophage polarization as shown by the increased infiltration of F4/80+Ly6Chigh macrophages, which was reversed by RvD2 treatment (Fig. 3C). Considering that there are early and late inflammatory processes involved in the development of HF, we further detected the inflammatory responses and proinflammatory macrophage polarization in cardiac tissue 4 weeks after TAC surgery. And we found that RvD2 treatment down-regulated the transcription of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CD80, CD86 and iNOS. Meanwhile, RvD2 treatment also decreased the infiltration of F4/80+Ly6Chigh macrophages (Fig. 3D–F). Bone marrow transplantation from Gpr18-deficient mice into WT mice blocked the protective effect of RvD2 on cardiac dysfunction and remodeling in TAC mice

In the WT mice transplanted with WT bone marrow, RvD2 treatment improved the cardiac dysfunction and remodeling, which was consistent with our previous findings (Fig. 4B–P). However, bone marrow transplantation from Gpr18 deficient mice into WT mice blocked the protective effect of RvD2 on cardiac dysfunction, as shown by increased LVIDd and LVIDs and reduced LVEF and LVFS (Fig. 4B–E). The results of WGA staining, HW/BW, HW/TL, and the expression of NPPA, NPPB, MYH7, and MYH6 revealed that the protective effect of RvD2 against hypertrophy was abolished in mice transplanted with KO bone marrow (Fig. 4F–M). Besides, the results of Masson staining, and the expression of COL1, COL3, TGF-β, CTGF, MMP2, and MMP9 revealed that the protective effect of RvD2 against fibrosis was also abolished in mice transplanted with KO bone marrow (Fig. 4N–P). All these results revealed that bone marrow transplantation from Gpr18 deficient mice into WT mice blocked the protective effect of RvD2 on cardiac dysfunction and remodeling in TAC mice. Discussion

In our study, we first revealed that RvD2 ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and remodeling induced by pressure overload. The absence of GPR18, the endogenous receptor of RvD2, abolished the protective role of RvD2 on cardiac function and construction. Meanwhile, RvD2 inhibited inflammatory responses and Ly6Chigh macrophage polarization in the early and late inflammatory processes involved in HF. Further study confirmed that bone marrow transplantation from Gpr18 deficient mice into WT mice blocked the protective effect of RvD2 on cardiac dysfunction and remodeling in TAC mice. Besides, Gpr18 deficiency blocked the down-regulatory effects of RvD2 in inflammatory responses and Ly6Chigh macrophage polarization. Consistent with experiments in vivo, RvD2 treatment ameliorated inflammatory response and inhibited the proinflammatory macrophage polarization in BMDMs, which was abolished by Gpr18 deficiency. Mechanistically, RvD2 treatment suppressed the phosphorylation of STAT1 and NF-κB p65 both in vivo and in vitro, and the application of agonists for STAT1 or NF-κB p65 abolished the protective effect of RvD2 in BMDMs. Taken together, these results suggest that RvD2/GPR18 axis ameliorates pressure overload-induced HF by inhibiting inflammatory responses and pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization.

Persistent and uncontrolled inflammation is appreciated to play a pivotal role in cardiovascular disease (28). Resolution of inflammation is not merely a passive return to homeostasis, but rather an active process mediated by specific molecules, including PUFAs-derived SPMs (29). The resolvins (Rv), protectins (PD), and maresins (MaR) are three potent mediator families of SPMs (9). Previous research has revealed that RvD1, E1, and MaR1 treatment can effectively alleviate sepsis-induced myocardial injury (26, 30, 31). RvD1 and RvE1 treatment ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and myocardial injury in doxorubicin-induced cardiac injury (25, 32). RvD1 treatment improved ventricular function by activating the inflammation resolving in mice accepted myocardial infarction (MI) surgery (33). Early treatment with RvE1 facilitated myocardial recovery from ischemia in MI mice (34). Besides, RvD1 attenuated pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis (12). In this study, we revealed that RvD2 treatment ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and remodeling induced by pressure overload, which was blocked by Gpr18 deficiency. However, there was no significant difference between WT + TAC and KO + TAC mice in cardiac dysfunction and remodeling. Considering that serum levels of RvD2 were not significantly elevated in TAC mice and there was no significant difference in serum RvD2 levels between WT + TAC mice and KO + TAC mice, we hypothesized that the role of GPR18 in TAC mice is dependent on the level of RvD2, and physiological doses of RvD2 may not be sufficient to affect the cardiac phenotype of TAC mice, which need further exploration. Overall, our study suggested that RvD2/GPR18 axis exerted an important role in pressure overload-induced cardiac dysfunction and remodeling, RvD2 might be a promising preventive option for reducing the burden of HF.

Inflammation is an important mechanism in the pathophysiology of HF. Macrophages, which are the predominant innate immune cells, play a key role in cardiac remodeling, dysregulation of macrophage polarization between the proinflammatory (Ly6Chigh) and anti-inflammatory (Ly6Clow) phenotypes promotes excessive inflammation and cardiac injury (35). It has been reported that SPMs regulate endogenous resolution programs by limiting neutrophil infiltration and stimulating macrophage-mediated clearance of apoptotic cells, cellular debris, and microbes, as well as cytokine production (36). As a specialized pro-resolving mediator, RvD2 has been shown to regulate macrophage polarization and phagocytosis through multiple mechanisms, thereby suppressing inflammation and promoting resolution (18). Previous studies have also shown that GPR18 is abundantly expressed in various types of immune cells, especially macrophages (18). In this study, we further validated the expression of GPR18 in cardiac macrophages. RvD2 treatment ameliorated inflammatory responses and inhibited proinflammatory macrophage polarization both in vivo (induced by pressure overload) and in vitro (induced by LPS + IFN-γ treatment). Similarly, the role of RvD2 was blocked by Gpr18 deficiency in vivo and in vitro. Besides, bone marrow transplantation from Gpr18 deficient mice into WT mice also blocked the protective effect of RvD2 on cardiac dysfunction, remodeling, inflammatory response, and proinflammatory macrophage polarization in TAC mice. Therefore, the protective effects of the RvD2/GPR18 axis in TAC mice may depend on the inflammatory response mediated by macrophages.

Previous studies have confirmed that STAT1 and NF-κB p65 pathways play critical roles on macrophage polarization (37, 38). Briefly, after being stimulated by IFN-γ or other activators, the macrophages can be primed rapidly via the STAT1 pathway, resulting in the transformation of macrophages to a proinflammatory phenotype (38). Besides, the NF-κB signaling promotes the production of proinflammatory mediators and promotes the proinflammatory macrophage polarization (38). Therefore, to determine the molecular mechanisms by which the RvD2/GPR18 axis regulated macrophage polarization, we detected the phosphorylation levels of STAT1 and NF-κB p65 in RvD2-treated TAC mice. We found that the STAT1 and NF-κB p65 signaling pathways were upregulated in pressure overload mice and RvD2 treatment inhibited the activation of both STAT1 and NF-κB p65 signaling pathways, which was further confirmed in BMDMs in vitro. Meanwhile, the application of agonists for STAT1 or NF-κB p65 abolished the protective effect of RvD2 in BMDMs. Our results revealed that RvD2/GPR18 axis inhibited inflammatory response and the proinflammatory macrophage polarization via STAT1 and NF-κB p65 signaling pathways, thereby ameliorating cardiac dysfunction and remodeling in TAC mice.

There were some limitations in this study. First, we identified the STAT1 and NF-κB p65 pathways as potential mediators of RvD2/GPR18 axis in TAC mice, but we did not investigate the mechanism by which RvD2/GPR18 axis regulated the STAT1 and NF-κB p65 pathways. Secondly, Gpr18 gene systemic knockout mice were used in this study. Macrophage-specific Gpr18 deficient mice may help us further understand the role and mechanism of GPR18.

Conclusion

In summary, we explored the role of RvD2/GPR18 axis in HF mice induced by pressure overload and found that RvD2/GPR18 axis inhibited inflammatory response and proinflammatory macrophage polarization both in vivo and in vitro via STAT1 and NF-κB p65 pathways, thereby ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and remodeling. Our findings suggest that the RvD2/GPR18 axis may be a potential strategy for reducing the burden of HF.
