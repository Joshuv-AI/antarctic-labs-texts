Correspondence- Educational and Socioeconomic Correlates of Stroke Risk Behaviors: Findings from the SPRINT INDIA Trial
Authors: TK Abins
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article titled “Educational and Socioeconomic Correlates of Stroke Risk Behaviors: Findings from the SPRINT INDIA Trial.”[1] In the trial, Verma et al. examined the educational differences in behavioral and clinical outcomes following stroke. The authors acknowledge several study limitations, including literacy barriers and incomplete tracking of intervention engagement. We commend their efforts for exploring the social determinants of health through a large multicenter cohort. Furthermore, we highlight additional methodological and statistical considerations that may influence the interpretation of the study findings.

First, the analysis was described as a post hoc exploration of educational strata within the SPRINT INDIA trial. While the original trial was designed to evaluate the efficacy of a structured prevention package, it did not report specific power calculations for detecting differences across educational subgroups. The authors employed chi-square tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine multiple baseline, behavioral, and laboratory variables. However, no adjustments for multiple comparisons were reported, which may increase the risk of type I error and warrants cautious interpretation of the findings.

Marked differences were observed in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics across education strata, as shown in the results tables. However, it remains unclear whether these differences were adequately accounted for in the outcome analyses. Although subgroup analyses were performed, the study did not report the use of multivariable regression models adjusting for potential confounders such as age, sex, baseline stroke severity, or socioeconomic indicators. Given the observed baseline imbalances, the possibility of residual confounding remains and may partly influence the associations reported.[2]

The study excluded 384 patients who were otherwise eligible, except for the lack of smartphone use, and a further 759 who declined participation. These may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly among lower-income, rural, or digitally disadvantaged populations. Given that randomization was conditioned on smartphone access, selection bias may occur. Moreover, only 71.1% of randomized participants completed the 1-year follow-up, leading to potential attrition bias. The article did not state whether loss to follow-up differed by educational category, stroke severity, or treatment arm. Such differential attrition may distort the observed associations, warranting its clarification. Conducting sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation or inverse probability weighting would enhance the robustness of the findings.

Educational level was initially recorded across multiple categories but later collapsed into three groups for analysis. While this approach simplifies interpretation, it may reduce granularity and potentially obscure nonlinear or graded associations across educational levels. The article does not clearly state whether this categorization was prespecified, nor does it report sensitivity analyses using the original categories or ordinal modelling approaches. This may limit a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between education and outcomes.

The primary outcome was a composite of recurrent stroke, high-risk transient ischemic attack, acute coronary syndrome, and death. While these components were also reported individually, they represent clinically heterogeneous events with differing frequencies and mechanisms. Given the low overall event rates (2.8–3.6% across groups), the use of a composite outcome may still obscure potential differences in individual components and should be interpreted with caution.

Marked variability was observed in several continuous variables, with standard deviations comparable to or exceeding their means. For example, physical activity scores ranged from 3,589 ± 3,796 to 5,498 ± 4,118 minutes across education groups. Similarly, occupational activity measures showed wide dispersion, with light-effort work reported as 960 ± 1,688 to 1,839 ± 2,545 minutes and moderate-effort work as 1,459 ± 3,139 to 2,286 ± 4,499 minutes. These patterns suggest possible skewness and the presence of outliers. As the study does not report assessment of distributional assumptions, the use of ANOVA may warrant caution. Reporting normality diagnostics or considering nonparametric approaches or data transformations could further strengthen the robustness of the analyses.[34]

The SPRINT INDIA trial enrolled participants from 31 centers. However, the analyses do not report accounting for clustering by study site. In multicenter studies, failure to account for intraclass correlation may lead to underestimation of standard errors and potentially inflated statistical significance. The use of multilevel models incorporating center as a random effect or robust variance estimation could provide a more appropriate analytical approach.[56] Although the methods section indicates that confidence intervals were calculated, they are not prominently reported in the results, which primarily emphasize P values. Greater inclusion of effect sizes with corresponding confidence intervals would enhance the interpretability of the findings by conveying both the magnitude and precision of the observed associations. Important secondary outcomes, particularly behavioral measures, appear to rely on self- or caregiver-reported data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Such biases could potentially vary across educational levels and may influence the observed associations.In addition, it is unclear whether this post hoc education-stratified analysis was supported by formal sample size or power calculations. Clarification in this regard would help determine whether the largely null findings reflect a true absence of association or limited statistical power.

The “no schooling” category included only 191 participants compared with >1,600 in the intermediate education group. This marked imbalance may reduce precision and yield unstable effect estimates, particularly in subgroup analyses. Separate analyses were conducted for the intervention and control arms and across numerous behavioral and laboratory outcomes. These separate analyses and the lack of clearly specified multiplicity adjustments increase the number of statistical comparisons and the likelihood of chance findings.

In the ‘Discussion’ section, the authors suggest that educational level may influence outcomes through factors such as medication adherence and lifestyle behaviors; however, in the absence of formal mediation analyses, these proposed pathways remain speculative and should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, as income and wealth were not directly measured and education was used as a proxy for socioeconomic status, this approach may oversimplify the multidimensional nature of social disadvantage and introduce residual confounding. Although overall engagement with the SMS and video intervention was reportedly low, outcomes were not analyzed according to the actual exposure or “dose” of the intervention, thereby limiting interpretation of its effectiveness across educational strata. In addition, while occupational effort was categorized as “light” or “moderate” and reported in hours, the methods used to quantify or validate these measures were not clearly described, raising concerns about their reliability. Despite the absence of significant differences in primary vascular outcomes across education groups, the authors advocate education-tailored strategies for secondary stroke prevention, a recommendation that may extend beyond the data presented and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, the analyses primarily rely on between-group comparisons at follow-up rather than modeling within-individual changes over time; the use of repeated-measures or mixed-effects models could have provided a more robust assessment of longitudinal changes and strengthened the study’s inferences.

Irrespective of these limitations, the authors are to be commended for addressing an important and underexplored dimension of secondary stroke prevention and leveraging a large multicenter cohort to examine the social determinants of health in the Indian context.
