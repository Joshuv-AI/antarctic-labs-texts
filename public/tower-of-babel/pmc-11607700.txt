Characteristics of the Spatiotemporal Distribution of Influenza Incidence and Its Driving Factors Among Residents in Mainland China From 2004 to 2018
Authors: Y R Yao, K Ma, Y S Li, H R Tan, J Y Zhang, Z M Zhang, Y S Lin, F M Fang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza is an acute respiratory infection that spreads rapidly and widely in densely populated areas with low vaccination coverage. The trends and drivers of the spatial and temporal dynamics of influenza incidence among residents of mainland China have not been systematically studied. This study comprehensively analyses the dynamics and spatial correlation of influenza using long‐term scale data and spatial panel data. It then identifies the interactive process of socio‐economic and natural elements on the incidence of influenza. The highest prevalence of influenza was found in the 0–4 years age group in mainland China (mean prevalence, 67.56/100,000). In addition, influenza in mainland China shows seasonality, with fall and winter being the periods of high incidence. Between 2014 and 2017, influenza incidence was clustered in Hubei and Anhui provinces, and the spatial clustering was statistically significant (Z value > 1.96, P < 0.05). Moreover, the directionality of influenza onset continued to increase each year. Specifically, the clustering of influenza onset was stronger in the northwest‐southeast direction than in the southwest‐northeast direction between 2014 and 2018. The significant role of socioeconomic factors as a primary influence on influenza incidence, while their interaction with natural factors, such as air quality (NOx and PM2.5) and climatic conditions can exacerbate regional outbreaks. This study provides a novel perspective for better prevention and control of influenza disease among mainland Chinese residents.Keywords: influenza, spatiotemporal characteristics, driving factors, mainland China

Introduction

Influenza or flu is a contagious viral infection affecting the respiratory system. It is caused by influenza viruses, with influenza A and influenza B being the primary infection‐causing strains (Mir et al., 2021). Influenza primarily spreads from person to person through respiratory droplets released when an infected individual coughs, sneezes, or talks (Glass & Glass, 2008). Common symptoms of influenza include high fever, chills, cough, sore throat, a runny or stuffy nose, muscle aches, fatigue, and headache (Duan et al., 2011). Influenza can lead to many complications, such as pneumonia, bronchitis, sinus infections, and otitis media, especially in high‐risk groups (Kuiken & Taubenberger, 2008). Hospitalization and even death may occur, particularly among vulnerable populations (Harpur et al., 2021). Each year, thousands of individuals succumb to complications related to influenza. Influenza‐attributable deaths were 2.1 times (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.5–3.4) higher than influenza‐certified deaths during the non‐pandemic period and 1.1 (95% CI, 1.0–1.8) times higher during the pandemic in the United States (Wong et al., 2015). However, the pattern of spatial and temporal changes in influenza incidence and the factors influencing it in population aggregation areas at geographic spatial and temporal scales are still ambiguous. Therefore, in‐depth research on influenza has important practical significance in healthcare. Systematic research on the spatial and temporal evolution of influenza is needed to help formulate prevention and control measures.

Traditional research on the incidence and transmission characteristics of infectious diseases is based mainly on case surveillance data. Researchers use surveillance data for influenza strain analysis, seasonal trend analysis, age and population analysis, epidemiological investigation, and mathematical modeling (Kondratiuk et al., 2022; Pierce et al., 2021; Simonsen et al., 2013). The traditional direction of econometric analysis provides a clear picture of point trends in influenza incidence; however, the study of influenza attribution and transmission behavior is one‐sided on a spatial scale. Moreover, most methods, such as multiple linear regression models and negative binomial regression, often overlook factors related to the temporal and spatial dimensions. Neglecting heterogeneity in time and space can result in missing crucial information and consequently lead to different conclusions (Duan et al., 2016; Rao et al., 2021). Spatial panel data provide an opportunity to study influenza incidence and prevalence more comprehensively and accurately, and to better understand the transmission patterns, spatial and temporal changes, and factors influencing influenza incidence. Spatial panel data can not only help identify seasonal trends, geographic hotspots, and patterns of influenza outbreaks, but also more accurately identify heterogeneity between different regions, including factors such as population density, climate differences, and health care facilities. To better understand high rates of regional influenza outbreaks.

The onset of flu is influenced by many factors, including natural and socioeconomic factors (Guo, Wang, Pei, Yu, et al., 2021). Together, these factors affect the spread of influenza and the severity of the epidemic. The spread and prevalence of influenza is affected by natural factors, such as the variability of influenza viruses (Li, Niu, et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023), climatic and seasonal factors (Zhang et al., 2022), animal hosts (Abid et al., 2016), and the immune status of individuals (Hinojosa et al., 2021). Cold and dry climates facilitate the spread of influenza viruses (Poirier et al., 2020). Influenza outbreaks typically occur in the fall and winter when people spend more time indoors, increasing the chances of transmission. Influenza viruses are commonly found in wild birds and can be transmitted to humans through animal hosts (Vittecoq et al., 2012). The animal–human contact can lead to the emergence of new influenza strains. Immunity plays a key role in the risk of influenza infection, and the immunity of a population may be affected by age, vaccination history, and vaccinations (Hinojosa et al., 2021). Socioeconomic factors, such as economic conditions, air pollution levels, population density and mobility, health and sanitation facilities, social behavior, and medical resources directly or indirectly affect the distribution characteristics and transmission risks of influenza (Liu et al., 2015; Meng et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2021). Poor economic conditions are typically accompanied by inadequate sanitation infrastructure and a lack of medical resources, which lead to diminished individual protective capacities, thereby increasing the potential for disease transmission. In addition, environmental factors such as air pollution, high population density, and frequent population mobility not only elevate the risk of infection but also broaden the geographical spread of influenza viruses. Furthermore, social behaviors, including personal hygiene practices and adherence to preventive measures, can significantly influence the epidemiological patterns of influenza. Consequently, risk assessments of influenza transmission and the development of control measures must account for a multidimensional analysis of socioeconomic factors to facilitate more precise epidemiological interventions and the formulation of public health policies (Armelagos et al., 2005; Luh et al., 2016). Therefore, understanding these factors can help develop more effective influenza prevention and control strategies to reduce the impact of influenza epidemics.

Factors, such as large population, low influenza vaccination rates, and complex environments, render mainland China an important site for the study of influenza transmission behaviors and drivers (Feng et al., 2021; Lei et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). Researchers have focused on influenza transmission behavior and dispersal patterns in mainland China in terms of spatial distribution characteristics, meteorological and environmental factors (Bedford et al., 2010; Li et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2016). Studies have shown that the incidence of influenza is higher in eastern and southern China than in other regions. The incidence of influenza shows a clear seasonal variation, with the highest incidence in winter, especially at high latitudes (Lei et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). In addition, there is a correlation of meteorological (temperature and humidity) and environmental factors (concentration of air pollutants) with influenza transmission. Overall, there was a positive correlation between influenza incidence and airborne concentrations of particulate matter (PM2.5), PM10, sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitric oxide (NO2), and carbon monoxide (CO), and a negative correlation with ozone (O3) (Yang et al., 2023). Research on disease hotspot distribution areas and transmission behavior focuses more on natural factors and conditions compared to socioeconomic factors. We argue that the level of economic development and urbanization within the region has a more significant effect on the spatio‐temporal aggregation of influenza than natural factors. The presence of geographic diversity in the genetic subgroups of influenza viruses in mainland China suggests that local transmission and widespread virus migration contribute to this phenomenon. Both small‐ and large‐scale population migration within China have contributed to the variation in the viral genetic make‐up (Kim et al., 2023). However, the relationship between different environmental factors and influenza transmission involves multiple variables (natural and socioeconomic), and more in‐depth studies are needed to address the complexity. In addition, the use of panel data is beneficial to gain insights into the behavior and mechanisms by which human activities influence flu transmission at both temporal and spatial scales.

Therefore, in this study, we chose to analyze the incidence of influenza among residents of mainland China. The dynamic changes and spatial distribution behavior of influenza were investigated from the time and space scales, respectively. The influence of natural environmental factors and socioeconomic factors on influenza incidence was eventually revealed. The objectives of the study were (a) to analyze the temporal dynamics of influenza incidence based on flu cases among residents of mainland China; (b) to explore the spatial distribution, clustering, and dynamic migration of influenza incidence through the spatial incidence data; and (c) to reveal the main controlling factors and mechanisms driving influenza incidence through natural (meteorological data and air pollution data) and socioeconomic elements (economic data and energy consumption data).

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Processing

Date on influenza cases in mainland China from 2004 to 2018 were downloaded from the Data Center of the China Public Health Science (http://www.phsciencedata.cn/Share/). According to the Law on Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases, all cases of influenza must be reported to China's National Statutory Infectious Disease Surveillance System (NSSIDS) within 24 hr of confirmed diagnosis in a hospital. The National Health and Wellness Commission of the People's Republic of China has established clinical diagnostic criteria for influenza in China (https://www.gov.cn/fuwu/2018‐11/27/content_5343680.htm (accessed on 13 December 2022)). By the end of 2018, the surveillance population comprised about 1,389,097,960 individuals, representing all regions within the mainland China. Data types, scales, times and sources for atmospheric pollutants, meteorological elements, socio‐economic elements, and demographic elements are presented in Table S1 in Supporting Information S1.

Statistical Methods

Time Series Analysis

Time series seasonal decomposition analysis was employed to examine the seasonal characteristics of influenza incidence in mainland China. Additionally, the seasonal index was calculated to assess seasonal patterns. The seasonal index is computed as the ratio of the average number of cases in a given month to the average monthly incidence rate over 15 × 12 months (2004–2018). An index value close to 1.0 indicates the absence of a seasonal trend (Rao et al., 2021).

Spatial Autocorrelation Analysis

The spatial correlation strength of influenza in China was assessed using spatial autocorrelation analysis with ArcGIS 10.2.2. The Global Moran's I index was utilized to measure the overall spatial autocorrelation among the 31 administrative regions. Generally, the Moran's I index ranges from −1.0 to +1.0. A value close to +1.0 suggests that similar incidences cluster together (referred to as clustering), whereas a value close to −1.0 indicates that dissimilar incidences cluster together (referred to as dispersion) (Rao et al., 2021).

A Moran's I index close to zero indicates no spatial autocorrelation, meaning that incidences are randomly distributed across the study area. To determine the hotspot incidences of influenza at the local level, the Getis‐Ord G i * statistic was employed. This statistic is a local spatial autocorrelation analysis method used to estimate the degree of clustering of influenza in China. If Z(G i *) > 1.96, it indicates a hot spot (a region with a high‐value spatial cluster), whereas Z(G i *) < −1.96 indicates a cold spot (a region with a low‐value spatial cluster) (Aral & Bakir, 2023).

Standard Deviation Ellipse Analysis

The spatial distribution of influenza incidence and the migration characteristics of incidence centers (2014–2018) can be described quantitatively using the standard deviation ellipse (SDE) function. The SDE function utilizes parameters, such as the center, long, and short axes to characterize geographic elements. The center of gravity represents the mean location of the incidence centers, and variations in its position indicate shifts in the overall distribution of influenza cases. The SDE long and short axes provide information about the elongation and dispersion of the incidence centers, which can help identify patterns of movement and clustering (Guo, Wang, Pei, Su, et al., 2021). The specific formulas and parameters of the wavelet analysis are shown in Text S1 in Supporting Information S1.

Optimal Parameter Geographic Detector Analysis

A geographical detector (GD) is a set of statistical methods used to detect spatial variations and reveal their underlying driving forces. These methods include factor detection, risk detection, interaction detection, and ecological detection (Li, Li, et al., 2022). However, traditional GDs require manual discretization of continuous variables, which can introduce subjectivity and suboptimal discretization.

The optimal parameter‐based geographical detector (OPGD) is an improvement of the traditional GD. It involves the use of various discretization methods and parameter combinations, such as equal‐interval, natural‐breaks, quantile‐based, geometric, standard deviation‐based, and manual methods, to achieve the optimal spatial discretization of continuous variables (Song et al., 2020). In this study, OPGD was utilized to analyze the driving forces behind the prevalence of influenza among the population of mainland China.

First, we calculated the q‐values for each continuous factor under different classification methods and different numbers of breakpoints. Q‐values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating stronger spatial variations in influenza prevalence and greater explanatory power of the factors. Classification methods were determined based on statistical rules, and the number of breakpoints was set between 3 and 10 categories.

The OPGD model selected the parameter combination (classification method and number of breakpoints) with the highest q‐value for spatial discretization. This helps identify spatial heterogeneity in influenza prevalence and assess the explanatory power of factors in explaining spatial variations. Subsequently, risk detection, interaction detection, and ecological detection are employed to analyze the impact of each factor on influenza prevalence.

Piecewise Structural Equation Model Analysis

The Piecewise structure equation model (PiecewiseSEM) can be used to establish causal relationships among multiple variables and effectively partition the direct and indirect effects between variables (Tian et al., 2021). Therefore, it has been used to estimate the direct and indirect impacts of energy consumption, economic development, meteorological conditions, and air pollution on influenza incidence in China. This study hypothesizes that four categories of environmental factors influence influenza incidence through the following pathways: (a) economic development, meteorological conditions, and air pollution directly affect influenza incidence; (b) energy consumption indirectly affects influenza incidence by influencing economic development, meteorological conditions, and air pollution; (c) economic development indirectly impacts influenza incidence by affecting meteorological conditions and air pollution; and (d) meteorological conditions indirectly affect influenza incidence by influencing air pollution. The validity of the model results was assessed using Fisher's C‐test. The model fit is gradually improved by modifying the influencing pathways based on model fit goodness (0 ≤ Fisher's C/df ≤ 2, 0.05 ≤ P ≤ 1.00) (Tian et al., 2021).

Results

The number of influenza cases in mainland China showed an increasing trend from 2004 to 2018 (Figure 1). Children (<14 years old) were the main population affected by influenza. Before 2014, the number of influenza cases in mainland China was low (blue area in Figure 1). However, after 2014, the number of influenza cases began to increase each year up to 765,186 cases by 2018. The profile showed that there were a greater number of cases of influenza in the 1‐year age group, 3‐year age group, 10‐year age group, and 25–30‐year age group than in other age groups in both 2018 and 2014. For the 1‐, 3‐, and 10‐year age groups, the number of cases showed a climbing trend with time (Figure 2).

A total of 27,517,200 cases of influenza were reported in 31 provinces and regions of mainland China from 2014 to 2018, with an average incidence rate of 13.69/100,000. The incidence rate varied significantly among age groups (P < 0.05), with the highest incidence rate in the 0–4 years age group (average: 67.56/100,000). The prevalence of influenza among children in the 0–4 age group was more than 3.93 times the average prevalence among all residents. At a specific time, there was an increasing trend in the incidence of the disease in all groups (annual percentage change [APC] > 0). In addition, of all groups, the APC values for the 0–4 years age group and the 65+ years age group showed the most dramatic changes (33.20% and 33.00%, respectively), indicating a trend toward higher prevalence over time in the two age groups mentioned above. These results indicated the need and urgency of influenza vaccination and control for children 0–4 years of age and people aged less than 65 years.

Temporal Dynamic Characteristics of Influenza

Influenza incidence showed minor fluctuations from 2004 to 2014, followed by a more noticeable upward trend in 2014 and a general increase in overall incidence (Figures 1a and 1c). The seasonal decomposition of the influenza time series revealed a distinct seasonal pattern (χ 2 trend = 981.53, P < 0.001), with a consistent peak from November of the previous year through April of the following year, reaching a maximum seasonal index of 1.65 in March. After May, influenza cases gradually declined (Figures 1b and 1e).

From 2004 to 2018, influenza incidence rates exhibited a fluctuating upward trend across various geographic regions in mainland China (Figures S1–S7 in Supporting Information S1). The seasonality of influenza in Northeast, North, and East China was consistent, with peak incidence occurring from November of the previous year to March of the following year (Figures S1–S3 in Supporting Information S1). In Central China, the peak extended from November to April, while in South China, it occurred from March to July (Figures S4 and S5 in Supporting Information S1). In contrast, Northwest and Southwest China experienced two distinct peak periods. Northwest China saw peaks from November to January and from March to April, whereas Southwest China had peaks from September to January and from March to April (Figures S6 and S7 in Supporting Information S1).

Spatial Distribution Characteristics of Influenza

The spatial distribution of influenza incidence varied over time between January 2004 and December 2018 (Figure 3). Before 2007, the areas with high influenza incidence rates were distributed in southwest and northwest China, particularly in Tibet (CR > 10/100,000). From 2008 to 2013, the high‐incidence area moved to northern, central, and southern China, with the Beijing‐Tianjin‐Hebei (BTH) region having a high incidence. Since 2014, influenza has shown a polycentric distribution in mainland China, with high incidence rates in southern, eastern, northern, central and northwest parts of China. In general, BTH region has always had a high incidence rate of influenza. In addition, the southern region of mainland China has become a high incidence area since 2014 (Figure 4).

Spatial Clustering Characteristics of Influenza

After 2014, the incidence of influenza in mainland China has shown a clear tendency to spread. Hotspot analysis was used to analyze the spatial distribution characteristics of influenza incidence during this period. Although the incidence had a general tendency to be dispersed, there was a spatial clustering characteristic between regions. Between 2014 and 2017, influenza incidence was clustered in the Hubei and Anhui provinces, and the spatial clustering was statistically significant (Z value > 1.96, P < 0.05). By 2018, only the Anhui province remained as a hotspot for influenza incidence. During 2015, in addition to the Hubei and Anhui provinces, the Zhejiang and Fujian provinces also showed clustering of influenza incidence. However, no statistical significance was seen for the cold spot areas (low Z value areas) of incidence (Z value > −1.96, P > 0.05).

Spatial Variation Characteristics of Influenza

The location of the mean center of influenza incidence in mainland China was not randomly distributed but was concentrated in specific regions from 2014 to 2018 (Figure 5 and Table S3 in Supporting Information S1). Incidence was clustered at the intersection of the Henan and Hubei provinces, with the geographical location shifting within the region from 112°47′5″E to 113°54′14″E and 32°15′32″N to 33°34′51″N (Table S3 in Supporting Information S1). Notably, The reduction in the area of the standard deviation ellipse suggests that regions with a high prevalence of influenza incidence in mainland China are increasingly clustering in the southeast. This clustering effect is becoming increasingly pronounced. To address this issue, we have provided additional explanations in the manuscript. To further analyze the discrete and directional trends in the gravity center of influenza incidence, a first‐order SDE of incidence was created using the SDE tool for 2014 to 2018. The results showed that the direction of its SDE ranged from 153.88° to 179.32° (153.88–179.32°NNW), indicating a general northwest‐southeast distribution of the incidence center. The long axis of its SDE was in the northwest‐southeast direction, with a length of 1,992.55–2,138.98 km, and the short axis was in the southwest‐northeast direction, with a length of 1,536.90–1,814.36 km. The ratio of the long axis to the short axis (elliptical flatness) ranged from 1.17 to 1.39, and the elliptical flatness gradually increased between 2014 and 2018. This indicated that the dispersion of the onset mean center was greater in the northwest‐southeast direction than in the southwest‐northeast direction. In addition, the directionality of influenza onset continued to increase with each year, that is, the clustering of influenza onset was stronger in the northwest‐southeast direction than in the southwest‐northeast direction from 2014 to 2018.

Mechanisms of Influenza Outbreaks Driven by Multiple Factors

Analysis of Driving Factors and Their Interactions

Factor detection with a POGD was employed to identify the contribution of a single factor to influenza incidence (Figure 6). Overall, the average q‐values indicated significantly higher values for anthropogenic activity factor at 0.3476 compared to the meteorological and atmospheric pollutant factors at 0.0413. The explanatory power of the anthropogenic activity factors in descending order was structure of energy consumption (SEC; q‐value 0.6998), share of secondary industrialization (SIR; 0.3461), disposable income per capita (PCDI; 0.3324), rate of urbanization (UR; 0.2073), and emissions of pollutants from exhaust gases (PWG; 0.1525; Figure S8a in Supporting Information S1). The explanatory power of meteorological and atmospheric pollutant factors for influenza incidence was relatively low compared to anthropogenic factors. Among them, the explanatory power in descending order was precipitation (PRE; 0.1638), maximum temperature (TMX; 0.0621), NO2 concentration (0.0407), O3 concentration (0.0394), wind speed (WS; 0.0209), CO concentration (0.0206), PM2.5 concentration (0.0193), diurnal temperature difference (DTR; 0.0116), and SO2 concentration (0.0095; Figure S8b in Supporting Information S1). The P values of the explanatory power of all factors were less than 0.01, and the detection results were all significant. The factor detections indicated that anthropogenic activities, such as SEC and industrialization, played a dominant role in influenza incidence in mainland China, and the explanatory power of meteorology and air pollution alone was weaker than that of anthropogenic activities.

Discussion

Causes of Spatial‐Temporal Changes of Influenza Incidence Rate

The incidence rate of influenza among residents in Chinese Mainland shows the characteristics of inter annual and seasonal fluctuation, mainly due to the impact of temperature, humidity, temperature changes and other meteorological factors on the incidence of influenza, showing obvious seasonal and geographical characteristics. In Figure 1, in the years 2009 and 2018, mainland China had a higher number of influenza cases than other years due to the outbreak of the H1N1 (2009) and A/H1N1 (2018) influenza virus epidemic (Fang et al., 2012; Ma et al., 2020). Differences in temperature and humidity across geographic regions lead to different peak seasons for influenza in the north and south (Shu et al., 2010). The main virus types or subtypes have also been prevalent in different regions with seasonal changes, and these different types of influenza viruses have contributed to the seasonal variability (Cui et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2017). In addition, the mass migration of people for the Chinese New Year, a traditional Chinese holiday, fueled a major outbreak of infectious diseases in March (Tang et al., 2010). The high incidence of influenza in mainland China from winter to spring can be attributed to a multifaceted interplay of factors. Low temperatures and low humidity create favorable conditions for the survival and transmission of influenza viruses, with cold and dry climate of winter enhancing the stability and transmissibility of virus (Park et al., 2020). Additionally, the increased density of indoor populations during winter and heightened social interactions during holiday periods contribute to the facilitation of virus spread (Huo et al., 2023). The antigenic variation and immune escape mechanisms of influenza viruses further affect epidemiological patterns, with the potential emergence of new pandemic strains in certain years (e.g., 2019 year). The peak in influenza cases observed in March may be a result of delayed transmission effects and a lag in epidemiological surveillance, leading to a peak in case numbers during late winter or early spring.

For the characteristics of the spatial distribution of influenza incidence, three reasons can justify the incidence of influenza being concentrated in the Anhui and Hubei provinces. First, Anhui and Hubei were located in the border area between southern and northern China. The daily temperature difference was greater in the spring season compared to other provinces, and the large diurnal temperature range could have led to the decrease in the resistance and immunity of people making them susceptible to infection (Zhang et al., 2023). Second, the influenza virus tends to survive for long periods in cold temperatures and dry air. The climate and environment (monsoon climatic zones) in spring in both provinces were suitable for bacterial viruses to multiply (especially H. influenzae) (Zhang et al., 2022). Highly contagious influenza viruses have led to a tendency for outbreaks of flu to cluster in both provinces. Third, the two provinces were not only among the most populous in China but also among the provinces with the highest population migration. The risk of influenza spreading was exacerbated by the mass movement of people during the spring festival holiday (Tang et al., 2010).

The center of influenza incidence gradually shifted toward the south (especially the south‐east) from 2014 to 2018, mainly influenced by differences in pathogens, climatic conditions, and population density and mobility. Southern influenza differed from northern influenza in terms of pathogens. Southern influenza was usually caused by influenza A viruses, whereas northern influenza by influenza B viruses. Influenza A viruses mutated rapidly and were more virulent and resistant to drugs in humans than influenza B viruses, making infection more severe. However, influenza A (H3N2) has predominantly been the prevalent strain in mainland China in recent years, and hence the incidence was clustered in the southern region (Lei et al., 2022). Both temperature and humidity have an important influence on the spread and reproduction of the virus (Liu et al., 2019). Influenza in the south tended to occur during wet seasons. Influenza in the south tended to occur during wet seasons, such as spring or autumn (Liang et al., 2023). In contrast, the northern climate was cold and dry, making it relatively difficult for viruses to spread and multiply. Therefore, the climatic conditions in the south make it easier for influenza to spread and for outbreaks to occur. In addition, the population density was relatively high in the south, and population migration was more frequent in the north. Densely populated cities were prone to outbreaks of infectious diseases and the movement of people tended to accelerate virus spread (Zhang et al., 2020). In contrast, the population in the northern regions was relatively more dispersed and less mobile, lowering the virus spread.

Drivers of Influenza Incidence and Its Mechanisms in Mainland Chinese Residents

Significant disparities in influenza incidence rates exist among different national regions, attributable to variations in economic development, population size, health infrastructure, public health policies, educational attainment, cultural and social behaviors, and climatic‐environmental factors (Hagiya et al., 2023; Iuliano et al., 2018; WHO, 2019). Globally, the incidence of influenza‐associated lower respiratory tract infections is estimated at 713.1 cases per 100,000 individuals. Regions with a high disease burden are predominantly located in East Asia, Northern Europe, and North America. Specifically, Russia (2,397.0/100,000) and India (1,011.6/100,000) report the highest disease burdens, followed by the United States (287.9/100,000), China (151.8/100,000), and Japan (141.5/100,000) (Troeger et al., 2019). The elevated incidence of influenza in temperate regions is also influenced by climatic conditions. Experimental epidemiological studies conducted by Lowen and Steel (2014) demonstrated a strong correlation between influenza incidence and local humidity and temperature, indicating that the spread of influenza viruses is significantly regulated by these environmental factors. This finding provides a long‐awaited explanation for the seasonal prevalence of influenza during winter months in these regions. In high‐income countries, socio‐economic inequalities further exacerbate the vulnerability of certain populations to influenza. An epidemiological study by Sloan et al. (2015) in Tennessee, USA, revealed that influenza hospitalization rates increased in conjunction with higher proportions of individuals living in poverty, in female‐headed households, in overcrowded households, and in densely populated areas. Conversely, hospitalization rates decreased as the proportion of college‐educated, employed, and health‐insured individuals rose. When data were stratified by race, higher rates of influenza hospitalization were also linked to lower socioeconomic status within communities. Overall, while high‐income countries typically manage influenza incidence more effectively, low‐income countries face greater challenges. This contrast underscores the critical need for global health assistance and equitable resource allocation.

Urbanization development, industrialization activities and atmospheric pollution have combined to drive changes in the incidence of influenza among mainland Chinese residents. But their roles show variability. For natural factors, studies have shown a significant positive correlation between atmospheric SO2 concentrations and respiratory morbidity (chronic bronchitis, bronchial asthma, and emphysema, etc.) (Rittweger et al., 2022; Thach et al., 2010). Increases in SO2 concentrations and precipitation have led directly to the formation of acid precipitation, which directly affects the human respiratory and immune system. This in turn accelerates the spread of influenza viruses. In addition, with the development of industry and the optimization of the coal‐based energy mix, it was possible to suppress the incidence of influenza.

The impact of air quality on influenza viruses is primarily mediated through several mechanisms, including effects on virus transmission pathways, host susceptibility, and the environmental stability of the virus (Fang et al., 2024; Li, Duan, et al., 2022). In environments with severe air pollution, the concentration of airborne particulate matter (e.g., PM2.5, PM10) is significantly higher, and these particles can serve as carriers for viruses, forming aerosols. Influenza viruses can attach to these particles and disperse through the air (Liu et al., 2021). Under conditions of poor air circulation, aerosols containing viruses can remain suspended for extended periods, thereby increasing their transmission range and the likelihood of infection (Bozic & Kanduc, 2021).

Air pollution also adversely affects the respiratory tract and immune system of host (Keulers et al., 2022). Chronic exposure to pollutants, particularly fine particulate matter, O3, and NOx, can induce or worsen chronic inflammation in the respiratory tract. This not only compromises the barrier function of the respiratory mucosa, making it more vulnerable to invasion by influenza viruses, but may also suppress the host's immune response by inducing oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby reducing the body's resistance to viral infections (Auerbach & Hernandez, 2012). This risk is especially pronounced in individuals with pre‐existing chronic respiratory conditions, where air pollution can further exacerbate their conditions, making them more susceptible to influenza virus infection. The direct effects of air pollution on the respiratory system include the triggering or exacerbation of respiratory diseases such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which can make a person more susceptible to the influenza virus (Choi et al., 2023). In addition, pollutants can damage respiratory epithelial cells and reduce their resistance, increasing the likelihood of viral invasion. Additionally, air quality often correlates with climatic conditions. Winter, typically the peak season for influenza, is characterized by poor air circulation and high humidity. These conditions, combined with elevated levels of air pollution, can increase the stability of viruses in the environment, prolonging their survival and increasing their transmission potential.

Air quality plays a significant role in the transmission and infection of influenza viruses by enhancing virus transmission pathways, increasing host susceptibility, and improving virus stability in the environment. The interplay of these mechanisms can exacerbate the severity of influenza outbreaks in areas with poor air quality. Therefore, improving air quality is not only crucial for reducing the incidence of respiratory diseases but also a key strategy in controlling the spread of infectious diseases such as influenza.

The level of economic development, air pollutants, and meteorological conditions have had a direct impact on influenza incidence, whereas the level of energy consumption has indirectly driven changes in influenza incidence by influencing the other three elements (Figure 7). The level of local economic development has had a direct impact on influenza morbidity and mortality (Duggal et al., 2016; Smith & Keogh‐Brown, 2013). The study confirmed that H1N1 influenza mortality was highest in South Asia (61% [95% CI, 50–71]) and sub‐Saharan Africa (53% [95% CI, 29–75]) compared to Western Europe (25% [95% CI 22–30]) (Duggal et al., 2016). The income level of the population had a direct impact on the level of influenza vaccination coverage, screening, and treatment, thus demonstrating that influenza morbidity and mortality were lower in high‐income countries than in low‐income countries. However, increased urbanization has shown a negative driving relationship with influenza incidence. In general, geographic clustering of influenza has generally been driven by patterns of influenza transmission through large population centers to surrounding communities (Bozick & Real, 2015; Czaja et al., 2020). Thus, urbanization has led to increased susceptibility in population subgroups (Chandrasekhar et al., 2017). There was a close connection between air pollution and influenza. Exposure to air pollutants (e.g., PM2.5 and NO2) reduced the ability of macrophages to phagocytose viruses and led to a macrophage‐dependent reduction in the inactivation of invasive pathogens, which severely exacerbated inflammation (Meng et al., 2021; Sava & Carlsten, 2012; Yao et al., 2019). In addition, studies have reported that exposure to PM2.5 and SO2 can lead to the upregulation of transcription factors and inflammation‐related cytokine genes, consequently triggering systemic inflammation and elevated oxidative stress (Meng et al., 2021; Xing et al., 2016; Zeng et al., 2023).

Specific measures to prevent and control influenza in mainland China should be approached from several angles. First, as research shows that the highest incidence of influenza occurs in the 0–4 age group, vaccination efforts should be intensified for this population, especially before the peak of the influenza season. In addition, given the strong seasonality of influenza, with the highest incidence typically occurring in March, early warnings should be issued, vaccination promoted and emergency measures strengthened in high‐incidence areas. Prevention efforts should also take into account spatial clustering trends, particularly in northern, central and south‐eastern regions, where medical resources should be better prepared and allocated. Climate and environmental factors play a crucial role in influenza transmission; reducing air pollution, improving sanitation and reducing population density can effectively reduce the spread of the virus. Particular attention should be paid to the indirect impact of energy use on climate conditions. Furthermore, as influenza transmission is closely linked to population movements, especially during periods of high travel such as the Chinese New Year, stricter surveillance and control measures for mobile populations should be implemented to reduce the spread of the virus.

Several limitations in this study need to be further addressed. First, the main limitation is the ecological design that limits the transferability of the results to the individual level. The availability of data constrained our capacity to conduct further analyses based on age, gender, and other demographic subgroups. Additionally, this study did not differentiate between various types of influenza, and the subtypes that exhibit heightened sensitivity to natural and socioeconomic factors remain unidentified.

Conclusions

This study highlights a complex and evolving pattern of influenza incidence in mainland China, revealing significant epidemiological insights over the period from 2004 to 2018. The increasing trend of influenza cases, particularly among vulnerable groups such as children aged 0–4 (average incidence rate, 67.56/100,000), underscores the urgent need for age‐targeted interventions. The pronounced seasonality of influenza, with peaks in March, suggests that temporal factors like temperature fluctuations are critical drivers, further exacerbated by increased population mobility. Spatially, the shifting epicenters of high incidence—from northern, central, and southern regions to the southeast—reflect broader socio‐environmental dynamics, including pathogen variability, population density, and regional economic disparities. These spatial patterns, statistically significant in clusters such as Hubei and Anhui provinces. The environmental conditions, particularly during winter when air circulation is poor, contribute to the stability and transmission potential of influenza viruses. Therefore, the coupling of socioeconomic and natural factors creates a complex landscape that intensifies the risk of influenza outbreaks, necessitating comprehensive public health strategies that address both environmental management and socioeconomic disparities.
