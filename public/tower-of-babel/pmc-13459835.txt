Hindfoot realignment after coronal plane correction: a novel radiographic angle on long-leg standing radiograph
Authors: Woo Sub Kim, Kyoung Min Lee, Ki Hyuk Sung, Youngwook Sim, Seung Yeol Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate assessment of hindfoot alignment is important when evaluating distal compensation after correction of varus knee osteoarthritis. Dedicated hindfoot imaging is not routinely obtained with standard long-leg radiographs. This study developed and validated a novel hindfoot alignment angle measurable on routine patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 170 operated lower limbs from adult patients who underwent high tibial osteotomy or total knee arthroplasty for varus knee osteoarthritis. Preoperative and postoperative patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs were analyzed. Interobserver reliability and construct validity were assessed in a 36-case subset with complete long-leg, foot, and Saltzman-view radiographs. Intraclass correlation coefficients were used for reliability. Pearson correlations, Shapiro–Wilk normality tests, Spearman sensitivity analyses, and Bland–Altman agreement analysis were performed.ResultsThe novel hindfoot alignment angle demonstrated excellent average-measure interobserver reliability (ICC = 0.905; 95% CI, 0.835–0.948). It showed a moderate correlation with the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle (r = 0.443, P = 0.007) and a weak correlation with the lateral talus–first metatarsal angle (r = 0.331, P = 0.048). Bland–Altman analysis showed a systematic positive bias of 7.96° for the novel angle relative to the Saltzman-view angle, with 95% limits of agreement from − 1.05° to 16.98°. In the overall cohort, the hip–knee–ankle angle changed from 8.02 ± 3.08° to − 0.74 ± 2.95° (P < 0.001), and the novel hindfoot alignment angle changed from 9.53 ± 3.85° to 14.09 ± 3.83° (P < 0.001). With change defined as postoperative minus preoperative value, change in hip–knee–ankle angle correlated mildly with change in the novel hindfoot alignment angle (r = − 0.301, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe novel hindfoot alignment angle is a reproducible radiographic parameter that can be measured on patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs and is moderately associated with Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment. The Bland–Altman findings indicate that the novel angle should not be used interchangeably with the Saltzman-view angle, but it may serve as a practical adjunct for assessing hindfoot alignment when long-leg radiographs are already being obtained.Level of evidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.Keywords: Hindfoot alignment, Subtalar joint, Long-leg standing radiograph, Varus knee osteoarthritis, High tibial osteotomy, Total knee arthroplasty, Lower-limb alignment, Bland–Altman analysis

Background

The subtalar joint, formed by the talus and calcaneus, contributes to three-dimensional motion of the hindfoot and plays an important role in maintaining plantigrade weight bearing during standing and gait [1, 2]. Hindfoot malalignment is observed in several foot and ankle disorders, including progressive collapsing foot deformity, a condition historically associated with posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, and post-traumatic hindfoot malalignment [3]. Although static radiographic measurements cannot fully represent dynamic foot and lower-limb function, they remain widely used for diagnosis, surgical planning, and longitudinal radiographic follow-up.

Dedicated hindfoot alignment radiographs, such as the Saltzman view, and weight-bearing computed tomography can be used to evaluate hindfoot alignment [4–6]. However, these additional examinations are not always obtained in patients undergoing routine evaluation for knee osteoarthritis. Knee malalignment can influence hindfoot posture, and varus and valgus knee alignment may produce different distal compensatory patterns [7, 8]. The present study focused on patients with varus knee osteoarthritis undergoing coronal-plane correction.

High tibial osteotomy and total knee arthroplasty are commonly performed to correct varus knee alignment, but the distal alignment response may differ depending on procedure, patient anatomy, and residual flexibility of the hindfoot and subtalar joint [9–11]. Full-length, patella-facing, weight-bearing radiographs are routinely acquired to evaluate coronal knee alignment, but their potential value for concurrent assessment of the ankle and hindfoot has not been fully established.

The purpose of this study was to develop and validate a novel hindfoot alignment angle measurable on routine patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs. We evaluated interobserver reliability, construct validity against established hindfoot and foot radiographic parameters, agreement with the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle, and longitudinal changes after high tibial osteotomy or total knee arthroplasty. We also performed subgroup analyses to compare changes after the two procedures.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations and study participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the study institution (IRB No. MJH 2025-12-026). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design. The dataset was accessed for research purposes on 17 April 2024. Data extraction was performed under the approved retrospective review protocol using the institutional electronic medical record and picture archiving and communication system. The analytic dataset was de-identified before statistical analysis, and investigators did not have access to information that could directly identify individual participants.

The overall longitudinal cohort comprised operated lower limbs from adult patients who underwent high tibial osteotomy or total knee arthroplasty for varus knee osteoarthritis and had preoperative and postoperative patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs available between February 2017 and December 2024. A 36-case validation subset with complete patella-facing long-leg radiographs, weight-bearing anteroposterior and lateral foot radiographs, and Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment radiographs was used for interobserver reliability and construct-validity analyses.

Eligibility criteria

The exclusion criteria were previous hindfoot or ankle surgery; previous fracture, neuromuscular deformity, severe extra-articular femoral or tibial deformity, or other severe lower-extremity deformity that could preclude accurate radiographic measurement; incomplete or poor-quality radiographs; age younger than 18 years; interval ankle or hindfoot surgery or trauma between preoperative and postoperative assessments; and ankle osteoarthritis of Takakura stage IIIa or higher.

Radiographic acquisition and measurements

Patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs were obtained with patients standing with equal weight distribution on both feet using a standardized institutional protocol. Patients were instructed to position the patellae forward to reduce rotational variability. Because this was a retrospective study, standardized positioning could not be directly supervised by the investigators; therefore, only radiographs acquired according to the institutional protocol and without obvious malrotation or positioning error were included. The X-ray beam was centered at the knee joint level, and the film-to-focus distance was maintained at 180 cm to reduce magnification effects. On patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs, the hip–knee–ankle angle, tibiotalar angle, novel hindfoot alignment angle, and novel varus–valgus angle were measured. The hip–knee–ankle angle was defined as the coronal-plane angle between the femoral mechanical axis and tibial mechanical axis on the full-length standing radiograph [12]. The tibiotalar angle was defined as the angle between the distal tibial plafond and the talar dome line [13].

On Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment radiographs, the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle and varus–valgus angle were measured. The varus–valgus angle was defined as the angle between the tibial long axis and a line connecting the superior margin of the sustentaculum tali to the lateral extremity of the calcaneus at the posterior talocalcaneal joint [10]. Weight-bearing anteroposterior and lateral foot radiographs were used to measure the anteroposterior talus–first metatarsal angle, talonavicular coverage angle, lateral talus–first metatarsal angle, and calcaneal pitch angle [14].

The novel varus–valgus angle was defined as the angle between the sustentaculum–lateral calcaneal line and a line drawn from the most inferior weight-bearing point of the calcaneal tuberosity shadow to the midpoint of the talar dome (Fig. 2). The novel hindfoot alignment angle was defined as the angle between the tibial anatomical axis and the line connecting the most inferior weight-bearing point of the calcaneal tuberosity shadow to the midpoint of the talar dome (Fig. 3). The tibial anatomical axis was drawn through the midpoints of the tibial shaft visible on the long-leg radiograph. All measurements were performed using the PACS platform (Infinitt, Seoul, South Korea).

Novel VV angle: Patella facing forward scanogram. The angle formed by the intersection of (1) a line connecting the most inferior point of the calcaneal tuberosity shadow to the midpoint of the talar dome on the scanogram and (2) a line connecting the superior margin of the sustentaculum tali to the lateral extremity of the calcaneus at the posterior surface of the talocalcaneal joint

Novel HA angle: Patella facing forward scanogram. The angle formed by the intersection of (1) a line connecting the most inferior point of the calcaneal tuberosity shadow to the midpoint of the talar dome on the scanogram and (2) anatomical axis of the tibia

Rater independence and blinding

For interobserver reliability, three orthopedic surgeons with 10, 4, and 3 years of clinical experience independently measured anonymized radiographs from the 36-case validation subset. The radiographs were reviewed in separate PACS worklists. Raters were blinded to patient identifiers, surgical procedure, measurement timing, and the measurements of the other raters. The measurements were collated only after all raters had completed their assessments.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as counts. Preoperative and postoperative values were compared using paired t-tests. Changes were defined as postoperative value minus preoperative value. Between-procedure differences in changes were assessed using Welch independent t-tests.

Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess associations between conventional radiographic parameters and novel angles and to assess associations between longitudinal alignment changes. Because several longitudinal change variables deviated from normality, Spearman rank correlations were also performed as sensitivity analyses for the principal correlations. Correlation strength was interpreted using Evans’ criteria: very weak (|r| < 0.20), weak (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), strong (0.60–0.79), and very strong (≥ 0.80) [15].

Interobserver reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). A two-way mixed-effects consistency model for average measures was used as the primary reliability model, because three independent raters measured the validation subset. ICC values were interpreted as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), and excellent (> 0.90). Agreement between the novel hindfoot alignment angle and the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis, including mean bias and 95% limits of agreement. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Case demographics and radiographic changes

A total of 170 operated lower-limb cases were included in the longitudinal cohort. The case-level sex distribution was 37 male and 133 female, and the mean age was 65.3 ± 8.5 years. The operated side was right in 77 cases and left in 93 cases. High tibial osteotomy was performed in 127 cases and total knee arthroplasty in 43 cases.

The hip–knee–ankle angle changed from 8.02 ± 3.08° preoperatively to − 0.74 ± 2.95° postoperatively (change, − 8.76 ± 3.16°; P < 0.001). The novel hindfoot alignment angle changed from 9.53 ± 3.85° to 14.09 ± 3.83° (change, 4.56 ± 3.22°; P < 0.001). The novel varus–valgus angle changed from 60.90 ± 4.89° to 58.47 ± 5.44° (change, − 2.43 ± 5.05°; P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Interobserver reliability

The 36-case validation subset was used for interobserver reliability analysis. Most radiographic parameters demonstrated good-to-excellent average-measure reliability. The novel hindfoot alignment angle showed excellent interobserver reliability (ICC = 0.905; 95% CI, 0.835–0.948). The novel varus–valgus angle showed good-to-excellent reliability (ICC = 0.856; 95% CI, 0.749–0.922). The Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle also showed good reliability (ICC = 0.892; 95% CI, 0.813–0.941). The complete reliability results are shown in Table 3.

Normality, construct validity, and agreement analysis

For variables included in the construct-validity correlation analysis, Shapiro–Wilk testing showed no significant deviation from normality for most measurements (P = 0.110–0.842), except for talonavicular coverage angle (W = 0.937, P = 0.040). The longitudinal change variables for the novel hindfoot alignment angle, novel varus–valgus angle, and tibiotalar angle showed non-normal distributions; therefore, Spearman sensitivity analyses were performed for the principal longitudinal correlations.

The novel hindfoot alignment angle showed a moderate correlation with the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle (r = 0.443, P = 0.007) and a weak correlation with the lateral talus–first metatarsal angle (r = 0.331, P = 0.048). It did not correlate significantly with the anteroposterior talus–first metatarsal angle, talonavicular coverage angle, or calcaneal pitch angle. The novel varus–valgus angle showed a strong correlation with the conventional varus–valgus angle (r = 0.695, P < 0.001) and a weak negative correlation with the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle (r = − 0.369, P = 0.027). Correlation between lower-limb correction and novel hindfoot angle changes

With change defined as postoperative minus preoperative value, the change in hip–knee–ankle angle was negatively correlated with the change in the novel hindfoot alignment angle (r = − 0.301, P < 0.001). Because the hip–knee–ankle angle decreased after correction while the novel hindfoot alignment angle increased, this negative correlation indicates that a greater reduction in varus alignment was associated with a greater increase in the novel hindfoot alignment angle. The Spearman sensitivity analysis showed a similar relationship (ρ = −0.299, P < 0.001).

The change in the novel hindfoot alignment angle was also negatively correlated with the change in the novel varus–valgus angle (r = − 0.449, P < 0.001; Spearman ρ = −0.385, P < 0.001). These findings are summarized in Table 5.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study was that the novel hindfoot alignment angle measured on patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs demonstrated excellent average-measure interobserver reliability and moderate construct validity against the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle. The novel varus–valgus angle also showed good-to-excellent reliability and a strong correlation with the conventional varus–valgus angle. These results suggest that long-leg standing radiographs, which are routinely obtained for lower-limb alignment assessment, can provide additional information about hindfoot alignment without requiring a separate radiographic acquisition.

The moderate correlation between the novel hindfoot alignment angle and the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle should be interpreted carefully. Although both measurements are intended to reflect coronal hindfoot orientation, they are not identical measurements. The novel angle uses a patella-facing long-leg radiographic projection and a tibial anatomical-axis reference, whereas the Saltzman-view angle is measured on a dedicated hindfoot alignment view. Radiographic superimposition, variability in the projected calcaneal contact point, differences in tibial reference-axis construction, foot progression angle, hindfoot morphology, and subtalar position may all influence the relationship between the two measurements. Therefore, the novel angle should be considered an adjunctive long-leg radiographic parameter rather than a direct substitute for a dedicated hindfoot alignment view.

The Bland–Altman analysis supports this interpretation. The novel hindfoot alignment angle showed a systematic positive bias of 7.96° compared with the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle, and the 95% limits of agreement were relatively wide. These findings indicate that the two methods should not be used interchangeably for absolute measurement. However, the presence of a reproducible and moderately correlated angle on routinely acquired long-leg radiographs may be useful for screening, longitudinal comparison within the same patient, and identifying patients who may benefit from dedicated hindfoot imaging.

Subgroup analysis showed that the novel hindfoot alignment angle increased significantly after both high tibial osteotomy and total knee arthroplasty, and the magnitude of change did not differ significantly between procedures. Although high tibial osteotomy and total knee arthroplasty differ biomechanically, both procedures substantially altered the coronal lower-limb axis in this cohort. The similar change in the novel hindfoot alignment angle may reflect a shared distal response to redistribution of coronal loading after correction of varus knee alignment. Nevertheless, the total knee arthroplasty subgroup was smaller than the high tibial osteotomy subgroup, and this study was not designed to determine procedure-specific mechanisms. Separate prospective studies with larger, balanced subgroups are needed.

Previous studies have reported that the relationship between knee correction and hindfoot alignment is influenced by preoperative hindfoot posture and residual compensatory capacity of the subtalar joint [9–11]. In the present study, when change was defined as postoperative minus preoperative value, a greater decrease in the hip–knee–ankle angle after correction was associated with a greater increase in the novel hindfoot alignment angle, although the strength of this association was mild. This finding is consistent with the concept that hindfoot adaptation after knee realignment is multifactorial and may depend on soft-tissue flexibility, subtalar and midtarsal joint mobility, foot morphology, and preexisting ankle or hindfoot degeneration. To avoid overinterpretation, we describe the longitudinal findings as associations rather than proof of causal hindfoot realignment [16, 17]. Recent systematic reviews further indicate that hindfoot alignment after total knee arthroplasty should be interpreted using both clinical and radiographic outcomes [18, 19].

The clinical relevance of this measurement may extend beyond knee realignment [20]. Foot and ankle surgeons frequently need to understand how hindfoot alignment relates to the overall lower-limb axis [21, 22]. A patella-facing long-leg radiograph may be helpful when both proximal alignment and hindfoot projection are clinically relevant, especially when additional imaging is not immediately available [23, 24]. However, validation in primary foot and ankle disorders, valgus knee deformity, and patients with severe hindfoot deformity is still required before broader application.

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective, and radiographic positioning could not be prospectively controlled. We attempted to reduce this limitation by including only radiographs obtained according to a standardized institutional protocol and excluding images with obvious malrotation or inadequate positioning. Second, the analyses were performed at the operated-limb/case level, and a small number of patients may have contributed more than one operated limb or procedure; within-patient clustering was not formally modeled. Third, high tibial osteotomy and total knee arthroplasty cases were analyzed together in the overall cohort. Although subgroup analyses were added, the two procedures may have different biomechanical effects and recovery patterns. Fourth, the construct-validity and reliability analyses were based on a 36-case subset with complete comparative imaging, which may limit generalizability. Fifth, the novel hindfoot alignment angle is a two-dimensional static radiographic measurement and cannot fully represent three-dimensional subtalar orientation or dynamic gait function. Sixth, this study focused on varus knee osteoarthritis; valgus knee deformity was not examined. Seventh, patient-reported outcome measures were not available, so the clinical effect of the radiographic changes on pain or function could not be evaluated; validated foot and ankle outcome tools should be incorporated in future prospective studies [25, 26]. Finally, the Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated that the novel angle and Saltzman-view angle are not interchangeable for absolute values.

Conclusion

The novel hindfoot alignment angle can be measured reproducibly on routine patella-facing long-leg standing radiographs. It demonstrated excellent average-measure interobserver reliability and a moderate correlation with the Saltzman-view hindfoot alignment angle, but Bland–Altman analysis indicated that the two measurements are not interchangeable. After correction of varus knee osteoarthritis with high tibial osteotomy or total knee arthroplasty, the novel hindfoot alignment angle changed significantly and was mildly associated with the magnitude of coronal lower-limb correction. The novel angle may serve as a practical adjunct for evaluating hindfoot alignment on long-leg radiographs, while dedicated hindfoot imaging remains necessary when precise hindfoot assessment is required.
