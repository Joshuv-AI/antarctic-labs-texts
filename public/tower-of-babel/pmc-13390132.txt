Comparison of early and late drainage interventions in necrotizing pancreatitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Gaofeng Zhang, Bing Zhao, Tengang Deng, Xiaofei He, Yongpin Chen, Changtao Zhong, Jie Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study was designed to evaluate differences in mortality, complication rates, and length of hospital stay between early (≤ 4 weeks) and delayed (> 4 weeks) minimally invasive drainage in patients with necrotizing pancreatitis (NP) through the synthesis of existing evidence.MethodsDatabases including PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched up to November 1, 2025. Eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies were included. Literature screening and data extraction were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, and a random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis.ResultsA total of 11 studies involving 1,697 patients were included. Meta-analysis results demonstrated that the mortality rate in the early drainage intervention group was significantly higher than that in the delayed drainage intervention group (RR = 1.38; 95%CI 1.05–1.81; P = 0.02), though the stability of this finding was poor. The proportion of patients requiring subsequent further debridement was significantly higher in the early drainage intervention group compared with the delayed drainage intervention group (RR = 2.05; 95%CI 1.26–3.31; P = 0.004). No significant differences were detected between the two groups in terms of length of hospital stay (P = 0.17), length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (P = 0.25), or the incidence of complications (P > 0.05).ConclusionThis meta-analysis supports prioritizing delayed drainage in the treatment of NP, as it reduces the need for subsequent invasive debridement procedures; however, early drainage may still hold significant value patients with infection or organ failure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04951-x.

Introduction

Necrotizing pancreatitis (NP) represents a severe complication of acute pancreatitis (AP), primarily characterized by the necrosis of pancreatic tissue and the adjacent adipose tissue [1]. It has been documented that approximately 20% to 30% of individuals with AP may progress to NP, with a subset of these cases advancing to infected necrotizing pancreatitis (INP) [2]. This progression can precipitate a rapid decline in the patient’s condition, with mortality rates escalating to between 20% and 39% [3]. Such cases are frequently accompanied by severe complications, including multiple organ failure, abdominal hemorrhage, and the formation of digestive tract fistulas [4].

The contemporary clinical management of NP predominantly adheres to the “delayed intervention” strategy [5]. International guidelines typically advocate for postponing any intervention for a minimum of four weeks following the onset of the disease [6, 7]. This delay allows for the complete liquefaction of necrotic tissue and the formation of a walled-off necrosis (WON) prior to intervention [8]. The rationale behind this recommendation is to minimize the risk of operation-related infections and enhance treatment outcomes [9]. This recommendation is primarily informed by research findings on early surgical debridement, which indicate that early surgical intervention is associated with increased risks of bleeding and infection spread due to indistinct tissue boundaries [10, 11]. In contrast, delayed surgical intervention has been shown to significantly improve patient prognosis [12].

In recent years, the advancement of minimally invasive techniques has led to the widespread adoption of interventional methods, such as endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage (EUS-GD) [13] and percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) [14], in the clinical management of NP. The feasibility and safety of early drainage intervention have increasingly garnered attention [15]. Research indicates that approximately 25% of patients develop a clear infection or persistent organ dysfunction within four weeks of onset [16, 17]. Excessive delays in drainage may miss the optimal window for infection control and organ function recovery, potentially exacerbating the condition [18]. A retrospective study by Trikudanathan et.al [19] demonstrated that among patients with infectious necrosis and respiratory failure who underwent early drainage, 75% showed improvement in organ function, suggesting that early intervention may be beneficial for some critically ill patients. The multicenter randomized controlled trial (POINTER trial) conducted by Boxhoorn et al. [20] demonstrated no significant differences in the comprehensive complication index and mortality rates between the early drainage group and the group that postponed intervention until encapsulated necrosis had developed. Notably, the early intervention group necessitated a higher number of invasive procedures. Furthermore, approximately 39% of patients in the delayed drainage group achieved recovery through conservative antibiotic treatment alone, suggesting that the delayed intervention strategy may help to circumvent certain unnecessary procedures.

Despite several previous meta-analyses having explored the outcomes of early versus delayed drainage for NP, the major existing meta-analyses were all published before the widespread dissemination of key multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs), such as the POINTER [20] and TIMING [21] trials. These high-quality RCTs employed more rigorous designs to control for bias, and their results have directly challenged or refined the traditional consensus based on earlier observational studies. Therefore, this updated systematic review and meta-analysis aims to comprehensively evaluate the differences in mortality, length of hospital stay, and complication rates between early and delayed drainage interventions by systematically incorporating and synthesizing the latest and highest-level evidence available up to 2025. This study aims to provide the most scientific and reliable evidence base at the current stage for the decision-making of this complex clinical problem.

Methods

Search strategy

This study was conducted in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22] and was duly registered in the PROSPERO database. A systematic retrieval of data was performed using several databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. In alignment with the research topic, key search terms such as necrotizing pancreatitis, drainage, early intervention, and delayed intervention, along with their respective synonyms, were identified. The search strategy was meticulously developed by integrating MeSH terms and free-text words, employing Boolean operators (AND, OR) to establish logical connections. Necessary modifications were implemented to accommodate the unique search syntax and functionalities of each database. The search period extended from the inception of each database to October 2025. Supplemental Table S1 provides the search strategies applicable to each database. Additionally, a manual review of the reference lists of the included studies was undertaken to ensure the comprehensiveness of the search.

Literature screening and data extraction

The literature screening process was independently undertaken by two researchers. Initially, the titles and abstracts of the retrieved literature were examined to exclude studies that were evidently misaligned with the research topic. Subsequently, the full texts of the selected studies were meticulously evaluated, with final inclusion determined according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements encountered during the screening process were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher to ensure consistency. The literature was managed using Zotero software.

For the studies included in this review, we will employ a self-designed standardized data extraction form to systematically gather relevant information. The extracted data will encompass the following categories: (1) fundamental study information, including the first author, publication year, country, and research design; (2) characteristics of the study participants, such as sample size, age, gender, and type of necrosis (aseptic or infectious); (3) specifics of the intervention measures, including precise definitions of the early and late drainage groups (with a demarcation at 4 weeks post-symptom onset) and descriptions of drainage methods (e.g., percutaneous puncture drainage, endoscopic drainage); and (4) outcome indicator data, comprising primary outcomes (all-cause mortality) and secondary outcomes (such as the proportion of patients undergoing subsequent debridement and other procedures, incidence of complications, duration of intensive care unit admission, and length of hospital stay). Upon completion of data extraction, a cross-verification process will be undertaken to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the collected data.

Risk-of-bias assessment

In the included RCTs, the “Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool” (RoB 2.0) [23], as recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration, was employed to evaluate the trials across five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in outcome measurement, and bias in the selection of the reported result. Each domain was assessed as having a “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or a “high risk of bias,” culminating in an overall risk of bias assessment. For cohort studies, the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Interventions (ROBINS-I) [24] tool was utilized to evaluate the trials across seven domains: confounding bias, bias in the selection of participants, bias in the classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in the measurement of outcomes, and bias in the selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “moderate risk,” “high risk,” or “severe risk.” All evaluations were independently conducted by two researchers, with any disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher.

Data analysis

This study used RevMan 5.4 and RStudio software to conduct the meta-analysis. For dichotomous variables, such as mortality and complication rates, the relative ratio (RR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. For continuous variables, such as hospital stay, the mean difference (MD) and its 95% CI were determined. In cases where studies only provided the median and interquartile range, the method proposed by Wan et al. [25] was employed to convert these values to the mean and standard deviation. To adequately account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the studies, the meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model.

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and the I² statistic, with the significance threshold set at p < 0.10; an I² < 50% was considered low heterogeneity, and I² ≥ 50% was considered high heterogeneity. To further investigate potential sources of heterogeneity and evaluate the robustness of the findings, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed. The subgroup analysis was stratified by study design (RCT, prospective, retrospective), geographical region (Eastern versus Western), and primary drainage method (PCD, EUS-GD, and PCD or/and EUS-GD). Sensitivity analyses involved re-conducting the meta-analysis after removing each study one by one to evaluate the impact of individual studies on the pooled effect size. When the number of included studies for a single outcome was no less than 10, Egger’s linear regression test and visual inspection of funnel plots were used to assess potential publication bias.

Results

Search process

A comprehensive search was performed across the four electronic databases, identifying a total of 521 studies. An additional 8 studies were identified through the examination and supplementation of relevant reference lists. Following the initial screening phase, 298 duplicate records were removed, resulting in 223 studies proceeding to the title, abstract, and keyword screening stage. At this stage, 165 studies were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 58 studies were then assessed, leading to the exclusion of a further 47 studies. Ultimately, 11 studies [8, 19–21, 26–31] satisfied the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into this systematic review. Description of included studies and participants

The included studies were published between 2018 and 2025, consisting of 2 RCTs [20, 21], 4 prospective cohort studies [18, 19, 26, 29], and 5 retrospective cohort studies [8, 27, 28, 30, 31]. Among these, 3 studies were conducted in China, 3 in India, 2 in the United States, 1 in the Netherlands, 1 in Pakistan, and 1 in Poland. The studies enrolled a total of 1,697 patients, with 792 in the early drainage intervention group and 905 in the delayed drainage intervention group. In 7 studies [8, 19, 26–30], the proportion of patients with INP in the early drainage group was significantly higher than that in the delayed group. All patients in the early drainage group underwent drainage within 4 weeks of the onset of AP, while those in the delayed drainage group received drainage more than 4 weeks after the onset of AP. Quality of included studies

We used the RoB 2.0 tool to conduct a risk of bias assessment on the 2 included RCTs. Results showed that in terms of “deviations from intended interventions”, the study by Ke et al. [21] was an open-label trial and was rated as “high risk”; the study by Boxhoom et al. [20] was considered to have “Some concerns” in this aspect due to the lack of relevant information. The risk of bias in all other domains was rated as “low risk” (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Two researchers independently used the ROBINS-I tool to assess the risk of bias in the 9 included cohort studies [8, 18, 19, 26–31]. Results indicated that 8 studies showed moderate risk, mainly due to confounding and selection bias; 1 study [8] showed low risk of bias because it adopted a propensity matching design. Regarding the risk of bias assessment, the consistency between the two assessors was high (the kappa values across all domains ranged from 0.759 to 1.00).

Primary outcomes

All 11 included studies [8, 18–21, 26–31] reported mortality data. The mortality rates of the early drainage intervention group and the delayed drainage intervention group were 17.6% (140/793) and 7.9% (72/903), respectively. Results of the pooled analysis showed a significant difference in mortality between the two groups (RR = 1.38; 95%CI 1.05–1.81; P = 0.02) (Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis indicated that after removing the study by Trikudanathan et al. [19], the results of the pooled analysis changed significantly (RR = 1.30; 95%CI 0.98–1.72; P = 0.07) (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating poor stability of this result.

Secondary outcomes

Ten studies [8, 19–21, 26–31] reported the need for subsequent further debridement procedures in patients of the two groups. Results of the pooled analysis showed that the early drainage intervention group had a significantly higher proportion of patients requiring subsequent further debridement and other procedures compared with the delayed drainage intervention group (RR = 2.05; 95%CI 1.26–3.31; P = 0.004) (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis results indicated that the results of this meta-analysis were robust.

Seven studies [18–20, 26–28, 31] reported the length of hospital stay of patients in the two groups, but there was significant heterogeneity among the studies (I²=93%). The pooled analysis results showed no significant difference in the length of hospital stay between the two groups (MD = 3.95; 95%CI -1.66–9.57; P = 0.17) (Fig. 4A). Five studies [18–20, 26, 28] reported the length of ICU stay of patients in the two groups, with significant heterogeneity among the studies (I²=87%). The pooled analysis results showed no significant difference in the length of ICU stay between the two groups (MD = 1.36; 95%CI -0.96–3.68; P = 0.25) (Fig. 4B). Sensitivity analysis results indicated that the meta-analysis results of the length of hospital stay and length of ICU stay were robust.

We also compared the complication profiles between the two groups. Among them, 6 studies [8, 18, 19, 21, 26, 31] reported pancreatic fistula, 11 studies [8, 18–21, 26–31] reported bleeding, 7 studies [8, 18–21, 26, 29] reported gastrointestinal fistula, 4 studies [8, 21, 26, 29] reported thrombosis, and 5 studies [8, 19, 20, 28, 29] reported pancreatic endocrine dysfunction. Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot for mortality revealed a generally symmetric distribution (Fig. 6). Additionally, results from Egger’s linear regression test (p = 0.102) did not indicate significant publication bias. Furthermore, funnel plots generated for the rate of further debridement intervention and bleeding complications (Supplementary Fig. 3) also showed a generally symmetric distribution. Egger’s linear regression test results for these two outcomes (p = 0.685 and p = 0.721) similarly did not suggest the presence of significant publication bias.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 11 studies published between 2018 and 2025, covering 1,697 patients with NP. It systematically compared the outcome differences between early (≤ 4 weeks) and delayed (> 4 weeks) minimally invasive drainage. The results showed that the overall mortality rate in the early drainage group was significantly higher than that in the delayed group (RR = 1.38, P = 0.02), but this result had poor stability. After excluding the retrospective study by Trikudanathan et al. [19], the difference was no longer statistically significant (RR = 1.30, P = 0.07). Among the secondary outcomes, the proportion of patients requiring subsequent debridement surgery was significantly higher in the early drainage group (RR = 2.05, P = 0.004). However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in hospital stay, ICU length of stay, or the incidence of major complications such as pancreatic fistula, hemorrhage, and gastrointestinal fistula (all P > 0.05).

Traditional guidelines for the management of AP recommend delayed drainage, with the core rationale derived from experiences in the open surgery era [17]. Specifically, unclear necrotic boundaries and incomplete tissue liquefaction were thought to increase surgical trauma and promote inflammation progression. However, with the popularization of minimally invasive drainage modalities such as percutaneous catheter drainage and endoscopic drainage, the validity of this 4-week “prohibition” has been increasingly challenged [6, 32]. The findings of this study contribute to this ongoing debate. While they indicate that the risks of early drainage may have been overestimated in some prior analyses due to confounding by disease severity [33], the data nonetheless underscore that early intervention is associated with quantifiable risks, most notably a significantly increased need for subsequent procedures. The observed outcome differences, particularly in mortality, appear to be heavily influenced by confounding factors related to patients’ baseline conditions. For instance, in the study by Trikudanathan et al. [19], 86% of patients in the early drainage group had infected necrosis and 44% had persistent organ failure (POF), whereas only 56.36% of patients in the delayed group had infected necrosis and 18% had POF. This imbalance in baseline disease severity may have overestimated the mortality difference associated with early drainage. In contrast, two RCTs [20, 21] found that no statistically significant differences in mortality between the two groups after balancing baseline confounders through randomization (13% vs. 10% and 12.3% vs. 10.8%, respectively). These results further support that drainage timing may not an independent determinant of outcomes.

The findings of this study reveal both consistencies and important discrepancies when compared with recent meta-analyses. The meta-analysis by Niu et al. [34], which included both open and minimally invasive interventions, supports delayed intervention to reduce mortality and pancreatic fistula risk. However, our study focuses specifically on minimally invasive drainage techniques and finds that although the early drainage group showed a trend toward higher mortality (RR = 1.38), the result was not stable, and no significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of hospital stay, ICU stay, or overall complications. This discrepancy may stem from the inclusion of open surgical data in Niu et al.’s analysis, while advances in minimally invasive techniques may have altered the risk-benefit profile [8]. Bomman et al. [35] specifically compared drainage timing for necrotic fluid collections (NFCs) and also reported higher mortality in the early group, but found no differences in complications or the need for subsequent debridement. By incorporating more recent studies and a larger sample size, our study further confirms that early drainage does not significantly increase complications or prolong hospital stay; meanwhile, more precise effect estimates (such as RR = 2.05 for subsequent debridement) suggest specific risks associated with early intervention. The meta-analysis by Kamal et al. [36] on endoscopic treatment of pancreatic necrotic collections (PNC) showed no significant differences in key outcomes including mortality between early and late treatment, providing safety support for early endoscopic drainage. Our study, by extending the analysis to all minimally invasive drainage methods and incorporating the latest RCTs, provides a comprehensive and current evidence synthesis. It confirms that delayed drainage maintains its advantage in reducing the need for invasive debridement, while also affirming that early drainage, when employed, does not appear to increase major complications or prolong hospitalization. The heterogeneity in mortality outcomes underscores the critical influence of patient selection.

The observed instability in mortality outcomes in the present study may largely reflect the concentrated effect of patient selection bias inherent in non-randomized designs [37]. In several included retrospective studies [30, 31], early drainage was mostly indicated for infected necrosis complicated by clinical deterioration (such as hyperthermia and refractory organ failure). In contrast, the delayed drainage group primarily received intervention for asymptomatic or stable symptomatic WON [27, 30]. This disease severity-driven timing selection inherently enriched the early drainage group with patients at high risk of death. For example, in the Indian cohort of Mallick et al. [31], 38% of the early group underwent intervention because of persistent organ failure, compared with only 16.2% in the delayed group. Rana et al. [30] further observed that the early drainage group had a significantly higher proportion of solid components in necrotic lesions (47.7% vs. 28.3%), with 44% of patients complicated by organ failure. Both factors are established independent predictors of mortality in NP [3]. In the subgroup analysis of endoscopic drainage, the early drainage group showed a significantly higher mortality rate (RR = 1.63, P = 0.03). This difference may be related to a higher proportion of high-risk patients, such as those with concurrent infection or organ failure, in the early drainage group compared to the delayed group. From a technical perspective, this discrepancy may also reflect the technical challenges associated with endoscopic procedures. Early acute necrotizing collections often lack a well-formed wall; therefore, endoscopic transgastric/transduodenal puncture during this phase is more prone to extraluminal leakage or gastrointestinal perforation [29]. For example, in the matched case-control study by Oblizajek et al. [38], although no perforations occurred in the early endoscopic drainage group, the proportion requiring necrosectomy reached 86%, significantly higher than the 30.4% in the delayed group. This difference in procedural complexity may have indirectly influenced the outcomes.

The proportion of patients requiring subsequent debridement was significantly higher in the early drainage group (RR = 2.05, P = 0.004), and this result should be interpreted in the context of the “step-up therapy principle” for NP [39]. The core goals of minimally invasive drainage are to “control infection, relieve compression, and promote liquefaction” rather than completely remove all necrotic tissue in a single procedure [6, 40]. Particularly in the early phase, necrotic lesions contain a high proportion of solid components [27, 30], making complete resolution difficult with drainage alone. Subsequent interventions such as endoscopic necrosectomy or video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement (VARD) are therefore often necessary. For example, in a propensity-matched study by Bomman et al. [8], 23.1% of patients in the early bimodal drainage (PCD + EUS-GD) group required subsequent procedures, but the final clinical success rate reached 92.3%, which was not significantly different from that of the delayed group (93.1%). Similarly, Boxhoorn et al. [20] showed that the proportion of patients needing necrosectomy was higher in the early group (51%) than in the delayed group (22%), yet there was no difference in the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) between the two groups (57 vs. 58, P = 0.90). These findings suggest that the higher debridement rate in the early drainage group does not indicate treatment failure, but rather a stepwise intervention guided by disease requirements. However, it unequivocally indicates a more protracted and procedurally intensive treatment pathway. In patients with infected necrosis or POF, early drainage can rapidly control sepsis and reverse organ failure [28], creating safer conditions for subsequent debridement [41]. In contrast, a strategy of delayed intervention allows a higher proportion of cases to be managed with a single, definitive drainage procedure, as necrotic lesions are more likely to be liquefied and encapsulated [42]. This difference underscores that the phase adaptability of treatment strategies carries a quantifiable procedural burden for early intervention, which is avoided in a significant proportion of patients by delaying drainage.

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidence of complications such as pancreatic fistula (RR = 1.22, P = 0.39) and hemorrhage (RR = 0.93, P = 0.69), which is directly attributed to advances in minimally invasive techniques in recent years. For percutaneous drainage, Mallick et al. [31] have confirmed that although early antegrade necrotic collection (ANC) drainage is associated with a slightly higher incidence of external pancreatic fistula (EPF) (24.4% vs. 34.2%, P = 0.034), 80% of these fistulas can be successfully closed with endoscopic stenting. For endoscopic drainage, the application of Lumen-Apposing Metal Stents (LAMS) has significantly reduced the risk of perforation. In a prospective study by Jagielski et al. [29], the perforation rate in the early group treated with LAMS was only 2.17%, showing no difference from the delayed group.

The absence of significant differences in hospital stay and ICU length of stay (both P > 0.05) challenges the traditional notion that early drainage prolongs hospitalization. A Chinese cohort study by Lu et al. [28] showed that although the median hospital stay was 40.28 days in the early group and 47.76 days in the delayed group (P = 0.211), 76.74% of patients in the early group avoided emergency open surgery through the step-up strategy of PCD combined with VARD. Zhang et al. [26] further demonstrated that among patients with POF, the reversal rate of organ failure in the early drainage group reached 72.2%, which was significantly higher than the 50% in the delayed group (P = 0.04). The advantage of “early disease control” may offset the potential prolongation of hospital stay caused by an increased number of procedures [21].

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the meta-analysis results for several outcomes, particularly the length of hospital and ICU stay, showed considerable statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 85%). This heterogeneity likely arises from three sources, which include clinical factors such as greater disease severity in early drainage groups, methodological factors like variations in techniques and definitions, and geographical factors including differences in healthcare systems and practices. Secondly, only five studies reported pancreatic endocrine dysfunction, and no studies focused on quality of life or the conversion rate to chronic pancreatitis. These outcomes are crucial for assessing patients’ long-term prognosis and should be incorporated in future research. Thirdly, some studies failed to specify the type of endoscopic stent, diameter of percutaneous drainage catheter, or specific methods of subsequent debridement, and these technical variations may have influenced the outcomes. Fourth, regarding the comparison of length of stay, the higher mortality rate observed in the early drainage group may have introduced bias, as deceased patients typically have a shorter final hospital stay. This competing risk could have attenuated the estimated difference in hospitalization duration between the groups, and the lack of survivor-only length of stay data in the included studies limits a definitive interpretation of this outcome. Finally, despite including recent randomized controlled trials in our analysis, the predominance of observational studies inherently carries risks of selection bias and confounding. Therefore, high‑quality multicenter randomized trials following uniform protocols are urgently needed to minimize these sources of heterogeneity and provide clearer evidence on the optimal timing of intervention.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis indicates that delayed drainage (> 4 weeks) is associated with a reduced need for subsequent invasive debridement procedures compared to early drainage. Although the difference in mortality was not stable, the data support the current guideline recommendation of delaying intervention when possible. Therefore, delayed drainage should remain the preferred strategy for most patients with NP. The decision for early drainage should be individualized and reserved for specific scenarios, such as infected necrosis with clinical deterioration, and must be made by a multidisciplinary team considering the patient’s infection status, organ function, and necrotic collection characteristics.
