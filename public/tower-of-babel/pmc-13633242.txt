Pharmacological modulation of GPR84 revealed by dual states structures and immune functional assays
Authors: Myung Kyung Choi, Dong Jin Park, Pankyung Kim, Hee Seong Choi, Sorin Myung, Youngki Yoo, Nienping Chang, Ga-Yeon Yoon, Hye Jin Kang, Sang-Jun Ha, Hyun-Soo Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
G-protein-coupled receptor 84 (GPR84) is an orphan class A GPCR selectively activated by medium chain fatty acids and highly expressed in immune cells, where it modulates pro-inflammatory signaling. The structural basis of GPR84 inactivation and antagonism has remained unclear, limiting the rational design of pathway-selective modulators despite its clinical relevance in metabolic inflammation and fibrotic diseases. Here, we report cryo-electron microscopy structures of human GPR84 in inactive and active states. The 3.5 Å inactive structure bound to the antagonist GLPG1205 reveals a lid-like conformation of extracellular loop 2 and an inward reorientation of Arg172, with the antagonist head group blocking the allosteric sodium-binding site. Molecular dynamics simulations further support these findings, identifying an aberrant TM5, TM6 lateral entry gate. By contrast, the 3.17 Å agonist ZQ-16, Gαi complex, shows a rearranged toggle switch and comparative analyses highlight extracellular loop 2 conformational plasticity. Immune functional assays in THP-1 cells demonstrated that ZQ-16 elicited GPR84-dependent activation and cytokine production, which were effectively abrogated by GLPG1205. Mutagenesis combined with functional assays validates key ligand interactions, providing a framework for the rational design of pathway selective GPR84 modulators.

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptor 84 (GPR84) is a class A GPCR predominantly expressed in immune cells such as monocytes, macrophages, and microglia1–4 and plays key roles in regulating metabolic and immune responses5,6. Unlike other human fatty-acid-sensing GPCRs, GPR40/120 (FFAR1 and FFAR4), which detect long chain fatty acids, and GPR41/43 (FFAR3 and FFAR2), which sense short chain fatty acids, GPR84 displays unique selectivity for medium chain fatty acids (MCFAs; C9–C14)1,7. Despite this specificity, its relatively low ligand affinity and the absence of a well-defined endogenous agonist leave it classified as an orphan receptor3.

GPR84 expression is strongly upregulated under inflammatory conditions, and its activation promotes macrophage polarization, cytokine release, and phagocytosis1,6. These properties link GPR84 to the pathophysiology of metabolic inflammation, fibrosis, and cancer8–15. Although canonical signaling proceeds through Gαi/o-mediated cAMP inhibition1, GPR84 can also engage β-arrestin, Gαq, and Gα12/13 in a ligand-dependent and cell-type-specific manner, suggesting that GPR84 possesses diverse downstream signaling capabilities2,5.

Functional studies have revealed both protective and pathogenic roles for GPR84. Loss-of-function models show impaired microglial activity in Alzheimer disease, hepatic lipid accumulation, atherosclerosis, abnormal eye development in Xenopus laevis, and regulation of murine skeletal muscle16–21. Conversely, GPR84 overexpression has been implicated in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), inflammatory bowel disease, ulcerative colitis, and neuropathic pain22–24. These bidirectional effects suggest that both agonists and antagonists may offer therapeutic value depending on disease context. However, this also highlights the potential for mechanism-based adverse effects, emphasizing the importance of aligning pharmacological strategies with disease-specific pathophysiology.

Pharmacological development has yielded selective small-molecule ligands that have deepened our understanding of GPR84 function. The synthetic agonist ZQ-16 (2-(hexylthio)-6-hydroxy-4(3H)-pyrimidinone) activates Gαi signaling and promotes pro-inflammatory macrophage responses25. By contrast, selective antagonist GLPG1205 (9-(2-cyclopropylethynyl)-2-[[(2S)-1,4-dioxan-2-yl]methoxy]-6,7-dihydropyrimido[6,1-a]isoquinolin-4-one), developed by Galapagos NV, exhibits antifibrotic activity but failed to meet primary end points in a phase II IPF trial26–30. Notably, GLPG1205’s defined scaffold provides a valuable chemical probe for structural studies. This underscores the need for high-resolution structural information particularly in the inactive state to enable rational design of pathway-selective modulators.

Here, we report cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of human GPR84 in dual conformations. The 3.17 Å active-state structure bound to selective agonist ZQ-16 and Gαiβγ heterotrimer captures the receptor in a fully active conformation under near-physiological conditions. We also report that the first inactive-state structure of GPR84 bound to GLPG1205 reveals the molecular basis for antagonist binding and receptor inactivation resolved at 3.5 Å. These structures offer key insights into ligand recognition and activation mechanisms, laying a structural bias for the rational design of GPR84 targeted therapeutics. These studies identified key ligand-contacting residues and demonstrated that distinct ligands drive specific signaling outcomes in macrophages, highlighting the biological relevance of ligand-specific GPR84 activation. Together, these findings define the molecular determinants of ligand recognition and signaling specificity, providing a structural framework to guide the rational design of selective modulators and to explore the receptor’s diverse physiological roles in disease contexts.

Methods

Construct design and protein expression

The human GPR84 gene (UniProt ID: Q9NQS5, residues 4–390) was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript. For structural stabilization in the inactive conformation, a thermostabilized apocytochrome b562RIL (BRIL) was inserted into the third intracellular loop (ICL3, residue 212–309, termed GPR84-BRIL). The heptapeptide sequence ERARSTL, derived from the sixth helix of the human adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR), was inserted between the C terminus of BRIL and GPR84 residue 309 to maintain helical continuity between TM helices 5 and 6. A tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site followed by a 10× His tag was appended to the C terminus of GPR84 for affinity purification and subsequent tag removal. For capturing GPR84 in its active conformation, the codon-optimized human GPR84 gene (residue 4–390) sequence was cloned into the pFastBac1 vector as a fusion with N-terminal signal peptide GP67-Flag tag (DYKDDDDK) and C-terminal TEV protease cleavage site between 10× His tag. Gαi1 was cloned into pFastBac1 vector, and 6× His-tagged human Gβ1 and human Gγ2 were cloned into pFastBac dual vector. The GPR84 and G protein baculovirus were generated and amplified to high titer P3 (>108 viral particles per ml). The expression in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells (2 × 106 cells per ml, ESF921 medium, Expression Systems) infected with GPR84-BRIL virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 for inactive structure and co-infected with GPR84-Gαi-Gβγ in a 1:2:1 ratio at an MOI of 5 for active structure. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 72 h post-infection, washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Purification of inactive-state GPR84–BRIL complex

Cell pellets were resuspended in 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl containing a protease inhibitor (PI) cocktail (Complete™, Roche) and lysed by Dounce homogenization. Following ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 1 h at 4 °C, the membrane pellets were resuspended in same buffer. To remove peripheral and loosely associated proteins, membranes were sequentially washed with buffers containing 1 M, 500 mM, and 300 mM NaCl, respectively, followed by homogenization and centrifugation at 100,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C after each wash. The washed membranes were solubilized for 2 h at 4 °C in solubilization buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1.5% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside (DDM), 0.3% (w/v) cholesterol hemisuccinate (CHS), and 15 μM GLPG1205 (Galapagos NV, Mechelen, Belgium). Insoluble debris was removed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 45 min at 4 °C. The clarified supernatant was incubated with TALON metal affinity resin (TaKaRa) at 4 °C for 2 h. The resin was washed with buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 0.05% (w/v) DDM, 0.005% (w/v) CHS, and 3 μM of the GLPG1205. Bound protein was eluted with buffer containing 300 mM imidazole, 0.03% (w/v) DDM, 0.006% (w/v) CHS, and 3 μM GLPG1205. The eluted protein was concentrated using a 100 kDa cut-off centrifugal concentrator (Amicon Ultra, Millipore) and desalted by buffer exchange into 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.03% (w/v) DDM, 0.006% (w/v) CHS, and 1 μM GLPG1205 using a PD Midi trap G-25 desalting column (Cytiva) to remove imidazole. The desalted protein was incubated with TEV protease at a mass ratio of 1:20 (TEV:protein) overnight at 4 °C to remove the C-terminal His tag. The sample was then subjected to a second round of TALON affinity purification of the cleaved target protein from uncleaved protein. The flow through containing tag-free GPR84 was collected, concentrated, and further purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in SEC buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.0 3% (w/v) DDM, 0.006% (w/v) CHS, and 1 μM GLPG1205. Peak fractions were collected and used for subsequent experiments. The anti-BRIL Fab and anti-Fab nanobodies (Nbs) were expressed in Escherichia coli and purified as previously reported31. The purified receptor was incubated with 1.5-fold and 2-fold molar excess of anti-BRIL Fab Nb and anti-Fab Nb, at 4 °C for 1 h. The mixture was concentrated to 500 µl and injected into an SEC column Superdex 200 10/300 GL equilibrated with the SEC buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.01% (w/v), LMNG 0.001% (w/v) CHS, and 1 μM GLPG1205. The peak fractions were concentrated to 12 mg/ml for cryo-EM studies.

Purification of active-state GPR84–Gαi protein complex

Cell pellets were resuspended in 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2 containing a PI (Complete, Roche). The suspension was further supplemented with 25 mU/ml Apyrase (NEB) before dounce homogenization. The membranes were solubilized by adding 1.5% (w/v) DDM (Anatrace), 0.15% (w/v) CHS (Anatrace), 13.5 μM ZQ-16 (Sigma-Aldrich) and rotating at 4 °C for 2 h. The solubilized fraction was isolated by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g at 4 °C for 40 min and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with TALON metal affinity resin (TaKaRa). The resin was washed with five column volumes of washing buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10 mM imidazole, 0.05% (w/v) DDM, 0.005% (w/v) CHS, and 2.7 μM of ZQ-16. The complex protein was then eluted with elution buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM imidazole, 0.05% (w/v) DDM, 0.005% (w/v) CHS, and 2.7 μM of ZQ-16 giving 3 min incubation for each elution. Elution sample was batch-bound to equilibrated M2 anti-Flag affinity resin, rotating at 4 °C for 1 h. The resin was packed into a glass column and washed with five column volumes of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.03% (w/v) DDM, 0.003% (w/v) CHS, and 2.7 μM of ZQ-16 before elution in the same buffer with 0.5 mg/ml Flag peptide also giving 3 min incubation for each elution. GPR84 complexes were then concentrated using a Vivaspin 20 centrifuge concentrator (Sigma-Aldrich) with a molecular cut-off of 100 kDa and further purified by SEC on Superose 6 increase 10/300 GL gel filtration column (Cytiva) with SEC buffer containing 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.03% (w/v) DDM, 0.003% (w/v) CHS, and 1.35 μM ZQ-16. Peak fractions were concentrated to 3 mg/ml for cryo-EM studies.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

The inactive-state, GPR84-BRIL, Fab, Nb, and GLPG1205 complex sample (4 µl) was applied to negatively glow-discharged holey carbon 300-mesh grids (Quantifoil R1.2/1.3) and flash-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) operated at 100% humidity and 4 °C (blot time: 4 s; blot force: 0; wait time: 5 s). Cryo-EM data were collected on a Titan Krios G4 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV and equipped with a Gatan K3 BioQuantum detector in electron-counting mode. Micrographs were acquired at a nominal magnification of 130,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.664 Å per pixel. A total of 8,575 movies were collected, each comprising 48 frames over a 3.85 s exposure, with a total dose of 69 electrons per Å2 and defocus values ranging from −0.7 μm to −2.0 μm.

For the active-state structure, 4 μl of the purified GPR84–ZQ-16–Gαiβγ complex was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300-mesh grids and vitrified under the same conditions (blot time: 4 s; blot force: 0; wait time: 0 s). Data collection was performed on a Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV and equipped with a Falcon IV direct electron detector in electron-counting mode. Images were recorded at a nominal magnification of 160,000×, yielding a calibrated pixel size of 0.7052 Å per pixel. A total of 16,820 movies were acquired with a total dose of 50 electrons per Å2 and a defocus range of −0.8 μm to −2.1 μm.

Cryo-EM image processing

Motion correction and contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation were performed using the Patch motion correction and Patch CTF algorithms in cryoSPARC (v4.2.1 for the GPR84-BRIL-Fab-Nb-GLPG1205 complex and v4.0.0 for the ZQ-16–GPR84–Gαiβγ complex)32. For the inactive complex, particles were initially picked using the blob picker, followed by multiple rounds of 2D classification to remove poorly defined particles. Ab initio reconstruction and non-uniform refinement resulted in a final map at a global resolution of 3.5 Å, as determined by the Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143 cut-off. For the active complex, particles were picked using the same blob picker algorithm, and after 2D classification, particles were selected for subsequent 3D analysis. Ab initio modeling and non-uniform refinement yielded a final reconstruction at a resolution of 3.17 Å, based on the FSC = 0.143 cut-off.

Model building and refinement

The AlphaFold3 (ref. 33) predicted that structures of GPR84-BRIL and BRIL-Fab-Nb (PDB: 6WW2) were used as the initial model for model rebuilding and refinement against the cryo-EM density map. The model was first docked into the map using UCSF Chimera34, followed by iterative manual adjustment and rebuilding in COOT. Real-space refinement was carried out using PHENIX software35. For the GPR84–Gαiβγ complex, the initial model (PDB: 8G05 and 8J19) was rigid body fitted into the cryo-EM density using Chimera and further refined in PHENIX. Geometric restraints for the ligands GLPG1205 and ZQ-16 were generated using the eLBOW module in PHENIX. Ligands were fitted based on clearly defined density features, and their stereochemistry was validated during refinement. The quality of the final models was assessed using the comprehensive model validation tools and MolProbity in PHENIX. Structural figures were generated using UCSF Chimera and PyMOL36. Model building and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assay

HEK293 cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin G, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. On day 1, cells were seeded into 6-well plates with white walls and clear bottoms at a density ranging from 350,000 to 400,000 cells per well in complete media. Before transfection, a DNA mixture was prepared by combining receptor plasmid and mini Gαi, β-arrestin1 (β-arr1), or β-arrestin2 (β-arr2) plasmids at a 1:6 molar ratio in a total volume of 250 μl Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium per well. Then, 2.1 μl of Transit 2020 transfection reagent was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The transfection complex was gently added to the plated HEK293 cells, which were subsequently incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The following day, cells were detached and reseeded into 96-well plates precoated with poly-L-lysine, at 200 μl per well in DMEM containing 1% dialyzed FBS, 1% penicillin G, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin. After overnight incubation, the medium was removed and the cells were washed once with 60 μl of assay buffer (1× HBSS, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4). For agonist assay, 30 μl of assay buffer (with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) was added to each well. Then, 30 μl of a 3× drug in assay buffer was added and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Afterward, 30 μl of 3× substrate coelenterazine h (final 5 μM) prepared in assay was added to each well and allowed to incubate for 10 min. For the antagonist assay, after addition of 30 μl assay buffer with 0.1% BSA, 30 μl of a 4× antagonist solution (GLPG1205 in assay buffer) was added and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Afterward, 30 μl of 4× EC80 of agonist solution (ZQ16 in assay buffer) was added and incubated for 10 min. Finally, 30 μl of 4× substrate was added to each well and allowed to incubate before measurement. BRET was measured at emission wavelengths 480 nm and 540 nm using a Tristar 5 plate reader, with six successive scans performed. The resulting data were processed and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Cell culture and macrophage polarization

Human THP-1 cells (ATCC, TIB-202) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Biowest) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, cat. no. 16000-044) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco). For macrophage differentiation, 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded in 12-well tissue-culture plate in 1 ml medium and cultured in RPMI-1640 containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 12.5 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich). After 24 h of PMA stimulation, the medium was replaced, and cells were allowed to rest for 24 h without any stimulation to promote differentiation. Cells were subsequently stimulated with 100 ng/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS, InvivoGen) by medium replacement. GPR84 agonist and antagonist were performed for 16 h of LPS stimulation. The GPR84 antagonist GLPG1205 was added 15 min before medium replacement with fresh medium containing both ZQ-16 and GLPG1205. For each treatment, the existing medium was replaced with fresh medium to avoid residual stimulation.

Staining for flow cytometry analysis and cytokine level assessment

Adherent cells were detached from tissue-culture plate using a cell scraper, washed with PBS containing 2% FBS and incubated with human Fc block (BD Bioscience, Cat# 564220) for 20 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were stained with antibodies against extracellular markers for 20 minutes at 4 °C in the presence of LIVE/DEAD kit (Invitrogen) to exclude dead cells from analysis. The following antibodies were used: CD11b (ICRF44), CD80 (2D10), CD86 (IT2.2), and PD-L1 (29E.2A3) (all from BioLegend, clones indicated in parentheses), CD14 (MφP9, BD Biosciences), and GPR84 (AGR-052-F, Alomone Labs, LTD). Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX LX (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo software. For cytokine level assessment process, culture supernatant was used. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, levels of five cytokines (IL-1β, CCL2, CCL3, CXCL8, and CXCL10) were measured using the LEGENDplex multiplex system (BioLegend). Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed with the software provided by the manufacturer.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted for human GPR84 in complex with the antagonist GLPG1205 (PDB: 9V6T, GPR84–bRIL–GLPG1205 complex). The BRIL fusion protein inserted into the ICL3 was removed, and 9 a, a linker corresponding to the native GPR84 sequence, was introduced to restore loop connectivity8. To construct the simulation system, the protein ligand complex was embedded in a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) bilayer within a 105 × 105 × 104 Å³ simulation box using CHARMM-GUI37. The system was solvated with 27,729 TIP3P water molecules, and 150 mM NaCl was added to neutralize the system and approximate physiological ionic strength. The final composition included 288 POPC molecules and the protein–ligand complex. MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2025.1 (ref. 38) with the CHARMM36 (refs. 39–41) force field applied to proteins, lipids, and ions. The ligand topology was generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF)42. Following energy minimization with the steepest descent algorithm, the system was equilibrated in the NVT (number of particles, volume and temperature) ensemble for 2 ns to allow relaxation of solvent and lipid components, followed by NPT (number of particles, pressure and temperature) equilibration in which positional restraints were progressively released in stages from lipids and solvent, protein side chains, the ligand, and finally the protein backbone (2.5 ns per stage). After equilibration, production simulations were carried out for 2 μs with a 2 fs time step. Periodic boundary conditions were applied throughout, and the system was maintained at 310 K and 1 bar. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the particle mesh Ewald43 method using a 12 Å cut-off, and van der Waals interactions were smoothly switched to zero between 10 Å and 12 Å. All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained with the LINCS algorithm44, enabling the 2 fs integration time step. Each system was simulated in triplicate with different initial velocities. Trajectory and structural data collected during the simulations were analyzed using VMD45.

Cell surface expression assay

Cell surface expression of wild-type (WT) and mutant GPR84 constructs was assessed using an ELISA-based chemiluminescence assay. HEK293 cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding FLAG-tagged receptor variants using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h post-transfection, cells were seeded into 384-well white plates (20,000 cells per well). Cells were fixed with 20 μl per well of 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and washed twice with 40 μl per well of PBS. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubation with 20 μl per well of 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The wells were then incubated with 20 μl per well of HRP-conjugated mouse monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. A8592), diluted 1:10,000 in 5% BSA/PBS, for 1 h at room temperature. After five washes with 80 μl per well of PBS, 20 μl per well of SuperSignal™ ELISA Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added. The luminescence signal was measured immediately at 425 nm using a CLARIOstar Plus multilabel plate reader (BMG LABTECH) within 5 min after substrate addition. To assess relative surface expression, signal intensities from each mutant were normalized to those from WT GPR84, which was included on every assay plate. All data were processed and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. For selected mutants exhibiting significantly reduced surface expression compared with WT (P < 0.05), transfection conditions were adjusted by increasing the amount of plasmid DNA (200–300 ng per well) to achieve more comparable expression levels before functional assays.

Results

Overall structure of the GLPG1205–GPR84 complex

To obtain a stable inactive conformation of GPR84, we explored multiple construct engineering strategies and extensive biochemical screening. We adopted a BRIL fusion strategy, systematically evaluating insertion sites within ICL3 as fiducial marker. Incorporation of a short helix segment (ERARSTLQK) derived from the A2A adenosine receptor (A2aAR) downstream of BRIL markedly enhanced protein stability31,46. We purified the BRIL-fused GPR84 and incubated it with an anti-BRIL Fab and anti-Fab Nbs46, which served as fiducial markers (Supplementary Fig. 1a,d). Using this construct, we determined the cryo-EM structure of human GPR84 in complex with the antagonist GLPG1205 at an overall resolution of 3.5 Å (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1c,e). Electron density maps showed that the Fab and Nb adopt a more membrane proximal orientation (Supplementary Fig. 1d), likely due to a kink at the BRIL–TM5 junction. The final model includes residues N terminus Asn8 (Asn8NT)–Leu3858.58 (Ballesteros–Weinstein numbering in superscript47) of GPR84 and adopts the canonical class A GPCR fold comprising 7TM (Supplementary Fig. 1f). A BRIL domain (residues Ala1–Gly107, chain B) was clearly resolved in the density together with the variable region of the bound Fab. Although the linker between TM6 and BRIL was disordered, BRIL formed a continuous helix with TM5 (Fig. 1a). Consistent with this, BRET-based functional assays confirmed that GLPG1205 completely abolishes GPR84-mediated Gαi signaling, with an EC50 of 0.163 nM, supporting the assignment of this conformation as an inactive state (Supplementary Figs. 3b and 10a and Supplementary Table 2).

Inactive GPR84 structure has the lid-like conformation of the second extracellular loop (ECL2), which adopts a β-strand stabilized fold that occludes the orthosteric-binding pocket (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3a). This configuration is stabilized by two disulfide bonds: between TM3 Cys933.25 and Cys168 of ECL2 (Cys168ECL2), Cys11NT and Cys166ECL2 (Fig. 1c). Importantly, BRET assays demonstrated that Cys166 ECL2A and Cys168ECL2A mutations within ECL2 have a key role in maintaining structural stabilization of GPR84 (Supplementary Figs. 3b and 10a and Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, Cys166ECL2A exhibited a partial loss, whereas Cys168ECL2A resulted in a loss-of-function phenotype.

Overall structure of the ZQ-16–GPR84–Gαiβγ complex and ligand binding site

To elucidate the activation mechanism of GPR84, we determined the cryo-EM structure of GPR84 in complex with the agonist ZQ-16 and Gαiβγ at a resolution of 3.17 Å (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2a,c,e). Cryo-EM density enabled model building of GPR84 from Ser10NT to Ala3808.53, including that the ligand-binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 2f) except ICL3 (Arg218ICL3–Ser3126.28) was disordered. Notably, TM5 extends beyond the typical helical length observed in class A GPCRs. Within the G protein heterotrimer, the β subunit is well resolved, whereas the γ subunit and the α-helical domain of Gαi are partially disordered (Supplementary Fig. 2b,d).

In GPR84 structure, ZQ-16 occupies the orthosteric pocket in an amphipathic configuration (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Its hydrophobic hexyl tail interacts with the hydrophobic cleft formed by TM3 to TM6, whereas the polar pyrimidine head group engages in hydrogen bonding with ECL2 and TM7 (Fig. 2a, b). Notably, the ECL2 adopts a lid-like conformation in previous GPR84 structures and our inactive structure8,48. In the ZQ-16 bound structure, ECL2 adopts a disordered loop configuration instead of the β sheet conformation (Fig. 1d). Structural rearrangement appears to be driven by the slightly deeper insertion of ZQ-16 into the orthosteric pocket and interacting ECL2 residues repositioned (Fig. 2d). The Phe170ECL2, which forms a cation–π interaction with Arg172ECL2, is also displaced downward, further contributing to the disruption of the β sheet architecture. The 4,6-hydroxyl group of pyrimidine ring in ZQ-16 forms hydrogen bonds with polar residues Arg172ECL2, Ser169ECL2, and Trp3607.43 and alanine substitutions of these residues substantially impaired the efficacy of ZQ-16 (Fig. 2b, e). Consistent with this interaction network, Ser169 ECL2A and Arg172 ECL2A resulted in an inactive phenotype, whereas Arg172 ECL2K led to a loss-of-function phenotype. The pyrimidine ring is also in proximity with Val165ECL2, Thr167ECL2, Ala3567.39, Leu1003.32, and Phe3356.51, supporting a network of van der Waals interactions (Fig. 2e, f). In line with this, Val165ECL2A showed reduced potency, whereas mutations at Thr167ECL2 and Phe3356.51A resulted in a loss-of-function phenotype. In addition, substitution of Ala3567.39 with bulkier or charged residues (Ala3567.39F and Ala3567.39D) rendered the receptor inactive, indicating that steric and chemical compatibility at this position are critical for ligand accommodation. The thioether linker of ZQ-16 lies in close proximity to Leu1003.32, enabling hydrophobic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4e) and hydrogen bond with Asn1043.36 and Thr3597.42 (Fig. 2b). Consistently, Asn1043.36A resulted in an inactive phenotype, whereas Thr3597.42A exhibited a partial loss-of-function phenotype (Fig. 2f). Moreover, Trp3607.43A and Phe170ECL2A mutations led to loss-of-function phenotypes.

Structural alignment of previously reported active-state GPR84 structures 6OAU (PDB: 8G05, 6-n-octylaminouracil)8 and LY237 (PDB: 8J19, 6-nonylpyridine-2,4-diol)48 and TM domains reveal minimal conformational deviation, with Cα RMSD of 0.56 Å and 0.71 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4a,c and Supplementary Fig. 13). Despite the high degree of structural conservation, notable differences were observed in the ECL2 and the ligand binding site (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). Despite being two carbon shorter than the tail of 6OAU, the thioether moiety in ZQ-16 imparts conformational flexibility, enabling it to occupy a similar hydrophobic subpocket (Supplementary Fig. 4a,e). Key contacts include Phe1013.33, Tyr3326.48, Ile1083.40, Tyr1865.46, Phe1524.57, Leu3366.52, and Phe3356.51, forming a tightly packed, nonpolar and hydrophobic environment (Fig. 2c). Although Leu1825.42 and Met1835.43 are positioned at a relatively longer distance, they contribute to form hydrophobic pocket. GPR84 displays marked sensitivity to the length of the tail of the ligand, with optimal activation observed for 9–12 carbons1,2,7. ZQ-16 structure also supports that this length selectivity arises from a hydrophobic blocking wall formed by Phe1524.57, Leu1825.42, and Tyr1865.46 on TM4,5 (Supplementary Fig. 4e), which limits the depth of tail insertion48. Mutations of most ZQ-16 interacting residues and the blocking-wall residues markedly reduced GPR84 activity (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Figs. 4g,h and 10a, and Supplementary Table 2) in BRET assay. Consistent with their roles, Leu1825.42A, Tyr1865.46A, and Met1835.43A exhibited reduced potency, whereas Phe1524.57A showed increased potency. In addition, Tyr692.53A and Phe1013.33A resulted in partial loss, whereas Tyr3326.48A, Ile1083.40A, and Leu1003.32A mutations led to inactive or loss of function phenotype.

Antagonist GLPG1205 ligand-binding site

Structurally, GLPG1205 consists of three distinct moieties: a central tricyclic core (a rigid, fused three-ring core), a dioxane head group, and a cyclopropylethynyl tail (Fig. 1a). The cryo-EM density of the GLPG1205–GPR84 complex allowed precise modeling of the central scaffold and dioxane ring. Although the density for the tail was relatively weak, its rigid, non-rotatable linkage to the central core enabled confident placement of its conformation. The ligand adopts a deeply and broadly embedded pose, spanning TM2, TM3, TM5, TM6, TM7, and ECL2, and engages an extensive network of interactions (Fig. 3a). Notably, GLPG1205 exhibits dioxane moiety inserted toward the intracellular region, whereas the tail projects toward the extracellular surface in different direction observed in agonists. The central tricyclic core is embedded within a cavity formed by TM2, TM3, TM6, and TM7 (Fig. 3b). The rigid scaffold forms stabilizing hydrophobic contacts with Leu1003.32, Val165ECL2, and Ala3567.39, while being sandwiched between Phe1013.33 and Phe3356.51 through aromatic-stacking interaction (Fig. 3c). These structural features are supported by BRET assays, as mutations Phe3356.51A, Ala3567.39F, Ala3567.39D, Leu1003.32A, Phe1013.33A, and Val165ECL2A impaired GLPG1205-mediated inhibition of GPR84 signaling (Fig. 3e). Consistent with their roles in stabilizing the antagonist, substitutions at these positions produced loss-of-function phenotypes, whereas Phe1013.33A caused only a partial loss of function. Notably, both Ala356F and Ala356D yielded inactive phenotypes. A key hydrogen bond is formed between the indole NH of Trp3607.43 and the lactam carbonyl of the central scaffold, anchoring its orientation, and the Trp3607.43A mutation diminished GLPG1205-mediated GPR84 inhibition (Fig. 3e). In addition, mutations at Met1835.43 and Asn1043.36 also resulted in inactive or loss of function phenotypes. In GPR84, the Asn1043.36–Tyr3326.48 functions as a non-canonical dual toggle switch48, substituting for the classical Trp6.48 transmission switch found in class A GPCRs49. In the inactive-state structure, Asn1043.36 instead forms a hydrogen bond with the ester linkage of the ligand, whereas Tyr3326.48 remains in an upward conformation (Fig. 3d, f). As a result, the toggle switch hydrogen bond is disengaged, reflecting a conformationally stabilized inactive state. Notably, additional mutations in residues proximal to the ligand binding site, including Phe170ECL2A, Tyr692.63A, and Arg172ECL2A, also resulted in loss-of-function or partial loss-of-function phenotypes, whereas Arg172 ECL2K failed to rescue activity (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Fig. 10a, and Supplementary Table 2).

Arginine guidance and hydrophobic gate wall at the ligand entry site

At the distal end, the cyclopropylethynyl tail of GLPG1205 extends into a lateral pocket formed by TM5, TM6, and ECL2 (Fig. 3a). Notably, Arg172ECL2, which adopts an inward facing conformation in the active state, undergoes a distinct rotameric shift to interact with the cyclopropyl moiety and engages in van der Waals contacts (Figs. 2b, 3c, and 4b and Supplementary Fig. 12a,b). The extracellular region of GPR84 displays a highly positive charged surface, with arginine residues including Arg172ECL2 (Fig. 4a, b). This electropositive surface may function as a “ligand fishing” that captures negatively charged ligands. Given that GPR84 functions as an MCFA receptor, free fatty acids carry a negative charge owing to their carboxylate head groups. These arginine residues are likely to facilitate electrostatic attraction and promote ligand pre-binding orientation near the pocket (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 5a). To experimentally evaluate this proposed arginine-cluster-mediated ligand-fishing mechanism, we performed alanine substitutions of positively charged residues in the extracellular region, followed by BRET assays using ZQ-16, 6OAU, and GLPG1205 (Figs. 2e and 3f, Supplementary Figs. 5b–e and 10a, and Supplementary Table 2). Alanine mutations at Arg201.32, Arg87ECL1, Arg943.26, Arg172ECL2, Arg1765.36, and Arg349ECL3 resulted in a pronounced loss-of-function or inactive phenotype across all three ligands, whereas Arg174 ECL2 and Arg344ECL3 showed minimal effects. Notably, the loss of signaling was consistently observed across chemically distinct ligands, suggesting that these extracellular arginine residues contribute to a shared mechanism of ligand recognition or initial capture. Collectively, these results support a model in which the arginine-enriched extracellular surface functions as an electrostatic guidance platform that facilitates ligand recruitment before engagement with the orthosteric pocket.

The lipophilic tail also engages hydrophobic interactions with Met1835.43, Gly1845.44, Phe1875.47, Leu3366.52, and Ile3406.56 (Fig. 4c). Although Ala3436.59, Val1885.48, and Ile3336.49 do not directly interact with antagonist, they constitute a lipophilic wall. Lateral ligand entry through TM interfaces has been reported in various lipid-sensing GPCRs50–54. Among class A GPCRs, the most frequently observed lateral ligand entry sites are located between TM1 and TM7 exemplified by cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1) and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1) and between TM3 and TM4, as seen in FFAR1 and corticotropin-releasing factor receptor 1 (CRF₁R). In GPR84, our structure reveals a distinct lateral opening between TM5 and TM6 that is absent in the active-state conformation, suggesting a potential ligand entry pathway (Fig. 4c). Alanine substitutions at these hydrophobic gate residues markedly impaired the GPR84 signaling in the BRET assay (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 10a, and Supplementary Table 2). For GLPG1205, mutations at Leu3366.52A, Thr1805.40A, Phe1875.47A, Ile3406.56A, and Gly1845.44W resulted in loss-of-function phenotypes. By contrast, the effects of these mutations were less pronounced for ZQ-16, with Leu3366.52A and Ile3406.56A showing partial loss of function, Thr180A displaying WT-like activity, and Gly1845.44W exhibiting reduced potency. Notably, Phe1875.47A resulted in increased potency.

Notably, Phe3356.51 induces a steric clash with both the core and the tail of GLPG1205, thereby preventing the TM5–TM6 region from adopting the more open conformation compared with the AlphaFold-predicted model (Supplementary Fig. 3c,d). Consequently, the lateral entry site is more clearly defined in our experimental structure. This TM5–TM6 vestibule, supported by previous MD simulations, may serve as a selective gateway for lipophilic ligand50. Our MD simulations revealed that GLPG1205 migrates from the lateral gate into the orthosteric binding site, indicating that this lateral gate serves as the ligand entry pathway (Supplementary Fig. 6c).

The TM5–TM6 interface hydrophobic residues Met1835.43, Val1885.48, and Leu1915.51 on TM5 and Ala3296.45, Ile3336.49, Leu3366.52, Ile3406.56, and Ala3436.59 on TM6 form a hydrophobic “gateway” that facilitates lateral ligand access (Fig. 4c). Alanine substitutions significantly impaired GPR84 activation and inhibition in the BRET assay (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 10a, and Supplementary Table 2). Previously reported MD simulations of lipid entry pathways in rhodopsin GPCR are regulated by phenylalanine residues that act as gatekeepers; Phe2085.43, Phe2736.56, and Phe2766.59 obstruct lipid insertion by forming a tightly packed aromatic barrier50. In GPR84, analogous positions are occupied by Met1835.43, Ile3406.56, and Ala3436.59, suggesting a gating mechanism that relies on hydrophobic interactions. To further assess whether this hydrophobic gateway is conserved across different agonists, we performed additional mutagenesis analysis using 6OAU (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Mutations at Leu3366.52A and Thr1805.40A exhibited effects comparable to those observed with ZQ-16, whereas Gly1845.44W and Ile3406.56A showed a partial loss-of-function phenotype in response to 6OAU relative to ZQ-16. These results suggest that although the overall architecture of the ligand entry pathway is conserved, certain residues contribute in a ligand-dependent manner, supporting a shared but adaptable mechanism of ligand access.

Capping the sodium-binding pocket by GLPG1205

The dioxane head group of GLPG1205 is deeply inserted into the TM cavity, adjacent to TM2 and TM3 (Fig. 3a, d). Structural alignment with sodium-bound GPCR structures, such as A2aAR (PDB: 4EIY), beta1 adrenoceptor (β₁AR, PDB: 4BVN), and δ-opioid receptor (δOR, PDB: 4N6H), reveals that the network maintains conserved inter-residue distances that support sodium ion coordination (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Although the equivalent residues Asp662.50, Asn3627.45, and Ser1073.39 are conserved, Asn3627.45 is markedly displaced upward, resulting in significantly extended distances to Asp662.50 and Ser1073.39, incompatible with stable ion binding. The resulting interaction footprint defines key pharmacophoric features required for antagonistic activity.

Structural differences between inactive and active states

To elucidate the activation mechanism of GPR84, we performed a comparative analysis of the inactive and active states. Superimposition of the TM helices yields a Cα RMSD of 2.25 Å, with conformational changes observed in TM5, TM6, and TM7, hallmarks of class A GPCR activation49,58 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 8a).

In the active-state structure, ZQ-16 adopts the lowest binding pose, driving Tyr3326.48 into a lower position and establishing the closest Tyr3326.48– Asn3627.45 interaction (Figs. 2d and 5b). At the same time, thioether linker forms a polar interaction with Asn1043.36, disrupting the Asn1043.36–Tyr3326.48 hydrogen bond (Fig. 5b). By contrast, 6OAU maintains moderate interactions with Asn1043.36 and Tyr3326.48, whereas LY237 fails to contact Asn1043.36 (refs. 8,48). This displacement induces a downward swing of Tyr3326.48 in the ZQ-16-bound structure, which sterically displaces Phe3286.44 outward and reorients Ile1083.40 of the conserved PIF motif49,58 (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 9b). These conformational rearrangements are accompanied by an inward movement of TM5 and outward displacement of TM6, forming a cytoplasmic cavity compatible with G protein engagement (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Simultaneously, Tyr3326.48 establishes a new hydrogen bond with Asn3627.45, promoting the inward displacement of TM7 (Fig. 5d). This reorganization is further supported by reduced distances between Asn3627.45 and Ser1073.39, as well as between Asn3667.49 and Ser1073.39 and Asn3627.45. Meanwhile, Asn3667.49 shifts away from Asp662.50, disrupting the canonical hydrogen bond and contributing to a collapse of the sodium-binding pocket55–57. Unlike typical class A GPCRs, in which Asn7.45 commonly forms a hydrogen bond with Asp2.50, no such interaction is observed in either of the GPR84 states. Subsequently, Asn3667.49 forms a new hydrogen bond with Tyr3707.53, completing the NPxxY motif and facilitating inward movement of TM749. This rearrangement is accompanied by inward shifts of Pro3677.50 and Tyr3707.53. The repositioned Tyr3707.53 also enables new polar contacts with Arg1183.50 from the GRY motif (Gly1173.49, Arg1183.50, and Tyr1193.51), a non-canonical variant of the class A DRY motif (Fig. 5e).

TM1–TM3 shift inward at the extracellular side and TM8 are repositioned closer to TM1, whereas TM4 remains largely unchanged (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 8a). ICL2 becomes more upright from a relaxed helical form to engage Gαi, and ICL1 transitions from a flexible loop upon activation. Overall, the distinct reconfiguration observed in the structure provides a mechanistic framework for understanding GPR84 activation at the atomic level.

Comparison of diverse agonists modulate GPR84 activation

Consistent with the canonical class A GPCRs, the α5 helix of Gαi inserts into the cytoplasmic cavity of GPR84 and interacts with TM2, TM3, TM5, and TM6, and is displaced upward by 1.3 Å toward the receptor core, forming an extensive hydrophobic interface with the G protein49 (Supplementary Fig. 9a).

Specifically, Gαi residues Ile344, Leu348, Cys351, Leu353, and Phe354 engage hydrophobic side chains on TM3, TM5, and TM6 of GPR84 (Supplementary Fig. 9c). In contrast to previous GPR84–Gαi complexes, in which Phe354 of Gαi participates in aromatic stacking with Phe3146.30, our structure reveals that Phe3146.30 is rotated outward and does not engage in this interaction48 (Supplementary Fig. 9d,c). Additionally, Cys351 α5 helix of Gαi forms a hydrogen bond with Arg1183.50 of DRY motif (Supplementary Fig. 9e). The elongated TM5 allows Tyr2155.75 to engage in π–π interactions with Phe334 and His322 of Gαi and hydrogen bond Gln333 of Gαi (Supplementary Fig. 9f). Notably, a unique salt bridge between Lys345 of Gαi and Glu3136.29 is observed (Supplementary Fig. 9h). Upon ZQ-16 binding, TM8 is displaced further toward TM1, leading to a hydrogen bond between Gln3768.49 and Asp350 of Gαi (Supplementary Fig. 9g). This also disrupts conserved Gβ–receptor interactions. Specifically, the salt bridge between Asp312 of Gβ and Lys50ICL1/Arg3878.60 of GPR84, as well as the cation–π interaction between Phe292 of Gβ and Lys3868.59, are no longer observed.

The αN terminus of Gαi positioned closer to the receptor compared with other GPR84-active structure (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Unlike most class A GPCRs, which typically feature a hydrophobic residue at position 34.51, GPR84 harbors an Lys126ICL2 at this site, mediating interactions with the αN helix of Gαi8,48 (Supplementary Fig. 9i). However, in the ZQ-16-bound structure, no direct contact is observed between Lys126ICL2 and Asp93 of Gαi. Phe128ICL2 and Phe132ICL2 engage in tight packing interactions with TM2–TM4, including Phe552.39, Leu1133.45, Leu1163.48, Leu1203.52, Leu1213.53, and Ile1374.42 (Supplementary Fig. 9j). This network is maintained across all GPR84 agonist complexes and likely supports the upward displacement of ICL2 (Supplementary Fig. 8a). These comparative analyses underscore that individual agonists may differentially stabilize active conformations of GPR84.

Macrophage polarization and cytokine analysis

LPS induces transcription of multiple pro-inflammatory genes, including cytokines and receptors involved in innate immune responses. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that GPR84 expression is upregulated in monocytes and macrophages upon LPS stimulation59. Although mean fluorescence intensity value showed some increment, the GPR84 expression was not substantial after 24 h stimulation of LPS. Given that 6OAU showed a plateau in the pro-phagocytic ability of BMDMs at higher concentrations8 and that ZQ-16 binds more strongly to GPR84 than 6OAU, we hypothesized that a lower concentration of ZQ-16 would be sufficient to induce activation in PMA-treated THP-1 cells. In addition to the expression of activation markers, ZQ-16 enhanced the functional capacity of macrophages to produce and secrete inflammatory cytokines. In culture supernatants, ZQ-16 stimulation increased the levels of IL-1β, CCL2, CCL3, and CXCL10 when GPR84 expression had been upregulated by LPS. However, similar to its effect on CD86 expression, GLPG1205 markedly reduced the ZQ-16-induced production of these cytokines (Fig. 6e).

Discussion

In this study, we determined cryo-EM structures of GPR84 in its inactive and active states, complemented by functional assays that collectively establish the molecular basis of receptor modulation. The inactive-state structure reveals how GLPG1205 stabilizes GPR84: its dioxane head group directly caps the canonical sodium-binding site formed by Asp2.50, Ser3.39, and Asn7.45. In most class A GPCRs, sodium binding stabilizes the inactive state and modulates receptor activation by bridging TM helixes55,56. Structures of A₂AAR, β₁AR, and δOR have established sodium coordination as a conserved feature among class A GPCRs 55–57. By displacing Asn7.45 and disrupting the hydrogen bond network, GLPG1205 abolishes sodium coordination. This mechanism may provide a framework for the rational design of GPR84 antagonists that target allosteric sites. By contrast, the ZQ-16-bound structure highlights a noncanonical activation pathway involving a distinctive toggle switch. Most class A GPCRs use Trp6.48 as the primary transmission switch49,58; however, in GPR84, this role is fulfilled by a dual toggle comprising Asn1043.36 and Tyr3326.48. ZQ-16 disrupts the Asn1043.36–Tyr3326.48 hydrogen bond and repositions Tyr3326.48 downward, initiating outward displacement of TM6 and inward movement of TM7. This cascade propagates to the PIF, NPxxY, and GRY motif, reorganizing the cytoplasmic cavity for G protein engagement. A notable feature observed across our structures is the conformational plasticity of ECL2. In the GLPG1205-bound structure, ECL2 adopts a rigid, β-strand-stabilized lid, consistent with previously reported GPR84 structures50–52. Conversely, in the ZQ-16 complex, ECL2 becomes disordered. This structural plasticity is consistent with earlier MD simulations proposing that ECL2 facilitates ligand entry8,48. Our findings emphasize that ECL2 conformational states are not static but dynamically coupled to ligand entry and receptor activation. A similar electrostatic guidance mechanism has been observed in chemokine receptors such as CXCR4, in which the negatively charged ligand CXCL12 is initially captured by positively charged extracellular regions before engaging the orthosteric pocket, consistent with a two-step binding mechanism61.

Our inactive state structure also reveals a lateral opening between TM5 and TM6 that becomes occluded in the active conformation. TM5–TM6 hydrophobic residues such as Met1835.43, Leu3366.52, and Ile3406.56, which collectively form a “hydrophobic gateway” and functional mutagenesis, demonstrated that disrupting these residues abolished antagonist activity, validating the functional relevance of this portal. In addition, membrane-embedded ligand entry through a lateral opening between TM5 and TM6 has been proposed in rhodopsin, in which MD simulations revealed that phenylalanine residues act as gatekeepers by forming a tightly packed aromatic barrier50. Interestingly, the GLPG1205-bound inactive structure revealed extra density in the region between TM5 and TM6 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). MD simulations indicated potential lipid interactions at this site, suggesting that it could accommodate a hydrophobic moiety POPC (Supplementary Fig. 6b,c). To further explore this possibility, we tested candidate molecules by fitting them into the electron density map. Given the lipid composition of sf9 insect cells, long chain fatty acids, and cholesterol hemisuccinate (CHS)50, which were added during purification, these components were plausible candidates (Supplementary Fig. 11b,c). However, in the human physiological context, it is conceivable that MCFAs, the endogenous ligands of GPR84, may enter the receptor via this lateral TM5–TM6 pathway (Supplementary Fig. 11c).

Comparisons across agonists reveal that differences in ligand tail length and head group chemistry translate into distinct stabilization of active conformations. ZQ-16 engages deeply with toggle switch residues, correlating with its enhanced ability to promote cytokine production in macrophages. Functional validation in THP-1 macrophages demonstrated that ZQ-16 robustly induces CD86 expression and cytokine secretion, whereas GLPG1205 abrogates these effects. These results link structural observations to immune phenotypes and provide mechanistic support for pharmacological manipulation of GPR84 in inflammatory settings. Consistent with this interpretation, the structural features identified in this study — including the conformational plasticity of ECL2, the electropositive extracellular surface, and the lateral opening between TM5 and TM6 — suggest mechanisms for ligand capture and membrane-embedded entry under altered lipid conditions. Dysregulated GPR84 activation has been implicated in diseases such as IPF and inflammatory bowel disease, both of which involve macrophage-mediated inflammation. Because GPR84 appears to be regulated primarily through receptor expression and ligand-dependent activation rather than pathogenic mutations, these structural insights provide a framework for the rational design of antagonists aimed at suppressing excessive receptor activation. GPR84 has been implicated in both protective and pathogenic roles across neurodegeneration, fibrosis, and metabolic disorders16–24. Our data explain how antagonists such as GLPG1205 suppress pro-inflammatory macrophage responses, consistent with its antifibrotic activity in preclinical models26–30. Conversely, agonists may amplify immune responses, potentially offering therapeutic opportunities in contexts in which macrophage activation is beneficial, such as infection or neurodegenerative disease16–19. These dual roles highlight the importance of pathway selective modulators tailored to disease context.

Many orphan GPCRs exhibit low affinity ligand interactions and lack clear endogenous agonists, complicating pharmacological targeting48. By characterizing the structural determinants of both inactivation and activation, our study establishes design principles for selective modulation. In addition, MD simulations revealed a movement of GLPG1205 within the binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c), providing a potential explanation for its limited efficacy in IPF trials and underscoring the need to develop improved antagonist scaffolds. Notably, the sodium-binding pocket represents a potential pharmacophore that can be occluded by synthetic antagonists, offering an underexplored avenue for selective drug development. Together, these findings provide structural insights that may enable the rational design of pathway-selective GPR84 modulators. Although comprehensive, this study has several limitations, including the absence of structures bound to endogenous ligands and reliance on synthetic agonists and antagonists. Moreover, the functional assays were performed exclusively in THP-1-derived macrophages, and the diversity of GPR84 signaling across cell types remains to be fully characterized. Future studies should therefore resolve structures of GPR84 bound to endogenous MCFA ligands, investigate ligand-entry pathways in greater detail, and assess signaling diversity — including β-arrestin recruitment — to clarify the therapeutic potential of GPR84 modulation.
