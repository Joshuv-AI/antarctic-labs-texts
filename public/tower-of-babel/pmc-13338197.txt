Facial shape and self-perceived personality traits in young adults
Authors: Nikolaos Gkantidis, Eleni Tsikouriadou, Demetrios Halazonetis, Carlalberta Verna, Georgios Kanavakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigated the association between facial shape and self-perceived personality and psychosocial traits. The study population consisted of 613 young adults (399 females and 214 males) aged 21–35 years, with similar socioeconomic and academic backgrounds. Facial shape was studied with three-dimensional photographs, that were digitized semi-automatically using special software. Facial shape was described with 1021 landmarks and shape variation was studied with geometric morphometric methods. Personality and psychosocial traits were studied using standardized and well-validated questionnaires, that were completed by all participants on the same visit that their facial image was acquired. Associations were studied with multivariate regression models, using principal components of shape as dependent variables and questionnaire scores and age as predictors. Due to the presence of sex dimorphism in facial shape, all analyses were performed separately in males and females. The results showed a significant association between self-perceived self-esteem and facial shape in females (η2 = 0.089; P-value = 0.003) but not in males (η2 = 0.056; P-value = 0.782). No other personality of psychosocial traits had a significant effect on facial shape. Introduction

Visual contact initiates the majority of human relationships and thus plays an important role in creating perceptions based on appearance. In the past, information regarding facial appearance was falsely used to make decisions about the potential for criminal activity, quality of character and social intentions of an individual, leading to the development of research fields such as “physiognomy”1–3. The scientific value of these fields has long been discredited; however, studies of the human face continue to attract attention in various fields including psychological and social sciences, medicine and dentistry4–7. Visual observation of a human face provides information about basic demographic characteristics such as age, sex and racial background, but also creates perceptions regarding physical and mental health, mood, personality and attractiveness8–11.

First impressions during human interaction are formed within 100ms and show little change with prolonged observation12. These impressions—trustworthiness, competence, and intelligence—are derived solely from facial appearance13–15. The ability to form strong first impressions is present even in neonates, suggesting it does not rely on higher-order cognitive functions16,17. Nevertheless, such impressions influence key judgments about individuals, including perceived mate quality, political leadership potential, task competence, and professional suitability6,14,18.

Current evidence suggests an association between facial characteristics and certain personality traits. Facial width-to-height ratio has been associated with dominance19 or aggressiveness20, smile attractiveness with neuroticism and self-esteem21 and facial shape with agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion22. Nevertheless, available data often rely on linear and angular measurements, oversimplifying the original morphology, or on heterogeneous populations that introduce uncontrolled confounding factors. Furthermore, the use of facial width-to-height ratio has been scrutinized because of the inherent limitations related to muscular function and expression23.

Studying human personality can be challenging due to its multiple facets and its high variability24,25, nevertheless, decades of research have led to the conclusion that the thousands of individual terms describing human personality can be condensed into five main personality traits, namely “extraversion”, “agreeableness”, “conscientiousness”, “neuroticism” and “openness”26. These are described as the “Big-Five” and are assessed in widely used, standardized questionnaires27–31. In this investigation, the “Big-Five” questionnaire was used, along with additional tools, to assess self-perceived personality and psychosocial traits in young adults and to examine their associations with facial shape. Previous research examining associations between facial morphology and personality has predominantly focused on other-perceived traits, investigating how observers attribute certain traits based on facial structure15,18–20. Such studies reflect social perception processes rather than the individuals’ own internal personality traits. In contrast, the present investigation examines the association between objective facial morphology and self-reported personality and psychosocial characteristics. By distinguishing self-perceived from externally attributed traits, this study aims to explore whether internal psychological constructs are related to facial morphology beyond social stereotyping mechanisms.

We hypothesized that self-perceived personality and psychosocial status may be associated with facial appearance. This hypothesis is grounded in evidence suggesting a shared embryologic and developmental origin of the face and brain, supported by common genetic loci and their structural proximity32,33. Genome-wide investigations have demonstrated these associations but found no evidence linking facial shape to psychiatric or behavioral disorders, indicating that earlier physiognomic claims were unfounded33. Nevertheless, it has been shown that facial shape shares genetic information with certain subcortical brain regions, such as the putamen and the caudate33. Furthermore, structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have presented volumetric differences in subcortical brain areas between individuals with different scores in standardized personality tests34. Available data from functional MRI studies support these observations and exhibit increased activity in different subcortical areas of the brain during positive or negative self-appraisal35. Within this framework, the present study examined associations between facial shape and self-perceived personality and psychosocial traits, thereby contributing to the understanding of how internal characteristics relate to outward human phenotypic features.

White population

Although there are no biological reasons to perform separate analyses in different ethnic groups, a sub-analysis was performed only in white participants, because they comprised the largest group in the sample.

The subgroup analysis in the white population presented similar findings to the main multivariate regression model. In white females, self-esteem scores were highly associated to facial shape, with stronger effect sizes compared to the entire sample population (η2 = 0.175; P-value < 0.001). SISE values were linked to shape variability explained by PC 4, 9 and 10, similarly to what was shown in the entire sample. In white males, there were no significant associations in any of the personality traits under investigation.

Discussion

The aim of the present investigation was to assess the relationship between facial shape, self-esteem and self-perceived personality traits. The incentive for the research question stemmed from previous research showing that certain subcortical brain areas might be related to the morphology of the face and also to functions of self-appraisal33–35. The results do not seem to provide strong support for this initial assumption, however some significant findings were revealed with potentially important implications regarding the association between facial shape and various human traits.

In this population of young adults with similar educational background, females appeared to link their self-esteem to their facial appearance. The association between self-esteem scores and facial shape was 8.9% (η2 = 0.089; Table 2), based on the applied regression model. In a previous investigation studying the same population, facial shape was associated to self-perceived facial attractiveness with an effect size of 4%, less than half of the effect size found here9. Although not studied extensively, self-perceived facial attractiveness has been shown to be influenced by self-esteem, as well as self-confidence38,39. The results of this study align with previous findings and imply that there is some objectivity in self-evaluations related to the actual shape of the face. Females, in this sample, who rated their self-esteem higher presented with more protruded lips. A possible explanation for this finding is that biologically, estrogen production may enhance lip fullness and thereby increase femininity of the face40. Facial femininity in females, is a strong visual signal of fertility and overall mating quality for males. Therefore, females with more pronounced lips could potentially have an advantage in forming romantic relationships with males6. This apparent advantage may explain the association between a higher self-esteem and more pronounced lips in the present female population.

In males, the same association was not found to be significant, implying that male self-esteem is probably not related to their actual facial appearance. Previous investigations have also shown that males tend to not be influenced by their actual physical traits when evaluating their own facial or smile attractiveness9,41. In regard to smiling, there are proposed biological mechanisms related to the levels of testosterone in the brain that may inhibit men to smile as much as women42. Nevertheless, there is no such biological evidence explaining the difference between sexes found here in relation to self-reported self-esteem. In the lack of available biological reasoning, it can be speculated that there may be social reasons making females more aware of their facial appearance to the extent that their self-esteem is affected by physical traits such as lip and cheek fullness, for example. Indeed, females are socially expected to be more attractive than men, because it influences their perceived mating value. In males, mating value is more related to traits such as intelligence, masculinity and socioeconomic status, putting less emphasis on actual facial attractiveness43. Thus, the link between facial appearance and self-esteem is expected to be stronger in females, which was also supported by the present results.

None of the other self-reported psychosocial traits examined in this investigation were found to have a significant association to facial shape. The 44-item Big-Five inventory (BFI-44) provides a well-validated personality profile and is thus a suitable tool to investigate such traits, also allowing for comparisons with other similar investigations44,45. Hu et al. studied the personality traits of a large sample of Chinese descent in relation to their facial shape and reported significant correlations between facial shape and all five traits assessed in the Big-Five inventory22. The authors of that paper included a large age range of participants from adolescence to mid-aged adulthood, however, there are fluctuations in personality traits within the span of a lifetime, with more significant changes occurring during pre-adulthood and during age seniority46,47. This strong confounder in their results was identified by the authors, who then repeated their initial analyses controlling for age. The effect sizes decreased significantly after this adjustment. The authors state that extraversion and agreeableness scores still showed a significant correlation with two shape PCs, but it is highly doubtful that these effect sizes are meaningful, provided that a very small percentage of shape variation explained by these PCs. We therefore interpreted the results of the study by Hu et al.22 with caution. The sample of the present investigation, although smaller in size, is more robust in the sense of population homogeneity, thus, providing robustness to our results. For example, our sample population comprised young adults of similar socioeconomic status (dental or medical students), in order to avoid possible confounding effects of this factor on the results.

The results of this study add valuable insight into facial appearance as a means of identification during social interactions and a source of overall variability among young adult humans. A visual encounter with a new face triggers an immediate subconscious response that establishes a certain level of trust within a fraction of a second12. A first encounter also sends perceptual cues regarding mental and physical health, professional competence, mating quality, intelligence and more6,49–50. Facial expressions tend to further supplement first visual impressions by creating additional perceptions regarding an individual’s physical or psychosocial traits. Smiling, for example, tends to increase perceived trustworthiness and give signals of self-confidence, politeness and happiness49,51,52. In addition to societal perceptions of a face, there are self-perceived traits that are associated with facial appearance. Self-perceived facial attractiveness predicts approximately 5% of facial shape variation in young adults9 and is significantly associated with self-esteem in females and males38. The inter-relationship between self-evaluations and objective physical and psychosocial traits provides a basis for speculation regarding the effect of self-perceived personality on facial appearance, as described through objective means of shape assessment analyses. Despite the absence of robust associations in the present study, we observed some trends that may justify further investigation of this research question in other age groups or populations.

Our results showing an association between objective facial morphology and self-perceived self-esteem in young adult females align with a body of evidence indicating that phenotypic facial features influence internal self-evaluative processes via social perception pathways. Prior work demonstrates that 3-dimensional measures of facial shape contribute significantly to self-perceived facial attractiveness, beyond purely psychological or cognitive predictors of attractiveness9. Facial morphology provides rapid social signals that are automatically processed in first impressions and guide adaptive social behavior, meaning that individuals with facial shapes culturally and biologically associated with attractiveness are more likely to receive positive social cues and affirming feedback49,50. These social responses, accumulated over repeated interactions, can shape self-concept by reinforcing beliefs about one’s social desirability and attractiveness, particularly in domains relevant to interpersonal relationships and appearance. The cultural amplification of appearance evaluation in young women further highlights why facial shape, as a perceptually salient component of attractiveness, would be linked to self-esteem in this demographic.

This pattern is consistent with sociometer theory, which posits that self-esteem functions as an internal gauge of perceived social value and relational acceptance. Self-perceived attractiveness, including perceived facial attractiveness, has been shown to predict self-esteem, with mediational effects through domains such as romantic self-confidence, suggesting that self-evaluative constructs in appearance domains are sensitive to both internal body perceptions and external social valuation processes38,53. Although our study is correlational, the observed connection between objective facial structure and self-esteem suggests that phenotypic cues affecting social feedback may play a substantive role in shaping appearance-based self-worth in young adult females. Future longitudinal work is warranted to disentangle directional effects and to explore potential moderators, such as cultural norms or individual differences in appearance contingency of self-worth.

The results of the present study should not be interpreted through the prism of physiognomy, a pseudo-science that has long been discredited within the scientific community. Instead, we show that facial shape is associated to self-perceptions and might be related developmentally to areas of the brain that also control perception32,33. It remains to be explored whether self-perceptions are also related to perceptions by external observers, in order to form a more rounded opinion regarding this topic.

Strengths and limitations

This study examined a population of young adults with similar educational background and professional profile. Therefore, and despite the ethnic diversity within the sample, the results should be generalized with caution and under the spectrum of the current population. For example, it is expected that a similar study in an older population could possibly lead to different conclusions, given that cognitive functions and perceptions tend to change with age.

It should also be noted that participants in this study were not able to look at their facial images while completing the questionnaire. Being able to look at their face while evaluating their personality, could also potentially influence results, however, it would also add a visual stimulus to the process and affect the authenticity of the responses.

Reliability testing did not include the performance of the camera system, as this has been tested and well-validated by previous investigations54,55. However, it cannot be completely excluded that inaccuracies in the function of the camera could have added to the random error. It can be assumed that due to the large sample size, such small sources of error would not have affected the reliability of the results.

A further limitation of this study is the cross-sectional design and the modeling of facial shape as the outcome variable, while parts of the discussion adopt an inverse conceptual framework. Therefore, causal directionality between facial morphology and psychosocial traits cannot be established and should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Facial shape is related to self-perceived self-esteem in young adult females. This association was not significant in male peers. There might be developmental and/or societal factors related to sex differences in the perception of personality and psychosocial traits.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this cross-sectional study was received from the Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Tufts University in Boston, Massachusetts (IRB#: 11181). Verbal and written consent was provided by all study participants prior to their enrollment.

Study population

Participants were recruited between September 2014 and November 2015 from pre-doctoral medical or dental students at Tufts University (Boston, Massachusetts), who voluntarily enrolled in the study. All were young adults aged 21–35 years (mean age: 25.45 years) and received $20 in gift card compensation. Individuals with craniofacial syndromes, visible facial deformities, or a history of facial plastic surgery were not included. The final study sample comprised 613 participants (214 males and 399 females) of diverse ethnic backgrounds, all born and raised in the United States and native English speakers. Detailed characteristics of the study population have been reported previously9.

3D facial image acquisition

Participants were first photographed three-dimensionally, in a resting facial expression, using a 3dMD camera system (3dMD, Atlanta GA, USA). They were positioned at a 100 cm-distance from the camera unit with their head in an upright position, according to the camera guidelines. They were asked to keep their teeth in slight contact without straining their lips and to keep their eyes open without stretching their forehead. More details about image acquisition and patient positioning have been reported previously9.

Facial shape definition

Three-dimensional photographs were uploaded into “ViewBox 4.1” software (dHAL Software, Kifissia, Greece) and were cropped in a standardized manner. The images were digitized using a pre-designed template composed of 29 curves and 1021 surface landmarks and semi-landmarks56, which was created on a surface image of a person who was not a study participant (Supplementary Fig. 1). The digitization process was performed semi-automatically following a previously described method9. After all landmarks were placed on their initial position, semi-landmarks were allowed to slide along their respective curves or on the image surface. This is a standard step when using semi-landmarks to describe facial shape, because they do not correspond to distinct anatomical structures and can thus not be considered as homologous, biological landmarks. The sliding process reduces the bending energy between individual shape configurations, maximizes homology between semi-landmarks and thereby decreases shape variability within the sample population56,57. After performing three cycles of sliding and reprojecting all semi-landmarks, no meaningful change in bending energy was observed and the resulting landmark configurations were considered to be homologous representations of the facial surfaces. These shape configurations were then superimposed with Generalized Procrustes Superimposition and landmark coordinates were transformed to Procrustes coordinates58. To limit the amount of shape variables and allow for reasonable interpretations of the results, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed, and shape variation was expressed through principal components of shape space (PCs).

Evaluation of psychosocial traits

After 3D facial image acquisition, participants completed a six-unit questionnaire (approximately 15 min) without access to their 3D image. The first two units recorded demographic information and addressed perceptions of facial and smile attractiveness. These results have been already reported9,41. Units 3–6 included standardized, widely used and validated psychometric instruments assessing self-esteem (Single-Item-Self-Esteem scale (SISE)59, sense of coherence (Short Antonovsky’s scale SOC-13)60, subjective well-being (Satisfaction with life scale61, Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS)62, and personality (Big-Five Inventory (BFI-44)44. Participants completed the questionnaires privately, without supervision, and were unaware of the study aim. A research team member remained nearby to address potential questions. Responses were entered into an Excel sheet (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft ©, Redmond WA, USA) by an independent operator, blinded to the study purpose. In order to generate a score for every psychosocial trait assessed by each unit of the questionnaire, the participants answers were transformed into test scores by a single examiner, experienced in using the scales included in the study questionnaire. This process was done in accordance with the available guidelines for each psychometric test44,58–61. For example, to generate a score for “extraversion” using the Big-5 questionnaire, answers from the questions 1, 6, 11, 16, 21, 26, 31 and 36 are combined, and the resulting value was used as the “extraversion” score in all statistical analyses. All psychosocial trait scores reported in the manuscript were derived in a similar manner.

Statistical analyses

Due to pronounced sexual dimorphism in adult facial shape9,63 and other biological differences, all statistical analyses were performed separately for females and males. Facial shape was described by the first 15 Principal Components, capturing more than 80% of sample variation. The association between facial shape and psychosocial traits was assessed with multivariate regression models after all necessary assumptions were tested and met. Shape principal components served as dependent variables, with personality traits as predictors. All questionnaire scores were entered simultaneously into the multivariable regression model. Statistical analyses were performed with “ViewBox 4.1” software and IBM SPSS statistics for Windows (Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) The level of statistical significance was set at p = 0.05.

Error of the method

Intra- and inter-operator digitization errors have been previously reported, confirming the method’s reproducibility and reliability9. To assess the reliability of the self-evaluation scores, 93 participants repeated the procedure after at least 1 month, with Bland-Altman plots assessing response repeatability. The reliability of the personality tests was evaluated using Crohnbach’s Alpha.
