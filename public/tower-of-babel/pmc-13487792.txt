Haemodialysis Nurses' Self‐Reported Cultural Competence and Responsiveness: A Cross‐Sectional Survey
Authors: Sara Aryal, Rochelle Wynne, Areum Hyun, Paul N Bennett, Melissa J Bloomer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeople receiving in‐centre haemodialysis have distinct cultural care needs and preferences, and nurses are expected to respond to these. However, haemodialysis nurses' cultural competence and responsiveness are unknown.ObjectivesTo examine nurses' cultural competence and responsiveness when caring for people with diverse cultural characteristics.DesignAn online cross‐sectional survey.ParticipantsHaemodialysis nurses from Australia and New Zealand (n = 123), recruited through the Renal Society of Australasia and professional networks.MeasurementsThe 25‐item Cultural Competence Assessment instrument measured cultural awareness and sensitivity, and culturally responsive behaviours. Demographic characteristics were also collected.ResultsOf 123 complete responses, overall cultural competence was high (M = 5.09, SD = 0.76), particularly awareness and sensitivity (M = 5.76, SD = 0.53), with significantly higher scores among those who had completed cultural awareness training (p = 0.009). In contrast, culturally responsive behaviours were moderate (M = 4.53, SD = 1.23), highlighting the gap between cultural competence and responsiveness. The lowest scoring areas were documentation of patients' cultural needs (M = 3.88, SD = 2.02) and access to cultural learning resources (M = 3.02, SD = 1.75), indicating limited supports. Qualitative findings reflected practices of culture care preservation and accommodation, with themes of cultural awareness and language differences highlighting barriers related to language and resources.ConclusionsHigh cultural competence does not necessarily translate into culturally responsive behaviour. Organisational supports, including guidance for documenting cultural needs, cultural assessment tools and accessible learning resources, may help strengthen culturally responsive haemodialysis care.Keywords: cultural competence, cultural diversity, cultural responsiveness, haemodialysis, nursing

Introduction

Recognising the importance of cultural care needs and preferences of patients in haemodialysis is important; however, adapting care to accommodate preferences can be challenging for nurses (Cervantes et al. 2021). Given the frequent and ongoing nature of haemodialysis care, nurses develop sustained therapeutic relationships with patients (Cousins et al. 2020), playing a central role in recognising and responding to their care needs and preferences. Nurses require not only cultural awareness and sensitivity (CAS) to identify patients' cultural care needs and preferences, but also competence and confidence to translate their understanding into actions and behaviours (Markey et al. 2023).

Cultural competence describes the process of acquiring the knowledge and skills to provide care that is responsive to diverse values, customs and beliefs (Sharifi et al. 2019). Cultural awareness is a cognitive concept that refers to knowledge and understanding of cultural similarities and differences, including values, beliefs and practices (Doorenbos et al. 2005). Cultural sensitivity represents the affective component of cultural competence, reflecting attitudes, values, and openness towards understanding and respecting diverse cultural perspectives (Schim et al. 2003).

Cultural responsiveness is about action and behaviours in response to knowledge and skills (Aryal et al. 2024). Culturally responsive care involves recognising and respecting differences in sociocultural contexts, values, preferences, health beliefs and behaviour, and accommodating these within care provision (Minnican and O'Toole 2020). While culturally responsive care is associated with improved patient experiences and outcomes, it is complex and multidimensional (Bellos et al. 2024; Benuto et al. 2021). One aspect of ensuring culturally responsive care is to improve nurses' cultural competence (Leininger 2002), thereby enhancing responsiveness to the cultural values, customs and beliefs of patients with diverse cultural characteristics (Minnican and O'Toole 2020).

Literature Review

People with diverse cultural characteristics may have unique care needs in haemodialysis according to aspects of their culture (Jui‐Chin et al. 2024), and may face challenges in receiving care that meets their cultural needs and preferences (Khatri and Assefa 2022). Previous studies have reported the challenges nurses face in providing care for people with diverse cultural characteristics receiving haemodialysis (Rix et al. 2013; Cervantes et al. 2021). These include communication barriers (Wilkinson et al. 2014), limited availability of professional interpreters (Zimbudzi et al. 2010), accommodation for culturally important practices (Sharma et al. 2019) and traditional food preferences (Mclean et al. 2021). Unmet needs linked to cultural diversity negatively impact patients' experiences and outcomes in haemodialysis (Hughes et al. 2019). Haemodialysis nurses have reported limited opportunities for cultural education and training to acquire knowledge and build skills in providing care that is responsive to diverse values, customs and beliefs (Rix et al. 2015; Krueger 2009). To our knowledge, no research has been conducted to measure cultural competence and cultural responsiveness of haemodialysis nurses in Australia and New Zealand. Therefore, the aim of this research was to examine the cultural competence and cultural responsiveness of nurses in providing care for people with diverse cultural characteristics in haemodialysis.

Materials and Methods

Design

An online cross‐sectional survey was used to answer the aim. Reporting is consistent with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (see Supporting Information S1) (Von Elm et al. 2014).

Setting and Participants

Registered and enroled nurses, respectively, in Australia and New Zealand with at least 3 months of haemodialysis experience were eligible to participate. Participants were primarily recruited through the Renal Society of Australasia (RSA), with additional recruitment conducted through snowball sampling within the investigators' professional networks. The RSA has approximately 2000 members; however, the number of registered and enrolled nurses from Australia and New Zealand eligible for the study was not publicly available. No formal sample size calculation was conducted, given the descriptive and exploratory nature of the study.

Survey Instrument

The survey instrument comprised four sections and was housed in the secure Lime Survey platform (Limesurvey GmbH 2024). Section Background, 1 included a self‐rated cultural competence item and the 25‐item Cultural Competence Assessment (CCA) questionnaire (Schim et al. 2003; Doorenbos et al. 2005). The CCA comprises two subscales: the CAS subscale, which includes 11 items rated on a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, with four items reverse scored and the Culturally Competent Behaviours (CCB) subscale, which includes 14 items measuring the frequency of CCB using a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from never to always. As the CCB subscale measures self‐reported behaviour rather than self‐efficacy in performing a behaviour, it serves as an indicator of cultural responsiveness (Te et al. 2019). Completion of all CCA items was required before respondents could proceed to subsequent sections.

The CCA was developed in the United States and is underpinned by the premise that cultural competence reflects an integration of cultural diversity experience, cultural awareness, sensitivity and CCB (Doorenbos et al. 2005). The CCA has been used in previous research to measure the cultural competence and responsiveness of clinicians (Te et al. 2019; Osmancevic et al. 2021; Doorenbos et al. 2016). The construct validity of the original CCA was established through factor analysis and further supported by comparing the CCA to another widely used cultural competence instrument (Campinha‐Bacote 1999), Inventory for Assessing the Process of Cultural Competence (Schim et al. 2003). Content and face validity of the original CCA were established by expert reviews and consensus. The initial internal consistency reliability was Cronbach's α = 0.92 (Schim et al. 2003), with the test–retest reliability of 0.89 (Doorenbos et al. 2005). In this study, Cronbach's alpha (α) values were 0.65 for CAS, 0.93 for CCB and 0.90 for the total CCA score.

Section Objectives, 2 contained items designed to collect respondent demographic data, aligned to Australian and New Zealand census classifications for country of birth, self‐identified cultural or ethnic background, primary language spoken at home and religious affiliation (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2026; Stats Tatauranga Aotearoa 2026). Professional characteristics were also collected in this section: highest qualification, current role, years of renal nursing experience and area of work within renal services.

In Section Design, 3, there were questions related to cultural diversity and awareness training, with an open‐ended option to describe the type, focus and content of the training undertaken. To further explore cultural responsiveness, Section Participants, 4 was comprised of three open‐ended questions: (i) Can you describe any specific actions or common practices you or your colleagues use to communicate with culturally diverse patients in your dialysis unit? (ii) What challenges do you or your colleagues face when caring for culturally diverse patients, and what actions, if any, do you take to address these challenges? (iii) If you have any other comments related to cultural care practices, please note them here. Completion of demographic, professional and open‐ended questions was optional.

Permission to use and modify the instrument was obtained from the original authors. Minor modifications were made to the instrument to reflect Australian English. Face validity and readability of the adapted survey were established through expert review by four individuals with haemodialysis and academic expertise (Kaitelidou et al. 2019). The survey was then pilot tested with the same group to assess feasibility, and pilot data were not included in the final analysis.

Data Collection

An invitation to complete the survey was distributed between February and April 2025, via an email invitation to all members of the RSA, with snowballing through professional networks. Consent was implied through completion. The survey remained open for 6 weeks, with reminder emails sent at 2 and 4 weeks. Potential participants were advised to only complete the survey once.

Data Analysis

Survey data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp 2021) and are reported according to SAMPL Guidelines (Lang and Altman 2015). An overall cultural competence score was calculated by summing responses across the 25 items of the CCA instrument and dividing by the number of completed items, producing a mean score ranging from 1 to 7, with CAS and CCB subscales calculated similarly (Doorenbos et al. 2005). Reverse‐scored items were recoded prior to analysis. CAS and CCB subscale scores and the overall CCA score were calculated using complete responses across all 25 CCA items. Scores of 1–3 indicate low levels, 4 moderate levels and 5–7 high levels across all constructs measured. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic characteristics using frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and cultural competence outcomes, including the overall score, CAS and CCB subscales, using mean (M) and standard deviation (SD).

Inferential analyses were conducted to examine associations between demographic characteristics and cultural competence outcomes. Independent samples t‐tests were used to compare CAS, CCB and overall cultural competence scores across dichotomous variables. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to examine differences across categories of renal nursing experience. Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated to examine relationships between age, CAS and CCB scores. Inferential analyses were conducted using complete responses for the relevant variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Open text survey response data were analysed using content analysis (Kyngäs 2020). First, data were analysed deductively, looking for alignment with Leininger's Culture Care Theory (Leininger 2002). Leininger's Culture Care Theory comprises (i) culture care preservation and/or maintenance, defined as professional actions or decisions that support the continuation of beneficial cultural values, beliefs, and practices, (ii) culture care accommodation and/or negotiation, referring to enabling actions that support individuals to adapt or negotiate cultural practices in ways that enhance health and wellbeing, and (iii) culture care repatterning and/or restructuring, which involves collaborative actions to modify or restructure care practices (Leininger 2002). Responses not aligning with these concepts were analysed inductively, with categories and themes to be derived directly from the data. Each open‐ended response was treated as the unit of coding. Responses were read and reread to achieve familiarity, then coded and organised into categories representing identifiable patterns. Initial coding and category development were undertaken by the first author and independently reviewed by a senior member of the research team with qualitative research expertise (MJB). Differences were discussed within the wider research team, and categories and themes were refined prior to finalisation. Reflexivity was addressed through ongoing consideration of researchers' professional perspectives and assumptions throughout the research process, particularly during data analysis and interpretation. Findings are presented narratively (Popay et al. 2006) and supported by illustrative quotations labelled according to survey respondent number.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant Human Research Ethics Committee. Permission was sought from RSA to support the research. Respondents received a plain language statement outlining the study purpose, consent processes and data management procedures prior to survey commencement. Participation was voluntary, and anonymity was maintained through the non‐collection of identifiable information.

Cultural Competence

The overall mean score of respondents' cultural competence was 5.09 (SD = 0.76), indicating a high level of cultural competence. The CAS subscale score was 5.76 (SD = 0.53), indicating a high level of CAS. The CCB subscale score was 4.53 (SD = 1.23), indicating a moderate level of CCB. These findings indicate that although respondents report high CAS, this may not equate to CCB. Most respondents perceived themselves as culturally competent, with nearly 90% (n = 110) rating themselves as competent or very competent, 5.7% (n = 7) neutral and 4.9% (n = 6) incompetent.

CAS Subscale

The item with the highest mean score reflected a strong endorsement of treating individuals with respect regardless of cultural heritage (M = 6.89, SD = 0.39). Other highly rated items related to recognising spiritual and religious beliefs (M = 6.29, SD = 0.66), assessing cultural diversity at individual and organisational levels (M = 6.17, SD = 0.86), and acknowledging that definitions of health care vary across cultures (M = 6.00, SD = 0.78). In contrast, lower mean scores were observed for items suggesting race as the primary determinant of culture (M = 3.92, SD = 1.72) or that individuals from the same cultural background think and act alike (M = 4.55, SD = 1.72), indicating limited endorsement of reductive views of culture (Table 2).

CCB Subscale

Responses to the CCB subscale showed variability across items (Table 3). Higher scores were observed for items related to interpersonal behaviours, including avoiding stereotypes (M = 5.82, SD = 1.25), recognising service barriers (M = 5.25, SD = 1.37), welcoming feedback (M = 5.38, SD = 1.70) and adapting services to cultural preferences (M = 5.04, SD = 1.63). In contrast, lower scores were observed for items related to documentation (M = 3.88, SD = 2.02) and access to cultural learning resources (M = 3.02, SD = 1.75). This suggests that while CCB were generally endorsed, practical aspects such as resource use and documentation were less consistently applied.

Cultural Diversity or Awareness Training

Among the total sample, 82 respondents reported receiving cultural diversity or awareness training, primarily through mandatory workplace programmes. This represented 66.7% of the full sample and 75.9% of those who answered this item. Training predominantly focused on cultural safety and trauma‐informed care for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and included content on cultural beliefs and customs, barriers to healthcare access, and historical, social and political influences.

Associations Between Demographic Characteristics and CCA Scores

Differences in CAS subscale scores were associated with language other than English spoken at home, religious affiliation, country of birth, and completion of cultural awareness training. Respondents who spoke English at home scored higher on the CAS subscale (M = 5.99, SD = 0.52) than those who spoke a language other than English at home (M = 5.54, SD = 0.49; t(104) = 4.63, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.71, 95% CI = [0.18–0.56]). Higher CAS scores were also associated with having no religious affiliation (M = 5.62, SD = 0.50; t(102) = 2.92, p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.42, 95% CI = [0.10–0.52]), being born in Australia (M = 5.92, SD = 0.54; t(103) = 2.48, p = 0.015, Cohen's d = 0.49, 95% CI = [0.05–0.48]), and having completed cultural awareness training (M = 5.84, SD = 0.53; t(121) = −2.65, p = 0.009, Cohen's d = 0.51, 95% CI = [−0.48 to −0.05]).

No significant differences were found in the CCB subscale or overall CCA scores across demographic and cultural awareness training variables. Education level and years of renal nursing experience were not associated with differences in any of the cultural competence outcomes (Table 4). Correlation analysis showed no association between age and any of the cultural competence scores. A modest positive correlation was found between CAS and CCB (r = 0.23, p = 0.012), indicating higher cultural awareness may be associated with higher culturally responsive behaviours.

Findings From Open‐Text Responses

A total of 59.3% (n = 73) respondents answered the first open‐ended question, 61.8% (n = 76) the second, and 17.9% (n = 22) the third, providing insights into cultural responsiveness in haemodialysis. Findings are presented in two sequential parts, with the first reporting responses aligned with concepts from Leininger's Culture Care Theory (Leininger 2002), followed by inductively derived themes from remaining responses. Responses aligned with two concepts from Leininger's Culture Care Theory: (i) culture care preservation and/or maintenance and (ii) culture care accommodation and/or negotiation. No responses reflected culture care repatterning and/or restructuring.

Culture Care Preservation and/or Maintenance

Some respondents emphasised the importance of respecting cultural values and practices, being open to differences, and considering individual preferences in care, such as: ‘I try to listen carefully and speak slowly, I try to show that I am in a caring role despite being culturally different’ (R13). Another highlighted the importance of asking patients directly: ‘…whether there is anything staff needs to be aware in regard to their culture…and respect their stories, culture, values, practices’ (R30). These comments reflect respondents' efforts to preserve and maintain patients' cultural identities within the haemodialysis setting. Emergent Themes

From the inductive content analysis, two themes were identified: (i) cultural awareness and (ii) language differences.

Theme One: Cultural Awareness

Respondents indicated awareness of patients' cultural backgrounds as an important influence on care provision. This was reflected in references to spending more time with patients and fostering relationships. For example, ‘…Aboriginal [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander] patients require more time to build a relationship. Having to spend time with them to build trust and confidence is usual’ (R31). Awareness of broader social and emotional impacts was also evident, with ‘…I have found patients of Indigenous [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander] background often struggle emotionally and mentally with a new diagnosis… often requiring extensive social supports…’ (R48).

Awareness of the influence of cultural practices and patient preferences on care decisions was also apparent. Respondents referred to ‘…respecting people's choices even if it may be detrimental to their overall health, e.g., food, beliefs, non‐attendance at appointments’ (R60). Respondents also emphasised respect for ‘… their way of communication, food preferences and cultural practices…’ (R71). The significance attached to these practices was noted, with ‘…certain practices although may seem unnecessary to us are important to the client’ (R53).

Respondents expressed awareness of limitations affecting patient care, particularly gaps in cultural knowledge, education and access to resources. This was reflected in comments about the absence of ‘training on cultures and how to treat or communicate better’ (R61), alongside calls for ‘more education, seminars or materials for staff’ (R7) and more resources to help ‘care for patients with diverse needs without prejudice’ (R9). Some respondents noted that: ‘we have been largely made aware how to care for First Nations people, however, we have such a diverse background of patients from many other different cultures which will need tailored service as well’ (R71).

Awareness of limited familiarity with specific cultural groups was also described. Responses referred to a ‘lack of knowledge on how to provide culturally appropriate care to different patients, especially with minorities’ (R71), limited understanding of ‘…Muslim, African and Chinese cultures’ (R59), and the challenges of caring for a ‘…wide range of cultures…’ (R47). Some respondents also identified potential approaches to address these limitations, including increasing workforce diversity, such as to ‘…employ Aboriginal [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander] nurse to meet diversity of our clients…’ (R40), and changes to service practices, including the use of ‘decolonising methods’ in healthcare settings (R76).

Theme 2: Language Differences

Language differences, particularly those arising from cultural and linguistic diversity, were a recurring concern among respondents and were linked to the multicultural nature of the Australian healthcare context, ‘Australia is a very multiculturally diverse country and language barriers can be a challenge that many of us can face…’ (R28). To overcome language differences and support patient comprehension, respondents described engaging professional interpreters (R62), using simple plain English (R50) minimising medical jargon (R71), using non‐verbal methods including facial expressions (R68), body language and hand gestures (R28) and language charts with pictures (R67).

Respondents also mentioned family members were sometimes used to overcome language barriers: ‘… we ask the patient to bring in a family [member] that speaks English to translate’ (R11). Others described using colleagues, such as: ‘…try to find a colleague who might speak that language with the patient…’ (R5) or ‘…allocate nurse to patient who has same culture…’ (R37). However, concerns were raised about the accuracy of interpretation when family members or staff were used, especially with family members who may not ‘…communicate the information to their loved one; in a manner that it was intended’ (R48). Questions were also raised about whether family involvement always reflected patients’ wishes, including situations where ‘…the patient doesn't want dialysis, but family say he does’ (R67).

Discussion

The findings highlight a gap between respondents' perceived cultural competence and their responsiveness. Although most respondents rated themselves as culturally competent, this self‐perception was not consistently reflected in responsive clinical actions. This mismatch suggests that cultural competence, particularly when framed primarily as awareness, knowledge or attitudes, may be insufficient to translate into culturally responsive clinical care. Consistent with previous research across diverse nursing contexts, including acute care, obstetric, gynaecologic and neonatal care and primary healthcare settings, nurses may value cultural care and have supportive intentions, yet experience difficulty in responding to the same (Osmancevic et al. 2023; Heitzler 2017; Starr and Wallace 2009).

The documentation of cultural assessments and evidence of responsiveness was a key concern identified by this study. Similar gaps in documenting cultural aspects of care have been reported previously (Červený et al. 2020; Cicolini et al. 2015). In haemodialysis, where nurse–patient relationships are long standing (Cousins et al. 2020), cultural knowledge may become tacit and assumed to be shared, reducing the perceived need for formal documentation. Challenges in documenting relational and culturally sensitive aspects of care have been noted in other sustained care contexts, such as oncology, where complex narratives are difficult to articulate and record (Hynnekleiv et al. 2025). More broadly, limitations of clinical documentation systems in supporting person‐centred and individualised care have been reported across healthcare settings (Forde‐Johnston et al. 2023).

The limited access to cultural education and training identified in this study has been highlighted previously (Osmancevic et al. 2023; Doorenbos et al. 2016; Cicolini et al. 2015). However, our findings extend this literature by highlighting that the content and focus of education are also important considerations. Education predominantly centred on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples' cultural safety, commonly addressing historical and structural drivers of health inequity, including the impacts of colonisation, the Stolen Generations and custody‐related trauma, and was typically delivered as one‐off mandatory training. While this focus is essential within the Australian context and similarly colonised countries (Arnold‐Ujvari et al. 2025), such approaches alone offer important but limited preparation for responding to the diversity and complexity of patients' cultural needs in this area of specialist practice.

Most interestingly, respondents described their own cultural background and ethnicity in diverse and multifaceted ways, drawing on overlapping descriptors rather than fixed categories. This suggests an understanding of cultural identity as personally defined and fluid, aligning with literature that cautions against standardised cultural classifications which risk oversimplifying cultural complexity (Bloomer et al. 2025; Dessie and Chen 2023). Recognition of this complexity was also evident in reported care practices, with respondents describing challenges in providing care for patients with diverse cultural characteristics and uncertainty about what constituted culturally appropriate care. This uncertainty was often linked to the complexity of cultural diversity encountered in practice, with perceived gaps in cultural knowledge reflecting the breadth and variability of cultural beliefs, values, and practices.

Limitations

This study relied on self‐reported measures of cultural competence and responsiveness, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Given the normative positioning of cultural responsiveness, respondents may have reported attitudes or behaviours that reflect professional ideals or perceived expectations rather than routine practice. This is particularly relevant to the present findings, which identified a gap between perceived cultural competence and culturally responsive behaviours, as self‐report may inflate assessments of competence while underrepresenting the practical challenges of enacting culturally responsive care. In addition, recruitment through the RSA and professional nursing networks, with the majority of respondents working in Australia, may have introduced selection bias. Nurses who are engaged with professional organisations or have an interest in cultural care and professional development may have been more likely to participate. This may have contributed to higher reported levels of cultural awareness and competence and should be considered when interpreting the findings, as they may not fully reflect the perspectives or practices of the broader haemodialysis nursing workforce. Furthermore, given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons, such as a Bonferroni correction, was applied. Consequently, the risk of Type I error may have been increased, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Implications for Clinical Practice

Strengthening culturally responsive haemodialysis care requires organisational support that extends beyond individual nurses' knowledge, intentions or goodwill. Services need systematic approaches to identifying and responding to patients' cultural needs, including the use of structured cultural assessment processes and consistent documentation within existing care plans. Making cultural needs visible within care records supports continuity, shared understanding, and accountability. Additionally, access to appropriate resources is essential to enable culturally responsive care. This includes timely access to professional interpreters, particularly for sensitive discussions such as treatment withdrawal decisions, where reliance on family members may not reflect patients' preferences or uphold informed consent. Education also plays a key role but should move beyond one‐off mandatory training to include ongoing, practice‐relevant learning within local haemodialysis settings. Educational approaches should be informed by the cultural diversity of patients served within individual units and, where appropriate, developed in partnership with lived experience advisors, local communities or cultural groups. Such approaches may better support nurses to recognise and respond to patients' diverse cultural needs and preferences.

Conclusion

Culturally responsive care in haemodialysis is influenced by both nurses' awareness and intentions and by features of the practice environment that shape how care is provided. Although nurses demonstrated cultural awareness, responsiveness was affected by factors such as access to cultural learning resources, documentation practices, and opportunities for practice‐relevant learning. Use of professional interpreters, flexibility in care provision and systems that support the identification and recording of patients' cultural needs may support culturally responsive haemodialysis care. Given the complexity and individuality of cultural identity, supporting nurses to tailor care within routine haemodialysis settings requires attention to both individual practice and organisational support.
