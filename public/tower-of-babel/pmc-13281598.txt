Impact of a shared pediatric palliative care program on end-of-life care trends among children: a 10-year retrospective study in Taiwan
Authors: Chia-Wen Hung, Li-Min Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly integration of pediatric palliative care (PPC) improves symptom control, communication, and goal-concordant decision-making. Background

Advances in pediatric critical care, oncology, and chronic disease management have led to increased survival among children with complex and life–limiting illnesses. While these advances are encouraging, they have also increased the number of children living with prolonged disease trajectories characterized by recurrent hospitalizations, invasive interventions, and significant physical and psychological burdens. Pediatric palliative care (PPC) has emerged as an essential component of comprehensive pediatric care, emphasizing symptom control, psychosocial support, and family–centered communication beginning at diagnosis rather than only at the terminal stage [1–3].

International guidelines recommend early integration of palliative care alongside disease–directed treatment. Early PPC involvement has been associated with better symptom management, clearer goals–of–care discussions, lower rates of invasive interventions, and enhanced family satisfaction [4–7]. In pediatric oncology, timely PPC consultation also facilitates advance care planning and reduces decisional conflict among caregivers [8–10].

Despite these benefits, PPC remains underutilized in many healthcare systems, particularly in Asian settings where cultural expectations, prognostic uncertainty, and limited pediatric hospice resources may contribute to delayed referral [11–13]. In Taiwan, most children with serious illnesses continue to die in hospitals and intensive care units (ICUs), reflecting a persistent reliance on high–intensity medical care at the end of life [11–13].

Long–term evaluations examining how institutional integration of pediatric palliative care influences clinical practice remain scarce, particularly in Asian contexts. To address this gap, two tertiary medical centers implemented a shared PPC model that embedded palliative specialists within primary pediatric teams. The present study aimed to evaluate ten–year trends in PPC utilization and end–of–life care indicators following implementation of this shared approach.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at two medical centers in southern Taiwan that serve as major referral centers for pediatric oncology, intensive care, and management of children with complex chronic conditions. These institutions provide comprehensive inpatient, intensive care, and subspecialty services and represent typical settings in which children with life-limiting illnesses receive end-of-life care in Taiwan.

All children aged 0–18 years who died between January 2008 and December 2017 were screened for eligibility. Neonatal perinatal deaths and cases discharged against medical advice were excluded because complete assessment of end-of-life processes could not be reliably obtained. After applying exclusion criteria, 294 decedents were included in the final analysis.

Flow diagram of patient selection

All pediatric decedents were used as the denominator because the study aimed to evaluate institution-level trends in end-of-life care indicators and PPC utilization among children who died during the study period. This approach is consistent with prior studies examining end-of-life care patterns in pediatric populations. Defining a denominator based on “PPC need” was not feasible in this retrospective design, as symptom burden, treatment complexity, and palliative care needs were not consistently documented using standardized criteria across the entire study period.

Shared pediatric palliative care program

A shared pediatric palliative care (PPC) program was formally implemented in 2011. Rather than transferring patients to a separate palliative unit, this model embedded PPC specialists within existing clinical teams. PPC physicians and nurses collaborated with primary treating teams to provide: (1) comprehensive symptom assessment and management, (2) psychosocial and family support, (3) structured family meetings, (4) advance care planning and goals–of–care discussions, and (5) consultation regarding treatment limitation decisions.

This approach preserved continuity of care and may help mitigate potential stigma associated with referral to “terminal care.” Similar shared or integrated models have been described as effective strategies for promoting earlier and more acceptable PPC involvement [8–10].

Consultations could be requested by primary physicians, triggered by complex symptom needs, or initiated when families required support for decision–making. Standardized documentation templates were incorporated into the electronic medical record to ensure consistent recording of family meetings and code status decisions. The standardized documentation templates included structured fields for recording family meeting content, code status decisions, symptom assessments, and palliative care interventions.

In practice, the shared PPC model was delivered by a multidisciplinary team that included physicians, nurses, case managers (for oncology patients), dietitians, pharmacists, palliative care specialists, social workers, and psychologists when needed. PPC specialists also participated in multidisciplinary discussions and ward-based clinical communication when needed. In addition, PPC specialists had access to electronic medical records, allowing them to identify patients with potential palliative care needs and to suggest appropriate referrals to the primary team when necessary. Following initiation of palliative care consultation, non-physician team members conducted regular weekly visits, while dietitians performed routine weekly nutritional assessments. PPC involvement was typically initiated through formal consultation requests by primary physicians, after which the palliative care team collaborated with the primary team to provide ongoing support. Multidisciplinary team meetings were held monthly, with additional meetings and family conferences convened as needed based on patient condition. Team members accessed patient information through electronic medical records and consultation documentation, allowing continuous monitoring and coordinated care.

Study phases

To evaluate temporal changes associated with program implementation, the study period was divided into three phases: Pre–implementation (2008–2010), Early post–implementation (2012–2014), Late post–implementation (2015–2017).

The year 2011 was treated as a transition period and excluded from phase comparisons to avoid contamination during early adoption.

Data collection

Clinical data were abstracted from electronic medical records using a structured data collection form that included variables related to PPC consultation (including timing and frequency), advance care planning, clinical characteristics, and end-of-life care indicators. Extracted variables included: (1) demographic characteristics (age at death, sex); (2) clinical characteristics (primary diagnosis [cancer/malignancy, congenital or chronic disease, acute/other], hospitalization status prior to death, intensive care utilization); and (3) end-of-life indicators (documentation of do-not-resuscitate [DNR] orders, documented family meetings, PPC consultation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation [CPR] during final hospitalization, and place of death [ICU vs. non-ICU]).

In this study, family meetings were defined as structured, documented discussions involving healthcare providers and family members to address prognosis, goals of care, and end-of-life decision-making.

These indicators were selected based on previously described quality measures for pediatric palliative care and end-of-life practice [9, 10]. Data collection was limited to predefined variables related to PPC consultation, advance care planning, and end-of-life care indicators, based on data availability and study objectives.

All variables related to pediatric palliative care (PPC), including DNR documentation, family meetings, and PPC consultation, were retrospectively abstracted from the medical records using a standardized data collection form. Data were collected at predefined time points prior to death, specifically the last day of life, one week before death, and one month before death. This standardized temporal framework was applied consistently across all patients to ensure comparability and to minimize potential bias related to variations in timing of data abstraction.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality prior to selecting appropriate summary statistics. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Comparisons across study phases were performed using chi–square tests for trend. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with receipt of PPC consultation. Variables included in the model were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature, including demographic characteristics (e.g., age at death, sex), diagnosis category, and indicators of disease severity or healthcare utilization (e.g., ICU utilization, DNR status). All selected covariates were entered into the model simultaneously. Categorical variables were analyzed as binary or categorical variables, while continuous variables (e.g., age at death) were treated as continuous variables. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

A two–sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital and Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital, Taiwan (IRB No. 201900372B0; KMUHIIRBSV(II)-20160042).

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of de-identified data.

Patient characteristics

Among the 294 children included in the study, 52.7% were male. Cancer and malignancy represented the largest diagnostic category (41.2%), followed by congenital or chronic diseases (34.7%) and acute or other conditions (24.4%). Trends in advance care planning

Following implementation of the shared PPC program, indicators of advance care planning increased significantly. The proportion of children with documented do–not–resuscitate (DNR) orders increased from 59.2% in the pre–implementation period to 73.9% in the late post–implementation period (p = .03). Similarly, documented family meetings increased more than fourfold, rising from 4.1% to 18.2% (p < .001). Trends are summarized in Table 2.

PPC consultation utilization

Formal PPC consultations increased significantly across the study periods. Consultation rates rose from 7.1% before implementation to 23.9% in the late post–implementation phase (p < .001) (Table 2).

High–intensity care at end of life

Rates of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) declined from 59.2% to 46.6%, but this reduction did not reach statistical significance (p = .09). Nearly all children were hospitalized prior to death (98.6%). The proportion of children with hospital stay longer than 14 days remained stable across study periods (59.2%, 66.7%, and 63.6%, respectively; p = .51), with a mean length of hospital stay of 46.63 ± 25.38 days.

Place of death remained similar across study phases (p = .60), with the majority of children dying in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) (87.8%, 84.3%, and 89.8%, respectively). Detailed end–of–life care indicators are summarized in Table 2.

Predictors of PPC consultation

Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that cancer diagnosis was strongly associated with PPC consultation (OR = 7.97, 95% CI 2.71–23.42). Increasing age at diagnosis was also associated with consultation (OR = 1.12 per year, 95% CI 1.06–1.18). Children who did not undergo CPR were more likely to receive PPC consultation (OR = 5.12, 95% CI 2.48–10.57). Conversely, absence of a family meeting was associated with lower likelihood of PPC involvement (OR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.09–0.47) (Table 3).

These results indicate that clinical and care-related factors were associated with the likelihood of receiving PPC consultation, reflecting patterns in referral and care processes. We did not assess whether predictors of PPC consultation changed over time following implementation of the embedded model. Future studies are needed to examine whether integrated PPC models can reduce disparities in referral patterns between cancer and non-cancer populations.

Discussion

This ten–year evaluation demonstrates that embedding PPC specialists within existing pediatric services can produce sustained improvements in advance care planning and interdisciplinary collaboration. The marked increases in DNR documentation, structured family meetings, and PPC consultations indicate that shared–care integration effectively lowers referral barriers and normalizes palliative involvement within routine practice. These findings are consistent with prior studies showing that early PPC engagement enhances communication quality and promotes goal–concordant decision–making [12–15].

The substantial growth in structured family meetings observed in our cohort is particularly noteworthy. Repeated, proactive conversations allow families to clarify prognosis, explore values, and prepare for difficult decisions. Previous work has demonstrated that such communication reduces decisional conflict and improves caregiver satisfaction [16–18]. Our results suggest that institutionalizing these discussions through a shared PPC model may meaningfully strengthen advance care planning processes.

However, the relatively low proportion of documented family meetings may reflect underdocumentation or variability in clinical practice, as discussions regarding prognosis and goals of care may have occurred outside of formally recorded meetings. This finding also highlights an opportunity to improve the standardization and documentation of structured family meetings in pediatric end-of-life care.

Despite these gains, ICU deaths and high–intensity treatments remained common. Similar patterns have been reported internationally across pediatric oncology and critical care populations, where aggressive interventions frequently persist near the end of life even after PPC involvement [19–21]. These findings suggest that consultation alone may be insufficient to substantially alter location-of-death outcomes. As increasing numbers of children live longer with complex chronic conditions, the demand for coordinated palliative support across prolonged illness trajectories continues to grow.

Logistic regression analysis showed that cancer diagnosis and increasing age were associated with a higher likelihood of PPC consultation, whereas absence of a family meeting was associated with lower PPC involvement. These findings suggest that referral patterns may still be influenced by underlying disease characteristics and care processes. The interpretation of regression results may depend on the reference groups used, and careful attention is required when interpreting the direction of associations.

We did not assess whether predictors of PPC consultation changed over time following implementation of the embedded model. Future studies are needed to examine whether integrated PPC models can reduce disparities in referral patterns between cancer and non-cancer populations.

Several mechanisms may explain this persistence. Children with complex chronic conditions often experience unpredictable deterioration, limiting opportunities for planned discharge. In addition, limited pediatric hospice availability and caregiver concerns regarding home care may restrict transitions outside the hospital. Studies examining place of death suggest that community–based services and home palliative support are critical for enabling non–ICU deaths [22–25].

From a systems perspective, shared PPC models appear particularly feasible and scalable. Embedding specialists within primary teams preserves continuity of care, reduces stigma associated with referral, and facilitates earlier collaboration. Similar integration frameworks have been proposed as sustainable strategies for expanding pediatric palliative capacity without major structural reorganization [26–28].

Policy initiatives may further enhance these effects. International experts increasingly advocate that PPC be considered a standard component of pediatric oncology and critical care rather than an optional service [29–32]. Our findings provide real–world evidence from an Asian healthcare system where PPC has historically been underutilized, demonstrating that integration can produce meaningful improvements over time.

Cultural expectations favoring life-prolonging treatment and limited availability of pediatric hospice services in Taiwan may contribute to the persistence of ICU deaths. These contextual factors highlight the importance of culturally responsive and community-based palliative care models in Asian healthcare systems.

This study has limitations, including its retrospective design and data derived from two centers. In addition, information regarding prior PPC involvement and reasons for hospital admission was not consistently available and therefore was not included in the analysis. The use of all pediatric decedents as the denominator may overestimate or underestimate the proportion of patients who required PPC services, as palliative care needs were not defined using standardized criteria across the study period. In addition, the study did not assess whether documented DNR orders or advance care plans were consistently honored in clinical practice.

The implementation of the embedded PPC model may not have been uniform across all clinical teams, and variations in practice patterns could not be fully captured. Although family meetings were conducted with participation from palliative care team members, detailed documentation of all multidisciplinary participants was not consistently available. This is partly because structured family meeting records were maintained as separate paper-based forms and were not fully accessible through the electronic medical record system used in this retrospective study. As a result, the full composition of the care team involved in each meeting could not be determined.

Furthermore, although high rates of ICU utilization at the end of life were observed, we were not able to directly assess whether this reflected misalignment between care delivery and family preferences, as patient and family perspectives were not systematically documented. In our setting, critically ill pediatric patients are commonly managed in intensive care units to provide advanced life-sustaining treatment. Transition to general wards typically occurs only when a do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order is established and further life-sustaining interventions are no longer pursued. This practice pattern may partly explain the high proportion of ICU utilization observed and suggests that ICU-based end-of-life care does not necessarily indicate misalignment with family preferences.

Nevertheless, the large cohort and decade–long observation period provide valuable insight into evolving practice patterns. Continued efforts to promote earlier referral, strengthen interdisciplinary education, and expand community resources are needed to further improve alignment between care delivery and family preferences. Overall, early, integrated PPC should be regarded as an essential element of high–quality pediatric healthcare [33].

Conclusions

Implementation of a shared pediatric palliative care model was associated with sustained improvements in advance care planning, interdisciplinary communication, and utilization of palliative services over time. Embedding palliative specialists within primary pediatric teams may contribute to reducing referral barriers and facilitating the integration of palliative care into routine clinical practice.

Despite these gains, the persistence of ICU deaths and high-intensity treatments underscores the influence of structural, cultural, and resource-related factors on end-of-life care patterns. Expanding community-based services, strengthening caregiver support, and promoting earlier referral strategies may be necessary to further align care delivery with family preferences.

In healthcare systems where pediatric palliative care has historically been underutilized, shared-care integration represents a feasible and scalable approach to improving care quality. Early and integrated PPC should be recognized as a fundamental component of high-quality pediatric healthcare.

These findings support the integration of pediatric palliative care as a core component of child health systems and may inform policy development in regions where services remain underutilized.
