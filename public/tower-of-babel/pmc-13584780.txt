Thermophysical Properties of [EMIM][SCN] in Hydroxy-Based Solvents: Combined Experimental and Theoretical Analysis
Authors: Malik Raihan Ahmad, Riyazuddeen, Mohammad Jane Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A comprehensive framework is presented for estimating the thermophysical properties, including densities (ρ), sound velocities (u), and dynamic viscosities (η) of [EMIM]­[SCN], 2-methoxyethanol (MEO), and 1-pentanol (PEN), and binary mixtures ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) at 1 atm pressure and various temperatures. To understand the molecular interactions and deviations in behavior, properties such as excess molar volumes (VE ), deviations in isentropic compressibilities (Δκs ), excess sound velocity (u E ) and dynamic viscosity deviations (Δη) were calculated. The derived parameters were fitted to the Redlich–Kister equation, yielding fitting coefficient parameters (Ai), standard deviations (σ), and correlation coefficients (R2), which indicate the quality and consistency of the experimental data. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis was conducted to elucidate potential interactions in these binary mixtures. Furthermore, the Prigogine-Flory-Patterson (PFP) theory was used to explain the potential excess-volumetric behavior and to forecast interaction trends in binary mixtures. Additionally, Density Functional Theory (DFT) was employed to study the electronic and intermolecular properties of pure and binary systems in terms of HOMO–LUMO gaps, reduced density gradient (RDG), noncovalent interactions (NCI), electrostatic potential (ESP) maps, and QTAIM analysis. For further analysis, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed, which give density, radial distribution function, mean square displacement (MSD), and self-diffusion coefficient (D). Taken together, these methods provide a detailed understanding of the thermophysical and molecular interactions in binary mixtures.

Introduction

Ionic liquids (ILs) are a type of salt that exists in a liquid state below 323.15 K. They are different from the common salt, like NaCl, which is symmetrical, tightly bound and efficiently packed, allowing it to remain in solid form up to 1000 K. In contrast, imidazolium-based ILs are asymmetrically packed, with greater intramolecular distance, which disrupts efficient crystal packing of the cation and anion, thereby lowering the melting point and contributing to their liquid phase at ambient temperature. , Besides their favorable thermophysical properties, ILs provide precise adjustability through the appropriate choice of cation and anion, earning the name “designer solvents”. Attention to imidazolium-based ILs has increased significantly due to their exceptional characteristics, including high thermal stability, negligible vapor pressure, wide liquidus range, wide chemical window, and designability. −

Over the past few decades, advances in IL-solvent systems interactions have improved the tunability of ILs, resulting in a significant increase in their applications across various scientific and industrial fields. Accordingly, the protic anion in ionic liquids, the increase in anion-solvent interaction has gained significant interest as a “green” solvent system for numerous industrial and medicinal applications, i.e., cellulose dissolutions, desulfurization, CO2 capture, pharmaceutical crystallization, catalysis, liquid–liquid extraction, and separation of an azeotropic mixture. ,

Thermodynamics and transport properties of pure ionic liquids and their binary mixtures are essential for developing accurate predictive models for industrial processes. In all these cases, properties such as densities (ρ), dynamic viscosities (η) and the speed of sound (u) are important properties for organizing and optimizing the various industrial processes. A comprehensive understanding of molecular behavior in a mixture is achieved by properly examining derived thermodynamic properties, such as excess molar volume, dynamic viscosity deviation, and excess isentropic compressibility.

Hydrocarbons and other nonpolar molecular solvents are widely used in industrial processes. In contrast, alcohols and amines, which are polar molecular solvents, exhibit unique intermolecular interactions with imidazolium-based ionic liquids. Molecular-level interactions are essential for understanding the behavior of key properties through experimental and theoretical methods. − ILs with the thiocyanate anion (SCN–) have attracted considerable attention because of their low viscosities. − Furthermore, they have been studied extensively across various industrial and research fields, including sulfur dioxide [SO2] capture processes, , electrolytes, − and separation processes. − Although several studies have been reported in the literature on IL, [EMIM]­[SCN] with different binary mixtures, − a critical examination indicates that each of these works is in a limited scope. Lladosa et al. measured the thermophysical properties of [EMIM]­[SCN] with small-chain alcohols but limited their analysis solely to experimental observables. Wang et al. elucidate the [EMIM]­[SCN] with propylene carbonate by investigating the excess properties. Weber and Kirchner introduced the ab initio MD simulations to explore the structural and dynamical behavior of pure [EMIM]­[SCN], but did not discuss the binary mixture. Wulf et al. and Yamada et al. implemented IR and Raman spectroscopy to explore the hydrogen bonding in [EMIM]­[SCN], yet without corroborating excess properties and/or DFT analysis. Domanska et al. report the density and excess molar volume of [EMIM]­[SCN] with water and alcohols, which exhibit deviations attributed to solvent-induced disruption of the ion-pair structure; however, no computational investigation has examined this interpretation at the molecular level. Consequently, none of the reported studies have specifically explored the possible molecular interaction and hydrogen-bonding network structure of 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium thiocyanate, [EMIM]­[SCN], with 2-methoxyethanol (MEO) and 1-pentanol (PEN) using experimental and computational approaches.

In this project, multiscale processes, including experimental measurements, MD simulations, and density functional theory calculations, have been performed to investigate the effects of the organic solvents MEO and PEN on the thermophysical properties of ionic liquid [EMIM]­[SCN]. The excess molar volume (V E ), excess speed of sound (u E ), isentropic compressibility deviations (Δκs ) and dynamic viscosity deviations (Δη) have been evaluated to study intermolecular-level interactions. The excess and deviation data have been fitted using the appropriate model to study its reliability. All derived parameters (V E , u E , Δκs , and Δη) have been fitted to the Redlich–Kister polynomial equation, and the data quality can be evaluated through the coefficient of determination (R2) and the standard deviation (σ). The Prigogine-Flory-Patterson theory has been employed to evaluate excess molar volume data and, subsequently, to calculate the interactional contribution, free volume, and internal pressure using the interaction parameter (χ 12). These interactions have been validated and are more clearly depicted through FTIR analysis. Density Functional Theory (DFT) has been employed to elucidate the microscopic structure and analyze the nature of interactions occurring in the ionic liquid-solvent binary system. To gain deeper insight and more quantitative information, different reactivity descriptors have been calculated. Visual analyses, such as reduced density gradient (RDG) and noncovalent interaction (NCI), are essential for deciphering molecular interactions. Mulliken charge analysis is presented to identify potential interaction sites by investigating the distribution of charges at individual atomic sites. Moreover, a Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM) analysis was performed in order to quantitatively analyze the electron density distribution at the bond critical points (BCPs), providing a detailed understanding of the nature and strength of interactions in the ionic liquid-organic solvent binary system. Topological parameters at the BCPs were calculated, namely the electron density (ρ(r)), its Laplacian of the electron density (∇2ρ­(r)), the total energy density (H­(r)), Electron localization Function (ELF), ellipticity (ε) and the Covalent Character Index (CVB, η) were computed to gain deeper insight into the characteristics and electronic around the binding site. A positive value of (∇2ρ­(r)) with a negative H­(r) value indicates a partially covalent character (as in hydrogen bonding), whereas both positive (∇2ρ­(r)) and H­(r) values indicate the nature of closed shell interactions of the electrostatic or van der Waals type. The ELF distribution and ellipticity values provide complementary information related to the degree of electron pairing and bond anisotropy, respectively, while the CVB (η index) offers a quantitative measure of the covalent contribution to the overall interaction. In this way, it is possible to have a whole characterization of the noncovalent interaction landscape within the studied mixtures at the atomic level. These descriptors then enable quantitative assessment among different types of noncovalent interactions that contribute to the microscopic nature of the studied mixtures. The MD simulation was carried out to analyze the bulk properties of the pure IL and the binary mixture with organic solvents. The MD enables understanding of how the structure changes over time, capturing the system’s dynamic behavior and linking structure and properties. Important properties, such as density, radial distribution function (RDF), spatial distribution function (SDF), and self-diffusion coefficients, describe the system’s properties and structure relationship.

Experimental Methods

Required Chemicals

The ionic liquid, [EMIM]­[SCN] (331717-63-6) with >99.00% purity, was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich without further purification. Additionally, 2-methoxyethanol (MEO) (109-86-4) and 1-pentanol (PEN) (71-41-0), with purities greater than 99.80% and 99.00%, respectively, were supplied by TCI and Sigma-Aldrich. The purities were confirmed by comparing the densities, dynamic viscosities, and sound velocities with literature data.

Equipment and Procedure

Sample Preparation

All the concerned binary mixtures were prepared carefully. The preparation of both binary mixtures ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) at different mole fractions were performed using the weighing method to ensure sample accuracy, employing a classic MS Mettler Toledo electronic digital balance with a precision of 1.0 × 10–4 grams. Every sample was prepared at least 24 h in advance to ensure the miscibility of the organic solvents with the IL.

Density and Sound Velocity Measurements

Density and sound velocity were measured using the DSA 5000 M digital vibrating tube density and sound velocity instrument. The instrument calibration was carried out using triply distilled water. The sample and instrument were kept at the working temperature with a resolution of 0.01 K. The instrument automatically corrects the viscosity-related error in density measurement. The combined expanded uncertainties found for the density and sound velocity at a confidence level of 0.95 and a coverage factor (k) of 2 are 1 is 1.0 × 10–4 (gm cm–3) and 0.5 (m s–1).

Dynamic Viscosity Measurement

Dynamic viscosities were measured using the DMA 2000, an automated falling-ball viscosity meter. The temperature is controlled to within 0.01 K by a Peltier thermostat. A broad range of dynamic viscosity measurements can be obtained using combinations of capillaries with different diameters, such as 1.59 mm, 1.8 mm, and 2.5 mm, spanning 1.0 mPa·s to 1700 mPa·s. The calibration of instruments at the specified temperatures is carried out in accordance with the relevant standard for the corresponding diameters, as detailed in the instrument’s manual. According to the manual, standards have been utilized, such as N-7.5 for 1.59 mm, N-26 for 1.8 mm, and N-100 for 2.5 mm capillaries. The expanded uncertainties for η measurements are determined to be Uc­(η) = 0.2 mPa·s for (<1 mPa.s), Uc­(η) = 0.6 mPa·s for (1 mPa·s to 10 mPa·s), Uc­(η) = 0.8 mPa·s for (10 mPa·s to 50 mPa·s) Uc­(η) = 0.90 mPa·s for (>50 mPa·s). All these expanded uncertainties are evaluated at a 95% confidence level and k = 2. The standard uncertainty in temperature measurement was found to be 0.01 K.

FTIR

A Bruker (Alpha II), Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer, equipped with ATR, was utilized to produce the IR spectra of pure IL ([EMIM]­[SCN]) and their binary mixtures ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN). The analysis was performed in the wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm–1 with a spectral resolution of 2 cm–1. A minimum of 32 scans were run to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

Computational Methods

Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations

All quantum-chemical calculations were carried out using DFT as implemented in Gaussian 09. The geometries of all the structural entities, i.e., ionic liquids and organic solvents, were optimized using the B3LYP functional with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction and Becke-Johnson (BJ) damping (B3LYP-D3BJ), which provides an enhanced treatment of noncovalent interactions. A polarizable-diffusion basis set 6–311++G­(d,p) was employed for all atoms to ensure an accurate representation of the effects. Geometry Optimization for all molecules was carried out under tight convergence criteria, and vibrational frequency analysis was conducted at the same level of theory to confirm the nature of the stationary points. The absence of imaginary frequencies indicates that all optimized structures correspond to local minima on the potential energy surface. Subsequently, thermodynamic parameters, i.e., thermal energy (Etherml), heat capacity (Cv), entropy (S), as well as dipole moments, global reactivity descriptors, and other important parameters, were calculated based on the optimized geometries. The descriptors were also used to analyze the reactivity, polarity, and interaction tendencies of the molecules in the IL binary mixture. ,

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

All MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS package. The equation of motion was integrated through the Velocity-Verlet algorithm with a 2.0 fs time step. The LINCS algorithm was used to constrain the covalent bond. The cutoff for the LJ and the Coulombic is set to 1.2 nm. The PME method has been used to model long-range electrostatic interactions. The Lorentz–Berthelot rules apply to cross-interactions between different types of atoms. The temperature, controlled via the V-rescale at 1 atm, is maintained using the Berendsen barostat with a time step of 2.0 ps, along with the periodic boundary conditions. All simulations were processed using the OPLS-AA force field, and molecular topologies were generated with the MKTOP script. The number of molecules used and the force field parameters of the simulated ionic liquid and organic solvents are listed in Tables S15 and S16 (Supporting Information). The MD simulation starts with energy minimization, which is implemented using the steepest-descent algorithm. Subsequently, a 10 ns temperature equilibrium simulation was performed in the NVT ensemble using the Berendsen algorithm. The pressure and density stabilization were achieved by performing a 10 ns simulation under the NPT ensemble, employing the Berendsen thermostat and Berendsen barostat with a relaxation time of 2 fs, while maintaining a reference pressure of 1 atm. Finally, a 20 ns production run was conducted using the velocity-rescaling thermostat and Berendsen barostat.

Results and Discussion

The densities (ρ), sound velocities (u) and the dynamic viscosities (η) of pure [EMIM]­[SCN], 2-methoxyethanol (MEO) and 1-pentanol (PEN) and their binary mixtures ([EMIM]­[SCN]+ MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) have been experimentally measured. These measurements were performed as a function of IL concentration at various temperatures, ranging from 298.15 to 323.15 K, under 1 atm pressure, and are presented in Tables S1–S4 (Supporting Information). Measurements of ρ, u, and η for the pure ionic liquid [EMIM]­[SCN] and two organic solvents, MEO and PEN, show very good agreement with literature data across all temperature ranges studied, as reported in Table S1. Depending on sample purity, instrument calibration, and the method employed by individual laboratories, agreement is generally within ±0.30% for density and sound velocity, but a slightly larger deviation was observed for dynamic viscosity (∼9.5% for [EMIM]­[SCN]). In the case of MEO, the density deviation is remarkably small (±0.30%), the sound velocity deviation is ±0.30%, and the dynamic viscosity deviation is up to ∼6.9% compared with the literature, which is attributable to differences in measurement techniques across studies. For PEN, the deviation in density and sound velocity is generally within ±6.14 and ±1.35%, respectively, with slightly larger viscosity deviation of (∼5.03%) arising from the differences in technique. It is clear that the thermophysical properties ρ, u, and η increase significantly with increasing IL concentration and decrease with increasing temperature. The thermal expansion coefficient (αp ) indicates the extent of temperature-induced expansion or contraction of fluids in all directions. The αp values for organic solvents are significantly higher than those of the ionic liquid, indicating greater thermally driven volumetric fluctuations in MEO and PEN compared to [EMIM]­[SCN]. It is revealing that organic solvents undergo greater contraction and expansion compared to the IL. Several derived properties, including excess and derived properties such as VE , Δκs , u E and Δη are calculated, using the experimental data, and reported in Tables S5–S8 (Supporting Information). The excess molar volume (V E ) has been calculated over the whole composition range at temperatures from 298.15 to 323.15 K, and the results are presented in Figure and Table S5 (Supporting Information) for the binary mixture of ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) shown in Figure a, whose values are negative over the whole range of temperature, as shown in Figure a, showing the strong heteronuclear interaction between the ionic liquid ions and MEO. These negative values arise from MEO molecules due to hydrogen bonding and ion-dipole interactions with the cation [EMIM]+ and the anion [SCN]-, which occupy the interstitial sites of the ionic liquid framework, leading to a net contraction in volume upon mixing. The magnitude of the VE decreases with increasing temperature, indicating that the specific heteronuclear interaction loosens as thermal motion increases in a binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO). In contrast, the case of ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) binary mixture, shown in Figure b, is positive throughout the whole mole fraction range at all temperatures, due to the imposed longer hydrophobic alkyl chain length of PEN, which disrupts the effect of molecular packing, reducing the ion-dipole interaction between the ionic liquid and the alcohol. Furthermore, the self-association of PEN molecules through PEN–PEN hydrogen bonding competes with [EMIM]­[SCN]-PEN heteronuclear interactions, thereby contributing to the observed volume expansion. For the ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) binary mixture, the magnitude of positive VE increases with temperature, reflecting the progressive disruption of the residual ionic liquid network at higher thermal energies.

The Prigogine-Flory-Patterson (PFP) model exhibits different interaction behaviors over a temperature range of 298.15 to 323.15 K for the excess molar volume (VE) in the binary mixtures ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN). For the ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) system, PFP and experimental results for V E show negative V E values, indicating consistent agreement between the two across all temperatures, supporting the accuracy of the PFP in modeling the volumetric contraction caused by ion-dipole and hydrogen-bond interactions involving the imidazolium cation, [SCN] anion, and hydroxyl/ether sites MEO. Among all three PFP contributions, the interactional contribution plays a significant role for the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO), which signifies the negative VE through the strong IL-MEO interaction due to hydrogen-bonding between [SCN]- and the MEO. The free volume V(fv)E is used as the principal contribution, which partially compensates for the volumetric reduction by reflecting in the enhancement to thermal disorder of the solvated system. In case of the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN), the free volume term V(fv)E is the dominant factor that derives the positive VE through the hydrophobic interaction by the pentyl chain length, which disrupts the ionic liquid-pen network are follow the self-association, while the V(int)E gives the partial compensating interaction at the polar site. Conversely, the ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) system exhibits VE values that are positive and closely matched via PFP, indicating a net expansion of volume due to the self-association of PEN and more effective packing induced by ions and ion-induced packing, compared to MEO. The greater free-volume contribution shows that, while PEN causes expansion via solvent-molecule networks formed by hydrogen bonding, the ionic liquid enables local electrostatic compression at the interface. In both ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN), with increasing temperature, the absolute value of χ 12 increases, indicating stronger interactions at higher temperatures. Opposite signs in V E values confirm a shift from dominant contraction (MEO) to dominant expansion (PEN). Overall, the PFP theory shows strong applicability at low IL concentrations in binary mixtures. The interaction parameters, χ 12 is only adjustable parameters in the chosen model PFP, which quantitatively measure the net interaction energy between unlike molecules (IL-Solvent) compared to like–like molecules (IL-IL and solvent–solvent). In case of the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO), a negative χ 12 was observed across the entire composition range, which indicates that the net IL-MEO cross-interactions, which primarily may be the hydrogen bonding between the MEO and [SCN]−, and ion-dipole interactions with [EMIM]+, are more favorable than the self-interactions of the pure component. In contrast, for the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN + PEN), observed positive χ 12 indicates that the net-cross interaction energy is unfavorable due to the long alkyl chain length of PEN, which disrupts the efficient molecular packing and self-association of PEN–PEN compared to IL-PEN contact, resulting in repulsive behavior, which is the cause of positive VE . Furthermore, the increase in χ 12, with rising temperature in the binary mixture, suggests that the thermodynamic force driving these interactions is enhanced as thermal energy increases in the MEO mixture. In the PEN binary mixture, the higher temperature enhanced the hydrophobic disruption of the IL framework, leads to an increase in the free-volume contribution VE.

FTIR Analysis

In the FTIR spectra of pure [EMIM]­[SCN], we observed both types of peaks associated with the imidazolium cation and thiocyanate anion. The peaks at 3144 cm–1 and 3091 cm–1 were attributed to C4,5-H and C2–H stretching vibrations, respectively. C–H bending and stretching vibrations are observed between 1470 cm–1 and 1350 cm–1, while the band occurring at 1165 cm–1 is due to stretching of CH3–N. The peaks at 2046 cm–1, 1567 cm–1, 619 cm–1, and 596 cm–1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of the thiocyanate (SCN), CN, C–S, and S–CN groups, respectively. FTIR spectra show specific transmittance bands in 2-methoxyethanol (MEO), indicating the presence of (O–H), symmetric and asymmetric stretching of (−CH3) and (−CH2−), as well as (C–O–C) and (C–OH). The strong broad band due to O–H stretching at 3400 cm–1 indicates significant intermolecular hydrogen bonding. The peaks at 2990 −2820 cm–1 result from asymmetric and symmetric stretching of the CH2 groups. A strong band at 1121 cm–1 is observed, with a band at 1057 cm–1 assigned to C–O–C and C–OH vibrations, confirming the presence of ether and alcoholic functional groups, respectively. PEN shows specific vibrational modes associated with its functional groups, including the hydroxyl (O–H) and methyl (C–H) groups. In pure PEN, the broad peak from 3200 cm–1 to 3500 cm–1 indicates O–H stretching vibrations, while the peaks between 2800 cm–1 and 3000 cm–1 result from C–H stretching vibrations of alkyl groups. Noncovalent interactions, such as inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding, have been further analyzed using the Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM), proposed by Richard Bader. , The covalent and noncovalent interactions are distinguished through the electron density (ρ) at the bond critical point (BCP). In the case of the noncovalent, the ρ value is small and (∇2ρ) more in compression of covalent interaction, the ρ value is more with less (∇2ρ) values. The local electronic energy density, (HBCP) was utilized for further clarification of covalent and noncovalent interactions. If the values of ∇2ρBCP < 0 and HBCP < 0 indicates the presence of a covalent interaction, if values ∇2ρBCP > 0 and HBCP > 0 shows the partial covalent interactions or presence of strong electrostatic interactions, while electrostatic interactions have ∇2ρBCP > 0 and HBCP < 0 are mainly electrostatic.

They show weak closed-shell interactions, characteristics of a hydrogen bond and an ionic salt bridge within the pure IL molecular framework. This decrease is much more pronounced in mixtures containing MEO and PEN due to the very high diffusion coefficient of pure organic solvents. Further, the addition of an ionic liquid introduces hydrogen bonding between the cation–anion and the cation-solvent, as explained later using the radial distribution functions between the relevant H atoms of these species. The relative trends of the self-diffusion coefficient of both the cation and anion rise monotonically upon decreasing IL mole fraction in both binary mixtures, which reflects the progressive dilution of the ionic network by organic solvent molecules. The D of MEO and PEN decreases with increasing ionic liquid concentration, consistent with increased viscosity of the mixture and the formation of hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl group of the solvent and [SCN]-, as confirmed by the RDF analysis. These outcomes are quantifiably supported by the simulation protocol and are consistent with the experimentally observed viscosity order reported in Table S3.

Conclusion

The experimentally measured values of densities (ρ), speed of sounds (u), and dynamic viscosities (η) for the pure ionic liquid (IL) and their binary mixtures ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) and ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) were systematically investigated across the entire mole fraction range and various temperatures. These measured thermophysical properties align well with existing literature, showing only slight deviations. All three measured experimental properties. They also decrease as temperature increases, with dynamic viscosity being particularly affected owing to the disruption of the hydrogen-bonding and Coulombic networks of the ionic liquid. The excess molar volume of the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO) was found to be negative across all mole fractions, indicating strong specific interaction enhanced packing, because of the hydrogen-bonding. In contrast, the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN) shows positive VE, indicating volume expansion upon mixing, which can lead to a reduction in interactions and the steric effect caused by the longer alkyl chain length of the PEN. The excess sound velocity has also been derived from the data, and it is positive across all temperatures and full mole fractions. Although their volumetric behaviors differ, both binary mixtures exhibit negative deviation in isentropic compressibility, indicating the formation of a denser, less compressible structure. Furthermore, the derived parameters were correlated using the Redlich–Kister polynomial, resulting in low standard deviations. The experimentally determined VE was further examined using the Prigogine-Flory-Patterson (PFP) theory. For the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + MEO), the interaction term predominates, reflecting strong IL-Solvent interactions, whereas for the binary mixture ([EMIM]­[SCN] + PEN), the free volume term is dominant, consistent with the hydrophobic expansion of the pentyl chain. Molecular dynamics simulations reliably reproduce experimental density data for pure substances and their binary mixtures, confirming the effectiveness of the OPLS-AA force field in modeling the thermodynamic properties of [EMIM]­[SCN]-alcohol systems in both pure and binary mixtures. The consistently negative excess molar volumes across all compositions indicate significant nonideal mixing, likely due to specific interactions. Radial and spatial distribution functions elucidate predominant hydrogen bonds between anion-alcohol and cation–anion, thereby clarifying the observed volume behaviors. Deviations in ionic liquid-rich areas and self-diffusion coefficients reveal the limitations of traditional force fields in accurately predicting transport properties. Additionally, Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations using the B3LYP-D3BJ functional level with the basis set 6–311++G­(d,p) confirm the dominance of noncovalent interactions, especially hydrogen bonding, through reactivity descriptors, NCI and RDG plots, along with BSSE-corrected interaction energies. Importantly, the cross-validation of FTIR spectral shifts, DFT-QTAIM bond critical point parameters and MD, using density, RDF and SDF, produces a well-coordinated, three-dimensional perspective across varied scales of observation: such as FT-IR measures the presence of a hydrogen bond at the macroscopic spectroscopic scale, DFT-QTAIM quantifies the strength, nature and covalent character of hydrogen bonds at the electronic level, and MD-RDF and SDF depict the spatial arrangement and persistence of hydrogen-bonds at the molecular level in both binary systems analyzed. Thus, the agreement of these three different approaches to assessing anion-alcohol hydrogen-bonding confirms that anion-alcohol hydrogen bonding is the dominant noncovalent interaction that governs the thermophysical nonideality of the two binary systems studied.
