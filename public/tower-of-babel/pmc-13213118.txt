Evaluating the Role of Subcoronal Botulinum Toxin Injection in the Management of Premature Ejaculation
Authors: Seyed Reza Hosseini, Mahdi Najarzadegan, Mohammad Mahdi Mehrabi, Hossein Chivaee, Abdolreza Mohammadi, Sara Ashtari, Fateme Guitynavard, Parsa Nikoufar, Seyed Mohammad Kazem Aghamir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Premature ejaculation (PE) is the most common male sexual dysfunction, which often causes significant psychological problems. Although botulinum toxin A (Botox) has shown promise for delaying ejaculation through chemodenervation, the optimal anatomical target remains uncertain. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of Botox injection into the penile prepuce and subcoronal region as a novel approach for lifelong PE.Methods:A prospective case series was conducted in 7 adult men (mean age 54.7 ± 6.5 years) diagnosed with lifelong PE (intravaginal ejaculatory latency time <1 minute, PEDT ⩾11). Participants received a single 100-unit Botox injection, administered subcutaneously into the prepuce and subcoronal region. The Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT) and Male Sexual Health Questionnaire (MSHQ) were completed before, 4 weeks, and 6 months after treatment to assess the efficacy.Results:Seven patients were included in the analysis. No significant changes were observed in PEDT scores at 4 weeks (18.00 ± 0.9; P = .7358) or at 6 months (17.71 ± 0.4; P = .762) compared with baseline. Similarly, MSHQ erection scores (5.00 ± 2.0 at 4 weeks; 4.85 ± 2.2 at 6 months), ejaculation scores (23.43 ± 3.8 at 4 weeks; 23.42 ± 3.1 at 6 months), and satisfaction scores (10.71 ± 4.07 at 4 weeks; 10.14 ± 3.5 at 6 months) did not differ significantly from baseline values (P > .05 for all comparisons). No adverse events or treatment-related side effects were reported throughout the study period.Conclusions:Subcoronal Botox injection was safe and well-tolerated, but it did not show significant improvements in ejaculatory control or sexual function scores. The findings suggest that sensory-targeted Botox administration alone is insufficient to enhance the ejaculatory reflex.Keywords: premature ejaculation, botulinum-A toxin, foreskin, botox, ejaculation

Introduction

Premature ejaculation (PE) is a common sexual dysfunction in men, defined as ejaculation occurring earlier than desired. 1 This is typically indicated by an intravaginal ejaculatory latency time (IELT) of 1 to 2 minutes or less, along with an inability to delay ejaculation during almost all instances of penetration, leading to personal distress.2,3 PE is classified as lifetime if it has been present since the individual’s first sexual experience or as acquired if it develops after a period of normal ejaculatory control. 4 PE affects a significant proportion of men worldwide, with epidemiological data indicating a global prevalence of 30% across various age groups. 5 Importantly, PE can have serious psychological and relational complications, as men and their partners often experience anxiety, frustration, and a diminished quality of life due to this condition. 6 Multiple studies have demonstrated that PE imposes a substantial psychosocial burden on affected individuals and their partners, leading to issues such as depression, relationship difficulties, and even a reluctance to engage in new relationships.6,7

Treatment of PE may include medications, behavioral strategies, and, in rare instances, surgery. First-line medical therapy typically consists of SSRIs, which act to delay ejaculation by increasing serotonergic activity.8,9 Dapoxetine, a short half-life SSRI, is the only drug currently approved for PE in many countries and has been proven to increase average IELT. 10 Other off-label SSRIs like paroxetine and sertraline can also be used, though side effects may affect compliance. 11 Additional medications, including tramadol and PDE5 inhibitors, may have benefits, particularly when used in combination with an SSRI in men with ED.12,13 Topical treatments, such as lidocaine-prilocaine creams, can reduce penile sensitivity and prolong IELT. 14 Behavioral and psychological approaches can be helpful, especially for performance anxiety. Surgical options exist for resistant cases but carry the risk of complications.14,15 Newer minimally invasive treatments, such as glans augmentation with hyaluronic acid fillers, show promise in delaying ejaculation. 16 The effective management of PE often requires an accommodated approach that includes SSRIs and other agents. 13

Botulinum toxin A (Botox) is being explored as a novel treatment for PE. By blocking the release of acetylcholine (ACh) at peripheral nerves, Botox causes temporary neuromuscular inhibition, which may delay ejaculation by reducing contractions of the genital muscles or sensory signaling.17,18 Several small clinical studies have examined Botox injections in various locations, such as the bulbospongiosus muscle, the penile root, and the ischiocavernosus muscles.19,20 Remarkably, recent research demonstrated that Botox injections resulted in significant short-term improvements in IELT and ejaculatory control at the 1-month follow-up. However, the benefits appeared to reduce between 3 and 6 months after treatment.18,21

Given the potential of Botox to delay ejaculation, our study investigates the penile prepuce (foreskin) as a new anatomical approach. The prepuce and adjacent subcoronal region contain dense sensory innervation, contributing to ejaculatory reflex sensitivity. 22 In principle, Botox injected subcutaneously under the prepuce may locally reduce sensory input during intercourse, related to how hyaluronic acid fillers above the glans nerve endings can blunt sensitivity. 21 To our knowledge, no prior study has specifically evaluated Botox injection in the prepuce for PE. Therefore, in this study, we conducted a prospective series in 7 men with primary PE, administering a defined dose of Botox into the foreskin and assessing changes in ejaculatory control.

Materials & Methods

Study Design

This was a prospective case series conducted to assess the efficacy and safety of subcutaneous Botox injection for the treatment of PE in adult males who had received all standard treatment approaches and remained resistant. All participants signed an informed consent form before enrollment. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed in the conduct of this study.

Participants

Eligible candidates had to meet the following: (1) age ⩾ 18 years and < 70 years; (2) history of lifelong PE, defined as IELT < 1 minutes in >90% of sexual encounters; (3) PEDT score ⩾ 11, indicating probable or severe PE; and (4) be involved in a stable heterosexual relationship with regular sexual activity of at least once a week. Exclusion criteria in the study included: (1) secondary PE due to erectile dysfunction, prostatitis, or thyroid disorders; (2) neurological or psychiatric disorders; (3) contraindications to Botox, including hypersensitivity, neuromuscular disorders, or concomitant use of aminoglycosides; (4) uncontrolled comorbidities, such as severe hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease; and (5) inability to complete questionnaires or follow-up assessments.

Data from structured interviews and medical records included the patients’ baseline characteristics: age, body mass index (BMI), underlying diseases, medications, frequency of hospital visits, smoking/alcohol history, and exercise.

Intervention

Patients received a single injection of 100 units of Botox (MASPORT® 500) diluted in 2 ml of normal saline. Injections were performed into the surrounding preputial tissue with a 30-gage insulin needle. The procedure was performed to target hypersensitive areas by modulating sensory nerve endings to reduce ejaculatory reflex hypersensitivity. An expert urologist performed injections in an outpatient setting. Patients were followed for 30 minutes post-procedure for any immediate adverse events.

Assessments

The efficacy of the treatment given was measured using validated questionnaires at baseline and 4 weeks after injection. The primary outcome was a change in PEDT score, a 5-item tool for assessing control over ejaculation, frequency of PE, minimal stimulation required, distress, and interpersonal difficulty. Scores for PEDT range from 0 to 20; a score ⩾ 11 indicates probable PE, with higher scores denoting greater severity.

Secondary outcomes were domain scores from the Male Sexual Health Questionnaire (MSHQ), a 16-item instrument divided into 3 domains:

Erection domain (questions 1-3; score range: 3-15; higher scores indicate better erectile function).

Erection domain (questions 1-3; score range: 3-15; higher scores indicate better erectile function).

Ejaculation domain (questions 1-7; score range: 7-35; higher scores reflect better ejaculatory function and control).

Ejaculation domain (questions 1-7; score range: 7-35; higher scores reflect better ejaculatory function and control).

Satisfaction domain (questions 1-6; score range: 6-30; higher scores indicate greater sexual satisfaction).

Satisfaction domain (questions 1-6; score range: 6-30; higher scores indicate greater sexual satisfaction).

Questionnaires were self-administered in a private setting. Both questionnaires were in the Farsi validated version. 23 Adverse events were recorded via patient self-report and clinical examination at follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Data were collected and entered into SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The domain scores for MSHQ and PEDT were calculated by summing the individual responses of items in that particular domain. Descriptive statistics included means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies/percentages for categorical variables. Changes in PEDT and MSHQ domain scores pre- and post-injection were evaluated with paired t-tests, and a 2-tailed p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The current study investigated the efficacy and safety of the injection of Botox into the penile subcoronal region for lifelong PE. In contrast to several previous studies that have targeted deeper perineal or penile musculature, our findings revealed no significant improvements in PEDT or MSHQ domain scores at 1 month. Such findings support the hypothesis that, unlike bulbospongiosus-targeted injections, preputial Botox administration might not significantly affect the ejaculatory reflex arc or sensory modulation to the degree necessary to alter ejaculatory latency.

Most of the previous trials and meta-analyses demonstrated that injecting Botox into the bulbospongiosus muscle leads to short-term improvements in IELT, which generally peak at 1 month and significantly decrease by 3 to 6 months.18,24,25 Typical increases ranged from 22 to 38 seconds in IELT, with some studies showing as much as 108% prolongation. 26 In contrast, the current study did not find any measurable improvement, even at 1 month. Similar to this study, Almekaty et al reported that injection of Botox into the bulbospongiosus muscle didn’t show efficacy compared to placebo. 19 This may be related to the anatomical and neurophysiological differences between the prepuce and the bulbospongiosus.

The divergent outcomes between subcoronal/preputial injection and bulbospongiosus-targeted injection may reflect the functional anatomy of the ejaculatory reflex. Ejaculation is coordinated by an integrated spinal reflex arc that includes afferent sensory input from the glans penis, frenulum, and prepuce via branches of the dorsal penile nerve, central processing within the lumbosacral spinal cord, and efferent autonomic and somatic output to the accessory glands and perineal striated muscles.27,28 Among these efferent structures, the bulbospongiosus muscle plays a direct mechanical role in the expulsion phase through rhythmic contractions that propel semen through the urethra. 29 Therefore, Botox injected into this muscle may delay ejaculation by reducing contractile strength or disrupting the somatic motor component of the reflex. In contrast, the subcoronal and preputial tissues are predominantly sensory structures with minimal muscular substrate. 22 Although these areas contain abundant mechanoreceptors and terminal nerve endings, superficial botulinum toxin administration is unlikely to sufficiently suppress the complex afferent signaling required to alter the central ejaculatory threshold meaningfully.30,31 Any reduction in local sensitivity may also be offset by preserved stimulation from the glans, frenulum, penile shaft, and psychogenic arousal pathways. This anatomical distinction suggests that motor modulation of the bulbospongiosus muscle may be a more biologically plausible therapeutic target than isolated sensory modulation at the subcoronal level.

The lack of change in PEDT and MSHQ scores in our cohort highlights that preputial sensory modulation alone is insufficient for achieving significant improvements in ejaculatory control. Although the mechanism of Botox to reduce cholinergic transmission could theoretically blunt afferent feedback, the scarcity of musculature in the prepuce limits its diffusion to relevant effector neurons. 32 Additionally, the vascularity of the prepuce may also accelerate the clearance of Botox, which would shorten the local bioavailability of the toxin at this site compared to intramuscular sites. In contrast, studies employing intramuscular injection reported sustained effects lasting up to 1 to 3 months, matching the duration of neuromuscular blockade known for the toxin. 33

Our results also align with recent meta-analyses that have questioned Botox’s clinical relevance for PE. Recently, Mubarak et al concluded that Botox produced modest, statistically marginal gains that were below the thresholds of patient-perceived benefit, and Guo et al, in a meta-analysis with a mean gain in IELT of 22.3 seconds at 1 month, reported no sustained improvement over time.24,34 These observations strongly suggest that there could be some intrinsic limitations in the biochemical action mechanism of Botox to tackle the complexities of ejaculatory control, which involves sensory, spinal, and cortical integration.4,6,19

Our study did not report any adverse event, unlike previous studies showing only mild, transient complications in 17.6% of participants.25,34 Lack of side effects may relate to the superficial plane of injection that did not cross either the periurethral or cavernosal areas. This shows that preputial Botox is a harmless procedure, but without effectiveness. The clinical implication, however, may be pivotal for future combinative use, for example, Botox with HA filler or subglanular microinjections, which can effectively suppress the sensory and reflex while not affecting its tolerability.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first investigation of subcutaneous botulinum toxin for the treatment of PE. Its novelty lies in a purely sensory-oriented approach, which differs from the motor-inhibitory paradigm reported in the existing literature. While the results of this study were negative, they significantly contribute to a deeper understanding of the sensory-motor dichotomy in ejaculatory control and highlight the importance of precise neuroanatomical targeting in future interventions. However, the study’s small sample size (n = 7) is a major limitation that prevents broad generalization of the findings. This small cohort limits statistical power and the robustness of the conclusions. Although the approach was novel, larger studies with more participants are necessary to verify the findings and explore the efficacy of sensory-targeted treatments for PE. Given Botox’s limited efficacy, even at optimal muscle sites, future research could investigate hybrid approaches such as co-administering Botox with serotonergic modulators or HA to potentially enhance both central and peripheral effects. Additionally, randomized controlled trials using objective IELT measurements and electromyographic monitoring of perineal muscle activity will be crucial in establishing dose–response relationships and duration–efficacy profiles. Moreover, mechanistic studies with neurophysiological mapping of sensory thresholds could offer greater insight into the neuroanatomical influence of various injection sites. This would allow for more rational site selection and better targeting of chemodenervation therapies in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Botox administration into the penile subcoronal region did not result in clinically significant improvements in PEDT or MSHQ domains among subjects with lifelong PE, in contrast to modest, temporary benefits following injections aimed at the bulbospongiosus. These findings imply that preputial and subcoronal sensory modulation through Botox is insufficient for ejaculatory delay and stress the importance of neurofunctionally relevant target selection. Although no efficacy was observed, excellent safety ensures further Botox investigations in combination with other modalities. These results help to refine the anatomical rationale for Botox use in PE and lead to a more mechanism-based approach toward future research.
