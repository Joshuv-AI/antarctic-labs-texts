The single-cell protein from Candida utilis as a functional protein ingredient for broilers
Authors: S. Tan, J.S. Ma, X.Y. Su, X.X. Xu, H.Q. Huang, T. Tu, F.F. Guan, H.Y. Luo, H.M. Yu, B. Yao, Y. Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The search for sustainable protein alternatives to soybean meal (SBM) in broilers diets has highlighted single-cell proteins as a promising candidate. Candida utilis protein (CUP) represents a potential substitute, yet its optimal inclusion level in broiler nutrition remains to be determined. A 42-day trial was conducted with 512 Arbor Acres broilers (1d old, initial body weights 45.00 ± 0.13 g) were purchased and randomly assigned to eight treatment groups. Treatment diets included 0% control group (CON), 1% CUP group (CUP1), 2% CUP group (CUP2), 3% CUP group (CUP3), 4% CUP group (CUP4), 5% CUP group (CUP5), 6% CUP group (CUP6) and 8% CUP group (CUP8). Growth performance exhibited a quadratic response (P < 0.001), with every phase average daily gain and average daily feed intake peaking at 4% inclusion and declining at 8%. Apparent digestibility of dry matter, crude protein and total amino acids was highest at the 3-4% inclusion levels (P < 0.05). Serum immunoglobulins immunoglobulin-Y (IgY), immunoglobulin-A (IgA), immunoglobulin-M (IgM), anti-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-10 (IL-10) and antioxidant enzyme activities superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) increased linearly with yeast supplementation (P < 0.001), whereas pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-2 (IL-2), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), C-reactive protein (CRP) and malondialdehyde (MDA) decreased linearly (P < 0.001). Spleen weight and abdominal fat percentage also rose with increasing inclusion. Microbial community analysis revealed numerical reductions in α diversity with increasing dietary CUP inclusion, whereas principal coordinate analysis demonstrated clear separation of bacterial communities across treatments, with the % CUP group showing the most pronounced deviation from the control group. These results demonstrate quadratic regression analysis using growth performance as the core indicator revealed a theoretically optimal CUP supplementation range of 1.6% to 2.5%. Combined with the measured application effects, the comprehensive recommended optimal inclusion level of CUP in broiler diets is 1.6% to 4.0%, with a recommended level of 4% for large-scale commercial production.

Introduction

Soybean meal (SBM) is a widely used and highly suitable protein source in broiler diets, characterized by high protein and energy contents, low crude fiber level, and a well-balanced amino acid (AA) profile that closely aligns with broiler nutritional requirements (CVB, 2021). However, the heavy reliance of China and many other countries on importing large quantities of SBM from South America has raised significant concerns regarding the environmental sustainability and supply security of birds meat production (Wangui et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). This dependence underscores the critical need to identify and validate sustainable alternative protein sources for the poultry industry. Single-cell proteins derived from microorganisms represent a promising solution. Candida utilis (syn. Cyberlindnera jadinii), in particular, stands out as a candidate with strong sustainability attributes. Its production requires minimal land input, features rapid cultivation cycles, and can utilize various renewable feedstocks, including agro-industrial by-products and wastes, thereby contributing to a sustainable bioeconomy (Salazar-López et al., 2022). For instance, studies have demonstrated the successful use of orange peel residues as a carbon source for C. utilis cultivation, yielding biomass with a high crude protein content and substantiating the efficacy of bioconversion strategies for agricultural residues (Carranza-Méndez et al., 2022; Kurcz et al., 2018). C. utilis is a well-recognized GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) organism with a long history of safe application in animal feed (Buerth et al., 2016). Nutritionally, C. utilis biomass possesses a high crude protein (38-56% on a dry matter basis, Cruz et al., 2020) and a favorable amino acid profile, with particularly high levels of lysine and threonine (Øverland et al., 2013). In terms of cultivation characteristics, C. utilis is a yeast species with broad temperature tolerance, acid resistance, and strong robustness under stress conditions (Buerth et al., 2016; Sousa-Silva et al., 2021). In addition to its fundamental nutrient contribution C. utilis biomass contains bioactive cell wall components, such as β-glucans and mannans, which have been shown to enhance gut health and immune function in broilers (Kyoung et al., 2023). The application potential of C. utilis biomass is further supported by its successful use as a partial replacement for conventional protein sources (e.g., fishmeal and SBM) in aquaculture and swine diets, with no adverse effects on growth performance and even demonstrated benefits for intestinal health (Øverland et al., 2013; Cruz et al., 2019; Grammes et al., 2019). To date, only two peer-reviewed studies have investigated the effects of dietary Candida utilis protein (CUP) supplementation in broiler chickens. One study demonstrated that dietary CUP inclusion at levels up to 10% exerted no adverse effects on growth performance (Cruz et al., 2020). In contrast, the second study, which drew its conclusions from alterations in growth performance and plasma metabolome profiles, proposed a potential upper inclusion limit of 4% CUP in broiler diets (Dayan et al., 2026).

Despite this promising background, a systematic, dose-response evaluation of CUP as an alternative to SBM across graded inclusion levels there is currently a paucity of data regarding its application in broiler formulations. Such an assessment is necessary to define not only its effects on growth performance but also its impact on nutrient digestibility, systemic immune and antioxidant status, and gut microbiota-especially at higher inclusion levels where bioactive cell wall components may exert more pronounced effects.

Therefore, this study comprehensively investigated the effects of dietary CUP supplementation at 0-8% inclusion levels on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, immune function, antioxidant status, and cecal microbiota and metabolites in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The ethics approval code is: IAS2025-221.

Nutrient composition and amino acid content of CUP

The C. utilis strain (CGMCC 2.615) was provided by the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center. This thermotolerant feed-grade yeast was used to produce CUP via submerged fermentation. The fermentation medium consisted of 2% glucose, 1% peptone, 0.5% yeast extract and 1% corn steep liquor, prepared on the basis of 1 ton of water. Medium pH was adjusted to 5.0-6.0, and sterilization was performed at 121°C for 15-20 min. Fermentation was conducted in a bioreactor with an inoculum size of 5% (v/v). The process lasted 60 h at 37°C, with an agitation speed of 200 rpm and an aeration rate of 1:1.5 (v/v/v). Fermentation was terminated when wet cell weight reached 300 g/L. The collected biomass was dried by centrifugal spray drying under the following conditions: inlet air temperature 160°C, outlet air temperature 80°C, and centrifugal speed 4000 rpm. The price of the CUP product used in experiment was 373 USD per ton (38% crude protein CUP, 9.82 USD per 1% CP). The market price of SBM on the reference date (462 USD per ton, 10.74 USD per 1% CP) was adopted as the baseline, and the CUP price was adjusted in line with changes in soybean meal price during the calculation.

Dry matter (DM) was determined following AOAC (2006) methods 930.15 and 925.10. Nitrogen content was measured by the Kjeldahl method AOAC (2006) using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Model KDY-9820, Shandong Haineng Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Dezhou, China); Crude protein (CP) was calculated as nitrogen × 6.25; Ether extract (EE) was measured according to AOAC (2006) method 954.02 using an automatic fat analyzer (Model SOX-406, Shandong Haineng Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.). The gross energy (GE) was measured according to the method of ISO 9831:1998 using a Parr 6400 automatic adiabatic calorimeter (Parr instrument company, Moline, IL). Phosphorus and Cal-ciumwere analyzed as per the method ISO17294-2: 2023. Amino acid composition of feed and fecal samples was determined per AOAC (2006) method 994.12 using an amino acid analyzer (Biochrom 30, UK). Amino acids were derivatized post-column with ninhydrin and quantified by absorbance at 570 nm and 440 nm. Sixteen amino acids were quantified based on chromatographic peak areas using OPENLAB software. Titanium dioxide (TiO₂, 0.2%) was used as an indigestible marker, TiO₂ content in feed and feces was determined according to the methods of (Short et al., 1996). The contents of β-glucans and mannans in CUP were determined according to the method described by NY/T 3477-2019 (2019). The change in reducing sugar content following the reaction of cell wall β-glucan and mannan with β-1,3-glucanase, cellulose (β-1,4-glucanase), β-1,3-1,4-glucanase and β-mannanase, respectively, was determined according to the method described by (Qi et al., 2004).

Broiler husbandry, experimental design, and dietary treatments

A total of 512 one-day-old male Arbor Acres (AA) broilers (initial body weights 45.00 ± 0.13 g) were purchased and randomly assigned to eight treatment groups. All broiler chicks were placed in a 3-tier-cage stainless steel barrier with one-side trough feeders. 8 replicates were allocated to each of the control and the CUP inclusion groups, for a total of 64 pens with 8 birds per pen. During the first 3 days, house temperature was maintained at 33°C, then reduced by 1°C every 2 days until reaching 25°C. Relative humidity was kept at 50-60 %. Feed and water were available ad libitum throughout the experiment. Lighting was provided 24 h for the first week after hatching, and, thereafter, a light pattern of 23 h light:1 h dark was adopted for the other experimental period.

The trial consisted of two phases: starter phase (days 1-21) and grower phase (days 22-42). Isonitrogenous diets were formulated for each feeding phase in accordance with the Chinese agricultural standard NY/T 33-2004 Nutritional Requirements of Chickens. All diets met or exceeded the full nutrient requirements of broilers, with the starter diet (d 1-21) provided in crumbled pellet form and the grower diet (d 22-42) in pelleted form. Within each phase, diets had identical levels of calcium, phosphorus, methionine, lysine, and threonine; the only variable was the inclusion level of CUP. TiO₂ was added as an indigestible marker for digestibility determinations. The experimental diets were formulated to contain graded levels of CUP at inclusion rates of 0% control group (CON), 1% CUP group (CUP1), 2% CUP group (CUP2), 3% CUP group (CUP3), 4% CUP group (CUP4), 5% CUP group (CUP5), 6% CUP group (CUP6) and 8% CUP group (CUP8), and all broilers were offered crumbled feed for the first 7 days, thereafter, pelleted diets were provided for the duration of the trial. Growth performance and economic evaluation

Body weight and feed consumption were recorded per replicate pen on days 1, 21 and 42, after 8-hour fast. Average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for starter, grower, and overall periods. Broilers were checked every day, and any birds that died or removed was weighed and used to correct for the ADFI and FCR accordingly.

For the economic benefit analysis for broiler feed and products were conducted based on prevailing local market prices and final body weight of broilers at the end of the experimental period. The calculation formulas for each economic indicator are specified as follows: Feed cost per kg of body weight gain (USD/kg gain) = (Diet cost per tonne / 1000) × FCR; Market revenue per bird (USD/bird) = Final live body weight × Market price of live broilers (USD/kg); Net profit per live bird (USD/bird) = Market revenue per bird - Total cost per bird (feed cost + day-old chick cost); Return on investment (ROI) ratio = Net profit per bird in the treatment group / Net profit per bird in the control group.

Sample collection

To determine CP and amino acid apparent digestibility, broilers had ad libitum access to feed throughout excreta samples collection. The indicator TiO2 was used in diets during all period, excreta samples for each cages were collected 2 days before the 21st and 42nd days. Excreta samples were lightly sprayed with 10 % HCl every 12 hours to fix volatile nitrogen and minimize nitrogen loss. Foreign material (feathers, scurf, spilled feed) was carefully removed. After collection, all excreta samples from each pen were mixed well, placed in Petri dishes and dried in a forced-air oven at 65°C. Dried samples were equilibrated for 24 at ambient temperature before weighing, then ground in a mill, passed through a 40-mesh screen (0.45-mm aperture), and stored in airtight bags at -20°C until analysis (Ma et al., 2024). On day 42, one bird per pen closest to the mean body weight (64 birds total) was selected, weighed, and blood samples was collected via jugular venipuncture using anticoagulant-free and heparin sodium vacuum tube. The samples in anticoagulant-free vacuum tube were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min to obtain serum (Shan et al., 2024). Serum and whole-blood samples were stored at 4°C for later analyses of immunoglobulins, antioxidant indices, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and T-cell subpopulations.

The birds were sacrificed by jugular exsanguination following euthanasia by CO2 inhalation. Organs (liver, spleen, bursa of Fabricius, and intestine) were excised intact and weighed to calculate immune organ indices (organ weight relative to live body weight at slaughter, Wang et al., 2025) and abdominal fat rate were determined following the methods specified in NY/T 823-2020 (2020). Cecal digesta samples were collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for microbiota and mycobiota analysis.

Apparent digestibility for nutrient

Apparent digestibility of DM, CP, and amino acids was calculated as: Apparent Digestibility (%) = [1 - (TiO₂ in feed / TiO₂ in excreta) × (Nutrient in excreta / Nutrient in feed)] × 100, where TiO₂ in feed and excreta represent absorbance values at 410 nm of feed and excreta samples as dry-matter basis, and nutrient in feed and excreta denote concentrations of the relevant nutrient in feed and excreta as dry-matter basis. Given the lack of experimentally determined metabolizable energy (ME) data for CUP, the ME value of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a species belonging to the same family Saccharomycetaceae as CUP, was adopted as a surrogate value for CUP in the present study.

Hematological parameters

Collected serum samples were used for the following measurements. Serum immunoglobulins, including immunoglobulin Y (IgY), immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin M (IgM), were determined using a Mindray BS-420 automated biochemistry analyzer (Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with HY-NE456 and HY-753 assay kits (Zhongsheng Beikong Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Inflammatory cytokines, namely interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), C-reactive protein (CRP) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Huaying Biological Technology Research Institute, Beijing, China). For antioxidant parameters, malondialdehyde (MDA) content and the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), superoxide dismutase (SOD), as well as total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), were assessed using colorimetric assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Absorbance values were read at 532 nm for MDA, 412 nm for GSH-Px, 550 nm for SOD, and 405 nm for T-AOC on a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China). Collected plasma samples were used for immunophenotyping analysis. Lymphocytes from whole blood were labeled with fluorescent-conjugated monoclonal antibodies targeting chicken CD3⁺ (Mouse Anti-Chicken CD3-AF647, 8200-31), CD4⁺ (Mouse Anti-Chicken CD4-PE, 8210-09), and CD8⁺ (Mouse Anti-Chicken CD8α-AF488, 8220-30) surface markers, and the proportions of T lymphocyte subpopulations were quantified by multicolor flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Composition and diversity of cecum microbiota and mycobiota

Cecal digesta samples were collected from 42-day-old broilers immediately after slaughter, transferred to cryopreservation tubes, and stored at -80°C until intestinal metagenomic analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal contents using the TIANMicrobe Magnetic Envir-DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. High-throughput sequencing was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) on the IlluminaNovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Prior to library construction, genomic DNA was sheared to an average insert size of 350 bp using a Covaris M220 focused ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). Paired-end libraries were prepared using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Raw sequencing reads were first processed with fastp (v0.23.0) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases (Phred quality score < 20), and reads containing ambiguous bases. To eliminate host DNA contamination, filtered reads were aligned against the birds reference genome (Gallus gallus GRCg6a, GCA_000002315.5) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5), and non-host reads were retained for downstream analysis. De novo assembly of clean reads was performed using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9; Li et al., 2015). Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from assembled contigs using Prodigal (v2.6.3; Hyatt et al., 2010) in metagenomic mode. A non-redundant gene catalog was constructed from predicted ORFs using CD-HIT (v4.6.1; Fu et al., 2012) with a 95% sequence identity threshold. Gene abundance profiles were generated by mapping high-quality reads back to the non-redundant catalog using SOAPaligner (v2.21; Li et al., 2008) with a 95% identity cutoff. Taxonomic annotation of non-redundant genes was performed by aligning amino acid sequences against the NCBI non-redundant (NR) protein database using DIAMOND (v0.8.35; Buchfink et al., 2015) with an E-value cutoff of ≤ 1e-5. Functional annotations were obtained by aligning sequences against the Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Carbohydrate-Active enZYmes (CAZy), Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD), and Pathogen-Host Interactions (PHI) databases using the same parameters. Differential abundance analysis at the taxonomic, functional, and gene levels was conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Statistical analysis

Data were collated in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using JMP-pro software (version19.0.0, SAS, Cary, NC). Outliers were removed using the mean ± 3 standard deviation (SD) criterion prior to statistical analysis. All data were subjected to one-way ANOVA, and Tukey's Honest Significant Difference test was used for post hoc comparisons when significant differences (P < 0.05) were detected. Linear and quadratic regression analyses were performed to determine the dose-response relationship between dietary CUP levels and growth performance, which served as the key evaluation indicator for optimizing CUP supplementation in broiler diets. Graphical representations of data were generated using GraphPad Prism (v9.5.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For metagenomic analysis, the control group, the group with the best growth performance, and the group with the worst growth performance were selected as typical representatives to analyze the differences in cecal microbiota among the three groups. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed and visualized using R (v3.2.5). Differentially abundant features were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score > 3.0.

Specifically, a quadratic regression model was constructed using the quadratic regression procedure to further elucidate the dose-response relationship between dietary CUP levels and growth performance, the primary endpoint for determining optimal CUP inclusion rates. The quadratic regression model was defined as:Y=aX2+bX+cwhere Y is the measured value of the growth performance indicator, X is the dietary CUP supplementation level (%), a is the quadratic coefficient, b is the linear coefficient, and c is the constant term. The optimal CUP supplementation level was calculated as the axis of symmetry of the quadratic curve: X0=b/−2a.

Results

Comparison of nutritional composition between Candida utilis protein and soybean meal

Table 1 compares the nutritional composition of CUP and SBM. The crude protein content of CUP was 38.30%, and the crude fat content was 6.67%, which was markedly higher than that of SBM (1.50%). The total phosphorus content of CUP (1.62%) was higher than that of SBM (0.78%), and the gross energy of CUP (4,532 kcal/kg) was also higher than that of SBM (4,190 kcal/kg). In addition, the anti-nutritional factors in the yeast cell wall of CUP included β-glucan at 23.60% and mannan at 5.10%.

Immunoglobulin concentrations rose significantly with higher CUP inclusion (P < 0.05), with values in the CUP8 group significantly greater than those in the CON group. By contrast, pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations decreased markedly with increasing dietary CUP levels (P < 0.001). In parallel, the concentrations of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 increased significantly in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.001).

For antioxidant capacity (detailed in Table 9), the activities of SOD and GSH-Px, MDA content, and T-AOC all showed highly significant quadratic responses to graded dietary CUP levels (P < 0.001). Compared with the CON group, SOD and GSH-Px activities and T-AOC increased linearly with rising CUP supplementation (P < 0.001), while MDA content followed a significant linear decrease (P < 0.001).

Discussion

Numerous studies have confirmed that yeast-derived microbial proteins improve broiler growth performance and intestinal health, but variations in strains, fermentation processes, and purification methods result in poor comparability of results. As a type of single-cell protein, CUP has advantages such as higher crude fat content and shorter production cycle compared with soybean meal. Its cell wall polysaccharides can regulate animal immunity and intestinal health, making it a high-quality alternative raw material for soybean meal reduction and substitution. The CUP used in this study its optimal supplementation level in broiler diets remains unstandardized. Combining the model-derived range and empirical peak value allows precise determination of the CUP supplementation threshold. These findings align with Cruz et al. (2020), who reported no adverse effects on performance when substituting CUP for 5% soybean meal. While their study established a baseline at 5% replacement, our systematic titration demonstrates that 4% inclusion optimizes growth and nutrient utilization. Conversely, higher inclusion levels depressed body weight, primarily due to thereduction of feed intake. This decline is likely attributable to reduced diet palatability (Grigore et al., 2025) and the anti-nutritional effects of yeast cell-wall non-starch polysaccharides (NSPs), specifically β-glucans and mannans (Kogan & Kocher, 2007). With respect to palatability, the high nucleic acid content of yeast biomass is another key factor influencing palatability. The nucleic acid content of yeast protein is approximately 3-9% (Edozien et al., 1970; Castro et al., 1971; Zee and Simard, 1975) on a dry matter basis. Metabolism of nucleic acids produces uric acid (Cruz et al., 2020), which increases metabolic burden in young chicks and indirectly suppresses feed intake at high inclusion levels. Furthermore, while nucleic acids function as umami-tasting compounds, excessive levels reduce feed preference in broilers (Yoshida et al., 2022). Additionally, the fine particulate nature of yeast biomass in CUP alters feed pellet texture. Relative to conventional protein sources, the yeast fraction generates greater friction and elevated temperatures within the pellet mill during processing, which modifies pellet durability index and in turn impairs feed palatability (Cruz et al., 2020). Although β-glucan concentrations in the CUP8 diet remained below the growth-inhibitory threshold reported for cereal-derived β-glucans (Choct et al., 1990), growth performance was still impaired. This observation indicates that the biological impact of NSPs is shaped by molecular structure as much as by concentration. Unlike the linear β-(1,3)-(1,4) polymers present in cereals, yeast β-glucans are insoluble, branched structures consisting of β-(1,3) and β-(1,6) linkages that function primarily as immunomodulators (Yu et al., 2018). Furthermore, in vitro evidence that exogenous β-(1,3)-glucanase and β-mannanase can effectively degrade CUP cell wall components (Fig. S1) suggests that enzyme supplementation may hydrolyze β-glucan and mannan, thereby mitigating the anti-nutritional properties of CUP.

Beyond feed intake limitations, high CUP levels altered nutrient utilization. While most nutrient digestibilities peaked at 4%, but in starter phase methionine digestibility declined linearly with increasing inclusion. Given that methionine is the first limiting amino acid in poultry, the drop from 93.70% (control) to 79.00% (CUP8) represents a critical nutritional bottleneck, likely caused by yeast cell-wall components hindering amino acid absorption (Rodríguez et al., 2013). Therefore, supplemental crystalline methionine may be necessary to overcome this deficiency when high levels of CUP are included in broiler diets.

Dietary CUP supplementation linearly enhanced broiler immune and antioxidant indices. The observed increases in serum immunoglobulins (IgY, IgA, IgM) confirm the robust immunomodulatory properties of yeast cell walls. At the mechanistic level, mannans exposed on the outer layer of CUP cell walls act as pathogen-associated molecular patterns, which are recognized by pattern recognition receptors on gut-resident immune cells including macrophages and dendritic cells. This recognition triggers a non-infectious, feed-induced immune response that diverts metabolic energy and nutrients away from growth toward immune system maintenance, rather than serving a protective function against pathogens (Kiarie et al., 2022). This shift reflects a systemic low-grade inflammatory state rather than acute bacterial inflammation, manifesting as Th1 suppression, Th2-biased immune deviation, and chronic low-grade allergic reactions. This immune deviation is characterized by elevated IL-4 and IL-10 levels, which enhance humoral immunity while suppressing Th1-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-2 and TNF-α (Moore et al., 2001). As IL-2 is indispensable for IFN-γ synthesis (Schroder et al., 2004), the downregulation of IL-2 directly accounts for the reduced IFN-γ concentrations. Meanwhile, the increased activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GSH-Px) together with reduced MDA levels further support the immunomodulatory and protective effects of CUP (Sheikh et al., 2020; Teng et al., 2023).

The cecal microbiota underwent a dose-dependent restructuring, with Bacteroides abundance increasing progressively with dietary inclusion levels. Gemmiger is primarily responsible for fiber degradation and short-chain fatty acid production. These taxonomic changes fundamentally altered the potential functions of the microbiome (Yin et al., 2023). This shift reflects an adaptive specialization toward degrading yeast glycans, facilitated by the extensive carbohydrate-active enzyme repertoire of Bacteroides (Lapébie et al., 2019). Although the increased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F / B) ratio is traditionally associated with enhanced energy harvesting (Salaheen et al., 2017), its coincidence here with reduced growth suggests the microbiota was specialized for yeast polysaccharide fermentation rather than efficient energy extraction from the basal diet. Moreover, the enrichment of anaerobic taxa (e.g., Anaeromassilibacillus) and the dominance of supplemented yeast in the mycobiota underscore a multi-kingdom ecological shift. The proliferation of eukaryotic protists like Blastocystis in the CUP8 group warrants further study regarding its impact on gut homeostasis (Nash et al., 2017), indicating that high-level CUP supplementation significantly reshaped the cecal microbiota structure of broilers.

Overall, CUP is a valuable protein source in broiler nutrition. At 4% inclusion, it provides balanced protein nutrition and immunomodulatory benefits while supporting a favorable gut microbiota, resulting in optimal growth. At higher levels, its role shifts toward that of a bioactive immunostimulant and antioxidant, but this is offset by metabolic costs, reduced FI, impaired methionine digestibility, and profound microbial restructuring. These trade-offs establish 4% as the practical inclusion ceiling for balancing productivity and metabolic health. Some limitations should be noted. First, the metabolizable energy value of CUP was estimated from data for Saccharomyces cerevisiae in the Chinese Tables of Feed Composition and Nutritive Values; a dedicated assay is needed to confirm energy values. Second, the industrial-grade CUP used in this study had a CP content of 38%, a level lower than the 50% CP measured in laboratory-cultivated CUP. By contrast, the EE content of the industrial product reached 6%, which was higher than that of its laboratory-cultivated counterpart. The possible reasons for this result are the differences in the C. utilis culture medium and the unoptimized fermentation time. These factors should be considered when interpreting dose-response outcomes. Future research should focus on strategies such as methionine supplementation or synergistic use of cell-wall-degrading enzymes to mitigate nutritional constraints at high inclusion levels, and further elucidate the long-term consequences of CUP-induced microbial shifts.

Conclusion

Under the experimental conditions of this study, quadratic regression analysis based on growth performance yielded a theoretically optimal dietary CUP inclusion range of 1.6% (based on d 1-21 BW) to 2.5% (based on d 1-21 FCR) for broilers. Nevertheless, supplementation with 4% CUP conferred additional functional benefits: it improved the apparent digestibility of dry matter, crude protein and amino acids, attenuated systemic inflammatory responses, and enhanced both humoral immunity and antioxidant capacity. Taking into account growth performance, immune-enhancing efficacy, and the marginal economic benefit of feed costs, a comprehensive recommended inclusion range of 1.6% to 4.0% is proposed for CUP in broiler diets over the entire 1-42 day rearing period. More specifically, an inclusion level of 2.5% is optimal for pursuing the best feed-to-gain ratio, while 4.0% is recommended to achieve maximum comprehensive production performance. For commercial flocks subjected to high-density breeding stress, the 2.5% inclusion level is advised to maintain intestinal microecological stability. For routine large-scale commercial production, a 4% dietary CUP supplementation level is recommended as the practical application standard.
