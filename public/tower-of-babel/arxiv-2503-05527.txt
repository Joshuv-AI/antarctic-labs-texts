Outer space and finiteness properties for symmetric automorphisms of RAAGs, and generalisations
Gabriel Corrigan

Abstract
. We define the symmetric (outer) automorphism group of a right-angled Artin group and construct for it a (spine of) Outer space. This ‘symmetric spine’ is a contractible cube complex upon which the symmetric outer automorphism group acts properly and cocompactly. One artefact of our technique is a strengthening of the proof of contractibility of the untwisted spine, mimicking the original proof that Culler–Vogtmann Outer space is contractible, which may be of independent interest. We apply our results to derive finiteness properties for certain subgroups of outer automorphisms. In particular, we prove that the subgroup consisting of those outer automorphisms which permute any given finite set of conjugacy classes of a right-angled Artin group is of type VF , and we show that the virtual cohomological dimension of the symmetric outer automorphism group is equal to both the dimension of the symmetric spine and the rank of a free abelian subgroup.

20F65, 20F28, 20F36.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview of results

Given a finite simplicial graph
\Gamma
, define the associated
right-angled Artin group (RAAG)

A_{\Gamma}
by the presentation

A_{\Gamma}\coloneqq\left\langle V(\Gamma)\;\;|\;\;[a,b]=1\;\text{if}\;\{a,b\}\in E(\Gamma)\right\rangle.

Hence, when
\Gamma
has no edges, the corresponding right-angled Artin group is simply the free group of rank
|V(\Gamma)|
, and when
\Gamma
is a complete graph,
A_{\Gamma}\cong\smash{\mathbb{Z}^{|V(\Gamma)|}}
.

We define a
symmetric automorphism
of a right-angled Artin group
A_{\Gamma}
to be one which sends each generator to a conjugate of a generator, or a conjugate of the inverse of a generator. These form the
symmetric automorphism group

\operatorname{\Sigma Aut}(A_{\Gamma})\leq\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
, and its image under the quotient by inner automorphisms is the
symmetric outer automorphism group

\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})\leq\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
.

Conjectured and partially proved by H. Servatius
[
Ser89
]
, and confirmed by Laurence
[
Lau95
]
, is a well-known generating set of
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
. One family of these generators is the so-called
twists
. Any outer automorphism which can be written as a product of generators which are not twists is called
untwisted
, and these form the
untwisted subgroup

U(A_{\Gamma})\leq\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
. Every symmetric outer automorphism is untwisted, so
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})\leq U(A_{\Gamma})
.

This is germane, as Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann
[
CSV17
]
have constructed an
untwisted Outer space

\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}^{U}
(denoted
\Sigma_{\Gamma}
in
[
CSV17
]
), which is a contractible complex with a proper
U(A_{\Gamma})
-action, and generalises the influential
Culler–Vogtmann Outer space

CV_{n}

[
CV86
]
. Just like
CV_{n}
,
\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}^{U}
has a deformation retract called its
spine

K_{\Gamma}
, which naturally has the structure of a cube complex and has a proper and cocompact
U(A_{\Gamma})
-action. Our first theorem is an analogous result for the symmetric outer automorphism group.

Theorem A

(
7.8
)
.

There exists a contractible cube complex upon which
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
acts properly and cocompactly.

We call this complex the
symmetric spine
for
A_{\Gamma}
and denote it by
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
. It is a cube complex and is a natural
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
-equivariant deformation retract of a
symmetric Outer space

\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
. This is obtained by considering the cubes of
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
to be arbitrary rectilinear parallelepipeds, in precisely the same way that
K_{\Gamma}
embeds into
\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}^{U}
as an equivariant deformation retract.

This is a generalisation of a result of Collins
[
Col89
]
, who provided a subcomplex
K_{n}^{\Sigma}
of the spine
K_{n}
of
CV_{n}
. This allowed Collins to determine the
virtual cohomological dimension
(
vcd
) of the symmetric (outer) automorphism group of the free group
F_{n}
. Analogously, our second theorem shows that the
vcd
of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
matches the dimension of the symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
.

Theorem B

(
8.10
)
.

The virtual cohomological dimension
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
is equal to the dimension of the symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
.

1.2.
Context and discussion of proof strategy

The proof of
Theorem
A
transfers Collins’s work on the symmetric spine for free groups
[
Col89
]
to the setting of Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s spine of untwisted Outer space for right-angled Artin groups
[
CSV17
]
. Both these papers rest heavily on Culler–Vogtmann’s seminal paper
[
CV86
]
in which the original Outer space
CV_{n}
was introduced. We now briefly mention the similarities and differences of these papers with the present document; in §
5
we expand on this discussion more carefully.

The cube complex structure of
K_{\Gamma}
is profitably viewed as a union of stars of
marked Salvetti complexes
(see §
2.1
and
Definition
4.4
). For any graph
\Gamma
, the
Salvetti complex

\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
is a cube complex with fundamental group isomorphic to
A_{\Gamma}
(generalising the
n
-petalled rose, which is the Salvetti complex for the free group
F_{n}
), while a
marking
on a Salvetti complex can be thought of as a labelling of the generators of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma})
with the elements of a generating set of
A_{\Gamma}
. An automorphism
\varphi
of
A_{\Gamma}
acts on
K_{\Gamma}
by changing the marking. The symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is built using
symmetric marked Salvetti complexes
(
Definitions
3.7
&
4.4
).

Our strategy for proving the symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is contractible may be summarised as follows:

(i)

define a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
on marked Salvetti complexes such that every symmetric marked Salvetti complex has norm less than every non-symmetric marked Salvetti complex;

(ii)

adapt §6 of
[
CSV17
]
, which proves contractibility of the untwisted spine
K_{\Gamma}
, to our new norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
. The construction with this norm yields an intermediary complex
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}
, which we can deduce is contractible;

(iii)

adapt §4 of
[
Col89
]
, which retracts
K_{n}
to
K_{n}^{\Sigma}
, to the theory of
\Gamma
-complexes, defining a deformation retraction
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}\to K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
.

The overall architecture of our argument is heavily influenced by Collins’s proof of contractibility of the symmetric spine in the free group case, which this work generalises. However, we are forced to make one significant departure from Collins’s paper
[
Col89
]
, as we now describe.

A key part of Collins’s original argument relies on the original proof, and not just the statement, of contractibility of the spine
K_{n}
of Culler–Vogtmann Outer space given in
[
CV86
]
. Along the way, Culler and Vogtmann actually proved contractibility of a host of intermediary subcomplexes of
K_{n}
which are not strictly necessary for the proof that
K_{n}
is contractible. However, Collins observed that one of these by-products,
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
, was an ideal complex from which to define a deformation retraction to the symmetric spine
K_{n}^{\Sigma}
. In our setting, the analogue of
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
is the complex
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}
as in Step (ii) above. However, the proof of contractibility of the untwisted spine
K_{\Gamma}
by Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann
[
CSV17
]
is more direct than the analogous proof of Culler–Vogtmann, so we do not have a contractible
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}
for free.

Consequently, aside from the generalisation of Collins’s work to the setting of the untwisted spine, our main technical contribution is in defining, and proving contractibility of, intermediary subcomplexes of
K_{\Gamma}
analogous to those that appear in Culler–Vogtmann’s original proof. One of these intermediary subcomplexes is
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}
as in Step (ii) above.

This is achieved by the following theorem. Fix a finite simplicial graph
\Gamma
. For an arbitrary finite set
\mathcal{W}
of conjugacy classes of
A_{\Gamma}
, we define a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
which orders the marked Salvetti complexes lexicographically with respect to a certain ordered abelian group. We define the complex
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
to be the union of the stars of all marked Salvetti complexes with minimal first entry in this lexicographical ordering.

Theorem C

(
6.12
)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be an arbitrary finite set of conjugacy classes of
A_{\Gamma}
. Then
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
is contractible.

The complex
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}
in Step (ii) above is realised as one of these
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
.

Aside from the proof of
Theorem
A
, we give another application of
Theorem
C
. The proof of (
[
CV86
]
, Corollary 6.1.4) generalises seamlessly from free groups to any right-angled Artin group, once
Theorem
C
is established. For any finite set
\mathcal{W}
of conjugacy classes of a right-angled Artin group
A_{\Gamma}
, let
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
be the subgroup consisting of those outer automorphisms which permute the elements of
\mathcal{W}
. Recall that a group is of
type VF
if it has a finite-index subgroup
G
which has a finite
K(G,1)
-complex (see
Definition
2.7
for more detail).

Corollary D

(
6.13
)
.

For any finite set
\mathcal{W}
of conjugacy classes of a right-angled Artin group
A_{\Gamma}
, the group
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
is type
VF
.

It is worth remarking on the calculation of
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
. A case readily available for analogy is that of the untwisted subgroup,
U(A_{\Gamma})
. Millard–Vogtmann
[
MV19
]
found free abelian subgroups of
U(A_{\Gamma})
of rank
\rho_{\Gamma}
, the
principal rank
; hence this is a lower bound for
\textsc{vcd}(U(A_{\Gamma}))
. On the other hand, it is a standard theorem that if a discrete group
G
acts properly and cocompactly on a proper contractible complex
X
, then
\textsc{vcd}(G)\leq\dim(X)
. Therefore
\dim(K_{\Gamma})
is an upper bound for
\textsc{vcd}(U(A_{\Gamma}))
. However, Millard–Vogtmann also showed that for some graphs
\Gamma
, the principal rank
\rho_{\Gamma}
is strictly less than
\dim(K_{\Gamma})
. Hence at least one of two somewhat surprising things occurs:
\textsc{vcd}(U(A_{\Gamma}))
is not algebraically realised by the (naturally-generated) free abelian subgroups of
U(A_{\Gamma})
, or not geometrically realised by the (also natural) untwisted spine. It has also been shown that the gap between
\textsc{vcd}(U(A_{\Gamma}))
and
\dim(K_{\Gamma})
can be arbitrarily large
[
Cor25
]
. [At time of writing, we know of no graph
\Gamma
for which
\textsc{vcd}(U(A_{\Gamma}))\neq\rho_{\Gamma}
.] However, we show that there are no such intricacies in the case of the symmetric outer automorphism group
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
. Adapting techniques from
[
MV19
]
, we have the following.

Proposition E

(
8.7
&
8.9
)
.

Fix a right-angled Artin group
A_{\Gamma}
. Then

(i)

\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
is bounded below by the
symmetric principal rank

\rho_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
, which is the rank of a (naturally-generated) free abelian subgroup;

(ii)

the dimension of the symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is equal to the principal rank
\rho_{\Gamma}
.

Combining these two statements with the bound
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))\leq\dim(K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma})
yields
Theorem
B
. In particular,
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
is realised both algebraically, by a free abelian subgroup, and geometrically, by the dimension of the relevant ‘spine’ (as is the case for free groups, for example).

From private correspondence, we believe that independent upcoming work of Peio Ardaiz Gale and Conchita Martínez Pérez also shows, using different methods, that
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
is realised as the rank of a free abelian subgroup.

There exists an algorithm, due to Day–Sale–Wade
[
DSW19
]
, which computes the
vcd
of any
relative outer automorphism group (RORG)
of a right-angled Artin group. Examples of RORGs include
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
,
U(A_{\Gamma})
(virtually), and the
pure symmetric outer automorphism group

P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, which consists of those symmetric outer automorphisms that send each generator to a conjugate of itself. In the notation of
[
DSW19
]
,
P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is the relative outer automorphism group
\operatorname{Out}\left(A_{\Gamma};\;\mathcal{H}^{t}\right)
where
\mathcal{H}=\{\langle v\rangle\;\colon\;v\in V(\Gamma)\}
. Since
P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is a finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, we have
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))=\textsc{vcd}\left(P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})\right)
. Hence
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
(and by
Theorem
B
,
\dim(K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma})
) can be calculated using Day–Sale–Wade’s algorithm. We remark also that this algorithm has been implemented in Python by Yutong Dai; a script can be found on Wade’s website
[
Wad
]
. Neither that algorithm, nor our work, gives a closed form for
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
purely in terms of the properties of
\Gamma
.

Much study has been made of automorphisms of free products. With groundwork laid by Fouxe-Rabinovitch (
[
FR40
]
,
[
FR41
]
) and by work (
[
CZ84
]
,
[
McC86
]
,
[
Col89
]
) stimulated by Culler–Vogtmann’s Outer space
CV_{n}

[
CV86
]
in the context of work on symmetric automorphisms of free groups, McCullough and Miller
[
MM96
]
built a complex
K(G)
, somewhat analogous to
CV_{n}
, for studying the (outer) symmetric automorphism groups of a free product
G=G_{1}\ast\cdots\ast G_{k}
. This allowed them to conclude various cohomological finiteness results, among others. A generalisation of this is Guirardel–Levitt’s
Outer space for a free product

G_{1}\ast\cdots\ast G_{k}\ast F_{r}
, where each
G_{i}
is freely indecomposable and not infinite cyclic
[
GL07
]
. In another direction, Griffin
[
Gri13
]
introduced a moduli space of combinatorial objects called ‘cactus products’ and uses this to compute the integral homology of
\Sigma\operatorname{Aut}(G_{1}\ast\cdots\ast G_{k})
. If a right-angled Artin group can be written as a free product, all of these results will apply; however, note that a right-angled Artin group
A_{\Gamma}
splits as a free product if and only if
\Gamma
is disconnected.

2.
(Symmetric) Automorphisms of RAAGs and finiteness properties

In this section we provide the necessary preliminaries regarding right-angled Artin groups and their (symmetric) automorphisms, introduce basic graph-theoretic terminology and notation which will be employed throughout the paper, and recall some relevant finiteness properties of discrete groups.

2.1.
Right-angled Artin groups

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite simplicial graph (that is, it has no loops and no multi-edges) whose vertices are labelled. Write
V(\Gamma)
and
E(\Gamma)
for its vertex and edge sets, respectively. The associated
right-angled Artin group (RAAG)
, denoted
A_{\Gamma}
, is defined by the presentation

A_{\Gamma}=\langle V(\Gamma)\;\;|\;\;[a,b]=1\;\text{if}\;\{a,b\}\in E(\Gamma)\rangle.

\Gamma
is the
defining graph
for
A_{\Gamma}
. We will blur the distinction between a vertex of
\Gamma
and the corresponding generator of
A_{\Gamma}
, and will communicate the data of a RAAG simply by its defining graph.

Every Artin group has an associated cell complex called the
Salvetti complex

[
Sal87
]
. For a RAAG
A_{\Gamma}
, the Salvetti complex
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
is a cube complex with a particularly simple description, as follows. There is one 0-cell, to which is attached a directed 1-cell for each generator. Then for each edge
\{a,b\}
in
\Gamma
, we glue in a 2-torus along the corresponding commutator
aba^{-1}b^{-1}
. Continuing inductively, for each
k
-clique in
\Gamma
(which corresponds to a set of
k
mutually commuting generators), we glue in a
k
-torus whose constituent
(k-1)
-tori correspond to the subcliques of our
k
-clique which have
k-1
vertices.

For example, the Salvetti complex of the free group of rank
n
is simply the
n
-petalled rose graph, while the Salvetti complex of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is an
n
-torus. The Salvetti complex of a RAAG associated to a triangle-free graph is the presentation complex of the RAAG. Note that
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma})\cong A_{\Gamma}
. Charney and Davis proved
[
CD96
]
that
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
is a
K(A_{\Gamma},1)
space, resolving the
K(\pi,1)
conjecture for RAAGs; it follows that RAAGs are torsion-free and biautomatic (as shown by Niblo and Reeves
[
NR97
]
), for instance.

We refer the reader to Charney’s introduction to RAAGs for further details
[
Cha07
]
.

2.2.
Graph-theoretic preliminaries

In this section, we collect some notation and terminology regarding defining graphs of RAAGs which we shall use throughout. For more details and proofs, we refer to
[
CV09
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

For
\Gamma
a simplicial graph, the
link

\operatorname{lk}({v})
of a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
is the full subgraph spanned by the vertices adjacent to
v
. The
star
of
v
is the full subgraph spanned by
v
and all vertices adjacent to it; denote it
\operatorname{st}({v})
.

We define a relation
\leq
on the vertices of
\Gamma
by saying that
v\leq w
if
\operatorname{lk}({v})
is contained in
\operatorname{st}({w})
. We may define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
V(\Gamma)
by saying that
v\sim w
if both
v\leq w
and
w\leq v
. Then
\leq
is a partial order on the set of equivalence classes. We will write
[v]
for the equivalence class of
v
.

A vertex
v
is said to be
maximal
if its equivalence class is maximal with respect to this partial order, i.e., for all
v^{\prime}\in[v]
, there is no vertex
w\notin[v]
with
v^{\prime}\leq w
.

One way to have
v\leq w
is if
\operatorname{lk}({v})\subseteq\operatorname{lk}({w})
; in this case we write
v\leq_{\circ}w
. We write
v<_{\circ}w
if there is a strict containment
\operatorname{lk}({v})\subsetneq\operatorname{lk}({w})
; in this case we say that
w

dominates

v
.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
is
principal
if there is no
w\in V(\Gamma)
with
v<_{\circ}w
. Otherwise, we say that
v
is
non-principal
.

All maximal vertices are principal. As observed in
[
MV19
]
, not all principal vertices are maximal. For example, in the triangle with leaves at two of its vertices, the third vertex of the triangle is principal but not maximal.

v

Figure 1.
v
is a principal vertex but is not maximal.

2.3.
Culler–Vogtmann Outer space
CV_{n}
and its spine
K_{n}

Let
F_{n}
denote the free group of rank
n
. In
[
CV86
]
, Culler and Vogtmann introduced what is now called
Culler–Vogtmann Outer space
, a contractible complex
CV_{n}
which has a proper action of
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
.
CV_{n}
has a natural deformation retract
K_{n}
called its
spine
, which is a contractible cube complex with a proper and cocompact action of
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
. The spine
K_{n}
is a very good ‘picture’ of
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
: for example, Bridson and Vogtmann
[
BV01
]
proved that the group of simplicial automorphisms of
K_{n}
is precisely
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
.

For surveys of some of the applications of
CV_{n}
, we refer the reader to
[
Vog02
]
,
[
Vog18
]
,
[
Vog22
]
.

Generalising
CV_{n}
, Charney and Vogtmann (initially with Crisp
[
CCV07
]
, then with Stambaugh
[
CSV17
]
, and finally the full construction with Bregman
[
BCV23
]
) have produced an ‘Outer space’
\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}
for any RAAG
A_{\Gamma}
. In particular, there is an
untwisted Outer space

\mathcal{O}^{U}_{\Gamma}
, first constructed in
[
CSV17
]
, which is a contractible complex with a proper action of
U(A_{\Gamma})
. In direct analogy with
CV_{n}
retracting onto its spine
K_{n}
, untwisted Outer space also has a
spine
, denoted
K_{\Gamma}
. The construction of
K_{\Gamma}
shall be closely mirrored in §
4.2
, when we build the symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
.

2.4.
Finiteness properties of groups

We briefly recall the definition of
virtual cohomological dimension
and that of a group being of
type F
or
type VF
; the reader is referred to
[
Bro82
]
and
[
Geo08
]
for further details.

2.4.1.
Virtual cohomological dimension

Cohomological dimension may be defined for any unital nonzero commutative ring
R
, but we shall only be interested in the case
R=\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.4

(cf.
[
Bro82
]
,
Lemma VIII.2.1
)
.

Let
G
be a discrete group, and consider
\mathbb{Z}
as the trivial
\mathbb{Z}G
-module, where
\mathbb{Z}G
is the group ring. Define
\textsc{cd}(G)=\textsc{cd}_{\mathbb{Z}}(G)
, the
cohomological dimension
of
G
, to be the least integer
n
such that
\mathbb{Z}
admits a projective resolution of
\mathbb{Z}G
-modules of length
n
, if such
n
exists, or to be infinite otherwise.

Equivalently (and hence the nomenclature),
\textsc{cd}(G)
is the supremum of the set of
n
such that the cohomology group
H^{n}(G;M)
does not vanish, for some
\mathbb{Z}G
-module
M
.

Observe that the cohomological dimension of a group with torsion is always infinite, as all the even cohomology groups with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}G
are nonzero. However, we have the following result of Serre
[
Ser71
]
, which can be found in
Ch. VIII.3
of
[
Bro82
]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Serre)
.

Let
G
be virtually torsion-free. Then all finite-index subgroups of
G
have the same cohomological dimension.

Hence for a group
G
which contains a finite-index torsion-free subgroup
H
, the
virtual cohomological dimension
of
G
, defined to be
\textsc{vcd}(G)\coloneqq\textsc{cd}(H)
, is well-defined.

Charney and Vogtmann have shown
[
CV09
]
that for any finite simplicial graph
\Gamma
,
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is virtually torsion-free and has finite virtual cohomological dimension. Hence
\textsc{vcd}(\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
is also finite.

We have the following geometric control over the virtual cohomological dimension.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
Bro82
]
,
Theorem VIII.11.1
)
.

Let
G
act properly and cocompactly on a proper contractible CW-complex
X
. Then
\textsc{vcd}(G)\leq\dim(X)
.

2.4.2.
Type
F
and type
VF

Definition 2.7
.

A group
G
is of
type F
if there exists a finite aspherical CW-complex with fundamental group isomorphic to
G
. We say that a group is of
type VF
if it has a finite-index subgroup which is type
F
.

Being of type
F
is a very strong finiteness condition; in particular, it implies finite generation and finite presentability. Groups with torsion cannot be type
F
, so the strongest we can ask of them is to be of type
VF
(that is, ‘virtually type
F
’). A group of type
(V)F
always satisfies the homological finiteness properties of being
types (V)FL
and
(V)FP
; in particular a group of type
VF
has finite virtual cohomological dimension.

The following appears as Corollary 5.12 in
[
DW19
]
; it follows from
Theorem 7.3.4
of
[
Geo08
]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

If a group
G
acts simplicially and cocompactly on a contractible simplicial complex
X
such that all stabilisers are of type
F
, then
G
is of type
F
.

In particular, if
G
acts freely, simplicially, and cocompactly on a simplicial complex, then
G
is of type
F
.

In particular, the result that the untwisted subgroup
U(A_{\Gamma})
(see §
2.6
) acts properly and cocompactly on the untwisted spine
K_{\Gamma}

[
CSV17
]
proves that
U(A_{\Gamma})
is of type
VF
(and so
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is too, when
U(A_{\Gamma})
is finite-index in
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
). More recently, Day–Wade
[
DW19
]
proved that
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is type
VF
for every defining graph
\Gamma
.

2.5.
Symmetric automorphisms

Definition 2.9
.

We say that an automorphism of a RAAG
A_{\Gamma}
is
symmetric
if it sends every generator to a conjugate of a generator, or the conjugate of an inverse of a generator.

Observe that the composition of two symmetric automorphisms is again symmetric. We denote the
symmetric automorphism group
of
A_{\Gamma}
by
\operatorname{\Sigma Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
; this is the subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
consisting of all symmetric automorphisms.

We say that an outer automorphism
[\varphi]\in\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is
symmetric
if it has at least one symmetric representative
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
. The composition of two symmetric outer automorphisms will therefore have a symmetric representative, so we may form the
symmetric outer automorphism group
,
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
; this is the image of
\operatorname{\Sigma Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
under the quotient
\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
.

2.6.
\Gamma
-Whitehead partitions and automorphisms

Conjecture and partially proved by H. Servatius
[
Ser89
]
and completely proved by Laurence
[
Lau95
]
,
\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
has the following set of generators (and hence
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is generated by their images).

Theorem-Definition 2.10

(
[
Lau95
]
, Theorem 6.9)
.

For any
\Gamma
,
\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
is generated by the following set of
elementary transformations
.

(1)

graph symmetries
: an automorphism of
\Gamma
permutes the generators of
A_{\Gamma}
and thus induces an element of
\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
.

(2)

Inversions
: a map which sends a generator
a\mapsto a^{-1}
and fixes all other generators.

(3)

Transvections
: for two vertices
v,w
of
\Gamma
with
v\leq w
, the map which sends
v\mapsto vw
or
wv
and fixes all other generators is an automorphism. These are split into two types:

•

twists
, if
v
and
w
are adjacent (in which case
vw=wv
);

•

folds
if
v,w
are not adjacent. In particular,
v\mapsto vw
is a
right fold
, while
v\mapsto wv
is a
left fold
.

(4)

Partial conjugations
: for a vertex
v
, let
C
be a connected component of
\Gamma\setminus\operatorname{st}({v})
. The map which sends every generator
c\in C
to the conjugation
vcv^{-1}
and fixes all other generators is called a
partial conjugation
(Laurence and Servatius refer to these as
locally inner
automorphisms).

Folds and partial conjugations can be realised as products of
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms
, which are defined using so-called
\Gamma
-Whitehead partitions
.

Let
V=V(\Gamma)
and write
V^{\pm}=V\cup V^{-1}
. Define the
double
of
\Gamma
to be the graph
\Gamma^{\pm}
which has vertex set
V^{\pm}
and where two vertices are joined by an edge if they commute in
A_{\Gamma}
but are not inverses. A
\Gamma
-Whitehead partition

\mathcal{P}
based at a vertex
m\in V
is a partition of
V^{\pm}
into three sets: two
sides

P
,
\overline{P}
, and the
link

\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
. The link
\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
contains all vertices adjacent to
m
in
\Gamma^{\pm}
. We place
m
and
m^{-1}
in different sides. The connected components of
\Gamma^{\pm}\setminus\operatorname{st}({m})^{\pm}
are then distributed between the sides
P
and
\overline{P}
, subject to the condition that
P
and
\overline{P}
must each contain at least two vertices. We call the pair
\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
a
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair, and write
\mathcal{P}=\left(P\;|\;\overline{P}\;|\;\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})\right)
. We will often refer to
\Gamma
-Whitehead partitions as
\Gamma
-partitions
(or even just
partitions
when
\Gamma
is clear). Examples of
\Gamma
-Whitehead partitions are given in
Figure
2
.

If a vertex
v
and its inverse lie on different sides of a
\Gamma
-Whitehead partition
\mathcal{P}
, then we say that
\mathcal{P}

splits

v
. Note that if
\mathcal{P}
splits
v\neq m
, then
v
and
v^{-1}
must lie in different connected components of
\Gamma^{\pm}\setminus\operatorname{st}({m})^{\pm}
, which means that
\operatorname{lk}({v})\subseteq\operatorname{lk}({m})
.

Each
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair
\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
determines an automorphism
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
of
A_{\Gamma}
as follows.

•

If
\mathcal{P}
splits
v\neq m
, then:

–

if
v\in P
, then
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)\colon v\mapsto vm^{-1}
;

–

if
v\in\overline{P}
, then
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)\colon v\mapsto mv
.

•

If
\{v,v^{-1}\}\subseteq P
, then
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)\colon v\mapsto mvm^{-1}
.

•

For all other
v
(including
v=m
),
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)(v)=v
.

We call
m
the
multiplier
of the
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism
\varphi(\mathcal{P},m)
.

One may realise a fold
v\mapsto vm^{-1}
or
v\mapsto mv
by taking a
\Gamma
-Whitehead partition based at
m
with positive side
\{m,v\}
or
\{m,v^{-1}\}
respectively. Similarly, a partial conjugation of the connected component
C\subseteq\Gamma^{\pm}\setminus\operatorname{st}({m})^{\pm}
by
m
is realised by taking the positive side
\{m,C\}
. Every
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism is a product of folds and partial conjugations.

The group generated by
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms along with inversions is studied in
[
BCV24
]
, where it is denoted
U^{0}(A_{\Gamma})
. Adding in automorphisms induced by graph symmetries generates
U(A_{\Gamma})
, the
untwisted subgroup
. Further adding twists to this generates the full outer automorphism group
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
.

2.7.
The pure symmetric automorphism group

The
pure symmetric automorphism group
of
A_{\Gamma}
, denoted
P\Sigma\operatorname{Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
, is the finite-index subgroup of
\operatorname{\Sigma Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
which requires all generators to be sent to a conjugate of themselves—that is, it excludes graph symmetries and inversions. The image after taking the quotient by inner automorphisms is the
pure symmetric outer automorphism group

P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
. Also known as the
basis-conjugating (outer) automorphism group
, it has been studied by Toinet
[
Toi11
]
, Koban–Piggott
[
KP13
]
, and Day–Wade
[
DW18
]
; in particular, a presentation is given by the first two of these (while a generating set was given by Laurence
[
Lau95
]
).

As discussed in §
1.2
, the pure symmetric outer automorphism group is an example of a
relative outer automorphism group (RORG)
. The class of RORGs was introduced in full generality by Day and Wade
[
DW19
]
, although it encompassed many previously-studied examples. We refer the reader to (
[
DW19
]
, §6) for a more complete survey of the literature surrounding RORGs.

In
[
DW18
]
, Day–Wade gave necessary and sufficient conditions for when
P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is itself a RAAG, which gives a way to find examples of RAAGs whose outer automorphism groups are not
virtual duality groups
(see also
[
Wad23
]
and
[
Wie24
]
). This is in contrast to both ends of the RAAGs spectrum:
\operatorname{Out}(F_{n})
and
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
are both virtual duality groups.

Since
P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is a finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, these two groups have the same virtual cohomological dimension.

3.
Symmetric
\Gamma
-complexes

3.1.
Symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a
\Gamma
-partition is
symmetric
if it splits no vertices (other than its base).

Proposition 3.2
.

\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is generated by inversions, graph symmetries, and those
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms which correspond to symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions.

Proof.

It is clear that all inversions and graph symmetries are symmetric automorphisms.

Let
(\mathcal{P},m)
be a
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair with
\mathcal{P}
symmetric. By definition, a symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions splits no vertices other than its base, so for every
v\neq m
with
v\in P
, we have
v^{-1}\in P
. Hence the corresponding
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
sends
v\mapsto mvm^{-1}
for each
v\in P
, and fixes all other generators. Therefore
\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
is also a symmetric automorphism.

Hence the subgroup
H\leq\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
generated by the (images of) inversions, graph symmetries, and
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms defined by symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions consists entirely of symmetric automorphisms, so
H\leq\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
.

Let
[\psi]\in\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, and let
\psi\in\operatorname{\Sigma Aut}(A_{\Gamma})
be a symmetric representative for
[\psi]
. By composing
\psi
with a series of inversions, we obtain an automorphism
\psi^{\prime}
which does not send any element of
V
to an element of
V^{-1}
.

Writing
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
, we may therefore write
\psi^{\prime}(v_{i})=g_{i}v_{\pi(i)}g_{i}^{-1}
for each
i=1,\dots,n
, where
\pi
is some element of the symmetric group
S_{n}
.

Note that if
v
and
w
are adjacent vertices in
\Gamma
, then
\psi^{\prime}(v)
and
\psi^{\prime}(w)
must be (conjugates of) adjacent vertices of
\Gamma
. In other words, the permutation of the vertices of
\Gamma
induced by
\pi
must respect the adjacency relations in
\Gamma
—that is, it is realisable as a graph symmetry. Composing
\psi^{\prime}~
with the inverse of the automorphism of
A_{\Gamma}
induced by this graph symmetry yields an automorphism
\psi^{\prime\prime}
which sends each generator
v\in V
to a conjugate of itself.

Now
\psi^{\prime\prime}
is an element of the pure symmetric outer automorphism group
P\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
. By work of Laurence
[
Lau95
]
(see also Toinet
[
Toi11
]
and Koban–Piggott
[
KP13
]
), this group has a standard generating set consisting of partial conjugations
\varphi_{C}^{m}
, defined as follows:

\varphi_{C}^{m}(v)=\begin{cases}mvm^{-1}\quad\quad\;\text{if~$v\in C$;}\\
v\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

where
C
is a connected component of
\Gamma\setminus\operatorname{st}({m})
. Now,
\varphi_{C}^{m}
is precisely the
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism defined by the
\Gamma
-partition based at
m
which has positive side
\{m,C,C^{-1}\}
. This is a symmetric
\Gamma
-partition. Hence
\psi
is a product of inversions, graph symmetries, and
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms defined by symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions, so
[\psi]\in H
, whence we have
H=\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
as required.
∎

3.2.
\Gamma

We recall the construction of

3.2.1.
Special cube complexes and hyperplane collapses

\Gamma
-complexes are certain
special
cube complexes, with some extra structure. Recall that a
special cube complex
is one which is locally
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
and has no hyperplanes which self-intersect or are
one-sided
,
self-osculating
, or
inter-osculating
; we refer to the original article of Haglund and Wise for the definitions
[
HW08
]
.

For any hyperplane
H
of a special cube complex
X
, we define the edges
dual
to
H
to be those edges of
X
whose interior has non-empty intersection with
H
. We can define an
orientation
on
H
by giving a consistent choice of orientation to the edges dual to
H
.

In a special cube complex
X
, one may define the
hyperplane collapse

X\smash{\sslash}H
for any hyperplane
H
. This is a map
c_{H}\colon X\to X\smash{\sslash}H
which collapses
\kappa(H)
, the carrier of
H
, orthogonally onto
H
. For any collection
\mathcal{H}
of hyperplanes, we write
X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{H}
for the cube complex obtained by collapsing all the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{H}
(in any order). We will call
\mathcal{H}

acyclic
if the collapse map
c_{\mathcal{H}}\colon X\to X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{H}
is a homotopy equivalence. [The notation
X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{H}
is not intended to evoke other notions using the double slash, such as homotopy quotients or GIT quotients; we take our lead from e.g.
[
BCV24
]
.]

3.3.
\Gamma
-complexes

Definition 3.4
.

A cube complex
X
is called a
\Gamma
-complex
if it is isomorphic to the underlying cube complex of a (possibly empty) blowup
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
. A
blowup structure
on
X
is the data that specifies it as a particular blowup
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
: that is, each hyperplane is labelled by a vertex of
\Gamma
or by a
\Gamma
-partition, the hyperplanes labelled by vertices are oriented, and the
\Gamma
-partitions form a compatible set. Any blowup structure on
X
determines a
collapse map

c_{\Pi}\colon X\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
which collapses all hyperplanes labelled by
\Gamma
-partitions.

A
\Gamma
-complex may admit many blowup structures. For example, a
\Gamma
-complex for an edgeless
\Gamma
is a graph which may have many maximal trees—and for each maximal tree, the remaining edges can be labelled by vertices of
\Gamma
and oriented in any way.

Definition 3.5
.

A set of hyperplanes
\mathcal{T}
in a
\Gamma
-complex is called
treelike
if the hyperplane collapse
c_{\mathcal{T}}
yields a cube complex isomorphic to
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
.

Suppose we have a cube complex
X
which we do not know to be a
\Gamma
-complex
a priori
. To prove that
X
is in fact a
\Gamma
-complex, we must find an acyclic collection
\mathcal{T}
of hyperplanes for which the collapse
X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{T}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
. Choosing one such isomorphism gives labels and orientations to the remaining hyperplanes. Next we must prove that each hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{T}
actually determines a valid
\Gamma
-partition. To do this, we can perform a hyperplane collapse along all of
\mathcal{T}\setminus H
, which (if
\mathcal{T}
is to be treelike) should leave us with a complex with exactly two vertices which corresponds to a single blowup along the
\Gamma
-partition labelling
H
. As at the end of §
3.2.2
, we can then read off the (purported)
\Gamma
-partition from this complex, by looking at which edges originate and terminate at which vertex.

Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann
[
CSV17
]
gave a construction which ensures that if we can one find one such treelike set in a cube complex
X
then the
\Gamma
-partitions which label the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{T}
form a compatible set; moreover, any treelike set in
X
enjoys this property. We summarise this construction in the following proposition and its proof.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
X
be a
\Gamma
-complex and let
\mathcal{T}
be a treelike set of hyperplanes in
X
. Then there is some compatible set
\Pi
of
\Gamma
-partitions and an isomorphism
X\cong\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{T}
is the set of hyperplanes labelled by the elements of
\Pi
.

Proof.

For full details, we refer to Section 4 of
[
CSV17
]
.

A chosen isomorphism
X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{T}\cong\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
orients and labels the hyperplanes not in
\mathcal{T}
by vertices of
\Gamma
. Label each edge dual to
H\in\mathcal{T}
by
H
. The cube subcomplex
C\subseteq X
formed of all cubes that have all edge-labels in
\mathcal{T}
can be given the following explicit description: it is a union of products of graphs with subcomplexes of the Salvetti complex.
\mathcal{T}
being treelike corresponds to a maximal forest in the union of these graphs.

C
contains all the vertices of
X
. A hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{T}
disconnects
C
into two pieces—thus decomposing the vertices of
X
into two sets,
Z
and
\overline{Z}
. Define a
\Gamma
-partition
\mathcal{P}_{H}=\left(P\;|\;\overline{P}\;|\;\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})\right)
as follows:

•

if
H_{v}\cap H\neq\emptyset
, then
v,v^{-1}\in\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
;

•

if
H_{v}\cap H=\emptyset
, and an edge dual to
H_{v}
terminates in
Z
, then
v\in P
, while if it terminates in
\overline{Z}
, then
v\in\overline{P}
;

•

if
H_{v}\cap H=\emptyset
, and an edge dual to
H_{v}
originates in
Z
, then
v^{-1}\in P
, while if it terminates in
\overline{Z}
, then
v^{-1}\in\overline{P}
.

It turns out that
\mathcal{P}_{H}

is
a valid
\Gamma
-partition. Roughly, one can find a vertex
m\in V
which is split by
\mathcal{P}_{H}
for which
\operatorname{lk}({m})^{\pm}=\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}_{H}})
by showing that
v\in\operatorname{lk}({m})
if and only if
H_{v}\cap H\neq\emptyset
. Imposing treelikeness of
\mathcal{T}
forces each connected component of
\Gamma^{\pm}\setminus\operatorname{st}({m})^{\pm}
to have its vertices all on one side of
\mathcal{P}_{H}
.
\mathcal{P}_{H}
is forced to be thick by the isomorphism
X\smash{\sslash}T\cong\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
.

Moreover, the set of
\Gamma
-partitions constructed in this way form a compatible set
\Pi
, and we have
X\cong\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
.
∎

So, any treelike set of hyperplanes in a
\Gamma
-complex is the set of partitions labelled by
\Gamma
-partitions in at least one blowup structure. The blowup structure is only specified once we additionally assign labels and orientations to the hyperplanes not in the treelike set, and this assignation can be changed by inverting vertices or according to a graph symmetry of
\Gamma
. Moreover, changing these assignations will also change the
\Gamma
-partitions labelling the hyperplanes in the treelike set, by the same signed permutation of vertices.

3.4.
Symmetric
\Gamma
-complexes

We are now ready to define
symmetric

\Gamma
-complexes.

Fix
\Gamma
and a
\Gamma
-complex
X
. Give
X
a blowup structure
X\cong\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
, and let
\mathcal{T}
be the (treelike) set of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
labelled by
\Gamma
-partitions. As in the proof of
Proposition
3.6
, any
H\in\mathcal{T}
partitions the vertices of
X
into two sets, say
\beta(H)
and
\overline{\beta}(H)
, and this can be used to read off the
\Gamma
-partition
\mathcal{P}_{H}
.

Note that such a partition of the vertices of
X
into
\beta(H)

\sqcup

\overline{\beta}(H)
makes sense for any hyperplane
H
in any treelike set
\mathcal{T}
of hyperplanes of
X
, by
Proposition
3.6
: for a given treelike set, pick a blowup structure on
X
for which
\mathcal{T}
is precisely the set of partitions labelled by
\Gamma
-partitions.

Definition 3.7
.

We say that a
\Gamma
-complex
X
is
symmetric
if for any treelike set of hyperplanes
\mathcal{T}
in
X
, and for any
H\in\mathcal{T}
, the edges traversing between
\beta(H)
and
\overline{\beta}(H)
are dual to one of exactly two hyperplanes, one of which is
H
and the other of which is not in
\mathcal{T}
. That is, for each
H\in\mathcal{T}
, there is some fixed
H^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{T}
, and the edges traversing between
\beta(H)
and
\overline{\beta}(H)
are dual to either
H
or
H^{\prime}
.

Equivalently, we may define a
\Gamma
-complex
X
to be
symmetric
if every edge lies in a unique (up to homotopy) ‘simple’ non-null-homotopic loop, where by a
simple loop
we mean one that cannot be decomposed as a concatenation of more than one non-null-homotopic loop. We will prove this equivalence later, in
Proposition
7.10
.

The following lemma justifies our terminology; its proof motivates the condition in
Definition
3.7
.

Lemma 3.8
.

A
\Gamma
-complex
X
is symmetric if and only if every blowup structure
X\cong\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
corresponds to a compatible set
\Pi
consisting only of symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions.

Proof.

The forward direction follows by construction. Put a blowup structure
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
on a symmetric
\Gamma
-complex
X
, and read off the corresponding
\Gamma
-partitions. Each
\Gamma
-partition
\mathcal{P}_{H}\in\Pi
corresponds to a hyperplane
H
in a treelike set
\mathcal{T}
; now apply to
H
the definition of symmetric
\Gamma
-complex (
Definition
3.7
) as follows. Let
H^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{T}
be as in
Definition
3.7
; that is, all edges with one end in
\beta(H)
and the other in
\overline{\beta}(H)
are dual to either
H
or
H^{\prime}
. Since
H^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{T}
,
H^{\prime}
must be labelled by some vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
. Now, for any vertex
w\in V(\Gamma)
,
w
is split by
\mathcal{P}_{H}
if and only if the edges labelled by
w
have one end at a vertex in
\beta(H)
and the other end at a vertex in
\overline{\beta}(H)
. Hence the only vertex split by
\mathcal{P}_{H}
is
v
. Hence
\mathcal{P}_{H}
is symmetric.

For the converse, let
\mathcal{T}
be a treelike set of partitions in
X
. By
Proposition
3.6
, there is some blowup structure
X\cong\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
such that
\mathcal{T}
is precisely the set of hyperplanes labelled by elements of
\Pi
. By hypothesis, each element of
\Pi
is a symmetric
\Gamma
-partition. Let
H\in\mathcal{T}
; since
\mathcal{P}_{H}
is symmetric, there is only one vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
split by
\mathcal{P}_{H}
. This means that all edges with one end at a vertex in
\beta(H)
and the other end at
\overline{\beta}(H)
are labelled by either
H
or
v
, so
H
satisfies the condition in
Definition
3.7
. This is true for every element of
\mathcal{T}
, and every treelike set
\mathcal{T}
, so
X
is symmetric, as required.
∎

4.2.
(Symmetric) marked
\Gamma
-complexes and the (symmetric) spine

In this subsection, we recall the definition of the (untwisted) spine
K_{\Gamma}
, as in
[
CSV17
]
, and analogously introduce the
symmetric spine

K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
.

Definition 4.4
.

A
marked
\Gamma
-complex
is a pair
\sigma=(X,\alpha)
, where
X
is a
\Gamma
-complex and
\alpha\colon X\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
is a homotopy equivalence. We say that
\alpha
is an
untwisted marking
if the composition

\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}{\xrightarrow{c_{\Pi}^{-1}}}\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}\cong X\xrightarrow{\alpha}\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}

induces an element of
U(A_{\Gamma})
.

A marked
\Gamma
-complex
(X,\alpha)
is
symmetric
if
X
is a symmetric
\Gamma
-complex and
\alpha
is a
symmetric marking
; that is, the composition

\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}{\xrightarrow{c_{\Pi}^{-1}}}\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}\cong X\xrightarrow{\alpha}\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}

induces an element of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
.

Two marked
\Gamma
-complexes
\sigma=(X,\alpha)
,
\sigma^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
are
equivalent
if there exists a cube complex isomorphism
h\colon X\to X^{\prime}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}\circ h\simeq\alpha
. We will sometimes use the term ‘marked
\Gamma
-complex’ to refer to one of these equivalence classes.

For a (symmetric) marked
\Gamma
-complex
(X,\alpha)
such that
X
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
, we call the equivalence class of
(X,\alpha)
a
(symmetric) marked Salvetti
.

We now define a partial order on marked
\Gamma
-complexes. If
\sigma=(X,\alpha)
is a marked
\Gamma
-complex,
\mathcal{T}
is a treelike set of hyperplanes in
X
with some subset
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and
c_{\mathcal{H}}\colon X\to X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{H}
is the collapse map, denote
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq\left(X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{H},\alpha\circ c_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}\right)
. Now for two marked
\Gamma
-complexes
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
, define
\sigma^{\prime}<\sigma
if
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
for some
\mathcal{H}
.

We can restrict this partial order to the set of symmetric
\Gamma
-complexes. We are now ready to define the spine
K_{\Gamma}
, and the symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
.

Definition 4.5
.

The
(untwisted) spine

K_{\Gamma}
is the simplicial complex which is the geometric realisation of the poset of (equivalence classes of) marked
\Gamma
-complexes.

The
symmetric spine

K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is the simplicial complex which is the geometric realisation of the poset of (equivalence classes of) symmetric marked
\Gamma
-complexes.

Identifying
\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
with the group of homotopy classes of maps
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
, define a left action of
U(A_{\Gamma})
(resp.
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
) on
K_{\Gamma}
(resp.
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
) by
\varphi\cdot(X,\alpha)=(X,\varphi\circ\alpha)
.

Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann
[
CSV17
]
now prove that
K_{\Gamma}
is connected, and its
U(A_{\Gamma})
-action is proper. We mimic their proofs to prove the analogous results in the symmetric case.

Lemma 4.6
.

The action of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
on
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is proper.

Proof.

Each symmetric marked
\Gamma
-complex can be collapsed to finitely many marked Salvettis, so it is sufficient to prove that the stabiliser of some (hence any) marked Salvetti is finite. Any isomorphism
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
takes the 1-skeleton to the 1-skeleton, so on
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{id}\right)
induces a permutation of
V^{\pm}
. So
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)\sim\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{id}\right)
if and only if
\alpha\in\Omega(A_{\Gamma})\leq\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, the (finite) group generated by graph symmetries and inversions.
∎

Let
(\mathcal{P},m)
be a symmetric
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair and
\alpha=\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
the corresponding (symmetric)
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism. By
Lemma
4.1
,
\alpha
is realised by a blowup-collapse

\alpha=c_{\mathcal{P}}\circ h_{m}\circ c_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\;\colon\;\;\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{P}}\xrightarrow{\cong}\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}.

This gives a path in
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
between
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{\id}\right)
and
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
:

\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{id}\right)<\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{P}},c_{\mathcal{P}}\right)\sim\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{P}},c_{\mathcal{P}}\circ h_{m}\right)=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\mathcal{P}},\alpha\circ c_{\mathcal{P}}\right)>\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right).

Now for any
\varphi\in\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, we can translate this path by
\varphi
to obtain a path from
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\right)
to
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\circ\alpha\right)
. This is called the
Whitehead move
at
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\right)
associated to
\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
. If
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\right)
, we will write
\sigma_{m}^{\mathcal{P}}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\circ\alpha\right)
.

Following
[
CSV17
]
, we can restate
Lemma
4.3
using this terminology.

Corollary 4.7

(Factorisation Lemma)
.

Let
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
be a marked Salvetti,
\Pi=\{\mathcal{P}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
a compatible set of symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions, and
\mathcal{T}
a treelike set of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi}
. We write
\sigma^{\Pi}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}^{\Pi},c_{\Pi}\circ\alpha\right)
.

Then with a suitable ordering of the elements of
\mathcal{H}
, there is a chain
\sigma=\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{\Pi}
such that each
\sigma_{i}
is connected to
\sigma_{i-1}
by a Whitehead move.

Proposition 4.8
.

K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is connected.

Proof.

Let
N\leq\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
be the subgroup generated by symmetric
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms (that is, generated by partial conjugations), and write
\Omega(A_{\Gamma})
for the (finite) subgroup generated by graph symmetries and inversions.

Let
\omega\in\Omega(A_{\Gamma})
, and let
\rho
be a partial conjugation based at
m\in V
. We show that
\omega^{-1}\rho\omega
is also a partial conjugation. Clearly, for any
v\in V
, if
\rho(\omega(v))=\omega(v)
, then
\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\rho\circ\omega\right)(v)=v
. The other possibility is that
\rho(\omega(v))=m\omega(v)m^{-1}
. Then

\left(\omega^{-1}\circ\rho\circ\omega\right)(v)=\omega^{-1}\left(m\omega(v)m^{-1}\right)=\omega^{-1}(m)v\omega^{-1}\left(m^{-1}\right).

Hence we recognise
\omega^{-1}\rho\omega
as a partial conjugation based at
\omega^{-1}(m)
. Note that if
\rho
is symmetric, then
\omega^{-1}\rho\omega
is too.

We conclude that
N\triangleleft\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is normal. Therefore, any
\varphi\in\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
can be factored as a product
\varphi=\varphi_{\Omega}\circ\varphi_{N}
, where
\varphi_{\Omega}
is a product of graph symmetries and inversions and
\varphi_{N}
is an element of
N
.

Since
\varphi_{\Omega}
can be represented by an isomorphism
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
, we have
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{\id}\right)\sim\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi_{\Omega}\right)
. By
Corollary
4.7
,
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi_{\Omega}\right)
is connected by a path in
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
to
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi_{\Omega}\circ\varphi_{N}\right)
. That is,
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{\id}\right)
is connected to
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\right)
by a path in
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
. But by the definition of the poset of (equivalence classes of) symmetric marked
\Gamma
-complexes, every vertex of
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
lies in the star of some marked Salvetti, so we’re done.
∎

The quotient of
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
by the action of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
is compact, so we now have a proper and cocompact action of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
on the connected complex
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
. It remains to show that
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is in fact contractible.

5.
Discussion of proof of contractibility of
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}

As discussed in the introduction (§
1.2
), our proof of contractibility of
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
proceeds in two broad steps, inspired by Collins
[
Col89
]
. We now outline our strategy in more detail and explain its relation to the main relevant literature.

Culler–Vogtmann
[
CV86
]
proved that Outer space
CV_{n}
is contractible by showing that its spine
K_{n}
, a deformation retract of
CV_{n}
, is contractible. Culler and Vogtmann defined a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{W}
on marked rose graphs (which are the marked Salvettis in the free group case) for an arbitrary finite set
W
of conjugacy classes of the free group. They then proved that the subcomplex
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}\subseteq K_{n}
, defined as the union of all marked roses which are minimal with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{W}
, is in fact a deformation retract of
K_{n}
. Their final step was to pick a set
W
for which
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
is obviously contractible, whence
K_{n}
is contractible.

This means that
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
is contractible for any choice of
W
. Collins
[
Col89
]
notes that by wisely choosing
W
so that the symmetric spine
K_{n}^{\Sigma}
is a subcomplex of
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
, contractibility of
K_{n}^{\Sigma}
follows by proving that
K_{n}^{\Sigma}
is a retract of
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
. This last part forms most of the technical work in
[
Col89
]
.

We set out to mimic this strategy, with marked Salvettis replacing marked roses, and the untwisted spine
K_{\Gamma}
replacing
K_{n}
. However, Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s proof
[
CSV17
]
of the contractibility of
K_{\Gamma}
is more direct than the analogous proof of Culler–Vogtmann
[
CV86
]
: they picked a single norm which totally well-orders the marked Salvettis, and built
K_{\Gamma}
star-of-marked-Salvetti by star-of-marked-Salvetti, with stars attached in increasing order according to this norm. Specialised to the free group case, this is almost a backwards version of Culler–Vogtmann’s original proof: fixing a judicious choice of conjugacy classes at the start, so therefore starting with the star of a single marked Salvetti (which is obviously contractible), and building up to the entire spine. This has been explained in detail by Vogtmann
[
Vog17
]
. The upshot is that the proof of contractibility of
K_{\Gamma}
in
[
CSV17
]
does not automatically give analogous contractible intermediary subspaces such as the
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
in the free group case.

Consequently, our main technical contribution is to define, and prove contractibility of, analogous spaces
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
for
\mathcal{W}
an arbitrary finite set of conjugacy classes in
A_{\Gamma}
. To do this, we combine the idea of a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{W}
from
[
CV86
]
with the ‘build upwards’ strategy of
[
CSV17
]
. Once we have proven that this new norm gives a total well-order of the marked Salvettis, we can replicate Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s inductive gluing argument. Stopping at the right height will build precisely the intermediary complex
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
. Hence, each of these intermediary complexes is contractible. Choosing
\mathcal{W}
to be the set of conjugacy classes of length at most two ‘recovers’ (or more precisely, simply is) Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s proof of contractibilty of
K_{\Gamma}
. On the other hand, choosing
\mathcal{W}
to be the set of conjugacy classes of length one yields a complex
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
which contains our symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
. This will be the space
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\text{sym}}}
as referred to in §
1.2
.

The main edit we make to Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s proof of contractibility of
K_{\Gamma}
is to change the norm on marked Salvettis. All that remains is then to show that the same proof goes through with the new norm. Essentially, we must check that all of §6 of
[
CSV17
]
works with our norm. Some results in (

, both of which can be circumvented in our case, as follows.

•

In the proof of Corollary 6.21, and therefore (indirectly) in proving Proposition 6.22, which states that Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s norm well-orders the set of marked Salvettis. However, we will prove that our norms well-order the set of marked Salvettis as a corollary of (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.21 & Proposition 6.22). This means that we do not need to go to the effort of proving results analogous to these in our setting—we can simply use Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s as they stand, and hence (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.20) also unaltered.

•

In the proof of Theorem 6.24, where it is used to guarantee that when one inductively glues the star of a marked Salvetti
\sigma
to the union of the stars of marked Salvettis with norm less than
\lVert\sigma\rVert
, the intersection along with one glues is nonempty. We skip this step in our corresponding proof, which means that we can only conclude that the spine
K_{\Gamma}
is a union of contractible components. However, since we know that
K_{\Gamma}
is connected, this knowledge is enough to deduce contractibility.

Therefore, we are actually able to avoid adapting Corollary 6.20 to our setting. As can be seen from
Figure
3
, this also obviates the need to transfer (Strong) Peak Reduction (Theorems 6.18, 6.19) and the Higgins–Lyndon Lemma (Lemma 6.17) to our setting. Whenever relevant, we will point out exactly where we avoid the use of Corollary 6.20 (and the results used in its proof). In
Figure
4
, we lay out the various results, and their relation to each other, which together prove contractibility of
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
, and also indicate the results from
[
CSV17
]
which they adapt.

We make one final comment regarding our choice of norm. In
[
CV86
]
, the norm on marked roses is defined as follows. For an arbitrary finite set
W
of conjugacy classes in
F_{n}
, and for
\rho
a marked rose, define

\lVert\rho\rVert\coloneqq n\sum_{w\in W}l(w),

where
l(w)
is the length function naturally associated to the marking on
\rho
. Then, for any fixed
W
, one defines
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
as the union of the stars of all marked roses which are minimal with respect to this norm. The final step of the proof of contractibility of the spine
K_{n}
is to notice that if one chooses
W
to be the set of conjugacy classes of length at most two, then
K_{\operatorname{min}(W)}
consists of exactly one (star of a) marked rose
\rho=(R_{n},\operatorname{id})
, and so is contractible.

A slightly more complicated norm, this time on marked Salvettis instead of marked roses, is used in
[
CSV17
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}=\left(g_{1},g_{2},\dots\right)
be a list of all the conjugacy classes of a RAAG
A_{\Gamma}
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{0}
be the set of all conjugacy classes which can be represented by a word of length at most two. For any marked Salvetti
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
and any conjugacy class
g
of
A_{\Gamma}
, define
\ell_{\sigma}(g)
to be the minimal length of a word in the free group
F\langle V(\Gamma)\rangle
representing an element of the conjugacy class
\alpha^{-1}(g)
in
A_{\Gamma}
(on roses, this is precisely the length function used in
[
CV86
]
as above). They now define the norm of
\sigma
to be

\lVert\sigma\rVert\coloneqq\left(\lVert\sigma\rVert_{0},\;\lVert\sigma\rVert_{1},\;\lVert\sigma\rVert_{2},\;\dots\right),

where

\lVert\sigma\rVert_{0}\coloneqq\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\ell_{\sigma}(g)\;\;;\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\lVert\sigma\rVert_{i}\coloneqq\ell_{\sigma}(g_{i}).

The norm is taken to live in the ordered abelian group
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{G}}
, ordered by the lexicographical order. In view of the suite of norms used in

, it would seem natural to define our new norms by allowing

to be any finite set of conjugacy classes. However, we will actually do something slightly different, which will allow us to use more of the properties of Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s norm

\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq\sum_{g\in\mathcal{W}}\ell_{\sigma}(g),

and then we define our new norm

\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}\coloneqq\left(\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}},\;\lVert\sigma\rVert\right)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{G}}.

This trick enables us to avoid proving an analogue of Corollary 6.20, as discussed above.

6.1.
The
\mathcal{W}
-norm of a marked Salvetti

In this section we define our adaptations of the norm defined in
[
CSV17
]
. This subsection is a direct transfer of (
[
CSV17
]
, §6.1) to our setting.

For a marked Salvetti
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
and a conjugacy class
g
of
A_{\Gamma}
, denote by
\ell_{\sigma}(g)
the minimal length of a word in the free group
F\langle V(\Gamma)\rangle
representing an element of the conjugacy class
\alpha^{-1}(g)
in
A_{\Gamma}
. In particular, if
\alpha=\operatorname{id}
, then
\ell_{\sigma}(g)
is the minimal word length of an element of
g
.

Identifying elements of
V(\Gamma)
with the 1-skeleton of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
,
\ell_{\sigma}(g)
can be thought of as the minimal length of an edge-path in the 1-skeleton of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
representing
\alpha^{-1}(g)
. As observed in
[
CSV17
]
, normal form for RAAGs (see
[
Cha07
]
) implies that this length function is well-defined. Moreover, if
\alpha
is an isometry of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
, then a minimal edge-path in the 1-skeleton of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
representing
\alpha^{-1}(g)
is the same length as one representing
g
—which matches the fact that
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)\sim\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{id}\right)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\left(g_{1},\;g_{2},\;,\dots\right)
be a list of the conjugacy classes of
A_{\Gamma}
, and fix
\mathcal{W}
, an arbitrary finite subset of
\mathcal{G}
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be the subset of conjugacy classes represented by a length of at most two.

Definition 6.1
.

For a marked Salvetti
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
, define the
\mathcal{W}
-norm
of
\sigma
to be

\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}\coloneqq\left(\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}},\;\lVert\sigma\rVert_{0},\;\lVert\sigma\rVert_{1},\;\lVert\sigma\rVert_{2},\;\dots\right)\;\;\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{G}},

where

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in\mathcal{W}}\ell_{\sigma}(g);

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma\rVert_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\ell_{\sigma}(g);

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma\rVert_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ell_{\sigma}\left(g_{i}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for~$i\geq 1$},

and we consider
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{G}}
as an ordered abelian group with the lexicographical ordering; write
\mathbf{0}\coloneqq\left(0,\;0,\;\dots\right)
for its identity element.

This follows the notation laid out in (
[
CSV17
]
§6); our only addition is the dependence on
\mathcal{W}
. In particular,
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}=\left(\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{W}},\;\lVert\cdot\rVert\right)
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the norm employed by Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann.

Proposition 6.2
.

\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
is a strict total well-ordering on the set of marked Salvettis.

Proof.

Due to (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.3),
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is a strict total order on the set of marked Salvettis. It follows that
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
is also a strict total order, by construction.

It remains to show that
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
is a well-ordering. Suppose, for contradiction, that it is not, so there exists an infinite decreasing chain of marked Salvettis with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
. Let
\sigma
be some member of this chain; we can then choose another,
\tau
, such that
\lVert\tau\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
. Then either
\lVert\tau\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}<\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
, or
\lVert\tau\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
and
\lVert\tau\rVert_{0}\leq\lVert\sigma\rVert_{0}
.

By (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.21), we know that for all
N\geq\lVert\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\operatorname{id}\right)\rVert_{0}
, there exist only finitely many marked Salvettis
\sigma
with
\lVert\sigma\rVert_{0}\leq N
. Since there are infinitely many
\tau
with
\lVert\tau\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
, we can pick
\tau
such that
\lVert\tau\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}<\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
. We can now repeat this argument
ad infinitum
, obtaining an infinite sequence of marked Salvettis which are strictly decreasing with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
. In other words, we have an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, a contradiction.
∎

In particular, there is a minimal marked Salvetti with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
.

As discussed in §
5
, this is one of the places in which we circumvent needing an analogue of (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.20).

6.2.
Effect of a Whitehead move on the
\mathcal{W}
-norm

We now aim to transplant what we need from (
[
CSV17
]
, §6.2). We refer the reader to the introduction of that subsection for a discussion of the geometric interpretation of
\ell_{\sigma}(g)
, and the motivation behind the following notation.

Fix a marked Salvetti
\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
. For a conjugacy class
g\in\mathcal{G}
, let
w
be a cyclically-reduced word for
\alpha^{-1}(g)
. For a
\Gamma
-partition
\mathcal{P}=\left(P\;|\;\overline{P}\;|\;\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})\right)
, let
|\mathcal{P}|_{w}
denote the number of cyclically adjacent letters
u_{i}u_{i+1}
in
w
such that
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}^{-1}
do not both lie in
P\cup\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
or both lie in
\overline{P}\cup\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
. For any vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
, let
|v|_{w}
denote the number of occurrences of
v
or
v^{-1}
in
w
.

We will use the following lemma from
[
CSV17
]
. Recall the notation before
Corollary
4.7
: for a
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism
\alpha=\varphi\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
, and a marked Salvetti
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\right)
, we write
\sigma_{m}^{\mathcal{P}}
for the Whitehead move at
\sigma
associated to
\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
; that is,
\sigma_{m}^{\mathcal{P}}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\varphi\circ\alpha\right)
.

Lemma 6.3

(
[
CSV17
]
, Lemma 6.4)
.

Let
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
be a marked Salvetti, let
\varphi=\left(\mathcal{P},m\right)
be a
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism, let
g
be a conjugacy class in
A_{\Gamma}
, and let
w
be a minimal length word representing
\alpha^{-1}(g)
. Then

\ell_{\sigma_{m}^{\mathcal{P}}}(g)=\ell_{\sigma}(g)+|\mathcal{P}|_{w}-|v|_{w}.

More generally, if
\sigma^{\prime}
is obtained from
\sigma
by blowing up a compatible set
\Pi=\{\mathcal{P}_{1},\;\dots,\;\mathcal{P}_{k}\}
of
\Gamma
-partitions and collapsing a treelike set
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},\;\dots,\;H_{k}\}
of hyperplanes dual to edges labelled
e_{v_{i}}
, then

\ell_{\sigma^{\prime}}(g)=\ell_{\sigma}(g)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\mathcal{P}_{i}|_{w}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|v_{i}|_{w}.

We now extend this notation as follows. For a marked Salvetti
\sigma=\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha\right)
and a
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism
\varphi=\left(\mathcal{P},v\right)
, define

|\mathcal{P}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}\coloneqq\left(|\mathcal{P}|_{\mathcal{W}},\;|\mathcal{P}|_{0},\;|\mathcal{P}|_{w(g_{1})},\;|\mathcal{P}|_{w(g_{2})},\;\dots\right),

where

•

w(g)
is a minimal length word representing
\alpha^{-1}(g)
, for any conjugacy class
g
of
A_{\Gamma}
;

•

|\mathcal{P}|_{0}\coloneqq\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}|\mathcal{P}|_{w(g)}
;

•

|\mathcal{P}|_{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq\sum_{g\in\mathcal{W}}|\mathcal{P}|_{w(g)}
.

Similarly define
|v|^{\prime}_{\sigma}\coloneqq\left(|v|_{\mathcal{W}},\;|v|_{0},\;|v|_{w(g_{1})},\;|v|_{w(g_{2})},\;\dots\right)
. We can now restate
Lemma
6.3
as follows:

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
\Pi
and
\mathcal{H}
be as in
Lemma
6.3
. Then

\lVert\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{\Pi}\rVert^{\prime}=\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\mathcal{P}_{i}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|v_{i}|_{\sigma}.

This is a direct translation of (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.6) to our new norms.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
\sigma
be a marked Salvetti and let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\Gamma
-partition. We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive
for
\sigma
if for some
v\in\operatorname{max}({\mathcal{P}})
the
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism
\varphi=\varphi=\left(\mathcal{P},v\right)
reduces
\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
, that is,
\lVert\sigma_{v}^{\mathcal{P}}\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
(or equivalently,
|\mathcal{P}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}<|v|^{\prime}_{\sigma}
).

Corollary 6.6

(cf.
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.8)
.

Let
\Pi
and
\mathcal{H}
be as in
Lemma
6.3
. If
\lVert\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{\Pi}\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
, then some
\mathcal{P}_{i}\in\Pi
is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of (
[
CSV17
]
, Corollary 6.8). By
Theorem
4.2
, we can order the elements of
\mathcal{H}
so that if
e_{v_{i}}
is the edge dual to
H_{i}
, then
\left(\mathcal{P}_{i},v_{i}\right)
is a
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair. If
\lVert\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}^{\Pi}\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
, then by
Corollary
6.4
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\mathcal{P}_{i}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|v_{i}|_{\sigma}<\mathbf{0}.

Hence we must have some
i
with
|\mathcal{P}_{i}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}<|v_{i}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}
. Hence
\lVert\sigma_{v_{i}}^{\mathcal{P}_{i}}\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
, and we’re done.
∎

6.3.
Proof of contractibility of
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}

We have now established analogues of Lemma 6.2, Corollary 6.8, and Proposition 6.22 from
[
CSV17
]
. We are skipping Corollary 6.20, and we have the Poset Lemma for free, so (as can be seen from
Figure
3
), all that remains is to obtain an analogue of the ‘Pushing Lemma’, (
[
CSV17
]
, Lemma 6.23).

We will obtain an analogue with minimal work, since Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann’s argument does not explicitly rely on their norm. However, the statement and proof do use notation and terminology which we have adapted to our new context, so we will give an abbreviation of the proof given in
[
CSV17
]
, with our adapted notation where necessary.

Star graphs
have been used to study automorphisms of free groups (see e.g.
[
Hoa95
]
). Charney–Stambaugh–Vogtmann develop this theory to work for RAAGs (see
[
CSV17
]
, §6.3). We will not reproduce this theory, but we will need one consequence, which we rephrase slightly (in
Corollary
6.9
).

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be
\Gamma
-partitions. We call the four side-side intersections
P\cap Q
,
P\cap\overline{Q}
,
\overline{P}\cap Q
,
\overline{P}\cap\overline{Q}

quadrants
(or possibly
\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\right)
-quadrants
). Two quadrants are
opposite
if one can be obtained from the other by switching sides of both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
.

It will now be convenient to allow (as we previously didn’t)
\Gamma
-partitions to have one side a singleton. That is,
\mathcal{P}=\left(P\;|\;\overline{P}\;|\;\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})\right)
with
P=\{v\}
for some
v\in V^{\pm}
. We call such a
\Gamma
-partition
degenerate
. The associated
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphism is an inversion, and for every marked Salvetti
\sigma
, and any word
w\in F(V)
we have
|\mathcal{P}|_{w}=|v|_{w}
—so in particular, a degenerate
\Gamma
-partition can never be
\mathcal{W}
-reductive.

Lemma 6.7

(
[
CSV17
]
, Lemma 6.16)
.

For any incompatible
\Gamma
-partitions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, with maximal elements
v
and
w
respectively, there is a pair of opposite quadrants such that each defines a (possibly degenerate)
\Gamma
-partition with maximal element in
\{v^{\pm},w^{\pm}\}
.

The proof of this lemma is entirely combinatorial, and does not depend on any choice of norm.

Lemma 6.8

(
[
CSV17
]
, Lemma 6.15, special case)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}
be incompatible
\Gamma
-partitions. Suppose that
P\cap\overline{Q}
and
\overline{P}\cap Q
are the two opposite quadrants generated by
Lemma
6.7
, and let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be the corresponding
\Gamma
-partitions. Then for any minimal length word
w
representing a conjugacy class
g
of
A_{\Gamma}
, we have

|\mathcal{X}|_{w}+|\mathcal{Y}|_{w}\leq|\mathcal{P}|_{w}+|\mathcal{Q}|_{w}.

Corollary 6.9
.

With the same setup as in
Lemma
6.8
, we have

|\mathcal{X}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}+|\mathcal{Y}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}\leq|\mathcal{P}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}+|\mathcal{Q}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}.

We are now ready for our version of the ‘Pushing Lemma’, (
[
CSV17
]
, Lemma 6.23).

Lemma 6.10

(Pushing Lemma)
.

Fix a marked Salvetti
\sigma
. Suppose that
\left(M,m\right)
is a
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair which is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
such that

(i)

\operatorname{lk}({M})
is maximal among links of
\Gamma
-partitions which are
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
;

(ii)

for any
\Gamma
-Whitehead pair
\left(Q,w\right)
with
\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{Q}})=\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{M}})
, we have
|\mathcal{M}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-|m|^{\prime}_{\sigma}<|\mathcal{Q}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-|w|^{\prime}_{\sigma}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\Gamma
-partition which is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
and which is not compatible with
\mathcal{M}
. Then at least one of the
\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}\right)
-quadrants involving the side of
\mathcal{M}
containing
m^{-1}
determines a
\Gamma
-partition which has link equal to
\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
and is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
.

Proof.

The proof of Lemma 6.23 in
[
CSV17
]
transfers exactly over to our case, using
Corollary
6.9
. We give a short summary.

\mathcal{P}
is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
, so
|\mathcal{X}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-|v|^{\prime}_{\sigma}<\mathbf{0}
for some
v\in\max{\mathcal{P}}
.

First suppose that
v\in P
,
v^{-1}\in\overline{P}
,
m\in M
, and
m^{-1}\in\overline{M}
. By Case (3) in the proof of
Lemma
6.7
(
[
CSV17
]
, Lemma 6.16), we have opposite
\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}\right)
-quadrants
X
,
Y
such that
\left(X,v\right)
and
\left(X,v^{-1}\right)
are
\Gamma
-Whitehead pairs, and opposite quadrants
X^{\prime}
,
Y^{\prime}
such that
\left(X^{\prime},m\right)
and
\left(Y^{\prime},m^{-1}\right)
. Applying
Corollary
6.9
to both pairs of quadrants, recalling that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
, and using hypothesis (ii), one can deduce that

\left(|\mathcal{Y}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-|m|^{\prime}_{\sigma}\right)+\left(|\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}|^{\prime}_{\sigma}-|m|^{\prime}_{\sigma}\right)<\mathbf{0},

and so that one of the pairs
\left(Y,m\right)
,
\left(Y^{\prime},m^{-1}\right)
is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
(and has link equal to
\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
), as required.

In any other case, a similar argument holds. Apply
Lemma
6.7
to find a pair of opposite
\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}\right)
-quadrants
X
,
Y
, and then using hypotheses (i) & (ii) along with
Corollary
6.9
, one can conclude that one or the other of these is reductive and has link equal to
\operatorname{lk}({\mathcal{P}})
, as required.
∎

Finally, we need the
Poset Lemma
, a special case of Quillen’s
Theorem A
from
[
Qui73
]
.

Lemma 6.11

(Poset Lemma)
.

Let
P
be a poset of finite height and let
f\colon P\to P
be a poset map such that
f(p)\leq p
for all
p\in P
. Then
f
induces a deformation retraction from the geometric realisation of
P
to the geometric realisation of the image
f(P)
.

We are now ready to prove our main result of this section, which was stated as
Theorem
C
in §
1.2
.

Theorem 6.12
.

For any
\Gamma
and any
\mathcal{W}
,
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
is contractible.

Proof.

Since
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
is a strict total well-order (by
Proposition
6.2
), there is a unique minimal marked Salvetti with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{\prime}
. We start with the star of this marked Salvetti, and then inductively add on stars of marked Salvettis in increasing order. Since the star of a marked Salvetti is always contractible, our main job is to show that the intersection along which we glue is contractible, if it is nonempty.

To this end, for any marked Salvetti
\sigma
, let

K_{<\sigma}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\lVert\tau\rVert^{\prime}<\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}}\operatorname{st}({\tau}).

We prove that if
\operatorname{st}({\sigma})\cap K_{<\sigma}
is nonempty, then it is contractible. This intersection consists of blowups
\sigma^{\Pi}
which can be collapsed to a marked Salvetti of
\mathcal{W}
-norm smaller than
\lVert\sigma\rVert^{\prime}
, where
\Pi
is an
ideal forest
—that is, a compatible set of
\Gamma
-partitions. We can identify
\operatorname{st}({\sigma})\cap K_{<\sigma}
as the geometric realisation of the poset of ideal forests ordered by inclusion. We will apply the Poset Lemma to contract this poset to a single point.

By
Corollary
6.6
, if
\Pi
is in
\operatorname{st}({\sigma})\cap K_{<\sigma}
, then
\Pi
contains a
\Gamma
-partition
\mathcal{P}
which is
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
. Hence the map which throws out from
\Pi
those
\Gamma
-partitions which are not
\mathcal{W}
-reductive for
\sigma
is a poset map, to which we can apply the Poset Lemma. Choose a pair
\left(M,m\right)
which satisfies the maximality conditions in the Pushing Lemma.

At this point the rest of the proof as in (
[
CSV17
]
, Theorem 6.24) follows through with no modification. This allows us to conclude that
\operatorname{st}({\sigma})\cap K_{<\sigma}
is contractible, if it is nonempty. Hence we can build the entirety of
K_{\Gamma}
as a union of contractible components. Since we already know that
K_{\Gamma}
is connected, we conclude that it is in fact contractible.

Moreover, we also build
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
in this fashion, simply by stopping after incorporating all those marked Salvettis with minimal
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
-component of the
\mathcal{W}
-norm. Hence
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
is also a union of contractible components. If
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
is disconnected, then since
K_{\Gamma}
is connected, there must be some marked Salvetti
\sigma\notin\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
such that
\operatorname{st}({\sigma})\cap K_{<\sigma}
consists of two different components, which contradicts its contractibility, as we just proved. Hence
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
had better be connected, and thus is contractible, as required.
∎

6.4.
A finiteness result for outer automorphisms with respect to a set of conjugacy classes

We pause the proof of contractibility of the symmetric spine to give a separate application of
Theorem
6.12
. For any finite set
\mathcal{W}
of conjugacy classes of
A_{\Gamma}
, let
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
be the subgroup consisting of outer automorphisms that permute the elements of
\mathcal{W}
.
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
is invariant under the action of this group. We can use this to prove that
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
is type
VF
, mirroring the proof of (
[
CV86
]
, Corollary 6.1.4), which is the analogous result in the free group setting. Recall (
Definition
2.7
) that a discrete group
G
is
type F
if there exists a
K(G,1)
which is a finite CW-complex, and a group is
type VF
if it is virtually type
F
.

Corollary 6.13
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a finite set of conjugacy classes of
A_{\Gamma}
. Then
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
is type
VF
.

Proof.

The stabiliser of each cell under the action of
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
consists of graph symmetries and inversions, so we can pass to a torsion-free finite-index normal subgroup
H\leq\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
which acts freely on
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
. By
Lemma
2.8
, it then suffices to prove that
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.68945pt}{$\displaystyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}{\raisebox{4.43945pt}{$\textstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}{\raisebox{3.3839pt}{$\scriptstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}{\raisebox{2.76909pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}}
is finite, as that implies that
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.68945pt}{$\displaystyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}}{\raisebox{4.43945pt}{$\textstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}}{\raisebox{3.3839pt}{$\scriptstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}}{\raisebox{2.76909pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}}}
is.

Fix a marked Salvetti
\sigma=(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma},\alpha)
. For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, there are only finitely many conjugacy classes
g
of
A_{\Gamma}
such that
\ell_{\sigma}(g)\leq k
. Hence there can only be finitely many finite sets
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
of conjugacy classes of
A_{\Gamma}
such that
\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}
.

Choose a marked Salvetti
\sigma_{0}
in
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
. Let
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}_{0},\mathcal{W}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{W}_{m}
be all the finite sets
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
of conjugacy classes such that
\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}
which are images of
\mathcal{W}
under an outer automorphism of
A_{\Gamma}
. Choose elements
\operatorname{id}=\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}\in\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
such that
\varphi_{i}(\mathcal{W}_{i})=\mathcal{W}
.

For any marked Salvetti
\sigma
and any
\varphi\in\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi\cdot\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in\mathcal{W}}\ell_{\varphi\cdot\sigma}(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in\mathcal{W}}\ell_{\sigma}(\varphi^{-1}(g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{W})}\ell_{\sigma}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{W})}.

Hence for each
i
, we have

\lVert\varphi_{i}\cdot\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\varphi_{i}^{-1}(\mathcal{W})}=\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}=\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}},

so
\varphi_{i}\cdot\sigma_{0}
is a marked Salvetti in
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
.

Let
\sigma
be any marked Salvetti in
\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
, and choose an automorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma})
such that
\varphi\cdot\sigma_{0}=\sigma
. Then we have

\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\varphi\cdot\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{W})},

and so
\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{W})=\mathcal{W}_{i}
for some

. Thus
\mathcal{W}=(\varphi_{i}\circ\varphi^{-1})(\mathcal{W})
. Now,
(\varphi_{i}\circ\varphi^{-1})\cdot\sigma=\varphi_{i}\cdot\sigma_{0}
, which means that
\sigma
is equivalent modulo
\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})
to one of a finite list of marked Salvettis. Hence the quotient
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.68945pt}{$\displaystyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}{\raisebox{4.43945pt}{$\textstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}{\raisebox{3.3839pt}{$\scriptstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}{\raisebox{2.76909pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{Out}_{\mathcal{W}}(A_{\Gamma})}$}}}
is finite, and we’re done.
∎

7.2.
Change of treelike set (CTS) automorphisms

To do this, we introduce
change of treelike set (CTS) automorphisms
. These are our analogue of the ‘change of maximal tree (CMT) automorphisms’ introduced by Gersten
[
Ger84
]
and utilised by Collins
[
Col89
]
.

Note that for any treelike set of hyperplanes in a
\Gamma
-complex
X
, the edges dual to the hyperplanes in the treelike set form no non-null-homotopic loops.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a treelike set of hyperplanes in a
\Gamma
-complex
X
. Let
X
have basepoint
x_{0}
in the 0-skeleton of
X
. For each hyperplane
\overline{H}
of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
, there is a unique hyperplane
H
of
X
such that
c_{\mathcal{T}}(H)=\overline{H}
. Moreover, for each edge
e_{\overline{H}}
dual to
\overline{H}
in
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
, there is a unique edge
e_{H}
dual to
H
in
X
. There are paths in the 1-skeleton of
X
along edges dual to hyperplanes in
\mathcal{T}
to each of the endpoints of each such edge
e_{H}
, since the edges labelled by elements of
\mathcal{T}
span the 0-skeleton of
X
. We claim that these paths are unique up to homotopy. Indeed, let
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
be two paths along edges dual to hyperplanes in
\mathcal{T}
, from
x_{0}
to the same endpoint of some edge
e_{H}
. Then the composition
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}
is a loop, which (since
\mathcal{T}
is treelike) must be null-homotopic. Hence
\gamma_{1}
is homotopic to
\gamma_{2}
.

Recall that since
H
is labelled by a vertex of
\Gamma
, all edges dual to
H
are oriented. Fix some such edge
e_{H}
, and denote its two endpoints by
s(e_{H})
and
t(e_{H})
, where the orientation travels along
e_{H}
from
s(e_{H})
to
t(e_{H})
. We have a path
\gamma
along edges dual to hyperplanes in
\mathcal{T}
from
x_{0}
to
s(e_{H})
, and a similar path
\delta
from
x_{0}
to
t(e_{H})
. Then
\gamma e_{H}\delta^{-1}
is a loop in the 1-skeleton of
X
. The collapse
c_{\mathcal{T}}
has canonical homotopy inverse given on
e_{\overline{H}}
by
c_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(e_{\overline{H}})=\gamma e_{H}\delta^{-1}
.

Now let
c_{\mathcal{T}}
and
c_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
be collapses
X\to\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
along treelike sets
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
respectively. This induces an automorphism
\left(c_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\right)_{\ast}\circ\left(c_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\right)_{\ast}\colon\pi_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\right)\to\pi_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\right)
, which we call a
change of treelike set (CTS) automorphism
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{T}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{X\smash{\sslash}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{\mathcal{T}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces=}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces=}

This can be calculated by evaluating
c_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
on the closed paths
c_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(e_{\overline{v}})
for each edge

of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
.

It will be helpful to briefly examine when a CTS automorphism
\left(c_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\right)_{\ast}\circ\left(c_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\right)_{\ast}
is symmetric. Recall that a symmetric automorphism is one where each generator
v\in V(\Gamma)
is sent to a conjugate of (possibly an inverse of) another generator. Therefore the paths
c_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}(e_{\overline{v}})
for
e_{\overline{v}}
an edge of
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
must be homotopic to a loop which contains only one edge dual to a hyperplane not in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Indeed, since the subcomplex
C^{\Pi}
formed of cubes with labels only in
\mathcal{T}
(cf.
[
BCV23
]
, §3.1) is
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
, it is contractible, so we can adjust the paths
\gamma,\delta\subseteq C^{\Pi}
so that each does not repeat any labels. Moreover, if
\delta
shares a label with
\gamma
, we can take the loop
\gamma e_{v}\delta^{-1}
to be formed of two loops, one of which is entirely contained in
C^{\Pi}
, so is null-homotopic. So we may assume that each label appears only once in the path
\gamma e_{v}\delta^{-1}
. Now evaluating this path on the collapse
c_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
, if this loop passes through more than one hyperplane not in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, the corresponding CTS automorphism will not be symmetric.

\smash{K_{\Gamma}^{\operatorname{min}(\mathcal{W})}}
, and Step (iii) is complete, which proves
Theorem
A
.

Corollary 7.9
.

The symmetric spine
K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma}
is contractible.

7.4.
Equivalent definition of symmetric
\Gamma
-complex

We can also deduce an equivalent condition for a
\Gamma
-complex to be symmetric, as mentioned after
Definition
3.7
. We call a non-null-homotopic loop
simple
if it cannot be decomposed as a concatenation of more than one non-null-homotopic loop.

Proposition 7.10
.

A
\Gamma
-complex is symmetric if and only if every edge lies in a unique homotopy class of simple non-null-homotopic loops.

Proof.

We do the backwards direction first, by contrapositive. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a treelike set in a non-symmetric
\Gamma
-complex
X
. Pick
H_{0}\in\mathcal{T}
, and suppose that
H_{0}
is not symmetric relative to
\mathcal{T}
; let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two of its companion hyperplanes, labelled by distinct vertices
v_{1}\neq v_{2}\in V(\Gamma)
. For each
i
, pick an edge
e_{i}
dual to
H_{i}
, and write
s(e_{i})
(resp.
t(e_{i})
) for the endpoint in
\beta(H_{0})
(resp.
\overline{\beta}(H_{0})
). Let
\gamma_{0i}
be the unique (up to homotopy) paths contained in
B(H_{0})
along edges dual to elements of
\mathcal{T}
from
s(e_{0})
to
s(e_{i})
, for
i=1,2
. Similarly, let
\delta_{0i}
be the unique (up to homotopy) paths contained in
\overline{B}(H_{0})
along edges dual to elements of
\mathcal{T}
from
t(e_{0})
to
t(e_{i})
. Now the paths
e_{0}\delta_{0i}e_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{0i}^{-1}
for
i=1,2
are both non-null-homotopic loops. Indeed, if one were null-homotopic, then its image under the homotopy equivalence
c_{\mathcal{T}}
would also be null-homotopic, which is a contradiction, as this image is the generator of
\pi_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\right)
corresponding to the vertex
v_{i}\in V(\Gamma)
. For the same reason, these two loops
e_{0}\delta_{0i}e_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{0i}^{-1}
cannot be homotopic to each other. Hence the edge
e_{0}
lies in two non-homotopic non-null-homotopic loops in
X
, whence this direction is complete.

Conversely, suppose that
X
is symmetric. Then every hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{T}
in a treelike set
\mathcal{T}
in
X
is symmetric relative to
X
. Let
e_{0}
be an arbitrary edge in
X
; we will prove that
e_{0}
lies in exactly one non-null-homotopic loop (up to homotopy). If
e_{0}
is a loop, then we’re done, so we may suppose this is not the case. Let
H_{0}
be the hyperplane dual to
e_{0}
. Since
e_{0}
is not a loop in
X
, we can take a treelike set
\mathcal{T}
containing
e_{0}
; by our assumption,
e_{0}
is symmetric relative to
\mathcal{T}
. Let
H_{1}
be the unique companion hyperplane to
H_{0}
, and let
v\in V(\Gamma)
be the label of
H_{1}
.

For
i=0,1
, let
s(e_{i})
(resp.
t(e_{i})
) denote the endpoint of
e_{i}
contained in
\beta(H_{0})
(resp.
\overline{\beta}(H_{0})
). Let
\gamma
(resp.
\delta
) denote the unique (up to homotopy) path contained in
B(H_{0})
(resp.
\overline{B}(H_{0})
) along edges dual to elements of
\mathcal{T}
from
s(e_{1})
to
s(e_{0})
(resp. from
t(e_{0})
to
t(e_{1})
). Then
\lambda\coloneqq e_{0}\delta e_{1}^{-1}\gamma
is a loop, and its image under
c_{\mathcal{T}}
is a non-null-homotopic loop in
\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}
corresponding to the generator
v\in\pi_{1}\left(\mathbb{S}_{\Gamma}\right)
. Hence
\lambda
is a non-null-homotopic loop in
X
.

It remains to prove that any other simple non-null-homotopic loop involving
e_{0}
is homotopic to
\lambda
. Let
\lambda^{\prime}
be one such; homotope
\lambda^{\prime}
so that it is of the form
\lambda^{\prime}=e_{0}\delta^{\prime}e_{1}^{-1}\gamma^{\prime}
, with
\gamma^{\prime}\subseteq B(H_{0})
and
\delta^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{B}(H_{0})
. [For clarity, we draw attention to the fact that
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\delta^{\prime}
are not required to consist entirely of edges dual to elements of
\mathcal{T}
.] If we can homotope
\gamma
onto
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\delta
onto
\delta^{\prime}
, then we are done, so without loss of generality suppose that
\delta
is not homotopic to
\delta^{\prime}
in
X
. If
\delta
is a loop, then it is null-homotopic, (and
t(e_{0})=t(e_{1})
) so
\delta^{\prime}
is a non-null-homotopic loop, in which case
\lambda^{\prime}
is not simple, which is a contradiction. Hence
\delta
is not a loop, so it is some non-empty path. If every edge of
\delta
appears in (a curve homotopic to)
\delta^{\prime}
, then once again
\lambda^{\prime}
is not simple. Therefore,
\delta
contains some edge
e
which does not appear in (a curve homotopic to)
\delta^{\prime}
.

Let
e
be dual to a hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{T}
. Since
X
is symmetric,
H
has a unique companion hyperplane. Since
e
forms part of the simple non-null-homotopic loop
\lambda
, this unique companion hyperplane must have a dual edge somewhere in
\lambda
. By
Lemma
7.2
, the only candidate edge is
e_{1}
, since it is the only one not dual to an element of
\mathcal{T}
. Hence
\overline{B}(H)\subseteq\overline{B}(H_{0})
, and

B(H)=\left(\overline{B}(H_{0})\setminus\overline{B}(H)\right)\cup B(H_{0}).

But now the path
\delta^{\prime}
begins in
\overline{B}(H_{0})\cap B(H)
and ends in
\overline{B}(H)
without ever leaving
\overline{B}(H_{0})
, which necessitates it involving
e
, which is a contradiction.
∎

In the case that
A_{\Gamma}
is free, this is precisely Collins’s original definition
[
Col89
]
that a graph is
symmetric
if every edge lies in a unique circuit.

8.
Dimension of the symmetric spine

In this section we study the relationship between the virtual cohomological dimension (

) of
\operatorname{\Sigma Out}(A_{\Gamma})
and the dimension of

. We recall the following fundamental theorem from §

Theorem 8.1

(
[

]
,
Theorem VIII.11.1
)
.

Let

Therefore, since

Work of Millard–Vogtmann
[

shows that the untwisted group of automorphisms,

, contains free abelian subgroups, with ‘natural’ generating sets. The rank of such a subgroup is a lower bound on
\textsc{vcd}(U(A_{\Gamma}))
. We summarise Theorems 4.25 & 4.28 from
[
MV19
]
in
Theorem
8.2
below; we first establish some notation.

For a subset

, denote by

the largest possible size of a compatible set of
\Gamma
-partitions, all of which are based at an element of

\Gamma
.

8.1.
Lower bound for
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))

Theorem 8.2

(
[
MV19
]
)
.

Fix a finite simplicial graph
\Gamma
.

(i)

For any set of principal
\Gamma
-partitions, multipliers can be chosen for the corresponding
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms so that they generate a free abelian subgroup of

(ii)

There exists a free abelian subgroup of

(iii)

Here, (i) & (ii) follow from (

be a compatible set of symmetric principal
\Gamma
-partitions of size
M^{\Sigma}(L)
.

By
Lemma
8.5
, we can assume that for any nonabelian equivalence class
[m]
, all partitions in
\Pi
are based at a single
m\in[m]
. Using
m
as the multiplier for each of these partitions, the associated symmetric outer
\Gamma
-Whitehead automorphisms pairwise commute.

If
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}\in\Pi
are based at
m
and
n
respectively with
[m,n]=1
, then
\varphi(\mathcal{P},m)
and
\varphi(\mathcal{Q},n)
commute.

If
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}\in\Pi
are based at
m
and
n
respectively with
[m,n]\neq 1
, then
\mathcal{P}
does not split
n
and
\mathcal{Q}
does not split
m
, since
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are symmetric. Hence by (
[
MV19
]
, Theorem 4.2),
\varphi(\mathcal{P},m)
and
\varphi(\mathcal{Q},n)
commute.

Hence we have a collection of infinite-order pairwise-commuting symmetric outer automorphisms of size
M^{\Sigma}(L)
. We are now at a stage in the proof of (
[
MV19
]
, Theorem 4.25) after which can be replicated without edit; this section proves that the elements of this collection are pairwise independent. This gives the result.
∎

Corollary 8.8
.

\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))\geq M^{\Sigma}(L)
.

8.2.
Upper bound for
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))

Proposition 8.9
.

M^{\Sigma}(L)=M^{\Sigma}(V)
.

Proof.

Our strategy is to start with a maximal compatible set
\Pi
of symmetric
\Gamma
-partitions (that is, one of size
M^{\Sigma}(V)
), and replace non-principal partitions with symmetric principal partitions, one-by-one.

If there are no non-principal partitions in
\Pi
, then we are done, so suppose this is not the case. By
Lemma
8.5
we may assume that for each principal nonabelian equivalence class and choice of representative
m
, we have
\Pi_{[m]}=\Pi_{m}
.

Let
u
be non-principal with
\Pi_{u}\neq\emptyset
. Choose a principal
m>_{\circ}u
. Since
d_{\Gamma}(u,m)=2
, both
m
and
m^{-1}
are always on the same side of an element of
\Pi_{[u]}
. Choose
Q
to be maximal among sides of elements of
\Pi_{[u]}
containing
\{m,m^{-1}\}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathcal{Q}
is based at
u
and moreover that
u\in Q
.

Since
u
and
m
do not commute, any partition based at
m
has a side either containing or contained in
Q
. Let
M
be the maximal side of an element of
\Pi_{m}
such that
m^{-1}\in M\subseteq Q
.

Define
P\coloneqq\left(Q\setminus M\right)\cup\{u^{-1}\}
. This defines a symmetric
\Gamma
-partition
\mathcal{P}
based at
m
which is incompatible with
\mathcal{Q}
, and so is not an element of
\Pi
. We aim to show that it can replace
\mathcal{Q}
. Note that
m\in P
.

If there does not exist
\mathcal{R}\in\Pi\setminus\mathcal{Q}
which is incompatible with
\mathcal{P}
then we can replace
\mathcal{Q}
with
\mathcal{P}
, and pass to considering another non-principal partition in
\Pi
. So suppose this is not the case; let
\mathcal{R}\in\Pi
be incompatible with
\mathcal{P}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is based at
n
.

Case I
:
n=u
.

If
\mathcal{R}
is based at
u
, then it has one side
R
which either contains or is contained in
Q
. By the maximality assumption in the choice of
\mathcal{Q}
, this side does not contain
\{m,m^{-1}\}
, so we must have
R\subseteq Q\setminus M
. Hence
R\subseteq P
, and so
\mathcal{R}
is compatible with
\mathcal{P}
, which is a contradiction.

Case II
:
n\neq u
.

Since
\mathcal{R}
is incompatible with
\mathcal{P}
, we have
d_{\Gamma}(n,m)\in\{0,2\}
. We cannot have
d_{\Gamma}(n,u)=1
, as
m>_{\circ}u
, so that would mean
d_{\Gamma}(n,m)=1
. Hence
\mathcal{R}
is not adjacent to
\mathcal{Q}
, so there exists a choice of sides
R^{\times}
,
Q^{\times}
such that
R^{\times}\cap Q^{\times}=\emptyset
.

Suppose that
Q^{\times}=Q
. This means that
u\notin R^{\times}
. We have dispensed with the case
n=u
, so since
\mathcal{R}
is symmetric, we also have
u^{-1}\notin R^{\times}
. Hence
R^{\times}\cap P=\emptyset
, which is a contradiction to the incompatibility of
\mathcal{R}
with
\mathcal{P}
.

Hence
Q^{\times}=\overline{Q}
, which means that
R^{\times}\subseteq Q
, which in turn means (since
\mathcal{Q}
is symmetric and not based at
n
) that
\{n,n^{-1}\}\subseteq Q
.

Case IIa
:
m=n
.

If
R^{\times}\ni m
, then
P\supseteq R^{\times}
, and we have a contradiction as
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
are incompatible. Hence
R^{\times}
contains
m^{-1}
. By the maximality assumption in the choice of
M
, we must have
R^{\times}\subseteq M
, whence
R^{\times}\cap P=\emptyset
, which is another contradiction.

Case IIb
:
m\neq n
.

Since
d_{\Gamma}(m,n)\neq 1
, if
\{m,m^{-1}\}\subsetneq R^{\times}
, then
\left(M\cup\{m\}\right)\cap R^{\times}=\emptyset
, in which case
R^{\times}\subseteq P
and we contradict incompatibility of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{P}
. Therefore
\left(M\cup\{m\}\right)\subseteq R^{\times}
. Since
Q
was chosen to be maximal among sides of elements of
\Pi_{[u]}
containing
\{m,m^{-1}\}
, we must have
n\notin[u]
. Hence either
n>_{\circ}u
or there exists
x\in\operatorname{lk}({u})\subseteq\operatorname{st}({m})
such that
x\notin\operatorname{lk}({n})
. Then
d_{\Gamma}(x,u)=d_{\Gamma}(x,m)=1
, which implies that
u
and
m
are in the same component of
\Gamma^{\pm}\setminus\operatorname{st}({n})^{\pm}
. But this is impossible, since
\mathcal{R}
separates
u
and
m
.

Hence
n>_{\circ}u
. In this case, repeat the argument with
n
replacing
m
, and with some
N\supseteq R^{\times}
based at
n
replacing
M
. If we reach the same point in this second iteration, finding some
n^{\prime}>_{\circ}u
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
based at
n^{\prime}
playing the same role as
\mathcal{R}
here, then we cannot have
n^{\prime}=m
. Indeed, if
n^{\prime}=m
then
R\cap R^{\prime}\ni n
,
\overline{R}\cap R^{\prime}\ni n^{-1}
,
R\cap\overline{R^{\prime}}\ni m
or
m^{-1}
, and
\overline{R}\cap\overline{R^{\prime}}\ni u
, which implies that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is incompatible with
\mathcal{R}
(as
d_{\Gamma}(n,m)\neq 1)
; this is a contradiction. Hence these iterations must terminate at some point, at which we have exhausted all ways of finding an element of
\Pi\setminus\mathcal{Q}
which is incompatible with
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, we may replace
\mathcal{Q}
in
\Pi
with
\mathcal{P}
.

We can now repeat this argument until every non-principal element of
\Pi
has been replaced by a symmetric principal partition, at which point we’re done.
∎

Theorem 8.10
.

\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))=\dim(K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma})
.

Proof.

By
Corollary
8.8
, we have
M^{\Sigma}(L)\leq\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))
. Combining this with

8.9
and the observation that
\textsc{vcd}(\Sigma\operatorname{Out}(A_{\Gamma}))\leq M^{\Sigma}(V)=\dim(K_{\Gamma}^{\Sigma})
gives the result.
