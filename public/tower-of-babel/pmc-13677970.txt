From a visible health problem to an actionable research priority: Development and demonstration of the VISIBLE-to-ACTION framework
Authors: Gilbert C. Magulod, Jamaica Castillo-Pedro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and demonstrate the VISIBLE-to-ACTION framework as a transparent method for moving from a documented health problem to a defensible research priority, while distinguishing research, evidence, decision, and implementation gaps. The framework addresses the limitation of conventional gap statements that rely primarily on claims that few studies exist without establishing why additional evidence is necessary.MethodsA structured conceptual synthesis was undertaken across four established literatures: health research priority setting, evidence-gap mapping, knowledge translation/evidence-informed policy, and implementation science. Core constructs were retained only when they addressed problem verification, expected-versus-observed conditions, evidence insufficiency, decision relevance, or actionability. The resulting framework was iteratively specified as seven VISIBLE stages plus an ACTION test. A worked application to diabetes self-management in the Philippines was then used to operationalize the framework. Candidate gaps were scored on seven explicitly defined 1–5 criteria using equal weights; scores were treated as a transparent reasoning aid, not as a validated instrument.ResultsThe framework extends conventional research-gap identification from a literature-counting exercise toward an evidence-to-action process. VISIBLE-to-ACTION links problem verification to a defined decision that remains unsupported by adequate evidence. The worked application distinguished practical, population/context, methodological, implementation/sustainability, and decision gaps and identified a priority question concerning which context-sensitive determinants and intervention components can be implemented equitably and sustained in routine primary care. Comparison with established approaches showed that the framework is complementary rather than a replacement: evidence-gap maps organize evidence, priority-setting methods select priorities, implementation frameworks explain uptake and sustainment, logic models connect activities to outcomes, and knowledge-translation approaches guide evidence use.ConclusionVISIBLE-to-ACTION provides a structured approach for developing research rationales that move beyond the assertion that “few studies exist” toward identifying evidence that is necessary for an important health decision. The framework is a conceptual reasoning tool with a worked demonstration, not a validated prioritization instrument. Its transferability should be tested in additional diseases, policy settings, emerging topics, and data-poor environments using stakeholder-based and reliability-focused evaluations.

Introduction

A strong health research proposal should do more than identify an interesting topic or list limitations of previous studies. It should demonstrate why the proposed investigation is necessary, timely, and capable of generating evidence that matters. Conventional research-gap statements often conclude with formulations such as “few studies have examined,” “limited evidence exists,” or “there is a lack of research.” Such statements may indicate a literature gap, but they do not necessarily demonstrate a public-health problem, an important uncertainty, or a decision that cannot currently be made.

Health research priority-setting guidance emphasizes explicit context, information gathering, criteria, methods for deciding priorities, implementation planning, evaluation, and transparency [3], [4]. Evidence-gap maps similarly organize what is known and where evidence is sparse, but they do not themselves determine which unanswered question should be funded or acted upon [6].

The VISIBLE-to-ACTION framework was developed to address this front-end reasoning problem. It retains the manuscript's original “What Should Be–What Is Actual–What Is the Gap” logic, but makes the transition from discrepancy to evidence necessity explicit. Its distinctive unit of analysis is not the absent article but the unresolved health decision: what important action cannot yet be chosen, targeted, implemented, or scaled confidently because the available evidence is insufficient, inconsistent, poorly transferable, or inadequately contextualized. The framework is not presented as a replacement for established priority-setting, evidence mapping, implementation, logic-model, or knowledge-translation approaches. Rather, it is a bridge among them. This distinction responds directly to the concern that the original manuscript combined familiar concepts without showing what the combination contributes.

Methods: Framework development and demonstration

A Framework development was used a structured conceptual synthesis. The literature was organized into four bodies of work: (1) health research priority setting and criteria-based prioritization [2], [3], [4], [5]; (2) evidence-gap mapping and transparent evidence identification [6]; (3) knowledge translation and evidence-informed policy, including the Knowledge-to-Action and EVIPNet approaches [7], [15], [16]; and (4) implementation science, including CFIR and RE-AIM [8], [9], [10]. Logic-model guidance was also reviewed because it explicitly connects activities with intended outcomes [11]. The purpose was not to conduct a systematic review or estimate an effect, but to identify the functions that a front-end research-gap method must perform and then specify where those functions were already addressed by established approaches.

The synthesis followed four analytic questions: (a) How is the underlying health problem established? (b) How is the difference between an expected and observed condition made explicit? (c) How are evidence limitations converted into a priority research question? and (d) How is the eventual contribution linked to an actionable decision? Concepts that addressed these functions were retained and arranged into a sequential but revisable pathway. The seven VISIBLE stages were then tested against the diabetes self-management example already used in the manuscript, with the additional requirement that every stage produce a traceable output.

No Delphi panel, formal expert-consensus exercise, psychometric validation, or inter-rater reliability study was conducted in different nationalities. Accordingly, the framework is reported as a conceptually derived and demonstrated framework rather than a validated instrument. The worked scoring exercise is intentionally transparent so that future users can reproduce, critique, and test it. For the demonstration, candidate gaps were scored from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high) on importance, urgency, decision relevance, evidence insufficiency, feasibility, equity, and actionability. Equal weights were used because no empirical basis exists for assigning differential weights at this stage. The overall score is the arithmetic mean of the seven criteria. Users should score independently when more than one assessor is available and then resolve disagreements through documented consensus or a prespecified median/mean rule. Sensitivity analysis should be used when alternative weights materially change the priority. These procedures are recommendations for use, not claims of established reliability.

The framework (Table 1) proposes that a defensible research rationale is constructed through seven VISIBLE stages followed by an ACTION test. Rather than treating the gap as a sentence placed at the end of an introduction, it treats gap identification as a systematic analytical process. The stages are linked but not strictly linear. New evidence may alter the observed condition, the evidence map may reveal that a proposed question is already answered, and stakeholder input may change the decision criteria. Distinguishing four gap types

The framework uses four gap labels that should not be treated as synonyms. A research gap is an unanswered question or insufficiently investigated relationship. An evidence gap is a deficiency in the quantity, quality, consistency, applicability, or synthesis of evidence needed to answer a question. A decision gap exists when an important health, programme, practice, or policy decision cannot be made confidently because the evidence is insufficient. An implementation gap is a failure to achieve intended delivery, uptake, fidelity, equity, or sustainment despite an intervention or evidence base that is sufficiently known to warrant action.

The causal sequence is therefore: a visible problem may reveal a research gap; appraisal of the literature determines whether that gap is an evidence gap; the evidence gap becomes a decision gap only when it constrains an identifiable decision; and an implementation gap is invoked when the main uncertainty concerns execution or sustainment of an intervention or policy. A single project may contain more than one type, but the researcher should state which gap is primary.

Worked application: Diabetes self-Management in the Philippines

The example below is deliberately treated as a demonstration rather than as empirical validation.Statement ExamplePhilippine evidence identifies a substantial diabetes burden, undiagnosed disease, limited insurance coverage, high out-of-pocket costs, and challenges in multidisciplinary management [1]. Regional evidence shows that diabetes self-management is multidimensional, while socioeconomic disadvantage, affordability, health literacy, cultural influences, competing priorities, and social support can shape self-management [12], [13], [14]. ASEAN evidence also indicates variation in how diabetes-care components are implemented across health systems [12].

As shown above, Importance reflects potential population-health consequence; urgency reflects the cost of delayed evidence; decision relevance reflects whether a named decision would change; evidence insufficiency reflects the extent to which existing evidence cannot answer that decision; feasibility reflects whether a credible study can reasonably be conducted in the target setting; equity reflects potential to address distributional disadvantage; and actionability reflects the plausibility that findings would change practice, programme design, implementation, or policy. The equal-weight demonstration yields a tie at the top (mean 5.00) between the practical, implementation/sustainability, and decision gaps.

Rather than forcing a numerical winner, the framework requires a second-order decision: the decision gap is the most integrative formulation because it incorporates the practical and implementation uncertainties into one identifiable choice. The resulting priority question is: “Which modifiable individual, household, community, and primary-care determinants, and which feasible intervention components, should be prioritized to improve equitable and sustained diabetes self-management in underserved Philippine primary-care settings?” This question is more specific than “few Philippine studies exist” and can be operationalized through mixed-method, implementation-informed research.

ACTION test and boundaries of transferability

The ACTION test asks three questions: (1) Actionable contribution what specific action could change if the uncertainty were resolved? (2) Translation who would use the evidence, where, and through what pathway? and (3) Outcome orientation which meaningful health, service, equity, or population outcome could improve? For the diabetes example, the potential users are primary-care teams, programme managers, researchers, and policymakers; the pathway is through locally feasible intervention selection and implementation; and relevant outcomes include sustained self-management, equitable access, service integration, and diabetes-related health outcomes.

Transferability must be moderated. The framework has been demonstrated here in one chronic-disease example and should not yet be described as validated across public-health fields. It may be difficult to apply when surveillance data are poor, the topic is rapidly emerging, evidence is almost absent, several competing decisions exist, or the expected condition is normatively contested. In such cases, the framework should explicitly label uncertainty, involve stakeholders, use multiple evidence sources, and avoid treating a low evidence volume as proof of a high-priority gap. Formal stakeholder consultation is especially important because research priority setting contains value judgments and equity considerations [5].

Discussion and conclusion

Theis framework addresses a key limitation of conventional gap statements: a literature gap is not automatically a consequential research priority. Its contribution is therefore modest but explicit. VISIBLE-to-ACTION organizes existing functions from priority setting, evidence mapping, knowledge translation, implementation science, and programme logic into a front-end audit trail that asks whether a visible problem leads to an identifiable discrepancy, an evidence limitation, a decision gap, and a plausible action pathway. This is a complementary architecture, not a claim that the underlying concepts are individually new. The principal limitations are equally important. The framework has not undergone Delphi validation in various context of nationalities, formal reliability testing, comparative testing against alternative prioritization methods, or prospective evaluation of whether users applying it generate better research questions or better decisions. The worked matrix is a reasoning aid and should not be interpreted as a psychometric score or a validated priority index. Future studies should test inter-rater agreement, sensitivity to weighting schemes, stakeholder acceptability, reproducibility, and performance across infectious disease, environmental health, maternal and child health, mental health, health systems, and emerging health threats. VISIBLE-to-ACTION offers a transparent method for moving from a visible health problem to a defensible and potentially actionable research rationale. Its most distinctive construct is the Decision Gap, the important decision that remains unsupported by sufficient evidence. The framework should now be treated as a testable methodological proposal rather than a validated instrument.
