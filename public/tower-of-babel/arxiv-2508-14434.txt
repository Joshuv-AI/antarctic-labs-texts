Breaking the Baryon Density–Hubble Constant Degeneracy in Fast Radio Burst Applications with Associated Gravitational Waves Preprint: APS/123-QED
Joscha N. Jahns-Schindler, Laura G. Spitler

Abstract
Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) are a unique probe of the cosmos, owing to dispersion caused by free electrons in the intergalactic medium (IGM). Two of the main quantities of interest are degenerate: the density of matter Ω b ​ f d \Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}} outside of galaxies and the Hubble constant H 0 H_{0} . Here, we present a new possibility of breaking the degeneracy without invoking early Universe priors on Ω b \Omega_{\mathrm{b}} . Assuming some FRBs originate in compact object mergers, the combination of dispersion and luminosity distance from the gravitational wave (GW) can be used to measure Ω b ​ h 2 ​ f d \Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}} (where h h is the dimensionless Hubble constant). We show that this measurement can be combined with the abundant FRBs that have a redshift measurement. This combination breaks the degeneracy with the Hubble constant. We develop a Bayesian framework and forecast that third-generation GW detectors are required to obtain meaningful constraints. We forecast that one year of Einstein Telescope operations can constrain H 0 H_{0} to ± 6 km s − 1 Mpc − 1 \pm$6\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$ and Ω b ​ h 2 ​ f d \Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}} to − 0.0016 + 0.0015 {}^{+0.0015}_{-0.0016} (68 % \,\% credible interval). The method can also be used with luminosity distances obtained through other means than GWs.



I
Introduction

The disagreement between measurements of the local expansion rate of the Universe and the Hubble constant inferred from early Universe probes is known as the Hubble tension and poses one of the biggest problems to the standard cosmological model
(
Riess et al. 2022
;
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024
)
(see
Abdalla et al. 2022
;
Hu and Wang 2023
, for recent reviews)
.
Because systematics are the leading order uncertainties, independent probes are essential to judge the significance of the disagreement.
One such proposed probe are Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs).
The dispersion measure (
{\mathrm{DM}}
) of FRBs is the integrated column density of electrons along their path.
As such, it is a proxy for distance that can be compared to the FRBs’ host galaxy redshift (
z
).
The relation depends on the geometry of the Universe and hence can be used to infer cosmological parameters.
However, the
{\mathrm{DM}}
-
z
relation depends on the combination
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}H_{0}
, with
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}
the density parameter for baryons and
f_{\mathrm{d}}
the fraction of diffuse baryons in the IGM and in galaxy halos.

Macquart et al. 2020
constrain
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h
using a fixed
f_{\mathrm{d}}
from taking the remainder of a census of baryons in galaxies, where we write the
h=H_{0}/$100\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$
.

James et al. 2022
break the degeneracy further by assuming
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}=0.02242
from
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
.
In this paper, we propose a new method to measure
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
using a subset of FRBs, proposed to be associated with Gravitational Waves (GWs).

The merger of two neutron stars (NSs) or of a NS with a black hole (BH) could potentially produce a GW and an FRB.
Although, NSs are now the favoured progenitor for the majority of the FRB population
(
Bochenek et al. 2020
;
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020
)
, mergers could still cause a fraction of FRBs.
The volumetric BNS merger rate of 10–
1700\text{\,}{\mathrm{Gpc}}^{-3}\text{\,}{\mathrm{yr}}^{-1}

Abbott et al. 2023

allows them to account for
\sim
2\text{\,}\%
of the observed FRBs, which have a rate of
7.3^{+8.8}_{-3.8}\times 10^{4}\,$\mathrm{b}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{s}$\,${\mathrm{Gpc}}^{-3}\text{\,}{\mathrm{yr}}^{-1}$
above an energy of
{10}^{39}\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}
(not corrected for beaming)
(
Shin et al. 2023
)
.
The accounted fraction could be higher if the coalescence or the merger product produce more than one FRB.
Theoretically, FRBs have been suggested to arise from NS mergers in over four different ways
(see
Zhang 2024
, for a comprehensive review)
.
The interactions of magnetic fields before the merger might result in an FRB
(
Lipunov and Panchenko 1996
;
Metzger and Zivancev 2016
)
.
A jet that is launched during the merger could interact with the surrounding medium and produce an FRB
(
Usov and Katz 2000
)
.
The NS resulting from the merger might emit FRBs in any way that has been suggested for NSs.
Finally, the newly formed NS can collapse into a BH if its mass exceeds a certain threshold.
When this happens, its whole magnetic field snaps and recombines, which might result in an FRB
(
Falcke and Rezzolla 2014
)
.
Additionally, NS-BH mergers have been proposed as an FRB source through the interaction of the BH with the NS magnetic field
(
Mingarelli et al. 2015
;
Bhattacharyya 2017
;
Abramowicz et al. 2017
)
.

Several observational studies have been conducted to search for associated GW-FRB events.

Rowlinson and Anderson 2019
put constraints on FRB counterparts of GRB 150424A with triggered observations of the Murchison Widefield Array
(
Tingay et al. 2013
, MWA)
.

Moroianu et al. 2022
reported a tentative association between GW190425 and FRB 190425A.

Panther et al. 2022
could also identify the most probable host of FRB 190425A to be the galaxy UGC10667 with
80\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
confidence.
However,
Bhardwaj et al. 2023
re-estimate the GW parameters under the assumption that UGC10667 is the host.
They argue that an FRB could not pass through the ejecta of a merger after only 2.5 hours without noticeable attenuation.
They further infer a viewing angle of
>$$
on the system, disfavouring the FRB emission in our direction.
Additionally,
Radice et al. 2024
argue that at the location and distance of FRB 190425A, a kilonova would have been observed.
Most recently,
Rowlinson et al. 2023
reported a tentative association of GRB 201006A with an FRB found with the Low Frequency Array
(
van Haarlem et al. 2013
, LOFAR,)
.
However in summary, no sufficiently significant association has yet been made that would allow establishing a definitive connection between GWs and FRBs.

Like classical probes, BNS merger as standard sirens can be used to measure
H_{0}
.
Here, the luminosity distance obtained from the chirp is compared to the redshift of the host galaxy.
This way,
Abbott et al. 2017a
obtained
H_{0}=70^{+12}_{-8}\,$\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$
from GW170817.
The campaign around GW170817 benefited from the fortunate circumstance that the event happened at a relatively close distance of only
40\text{\,}\mathrm{Mpc}

(
Abbott et al. 2017b
,
z=0.01
)
.
Gradual improvements of GW experiments and eventually the third-generation detectors, Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer
(
Punturo et al. 2010
;
Evans et al. 2021
)
, will detect GWs to greater distances.
This will pose challenges for multi frequency follow-up campaigns similar to those conducted to find GW170817.
It comes as a convenient advantage that most telescopes used for FRB searches come with a large field of view.
ASKAP for example has a
30\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{2}
field of view and could almost cover the 31-
{\mathrm{}}^{2}
initial LIGO-Virgo localisation region of GW170817 in a single pointing.
Should BNS merger emit FRBs, these could lead future multi-messenger follow-up.

Wei et al. 2018
have previously developed a method
(
Gao et al. 2014
, largely based on)
to use luminosity distances,
D\!_{L}
, from GW–FRB associations together with
{\mathrm{DM}}
s and redshifts to measure
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
w
in a
w
CDM cosmology.

Li et al. 2019
proposed to employ the ratio of
D\!_{L}/{\mathrm{DM}}

(
Gao et al. 2014
)
to measure
f_{\mathrm{d}}
using a minimum
\chi^{2}
-statistic.

Motivated by the tentative associations with unclear hosts, and since there will always be FRBs whose redshift cannot be obtained
(
Marnoch et al. 2023
;
Jahns-Schindler et al. 2023
)
, we developed a new method to use GW–FRB associations that does not require a redshift.
We derive the full solution for the DM–
D\!_{L}
relation by inverting
D\!_{L}(z)
.
We only use the
{\mathrm{DM}}
and
D\!_{L}
of a subpopulation of FRBs and combine it with normal FRBs with measured redshifts.
This combination breaks the degeneracy in the
{\mathrm{DM}}
-
z
relation and yields
H_{0}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
separately.

II
Methods

The method we propose is based on the measurement of the
{\mathrm{DM}}
from the FRB and the luminosity distance,
D\!_{L}
, derived from the GW.
Both quantities are imprecise distance measurements.
The leading uncertainty in
D\!_{L}
comes from the degeneracy with the inclination angle of the merger system.
The reason is that the signal from a farther, face-on merger system can have a very similar shape as a closer but more edge-on system.
The measured dispersion is a composite of contributions from the host galaxy
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
, the diffuse cosmic plasma in the IGM and the circumgalactic medium of intervening galaxies
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
, and the Milky Way
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}
, including its halo
(
Deng and Zhang 2014
)
:

{\mathrm{DM}}={\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}+{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}+\frac{{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}}{1+z}\,.

(1)

The distance of the FRB can be estimated using the sight line-averaged
\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z)
.
The leading uncertainties in
{\mathrm{DM}}
stem from sight line-to-sight line variations in the baryon density (e.g. how many filaments are intersected) and from the uncertain
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
.
We will marginalise over the probability distribution functions of
D\!_{L}
and
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
in a Bayesian framework. But first, we derive the sight line-averaged
\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(D\!_{L})
.

II.1
The
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
D\!_{L}
relation

We assume a flat
\Lambda
-cold-dark-matter (
\Lambda
CDM) universe where the redshift-dependent Hubble parameter is given by the Friedmann equation
(
Peacock 1999
, e.g.)
in the form

H(z)=H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}\,,

(2)

where
H_{0}
is the Hubble constant,
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}{\rho_{0}}
the present-day matter density in terms of the critical density
\rho_{0}=\frac{3H_{0}^{2}}{8\pi G}
with the gravitational constant
G
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=\frac{\rho_{\Lambda}}{\rho_{0}}
the dark energy density, which can be expressed in terms of the cosmological constant
\Lambda
as
\rho_{\Lambda}=\Lambda c^{2}/(8\pi G)
.
The luminosity distance for a source at redshift
z
follows as

D\!_{L}(z)=(1+z)\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\differential{z}\,.

(3)

The average of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
over many sight lines is given as
(
Ioka 2003
;
Inoue 2004
;
Deng and Zhang 2014
)

\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z)=\frac{3c}{8\pi Gm_{\mathrm{p}}}\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}H_{0}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{(1+z)\,f_{\mathrm{d}}(z)\,\chi(z)}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\differential{z}\,,

(4)

where
m_{\mathrm{p}}
is the proton mass and
\chi(z)
the ionisation fraction at a given redshift, which can be expressed in terms of the ionisation fractions of hydrogen and helium
\chi(z)=\frac{3}{4}\chi_{\mathrm{H}}(z)+\frac{1}{8}\chi_{\mathrm{He}}(z)
.
In this work, we will consider only low-
z
FRBs, so we assume hydrogen and helium to be fully ionised, i.e.,
\chi_{\mathrm{H}}(z)=\chi_{\mathrm{He}}(z)=1
, and we assume
f_{\mathrm{d}}
is constant.
Eq. (
4
) then becomes

\displaystyle\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z)=

\displaystyle\frac{3c}{8\pi Gm_{\mathrm{p}}}\frac{7}{8}\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}H_{0}

(5)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1+z}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}(1+z)^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}}}\differential{z}\,.

This famous
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
relation depends on the product
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}H_{0}
, such that these quantities can not be inferred individually.
This degeneracy can only be broken with additional constraints, e.g., from measurements of the cosmic microwave background.

If only
{\mathrm{DM}}
and
D\!_{L}
are available while
z
is not directly measurable, we can invert
D\!_{L}(z)
to obtain

\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(D\!_{L})
.
The inversion of
D\!_{L}(z)
can only be done numerically, but this does not hinder us from using the relationship.
We will refer to the inverted function as
z(D\!_{L})
and write

\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(D\!_{L})=\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z(D\!_{L}))\,.

(6)

The Taylor approximation can be done analytically; the first-order term is

\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(D\!_{L})\approx\frac{3}{8\pi Gm_{\mathrm{p}}}\frac{7}{8}\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}H_{0}^{2}\,D\!_{L}\,.

(7)

Interestingly, this equation is proportional to
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}H_{0}^{2}
, while the classic
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
relation has a proportionality to
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}H_{0}
.
Combining the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
D\!_{L}
relation with the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
relation, i.e., GW-FRB associations with independent FRBs that have a measured redshift, we can break the degeneracy between
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}f_{\mathrm{d}}
and
H_{0}
.

Figure 1:
Curves along which parameters are degenerate in different methods.
Measuring both relations breaks the degeneracy by allowing only the intersection of the two curves.

Fig.
1
illustrates the curves along which perfectly measured relations would still allow parameters to be free. If the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
and
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
D\!_{L}
relations are both measured, only the intersection of the two relations is allowed and the degeneracy is broken.
The degeneracy can also be broken by using only the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
D\!_{L}
relation with FRBs at different redshift.
The reason is the deviation of the full solution from the linear relation.
Due to large uncertainties in single measurements, this variation will only give useful constraints long after the combination with the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
relation.

II.2
Bayesian framework

To utilise the derived relation and combine it with other FRB data, we use a Bayesian framework.
From Bayes rule
(see e.g.
Jaynes and Bretthorst 2003
, for an introduction)
, the posterior probability distribution is proportional to the product of the likelihood and the prior probability:

\displaystyle p(

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0}\mid{\mathrm{DM}},D\!_{L})

\displaystyle\propto p({\mathrm{DM}},D\!_{L}\mid\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})\,p(\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}p({\mathrm{DM}}_{i},{D\!_{L}}_{i}\mid\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})\,p(\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})\,,

(8)

where
i
goes over all FRBs.

In this exploratory work, we will restrict ourselves to the two free parameters
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
and
H_{0}
, but additional parameters should be left free in further studies.
We assume that the Milky Way contribution can be subtracted from the measured
{\mathrm{DM}}
with sufficient precision, such that we can use the resulting excess
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}={\mathrm{DM}}-{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}={\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}+{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}/(1+z)
.

We follow
Macquart et al. 2020
in the likelihood definitions of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
and
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
.
We describe
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
by a log-normal PDF in the formulation of
Jahns-Schindler et al. 2023
with median
{\mathrm{DM}}_{0}
and the standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}
of the
{\mathrm{DM}}

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{host}}({\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}

\displaystyle\mid{\mathrm{DM}}_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{host}})=\frac{\log_{10}(e)}{{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}\sqrt{2\pi}}

(9)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\frac{(\log_{10}{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}-\log_{10}{\mathrm{DM}}_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}^{2}}\right)\,.

We describe the PDF of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
for a given
D\!_{L}
in terms of
\Delta=\frac{{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}}{\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z(D\!_{L}))}
and with the fluctuation parameter
F
as

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{cosm}}({\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\mid

\displaystyle D\!_{L},\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0},F)=

\displaystyle\frac{A\Delta^{-3}}{{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\Delta^{-3}-C_{0})^{2}}{18\,\sigma^{2}}\right)\,,

(10)

where
\sigma=F/\sqrt{z(D\!_{L})}
,
A
is the normalisation constant and
C_{0}
is fixed by the condition
\langle\Delta\rangle=1
. For the assumed flat universe, the normalisation can be calculated analytically and is given in the appendix.

Because
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}=({\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}-{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}})\times(1+z)
, the above equations still leave some freedom for either
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
or
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
.
We, therefore, use a flat prior for
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
between 0 and
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}
and marginalise over it.
Additionally,
D\!_{L}
is not an exact measurement but described by a posterior distribution resulting from the GW analysis.
When simulating data, we assume that the uncertainty of
D\!_{L}
follows a normal distribution.
Finally, we incorporate the constraints on
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}H_{0}f_{\mathrm{d}}
from FRBs with redshifts (and without GWs)
as a prior in the form

p_{H_{0}}(H_{0}\mid\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}})

and put a flat prior distribution for
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
.
Collecting everything in Eq. (
8
) and integrating over
D\!_{L}
and
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}
, yields

\displaystyle p(

\displaystyle{\mathrm{DM}}_{i},{D\!_{L}}_{i}\mid\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})\,p(\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}

\displaystyle h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})=\int\differential{\DL}\int_{0}^{{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}}\differential{\DMc}\,p({\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}},D\!_{L}\mid\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})\,p(\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\DL}\int_{0}^{{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}}\differential{\DMc}\,

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{cosm}}({\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\mid D\!_{L},\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}},H_{0},F)\,p_{\mathrm{host}}({\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{exc}}-{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\mid{\mathrm{DM}}_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{host}})

\displaystyle\times p_{H_{0}}(H_{0}\mid\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}})\,p(D\!_{L})\,p(\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}})

(11)

where
F
is fixed but can be marginalised over in future work, and
{\mathrm{DM}}_{0}
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}

As conservative values, we pick 10 and

As the FRB-

III.3
Leaving the host DM free

So far, we have assumed that
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
in the GW-FRB population has the same distribution as
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
of the general FRB population, this is unlikely if the progenitors are different.
The FRBs coming from mergers could, e.g., come from an older stellar population, which would also imply a lower
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
compared to an FRB population of young magnetars.
This requires a separate inference of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
for the GW-FRB population.

We model
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
as a log-normal distribution with parameters
{\mathrm{DM}}_{0}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}
and leave them as free parameters in our inference.
As input values, we take
{\mathrm{DM}}_{0}=$100\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}=0.57
.
Because of the dependency on
z
in Eq.
1
, a realistic spread in
z
would now help in constraining the
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
parameters and give more realistic forecasts.
We therefore distribute the sources in
z
.
We choose a normal distribution requiring values to be positive, with 20 FRBs with
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0.1,0.1)
and 100 FRBs with
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0.2,0.2)
.
This choice is motivated again by fig. 15 of
Iacovelli et al. 2022
.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Comparison to other probes

The constraints on
H_{0}
we forecast for 120 events are tighter than the current constraints from FRB
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
measurements with CMB priors on
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}
, which yield an uncertainty of
\sigma_{H_{0}}=^{+22}_{-12}\,$\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$

(
James et al. 2022
,
90\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
credible;)
.
Compared to the assumed future constraints of
\sigma_{H_{0}}=$0.8\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$
, we are loosing a factor 7.5 in precision; the price we pay for independence from early Universe measurements.

Compared to current measurements, the predictions exceed some less precise, current probes like GWs
(
Abbott et al. 2021
,
H_{0}=69^{+16}_{-8}\,$\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}$
(
68\text{\,}\%
credible),)
, but we would have to accumulate several years of data to reach the current precision of, e.g., Type Ia supernovae calibrated with Cepheids and parallax measurements
(
Riess et al. 2022
,
73.04(\pm 1.04)\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}
,)
or from Baryonic Acoustic Oscillations
(
DESI Collaboration et al. 2024
,
68.52(\pm 0.62)\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}
,)
.
In the end, how well we can understand the systematics in FRB
{\mathrm{DM}}
s will be decisive.

IV.2
Reviewing assumptions

The assumptions of the forecast were conservative in several ways.
The assumed distribution of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
from
James et al. 2022
given by
{\mathrm{DM}}_{0}=170^{+59}_{-47}\,${\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$
(all uncertainties in this discussion are 68% credible) and
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}=0.57^{+0.13}_{-0.09}
is high in comparison to the results of other studies.

Shin et al. 2023
for example find
{\mathrm{DM}}_{0}=84^{+69}_{-49}\,${\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{host}}=0.41^{+0.21}_{-0.20}
.
This would result in a much narrower PDF of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
and in turn much narrower posterior distributions.

Our assumption of vanishing
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}
is not always warranted.
The halo contribution is known with an uncertainty of
\sim 10\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}
, which is certainly much smaller than the uncertainty of
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
and can be absorbed by it.
Ideally yet, it is modelled separately as, e.g.,
Hoffmann et al. 2024
do with
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}\sim\mathcal{N}($50\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$,$15\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$)
.
In particular at low galactic latitudes, model uncertainties in the MW ISM can be larger than the halo uncertainty and one problem is that the uncertainties themselves are not very well known
(
Price et al. 2021
)
.
The effects from wrongly estimated
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}
can be estimated using the approximation
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\approx z\times$1000\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-3}\text{\,}\mathrm{pc}$
.
Generally, an underestimated
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{MW}}
will produce an estimate of
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
and
H_{0}
that is too high.
This potential bias is shared with all FRBs and is therefore one of the primary goals that will be addressed with close-by FRBs.

The uncertainty in each
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
could be reduced by detailed optical follow-up.
By taking spectra of galaxies close to the sight line, one can attribute a part of the
{\mathrm{DM}}
to their halo and identify filaments or galaxy clusters along the sight line
(
Simha et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
Khrykin et al. 2024
)
.

Although we derive it in the context of a GW-FRB association, the method presented in this paper only requires a luminosity distance measurement of any kind in combination with the
{\mathrm{DM}}
of an FRB.

IV.3
Preferred direction of FRB emission

In this work, we implicitly assumed that the emission direction of the FRB is independent of the inclination of the merger system with respect to the observer.
If instead there is a preferred direction, the chances of simultaneous detection of FRBs and GWs are affected, and the
D\!_{L}
inference can be biased.
If FRBs are preferentially emitted towards the poles but isotropic emission is assumed, the inferred
D\!_{L}
is lower than the real value, resulting in
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
and
H_{0}
being biased high.

Some models for FRB emission from BNS mergers favour a direction, but often the direction is not explicitly discussed or not predicted.
In models where the FRB emerges from the jet
(
Goodman 1986
;
Paczynski 1986
;
Usov and Katz 2000
)
or the GW itself
(
Moortgat and Kuijpers 2003
;
McWilliams and Levin 2011
)
, the direction of the rotational axis is preferred.
In models with pulsar-like emission
(
Totani 2013
, e.g.)
, the direction of the pulsar magnetic field is preferred, which is expected to align with the system’s rotational axis during inspiral
(
Cheng and Yu 2014
;
Giacomazzo et al. 2015
)
; yet more complex magnetic fields might allow emission in other directions.
Ultimately, it is unclear which models can produce FRBs.

To find the correct prior, the beaming direction of the FRB with respect to the merger axis needs to be studied.
One possibility is to compare the FRB brightness with the brightness of other electro-magnetic counterparts, e.g., X-rays and gamma rays.

IV.4
Future detectors not considered here

Apart from the Einstein Telescope considered here, additional 2.5 and third-generation GW detectors are being planned.
The Cosmic Explorer
(
Evans et al. 2021
)
is planned to start operations in 2035.

Iacovelli et al. 2022
predict the BNS merger rates to increase from
910160\,000
per year for the Einstein Telescope alone to
3800650\,000
per year for the combination of Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer.
The precision in the luminosity measurements will also increase and therefore the number of events with a
<
10\text{\,}\%
uncertainty from
\sim
20 to
\sim
2000.

In terms of FRB detections, the most suitable telescopes will be ones with a very wide field of view, like the Bustling Universe Radio Survey Telescope in Taiwan
(
Lin et al. 2022
, BURSTT,)
.

IV.5
Mergers with redshift measurements

In this work, we explicitly assumed events without measured redshifts.
If the redshift is measured, the
D\!_{L}
–
z
relation could provide the
H_{0}
measurement to directly break the degeneracy in the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
relation.
It would be straightforward to extend our Bayesian framework to include the redshift.

However, when assuming perfectly known redshifts, the likelihoods of
{\mathrm{DM}}
and
D\!_{L}
factorise and become independent.
This method would therefore be similar to combining the FRB data with some external
H_{0}
measurements.
The joint analysis of
D\!_{L}
,
{\mathrm{DM}}
, and
z
will be favourable, when additional cosmological parameters are considered or when the redshift uncertainty from peculiar velocities becomes relevant, which is often the case at low redshifts.

Previous works mentioned in the introduction have suggested other methods to use the three measurements of
D\!_{L}
,
{\mathrm{DM}}
and
z
.

Gao et al. 2014
propose
D\!_{L}(z)/\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z)
as a probe of the scatter in
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}
and the evolution in the scatter with redshift.
They argue that the division makes it less dependent on cosmological parameters because the integrals in Eq.
3
and Eq.
5
almost cancel out.

Wei et al. 2018
suggested to instead use the product
D\!_{L}\times\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z)
, which is independent of
H_{0}
, to measure
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
w
in the
w
CDM model.

Li et al. 2019
again use the ratio
D\!_{L}/\langle{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{cosm}}\rangle(z)
but with the aim of measuring
f_{\mathrm{d}}
, similar to this study.

At several points in theses papers, it is claimed that
z
and
D_{L}
can be used interchangeably and it is sometimes not clear which one is used in their mock observations. However, this is not true as calculating, e.g.,
D_{L}
from
z
depends on cosmology so if only
z
is measured, the
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
relation needs to be used and vice versa.

Furthermore, all these methods take the product or ratios of the measurements, discarding information from the individual values.
Their advantages of being less dependent on
H_{0}
or other cosmological parameters will also be there in a Bayesian framework.
Therefore, and because it is more flexible and clear, our Bayesian framework should generally be advantageous.

V
Conclusion

We developed and tested a new method to use FRBs with an associated luminosity distance measurement as a cosmological tool.
This luminosity distance could most likely come from a GW counterpart if FRBs arise during a BNS merger.
The
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
D\!_{L}
measurements allow inferring
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
and
H_{0}
when combined with independent
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
measurements from localised FRBs.
The combination of measurements breaks the degeneracy between
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
and
H_{0}
that each measurement would have individually, without using external priors from cosmic microwave background or supernovae measurements.
We developed a Bayesian framework to use the
{\mathrm{DM}}
and
D\!_{L}
measurements and combine them with the existing
{\mathrm{DM}}
–
z
measurements.
We find that third-generation GW detectors like the Einstein Telescope and the Cosmic Explorer are required to obtain meaningful constraints.
According to our simulations, 120 events could constrain
H_{0}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
to
\pm 6\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{-1}
and
{}^{+0.15}_{-0.16}\times 10^{-2}
, respectively.
When assuming that the
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
of FRBs that are associated with GWs is distributed differently than
{\mathrm{DM}}_{\mathrm{host}}
of the general FRB population, we obtain uncertainties of
{}^{+8}_{-8}
and
{}^{+0.21}_{-0.20}\times 10^{-2}
for
H_{0}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}f_{\mathrm{d}}
, respectively.

The simulation and inference code used in this article is available at
https://github.com/JoschaJ/DMDLinference
.
