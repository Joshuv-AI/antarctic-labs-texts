Excitation energy transfer and trapping in Photosystem I of the green alga Bryopsis corticulans
Authors: Parveen Akhtar, Wenda Wang, Sumit Singhal, Ferenc Sarlós, Jian-Ren Shen, Petar H. Lambrev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Photosystem I (PSI) from the siphonous green alga Bryopsis corticulans carries ten subunits of Light-Harvesting Complex I (LHCI) that enlarge its absorption cross-section yet must still deliver excitations rapidly to the P700 reaction center. Here we investigate the light-harvesting function of PSI–LHCI, the excitation energy transfer within the supercomplex and how it affects the overall photochemical trapping lifetime. We performed picosecond time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy at room temperature and 77 K and femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy on purified PSI–LHCI supercomplex. Ultrafast spectroscopy showed that spectral equilibration in the antenna occurs mainly in the first 1 ps after excitation and is virtually complete within 10 ps. Nonetheless, the fluorescence lifetime of the supercomplex was 49–55 (±1) ps, depending on excitation wavelength, compared to 22 ps in the core complex. This result suggests that energy transfer continues over tens of ps, long after thermal equilibration between high- and low-energy excited states. The fluorescence analysis suggests that two factors contribute to the increased excitation lifetime – a thermodynamic factor, directly stemming from the increased antenna size as well as a kinetic factor, i.e. a rate limitation of trapping imposed by the energy transfer from LHCI to PSI. The average time of excitation transfer from LHCI to the reaction center was estimated to be close to 30 ps. Therefore, energy transfer from the enlarged antenna does not impose a sizeable kinetic limitation to the photochemical quantum efficiency of the photosystem.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11120-025-01181-2.

Introduction

Photosystem I (PSI) in eukaryotic phototrophs shows diverse structural organization and composition especially regarding its peripheral light-harvesting antenna system (Nelson and Junge 2015; Tian and Chen 2024; Nelson 2024). In the green lineage, including green algae (Chlorophyta) and land plants, the PSI core complex binds several subunits of light-harvesting complex I (LHCI) consisting of Lhca-family polypeptide, chlorophyll (Chl) a, b and carotenoid (xanthophyll) pigments. Higher plants have six distinct Lhca proteins (Klimmek et al. 2006), of which Lhca1–4, the major types, are constitutively produced and associated in a single copy with the PSI core complex. In the PSI–LHCI supercomplex, the four LHCIs are arranged in two heterodimers in a half-moon shape on one side of the core complex, facing the PsaF core subunit (Mazor et al. 2015; Qin et al. 2015b). The four LHCI subunits together enhance the absorption cross section of PSI by about 50%. LHCI also functions to increase the wavelength range of absorbed light, thanks to the blue-green-absorbing xanthophylls and blue-shifted absorption of Chl b. At the same time, LHCI, particularly Lhca3 and Lhca4, contain long-wavelength red-shifted forms of Chl a, or “red Chls”, absorbing light in the range 700–720 nm, hence increasing the far-red absorption of PSI.

The light available for photosynthesis in aquatic habitats is attenuated and spectrally filtered by the water column, lowering the photon flux density and shifting the spectrum to the blue-green range. As a result, aquatic, submerged algae face chronic low-light conditions and can benefit from a larger light-harvesting antenna cross-section. Consequently, green algae are often found to have a larger light-harvesting system, composed of more Lhca subunits having different arrangements (Tian and Chen 2024; Suga and Shen 2020). The model green alga C. reinhardtii binds between seven and ten Lhcas, depending on the environmental conditions (Suga et al. 2019; Su et al. 2019). Eight Lhcas form a double semi-ring structure on the PsaF side, while the remaining two (Lhca2 and Lhca9) are located on the opposite side (PsaL, PsaH). High-resolution structures of PSI binding 10 Lhcas have also been determined from other green algae – Chlorella ohadii, Botryococcus braunii, and Bryopsis corticulans (B. corticulans), showing generally similar arrangement (Caspy et al. 2021; Qin et al. 2019; van den Berg Te et al. 2020). The binding of LHCI to PSI in algae is flexible under environmental stresses such as fluctuating light intensities, far-red light, and nutrient deficiencies (Suga and Shen 2020).

Even between related species with generally similar PSI architectures, there can be marked differences in the structure and composition, not only in Lhca but also in the core complex. Here we focus on the siphonous (unicellular, multinucleate) green macroalga B. corticulans, that inhabits marine coastal regions and intertidal zones and thus experiences severe fluctuations in light intensity (Giovagnetti et al. 2018). The PSI of B. corticulans comprises 13 core subunits and 10 Lhcas (Qin et al. 2019). Eight Lhcas form a double semi-ring on the PsaF side (Lhca-a to Lhca-h), with another two (Lhca-I, Lhca-j) on the opposite side. The PSI core complex of B. corticulans lacks two subunits found in higher plants and C. reinhardtii – PsaN and PsaO. On the other hand, it contains subunit M, not present in C. reinhardtii. Consequently, B. corticulans displays distinct molecular interactions and docking of Lhcas – the heterodimer Lhca-i–Lhca-j is more stably attached to the core, in a configuration resembling red algal PSI–LHCI (Pi et al. 2018), and is thought to facilitate energy transfer (Qin et al. 2019). In addition to its distinct LHCI arrangement, the PSI–LHCI of B. corticulans stands out for its specific carotenoid composition and a high carotenoid-to-Chl ratio (Qin et al. 2015a). The LHCIs bind an average of 8 Chl a and 5 Chl b per subunit. The differences in antenna size, arrangement and pigment composition represent adaptations to optimize photosynthesis in the highly variable environment of B. corticulans.

The photochemical efficiency, the dynamics of excitation energy transfer (EET) and trapping in the PSI–LHCI supercomplexes can be significantly influenced by the organization of the peripheral light-harvesting antenna. In general, PSI has a very high photochemical quantum yield (97–99%) due to the combination of fast energy transfer in the antenna and charge separation in the P700 reaction center (RC). The effective trapping time in the PSI core is 20–50 ps in most organisms (Gobets and van Grondelle 2001). In the PSI–LHCI supercomplex in higher plants, the trapping time extends to 50–60 ps due to the additional Lhca subunits and the presence of red Chls within the Lhca, whereas in the isolated core complex the trapping lifetime is close to 20 ps (Croce and van Amerongen H 2020; Slavov et al. 2008; Wientjes et al. 2011b, Wientjes et al. 2011a; Bos et al. 2023). A similar trend has been observed in green-algal PSI. Lifetimes of 20–26 ps have been reported for the PSI core complex of C. reinhardtii (Melkozernov et al. 2004; Gibasiewicz et al. 2001, 2007), whereas the average trapping times in intact PSI–LHCI is around 50 ps (Le Quiniou et al. 2015). This is noteworthy considering that the algal PSI–LHCI incorporates 4–6 Lhca subunits more than its higher plant counterpart. Comparable findings have been reported for other chlorophytes, for example, PSI–LHCI of the colonial alga Botryococcus braunii exhibits a lifetime of 48–58 ps, depending on excitation wavelength (van den Berg et al. 2020). A femtosecond transient absorption study on PSI from Chlorella ohadii found main trapping lifetime components of 25 and 70 ps, depending on excitation (Cherepanov et al. 2024). It can be said that the average trapping time, hence the photochemical efficiency, in PSI–LHCI of green algae remains comparable to that of higher plants.

Two opposing factors modulate the effective rate constant of trapping. (i) A thermodynamic dilution term slows trapping as the number of pigments increases. (ii) A compensating enthalpic funnel, created when peripheral Chls absorb slightly higher energy than core Chls, biases the equilibrated excitation population toward the RC and partly offsets the size penalty. Conversely, low-energy spectral red forms decelerate the effective photochemical trapping by confining the excitation energy in LHCI. It is thought that the number and spectra of the red forms have a stronger impact on the trapping kinetics than the overall antenna size. Green-algal Lhcas generally lack red Chls, eliminating the thermodynamic bottleneck. Algal PSI complexes in general may contain Chls spectral forms that are red-shifted but to a lesser extent compared to higher plants (Santabarbara et al. 2020). The potential delay in energy trapping caused by the larger antenna is effectively counterbalanced by the absence of extreme red Chls, allowing green algae to maintain efficient light-harvesting kinetics despite a more extensive antenna system.

Here we extend the knowledge on the impact of the enlarged antenna on the energy transfer and trapping in green-algal PSI by spectroscopic analysis of isolated PSI–LHCI complexes from B. corticulans. We will show that the thermodynamic dilution is not the main factor determining the kinetics, but rather the rate of Förster energy transfer between Chls in LHCI and PSI, which depends on the specific design and molecular interactions of the antenna system. Despite the fact that spectral equilibration is virtually complete in less than 10 ps after excitation, the data suggest that the average excitation transfer time from LHCI to the RC is tens of ps.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

B. corticulans was collected from intertidal coastal areas around Qingdao, China and PSI−LHCI was isolated and purified as described in (Qin et al. 2015a). The PSI−LHCI samples used in the present study are identical; biochemical and spectroscopic characterization can be found in the above article. The samples were frozen until used and then resuspended in 20 mM Tricine buffer (pH 7.5) and 0.03% n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside (β-DDM). For the measurements at 77 K, the buffer was supplemented with 60% glycerol.

Absorption and circular dichroism spectroscopy

Absorption and circular dichroism (CD) spectra in the range of 350–750 nm were recorded at room temperature with an Evolution 500 dual-beam spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a J-815 spectropolarimeter (Jasco, Japan), respectively. The measurements were performed in a quartz cuvette of 1 cm optical path length with 1 nm (absorption) or 2 nm (CD) spectral bandwidth. For CD measurements, an integration time of 2 s and scanning speed of 100 nm min−1 was used.

Steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy

Fluorescence emission spectra in the visible range were measured at 77 K using a FP-8500 (Jasco, Japan) spectrofluorometer and a home-built accessory filled with liquid nitrogen. The samples were diluted to absorbance of 0.1 per cm at the red maximum. Emission spectra in the range of 640–780 nm were recorded with excitation wavelength of 440 nm and 475 nm and excitation/emission bandwidth of 2.5 nm. The measurements were performed with 1 nm increment and 4 s integration time. The spectra are corrected for the spectral sensitivity of the instrument using a calibrated light source (ESC-842, Jasco) as a reference.

Time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy

Picosecond time-resolved fluorescence measurements were performed with a time-correlated single-photon counting instrument (FluoTime 200/PicoHarp 300 spectrometer, PicoQuant). Excitation pulses were provided by Fianium WhiteLase Micro (NKT Photonics, UK) supercontinuum laser with a repetition rate of 20 MHz. Excitation wavelengths of 440 and 480 nm were used to excite selectively either Chls a or b. The fluorescence decays were recorded at wavelengths of 664–744 nm with 8 nm steps, at room temperature, and 665–750 nm with 5 nm steps at 77 K. All the samples were diluted to an absorbance of 0.03 at the excitation wavelength. For the room temperature measurements, the suspension was placed in 1 mm flow cell and continuously circulated. Measurements were also performed in the presence of 1 mM potassium ferricyanide to keep the RCs oxidized. The total instrument response function (IRF) measured using 1% Ludox as scattering solution has full width at half maximum (FWHM) of ≈ 40 and ≈60 ps with 440 and 480 nm excitation respectively. The data are corrected for the spectral response of the detector using a calibrated light source (ESC-842, Jasco). Global multiexponential lifetime analysis with IRF reconvolution was performed using MATLAB.

For 77 K measurements, the dark-adapted suspension was placed in a 1 mm demountable cryogenic quartz cell and cooled in an optical cryostat (Optistat DN, Oxford Instruments, UK) in the presence of 10 μM phenazine methosulfate and 20 mM sodium ascorbate as electron donors. For measurements with closed (oxidized) RCs, 1 mM potassium ferricyanide was added instead, and the sample was preilluminated with white light for 5 minutes before freezing.

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy

Broadband pump-probe transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) was performed with femtosecond supercontinuum pulses at 100 kHz repetition rate. White-light supercontinuum (WLC) generation was achieved through a two-step spectral broadening process. In the first step, near-infrared laser pulses from the HR1 laser system at ELI-ALPS (1030 nm central wavelength, 30 fs temporal width, 0.2 mJ pulse energy) were focused into a 1 m hollow-core fiber (HCF) filled with argon (Ar) gas, resulting in initial spectral broadening. The output pulses were then compressed to approximately 10 fs using dispersion-compensating mirrors. In the second step, further spectral broadening was achieved by focusing the beam with a spherical mirror (f = 1.5 m) into a 1 m long chamber filled with krypton (Kr) gas at 2.5 bar pressure, generating the white-light supercontinuum. After compressing the pulse width with a pair of chirp compensating mirrors, the white light was subsequently collimated with a curved mirror (f = 0.75 m) and split into two parts by using a beam sampler. The reflected beam (~2%) was used as probe pulses and the transmitted beam was used as a pump pulses. The phase and intensity of the pump pulses were modulated at 100 kHz by an AOM pulse shaper (Phasetech Spectroscopy, USA). The phase difference between consecutive pulses in the pulse train was set to 180° and these pulse pairs were chopped on and off at 50 kHz. Pump and probe beams were collected on the same parabolic mirror to focus them at a small angle onto the same sample spot. The probe pulses at the sample were delayed by a mechanical translational stage. The probe beam transmitted through the sample was collimated by another parabolic mirror and further guided into a spectrograph to collect probe spectra with and without preceding pump. The spectral detector was synchronized with the laser pulses for shot-to-shot detection. The pump pulses were centered at 685 nm spanning a 90-nm window, 4 nJ energy, 30–40 fs temporal width; the focused spot diameter was ~80 μm. The relative angle of the linear polarization of the pump and probe pulses was set to 54.7° (magic-angle) if not specified otherwise, or at 0° and 90° for measurements of the anisotropy decay. The PSI sample solution with an absorbance of 0.35 at 680 nm was purged with Ar and placed in a 1 mm quartz cuvette.

Results

Absorption, CD and fluorescence spectra

The absorption and CD spectra of PSI-LHCI (Fig. 1) from B. corticulans reveal key differences in pigment composition and structural organization compared to the PSI supercomplex of higher plants. The absorption spectrum exhibits a broader shoulder in the 500–600 nm region, reflecting variations in the carotenoid composition; specifically, it contains siphonaxanthin and siphonein instead of the luteins found in plant PSI. While both types share the main Chl a absorption peaks around 430–440 nm and 670–680 nm, B. corticulans shows higher absorption on the short-wavelength side of the Chl Qy band, that is consistent with a higher number of Chl b. The CD spectrum of B. corticulans in the Chl Qy region is blue-shifted compared to plant PSI. The positive-negative Chl a doublet band with maxima at 665 and 681 nm has relatively smaller amplitude than in plant PSI, and the negative Chl b band at 652 nm has a larger amplitude. In the blue-green region, B. corticulans lacks the distinct positive CD peak at ~ 511 nm seen in plant PSI, which originates from β-carotenes in the PSI core; this difference reflects the presence of α-carotenoids in B. corticulans. Excitation energy transfer and trapping at room temperature

To investigate the energy transfer efficiency and trapping in PSI, we performed time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy on PSI–LHCI supercomplexes using excitation wavelengths of 440 nm (targeting mainly the PSI core which has a higher number of Chl a) and 480 nm (preferentially exciting Chl b in LHCI). The kinetic traces recorded at different excitation wavelengths were then subjected to global lifetime analysis. The fluorescence decay curves were well described by a three-exponential fit, Example kinetic traces and fits are shown in Supplemental Figure S1. The resulting decay-associated emission spectra (DAES) with corresponding lifetimes are given in the Fig. 3. The main decay component has a lifetime of 49/55 (±1) ps constituting the majority of the amplitude (~90%). This component represents the main trapping of antenna excitations by the RC, and it is similar to PSI of C. reinhardtii (Le Quiniou et al. 2015). The second component with lifetime of 200–300 (±20) ps (whose amplitude varies depending on the excitation light (2–3% contribution) may represent minor subpopulations of complexes with slower energy transfer/trapping. The slowest nanosecond component has a small amplitude (~5%) and a blue-shifted emission profile with a maximum at 680 nm, which indicates that it originates from uncoupled Chls.Fig. 3Decay-associated emission spectra of PSI–LHCI obtained from global analysis of fluorescence decays measured with 440 nm (A) and with 480 nm excitation (B). Data is average of three independent measurements, the shaded area represents standard error

Decay-associated emission spectra of PSI–LHCI obtained from global analysis of fluorescence decays measured with 440 nm (A) and with 480 nm excitation (B). Data is average of three independent measurements, the shaded area represents standard error

After subjecting the supercomplexes to a harsher detergent treatment to break up the LHCI antenna, the main decay lifetime shortened to ~ 22 ps (Supplementary Fig. S2) which is comparable to the lifetime of the PSI core complex in higher plants and green algae (Wientjes et al. 2011b; Akhtar et al. 2018; Gibasiewicz et al. 2001). In other words, the presence of LHCI more than doubles the average lifetime of the antenna excitations.

The time-resolved fluorescence measurements did not reveal any time-dependent spectral changes that could be clearly assigned to energy transfer alone, suggesting that the initial thermal equilibration among antenna states of different energies is faster than the time resolution of the measurement (~10 ps). The very fast system equilibration indicates that all Lhcas are well-connected, despite their long distance to the PSI core.

Time-resolved fluorescence at 77 K

Time-resolved fluorescence measurements were performed at 77 K to identify the different Chl spectral forms and resolve the corresponding dynamics of excitation relaxation and trapping. At a temperature of 77 K, energy transfer is slowed down compared to room temperature and uphill EET is severely limited. Excitations migrate toward the lowest-energy electronic states, i.e. from the bulk antenna Chls to the red Chls in the PSI core, enhancing the emission from red Chls. Five lifetimes were necessary to satisfactorily describe the fluorescence decays over a 4-ns time range (Fig. 4). The first component with the lifetime of 20 ± 1 ps having positive and negative peaks in the DAS represents EET between bulk and red Chls (680 to 710 nm). Note that the DAS shape is not identical for 440 nm and 480 nm excitation; however, this could be a systematic fit error, especially when using broader 480 nm pulses (60 ps FWHM). The second component with the lifetime of 64 ± 1 ps has a very broad spectrum with a peak at ~ 700 nm and it becomes zero at around 715 nm upon 440 nm excitation. When 480 nm excitation light was used, this component showed EET from 690 to 700 to 710–715 states. In both cases, the majority of excitations decay with this lifetimes as indicated by the amplitude (50% contribution). A similar EET component was observed in PSI–LHCI of higher plant (Akhtar and Lambrev 2020). This component is not associated with the PSI core but an energy equilibration between red Chls in the core and Lhcas. The fluorescence above 700 nm decays with two main lifetimes − 200–300 ps and 0.9–1 ns, characterized by positively-signed, broad DAES with maxima around 705 and 715 nm, respectively. This indicates that the system contains at least two types of red Chls. To test how the oxidation state of the RC may affect the fluorescence decay kinetics, measurements were also performed in two different conditions: “closed-RC”, wherein the PSI RCs were pre-oxidized (P700+) by preillumination in the presence of 1 mM potassium ferricyanide before freezing, and “open-RC”, wherein the samples were frozen in complete darkness in the presence of ascorbate as an electron donor and phenazine methosulfate as a mediator. Under these conditions, approximately half of the RCs return to the neutral state after excitation and behave as “open” (Schlodder et al. 1998). The presence of open RCs led to small but measurable changes in the fluorescence kinetics (Supplementary Fig. S3). The lifetime of the second-fastest decay component (65±1 ps) was shorter than in PSI with closed RCs (75±3 ps) and its amplitude was higher relative to the longer-lived components throughout the wavelength range. As a result, the average fluorescence lifetime was shorter compared to closed RC. Thus, trapping of Chl excitations by the neutral P700 is more effective than by the oxidized P700+.

Transient absorption spectroscopy

To reveal information about the excitation dynamics on shorter timescales ( < 10 ps), we performed pump-probe transient absorption spectroscopy of PSI–LHCI at room temperature (Fig. 5). Absorption difference spectra were recorded with broadband excitation pulses centered at 680 nm at 70 pump-probe delays between 0.1 and 800 ps. The spectra are characterized by a negative band with a maximum at 685 nm, representing the ground state bleaching and stimulated emission band of the Qy transition of Chl a, and a positive excited-state absorption band at 640–660 nm. A small red shift of the main negative band is observed in the first 2 ps that reflects thermal equilibration among the antenna Chls. Between 0.5 and 10 ps, a broad shoulder appears around 700–710 nm, which can be assigned to the population of red Chls (note that we do not expect to observe the charge separation P700A1 → P700+A1− as the RCs are kept oxidized in the absence of fast electron donors).Fig. 5Transient absorption spectroscopy of PSI–LHCI. To obtain an estimate of the characteristic timescales and spectral components of the system kinetics, we applied global lifetime analysis of the TAS data (van Stokkum et al. 2004; Ruckebusch et al. 2012). The spectral evolution associated with these lifetimes is represented by the evolution-associated absorption difference spectra (EADS) and decay-associated absorption difference spectra (DADS). The former correspond to the transient spectra of intermediate states if the system kinetics were following a linear sequential model. The DADS, on the other hand, are simply the wavelength-dependent pre-exponential amplitudes if the kinetics is represented as a sum of exponentials.

Five lifetimes were necessary to adequately describe the kinetics from 100 fs to 800 ps. The first two lifetimes, 0.44 ps and 2.6 ps, bear the characteristics of EET – the negative maximum of the EADS gradually shifts to longer wavelengths and a shoulder appears on the far-red side (700–710 nm) of the main band (Fig. 5). The spectral shift is more obvious in the DADS, which show negative bands at shorter wavelengths and positive bands at longer wavelengths. Thus, the 0.44 ps lifetime is dominated by decay of Chl b states (~650 nm) and shorter-wavelength Chl a (670 nm) and concomitant rise of longer-wavelength Chl a states (680–690 nm). The 2.6 ps DADS in turn shows decay around 680 nm and rise around 700 nm. The three longer lifetimes primarily represent excited-state decay, observed as gradual loss of the transient absorption signal. The main excitation decay pathway (contributing 70% of the decay amplitude at 690–700 nm) has a 45 ps lifetime – closely corresponding to the fluorescence lifetime. However, the faster decay component (13 ps), with a relative amplitude of 24%, suggests that the trapping kinetics is more complex compared to the recorded picosecond fluorescence decay kinetics.

In summary, the TAS data show that Chl b-a energy transfer and spectral equilibration within the PSI–LHCI antenna occurs mainly in the first several hundred femtoseconds, the lowest-energy Chls are populated on a timescale of 2–3 ps, while trapping of antenna excitations by the RC proceeds for tens to hundreds of ps.

Discussion

The structural architecture of PSI from the marine alga B. corticulans (Qin et al. 2019) is very similar to the freshwater C. reinhardtii (Su et al. 2019), despite the differences in their ecological habitat, morphology, pigment composition, or strategies for coping with fluctuating light. The binding of 10 Lhca subunits to the PSI core extends the antenna size by 150% - from 99 to 244 Chls in total. Compared to plant PSI, binding of additional 145 Chls represents a significant enlargement. Apart from the different size, the green-algal PSI displays a distinct absorption spectrum. The different carotenoid composition, higher Chl b content, and the absence of the red Chls, characteristic to plant PSI, together results in higher absorption of shorter-wavelength blue-green and red-orange, at the expense of long-wavelength far-red light. The altered pigment composition leads to pronounced differences in the absorption and CD spectra, which can potentially affect the photochemical function of the system. Here we investigate how the enlarged and spectrally tuned PSI antenna affect the photochemical efficiency and trapping kinetics.

Impact of the antenna size on the trapping lifetime

The amplitude-weighted average fluorescence lifetime, calculated from the DAES, is 54 ps upon 440 nm excitation and 59 ps upon 480 nm excitation. If we exclude a minor long-lived component resolved in the fluorescence measurements, that probably originates from a small fraction of the complexes, the lifetimes become 49 ± 0.4 and 55 ± 1 ps. We conclude that despite the large antenna, PSI in B. corticulans performs photochemistry with a high quantum yield (98%), typical for PSI across photosynthetic clades. The fluorescence lifetime is closely comparable to the lifetime reported for PSI–LHCI in C. reinhardtii (Le Quiniou et al. 2015; Tian et al. 2017). Several other reports on isolated PSI–LHCI of C. reinhardtii have led to different results (Melkozernov et al. 2004; Giera et al. 2014; Ihalainen et al. 2005), which could be explained with differences in the protein composition of the preparations and the easy loss of Lhca subunits in this alga.

It is informative to compare the fluorescence lifetime of the PSI–LHCI supercomplex with the trapping time in the isolated PSI core complex. The lifetime of 22 ps, recorded after detergent treatment of the PSI–LHCI, is similar to the average fluorescence lifetime of higher-plant PSI core complex − 23 ps (Slavov et al. 2008; Akhtar et al. 2018), as well as the PSI core complex of the diatom T. pseudonana − 21 ps (Akhtar et al. 2025) and C. reinhardtii (Melkozernov et al. 2004; Gibasiewicz et al. 2001, 2007). Thus, the binding of Lhca slows down the trapping by a factor of 2.2–2.6.

There are two underlying factors contributing to the slowdown of the effective trapping with antenna size: a thermodynamic and a kinetic one. The thermodynamic factor means that the rate of trapping, or charge separation, ktr, is proportional to the probability of excitations residing on the RC, prc. In the case of very fast EET among a bed of isoenergetic pigments, the probability is the inverse of the antenna size N:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = {1 \over {{k_{cs}} \cdot {p_{rc}}}} = {N \over {{k_{cs}}}}$$\end{document}

where kcs is the intrinsic rate of charge separation. Here for simplicity, the RC is taken as a single pigment site. It follows from the above relationship, that the trapping lifetime scales linearly with the antenna size, if trapping is limited entirely by the statistical thermodynamic effect (entropy) and neglecting any spectral differences between the peripheral and core antenna Chls (enthalpy).

Considering the trapping time of 22 ps and the number of antenna Chl a molecules in the PSI core complex and in LHCI, 97 and 76, respectively (Qin et al. 2015a), the intrinsic charge separation rate constant is

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k_{{\rm{cs}}}} = {{{N_{{\rm{PSI}}}}} \over {{\tau _{{\rm{PSI}}}}}} = {{97} \over {22}} = 4.4 {\rm{p}}{{\rm{s}}^{ - 1}}.$$\end{document}

Then the pure statistical lifetime of the PSI–LHCI complex would be

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = {{{N_{{\rm{LHCI}}}} + {N_{{\rm{PSI}}}}} \over {{k_{{\rm{cs}}}}}} = {{76 + 97} \over {4.4}} = 39.3 {\rm{ps}}.$$\end{document}

Chl b is neglected here, as excitations are almost fully transferred to Chl a within a few ps. However, enthalpy also plays a role – if the average excitation energy of the peripheral antenna Chls is higher than the core Chls, this creates an energy funnel, shifting the equilibrated excitation toward the core and thus increasing prc. To account for this, a Boltzmann term in the form of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e^{ - {{\Delta E} \over {{k_B}T}}}}$$\end{document} is included, where ΔE is the mean excitation energy difference between LHCI and PSI, that can be estimated from their absorption spectra:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = {{{N_{{\rm{LHCI}}}} \cdot {e^{ - {{{\rm{\Delta }}E} \over {{k_B}T}}}} + {N_{{\rm{PSI}}}}} \over {{k_{{\rm{cs}}}}}} = {{76 \cdot 0.5 + 97} \over {4.4}} = 30.6 {\rm{ps}}.$$\end{document}

Here the Boltzmann factor of 0.45 corresponds to about 18 meV energy difference or 7 nm shift in the absorption spectrum (Qin et al. 2015a). Hence, the thermodynamic factor alone cannot account for the observed trapping times of 50–60 ps, and the kinetics of EET also plays a role.

The rate of energy transfer between LHCI and the PSI core

If the observed effective trapping lifetime of PSI–LHCI is 54 ps (440 nm excitation) and the predicted lifetime assuming non-limiting EET is ~ 30 ps, then the rate of EET from the peripheral antenna to the RC must pose a significant limitation to the overall trapping rate. The excitation wavelength dependence of the fluorescence lifetime provides further support to this conclusion. We can calculate the average lifetime of excitations created in either LHCI or the PSI core \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{PSI}}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{LHCI}}}}$$\end{document}, using the relationship:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = \alpha {\tau _{{\rm{LHCI}}}} + \beta {\tau _{{\rm{PSI}}}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau}$$\end{document} is the measured lifetime of the PSI–LHCI supercomplex and α and β = 1− α are the fractional initial excitation coefficients of LHCI and the PSI core, respectively (Giera et al. 2014). Knowing the average lifetimes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{1}}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{2}}}}$$\end{document}, and initial excitation coefficients, α1 and α2, at two different excitation wavelengths, we can solve the linear system to determine \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{LHCI}}}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{PSI}}}}$$\end{document}and the effective migration time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{{mig}}}}$$\end{document} (van Oort et al. 2008):

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{\rm{mig}}}} = {\tau _{{\rm{LHCI}}}} - {\tau _{{\rm{PSI}}}} = {{{\tau _1} - {\tau _2}} \over {{\alpha _1} - {\alpha _2}}}$$\end{document}

From the number of Chl a and b in PSI and LHCI (Qin et al. 2015a) and their absorption spectra, we can approximately estimate the fractional excitation of PSI/LHCI to be 0.5/0.5 and 0.3/0.7 for 440 and 480 nm excitation wavelength, respectively. Solving the equations, we obtain for the exciton migration time between LHCI and the core:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau _{{\rm{mig}}}} = {{54 - 59} \over {0.5 - 0.7}} = 25 {\rm{ps}}.$$\end{document}

Giera et al. (2014) have estimated the migration time in C. reinhardtii PSI–LHCI to be 10–20 ps, using a similar approach. However, their reported fluorescence lifetimes, from streak-camera measurements, are considerably shorter than other reports (Le Quiniou et al. 2015). The differences might be due to the sample preparation, experimental conditions, for example the presence of exciton annihilation, or the instrument time resolution. Our TAS data revealed additional fast excitation decay lifetime (13 ps) that was not resolved in fluorescence measurements. Therefore, the analysis based on the fluorescence lifetimes alone can only give an approximate estimation of the energy migration time.

Despite some uncertainty in the energy migration time, it clearly exceeds, by an order of magnitude, the timescale of spectral equilibration resolved by ultrafast spectroscopy. Both TAS and anisotropy decay analyses showed that the main timescales of energy equilibration are 0.4 ps and 2–4 ps. This result is in agreement with published transient absorption data on PSI from green algae (Gibasiewicz et al. 2001, 2002; Melkozernov et al. 2005) as well as other photosynthetic organisms (Akhtar et al. 2021b). Taking the fluorescence, TAS and anisotropy results together, it is evident that excitation energy migration continues after thermal equilibration and depolarization of the antenna system, concurrently with the photochemical trapping.

The results and analysis presented above suggest that the additional 10 Lhca antenna subunits bound to the PSI core complex add approximately 20–30 ps to the total trapping time, contributed both by the thermodynamic equilibrium of the larger antenna bed, and to a larger extent, by the excitation transfer time between the Lhcas and the core antenna. These factors impose a small penalty to the photochemical quantum yield, that cannot be considered as a limitation to acquiring an even larger antenna. Nonetheless, the results add to a substantial body of data on PSI from different photosynthetic organisms that confine the trapping lifetime of PSI–LHCI between 40 and 60 ps (Croce and van Amerongen H 2020). A common pattern can be identified, where plants (from bryophytes to angiosperms) and algae (in red and green lineages) balance between a larger antenna size and more red-shifted forms such that the trapping lifetime falls within this narrow range. It could be postulated that this time is an evolution-imposed limit to the optimal function of PSI. The precise reasons probably deserve further investigation, but one can speculate that a longer excitation lifetime could increase the risk of photodamage of PSI, which is costly to repair or replace (as compared with the more modular, higher-turnover Photosystem II).

Spectral forms and dynamics of red Chls

Higher-plant PSI employs red Chls in the peripheral antenna complex, as well as in the core complex, to enhance the absorption of far-red light. The core complex contains red Chls with emission peaks from 705 to 720 nm (Akhtar and Lambrev 2020). The red states emitting at 720 nm have also been found in the PSI core of Physcomitrella patens (Liu et al. 2025). The PSI core of the model green alga C. reinhardtii contains two red Chls with emission peaks at 702 nm and 715 nm (Giera et al. 2018; Le Quiniou et al. 2015). In comparison, the lowest-energy states in PSI of B. corticulans emit at wavelengths 712–715 nm. Qin et al. (2015a) have shown that the far-red emission of B. corticulans PSI originates from the core complex, whereas the emission maxima of isolated LHCI are around 680 nm. Therefore, the red Chls in B. corticulans are located in the core complex, which is similar to C. reinhardtii, and neither of the Lhca subunits possesses red Chl forms as the ones present in Lhca3 and Lhca4 of higher plants (Croce et al. 2002; Castelletti et al. 2003). Since the red Chls are in the core antenna, energy equilibration between the bulk and red Chls is very fast (1–5 ps at room temperature) – similar to observations on plant PSI core (Wientjes et al. 2011b; Akhtar et al. 2018) and cyanobacterial PSI (Akhtar et al. 2021a). Similar equilibration times (4–6 ps) were reported for PSI of C. reinhardtii by TAS (Gibasiewicz et al. 2007, 2001) and streak-camera measurements (Ihalainen et al. 2005; Giera et al. 2014).

The low-temperature (77 K) time-resolved fluorescence measurements resolved two pools of red Chls with emission maxima around 705 and 715 nm – also in agreement with data on C. reinhardtii. The states are populated on a timescale of ~ 20 ps after initial excitation at 77 K. A faster equilibration component (8 ps), observed in C. reinhardtii PSI by a streak camera (Giera et al. 2018) is probably also present but not resolved in the current measurements. It is worth noting that even at 77 K, the decay lifetime at 680 nm depends on the initial excitation (20 vs. 24 ps for 440 and 480 nm, respectively), suggesting that the trapping of bulk antenna states in LHCI by the red Chls is 10–20 ps slower than the trapping of Chl excitations in the core antenna. The red Chl pools in turn have decay lifetimes of 0.3 and 1 ns. The fluorescence lifetimes reflect primarily the slow Förster energy transfer from the red Chls, either directly to P700 or, more likely, via thermally-activated uphill transfer to higher-energy excitonic antenna states and eventually P700 (Giera et al. 2018; Akhtar et al. 2021a).

Conclusions

We have analyzed the efficiency and dynamics of excitation trapping in PSI of B. corticulans binding 10 Lhca subunits. The substantial differences in pigment composition, especially regarding the Chl b content and the different carotenoid species, the PSI–LHCI complex of B. corticulans has spectral features that distinguish it not only from higher-plant PSI but also from PSI of the more closely related green alga C. reinhardtii, which has a similar structural architecture. Despite the pigment and spectral differences, the overall kinetics of EET and trapping in B. corticulans are comparable to C. reinhardtii, as far as such comparison can be made based on the available data.

The large peripheral antenna has measurable impact on the effective trapping of excitations by two mechanisms: 1) thermal equilibration among the larger interconnected pool of Chls slows down the trapping by about 50% (10 ps); 2) the rate of exciton migration from the Lhca subunits to the core imposes a further limit on the effective trapping (20–30 ps), depending on where excitations are created. The overall EET outlasts the energy equilibration among antenna states, which takes place mainly on timescales < 1 ps and up to 4 ps, at room temperature. The overall slowdown of trapping results in a reduction of the photochemical quantum yield by only ~1%, hence, the quantum yield alone is probably not the main factor limiting the antenna size in evolutionary aspect.

The analysis presented here does not touch upon specific energy pathways, kinetic limitations, or individual contributions of Lhca proteins, which could be addressed by performing calculations based on the structure of the B. corticulans PSI. On the other hand, the data presented here can be useful in validating and calibrating any such theoretical models.
