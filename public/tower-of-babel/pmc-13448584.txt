Risk-of-bias assessment in prognostic cohort studies: a methodological comparison study of structured appraisal tools in acute pulmonary embolism research
Authors: Ludovica Anna Cimini, Frederikus A Klok, Marc Carrier, Kerstin de Wit, Scott C Woller, Andrea Galeazzo Rigutini, Roupen Odabashian, Rosa Talerico, Maria Giovanna Ranalli, Cecilia Becattini

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo compare the reliability of four commonly used structured tools for risk-of-bias assessment in prognostic cohort studies and evaluate their agreement with expert appraisal.DesignMethodological comparison study.SettingSecondary analysis of published prognostic cohort studies in patients with acute pulmonary embolism from a previously conducted systematic review.ParticipantsSixty-three cohort studies assessing the prognostic role of echocardiography in patients with acute pulmonary embolism.Primary and secondary outcome measuresFour independent reviewers assessed study quality/risk of bias using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), Risk Of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I), Quality In Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) and Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2). Concomitantly and independently, expert reviewers’ implicit global judgements were used as an external comparator. Internal consistency was generally low across tools, while ceiling effects were observed for NOS and QUIPS. These findings support further refinement and standardisation of risk-of-bias assessment methods for observational prognostic research.Keywords: Methods, Prognosis, Quality Improvement

Background

The critical appraisal of observational studies is essential to interpret the strength of findings, support decisions regarding inclusion in systematic reviews and meta-analyses and inform the peer-review and publication process.1 2 Critical appraisal broadly refers to the structured evaluation of the methodological rigour, validity and relevance of a study. However, important conceptual distinctions exist between methodological quality assessment and risk-of-bias assessment.3–5 Risk of bias refers to the likelihood that flaws in study design, conduct, analysis or reporting may lead to a systematic deviation from the true effect estimate. In contrast, methodological quality is a broader concept that may also encompass aspects such as reporting completeness, transparency and general methodological soundness.3 The lack of clear distinction between these concepts has contributed to inconsistency in the selection and interpretation of study evaluation tools.

Selecting the most appropriate tool is particularly challenging for prognostic cohort studies, given their methodological heterogeneity in terms of study design, analytical strategies and the presence or absence of comparison groups. Several structured tools are currently available, including the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I), Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) and the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2).6–10 These tools were originally developed for different research questions, including intervention effectiveness, prognosis and diagnostic accuracy, but are frequently applied outside their intended scope. Currently, no consensus exists regarding which tool provides the most valid and reliable assessment of prognostic cohort studies.3

In systematic reviews and meta-analyses, study evaluation may substantially influence the interpretation of pooled estimates as well as decisions regarding study inclusion or exclusion.11–15 Therefore, valid and reliable approaches to risk-of-bias assessment are essential not only for methodological rigour but also to ensure clinically meaningful conclusions.

This study aimed to compare the reliability of four commonly used structured tools for risk-of-bias assessment in prognostic cohort studies and to evaluate their agreement with expert appraisal.

Methods

Study design

The study consisted of two phases, both using the same predefined set of 63 prognostic cohort studies for quality assessment (figure 1). The 63 studies were all derived from a previously conducted critical review investigating the prognostic performance of different echocardiographic parameters in patients with acute pulmonary embolism16 (online supplemental table 1). In phase I, four reviewers (LAC, AGR, RT and RO) independently assessed the quality of each study using NOS, ROBINS-I, QUIPS and QUADAS-2. The results obtained by different reviewers, both as overall judgements and at the domain and item levels, were compared with assess the tools’ reliability.

Study design.

In phase II, each study was assessed by an expert reviewer (FAK, CB or SW), who implicitly classified each study as having ‘high’, ‘moderate’, ‘low’ or ‘critically low’ as global methodological judgement. The results of the implicit quality assessment by the expert reviewers were considered the reference comparator and were compared with those obtained using the structured tools in phase I.

Quality assessment

The selection of the four tools was based on both methodological rationale and contextual relevance to the included studies. The NOS was chosen for its ease of use, widespread use and frequent application in systematic reviews of observational studies; ROBINS-I is considered to be one of the most comprehensive tools for risk-of-bias assessment in non-randomised studies and is recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration. QUIPS was included due to its specific development for prognostic studies. Although designed for diagnostic test accuracy studies, QUADAS-2 was also considered appropriate as all studies aimed at assessing echocardiography as a predictor of prognosis.

Descriptions of NOS, ROBINS-I, QUIPS and QUADAS-2 are provided in online supplemental material appendix 1. These tools evaluate three (NOS), seven (ROBINS-I), six (QUIPS) and four (QUADAS-2) domains, respectively. QUADAS-2 and NOS contain 19 and 8 items, respectively, QUIPS 43 items, while ROBINS-I includes 20 key items and 35 items overall. Because the tools included use different scoring systems, tool-specific outputs were pragmatically converted into common categories to allow direct comparisons across instruments.

Each item in the NOS scale is scored 0 or 1, depending on whether the criterion is fulfilled. The sum of scores at individual items provides the final study quality rating according to NOS. Items in QUADAS-2 are rated ‘low’, ‘high’ or ‘unclear’ without a final numerical score. In QUIPS, each item can be rated as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘partial’ or ‘unsure’, while the overall risk of bias is categorised as ‘high’, ‘moderate’ or ‘low’ without a final numerical score. Each item in ROBINS-I categorises the risk of bias as ‘low’, ‘moderate’, ‘serious’, ‘critical’ or ‘no information’. Additionally, ROBINS-I provides a global qualitative judgement for the overall risk of bias in each assessed study. These tools are frequently applied in systematic reviews of prognostic observational studies, particularly in the field of pulmonary embolism.

Expert reviewers, each with substantial clinical expertise in venous thromboembolism and a strong background in prognostic research methodology, independently appraised the included studies. The 63 studies were evenly distributed among the three experts, who assigned a global quality judgement (‘critically low’, ‘low’, ‘moderate’ or ‘high’) based on implicit evaluation. Reviewers were blinded to the structured tool ratings and were not asked to apply a standardised checklist, thereby emulating the unstructured critical appraisal approach frequently employed in qualitative evidence syntheses.

Statistical analysis

Assessment of reliability

Gwet’s agreement coefficient (Gwet’s AC1) was used to calculate the inter-rater reliability for quality assessment.17 Gwet’s AC1 coefficients were classified according to Landis and Koch’s scale (less than 0 corresponded to poor agreement; 0–0.2 slight agreement; 0.21–0.4 fair agreement; 0.41–0.6 moderate agreement; 0.61–0.8 substantial agreement; 0.81–1 almost perfect agreement).18

To allow comparisons between tools, the final scorings obtained by NOS for each paper (ranging from 1 to 8) were reclassified as follows: 1–3 was graded as ‘critically low quality’, 4 as ‘low quality’, 5–6 as ‘moderate quality’ and 7–8 as ‘high quality’.

As QUADAS-2 and QUIPS do not provide a final scoring, these were created ad hoc (online supplemental methods).

The inter-rater reliability was also assessed at the domain and item level. For ROBINS-I, analysis of optional items was performed in case answers were provided by at least three raters.

Comparison with expert appraisal

To evaluate the agreement with expert appraisal of NOS, ROBINS-I, QUIPS and QUADAS-2, the quality assessment results from each tool were compared with those of the expert appraisal (reference comparator).

Features of the tools

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, calculated separately for each tool. Cronbach’s alpha value between 0.70 and 0.95 was considered acceptable.19 Floor and ceiling effects were identified when more than 15% of studies received the lowest or highest possible scores, respectively. These effects were calculated separately for each rater and each tool.20

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; V.4.3.1) with the irrCAC, psych, dplyr and tidyr packages.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The results of the risk-of-bias assessment obtained by the four reviewers using the selected tools are reported in figure 2, table 1, online supplemental figure 1. When assessed using NOS, ROBINS-I, most studies were rated as moderate risk of bias, with only a minority judged high (or critical, for ROBINS-I) or low risk. QUIPS, however, classified over 55% of studies as low risk, and this pattern was consistent across reviewers. Across tools, agreement was greatest for identifying high-risk studies, whereas classifications of low risk showed more variability. Inter-rater agreement within each tool was fair for NOS (Gwet’s AC1 0.25; 95% CI 0.15 to 0.34), ROBINS-I (0.38; 95% CI 0.28 to 0.47) and QUADAS-2 (0.35; 95% CI 0.27 to 0.43), but almost perfect for QUIPS (0.85; 95% CI 0.73 to 0.93).

Inter-rater agreement assessed at the domain level

The inter-rater agreement assessed at the domain level within each tool was highly variable and is reported in figure 3. Agreement patterns varied by tool and domain. For NOS, agreement ranged from fair to substantial (selection: moderate; comparability: fair; outcome: substantial). For ROBINS-I, agreement reached almost perfect for ‘bias due to deviations from intended interventions’ and ‘selection into the study’; was substantial for ‘bias in classification of interventions’; moderate for ‘selection of the reported result’; fair for ‘missing data’ and ‘measurement of outcomes’ and slight for ‘bias due to confounding’. In QUADAS-2, agreement was substantial for the reference standard, moderate for patient selection and flow and timing, and fair for the index test. For QUIPS, agreement was almost perfect for study participation, substantial for attrition, moderate for prognostic factor and outcome measurement, fair for confounding and poor for statistical analysis and reporting.

Inter-rater agreement assessed at the item level

For the NOS scale, inter-rater agreement was slight in two items in the domain ‘selection of cohort’ and in particular ‘selection of non-exposed cohort’ (Gwet’s AC1 coefficient 0.05, 95% CI −0.22 to 0.08) and ‘demonstration that the outcome of interest was not present at the beginning of the study’ (Gwet’s AC1 coefficient 0.11, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.20) (online supplemental table 2). An almost perfect agreement was observed for one item in the ‘outcome’ domain and in particular ‘was follow-up long enough for outcomes to occur?’ (Gwet’s AC1 coefficient 0.94, 95% CI 0.89 to 0.98).

For ROBINS-I, the analysis at the item level is reported in online supplemental table 3. The analysis at item level was not possible for the domain ‘bias due to deviations for intended interventions’ because it was not applicable to the assessed studies. A slight agreement was obtained for six items belonging to different domains and substantial agreement for three items overall. The agreement was moderate or fair in seven items each.

In QUADAS-2, agreement widely varied among the items (online supplemental table 4). A slight agreement was observed in two items: ‘Were the index test results interpreted without knowledge of the results of the reference standard?’ (Gwet’s AC1 coefficient 0.15, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.25), and ‘Could the conduct or interpretation of the index test have introduced bias?’ (Gwet’s AC1 coefficient 0.16, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.27) which belong to the index test domain. Six items showed a fair inter-rater agreement, three moderate, six substantial, and one almost perfect agreement.

Inter-rater agreement for QUIPS’ items is reported in online supplemental table 5. One item presented poor agreement (Gwet’s AC1 coefficient −0.05, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.10), four items slight and eight items fair agreement. Almost perfect agreement was observed for nine items and substantial agreement for five items; the remaining items showed moderate agreement.

Tools feature analysis

Internal consistency was below the acceptable threshold (Cronbach’s α<0.70) for most tool–rater combinations; an exception was QUIPS. The NOS showed low internal consistency across raters, with a mean Cronbach’s alpha of 0.33. Individual rater values were 0.51, 0.29, –0.12 and 0.65, indicating marked variability in item-level agreement. The ROBINS-I tool also demonstrated low consistency, with a mean alpha of 0.28 and substantial inter-rater variability (–0.04 for REV1, 0.37 for REV2, 0.63 for REV3 and 0.14 for REV4). For the QUIPS tool, internal consistency varied widely, with alpha values of 0.40 (REV1), 0.34 (REV2), 0.34 (REV3) and 0.95 (REV4). The QUADAS-2 tool showed generally low internal consistency, with individual alpha values ranging from 0.26 to 0.52 and a mean of 0.38.

A ceiling effect was observed on the NOS scale, with most ratings in the ‘Moderate quality’ category, especially for reviewers 3 and 4. Ceiling effects were also noted for QUIPS and, to a lesser extent, QUADAS-2, while no clear floor or ceiling effects were detected in ROBINS-I (table 1).

Comparison with expert appraisal

According to the expert reviewer assessment, five studies (7.9%) were judged as critically low, 33 (52.4%) as low, 24 (38.1%) as moderate and one (1.6%) as high quality. Agreement between structured tools and expert appraisal was low for NOS, QUADAS-2 and QUIPS and fair for ROBINS-I (table 2). In particular, no study scored as critically low or high quality by NOS were also scored as critically low or high quality by the expert. Twenty per cent to 30.5% of studies scored as low quality by NOS were also scored as low quality by the expert. Less than 10% of the studies scoring moderate quality by NOS also scored moderate quality by expert judgement. Inter-rater agreement improved when comparing results by ROBINS-I versus the expert. The lowest agreement in quality assessment was observed when comparing the expert versus QUADAS-2 and QUIPS. Regarding the QUIPS tool, Gwet’s AC1 coefficient ranged from −0.19 (poor) to 0.10 (slight), indicating a low level of inter-rater agreement. Notably, no study was rated as critically low by any reviewer, while the majority of studies were classified as high quality (ranging from 57.4% to 86.9% across reviewers).

Discussion

Our study reveals important differences in the inter-rater reliability of widely used tools for the assessment of risk of bias and methodological appraisal in prognostic cohort studies. Inter-rater agreement was fair for all assessed tools except for QUIPS, which showed almost perfect agreement. However, several tools provided more favourable ratings compared with expert judgement. NOS, ROBINS-I, QUADAS-2 and QUIPS consistently identified studies at high risk of bias, whereas greater discrepancies emerged in the identification of studies judged to be at low risk of bias. Inter-rater reliability and concordance with expert appraisal varied substantially across instruments.

The methodological appraisal of observational studies remains particularly challenging because these studies are inherently vulnerable to bias and confounding, while no universally accepted assessment strategy currently exists.21 NOS was originally developed through a Delphi process to facilitate the appraisal of non-randomised studies in systematic reviews.6 ROBINS-I was specifically designed to evaluate ROBINS-I.7 QUADAS-2 was developed to assess the risk of bias and applicability of diagnostic accuracy studies and is recommended by several organisations, including the Cochrane Collaboration and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence.8 QUIPS was specifically designed for prognostic factor studies and is recommended by the Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group for prognostic systematic reviews.9 Despite these different intended purposes, these tools are frequently applied across heterogeneous observational research settings, including prognostic cohort studies.

In our study, these commonly used tools demonstrated overall modest inter-rater agreement. QUIPS showed more favourable agreement, although with a tendency towards more optimistic ratings. Several domains across tools were difficult to apply consistently, particularly those related to interventions, comparators or reference standards that may not fully align with prognostic study designs.22

NOS, ROBINS-I, QUADAS-2 and QUIPS also demonstrated limited concordance with expert appraisal. These findings further support the complexity of evaluating bias in observational prognostic research. ROBINS-I exhibited the highest concordance with expert judgement and showed a relatively balanced distribution of ratings. Although some domains require substantial methodological expertise (particularly bias due to confounding) its structured framework and domain-based approach may facilitate the appraisal of prognostic cohort studies. QUADAS-2, originally developed for diagnostic accuracy studies, showed suboptimal applicability in this setting. QUIPS, while specifically developed for prognostic research, demonstrated a potential ceiling effect that may reduce its discriminative ability across studies.

NOS and QUADAS-2 exhibited the lowest inter-rater agreement and the lowest concordance with expert appraisal. This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting high variability in NOS domain interpretation and limited reliability of QUADAS-2.22 23 24 The limited applicability of several NOS domains to prognostic cohort studies, together with the conceptual mismatch between QUADAS-2 and prognostic research designs, may partly explain these findings. In particular, the NOS comparability domain relies on the identification of clinically relevant confounders, and incomplete reporting of adjustment strategies in prognostic studies may further increase interpretative variability across reviewers. Although QUIPS was specifically developed for prognostic studies, variability at the item level suggests that some domains may still be interpreted inconsistently across reviewers.

One reviewer using ROBINS-I showed particularly strong agreement with expert appraisal, suggesting a possible ‘reviewer-related’ effect. This finding further highlights that structured assessment tools do not eliminate subjectivity and that methodological expertise and reviewer training and experience remain important determinants of reproducibility. Although expert judgement was used as the reference comparator, it should not be considered a gold standard, but rather a pragmatic benchmark grounded in methodological experience.

Our study has several strengths. Expert judgement provided a valuable benchmark for comparison across the assessment tools. The comparative evaluation of four commonly used instruments allowed direct comparison across tools against a consistent reference comparator.

Limitations include the application of these tools to prognostic studies without control groups, for which some instruments (particularly ROBINS-I) were not specifically designed. Additionally, to limit the burden of expert reviewers, each included study was assessed by a single expert acting as the reference comparator; therefore, formal assessment of inter-expert agreement was not feasible. This approach may introduce subjectivity, although all assessments were performed independently and blinded. Furthermore, because several tools were not originally designed to generate standardised global ratings, the harmonisation of tool-specific classifications into common categories may have influenced agreement patterns and contributed to ceiling effects. Different threshold selections and categorisation strategies may also have influenced agreement estimates and the distribution of final ratings.

Notably, our findings raise the hypothesis that incorporating risk of bias–based sensitivity analyses into the original meta-analysis on echocardiographic prognostic markers could have influenced the overall conclusions. Studies judged to be at substantial risk of bias might have warranted exclusion or down-weighting, potentially altering pooled estimates. This observation further supports the clinical importance of rigorous risk-of-bias assessment in prognostic meta-analyses, where methodological heterogeneity is common.

In summary, our findings support the use of ROBINS-I and QUIPS for risk-of-bias assessment in prognostic cohort studies, with the choice depending on the balance between comprehensiveness and usability. ROBINS-I showed the strongest alignment with expert judgement, whereas QUIPS provided more consistent (although potentially more optimistic) ratings. Reviewers and guideline developers should remain aware of the intended scope and methodological assumptions underlying each tool.

Conclusion

This study provides comparative evidence on the performance of four widely used tools for methodological appraisal and risk-of-bias assessment in observational research. Our findings suggest that ROBINS-I and QUIPS may represent useful options for risk-of-bias assessment in prognostic cohort studies. However, they also highlight the need for further research to refine existing instruments, develop standardised assessment frameworks and promote greater consensus regarding study appraisal in observational clinical research.
