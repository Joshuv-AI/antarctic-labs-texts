Response to Commentary on “Effectiveness of a Single‐Use Polyurethane Foam Positioning Pad Compared With Five‐Layer Foam Sacral Dressing for Pressure Ulcer Prevention in the Operating Room: A Randomized Controlled Clinical Trial”
Authors: Ebtisam Abdellatif Elhihi, Samah Ali Almuwallad, Bayan Mansour Alqrashi, Esmail Mohamed Nada, Naif Ahmed Alduaiji, Abdullah Abdulrahman Alfakeeh
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We sincerely thank the reader for their thoughtful and constructive comments regarding our article, “Effectiveness of a Single‐Use Polyurethane Foam Positioning Pad Compared with Five‐Layer Foam Sacral Dressing for Pressure Ulcer Prevention in the Operating Room: A Randomized Controlled Clinical Trial.” We appreciate the opportunity to clarify our study design, analysis, and interpretation. Below, we provide point‐by‐point responses:

1‐ Analysis Population and Attrition

Response: We appreciate this important observation. A total of 111 patients were screened, with 108 randomized [1]. Eight people were eliminated after randomization:

Three patients died or were placed on ECMO before their outcomes could be assessed.

Since pressure ulcer risk is substantially related with operation time and hemodynamic instability, adding these cases would have compromised the internal validity of our analysis. While this approach is similar to a modified ITT strategy, we recognize this as a drawback and believe that future studies should incorporate a rigorous ITT analysis complemented by sensitivity analyses to improve the robustness of effect estimates.

2‐ Open‐Label Design and Outcome Assessment

Response: We agree that our open‐label design posed challenges for blinding due to the distinctive appearance and application methods of the interventions. However, we minimized potential bias by:

Using the Bates‐Jensen Wound Assessment Tool, a validated, standardized instrument widely used in clinical trials.

Using the Bates‐Jensen Wound Assessment Tool, a validated, standardized instrument widely used in clinical trials.

Assigning all wound assessments to the head nurse of the wound management team, ensuring consistency and reducing inter‐rater variability.

We recognize that blinded photographic adjudication or objective metrics will enhance future research and want to incorporate these approaches into the next trials.

3‐ Handling of Baseline and Perioperative Covariates

Response: As shown in Table 1 of our study, randomization successfully balanced the two groups across the major baseline parameters, including age, BMI, comorbidities, type of cardiac procedure, and surgical length (all p > 0.05), demonstrating the internal validity of our findings. While there was a somewhat higher proportion of male patients in the polyurethane group (78% vs. 62%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.08) and is unlikely to have introduced significant bias.

Perioperative variables such as vasopressor use, cross‐clamp time, perfusion pressures, intraoperative temperature, and glucose management were tracked as part of regular clinical care but were excluded from the statistical model. This choice was based on two primary considerations:

1. The low occurrence rate (3%) reduced the statistical ability to make meaningful multivariable adjustments.

2. Randomization reduces the chance of systematic differences in these variables between groups.

We recognize, however, that future research with larger sample sizes should use covariate‐adjusted models and possibly stratify patients based on significant intraoperative exposures to improve precision and control for residual confounding.

4‐ Outcome Window and Clinical Prominence

Response: In our study, we used the Bates‐Jensen Wound Assessment Tool to assess pressure ulcers immediately after the operation and again on Days 3 and 7 for both groups. The 7‐day follow‐up cutoff was selected based on existing research indicating that the majority of intraoperatively acquired pressure ulcers develop within the first 3–7 days after surgery [2, 3]. Furthermore, this timeframe was comparable with the hospital's routine postoperative monitoring strategy, which ensured data consistency and the feasibility of follow‐up assessments.

5‐ Economic Analysis

Response: In our study, the economic analysis was conducted from the hospital's perspective and was purposefully structured as a preliminary evaluation focused on direct material costs, as the application times for both interventions were equal.

To supplement the clinical data, we estimated the cost‐effectiveness ratio as the cost per ulcer‐free patient in each group, as well as the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio, which assesses the incremental cost per additional ulcer‐free patient when utilizing the more expensive intervention. Based on clinical outcomes (94% ulcer‐free rate in the polyurethane foam group vs. 100% in the five‐layer foam group), the five‐layer foam sacral dressing had a better cost‐effectiveness profile, giving complete ulcer avoidance at a far lower overall cost.

We agree, however, that given the low overall incidence of pressure ulcers (3%) and the short 7‐day follow‐up period, cost minimization analysis could have been an alternate option. Future research will try to undertake a full cost‐effectiveness evaluation that includes:
