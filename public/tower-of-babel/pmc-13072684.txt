Measuring physical literacy in Mi'kma'ki: an initial determination of Indigenous (Mi'kmaw) students' physical literacy
Authors: Daniel B Robinson, Sebastian Harenberg, Tex Marshall, Sherra Rogers, Mickey Jutras, Nathan Hall, William Walters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical literacy is a critical component of children's overall wellness and academic success. While it has been discussed in Indigenous contexts, no published research has yet systematically measured physical literacy among Indigenous children in Canada. This study provides the first baseline measurements of physical literacy in Indigenous/Mi'kmaw students aged 9–12 attending schools within Mi'kmaw Kina'matnewey (MK), a unified Indigenous education authority in Mi'kma'ki—the ancestral and unceded territory of the Mi'kmaq people in Nova Scotia, Canada. Using the Canadian Assessment of Physical Literacy, Second Edition (CAPL-2), researchers evaluated 81 students across four physical literacy domains: motivation and confidence, daily behaviour, knowledge and understanding, and physical competence. Analyses examined patterns by age and gender. Results showed that more than 90% of students scored within the beginning or progressing categories for physical competence and overall physical literacy, mirroring but falling below national CAPL-2 trends. Significant age-related differences emerged, with 11- and 12-year-olds outperforming younger peers on movement skills, knowledge, and overall physical literacy. No significant gender differences were observed. These findings establish the first empirical snapshot of Indigenous/Mi'kmaw students' physical literacy and highlight opportunities for culturally grounded, community-driven interventions to support Indigenous/Mi'kmaw children's movement development, holistic well-being, and lifelong physical activity.Keywords: Physical literacy, measurement, assessment, Indigenous, motivation, confidence, daily behavior, knowledge and understanding, physical competence

Introduction

That there is an achievement “gap” with respect to the academic performance of Indigenous students is beyond dispute (e.g. see Carr [1,2]).1 Indeed, there is plenty of documentary evidence highlighting the disparities in academic performance for Indigenous students through a variety of measures, including course grades, standardised test scores and high school and post-secondary completion rates [3,4]. In recognition of deleterious historic and ongoing impacts of government-imposed policies that harmed Indigenous people, including the residential school system, scholars have called for such gaps instead to be reframed as educational debts [5]. Literature is also clear that Indigenous peoples, communities and knowledge systems are best situated to address Indigenous educational issues [6,7].

Critically conscious allied-settler and Indigenous scholars, researchers, educators and legislators have been attending to these observations in recent years, with culturally relevant and socially just actions and responses (e.g. see [9]). For example, some have appealed for culturally relevant or sustaining pedagogy [10], identified “best” practices to indigenise curriculum and embed Indigenous ways of knowing into teacher education programs [11], improved upon teacher candidates' understanding of Indigenous peoples and the impact of colonisation on Indigenous communities [12] and fostered dialogues necessary for critical peacebuilding education [13].

Perhaps as successful as any response in any Canadian province/territories has been the establishment of Mi'kmaw Kina'matnewey (MK), a “unified team of chiefs, staff, parents and educators who advocate on behalf of and represent the educational interests of [Mi'kmaw] communities, [while protecting] the educational and Mi'kmaw language rights of the Mi'kmaq people” ([14], para. 2). Indeed, MK has frequently been highlighted in Indigenous education scholarship as a leading example of community-controlled educational governance and decolonising educational practice in Canada (e.g. see [6,15,16]). There is no shortage of empirical data showcasing the important accomplishments of MK, particularly as they relate to student and school success (e.g. see [17–19]). These successes include improvements in graduation rates, numeracy and literacy assessment scores, attendance rates, and post-secondary enrolments, among others [14,16,17,19,20]. Importantly, Indigenous self-governance of education through MK has centred Mi'kmaw ways of knowing, being, and doing into all aspects of schooling [16].

Indigenous epistemologies are holistic [6]; a holistic view identifies the interwoven aspects of life, including the physical self. And, notwithstanding the importance of identifying and addressing educational debts in relation to, say, numeracy and/or literacy, increasingly school communities are being called upon to widen their focus upon a more holistic consideration of students' literacy (and wellness): physical literacy. Physical literacy is a holistic concept that fosters lifelong physical activity through interconnected affective, physical, cognitive and behavioural domains [21]. A focus on physical literacy is especially relevant in today's educational milieu, where evidence shows parallel debts in key health-related areas—including health promotion and education, rising rates of overweight and obesity, increasing inactivity and the growing prevalence of non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes [22,23].

Research purpose

Physical literacy is an increasingly familiar form of literacy in contemporary school communities, as well as a recognised health-determining marker of wellness [24,25]. It is also the case that student wellness is strongly associated with academic performance [26,27]. Additionally, and importantly, physical literacy has now been measured across all of Canada (see [28]) but not yet within any Mi'kmaw (or Indigenous) communities; these data are simply non-existent within the literature.

Given these observations, we have recognised the need to measure Mi'kmaw students' “baseline” physical literacy; this recognised need came, principally, from MK's Manager of Mi'kmaw Sport. Such data would enable researchers, educators, and, most critically, Mi'kmaw community partners to have a necessary foundational understanding of these students' physical literacy. With such an understanding, potential applications include drawing initial comparisons between these Indigenous students' baseline scores and existing research with other populations to determine, for example, if a physical literacy educational debt exists (i.e. Is there a difference between these Indigenous students' physical literacy and the physical literacy of other students in Canada?). Moreover, and more importantly, with this initial information may future researchers, educators and Mi'kmaw community partners be enabled to co-design (culturally responsive) programs/practices as interventions meant to improve upon targeted areas of Mi'kmaw students' physical literacy. So, it is in this context that we share the purpose of this research was to measure the physical literacy of Mi'kmaw students, aged 9–12 (in grades 4–6), within MK's community schools.

Physical literacy, elaborated

Physical literacy is a term and concept that is commonplace within many Western physical education and sport contexts [29–31]. Since [32] first invited “a debate” about the then-(re)emerging concept 25 years ago, a steady stream of physical education and sport pedagogy researchers, among others, have engaged in said debate, as well as in related philosophical, conceptual, and research-related explorations. Multiple reviews of the concept(ualisation) of physical literacy have highlighted, repeatedly, that the field is still very much without a common shared or core definition and/or operationalisation (e.g. see [33–41]).

This ambiguity is, in some respects, less than ideal. Certainly, it invites immediate critique of many physical literacy-related empirical claims, and it suggests further physical literacy conceptualisation-related research, commentary and consensus-building efforts are warranted [33,42]. At the same time, we acknowledge that some scholars have articulated compelling reasons to resist efforts toward a single consensual definition, instead advocating for openness to multiple interpretations shaped by evolving understandings, diverse contexts, and disciplinary differences (e.g. see [31,34]). Notwithstanding the observation that various (research) groups have, independently, operationalised the physical literary construct differently, a consideration of many of these highlights affective (e.g. confidence, motivation, self-esteem), cognitive (knowledge and understanding), and physical (e.g. motor skill competence) domains within most-to-all of them [31,34,43]. So, though the concept is still without a universally accepted definition and understanding, these three domains are certainly familiar to all who engage with the concept.

Physical literacy, defined (in Canada)

For many years, three Canadian organisations with histories of leadership related to physical literacy (Sport for Life, Physical and Health Education Canada [PHE Canada], and the Healthy Active Living and Obesity Research Group [HALO]) had also recognised these three domains, in addition to a behavioural domain [44]. Wanting for some necessary alignment of the physical literacy term/concept across Canada, these three organisations engaged with others across the nation (i.e. over 200 individuals or organisations) to establish Canada's Physical Literacy Consensus Statement [44,45]. Canada's Physical Literacy Consensus Statement leans heavily upon Whitehead's [46] conception and, accordingly, utilises the definition of physical literacy also offered by the International Physical Literacy Association (IPLA), namely, “the motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge and understanding to value and take responsibility for engagement in physical activities for life” ([45], p. 1). This conceptualisation of physical literacy includes elements that are categorised as affective (motivation and confidence), behavioural (engaging in physical activities for life), cognitive (knowledge and understanding) and physical (physical competence).

Measuring physical literacy

Measuring physical literacy has similarly faced discussion and debate, with some presenting fair scepticisms and questions about the practice. For example, some have questioned the appropriateness of ideals such as empowerment, embodiment and social relatedness being subject to measurement [30,47,48]. Others have wondered about the ongoing absence of a common language surrounding physical literacy and physical literacy measurements, and the impact this might have on the interpretation of measurements [30,47]. Relatedly, some have recognised that although physical literacy has multiple domains, some measurements, curiously, privilege some over others [48,49]. And others have wondered about idealised measurement standards, asking if physical literacy measurement standards ought to be criterion-referenced, norm-referenced or something else [43]. Despite these measurement-related issues and uncertainties, several individuals or organisations have developed and utilised physical literacy measurement measures in recent years.

Physical literacy measurement measures

Barnett et al. [50], Edwards et al. [42], Jean de Dieu and Zhou [49], Robinson and Randall [48], Shearer et al. [51] and Young et al. [52]—among others—have reviewed and/or evaluated several specific physical literacy measurement measures. Their research has demonstrated that physical literacy measurement measures are in use within many countries from the Global North and beyond [50,51], align with differing (yet similar) definitions of physical literacy [49], utilise both qualitative and quantitative measures [42,48,51], and measure multiple domains [42,49–52]. These measurement measures also attend to physical literacy's foundational philosophical foundations (e.g. monism, phenomenology) to differing degrees [42,48], enjoy differing levels of instrument validity and reliability [49–51], and can boast differing degrees of usability and feasibility [48,51]. With so many possible physical literacy measurement “tools” available, it is understandably difficult to know what is best (or better).

Canada's physical literacy measurement measures

Physical literacy measurement measures included within the reviews and evaluations mentioned above include three especially notable ones from Canada—notable because all published reviews of the topic include attention to these three Canadian tools. These include Sport for Life's suite of PLAY tools (e.g. PLAYbasic, PLAYcoach), PHE Canada's Passport for Life and HALO's Canadian Assessment of Physical Literacy (CAPL; [49–52]). Of these three physical literacy measures, the CAPL measures the greatest number of physical domain elements (e.g. agility, cardiovascular endurance; [50], has some of the highest levels of content validity and construct validity [48–50], and is one of the most useable and feasible [51]; it also aligns very closely with Canada's Physical Literacy Consensus Statement [44,45]. Despite these strengths, CAPL has also been subject to critique. Some scholars have raised concerns regarding its (over)emphasis on physical fitness, alongside questions about its alignment with physical literacy's philosophical roots in existentialism and phenomenology—including the interconnected principles of monism and embodiment [48,53,54].

The CAPL and CAPL-2

The CAPL was first used in 2013 and within 5 y, had been used to measure the physical literacy of over 10,000 children in Canada [28,55]. After this intensive data collection phase, a research team attended to discovered errors and “lessons learned” from their use of the CAPL. This brought about some responsive revisions to the CAPL and the subsequent creation of the Canadian Assessment of Physical Literacy, Second Edition (CAPL-2). This resulted in some measures from the original CAPL being eliminated (e.g. grip strength measurements, BMI measurements and calculations, waist circumference measurements, self-reported sedentary time measures; [55].

The revised CAPL-2 now includes three physical competence protocols, two daily behaviour protocols, and a 22-item questionnaire related to knowledge and understanding and motivation and confidence [55]. For each of these four domain scores, the CAPL-2 provides a rubric for boys and for girls, so that total scores can be categorised with one of four descriptors: beginning, progressing, achieving, or excelling [56]. Following is a summary of the four domains' measurement tasks and their relative point values (the CAPL-2 provides an overall physical literacy score out of 100).

Motivation and confidence. Motivation and confidence (30 points) measurement tasks include the following: three Likert-type questions for four different motivation/confidence-related constructs: intrinsic motivation, competence, predilection and adequacy (7.5 points, each).

Daily behaviour. Daily behaviour (30 points) measurement tasks include the following: average daily step count is measured by use of a pedometer for a one-week period (25 points) and moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) is measured by use of a self-reported measure for a one-week period (5 points).

Knowledge and understanding. Knowledge and understanding (10 points) measurement tasks include the following: a “fill in the blank” exercise in which six physical activity-related words (e.g. endurance, pulse) must be used correctly (6 points) and four physical activity- and physical fitness-related multiple-choice questions (1 point, each).

Physical competence. Physical competence (30 points) measurement tasks include the following: cardiorespiratory fitness is measured using FitnessGram's 15- or 20-metre Progressive Aerobic Cardiovascular Endurance Run (PACER; 10 points); motor skills are measured using the Canadian Agility and Movement Skill Assessment (CAMSA; 10 points) and muscular endurance is measured using the abdominal plank test (10 points).

CAPL and CAPL-2 measurements

CAPL, beginning in Canada. The first and largest study that utilised the original CAPL occurred in Canada [28]. This multi-site, cross-sectional study measured physical literacy in 10,034 children aged 8–12 years. Mean total scores were approximately 63/100 for both genders, placing most children at a progressing rather than achieving level, with only the knowledge and understanding domain reaching achieving. Also, boys outperformed girls in aerobic fitness (PACER), movement skills (CAMSA) and plank measures, whereas girls showed greater flexibility (sit-and-reach; this flexibility task is no longer a part of CAPL-2). Daily step counts were higher in boys and declined with age. Tremblay et al. [28] concluded that these findings constitute a national baseline, and they highlighted the need for targeted interventions to enhance physical literacy among children in Canada.

CAPL-2, subsequent Canadian applications. Canadian applications of the CAPL-2 have expanded substantially in recent years, with studies by Blais et al. [57], Coyne et al. [58], and Liu et al. [59] demonstrating its utility across diverse populations and instructional contexts. Blais et al.'s explanatory sequential mixed-methods study measured physical literacy among 99 children aged 8–12 years with chronic medical conditions, finding that most scored in the beginning/progressing range (mean ≈ 56/100), with relatively strong motivation and confidence but weaker physical competence and daily behaviour, and that boys outperformed girls quantitatively. They concluded that children with chronic conditions can achieve important physical literacy outcomes even without meeting normative competence standards and recommended interventions that prioritise positive and engaging activity experiences. Coyne et al.'s [60] large cross-sectional study (n = 1059) examined whether objectively measured MVPA could be predicted from CAPL-2 domain scores, reporting that boys accumulated more MVPA, girls demonstrated higher overall physical literacy, and MVPA showed modest positive associations with overall CAPL-2, daily behaviour, physical competence, and motivation and confidence (but not knowledge and understanding), leading to recommendations to integrate objective MVPA metrics into CAPL-2 scoring while acknowledging design limitations. A complementary quasi-experimental study by Coyne et al. [58] evaluated a 10-week Run Jump Throw Wheel (RJTW) program in grades 4–6 (n = 310), showing a 3.3 ± 8.8-point increase in overall physical literacy driven by gains in physical competence and knowledge and understanding, with no differences by grade or gender and larger improvements in a suburban school, supporting RJTW as a viable school-based approach while noting the need for strategies to increase daily activity behaviours. Finally, Liu et al.'s [59] quasi-experimental comparison of sport education versus direct instruction across ten grade 3–5 lessons (n = 62) found significantly greater improvements in overall physical literacy for the sport education group, attributable specifically to gains in motivation and confidence, with no significant changes in physical competence, daily behaviour, or knowledge and understanding in either condition, prompting recommendations for longer or broader interventions that better target physical competence.

CAPL-2, international applications. Subsequent studies that utilised the revised CAPL-2 have occurred in many other countries, including China [61], Denmark [62], Greece [63], Iran [64], Italy [65], Pakistan [66] and Spain [67], among others. In these studies, researchers had children aged 8–12 years complete the CAPL-2, as they investigated several physical literacy-related factors and features. Some established national baseline measures [64], while others investigated the relationship between perceived and measured physical literacy [61,68], the relationship between active transportation and physical literacy [62], the relationship between cardiorespiratory fitness and physical literacy [66], and the relationship between active breaks and physical literacy [67]. Lastly [63], researched the potential for purposeful interventions (related to gymnastics instruction) to improve upon physical literacy.

Indigenous peoples/children and physical literacy

In more recent years, some Indigenous and allied scholars have been engaging with physical literacy as it relates to Indigenous peoples/children (e.g. see [16,69–72]). Not surprisingly, all this work has been situated within countries that have histories of colonisation—specifically, Australia, New Zealand/Aotearoa, and Canada (it is a curious observation that there is an absence of such a focus within the United States). Across this literature, physical literacy is oftentimes framed as a holistic, relational concept that resonates strongly with Indigenous knowledges—connecting movement to culture, spirituality, community, and land. Indigenous educators and mentors emphasise wisdom sharing, youth-centred pedagogy, and relational support, often speaking of physical literacy and “being active for life” in the same breath, and advocating for programs that embed culture and Elders' teachings [70]. For example, the Nature's Way–Our Way early-years initiative operationalises this alignment through Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), co-creating land- and nature-based activities and risky/adventurous play with Elders and families, and producing culturally rooted activities for First Nations and Métis childcare settings [69]. A land-based case study of deer hunting further illustrates how traditional practices can cultivate some core elements of physical literacy (motivation, confidence, competence, knowledge), while also exceeding physical activity guidelines and strengthening community roles and intergenerational learning [16]. Decolonising perspectives invite a reframing of physical literacy as an ongoing, place-responsive “doing” that resists settler separations from land, recognises urban spaces as Indigenous places, and situates movement within ecological and social justice [71]. Practical assessment work with Indigenous Australian children finds that a pictorial narrative questionnaire (PL-C Quest) is broadly understood as intended, with children drawing on cultural experiences (e.g. carrying siblings, inclusive play) when making sense of items—evidence that culturally sensitive, story-infused tools can capture Indigenous children's self-perceived physical literacy [72].

Recent scholarship further deepens this relational framing by positioning physical literacy within Indigenous land-based ontologies and ethical research spaces. For example, Nature's Way–Our Way initiative has been theorised through Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), which braids Indigenous knowledge(s) and Western movement sciences while maintaining their distinct epistemological integrity, emphasising relational accountability, sovereignty and cultural renewal rather than assimilation into dominant frameworks [73].

Complementing this work, a pilot case assemblage grounded in new materialist and relational methodologies demonstrates how physical literacy can be (re)storied through child–land relationships, showing that movement emerges not simply as individual skill acquisition but as a process of becoming within interconnected social, cultural, and ecological environments [21]. This work extends the existing literature by foregrounding radical relationality—where land, story and community actively shape children's movement experiences and well-being.

Together, these studies suggest programs should privilege co-creation, land-based learning and community sovereignty while ensuring that measures reflect Indigenous contexts and languages of movement [70,72]. They also suggest that culturally rooted physical literacy initiatives may function as protective factors for Indigenous children's holistic wellness when grounded in relational ontologies and culturally meaningful storytelling practices [21]. At the same time, they surface persistent tensions in terminology and implementation, implying that centring “active for life” outcomes and place-anchored practice may matter more than policing definitions [70]. In short, Indigenous-led, land-connected approaches—whether in early childhood centres or on the land hunting—offer powerful pathways to nurture physical literacy as collective, embodied well-being.

Notwithstanding the Indigenous-physical literacy connections detailed here (many of which were drawn by Indigenous peoples themselves), it is important to note others have problematised physical literacy—suggesting that some critical questioning of physical literacy as a potentially colonising concept is in order [74], as is a need to more authentically invite Indigenous peoples' perspectives into an until-recently entirely Western/Eurocentric concept [70]. Indeed, scholars working within Nature's Way–Our Way explicitly note that physical literacy has colonial genealogies and must be approached carefully to avoid co-opting Indigenous knowledge(s), advocating instead for anticolonial approaches that prioritise Indigenous self-determination and relational ethics [73].

Lastly, despite this growing body of literature related to Indigenous peoples/children and physical literacy, there have been no published accounts of research related to measurement of Indigenous children's physical literacy. While it is possible (or likely) that some of the participants in some of the Canadian studies listed above were Indigenous, none of the authors make mention of this possibility. Also, this is similarly true of other Canadian physical literacy measurement research literature where the PLAY suite of tools or Passport for Life were used (e.g. see [75,76]). There is simply no such literature that exists.

Research methods

Research context

MK serves 12 of the 13 Mi'kmaq communities found within Nova Scotian Mi'kma'ki territory. This research occurred in 10 of these 12 communities. This research project was envisioned and established through the leadership offered and partnership welcomed by Tex Marshall, a Mi'kmaw sport and physical activity leader who is also MK's Manager of Mi'kmaw Sport. A longstanding professional relationship (15-plus years) with Tex and MK allowed researchers from St. Francis Xavier University to engage with all MK communities so that he (and they) would be enabled to co-develop a baseline understanding of physical literacy for Mi'kmaw students attending MK schools. Ethical approval was granted by the St. Francis Xavier University Research Ethics Board and by Mi'kmaw Ethics Watch.

Respect, responsibility, relevance and reciprocity

As this research project was conducted in partnership with the MK educational system, the research adhered to the four R's frameworks of Indigenous research: respect, responsibility, relevance and reciprocity [7]. The research was conducted respectfully at all stages with MK's Manager of Mi'kmaw Sport, as a member of the research team who connected the researchers with MK school staff. Responsibility existed throughout the research, as principles of ethical conduct were strictly adhered to. This included informed consent being provided by school principals, participants, and their caregivers; sharing results in an understandable format and the ownership of data by the MK system. The research project was relevant in that it was envisioned and requested by the MK system to better understand the physical literacy of their students. Reciprocity lives in the project design in that there is benefit for the MK system in understanding baseline physical literacy measures. A forthcoming collaborative project will see members of the research team continue to work with MK schools to purposefully improve from the baseline measures presented in this paper.

Participants

There are 10 MK schools with students in grades 4–6 (approximately ages 9–12). These schools vary in population, with these grade 4–6 cohorts ranging in size, approximately, from five to 170. All students in grades 4–6 from these 10 schools were invited to participate. Participation in the research was voluntary, and written consent and assent was collected for all participants. In all, 81 students participated in this research. Of these 81, 43 were girls and 38 were boys. These 81 students were the following ages: aged 9 = 21, aged 10 = 31, aged 11 = 20, and aged 12 = 9.

Data collection

Apart from daily behaviour step count measures, all the data were collected at each of the school sites over 1 or 2 days. With participants arranged into smaller groups (approximately 3–6), a small team of researchers facilitated all measures with participants. Therefore, as some participants completed one physical competence measure (e.g. CAMSA) with two or more researchers, others completed another knowledge and understanding measure (e.g. multiple choice questions). To collect step count data, participants were instructed on how to use their pedometers and the tracking forms provided to them; researchers returned after one week to collect the pedometers and tracking forms. All protocols for data collection followed those detailed in the second edition of the Canadian Assessment of Physical Literacy: Manual for Test Administration (see [56]).

Data analysis

Before descriptive or inferential statistics were calculated, the data were checked for completeness. Most variables had little to no missing data (<15%). Following the recommendations of Longmuir et al. [77], an algorithm was used to compute the composite scores apart from the average daily step count. Like other studies, step count was among the variables with considerable missing data [78]; in the present study, only 13 out of 81 participants (16.05%) provided step count data. After careful consideration, the scores of the 68 participants who did not report a value for step count were not replaced. Rather, the total score for physical literacy was calculated out of 70 points (i.e. by taking the sum of the scores of the motivation and confidence, knowledge and understanding, and physical competence domains) and scaled so that the score reflects a value out of 100 points (multiplication by 10/7 = 1.43). Only for the 13 participants who reported a step count score, the sum of all CAPL-2 domains (including the dimension of daily behaviour) was calculated. The reported descriptive information in Tables 1 and 2 for step count as well as the domain score for daily behaviour reflects the scores reported by those 13 participants.

For comparisons between genders, reported as boys or girls, independent t tests with Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated for continuous variables. For categorical variables, χ2 analyses with Cramer's V effect sizes were conducted. For the comparison between age groups (9-, 10-, 11-, and 12-year-olds), one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) with η2partial effect sizes were calculated for continuous variables. If a significant result was found, Tukey post hoc t tests were performed to locate significant differences between the individual age groups. Again, χ2 analyses with Cramer's V effect sizes were conducted for continuous variables. A significance level of 0.05 was set for all analyses. The statistical analyses were performed in JASP 0.19.3.

Notably, half of the schools provided data from more than 10 participants, while four schools only recruited three or fewer students (these four were very small schools). Discussion

The purpose of this study was to generate baseline physical literacy data for Mi'kmaw students aged 9–12 in MK schools using the CAPL-2, contributing the first known dataset of its kind involving Indigenous students in Canada. The results provide several noteworthy observations regarding students' physical literacy profiles, patterns across age and gender, and broader implications for future programming, assessment and research. Overall, the findings suggest that most participating students are situated within the beginning or progressing categories of physical literacy, with relatively few achieving or excelling according to CAPL-2 benchmarks. This pattern is generally consistent with national research (though lower) using CAPL-2, and it is very consistent with the findings of Blais et al. [57], highlighting areas that may warrant targeted, context-specific intervention and further inquiry.

A primary finding of this study is that more than 90% of participants were classified into the beginning or progressing categories for physical competence and overall physical literacy. This aligns with national baseline data reported by Tremblay et al. [28], who found most children in Canada scored in the progressing category and that relatively few reached achieving or excelling levels. In this and other settings, overall physical literacy tends to centre around mid-range scores, with variability in the physical competence and daily behaviour domains. In this sense, these Mi'kmaw students' results are not anomalous; rather, they mirror a broader trend, suggesting that achieving or excelling on the CAPL-2 benchmarks is relatively uncommon in other populations of children aged 8–12. Still, a closer consideration of these data alongside detailed data sets from national inventories might highlight some differences warranting attention.

While the overall trend is consistent with other studies, it is notable that physical competence scores were especially concentrated in the lower two categories. This is important because physical competence is often the domain most strongly associated with children's physical activity participation and confidence (e.g. [60,61]). The low distribution of scores across CAMSA, PACER and plank measurements suggests potential areas for targeted programming in MK schools, particularly interventions focused on foundational movement skills, muscular endurance and aerobic fitness. These findings also provide a necessary baseline against which future initiatives—such as school-based movement programs, land-based activities, and/or culturally relevant (i.e. with attention placed upon relationality, land-based pedagogy, wisdom traditions and culture/spirituality) physical literacy interventions—can be evaluated. Importantly, all these potential future initiatives could occur (ideally) within regular physical education programming—and/or outside of it (e.g. intramural and/or extracurricular activities).

Another important observation relates to developmental differences. Age-related patterns were consistent with expectations for skill acquisition and physical literacy development [55]. Eleven-year-olds performed significantly better than nine- and ten-year-olds in overall physical literacy, CAMSA scores, and knowledge and understanding, and twelve-year-olds also demonstrated significantly higher knowledge and understanding scores. These patterns align with CAPL-2 literature showing that physical literacy tends to increase with age in late childhood and that older children generally perform better on movement skills and knowledge-based tasks (e.g. [28,68]). Importantly, these age-based differences reinforce that skill development is cumulative and that modest improvements across school years may be expected as children gain competence, familiarity with activities, and greater conceptual understanding. For MK communities, these findings underscore the value of longitudinal or sequential programming that builds across grade levels, allowing students to revisit, refine and deepen competencies over time.

In contrast, gender-based comparisons did not yield statistically significant differences in any domain, although plank performance and overall physical competence approached significance, with effect sizes suggesting somewhat stronger performance among boys. This trend is consistent with other CAPL-2 studies reporting modest male advantages in physical competence measures—particularly aerobic fitness and movement skill measurements [28,49]. The absence of significant gender differences in the current sample may reflect the relatively small sample size, but it also suggests that gender disparities in this population may be less pronounced than in some other Canadian samples. This is an encouraging finding, particularly considering persistent gender-based gaps in physical activity participation documented in other contexts.

The daily behaviour domain warrants additional consideration. Only 13 participants provided usable pedometer data, preventing meaningful analysis or categorisation of the daily behaviour scores. High levels of missing step count data are common in physical literacy research [78], often due to inconsistent device wear, device malfunction or logistical constraints, especially in school-based studies. However, the absence of a reliable daily behaviour measure limits the interpretation of overall physical literacy and restricts the understanding of activity patterns that may influence or interact with physical competence, motivation and knowledge scores. Future research in MK communities may benefit from alternative approaches to measuring daily behaviour, such as accelerometer-based monitoring, structured logging supported by teachers or shorter monitoring periods supported by community facilitators. Reliable data in this domain are crucial, given its theoretical and empirical connections to lifelong physical activity engagement.

Despite some limitations, the present findings contribute uniquely to the physical literacy literature. To date, no published studies have reported on measured physical literacy in Indigenous children using CAPL-2 (or any other measure), despite increasing scholarly attention to the connections between Indigenous worldviews, movement, land-based learning and holistic wellness (e.g. see [16,69,70]). The lack of comparative Indigenous data has prevented researchers, educators and Indigenous communities from examining the potential existence of a physical literacy debt analogous to that observed in academic and wellness-related domains. This study provides the first dataset that can begin to support such comparisons while also offering a model for respectful, collaboratively guided research shaped by reciprocity, relevance and community-defined needs.

The study's findings also carry several practical implications. First, they suggest the potential value of co-designed, culturally relevant physical literacy-related programming within MK schools. Others have engaged in such co-design efforts with(in) Indigenous communities, and have seen participants reap the benefits (e.g. [69–71,79]. Although the CAPL-2 is grounded in Western conceptualisations of physical literacy, its data may productively inform the development of programs that integrate Mi'kmaw knowledge, land-based pedagogy, and community priorities. Such programs may draw from the broader Indigenous physical literacy literature, which emphasises connection to land, relational teaching, community involvement, and movement-through-culture. Second, the baseline data offer MK educators and leaders a foundation from which to track progress over time and evaluate the effectiveness of their own interventions. Finally, the study underscores the importance of ongoing partnership-based research, particularly research that supports community sovereignty over data and decision-making.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and uneven participation across school sites limit generalisability. The substantial amount of missing daily behaviour data restricts conclusions about physical activity patterns. Time and space constraints within schools may also have influenced performance on certain CAPL-2 tasks. Despite these limitations, the study's strengths—including its community-directed design, adherence to ethical principles grounded in respect and reciprocity, and its contribution of entirely novel data—position it as an important first step in understanding and supporting physical literacy development in Mi'kmaw children and youth.

In sum, this study provides the first empirical snapshot of physical literacy among Mi'kmaw students in MK schools, revealing patterns such as other child populations while identifying specific areas for future research and targeted intervention. These findings establish a foundational baseline and create opportunities for subsequent community-driven, culturally relevant strategies to enhance physical literacy and holistic wellness among Mi'kmaw children and youth.

A cautionary note

Though not previously introduced or discussed within this manuscript as a limitation, we recognise that it is fair and important (perhaps, necessary) to question the cultural relevance of the physical literacy concept generally and the CAPL-2 specifically for Mi'kmaw (and Indigenous) children/peoples. Both physical literacy and the CAPL-2 are Western-rooted constructs with little-to-no initial invitation from Indigenous peoples. Given this, there is an obvious opportunity (or need) to critically consider how such constructs might be lacking in cultural relevance and, by extension, application and meaningfulness. Therefore, just as validation studies of CAPL-2 translations have occurred with Chinese, Danish, German, Italian, Polish and Portuguese children (see [65,80–83]), undertaking similar exercises with Mi'kmaw (and Indigenous) children/peoples makes sense. Moreover, language is not the only variable to consider here (English was the second language for some of our Mi'kmaw participants). As was the case with the work undertaken by Iannaccone et al. [65], it may well be the case that Mi'kmaw/Indigenous cultural adaptations of CAPL-2 are warranted or necessary. Moreover, it may be the case that other physical literacy measurement tools (e.g. the PLAY suite of tools or Passport for Life)—or adaptations of them—are more appropriate for Indigenous children than is the existing CAPL-2.
