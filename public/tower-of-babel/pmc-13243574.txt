Achilles tendon exhibits distinct transcriptomic changes with increased mitochondrial energy production under lunar gravity
Authors: Taro Kasai, Toshiya Tsubaki, Takumi Matsumoto, Yasuhide Iwanaga, Asuka Terashima, Yasunori Omata, Sakae Tanaka, Taku Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWithin the musculoskeletal system, the effects of reduced gravity on muscle and bone are well characterized, whereas the role of tendons remains poorly understood. In this study, we investigated adaptations of the Achilles tendon to lunar gravity using RNA-seq of mice maintained on the International Space Station for 25–26 days. Compared with quadriceps femoris muscle and brown adipose tissue, more genes were upregulated in the Achilles tendon under lunar gravity. Enrichment analyses revealed significant upregulation of mitochondrial energy production, while extracellular matrix organization and collagen fiber organization were downregulated. Histological analyses revealed unchanged tendon fiber density but reduced mature collagen fibers, accompanied by increased ATP5B protein expression in the lunar gravity group. These findings indicate a tendon-specific enhancement of mitochondrial energy production under lunar gravity, potentially linked to impaired collagen maturation. Our insights may contribute to the development of therapeutic strategies for tendon degeneration associated with aging and disuse.Subject terms: Musculoskeletal abnormalities, Molecular medicine

Introduction

The musculoskeletal system is highly responsive to mechanical loading, with its structure and function strongly influenced by mechanical forces. In the microgravity environment of space, significant alterations occur, affecting both the morphology and molecular regulation of musculoskeletal tisues1–6. Among these, muscle and bone have been the primary focus of microgravity research. Previous studies have demonstrated that skeletal muscle undergoes marked atrophy due to decreased gravitational loading, leading to reductions in muscle fiber cross-sectional area and shifts in fiber-type composition7–9. Similarly, bone loss is well documented, with decreased bone mineral density and altered bone remodeling processes observed during extended spaceflight6,10–12.

Tendons, which transmit forces from muscles to bones, play a crucial role in mechanotransduction within the musculoskeletal system. Recent studies in tendon biology have increasingly characterized the transcriptomic profiles of tendons, revealing gene expression signatures distinct from those of muscle and bone13–17. Notably, tendon-specific knock-in mice carrying a gain-of-function mutation in the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1 exhibited enhanced jumping ability, increased running speed, greater tendon flexibility, and improved elastic energy storage, consistent with the higher frequency of PIEZO1 mutations observed in sprinters17.

Previous studies have investigated the effects of unloading on tendons using both in vitro and in vivo models. In an in vitro model of tendon-like tissue derived from human tendon cells, removal of tension led to changes in integrin expression within a few days, a decrease in tendon cell differentiation markers, and disruption of tendon structure, while expression of pro-inflammatory molecules increased18. In a rat hindlimb suspension model, three weeks of suspension reduced Achilles tendon stiffness19. In a rabbit hindlimb immobilization model using an eight-week cast, the Achilles tendon did not exhibit atrophy or rupture but showed decreased stiffness20. Taken together, these unloading studies on tendons have largely focused on structural and biomechanical changes, with gene expression analyses restricted to a limited set of targeted genes. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying tendon adaptation to unloading, comprehensive transcriptomic analyses are required. Notably, comprehensive transcriptomic analyses of tendon tissues from animal models exposed to actual microgravity environments in space have not yet been conducted, representing an unmet need in musculoskeletal research.

To promote the effective utilization of mouse tissue samples from space missions, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) launched an open call for sample sharing in 2022. These tissue samples were obtained from mice exposed to lunar gravity conditions within the “Kibo” module, JAXA’s animal experiment facility onboard the International Space Station (ISS) (https://iss.jaxa.jp/en/kibo/). JAXA developed the multiple artificial-gravity research system (MARS), which employs a centrifuge to generate artificial gravity, enabling comparative studies on the effects of different gravity levels in space21. The samples analyzed in this study were derived from mice raised under a 1/6 Earth gravity environment created by the MARS, which closely mimics the gravitational conditions on the lunar surface. Previous studies using the MARS system have demonstrated the novel impact of lunar gravity on the transcriptome of skeletal muscle and bone, highlighting organ-specific variations in responsiveness to lunar gravity8,10.

Recently, we had the opportunity to analyze Achilles tendon samples from mice exposed to lunar gravity, as well as quadriceps femoris muscle and brown adipose tissue, provided by JAXA. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the comprehensive transcriptomic and morphological changes in Achilles tendons under lunar gravity compared with Earth gravity. To achieve this, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and histological analyses on Achilles tendons, quadriceps femoris muscle, and brown adipose tissue, highlighting tendon-specific responses to lunar gravity.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6 J male mice (9 weeks old) were used in the Mouse Habitat Unit (MHU)-4 and MHU-5 missions. Only male mice were selected to minimize potential confounding effects of sex-related biological variability. Nine-week-old mice were selected as young adults to provide stable baseline physiology across multiple organs analyzed in the MHU missions, with the present study focusing on the musculoskeletal tissues as part of this broader project. Mice for the space experiment were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), whereas those for the ground experiment were purchased from Charles River Laboratories Japan (Yokohama, Japan). All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA; Protocol Numbers: 018-011D for MHU-4 and 018-036D for MHU-5), Explora BioLabs (Study Numbers: EB19-003 for MHU-4 and SP19-003 for MHU-5), and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA; Protocol Numbers: FLT-18-118 for MHU-4 and JAXA MHU-5/FLT-19-121 for MHU-5). These experiments were conducted in full compliance with relevant guidelines and legal regulations in both Japan and the United States.

Experiments conducted in space and on Earth

To investigate the effects of low gravitational forces on mice, spaceflight missions MHU-4 and MHU-58 were conducted by JAXA (Fig. 1). In each mission, six male C57BL/6 J mice (9 weeks old) were launched aboard SpX-17 (MHU-4, May 7, 2019) and SpX-20 (MHU-5, March 10, 2020) from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center to the ISS, where they were housed for approximately one month. In both missions, the mice were maintained under partial gravity (1/6 g), simulating lunar gravity, using MARS21 (MARS-S in MHU-4 and MARS-L in MHU-5). The Earth gravity (1 g) control group consisted of age-matched mice (8–9 weeks old) housed under identical conditions on Earth. In both space missions, the mice were able to make contact with the bottom of their habitat cages using their forelimbs and hindlimbs under lunar gravity.

MARS, multiple artificial-gravity research system.

The spaceflight and ground control mice were maintained under identical housing conditions as closely as possible, except for the gravitational environment. Lighting in the habitat cage was designed to account for circadian rhythms and followed a 12 h light/dark cycle. The cage unit was equipped with an automatic water dispenser and feeder, enabling continuous access to water and food.

Upon return, they exhibited no visible abnormalities in their eyes, ears, teeth, fur, or tails. However, three mice in MHU-5 experienced significant weight loss due to an issue with the water nozzle in the transport cage unit and were excluded from further analysis (Supplementary Table 1). The extent of weight change in the lunar gravity groups was similar between MHU-4 and MHU-5 (Supplementary Table 1). A ground-based test confirmed that the weight loss observed in MHU-5 could be reproduced under similar conditions. Therefore, the final sample size was as follows: three mice per group were analyzed by RNA-seq in the MHU-4 mission, and five mice per group were evaluated histologically in both the MHU-4 and MHU-5 missions. The detailed mission protocols have been previously described8.

Sample collection and storage

The MHU-4 mission samples were processed in the United States in June 2019, and the MHU-5 mission samples in April 2020. All mice were euthanized by exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia on the second day after returning to Earth. Tissue samples were collected from the Achilles tendons, quadriceps femoris muscles, and brown adipose tissue surrounding the scapula. All tissue samples were stored at −80 °C. Initial sample handling differed according to tissue-specific RNA stability. Achilles tendon samples were immersed in RNAlater22 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) due to their low RNA yield and susceptibility to degradation, quadriceps femoris muscle samples were processed on dry ice as muscle RNA is relatively stable, and brown adipose tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen to minimize rapid RNA degradation associated with high lipid content. Samples from the Earth control group were prepared using the same method as those from the space experiments. In the MHU-4 mission, samples were processed in December 2019, while MHU-5 samples were processed in October 2020. The Achilles tendons, quadriceps femoris muscles, and brown adipose tissue from spaceflight and Earth control mice were provided to the University of Tokyo in March 2023 as part of JAXA’s Mouse Habitat Unit Biospecimen Sharing Project.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the Achilles tendons, quadriceps femoris muscles, and brown adipose tissue. These tissues were homogenized using zirconia beads in TRI Reagent (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan). Total RNA was then extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Kit (Zymo Research, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Regarding the Achilles tendons, both Achilles tendons from each mouse were pooled for RNA extraction, because the RNA yield from a single tendon was insufficient for RNA-seq. Therefore, the samples used for RNA-seq were not matched to those used for histological analyses.

Extracted RNA was used for transcriptome sequencing. Sequence libraries were prepared using the SMART-Seq mRNA (Takara Bio, Japan) and Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System using 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. The sequenced reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39/mm39), and gene expression counts along with transcripts per million values were calculated using DRAGEN version 4.3. iDEP23,24 version 2.01 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/idep/) was used for exploratory data analysis, differential gene expression analysis, and enrichment analysis.

Histological analyses of Achilles tendons

Achilles tendon samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin at room temperature overnight, followed by delipidation with 100% methanol at room temperature overnight, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut to a thickness of 4 μm for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed according to standard protocols. To quantify Achilles tendon fiber density on H&E-stained sections, the contour of the Achilles tendon was manually delineated as a region of interest (ROI), the tendon fiber area within the ROI was quantified, and tendon fiber density was calculated as the ratio of tendon fiber area to the total ROI using QuPath25 version 0.5.1. For collagen fiber visualization, Picrosirius Red staining was performed using a commercial kit (ScyTek Laboratories, PSR-1, Utah, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Collagen birefringence was examined under polarized light microscopy (BZ-X 1000; Keyence, Osaka, Japan), and the ratio of red/orange to green fibers was quantified using the cell count function of the accompanying analysis software (BZ-X 1000 Analyzer; Keyence, Osaka, Japan) to assess collagen fiber maturity. For immunohistochemistry, sections were treated with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in EDTA buffer at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit anti-ATP5B antibody (1:200 dilution; Proteintech, #17247-1-AP, Rosemont, IL, USA). Following a 30-min incubation with a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody, Histofine Simple Stain DAB (Nichirei Biosciences, #415172, Tokyo, Japan) was used to visualize positive signals.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as bar plots, with each value plotted as a dot. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (except for RNA-seq analysis, as explained earlier in this manuscript). For normally distributed data, an unpaired t-test was used to compare two groups. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Sample size determination was based on previous studies from the same spaceflight mission that performed RNA-seq and histological analyses of musculoskeletal tissues using comparable group sizes8,10. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using PRISM version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Pronounced transcriptomic changes in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity compared to muscles and adipose tissue

We first extracted RNA from the frozen tissue samples and validated the purity of the RNA samples (Table 1). We then performed RNA-seq analysis to examine transcriptomic differences between the Achilles tendon, quadriceps femoris muscle, and brown adipose tissue (n = 3 mice per tissue per group). Principal component analysis revealed that the quadriceps femoris muscle and brown adipose tissue samples were closely clustered in both the Earth and lunar gravity groups (Fig. 2a), indicating minimal transcriptomic deviation. In contrast, the Achilles tendon samples exhibited significant separation between the two groups (Fig. 2a), highlighting distinct transcriptomic changes under lunar gravity. Hierarchical clustering of the RNA-seq data showed that samples from the Earth and lunar gravity groups were clearly separated, indicating that this sample size (n = 3 mice per tissue per group) was sufficient to detect transcriptional changes (Fig. 2b). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed a marked upregulation of 1,433 genes in the Achilles tendons of the lunar gravity group compared with those of the Earth gravity group (Fig. 2c). The scatter plots visually corroborate this extensive upregulation, emphasizing the Achilles tendon’s heightened sensitivity to lunar gravity conditions compared to the other tissues analyzed (Fig. 2d).

Striking upregulation of mitochondrial energy production and downregulation of structural organization in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity

To evaluate the effects of lunar gravity on the Achilles tendons, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 3). In the Biological Process category, GO terms associated with energy metabolism, including oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, were markedly upregulated in the lunar gravity group compared to the Earth gravity group (Fig. 3). In contrast, GO terms associated with structural organization, including extracellular structure organization and cytoskeletal organization, were downregulated (Fig. 3). In the Cellular Component category, mitochondrial-related terms were prominently upregulated, whereas terms associated with the extracellular matrix and collagen fibers were downregulated (Fig. 3). In the Molecular Function category, terms associated with ATP synthase activity and oxidoreductase activity were upregulated, while terms related to collagen binding and extracellular matrix structural constituents were downregulated (Fig. 3). These findings collectively indicate that mitochondrial energy production was markedly increased under lunar gravity compared with Earth gravity, whereas Achilles tendon composition may have been impaired.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of Achilles tendons.

Using iDEP version 2.01, the top 10 upregulated and downregulated GO terms were analyzed for Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function, comparing the lunar gravity group to the Earth gravity group.

Unique upregulation of mitochondrial energy production-related genes in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity

To validate the results of the enrichment analysis, we examined DEGs associated with collagen fibers, extracellular matrix, inflammation, and ossification in the Achilles tendons of the lunar gravity and Earth gravity groups (Fig. 4a). Genes associated with collagen fiber organization (Col1a1, Col1a2, Col3a1, Col5a2) and extracellular matrix components (Dcn, Fmod, Comp) were significantly downregulated in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity (Fig. 4a). In contrast, matrix metalloproteinases (Mmp2, Mmp3, Mmp13), which mediate extracellular matrix remodeling, collagen turnover, and inflammatory responses, did not show significant changes (Fig. 4a). Genes associated with ossification (Sox9) and early fibrocartilaginous changes (Acan, Bgn, Has2) also did not show significant changes (Fig. 4a).

Genes upregulated in the lunar gravity group compared to the Earth gravity group are shown in red, unchanged genes in gray, and downregulated genes in blue (FDR cutoff: 0.1, minimum fold-change: 2).

Next, we examined DEGs associated with mitochondrial energy production in the Achilles tendons (Fig. 4b). Numerous genes related to ATP synthesis (Atp5d, Atp5e, Atp5j, Atp5b, Atp5h, Atp5pb, Atp5g1, Atp1a2, Atp1b4, Atp5g3, Atp5a1, Atp5c1, Atp1b1, Atpaf1, Atp2a2, Atp2b3, Atp5j2, Atp1b2, Atpaf2, Atp5g2, Atp5md, Atp5mpl, Atp6v0c, Atp6v1b2, Atp6v1g1, Atp5pb-ps), glycolysis (Aldoa, Hk2, Gapdh, Pfkm, Pgam2), and mitochondrial function (mt-Cytb) were significantly upregulated in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity (Fig. 4b). Notably, most of these mitochondrial energy production-related genes were not upregulated in the quadriceps femoris muscles (Fig. 4c) or brown adipose tissue (Fig. 4d) under lunar gravity. These findings support the enrichment analysis results, demonstrating that mitochondrial energy production was markedly and uniquely upregulated in the Achilles tendon exposed to lunar gravity.

Achilles tendon maintains fiber density but exhibits decreased mature collagen fibers after approximately one month under lunar gravity

We conducted a histological analysis of the Achilles tendon to further investigate these findings (n = 5 mice per group). First, we performed a quantitative assessment of tendon fiber density using H&E staining (Fig. 5a). A comparison between the two groups revealed no significant difference in tendon fiber density (P = 0.52) (Fig. 5b).

Next, we performed Picrosirius Red staining and quantitatively evaluated collagen fiber maturity under polarized light microscopy (Fig. 5c). The proportion of red/orange birefringence, representing more mature collagen fibers, relative to green birefringence, representing less mature collagen fibers, was compared between two groups (Fig. 5c). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant decrease in red/orange collagen fiber proportions under lunar gravity compared with Earth gravity (P = 0.001) (Fig. 5d).

Achilles tendon exhibits increased mitochondrial energy production after approximately one month under lunar gravity

We examined the protein expression levels of Atp5b, a core subunit of mitochondrial ATP synthase and a commonly used indicator of mitochondrial energy production26,27. In the RNA-seq analysis, Atp5b was identified as an upregulated DEG in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity compared to Earth gravity (Fig. 6a). Given that the Achilles tendon has been reported to exhibit region-specific transcriptomic signatures in response to mechanical loading16, immunostaining for Atp5b was analyzed separately in the proximal, middle, and distal regions (Fig. 6b). The proportions of Atp5b-positive cells were then compared between the Earth gravity and lunar gravity groups (Fig. 6c). Quantitative immunohistochemistry analysis revealed a significant increase in Atp5b-positive cell proportions under lunar gravity compared with Earth gravity across all three regions (proximal, P = 0.032; middle, P = 0.008; distal, P = 0.004) (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated unique transcriptomic changes in the Achilles tendons of mice exposed to lunar gravity, compared to muscle and adipose tissues. After approximately one month under lunar gravity, the Achilles tendons exhibited significantly more DEGs than the other two tissues. GO enrichment analyses revealed a significant upregulation of mitochondrial energy production and a downregulation of structural organization in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity. Histological analysis confirmed that tendon fiber density was unchanged, while mature collagen fibers were decreased. In addition, Atp5b expression was upregulated at the protein level in the Achilles tendons, suggesting increased mitochondrial energy production in tendon cells under lunar gravity. Notably, the upregulation of genes associated with mitochondrial energy production was not observed in muscle or adipose tissues. These findings suggest that the Achilles tendons exhibit a tendon-specific increase in mitochondrial energy production under lunar gravity, potentially associated with the observed reduction in mature collagen fibers.

To date, musculoskeletal research in space has primarily focused on bone and skeletal muscle. Previous studies using mouse samples from the JAXA MHU-4 and MHU-5 missions demonstrated lunar gravity-induced bone loss10 and muscle fiber-type shifts8. These established observations provide an important physiological context. The present study extends this framework by addressing how tendon responds at the transcriptomic level under lunar gravity.

In contrast to the clear structural changes observed in bones and muscles, the present study detected no obvious changes in Achilles tendon fiber density after approximately one month of exposure to lunar gravity. A previous study in patients with chronic spinal cord injury similarly reported that tendon cross-sectional area was preserved during the first 10 years and declined only after extremely long-term inactivity, such as 24 years28. In contrast, we observed a reduction in mature collagen fibers in the Achilles tendon after approximately one month under lunar gravity. Similarly, in a canine forepaw suspension model, collagen type I expression was not significantly altered at 21 days but was significantly decreased at 42 days29, indicating that the reduction in mature collagen at approximately one month in our study may reflect an early remodeling process. On the other hand, tendon biomechanical properties have been reported to change relatively early during unloading. For example, three weeks of hindlimb suspension in rats reduced Achilles tendon stiffness19, and an eight-week cast immobilization in rabbits similarly resulted in decreased tendon stiffness20. Taken together, these findings suggest that the reduction in mature collagen fibers observed in the present study may potentially contribute to impaired tendon stiffness under lunar gravity.

Previous in vitro and ex vivo studies of tendon loading have demonstrated that excessive mechanical loading can activate inflammatory pathways in tendon cells and may contribute to calcific changes associated with tendinopathy30. However, under the unloading condition of lunar gravity in the present study, transcriptional changes in genes primarily involved in inflammatory activation or ossification-related pathways were limited. This contrast suggests that tendon responses to unloading are not simply the inverse of those induced by mechanical overloading.

Notably, our findings demonstrate a marked upregulation of mitochondrial energy production in tendons under lunar gravity. Mitochondria are classically known as the powerhouse of the cell, producing ATP through oxidative phosphorylation31. Additionally, recent findings suggest that mitochondria play multiple roles, including maintaining redox homeostasis, managing metabolic waste, and generating biosynthetic precursors for macromolecules31. However, oxidative phosphorylation not only generates large amounts of energy but also produces significant levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can induce oxidative stress32,33. In tendinopathy, various mitochondrial abnormalities have been reported, including a decrease in the number of intracellular mitochondria and reduced expression of genes associated with mitochondrial iron transport, respiration, ATP synthase, and mitophagy34. In particular, sustained oxidative stress has been suggested to contribute to fibrosis and adhesion following acute tendon injury and degenerative tendinopathy35. Given these previous findings, it is speculative that prolonged enhancement of energy production under low-gravity conditions may potentially lead to tendon degeneration through excessive ROS, although tendon-specific mitochondrial roles remain unclear.

This study had some limitations. First, all data were obtained from frozen tissues provided by JAXA. Consequently, due to limited sample availability, our analyses were restricted to comprehensive transcriptomic profiling and basic histological examinations, and were unable to perform additional examinations, including evaluation of ROS markers, oxidative damage markers, or antioxidant responses, to determine how the increased mitochondrial energy production is used. Second, for similar reasons, the stiffness and strength of tendons could not be evaluated. Although no obvious changes in tendon fiber density were detected after approximately one month of exposure to lunar gravity, mature collagen fibers were decreased, suggesting that the biomechanical properties of tendons may have been affected. Third, although all samples were processed at the same time after return, storage conditions differed across tissue types due to mission-related constraints. Therefore, we could not exclude the possibility that differences in storage conditions may have influenced cross-tissue comparisons. Importantly, however, within each tissue, the lunar gravity and Earth gravity samples were stored under identical conditions, thereby minimizing potential bias in the within-tissue comparisons. Fourth, tissues were collected on the second day after returning to Earth. Therefore, skeletal muscle and brown adipose tissue may have recovered their transcriptomic profiles more rapidly than tendons, potentially contributing to the smaller number of DEGs observed in these tissues. Future time-course studies will be needed to clarify tissue-specific recovery dynamics after reloading.

In conclusion, this study revealed distinct transcriptomic changes in the Achilles tendons under lunar gravity, particularly the upregulation of genes associated with mitochondrial energy production. Notably, Atp5b protein expression was increased, while mature collagen fibers were decreased under lunar gravity. Although the biological significance of the increased mitochondrial energy production observed under lunar gravity remains unclear, tendon cells exhibit high sensitivity to gravitational changes, and their energy systems are likely regulated by mechanical loading. These findings contribute to a better understanding of tendon biology and may aid in the development of novel therapeutics for tendon disorders.
