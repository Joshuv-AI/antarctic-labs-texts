A Precise Performance Analysis of the Randomized Singular Value Decomposition
Danil Akhtiamov ∗, Reza Ghane ∗, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
The Randomized Singular Value Decomposition (RSVD) is a widely used algorithm for efficiently computing low-rank approximations of large matrices, without the need to construct a full-blown SVD. Of interest, of course, is the approximation error of RSVD compared to the optimal low-rank approximation error obtained from the SVD. While the literature provides various upper and lower error bounds for RSVD, in this paper we derive precise asymptotic expressions that characterize its approximation error as the matrix dimensions grow to infinity. Our expressions depend only on the singular values of the matrix, and we evaluate them for two important matrix ensembles: those with power law and bilevel singular value distributions. Our results aim to quantify the gap between the existing theoretical bounds and the actual performance of RSVD. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to polynomial-filtered RSVD, deriving performance characterizations that provide insights into optimal filter selection.

I
Introduction

The task of finding a low-rank approximation of a matrix lies at the cornerstone of many computational problems in data science and signal processing. For example, principal component analysis
[
7
]
, latent semantic indexing
[
2
]
and collaborative filtering
[
8
]
all fundamentally rely on extracting the dominant singular values and vectors from large-scale matrices. However, as modern datasets grow to unprecedented scales, traditional deterministic algorithms for computing the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) face prohibitive computational and memory constraints. Randomized Singular Value Decomposition (RSVD)
[
5
,
11
]
has emerged as a powerful alternative, leveraging randomized sampling techniques to compute decent low-rank approximations with dramatically reduced computational complexity. By exploiting the inherent low-rank structure present in many real-world matrices, RSVD achieves approximation errors comparable to deterministic methods, while reducing the time complexity from
O(dn\min(d,n))
to
O(dnk)
, where
n
and
d
are the dimensions of the matrix, and
k
is the rank of the approximation. In this work, we develop a novel analysis of RSVD performance and its polynomial-filtered generalizations using Gaussian Comparison Inequalities
[
3
,
4
]
. We derive exact asymptotic expressions for the approximation error that enable us to quantify the tightness of existing theoretical bounds and provide insights into optimal filter selection. Our precise characterizations not only illuminate the strengths and limitations of current theoretical guarantees but also enable us to improve upon existing upper bounds. By comparing our results with bounds from the literature, we provide a more complete picture of RSVD behavior in practice.

The core intuition behind Algorithm

, which is ”approximately rank

in Algorithm

As for Algorithm

acts as a spectral filter, amplifying dominant singular values while suppressing smaller ones. The choice of

II-B

Related Works

As mentioned in the abstract, the main advantage of the present paper is that its results are
tight
in the asymptotic regime. The purpose of this section is to elucidate the significance of our results compared to the existing results in the literature.

The seminal work of
[
5
]
established approximation error bounds for various randomized low-rank matrix approximations with respect to different matrix norms. More recently,
[
11
]
provided simplified analyses yielding cleaner bounds, that can be stated as follows in the case of the Frobenius norm:

Theorem 1

(Theorem 8.1 in
[
11
]
)
.

Denote the output of Algorithm
1
for input
\mathbf{A}
and Gaussian
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
by
\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})
and consider an arbitrary
r<k-1
. It holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})\|_{F}^{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{r}{k-r-1}\right)\sum_{j>r}\sigma_{j}^{2}

The work of
[
12
]
approached the problem from a different angle, establishing
worst-case
lower bounds. We do not quote the exact statement from
[
12
]
to avoid confusion, as the approximation error in
[
12
]
is measured with respect to the
operator norm
. However, we believe that their results can be transferred to the case of the Frobenius norm with minor adjustments. To give a flavor of their main result, they constructed examples of matrices
\mathbf{A}
, for which the bounds from
[
5
]
match the actual performance up to a multiplicative factor of
1-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
. This result demonstrates that the upper bounds of
[
5
]
cannot be improved for all
\mathbf{A}
simultaneously.

Our results differ from these prior works in several ways:

1.

Asymptotic exactness vs. upper bounds
: While previous works provide upper bounds (and matching lower bounds in worst case), we establish asymptotic equivalences.

2.

Full spectral characterization
: The bound in Theorem
1
depends only on the tail sum
\sum_{j>r}\sigma_{j}^{2}
. In contrast, our implicit characterization involves all singular values
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
, capturing how the entire spectral distribution affects the approximation error. This gives a more nuanced picture: two matrices with the same tail sum can have different approximation errors depending on their full spectra.

3.

Unified framework for filtered variants
: While previous analyses treat different algorithmic variants separately, Theorem
3
provides a unified framework. By introducing polynomial filters
\chi
, we can analyze many algorithms simultaneously.

4.

Instance-specific vs. worst-case analysis
: The lower bounds of
[
12
]
are worst-case results. Our results are instance-specific, providing the exact asymptotic error for any given singular value distribution.

III
Main Results

We would like to derive asymptotic predictions for the performance of the RSVD. This requires the following definitions:

Definition 1

(RSVD Admissible Sequence)
.

We call an RSVD admissible sequence any sequence of the form
\{(n_{i},d_{i},m_{i},k_{i},\mathbf{A}^{(i)})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mathbf{A}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times d_{i}}
and
k_{i}\leq\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}^{(i)})=m_{i}
are such that
k_{i}\to\infty
. We will denote the non-zero singular values of
\mathbf{A}^{(i)}
by
\sigma_{1}^{{i}},\dots,\sigma_{m_{i}}^{{i}}
.

Definition 2

(Asymptotic Equivalence)
.

Given two sequences
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{b_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we say that they are asymptotically equivalent if
\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}=1
. We denote this by
a_{i}\sim b_{i}
.

For notational brevity, we shall henceforth drop the index
i
and write
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
to denote a generic element of the ensemble, whose singular values are
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}
, with the understanding that all statements refer to the asymptotic regime as the matrix dimensions grow. We will drop the index
i
from the dimensions too.

III-A

Main Theorems

In this section we characterize precisely the approximation error of RSVD with and without polynomial filters.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic Performance of RSVD)
.

Let
(n,d,m,k,\mathbf{A})
be an RSVD admissible sequence. Define
\tilde{\Theta}
as the unique positive solution of

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma^{2}_{j}}{\tilde{\Theta}+k\sigma^{2}_{j}}=1

Denote the output of Algorithm
1
with a Gaussian matrix
\bm{\Omega}
applied to
\mathbf{A}
by
\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})
. Then the following asymptotic equivalence holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})\|_{F}^{2}\sim\tilde{\Theta}

We require the following formal definition of the polynomial filters.

Definition 3

(Polynomial filters)
.

For the purposes of the present work, a filter is an arbitrary polynomial
\chi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Given a matrix
\mathbf{X}
with SVD decomposition
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}
, we define
\chi(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{U}\chi(\bm{\Sigma})\mathbf{V}
, where
\chi(\bm{\Sigma})
is applied element wise to each element of the diagonal.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(n,d,m,k,\mathbf{A})
be an RSVD admissible sequence and
\chi
be a filter. Define
\theta_{0}
and
\tilde{\Theta}
as the unique positive numbers satisfying:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{1+\theta_{0}\chi(\sigma_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=m-k

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{1+\theta_{0}\chi(\sigma_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}

Denote the output of Algorithm
2
with a Gaussian matrix
\bm{\Omega}
applied to
\mathbf{A}
by
\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})
. Then the following asymptotic equivalence holds :

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})\|_{F}^{2}\sim\tilde{\Theta}

Remark 1
.

It should be noted that the expression we get for the approximation error for
\chi(x)=x
in Theorem
3
is not the same as the expression resulting from Theorem
2
. It can actually be shown that the resulting expressions are equivalent, but we prefer to keep the current form of Theorem
2
, as the corresponding equation appears simpler.

III-B

Implications

Define

\displaystyle C=C(k,r,m):=\frac{(m-k)k}{(m-r)(k-r)}

The following propositions give asymptotic upper bounds for the approximation error of the RSVD in terms of an arbitrary
r<k
. Note that these bounds are marginally better than Theorem
1
since
r<k
.

Proposition 1
.

In terms of the notation from Theorem
2
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\sigma_{j}^{2}\leq\tilde{\Theta}\leq C\sum_{j=r+1}^{m}\sigma_{j}^{2}

Note that the lower bound in Proposition
1
follows readily from Eckart-Young theorem. The following result provides an asymptotic upper bound for the approximation error of arbitrary polynomial filters.

Proposition 2
.

In terms of the notation from Theorem
3
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Theta}\leq\sum_{j=r+1}^{m}\sigma_{j}^{2}+\frac{C}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\chi(\sigma_{j})^{2}}\cdot\sum_{j=r+1}^{m}\chi(\sigma_{j})^{2}

It turns out, in the case of the Bilevel Ensemble and the Power law Ensemble, the asymptotic error and the structure of an optimal filter can characterized explicitly as follows.

Corollary 1
.

•

(Bilevel Ensemble).
Assume that

\sigma_{1}=\dots=\sigma_{r}=a
and
\sigma_{r+1}=\dots=\sigma_{m}=b
, with
a>b
. Then the approximation error would be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})\|_{F}^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2}\Bigl((r-k)a^{2}+(m-r-k)b^{2}+

\displaystyle\sqrt{((r-k)a^{2}+(m-r-k)b^{2})^{2}+4a^{2}b^{2}(m-k)}\Bigr)

In this setting for
k\geq r+1
, the optimal polynomial filter is of form
\chi(\cdot):=(\cdot)^{q}
where
q\rightarrow\infty
and yields an error of
(m-k)b^{2}
, the fundamental lower bound from Proposition
1
.

•

(Power law Ensemble).
Assume that
\sigma_{i}=i^{-\alpha}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\Omega})\|_{F}^{2}\sim\frac{1}{\Bigl(k\text{sinc}(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha})\Bigr)^{2\alpha}}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{where }\text{sinc}(x)=\frac{\sin(x)}{x}

In this setting,
\chi(\cdot):=(\cdot)^{q}
where
q\rightarrow\infty
yields an error of
\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}i^{-2\alpha}
, the fundamental lower bound from Proposition
1
.

Remark 2
.

For the bilevel ensemble, the upper bound in Theorem
1
yields

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{k-r-1}(m-r)b^{2}

(3)

whereas, assuming
k=o(m)
and
\frac{a}{b}=\Theta(1)
, (
1
) asymptotically equals

(m-k)b^{2}

If the difference
k-r
is constant, we conclude that our prediction (
1
) is tighter than (
3
) by a factor of
\Theta(k)
.

Remark 3
.

For the power law ensemble, the upper bound in Theorem
1
yields approximately

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{k-r-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2\alpha-1}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{2\alpha-1}}

(4)

The bound (
4
) is
\Theta(k^{2})
- fold greater than (
2
) if the oversampling
k-r
is constant and
\Theta(k)
- fold greater if
r
is a finite fraction of
k
.

Our Approach

IV-A

Variational reformulation

The following key lemma provides a variation formulation for the RSVD algorithm which allows us to apply Gaussian Comparison Inequalities
[
3
,
4
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\chi
be a polynomial filter and assume that
\mathrm{rank}(\chi(\mathbf{A})\bm{\Omega})=k
under the notation of Algorithm
2
. Then the output of Algorithm
2
can be found as
\hat{\mathbf{A}}=\chi(\mathbf{A})\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}
, where
\mathbf{B}
is defined uniquely via the following objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}\|\mathbf{A}-\chi(\mathbf{A})\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}

When
\chi(x)=x
, this description captures the output of Algorithm
1
.

Remark 4
.

Define the approximation error of Algorithm
2
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}

(5)

We see from Lemma
1
that (
5
) equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\min_{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}\|\mathbf{A}-\chi(\mathbf{A})\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}

IV-B

Proof of Theorem
2

We present the proof of Theorem
2
below. The proof of Theorem
3
follows the same steps and is omitted here due to the lack of space.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Consider the truncated SVD decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{V},\text{ where }m=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A}),\;\bm{\Sigma}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\quad\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{m}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{T}

Due to rotational invariance of the Frobenius norm we have:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{U}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{U}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{\Sigma}(\mathbf{V}\bm{\Omega})(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{T})\|_{F}^{2}

Noting that
\mathbf{V}\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
is also i.i.d. standard Gaussian and
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{V})=m
, we redefine
\bm{\Omega}:=\mathbf{V}\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
,
\mathbf{B}:=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{T}
and arrive at the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}\min_{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}}\|\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}

Introducing the Fenchel dual variable
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
for the quadratic loss
\|\cdot\|_{F}^{2}
we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}

(6)

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}}\max_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}}\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{C}^{T}\left(\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\right)\right)-\frac{\|\mathbf{C}\|_{F}^{2}}{4}

Denote the optimization variables

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{m}),\quad\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{m}),\quad\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

Expression (
6
) decomposes as:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}\min_{\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}}}\max_{\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{T}\left(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}_{i}\right)-\frac{\|\mathbf{C}_{i}\|^{2}}{4}

Define the constraint sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}}:=\Bigl\{\mathbf{B}_{i}:\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}-\sqrt{k}}\frac{\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{A})}{\sigma_{m}(\mathbf{A})}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}}:=\{\mathbf{C}_{i}:\|\mathbf{C}_{i}\|\leq\sigma_{i}\}

Consider the following pair of optimizations:

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(\bm{\Omega}):=\max_{\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}}}\min_{\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}}}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}_{i}+\sigma_{i}\mathbf{C}_{ii}-\frac{\|\mathbf{C}_{i}\|^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}):=\max_{\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}}}\min_{\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{g}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\|+\mathbf{B}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}\|\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{C}_{i}\|

\displaystyle+\sigma_{i}\mathbf{C}_{ii}-\frac{\|\mathbf{C}_{i}\|^{2}}{4}

(7)

We further define
\phi^{HW}_{i}(\mathbf{h}):=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{1+\theta_{\mathbf{h}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}
and
c_{i}:=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{1+\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}}
for
i\in[m]
where
\theta_{\mathbf{h}}
and
\theta
are defined via

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{1+\theta_{\mathbf{h}}\sigma_{j}^{2}}

\displaystyle=m-\|\mathbf{h}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{1+\theta\sigma_{j}^{2}}

\displaystyle=m-k

Going back to (
6
), we observe that it can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}\min_{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}}\|\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\max_{\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\min_{\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}_{i}+\sigma_{i}\mathbf{C}_{ii}-\frac{\|\mathbf{C}_{i}\|^{2}}{4}

From Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}\max_{\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\min_{\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{T}\bm{\Omega}\mathbf{B}_{i}+\sigma_{i}\mathbf{C}_{ii}-\frac{\|\mathbf{C}_{i}\|^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}\Phi_{i}(\bm{\Omega})

Indeed, according to Lemma
2
, the optimal
\mathbf{C}_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}_{i}
belong in the sets
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{C}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}}
with probability approaching
1
as
m\to\infty
and the objective is bounded outside of these sets according to the same lemma. In Lemma
3
located in the Appendix, we prove that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}}\Phi_{i}(\bm{\Omega})-c_{i}\right|\leq 2\left|\mathbb{E}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})-c_{i}\right|

We then proceed to apply the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle 2\left|\mathbb{E}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})-c_{i}\right|\leq

\displaystyle 2|\mathbb{E}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})-\mathbb{E}\phi^{HW}_{i}(\mathbf{h})|

\displaystyle\quad+2\left|\mathbb{E}\phi^{HW}_{i}(\mathbf{h})-c_{i}\right|

After that, we prove that
\mathbb{E}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\sim\mathbb{E}\phi^{HW}_{i}(\mathbf{h})
and
\mathbb{E}\phi^{HW}_{i}(\mathbf{h})\sim c_{i}
in the Appendix, in Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
respectively. Hence, it suffices to prove that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=\tilde{\Theta}
. The latter is a straightforward algebra verification:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{1+\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)=\frac{k}{\theta}

It only remains to check that
\tilde{\Theta}=\frac{k}{\theta}
, which is true since, by definition of
\theta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\frac{k}{\theta}+k\sigma_{i}^{2}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}}{1+\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)=1

∎

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

We have derived exact asymptotic characterizations for RSVD performance that capture the full spectral structure of the data matrix and naturally extend to polynomial-filtered variants. Our results complement existing literature by bridging the gap between worst-case guarantees and actual performance: while prior work establishes what can be guaranteed in general, our analysis reveals what actually occurs for specific problem instances in the large-scale limit.
Our approach opens several avenues for future research. The framework developed here can be extended to investigate other properties of RSVD approximation error, including finite-sample corrections to our asymptotic results and the universality of approximation error across different choices of sketching matrices
\bm{\Omega}
. These directions would further enhance our understanding of RSVD behavior beyond the asymptotic regime and across diverse randomization strategies.
