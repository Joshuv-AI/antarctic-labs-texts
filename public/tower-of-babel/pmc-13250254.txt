Abdominal Wall Desmoid Tumor Misdiagnosed as Schwannoma: A Case Report
Authors: Faten Limaiem, Mohamed Hajri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present the case of a 35‐year‐old woman with an abdominal wall mass initially diagnosed radiologically as a schwannoma. Histopathology revealed desmoid fibromatosis through β‐catenin positivity, despite no trauma or surgical history. Complete excision with negative margins was curative, highlighting immunohistochemistry's diagnostic value and surgical efficacy.Keywords: abdominal wall, case report, desmoid tumor, immunohistochemistry, pathology, soft tissue

Introduction

Desmoid fibromatosis (DF) is a rare, locally aggressive fibroblastic tumor with infiltrative growth and frequent recurrence despite lacking metastatic potential [1, 2, 3]. It mainly affects women in their 30s, commonly involving the abdominal wall or extra‐abdominal soft tissues [1, 2]. DF is associated with Wnt/β‐catenin pathway dysregulation from CTNNB1 or APC mutations, particularly in Gardner syndrome–related intra‐abdominal cases.

Case Presentation

A 35‐year‐old gravida 2 para 2 woman with no significant medical or surgical history presented with an eight‐month history of a progressively enlarging painful mass in the left hypochondrium. She denied trauma, weight loss, or systemic symptoms. Examination revealed a firm, tender left hypochondrial mass without skin changes or lymphadenopathy, and laboratory tests were unremarkable. Clinically, a soft tissue tumor such as schwannoma or neurofibroma was initially suspected. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a heterogeneous hypoechoic subcutaneous parietal mass. MRI of the abdominal wall showed a fusiform mass measuring 2.5 × 4.5 cm, arising from the external oblique muscle. The lesion was hyperintense on T1‐ and T2‐weighted sequences, especially with fat suppression (Figures 1 and 2). Diffusion‐weighted imaging showed high signal with mild ADC restriction. Following gadolinium injection, the mass exhibited intense contrast enhancement with moderate peripheral ring enhancement, without infiltration into adjacent fat, deeper muscles, or intra‐abdominal structures. A schwannoma was initially suspected. The mass was surgically excised. Grossly, it measured 5.5 × 4.5 × 2 cm and appeared firm and pearly white with a fasciculated texture (Figure 3). Microscopic examination showed a poorly circumscribed, non‐encapsulated spindle cell proliferation infiltrating skeletal muscle, with minimal atypia and a mitotic index of 4 per 10 high‐power fields (Figure 4A,B). Numerous thin‐walled vessels with surrounding perivascular edema were noted (Figure 4C). Immunohistochemistry revealed strong nuclear β‐catenin expression (Figure 4D), while tumor cells were negative for S100 protein and SMA. The tumor was completely excised with negative margins. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. At 2 months, the patient was asymptomatic with no recurrence. Owing to the risk of local relapse, she remains under clinical and imaging follow‐up every 6 months.

Conclusion

Abdominal wall desmoid fibromatosis presents a diagnostic challenge, with differentials including scar tissue, Gardner fibroma, nodular fasciitis, proliferative fasciitis/myositis, myofibroma/myofibromatosis, calcifying fibrous pseudotumor, solitary fibrous tumor, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, leiomyoma, nerve sheath tumors, fibrosarcoma, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, low‐grade myofibroblastic sarcoma, and low‐grade fibromyxoid sarcoma.

Unlike schwannomas, which show well‐defined margins and characteristic MRI signs, desmoid fibromatosis typically exhibits ill‐defined, infiltrative borders reflecting its locally aggressive behavior. Diagnosis relies on histopathology and β‐catenin immunohistochemistry. Management is multidisciplinary and individualized: indolent lesions may be observed, whereas progressive or symptomatic tumors require complete excision with negative margins, often with mesh repair. Systemic therapy, radiotherapy, or targeted agents are options for unresectable or recurrent cases.
