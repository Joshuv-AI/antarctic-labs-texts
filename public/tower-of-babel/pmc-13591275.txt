State-level paid sick leave: impacts of policy design on access by immigrant workers
Authors: Zhenyi Luo, Aleta Sprague, Michael McCormack, Jody Heymann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccess to paid sick days (PSDs) matters deeply to public health and workers’ economic security. State-level PSD laws have expanded rapidly, yet little is known about whether they are closing coverage gaps for immigrant workers, who are both overrepresented in public-facing jobs and disproportionately excluded from employer-provided PSD.MethodsWe built a state-level database of eligibility rules in all 15 states and DC with statewide PSD laws in effect as of 2024 and linked them to labor force data from the 2024 Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement. We assessed how exclusions based on independent contractor status, firm size, minimum weekly hours, and annual work requirements shaped PSD coverage differences between citizens and noncitizens.ResultsOverall, coverage was lower among noncitizens than citizens (84.0% vs 86.9%, P < .05). Significant disparities were identified in 6 jurisdictions, with the largest gap in Connecticut (49.3% vs 65.8%). Policy simulations showed that excluding independent contractors and small-firm employees were primary drivers of disparities. Removing these exclusions narrowed gaps and sometimes resulted in near-universal coverage.ConclusionRemoving exclusions from state-level PSD policies would reduce inequities and increase access for immigrant workers, with benefits for all.Keywords: paid sick days, immigrant workers, health equity, social determinants of health

Introduction

The United States is one of just 9 countries in the world without paid sick leave.1 Despite a wealth of research demonstrating that paid sick leave reduces the spread of illness,2,3 decreases turnover and presenteeism,4 increases access to preventive care,5-7 protects households from job and income loss,8 and reduces mortality,9 the United States stands alone among its economic peers in failing to provide this basic guarantee.10

In the absence of a federal policy, major gaps and disparities persist in access to paid sick leave, which is largely at the discretion of employers. Among private sector workers, 94% in the top income quartile, compared to just 58% in the bottom, have access to any paid sick days.11 Workers in certain occupations are also disproportionately likely to lack paid sick days. For example, just 65% of service workers have paid sick days, alongside just 69% of workers in construction, extraction, farming, fishing, and forestry occupations.11

These income and occupational disparities in access to employer-provided sick days map onto disparities in leave access by race/ethnicity and citizenship status. While immigrants hold a wide range of occupations in the United States, they are overrepresented in service and construction jobs,12 and the National Health Interview Survey has long found substantially lower access to paid sick days among immigrant workers compared to non-immigrants.13,14 Studies dating back decades have also found that Latinx workers, who represent the largest share of the immigrant workforce in the United States,12 report the lowest rates of paid sick leave across racial/ethnic groups.15-17

Inequitable access to leave in turn leads to inequitable health and economic outcomes. Studies examining which workers faced the highest risks at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic make this clear. In California, Latino men working in food and agriculture experienced the highest relative increase in mortality from March to November 2020 compared to workers from other racial groups or in other occupations.18 A separate study found that excess mortality rates among Latino men in California would have been 10% lower if Latino men had the same income and occupational distribution as White men.19 Along with being less likely to have leave,17 Latinx workers were overrepresented in jobs that could not be done remotely.20

In addition to the consequences for workers themselves, gaps in access to paid sick days affect clients, customers, patients, and others with whom workers interact. Paid sick leave has been shown to lower transmission of contagious illnesses like COVID-19 and the flu, both within workplaces and across broader communities.21,2,3,22 However, the exclusion of broad groups of workers from access to paid sick days, particularly when those workers are overrepresented in public-facing or caregiving roles, curtails these benefits.

In recent years, states and cities have increasingly been taking action to enact their own paid sick days policies. As of 2026, 18 states and 18 cities and counties have passed paid sick days, and active campaigns are underway elsewhere.23 Typically, these policies allow workers to accrue job-protected paid sick leave by the hour in a set proportion to the total hours they work, up to an annual maximum—commonly between 40 and 72 hours. Workers are eligible to use their paid sick days both to meet their own health needs and to care for a sick child or other family member. However, the specific design of these policies may shape the extent to which they cover workers who have been disproportionately left out through the employer-paid leave model. Similar to state approaches to paid family and medical leave, some states with paid sick days limit eligibility based on weekly hours worked or employer size. All states also exclude independent contractors. Past research has found that minimum firm size requirements for paid and unpaid family and medical leave disproportionately exclude Latinx workers, while minimum hours requirements leave out more women.24-26

However, no prior studies have examined to what extent specific features of policy design shape access to state-level paid sick days by immigrant households. This study fills that gap, assessing how policy features including the exclusion of independent contractors, minimum firm size requirements, and minimum weekly hours requirements affect access by noncitizens. While all states that have passed paid sick days have expanded coverage overall, this study provides the first analysis of whether these expansions have also reduced structural disparities in coverage by citizenship.

Data and methods

This study involved 2 main steps. First, we built a state-level database capturing statutory exclusion criteria in statewide paid sick days laws in all states with such laws in effect by the end of 2024. Second, we linked these policy rules to individual-level labor force data to assess how state exclusion criteria affected paid sick days coverage, with a particular focus on disparities between citizens and noncitizens.

Policy data and coding

Our analysis included the 15 states and the District of Columbia that had enacted statewide paid sick days laws by the end of 2024: Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Washington. The end date of 2024 was chosen because 2025 labor force characteristics data were not yet available at the time of analysis. We restricted the selection of states to those with statewide paid sick days laws. Paid sick days laws provide paid leave for workers to take short-term leave for their own or a family member's routine illness or to obtain medical care, including preventive care.27 We excluded jurisdictions that were only covered by other types of leave policies. These included paid family and medical leave, which provides wage replacement during extended leave for qualifying family or medical reasons;27 paid time off, which provides paid leave that may be used for a broad range of purposes, including illness, emergencies, and vacation;27 and unpaid leave, including leave provided under the Family and Medical Leave Act, which provides job-protected leave without wage replacement for qualifying family or medical reasons.27

State legislation was sourced from both government websites and secondary references from Family Values at Work and A Better Balance.23,28 Two independent coders built a paid sick days database by reviewing primary source state legislation. From the eligibility sections of each state's paid sick days statute, we extracted threshold requirements for firm size, minimum hours worked per week, minimum weeks worked per year, and exclusions of independent contractors. Features of the laws were verified by comparing the 2 researchers’ independent coding and cross-checking coding decisions against available primary and secondary data from Family Values at Work and secondary data from A Better Balance.

Data sources on labor force characteristics

Labor force characteristics were obtained from the Current Population Survey (CPS), a monthly survey conducted by the United States Census Bureau for the Bureau of Labor Statistics.29 The CPS is designed to be nationally representative of the US civilian, noninstitutionalized population across all 50 states and the District of Columbia. For this analysis, we used the Annual Social and Economic Supplement (ASEC) to the CPS, which is administered each March and collects more extensive information on households and topics than the basic monthly survey. The ASEC includes detailed measures on demographics, work experience, income, poverty, health insurance, and migration.30

We analyzed data from the 2024 ASEC to correspond to the policy period examined in this study. Our analytic sample was restricted to adults aged 18-64 working in the private sector (n = 17 050) or self-employed (n = 2097) residing in the 15 states and the District of Columbia with paid sick days laws that had taken effect by 2024.

Variables/measures

Demographics

Citizenship was obtained from a 5-category item (Native, born in the United States; Native, born in Puerto Rico or a US outlying area; Native, born abroad of US parent(s); Foreign born, US citizen by naturalization; and Foreign born, not a US citizen) and recoded into a binary measure, with the first 4 categories coded as “citizen” and the last category coded as “noncitizen.”

Policy eligibility criteria

To assess whether respondents met state-specific paid sick days firm size requirements, we used the question: “Counting all locations where this employer operates, what is the total number of persons who work for the respondent's employer?”. To assess whether respondents met minimum weekly hours requirements, we used the question: “How many hours per week does the respondent usually work at this job?”. To assess whether respondents met minimum annual weeks worked requirements, we used the question: “In how many weeks did the respondent work, even for a few hours? (Include paid vacation and sick leave as work)”. Independent contractors were identified from the “Individual class of worker” variable, with those reporting “self-employed, not incorporated” classified as independent contractors.

Analysis

We implemented a multi-stage analytic strategy to assess differences in paid sick days coverage and to evaluate the potential impact of policy changes.

Pooled analysis and state-specific analysis

A binary indicator of exclusion from paid sick days coverage was constructed based on each state's statutory provisions. Individuals were coded as excluded if they failed to meet firm-size requirements, minimum hours worked per week, minimum weeks worked per year, or if they were classified as independent contractors. We pooled data across the 15 states and the District of Columbia to assess overall inequities in paid sick days coverage by citizenship status, using Pearson chi-square tests to evaluate statistical significance. State-specific analyses were also conducted using Pearson chi-square tests to determine whether differences were present within individual jurisdictions.

Policy simulations

For states with statistically significant disparities, we simulated counterfactual scenarios by sequentially removing statutory barriers through deterministic recoding (eg, reclassifying workers who failed firm-size thresholds, independent contractor status, or minimum hours or weeks requirements as eligible). After each scenario, we recalculated group-specific coverage and re-tested disparities. A scenario was considered to have “significantly eliminated” the disparity when group contrasts were no longer statistically significant (χ2 P ≥ .05). All analyses were conducted in Stata 17, with 2-sided statistical tests and significance defined as P < .05.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted 2 sensitivity analyses. First, because the CPS ASEC question on annual weeks worked (“In how many weeks did the respondent work, even for a few hours?”) does not distinguish whether those weeks were worked for a single employer or across multiple employers, respondents who appeared to satisfy state minimum annual work requirements could potentially be misclassified if their weeks were accumulated across different employers. To assess this possibility, we used the CPS ASEC question that records the number of employers a respondent worked for during the reference year and repeated all analyses after restricting the sample to respondents who reported working for only one employer. Second, policy simulations were conducted in jurisdictions without statistically significant citizenship disparities to assess whether removing statutory eligibility restrictions could increase overall coverage without creating new citizenship disparities.

Pooling all 16 jurisdictions, a statistically significant disparity in coverage was observed by citizenship status. Overall coverage was 86.9% among citizens and 84.0% among noncitizens. Firm-size thresholds were another important mechanism. In Connecticut and Massachusetts, removal of the firm-size threshold substantially reduced the citizenship gap, and group differences were no longer statistically significant. In contrast, removing work-duration requirements increased overall coverage but did not significantly reduce citizenship-based disparities. In California and Vermont, where minimum weekly hours requirements (VT) and minimum annual days requirements (VT and CA) were in place, removing either requirement reduced the gap and narrowed the disparity, but differences remained statistically significant.

Given that exclusion of independent contractors was the most common statutory contributor to observed disparities, we further examined whether there were differences in the proportion of independent contractors who were citizens, as well as whether the jurisdictions had more independent contractors overall. The overall proportion of independent contractors was below 10% in all 16 jurisdictions. Significant differences in independent contractor status by citizenship were observed in Arizona (12.1% vs 6.3%, P = .015), California (9.4% vs 7.2%, P = .011), the District of Columbia (10.5% vs 3.3%, P = .006), and Vermont (21.7% vs 8.9%, P = .038), all of which were among the jurisdictions with statistically significant citizenship disparities in paid sick days coverage.

By contrast, Oregon had one of the highest overall proportions of independent contractors (8.2%), but independent contractor status did not differ significantly by citizenship, nor was there a statistically significant citizenship disparity in paid sick days coverage (74.6% vs 71.4%). These findings suggest that citizenship disparities were closely related to differences in independent contractor status between citizens and noncitizens within a jurisdiction.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis 1: restricting the sample to respondents with one employer

To evaluate the potential misclassification arising from respondents who worked for multiple employers during the reference year, we repeated all analyses after restricting the sample to respondents who reported working for only one employer during the reference year, representing 89.3% of the original analytic sample. The conclusions presented above remained unchanged.

Overall paid sick days coverage changed only slightly, from 86.5% in the primary analysis to 86.6% in the sensitivity analysis (Table S1). Connecticut and Michigan remained the jurisdictions with the lowest overall coverage rates. Four of the 6 jurisdictions with statistically significant citizenship disparities in the primary analysis remained statistically significant, while the California estimate became marginally significant (P = .059), which may reflect the reduced sample size in the sensitivity analysis.

Results from the policy simulations were similar to those in the primary analysis (Table S2). Inclusion of independent contractors continued to eliminate citizenship disparities in Arizona, the District of Columbia, and Vermont, and removal of the firm-size threshold continued to eliminate disparities in Connecticut. In Vermont, removing the part-time requirement or part-year requirement continued to increase coverage but did not eliminate the citizenship disparity.

Sensitivity analysis 2: policy simulations in jurisdictions without statistically significant citizenship disparities

As a second sensitivity analysis, we conducted policy simulations in the 10 jurisdictions without statistically significant citizenship disparities to examine whether removing statutory eligibility restrictions could increase coverage while creating new citizenship disparities (Table S3).

In 9 of the 10 jurisdictions, removing statutory eligibility restrictions increased coverage without creating statistically significant citizenship disparities. The exceptions were 2 hypothetical interventions in Maryland. Removing the firm-size threshold or the minimum weekly hours requirement increased coverage for both citizens and noncitizens but resulted in statistically significant citizenship disparities because coverage increased more for citizens than for noncitizens.

To better understand these findings, we examined the distribution of workers affected by the Maryland eligibility restrictions (data not shown). Removing the firm-size threshold disproportionately benefited noncitizens because a significantly larger proportion of noncitizens worked for employers with fewer than 15 employees than citizens. In contrast, removing the minimum weekly hours requirement disproportionately benefited citizens because a significantly larger proportion of citizens worked fewer than 12 hours per week than noncitizens. Consistent with the findings for independent contractor exclusions in the section “Policy simulations: citizenship disparities,” these results suggest that the impact of changes in eligibility restrictions on citizenship disparities depends on how the affected workers are distributed between citizens and noncitizens.

Discussion

While paid sick leave is necessary for all workers, it is especially critical in the types of public-facing and physically demanding occupations in which immigrant workers are overrepresented. We find that across states, noncitizens are less likely to have access to statutory paid sick days than citizens. Although the overall difference in eligibility between citizens and noncitizens was modest (3 percentage points), in some states, larger disparities existed, which exceeded 15 percentage points in 2 states.

The most common policy features creating these differences and disparities were the exclusion of independent contractors, found in all states with paid sick days, and the exclusion of workers based on firm size, found in 6 states. When these eligibility rules were eliminated, gaps in access between citizens and noncitizens substantially narrowed or disappeared, with some states reaching near-universal coverage. For example, in Connecticut, removing the firm-size threshold (50 employees) eliminated the 16-percentage-point gap in eligibility between citizens and noncitizens, and increased coverage for both groups to roughly 92%. In Arizona, including independent contractors closed a 6-percentage-point gap and resulted in nearly universal coverage.

These findings also help explain why Connecticut and Michigan had the lowest overall coverage levels. Connecticut and Michigan had the highest firm-size threshold (50 employees) among the jurisdictions included in this study, whereas all other states with firm-size thresholds used substantially lower cutoffs. In Connecticut, this high threshold not only reduced overall coverage but also contributed to the large citizenship disparity because noncitizens in Connecticut were less likely than citizens to work for employers meeting the 50-employee threshold. Michigan's low coverage likely reflects the combined effects of multiple eligibility restrictions. Unlike any other jurisdiction in our analysis, Michigan simultaneously imposed all 4 statutory restrictions considered in this study: minimum weekly hours, minimum annual work requirements, a firm-size threshold, and exclusion of independent contractors.

These findings make clear that addressing citizenship-based disparities in paid sick days access is feasible, and previous literature demonstrates that broadening access to paid sick days matters to health equity, quality of work, and reducing disease spread for all. Alongside supporting access to care and recovery when a worker is physically ill, paid sick days have been shown to increase access to preventive services such as cholesterol testing and cancer screenings,31,32 to improve workers’ mental health,33 to reduce emergency department visits,34 and to improve access to both medical and dental care for children.35,36 Moreover, both through these broad public health benefits and by reducing the spread of illnesses like influenza, increasing noncitizens’ access to paid sick days has marked benefits for citizens as well, given noncitizens’ overrepresentation in caregiving and service sector roles that involve substantial interaction with other people. States’ enactment of their own paid sick day policies has substantially increased access nationwide. Nevertheless, inequities in coverage remain, and this study identifies steps states could consider taking to help close these gaps.

Although the specific changes that would most substantially reduce inequities varied across states, the exclusion of independent contractors was the most consistent statutory source of citizenship-based disparities. Insurance-based portable benefits systems, which businesses contracting with the self-employed can pay into, show potential as a mechanism for closing gaps in access between independent contractors and other workers.37 At the same time, it is important to distinguish between “portable benefits” tied to specific gig employers—which are essentially just savings accounts and have in some cases been paired with legislative proposals to exempt those companies from employer classification laws—from more comprehensive models available to all workers, and which genuinely pool risk and preserve employment rights.37,38 These types of shared funds could be built directly into state paid sick day policies.39 Some jurisdictions have already successfully established portable benefits programs covering certain self-employed workers, such as Philadelphia's paid sick days for domestic workers, which requires all “hiring entities” to pay into a shared fund based on hours of work (Philadelphia Code Chapter 9-4500).

Most high-income countries provide both sick days and longer-term medical leave. In some cases, employers have responsibility for funding the first few days of sick leave, whereas social insurance covers longer leaves.40 For example, Belgium mandates 14 days of short-term sick leave paid by the employer in addition to medical leave of up to one year funded by social security. While a traditional social insurance model might not be the most efficient way to cover paid sick days, it is an effective approach to providing longer-term medical leave, and most high-income countries with these policies cover the self-employed for longer leave periods.24 Similarly, nearly all countries globally that provide sick leave do so without excluding workers based on the size of their employer; just 3 have narrow exemptions.24

This study has limitations. First, while we based our analysis on the best available labor force statistics, research suggests that household survey data may underestimate the numbers of workers who are classified as independent contractors; Black and Latinx workers are especially likely to be undercounted.41 As a result, the share of workers excluded by independent contractor exemptions, and the share that would be newly eligible if independent contractors were covered, may be greater than our findings suggest. Similarly, it is possible that workers’ self-reports of some work or firm characteristics (eg, employer size) may be inaccurate, which could lead to measurement errors in the analysis.

Second, while we measure laws on the books, we do not evaluate implementation. Past research has documented how a substantial share of low-wage workers—including a particularly high proportion of Latinx workers and those in food service—have experienced employer retaliation for taking paid sick days and/or avoid taking paid sick days out of fear of retaliation.42-44 Amidst a political climate where immigrant workers are facing heightened scrutiny in all aspects of public life, ensuring that immigrant workers are both aware of their eligibility for paid sick days and can exercise their rights to take those sick days free from retaliation will substantially shape the effectiveness of these policies.

Conclusion

States that have passed paid sick days are filling a critical gap in federal law. Nevertheless, many workers remain ineligible, including a disproportionate share of immigrant workers; these gaps undermine the public health benefits of paid sick days. Extending paid sick days to independent contractors and removing exclusions based on firm size would improve overall coverage and close the gaps in access between citizens and noncitizens, with important benefits for all.
