Retrospective analysis of mortality and the influence of polypharmacy in geriatric staircase falls
Authors: Niklas Rutsch, Meike Vitale, Sabrina N Jegerlehner, Jolanta Klukowska-Rötzler, Aristomenis Exadaktylos, Christoph E Albers, Christian Tinner, Sebastian F Bigdon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStaircase falls are a significant cause of emergency department visits among older adults (≥ 65 years) and are associated with increased morbidity and mortality. While previous research has explored risk factors for injury severity, the role of polypharmacy (≥ 5 regular medications) in influencing injury patterns and clinical outcomes remains unclear.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using patient records from a university hospital emergency department within a five-year observation period, with a minimum follow-up of two years. Demographic data, health status, injury patterns, and outcomes were analyzed. Survival analysis was performed to assess the impact of polypharmacy on long-term survival.ResultsA total of 529 patients were included, of whom 243 (45.9%) were classified as polypharmacy patients. Polypharmacy patients were significantly older (mean age 79.18 vs. 76.45 years, p < 0.001) and had higher comorbidity burdens, including increased BMI, dementia, depression, and previous falls (p < 0.05). Although polypharmacy was not significantly associated with specific injury patterns, it was linked to worse clinical outcomes. Patients with polypharmacy were more likely to require inpatient admission (70.8% vs. 62.2%) and had lower long-term survival rates (p = 0.014). After adjusting for covariates, polypharmacy remained an independent risk factor for mortality (p = 0.042).ConclusionPolypharmacy is prevalent among older adults with staircase falls and is associated with increased frailty, comorbidities, and poor clinical outcomes, including higher mortality. These findings underscore the need for targeted medication management and comprehensive fall prevention strategies to improve outcomes in this vulnerable population group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07627-z.Keywords: Geriatrics, Falls, Polypharmacy, Orthogeriatrics

Background

Staircase falls are a common reason for referral to the emergency department in older patients (≥ 65 years). The incidence of staircase falls is significantly higher in older patients than in younger patients, especially because moving up and down stairs is one of the most dangerous activities of daily living for the former [1, 2]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, approximately 36 million falls are reported annually among older adults in the United States, with 32,000 (0.89%) of these falls resulting in death, highlighting the critical nature of stair falls in this population [3]. Stair falls in older adults are associated with the risk of disability, morbidity, and mortality [4–6]. Polypharmacy is a well-established risk factor for falls and adverse outcomes in older adults [7, 8]. However, it remains unclear whether its impact is comparable across different fall mechanisms. In particular, it is unknown whether the effects of polypharmacy observed in low-energy falls also apply to staircase falls, which may be more strongly influenced by environmental and mechanical factors. To date, the relationships between risk factors, injury patterns, and severity after falling down the stairs in older adults have hardly been researched to date [2]. Chatha et al. showed that age and intoxication influence injury patterns and severity of injury in stair falls [9]. Behera et al. identified traumatic brain injury as the most common fatal trauma in accidental stair falls [10]. This finding may help to guide more rapid therapeutic interventions. Due to reduced protective reflexes, often reduced bone quality, and sarcopenia in advanced age, injuries to the musculoskeletal system, and in particular the spine, are common injury patterns in older adults. The influence of morbidity factors on the pattern and severity of spinal injuries in older adults after falls from stairs has not yet been investigated.

In our retrospective analysis, our primary aim was to analyze the influence of polypharmacy on mortality in older adults after staircase falls. This will help optimize risk assessment and targeted diagnostics after a fall down the stairs to initiate faster and targeted therapy.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study according to the STROBE Guidelines (checklist as supplement 1) including patients admitted to the emergency department (ED) of Inselspital between 2015 and 2019 [11].

Electronic search

In the electronic patient record database (Ecare) of the emergency department (ED) of Inselspital we searched for “Fall” and “Staircase” and manually screened each case to determine whether the trauma mechanism was a staircase fall. The observational period was between 2015 and 2019 and the minimum follow-up (until record access) was two years after admission to the ED. We included all patients aged ≥ 65 years who presented with a staircase fall in the emergency department. Patients with incomplete records (especially regarding their regular medication) that could not be retrieved through case file review were excluded. Polypharmacy was defined, according to Varghese et al. as five or more regular medications [12].

Data collection

General admission data

Data collected upon admission included the year, season (Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter) to explore potential variations in fall frequency related to environmental factors, time of admission, mode of admission (self-referral, ambulance, air rescue, general practitioner, referral from another hospital), triage category [1–4], and whether the trauma/shock room was utilized.

Sociodemographic data

Sociodemographic information comprised age, gender (male, female), and living situation (alone, with family, or nursing home).

Health data

Health-related data included body mass index (BMI), vision impairment, muscle strength of the upper and lower extremities, use of walking aids, previous orthopedic surgeries (knee or hip replacement), number of chronic diseases, history of previous falls or falls on stairs, and mental health status (assessed by presence of depression, substance abuse, use of psychotropic medications, and alcohol consumption).

Outcome data

Outcome data included discharge destination (home, external hospital, inpatient admission to specific department), in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality and mortality within the follow-up period.

Statistics

All statistics were performed in R. Population characteristics were described using the “tableone” package in the “RStudio” programming environment [13, 14]. Continuous variables were compared using t-tests, and categorical variables using chi-squared tests. To assess the cumulative incidence of death after a staircase fall, we used the crr-function of the “survival” and “tidycmprsk” packages [15, 16]. The difference between the survival curves based on polypharmacy status was assessed using a Cox regression model. Missing values for adjusted covariate analysis of the cumulative risk ratio were imputed using the median of the respective groups. Statistical significance was defined as alpha = 0.05.

Results

A total of 529 patients were analyzed, of whom 286 (54.1%) did not have polypharmacy and 243 (45.9%) had polypharmacy.

Emergency presentation

The seasonal distribution did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.411). In both groups the seasonal presentation varied only from 23% to 28%. In addition, the daytime of presentation did not show a significant difference between groups (p = 0.234) (Table 1).

Univariate analysis

The mode of hospital entry did not differ between the two groups, with a majority of patients admitted via ambulance (53.1% in the non-polypharmacy group vs. 52.7% in polypharmacy group), followed by self-assignment (17.8% vs. 16.5%) and transfer from another hospital (16.1% vs. 12.3%). A smaller proportion was sent by air ambulance (4.5% vs. 6.6%) or referred by general practitioners (1.0% vs. 3.7%).

The distribution of triage categories did not significantly differ (p = 0.179), with the majority in category 2 (38.1% vs. 44.0%), followed by category 3 (37.8% vs. 35.0%) and category 1 (21.7% vs. 19.8%). Patients presenting via the shock room were similarly distributed between the groups (38.5% vs. 38.7%, p = 1.000).

Age and gender

Patients in the polypharmacy group were significantly older, with a mean age of 79.18 years (SD = 7.41), compared to 76.45 years (SD = 7.52) in the non-polypharmacy group (p < 0.001). The sex distribution was similar, with 10.5% females in the non-polypharmacy group and 9.9% in the polypharmacy group (p = 0.930).

Living status and mobility

Living arrangements did not significantly differ (p = 0.053) between the groups: 60.5% of non-polypharmacy patients lived with their family or spouse, compared to 69.1% of polypharmacy patients. More patients without polypharmacy lived alone (31.8% vs. 23.9%), whereas nursing home residency was slightly higher in the polypharmacy group (5.3% vs. 3.1%). The use of walking aids was significantly more common in the polypharmacy group (1.7% vs. 7.8%, p = 0.007).

Health status

The mean BMI was significantly higher in the polypharmacy group (26.84, SD = 5.24) than in the non-polypharmacy group (25.17, SD = 4.70, p = 0.001).

The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was significantly higher in the polypharmacy group (2.92, SD = 1.91) than in the non-polypharmacy group (2.06, SD = 1.63, p < 0.001). The number of chronic diseases was also significantly higher in the polypharmacy group (5.03, SD = 2.34) than in the non-polypharmacy group (2.10, SD = 1.26, p < 0.001).

A significant difference was observed in previous surgical history (p = 0.046). Among patients without polypharmacy, 91.6% had no previous surgery, compared with 84.0% in the polypharmacy group. Knee surgeries were more common in the polypharmacy group (5.3% vs. 2.1%), as were hip surgeries (7.8% vs. 4.2%).

Previous falls and staircase falls were significantly more frequent in the polypharmacy group (p = 0.003). Among patients without polypharmacy, 92.7% had no history of falls, compared with 84.4% in the polypharmacy group. Falls occurred in 14.0% of patients with polypharmacy versus 7.3% in patients without polypharmacy. No significant differences were observed in rates of vertigo (p = 0.228).

Dementia was significantly more common in the polypharmacy group (15.2% vs. 7.0%, p = 0.04), and depression was also significantly higher in the polypharmacy group (14.4% vs. 4.5%, p < 0.001).

Substance use did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.128). Most patients reported no substance use (86.4% vs. 90.1%).

Injury patterns

Overall, no statistically significant differences in injury distribution were observed between older adults with and without polypharmacy (Table 2). Traumatic brain injury was observed most frequently (39.5% vs. 38.7%, p = 0.92). Overall, spine injuries occurred in 14.3% of non-polypharmacy and 14.8% of polypharmacy patients (p = 0.88), with similar distributions across cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions (Table 2). Injuries of the extremities, thoracic and abdominal injuries, as well as clavicle and other injuries showed no significant differences between groups. Notably, lumbar spine injuries were numerically more frequent in the non-polypharmacy group (4.5% vs. 1.2%. p = 0.05), while thoracic spine injuries occurred more often in the polypharmacy group (5.3% vs. 2.4%, p = 0.13) (Table 2).

Discharge outcome

The discharge outcomes differed significantly between the groups (p = 0.026). In the non-polypharmacy group, 33.6% were discharged home, compared with 23.0% in the polypharmacy group. More polypharmacy patients were transferred to internal wards (70.8% vs. 62.2%). Transfers to another hospital were similar (3.1% vs. 5.8%), and deaths in the emergency department were low in both groups (1.0% vs. 0.4%) (Table 1).

Mortality

One-hundred-eighteen patients died during follow-up period. Staircase falls had a mortality rate of 7.75% (n = 41) in the first 30 days after presentation in the emergency department, and 14.93% (n = 79) of patients died within the first year.

There was a significant difference (p = 0.014) in survival based only on the polypharmacy of the patients (Fig. 1A, B). When adjusted for covariates (age, sex, BMI, chronic diseases, and the interaction of chronic diseases), this difference remains significant (HR 2.42; 95% CI 1.03–5.68; p = 0.042) (Fig. 1C). Older age was a significant risk factor for a lower chance of long-term survival (HR 1.07; 95% CI 1.04–1.10; p < 0.001) at the time of injury. A higher BMI was a significant protective factor for survival (HR 0.94; 95% CI 0.91–0.98; p = 0.002).

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the impact of polypharmacy in older adults who present to the emergency department after staircase falls. Our findings highlight key differences in patient characteristics, health status, and outcomes between those with and without polypharmacy, while also revealing that polypharmacy does not significantly influence injury patterns.

Polypharmacy and patient characteristics

We observed that polypharmacy was associated with significantly older age and a higher burden of chronic diseases. Patients in the polypharmacy group were, on average, older than their non-polypharmacy counterparts and had a higher Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). This aligns with previous research indicating that polypharmacy is often a marker of multimorbidity in older adults [17]. Moreover, the polypharmacy group had a significantly higher BMI, which has been previously linked to frailty and fall risk in older adults [18].

A significant difference was also found in living situations, with more patients with polypharmacy living with their family rather than alone. While this may reflect increased dependency in this group, it underscores the importance of caregiver support in managing medication regimens and preventing falls. Furthermore, mobility differences were evident, as patients with polypharmacy were more likely to use walking aids, suggesting a greater degree of physical impairment.

Polypharmacy and injury patterns

Interestingly, our analysis did not find a significant association between polypharmacy and specific injury patterns after a fall on the staircase (Table 2). This suggests that although polypharmacy is a strong indicator of frailty and chronic illness, it does not necessarily predispose patients to a particular type or severity of trauma. Previous studies have suggested that polypharmacy might increase fall risk due to medication side effects, such as dizziness and impaired balance; however, its direct role in injury severity remains unclear [19].

Despite this, we found that patients with polypharmacy had significantly higher rates of prior falls, including previous staircase falls. This supports the notion that medication burden may contribute to repeated fall episodes, possibly due to cognitive impairment, dizziness or polypharmacy-related side effects. The significantly higher prevalence of dementia and depression in the polypharmacy group further underscores this potential link, as both conditions are associated with an increased risk of falls [20, 21].

Polypharmacy and clinical outcomes

A key finding of our study was that polypharmacy was associated with poor clinical outcomes. Fewer polypharmacy patients were discharged home after their ED visits, and more required inpatient admission. These results suggest that patients with polypharmacy may have greater overall vulnerability, necessitating extended hospitalization and medical care. The increased rate of hospital admissions aligns with prior research demonstrating that polypharmacy contributes to longer hospital stays and higher healthcare utilization among older adults [21].

Mortality outcomes were also significantly worse in patients with polypharmacy. The overall 30-day mortality rate following a staircase fall was 7.75%, and survival analysis demonstrated a significantly lower long-term survival rate in the polypharmacy group. Even after adjusting for key covariates, such as age, sex, BMI, and chronic diseases, polypharmacy remained an independent risk factor for mortality. This finding underscores the broader impact of polypharmacy beyond fall risk, highlighting its role decline of long-term health [21].

Clinical implications

Our study emphasizes the need for targeted interventions in older adults experiencing staircase falls while on polypharmacy. Medication review and deprescribing initiatives can play crucial roles in fall prevention strategies. Given the poorer outcomes observed in this population, healthcare providers should prioritize fall-risk assessments and early rehabilitation strategies. We observed a significantly higher prevalence of prior falls in the polypharmacy group. This may reflect a bidirectional relationship, as prior falls could lead to an increased medication burden, while longitudinal studies have also reported an increased fall risk in patients with polypharmacy [8]. However, the direction of this association cannot be determined in this retrospective analysis.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, this study had several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it is inherently limited by the quality and completeness of the available medical records. No information on the mechanical injury mechanism (e.g., height of stairs) was available. Moreover, given the emergency department setting, frailty was not formally measured. Additionally, we did not assess medication classes, dosages, or potential drug interactions in detail, which limits our ability to identify specific medications or combinations associated with adverse outcomes or to explore underlying mechanisms. The retrospective nature of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between polypharmacy and clinical outcomes. Future studies should focus on the prospective assessment of polypharmacy by incorporating medication types, dosages, and potential drug interactions.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings underscore that polypharmacy is a prevalent risk factor among older adults and was not associated with specific injury patterns after staircase falls. Polypharmacy was closely correlated with multimorbidity, which in turn was associated with poorer clinical outcomes and higher mortality. These results advocate for the integration of comprehensive medication reviews into fall prevention strategies for older adults with polypharmacy to improve long-term outcomes and reduce the healthcare burden.
