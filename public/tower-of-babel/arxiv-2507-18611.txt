Topological constraint on crystalline current
Tomohiro Soejima (副島智大), Junkai Dong (董焌锴), Ophelia Evelyn Sommer, Daniel E. Parker, Ashvin Vishwanath

Abstract
How much current does a sliding electron crystal carry? The answer to this simple question has important implications for the dynamic properties of the crystal, such as the frequency of its cyclotron motion, and its phonon spectrum. In this work we introduce a precise definition of a sliding crystal and compute the corresponding current 𝒋 c \boldsymbol{j}_{c} for topological electron crystals in the presence of magnetic field. Our result is fully non-perturbative, does not rely on Galilean invariance, and applies equally to Wigner crystals and (anomalous) Hall crystals. In terms of the electron density ρ \rho and magnetic flux density ϕ \phi , we find that 𝒋 c = e ⁡ ( ρ − C ​ ϕ ) ​ 𝒗 \boldsymbol{j}_{c}=e(\rho-C\phi)\boldsymbol{v} . Surprisingly, the current receives a contribution from the many-body Chern number C C of the crystal. When ρ = C ​ ϕ \rho=C\phi , sliding crystals therefore carry zero current. The crystalline current fixes the Lorentz force felt by the sliding crystal and the dispersion of low-energy phonons of such crystals. This gives us a simple counting rule for the number of gapless phonons: if a sliding crystal carries nonzero current in a magnetic field, there is a single gapless mode, while otherwise there are two gapless modes. This result can also be understood from anomaly-matching of emanant discrete translation symmetries — an idea that is also applicable to the dispersion of skyrmion crystals. Our results lead to novel experimental implications and invite further conceptual developments for electron crystals.

, the IR translation operators can commute despite the magnetic translation algebra, as the Chern number can cancel the contribution from the magnetic field.

We can understand this as a case of “emanant symmetry”
cheng2023lieb
: the microscopic discrete translations get embedded into the effective field theory as discrete translations, and their anomalies need to be matched.
As discussed in Ref.
seiberg
;
seiberg2025anomalouscontinuoustranslations
and Appendix
D
, the same consideration applies to the dynamics of skyrmions, and by extension, skyrmion crystals
nagaosa2013topological
. The UV theory of skyrmion crystals are described by a lattice of spins, which have commuting discrete lattice translations, analogous to what we have for topological crystals when
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
. This gets embedded to the IR theory as discrete translations, whose lack of anomaly fixes the Berry phase term to zero up to an integer offset. This offset turns out to be given by the winding number of the skyrmion
stone1996magnus
;
watanabe2014noncommuting
, giving rise to anomalous dispersion for the skyrmion crystal
zang2011dynamics
. These two examples provide an important lesson: discrete emanant symmetry can constrain the Berry phase term
up to a topological invariant
, whose identity must be established microscopically.

Generalization to Fractional Hall crystals—
Our discussion so far assumed crystalline insulators with unique ground states under pinning, forcing the Chern number
C
to be an integer.
In fact, Eq. (
1
) generalizes easily to fractional Hall crystals with fractional
C
. For the Galilean invariant case, the argument above does not depend on whether
\sigma_{xy}
takes a fractional value. The main challenge for a more rigorous proof is the bookkeeping required for multiple quasi-degenerate ground states,
which we address carefully in Appendix
C
. We also find that fractional states modify the topological constraint Eq. (
29
) imposed by emanant symmetry.

Experimental implications—
Our most striking prediction is the vanishing of the current
\boldsymbol{j}_{c}
for full Hall crystals.
This results in two branches of gapless phonons in the collective mode spectrum.
In the
\rho-\phi
phase diagram of topological crystals, measurements of the phonon spectrum can reveal the enhancement from one to two gapless modes exactly when
\rho=C\phi
. Full Hall crystals are further distinguished by their unusual electromagnetic response: as they carry zero current when sliding, electric fields do not couple to their center of mass motion. As we show in Appendix
B
, this can be detected by longitudinal conductivity of a pinned crystal: to the lowest order in
\omega

\sigma^{xx}(\omega)\propto s^{2}\omega
. At large voltage, we expect a large non-linearity in the response, resulting from depinning of the crystal
gruner1988dynamics
;
gruner2018density
;
fisher1998collective
;
brazovskii2004pinning
;
reichhardt2016depinning
;

.

We close by pointing out a natural place to look for full Hall crystals: bilayer quantum Hall systems at filling
\nu=\nu_{t}+\nu_{b}\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\nu_{t}
and
\nu_{b}
are filling of the top and bottom layers. When
\nu_{t}\ll\nu_{b}

\nu_{t}

, just as in excitonic condensates

;
narozhny2016coulomb
.

Appendix A
Berry phase for the sliding crystal

We will now compute Eq. (
26
), i.e. the Berry phase corresponding to the trajectory of states
\ket{\Psi(t)}=\ket{\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\Phi}=0}
is indeed that corresponding to
\beta=eB(1-C\phi/\rho)
. We compute the Berry curvature
\Omega(\boldsymbol{u})
as

\Omega(\boldsymbol{u})=\lim_{\delta u\to 0}\frac{\textrm{Im}\prod_{j=1}^{4}\braket{\boldsymbol{u}^{(j)}|\boldsymbol{u}^{(j+1)}}}{\delta u^{2}}

(30)

in which we take
\boldsymbol{u}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{u}^{(5)}=\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{(2)}=\boldsymbol{u}+(\delta u,0),\boldsymbol{u}^{(3)}=\boldsymbol{u}+(\delta u,\delta u),\boldsymbol{u}^{(4)}=\boldsymbol{u}+(0,\delta u)
, with the flux fixed at
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=0
throughout.

Recall from the definition of
\ket{\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\Phi}=0}
:

\ket{\boldsymbol{u}(t);\boldsymbol{\Phi}=0}=T_{\boldsymbol{u}}\ket{\boldsymbol{u}=0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}=-eB(L_{x}u_{y},-L_{y}u_{x})}

(31)

Then, we may simplify the overlaps as

\prod_{j}\bra{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(j)}}T^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{u}^{(j)}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}^{(j+1)}}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(j+1)}}=e^{iN_{e}eB\delta u^{2}}\prod_{j}\bra{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(j)}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}^{(j+1)}-\boldsymbol{u^{(j)}}}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(j+1)}},

(32)

where we have used
T_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}=e^{ieB\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}}
, and defined
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(j)}=-eB(L_{x}u_{y}^{(j)},-L_{y}u_{x}^{(j)})
. The prefactor corresponds to the Aharonov-Bohm phase accumulated.

We now identify the second term contributes a phase proportional to the many-body Chern number. To see that, we will examine one of the overlaps explicitly; the others are directly analogous. Consider

\bra{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1)}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}^{(2)}-\boldsymbol{u^{(1)}}}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}=\bra{0;-eB(L_{x}u_{y},-L_{y}u_{x})}T_{(\delta u,0)}\ket{0;-eB(L_{x}u_{y},-L_{y}(u_{x}+\delta u))}.

(33)

We now define

\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}=e^{-i\sum_{a}(\Phi_{x}\cdot\hat{x}_{a}/L_{x}+\Phi_{y}\cdot\hat{y}_{a}/L_{y})}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}},

(34)

which are the many-body Bloch states that are periodic under magnetic translations by
\boldsymbol{L}_{1,2}
, i.e.
\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{0}}.
Here
a\in[1,N_{e}]
labels the different particles. The overlaps above can thus be simplified using the many-body Bloch states as

\displaystyle\bra{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1)}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}^{(2)}-\boldsymbol{u^{(1)}}}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}

\displaystyle=e^{iN_{e}\Phi^{(2)}_{x}\delta u/L_{x}}\braket{u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1)}}|e^{ieB\delta u\sum_{a}y_{a}}T_{(\delta u,0)}|u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}}

(35)

in which we have used the definition of the many-body Bloch states.

Now we simplify the operator in the overlap. The linear order term in
\delta u
is going to contribute to the Berry connection, and thus we only need to be accurate to that order:

e^{ieB\delta u\sum_{a}y_{a}}T_{(\delta u,0)}=1+i\delta u\left(eB\sum_{a}y_{a}+R_{1,a}\right)+O(\delta u^{2})=1+i\delta u\sum_{a}\pi_{1,a}+O(\delta u^{2})

(36)

in which we have expanded the magnetic translation using its generator (the guiding center)
\boldsymbol{R}_{a}=\boldsymbol{p}_{a}-e\boldsymbol{A}_{a}+eB(\hat{z}\times\boldsymbol{r})_{a}
, and the definition of the kinetic momentum
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{a}=\boldsymbol{p}_{a}-e\boldsymbol{A}_{a}
. This can be understood physically as follows: since both
\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}
are periodic under magnetic translations, the operator in the middle must be a translation operator that commutes with magnetic translations, which can only be generated by kinetic momentum.

Now we are ready to compute the overlap Eq. (
35
) to order
\delta u
:

\displaystyle\bra{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1)}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}^{(2)}-\boldsymbol{u^{(1)}}}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}

\displaystyle=1+i\left(\frac{N_{e}\Phi_{x}^{(2)}}{L_{x}}-i\braket{\partial_{\Phi^{(2)}_{y}}u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}|u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}}\frac{\partial\Phi^{(2)}_{y}}{\partial\delta u}+\braket{u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}|\sum_{a}\pi_{a}|u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}}\right)\delta u+O(\delta u^{2})

(37)

\displaystyle=1+i\left(ieBL_{y}\braket{\partial_{\Phi^{(2)}_{y}}u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}|u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}}+\braket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}|\sum_{a}\pi_{a}|0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}\right)\delta u+O(\delta u^{2})

\displaystyle=1+ieBL_{y}\tilde{A}_{y}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)})\delta u+O(\delta u^{2})\equiv 1+iA_{x}(\boldsymbol{u})\delta u+O(\delta u^{2})

Here in the second line we collected the first and third term using the definition of the many-body Bloch state. In the third line we simplified the first term using the many-body Berry connection
\tilde{A}_{a}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})=-i\braket{u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}|\partial_{\Phi_{a}}u_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}
, and the expectation value of the kinetic momentum
\braket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}|\sum_{a}\pi_{a}|0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(2)}}
. This expectation vanishes, since it can be written as a derivative of a local gauge-invariant operator
\pi^{2}/2
with respect to the flux
(\Phi_{x},\Phi_{y})

[
24
]
.
Physically it makes sense for the expectation to vanish: in the case of a Galilean symmetric system one can identify the mechanical momentum with the center-of-mass velocity. Given that we have pinned the crystal, it should not have the freedom to slide.

Computing the other overlaps, we find the gauge potential
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{u})
defined in the last line of Eq. (
37
) to be

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{u})=(eBL_{y}\tilde{A}_{y}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}),-eBL_{x}\tilde{A}_{x}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})).

(38)

Recall
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=-eB(L_{x}u_{y},-L_{y}u_{x})
, we see that the curvature with respect to the gauge potential is

\Omega(\boldsymbol{u})=\partial_{u_{x}}A_{y}-\partial_{u_{y}}A_{x}=e^{2}B^{2}L_{x}^{2}L_{y}^{2}(\partial_{\Phi_{x}}\tilde{A}_{y}-\partial_{\Phi_{y}}\tilde{A}_{x})=-e^{2}B^{2}L_{x}^{2}L_{y}^{2}\tilde{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})

(39)

Here, we used the perhaps controversial convention for the many-body Berry curvature
\tilde{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})=-\partial_{\Phi_{x}}\tilde{A}_{y}+\partial_{\Phi_{y}}\tilde{A}_{x}
, such that for
eB>0
the lowest Landau level has
C=1
. We now use the independence of many-body Berry curvature
\tilde{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
with respect to the boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{\Phi}

[
24
]
, and substitute in its thermodynamic value
\tilde{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})=C/2\pi
. Using the Berry curvature to simplify the RHS of Eq. (
32
) and plugging it into Eq. (
30
), we obtain

\Omega(\boldsymbol{u})=eB\left[N_{e}-\frac{eBL_{x}L_{y}C}{2\pi}\right]=N_{e}\,eB\left(1-\frac{C\phi}{\rho}\right).

(40)

Identifying
\Omega(\boldsymbol{u})=N_{e}\beta
, we finally conclude that
\beta=eB\left(1-C\phi/\rho\right).

Appendix B
Response of pinned Hall crystals

Consider the low frequency
\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{0}
electromagnetic response of a Hall crystal with phonons, which may described by the following action

S^{\mathrm{phonon}}[\boldsymbol{u}]=\frac{\rho}{2}\int dtd^{2}\boldsymbol{r}~-\Lambda\boldsymbol{u}^{2}+\beta\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{u}+m\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}+\Omega m^{2}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}=\frac{\rho L_{x}L_{y}}{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}~u^{a}(\omega)u^{b}(-\omega)D_{ab}^{-1}(\omega)

(41)

corresponding to a strength pinning characterized by
\Lambda
, an effective Lorentz force field characterised by
\beta=seB/\rho A_{\mathrm{uc}}
, a ‘mass’
m
and phonon Berry curvature
\Omega
. Here
s/A_{\mathrm{uc}}=\rho-C\phi=\rho\tilde{s}
where
s
is the bound charge topological invariant of the Widom-Streda formula, and
\phi=N_{\Phi}/L_{x}L_{y}
is the flux density. The electromagnetic coupling between the vector potential
\boldsymbol{A}(\omega)=\frac{\boldsymbol{E}(\omega)}{i\omega}
(working in temporal gauge) and the polarisation evidently takes the form

S^{\mathrm{coupling}}[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{A}]=\rho\tilde{s}e\int dtd^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}+\rho\alpha e\int dtd^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}\times\boldsymbol{E}

(42)

since the change of the polarization is given by
\tilde{s}\rho e
. The second term arising from velocity dependent polarization, which can, for example, be caused by Lorentz force acting on excitons.
Including the Chern-Simons term for the many-body Chern number
C
, the full action is therefore

S[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{A}]=S^{\mathrm{phonon}}[\boldsymbol{u}]+S^{\mathrm{coupling}}[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{A}]+\frac{e^{2}C}{4\pi}\int dtd^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{A}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{A}}+\dots

(43)

The coefficient of the Chern-Simons term ensures quantized response for pinned crystals.
To study linear response to leading order in
\omega
, it is sufficient to keep quadratic order terms in
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{A}
and lowest order in
\omega
. The electromagnetic response is straightforward to obtain by integration out
\boldsymbol{u}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\rho L_{x}L_{y}}{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}~u^{a}(\omega)u^{b}(-\omega)D_{ab}^{-1}(\omega)+i\omega\tilde{s}e[u^{a}(-\omega)A_{a}(\omega)-u^{a}(\omega)A_{a}(-\omega)]+\omega^{2}\alpha e\epsilon_{ab}[u_{a}(-\omega)A_{b}(\omega)+u_{a}(\omega)A_{b}(-\omega)]

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho L_{x}L_{y}}{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}~([u^{a}(\omega)+(i\omega\tilde{s}eA_{c}(\omega)+\omega^{2}\alpha eA_{e}(\omega)\epsilon^{ce})D^{ca}(\omega)][u^{b}(-\omega)+(-i\omega\tilde{s}eA_{d}(-\omega)+\omega^{2}\alpha eA_{f}(-\omega)\epsilon^{df})D^{bd}(\omega)]D_{ab}^{-1}(\omega)

(45)

\displaystyle-e^{2}\left(\tilde{s}^{2}e\omega^{2}D^{ab}(\omega)+i2\omega^{3}\bar{s}\alpha D^{ac}(\omega)\epsilon^{cb}+\omega^{4}\alpha^{2}D^{cd}(\omega)\epsilon^{ac}\epsilon^{db}\right)A_{a}(\omega)A_{b}(-\omega))

(46)

A simple shift in the phonon path integral gives the effective electromagnetic action

S^{\mathrm{EM}}=\frac{e^{2}L_{x}L_{y}}{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left[-\rho\bar{s}^{2}\omega^{2}D^{ab}(\omega)+i2\omega^{3}\rho\bar{s}\alpha D^{ac}(\omega)\epsilon^{cb}+\omega^{4}\rho\alpha^{2}D^{cd}\epsilon^{ac}\epsilon^{db}+\frac{i\omega C}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab}\right]A_{a}(\omega)A_{b}(-\omega)

(47)

Computing the electromagnetic response as

j_{a}=\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\frac{\delta S^{\mathrm{EM}}}{\delta A_{a}(-\omega)}=e^{2}\left(i\rho\tilde{s}^{2}\omega D^{ab}(\omega)+2\omega^{2}\rho\bar{s}\alpha D^{ac}(\omega)\epsilon^{cb}-i\omega^{3}\rho\alpha^{2}D^{cd}\epsilon^{ac}\epsilon^{db}+\frac{C\epsilon^{ab}}{2\pi}\right)E_{b}(\omega)

(48)

which means the conductivity tensor is unsurprisingly

\sigma^{ab}(\omega)=e^{2}\left(i\rho\tilde{s}^{2}\omega D^{ab}(\omega)+2\omega^{2}\rho\bar{s}\alpha D^{ac}(\omega)\epsilon^{cb}-i\omega^{3}\rho\alpha^{2}D^{cd}\epsilon^{ac}\epsilon^{db}+\frac{C\epsilon^{ab}}{2\pi}\right)

(49)

The inverse phonon Green’s function takes the form

\displaystyle D_{ab}^{-1}(\omega)

\displaystyle=(-\Lambda+m\omega^{2})\delta_{ab}-i\omega\epsilon_{ab}(\beta+m^{2}\omega^{2}\Omega)

(50)

\displaystyle D^{ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(-\Lambda+m\omega^{2})^{2}-\omega^{2}(\beta+m^{2}\omega^{2}\Omega)^{2}}[(-\Lambda+m\omega^{2})\delta_{ab}+i\omega\epsilon_{ab}(\beta+m^{2}\omega^{2}\Omega)].

(51)

Combining these results we get

\displaystyle\sigma^{ab}(\omega)=\frac{e^{2}\rho}{(-\Lambda+m\omega^{2})^{2}-\omega^{2}(\beta+m^{2}\omega^{2}\Omega)^{2}}(

\displaystyle[(-\Lambda+m\omega^{2})(i\bar{s}^{2}\omega-i\omega^{3}\alpha^{2})+i2\omega^{3}\bar{s}\alpha(\beta+m^{2}\omega^{2}\Omega)]\delta_{ab}

(52)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle[(\beta+m^{2}\omega^{2}\Omega)(-\bar{s}^{2}\omega^{2}+\omega^{4}\alpha^{2})-2\omega^{2}\bar{s}\alpha(-\Lambda+m\omega^{2})]\epsilon^{ab})

(53)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}C\epsilon^{ab}}{2\pi}

(54)

If
\Lambda
is finite, as
\omega\to 0
the phonons do not contribute to the conductivity and we find, unsurprisingly, an insulator with

\sigma^{ab}(\omega\approx 0)=\frac{e^{2}\rho}{\Lambda^{2}}\left(-i\Lambda\bar{s}^{2}\omega\delta^{ab}-(\beta\bar{s}^{2}-2\bar{s}{\alpha\Lambda})\omega^{2}\epsilon^{ab}\right)+\frac{e^{2}C}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab}\to\frac{e^{2}C}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab},

(55)

where we kept terms up to quadratic order in
\omega
.
Notably, when
\bar{s}=0
, the conductivity vanishes up to linear order. This can be used as a signature of full Hall crystals.

If
\Lambda=0
, but
\beta\neq 0
, the conductivity becomes

\sigma^{ab}(\omega\to 0)=\frac{e^{2}\epsilon^{ab}}{2\pi}\left(C+\frac{2\pi\rho\tilde{s}^{2}}{\beta}\right)=\frac{e^{2}\epsilon^{ab}}{2\pi}\left(C+(\rho-C\phi)\frac{2\pi}{eB}\right)=\frac{e\rho}{B}\epsilon^{ab}

(56)

Thus in the unpinned case with a magnetic field, the Hall conductivity is indistinguishable from a free electron gas, reflecting emergent Galilean symmetry of the action.
If
\Lambda=\beta=0
but
\tilde{s}=1
, corresponding to an (anomalous hall) crystal absent magnetic field the conductivity takes the form

\sigma^{ab}(\omega\to 0)=\frac{i\rho e^{2}}{m(\omega+i0^{+})}\delta^{ab}+\frac{e^{2}}{2\pi}\left(C-2\pi\rho\Omega-4\pi\rho\frac{\alpha}{m}\right)\epsilon^{ab}

(57)

The longitudinal conductivity of crystal diverges giving rise to the Drude weight. The Hall conductivity has two contributions. The first contribution
C-2\pi\rho\Omega
has already been discussed in Ref.
[
58
]
, and it indicates that the hall conductivity is modified from the many body Chern number by the presence of center of mass momentum Berry curvature
\Omega
, which is not expected to be quantized.
The second term, on the other hand, arises from the coupling of velocity with polarization.
Finally, when
\Lambda=s=0
, we have

\sigma^{ab}(\omega\approx 0)=\frac{e^{2}C}{2\pi}\epsilon^{ab}+e^{2}\alpha^{2}\rho\left[\frac{-i\omega}{m}{\delta^{ab}+\Omega\omega^{2}\epsilon^{ab}}\right]

(58)

Appendix C
Fractional Hall crystals

Let us now consider fractional Hall crystals. Similarly to the main text, we consider a Hamiltonian with a pinning potential. Importantly, however, we do not require a unique ground state and instead allow
N_{\mathrm{gs}}
-fold degenerate low-energy subspace.

To define its many-body Chern number, we consider an adiabatic flux threading process according to the time-dependent Hamiltonian

H(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\Phi})=\sum_{j}\hat{h}(\boldsymbol{p}_{j}-e\boldsymbol{A})+\sum_{i<j}\hat{V}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}_{j})+\sum_{j}\lambda U(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}-\boldsymbol{u})\text{ where }\boldsymbol{A}=(-By,0)+\left(\frac{\Phi_{x}}{eL_{x}},\frac{\Phi_{y}}{eL_{y}}\right)

(59)

We consider a process starting from an initial state
\ket{\Psi_{0}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}=0}
and time-dependent flux
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)
according to the Hamiltonian
H(0,\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t))
.
This convention fixes the boundary condition constant while changing the gauge field to attain different flux sectors. To go back to the original Hamiltonian, we must act with the boundary condition changing operator

e^{-i\sum_{m}\sum_{a}\frac{\Phi_{a}(t)}{L_{a}}\hat{r}_{m,a}},

(60)

where
\hat{r}_{m,a}
is the position of the
m
-th electron along the
a
direction. After this, the wavefunction lives in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)}
with the correct boundary conditions.
Denoting the solution to this adiabatic process by
\ket{\Psi_{0},\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)}
, the many-body Chern number is given by

C=2\pi\frac{dP_{x}}{dt}\left(\frac{d\Phi_{y}}{dt}\right)^{-1}\text{ where }P_{x}=\frac{L_{x}}{2\pi}\mathrm{Im}\log\bra{\Psi_{0},\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)}e^{2\pi i\hat{x}/L_{x}}\ket{\Psi_{0},\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)}.

(61)

This is analogous to Eq. (
12
), except we were careful to define it for an adiabatic process due to the degeneracy of the ground state subspace.

We can analogously define a sliding fractional Hall crystal as the solution to time-dependent Schödinger equation arising from
\lambda U(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{u}(t))
, starting from the same initial state
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
. We denote the solution as
\ket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}(t)}
. Now, we note that

T^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}H(\boldsymbol{u}(t),0))T_{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}=H(0,\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)),\text{ where }\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)=-eB(L_{x}u_{y}(t),-L_{y}u_{x}(t)).

(62)

From this, we see that

\ket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}(t)}=T_{\boldsymbol{u}(t)}\ket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)}.

(63)

As in the derivation in the main text, the change in the many-body polarization is now split into the part from the magnetic translation operator and the part from flux threading. The rest of the derivation in the main text goes through, replacing the definition of the many-body Chern number by Eq.
61
.

We can similarly compute the Berry phase as

\prod\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}(t_{j})|{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}(t_{j+1})}}=\prod_{j}\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{j})|T^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{j}}T_{\boldsymbol{u}_{j+1}}|{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{j+1})}}=e^{iN_{e}B\delta u^{2}}\prod_{j}\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{j})|T_{\boldsymbol{u}_{j+1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{j}}|{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{j+1})}}.

(64)

which gives us a contribution arising from fractional many-body Berry curvature
\tilde{\Omega}=C/2\pi
.

C.1
Implication for translation algebra

An interesting consequence of the degeneracy can be seen in the algebra of translations. Recall that in the case of a unique ground state, we had
\ket{\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=T_{\boldsymbol{u}}\ket{0;\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, which implies

\ket{\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{j};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\propto T_{\boldsymbol{b}_{j}}\ket{\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}

(65)

for the minimal translations
\boldsymbol{b}_{j}
. Using this, we can compute the Berry phase around a rectangle tended by
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}|\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\braket{\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Phi}|\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\braket{\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Phi}|\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\braket{\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Phi}|\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{iN_{e}B\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}|\braket{\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}|\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}|^{4}=e^{iN_{e}B\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}=e^{i2\pi\frac{N_{e}}{N_{\Phi}}}=e^{i2\pi\nu}

(67)

In fact, this is simply a rephrasing of the anomaly matching constraint(Eq.
28
).
On the other hand, for a fractional state we saw the Berry phase for the smallest loop should be given by

\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}|\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}}\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}|\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2}|\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}\braket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{2}|\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{u}}=e^{i2\pi(\nu-C)}.

(68)

For fractional
C
, this does not agree with the
e^{i2\pi\nu}
. The resolution comes from the fact

\ket{\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{b}_{j};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\neq T_{\boldsymbol{b}_{j}}\ket{\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\Phi}},

(69)

at least for one of
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
or
\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
.
This is a natural consequence of the fractional nature of the state, which requires
\ket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\neq\ket{\Psi_{0};\boldsymbol{\Phi}+2\pi\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}
, as
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is changed adiabatically.

Eq. (
69
) implies translations by
T_{\boldsymbol{b}_{j}}
do not correspond to the translation of a sliding crystal. In the language of emanant symmetries, this means that microscopic translation operators do not get embedded as translation operators in the IR. Instead, it is embedded as an operator
U_{i}
that acts nontrivially on the topological sector:

T_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}^{\mathrm{UV}}\to T^{\mathrm{IR}}_{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}U_{i}.

(70)

For
\nu=1/3
Laughlin state, for example, the action on the topological sector can be chosen to be

U_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}&0\\
0&0&e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{3}}\end{pmatrix},\quad U_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(71)

Then, UV translation operators that map to topological sector preserving IR operators are given by

T_{\boldsymbol{b}_{1}}^{\mathrm{UV}}\to T_{\boldsymbol{b}_{1}}^{\mathrm{IR}},(T^{\mathrm{UV}}_{\boldsymbol{b}_{2}})^{3}\to T^{\mathrm{IR}}_{3\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}.

(72)

This changes the anomaly matching condition to

\beta=B(\nu-m)\phi/\rho\text{ where }m\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{3},

(73)

which is compatible with the fractionalization of the Chern number.

For interested readers, we note that a similar analysis on the role of discrete translation symmetry was performed for Dirac-composite Fermi liquid in the Appendix G of Ref.
[
62
]
.

Appendix D
Emanant symmetry of skyrmions

In this appendix we discuss and contrast phonons in skyrmion crystals
[
45
,
48
]
with the phonons for full Hall crystals that we discussed in the main text. For skyrmions, it has been found that although there are commuting lattice translations, the low energy action has a Berry phase term and thus appear as if the translations are non-commutative
[
46
,
47
]
. In the main text, we saw the reverse is true: for the full Hall crystals, even though the UV translations are magnetic and thus do not commute, the phonons appear as if there are commuting translations. There are no new physical results in this appendix: it is a review of well-known results in a form suitable for our context.

D.1
Computing the Berry phase of a moving skyrmion

We compute the Berry phase associated with a moving skyrmion. This section is a slight modification of the treatment in Ref.
[
46
]
, emphasizing the role played by the underlying lattice.

Let us consider a lattice of spin-
s
, which are located at positions
a(m,n);m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
with periodic boundary conditions in
x
and
y
directions set by
L_{x}=N_{x}a,L_{y}=N_{y}a
. This is meant to model an ionic crystal, upon which a skyrmionic texture can develop.

We consider spin coherent states in this Hilbert space, which can be written as
\otimes_{j}\ket{\hat{n}_{j}}

where
\hat{n}_{j}
is a unit vector. The dynamics of these spin-coherent states can be mapped to the smooth continuum spin configuration
n(\boldsymbol{r})
by identifying

n(\boldsymbol{r})\to\otimes_{j}\ket{\hat{n}_{j}=n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})},

(74)

where
\boldsymbol{r}_{j}
is the coordinate at site
j
. We can define a skyrmionic texture in our discretized Hilbert space by those corresponding to continuum
n(\boldsymbol{r})
with skyrmionic texture. As long as
n(\boldsymbol{r})
is slowly-varying, there is no ambiguity in the assignment of the skyrmion winding number to this lattice configuration.

Similar to the main text, we now compute the Berry phase associated with a trajectory of a displaced skyrmion. We will define the trajectory of the quantum state to be generated by a time dependent texture
n(\boldsymbol{r},t)
with period
T
.
This corresponds to a parametrized set of states
\otimes_{j}\ket{n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j},t)}
. The Berry phase associated with this process is well-defined in the discretized Hilbert space, and is given by

\varphi=\int_{0}^{T}dt\sum_{j}\bra{n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j},t)}i\partial_{t}\ket{n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j},t)}=s\sum_{j}\Omega_{j},

(75)

where
\Omega_{j}
is the solid angle enclosed by the path
n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j},t)
.

Now consider, specifically, a trajectory of the form
n(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{u}(t))
, where
n
is a skyrmionic texture and
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
is the “center-of-mass” motion of the skyrmion. Then each spin undergoes a time evolution according to the local skyrmionic spin texture:

\varphi_{j}=\int_{0}^{T}dt\bra{n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}-\boldsymbol{u}(t)t)}i\partial_{t}\ket{n(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}-\boldsymbol{u}(t))}=sA\lambda_{j},\quad\lambda_{j}=\hat{n}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})\cdot\partial_{x}\hat{n}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})\times\partial_{y}\hat{n}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j}),

(76)

where
A
is the signed area associated with the path
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
, and
\lambda_{j}
is the skyrmion density at location
\boldsymbol{r}_{j}
. The skyrmion density is quantized to be

a^{2}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=4\pi C,

(77)

where
C
is the winding number of the skyrmion. Combining these results, we find the associated Berry phase to be

s\sum_{j}\Omega_{j}=sA\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=\frac{4\pi sA}{a^{2}}C.

(78)

We see that the Berry phase is proportional to the skyrmion number.

Let us now consider the effective action of the skyrmion motion. The only degree of freedom is given by the center of mass position
\boldsymbol{u}
. The action to the lowest order in time derivatives is

S=\frac{1}{2}\int dt(m\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}^{2}+\beta\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}).

(79)

The Berry phase associated with a loop of area
A
is then given by

\varphi=\beta A.

(80)

Comparing IR and UV computations, we find

\beta=\frac{4\pi s}{a^{2}}C.

(81)

Let us now consider this from the point of view of emanant symmetries. On the UV side, the microscopic translation shifts
\boldsymbol{u}
by
(a,0)
and
(0,a)
. Since these translations commute, we must have the Berry phase associated with going around the unit cell, which has area
a^{2}
, must be trivial:

\beta a^{2}=2\pi m\to\beta=\frac{2\pi}{a^{2}}m.

(82)

Since
4\pi s\equiv 0\mod 2\pi
, the Berry phase obtained in Eq.
81
is consistent with the emanant symmetry constraint.

We note that the action(Eq.
79
) is equivalent to that of a particle in a nonzero magnetic field. As a result, skyrmions feel Magnus force — the counterpart of the Lorentz force on charged particles. This mixes longitudinal and transversal modes, resulting in anomalous dispersion for Skyrmion crystals
[
48
]
.

If we quantize the skyrmion action(Eq.
79
), we see that the momentum operators
P_{i}
no longer commute:

[P_{x},P_{y}]=i\frac{4\pi s}{a^{2}}C.

(83)

The quantization of the non-commutativity was discussed in detail from the field theory perspective in Ref.
[
47
]
.

In the analysis above, we focused on the simplified action of the form Eq.
79
, that only keeps the center of mass degree of freedom. Using coherent state path integral, one can instead build a non-linear sigma model for the ferromagnet:

\mathcal{L}=s\dot{\phi}(\cos\theta-1)-\frac{J}{2}\partial_{i}\hat{n}\cdot\partial_{i}\hat{n},

(84)

where the first term corresponds to the Berry phase term. Such a field theory does not admit a well-defined momentum operator, unless you fix a topological sector, and instead one must deal with discrete translation operators
[
63
]
. These discrete translation operators exactly correspond to lattice translation operators in the UV, as was anticipated in the emanant symmetry discussion.
