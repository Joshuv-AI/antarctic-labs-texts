Refining RhIG Prophylaxis Guidelines: Asian-Type DEL as RhD-Positive and Unresolved Dosing Challenges in RhD Epitope-Deficient Variants
Authors: Jeonghun Jo, Eun Suh Sung, Minjeong Nam, Duck Cho
Published: 2026

Rho(D) immune globulin (RhIG) prophylaxis substantially reduces anti-D alloimmunization in RhD-negative pregnancies [1]. However, management strategies for women with RhD variants remain inconsistent, reflecting a persistent gap between accumulating evidence and current clinical guidelines. A notable exception is for women with molecularly defined weak D types (such as weak D types 1, 2, 3, 4.0, and 4.1), which represent a D antigen epitope phenotype characterized by diminished expression of complete D epitopes; for this population, the current guidelines recommend management as RhD-positive without the need for RhIG prophylaxis [2, 3].

Although Asian-type DEL (RHD c.1227G>A) expresses only trace amounts of D antigen, it presents complete D antigen epitopes and does not induce anti-D alloantibodies even after exposure to RhD-positive blood, establishing its immunological equivalence to RhD positivity [4, 5]. Despite this evidence, it has not yet been incorporated in any clinical guideline. Jeong et al. [4] recently advocated for its inclusion and emphasized the urgency of formal adoption. Genotype-based risk stratification has further revealed that the risk of alloimmunization is determined primarily by epitope completeness rather than antigen density. Given the relatively high prevalence of Asian-type DEL in East Asian populations [5, 6], its explicit inclusion in guideline-level recommendations is warranted.

In this context, understanding the geographic distribution of Asian-type DEL is crucial. While this allele is commonly noted in East Asian and Southeast Asian populations and frequently contributes to serological discrepancies, its prevalence in South Asian populations is negligible [7]. This geographic distinction enables laboratories to identify region-specific RhD variants and implement targeted molecular screening strategies. Asian-type DEL is found in 10%–30% of serologically D-negative individuals in East and Southeast Asia, including approximately 17% in Korea [4]. Systematic identification and reclassification of these individuals as RhD-positive could substantially reduce unnecessary RhIG administration and improve stewardship of this limited resource [8]. However, RHD genotyping is not routinely performed for all serologically RhD-negative individuals, posing a practical limitation. A phenotype-guided approach may improve cost-effectiveness by limiting RHD genotyping to serologically D-negative individuals who do not exhibit the C-E-c+e+ (ce) phenotype, which is highly predictive of complete RHD deletion [4].

Most other RhD variants, including partial D variants and weak D or DEL variants associated with epitope loss, are considered to pose a risk of anti-D alloimmunization and, therefore, women with these phenotypes are managed as RhD-negative, with RhIG prophylaxis administered when clinically indicated [9]. However, this clinical categorization continues to evolve. Emerging molecular evidence suggests that individuals with certain weak D variants currently managed as RhD-negative may retain structurally intact D antigen epitopes despite reduced antigen expression. For example, weak D type 15 may not exhibit clinically relevant epitope deficiency [10]. Additionally, transcript analyses, including the minigene study by Fichou et al. [11], have demonstrated that aberrant splicing underlies several RhD variant phenotypes. This mechanism may explain variants such as RHD c.960G>A, which is prevalent in East Asian populations and generates residual full-length transcripts capable of expressing structurally intact D epitopes. Anti-D antibodies in weak D individuals have also been subsequently identified as autoantibodies rather than alloantibodies, corroborating the observations in weak D types 1 and 2. Collectively, these findings suggest that risk stratification, in future, may increasingly rely on molecular characterization and epitope completeness rather than serological phenotype alone. With additional molecular and clinical validation, selected variants may warrant consideration for future inclusion in RhIG-exempt guidelines.

In practice, the pattern of RhIG administration in pregnant women harboring these variants remains inconsistent. The practical complexity of RhD variant management is also reflected in a recent case-based evaluation of transfusion decision-making that included scenarios involving Asian-type DEL, weak D, and RhIG use [12]. A College of American Pathologists-based multicenter survey by Ramsey et al. [13] demonstrated substantial inter-laboratory variability in the management of serological weak D pregnancies. Notably, among 36 laboratories that sought and reported maternal serologic weak D phenotypes, 28% (10/36) laboratories did not recommend RhIG administration even when the newborn was RhD-positive, highlighting a critical gap in which prophylaxis may be inadvertently omitted.

Beyond inconsistent implementation, consensus dosing strategies have not been systematically established. Although standard dosing is generally effective, whether dose optimization based on antigen density or epitope status could further enhance prophylactic efficacy remains unknown. An additional consideration is that RhIG administered to mothers with RhD variants differs mechanistically from its use in truly RhD-negative women: RhIG molecules may bind to the mother’s variant red cells, potentially reducing the amount available to neutralize fetal RhD-positive red blood cells, thereby affecting prophylactic efficiency. RhIG bound to maternal red cells could also theoretically induce mild hemolysis. However, because therapeutic RhIG administered to RhD-positive patients with immune thrombocytopenia is rarely associated with clinically significant hemolysis, the hemolytic risk in mothers with RhD variants presumably is very low [14]. In conclusion, the current evidence supporting guideline-level inclusion of Asian-type DEL in the RhIG-exempt category alongside weak D types 1, 2, 3, 4.0, and 4.1 is compelling and supports formal adoption. Given its high prevalence in East Asian populations and negligible incidence in South Asian population, recognition of the geographic distribution of Asian-type DEL may facilitate more efficient molecular screening strategies and improve RhIG stewardship [7]. Conversely, RhIG prophylaxis remains essential for patients harboring variants associated with clinically relevant D antigen epitope loss. Future studies are needed to define optimal RhIG dosing strategies, refine molecular risk stratification, and address the implementation gaps in current practice.
