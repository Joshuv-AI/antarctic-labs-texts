The “fidget spinner” sign in cardioembolic bilateral medial medullary infarction: an imaging clue to proximal anterior spinal artery occlusion
Authors: Can Çubuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bilateral medial medullary infarction is a rare and clinically severe form of brainstem stroke. A patient presented with acute neurological deficits, and diffusion-weighted imaging revealed a distinctive pattern resembling a “fidget spinner.” CT angiography demonstrated marked asymmetry of the vertebral arteries, with hypoplasia of the right vertebral artery. A review of 97 previously reported cases confirmed the exceptional rarity of cardioembolic etiology in this condition. In this context, the present case represents an uncommon coexistence of a rare anatomical pattern and a rare vascular mechanism. These findings suggest that infarct morphology on diffusion-weighted imaging may reflect underlying vascular anatomy and the level of arterial involvement in bilateral medial medullary infarction.

Introduction

Medial medullary infarction is an uncommon subtype of ischemic stroke, accounting for approximately 0.5%–1.5% of all stroke cases. Bilateral medial medullary infarction is an exceedingly rare entity and is typically associated with severe neurological deficits and poor functional outcomes. 1 While large-artery atherosclerosis represents the most frequently reported etiological mechanism, cardioembolic causes are distinctly uncommon in this setting. 2

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) plays a critical role in the diagnosis of brainstem infarctions, with bilateral medial medullary lesions classically demonstrating the “heart appearance.” 1 However, variations in infarct morphology and their potential relationship to the underlying vascular mechanism remain poorly characterized.

This report describes a rare case of cardioembolic bilateral medial medullary infarction presenting with a distinctive DWI configuration resembling a “fidget spinner,” and proposes a mechanistic explanation linking infarct morphology to proximal anterior spinal artery occlusion.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old male with hypertension was admitted with dysarthria and dysphagia; however, he subsequently underwent rapid neurological deterioration, progressing to quadriplegia and respiratory failure, ultimately necessitating endotracheal intubation. Neurological examination revealed dysarthria, nystagmus, bilateral hypoglossal palsy, severe dysphagia, and quadriplegia.

Computed tomography angiography demonstrated hypoplasia of the right vertebral artery, while the anterior spinal artery was not visualized (Figure 1). Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed bilateral medial medullary infarctions on axial diffusion-weighted sequences, forming a configuration reminiscent of a “fidget spinner” (Figures 2 and 3). Cardiac evaluation identified atrial fibrillation as the most likely embolic source. No alternative stroke mechanism was identified after etiological evaluation.

The patient presented approximately 6 hours after symptom onset; consequently, the time window for intravenous thrombolysis had elapsed, and intravenous tissue plasminogen activator was not administered. Vascular imaging did not demonstrate a large-vessel occlusion amenable to mechanical thrombectomy. The patient was admitted to the neurological intensive care unit and required endotracheal intubation due to progressive respiratory failure; he was subsequently extubated following clinical stabilization. Cardiac evaluation revealed newly diagnosed atrial fibrillation on admission electrocardiography. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated left ventricular hypertrophy with a preserved ejection fraction of 55%, without evidence of intracardiac thrombus, valvular disease, or septal defect. Computed tomography angiography of the cervical and intracranial vessels demonstrated no significant stenosis in the internal carotid arteries or major intracranial vessels. Routine biochemical investigations, including fasting serum glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and a full lipid profile, were within normal limits. Hypercoagulability screening was unremarkable. Furthermore, homocysteine levels and vasculitic screening panel were within normal limits, additionally excluding hyperhomocysteinemia and vasculitis as potential contributing mechanisms. In the absence of identifiable alternative stroke mechanisms, a cardioembolic etiology secondary to atrial fibrillation was considered the most likely diagnosis according to TOAST classification criteria, although branch atheromatous disease cannot be definitively excluded based on a single case observation. At hospital discharge, the patient’s functional status was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale, with a score of 4, indicating moderately severe disability.

Discussion

Bilateral medial medullary infarction is a rare but devastating stroke subtype, most commonly attributed to large-artery atherosclerosis or branch occlusive disease. Cardioembolic involvement of this territory remains exceptionally uncommon.

In normal vascular anatomy, paramedian branches arising from both vertebral arteries supply the medial medullary region.3,4 Although bilateral medial medullary infarction is inherently rare, cardioembolic involvement of this territory is particularly uncommon 1 .

A systematic search of the PubMed database was conducted in February 2026 to identify all previously published cases of bilateral medial medullary infarction. The search terms used were: (“bilateral medial medullary infarction”) OR (“bilateral medial medullary stroke”) OR (“bilateral medullary infarction”). No language restrictions were applied. All case reports and case series reporting individual patients with radiologically confirmed bilateral medial medullary infarction were included. Reviews without individual patient data, editorials, and abstracts without full-text availability were excluded. A total of 97 patients from the available literature (1992–2026) were identified and included in Table 1 (Supplemental material) of the summary.

Among cases with available demographic data, males predominated. Notably, a definite cardioembolic source was documented in only one patient, while cardioembolism could not be excluded in another, underscoring the exceptional rarity of this mechanism in bilateral medial medullary infarction. These findings further support the unusual etiological profile of the present case. Recent data suggest that bilateral medial medullary infarctions with long ventrodorsal extension and characteristic Y- or heart-shaped configurations may be associated with more severe clinical presentations, including respiratory compromise, whereas rostral involvement has not been consistently associated with clinical severity. 77 Within this context, the present case may represent an uncommon convergence of a rare anatomical pattern and an atypical vascular mechanism.

Although diffusion-weighted imaging in bilateral medial medullary infarction most commonly demonstrates the “heart appearance,” 1 other descriptive analogy, such as the “AirPod sign,” 61 has been reported but largely reflects a similar pattern of symmetric paramedian involvement. Distinct infarct configurations associated with differing vascular mechanisms remain insufficiently characterized.

The “fidget spinner” sign may represent a distinct vascular mechanism. Proximal cardioembolic occlusion of the anterior spinal artery originating from a hemodynamically dominant vertebral artery may result in a broader ventral infarction than typically seen in perforator disease. Here, hypoplasia of the right vertebral artery likely conferred left vertebral dominance, predisposing the anterior spinal branch to embolic occlusion and culminating in a more extensive bilateral ischemic territory. This observation raises the possibility that diffusion-weighted infarct morphology may serve as a surrogate marker of the level of arterial occlusion in bilateral medial medullary infarction, potentially helping differentiate proximal anterior spinal artery compromise from distal perforator involvement. Although causality cannot be definitively established from a single observation, the proposed mechanism is anatomically plausible.

In conclusion, while the heart sign, Y sign, and AirPod sign each reflect bilateral paramedian involvement through distal perforating branches, the fidget spinner sign described herein appears to represent a distinct topographic variant, characterized by broader ventral extension consistent with proximal anterior spinal artery territory ischemia rather than isolated perforator occlusion. This morphological distinction may carry etiological implications, potentially differentiating cardioembolic proximal occlusion from perforator-level branch atheromatous disease.

Future accumulation of similar cases may clarify whether specific imaging patterns correspond to distinct vascular mechanisms and improve etiological inference in this rare stroke subtype.

Conclusion

The “fidget spinner” sign may represent an imaging clue to proximal anterior spinal artery occlusion and a potential indicator of cardioembolic involvement in bilateral medial medullary infarction. Recognition of this distinctive radiological pattern may facilitate earlier etiological evaluation and support mechanism-directed secondary prevention. In this context, infarct morphology on diffusion-weighted imaging may provide valuable insight into the underlying stroke mechanism in rare brainstem infarctions.
