Navigating the Conversation: How Can the News Media Responsibly Communicate Drug Use to the Australian Community?
Authors: Steph Kershaw, Jenny Valentish, Jack Nagle, Elizabeth Paton, Emily Stockings, Cath Chapman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNews media plays an integral role in facilitating public discourse, influencing attitudes and shaping policy decisions on drugs. Negative, sensationalist or inaccurate media coverage of drugs can lead to negative or stigmatising attitudes towards people who use drugs or who are associated with drugs. This can and should be changed. The national Mindframe guidelines—which support safe and accurate reporting of suicide, mental health and alcohol and other drug use—have resulted in significant reductions in the financial and health cost ($596 million over a 5‐year period and 107 quality‐adjusted life years gained) associated with suicide in Australia, but are yet to demonstrate similar outcomes for alcohol and other drug use. The upcoming renewal of the Australian National Drug Strategy and ongoing policy discussions across the country represent a key opportunity to address stigma and improve non‐judgemental media reporting on drugs. Less than half of national drug strategies globally provide specific actions to address stigma. The new Australian National Drug Strategy has an opportunity to show international leadership by providing clear actions and targets for addressing drug related stigma, including improving the reporting of alcohol and other drugs through greater investment and support in the dissemination, implementation and training of media in guidelines focused on responsible reporting of these issues.

Commentary

Journalistic media (referred to as news media) plays an integral role in facilitating public discourse, influencing attitudes and opinions and shaping policy decisions on drugs. News media, which encompasses traditional print, broadcasting and digital fora, commonly report on drugs and drug‐related issues. However, the coverage is often negative, sensationalist or inaccurate with a disproportionate focus on crime or justice‐related topics [1, 2, 3]. This can lead to stigmatising attitudes towards people who use drugs, and stigma by association for health workers and family members [4, 5]. Stigma is an enduring mark of social disgrace that leads people to be devalued, isolated and discriminated against [6]. While various health conditions attract harmful stigma, drugs and drug use disorders attract a particularly high level [7, 8] and methamphetamine, heroin and cocaine are more likely to attract stigma than cannabis and alcohol [9, 10]. Several drug policy reform activities have recently been piloted or implemented in Australia including decriminalisation in the Australian Capital Territory [11], the NSW Drug Summit [12] and investment in drug checking services [13, 14, 15]. It is likely we will see an increase in media coverage and discussion of drugs, highlighting the importance of non‐judgemental discourse regarding alcohol and other drug (AOD) use.

There are several salient examples of how particular drugs, minority groups or intersecting issues are maligned in news media [16, 17, 18]. Methamphetamine use, for example, is often referred to in the media as ‘evil’, ‘criminal’ or ‘deviant’ [2, 3, 19]. In 2018, AOD Media Watch found the term ‘ice capital of Victoria’ was used to cover Shepparton, Sale, Hamilton, Horsham, Geelong and Warrnambool with the ‘ice capital of Australia’ identified variously as Western Australia, Darwin, Adelaide and Mount Gambier [20]. Another example is media stereotyping of women who use drugs as ‘bad’ or ‘immoral’ [21]. One headline in 2016, syndicated amongst many publications, was: ‘Children murdered in SA went hungry as slain mother put ice habit ahead of her family’ [22]. The man who murdered the mother and children was not mentioned until the sixth paragraph. Woman's Day, Herald Sun, The Age and The Daily Mail have all used the term ‘ice queens’ to refer to women who use and sell methamphetamine. In 2018, Woman's Day ran an article about three women in a Victorian country town: ‘An underworld trio of glamorous selfie‐loving blondes with a passion for fast cars, guns and cash’ and ‘These evil young women almost destroyed a little Victorian city by peddling an illicit drug to the townsfolk’. Further highlighting the ‘us’ versus ‘them’ divide in news media discourse.

These widespread media representations have a profound negative impact on people who use drugs, who often internalise the negative view of themselves projected by mainstream culture and media [23, 24]. Internalised stigma, or self‐stigma, is a common barrier to uptake of evidence‐based harm reduction services such as needle exchange and safe injecting rooms [25]. This is a critical opportunity lost: an increasing body of evidence indicates that health promotion and harm reduction‐based interventions do work [26]. Yet most federal investment in drug initiatives in Australia remains in law enforcement with less than 2% invested in harm reduction [27]. This is despite evidence that criminalising drug use does not reduce drug use and can lead to increased harms [28]. Meaningful change in public discourse via news media—particularly centring drug use as a health issue—is a crucial step towards challenging how we think about people who use drugs and the policies and services that support them.

The Mindframe Initiative was established in 1999 by Everymind and funded by the Australian Government under the National Suicide Prevention Leadership and Support Program. The purpose of the Mindframe initiative was to develop and implement guidelines to support safe and non‐stigmatising reporting and public communication about suicide and mental ill‐health [29]. The guidelines for sensitive reporting of suicide have been shown to improve media reporting [30]. Pirkis et al. [31] compared coverage pre‐ and post‐introduction of the suicide guidelines and found improvement in overall quality of Australian news media reporting when measured over 5 years. A cost‐effectiveness study in 2022, found the guidelines led to a public health cost‐saving of approximately $596 million over 5 years when compared with no intervention. This is a 94:1 return on investment and an estimated 107 quality‐adjusted life years gained [30]. While the guidelines themselves are not unique, with other media reporting guidelines showing significant similarities in recommendations [32], rather it is the uniquely comprehensive implementation program [33]. To support their uptake, the program also delivers training to build the skills of media professionals, both in the newsroom and in pre‐service training in university journalism programs across the country; offers real‐time support and advice to media on stories and critical incidents; works to embeds guidelines into media codes of practice; and monitors media and responds to community concerns through the StigmaWatch program (administered by SANE Australia) [34]. To complete the program, The Mindframe team also offer resources, training and real‐time support to enable individuals and organisations working with the media as sources to complete the program. This includes government departments and agencies (e.g., the Australian Bureau of Statistics), the mental health and suicide prevention sector, living‐lived experience groups and communities. This wrap‐around or 360° approach, pre‐ and post‐publication or broadcast, approach is intended to ensure that any suicide related material reaching Australian audience is as safe and sensitive as possible.

In 2017, Everymind was commissioned by the Australian Government Department of Health to expand the Mindframe Initiative to include guidelines for the reporting of AOD in the Australian media. Launched in March 2019, the freely available Mindframe AOD guidelines provide information on helpful and harmful ways to communicate about AOD in news media, along with specific guidance on language and inclusion of help‐seeking information [35]. Despite the significant public health success of the suicide reporting guidelines—including Australia's recognition by the World Health Organization as a country that has achieved positive behaviour change for media reporting on suicide [36]—barriers to the effective implementation of the Mindframe AOD guidelines remain. First, ongoing implementation remains unfunded. While the AOD guidelines have been rolled into the broader Mindframe program using a small portion of its core funding, there is no ongoing financial backing to support implementation. Second is time: in a media environment where newsrooms are shrinking but 24‐h news cycles increase load, journalists are time poor [37]. Similarly, in universities, shortened semesters or moves to trimesters make it difficult to add non‐core training components to already packed curricula [38]. Third, unlike the media reporting guidelines for suicide which have been incorporated into national suicide prevention strategies since in 2000 [39], the safe reporting media guidelines for AOD have not yet been incorporated in the Australian National Drug Strategy [40]. Although this is unsurprising as the Mindframe guidelines weren't released until 2019. With Australia's decade long National Drug Strategy due for renewal in 2026 and Australia's Drug and Alcohol Program currently undergoing significant reform, this is a timely opportunity to encourage improved media reporting [41].

Reducing stigma around people who use drugs lies at the heart of responsible media reporting guidelines, yet stigma remains a little addressed topic in broader drug strategy. Globally, less than half of national drug strategies or action plans explicitly mention how they plan to address or reduce stigma and only two mention media as a specific target group for training to improve accurate non‐stigmatising reporting on AOD [42]. In the current Australian National Drug Strategy [40], there are three mentions of stigma, but no specific actions regarding how stigma will be addressed or reduced. An opportunity exists to develop an action plan as part of the new National Drug Strategy to address stigma and discrimination towards people who use drugs, and specifically to support the media in the responsible reporting of drug‐related issues. This could also provide a strong response to the third recommendation from the New South Wales Special Commission on ‘Ice’ (2019) that ‘the NSW Government work with the Commonwealth, other state and territory governments and the Australian Press Council [to] strengthen the implementation of the Mindframe media guidelines, including monitoring of media reporting that may contribute to stigma’ [43]. Additionally, key priority action 9.1 from the 2024 NSW Drug Summit Report urged ‘… that the NSW Government collaborates with relevant stakeholders to strengthen the implementation of the Mindframe guidelines for the media’ [12]. Some action towards implementing this recommendation has commenced, with Mindframe incorporating AOD into its media monitoring and community‐focused program (StigmaWatch). Media reporting to AOD concerns is now monitored by Mindframe, with creators of non‐adherent material contacted and offered advice and support regarding changing or removing material. However, this is only a small step towards the comprehensive implementation training and support program that has made the guidelines on suicide a success. Any approach to addressing stigma in media reporting also needs to work in the face of the attention economy and ‘clickbait’ digital media landscape—where journalists are incentivised to increase web traffic via clicks and shares [44].

News media reporting is pervasive with broad universal reach, and while it is not the only avenue to address stigma, this commentary highlights that without addressing (often negative) influence on the public rhetoric around drugs, it will be difficult for the community to support stigma and harm reduction policies and initiatives [49]. Acknowledging and addressing the undercurrents of stigma and discrimination in the community is as crucial as changing pejorative media depictions. We have a long way to go but collective effort to shape a public discourse of empowerment and support rather than blame or shame is a critical step. The adoption of media reporting guides for AOD is one key first step that news media can take to responsibly communicate drug use to the Australian community.
