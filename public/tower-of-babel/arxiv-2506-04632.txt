Composing Agents to Minimize Worst-case Risk
Guruprerana Shabadi, Rajeev Alur

Abstract
From software development to robot control, modern agentic systems decompose complex objectives into a sequence of subtasks and choose a set of specialized AI agents to complete them. We formalize an agentic workflow as a directed acyclic graph, called an agent graph, where edges represent AI agents and paths correspond to feasible compositions of agents. When deploying these systems in the real world, we need to choose compositions of agents that not only maximize the task success, but also minimize risk where the risk captures requirements like safety, fairness, and privacy. This additionally requires carefully analyzing the low-probability (tail) behaviors of compositions of agents. In this work, we consider worst-case risk minimization over the set of feasible agent compositions. We define worst-case risk as the tail quantile—also known as value-at-risk—of the loss distribution of the agent composition where the loss quantifies the risk associated with agent behaviors. We introduce an efficient algorithm that traverses the agent graph and finds a near-optimal composition of agents by approximating the value-at-risk via a union bound and dynamic programming. Furthermore, we prove that the approximation is near-optimal asymptotically for a broad class of practical loss functions. To evaluate our framework, we consider a suite of video game-like control benchmarks that require composing several agents trained with reinforcement learning and demonstrate our algorithm’s effectiveness in approximating the value-at-risk and identifying the optimal agent composition.

Introduction

Modern agentic workflows orchestrate specialized AI agents to tackle complex tasks demanding a diverse range of skills.
Typically, a high-level planning agent decomposes the main task into a sequence of subtasks and selects appropriate worker agents to execute them.
These worker agents can be powerful pre-trained models, such as large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), or control policies trained with reinforcement learning or imitation learning.
Such systems have demonstrated considerable success in automating tasks across various domains, including software development
[
34
,
24
,
14
]
and robot control
[

,

.

In addition to maximizing task success, deploying agentic systems in the real world requires minimizing various forms of risk.
These risks encompass requirements like safety, fairness, and privacy.
The stochastic nature of both environment and agents means that we have to rigorously analyze low-probability but high-consequence tail behaviors that emerge when multiple agents interact in sequence.
Thus, quantifying and minimizing these compositional risks becomes essential for responsible deployment.

Consider the toy environment MouseNav shown in

Suppose we have access to four control agents:

In this work, we represent feasible agent compositions as an
agent graph
—a directed acyclic graph in which each agent is represented by an edge and an agent composition is given by a directed path in the graph (as seen in
Figure

1
).
Next, we quantify the risk associated with an agent’s behavior with real-valued loss functions.
For example, we can quantify the safety of the control agent
\pi_{1}
with a function
L_{1}
that takes a trajectory of motion of the mouse and returns the negative minimum distance between the trajectory and the traps.
Note that we define the loss to be the negative minimum distance because we prefer being as far as possible from the traps.
Subsequently, we define the loss of a composition of agents as the maximum of the losses incurred by each agent since we are concerned with providing worst-case guarantees.
In the case of the composition
M\xrightarrow{\pi_{1}}A\xrightarrow{\pi_{3}}C
, the loss would be given by
\max(L_{1},L_{3})
and again corresponds to the negative minimum distance between a trajectory and the traps except now the trajectory is a sequence of two trajectories: the first one from executing
\pi_{1}
and then from
\pi_{3}
.

Given an agent graph, our objective is to find the path representing an agent composition that minimizes the
worst-case risk
.
Worst-case risk is defined as the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the loss distribution of the agent composition for a user-defined risk budget
\alpha>0
.
This is also commonly known as the value-at-risk at level
\alpha
denoted
\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}
.
Rephrasing in the context of the MouseNav example, minimizing the worst-case risk would maximize a constant
D>0
such that the chosen composition of agents ensures that the robot mouse stays at least distance
D
away from the traps with probability at least
1-\alpha
.
In the rest of the work, we only assume black-box sampling access to the agents and the input distributions, and so we have to estimate the value-at-risk using samples.
A straightforward approach to minimizing the worst-case risk is to consider each feasible agent composition, estimate its value-at-risk, and pick the optimal composition.
However, this method is inefficient since the graph can have an exponential number of compositions as a function of the number of agents.

To overcome this hurdle, we propose an efficient algorithm that only depends polynomially on the number of agents.
It works by incrementally building a near-optimal path while traversing the agent graph.
At its heart is a quantile estimation method that approximates the value-at-risk of a composition of agents by dividing the risk budget
\alpha
amongst the agents and applying a union bound.
It then finds the optimal allocation of the risk budget over a discretized set of budgets using a dynamic programming algorithm.
We further prove that this approximation is near-optimal asymptotically when the loss of each agent can be represented by an independent random variable.
This independence assumption is reasonable in practice especially when the losses are functions of the agent behavior and do not directly impact task accuracy or performance.

We implement our algorithm and test its performance on a series of reinforcement learning (RL) environments.
Here, we look at long-horizon tasks that require composing several agents trained with RL to complete them.
We consider two forms of risk minimization—safety in navigation and resource consumption—and design suitable loss functions that capture these requirements.
We confirm empirically that our algorithm produces tight approximations of the value-at-risk thereby finding optimal agent compositions and also scales favorably to higher number of agents.

In summary, our contributions consist of the following:

1.

We formalize the worst-case risk minimization objective for agentic workflows as the problem of finding an agent composition that minimizes the value-at-risk of losses quantifying risk (
Section

2
).

2.

Then, we introduce an efficient algorithm that approximates this optimization problem with a quantile estimation method using the union bound and dynamic programming. Furthermore, we prove that this approximation is near-optimal asymptotically under independence assumptions. (
Section

3
)

3.

Finally, we evaluate the algorithm on compositional reinforcement learning benchmarks and; we show that the algorithm succeeds in finding the optimal path and tightly approximates the value-at-risk (
Section

4
).

Related work

Our work builds upon a large body of literature on automated task planning under uncertainty; we refer to
Ghallab et al. 2016
for a comprehensive treatment of this subject.
Traditionally, the planning problem is modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP) over which the expected (un)discounted cumulative losses are minimized using algorithms like value iteration or reinforcement learning
[
31
]
.
Towards designing risk-aware agents, recent works have instead considered the optimization of risk measures that look at the tail of the loss distribution instead of the expected loss.

Ahmadi et al. 2021

Kretínský and Meggendorfer 2018
study risk minimization for reachability and mean-payoff objectives in MDPs, while
Bastani et al. 2022
,
Wang et al. 2023
,
Greenberg et al. 2022
study the same in the context of reinforcement learning.
In these works, the considered risk measures fall in the class of coherent risk measures
[
2
]
that include measures like the conditional value-at-risk and the entropic value-at-risk.
However, all of these works are in contrast to our framework where we aggregate the losses by considering the maximum incurred loss instead of the cumulative loss.

Related lines of research in control and robotics include hierarchical reinforcement learning
[
17
,
7
,
35
,
15
,
21
]
and task and motion planning (TAMP)
[
18
]
in which long-horizon control objectives are decomposed into subtasks that are carried out by motion planners or other low-level controllers trained with RL or imitation learning.
Similar frameworks have been proposed for automatic generation of agentic workflows to automate software development tasks
[
24
,
34
,
14
]
that sequence LLM-agents to complete smaller subtasks.
These methods typically optimize the overall task success probability and do not consider risk minimization.

Lastly, our work is related to conformal prediction
[
29
,
25
]
methods for ML models which also requires quantile estimation of the non-conformity score.
In particular, conformal prediction has been applied to sequential prediction settings like for compositions of models
[

,

]
and for time-series prediction
[

,
4
,

Worst-case Risk Minimization Over Agent Graphs

In this work, an
agent
is any machine learning model
f:X\to\mathfrak{D}(T\times Y)
where
X
is the input domain,
T
is the set of computational traces capturing the set of agent behaviors,
Y
is the output domain, and
\mathfrak{D}(T\times Y)
is the set of probability distributions over
T\times Y
.
For example, for an agent controlling a robot,
X
is the set of initial states,
T
is the set of trajectories of the robot in its environment, and
Y
is the set of final states.
In the case of a language model,
X
is the set of prompts,
Y
is the set of final responses, and
T
is the set of traces used to produce the final response.
This can be the Chain-of-Thought reasoning traces, or interactions with a tool like retrieval from the web or running code.
We consider a distribution over traces and outputs, as opposed to a single output, to enable modeling stochastic agent behaviors along with interactions with a stochastic environment.
We only assume sampling access to the distribution over outputs and denote
(t,y)\sim f(x)
for sampling a trace-output pair
(t,y)
from the agent
f
for the input
x
.

We quantify the risk associated with an agent’s trace by a loss function
L:T\to\mathbb{R}
.
This formulation allows us to decouple risk, which depends on the agent’s overall behavior, from task accuracy, which only depends on the final output.
Thus, the loss function can encode desiderata of the agent’s behavior like safety, privacy, and fairness.
For example, in the MouseNav environment from
Figure

1
, the loss function quantifies how safe a control policy is on a trajectory of the mouse.

Observe that given a distribution over the inputs
\mathcal{D}(X)
, the agent
f
induces distributions
\mathcal{D}_{f}(T)
and
\mathcal{D}_{f}(Y)
over the set of traces and outputs respectively.
We can sample
t\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}(T)
or
y\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}(Y)
by first sampling
x\sim\mathcal{D}(X)
, then sampling
(t,y)\sim f(x)
, and dropping one of the two components.
Evaluating the loss on the distribution of traces gives us a distribution over the losses the agent incurs.
Since we are concerned with providing worst-case guarantees, we focus on analyzing tail behaviors of the loss distribution.
In particular, the quantity we are interested in optimizing is the
value-at-risk
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, denoted
\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}
.

\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}
is a risk measure of the loss distribution that corresponds to the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the distribution.
In other words, a
(1-\alpha)
fraction of the traces will have a loss smaller than the
\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}
.
To formally define it, let
Z_{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f}(T)
be a random variable over the traces of the agent
f
.
Then we can express

\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}[L(Z_{f})]\coloneqq\textnormal{quantile}(L(Z_{f}),1-\alpha)=\inf\left\{q\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbf{Pr}[L(Z_{f})\leq q]\geq 1-\alpha\right\}

(1)

We now shift our attention to compositions of agents. Agents can be composed sequentially to complete long-horizon objectives.
A composition of agents sequentially transforms inputs in which the output of one agent becomes the input for the next agent.
Given a long-horizon objective, we model all feasible agent compositions that achieve the objective as a directed acyclic graph called an
agent graph
.

Definition

(Agent graph)
.

An agent graph
G
is a tuple
(V,E,X,T,F,L,s,t,\mathcal{D}_{s})
with the following components:

1.

A set of vertices
V
and edges

that form a directed acyclic graph.

2.

X
associates a domain
X_{v}
to each vertex
v\in V
.

3.

To each edge

,

associates a trace set

,

associates an agent
f_{e}:X_{u}\to\mathfrak{D}(T_{e}\times X_{v})
, and
L
associates a loss function
L_{e}:T_{e}\to\mathbb{R}
.

4.

s\in V
denotes the source vertex and

is the initial input distribution over the domain
X_{s}
.

5.

t\in V
denotes the terminal vertex and

represents the output domain of the long-horizon objective.

Consider an agent graph
G
.
Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq V^{*}
be the set of directed paths from
s
to
t
.
Consider
p=v_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}\dots\xrightarrow{e_{m}}v_{m+1}\in\mathcal{P}
with

and
v_{m+1}=t
, and
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
the directed edges along the path.
Equipped with the initial input distribution
\mathcal{D}_{s}
, the sequence of agents
(f_{e_{1}},\dots,f_{e_{m}})
induces a joint distribution
\mathcal{D}(T_{e_{1}}\times\cdots\times T_{e_{m}})
which we denote
\mathcal{D}_{p}

We can sample

by sampling

(t_{1},x_{2})\sim f_{e_{1}}(x_{1})

(t_{2},x_{3})\sim f_{e_{2}}(x_{2})
, until
(t_{m},x_{m+1})\sim f_{e_{m}}(x_{m})

We refer to a sample from

We also define a composed loss function along the path
L_{p}:T_{e_{1}}\times\cdots\times T_{e_{m}}\to\mathbb{R}

L_{p}(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\coloneqq\max_{i}\{L_{e_{i}}(t_{i})\}

(2)

which is the maximum of losses incurred by the sequence of trajectories
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})
.
This definition is consistent with our goal of providing worst-case guarantees.
Nonetheless, there may be other sensible definitions; e.g., when the losses represent monetary cost, a natural quantity is the cumulative loss which has been studied previously in the context of risk minimization
[
1
,
19
]
.

We now have all the ingredients to state our optimization objective over an agent graph that we call the
worst-case risk minimization
(WoRM) objective.

Objective

(WoRM)
.

Let
G=(V,E,X,T,F,L,s,t,\mathcal{D}_{s})
be an agent graph and let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq V^{*}
be the set of directed paths from
s

t

For a given risk level

\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}[L_{p}(Z_{p})].

(3)

Rephrasing in words, the optimization objective corresponds to finding a path from
s
to
t
in the agent graph
G
that minimizes the value-at-risk of the
maximum
of the losses incurred by the agents along the path.

Towards optimizing the
WoRM
objective over an agent graph
G
, we remark that a simple procedure to find the optimal path is to estimate the
\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}
along each path and pick the path that minimizes it.
We can estimate the
\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}
by computing the empirical
(1-\alpha)
-quantile on samples drawn from the loss along a path
L_{p}(Z_{p})
.
While this is asymptotically optimal, it can be inefficient when the graph has an exponential number of paths as a function of the number of vertices.

To circumvent this, we present an efficient algorithm that only depends polynomially on the number of vertices in the agent graph and is also asymptotically near-optimal under certain assumptions.

BucketedVaR
is a dynamic programming algorithm that processes the vertices in the agent graph in the topological order and incrementally builds a near-optimal path.

To understand how it works, we first present how we can estimate the value-at-risk of the loss distribution of a single path incrementally.
To this end, consider a path in an agent graph
p=v_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}\dots\xrightarrow{e_{m}}v_{m+1}
with
v_{1}
being the source vertex and
v_{m+1}
the target vertex.
Let
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})\sim\mathcal{D}(T_{e_{1}}\times\cdots\times T_{e_{m}})
be a sequence of random variables representing the sequences of traces of the agents along the path.
Then define
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}
to be real-valued random variables representing the losses incurred by the agents along each edge. Formally, we can write
R_{1}\coloneqq L_{e_{1}}(Z_{1}),\dots,R_{m}\coloneqq L_{e_{m}}(Z_{m})
.
If
L_{p}
is the composed loss function along the path as defined in

2

L_{p}(Z)
as
\max(R_{1},\dots,R_{m})

A key observation that enables the incremental estimation of
\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}[L_{p}(Z)]
(or equivalently, the

\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha_{i}}[R_{i}]

Following this, we can apply the union bound to obtain

\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}[L_{p}(Z)]=\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha}[\max(R_{1},\dots,R_{m})]\leq\max_{i}\{\textnormal{VaR}_{\alpha_{i}}[R_{i}]\}.

(4)

This method allows us to estimate the value-at-risk of each edge loss variable independently and then combine the estimates at the end by computing their maximum.
However, we need to optimize the allocation of the risk budgets

along each edge. Intuitively, allocating a higher risk budget to higher edge losses would result in a lower value-at-risk for that edge since it would correspond to a lower quantile.

BucketedVaR
optimizes this by searching for risk budget allocations in a discretized set of

B=\{0,\frac{\alpha}{d},2\frac{\alpha}{d},\dots,\alpha\}
for a given discretization factor
d\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}

At each vertex

\bar{\alpha}

It achieves this by considering each predecessor

of

to the loss along the edge

The optimal path for

Takeaways.

Through our benchmarks, we have illustrated that we can use our framework to model risk associated with agent compositions like safety and resource consumption.
Then, as seen in
Table

1
, we showed that the
BucketedVaR
algorithm produces estimates of the value-at-risk that are tight with respect to the optimal estimate and also achieves empirical coverage very close to the desired level of
1-\alpha
.
Furthermore, we are able to confirm our theoretical result that the approximation improves with growing sample size and granularity of the risk budget division given by the number of buckets.
We also observe that it scales to longer paths with more agents.

5
Limitations

A limitation of the
BucketedVaR
algorithm is that we require the losses of agents to be independent to obtain tight estimates of the value-at-risk.
For instance, this is violated when an agent produces a safe trajectory and ends in a state from which the next agent cannot be safe.
In such cases, we are forced to rely on the inefficient baseline algorithm.
Yet another limitation of our framework is that we may not always have well defined loss functions that quantify risk like in the case of an LLM agent.
Here, we may have to rely on LLM-as-a-judge methods or human annotations which can be expensive and noisy.

6
Conclusion and future directions

In summary, we introduced a framework for choosing optimal agent compositions to minimize worst-case risk criteria that can encode safety, privacy, and fairness desiderata.
Our dynamic programming approach efficiently allocates the risk budget across agents, supporting tight approximations under mild independence assumptions.
Empirical results on compositional RL benchmarks confirm its effectiveness in identifying optimal paths and quantifying tail behaviors, paving the way for more reliable agentic workflows.

A direction for future work is to incorporate the
BucketedVaR
algorithm in existing agentic frameworks and evaluate it on LLM-agents.
On the theoretical front, an interesting direction is to explore whether we can develop online learning algorithms to minimize worst-case risk.
Such an algorithm would have to pick a composition of agents at every turn and minimize long-term regret with respect to the optimal composition.

We first establish the correctness of the algorithm by showing that, with high probability, the value-at-risk estimate

\alpha_{i}
be the risk budget allocated to edge
e_{i}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}=\alpha

For each edge
e_{i}

R_{i}=L_{e_{i}}(Z_{i})
be the random variable representing the loss along that edge. The loss along the entire path is given by
L_{p}(Z_{p})=\max(R_{1},\ldots,R_{k-1})

By the union bound property of probabilities, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\max(R_{1},\ldots,R_{k-1})>q\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\Pr[R_{i}>q]

(7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}

(8)

\displaystyle=\alpha

(9)

where we set
q
such that
\Pr[R_{i}>q]=\alpha_{i}

This implies that

Theorem

2
.

Under the independence assumption of losses along edges, we can provide a tighter analysis of the optimality of the BucketedVaR algorithm.

Let
p^{*}=v_{1}^{*}\rightarrow v_{2}^{*}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow v_{k^{*}}^{*}
be the optimal path minimizing the value-at-risk, where
v_{1}^{*}=s
and
v_{k^{*}}^{*}=t
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,k^{*}-1\}
, let
e_{i}^{*}=(v_{i}^{*},v_{i+1}^{*})
be the edges along this path.

Let
R_{1}^{*},\ldots,R_{k^{*}-1}^{*}
be the independent random variables representing the losses along the edges of the optimal path. The loss along the entire optimal path is given by
L_{p^{*}}(Z_{p^{*}})=\max(R_{1}^{*},\ldots,R_{k^{*}-1}^{*})
.

For independent random variables, the CDF of the maximum is the product of the individual CDFs

F_{\max}(x)=\Pr[\max(R_{1}^{*},\ldots,R_{k^{*}-1}^{*})\leq x]=\prod_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}\Pr[R_{i}^{*}\leq x]=\prod_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}F_{i}(x)

(12)

Now, suppose we optimally allocate the risk budget
\alpha
among the edges of the optimal path, assigning
\alpha_{i}^{*}
to edge
e_{i}^{*}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}\alpha_{i}^{*}=\alpha
.

The optimal allocation would ensure that the
(1-\alpha_{i}^{*})
-quantile is the same for all edges, which we’ll denote as
q^{*}
. This means
F_{i}(q^{*})=1-\alpha_{i}^{*}
for all
i
. Since BucketedVaR searches over discretized buckets, as
d\rightarrow\infty

Under this optimal allocation, the CDF of the maximum at
q^{*}
is

\displaystyle F_{\max}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}F_{i}(q^{*})

(13)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}(1-\alpha_{i}^{*})

(14)

To upper bound this product, we use the inequality
(1-x)\leq e^{-x}
which holds for all
x\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}(1-\alpha_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}e^{-\alpha_{i}^{*}}

(15)

\displaystyle=e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k^{*}-1}\alpha_{i}^{*}}

(16)

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha}

(17)

For all
\alpha>0

e^{-\alpha}=1-\alpha+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2!}-\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3!}+\frac{\alpha^{4}}{4!}-\ldots

\alpha>0

h^{\prime}(\alpha)=-1+\alpha+e^{-\alpha}

\alpha=0

h^{\prime}(0)=0

Computing the second derivative,
h^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)=1-e^{-\alpha}

h^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)>0

\alpha>0
