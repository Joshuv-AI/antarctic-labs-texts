Combined Laparoscopic and Colonoscopic Retrieval of Multiple Ingested Magnetic Beads Without Enterotomy in Children: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Chong Shen, Yaqiang Hong, Hailin Li, Hao Chen, Jingfeng Bao, Wenjun Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMagnetic foreign body ingestion is one of the high-risk accidental injuries in pediatric emergency medicine. Especially when multiple magnets are swallowed, they can attract each other rapidly and cause sustained compression of the intervening intestinal walls, which may lead to necrosis and perforation. This report presents a successful pediatric case of retrieving multiple magnetic beads from the ileum using laparoscopy combined with colonoscopy without enterotomy, aiming to provide practical insights for individualized, organ-preserving management of these patients.Case PresentationA 4-year and 11-month-old boy presented to the emergency department approximately 1 hour after ingesting four high-powered magnetic beads. Initial emergency upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed no foreign body in the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum. Conservative management was initiated with close clinical and radiographic monitoring. Serial abdominal radiographs over three consecutive days demonstrated persistent localization of the magnetic beads in the right lower abdomen without migration. To avoid the risks such as prolonged intestinal compression and subsequent necrosis, we performed an emergency laparoscopic exploration. Intraoperatively, the intestinal tract was examined and gently manipulated to ensure the magnetic beads passed smoothly through the ileocecal valve as a whole, allowing complete removal through colonoscopy. Oral intake of fluids was resumed on postoperative day 1, and the child was discharged on postoperative day 3 without any complications.ConclusionMultiple magnetic foreign bodies retained in proximity to the ileocecal valve without intestinal wall compression or necrosis may be suitable candidates for combined laparoscopic and endoscopic retrieval without enterotomy, thereby optimizing intestinal preservation and facilitating patient recovery.

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion is a frequent pediatric emergency, with peak incidence occurring in children aged 6 months to 4 years. While magnetic foreign bodies account for only approximately 1.97% of all ingested objects, the incidence of adverse events caused by them has increased rapidly in recent decades.1–3 The widespread application of high-powered rare-earth magnets (such as NdFeB, neodymium-iron-boron) in children’s toys is a key contributing factor. When multiple magnets are ingested, they can easily attract each other and result in transmural apposition across adjacent loops of bowel, causing sustained compression of the intervening intestinal wall, leading to ischemia, necrosis, perforation, peritonitis, and even septic shock.4,5

A clinical report from the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) Endoscopy Committee indicates that only about 15% of patients can achieve spontaneous passage of magnets through conservative treatment. In contrast, most children who have swallowed multiple magnets often need to undergo endoscopic or surgical treatment.4 Thus, magnet ingestion in pediatric patients is a serious health issue that deserves attention. Previous interventions at pediatric centers have typically relied on single laparoscopic exploration or open surgery, and in certain cases even secondary surgical procedures, with rare instances of combined treatment approaches performed during a single operation.3,6,7 In this article, we present a case of successful laparoscopic combined with colonoscopic removal of multiple ingested magnetic beads without enterotomy, and conduct a literature review of magnetic foreign bodies ingestion in children, with the aim of providing a reference for clinical pediatric surgeons.

Case Presentation

A 4-year and 11-month-old boy presented to the emergency department 1 hour after unintentional ingestion of four magnetic beads (Figure 1). On admission, his vital signs were stable: temperature 36.0 °C; pulse rate 96 beats per minute; respiratory rate 24 breaths per minute; blood pressure 107/66 mmHg. Physical examination revealed no abdominal tenderness, guarding, fever, or vomiting. Initial upright abdominal X-ray revealed multiple high-density, radiopaque foreign bodies in the upper-to-mid abdomen (Figure 2A and B), highly suggesting that the ingested magnets could be still in the upper digestive tract (stomach or duodenum). Following clinical assessment and routine pre-endoscopic workup, urgent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed for prompt retrieval of multiple magnetic foreign bodies. Unfortunately, careful endoscopic examination of the esophagus, gastric lumen, duodenal bulb and descending duodenum failed to identify any retained foreign bodies (Figure 3A–D). Subsequent abdominal X-ray showed that the position of foreign bodies had changed to the right lower abdomen. Given that the child had no signs of acute abdominal conditions, and considering the family’s informed preference for conservative treatment, the patient was initiated on bowel rest and intravenous fluid support. Serial abdominal radiographs obtained over three consecutive days revealed persistent localization of the foreign bodies in the right lower abdomen without migration. The patient began experiencing occasional abdominal pain, and follow-up radiography confirmed foreign bodies fixation on day 4 after admission (Figures 4A and B). It could not be ruled out that the magnetic beads had attracted and adhered to the intestinal wall, even causing intestinal wall compression, necrosis, and perforation. After communicating the condition with the family again, the laparoscopic exploration was undertaken. During the operation, patient was positioned supine. A 10-mm trocar was introduced at the umbilicus, and two 5-mm trocars were placed in the left lower abdomen and midline of the lower abdomen. During retrograde exploration from the ileocecal junction, the atraumatic grasper was attracted by magnetic beads at approximately 15 cm, confirming their retention within the terminal ileum. Comprehensive inspection of the adjacent intestinal segments demonstrated no evidence of ischemia, necrosis, perforation, or adhesions. When attempting to grasp the proximal bowel wall during surgery, the beads exhibited free mobility, indicating absence of mucosal adherence. Therefore, we gently manipulated the affected intestinal segment to facilitate distal migration of the beads. All four magnetic beads subsequently traversed the ileocecal valve en bloc into the cecum and advanced into ascending colon (Figure 5A). Concurrently, endoscopic support was mobilized. The beads were completely retrieved under colonoscopy using a retrieval net (Figures 5B and C), without causing any severe mucosal injury or significant hemorrhage. The combined laparoscopic and endoscopic procedure was completed in 1 hour and 53 minutes. Oral intake was resumed on postoperative day 1 with clear fluids, followed by progressive advancement to a regular diet, unaccompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 in stable condition. As shown in Table 1, accidental ingestion of magnetic foreign bodies occurs predominantly in male children, with affected individuals ranging in age from 1 to 11 years. Multiple retrospective clinical studies have consistently reported that children ingesting fewer magnets tend to be significantly older than those ingesting larger numbers. Among these patients, abdominal pain and vomiting were the most frequent initial symptoms, followed by abdominal distension or fever. Alansari et al5 reported a significantly higher incidence of clinical manifestations in children who ingested multiple magnetic foreign bodies compared with those who ingested a single magnet (60.2% vs 8.9%). Jin et al14 observed that surgical intervention was strongly associated with the presence of clinical symptoms (62.32% vs 0.00%), which is usually due to acute abdominal conditions such as intestinal obstruction, perforation or torsion caused by magnetic foreign bodies.

Upright abdominal X-ray remains the first-line imaging modality for suspected magnetic foreign body ingestion. When the X-ray diagnosis is difficult or the quantity and location cannot be determined, computed tomography (CT) can be selected. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrates limited sensitivity for detecting magnetic foreign bodies and is therefore not routinely recommended as an initial diagnostic tool in current guidelines and most published studies. However, Xin et al17 reported that high-frequency ultrasound may aid in the early detection of gastrointestinal wall compression or perforation.18 This is a beneficial supplement to radiological examination and may reduce radiation damage and unnecessary surgical trauma to children. Of course, laboratory parameters are also important evidences for guiding the clinical management. Li et al1 reported significantly elevated peripheral white blood cell (WBC) counts and C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations in patients with confirmed intestinal perforation compared with those without (p < 0.05), indicating that elevated WBC count and/or CRP concentration are associated with perforation. In such cases, surgical treatment should be performed as soon as possible. These findings are also corroborated by Chen et al.6

Management of magnet ingestion is guided by the number and anatomic location of ingested magnets, as well as the presence or absence of complications such as gastrointestinal perforation, fistula formation, or peritonitis. According to the guidelines issued by NASPGHAN, ingestion of a single magnet typically warrants observation and conservative management, whereas ingestion of two or more magnets requires prompt endoscopic or surgical intervention, even if the patient is currently asymptomatic.4 The studies included in this research indicate that laparoscopic surgery is the preferred surgical approach in most pediatric surgical centers. Compared with laparotomy, laparoscopic exploration has the advantages of directly visualizing the location of foreign bodies and checking for intestinal necrosis or perforation. Moreover, it supports precise intraoperative planning for conversion to laparotomy, allowing for targeted incision placement that minimizes tissue trauma and promotes faster postoperative recovery.11 A recent study from China shows that the average hospital stay for laparoscopic surgery is shorter than that for laparotomy.6

Based on previous research, it has been found that children who have swallowed magnets often have a slow onset and atypical symptoms.1,18 Once diagnosis and treatment are delayed, it can lead to life-threatening serious consequences. Nevertheless, consensus on optimal timing for clinical intervention remains lacking. A multicenter study conducted in the Middle East reported that ingested multiple magnets can rapidly approximate across intestinal loops within 12–48 hours and may cause necrosis and perforation.5 Huang et al recommended that children who swallowed multiple magnets within 4 hours and had X-ray confirmation that the magnets were in the stomach should undergo emergency gastroscopy as soon as possible. For those with typical clinical symptoms (such as abdominal pain, vomiting, abdominal distension, fever, etc) and radiographic confirmation of retained foreign bodies or with acute abdominal conditions, emergent surgical intervention is indicated.2

Significant heterogeneity exists in clinical practice regarding the optimal timing of intervention for asymptomatic gastrointestinal magnetic foreign bodies lodged between the ligament of Treitz and the ileocecal valve—a region representing the most frequent site of retention. NASPGHAN guidelines recommend that pediatric surgical centers with appropriate expertise and endoscopic capability perform urgent enteroscopy for retrieval.4 In contrast, Zhang et al advocate serial abdominal X-ray over 3–5 days to monitor transit dynamics,11 whereas Chen et al propose a conservative observation period of 4–6 days, with emergent surgical exploration indicated if radiographic imaging demonstrates no change in magnet position across two consecutive days.6 Collectively, the treatment of children who have swallowed magnetic foreign bodies should be more individualized. Clinicians should attach great importance to such patients and make comprehensive decisions on treatment plans based on clinical manifestations, symptoms, and auxiliary examinations.

This case report describes a rare instance in which combined laparoscopic and endoscopic retrieval was used to remove magnetic beads from the gastrointestinal tract of a pediatric patient. In most cases, surgical intervention (laparoscopic surgery or laparotomy) is indicated when radiographic surveillance over multiple days demonstrates persistent foreign body impaction. This patient underwent urgent laparoscopy after three consecutive days of static imaging findings and the emergence of symptomatic abdominal discomfort. Ultimately, complete retrieval was achieved through seamless intraoperative collaboration between laparoscopy and colonoscopy. The feasibility and safety of this approach relied on several critical determinants: first, the limited number of magnetic beads ingested without mucosal adherence or mechanical compression of the intestinal wall avoided ischemia, necrosis, or perforation; second, the magnetic beads were located near the ileocecal region, facilitating controlled laparoscopic manipulation to promote passage across the ileocecal valve and enabling subsequent endoscopic intervention; third, the availability of an experienced endoscopy team capable of immediate, real-time colonoscopic intervention during the procedure. Owing to this multidisciplinary collaboration and effective bowel protection strategy, the patient resumed oral intake on postoperative day 2 and was discharged home uneventfully, resulting in a markedly reduced length of hospital stay.

Prevention of magnetic foreign body ingestion in children remains the paramount clinical and public health priority. The healthcare institutions should strengthen regulatory oversight and public health education to make parents and caregivers of pediatric patients fully aware of the dangers of magnetic foreign objects. Clinicians, caregivers, and guardians share a collective responsibility to maintain vigilance and implement preventive measures to mitigate injury risk and safeguard children’s health and safety.

Conclusion

Management of pediatric patients with multiple ingested foreign bodies should be more individualized, with therapeutic decisions based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical status, symptoms, and ancillary examinations. For patients presenting with clinical symptoms, diagnostic laparoscopy is the initial procedure of choice. When multiple ingested foreign bodies are retained in proximity to the ileocecal valve without intestinal wall compression or necrosis, such cases may be suitable for combined endoscopic retrieval without enterotomy.
