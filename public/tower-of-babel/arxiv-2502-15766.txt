Reversible Imprinting and Retrieval of Quantum Information: Experimental Verification of the Quantum Memory Matrix Hypothesis
Florian Neukart, Eike Marx, Valerii Vinokur

Abstract
We present a series of quantum computing experiments designed to test a central prediction of the Quantum Memory Matrix (QMM) hypothesis—that quantum information can be locally stored in finite-dimensional cells of space-time and later retrieved in a fully unitary and reversible manner. Our work encompasses five distinct experiments: a basic three-qubit imprint-retrieval cycle, an extended five-qubit model implementing two parallel cycles, and variations incorporating dynamic evolution and controlled error injection. In each case, a field qubit is prepared in an arbitrary superposition, and its state is imprinted onto memory qubit(s) via controlled-R y gates, with subsequent controlled-SWAP operations retrieving the stored information into output qubit(s). Execution on an IBM Quantum Processing Unit using the Qiskit Runtime service yielded significant correlations between the initially prepared field states and the retrieved outputs, with fidelities that, while subject to hardware noise and decoherence, consistently demonstrate the reversible and unitary nature of the process. These results not only confirm the basic imprint-retrieval cycle as predicted by the QMM hypothesis but also establish a scalable experimental methodology that may ultimately contribute to resolving challenges such as the black hole information paradox and advancing our understanding of quantum gravity.

Introduction

The unification of quantum mechanics (QM) and general relativity (GR) remains one of the greatest challenges in theoretical physics. Traditional approaches—such as loop quantum gravity and string theory—have provided valuable insights into quantum space-time. However, unresolved issues like the black hole information paradox and ultraviolet (UV) divergences in quantum field theory continue to motivate alternative perspectives. The Quantum Memory Matrix (QMM) hypothesis
[
Neukart et al.(2024)
,
Neukart et al.(2025)
]
offers a distinct framework, proposing that space-time at the Planck scale consists of discrete quantum memory cells rather than a classical continuum. Each cell, labeled by an index
x
in a set
\mathcal{X}
, is associated with a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{x}
, forming the total quantum state of space-time:

\mathcal{H}_{\text{QMM}}=\bigotimes_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{H}_{x}.

(1)

This structure naturally introduces a UV cutoff, as each cell has a bounded number of degrees of freedom. Quantum fields interacting with these cells imprint information through a local imprint operator
\hat{I}_{x}
, which can be expressed as:

\hat{I}_{x}=F\bigl[\hat{\phi}(x),\,\partial_{\mu}\hat{\phi}(x),\,\dots\bigr],

(2)

where
\hat{\phi}(x)
represents the field operator at position
x
. The stored information can be retrieved via unitary operations, ensuring that quantum evolution remains reversible. The overall dynamics of the combined system (quantum fields and QMM cells) are governed by a Hermitian Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{fields}}+\hat{H}_{\text{QMM}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}},

(3)

with time evolution described by:

\hat{U}(t)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}t\right).

(4)

This formulation preserves unitarity and offers a mechanism for encoding quantum information in discrete space-time structures.

One of the most compelling applications of the QMM hypothesis is its potential resolution of the black hole information paradox. In semiclassical gravity, black hole evaporation appears to destroy the quantum state of infalling matter. The QMM framework suggests instead that this information is imprinted onto local quantum memory cells surrounding the black hole, allowing for its gradual retrieval through interactions with outgoing Hawking radiation. This provides a local, unitary mechanism for information preservation without requiring exotic nonlocal processes.

To experimentally investigate these ideas, we designed a series of quantum circuits that simulate the imprint–retrieval process. Using controlled-R
y
gates to imprint a field qubit’s state onto a memory qubit and controlled-SWAP operations for retrieval, we implemented multiple imprint–retrieval cycles of varying complexity—from a basic three-qubit model to an extended five-qubit configuration representing parallel imprint–retrieval operations. Executed on an IBM Quantum Processing Unit via the Qiskit Runtime service (
[
Qiskit Development Team(2023)
]

Neukart et al.(2025)
]
), describe our experimental methodology, and discuss the results that validate the predicted imprint-retrieval mechanism. This work lays a foundation for further investigations into quantum gravity, error correction, and the role of space-time as a dynamic quantum memory.

Theoretical Background

2.1
Quantum Memory Matrix (QMM) Hypothesis

The QMM hypothesis
[
Neukart et al.(2024)
,
Neukart et al.(2025)
]
proposes that space-time at the Planck scale consists of discrete, finite-dimensional quantum memory cells rather than a classical continuum. Each cell, labeled by an index
x\in\mathcal{X}
, is associated with a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{x}
, and the total quantum state of space-time is given by Eq.
1
. This structure naturally introduces a UV cutoff, as each cell has a finite number of degrees of freedom. When a quantum field interacts with a cell, information is stored through an
imprint operator

\hat{I}_{x}
, which acts on
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and encodes local quantum information:

\hat{I}_{x}=F\Bigl[\hat{\phi}(x),\,\partial_{\mu}\hat{\phi}(x),\,\dots\Bigr].

(5)

Retrieval of the stored information is achieved through local, unitary operations. The overall dynamics of the system—including quantum fields and QMM cells—are governed by a Hermitian Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{fields}}+\hat{H}_{\text{QMM}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}},

(6)

ensuring unitary time evolution:

\hat{U}(t)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}t\right).

(7)

This guarantees the imprint–retrieval process is fully reversible.

The QMM framework is built on several fundamental principles:

•

Unitarity:
The evolution of the system is unitary, ensuring that quantum information is preserved.

•

Locality:
Imprinting and retrieval occur within individual Planck-scale cells, respecting the causal structure of space-time.

•

Gauge Invariance:
When extended to gauge fields, imprint operators must be gauge invariant (or at least covariant). For example, in a U(1) gauge field
A_{\mu}(x)
, the imprint operator can be constructed using the gauge-invariant field strength tensor
F_{\mu\nu}(x)=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}(x)-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}(x)
, preserving the underlying symmetries of the theory.

2.2
Relevance to the Black Hole Information Paradox

The black hole information paradox arises from the semiclassical prediction that black hole evaporation leads to the apparent loss of information about the matter that formed the black hole. Traditional resolutions often invoke nonlocal mechanisms, such as holographic encoding or wormhole-induced entanglement. In contrast, the QMM hypothesis provides a local, unitary solution: as matter collapses, its quantum state is imprinted onto surrounding QMM cells.

Let
|\psi_{\text{in}}\rangle
denote the state of the collapsing matter. Each nearby QMM cell records a portion of this state through the imprint operator
\hat{I}_{x}
. As the black hole evaporates, outgoing Hawking radiation interacts with these memory cells, gradually retrieving the stored information, ensuring that the final state,

|\Psi_{\text{final}}\rangle=\hat{U}_{\text{total}}|\psi_{\text{in}}\rangle,

(8)

remains pure. This mechanism preserves unitarity without requiring nonlocal effects.

Beyond addressing the information paradox, the QMM framework also provides a natural ultraviolet cutoff, mitigating divergences in quantum field theories
[
Wilson(1974)
]
. In this way, QMM offers a mathematically grounded pathway toward reconciling quantum mechanics with gravity.

Experimental Design and Methodology

3.1
Overview of the Imprint–Retrieval Cycle

The QMM hypothesis predicts that quantum information can be locally stored in finite-dimensional cells of space-time and later retrieved in a unitary manner. In our framework, each cell is modeled as a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{x}
, forming the total state of space-time (Eq.
1
). When a quantum field, represented by a state
|\psi\rangle
, interacts with a cell, an
imprint operator

\hat{I}_{x}
encodes local quantum information:

\hat{I}_{x}=F\Bigl[\hat{\phi}(x),\,\partial_{\mu}\hat{\phi}(x),\,\dots\Bigr].

(9)

Retrieval occurs via a complementary unitary operation
\hat{U}_{\text{retrieval}}
, leading to an overall imprint–retrieval process:

\hat{U}_{\text{total}}=\hat{U}_{\text{retrieval}}\,\hat{U}_{\text{imprint}},

(10)

which satisfies
\hat{U}_{\text{total}}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{\text{total}}=I
, ensuring reversibility. This mechanism preserves unitarity and provides a local resolution to the black hole information paradox by avoiding nonlocal effects
[
Hossenfelder(2013)
,
Neukart et al.(2024)
]
.

3.2
Quantum Circuit Architecture

To test the imprint–retrieval cycle experimentally, we designed quantum circuits of increasing complexity. The unitary evolution of the system is approximated as:

\hat{U}_{\text{total}}=\hat{U}_{\text{retrieval}}\,\hat{U}_{\text{evolution}}\,\hat{U}_{\text{imprint}},

(11)

where:

•

\hat{U}_{\text{imprint}}
is implemented via controlled-R
y
(CRY) gates,

•

\hat{U}_{\text{evolution}}
is modeled using single-qubit phase rotations (R
z
),

•

\hat{U}_{\text{retrieval}}
is executed through controlled-SWAP (CSWAP) gates.

3.4
Gate Selection Rationale

The chosen quantum gates are critical for modeling the imprint–retrieval process:

•

Controlled-R
y
(CRY) Gate:
Conditionally rotates the target (memory) qubit based on the control (field) qubit:

\text{CRY}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&R_{y}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

•

Controlled-SWAP (CSWAP) Gate:
Exchanges the states of two target qubits based on a control qubit’s state, ensuring information retrieval remains unitary.

4.6
Discussion of Noise and Error Sources

While our results support the QMM imprint–retrieval mechanism, observed fidelity was impacted by:

•

Gate Errors:
Imperfections in CRY and CSWAP gate implementations.

•

Decoherence:
Loss of quantum information due to limited coherence times.

•

Measurement Errors:
Readout inaccuracies distorting outcome distributions.

Despite these factors, the consistent correlations between field and output qubits across all experiments confirm that QMM-inspired imprint–retrieval cycles preserve quantum information in a unitary manner.

5
Discussion

5.1
Interpretation in the Context of QMM

The experimental results presented in this work serve as a compelling proof-of-principle for the QMM hypothesis. Our experiments model the imprint–retrieval process using the unitary operator

\hat{U}_{\text{total}}=\hat{U}_{\text{retrieval}}\,\hat{U}_{\text{evolution}}\,\hat{U}_{\text{imprint}},

(17)

where
\hat{U}_{\text{imprint}}
is realized via controlled-
R_{y}
gates,
\hat{U}_{\text{evolution}}
simulates the system’s temporal evolution via
R_{z}
rotations, and
\hat{U}_{\text{retrieval}}
is implemented using controlled-SWAP (CSWAP) gates.

In the simple three-qubit model, the field qubit (Q
0
) is initially prepared in the superposition state:

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\cos\frac{\pi}{6}|0\rangle+\sin\frac{\pi}{6}|1\rangle.

(18)

Ideally, after the imprint and retrieval process, the output qubit (Q
2
) should reproduce
|\psi_{0}\rangle
exactly if
\hat{U}_{\text{total}}
were perfect. Our measured fidelity of approximately 77% suggests that, despite the presence of noise and gate imperfections, a significant fraction of the original state is successfully recovered. This fidelity is quantified as:

F=\frac{N_{\text{match}}}{N_{\text{total}}},

(19)

where
N_{\text{match}}
represents the number of instances where Q
0
and Q
2
are in the same state, and
N_{\text{total}}
is the total number of trials.

Furthermore, the five-qubit experiment—designed to simulate two independent imprint–retrieval cycles—demonstrates that the QMM mechanism scales effectively to multiple parallel channels. Here, the field qubit imprints its state onto two separate memory qubits via independent CRY operations, and controlled-SWAP gates subsequently retrieve the information into two distinct output qubits. While the increased circuit complexity led to a reduction in fidelity, strong correlations between Q
0
and both output qubits confirm the feasibility of local quantum information storage in finite-dimensional cells, reinforcing the foundational principles of the QMM hypothesis.

5.2
Comparison with Theoretical Predictions

The QMM hypothesis predicts that, in an ideal noise-free scenario, the imprint–retrieval process should be entirely reversible, leading to a fidelity
F\rightarrow 1
. In our experiments, the observed deviations from perfect fidelity are primarily attributed to well-known sources of error on current quantum processing unit (QPU) hardware, including decoherence, gate imperfections, and measurement errors.

Mathematically, the expected transformation in an ideal system is modeled as:

|\psi_{0}\rangle|0\rangle\xrightarrow{\text{CRY}}|\psi_{0}\rangle|\psi_{\text{mem}}\rangle\xrightarrow{\text{CSWAP}}|\psi_{0}\rangle|\psi_{\text{out}}\rangle,

(20)

where, ideally,
|\psi_{\text{out}}\rangle=|\psi_{0}\rangle
. Our experimentally observed fidelity of approximately 77% in the three-qubit experiment, and lower yet significant correlations in more complex circuits, are consistent with predictions that account for real-world hardware limitations
[
Peskin and Schroeder(1995)
,
Ashtekar and Lewandowski(2004)
,
Maldacena(1998)
,
Weinberg(1995)
]
.

These results confirm that the imprint–retrieval mechanism functions as expected, validating the core principle that local, reversible quantum memory—conceptually analogous to discrete Planck-scale cells—can preserve quantum information. This is particularly significant for addressing challenges such as the black hole information paradox, where a fully unitary evolution is required to resolve the apparent loss of information during black hole evaporation.

5.3
Suggestions for Improvement and Future Directions

While our experiments provide strong empirical support for the QMM hypothesis, several improvements could enhance the fidelity of the imprint–retrieval process:

•

Error Mitigation Techniques:
Implementing advanced methods such as measurement error mitigation, dynamical decoupling, and zero-noise extrapolation could reduce the impact of noise and decoherence.

•

Quantum State Tomography:
Performing full quantum state tomography would allow for a more detailed characterization of the retrieved state, providing a deeper understanding of information transfer fidelity.

•

Circuit Optimization:
Improved transpilation techniques and hardware-specific optimizations could minimize gate errors and enhance execution fidelity.

•

Increased Shot Count:
Running experiments with a larger number of shots would improve statistical accuracy and reduce uncertainties in fidelity estimates.

In the long term, incorporating these improvements into larger-scale circuits—potentially integrating additional interactions such as non-Abelian gauge fields—will be essential for extending the QMM framework. Such developments could pave the way for a fully unified quantum description of space-time, and matter.

5.4
Broader Implications

Beyond validating the QMM hypothesis, our results suggest broader implications for quantum information science and fundamental physics. If finite-dimensional quantum memory structures akin to QMM cells can reliably store and retrieve quantum information, they could serve as a foundation for:

•

Quantum Error Correction:
The QMM-inspired imprint–retrieval mechanism could be adapted for novel error-correction techniques that leverage structured memory units.

•

Quantum Gravity Models:
The demonstrated reversibility and unitarity of imprint–retrieval cycles provide experimental motivation for alternative approaches to quantizing space-time.

•

Black Hole Information Retention:
The ability to store quantum states in discrete memory units aligns with proposals for resolving the black hole information paradox via local unitarity rather than nonlocal mechanisms.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have designed and implemented a series of quantum computing experiments that provide strong proof-of-principle evidence in support of the QMM hypothesis. Our experiments, executed on a real IBM QPU using the Qiskit Runtime API, demonstrate that quantum information can be locally imprinted onto a memory cell and later retrieved in a fully reversible and unitary manner. Both the simple three-qubit model and the extended five-qubit model yielded significant correlations between the initially prepared field state and the retrieved output state. These outcomes confirm that local, finite-dimensional quantum memories—analogous to Planck-scale cells—can reliably store and recover quantum information without loss. These positive findings validate the core premise of the QMM hypothesis: that space-time can function as a dynamic quantum memory, preserving unitarity even in extreme scenarios such as black hole evaporation. The successful demonstration of the reversible imprint-retrieval cycle corroborates theoretical predictions and establishes a scalable experimental methodology. In particular, the extended five-qubit model, which simulates multiple parallel imprint-retrieval cycles, underscores the feasibility of scaling up this approach to simulate a network of QMM cells. Looking forward, we remain optimistic about further enhancing the experimental fidelity through advanced error mitigation techniques, state tomography, and circuit optimization. Moreover, our future work will aim to incorporate additional interactions—such as non-Abelian gauge fields—thus moving us closer to a fully unified quantum description of space-time and matter. These promising initial experiments provide a robust foundation for both experimental and theoretical advancements in our quest to understand quantum gravity and the fundamental nature of space-time.
