AMaPO: Adaptive Margin-attached Preference Optimization for Language Model Alignment
Ruibo Deng, Duanyu Feng, Wenqiang Lei

Abstract
Offline preference optimization offers a simpler and more stable alternative to RLHF for aligning language models. However, their effectiveness is critically dependent on ranking accuracy, a metric where further gains are highly impactful. This limitation arises from a fundamental problem that we identify and formalize as the Overfitting-Underfitting Dilemma: current margin designs cause models to apply excessive, wasteful gradients to correctly ranked samples (overfitting) while providing insufficient corrective signals for misranked ones (underfitting). To resolve this dilemma, we propose Adaptive Margin-attached Preference Optimization (AMaPO) , a simple yet principled algorithm. AMaPO employs an instance-wise adaptive margin, refined by Z-normalization and exponential scaling, which dynamically reallocates learning effort by amplifying gradients for misranked samples and suppressing them for correct ones. Extensive experiments on widely used benchmarks demonstrate that AMaPO not only achieves better ranking accuracy and superior downstream alignment performance, but targeted analysis also confirms that it successfully mitigates the core overfitting and underfitting issues. 1 1 1 Our code: https://github.com/Shiroha-Offical/AMaPO

Introduction

Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences, ensuring they are helpful, honest, and harmless, also known as preference learning, is crucial for LLM research
(
4
;
34
)
. A standard approach for this task is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), a powerful online algorithm that first trains a reward model on human preferences and then uses reinforcement learning to optimize the LLMs
(
34
;
40
)
. Despite its demonstrated success, RLHF is notoriously complex and prone to training instabilities, presenting significant practical challenges
(
11
)
. To circumvent these issues, a new family of offline alignment algorithms has emerged, led by methods like DPO
(
37
)
, IPO
(
3
)
, KTO
(
12
)
and FocalPO
(
28
)
. These approaches offer superior simplicity and stability by directly optimizing the LLMs to favor preferred responses over dispreferred ones, thereby bypassing the need for an explicit reward model and the complexities of RL. At its core, the efficacy of this entire paradigm is the ranking accuracy of an implicit reward model, which dictates how well the optimization process can distinguish between desirable and undesirable outputs.

Therefore, many subsequent DPO-style methods have been used to improve ranking accuracy, either explicitly prioritizing ranking
(
7
)
or indirectly fostering this capability through other modifications. A primary direction of these methods is to manipulate the implicit reward function, often by incorporating a reward margin to better separate preferred and dispreferred responses. Innovations in this direction include introducing fixed or dynamic margins
(
33
;
46
)
or updating the reference model to create more favorable optimization landscapes
(
16
)
. Although these methods report improved performance, their contributions are typically validated empirically, without a unified theoretical perspective to explain how these methods dynamically affect ranking accuracy during training and to compare with each others. Separately, emerging work has analyzed the gradient dynamics of preference learning algorithms
(
13
;
50
;
51
;
31
)
. However, these studies often fail to explicitly connect their findings on gradient properties, such as magnitude or conflict, with the resulting changes in ranking accuracy. Therefore, we argue that a crucial analytical bridge is missing: a framework that not only examines the gradient dynamics of these advanced DPO variants but also directly links them to the dynamic evolution of ranking accuracy.

To bridge this analytical gap, we introduce a unified framework that analyzes DPO-style methods through the lens of the reward margin. First, we reformulate existing algorithms, including DPO and its variants, into a generalized objective function based on different margin designs. This unification establishes a direct, formal connection between the algorithmic structure of each method and its capacity to optimize ranking accuracy. Within this framework, we then employ gradient dynamics analysis to investigate how these distinct margin formulations shape the learning trajectory of ranking accuracy. Our analysis reveals a fundamental flaw in the standard DPO mechanism: for samples that are already correctly ranked, its margin design leads to
overfitting
by assigning excessively large gradients, wasting capacity on these easy samples. Conversely, for incorrectly ranked samples where learning is most critical, the margin often results in
underfitting
by providing insufficient gradients, thereby hindering the correction of errors. We also find that while some of subsequent DPO-style methods attempt to address this, they fail to fully resolve this core tension.

Motivated by our analysis, we introduce AMaPO, an algorithm designed to resolve this overfitting-underfitting dilemma. AMaPO’s core strategy is an instance-wise adaptive margin, refined by Z-normalization and exponential scaling. By dynamically assigning large corrective margins to underfit samples and a zero margin to correctly ranked ones, it effectively reallocates learning effort to where it is most needed. Our extensive experiments on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that AMaPO not only achieves state-of-the-art ranking accuracy and superior downstream alignment performance, but targeted analysis also confirms that it successfully mitigates the core overfitting and underfitting issues, validating our approach.

The main contributions of this paper are: (i) We introduce a unified margin-based framework to analyze gradient dynamics of DPO-style algorithms, which reveals an overfitting-underfitting dilemma: existing methods expend excessive gradient on already-learned preferences (overfitting) while providing insufficient signal to correct misranked ones (underfitting). (ii) We propose Adaptive Margin-attached Preference Optimization (AMaPO), a principled algorithm designed to resolve this dilemma. AMaPO employs an instance-wise adaptive margin and refined by Z-normalization and exponential scaling, that reallocates learning effort by amplifying gradients for misranked samples and suppressing them for correctly ranked ones. (iii) Extensive experiments demonstrate AMaPO achieves state-of-the-art ranking accuracy, solving the overfitting-underfitting dilemma and leading to superior downstream alignment performance like instruction-following and complex reasoning.

Related works

Preference Learning Algorithms

The field of preference learning for LLM alignment is dominated by two primary approaches: online and offline. Online methods, exemplified by Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
4
;
34
)
, employ a complex two-stage process of reward modeling and RL-based optimization. Offline methods, initiated by Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
37
)
, directly optimize the LLM on preference data, obviating practical challenges such as the need for a separate reward model and training instability. Optimized DPO variants generally fall into two categories: reformulating the core loss function
(
3
;
12
)
, or incorporating an explicit reward margin to separate preference pairs robustly
(
5
;
44
)
. This margin-based approach has been realized through employing fixed or instance-dependent margins to improve learning stability and handle potential data noise
(
33
;
22
;
2
)
. Others achieve a form of dynamic regularization by progressively updating the reference policy itself, which effectively creates an adaptive margin throughout the training process
(
16
;
46
)
.
Despite these diverse, the central mechanism common to all these DPO-style algorithms is the implicit reward function. This makes their ability to learn to classify preferences correctly, known as the ranking accuracy
(
7
)
, the paramount determinant of their success. However, the field currently lacks a unified framework to analyze how these different methods impact the evolution of ranking accuracy, leaving a critical gap in our understanding.

Preference Learning Theory

Concurrent with the development of preference learning algorithms, a body of theoretical work has emerged to analyze their underlying mechanisms. These inquiries generally follow two primary perspectives: gradient dynamics and divergence analysis. From the gradient perspective, for example,
(
13
)
reveals the limited learning capacity of DPO through field theory and
(
51
)
identify issues such as gradient entanglement in margin-based methods. From divergence perspective, it examines how DPO optimizes the model distribution
\pi_{\theta}
towards the distribution of chosen response. While DPO implicitly optimizes a forward KL-divergence, recent work identifies promoting mode-seeking behavior by optimizing a reverse KL-divergence or Total Variation distance as a crucial property for preference alignment
(
42
;
48
;
47
)
. While these analyses are foundational, they typically examine each algorithm as a distinct entity. In this paper, we also want to build upon the tradition of gradient analysis but introduce a novel, unifying perspective. Based on the unification, we further explicitly connect the specific gradient properties of each method to their dynamic impact on ranking accuracy.

Preliminaries

This section establishes the technical foundations for our analysis. First, we detail Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
37
)
, the seminal algorithm for offline preference learning that serves as the basis for the methods we investigate. Second, we formalize the concept of the reward margin, which is used to construct a unified representation of various DPO-style algorithms. Finally, we define ranking accuracy, the primary metric used throughout our work to evaluate the efficacy of these alignment methods.

3.1
Direct Preference Optimization

Preference Data and the Bradley-Terry Model.

Offline preference optimization relies on a static dataset of preferences,
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Each entry consists of a prompt
x^{(i)}
and a pair of responses,
(y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})
, where
y_{w}
is preferred over
y_{l}
, denoted as
y_{w}\succ y_{l}
. This preference is typically provided by a human annotator or a powerful reward model. A standard assumption in the field is that these preferences are drawn from a distribution that follows the Bradley-Terry (BT) model
(
6
)
, which models the probability of preferring
y_{w}
over
y_{l}
as a logistic function of the difference in their latent reward values:
p(y_{w}\succ y_{l}|x)=\sigma(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l}))
.

The Direct Preference Optimization.

Instead of explicitly training a reward model
r(x,y)
, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
37
)
derives an analytical mapping from the optimal policy to the reward function. This allows DPO to use the log-likelihood of the policy to implicitly represent the reward function via a closed-form expression with the optimal policy:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\mathcal{D})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}

\displaystyle-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}\big)\big],

(1)

where
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a fixed reference model, and
\beta
is a temperature parameter that scales the reward.

3.2
A Unified Margin-based Framework

Recent theoretical works have sought to unify DPO-style algorithms under a single mathematical representation
(
51
;
30
)
. A common approach is to formulate a general loss function that captures variations in reward shaping and regularization. However, with the increasing prominence of methods that explicitly engineer a reward margin
(
52
;
46
)
, these existing frameworks can represent such methods only indirectly, obscuring the margin’s central role.

To create a more direct analytical tool that reflects this research trend, we extend the prior work
(
51
)
by proposing a refined unified objective with the margin,
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{unified}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle-(m(h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})-\gamma)

\displaystyle+\Lambda\left(\log\pi_{w}\right)).

(2)

Here,
h_{w}(x)
and
h_{l}(x)
represent transformation or adjustment functions applied to the log-probabilities of the preferred and dispreferred responses, respectively.

\pi_{w}
and
\pi_{l}
represent
\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{w}|x\right)
and
\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{l}|x\right)
for simplicity, respectively.
The term
m(x)
is a scoring function that computes the difference between these transformed values with the margin
\gamma
. Finally,
\Lambda\left(\log\pi_{w}\right)
is an optional auxiliary term that some models incorporate to encourage additional learning specifically on the preferred response.
This formulation allows us to directly compare algorithms based on their margin design. For instance:

DPO
: The original DPO algorithm fits this framework with
h_{w}(x)=h_{l}(x)=\beta x
,
m(x)=\log\sigma(x)
and a fixed margin of
\gamma=\beta(\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}|x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}|x))
.

SimPO
: Simple Preference Optimization (SimPO)
(
33
)
is a highly effective DPO variant, shown as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SimPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\mathcal{D})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,y_{w},y_{l}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log\sigma\big(\tfrac{\beta}{|y_{w}|}\log\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{w}|x\right)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\beta}{|y_{l}|}\log\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{l}|x\right)-C\big)\big].

(3)

It fits our framework by defining
h_{w}(x)=\frac{\beta}{|y_{w}|}x
,
h_{l}(x)=\frac{\beta}{|y_{l}|}x
,
m(x)=\log\sigma(x)
and a tunable margin
\gamma=C>0
.

This margin-focused unification serves as the cornerstone of our gradient analysis.

3.3
Ranking Accuracy

While the ultimate goal of alignment is to produce generations that humans prefer, which is often evaluated by win-rate against a baseline model
(
55
)
, this metric is costly and ill-suited for tracking training dynamics. Therefore, a more direct and practical metric is crucial for analyzing the learning process. Ranking accuracy
(
29
;
7
)
directly measures whether the policy (training) model
\pi_{\theta}
itself assigns a higher likelihood to the preferred response over the dispreferred one for a given prompt. Formally, it is defined as:

\mathcal{R}(\pi_{\theta};\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\left(x,y_{w},y_{l}\right)\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)>\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)\right],

(4)

where
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. A higher ranking accuracy indicates that the policy’s preference ordering aligns with the ground-truth data, and it serves as a predictor of the downstream performance within a certain KL divergence range
(
7
)
. Furthermore, for our theoretical analysis, we utilize the instance-wise ranking accuracy derived from our unified framework (Eq.
2
), defined as
h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})
. For the vast majority of DPO-style methods where the transformations
h_{w}
and
h_{l}
are linear or monotonic, the sign of this score is equivalent to the standard ranking accuracy (i.e.,
h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})>0\iff\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)>\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)
). Throughout this paper, we use this ranking accuracy as the primary lens for analysis.

In this section, we leverage the unified margin-based framework established in the previous section to conduct a detailed theoretical analysis and construct our related method. Our primary goal is to understand how the margin design in DPO and its variants affects the learning dynamics of ranking accuracy. Through this gradient-level investigation, we diagnose the key limitations of existing approaches and, from these findings, distill a set of desiderata for a more effective margin function. Building on these principles, we then introduce
Adaptive Margin-attached Preference Optimization (AMaPO)
, a novel preference optimization algorithm that assigns an adaptive margin specifically designed to manipulate gradient magnitude, thereby improving both ranking accuracy and overall alignment performance.

4.1
Gradient Dynamics and the Ideal Margin

Based on the unified objective in Eq.
2
, we can derive a general gradient formulation for DPO-style algorithms. The gradient with respect to the model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{unified}}

\displaystyle\left(\theta\right)=-m^{\prime}(h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})-\gamma)

\displaystyle(h_{w}^{\prime}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}^{\prime}(\log\pi_{l}))-\Lambda^{\prime}\left(\log\pi_{w}\right).

(5)

Specifically, we define
d_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=m^{\prime}(h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})-\gamma)
, which comprises log-probabilities of responses (
h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})
,
h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})
) and margin
\gamma
and significantly affects the gradient magnitude
3
3

3

For simplicity, we omit the contribution of
\Lambda^{\prime}(\log\pi_{w})
to the gradient magnitude, as
\Lambda^{\prime}(\log\pi_{w})
is typically a constant and not related to the
\gamma
.
.

This insight reveals a crucial mechanism: for a given state of the policy
\pi_{\theta}
, the
margin
\gamma
is the primary lever to control the learning rate
. Since the gradient magnitude
d_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is governed by

d_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=m^{\prime}(h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})-\gamma)

(6)

where
m^{\prime}
is usually a monotonically decreasing function and
m^{\prime}(x)>0
(as
m(x)=\log\sigma(x)
), the choice of
\gamma
directly determines whether the learning signal is amplified or suppressed. A poorly designed margin leads directly to what we term the Overfitting-Underfitting Dilemma.

Definition 1
(The Overfitting-Underfitting Dilemma)

An algorithm is susceptible to this dilemma when its margin
\gamma
causes inefficient optimization:

•

Overfitting:
A large margin produces wasteful gradients for correctly ranked samples, forcing the model to over-optimize on these samples.

•

Underfitting:
A small margin yields small gradients for incorrectly ranked samples, stifling learning where it is most needed.

Table
1
provides a concrete illustration of these cases. Resolving this dilemma requires a margin that adheres to a key principle: it must be
dynamically adaptive
to the policy’s instance-wise ranking accuracy. This motivates the concept of an Oracle Ranking Margin.

Definition 2
(Oracle Ranking Margin)

The
Oracle Ranking Margin
,
\gamma^{*}
, is an instance-specific, non-negative threshold that acts as a dynamic learning target
4
4

4

We provide further theoretical analysis in Appendix
C.1
, distinguishing between incorrectly ranked samples that require a strong learning signal
r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})<\gamma^{*}
, and correctly ranked ones whose gradients should be suppressed to prevent overfitting
r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})>\gamma^{*}
. The instance-wise ranking accuracy
r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})
is defined as
h_{w}(\log\pi_{w})-h_{l}(\log\pi_{l})
.

With these principles established, to thoroughly investigate whether existing DPO-style algorithms satisfy the above demands, our analysis focuses on two of the most celebrated objectives, including DPO and SimPO

.

Analysis of DPO.

The margin,
\gamma=\beta\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}|x)-\beta\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}|x)
derived from the reference model, can neither dynamically adapt to instance-wise ranking correctness nor remaining positive. This irrelevance suggests that DPO can reproduce all cases above, which might result in overfitting and underfitting, as illustrated by case 2 and case 3.

Analysis of SimPO.

Although
\gamma=C>0
is guaranteed to remain positive, it overlooks the variability inherent in instance-wise ranking correctness. This rigidity suggests that SimPO can reproduce case 2 and case 4, which could lead to overfitting as shown in case 2.

Our analysis reveals that margin designs of both DPO and SimPO fail to proactively adapt based on instance-wise ranking correctness, leading to overfitting and underfitting in preference learning and, consequently, suboptimal ranking accuracy and alignment performance.

4.2
AMaPO: Adaptive Margin-attached Preference Optimization

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, we introduce
Adaptive Margin-attached Preference Optimization (AMaPO)
.
Our approach is designed to directly resolve the Overfitting-Underfitting Dilemma by dynamically adjusting the gradient magnitude for each sample. We build upon the robust SimPO
(
33
)
framework, but our key innovation lies in replacing its static margin with a principled, instance-wise adaptive margin,
\gamma(x,y_{w},y_{l})
. The general objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AMaPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\mathcal{D})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log\sigma\big(r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})

\displaystyle-\gamma(x,y_{w},y_{l})\big)\big],

(7)

where
r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})=\frac{\beta}{|y_{w}|}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)-\frac{\beta}{|y_{l}|}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)
is the implicit ranking accuracy, and
m(x)=\log\sigma(x)
as Eq.
2
. The following subsections detail the design of our adaptive margin,
\gamma(x,y_{w},y_{l})
.

Formulating the Ideal Adaptive Margin.

Our theoretical analysis concluded that an ideal margin must be
dynamically adaptive
. To satisfy this, from definition
2
, the margin should reflect the gap between the policy’s performance and the ideal target set by the Oracle Ranking Margin,
\gamma^{*}
. We therefore formulate the ideal adaptive margin as:

\gamma^{*}(x,y_{w},y_{l})=\mathbb{I}\left[(\gamma^{*}-r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l}))>0\right]\cdot\gamma^{*}.

(8)

This formulation is inherently dynamic and directly addresses the dilemma. For incorrectly ranked samples where
r_{\pi_{\theta}}\leq 0<\gamma^{*}
, the margin is positive and large, amplifying the corrective gradient to mitigate underfitting. For correctly ranked samples where
r_{\pi_{\theta}}>\gamma^{*}\geq 0
, the margin becomes zero, suppressing the gradient and preventing overfitting.

Estimating the Oracle Margin in Practice.

Since the true Oracle Ranking Margin
\gamma^{*}
is inaccessible, we must estimate it. Motivated by empirical findings that the implicit reward distributions of aligned models are often right-skewed
(
36
)
, we propose using the mean implicit margin
\mu_{r}
within the current training batch
B
as a robust, annotation-free proxy for
\gamma^{*}
. To make the ideal margin from Eq.
8
tractable by relaxing the non-negativity constraint (i.e., the
\max(\cdot,0)
function), while also stabilizing the estimation and scaling it appropriately, we apply Z-score normalization
(
35
)
. This yields:

\gamma(x,y_{w},y_{l})=\max\big(\tfrac{\mu_{r}-r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})}{\sigma_{r}}\cdot\mu_{r},0\big),

(9)

where
\mu_{r}
and
\sigma_{r}
are the mean and standard deviation of
r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})
computed within batch
B
. The term
\frac{\mu_{r}-r_{\pi_{\theta}}}{\sigma_{r}}
calculates the normalized ”difficulty” of the sample, which is then scaled by the estimated oracle target
\mu_{r}
itself.

Margin Scaling for Quality Representation.

The margin from Eq.
9
effectively captures the relative difficulty of a sample. However, empirical results find that the log probability value might not truthfully reflect the quality of generated sequence
(
18
)
. To better represent the quality gap between responses and speed up the training of the hard incorrectly ranked samples, we introduce a scaling function. Inspired by the strong correlation between perplexity (PPL) and generation quality
(
32
;
15
)
, we use an exponential scaling function. The scaled margin is
6
6

6

This elegantly transforms our additive, log-space margin into the geometric mean of the PPL ratio within the batch. The derivation is in Appendix
C.2
.
:

h_{\gamma}(\gamma)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\gamma=0,\\
\beta\cdot e^{\gamma}&\text{if }\gamma>0.\end{cases}

(10)

Final Objective.

Finally, to ensure that our adaptive margin serves as a fixed target for each sample within an optimization step, we apply a stop-gradient operation (
\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]
). This prevents the gradient from flowing back through the margin calculation. Incorporating all components, the final AMaPO objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AMaPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\mathcal{D})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log\sigma\big(r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})

\displaystyle-h_{\gamma}(\mathrm{sg}[\gamma(x,y_{w},y_{l})])\big)\big].

(11)

In summary, by employing an adaptive margin derived from the model’s current ranking correctness, AMaPO effectively manipulates the gradient to focus on learning from its errors. This design directly resolves the overfitting and underfitting issues inherent in prior methods, leading to improved ranking accuracy and superior alignment performance.

Results on the Overfitting-Underfitting Problem.

To further validate our theoretical claims regarding the overfitting-underfitting dilemma, we evaluate model performance across four generalization scenarios.

Ablations and Further Analysis

This section delves deeper into the components and behavior of AMaPO. We begin by ablating the modules, such as Z-normalization and exponential scaling, to confirm their necessity. Our main analysis then focuses on the hyperparameter
\beta
, which controls the strength of the adaptive margin.

Importance of the components of AMaPO.

Due to the significant computational resources required, we conduct this ablation study on the Llama3-8B-Base and Llama3-8B-Instruct models, evaluating performance on key downstream benchmarks. Our results in Table
5
shows that the complete AMaPO design, which integrates Z-normalization (can also been shown as an alternative of linear scaling functions), exponential scaling, and a zero margin for correctly ranked samples, is critical for achieving robust and well-rounded performance. While most ablated variants still outperform the DPO baseline, removing any single component results in a notable performance degradation. This is particularly evident on the Llama3-8B-Base model, where removing exponential scaling and Z-normalization (‘w/o adaptive‘) degrades the AlpacaEval 2 LC and WR to 20.7% and 16.7%, below the full AMaPO’s 26.3% and 21.4%. Similarly, removing any components of AMaPO on the Llama3-8B-Instruct model causes the score on MT-Bench to drop from AMaPO’s 7.2 to about 7.0. Thus, the synergistic combination of all components in AMaPO is essential for a better adaptive margin and ensuring consistently superior performance across diverse evaluation settings.

Influence of
\beta
.

To investigate the influence of our sole hyperparameter,
\beta
, we trained AMaPO on the UltraFeedback dataset using the Llama3-8B-Base model with varying
\beta
values. As shown in Figure
1(a)
, our analysis on the AlpacaEval 2 and UltraFeedback test sets reveals a clear inverted U-shaped relationship between
\beta
and performance. Both ranking accuracy and the LC win rate initially increase with
\beta
before declining, indicating that an optimal regularization strength is empirically around
\beta=3
. The underlying reason for this trend is revealed by analyzing the probability distributions on the UltraFeedback test set (Figures
1(b)
and
1(c)
). As
\beta
increases, the distribution of the probability margin,
\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)
, sharpens significantly, but the value at the peak of the likelihood of winning responses,
\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)
, decreases. This reveals a critical trade-off: while a stronger regularization (a higher
\beta
) initially forces the model to more accurately fit our adaptive margin, leading to performance gains, an excessively high
\beta
causes the likelihood distribution to become overly peaked, resulting in model degeneration and a subsequent drop in performance.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we presented a novel analysis of offline preference optimization and proposed a new algorithm, AMaPO, to address the dilemma we identified. Through a unified margin-based framework, our gradient analysis revealed that methods like DPO suffer from an overfitting-underfitting dilemma, inefficiently learning from preference data. AMaPO rectifies this by introducing an instance-wise adaptive margin that intelligently prioritizes difficult, misranked samples while ignoring those already learned. Extensive experiments show that AMaPO has a better ranking accuracy, leading to a better performance on downstream tasks. By providing both a more effective alignment algorithm and a sharper analytical lens, this work paves the way for more principled advances in preference optimization.

Appendix A
Limitations and Future Work

While our work introduces a principled approach to preference optimization and demonstrates its effectiveness, we identify several promising avenues for future research that arise from the scope and limitations of our study.

Scaling.

Our experiments were conducted on models up to the 8B parameter scale. A crucial next step is to investigate the performance and dynamics of AMaPO on much larger models (e.g., 70B and beyond), where phenomena like overfitting and underfitting may manifest differently.

Generality of the Oracle Margin Estimator.

We propose using the batch-wise mean implicit margin as a practical and effective proxy for the inaccessible Oracle Ranking Margin. However, the optimality of this non-parametric estimator may not be universal. Its performance could be influenced by factors such as the quality and diversity of the initial SFT model or the noise level and distribution of the preference dataset. Future research could explore more sophisticated estimators for the oracle margin, potentially involving lightweight parametric models or meta-learning approaches, to further enhance the adaptability of the margin.

Alternatives of scaling functions.

In this work, the scaling function serves to appropriately amplify the gradient magnitude of hard, incorrectly ranked samples. AMaPO exclusively consider the widely-used exponential function and Z-normalization as the nonlinear and linear scaling functions, respectively, which elegantly transform our additive, log-space margin into the geometric mean of the PPL ratio within the batch. Although our ablation studies demonstrate that the popular linear and non-linear function do work, a comprehensive exploration of better scaling functions would further improve the margin-based preference optimization algorithms.

Scope of the Gradient Analysis.

Our theoretical motivation is grounded in a gradient-level analysis that examines the instantaneous effect of the margin on the learning signal. This static snapshot is powerful for algorithm design but does not constitute a full analysis of the dynamic training process over time. A compelling direction for future work would be to study the entire training trajectory, exploring how AMaPO’s adaptive margin influences the loss landscape, convergence properties, and the evolution of the model’s internal representations throughout the optimization process.

Further Theoretical Analysis

C.1
Analysis towards Oracle Ranking Margin

We have empirically claimed that Oracle Ranking Margin
\gamma^{*}
is an instance-specific, non-negative threshold, as stated in our Definition
2
:

\gamma^{*}=r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})

where
r^{*}(\cdot)
represents the optimal reward function.

Here, we theoretically verify our claim regarding the properties of
\gamma^{*}
based on the theoretical analysis and proof in
(
37
;
30
)
:

Lemma 1

All reward classes under the BT model can be represented with the reparameterization
r(x,y)=\beta\log\pi_{\theta}(y|x)
for some policy model
\pi_{\theta}(y|x)
.

Therefore, the optimal reward function
r^{*}(\cdot)
can be reparameterized with a certain policy model
\pi^{*}_{\theta}(y|x)
as follows:

\gamma^{*}(x,y_{w},y_{l})=\beta\log\pi^{*}_{\theta}(x|y_{w})-\beta\log\pi^{*}_{\theta}(x|y_{l})

(12)

where
\pi^{*}_{\theta}(x|y)
varies with
x
and
y
, and thus
\gamma^{*}
should be an instance-specific threshold
\gamma^{*}(x,y_{w},y_{l})
.

Meanwhile, the reparameterization of
\gamma^{*}
as presented in Eq
12
lends theoretical backing to our estimation method for
\gamma^{*}
in Eq
9
, which leverages the mean log probability of responses within the current training batch to estimate
\gamma^{*}
.

C.2
Derivations for Margin Scaling

In our method, we introduced the exponential scaling function,
h_{\gamma}(\gamma)
, with the motivation that it better represents the quality gap between responses by leveraging perplexity (PPL). This appendix provides the formal mathematical justification for this choice. We will show that the exponential form has a compelling and intuitive interpretation: it is directly related to the geometric mean of the PPL ratios between the losing and winning responses, adjusted by the sample’s specific difficulty.

For
\gamma>0
in Eq.
10
, we have

h_{\gamma}(\gamma)=\beta e^{\gamma}=\beta e^{\alpha\mu_{r}}=\beta{(e^{\mu_{r}})}^{\alpha},\\

where
\alpha=\frac{\mu_{r}-r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})}{\sigma_{r}}
is the Z-normalization coefficient for
r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{w},y_{l})
, and
\beta
is the scaling constant. Therefore, we can focus on
e^{\mu_{r}}
, where

\displaystyle e^{\mu_{\gamma}}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}({\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}|y^{i}_{w}|\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}_{w}|x^{i})-|y^{i}_{l}|\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}_{l}|x)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\prod_{i=1}^{|B|}\mathrm{exp}(|y^{i}_{w}|\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}_{w}|x^{i})-|y^{i}_{l}|\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}_{l}|x)))^{\frac{1}{|B|}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\prod_{i=1}^{|B|}\frac{\mathrm{exp}(-|y^{i}_{l}|\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}_{l}|x))}{\mathrm{exp}(-|y^{i}_{w}|\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}_{w}|x^{i}))})^{\frac{1}{|B|}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\prod_{i=1}^{|B|}\frac{\mathrm{PPL}(y^{i}_{l}|x)}{\mathrm{PPL}(y^{i}_{w}|x)})^{\frac{1}{|B|}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\frac{\mathrm{PPL}(y_{l}|x)}{\mathrm{PPL}(y_{w}|x)}),

which means that the exponential scaling function represents the geometric mean of the ratios of PPL for losing and winning responses within the training batch
B
, capturing their quality difference.

Finally, we have an intuitive understanding of Margin Scaling, which represents the quality gap between responses within the training batch.

h_{\gamma}(\gamma)=\beta G(\frac{\mathrm{PPL}(y_{l}|x)}{\mathrm{PPL}(y_{w}|x)})^{\alpha}.

Ablation Study of Oracle Margin Estimation

To validate the robustness of our proposed estimation methods based on the mean implicit reward margin within the batch, we conduct ablation study towards the batch size hyperparameter. To ensure a fair comparison, we adjusted the batch size for oracle margin estimation while holding the total number of gradient update steps constant (the same as default setup) for under Llama3-8B-Base setup on Ultrafeedback Binarized dataset. As shown in Table

Specifically, an increasing batch size leads to a performance drop on the RM-Bench Easy accuracy but a gain on the Hard accuracy. This implies that AMaPO amplifies the gradients for hard, incorrectly ranked samples, demonstrating that a larger batch size facilitates a more precise estimation of the oracle margin. However, results on AlpacaEval2 show an opposite trend. This is consistent with our ablation studies over
\beta
in Section
5.3

2

AMaPO decreases generation length and improves generation quality.
As we have observed that AMaPO exhibits a significant on AlpacaEval2 LC win rate improvement compared to other baselines, to further analyze the underlying working inners, we first report the detailed results of AlpacaEval2 including the strandard deviation and generation length. As shown in Table
13
, although AMaPO outperforms baselines on WR slightly, it generates response extremely shorter than comparable baselines. Notably, AMaPO decreases the generation length by up to 203 tokens compared to SimPO. This suggests that AMaPO can effectively control the verbosity of the generated responses, and meanwhile improve the generation quality to a certain extent. Also, we find that the designs of adaptive margin profoundly impact the generation length, as shown in Table
12
. Specifically, removing the zero setting for correctly ranked samples increases the generation length significantly. AMaPO

Instance segmentation models, such as Mask R-CNN, typically evaluate their performance using metrics like Average Precision (AP) and Intersection over Union (IoU). While these metrics provide a quantitative measure of performance, incorporating human knowledge can help improve instance segmentation models in several ways:

1.
Annotate challenging cases
: Identify and annotate instances with complex or ambiguous boundaries, occlusions, or unusual appearances. This helps the model learn to handle these edge cases better.

2.
Provide domain-specific knowledge
: Incorporate domain expertise into the annotation process by highlighting important features, such as texture, color, or shape, that are relevant to the specific application (e.g., medical imaging, autonomous driving).

3.
Use weak supervision
: Instead of annotating every instance, use weak supervision techniques like:

-

Bounding box annotations: Provide approximate bounding boxes around instances, allowing the model to learn from rough estimates.

-

Semantic segmentation masks: Annotate classes or categories, rather than individual instances, to help the model understand the context and relationships between objects.

4.
Regularly update and refine annotations
: As the model improves, revisit and refine the annotations to ensure they remain accurate and relevant. This helps the model adapt to its own mistakes and learn from corrections.

5.
Use transfer learning and multi-task learning
: Initialize the model with pre-trained weights on a related task or dataset, and then fine-tune it on your specific instance segmentation task. This can leverage knowledge from other domains or tasks to improve performance.

6.
Incorporate prior knowledge into the model architecture
: Design the model to incorporate domain-specific constraints or features, such as:

-

Using attention mechanisms to focus on relevant regions or features.

-

Incorporating shape priors or geometric constraints to improve instance segmentation.

7.
Use human-in-the-loop evaluation
: Involve human evaluators in the evaluation process to:

-

Provide feedback on model performance and identify areas for improvement.

-

Correct mistakes and annotate challenging cases.

-

Validate the model’s output and ensure it aligns with human expectations.

Explore alternative evaluation metrics
: Consider using metrics that better capture the nuances of instance segmentation, such as:

-

Instance-level metrics like mean Average Precision (mAP) or mean Average Recall (mAR).

-

Metrics that account for instance size, orientation, or aspect ratio.

9.
Regularly monitor and analyze model behavior
: Use visualization tools and techniques to understand how the model is making predictions, identifying biases, and areas where it struggles. This helps you to refine the model and annotations accordingly.

10.
Collaborate with domain experts
: Work closely with experts in the domain to ensure the model is aligned with their understanding of the problem and to incorporate their insights into the annotation process and model development.

By incorporating these strategies, you can leverage human knowledge to improve the performance and robustness of your instance segmentation model, ultimately leading to better AP metrics and more accurate results.
