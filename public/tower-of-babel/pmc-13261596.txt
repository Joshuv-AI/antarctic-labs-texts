State of Health Estimation for Lithium-Ion Batteries via Fused Impedance Feature and V‑Transformer-AGLU Network
Authors: Yao Fang, Xuan Zhang, Yanchun Wang, Chunlan Yang, Chaolong Zhang, Anxiang Wang, Kaixin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate State of Health (SoH) estimation is critical to ensuring the safety and optimizing the performance of lithium-ion batteries. To address the limitations of existing methods in capturing the complex aging mechanisms and long-range temporal dependencies, this paper proposes a novel SoH estimation approach for lithium-ion batteries that integrates the Fused Impedance Feature (FIF) with a Vision Transformer embedded with an Adaptive Gated Linear Unit (V-Transformer-AGLU). Specifically, the FIF is constructed by fusing the Mean and Median of dynamic impedance, calculated from individual cells within the SoC range of 0.3–0.7. This fusion strategy explicitly balances the sensitivity of the Mean to global degradation trends with the robustness of the Median to local anomalies and noise. Furthermore, FIF exhibits particular effectiveness in modeling the SoH for series-connected battery packs with cell inconsistency. Subsequently, an efficient V-Transformer-AGLU model is constructed, which marks the first application of the V-Transformer to one-dimensional temporal SoH prediction for battery systems. The model adopts a patching mechanism to capture global dependencies, and AGLU is employed to substitute the conventional Feedforward network for facilitating dynamic information fusion and enhancing robustness against the nonstationary characteristics of battery signals. Experimental results verify the high accuracy and generalization robustness of the proposed method, which achieves a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 0.0054, a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.0043, and a coefficient of determination (R 2) of 0.9868 on the 8-cell pack and maintains outstanding performance across different packs. These results provide valuable technical support for the online monitoring of battery management systems (BMS).

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries have become the core power source for electric vehicles (EVs), energy storage systems (ESS), and portable electronic devices due to their high energy density, long cycle life, and low self-discharge rate. − However, during long-term charge and discharge cycles, batteries inevitably undergo performance degradation, manifested as capacity fade and internal resistance growth, which results in a decline in state of health (SoH). , Accurate and reliable online SoH estimation is critical for ensuring the safe operation of battery systems, optimizing energy management strategies, extending service life, and preventing safety incidents. ,

The SoH of lithium-ion battery pack is defined using the capacity ratio, which is the ratio of the current capacity to the rated capacity, as follows 1 SoH=Qcurrent−capacityQrated−capacity×100% where Q current–capacity represents the current discharge capacity, and Q rated–capacity represents the rated capacity.

Currently, SoH estimation methods can be mainly categorized into three types: direct measurement methods, model-based methods, and data-driven methods. Direct measurement methods are intuitive approaches, which involve directly measuring physical quantities that reflect the battery’s health status through specific experiments or measurement techniques. These methods primarily include Coulomb counting, open circuit voltage (OCV), and internal resistance estimation. However, direct measurement methods often require interrupting the normal operation of the battery, and some methods have high equipment requirements, which limits their widespread application in online monitoring.

Model-based methods estimate SoH by constructing mathematical models that describe the internal physical-chemical processes or external characteristics of the battery. These methods can be further divided into: electrochemical model-based methods and equivalent circuit model (ECM)-based methods. For instance, Chen et al. proposed a SoH estimation method based on Kullback–Leibler divergence to address the dependency of traditional health indicator (HI) extraction methods on expert and prior knowledge, as well as their susceptibility to measurement noise. Xu et al. combined a simplified electrochemical model with an ECM, using online model parameter identification and unscented Kalman filtering to simultaneously estimate both the state of charge (SoC) and SoH of lithium-ion batteries. However, such parameter identification is complex and computationally expensive, and the modeling of aging mechanisms is often oversimplified, making it difficult to adapt to the nonlinearity and uncertainties encountered under practical operating conditions. Furthermore, accurately modeling the interactions and inconsistencies between cells within a pack poses an additional layer of complexity for model-based approaches.

Data-driven methods estimate SoH by establishing input-output mappings derived from historical data, and have garnered significant attention in recent years. , These methods typically consist of two core components: feature extraction and model development. , Features commonly include constant current charge time (TCC), average voltage during constant current charging (Vavg-CC), peak of incremental capacity (ICA) curve (ICA-Peak), area under the ICA curve, − Differential voltage analysis (DVA), and internal resistance indicators based on electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). ICA and DVA can effectively reveal battery aging information, but they require high-precision current/voltage sampling and are sensitive to noise. EIS has limitations including high equipment costs, time-intensive measurements, and poor stability. To overcome these limitations, researchers have begun exploring the extraction of dynamic internal resistance as a proxy feature from conventional charge–discharge curves. − This method requires only voltage, current, and SoC data. This calculation, performed for each cell, captures the inherent cell-to-cell inconsistency from standard battery management systems (BMS), making it highly suitable for engineering applications. However, extending these methods to battery packs introduces unique challenges. Cell-to-cell inconsistencies, arising from manufacturing tolerances, uneven thermal distributions, and differential aging rates, can significantly impact the overall pack’s performance and SoH. However, traditional features and models developed for single cells may not adequately capture the collective degradation behavior and the evolving imbalances within the pack. Existing dynamic internal resistance features often use a single statistical measure, which does not fully integrate the advantages of different statistical perspectives. Notably, there remains a lack of features for SoH estimation specifically designed to handle the complexities and inconsistencies inherent in battery packs.

Model development involves constructing a prediction model that relates the extracted features to the SoH. Early estimation models predominantly relied on machine learning algorithms, such as support vector regression (SVR), Gaussian process regression (GPR), linear regression (LR), and random forest (RF). However, these methods are limited in handling high-dimensional, nonstationary time-series signals, and struggle to capture the long-term dependencies in the battery aging process. Recurrent neural networks (RNN) and their variants long short-term memory (LSTM) , and gated recurrent units (GRU) , excel at sequential modeling. For example, Zhang et al. proposed a method combining multiphysics features with a CNN-EFC-BiLSTM model to rapidly and accurately estimate SoH in batteries with nonzero initial charge states. Zhang et al. also introduced a double correlation analysis, and subsequently built a GRU-based model for SoH estimation. In comparison to LSTM and GRU, the Transformer model is based entirely on the attention mechanism. It can process all elements of the input sequence in parallel and long-range dependencies more effectively, thereby leading to significant performance improvements in natural language processing tasks. , Similarly, Tao et al. achieved precise SoH estimation for electric vehicle battery packs using incremental capacity curves (ICCs) and the S-transform model. Nevertheless, Transformer retains inherent drawbacks when processing one-dimensional long sequences, such as the quadratic growth of computational complexity with sequence length and limitations in modeling periodic patterns stemming from positional encoding issues.

Vision Transformer (V-Transformer) uses a global self-attention mechanism to process image sequences that are divided into patches. This approach effectively reduces the sequence length, while preserving strong long-range dependency modeling capability. Consequently, V-Transformer has been successfully adapted and applied to diverse tasks beyond computer vision, demonstrating its versatility. Examples include such as ECG classification, transportation prediction, financial Analysis, power load forecasting, and remaining useful life prediction for industrial equipment. However, feedforward network in V-Transformer may lack sufficient expressive power when applied to complex nonlinear dynamic systems like battery aging. This could make it difficult to capture the subtle nonlinearity during the SoH degradation process.

To address the challenges mentioned above, this paper proposes a novel SoH estimation framework that integrates FIF with V-Transformer-AGLU. First, the voltage and current of each cell in the battery pack are measured during the charging cycle, and the average and median values of dynamic impedance for each cell is calculated within the stable SoC range. Next, the fused impedance feature (FIF) is generated using a weighted average method between average value and median value. Finally, the proposed V-Transformer-AGLU is used to estimate the SoH of Lithium-ion battery pack. The main contributions are as follows:(1) Introduction of FIF Features: This study introduces the FIF, a novel feature for multicell battery pack. It is constructed by performing a weighted fusion of the mean and median of the dynamic impedance from individual cells within the stable SoC range [0.3, 0.7]. This fusion strategy balances the sensitivity of the mean to overall aging mechanisms with the robustness of the median to noise, effectively capturing cell-to-cell inconsistencies and nonstationarity.(2) Design of the V-Transformer-AGLU Model: This paper introduces the V-Transformer architecture for one-dimensional battery aging sequence modeling, which efficiently captures long-range dependencies across cycles via the temporal patching mechanism. Furthermore, an Adaptive Gated Linear Unit (AGLU) is proposed to replace the conventional feedforward network. This module dynamically fuses signal magnitude and structural information, which substantially improves the model’s robustness to nonstationary battery signals and strengthens its capability to model complex nonlinear aging behaviors.(3) Comprehensive Experimental Validation: Extensive experiments are conducted on real-world battery pack data sets, including cross-pack generalization tests on 8-cell pack, 6-cell pack, and 2-cell pack. The proposed method achieves superior accuracy (RMSE = 0.0054, MAE = 0.0043, coefficient of determination (R 2) = 0.9868) and outperforms mainstream features (TCC, Vavg-CC, ICA-Peak), advanced deep-learning models (CNN-Transformer, CNN-BiLSTM, CNN-BiGRU), and traditional machine-learning models (SVR, LR, RF, GPR). More importantly, the method maintains stable and high prediction performance across different battery pack configurations without parameter retuning, demonstrating strong practical generalization and engineering applicability.

Feature Extraction

Dynamic Impedance Calculation for Individual Cells

Accurate SoH estimation for series-connected battery pack depends on high-quality features that can sensitively reflect the internal degradation mechanisms of individual cells while accounting for their inherent inconsistencies. Cell-to-cell variations and inconsistent aging rates are key factors affecting the overall performance of battery pack. To characterize these inconsistencies, the voltage and current data of each cell recorded during the charging process are analyzed separately.

It is well documented that the polarization behavior is relatively stable within the SoC range of 0.3–0.7, where it exhibits high sensitivity to battery aging. − This specific SoC window is selected to isolate the degradation from the inherent nonlinearities. The procedures below detail the dynamic impedance calculation for individual cells in the pack.

Data Segmentation (Individual Cell): For each charging cycle, the voltage (Vm ) and current (Im ) for each cell m (where m = 1,2,···,M and M is the total number of cells in the pack) are processed separately. The data points for cell m are identified and isolated from its respective charging profile. Let data points for cell m be denoted as {(V m,i ,I m,i )} for i from start_index m to end_index m , Here, i is an index variable representing the sequential order of data points within the SoC window [0.3, 0.7] for cell m, where SoC m,start_index m ≈ 0.3 and SoC m,end_index m ≈ 0.7.

Identification of Boundary Points (Individual Cell): Within the isolated segment for cell m, the voltage value at the beginning of the window (corresponding to i = start_index m ) is defined as V m,start = V m,start_index m , and the voltage value at the end of the window (corresponding to i = end_index m ) is defined as V m,end = V m,end_index m .

Calculation of Average Current (Individual Cell): The average charging current I̅ cc,m for cell m during the CC charging phase within its specific SoC window [0.3, 0.7] is calculated by averaging the current value I m,i for all indices i from start_index m to end_index m 2 I̅cc,m=1Nwin,m∑i=start_indexmend_indexmIm,i where N win,m = end_index m – start_index m + 1 is the number of data points within the SoC window [0.3, 0.7] for cell m.

This calculation is performed for each cell. This process captures the inherent cell-to-cell inconsistency in terms of internal resistance characteristics.

Fused Impedance Feature Extraction for Pack

The FIF is designed to aggregate the individually calculated Imdynamic,m values from all cells in the pack into a representative feature that encapsulates the heath of battery pack. After calculating Imdynamic,m for all M cells, a vector Z in each cycle is formed: Z = [Imdynamic,1,Imdynamic,2,···,Imdynamic,M ]. This vector Z inherently carries the information about the cell-to-cell inconsistency. To summarize this information, two key statistical measures are computed from the vector Z

The average value Imavg is calculated as 4 Imavg=1M∑m=1MImdynamic,m

Imavg reflects the overall energy loss of the battery pack. It is highly sensitive to the cumulative aging mechanisms, such as the growth of the SEI layer across the entire pack.

The impedance data is arranged in ascending order, satisfying Imdynamic,1 ≤ Imdynamic,2··· ≤ Imdynamic,m . The median value Immedian can be expressed as 5 Immedian={Im(M+1)/2Misodd12[Im(M)/2+Im(M+1)/2]Miseven

Immedian is highly robust to noise and outliers. It effectively represents the “typical” degradation state of most cells, which provides strong immunity to measurement artifacts, and offers a stable health baseline for the entire battery pack.

To fully leverage the informational advantages of both statistical measures, this paper proposes a FIF, which constructs a single robust indicator by weighted fusion of the average and median value of dynamic impedance. It can be defined as 6 FIF=ω1Imavg+(1−ω1)Immedian where ω1 is the fusion weight coefficient. A sensitivity analysis confirmed that ω = 0.5 provides a balanced contribution from both statistical measures, yielding optimal performance for SoH estimation in our experiments.

FIF not only preserves the physical property that impedance monotonically increases with battery aging, but also effectively mitigates the interference from measurement noise and local fluctuations. Specifically, the average impedance Imavg reflects the overall energy loss and the cumulative effect of aging mechanisms like SEI layer growth across the entire data segment within the stable SoC range [0.3, 0.7]. Furthermore, studies using Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) have confirmed that impedance characteristics in this range serve as increasingly sensitive degradation indicators. Conversely, the median impedance Immedian demonstrates robustness to outliers and provides a stable measure of the central tendency of the impedance distribution, which is particularly valuable in this sensitive range. By fusing these two statistical perspectives through weighted averaging, FIF achieves a balance between sensitivity to the global aging trend and robustness against noise. This fused approach leverages the stable and aging-sensitive nature of the medium SoC range to achieve more accurate SoH estimation.

Model Description

SOH estimation is fundamentally a regression problem that extracts degradation trends from nonstationary, variable-length time-series data. Although traditional RNNs and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can model local dynamics, they have inherent limitations in capturing long-term dependencies across cycles. While the Transformer architecture possesses powerful global modeling capabilities, its encoder-decoder structure is redundant for sequence-to-scalar regression tasks. Furthermore, it is sensitive to input length, making it difficult to adapt to incomplete or asynchronous data segments collected in practical BMS.

Therefore, this paper proposes V-Transformer-AGLU model, as shown in Figure . The core idea is to transfer the V-Transformer from the image domain to one-dimensional battery aging time series modeling, and to address the signal magnitude drift issue by introducing an AGLU.

V-Transform-AGLU Model Structure

Given a SoH feature sequence X = [f 1,f 2,···,f L ], where L represents the length of the continuous charging cycle sequence, and f i denotes the corresponding FIF scalar value for the i–th cycle.

The model first performs temporal patch embedding. 7 Z0=[z1,...zN]T=linear(flatten(patch(X))) where the function Patch(·) divides the sequence length L (sequence length = 10) into N = L/p nonoverlapping subsequences, each with a length of p (patch_size = 2). This operation not only reduces the sequence length but also enables the model to capture degradation patterns within local windows. Each block is mapped to a 1D-dimensional embedding space through a learnable linear projection layer.

To preserve the temporal order, a fixed sinusoidal positional encoding E pos is introduced and added to the embedding vectors 8 Z=Z0+Epos

Unlike the image-based V-Transformer, the positional encoding E pos here directly reflects the cycle number rather than spatial coordinates, ensuring that the model recognizes the irreversibility of the aging process.

Then, input sequence is then fed into the Transformer encoder block. Each block adopts a Post-Layer Normalization structure, and performs Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) followed by a feedforward transformation. In this paper, the standard Feedforward Neural Network (FFN) is replaced with the proposed AGLU module.

Subsequently, the AGLU module processes the output from the attention layer 11 Hnor=LayerNorm(H) 12 Zout=AGLU(Hnor)+H where LayerNorm(·)­denotes layer normalization without affine parameters; AGLU(·) represents the function characterizing the AGLU module, which is discussed in Section . This Post-Layer Normalization structure ensures that the inputs to both the attention and feedforward sublayers are normalized, promoting stable gradient propagation during training.

Finally, global average pooling layer is used to aggregate all the patch embeddings, generating a global representation h. 13 h=1N∑i=1Nzifinal

This approach avoids the overfitting risk of learnable tokens in small sample scenarios while significantly reducing the number of parameters. Finally, a lightweight MLP head with Dropout regularization is used to output the normalized SoH prediction value. 14 ŷ=MLPreg(h)=Dense(Dropout(ReLU(Dense(h)))) where Dense(x) = Wx + b denotes a fully connected layer that applies an affine transformation to the input; ReLU(x) = max (0,x) denotes the Rectified Linear Unit activation function, which introduces nonlinearity into the network; Dropout(•) is a regularization technique that randomly sets a fraction of input units to zero during training (with a predefined dropout rate) to prevent overfitting; MLPreg(·) denotes a lightweight multilayer perceptron regression head composed of the above operations, mapping the global representation h to the final SoH prediction ŷ.

Adaptive Gated Linear Unit (AGLU)

The amplitude of battery signals exhibits significant drift caused by multiple factors, including battery aging, temperature fluctuations, and load variations. Conventional fixed activation functions (e.g., ReLU and GELU) are inadequate for handling such nonstationary characteristics, since the response depends solely on local values and lacks the ability to capture global distribution patterns or contextual variations. Consequently, these functions demonstrate suboptimal performance under diverse operational conditions or under different battery systems. To address this limitation, SiGLU incorporates a Sigmoid-based gating mechanism to modulate the output of linear transformations, by adopting the form σ­(W g x)⊙(W v x), which facilitates an input-adaptive soft selection process. However, gating signal σ­(W g x) has the issue of easily saturating, leading to a loss of discriminative power when the battery signal experiences amplitude drift.

To overcome the above issue, AGLU is proposed. AGLU is specifically designed to enhance robustness against the nonstationary and nonlinear nature of battery aging signals. It utilizes a learnable gating mechanism that can dynamically balance the preservation of raw amplitude information with the capture of normalized structural patterns, thus adapting to signal drift.

Given an input x, AGLU is as follows 15 AGLU(x)=σ(Wgx+αLayerNorm(Wgx))⊙(Wvx)︸adaptive⁢gate where W g and W v are a learnable weight matrix; σ is the sigmoid function; ⊙represents element-wise multiplication; α is a learnable scalar parameter, initialized to zero.

When α = 0, AGLU reduces to the standard SiGLU. During the training process, α is optimized jointly with other model parameters through backpropagation. The training dynamics allow α to automatically adjust the balance between the original amplitude information W g x and the normalized structural information LayerNorm­(W g x). This adaptive mechanism enables AGLU to dynamically modulate the intensity of nonlinear transformations based on the input features and the specific degradation patterns encountered. On one hand, the unnormalized path (W g x) retains discriminative cues related to the physical magnitude of the signal. On the other hand, the normalized path LayerNorm­(W g x) provides robust structural information that is less sensitive to input scale variations caused by different batteries, aging stages, or operating conditions. This capability is critical for achieving high-precision SoH estimation under cross-battery generalization scenarios.

Experimental Procedure, Results, and Analysis

Experimental Data and Experimental Procedure

Experimental Data

The experimental data are sourced from aging data of lithium-ion battery packs obtained through laboratory measurements. The materials used in the experiment are Lishen 18650 NCM (Nickel Cobalt Manganese) lithium-ion batteries. Each single cell has a nominal capacity of 2.5 Ah and a voltage of 3.6 V. The lithium-ion battery pack is made up of 8 cells connected in series. Based on the NEWARE high-performance battery testing system set up in the laboratory, aging tests are conducted on the battery pack and individual cells under room temperature conditions, and the aging data of the lithium-ion battery pack and each cell are recorded. The battery pack aging testing system is shown in Figure . This system uses the high-precision Arbin LBT battery testing equipment (60 V, 50 A, 4 channels) as the main charge and discharge testing platform, integrated with a host computer monitoring interface for data acquisition. Furthermore, a temperature measurement module is supplemented for real-time temperature monitoring. This configuration allows for the collection of aging data in the lithium-ion battery pack under room temperature conditions.

Aging test system for lithium-ion battery packs.

The aging experiment for the lithium-ion battery pack begins with constant current charging at 1.25 A until the terminal voltage of lithium-ion battery pack reaches the cutoff voltage of 33.6 V. Then, lithium-ion battery pack is charged at a constant voltage of 33.6 V until current drops to 48 mA, charging process is terminated. Finally, the battery pack discharges at a constant current of 2.5 A. The experiment is terminated when the maximum discharge capacity of the battery pack reaches the failure threshold. After 804 charging–discharging cycles, the capacity degraded to 71.8%, the aging test ended. The capacity degradation trend of the lithium-ion battery pack is shown in Figure .

V-Transformer-AGLU Algorithm Results

Correlation Analysis Between FIF and SoH

This study aims to validate the correlation between FIF and battery aging. To determine the optimal fusion strategy, we analyzed the impact of fusion weights on feature performance across 8-cell, 6-cell, and 2-cell data sets, the experiment results are shown in Figure . The results indicate that for multicell series systems (8-cell and 6-cell), higher weights for the median feature generally yield slightly better correlations due to its superior robustness against outliers and cell inconsistencies. In contrast, for the 2-cell data set, the correlation remains invariant to weight changes, as the mean and median values are numerically identical when the sample size is two. Despite these minor variations, the overall fluctuation of the correlation coefficient across different weight settings is small, demonstrating the stability of the constructed feature. Therefore, an equal weighting factor (ω1 = 0.5) is adopted as the default setting to ensure simplicity, reproducibility, and generalization capability.

Pearson correlation coefficients under different ω1 for various battery packs.

Using this setting, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the FIF and SoH is calculated to be −0.9961 for the 8-cell battery pack. As shown in Figure , the trends of FIF and SoH change with respect to the cycle number are illustrated specifically for 8-cell configuration. The FIF exhibits a distinct growth pattern as the cycle increases, while the SoH shows a corresponding decline. This is primarily attributed to the irreversible increase in internal resistance during battery cycling. Such results visually demonstrate that the FIF can effectively capture the physical aging mechanisms of the battery.

Trend of FIF and SoH changes with cycles (8-cell battery pack).

SoH Estimation Performance Under the 50%–50% Train-Test Split

Conclusion

This paper addresses the issues of weak feature representation ability and insufficient long-range dependency modeling in the SoH estimation. We propose a SoH estimation framework based on FIF and V-Transformer-AGLU. FIF integrates the weighted mean and median of dynamic impedance within the SoC range of 0.3–0.7, which balances aging sensitivity and noise resistance. This feature allows the FIF to adequately reflect intrinsic degradation mechanisms such as SEI film growth, making it particularly suitable for battery packs characterized by cell inconsistencies. Furthermore, we introduce V-Transformer for the first time in one-dimensional aging sequence modeling, efficiently capturing global cross-cycle dependencies through the temporal patching mechanism. By combining with the AGLU, which dynamically integrates magnitude and structural information, the model significantly enhances robustness to nonstationary signals.

Comprehensive experiments are conducted on real-world battery pack data sets, including cross-pack generalization tests on 8-cell, 6-cell, and 2-cell packs. The proposed method achieves an RMSE as low as 0.0054 and an R 2 of 0.9868, significantly outperforming mainstream features (TCC, Vavg-CC, ICA-Peak) and other models. Meanwhile, the method maintains high prediction accuracy without parameter retuning across different pack configurations, it verifies strong cross-pack generalization ability. This research provides an efficient and practical new method for SoH estimation. Future work will focus on model lightweighting and transfer applications in complex operating conditions under different temperature.
