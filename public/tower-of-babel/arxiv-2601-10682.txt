Implementation of Oblivious Transfer over Binary-Input AWGN Channels by Polar Codes
Pin-Hsun Lin 1 , Hadi Aghaee 1 , Christian Deppe 1 , Eduard A. Jorswieck 1 , Holger Boche 2

Abstract
We develop a ( 2 1 ) \binom{2}{1} -oblivious transfer (OT) protocol over the binary-input additive white Gaussian noise (BI–AWGN) channel using polar codes. The scheme uses two decoder views linked by automorphisms of the polar transform and publicly draws the encoder at random from the corresponding automorphism group. This yields perfect receiver privacy at any finite blocklength, since the public encoder distribution is independent of the receiver’s choice bit. Sender privacy is obtained asymptotically via channel polarization combined with privacy amplification. Because the construction deliberately injects randomness on selected bad bit-channels, we derive a relaxed reliability criterion and evaluate finite-blocklength performance. Finally, we characterize the polar-transform automorphisms as bit-level permutations of bit-channel indices, and exploit this structure to derive and optimize an achievable finite-blocklength OT rate.

I
Introduction

Oblivious transfer (OT) is a fundamental building block for two-party secure computation: once
\binom{2}{1}
–OT is available, any polynomial-time functionality can be securely computed by standard composition, so OT sits neatly between low-level communication and high-level privacy services
[
20
,
7
]
. In practice, OT powers private set intersection, private information retrieval, commit-and-reveal / contract-signing, and private learning and inference. It can be realized in two qualitatively different ways: (i) information-theoretically, from noisy communication resources without computational assumptions; and (ii) computationally, from a small public-key “seed” plus fast symmetric-key extensions. This dual nature makes OT central to both cryptography and communications
[
4
,
22
]
.

At the same time, OT cannot be constructed from scratch from arbitrary weak randomness, reflecting a central impossibility phenomenon for interactive cryptographic protocols based on indistinguishability—including bit commitment, zero-knowledge, secret sharing, and secure two-party computation—even against efficient adversaries. Dodis et al. show that OT cannot be realized using imperfect randomness from any weak entropy source, including slightly imperfect Santha–Vazirani (SV) sources
[
13
,
26
]
. Roughly, any two functions that produce computationally indistinguishable outputs from such sources must agree on almost all inputs, contradicting the inherent unpredictability required for OT. Thus, one must either assume stronger (e.g., cryptographic) primitives or exploit richer physical randomness such as channel noise.

Classical work defined and related several OT variants—Rabin’s OT
[
25
]
, Even–Goldreich–Lempel’s
1
-out-of-
2
OT, and Crépeau’s equivalence between forms of OT
[
15
,
9
]
—and Crépeau–Kilian showed how to achieve OT from weakened assumptions
[
7
]
. Beaver’s “random OT” cleanly separated the generation of an offline OT correlation from its online consumption
[
4
]
. Moving into
information-theoretic
OT, noisy-channel realizations appeared first: Crépeau gave protocols from noisy channels
[
10
]
, and efficient unconditional OT from (almost) any noisy channel was later obtained by Crépeau, Morozov, and Wolf
[
8
]
. For specific channel models, Stebila–Wolf proved feasibility for nontrivial binary symmetric channels (BSCs)
[
33
]
, and Imai, Morozov, and Nascimento studied the oblivious transfer capacity of erasure channels
[
19
]
. The landscape was further clarified by symmetry-based characterizations
[
37
]
and models with weak/noisy assumptions
[
38
]
.

Noisy channels provide a much richer entropy source than weak randomness: although their overall behavior can be characterized statistically, the exact noise pattern varies unpredictably across transmissions, making repeated outputs almost never identical. This insight motivated a capacity-oriented viewpoint. Ahlswede and Csiszár initiated the study of OT capacity from noisy resources
[
1
]
, while Nascimento and Winter gave a general formulation of OT capacity for noisy correlations, with both achievability and converse bounds
[
23
,
22
]
. Their results were refined for generalized-erasure-type models and related settings
[
2
,
19
]
. Beyond point-to-point links, shared noisy channels—where multiple users interact with a common medium—remain comparatively less explored, but they offer correlated noise that can enhance privacy, reduce communication cost, and support joint encoding/decoding, making them promising for extending OT to broadcast and multiple-access scenarios. In the context of symmetric private information retrieval (SPIR), this line of research has been studied under the OT framework, where a noisy channel between the parties is leveraged to achieve information-theoretic security; see, for example,
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
. These works also develop achievability schemes that exploit physical randomness to induce information asymmetry between the parties.

More recently, these information-theoretic limits have been revisited with practical, coding-based protocols. Oggier and Z’emor derive an explicit coding-theoretic OT protocol over binary symmetric channels that is secure against malicious behavior
[
24
]
. Their constructions achieve a concrete positive rate using linear codes whose Schur squares are asymptotically good, relying on standard building blocks such as polar codes for reliability. Recent
generalized erasure channel (GEC)
-based OT protocol construction
[
34
]
starts from discrete channels with finite alphabets and emulates generalized
erasures via alphabet extension and subspace/complement labeling induced by linear constraints from the polar

Moreover, the bit-channel mutual information translates the secrecy constraints

and
Secrecy for Alice (SfA)

(4)

To make the permutation step in our OT construction explicit, efficiently

Compared with the constructions in

\textsf{C}_{\mathrm{OT}}(W)
is the supremum of all achievable OT rates.

III
Main results

In this section, we explain our motivation, introduce the proposed OT protocol, derive a relaxed reliability and prove the
SfA
and
SfB
followed by an illustrative example.

III-A

Motivation and setup

The asymmetric information at the transmitter Alice and the receiver Bob is essential for
OT
protocol design. There exists an elegant and simple
OT
protocol by transmitting over
BEC
without channel coding, because such a use of
BEC
efficiently provides such asymmetry. In contrast, in the classical use of polar codes,
BBCs
are frozen to, e.g., zeros,
BBCs
are completely useless for the receiver, while
GBCs
can be successfully decoded, when the code is properly designed. In this way, the transmitter and the receiver shares identical information, which cannot be used for
OT
.

Our work is motivated by the presence of
BBCs
, which naturally admit an erasure-like abstraction. The simulation results below support this motivation: even when a subset of
BBCs
is used to carry random bits unknown to Bob, the decoder remains operational with a controlled performance degradation, providing the intended asymmetry. The setting is as follows. The polar codes decoded by
belief propagation (BP)
following the construction in
[
5
,
6
]
.
We consider blocklength
n=
512 (dashed curves) and
n=
1024 (solid curves), code rate as
1/2
,
10^{4}
transmitted codewords, SNRs= 1, 2, 3, and 4 dB. The cases of
n=512
and
n=1024
consider numbers of random bits unknown to Bob on
BBCs
as rand=0, 2, 4, 8 and 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 16, respectively. Note that rnd=0 means that it is the classical way of using polar codes. The uncoded
binary phase-shift keying (BPSK)
curve serves as a baseline.
By observing Fig.
1
, we see that even when random bits are inserted on a subset of
BBCs
, the decoder can still operate reliably as long as the injected portion is chosen appropriately. In particular, the
bit error rate (BER)
increases in a controlled manner, depending on the target value. This controlled degradation provides the design degree of freedom, the
asymmetric information/view
at Alice and Bob, which can be exploited to construct an
OT
protocol.
By this way, we can emulate an erasure-like abstraction reminiscent of a
BEC
, while keeping the
GBCs
sufficiently reliable for the intended reconstruction.
In short, the idea behind Fig.
1
illustrates a practical way that lets us trade reliability for erasures.

Figure 1:
BER comparison of letting
BBCs
carry random bits unknown to Bob.

Note that in the following, we will propose a protocol relying on left-permuting the polarization matrix
\mathbf{T}
, i.e., permuting the rows of
\mathbf{T}
.
By associativity of the linear encoding operation, encoding with a row-permuted
\mathbf{T}
is equivalent to encoding with the original
\mathbf{T}
, but with the input message bits permuted.
In polar coding, for a given
\mathbf{T}
and channel, each input bit together with the channel output and the previously decoded bits (under successive decoding) defines a synthesized bit-channel.
Hence, when we view the permutation as a relabeling of the input-bit indices, permuting the input bits induces the same permutation of the bit-channels.
Therefore, if the bit-channel qualities are ordered by a performance metric, e.g., mutual information or Bhattacharyya parameter, then under the permuted labeling the quality order is permuted accordingly.
This fact will be used repeatedly in developing the proposed protocol.

III-D

Reliability

In our OT protocol, Alice intentionally injects independent random bits on
selected
BBCs
to control information leakage, where the corresponding secrecy
analysis is developed in the next section. This is a non-standard use of a polar
code: these
BBCs
are not merely frozen, and their
randomness can propagate through the polar transform and influence the decoding
result of the bits that Bob uses to generate the key for recovering
M_{b}
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no error analysis in
the literature for this setting. Therefore, we first estimate the resulting
hash-input decoding error probability

\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}:=\Pr(\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}\neq U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}})
, where
\mathcal{S}_{b}\subseteq[n]
denotes the index set of hash-input bits
used to generate the key
K_{b}
, and then use the empirical estimate of
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}
as a tool to upper bound the OT reliability error
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}
through

\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}:=\Pr(\widehat{M}_{b}\neq M_{b})\leq\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}
.

Lemma 3
.

Fix
b\in\{0,1\}
. Let
g
be drawn from a
universal hash families (UHF)
family and then fixed, and define

K_{b}:=g(U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}})
and
\widehat{K}_{b}:=g(\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}})
.
Define
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,key}}:=\Pr(\widehat{K}_{b}\neq K_{b})
.
Assume
\widehat{M}_{b}
is decoded by one-time-pad using
\widehat{K}_{b}
at Bob. Then the following holds:

\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}=\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,key}}\;\leq\;\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}.

(17)

Proof.

Define the events

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}:=\{\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}\neq U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}\}

and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}:=\{\widehat{K}_{b}\neq K_{b}\}
.
If
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}^{c}
occurs, i.e.,
\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}=U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}
, then

\widehat{K}_{b}=g(\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}})=g(U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}})=K_{b}
, so

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}
cannot occur, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}^{c}=\emptyset
, and thus

\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}^{c})=0
.
Therefore,

\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}})=\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}})+\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}^{c})=\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{key}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}})\leq\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}})
,
i.e.,
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,key}}\leq\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}
.
Finally, since
\widehat{M}_{b}=(M_{b}\oplus K_{b})\oplus\widehat{K}_{b}
, we have

\widehat{M}_{b}\neq M_{b}
if and only if
\widehat{K}_{b}\neq K_{b}
, and therefore

\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}=\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,key}}
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[n]
denote the set of bit-channel indices
i
for which the random
U_{i}
is unknown to Bob a priori. In our OT construction,
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}_{b}\cup\mathcal{R}_{b}
includes the set of indices of random bits placed on
GBCs

U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}=(U_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{b}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{b}
, and the set of indices of the random bits placed on
BBCs
, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{b}
. In addition, bit-channel indices belonging to
\mathcal{A}^{c}
are frozen to zero. Let
i^{\star}:=\max(\mathcal{S}_{b})
. Since
successive cancellation deocder (SCD)
proceeds sequentially in the index order
1,2,\dots,n
, the decisions on the hash-input bits in
\mathcal{S}_{b}
depend only on the decoder’s past decisions up to time
i^{\star}
. Therefore, only unfrozen/unknown indices before
i^{\star}
can influence the correctness of
\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}
through error propagation. This motivates the prefix set

\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}:=\mathcal{A}\cap\{1,2,\dots,i^{\star}\}
, which collects exactly those indices that are decided by the SCD and occur no later than the last hash-input index.

Lemma 4
.

Using SCD, the decoding error of the hash input satisfies

\displaystyle\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}\leq\Pr(\exists\,j\in\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}:\ \widehat{U}_{j}\neq U_{j})\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}}Z(W_{n}^{(j)})

(18)

Proof.

Recall
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}:=\{\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}\neq U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}\}
and define

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}}:=\{\exists\,j\in\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}:\ \widehat{U}_{j}\neq U_{j}\}
.
If
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{c}
occurs, then all indices in
\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}
are decoded correctly. Since
\mathcal{S}_{b}\subseteq\{1,\dots,i^{\star}\}
, this implies
\widehat{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}=U_{\mathcal{S}_{b}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}
cannot occur. Equivalently,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{c}=\emptyset
. Hence
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}=\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}}\bigr)\,\cup\,\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}}^{c}\bigr)=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}},
which implies that
\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}})=\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hin}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}})\leq\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}})
. For the second inequality, we can apply the standard bound from polar codes analysis
[
3
]
to the set
\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}
to obtain
\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pre}})\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}}Z(W_{n}^{(j)})
.
∎

Combine Lemma
3
and Lemma
4
, we can have a simple upper bound of
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}=\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,key}}\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{\leq i^{\star}}}Z(W_{n}^{(j)}).

(19)

However, the rightmost term takes into account the error probabilities of
BBCs
, which implies that it is loose.
In the following, we develop an upper bound on
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}
with a prescribed confidence level via
Monte–Carlo simulation.
In each trial, we declare an error if the polar-decoding is wrong, i.e., Bob fails to reconstruct the hash input for key generation. This error event implies an OT error, and thus its probability upper-bounds

\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}
. Consequently, it suffices to upper-bound this error probability from Monte–Carlo
simulation, which yields a relaxed version of the reliability requirement in (
5
).

Lemma 5
.

Fix the protocol parameters and run
M
independent Monte–Carlo trials. Let
k\in\{0,\dots,\textsf{M}\}

be the number of errors.
For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, define
\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(k;\textsf{M},\delta)
as the unique
u\in(0,1]
satisfying

\Pr(X\leq k)=\delta
, where
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\textsf{M},u)
. Then

\displaystyle\Pr\!\Big(\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}\leq\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(k;\textsf{M},\delta)\Big)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

(20)

The proof is relegated to Appendix
A
.

Remark 1
.

In principle, we want a deterministic reliability constraint

\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}\leq\varepsilon
as in (
19
). However, obtaining a tractable analytic upper bound on
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}
with sufficient tightness is difficult.
We therefore relax this deterministic constraint by a statistical one
based on Monte–Carlo experiments. In particular, we run the polar code decoding
M
times and let

K:=\sum_{t=1}^{\textsf{M}}E_{t}
be the number of errors, where

E_{t}:=\mathds{1}\{\text{the $t$-th trial fails}\}
as defined in Lemma
5
.
Let
p:=\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}}
, we have
K\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\textsf{M},p)
.
Given the observation
K=k
, we can compute the upper confidence bound

\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(k;\textsf{M},\delta)
, which is a deterministic number.
Then we can guarantee (
20
). Therefore, enforcing the verifiable condition
\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(k;\textsf{M},\delta)\leq\varepsilon

implies that
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}\leq\varepsilon

with confidence at least
1-\delta
.

Example 2
.

Assume we use
\textsf{M}=10^{6}
i.i.d. trials to conduct the Monte-Carlo simulation, which measures an empirical error probability
\widehat{p}=10^{-3}
. Let
\delta=10^{-6}
. Then, we have
K=\widehat{p}\cdot\textsf{M}=1000
errors. Since
K\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\textsf{M},\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,hin}})
, Lemma
5
yields

\Pr(\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}\leq\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(K;\textsf{M},\delta))\geq 1-\delta
, where for
K<\textsf{M}
,

\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(K;\textsf{M},\delta)=I^{-1}_{1-\delta}(K+1,\textsf{M}-K)
, where
I_{x}(a,b):=\frac{1}{B(a,b)}\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt,\,B(a,b):=\;\int_{0}^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt
is the beta function.
Substituting in
(K,\textsf{M},\delta)=(1000,10^{6},10^{-6})
gives

\overline{p}_{\mathrm{CP}}(1000;10^{6},10^{-6})=1.16\times 10^{-3}
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-10^{-6}
,
\textsf{P}_{\mathrm{e,OT}}\leq 1.1586\times 10^{-3}
.

III-E

SfA
and
SfB

In this section, we prove
SfA
and
SfB
. We first do the following setup. Over a BI–AWGN channel with
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)

\rho
, polarization splits the bit-channels into
GBCs
and
BBCs
indices. In particular, in the asymptotic case,
GBCs
behave as noiseless (non-erasures) and
BBCs
as useless
(erasures). In the asymptotic regime, no privacy-amplification is needed. In contrast, at finite
n
the impact of imperfections and the proposed solutions are discussed in Sec.
III-H
. Fix
\mathbf{A}\in\Aut(\mathbf{T})
with order
N
(cf. Definition
6
) and let permutation matrices act on left multiplication,
\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{T}\mapsto\mathbf{P}\mathbf{T}
. Consider a vanishing sequence
\gamma_{n}\downarrow 0,\,n\in\mathds{N}
, and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{T})

\displaystyle:=\{i:\ I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{T};\rho)\geq 1-\gamma_{n}\},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T})

\displaystyle:=\{i:\ I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{T};\rho)\leq\gamma_{n}\},

(22)

where we additionally parameterize
I_{i}^{(n)}
as
I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{T};\rho)
, to emphasize that the bit-channel capacity is a function of the polarization matrix
\mathbf{T}
and the channel, i.e., here, via the
SNR

\rho
for the
AWGN
channel. Note that the bit–channel mutual information

I_{i}=I(U_{i};Y^{n},U^{i-1}),\,i\in[n]
is defined by the joint
distribution
(U^{n},Y^{n})
and do not depend on a particular decoder.
In particular, for any
i
and any decoding strategy applied to
Y^{n}
, data processing
gives
I(U_{i};Y^{n})\leq I(U_{i};Y^{n},U^{i-1})=I_{i}
. Thus, if an index is
classified into
BBCs
in the sense
I_{i}\leq\gamma_{n}
, no decoder can
extract more than
\gamma_{n}
bits of information about that random bit.

In our OT protocol, Alice injects independent uniform random bits on selected
indices. Although these bits are random a priori, at finite blocklength the
corresponding bit–channels are not perfectly polarized, so mutual
information of
BBCs
is typically small but not zero. As a result, Bob’s channel output
(and any decoder side information, including previously decoded bits) can still
be statistically correlated with these injected bits, leading to nonzero leakage.
Moreover, the public permutation/automorphism used in the protocol may map some
of these indices to positions that are comparatively more reliable for Bob,
which can further increase this finite-
n
leakage. Therefore, we require an
explicit upper bound on how much information Bob can obtain about the injected
random bits to design the privacy-amplification that removes the
residual leakage. The following lemma quantifies this leakage in terms of the
bit–channel mutual information.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\gamma_{n}\in[0,1]
and let
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}):=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(\gamma_{n})
.
Fix any subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T})
and list its elements as

i_{1}<\dots<i_{|\mathcal{S}|}
.
Assume
U_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\tfrac{1}{2})
independently for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
, and set
U_{i}:=0
for all

i\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T})\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Then, we have

I\bigl(U_{\mathcal{S}};\,Y^{n}\bigr)\;\leq\;|\mathcal{S}|\,\gamma_{n}.

The proof is relegated to Appendix
B
. In particular,
if Alice places independent random bits on a subset

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(\gamma_{n})
, even though these bits cannot be
reliably decoded by Bob, the total information leakage is at most
|\mathcal{S}|\,\gamma_{n}
, which can be removed
by privacy amplification. Detailed discussion will be given in Section
III-H
.

Remark 2
.

The choice of the selected set

induces a tradeoff among
(leakage, reliability, OT rate), as follows.

•

Leakage impact:

upper bounds the leakage due to the injected random bits on

by

Hence, enlarging

Moreover, polar code analysis yields

. Therefore, if we select

, then the OT bit-rate contributed by these

. Moreover, the leakage vanishes for the usual polar choice

•

Reliability impact:

Lemma

For fixed

nondecreasing
†
†

†

(\textsf{M},\delta)

Characterization of

In this section we show that every automorphism of

Lemma 13

\displaystyle\big|\Pr\big((\widetilde{M},\widetilde{C},\widetilde{E})\in\mathcal{A}\big)-\Pr\big((\widetilde{M},\widetilde{C}^{\prime},\widetilde{E}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\big)\big|

IV
Conclusion

We developed an explicit, implementation-friendly polar-code based 2-1 OT protocol over binary-input memoryless
channels, and we quantified its performance over the BI–AWGN channel.
The construction views polarization through a virtual binary-erasure interface: reliable bit-channels carry Bob’s
chosen message, while a paired set of unreliable bit-channels is arranged to convey essentially no information about
the unchosen message.
To mitigate leakage caused by revealing the polarization transform, we restricted to permutations that preserve the
polar transform and used them to create different views of the good/bad index structure at Alice and Bob.
We further strengthened the erasure-like behavior at finite blocklength by injecting random bits on a carefully
selected subset of unreliable bit-channels and applying privacy amplification via universal hashing.
A central technical contribution was a complete characterization of the transform-preserving permutations:
we showed that they are exactly those induced by reordering the stages of the polar transform (equivalently,
permuting the bit positions of the binary index representation), yielding a concrete and fully implementable family of
admissible permutations.
Building on this structure, we introduced a finite-blocklength OT-rate optimization method that selects both the
permutation and the paired index sets using standard polar-code reliability estimates, enabling fast OT-rate
evaluation under leakage and reliability constraints.
All information-theoretic claims were proved within the paper, and extensive simulations corroborated the predicted
leakage–reliability–rate tradeoffs.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{m}
with bit-wise order

x\leq_{b}y\mbox{ iff }x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in[m]

9

\mathbf{T}_{x,y}=\mathds{1}\{\,y\leq_{b}x\,\}

x,y\in\mathcal{X}

\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
be a bijection, and let

\mathbf{P}_{\pi}
be the corresponding permutation matrix, whose

x
-th column is
e_{\pi(x)}
, i.e., (
30

Then
(\mathbf{P}_{\pi}^{\top})_{x,u}=(\mathbf{P}_{\pi})_{u,x}
.

For any

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbf{P}_{\pi}^{\top}\mathbf{T}\,\mathbf{P}_{\pi}\bigr)_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}(\mathbf{P}_{\pi}^{\top})_{x,u}\,\mathbf{T}_{u,v}\,(\mathbf{P}_{\pi})_{v,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}(\mathbf{P}_{\pi})_{u,x}\,\mathbf{T}_{u,v}\,(\mathbf{P}_{\pi})_{v,y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v}\mathds{1}\{u=\pi(x)\}\,\mathbf{T}_{u,v}\,\mathds{1}\{v=\pi(y)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}_{\pi(x),\pi(y)}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}\{\,\pi(y)\leq_{b}\pi(x)\,\}.

\mathbf{P}_{\pi}^{\top}\mathbf{T}\,\mathbf{P}_{\pi}=\mathbf{T}

110

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{Q}

\pi_{\mathbf{Q}}

\mathcal{X}

9

\mathbf{T}_{u,v}=\mathds{1}\{\,v\leq u\,\}.

) states that their logarithms coincide up to an
o(\sqrt{n})
term. Now fix
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\subseteq[n]
and let

Z:=(Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}}).
Set the source to be recovered as

\tilde{X}:=U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}|},
so
\tilde{X}
takes values in a finite alphabet. Note that
Y^{n}
may be continuous while
\tilde{X}
is discrete,
P_{\tilde{X}|Z}(x|z)
is well-defined and the Slepian–Wolf bounds used in
[
16
, Sec. 3.1–Sec. 3.2]

remain valid for the pair
(\tilde{X},Z)
, due to
[
16
, Remark 1]
.
Applying Lemma
18
with side information
Z
and target error

\varepsilon_{\rm sw}
, there exists a Slepian–Wolf encoder that outputs a public message

M_{p}\in\{1,\dots,\textsf{M}\}
such that Bob can reconstruct
\tilde{X}=U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}
from
(Z,M_{p})
with
error probability at most
\varepsilon_{\rm sw}
, provided that

\ell_{\rm SWC}\;\geq\;\log_{2}\textsf{M}(n,\varepsilon_{\rm sw})\;=\;H(\tilde{X}|Z)\;+\;\beta_{n}(\varepsilon_{\rm sw}),

(159)

where
\beta_{n}(\varepsilon_{\rm sw}):=\sqrt{n\,\textsf{V}(\tilde{X}|Z)}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\varepsilon_{\rm sw})+o(\sqrt{n}),\,\textsf{V}(\tilde{X}|Z):=\operatorname{Var}\!\left[-\log_{2}P_{\tilde{X}|Z}(\tilde{X}|Z)\right].

It remains to upper bound the first-order term
H(\tilde{X}|Z)=H(U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\,|\,Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}})

by the sum on the RHS of (
50
).

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{m}\}
.
By the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle H(U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\,|\,Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{m}H\!\left(U_{i_{t}}\,\middle|\,Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}},U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{t-1}}\right).

(160)

In the following, we split all past bits
U^{i_{t}-1}

and future side-information bits with indices
\geq i_{t}
. By such a way, we can drop the additional future bits and upper bound
the term by
H(U_{i_{t}}\,|\,Y^{n},U^{i_{t}-1})
.

By definition of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}
, it is clear that

\displaystyle\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1}\}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}\cap[i_{t}-1].

(161)

Then we can derive the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1}\}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}\cap[i_{t}-1]\bigr)

(162)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}\bigr)\cap\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup[i_{t}-1]\bigr)

(163)

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}[n]\cap\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup[i_{t}-1]\bigr)

(164)

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{=}\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup[i_{t}-1]

(165)

\displaystyle\overset{(e)}{=}\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cap[i_{t}-1]\bigr)\ \cup\ \bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cap\{i_{t},\ldots,n\}\bigr)\ \cup\ [i_{t}-1]

(166)

\displaystyle\overset{(f)}{=}[i_{t}-1]\ \cup\ \bigl(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cap\{i_{t},\ldots,n\}\bigr),

(167)

where (a) is due to (
161
), (b) is due to
\mathcal{A}\cup(\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{C})=(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B})\cap(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{C})
, (c) is due to
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}=[n]
, (d) is due to
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cup[i_{t}-1]\subseteq[n]
, (e) is by splitting
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}
into two subsets, (f) is due to
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cap[i_{t}-1]=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1}\}\subseteq[i_{t}-1]
.

From (
167
), we have

\displaystyle\{U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}},U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{t-1}}\}=\{U^{i_{t}-1},\,U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cap\{i_{t},\ldots,n\}}\}.

(168)

Consequently, we can derive

\displaystyle H\!\left(U_{i_{t}}\,\middle|\,Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}},U_{i_{1}},\ldots,U_{i_{t-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=H\!\left(U_{i_{t}}\,\middle|\,Y^{n},U^{i_{t}-1},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}\cap\{i_{t},\ldots,n\}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq H\!\left(U_{i_{t}}\,\middle|\,Y^{n},U^{i_{t}-1}\right).

(169)

Combining (
160
) and (
169
), we have

H(U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\,|\,Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}})\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}H(U_{i}\,|\,Y^{n},U^{i-1}).

By definition of the polarized bit-channel mutual information,
I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{F}):=I(U_{i};\,Y^{n},U^{i-1})
,
and since
U_{i}
is uniform on
\{0,1\}
, we have

H(U_{i}\,|\,Y^{n},U^{i-1})=H(U_{i})-I(U_{i};\,Y^{n},U^{i-1})=1-I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{F}).

Therefore,

H(U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\,|\,Y^{n},U_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}})\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\bigl(1-I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{F})\bigr).

Thus, if
\ell_{\rm SWC}
satisfies

\ell_{\rm SWC}\;\geq\;\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm SI}^{c}}\bigl(1-I_{i}^{(n)}(\mathbf{F})\bigr)\;+\;\beta_{n}(\varepsilon_{\rm sw}),

then it also satisfies
\ell_{\rm SWC}\geq H(\tilde{X}|Z)+\beta_{n}(\varepsilon_{\rm sw})
by (
159
).
Hence the Slepian–Wolf encoder guaranteed by Lemma
18
exists, and we complete the proof.
∎

Appendix N
Proof of Lemma
18

Proof.

Although the main result in
[
16
]
is written for discrete alphabets,
[
16
, Remark 1]
explains how to extend the entropy-based quantities to the case where
X
is discrete and
Y
may be continuous. In the following, we show the derivation for this extension. Let
X
take values in a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
, and let
Y
take values in a continuous alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
equipped with a reference measure
\mu
. Assume
P_{Y}\ll\mu
and define the Radon–Nikodym derivative
p_{Y}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}
, i.e.,
P_{Y}(\mathrm{d}y)=p_{Y}(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
. Similarly, assume
Q_{Y}\ll\mu
and define
q_{Y}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}
, i.e.,
Q_{Y}(\mathrm{d}y)=q_{Y}(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
, so that

P_{XY}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=P_{X|Y}(x|y)\,p_{Y}(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
.
Consider a Slepian–Wolf source code
\Psi=(e,d)
with encoder

e:\mathcal{X}\to\{1,\dots,\textsf{M}\}
and decoder

d:\{1,\dots,\textsf{M}\}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
.
Define the decoding error probability

P_{s}[\Psi]:=\Pr(X\neq d(e(X),Y))
and the optimal error at message size
M
as

P_{s}(\textsf{M}):=\inf_{\Psi}P_{s}[\Psi]
as in
[
16
, (109)–(112)]
.
Moreover, let
\overline{P}_{s}(\textsf{M})
denote the corresponding error criterion for the hash-based construction in
[
16
, (113)–(114)]
, and let
\textsf{M}(n,\varepsilon)
and

\overline{\textsf{M}}(n,\varepsilon)
be the encoder output size under blocklength-
n
defined in

[
16
, Eqs. (109)–(116)]
. With this convention, any expression in
[
16
, Sec. 3.2]
written as an expectation or
probability under
P_{XY}
carries over with the same algebra. The only change is that sums over
y
are
replaced by integrals, i.e.,
\sum_{y}
becomes
\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)
.
Consequently, the corresponding bounds in
[
16
, Sec. 3.2]
remain valid for our setting
(discrete
\mathcal{X}
and continuous
\mathcal{Y}
).

Recall the following achievability bound from
[
16
, Lemma 13]
:
for any message size
M
,

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{s}(\textsf{M})\;\leq\;\inf_{\gamma\geq 0}\Bigl[P_{XY}\!\Bigl(\log_{2}\frac{1}{P_{X|Y}(X|Y)}>\gamma\Bigr)+\frac{e^{\gamma}}{\textsf{M}}\Bigr].

(170)

This statement depends only on the random variable
\log_{2}\frac{1}{P_{X|Y}(X|Y)}
under
P_{XY}
and
therefore remains valid when
Y
is continuous by
[
16
, Remark 1]
.

Define the conditional information density
\imath_{X|Y}(x;y)\;:=\;\log_{2}\frac{1}{P_{X|Y}(x|y)}.

After applying (
170
) to the
n
-fold i.i.d. model, by memorylessness we have:

-\log_{2}P_{X^{n}|Y^{n}}(X^{n}|Y^{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\imath_{X|Y}(X_{i};Y_{i}).

Let
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\imath_{X|Y}(X_{i};Y_{i})
. By the
central limit theorem (CLT)
under finite second moment assumption, we have the convergence in distribution

\frac{S_{n}-nH(X|Y)}{\sqrt{n\textsf{V}(X|Y)}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\textsf{V}(X|Y):=\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\log_{2}\frac{1}{P_{X|Y}(X|Y)}\bigr]
is the variance of information density
defined in
[
16
, (9)]
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<1
and set
\textsf{R}:=\sqrt{\textsf{V}(X|Y)}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\varepsilon).

Choose

\textsf{M}:=\exp\!\bigl(nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R}\bigr),\mbox{ and }\gamma:=nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R}-n^{1/4}

as in
[
16
, Proof of Theorem 11]
. Then we have:

\displaystyle\Pr(S_{n}>\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\frac{S_{n}-nH(X|Y)}{\sqrt{n\,\textsf{V}(X|Y)}}>\frac{\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R}-n^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n\,\textsf{V}(X|Y)}}\right)\to\varepsilon

(171)

by the
CLT
and the choice of
R
.
Moreover, the penalty term in (
170
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\gamma}}{\textsf{M}}\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\bigl(nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R}-n^{1/4}\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R}\bigr)}\;=\;\exp(-n^{1/4})\;\to\;0.

(172)

Substitute the above choice of
\gamma
into (
170
), we have:

\displaystyle\overline{P}_{s}^{(n)}(\textsf{M})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\!\Bigl(\log_{2}\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}|Y^{n}}(X^{n}|Y^{n})}>\gamma\Bigr)+\frac{e^{\gamma}}{\textsf{M}}=\Pr(S_{n}>\gamma)+\frac{e^{\gamma}}{\textsf{M}}.

(173)

By definition of
\overline{\textsf{M}}(n,\varepsilon)
(cf.
[
16
, Eqs. (109)–(116)]
),
(
173
) implies that for any fixed
\delta>0
, there exists
n_{0}(\delta)
such that
for all
n\geq n_{0}(\delta)
,

\overline{P}_{s}^{(n)}(\textsf{M})\leq\varepsilon+\delta,\qquad\text{with }\ \log\textsf{M}=nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R}.

Equivalently, for all sufficiently large
n
, a message size
\textsf{M}=\exp(nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\,\textsf{R})
is achievable up to an
o(\sqrt{n})
gap in the exponent. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\log_{2}\overline{\textsf{M}}(n,\varepsilon)\leq nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\textsf{R}+o(\sqrt{n}).

(174)

A converse is given in
[
16
, Lemma 18]
: for any
Q_{Y}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
,

\displaystyle P_{s}(\textsf{M})\;\geq\;\sup_{\gamma\geq 0}\Bigl[P_{XY}\!\Bigl(\log_{2}\frac{Q_{Y}(Y)}{P_{XY}(X,Y)}>\gamma\Bigr)-\frac{\textsf{M}}{e^{\gamma}}\Bigr].

(175)

Choosing
Q_{Y}=P_{Y}
and applying (
175
) to the
n
-fold i.i.d. model, the random term inside the probability becomes

\log_{2}\frac{Q_{Y^{n}}(Y^{n})}{P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}(X^{n},Y^{n})}=S_{n}
. In other words, the converse bound
(
175
) depends on the same normalized sum
S_{n}
that appeared in the achievability part.
Again, validity for continuous
Y
is ensured by
[
16
, Remark 1]
.

Now take the same
\textsf{M}=\exp(nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\textsf{R})
as the achievability part, but choose

\gamma:=nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\textsf{R}+n^{1/4}
as in
[
16
, Proof of Theorem 11]
.
Then the same CLT argument used to justify (
171
) yields
\Pr(S_{n}>\gamma)\to\varepsilon
, while the penalty term satisfies
\frac{\textsf{M}}{e^{\gamma}}=\exp(-n^{1/4})\to 0.

Substituting these into (
175
) gives
\liminf_{n\to\infty}P_{s}^{(n)}(\textsf{M})\geq\varepsilon
.
As in
[
16
, Proof of Theorem 11]
, by taking
R
arbitrarily close to
\sqrt{\textsf{V}(X|Y)}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)
from below, we obtain that
for all sufficiently large
n
,
P_{s}^{(n)}(\textsf{M})>\varepsilon
. By the definition of
\textsf{M}(n,\varepsilon)
in

[
16
, Eqs. (115)–(116)]
, for all sufficiently large
n
, we have

\displaystyle\log_{2}\textsf{M}(n,\varepsilon)\geq nH(X|Y)+\sqrt{n}\textsf{R}+o(\sqrt{n}).

(176)

After matching (
174
) and (
176
), we complete the proof.
∎
