The association between animal‑based and plant‑based protein intake with sleep quality, mood, anxiety, depression and stress among elderly: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Batoul Ghosn, Hanieh Abbasi, Parisa Nezhad Hajian, Leila Azadbakht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDietary protein intake may influence sleep quality, mood, anxiety, depression, and stress in older adults, but evidence is limited. This study examined the impact of protein source (animal vs. plant) and quantity on these outcomes in the elderly.MethodsIn this cross‑sectional study, 396 Iranian adults aged 65–85 years with sleep or mental health challenges were recruited from hospitals and clinics. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 168‑item Food Frequency Questionnaire. Psychological health was measured with the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS‑21), sleep quality with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and mood with the Profile of Mood States (POMS).ResultsAfter adjustment for covariates, higher total protein intake was marginally associated with better sleep quality (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.11–0.96) and showed a non‑significant trend toward improved mood (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.14–1.27). Interactions were observed with COVID‑19 history, smoking, and gender. For example, individuals with high protein intake and prior COVID‑19 infection had lower odds of depression (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.76) and a non‑significant trend toward lower stress. Men with high protein intake had lower odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07–0.53) and anxiety (OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.18–0.84). Higher plant protein intake was associated with increased odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.36–5.49). Animal protein intake was marginally linked to poorer sleep (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.51–2.29) but associated with reduced odds of poor mood (OR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.10–0.57) and depression (OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.18–0.85). No significant associations were found between total protein intake and depression, anxiety, or stress in fully adjusted models.ConclusionHigher total protein intake was associated with some improvements in sleep and mood, but findings were inconsistent across outcomes. Animal protein appeared beneficial for mood and depression, whereas both plant and animal protein were linked to poorer sleep quality. These mixed results highlight the complexity of diet–mental health relationships and the need for cautious interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25857-5.

Introduction

Sleep disorders, depression, and anxiety are major public health concerns among older adults [1–3]. In Iran, about 44.1% of adults experience some form of sleep disorder [4], while the global prevalence of anxiety and depression in this age group is estimated at 28% [1, 5]. These conditions not only reduce quality of life but are also linked to higher healthcare costs, suicide risk, and mortality [2, 6–8].

Several biological, psychological, and lifestyle factors contribute to these conditions, including smoking and prior COVID‑19 infection [9]. Dietary factors, particularly macronutrient intake, are also recognized as important influences on mental health and sleep [10]. Among macronutrients, protein is essential for cognitive and psychological health [11, 12] and plays a role in sleep regulation [13–17]. However, evidence on protein source, especially differences between plant and animal sources, remains inconsistent. Some studies report beneficial associations of total protein intake with mood and sleep [18–20] while others find no significant relationships.

Older adults are particularly vulnerable to both malnutrition and mental health challenges [3, 21] making it important to clarify how protein source (animal versus plant) and quantity relate to sleep and psychological outcomes. Yet, many studies have not distinguished between plant and animal protein or examined outcomes beyond depression. Few have also considered potential effect modifiers such as gender, smoking, or COVID-19 history [22, 23] Inconsistent findings, especially regarding protein source, highlight the need for more comprehensive research.

Despite growing evidence, significant gaps remain in understanding the link between protein intake and mental or sleep health. Many studies have not distinguished between plant and animal protein sources or assessed psychological domains beyond depression. For instance, Li et al. (2020) associated total protein with depression but did not differentiate by source, while Wirth et al. (2024) focused on sleep but lacked broader psychological assessment, Moreover, few studies have examined how factors such as gender, smoking, or COVID‑19 history might modify these associations in older adults. Inconsistent findings—especially regarding protein source—highlight the need for more comprehensive, stratified research. To date, few studies have evaluated both protein source (animal versus plant) and quantity in relation to multiple psychological outcomes while accounting for key demographic and health variables. In addition, an Iranian study on women reported no significant associations between dietary inflammatory index (DII), mean adequacy ratio (MAR), dietary energy density (DED), and mental health outcomes measured by DASS‑21, underscoring the complexity of diet–mental health relationships in the local context [24].

Therefore, the objective of this study is to assess the association between protein source (animal vs. plant) and quantity with sleep, mood, anxiety, depression, and stress among older adults. By addressing these gaps, this study aims to provide clearer evidence on the role of dietary protein in geriatric mental health and sleep regulation.

Methods

We recruited community-dwelling elderly outpatients aged 65–85 years from hospitals and clinics affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (southern Tehran) between October 2021 and May 2022, using simple random sampling of appointment lists. Eligibility was based on age and attendance as outpatients at hospitals or affiliated clinics, regardless of their reason for visit, provided they were medically stable and able to complete questionnaires. Eligibility was not determined by pre‑existing psychiatric diagnoses or specific scores on psychological assessments. Individuals with stable chronic cardiometabolic conditions (e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes) stable thyroid conditions, and a history of COVID-19 infection managed with standard medications were eligible, and these conditions were adjusted for as covariates in the analysis. Exclusion criteria included current use of medications known to directly affect sleep, mood, anxiety, stress, or appetite (such as antidepressants, anxiolytics, prescribed hypnotics, antipsychotics, or systemic corticosteroids), acute severe illness, decompensated psychiatric disorders, or cognitive impairment that prevented valid questionnaire completion. All participants completed standardized assessments of psychological health and sleep, including the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Profile of Mood States (POMS). These instruments provided validated measures of depression, anxiety, stress, sleep quality, and mood disturbance. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1399.299) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The ethics approval also covered consent for publication, and verbal consent procedures were documented in accordance with the committee’s guidelines.

Sample size calculation

This cross-sectional study involved 398 elderly residents of southern Tehran. Participants were selected from healthcare centers connected to Tehran University of Medical Sciences between October 2021 and May 2022 using simple random sampling. To qualify, participants had to be 65–85 years old and maintain their usual diet without medically prescribed changes. Exclusion targeted current use of medications known to directly influence sleep, mood, anxiety, stress, or appetite (e.g., antidepressants, anxiolytics, prescribed hypnotics, antipsychotics, systemic corticosteroids). Participants with stable chronic cardiometabolic conditions managed with standard medications were eligible and these were adjusted for as covariates. Abdominal obesity was chosen as the main dependent variable for determining the sample size, as it required the largest sample. A target of at least 400 participants was set to account for potential dropouts. Although the calculation was based on abdominal obesity, the final sample of 398 participants provided adequate statistical power for the current analysis. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that the study had > 80% power to detect the smallest observed odds ratio (OR = 0.24) at α = 0.05, supporting the interpretability of the results.

Assessment of diet

Dietary intake information was collected from participants using a validated semi-quantitative 168-item Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [25, 26]. The FFQ used in this study was validated for the Iranian population [25]. The FFQ was previously validated within the context of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (Mirmiran et al., 2010) [25]. These validation studies support the cultural and linguistic appropriateness of the tools for assessing dietary intake among Iranian older adults. In face-to-face interviews, participants reported how often and in what quantities they consumed various foods over the past year. The FFQ provided standardized serving sizes, enabling participants to specify their consumption frequency as daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly. Daily intake was calculated by considering portion size and frequency, then converted into grams per day using household measurements [27]. Nutrient values, such as energy and other nutrients, were analyzed with Nutritionist 4 software, customized for Iranian foods [28, 29].

Protein source classification

This study classified proteins by source—animal or plant—due to inherent differences in amino acid composition, digestibility, and potential health effects [30]. Animal proteins are considered “complete,” providing all nine essential amino acids in optimal human-aligned proportions [31]. While many plant proteins may be lower in one or more essential amino acids, diverse plant combinations can yield a complete profile [31]. Digestibility also varies, often being lower in some plant proteins [32]. This source-based distinction contextualizes how different protein types might influence outcomes like sleep, mood, depression, anxiety, and stress. For instance, animal protein supports muscle mass and metabolic health [33], while plant proteins are associated with anti-inflammatory effects and improved cardiovascular markers [34]. In this study, we operationalized protein source as animal‑based vs. plant‑based intake, rather than formal measures of protein quality (e.g., PDCAAS, DIAAS), which cannot be derived from FFQ data,.using the Nutritionist 4 software. Animal protein included meats, poultry, fish, eggs, and dairy, while plant protein comprised legumes, grains, nuts, seeds, and vegetables. For mixed dishes, ingredients were disaggregated to assign protein to the appropriate source. We acknowledge that this binary classification is a simplification, as it does not account for digestibility-corrected scores (e.g., PDCAAS, DIAAS), detailed amino acid profiles, or other nutritional components such as fiber and anti-nutrients. These calculations were not feasible with FFQ data. Nonetheless, source-based categorization is a widely used and practical approach in epidemiological studies to examine broad dietary patterns and their health implications. While appropriate for our objectives, this simplification requires cautious interpretation given the broader complexities of protein source and the potential for misclassification in mixed or processed foods.

Psychological health assessment

We used Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale − 21 Items (DASS-21) to evaluate symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. The DASS-21 (Lovibond & Lovibond) [35] is divided into three sections, each with seven items focused on one specific condition [36]. Depression symptoms were assessed through indicators such as dysphoria, hopelessness, life devaluation, self-criticism, lack of interest, anhedonia, and inertia. The anxiety section addressed physiological arousal, muscle responses, and emotional experiences of anxiety. The stress section focused on ongoing levels of non-specific arousal. In our study, we applied predefined cut-off scores to determine the severity of depression, anxiety, and stress: a score of 8 or higher indicated anxiety, 10 or higher indicated depression, and 15 or higher signified stress. These thresholds were consistent with the DASS-21 guidelines and previous research. The DASS-21 has been tested for reliability and validity in both local and international contexts and is available in 34 languages [37, 38]. The DASS-21 has been validated for use in the Iranian population, demonstrating good to excellent internal consistency with reported Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.77 for the depression, 0.85 for the anxiety, and 0.87 for the stress subscales [38],

Sleep quality assessment

At the start of the study, sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire [39]. The PSQI (Buysse et al.) is a trusted and valid tool for identifying sleep disorders in both clinical and non-clinical populations [38, 40]. The Persian version of the PSQI has been validated and shown to have high reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 for the global score in an Iranian sample [41, 42]. It assessed sleep quality over the previous month, covering various aspects of sleep through 19 items across seven subscales: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency (time taken to fall asleep), sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency (hours slept divided by hours in bed), sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction (difficulty staying awake). Each subscale received a score between 0 and 3, and these scores were combined to form a “global” score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. A global score of ≤ 5 was considered indicative of optimal sleep quality. Participants with poor sleep quality, identified by a global PSQI score > 5, were further categorized as having borderline [6–8] or poor (≥ 9) sleep quality [43]. Those with a PSQI score of ≥ 5 were classified as poor sleepers, while those scoring < 5 were considered good sleepers.

Mood assessment

To assess the severity of mood disorders, we used the Profile of Mood States (POMS) questionnaire. The reliability and validity of this tool had been previously confirmed in the Iranian population (Tirgari et al. (2012)) [44], with the Total Mood Disturbance score showing high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94). A widely used instrument in cognitive studies, the POMS long version consists of 65 items. Participants were asked to read each item and respond using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very high), based on how they felt while completing the questionnaire.

The POMS evaluates six mood domains: Depression-Dejection, Vigor-Activity, Tension-Anxiety, Anger-Hostility, Confusion-Bewilderment, and Fatigue-Inertia. The Total Mood Disturbance (TMD) score was calculated by adding the scores of the five negative mood domains and subtracting the vigor score. Higher scores indicated a greater severity of mood disturbance. The total mood scores ranged from − 28 to 134, with lower scores reflecting a more positive mood. Since there was no established cut-off for mood classification, the scores were divided into tertiles: scores above 40 indicated a poor mood (third tertile), scores between 9 and 39 suggested an average mood (second tertile), and scores below 9 indicated a highly positive mood (first tertile).

Assessment of other variables

Height and body weight of participants were measured. Body weight was recorded with participants barefoot and wearing lightweight clothing using a Seca model digital scale, accurate to 0.1 kg. Height was measured with individuals standing without shoes, ensuring their head, buttocks, and heels were aligned against the wall while looking straight ahead. A tape measure with an accuracy of 0.1 cm was used to obtain the height measurement. Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest with a non-stretchable tape measure, accurate to 0.1 cm. Hip circumference was measured at the widest part of the hips using a non-stretchable tape measure with the same precision.

Demographic and lifestyle information were gathered through structured questionnaires. Participants were interviewed about their gender, age, marital status (single or married), education level (below high school, high school, or higher), and income (≤ 20 million rials [≤ $476], 20–60 million rials [$476–$1,429], ≥ 60 million rials [≥ $1,429]). Educational attainment was assessed based on the number of years of schooling completed. Lifestyle data included smoking habits, with participants indicating whether they were smokers or non-smokers. During face-to-face interviews, trained staff used this structured questionnaire to also ask participants about their medication history. Specific questions were asked to identify the use of prescribed medications that would lead to exclusion as detailed above.

Statistical analysis

The general characteristics of participants were analyzed using ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Dietary intakes across tertiles of protein source, as determined by animal and plant protein, were examined using ANCOVA. The relationship between protein source and sleep, mood, depression, anxiety, and stress were assessed using conditional logistic regression in different models. These models were constructed as follows, with confounders selected based on previous research [45–47]. Crude model: No adjustments were made. Model 1: Adjusted for age (continuous) and sex (categorical: male/female). These fundamental demographic factors are known to influence both dietary intake and mental/sleep health outcomes in elderly populations. Model 2: Further adjusted for lifestyle, socioeconomic, and health-related factors including energy intake (continuous), smoking status (categorical: smoker/non-smoker), marital status (categorical: married/single/widowed/divorced), socioeconomic status (SES) (categorical: low/middle/high income), body mass index (BMI) (continuous), history of COVID-19 infection (categorical: yes/no), the presence of dyslipidemia (categorical: yes/no), hypertension (categorical: yes/no), and stable thyroid disease (categorical: yes/no). These additional covariates were included to account for their known or potential roles as confounders or effect modifiers in the relationship between dietary patterns and mental/sleep health in older adults. Interaction terms (protein × COVID‑19, protein × smoking, protein × gender) were explicitly included in the regression models. Full regression outputs, including β coefficients, standard errors (SE), odds ratios with 95% CIs, and P-interaction values, are provided in Supplementary File 1. Protein intake was categorized into tertiles to ensure adequate sample size and statistical power within each group, and to facilitate clearer interpretation for clinical and public health audiences. While tertiles may not fully capture potential non-linear associations, this approach provided a balanced distribution of participants across categories. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

Results

Of the 398 participants enrolled, complete data was available for 396 individuals. Missing values were due to incomplete dietary or questionnaire responses (< 1% per variable).

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics according to animal and plant protein intake. No significant differences were found in age, gender, marital status, smoking, BMI, or previous COVID-19 infection across the protein intake groups. However, significant differences were observed in socioeconomic status (SES), with participants in the lowest animal protein intake category being more likely to belong to a lower SES group (P < 0.001). Sleep quality varied significantly across the plant protein intake groups (P < 0.001), with individuals in the lowest plant protein intake category reporting poorer sleep. Additionally, lower animal protein intake was associated with higher prevalence of depression and stress, while those in the highest animal protein intake group had a greater prevalence of poor mood (P < 0.001). For plant protein intake, the prevalence of stress was higher among those with the lowest intake (P = 0.031), but no significant differences in mood were observed across the plant protein intake categories.

Table 2 represents dietary intakes based on the source of protein. Energy intake significantly increased across tertiles for both animal and plant protein groups (P < 0.001). Animal protein intake showed a significant increase in protein, fat, cholesterol, and saturated fatty acids (P < 0.001), while plant protein intake had notable increases in fat (P = 0.007), MUFA (P = 0.030), PUFA (P = 0.004), and vitamin C (P < 0.001). No significant differences in protein intake were found for plant protein (P = 0.446). Calcium intake increased significantly across tertiles of animal protein intake (P < 0.001), but not for plant protein. Vitamin B12 increased significantly with both animal (P < 0.001) and plant protein intake (P = 0.033). Iron, magnesium, folate, and vitamin C also showed significant increases across tertiles of plant protein intake. In contrast, omega‑3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) did not show significant trends in either group.

For animal protein, participants in the highest tertile had increased odds of poor sleep quality compared to the lowest, though this weakened after adjustment (fully adjusted OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.51–2.29, β = 0.075, SE = 0.39, P-value = 0.061, P-trend = 0.061). In contrast, higher animal protein intake was consistently associated with reduced odds of poor mood (OR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.10–0.57, β = 1.414, SE = 0.45, P-trend < 0.001) and depression (OR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.18–0.85, β = 0.949, SE = 0.41, P-trend = 0.023). Interaction analyses indicated that COVID‑19 history, smoking, and gender modified some associations. For plant protein, individuals with high intake and prior COVID‑19 infection had increased odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 0.63–2.80) but reduced odds of anxiety (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16–0.74). Smoking status also modified the association with sleep, with smokers showing lower odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.28–1.61). Gender interactions suggested that men with high plant protein intake had lower odds of depression (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.23–1.00), anxiety (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.30–1.20), and stress (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.17–0.76).

Smoking modified associations with depression (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.32–3.50) and anxiety (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.21–1.67). Gender interactions showed that men with high animal protein intake had lower odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.22–1.00) and trends toward reduced mood disturbance and depression.

Discussion

This study explored the complex associations between the quantity and source of protein intake and mental well-being and sleep quality in an elderly Iranian population. A novel aspect was the inclusion of interaction analyses with COVID-19 history, smoking, and gender. Our findings present a nuanced picture: while higher total protein intake was associated with better sleep quality and mental health, higher isolated intake of either plant or animal protein was linked to worsened sleep quality.

Regarding total protein intake and sleep quality, the marginally significant improvement observed with the highest total protein intake aligns with some previous research linking lower protein intake to poorer sleep quality [48, 49]. However, this contrasts with other large-scale studies and a meta-analysis that found no clear association, highlighting inconsistencies in the literature [50, 51]. A potential mechanism for a positive effect involves tryptophan, an essential amino acid and a precursor to the sleep-regulating hormone melatonin [52–54]. Adequate total protein intake likely ensures a balanced supply of essential amino acids, including sufficient tryptophan for serotonin and melatonin synthesis, which is crucial for regulating the sleep-wake cycle. For instance, a randomized trial by Zhou et al. (2016) found that higher-protein diets were associated with better sleep indexes, likely by ensuring tryptophan was not outcompeted by other large neutral amino acids (LNAAs) for transport across the blood-brain barrier [17]. While our findings indicate associations between protein intake and psychological outcomes, cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and it is possible that poor mood or sleep disturbances influence dietary patterns, such as reduced animal‑based food intake.

Protein source was operationalized in this study based on its source: distinguishing between plant-based and animal-based proteins [55]. This classification is grounded in established nutritional science, where animal proteins are typically considered “high quality” or “complete,” as they contain all essential amino acids in optimal proportions for human needs [56]. In contrast, many plant proteins lack one or more essential amino acids, though combinations of plant sources can provide a complete amino acid profile. However, using protein source as a proxy for quality is over-simplistic and a major limitation [57]. For instance, this approach does not account for metrics like digestibility or the specific balance of amino acids, such as the tryptophan-to-large-neutral-amino-acid (LNAA) ratio, which is critical for serotonin synthesis and may be more favorable in certain plant-based foods. Furthermore, metabolic effects, such as the higher production of urea from animal protein, are not captured by a simple source-based label.For example, plant proteins may still support neurotransmitter synthesis due to their higher tryptophan-to-large-neutral-amino-acid (LNAA) ratio, and their carbohydrate content can facilitate tryptophan transport across the blood-brain barrier [57]. These complexities suggest that “quality” extends beyond completeness or digestibility, encompassing context-dependent metabolic effects that influence sleep, mental health, or metabolic by-products like urea [53]. Our findings, showing worsened sleep quality with higher isolated plant or animal protein intakes despite their different amino acid profiles, underscore the need for a more nuanced understanding of protein source.

Conversely, a central finding was that higher individual intake of both plant and animal protein was associated with worsened sleep quality. This appears inconsistent with the observation that plant protein intake was linked with improved mental health outcomes in certain subgroups (e.g., smokers, men). This paradox may reflect the complex, context-dependent effects of diet. While plant proteins can provide a favorable tryptophan-to-LNAA ratio and carbohydrate content that support serotonin synthesis and mood regulation [53, 54], their high fiber and legume content may also cause gastrointestinal discomfort that disrupts sleep quality in older adults [58, 59].

Methodological factors such as residual confounding, reverse causation (e.g., individuals with poor sleep altering their diet), and the simplified use of protein source as a proxy for quality may contribute to these divergent findings. Another plausible explanation relates to protein metabolism and nocturia. Higher animal protein intake increases urea and uric acid production, which can promote diuresis and exacerbate nocturia, a common issue in older adults that fragments sleep [60, 61]. In contrast, when protein is consumed in balanced amounts from mixed sources (as reflected in total protein intake), the net effect may support sleep quality by ensuring adequate tryptophan availability without excessive nocturnal diuresis. This may reconcile why total protein appeared beneficial in Table 3, whereas isolated plant or animal protein showed adverse associations in Table 4.

The negative association with high animal protein intake is consistent with some studies like the recent prospective cohort analysis by Wirth et al. (2024) [23] but not others [62] Mechanistically, this effect may be explained by increased urea production, leading to greater diuresis that can exacerbate nocturia and cause sleep fragmentation in older adults [61, 63]. The negative association with plant protein contrasts with research suggesting a sleep-promoting effect [23, 52].This unexpected negative association for plant protein could be due to confounding dietary components; diets extremely high in plant protein are often rich in fiber and legumes, which may cause gastrointestinal discomfort and bloating, thereby disrupting sleep quality in this vulnerable population [58, 59]. These discrepancies highlight differences in study populations and methodologies. For example, a recent Iranian study in women found no significant associations between DII, MAR, DED, and mental health outcomes (DASS-21), which contrasts with our protein-focused findings and highlights how different dietary indices may yield divergent results [24]. This seemingly paradoxical result suggests that the benefit observed with high total protein intake may stem from a balanced dietary pattern, whereas a high intake of protein from a single source (either plant or animal) could be a marker for a less balanced diet, which itself may negatively impact sleep quality.

In contrast to the sleep outcomes, our results indicated a more consistently positive association between protein intake and mental health. Specifically, elderly individuals with the highest intake of animal and total protein were more likely to report better mood and lower levels of depression and stress. This aligns with previous cross-sectional studies linking higher protein intake, particularly from dairy and other animal sources, to reduced depressive symptoms [15, 22, 64–66], although not all studies have found this association [64, 67]. The proposed mechanism centers on the role of essential amino acids like tryptophan and tyrosine, abundant in animal proteins, as precursors to key mood-regulating neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine [68–71]. Furthermore, animal-based foods are dense sources of critical micronutrient cofactors for neurotransmitter synthesis, including vitamin B12 [72], vitamin B6 [73], zinc [74], and highly bioavailable heme iron [75], deficiencies of which are independently linked to depression.

A novel aspect of this study was the exploration of several interaction effects. These findings suggest that adequate protein intake may be associated with a form of “nutritional resilience “against physiological and psychological stressors. For instance, a history of COVID-19 infection interacted with high total protein intake to predict improved mental health, plausibly because sufficient amino acids are vital for immune recovery and for replenishing neurotransmitter pools that may be depleted by post-viral inflammation. Yet, it interacted with high plant protein intake to predict worsened sleep, highlighting that specific dietary components may be less tolerated during post-viral recovery periods. Furthermore, high plant protein intake was associated with better sleep and mental health among smokers, suggesting a potential mitigating effect. While direct literature on these specific interactions is scarce, it is plausible that adequate protein intake supports neurotransmitter synthesis [76], potentially counteracting stressors like post-viral symptoms or the physiological impacts of smoking [22, 77–79].

Gender-specific interactions also yielded noteworthy results. In our analysis, elderly men with high total protein intake showed different patterns across outcomes: while they experienced better sleep quality in the fully adjusted models, some subgroup analyses suggested less favorable associations. This underscores the importance of interpreting these findings cautiously and considering possible effect modification by gender, lifestyle, and health status. A high-protein diet increases urea production and diuresis (88), which can worsen nocturia, leading to sleep fragmentation [23, 63, 80]. Disrupted sleep quality is a known risk factor for poor mental health outcomes [81]. Remarkably, this association was reversed for high plant protein intake, which was linked to better outcomes in men. This may be because plant proteins typically produce less urea, potentially reducing the diuretic effect and subsequent sleep disruption [81].

This study has several strengths, including its comprehensive examination of protein intake in relation to mental well-being and sleep quality, and the novel consideration of interactions with COVID-19, smoking, and gender in an underrepresented elderly population. The use of validated questionnaires (168-item FFQ, DASS-21, PSQI, POMS) and face-to-face data collection are also notable strengths. Although tertile categorization allows robust group comparisons, it may obscure potential non-linear relationships between protein intake and outcomes. Future studies with larger sample sizes should consider continuous or finer categorizations (e.g., quintiles) to further explore these associations.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Due to the study’s cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as associations, not causal links, as the direction of the relationship cannot be determined. For instance, the link between higher plant protein and poor sleep quality could arise if individuals with existing sleep issues alter their diet in response. Likewise, depression can suppress appetite and reduce energy for meal preparation, which could explain why lower animal protein intake is associated with poorer mood. Therefore, the observed dietary patterns may be a consequence, not a cause, of the participants’ underlying health. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are essential to clarify the directionality of these associations. Additionally, the self-reported nature of the data means the results may be subject to recall bias. Although validated questionnaires were used, the absence of a formal pre-screening step may be a limitation. Residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle or socioeconomic factors remains possible.

Another key limitation is the simplified operationalization of protein based solely on source (animal vs. plant). This approach does not account for differences in amino acid profiles, digestibility, or other nutritional components such as fiber, anti-nutrients, and micronutrient density. As a result, there is potential for misclassification, particularly in composite or culturally specific dishes. Future studies should consider more nuanced metrics such as PDCAAS or DIAAS to better capture protein source and its physiological relevance. The lack of objective sleep measures (e.g., polysomnography) also limits precision, and findings may have limited generalizability.

The sample was restricted to Iranian elderly from one geographic region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Cultural dietary practices, such as reliance on plant-based staples and traditional mixed dishes, may shape the associations observed and differ from those in other countries or regions. These cultural dietary patterns could partly explain the observed associations and may not be directly generalizable to populations with different food environments. Finally, reverse causation cannot be ruled out, and the sample size calculation was based on an outcome from the parent study, which should be considered when interpreting the results. However, post hoc power analysis confirmed that the sample size achieved was sufficient to detect the smallest effect size observed in our study (OR = 0.24), thereby supporting the robustness of our findings. Although missing data was minimal, we addressed this by reporting missing values, conducting complete case analyses, and performing multiple imputation sensitivity analyses, which yielded consistent results.

Conclusion

This study among elderly adults found that higher total protein intake was associated with better sleep quality, while greater animal protein intake was linked to improved mood and lower odds of depression and stress. In contrast, higher intakes of plant or animal protein individually were associated with poorer sleep quality, and subgroup analyses revealed divergent effects by gender, smoking, and COVID‑19 history. For example, high plant protein intake was linked to worsened sleep among those with prior COVID‑19 infection but to improved outcomes among smokers, while gender-specific analyses suggested both beneficial and less favorable associations depending on context. Taken together, these findings indicate a mixed pattern of associations—favorable for mood but adverse or inconsistent for sleep—highlighting the complexity and sometimes contradictory effects of protein sources on health. Due to the cross‑sectional design, causality cannot be inferred, and our simplified classification of protein by source is a further limitation. Overall, these results challenge conventional notions of protein based solely on source and underscore the need for a nuanced, context‑dependent understanding. Future research should clarify underlying mechanisms and support the development of personalized dietary recommendations that consider health status, lifestyle, and gender to optimize mental well‑being and sleep quality in older adults.
