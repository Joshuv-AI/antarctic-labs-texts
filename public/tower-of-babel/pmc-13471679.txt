Immunoinformatics-guided design of a multi-epitope vaccine targeting WISP1 for gastric cancer
Authors: Negar Mottaghi-Dastjerdi, Behzad Shahbazi, Khadijeh Ahmadi, Mohammad Soltany-Rezaee-Rad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and effective preventive or therapeutic vaccines are still lacking. WNT1‑inducible signalling pathway protein 1 (WISP1/CCN4) is a secreted matricellular protein that is overexpressed in gastric tumours and associated with poor prognosis, making it a promising immunotherapy target. We aimed to design a multi‑epitope protein vaccine candidate targeting WISP1 using an immunoinformatics workflow.ResultsWe predicted linear B‑cell epitopes from WISP1 and filtered them for antigenicity, non-allergenicity, and non-toxicity. High‑scoring MHC class I and class II T‑cell epitopes with broad HLA coverage were then selected. The final construct combined validated B‑ and T‑cell epitopes with appropriate linkers and a TLR‑agonist adjuvant to enhance immunogenicity. Physicochemical profiling indicated that the construct is stable, soluble, hydrophilic and antigenic, with no predicted allergenicity or toxicity. Secondary and tertiary structures were modelled, refined and validated, revealing proper folding and favourable stereochemical quality. Molecular docking showed strong binding to innate immune receptors, particularly TLR4, and molecular dynamics simulations confirmed stable receptor–vaccine interactions with low structural deviation. Binding‑free energy analysis further supported these results. Immune simulations predicted robust primary and secondary immune responses characterised by sustained IgG/IgM production, increased IFN‑γ and IL‑2, and activation of memory B and T cells. Codon optimisation and in‑silico cloning suggested feasibility for experimental expression.ConclusionsThese results indicate that the proposed WISP1 multi‑epitope vaccine is antigenic, safe, structurally stable and capable of eliciting broad humoral and cellular immune responses in silico. This work provides a testable candidate for experimental validation and highlights secreted CCN family proteins as novel targets for gastric cancer vaccines.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01154-w.

Introduction

Gastric (stomach) cancer (GC) is among the deadliest cancers, ranking as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death and the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer [1]. Despite remarkable progress in chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and surgery, late-stage GC patient survival remains poor (5-year overall survival < 35%) [2]. As such, these problems underscore the need for new therapies that will support traditional modalities and yield sustainable clinical outcomes. Immunotherapy has been promising among the new alternatives and has the ability to achieve targeted and long-lasting anti-tumor activities by mobilizing the host immune machinery [3].

Within cancer immunotherapy, cancer vaccines have generated considerable enthusiasm as a technique of stimulating cellular and humoral immunity against tumor-associated antigens (TAAs). Different TAAs, including MUC1, HER2/neu, and human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT), were examined as vaccine targets in spite of being of an endogenous origin, due to their aberrantly increased expression and tumor-selective activity [4–6]. These examples show that an endogenous protein can still serve as an effective immunotherapeutic target, regardless of its origin, by revealing tumor-selective expression patterns.

WNT1-inducible signaling pathway protein 1, WISP1/CCN4, has recently been identified as an attractive candidate for GC immunotherapy. Although WISP1 is an endogenous matricellular protein involved in development and tissue repair, accumulating evidence shows that it is markedly overexpressed in GC samples relative to non-cancerous mucosa and shows a strong link to unfavorable outcomes, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), tumor microenvironment remodeling, and drug resistance. Significantly, because WISP1 is secreted and subject to immune surveillance, and its tumor-associated overexpression is distinguishable from baseline physiological expression, it has the profile of a TAA and a compelling rationale for incorporation into a vaccine strategy [7–10].

Therapeutic strategies against WISP1 to date have focused primarily on monoclonal antibodies that temporarily neutralize its signaling function. While antibody therapy is promising in itself, it only provides passive immunity and must be repeated frequently. In contrast, designing a multi-epitope protein vaccine offers potential for inducing a more long-lasting anti-tumor response by inducing long-term immunologic memory against tumor cells that abnormally express WISP1. Moreover, with the application of immunoinformatics-driven epitope prediction and cross-reactivity assessment using BLAST, the incorporation of epitopes with low homology to host proteins, is selectively permitted, thereby lowering the risk of autoimmunity [11, 12].

Although WISP1 is implicated in GC, no vaccine-design efforts have targeted WISP1. This represents a critical knowledge gap and an opportunity to attempt new, bioinformatics-aided vaccine construction against this TAA. Importantly, the convergence of three developments makes the present study timely and urgent: (i) the persistent global mortality burden of gastric cancer despite modern systemic therapies [1, 2]; (ii) emerging evidence that WISP1 is not merely a prognostic biomarker but a functional driver of tumor progression, immune remodeling, and therapy resistance in gastric and other solid tumors [7–10, 13]; and (iii) the maturation of immunoinformatics-guided vaccine design into a reproducible and experimentally actionable strategy. While WISP1-directed monoclonal antibodies provide proof-of-principle that this protein is druggable, no active immunization strategy has yet been explored to induce durable immune memory against WISP1-expressing tumor cells. To our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic attempt to translate WISP1 biology into a therapeutic cancer vaccine framework, addressing an unmet need in gastric cancer immunotherapy. Recent immunoinformatics studies have demonstrated that multi-epitope vaccine constructs designed entirely in silico can yield structurally stable and immunogenic candidates that are suitable for downstream experimental validation, in both infectious disease and cancer contexts [14–16]. Collectively, these advances underscore that contemporary immunoinformatics pipelines extend beyond theoretical prediction and can reliably guide rational, testable vaccine design.

Previous systems biology and immunoinformatics studies have demonstrated the utility of gene network modeling, epitope prioritization, and AI-assisted analysis in identifying therapeutic targets across multiple cancer types. Consistent with this framework, oncogenic pathways have been reported in hepatocellular carcinoma [17, 18], miRNA-mediated control of the Wnt/β-catenin axis has been characterized in relation to tumor growth [19, 20], and drug-resistance mechanisms in gastric and esophageal cancers have been examined through expression- and network-based analyses [21–27]. In parallel, the expanding role of artificial intelligence in pharmaceutical sciences and genome-level cancer research has been highlighted [28, 29]. Collectively, these studies provide the methodological foundation and scientific rationale for extending immunoinformatics-driven vaccine design to WISP1 (CCN4) in gastric cancer.

In this study, we employed an immunoinformatics pipeline for the development of a multi-epitope vaccine candidate targeting WISP1, aiming to stimulate robust lymphocyte-mediated responses. The objectives were: (i) to predict and prioritize immunogenic B-cell, MHC class I and class II epitopes from WISP1; (ii) to design a construct incorporating linkers and an adjuvant; (iii) to establish its physicochemical and structural and immunologic properties; and (iv) to evaluate binding to innate immune receptors using molecular docking and molecular dynamics. By leveraging computational tools, this work provides a rational foundation for vaccine development against WISP1 in GC. Accordingly, we adopt a chemistry-forward, hypothesis-driven framework that couples epitope and linker selection with physicochemical developability profiling and receptor-level biophysics (docking and molecular dynamics) to guide design decisions. An overview of the study workflow is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Because WISP1 is a self-derived, secreted protein, the principal safety concern is on-target/off-tumor reactivity. To mitigate this risk, we immunofocused the construct on B- and T-cell epitopes predicted to be tumor-accessible while excluding conserved/functional motifs and peptides with high human-proteome homology, allergenicity, or toxicity, and we prioritized broad HLA class I/II coverage to distribute responses across nonredundant regions. Definitive safety cannot be inferred from computation alone; standard preclinical practice recommends cross-reactivity screening on normal human tissues and dose-escalation toxicology in immunocompetent models before translation.

Methods

Protein sequence retrieval and localization

As the initial step of computational development of a GC vaccine directed at WISP1, the UniProt database (ID: O95388, CCN4_HUMAN) was used for fetching the protein sequence of WISP1. The presence of transmembrane helices was evaluated using TMHMM to confirm that WISP1 lacks membrane-spanning domains, consistent with its known secreted nature. For clarity, a high-level flowchart of the computational pipeline is presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

B-cell epitope prediction and selection

B-cell epitopes were predicted with ABCPred using the WISP1 sequence (UniProt O95388; CCN4_HUMAN) with threshold = 0.51, window = 16, and overlap enabled. Epitopes with probability ≥ 0.51 were retained. Post-filters: VaxiJen ≥ 0.50, Non-allergen (AllerTOP v2.0) [30], and Non-toxic (ToxinPred2). ABCPred was selected due to its validated performance in linear B-cell epitope prediction using recurrent neural networks [31].

Identification and selection of MHC-I and MHC-II epitopes

T-cell epitopes were predicted using the IEDB platform. For MHC-I, NetMHCpan 4.1 EL was applied to 9–10-mer peptides, while MHC-II epitopes were predicted using the IEDB-recommended NetMHCIIpan method on peptides. Selection criteria included a percentile rank ≤ 1.0 (strong binders), antigenicity (VaxiJen ≥ 0.50), non-allergenicity (AllerTOP v2.0), and non-toxicity (ToxinPred2). Peptides meeting all filters were prioritized using a composite ranking of normalized VaxiJen scores and inverse percentile rank, favoring broader HLA coverage and reduced redundancy by retaining longer overlapping sequences.

To further characterize the functional helper T-cell potential of shortlisted MHC-II epitopes, in silico cytokine-induction analyses were performed. IFN-γ–inducing propensity was evaluated using the IFNepitope server (hybrid/SVM model), while IL-4 induction was assessed using the IL4pred server. These analyses were used to annotate cytokine bias of candidate epitopes and were considered supportive descriptors rather than strict exclusion criteria during final construct assembly. The final set comprised ten MHC-I and five MHC-II epitopes retained for vaccine construction. Due to the context-dependent nature and limited predictive accuracy of current immunogenicity and cytokine-induction tools, these parameters were not used as strict exclusion criteria, but rather as complementary descriptors within a multi-parameter selection framework.

The crystallographic structures of MHC class I (HLA-A*02:03; PDB ID: 3OX8) and MHC class II (HLA-DRB1 × 04:01; PDB ID: 5JLZ) molecules were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. These receptors were selected as representative MHC class I and class II molecules with documented relevance in human populations and widespread use as structural templates in immunoinformatics docking studies. For docking analyses, one representative MHC class I and one MHC class II allele were selected to provide structural validation of peptide–MHC compatibility, as docking all epitope–allele combinations would be computationally redundant given the strong binding predictions already obtained from IEDB. The three-dimensional conformations of the selected T-cell epitopes were built and energy-minimized using UCSF Chimera version 1.10.2 prior to docking. Molecular docking simulations were conducted using PyRx, and binding free energies and dissociation constants were estimated using the PRODIGY web server to assess the interactions between the predicted epitopes and their corresponding MHC molecules.

In silico cross-reactivity and autoimmunity risk assessment

To evaluate potential cross-reactivity and the risk of autoimmune responses, all predicted B-cell, MHC class I, and MHC class II epitopes derived from WISP1 were subjected to sequence similarity analysis against the human proteome using BLASTp (NCBI). Searches were performed against the non-redundant (nr) protein database, with results restricted to Homo sapiens (taxid: 9606). Given the short length of epitope peptides, BLASTp parameters were automatically adjusted by the BLAST server to optimize sensitivity for short input sequences. For each epitope, the expected self-hit (WISP1/CCN4 isoforms) and the best non-WISP1 human off-target hit were identified. The following quantitative metrics were extracted and recorded for each hit: percent sequence identity (%ID), query coverage, E-value, and aligned length (amino acids). These parameters were used to systematically assess the degree of similarity between candidate epitopes and unrelated human proteins, thereby estimating the theoretical risk of cross-reactivity and autoimmunity. Detailed BLASTp outputs and extracted quantitative metrics for B-cell epitopes and T-cell epitopes are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Population coverage analysis

Population coverage of the selected MHC class I epitopes was evaluated using the IEDB Population Coverage tool. Each epitope was analyzed together with its restricting HLA allele, and coverage was calculated for the global population as well as gastric cancer–relevant regions, including East Asia and Northeast Asia. Coverage metrics included projected population coverage (%), average number of epitope–HLA combinations recognized per individual (average hit), and the minimum number of epitope hits recognized by 90% of the population (PC90).

Design of the vaccine framework

The selected B-cell, MHC-I, and MHC-II epitopes were assembled into a multi-epitope construct using assigned linkers (KK, GGGGS for B-cell; AAY for MHC-I; GPGPG for MHC-II; EAAAK for adjuvant). Thymosin α1 served as an adjuvant, and a C-terminal His-tag (HHHHHH) facilitated purification.

Rather than using the full-length WISP1 protein, which retains intrinsic biological activity and may increase the risk of on-target/off-tumor effects, an epitope-level strategy was adopted. Short B-cell and T-cell epitopes were selectively assembled after systematic filtering for antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and human proteome similarity, allowing immunogenic regions to be retained while minimizing potential safety concerns associated with whole-protein vaccination.

Assessment of antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity

The designed construct underwent evaluation of antigenicity through VaxiJen v2.0 (threshold 0.5), allergenic potential with AllerTOP v2.0, and toxicity prediction via ToxinPred2 based on sequence and physicochemical characteristics.

Physicochemical properties of candidate vaccine

The 274-amino-acid vaccine construct was analyzed by using ProtParam (ExPASy) to determine molecular weight, half-life in various hosts, isoelectric point, aliphatic and instability indices, and GRAVY score to obtain details on hydrophobicity and stability.

Secondary and tertiary structure prediction

Secondary structure was predicted with the GOR IV server, which uses sequence context to predict α-helices, β-strands, turns, and coils.

The tertiary structure was predicted with Robetta and AlphaFold, then refined with GalaxyRefine and analyzed against SAVES v6.0 (PROCHECK, ERRAT). Additional assessment was carried out using ProSA (Z-score) and MolProbity (score, clash score); subsequently, the highest quality model was chosen for subsequent analysis.

Docking with immune receptors

Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) were selected as docking targets based on their established roles in recognizing extracellular and surface-associated protein ligands and their relevance to protein- and peptide-based cancer vaccines [32]. Unlike intracellular TLRs (e.g., TLR3, TLR7/8, and TLR9), which primarily recognize nucleic acid motifs within endosomal compartments [33], TLR2 and TLR4 are expressed on the cell surface of antigen-presenting cells and are directly involved in sensing exogenous protein antigens [34]. Activation of TLR2 and TLR4 promotes dendritic cell maturation, upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, thereby enhancing antigen processing and presentation to both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells [35]. Given that the designed multi-epitope vaccine is a recombinant protein construct intended for extracellular uptake, TLR2 and TLR4 represent biologically relevant innate immune receptors for evaluating potential adjuvant-like interactions. The TLR2 (PDB: 5d3i) and TLR4 (PDB: 7mlm) crystal structures were extracted, processed in UCSF Chimera, and key active-site residues were identified (Pro352, Phe322, Ile319, Leu324, Phe325, Phe349, Tyr326, Val348, and Leu317 for TLR2 [36], and Gln339, Lys263, Ser413, Arg380, Ser386, Lys341, and Arg434 for TLR4 [37]). Interactions with the vaccine construct were analyzed using ClusPro2 [38], visualized with PDBsum, and binding affinities predicted by PRODIGY [39].

Simulation of Molecular dynamics (MD)

The vaccine’s MD simulations alone and in complex with TLR2/TLR4 were performed in GROMACS 2022 using the OPLS force field [40]. Systems were solvated (SPC water, 10 Å), neutralized with Na+/Cl−, equilibrated under NVT (300 K, 100 ps) and NPT (300 K, 1 bar, 100 ps), and run for 200 ns with 2 fs timesteps, a duration sufficient to assess complex stability and convergence. Electrostatics were handled with PME (0.16 nm grid, 10 Å cutoff) and van der Waals at 1 nm. The stability of the system was assessed using root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), define secondary structure of proteins (DSSP) analysis, hydrogen bond analysis (H-bond), and radius of gyration (Rg) [41].

Binding-free energy (BFE) calculation

The HawkDock web server calculated the vaccine/TLR2 and vaccine/TLR4 complex’s BFE. The MM-GBSA approach was utilized by this web server to calculate the interaction energy. Immune simulation

The vaccine’s immunogenicity was studied using the C-ImmSim server. Three shots were administered at the time steps of 1, 84, and 168 for 1000 steps with default configurations, random seed 12,345, and a volume of 10 μL.

Optimization of the Codon and Computational Cloning

The construct sequence was optimized for codons by JCat for E. coli K-12 expression with regard to GC content and CAI [43]. Then, it was cloned into pET28a in SnapGene and double digestion was validated in silico by agarose simulation.

Prediction of epitopes binding into MHC class I and II

After preliminary ranking using IEDB prediction scores, epitopes binding to MHC class I and II molecules with percentile ranks > 1.0 were excluded. Antigenicity of the remaining epitopes was evaluated using VaxiJen v2.0 (threshold 0.5). Scores were normalized and combined with IEDB values to prioritize candidates. The top-ranked epitopes were further screened for allergenicity (AllerTOP v2.0) and toxicity (ToxinPred2), and only non-allergenic and non-toxic sequences were retained. Cross-reactivity and autoimmunity risk evaluation of selected epitopes

To assess the potential for cross-reactivity with non-target human proteins, all selected B-cell, MHC-I, and MHC-II epitopes were analyzed by BLASTp against the human proteome. As expected, each epitope showed a perfect self-hit to WISP1/CCN4 isoforms (100% identity and full query coverage), confirming their correct derivation from the target antigen. Importantly, the best non-WISP1 human matches generally exhibited partial sequence similarity, characterized by short aligned regions, reduced query coverage, and relatively high E-values, consistent with weak and non-specific homology typical of short peptide searches. For B-cell epitopes, several peptides showed no significant non-WISP1 human hits, while others aligned only partially to unrelated proteins such as immunoglobulin junction regions or structural repeat proteins. A similar pattern was observed for MHC-I and MHC-II epitopes. Although some off-target matches displayed moderate percent identity over short stretches, these alignments involved limited aligned lengths and low statistical significance, suggesting a low likelihood of biologically relevant cross-reactivity. Notably, the single MHC-II epitope analyzed (NDIFADLESYPDF) showed a strong self-hit to WISP1 but only a partial off-target alignment to a zinc finger protein with limited coverage and a higher E-value. Collectively, these results indicate that the selected epitopes demonstrate high specificity for WISP1, with no strong or extensive homology to unrelated human proteins, supporting a low predicted risk of unintended autoimmune cross-reactivity at the sequence level. Detailed quantitative BLAST metrics for B-cell epitopes and for MHC-I/MHC-II epitopes are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Design and assessment of the construct

To construct a vaccine, six B lymphocyte, nine MHC-I, and one MHC-II epitopes were implemented. Their folding and function were preserved with linkers (KK and GGGGS for B-cell, AAY for MHC-I, GPGPG for MHC-II, and EAAAK for adjuvant integration). Thymosin α1 (Tα1) was chosen because it has established clinical safety and immunomodulatory activity. It is a licensed therapeutic agent for viral infections and for immunotherapy of cancers, in which it boosts both innate and adaptive immunity. Of particular relevance to a therapeutic tumor vaccine, Tα1 induces Th1-type immunity, stimulates dendritic cells and T cells, and enhances antigen presentation by upregulating MHC antigens. These activities would all be advantageous for a therapeutic tumor vaccine because they promote strong cytotoxic T-cell immunity to tumor antigens like WISP1 [44]. Also, a His-tag was introduced at the C-terminal aimed at facilitating purification and identification. Molecular dynamics analysis

MD simulations were performed on unbound TLR2 and TLR4 for 200 ns to stabilize their structures under physiological conditions. The vaccine construct was also simulated alone and in complex with both receptors. Mean RMSD values were 0.38 nm for TLR2, 0.28 nm for TLR4, 1.02 nm for the vaccine, 1.4 nm for the vaccine–TLR2 complex, and 0.74 nm for the vaccine–TLR4 complex. The vaccine–TLR4 complex showed the greatest stability (Fig. 6a). The structural rigidity (Rg) was also stable, and the means were 3.1, 3.08, 2.14, 3.55, and 3.24 nm for TLR2, TLR4, vaccine, vaccine–TLR2, and vaccine–TLR4, respectively (Fig. 6b). The RMSF analysis showed that there were very small changes in residue levels across all systems. TLR4 complexes were the most stable, whereas TLR2 complexes only showed changes of less than 1 nm (Fig. 6c). Hydrogen bond analysis demonstrated that the vaccine candidate-TLR2 complex formed a slightly higher average number of hydrogen bonds (12.1) compared to the vaccine candidate-TLR4 complex (11.37) over the 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation (Fig. 6d).

Immune simulation

C-ImmSim predicted robust humoral and cellular responses, with elevated primary and secondary responses and reduced antigen levels (Fig. 7a–i). The designed vaccine candidate exhibited high immunodominance, as reflected by robust primary responses and markedly enhanced secondary responses accompanied by efficient antigen clearance. B-cell and memory B-cell populations increased after each immunization and persisted throughout the 350-day simulation period, indicating the establishment of long-term immune memory (Fig. 7a).

Antibody kinetics demonstrated a typical primary–secondary response pattern, with early IgM production followed by a pronounced increase in IgG after booster doses, concurrent with a progressive reduction in antigen levels (Fig. 7b). Active and TH cells remained elevated after each dose (Fig. 7c–d), supporting sustained humoral and cellular immunity, while cytotoxic T cells showed a gradual and stable expansion over time (Fig. 7e–f).

In addition, innate immune components, including NK cells and macrophages, were consistently enhanced during the simulation (Fig. 7g–h). Cytokine profiling revealed increased levels of IFN-γ, along with regulatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β (Fig. 7i), suggesting a balanced immune response that supports both effective effector functions and immune regulation. Collectively, these results indicate that the vaccine construct is capable of inducing durable and balanced immune responses in silico.

Optimization of the Codon and computational cloning

Back-translation and codon optimization of the construct were performed using JCat. The optimized vaccine nucleotide sequence had a CAI of 1.0 and 55.72% GC content. After codon optimization, 834 nucleotides was the sequence length of the designed structure. The simulation of the optimized construct sequence in pET-28a (+) by SnapGene indicated the construct’s sequence is suitable for cloning into pET-28a (+) (Fig. 8a). The beginning and end of the sequence of the construct were digested with XbaI and XhoI enzymes, respectively, and the double digestion procedure by these enzymes showed the presence of the designed construct (834 bp) and the vector (pET-28a(+), 5231 bp) (Fig. 8b).

Discussion

This study presents a rational, immunoinformatics-based design of a multi-epitope vaccine directed against WISP1 (CCN4) for GC. Selecting a multi-epitope (MEV) construct rather than full-length WISP1 was driven by safety and immunofocusing considerations inherent to self, bioactive matricellular proteins. The MEV approach enabled exclusion of regions predicted to be allergenic, toxic, or highly homologous to human proteins, while avoiding intact functional domains (e.g., VWC/TSP1/CT) involved in stromal signaling. By combining B-cell and broadly reactive HLA-I/II epitopes, the construct targets protective determinants, augments population coverage, and supports coordinated CD4+/CD8+ responses. The modular format also permits explicit adjuvant/linker engineering (TLR4-oriented) and reproducible manufacturing. Together, these features provide a stronger risk–benefit profile than presenting the native WISP1 protein. Notably, similar immunoinformatics pipelines integrating epitope prioritization, multi-epitope construct engineering, molecular docking, and immune simulation have been successfully applied to cancer antigens such as CD171 in glioblastoma, providing independent support for the translational relevance and methodological validity of this strategy in oncologic vaccine design [14].

In line with our objective, we first verified that WISP1 is a secreted, extracellular protein, thereby accessible to humoral and cellular immune recognition. Building on this, we prioritized B lymphocyte and T lymphocyte epitopes with favorable antigenicity, non-allergenicity, as well as non-toxicity, and assembled them into a construct with well-validated linkers and an immunostimulatory adjuvant (thymosin-α1). Molecular docking was performed to investigate whether selected T-cell epitopes could establish favorable interactions with HLA alleles that are highly prevalent and clinically relevant in gastric cancer. Docking results showed that selected MHC class I and class II epitopes exhibited stable and favorable binding affinities for HLA-A02:03 and HLA-DRB1 × 04:01, respectively, indicating efficient peptide-MHC complex formation. Several epitopes exhibited binding energies in the ranges typically associated with efficient antigen presentation, indicating their potential to elicit cytotoxic and helper T-cell responses.

Secondary-structure prediction indicated a substantial proportion of flexible coils/turns alongside ordered helices/strands, which is relevant because surface-exposed, loop-rich regions are commonly associated with linear B-cell epitope accessibility and antibody recognition [46]. Tertiary-structure modeling and refinement yielded a stereochemically plausible fold supported by standard quality metrics (Ramachandran statistics, ERRAT and ProSA), suggesting that the designed epitopes can be displayed in a structurally stable framework rather than arising from an implausible or highly strained model [47]. While such structural validation cannot by itself prove immunogenicity, it strengthens confidence in candidate prioritization by supporting proper epitope presentation, reducing aggregation/misfolding risk, and providing a reliable template for downstream receptor docking and dynamics analyses.

Although IFN-γ induction is a desirable feature for Th1-skewed antitumor immunity, cytokine prediction tools are inherently context-dependent and do not fully capture the integrated effects of adjuvants, epitope hierarchy, and CD8+ T-cell dominance. In the present design, MHC-II epitope selection was primarily guided by binding affinity, antigenicity, and safety, while cytokine-induction profiles were used as supportive annotations. The inclusion of thymosin-α1, multiple high-affinity CD8+ T-cell epitopes, and stable TLR4 engagement is expected to collectively favor cellular immune polarization despite variability in single-epitope cytokine predictions.

Functionally, the construct docked favorably to human TLR2 and, more strongly, to TLR4, and remained stable during 200-ns MD simulations. MM/GBSA estimates further indicated a lower binding free energy (more favorable) for the TLR4 complex than for TLR2, consistent with the MD readouts. Finally, in silico immune simulations suggested robust primary and secondary responses with durable B-cell and T-cell memory and induction of Th1-skewing cytokines. Altogether, these results provide a computational foundation for developing a WISP1-directed therapeutic vaccine candidate for GC.

While immunoinformatics tools have become integral to rational vaccine design, their predictions remain probabilistic and must be interpreted within defined accuracy limits. Notably, immunogenicity prediction tools, including IEDB-based predictors, remain inherently limited and context-dependent, and therefore are typically used as supportive rather than definitive criteria in multi-epitope vaccine design. For MHC class I and II binding, NetMHCpan-based approaches have demonstrated high predictive performance through integration of peptide–MHC binding affinity and eluted ligand datasets, and continue to improve with deep learning–based generalization strategies [48, 49]. Antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity predictors such as VaxiJen, AllerTOP, and ToxinPred2 are widely applied as conservative screening tools in multi-epitope vaccine pipelines, but do not substitute for experimental immunogenicity or safety testing [12, 30, 31, 46, 47]. Structural modeling, docking, and MM/GBSA analyses provide physically grounded insights into stability and receptor engagement, yet represent simplified approximations of dynamic immune environments [38, 39, 42]. Likewise, immune simulation platforms capture qualitative trends in immune activation and memory formation but cannot fully reproduce the complexity of in vivo cellular immunity [50–52]. Accordingly, the present predictions are intended to prioritize and de-risk candidate selection rather than to serve as definitive measures of vaccine efficacy, underscoring the need for subsequent experimental validation.

Recent literature supports several pillars of this design. First, CCN4/WISP1 has emerged as a tumor-promoting matricellular protein that shapes the tumor microenvironment, EMT, and immune contexture across cancers; these properties have renewed interest in CCN4 as an immune-visible target when aberrantly overexpressed in tumors [9]. (For broader CCN4 biology and immune–stroma interactions, see contemporary reviews synthesizing evidence across solid tumors [13]. Second, the decision to engineer a construct capable of engaging TLR4 is biologically coherent: TLR agonists are among the most validated vaccine adjuvant classes for eliciting durable Th1-biased responses and potent antigen presentation, with TLR4 agonism (e.g., MPLA) translated to licensed human vaccines and actively optimized in current adjuvant platforms [53–55]. TLR2 and TLR4 were specifically selected for docking analysis because they are plasma membrane–localized receptors that recognize extracellular protein and peptide ligands, making them particularly relevant for recombinant protein and multi-epitope vaccine constructs. In contrast, intracellular or endosomal TLRs (such as TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9) primarily sense nucleic acids and are therefore less applicable to peptide-based vaccines lacking nucleic acid components. Activation of TLR2 and TLR4 has been shown to promote dendritic cell maturation, pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and Th1-skewed immune polarization, all of which are essential for effective anti-tumor immunity. Accordingly, docking with TLR2 and TLR4 provides mechanistic insight into the innate immune activation potential of the proposed WISP1-based multi-epitope vaccine. Third, our choice of Tα1 as the intrinsic adjuvant is supported by recent clinical-immunologic evidence showing that Tα1 augments dendritic-cell activation, enhances antigen presentation, and reinforces T-cell effector and memory responses, mechanisms directly aligned with therapeutic cancer vaccination [56, 57]. Finally, multi-epitope vaccine pipelines that combine epitope filtering for antigenicity, allergenicity/toxicity screening, structural validation, and docking/MD against innate sensors have matured, with contemporary exemplars indicating that such in silico designs can yield constructs that are stable, immunogenic, and ready for experimental testing [50, 51].

Given that effective cancer vaccination relies predominantly on cellular immunity [52], the predicted T-cell responses are particularly relevant. The immune simulations indicated sustained activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Tc cells), which are central mediators of direct tumor cell killing through perforin–granzyme and Fas–FasL pathways [58]. In parallel, the expansion and persistence of helper T cells (Th cells) supports effective priming, cytokine production, and long-term immune memory [59]. The observed Th-associated cytokine profile, including IFN-γ, is consistent with a Th1-skewed response, which is widely recognized as critical for antitumor efficacy. Together, these findings suggest that the designed vaccine construct is capable of eliciting coordinated CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses, a prerequisite for durable immune control of solid tumors such as gastric cancer.

Beyond gastric cancer, a growing body of immunoinformatics work has established multi‑epitope vaccines as a versatile platform across diverse pathogens and disease areas. Roy et al. integrated pan‑genome analysis with reverse vaccinology and epitope‑based design to construct a multi‑epitope vaccine against Herpes simplex virus type 1, illustrating how systematic target selection, rigorous antigenicity/toxicity filtering, and epitope clustering can converge on a compact, highly immunogenic construct [60]. Similarly, a recent human cytomegalovirus study combined pan‑genome screening with reverse vaccinology to prioritize conserved envelope and tegument proteins, followed by multi‑epitope assembly, structural modeling, docking, and immune simulations, closely mirroring the hierarchical workflow used here [61]. In another line of work, artificial intelligence‑driven reverse vaccinology and immunoinformatics were applied to prioritize epitope‑based vaccine candidates against Neisseria gonorrhoeae, further supporting the robustness and transferability of multi‑epitope design pipelines across distinct pathogens and disease settings [62]. Compared with these infection‑focused vaccines, the present WISP1‑based construct uniquely targets a secreted matricellular protein that is overexpressed in gastric tumours and accessible within the tumour microenvironment, while still leveraging analogous epitope prediction, construct engineering, receptor‑level biophysics, and in silico immune‑profiling frameworks to rationally guide vaccine design in an oncologic context.

Biologically and clinically, a WISP1-targeted immunotherapy is attractive for several reasons. As a secreted TAA enriched in GC and implicated in tumor–stroma remodeling [7, 63], WISP1 offers (i) immune accessibility without the constraints of intracellular antigen processing for B-cell recognition; (ii) the potential to disrupt protumorigenic signaling axes and EMT indirectly via immune pressure; and (iii) a route to broaden patient coverage by combining multiple B lymphocyte and T lymphocyte epitopes across MHC classes I and II alleles. The observed preference for TLR4 engagement is also practical: TLR4-primed antigen presentation favors the cross-priming of CD8+ T lymphocyte and the production of durable memory, both central to sustained control of solid tumors [64]. Moreover, the predicted Th1-skewed cytokine profile in our immune simulations (e.g., IFN-γ elevation) is congruent with antitumor efficacy and with the known immunobiology of Tα1 adjuvanticity [44, 65].

As a self-derived tumor-associated antigen (TAA), WISP1 raises safety considerations. The principal risk is on-target/off-tumor reactivity. Risk was mitigated in silico by excluding peptides predicted to be allergenic or toxic, prioritizing tumor-accessible extracellular regions, and screening out sequences with high identity to other human proteins. Definitive safety cannot be inferred from computation alone; standard preclinical practice recommends in vitro cross-reactivity testing (primary human cells and normal-tissue lysates), cytokine-release/autoantibody profiling, and dose-escalation toxicology with organ histopathology in immunocompetent models to confirm specificity.

This work has several strengths. Methodologically, we used conservative, consensus-style epitope triaging (antigenicity, non-allergenicity, non-toxicity), applied state-of-the-art tertiary modeling and multi-metric quality control, and tested receptor engagement and complex stability with docking, MD, and MM/GBSA. Conceptually, we focused on a secreted, immuno-visible, and functionally relevant TAA; operationally, we embedded an adjuvant (Tα1) with contemporary evidence of clinical safety and mechanistic suitability for cancer immunotherapy.

Because WISP1 is an endogenous matricellular protein with low-level expression in certain normal tissues, mitigation of potential autoimmunity was a central consideration during epitope selection. To address this, all candidate B-cell and T-cell epitopes were subjected to a multi-layered in silico safety filtering strategy. First, epitopes were screened for antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity, retaining only peptides predicted to be antigenic yet non-allergenic and non-toxic. Second, BLASTp analysis against the human proteome was performed for each final epitope to assess cross-reactivity. Epitopes exhibiting full-length identity were confined to WISP1 isoforms, while off-target human matches showed partial alignments with limited identity and short alignment lengths, reducing the likelihood of cross-reactive immune recognition. Third, conserved or functionally critical motifs were avoided, and overlapping peptides were consolidated to minimize redundancy. Collectively, these steps were designed to enrich for tumor-associated epitopes preferentially accessible in WISP1-overexpressing gastric cancer cells while minimizing recognition of normal tissues. In summary, our data nominate WISP1 as a tractable, immune-visible TAA in GC and provide a well-vetted, multi-epitope vaccine candidate with predicted TLR4-favored engagement, structural stability, and durable immunogenicity. By integrating rigorous epitope selection with structural and receptor-level validation, and by selecting an evidence-based adjuvant, the present work lays a credible foundation for preclinical testing and, ultimately, for evaluating WISP1-targeted vaccination as a novel therapeutic avenue in gastric cancer.

Limitations and future directions

Limitations of the present study should be acknowledged: All findings are in silico and require empirical validation. Accordingly, definitive assessment of autoimmunity risk requires experimental validation, including normal tissue cross-reactivity assays, cytokine-release profiling, and dose-escalation toxicology studies in appropriate preclinical models. Epitope immunogenicity can differ in primary human APCs and across HLA haplotypes; proteasomal processing, epitope competition, and epitope dominance were not experimentally assessed. While docking and MD suggest preferential TLR4 engagement, receptor binding in cells is influenced by membrane context and co-receptors (e.g., CD14/MD-2), which were not modeled here. Furthermore, although C-ImmSim offers useful first-pass predictions, it cannot substitute for in vivo immunogenicity, safety, and antitumor efficacy testing. Finally, WISP1 expression heterogeneity across GC subtypes and stages will need careful patient-selection strategies in eventual trials. In addition, dedicated immunogenicity prediction tools were not used as strict filtering criteria, as their predictive performance remains variable and context-dependent; this represents a potential area for further refinement in future studies.

Future studies should therefore (i) validate epitope presentation by mass spectrometry and measure T- and B-cell responses in human PBMCs and HLA-diverse murine models; (ii) compare Tα1 with, or combine it alongside, TLR4/TLR3 agonists (e.g., MPLA, poly I:C) to optimize magnitude and quality of responses; (iii) confirm receptor engagement using cell-based TLR reporter assays and assess downstream NF-κB/IRF signaling; (iv) perform tumor-bearing mouse studies to quantify antitumor activity, memory, and safety; and (v) explore rational combinations with checkpoint inhibitors or chemotherapy, in keeping with contemporary cancer-vaccine strategies. Parallel translational work should map WISP1 expression and secretion in GC cohorts and investigate whether serum WISP1 could serve as a pharmacodynamic or stratification biomarker.

Conclusion

This work describes the first bioinformatics-based design of a WISP1-targeting vaccine with multiple epitopes against GC. By combining careful epitope selection with immunogenicity and safety filtering, structure-based validation, and receptor-level docking and dynamics, we derived a construct that should be stable, immunogenic, and have the ability to engage TLR4 to facilitate sustained antitumor responses. These observations align with the study aim of nominating WISP1 as a rational, immune-accessible target for vaccination and extend therapeutic approaches to extracellular matricellular proteins, an otherwise relatively unstudied class of target molecules in cancer therapy. Although the interpretations reflect computational predictions and can’t reflect the full richness of the tumor microenvironment complexity and inter-patient variability, they constitute a plausible basis warranting experimental assessment. Here, follow-up studies should emphasize in vitro demonstration of dendritic cell activation and induction of cytokine production, complemented by in vivo evaluation of antitumor activity and safety of the lead construct of interest in appropriate GC model systems. Overall, the present work places WISP1-directed vaccination on the table as a promising option for expanding immunotherapeutic intervention strategies in GC.
