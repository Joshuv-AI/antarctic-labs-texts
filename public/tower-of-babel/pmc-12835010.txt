Electro-organic polymerization of aniline derivative on carbon-based electrode and its pH sensing in food samples
Authors: Nandimalla Vishnu, Bhaskar Yamajala, Prasantha R Mudimela, Subramanian Nellaiappan, Manickam Selvaraj, Arun Karnwal, Deepak Kumar, Natalia Nesterova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoly(1-naphthylamine) (pNPA), similar to polyaniline, is a significant conducting polymer frequently synthesized through electrochemical methods in acidic or non-aqueous environments. Unlike conventional preparation, this work demonstrates the fabrication of pNPA via a neutral pH-assisted open-circuit potential technique on a multiwalled carbon nanotube modified glassy carbon electrode (GCE/CNT@pNPA). Electrochemical cycling of 1-naphthylamine (1-NPA) adsorbed GCE/CNT in pH 7 phosphate buffer solution (PBS) led to the formation of a redox active polymeric structure. Physicochemical characterization of CNT@pNPA by microscopic (SEM) and spectroscopic (Raman and FTIR) methods confirmed the electro-organic synthesis of pNPA on CNT. Notably, GCE/CNT@pNPA showed good Nernstian behavior with negligible electrocatalytic activity to various electroactive biochemicals. The stability of peak current and potential further highlighted the selective electrochemical pH sensing capability of GCE/CNT@pNPA. Under optimal differential pulse voltammetry conditions, the GCE/CNT@pNPA was extended for analysis of pH values in various food samples. In near future, GCE/CNT@pNPA can be further miniaturized and could be extended for online monitoring of pH in various samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33340-9.Keywords: Aniline, Carbon nanotube, Electrochemical polymerization, Food, PH, SensorSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

1-Naphthylamine (1-NPA), a naphthalene-derived aromatic amine with a primary amino group, is a priority pollutant classified as a Group III carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and enters aquatic ecosystems via industrial discharge from dyestuff, photographic, pharmaceutical, and agrochemical sectors1,2 Usually, adsorption technology is used to remove α-NPA from the contaminated water. Meanwhile several researchers found that carbon nanomaterials with large surface area and high reactivity are effective adsorbents for removal of toxic chemicals3. In our previous report, we observed surface of carbon nanotube (CNT) is a key factor for the unique and novel preparation of polyaniline (pANI) film in neutral pH4. Usually polyaniline (pANI) is a well-studied pH-responsive polymer film, recognized for its oxidation mechanisms and chemical behavior, while naphthylamine, with its two aromatic rings, exhibits stronger bonding and enhanced pH-responsive properties in its polymeric form4.

In addition, CNT is considered as a potential carbon material for its large surface area, high conductivity, rigid and ordered structural arrangements. Thus, it has been used in preparation of chemically modified electrodes and extended for various electrochemical applications5–7. Likewise, in this work, we used the superior activity of polynaphthylamine (pNPA) on multiwalled CNT (CNT) to develop an electrochemical pH sensor.

Solution pH measurement is crucial for clinical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, industrial processes, and diverse scientific and technological applications. However, based on the application and precision, various pH sensors are developed8. Mostly the developed sensors range from low precision pH strips to very precise pH meters based on glass electrodes. At present, glass electrodes are commonly used for their high sensitivity and selectivity, fast response and long lifetime, if they carefully handled. However, these electrodes are fragile, limiting their practicality for field analysis. Furthermore, glass electrodes are susceptible to alkali errors and membrane dehydration, which contribute to instability and reduced sensitivity to hydrogen activity. This method also necessitates frequent and regular recalibration before use8. Among various types, electrochemical techniques are more reliable and widely used for their selectivity, sensitivity, accuracy, low-cost, miniaturization, high dynamic range and simplicity9–15. For instance, voltammetric pH sensors are based upon the shift of pH sensitive peak potential of the redox species4,8,16,17. Herein, we report a voltammetric pH sensor developed using 1-naphthylamine (1-NPA) and CNT-modified electrode. In near future, CNT@pNPA can be further miniaturized18 and could be extended as a flexible sensor19 or low-cost electrode20 for monitoring of pH.

Furthermore, the World Health Organization highlights the significance of pH monitoring in food safety, quality control, and regulatory compliance. The spoilage process in food is largely due to microbial and yeast growth, leading to the catabolism of original chemical compounds such as glucose, lactic acid, and specific amino acids. These metabolic activities alter the pH level, which serves as a key indicator of food quality21. Therefore, continuous pH monitoring plays a crucial role in assessing food safety and maintaining quality standards.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Multiwalled carbon nanotube (CNT) with a carbon-based purity of approximately 90 wt%, an outer diameter of 10–15 nm, an inner diameter of 2–6 nm, and a length ranging from 0.1 to 10 μm were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. 1-NPA was obtained from CDH Fine Chemicals. The chemicals utilized in the work were of AR grade and were used without additional purification. Unless otherwise stated, a 0.1 M pH 7 phosphate buffer solution (PBS) at pH 7 served as the supporting electrolyte for electrochemical experiments.

Instrumentation

CHI760E workstation with three-electrode cell is employed to perform electrochemical experiments. The three-electrode cell is a 10 mL electrochemical cell with a modified GCE or GCE (0.0707 cm2 geometrical surface area), Ag/AgCl and Pt electrode as working, reference and counter electrodes, respectively. The morphological characteristics of CNT and CNT@PNPA were examined using FEI Quanta FEG 200-HR SEM. Shimadzu IR Affinity-1 spectrometer with the KBr pellet method is used for FTIR spectroscopy studies. Raman spectroscopic analysis was conducted with an Agiltron Peakseeker Pro Raman Spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm laser probe. For FTIR analysis, the CNT@pNPA was carefully scratched from the GCE surface and analyzed.

Electrode Preparation procedure

Before fabrication, the glassy carbon electrode (GCE) underwent both mechanical and electrochemical cleaning. Mechanically, the surface was polished using 1-micron Al₂O₃ powder with a Bio Analytical Systems polishing kit and the electrode was sequentially rinsed with acetone and deionized water. Electrochemical cleaning was performed via cyclic voltammetry for ten cycles (n = 10) within a potential range of −0.2 to 1 V in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7) at a scan rate of 50 mV s⁻¹. Subsequently, a 5 µL of CNT dispersion (2 mg CNT in 500 µL ethanol) was carefully deposited onto the surface of the glassy carbon electrode (GCE) using the drop-casting technique. Following drop casting, the electrode was left undisturbed under ambient conditions to facilitate solvent evaporation, ensuring uniform film formation and adhesion.

The GCE/CNT@pNPA electrode was prepared by two steps. In step I: dipping the GCE/CNT electrode in a 1 mM 1-NPA solution under open-circuit potential-time (OCPT) conditions for 60 s i.e., simple dipping of GCE/CNT in 1 mM 1-NPA stock solution for 60 s. Step II: electrochemical cycling of 1-NPA adsorbed GCE/CNT electrode in pH 7.4 Phosphate Buffer Solution (PBS). The optimal conditions for step II are - potential range: − 0.5 V to + 0.5 V, scan rate: 50 mV/s, 10 cycles. The resulting stable electrode was labelled as GCE/CNT@pNPA. (Note: The current research work is different from our previous reported work4. In the previous reported work, preparation of CNT@pNPA electrode is achieved by continuous potential cycling of Au/CNT in a freshly prepared 1 mM NPA solution). For pH measurements, differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) was performed under optimized parameters: initial potential of −0.85 V, final potential of + 0.85 V, increment potential of 0.004 V, pulse amplitude of 0.05 V, pulse width of 0.06 s, sample width of 0.02 s, quiet time of 2 s, pulse period of 0.5 s, and sensitivity of 0.001 A/V.

Real sample analyses: Six real world samples (#1 white vinegar, #2 lemon juice, #3 tamarind juice, #4 baking soda, #5 butter milk and #6 buffalo milk) were studied for pH analyses. The same real samples were analyzed using the conventional pH meter. Note: No additional supporting electrolyte was used during the analysis.

Results and discussions

Electrochemical polymerization of 1-NPA to pNPA on CNT modified GCE

Unlike to our previous report4, GCE/CNT dipped in 1-NPA for 60 s yielded a CNT@pNPA in pH 7 PBS. In buffer solution, 1-NPA adsorbed GCE/CNT displayed a huge irreversible peak at about ~ 0.35 V in the first segment and subsequent appearance of a redox peak (A1/C1) at an equilibrium potential, E1/2 ((Ep.a. + Epc)/2) = 85 ± 2 mV (Fig. 1A, curve a). The redox peak E1/2 is noticed with a peak-to-peak separation, ΔEp= Ep.a.-Epc, where Ep.a. and Epc represent anodic and cathodic peak potentials, measured at 170 ± 2 mV. Likewise, the electrochemical experiment with 1-NPA dipped GCE failed to display the redox peaks of pNPA in pH 7 PBS. This result shows that the monomer 1-NPA exhibits no electrochemical activity under neutral pH conditions (Fig. 1A, curve b). Furthermore, ten consecutive CV cycles performed on the GCE/CNT@pNPA exhibited a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 3.6%, confirming its stability. In addition, the surface area (Γ) of the GCE/CNT@pNPA was calculated using the following formula: Γ = Q/nAF, where Q is the charge consumed, obtained from integrating the anodic peak area of Fig. 1A, n, the number of electrons consumed, and A the glassy carbon electrode geometric area (0.0707 cm2). Γ was 7.93 × 10− 9 mol cm− 2.

(A) Twenty continuous CV responses of 1-NPA adsorbed (t = 60 s) GCE/CNT and GCE as GCE/CNT@pNPA (curve a) and GCE@pNPA (curve b) in pH 7 PBS at scan rate = 50 mV/s. (B) Effect of OCPT time vs. anodic peak current response of GCE/CNT@pNPA. (C) and (D) are the DPV responses of CNT@pNPA electrode in pH 7 PBS at two different potential windows.

In order to optimize the adsorption/dipping time in open circuit potential-time (OCPT), several control measurements were performed varying the time (Fig. 1B). The OCPT time was optimized with respect to peak current and stable redox peak response as 60 s. The electron-transfer characteristics of GCE/CNT@pNPA were investigated by examining the influence of CV scan rate on the redox peak. Figure S1 shows a typical CV response of the GCE/CNT@pNPA at different scan rate (10, 30, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 mV s− 1). The ratio of cathodic-to-anodic peak currents (ipc/ip.a.) at various scan rates was almost unity with and regression coefficient (R2) = 0.995. The slope value of log ip vs. log v was 0.82, which is in-between the ideal values of 1 and 0.5 respectively for adsorption and diffusion-controlled systems, indicating operation of mixed adsorption and diffusion-controlled electron-transfer processes at redox peak indicating a surface-confined electron-transfer behavior of the modified electrode. Figure 1C, D depict the DPV responses of GCE/CNT@pNPA in pH 7 PBS. The electrochemical polymerization of 1-NPA to pNPA on CNT surface follows as our previous report4. In brief, the anodic peak at 0.35 V could be due to the initiation of electro-organic synthesis of 1-NPA to pNPA, which is subsequently involved in polymerization as 1-NPA on CNT surface as pNPA.

Physicochemical characterization of CNT@pNPA

To trace the polymer morphology and structure of pNPA on CNT various characterization studies were performed. Similar to our previous reports4,16, the SEM analysis of CNT (Figure S2) and CNT@pNPA (Fig. 2A) revealed an agglomerated granular morphology distributed across the CNT surface, suggesting the presence of discontinuous polymeric structures. This morphological feature indicates the non-uniform deposition of pNPA onto the nanotube framework, potentially influencing the material’s interfacial properties and electrochemical performance.

(A) SEM image of CNT@pNPA, Raman (B) and FTIR (C) spectra of CNT (plot a) and CNT@pNPA (plot b).

The Raman spectra of CNT and CNT@pNPA revealed a significant variation in the intensity ratio of the D band (ID) to the G band (IG). The G band corresponds to the characteristic sp2 carbon structure of graphite, while the D band is related to structural disorder in the sp2 structure. The ID/IG ratio decreased from 0.535 to 0.456, indicating a change in the degree of disorder within the material as shown in Fig. 2B. The observed decrease in value could be attributed to the interaction between the conjugated π-electron system of pNPA and CNTs. This interaction likely arises from π-π stacking, which facilitates electronic coupling between the delocalized electrons of pNPA and the graphitic structure of MWCNTs. Such interactions can influence charge transfer dynamics, leading to modifications in the electronic properties of the system and ultimately contributing to the observed reduction in value. FTIR spectrum of CNT@pNPA displayed appreciable vibration peaks corresponding to the quinonoid and benzenoid like structure similar to our previous observation on CNT@pNPA (Fig. 2C). In addition, FTIR signals at ~ 700–750 cm− 1 corresponding to the 1,4-coupling and 1,5-coupling of 1-NPA units of the pNPA were identified.

Based on the experimental results, it is hypothesized that the para position of 1-NPA plays a crucial role in the polymerization and follows ECE mechanism (Figure. S3). This hypothesis is confirmed by repeating the electrochemical polymerization experiments conducted with 2-NPA, where the para position is hindered by fused aromatic rings, leading to a distinct polymerization behavior.

Electroanalytical application: CNT@pNPA as a voltammetric pH sensor

Initially, electrocatalytic activity of CNT@pNPA was tested with uric acid (UA), dopamine (DA), nitrite (NO2−), cysteine (CySH), glucose (Glu), ascorbic acid (AA), NADH and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Unexpectedly, the electrode found to be electro-inactive to the above-mentioned chemicals, wherein, there is no change in the peak potential and peak current of redox peak in presence and absence were observed (Fig. 3A).

(A) Plot of ip.a. vs. various analytes (uric acid (UA), dopamine (DA), nitrite (NO2−), cysteine (CySH), glucose (Glu), ascorbic acid (AA), NADH and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)) in pH 7 PBS at v = 50 mV s− 1. (B) Effect of solution pH (2–11) on CV response of GCE/CNT@pNPA at v = 50 mV s− 1 and (C) its Ep vs. pH plots.

Presumably structure and orientation of pNPA formed on CNT are not favorable to mediate the electrochemical reactions. Conversely, the lack of electrocatalytic activity is a clear advantage for using it to other applications like pH sensing. Figure 3B shows the CV response of CNT@pNPA in solutions with varying pH. A regular shift in the redox peak potential was observed as a function of pH. The anodic peak potential (Ep.a.) plotted against pH exhibited a linear relationship with a slope of −58 ± 2 mV pH⁻¹, indicating the electrode’s strong Nernstian behavior and excellent pH sensitivity (Fig. 3C).

On the other hand, DPV technique provides highly sensitive analyte detection within a single voltammetric scan while maintaining experimental simplicity. The DPV parameters are systematically optimized, with the potential range adjusted between − 0.85 V and 0.85 V. Under optimal DPV conditions, the CNT@pNPA displayed a peak at −0.078 V (Fig. 1C, D). Further, CNT@pNPA shifted its peak position at 55 ± 2 mV pH− 1 (n = 3; inset of Fig. 4) in different pH solutions ranging from 2 to 11 which is comparable with Fig. 3C. The less deviation might be due to intact-ness during repeated measurements by modified electrode, ensuring better repeatability and reproducibility with the consistent electron-transfer behavior.

Effect of solution pH (2–11) on DPV response of CNT@pNPA at optimal DPV conditions and its inset is Ep vs. pH plots.

The pH of these samples was determined using a calibration curve derived from the DPV responses of the CNT@pNPA electrode (inset of Fig. 4). The pH difference (% error) presented in Table 1 is significantly lower, underscoring the high accuracy and practical reliability of the developed sensor. The excellent agreement between the pH values of GCE/CNT@pNPA and those obtained from the standard pH-meter can be attributed to the synergistic effect of the CNT layer and the polymer coating. The CNT network provides superior conductivity and rapid electron-transfer pathways, while the polymer film ensures a stable, well-adhered, and reproducible sensing interface. Together, these components minimize signal fluctuations and matrix interference, resulting in a robust pH response and precise pH estimation across all tested real samples.

On the other hand, several voltammetric pH sensors based on carbon–polymer composite chemically modified electrodes have been reported; however, they fail to exhibit superior performance compared to our pH electrode (i.e., GCE/CNT@pNPA). This could be mainly due to their limited electron-transfer efficiency, restricted proton accessibility caused by dense composite surfaces, and poor polymer integration that often leads to instability during repeated cycling. In addition, many of these sensors suffer from narrow linear pH ranges and lower signal-to-noise ratios8,17. Importantly, some of reported systems also lack real-sample analyses22. Overall, this comparative analysis depicts the reliability of the CNT@pNPA electrode as one of the efficient pH sensors.

In near future, the sensor can be further miniaturized and extended to various real samples.

Conclusions

The electrochemical polymerization of 1-naphthylamine into polynaphthylamine (pNPA) on multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs) was successfully achieved in a phosphate buffer solution at pH 7, forming a CNT@pNPA composite. In contrast, a control experiment using an unmodified glassy carbon electrode (GCE) showed no similar polymerization. The synthesized CNT@pNPA composite was analyzed using SEM, Raman, and FTIR spectroscopy, alongside control samples for comparative evaluation. Electrochemical analysis revealed a surface-confined redox peak exhibiting Nernstian behavior, demonstrating reliable electrochemical properties. indicating a stable and reversible electrochemical response. The CNT@pNPA-modified electrode was subsequently employed as an electrochemical sensor to determine pH levels across a range of 2–8 in various food samples, demonstrating its functional applicability in practical pH measurement.
