Janus Kinase inhibitors associated with reduced anterior uveitis risk in autoimmune diseases
Authors: Shiao-Ling Wu, Ssu-Yu Pan, Chien-Hsiang Weng, Jun-Fu Lin, Yun Hsia, Mey-Fann Lee, Yu-Wen Chu, Wei-Ting Ho, Shun-Ping Huang, De-Xian Li, Hui-Ju Lin, I-Jong Wang, Yi-Ming Chen, Chien-Chih Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the impact of Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) compared to anti-tumor necrosis factor-α antibodies (anti-TNF) on anterior uveitis (AU) risk in patients with autoimmune diseases.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed deidentified global electronic medical records from December 2011 to December 2023, with 2-year follow-up. Adults with ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, or inflammatory bowel disease newly prescribed JAKi or anti-TNF were included. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 3,332 matched pairs were analyzed. AU incidence was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis.ResultsJAKi was associated with lower AU risk compared to total anti-TNF (HR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.20–0.92) and non-monoclonal anti-TNF (HR: 0.28, 95% CI: 0.11–0.68). In ankylosing spondylitis, JAKi showed lower risk versus all anti-TNF biologics: total (HR: 0.30, 95% CI: 0.10–0.91), non-monoclonal (HR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–0.99), and monoclonal anti-TNF (HR: 0.26, 95% CI: 0.09–0.78).ConclusionsJAKi use is associated with reduced AU incidence compared to anti-TNF therapies, particularly in ankylosing spondylitis, supporting JAKi as a potential treatment option for autoimmune-related uveitis.

Introduction

Among non-infectious uveitis (NIU), acute anterior uveitis (AU) is the most prevalent, constituting approximately 81% of all cases. AU frequently serves as the initial manifestation of spondyloarthropathies (SpA), including ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, and psoriasis [1–4]. The recurrent nature of AU and its potential complications present critical challenges in prevention, early diagnosis, and effective treatment [2].

Current therapies for SpA-related diseases, including AU, face substantial limitations. Even with monoclonal anti-TNF, which is considered first-line biologic therapy for SpA-related uveitis by international guidelines [4], frequent relapses, and complications persist, emphasizing the need for alternative therapeutic strategies [3, 5].

JAKi represents a promising class of targeted immunomodulatory agents for managing NIU, by inhibiting the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, which plays a central role in immune-mediated inflammation [6]. Preclinical studies in experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) models have demonstrated the efficacy of JAKi, particularly JAK1 inhibitor agents such as upadacitinib, in reducing inflammatory markers and preventing relapses [7, 8]. Early clinical evidence further supports their efficacy in managing refractory non-infectious inflammatory ocular diseases, including AU, with notable reductions in relapse rates [9].

Recent clinical trials have explored several novel therapeutic agents for NIU, including JAKi. However, these Phase 2/3 trials have primarily targeted non-anterior uveitis, despite AU being the most common form of autoimmune-associated ocular inflammation [10–12]. Current research is limited by significant methodological challenges, including small sample sizes, short follow-up durations, and heterogeneous assessment protocols [9, 13].

To address these gaps, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy of JAKi in reducing the risk of AU in patients with underlying autoimmune diseases. By directly comparing JAKi with established anti-TNF agents, this study aims to generate robust clinical evidence to guide future therapeutic strategies for refractory AU in the context of systemic autoimmune diseases.

Methods

The data used in this study was collected on January 13–14, 2025 from the TriNetX “Research” Network, which provided access to electronic medical records (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, genomic information) from approximately 135,645,991 patients across 103 healthcare organizations. All data was obtained from TriNetX, a global federated network of electronic medical records encompassing approximately 150 million patients from over 200 healthcare organizations. All data underwent de-identification in compliance with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule standards. This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (IRB NO: CE24430C).

Study design

This retrospective cohort study examined adults aged 18 years or older identified via International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, or inflammatory bowel disease (including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease) who received JAKi or anti-TNF. To reduce enrollment bias, we excluded patients with pre-existing NIU within 12 months prior to the index date. Notably, baseline use of anti-uveitis medications (e.g., steroids, csDMARDs) was not an exclusion criterion but was strictly balanced using 1:1 propensity score matching. The study population was divided into two cohorts: patients receiving JAKi (tofacitinib or upadacitinib) and those receiving anti-TNF (adalimumab, infliximab, golimumab, etanercept, or certolizumab pegol). The selection of tofacitinib and upadacitinib was based on their U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for treating ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, ulcerative colitis, and Crohn’s disease. Anti-TNF were further divided into two groups (monoclonal and non-monoclonal anti-TNF) and compared to the JAKi group. Monoclonal anti-TNF (adalimumab, infliximab, and golimumab) have an established therapeutic role in NIU treatment, supported by preclinical and clinical studies [13], particularly highlighted by adalimumab’s FDA approval for uveitis treatment [14]. The patient enrollment commenced on December 20, 2011, coinciding with the first FDA approval of tofacitinib, and concluded at the end of 2023.

Patients who initiated biologic therapy with either a JAK inhibitor or an anti-TNF were included, with the index date defined as the initial prescription. The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of AU, identified via ICD-10 codes. Study participants were followed for 24 months from the index date until either the occurrence of AU or the end of the study period. To maintain treatment specificity and minimize confounding, we applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of non-infectious uveitis (NIU) within 12 months prior to the index date, to reduce confounding from preexisting ocular inflammation; (2) combination therapy (concomitant JAKi and anti-TNF); (3) initiation of other biologics (anti-IL-17, IL-23, IL-12/23, IL-6) after the index date; (4) crossover between JAKi and anti-TNF; and (5) lack of documented continuation of JAKi or anti-TNF therapy between 6 months and 2 years after the index date. The TriNetX platform captures longitudinal medication data using standardized RxNorm codes, enabling the reliable detection of medication changes. In this study, patients were identified and excluded if any non-index therapeutic class codes were detected following the index date, ensuring that risk estimates reflect clinical outcomes associated with single-class exposure.

Statistical analysis

A 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) methodology was implemented to minimize baseline differences between the JAKi and Anti-TNF groups. Balance between matched groups was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating negligible variation. Matching variables included demographic factors (age, sex, race) smoking status, clinical comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease, vitamin D deficiency), disease activity markers (ESR, CRP levels), and prior medication use (glucocorticoids, csDMARDs). Variables included in the propensity score matching were selected based on their established or potential association with uveitis risk. Specifically, vitamin D deficiency and nicotine dependence were included as covariates because they have been identified as significant modifiable risk factors for non-infectious uveitis in several epidemiological studies [15, 16]. Notably, a previous diagnosis of uveitis was not included as a matching covariate because it served as an exclusion criterion, ensuring the analysis cohort was free of recent NIU at baseline. These covariates were collected within 6 months before the index date. We conducted stratified analyses to evaluate treatment efficacy across different medication classes and disease subtypes. Anti-TNF were categorized into three groups: monoclonal anti-TNF, non-monoclonal anti-TNF, and the combined total anti-TNF cohort. JAKi were stratified into tofacitinib (a non-selective JAKi) and upadacitinib (a selective JAK1 inhibitor) subgroups. Further stratification was performed based on underlying autoimmune conditions, including ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, ulcerative colitis, and Crohn’s disease. All comparative analyses maintained a 2-year follow-up period to ensure consistent assessment of outcomes.

Data extraction and statistical analyses were conducted in January and February of 2025 using the built-in functions of the TriNetX platform, which facilitate patient selection, data analysis, and result generation. To comply with HIPAA regulations, TriNetX displays event counts ≤ 10 as obscured values in the output interface; however, the platform’s statistical engine performs all computations using exact, individual-level data within a secure environment, and all reported HRs and confidence intervals are derived from the platform. Cox regression models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were utilized to evaluate survival probability, with censoring applied for patients lost to follow-up. Patient demographics and diagnoses were determined using ICD-10 codes as detailed in Table 3 in Appendix. Our study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statements for cohort studies (Table 4 in Appendix).

In stratified analyses comparing individual JAK inhibitors with non-monoclonal anti-TNF therapy (Table 2), both tofacitinib (HR: 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.73) and upadacitinib (HR: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.01–0.65) were significantly associated with a lower incidence of AU. When compared with the total anti-TNF group, only upadacitinib showed a significant association with AU risk (HR: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.02–0.99). Neither tofacitinib nor upadacitinib demonstrated significant differences in AU risk when compared specifically with monoclonal anti-TNF therapy.

Stratified analyses of different systemic autoimmune diseases showed that the effectiveness of JAKi in preventing AU retained in patients with ankylosing spondylitis when compared to total anti-TNF (HR: 0.30, 95% CI: 0.10–0.91), non-monoclonal anti-TNF (HR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–0.99), and monoclonal anti-TNF (HR: 0.26, 95% CI: 0.09–0.78) (Fig. 3 in Appendix). The outcomes for each stratification analysis are illustrated in Fig. 3 in Appendix. In the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of the ankylosing spondylitis subgroup, the 2-year AU-free survival probability was significantly higher with JAKi compared to total anti-TNF (98.74% versus 95.62%, log-rank p = 0.023), non-monoclonal anti-TNF (98.66% versus 95.64%, log-rank p = 0.037), and monoclonal anti-TNF agents (98.74% versus 95.18%, log-rank p = 0.010) (Fig. 4 in Appendix).

Discussion

Our study identifies a significant association between JAKi use and reduced incidence of AU, compared to anti-TNF. This association was especially notable in patients with ankylosing spondylitis, suggesting a potential therapeutic role for JAKi in managing autoimmune-related uveitis. These findings are consistent with mechanistic studies implicating JAK-STAT signaling in uveitis pathogenesis.

The JAK/STAT pathway is a key regulator of autoimmune and inflammatory processes, including those involved in uveitis. It mediates cytokine signaling that drives the differentiation of pathogenic Th1 and Th17 cells, primarily through STAT1 and STAT3 activation [17–20]. Experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) models have demonstrated that JAK inhibition effectively reduces inflammation by suppressing Th17 cell proliferation, IL-23/IL-17 A signaling, and inflammatory cell infiltration into ocular tissues [21]. JAKi also modulate Th1-driven pathways, with tofacitinib showing a distinct mechanism by reducing IFN-γ production [22, 23].

Research has demonstrated significantly elevated JAK-1 and JAK-3 expression in CD3 + T cells and CD14 + monocytes from ankylosing spondylitis patients. Importantly, JAK-1 expression levels correlate with inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) and clinical manifestations including peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, and importantly, uveitis [24]. Additional evidence has identified a JAK2 locus haplotype associated with ankylosing spondylitis pathogenesis [25]. Collectively, these molecular and clinical findings establish the JAK-STAT signaling axis as a promising therapeutic target in uveitis [8, 26, 27].

Our findings demonstrate that JAKi, regardless of their selectivity profile, showed comparable efficacy in reducing AU risk among patients with autoimmune diseases. This suggests that the lower AU risk may be a class effect of JAK inhibition rather than dependent on inhibitor selectivity. We evaluated two JAKi with distinct targeting profiles: tofacitinib, a non-selective JAKi predominantly targeting JAK3 and JAK1, with partial activity on JAK2; and upadacitinib, a selective JAK1 inhibitor with higher specificity [27, 28]. By inhibiting JAK1/JAK3 activity, these agents disrupt downstream signaling cascades responsible for pro-inflammatory cytokine production, particularly IL-17, which plays a central role in uveitis pathogenesis. The discrepancy between individual and combined JAKi analyses may be attributed to reduced statistical power in the smaller sample size in stratified analyses.

Previous evidence, including a landmark registry-based study by Lim et al., has demonstrated that monoclonal anti-TNF antibodies are more effective than etanercept in preventing uveitis recurrence [29]. Several meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have extended these findings by showing that monoclonal anti-TNF agents significantly reduce new-onset acute uveitis (AU) in axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), with a pooled incidence-rate ratio of approximately 0.32 compared to placebo [13, 30]. Our findings align with this trend, providing further real-world context for treatment selection. Meanwhile, prospective cohort studies and observational research have consistently indicated that JAKi are effective in refractory cases of NIU and AU. The same network meta-analysis also demonstrated that JAKi offer efficacy comparable to monoclonal anti-TNF and anti-IL-17 agents, particularly in refractory AU cases [13]. However, these studies also highlight key research gaps, including small sample sizes, limited long-term follow-up, and inconsistent subgroup analyses [9, 13]. Recent clinical trials further contextualize the role of JAKi in NIU treatment [10–12], suggesting that newer JAKi, particularly those with combined JAK/TYK inhibition, may enhance treatment efficacy for non-anterior uveitis.

Unlike clinical trials such as NEPTUNE, HUMBOLDT, and VISUAL that primarily focus on non-anterior uveitis [10–12], our study sheds light on AU, adding a broader clinical context for treatment comparisons. We evaluated FDA-approved JAKi (tofacitinib and upadacitinib) that currently lack dedicated NIU trials, highlighting their potential therapeutic role in AU, the most prevalent NIU subtype.

Clinical applicability and patient-centered considerations

The convenience of oral JAKi may improve adherence compared to injectable biologics. Their broad immunomodulatory effects may also provide advantages in complex cases involving systemic autoimmune diseases with multiple inflammatory manifestations. However, differences in safety profiles, particularly infection risks and long-term safety concerns, require further investigation to inform clinical decision-making. Clinicians should consider comorbidities, preferences for administration route, and prior treatment responses when selecting between JAKi and anti-TNF for patients at risk of AU.

Strengths and limitations

This study leverages a large, diverse dataset to evaluate real-world treatment patterns and outcomes, providing valuable insights into the comparative efficacy of JAKi and anti-TNF with comprehensive assessment of glucocorticoid, conventional DMARD-sparing effects along with evaluation of comorbidities through PSM. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, due to the retrospective observational design, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Although we implemented a 12-month look-back period to exclude recent NIU cases, potential channeling bias (confounding by indication) cannot be entirely ruled out. Clinicians may preferentially prescribe monoclonal anti-TNF to patients with a remote history of uveitis (beyond the exclusion window) given the drug’s established efficacy for ocular inflammation. Consequently, the anti-TNF cohort might have possessed a higher underlying predisposition to uveitis compared to the JAKi cohort. This residual confounding could theoretically inflate the observed uveitis risk in the anti-TNF group, suggesting that our findings may be conservative estimates of the comparative safety/efficacy. Furthermore, the strict exclusion of patients with an NIU diagnosis within 12 months prior to the index date warrants consideration. While this washout period was necessary to capture incident cases and reduce baseline confounding, it inherently selects for a population with a lower baseline ocular risk. Therefore, our reported incidence rates likely underestimate the overall burden of uveitis in broader clinical practice, which includes patients with chronic or frequently relapsing disease. Consequently, our findings are strictly generalizable to patients initiating therapy during a period of ocular quiescence, and may not apply to those with active uveitis at baseline. Second, reliance on electronic medical records may result in disease misclassification and limit diagnostic accuracy. Third, the small sample size in individual JAKi analyses, coupled with low event counts for AU outcomes, constrains the generalizability and robustness of our conclusions for this specific outcome. These low event counts resulted in reduced statistical power and wide confidence intervals, indicating a high degree of statistical uncertainty. Consequently, these comparative estimates should be interpreted with caution, as the study may be underpowered to detect potential differences between treatment groups regarding AU risk. Future studies should prioritize prospective designs, longer follow-up durations, and larger, ethnically diverse cohorts to validate these associations and further explore the long-term safety and efficacy of JAKi in AU.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence supporting JAKi as a potential treatment option for autoimmune-related uveitis. Our findings suggest an association between JAKi use and a reduced incidence of AU, particularly in patients with ankylosing spondylitis. Future large prospective studies are needed to further evaluate this potential benefit and to determine the optimal role of JAKi in the management of patients at risk for autoimmune-related anterior uveitis.
