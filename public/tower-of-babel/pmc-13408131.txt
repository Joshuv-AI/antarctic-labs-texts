Reporting and data systems for laparoscopic cholecystectomy with findings of a user adoption survey
Authors: Raviteja Sista, Hitesh Bisht, Riya Agrawal, Saquib Hossain, Utpal De, Partha P. Chakrabarti, Debdoot Sheet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy (LC) is the gold standard operation for symptomatic cholelithiasis with high incidence (7.5%) and complication (2%) rates. Pre-/intra-/post-intervention information in the form of multimodal data is recorded in various unstructured formats across different centres. The need to archive and retrieve them across centres motivates the need for the development of standardized Reporting And Data Systems (RADS). This study aims to develop a comprehensive set of multimodality-correlated RADS for LC. The present work has developed a set of RADS, which records pre-operative findings, ultrasonography findings, operating theatre findings, procedure video findings, and post-operative follow-up. Additionally, a Surgeon-specific RADS is also proposed to record the Surgeon’s skill. A cohort of 100 Surgeons with varying expertise in LC have reviewed the RADS, with 73% agreeing that the LC-RADS effectively record relevant data and 77% preferring to use them regularly in surgical practice. LC-RADS is a center-agnostic proforma that standardizes data collection in surgical interventions for enhancing the quality of patient care management.

Introduction

Reporting and Data Systems (RADS) were initially developed to objectively report and capture radiology findings. Post the initial usage of RADS for mammograms, RADS were extensively developed for various other medical imaging techniques such as Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Ultrasound, etc. Diagnosis of diseases from radiological data has been objectified through the development of RADS targeting specific modalities of medical data and specific diseases. The objective nature of the RADS helps not only in the diagnosis of medical conditions but also results in effective data capture. Extending the paradigm of structured reporting of radiological findings to report surgical processes, there exist only a handful of reporting proforma covering specific portions of the surgical process. However, the applications of surgical data are numerous such as prediction of surgical events, operative difficulty, post-operative complications, length of hospital stay, etc. This work’s focus is on designing and developing objectified RADS for laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC).

Need for structured data collection

Electronic Health Records (EHR) are used to capture and maintain data in an objectified and structured format. Substantial volumes of quality multi-modal data collected in such formats enable further research advancements to improve patient care32–37. A few advancements are able to generate surgery descriptions, predict adverse surgical events and the patient’s post-operative stay, which are possible solely due to the underlying structure and uniformity in the data records38–42. However, the structured formats of EHR do not encapsulate the surgical processes end-to-end. While there exists a WHO safety checklist for tallying the equipment used in the surgery, it is procedure-specific and does not record the technical aspects of the surgery, nor the expertise of the personnel involved43. Thus, the need for a structured data recording format for surgical processes is evident.

Data reporting formats for laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Structured recording of surgical data starts with taking operative notes in an objectified manner such that its quality is high and is rich in information. Another advantage is that the data could be filled in by the Surgeon or the residents of any level without any significant difference in the reported information44–46. There have been reporting formats previously developed for recording the intra-operative details to improve the quality of operative notes as shown in Table 1. Although these works have focused on developing a data reporting proforma with the intent of improving the quality of operative notes, there is a huge variation between these formats in terms of the recorded parameters. Thus, making the developed proforma study specific whose acceptance by a cohort of active practitioners is neither discussed nor the data recorded is compatible with inter-study /inter-centre analysis.

Data reporting systems

Standardized data collection and transmission across multiple centres creates an information-rich ecosystem that caters to patient-specific needs47. The key challenges of such an ecosystem include collaboration among stakeholders, data acquisition, access, exchange and annotation processes. However, the crux of this ecosystem lies in acquiring data digitally and in a standardized format48,49. Creating such a framework for surgical data involves developing specific structured proforma to digitally record end-to-end surgery data. The spectrum of surgical data is wide-spanning, from patient-specific data, covering operating theatre (OT) setup, equipment, the performance of individual professionals, team coordination, including ethical considerations50. This spectrum of data also spreads across time right from admission to discharge of the patient. Such multi-modal data spanning across time requires standardized systems to seamlessly record and maintain the data51,52. Additionally, standardized systems for data recording are preferred in the purview of the legal aspects and considerations53. As discussed in Table 1, such multi-modal data systems have different applications most of which focus on improving surgical outcomes and patient care54–58.

In summary, surgical data with its enormous applications needs standardized formats for data recording which aid the overall improvement of the healthcare systems and in turn, improve patient safety as well. Such standardized formats are required to be uniform across different centers and Surgeons so that over time a digital corpus of surgical data is created. In the context of data related to LC, there is no such hospital/Surgeon agnostic proforma/RADS available for effective reporting of the data so far. This work proposes a set of RADS that covers the entire process of LC end-to-end. These RADS were initially developed and validated by a group of 3 Surgeons. The developed RADS was scrutinized for their effectiveness and usability by a cohort of 100 Surgeons through participation in two surveys.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section “Materials and methods” covers the methods developed for acquiring the data related to LC. Section “Survey results and summary” summarizes the users’ opinions from the surveys on acceptance and adoption of the developed RADS. Section “Conclusion” provides the concluding remarks emphasizing the key aspects of LC-RADS, and the section “Future direction” provides an insight into the possibilities unlocked through LC-RADS.

Materials and methods

LC-RADS is designed to cover the data related to the entire surgical journey of LC, from admission to discharge. This journey is divided into, pre-operative, intra-operative and post-operative stages. Each of these stages has data from different modalities, making data acquisition and summarization difficult. The vital information required to be recorded from different processes within each stage is identified and shown in Fig. 1. Pre-operative Stage: This stage spans from admission till the start of surgery. This stage specifically consists of history and clinical findings, diagnostics tests and pre-anesthetic checkups. These tests include clinical tests, laboratory tests and abdominal sonography. In order to efficiently record the information from all of these tests, the History, Clinical and Laboratory (HCL) RADS and Ultrasonography (USG) RADS are designed.

Intra-operative Stage: This stage pertains to the actual surgical procedure. The critical information from this stage is related to the steps performed during surgery, difficulties associated in performing them, any complications involved and their seriousness. In order to record these, Operating Theatre Findings (OTF) RADS is designed. As this is a laparoscopic surgery, where the recorded video is available post-surgery, the Per-Operative Review (POR) RADS is used to record the operative details based on evidence from the recorded operative video. Additionally, the skill of the operating Surgeon are recorded using the Surgeon’s Expertise and Skill (SES) RADS.

Post-Operative Stage: This stage spans from early post-operative recovery, through the period until discharge of the patient. The stage majorly focuses on observing the patient for post-operative complications. In order to record all the follow-up-related information, the Post-Operative Follow-up (POF) RADS is designed.

This set of RADS (LC-RADS) designed to maximize the acquisition of information related to the entire process of performing a LC are as follows:

History, clinical, laboratory (HCL) RADS

HCL RADS is a single sheet designed to capture the history, clinical and laboratory test results of the patient in three individual sections. The history section focuses on summarizing the patient’s symptoms, previous surgeries and basic details of the patient at the time of admission. The clinical section summarizes the findings by the clinician upon physical examination of the patient. The laboratory section summarises the results of the tests conducted. HCL RADS is expected to be filled either by the assisting Physician or the main Physician attending the patient.

Ultrasonography (USG) RADS

USG RADS is designed to report the observations made during the patient’s ultrasonography scans by the Sonographer. This RADS mainly covers the aspects related to the gallbladder, gallbladder wall, visual inspection of the critical structures and the final diagnosis by the Sonographer.

Operating theatre findings (OTF) RADS

OTF RADS is designed to be filled by the operating Surgeon immediately after performing the surgery to record the important aspects of the surgery. The OTF RADS is designed in a piece-wise fashion to focus on capturing information related to individual phases of the surgery. Also, within each phase, the sequence of actions and the difficulty in performing those actions are reported by the Surgeon on a five-point scale similar to Nassar scale13, from very easy to very difficult. In addition, this RADS also documents the overall difficulty faced by the Surgeon in performing the surgery. This RADS is designed to be quickly filled up by the Surgeon immediately after performing the surgery.

Per-operative review (POR) RADS

POR RADS is designed to assist the OTF RADS in the detailed capture of the information related to the surgery and the associated operative difficulty. The concept behind designing an additional RADS to capture the surgery is solely due to the busy OTs. This RADS, like OTF, captures all the phases of LC. However, the level of detail at which this RADS captures the information is much greater than OTF. As the name suggests, this RADS is expected to be filled while reviewing the recorded operative video, at a later time after the operation. In addition to the operating difficulty of each surgical action, the occurrence of a complication, type of complication, actions performed to manage the complications, the duration for each action and the toolset used for performing the actions are also captured. Similar information is also captured cumulatively for the entire surgery. This RADS is provisioned to be filled either by the operating Surgeon by revisiting the operative video or by any other Surgeon by reviewing the operative video.

Post-operative follow-up (POF) RADS

POF RADS is designed to capture the information related to the patient’s condition when they are under the post-operative observation period. This is usually a 1-5 day period. It is designed to record vital information related to postoperative complications and the overall patient’s condition on a day-to-day basis for the observation period or until the discharge of the patient. This RADS is expected to be filled in only once per day by the attending Physician or the junior Physician monitoring the patient during their daily rounds.

Surgeon’s experience and skill (SES) RADS

SES RADS is the only RADS that does not record the data related to a patient. This RADS is solely designed for operating and reviewing Surgeons. The main focus of this RADS is to understand the spectrum of operative difficulty as perceived by the Surgeon. The term operative difficulty of an action is very subjective and is majorly subjected to the patient and as well as the operating Surgeon along with many minor factors such as the skill and expertise of Assistant Surgeons, OT setup and equipment availability, etc. To cover the information related to the surgery, both these major factors have to be accounted for along with the minor ones. However, this work only limits itself to the major factors for now. The operative difficulty subjected to the patient is already being recorded as part of the OTF and POR RADS. The SES RADS records the operative difficulty of each possible action in the surgery made by the Surgeon, i.e., the operative difficulty of an ideal action based on the experience of the Surgeon. To maintain uniformity with other RADS, SES RADS also records the difficulty grading on a five-point scale from very easy to very difficult. This RADS is expected to be filled in by a Surgeon once a year or after performing a significant number of laparoscopic cholecystectomies.

The designed LC-RADS are made available in two modes: PDFs of the forms serve as the physical version of the RADS, and a Google form for each RADS is developed, representing the digital version of the RADS. These are developed for survey participants to try out and experience filling out the RADS, based on which they could answer the surveys. Readers willing to try the RADS are encouraged to choose from either format, and for day-to-day data collection, downloading the physical RADS is suggested. The physical and digital LC-RADS versions are available in Supplementary Table S1.

Survey results and summary

To scrutinize the effectiveness and correctness of the developed set of LC-RADS, two surveys were conducted through a cohort of 100 Surgeons from different geographical locations. These Surgeons are members of the Association of Surgeons of India (ASI). Survey 1 is short with a combined questionnaire covering the entire LC-RADS. Survey 2 is an extension of Survey 1 seeking detailed feedback on each RADS separately. Survey 2 is limited to a cohort of 31 Surgeons. The details of the surveys are mentioned in supplementary materials (Table S2). Need for LC-RADS

The overall summary of the two surveys as presented in Fig. 2, emphasises the need for structured methods of data recording in order to maintain data in the digital age, for various supplementary tasks. The need for such a method for LC is agreed upon by 75% of the survey participants. There were 15% and 10% of participants respectively who were unsure and opined that LC-RADS is not required. However, the major reason behind 25% of them being either unsure or not in favour of LC-RADS is due to the difficulty in managing the time to fill up the RADS, especially the OTF RADS while they practice in busy OTs. There were no other compelling reasons mentioned by the participants against its adoption. To deal with this issue, we propose a simpler alternative which includes the additional assisting / junior Surgeons to fill up the OTF RADS during the surgery and have it verified later on by the main Surgeon for final sign-off. Otherwise, the main Surgeon can also fill the same right after the surgery. On the other hand, in order to accommodate busy OTs, the OTF RADS is already designed to be a single sheet of multiple-choice questions and answers compared to the detailed case operative notes the assisting/junior Surgeons write based on the surgery. Given a predefined format and questions to fill up makes the data capturing process faster compared to traditional approaches where each case report would have different kinds of information based on the case and the person writing the report.

Sufficiency of LC-RADS

In order to address the issue of LC-RADS being capable of recording various LC cases of varying difficulty while capturing every piece of basic information to the finest detail, the participants were asked to comment on the completeness of the RADS. Interestingly, 73% of participants opted that LC-RADS is sufficient to capture the basic information on the end-to-end process of LC. Out of the remaining 27%, 11% were not sure and 16% felt that LC-RADS needs to include additional parameters. The suggestions given by the participants on the completeness of the LC-RADS majorly focused on including parameters related to information obtained using additional imaging devices intra-operatively, variants of LC such as subtotal cholecystectomy, use of any advanced equipment and the latest changes to process due to such equipment. It is to be noted that the basic idea of LC-RADS is two-fold, one is to record the basic information from an operative difficulty and post-operative complications point of view, and the second is to make LC-RADS generic such that it could be used anywhere from a remote environment with minimalist operative settings to a well-equipped corporate healthcare centre. LC-RADS can be updated with time based on any received feedback, and the currently proposed version is a basic one focused on bringing the data captured across a variety of operative settings and different geographical settings onto a single schema such that it results in a data corpus on LC, which would be useful for related downstream applications.

Adoption of LC-RADS

In response to the question of using LC-RADS at their respective health centres, 77% of the participants were willing to use it to record and store the entire case information for the end-to-end process of LC and 23% were either unsure or were not willing to use LC-RADS. The reasons behind the decision of these 23% of participants are the same as the reasons listed in the case of the need for LC-RADS, lengthy forms, time to fill and difficulty in inclusion into daily surgical routines. A few participants also suggested having an application which is to be used for collecting data on the fly. On these grounds, this work proposes a digital version of LC-RADS to aid in faster and paperless collection of information. Based on Survey 2, 65% of the participants are inclined towards Digital LC-RADS and the remaining 35% are comfortable using physical LC-RADS. Suggestions to LC-RADS

There were many suggestions from the participants aiming to improve LC-RADS. These responses were categorized, and a summary of those with few sample responses is provided in Table 3. The survey findings highlight that while a majority of respondents provided no suggestions for modifications to RADS (76% in Survey 1, varying between 19–65% across RADS in Survey 2), several recurring themes emerged which were grouped into categories as shown in Table 3. Common suggestions included reducing form length to avoid redundancy, adding or modifying data fields (e.g., coagulation history, bile duct and gallbladder wall size, intra-operative timeouts, adhesions, and postoperative details), and making sections either more detailed (e.g., operative steps, port creation, adhesiolysis, Callot’s dissection) or more concise. Imaging modality inclusions such as MRCP, MRI, CT, and intra-operative cholangiography were also recommended. Other notable inclusions were related to operative processes (subtotal, partial, retrograde cholecystectomies and bailout procedures), postoperative outcomes (pain, surgical site infections, drain status), and broader aspects like surgeon expertise, instrument quality, and anesthesia recovery. Across RADS types, “Others” category responses frequently emphasized the need for greater precision, anatomical variation documentation, use of advanced imaging, and adaptation to operative challenges. Readers are advised to refer to the survey results in the supplementary materials for detailed responses. In summary, the feedback suggests balancing comprehensiveness with usability, minimizing unnecessary sections, and tailoring data fields to enhance clinical relevance. However, the scope of this work limits it to the fundamentals of LC and commonly occurring adversities in this procedure. Nonetheless, LC-RADS is expected to evolve, making its scope broader and deeper based on the suggestions from the participants of the survey. The current version of LC-RADS itself has numerous applications, such as standardization of surgical information capture, digital patient records, objectified outcomes, research and analytical support, medico-legal support, disease and patient management, and so on. In short, the crux of the idea behind this work is to have a simple proforma that standardizes the information capture and makes digital surgical data available for various data-led research and Application of LC-RADS

A subset of LC-RADS was employed in the data collection of 25 patients as part of a pilot study to predict the operative adversity pre-operatively59. This study utilizes HCL, USG, OTF, POR, and SES RADS to collect relevant data related to the LC. The data thus collected was later digitized using the developed Google forms. This data is made available as part of the supplementary materials (Table S3) and the average time to collect such data using the proposed templates is shown in Table 4. The pilot study emphasizes the importance of data from different RADS covering various stages of the operative procedure for predicting adversity. It explores the relationships between multiple parameters across different RADS, highlighting their significance and correlation with surgical adversities. The adversity prediction model proposed in the pilot study helps Surgeons with pre-operative clinical decisions and thus reduces injuries during LC. All this is possible solely due to structured reporting of coarse data enabled by LC-RADS. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study responds to the pressing need for improved data management in LC by introducing a standardized reporting and data system (LC-RADS). LC, a widely performed minimally invasive intervention, often grapples with high incidence and complication rates. However, none of the previous works on surgical data capture cater to the need to capture the entire spectrum of surgical data. The LC-RADS developed in this study is uniquely designed to collate pre-operative, ultrasonography, operating theatre, procedure video, and post-operative findings, with an additional focus on capturing Surgeon-specific data. Thus, it focuses on digitally capturing surgical data end-to-end in a specified and uniform format. Centre-agnostic and generic nature of LC-RADS enables it to be used in a remote environment with minimalist operative settings, as well as a well-equipped corporate healthcare centre. LC-RADS is another step towards standardization of surgical information capture, digital patient records, objectified outcomes, research and analytical support, medico-legal support, disease and patient management in the realm of surgical data management and applications. The effectiveness of LC-RADS is evaluated by 100 Surgeons. The positive feedback from a diverse cohort of 100 Surgeons, where 73% acknowledged the efficacy of data recording and 77% expressed a preference for integrating LC-RADS into their daily practice, highlights the potential impact of this standardized approach. Beyond the procedure of LC, the use of RADS extends to enhancing the quality of patient care and, more broadly, serving as a model for the development of similar reporting and data systems in diverse surgical interventions. LC-RADS, with its centre-agnostic design, stands poised to contribute to ongoing advancements in healthcare practices, fostering collaboration, and driving innovation in surgical data science for improving patient outcomes.

Future direction

The developed set of LC-RADS serves as a template for LC data collection. Upon complete digitization and optimization of this template for integration into existing EHR systems, an opportunity arises for large-scale deployment, validation, and feedback collection to improve LC-RADS. In later stages, methods based on artificial intelligence (AI), such as speech-to-text (STT), text-to-speech (TTS) models, and large language models (LLMs), could be integrated to develop an automated voice-based data collection agent for seamless and touchless recording of patient data. Such agents would provide realtime feedback and analytics on the data fields required in the RADS and aid in continuous improvement and development.
