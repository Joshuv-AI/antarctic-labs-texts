Personalized management of platelet transfusion refractoriness: risk factors, cross-matching, and IVIG strategies
Authors: Jixin Li, Linting Wang, Yi Qin, Yingyin Liang, Piao Lv, Yuan Yao, Xinxin Fan, Huayou Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatelet transfusion refractoriness (PTR) is a major clinical challenge in hematologic patients, yet real-world evidence comparing transfusion strategies—particularly the role of cross-matched platelets and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)—remains limited.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 144 hematologic patients diagnosed with PTR at Nanfang Hospital (2019–2022), totalling 1,345 transfusion episodes. Patients were stratified by platelet antibody status. Four transfusion regimens were compared: random platelets, cross-matched platelets, random platelets + IVIG, and cross-matched platelets + IVIG. The primary endpoint was 24-h corrected count increment (CCI). Logistic regression identified factors associated with antibody development. The primary analysis was performed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with a random intercept for patients to account for within-patient correlation. Fixed effects included transfusion strategy along with prespecified episode-level and patient-level covariates. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the findings.ResultsFemale sex emerged as the strongest independent predictor for the development of platelet antibodies (OR: 4.18, 95% CI: 2.25–8.24). Other univariate risk factors included age, obstetric history, diagnosis, and chemotherapy cycles. Among antibody-positive patients, both cross-matched platelets and IVIG combined with random platelets were associated with improved 24 h CCI compared with random platelets alone (all pHolm < 0.001), cross-matched platelets were associated with higher 24 h CCI relative to random plus IVIG (pHolm = 0.019), whereas cross-matched platelets plus IVIG demonstrated no additional benefit vs. cross-matched platelets alone in this cohort (pHolm = 0.444). In antibody-negative patients, no statistically significant difference in 24 h CCI was observed across the four transfusion strategies (p = 0.244). Consistent with the LMM results, the overall effect of transfusion strategy was not statistically significant among antibody-negative patients (p = 0.692) but was significant among antibody-positive patients (p < 0.001). Similar results were obtained using GEE, supporting the robustness of these findings.ConclusionAmong patients with antibody-negative PTR, cross-matched platelets and IVIG demonstrated no additional benefit in this cohort. For antibody-positive PTR, cross-matched platelets showed the most favorable response in this cohort; when unavailable, high-dose IVIG with random platelets may be preferentially considered. These comparative treatment findings require prospective validation.

Introduction

Platelet transfusions are essential for patients with cancer, hematologic malignancies, bone marrow failure, and recipients of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation—they prevent severe bleeding (1, 2). But PTR—failure to achieve expected posttransfusion increments—can complicate care and threaten survival (3, 4). Reported PTR incidence in hematologic patients ranges from 7% to 34%, depending on the population and definition (5–7). The standard definition requires two consecutive transfusions of ABO-identical, fresh platelets without the expected rise in platelet count or clinical improvement (8–10).

Management strategies for PTR depend on the underlying cause, especially the presence of platelet antibodies (11–13). For immune-mediated PTR, guidelines recommend crossmatch-compatible or HLA-selected platelets. For non-immune causes, treatment targets the primary condition. But how best to diagnose immune PTR (IPTR) is still unclear (14). Solid-phase red cell adherence assay (SPRCA) and other methods can miss low-titer or low-affinity antibodies, so clinically relevant alloimmunization may go undetected.

On the other hand, for antibody-negative patients in whom non-immune causes are not obvious, evidence to guide platelet selection is limited. Our study may help reduce unnecessary use of cross-matched platelet products and associated resource use.

Other immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) therapies, particularly IVIG, have been used for immune PTR (11), but evidence for benefit is conflicting (15–17). We therefore asked: what factors predict platelet antibody development in hematologic patients with PTR? And how well do cross-matched platelets with or without IVIG perform in these patients? Our findings can help identify high-risk patients for alloimmunization, guide rational use of cross-matched platelets and IVIG, and improve resource allocation.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

We retrospectively analyzed patients with hematologic disorders diagnosed with platelet transfusion refractoriness (PTR) at the Department of Hematology, Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, between January 2019 and June 2022. PTR was defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria after two consecutive ABO-identical random apheresis platelet transfusions: 1-h corrected count increment (CCI) < 7.5, 24-h CCI < 4.5 (18).

Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of a hematologic disorder; (2) meeting PTR criteria; and (3) complete clinical data, including age, sex, ABO blood type, height, weight, disease diagnosis, obstetric history, treatment history, platelet transfusion details (time, dose, complete blood count within 24 h before and after transfusion), body temperature, and IVIG infusion data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) no platelet transfusion; or (2) non-refractory response.

Grouping

Patients were stratified by platelet antibody status (positive vs. negative); by age (≤30, 31–50, >50 years); by sex; by obstetric history (women only); by ABO type; by diagnosis [acute/chronic myeloid leukemia (AML/CML), acute/chronic lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL/CLL), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), lymphoma, other]; and by cumulative platelet transfusions (1–3, 4–6, 7–9, ≥10). Transfusion episodes were categorized into four regimens: random platelets, cross-matched platelets, random platelets plus IVIG, and cross-matched platelets plus IVIG.

Platelet transfusion

Indications followed published guidelines (11, 12, 19): prophylactic transfusion when platelet count <10 × 109/L without bleeding; when <20 × 109/L with bleeding risk; and higher thresholds (50 × 109/L) for active bleeding, surgery, or invasive procedures. Red cells and plasma were given per standard indications.

Apheresis platelets were from Guangzhou Blood Center, ABO- and Rh-matched, prepared to national quality standards (≥2.5 × 1011 platelets per unit), stored at 22 ± 2 °C with agitation, and used within expiry.

IVIG administration

IVIG (5–25 g) was infused intravenously within 24 h before platelet transfusion, over 2 h.

Outcome measures

We used 24-h CCI as the primary efficacy metric (18, 20), calculated as:CCI=(post-transfusionplateletcount–pre-transfusionplateletcount)×bodysurfacearea(m2)/numberofplateletstransfused(1011)

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from the hospital's electronic medical records, including admission and discharge notes, nursing records, laboratory results, and examinations. Baseline data were collected at the time of first PTR diagnosis.

Collected variables included: age, sex, ABO type, height, weight, diagnosis, obstetric history, treatment history, outcome; platelet transfusion details (time, cumulative number, dose, platelet counts within 24 h before/after transfusion, body temperature); and clinical conditions at transfusion [infection, fever, sepsis, splenomegaly, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)].

Platelet antibody detection

Anti-HLA class I and anti-HPA antibodies [against GP Ib/IX, GP IIb/IIIa, GP Ia/IIa, and GP IV (CD36)] were detected using solid-phase enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Lifecodes Pakplus, Immucor, USA). Plates were read on an EL × 800GTI microplate reader (BioTek). A sample was considered positive when the optical density was ≥2-fold the negative control.

Platelet cross-matching

Solid-phase red cell adherence assay (SPRCA) was used (MASPAT, Sanquin, the Netherlands). Donor platelets were immobilized on microplate wells pre-coated with murine monoclonal anti-platelet antibodies. Recipient serum was added with low-ionic-strength solution. Bound antibodies were detected with anti-human IgG and MASPAT indicator red cells. Positive reactions (presence of platelet antibodies) showed a uniform layer of red cells; negative reactions showed a compact button.

Statistical analysis

The dataset had a two-level hierarchical structure, with platelet transfusion episodes (level 1) nested within patients (level 2). Analyses were performed separately in the platelet antibody–negative and platelet antibody–positive cohorts.

In the antibody-negative cohort, 710 episodes were contributed by 83 patients (median 7 episodes per patient, IQR: 4–12, range: 2–27); in the antibody-positive cohort, 635 episodes were contributed by 61 patients (median: 9, IQR: 6–13, range: 3–39). Importantly, every patient in both cohorts received at least two of the four transfusion strategies (antibody-negative: 6.0% two strategies, 63.9% three, 30.1% four; antibody-positive: 1.6%, 67.2% and 31.1%, respectively), so that the design was fully connected and partially crossed rather than nested, and all between-strategy contrasts were informed by within-patient comparisons.

Sensitivity analyses. The following pre-specified sensitivity analyses were performed: (a) unadjusted LMM; (b) LMM restricted to the first episode of each strategy per patient; (c) exclusion of patients contributing more than the 95th percentile of episodes; (d) rank-based (van der Waerden inverse-normal) LMM, given the right-skewed distribution of CCI (skewness 1.56 and 1.06); (e) LMM allowing heteroscedastic residual variance across strategies; and (f) GEE with alternative working correlation structures. Ethics

The retrospective protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Ethical approval number: NFEC-2018-143) prior to initiation of study, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. Originally granted in 2018 as a prospective approval for an ongoing clinical database, this ethics approval formally covers the entire patient-enrolment period from January 2019 to June 2022; no separate renewal or extension was required.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

We included 144 patients with hematologic disorders diagnosed with PTR at the Nanfang Hospital between January 2019 and June 2022. A total of 1,345 platelet transfusion episodes were analyzed, the distribution of which by patient and strategy, and the number of patients shared by each pair of strategies, are given in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

The 1,345 transfusion episodes were contributed by 144 patients (83 antibody-negative and 61 antibody-positive). All patients received more than one transfusion strategy during the study period (Supplementary Table S1), and each pair of strategies was shared by a substantial number of patients (20–77; Supplementary Table S2), indicating a fully connected, partially crossed design. Within-patient clustering of 24-h CCI was statistically significant in both cohorts (likelihood-ratio test for the patient random intercept: χ2 = 32.06, p < 0.001 in antibody-negative patients; χ2 = 13.38, p < 0.001 in antibody-positive patients). The ICC was 0.186 in antibody-negative patients and 0.124 in antibody-positive patients; that is, 12%–19% of the total variability in 24 h CCI was attributable to stable between-patient differences. With mean cluster sizes of 8.6 and 10.4 episodes, the conventional design effects were 2.41 and 2.17, corresponding to effective sample sizes of approximately 295 and 293 independent observations rather than 710 and 635, and to an inflation of standard errors by factors of 1.55 and 1.47 had clustering been ignored for patient-level comparisons.

Mean patient age was 42.38 ± 13.79 years; 68 patients (47.22%) were male, 76 patients (52.78%) were female. Platelet antibodies were positive in 61 patients (42.36%) and negative in 83 (57.64%). Diagnoses included acute/chronic myeloid leukemia (AML/CML, n = 72, 50.00%), acute/chronic lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL/CLL, n = 33, 22.92%), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS, n = 20, 13.89%), lymphoma (n = 7, 4.86%), and other hematologic diseases (n = 12, 8.33%). Baseline clinical covariates (splenomegaly, GVHD, infection, sepsis, and DIC) were comparable between the antibody-negative and antibody-positive groups, with no statistically significant inter-group differences. Factors associated with platelet antibody development

Univariate logistic regression

We examined age, sex, blood type, diagnosis, obstetric history, cumulative platelet transfusions, and number of chemotherapy cycles as independent variables, with antibody status as the dependent variable. ABO blood type (p = 0.639) and cumulative platelet transfusions (p = 0.705) showed no significant association. Significant associations were found for sex (p < 0.001), age (p = 0.039), diagnosis (p = 0.002), number of chemotherapy cycles (p < 0.001), and obstetric history in women (p = 0.047).

Women had a 7.09-fold higher risk of developing antibodies than men (OR: 7.09, 95% CI: 3.37–15.80). Using patients aged ≤30 years as the reference group, patients aged 31–50 years had a 2.98-fold higher risk of antibody development (OR: 2.98, 95% CI: 1.19–8.10), and those aged >50 years had a 2.93-fold higher risk (OR: 2.93, 95% CI: 1.14–8.19). Risk of platelet antibody development was highest during the first chemotherapy cycle. Using ≤1 chemotherapy cycle as the reference group, patients who received 2–4 cycles had a 71% lower risk of antibody development (OR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12–0.65) and those with ≥5 cycles had a 78% lower risk (OR: 0.22, 95% CI: 0.09–0.51). Compared with patients with other hematologic disorders, those with MDS had an 8.24-fold higher risk of antibody development (OR: 8.24, 95% CI: 1.57–71.70), and patients with AML/CML exhibited a 4.92-fold elevated risk (OR: 4.92, 95% CI: 1.17–37.00). Among women, those with a history of pregnancy had higher antibody positivity rates than those without. Results are summarized in Table 2.

Multivariate logistic regression

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Variables with a VIF > 5 were excluded from the final multivariate logistic regression model. Owing to substantial multicollinearity between obstetric history and sex, obstetric history was removed, and only sex was retained in the model. After adjusting for covariates, sex remained an independent risk factor for platelet antibody development (p < 0.001). Notably, accounting for within-patient correlation altered the results in both directions relative to a naive episode-level analysis: the benefit of adding IVIG to random platelets, which was not statistically significant when episodes were treated as independent (Tukey-adjusted p = 0.224), became significant in the mixed model (p = 0.024), because this contrast is largely informed by within-patient comparisons (Supplementary Table S3B); conversely, the apparent advantage of cross-matched platelets plus IVIG over random platelets alone was attenuated (p = 0.005 to p = 0.043). This illustrates that ignoring clustering may both conceal and exaggerate treatment effects, and supports the use of models that explicitly represent the hierarchical structure of the data.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study examined factors linked to platelet antibody development in hematologic patients with PTR and assessed the clinical efficacy of cross-matched platelets and IVIG. The findings inform transfusion strategies for antibody-negative and antibody-positive PTR patients, help identify who benefits from cross-matched platelets or IVIG, and support rational allocation of cross-matched platelets.

A methodological strength of the present study is that transfusion strategy varied within patients in the entire cohort, so that every patient acted as his or her own control. This allowed the strategy effect to be decomposed into within- and between-patient components; the finding that the effects were driven entirely by within-patient contrasts, with non-significant contextual effects, argues against confounding by indication—an important consideration in a retrospective study in which cross-matched platelets and IVIG are preferentially allocated to patients perceived as refractory. Consistency of the estimates across mixed-effects models, population-averaged GEE models, non-parametric incomplete-block tests and self-matched paired comparisons further supports the robustness of the conclusions.

Platelet antibodies were detected in 42.36% of 144 PTR patients—higher than the 10%–25% reported by Cohn et al. (21) and the 18% reported by Legler et al. (5). This difference likely reflects our study population: all had hematologic disorders requiring repeated platelet transfusions during chemotherapy-induced marrow suppression or after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Because current practice ensures only ABO and Rh matching without routine HLA or HPA genotyping, transfusion of mismatched platelets readily triggers alloimmunization.

Univariate analysis identified sex, age, obstetric history, diagnosis, and chemotherapy cycles as factors associated with platelet antibody development. After multivariate adjustment, female sex remained the strongest independent predictor (OR: 4.18, 95% CI: 2.25–8.24). Several mechanisms explain this disparity: pregnancy-related alloimmunization (including unrecognized miscarriages), estrogen-enhanced B-cell survival and antibody production, and X-linked immune-regulatory genes (e.g., TLR7, FOXP3) with differential expression between sexes. Women with a history of pregnancy are at particular risk because maternal–fetal antigen incompatibility exposes them to paternal HLA/HPA antigens. In a blood donor study, HLA antibody positivity was 17.3% in women; among those with prior pregnancy, the rate reached 24.4%, compared with 1.7% in transfused men and 1.0% in non-transfused men (22). Positivity rose with the number of pregnancies: 1.7% (0), 11.2% (1), 22.5% (2), 27.5% (3), and 32.2% (≥4) (22).

Older age also increased risk, likely reflecting longer disease duration and greater cumulative exposure to immunogenic stimuli (transfusions, pregnancy). We found no association between cumulative platelet transfusions and antibody development, consistent with Dutcher (23) and Schiffer (24), who reported no dose–response relationship. In our study, many patients receiving ≥6 transfusions had complex clinical courses with nonimmune factors, which may have confounded the analysis. A prospective study tracking the same cohort from first transfusion to antibody appearance would better address this question.

The risk of antibody development decreased with more chemotherapy cycles, suggesting that immune-mediated PTR occurs mostly during the first cycle. Chemotherapy suppresses immune function, reducing B lymphocytes and plasma cells and thus lowering antibody production. Additional cycles also increase the likelihood of nonimmune factors such as infection, fever, sepsis, and DIC. Chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia may arise from vascular endothelial damage, leading to platelet adhesion and consumption; cumulative toxicity suppresses megakaryopoiesis and directly destroys transfused platelets (25).

Univariate analysis identified MDS and AML/CML as risk factors, but multivariate analysis did not confirm this. Multivariate analyses in the present study suggested a potential association between lymphoma and antibody production. Nevertheless, this finding is constrained by the extremely small subgroup sample size, wide confidence intervals and insufficient statistical power. The true effect of lymphoma on antibody production should therefore be evaluated with caution. Further multicenter, large-sample prospective clinical studies are required to validate this observation.

Although age, chemotherapy cycles, and obstetric history showed significant associations in univariate analysis, these factors were no longer statistically significant following multivariate adjustment. This finding is likely attributable to the limited sample size and residual selection bias.

No consensus exists on a gold standard test for diagnosing immune PTR (14). Based on our findings, we suggest platelet antibody screening for patients with a history of pregnancy, those with MDS or AML/CML, and patients receiving their first chemotherapy cycle, which may facilitate early identification of patients at high risk of alloimmunization.

Among patients with antibody-negative PTR, cross-matched platelets and IVIG demonstrated no additional benefit in this cohort. Some clinicians routinely administer cross-matched platelets for antibody-negative PTR patients; however, our findings do not lend support to this clinical practice. For these patients, clinicians may focus on identifying and managing non-immune factors such as splenomegaly, DIC, fever, infection, persistent bleeding, and antibiotic use.

This study provides real-world evidence to guide platelet transfusion strategy selection for antibody-negative patients with PTR. Studies that exclude patients with non-immune factors overestimate the efficacy of cross-matched platelets (26).

In antibody-positive PTR, cross-matched platelets yielded the most favorable response in our cohort. This agrees with Sinwatcharaphirom (27), Wiita (28), and Kaur (29). Although large blood centers may maintain HLA/HPA-typed donor registries, such resources are not widely available. Serologic cross-matching using SPRCA provides a rapid, effective, and broadly applicable alternative.

Among patients with antibody-positive PTR, IVIG combined with random platelets was associated with improved 24 h CCI. IVIG enhances platelet transfusion responses by binding antigens, activating complement, blocking Fcγ receptors on mononuclear phagocytes, and reducing platelet destruction (12, 17, 30). However, in this cohort, cross-matched platelet transfusion yielded superior 24 h CCI values relative to random platelet transfusion combined with high-dose IVIG (pHolm = 0.019). Kickler et al. (31) also advised against using IVIG in place of compatible platelets, noting that IVIG improves 1-h but not 24-h CCI. Nevertheless, in emergencies when cross-matched platelets are unavailable, high-dose IVIG combined with random platelets may be preferentially considered.

Notably, cross-matched platelets combined with IVIG showed no additional benefit compared with cross-matched platelets alone in this cohort (pHolm = 0.444). Several potential mechanisms may account for this absence of additive therapeutic effect. Cross-matched platelets already circumvent the major pathway of immune destruction—HLA or HPA antibody-mediated clearance. IVIG's primary mechanism in alloimmune thrombocytopenia is Fcγ receptor blockade on splenic macrophages; once incompatible antigens are avoided by cross-matching, the target for IVIG disappears. IVIG may also paradoxically suppress endogenous platelet production or interfere with platelet survival, although this interpretation remains speculative.

In addition to these mechanistic considerations, the observed findings may partly stem from study-specific limitations. On the one hand, heterogeneous IVIG dosing in our real-world cohort represents an important confounding factor; on the other hand, inter-individual variability in patients' immune status and underlying disease severity could also contribute to these outcomes. This is a retrospective real-world study, in which IVIG administration was highly dependent on individual clinical decisions by attending physicians, with doses ranging from 5 g to 25 g. The absence of a weight-based standardized dosing regimen (e.g., 0.4 g/kg) and lack of fixed administration timing represent key limitations of the present study. Variable therapeutic exposure arising from this heterogeneity may mask the genuine clinical benefit of IVIG. This limitation weakens the evidentiary strength of our clinical recommendation against combining cross-matched platelets with IVIG. Future prospective controlled studies adopting weight-standardized dosing and fixed administration timing are warranted to clarify the true therapeutic effect of IVIG in this setting.

Some patients receiving both cross-matched platelets and IVIG were critically ill, and IVIG may have been given for infection control or B-cell depletion rather than to enhance platelet increments. As a retrospective single-center real-world study, the administration of cross-matched platelet transfusion and IVIG treatment relied on individualized clinical decisions by attending physicians. Specifically, patients with critical illness and higher inflammatory burden were more likely to receive IVIG intervention, while those with refractory antibody positivity tended to be prescribed cross-matched platelet transfusion. This indication bias may impact the comparative efficacy results of different treatment regimens. Although this study adopted multi-covariate adjustment and stratification (antibody negative/positive) to mitigate bias, the retrospective single-center design still cannot completely eliminate the residual bias caused by individual baseline differences among patients. Therefore, the conclusions of this study need to be further verified by multi-center prospective studies.

Our study demonstrated that female sex is the strongest independent predictor of platelet antibody development, with direct clinical utility. We recommend early platelet antibody screening for female PTR patients, especially those of childbearing age or with a history of pregnancy, preferably during the first chemotherapy cycle or at at the initial diagnosis of PTR. Such targeted screening facilitates the early recognition of patients who may benefit from cross-matched platelet transfusion. These findings support a stratified and personalized approach to PTR management, which may help reduce unnecessary use of cross-matched platelet products and the associated resource use.

PTR remains a multifactorial clinical challenge. Some patients with immune-mediated PTR exhibit suboptimal responses even to cross-matched platelets transfusion, potentially owing to unidentified antibodies or coexisting non-immune factors. Prompt post-transfusion efficacy assessment and individualized clinical workup are therefore essential for optimal patient management.

Conclusions

Among hematologic PTR patients, platelet antibody formation links to sex, obstetric history, age, diagnosis and chemotherapy cycles; sex is the sole independent predictor. Among patients with antibody-negative PTR, cross-matched platelets and IVIG demonstrated no additional benefit in this cohort. For antibody-positive PTR, cross-matched platelets yielded the most favorable response in our cohort; when unavailable, high-dose IVIG combined with random platelets may be preferentially considered. These findings support a stratified and personalized approach to PTR management. These comparative treatment findings require prospective validation.
