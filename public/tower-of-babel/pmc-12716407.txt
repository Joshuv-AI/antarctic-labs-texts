Neisseria meningitidis bacteraemia and lobar pneumonia in a previously healthy, fully immunised (including meningococcal vaccines) Irish teenager
Authors: Sarah Lacey, Edina Moylett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAn adolescent female patient presented to the Emergency Department of a large tertiary hospital in Ireland, with a 1-day history of chest and left axillary pain, and a 5-day history of productive cough, fever, reduced oral intake and lethargy; no previous medical history, sick contacts or recent travel. Vaccinations were up to date. Symptomatology, raised inflammatory markers, radiographic findings, coupled with relevant auscultatory findings, led to a diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia. Less than 24 hours after presentation, Neisseria meningitidis was isolated from a blood culture, prompting patient isolation and commencement of high-dose ceftriaxone. Close contacts received prophylaxis, and public health authorities were notified. Meningococcal pneumonia, although infrequently seen in adults, has been reported in the paediatric population to date only rarely. This case serves as a reminder that meningococcal disease is notifiable, requiring prompt reporting to public health authorities, patient isolation and prophylaxis for close contacts. It also highlights meningococcal pneumonia as a cause of paediatric pneumonia, even in fully vaccinated, immunocompetent children, and the importance of obtaining blood samples for culture before commencing antibiotic therapy to maximise microbiological confirmation.

Background

Meningococcal pneumonia is a rare manifestation of invasive Neisseria meningitidis infection, especially in children, with few reported cases worldwide. While N. meningitidis is classically associated with meningitis and septicaemia, pneumonia caused by this pathogen remains rare and diagnostically challenging due to its non-specific clinical features that mimic other bacterial pneumonia. The disease occurs either through aspiration of colonised nasopharyngeal secretions or haematogenous spread during bacteraemia. Early microbiological diagnosis is essential and achieved by obtaining blood cultures before antibiotic initiation, where possible, as prior antibiotics can hinder pathogen identification. Sputum cultures should also be obtained. Despite routine vaccination, meningococcal infection can affect fully immunised, otherwise healthy children, reflecting possible evolving serogroup patterns and vaccine limitations, emphasising a need for clinician awareness in practice.

Case presentation

An adolescent female presented to the Emergency Department (ED) by ambulance, with a 5-day history of progressively worsening productive cough, fever (undocumented), reduced appetite and lethargy. She had been seen in the ED only 12 hours prior and discharged with safety netting advice, following a diagnosis of viral upper respiratory tract infection. The patient represented due to abrupt onset of localised pain in the left lower axilla and chest, rated 6/10 in severity, associated with mild haemoptysis; she had been generally unwell, with bouts of coughing for 2 months, and on one instance received a course of oral steroids from her general practitioner. The patient denied other upper respiratory symptoms, vomiting, diarrhoea, weight loss, night sweats and rash. The patient’s chest pain was not worsened by leaning forward or lying flat. There was no history of sick contacts or recent travel. Previous history and family history were unremarkable. Vaccinations were up to date, including routine Meningococcal serogroup C (MenC) and Meningococcal serogroups A, C, W and Y (MenACWY) vaccination according to the Irish immunisation schedule.

At presentation, the patient had a temperature of 38.1°C and a heart rate of 119 beats per minute (normal range for age 60–100 years). There were no signs of respiratory distress; respiratory rate was 16 breaths per minute (normal range for age 15–20 years), with an oxygen saturation of 96% on room air, and no increased work of breathing. The patient was alert (GCS 15/15), but lethargic. On examination, she had reduced air entry over the lung field. The remainder of her respiratory examination was unremarkable. The patient had a normal cardiovascular, neurological, gastrointestinal, ear, nose and throat, skin and musculoskeletal examination.

Investigations

Initial blood work revealed an elevated white cell count (WCC) of 17.5×10⁹/L (normal range 4–10), neutrophil count 15.56×10⁹/L (normal range 2–7) and C reactive protein (CRP) of 81 mg/L (normal range 0–5), and a marginally low platelet 146×10⁹/L (normal range 150–400) and lymphocyte count 0.8×10⁹/L (normal range 1.2–5.2). Admission venous blood gas, blood chemistry, blood glucose, renal and liver function tests were within normal range. A nasopharyngeal aspirate sent for rapid antigen testing for influenza A, influenza B, respiratory syncytial virus and SARS-CoV-2 was negative. An ECG showed sinus tachycardia with no ST-segment changes. A chest X-ray identified a dense consolidation in the left upper lobe (see figure 1). Blood and sputum cultures were obtained; positive blood culture <24 hours (N. meningitidis), no pathogenic bacteria isolated from sputum. Mycoplasma pneumoniae IgM confirmed negative.

Differential diagnosis

Initially, viral or bacterial community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) was the working diagnosis. Bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus and M. pneumoniae are recognised causative organisms of CAP in the paediatric population. Viral CAP was considered and excluded by negative rapid antigen tests for influenza A and B, Respiratory syncytial virus and SARS-CoV-2, the most common causative viruses; additionally, PCR testing for other viral pathogens was negative, including adenovirus, human metapneumovirus, human rhino/enterovirus, parainfluenza virus 3 and 4. Sputum and blood cultures were collected to identify the causative pathogen and guide antibiotic therapy. In the meantime, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was initiated. Pulmonary embolism was considered due to chest pain, with haemoptysis, but was unlikely given the patient’s age, a low Wells score and absence of risk factors such as recent immobilisation, surgery, malignancy or previous thrombotic events. Normal oxygen levels and no signs of deep vein thrombosis further reduced this likelihood. Cardiac inflammation, including pericarditis and myocarditis, was briefly considered due to the history of chest pain, with fever. However, the absence of changes in the patient’s chest pain, with postural changes such as lying flat or leaning forward, the absence of pericardial rub, no ECG changes typical of pericarditis and stable cardiovascular examination made these diagnoses unlikely. Tuberculosis was explored as a cause of the patient’s symptoms; fever, cough, chest pain and haemoptysis. However, the absence of night sweats, weight loss, no known tuberculosis exposure, and previous Bacille Calmette-Guérin vaccine, made this diagnosis unlikely. Typical chest X-ray features of tuberculosis such as bihilar lymphadenopathy, pulmonary infiltrates or cavitations to the upper lobes were not identified, helping to rule out this diagnosis.

Treatment

The patient was admitted and commenced on intravenous amoxicillin clavulanate 1.2 g intravenously 8 hourly and azithromycin 500 mg orally 24 hourly, to cover typical and atypical causes of bacterial CAP. Fluid bolus of 500 mL 0.9% sodium chloride, followed by 24 hours of maintenance fluid, due to reduced oral intake and tachycardia. One further and final fever of 38.2°C on day 1 of admission.

Day 2 of admission, and within 24 hours of the blood samples being sent for culture, N. meningitidis was isolated, and antibiotic therapy was changed to intravenous high-dose ceftriaxone (80 mg/kg intravenously 24 hourly) for a total of seven days; a lumbar puncture was carried out before the antimicrobial change. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was unremarkable; 0 white blood cells/cm3, glucose 4.3 mmol/L, total protein 0.14 g/L (normal range 0.05– 0.35), red blood cell count was 0×10⁶. The patient’s serum glucose was within normal range. At this time, the patient’s clinical state did not raise concern for meningitis; Glascow Coma Scale (GCS) was 15/15, without photophobia, and Brudzinski and Kernig’s signs were negative. Productive cough, with chest pain, was ongoing, supporting the working diagnosis of lobar pneumonia. Owing to the above, the CSF sample was not sent for culture or PCR. Serum WCC had now risen to 18.9×10⁹/L, and CRP was 283 mg/L. Repeat blood cultures were documented negative; baseline immune function including classical and alternate pathway complement function was confirmed normal. Day 5 of admission, blood work was much improved; WCC was 5×10⁹/L (normal), and CRP was 43 mg/L. Azithromycin 500 mg orally 24 hourly was commenced for 3 days on day 5 of admission, due to slow recovery, to ensure cover of atypical CAP causative organisms; chest X-ray repeated (see figure 2). Following completion of seven days ceftriaxone, the patient was discharged home, with plans to repeat her chest X-ray and complete immune work-up in recovery. On discharge, a 7-day course of oral amoxicillin/clavulanate was prescribed given the rarity of N. meningitidis pneumonia in this age group.

PA chest X-ray on day 5 of admission. Persisting dense consolidation in the upper lobe of the left lung; the degree of opacification of this consolidation has increased in comparison to the previous chest X-ray. The right lung is clear.

Outcome and follow-up

Two weeks of antibiotic therapy were completed; 1 week intravenously and 1 week orally. There was complete symptom recovery from a respiratory point of view at 4 weeks; however, lethargy, while improving, was ongoing. A chest X-ray was repeated at four weeks, which showed complete resolution of pneumonia (see figure 3). Serum PCR confirmed N. meningitidis serogroup Y as the causative organism (penicillin MIC, 0.064 µg/mL; cefotaxime MIC 0.004 µg/mL). Follow-up evaluation of immune status included immunoglobulin levels, vaccine response levels in addition to Men Y post-infection titre (UK lab), all normal (see table 1). Full blood count and haematinics (ferritin, B12 and folate) were assessed to rule out anaemia as a cause of ongoing fatigue; all normal.

Discussion

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD), caused by the gram-negative diplococcus N. meningitidis, is a rare but life-threatening infection.1 Although global incidence is estimated at 5 00 000 cases annually, fewer than 1 per 100 000 occur in North America and Europe combined.2 IMD typically presents as meningitis (30%–60%) or septicaemia (20%–30%).1 Although meningococcal pneumonia accounts for just 5%–15% of IMD cases globally, it is rarer in children, with few published cases, making this presentation particularly noteworthy.2 The incidence of meningococcal serogroup Y has been increasing in Europe and North America, and is the most common serogroup associated with meningococcal pneumonia.3

N. meningitidis commonly colonises the nasopharynx without always causing disease—asymptomatic carriage. Carriage rates rise during childhood, peaking at 23.7% by age 19 years before declining with age.4 The combination of increasing colonisation and loss of maternal antibodies in early life contributes to the higher incidence of IMD in paediatric populations.5 N. meningitidis virulence stems from its polysaccharide capsule, which impedes phagocytosis and endotoxin release, triggering a strong inflammatory response.6

Meningococcal pneumonia may result from aspiration of colonised secretions or haematogenous spread during bacteraemia (as in this case).2 Clinically, fever, chills and pleuritic chest pain were reported in over 50% of patients, as was seen in our patient. In contrast, productive cough and dyspnoea occur in only 31% and 23% of cases, respectively.2 Recognised risk factors for IMD, including meningococcal pneumonia, include immunodeficiency (eg, complement deficiency, HIV), chronic illness, close contact with infected individuals and environmental exposures that increase nasopharyngeal carriage.7 Preceding influenza or pharyngitis has also been linked.8 In this case, the patient was immunocompetent, fully vaccinated and had no identifiable exposures, highlighting that meningococcal pneumonia can arise in otherwise healthy children. We could speculate whether she had a preceding influenza history in the months before her pneumonia diagnosis, given that she had felt generally unwell for several months, with bouts of fatigue and coughing, and at one point attended her GP with these complaints. During this time influenza was in circulation. The patient had not received seasonal influenza vaccination.

Microbiological confirmation of IMD, and in our case, meningococcal pneumonia, is essential. Blood cultures should be obtained before antibiotic initiation, where possible, to increase the diagnostic yield.9 Sputum cultures (where age appropriate in paediatric practice) should also be obtained, although they may be unreliable as some patients may be asymptomatic carriers of N. meningitidis, and this can result in false positive cultures.2 First-line treatment involves third-generation intravenous cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone or cefotaxime.10 Penicillins were previously first-line therapy but are now likely ineffective due to microbial resistance. Prompt antibiotic therapy and supportive care are crucial in preventing complications, including pleural effusion, empyema, lung abscess and pericarditis.2 One paediatric case of meningococcal pneumonia complicated by empyema has been reported in North America.9

As a notifiable condition, IMD requires prompt reporting to initiate contact tracing and chemoprophylaxis—typically with rifampicin, or ceftriaxone, or azithromycin—as per the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention guidance.11 While the introduction of the MenACWY vaccine has led to over a 90% fall in the incidence of IMD from serogroups C, W and Y, IMD can still occur.12 This case reinforces the importance of clinical vigilance, even in vaccinated children without classic risk factors.

Patient’s perspective

I hadn't been feeling well since Christmas, with a cough, sore throat, runny nose and a general feeling of being unwell. I went to see my GP and got antibiotics and a nasal spray. I felt slightly better afterwards for a few days but went back to feeling unwell again with the same symptoms after that. I stayed home from school a few weeks later, and again got antibiotics and steroids from my GP, but this did little for my symptoms, which gradually got worse over the coming weeks. Eight weeks after I first developed symptoms, my energy level was very low, and I had a fever. A few days later, I felt nauseous and had pain in my legs. I felt very hot and cold to the touch and had a sore throat and headache. I attended the GP again, who said this time that I had crackling in my lungs and a high temperature; I was finding it a bit hard to breathe. I went to the ED, was seen, and discharged the same day, after being diagnosed with a viral illness. After I was sent home from ED, about 1 hour later, I started to get an intense pain in my left side and started spitting up blood, so my mum called an ambulance. The first two days in hospital were possibly the worst I felt, and then I started to feel better from then on.
