Genetic diversity in melanomagenesis: a comprehensive analysis of BRAF, NRAS, KIT and novel mutations across melanoma subtypes
Authors: Ömer Fahrettin Göze, Reinhard Buettner, Margarete Odenthal, Carina Heydt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimMelanomagenesis involves genetic alterations affecting multiple oncogenic pathways, with BRAF and NRAS mutations representing frequently studied drivers. Recent evidence suggests melanoma development encompasses a broader spectrum including KIT mutations and novel variants exhibiting population-specific patterns. This study aimed to characterize the genetic landscape of melanomagenesis by analyzing BRAF, NRAS, KIT and novel mutations across melanoma subtypes in a Turkish cohort.MethodsSixty-six genetic materials from 55 melanoma patients (2000–2016) were analyzed using targeted next-generation sequencing of 17 melanoma-relevant genes. Mutation profiling characterized genetic diversity across histological subtypes, with associations between molecular alterations and clinicopathological parameters evaluated using Fisher’s exact test and Kruskal-Wallis test.ResultsSignificant genetic diversity was observed, with 60% of cases harboring mutations. Analysis identified 22 wild-type cases, 13 BRAF mutations (23.6%), 4 NRAS mutations (7.3%), 6 KIT mutations (10.9%), and various alterations in TP53, KNSTRN, KRAS, PIK3CA, CDKN2A, OXA1L, and RAC1. Multiple concurrent mutations occurred in 5 cases (9.1%). Notably, BRAF mutation patterns differed substantially from Western populations: complete absence in superficial spreading melanomas (0.0% versus 50–70% in Caucasian cohorts) with enrichment in nodular melanomas (36.4%). KIT mutations showed significant enrichment in mucosal melanomas (33.3%, P = 0.003). A novel OXA1L frameshift mutation (p.A54Sfs*100) was identified.ConclusionMelanomagenesis in the Turkish population demonstrates substantial genetic diversity with population-specific mutation patterns that diverge from Western paradigms. The absence of BRAF mutations in superficial spreading melanomas suggests distinct genetic pathways potentially related to different UV exposure patterns, genetic susceptibility, or gene-environment interactions. These findings have important implications for precision medicine, as therapeutic strategies developed in Western populations may require adaptation for diverse ethnic groups. Comprehensive genomic profiling including NF1 in larger multi-institutional cohorts is needed to fully characterize population-specific melanomagenesis mechanisms.Keywords: Melanomagenesis, Genetic diversity, BRAF, NRAS, KIT, Novel mutations, Metastasis, Melanoma

Introduction

Melanomagenesis represents one of the most genetically heterogeneous oncogenic processes in human malignancy, characterized by alterations across multiple interconnected signaling pathways that drive the transformation of normal melanocytes into aggressive malignant melanoma [1]. The molecular landscape of melanoma is predominantly shaped by dysregulation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, where mutations in BRAF, NRAS, and KIT genes constitute the primary oncogenic drivers [2–4]. BRAF mutations, particularly the V600E variant, occur in approximately 40–50% of melanomas in Caucasian populations and represent critical therapeutic targets [5, 6]. NRAS mutations, predominantly affecting codon Q61, account for 15–20% of cases and are associated with distinct clinical characteristics [6]. KIT mutations are enriched in acral, mucosal, and chronically sun-damaged melanomas, highlighting the molecular heterogeneity across anatomical subtypes [7]. Beyond these canonical drivers, emerging evidence indicates that melanomagenesis involves a broader spectrum of genetic alterations including mutations in TP53, CDKN2A, and novel oncogenic variants that contribute to tumor development through diverse mechanisms involving cell cycle dysregulation, DNA repair deficiency, and immune evasion [2, 8].

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that melanoma genetics exhibit substantial inter-ethnic and geographic variability, challenging the universal applicability of mutation paradigms derived predominantly from Western populations [6, 9, 10]. Asian and Middle Eastern cohorts frequently display lower BRAF mutation frequencies, higher proportions of KIT-mutant melanomas, and distinct distribution patterns of driver mutations across histological subtypes compared to Caucasian populations [9–11]. These population-specific genetic patterns have profound implications for precision medicine approaches, as therapeutic strategies validated in predominantly Caucasian cohorts may require adaptation for optimal efficacy in ethnically diverse populations [10]. Furthermore, the identification of novel mutations and cooperative genetic interactions unique to specific geographic regions suggests that comprehensive genomic profiling across diverse populations is essential for elucidating the complete spectrum of melanomagenesis and developing globally applicable treatment paradigms [1, 8, 12]. Understanding these population-specific patterns is particularly relevant for targeted therapy selection, as BRAF and MEK inhibitors, KIT inhibitors, and immune checkpoint blockade strategies demonstrate variable efficacy depending on the underlying genetic architecture of melanoma [13, 14].

Despite the critical importance of population-specific genetic characterization, melanoma genomic data from Turkish and Middle Eastern populations remain limited, with most comprehensive sequencing studies conducted in North American, European, and Australian cohorts [15]. This knowledge gap potentially limits the applicability of current therapeutic guidelines to diverse patient populations and may obscure important genetic drivers specific to these regions. The aim of this study was to characterize the genetic landscape of melanomagenesis in a Turkish patient cohort through targeted next-generation sequencing of BRAF, NRAS, KIT, and other melanoma-relevant genes across different histological subtypes. Specific objectives included: (1) determining the frequency and spectrum of mutations in canonical melanoma driver genes; (2) identifying novel genetic alterations and mutation patterns specific to this population; (3) exploring associations between molecular subtypes and clinicopathological characteristics; and (4) investigating genetic heterogeneity in paired primary and metastatic samples. By characterizing the genetic diversity in this understudied population, this study aims to contribute to the global understanding of melanomagenesis and inform precision medicine approaches tailored to diverse ethnic backgrounds.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This genetic analysis of melanomagenesis was conducted using tissue specimens from 55 patients diagnosed with malignant melanoma between January 2000 and December 2016 at the Sivas Cumhuriyet University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Medical Pathology. A total of 66 genetic materials from 60 tissue specimens were analyzed, including 11 cases with sequential samples (primary tumor and corresponding metastasis). All tissue blocks and corresponding histological slides were retrieved from the departmental archives and underwent systematic re-examination by two experienced pathologists to ensure diagnostic accuracy and optimal tissue selection for molecular analysis. All hematoxylin and eosin (H-E)-stained sections were examined using standardized light microscopy protocols to ensure consistent morphological evaluation across the study cohort.

Sample size justification

While formal prospective power calculations were not performed, our sample size of 55 patients (66 specimens) was determined based on several considerations. First, this represents a comprehensive consecutive case series from a single tertiary center over 16 years, providing an unselected cohort representative of the regional melanoma population. Second, previous melanoma sequencing studies have demonstrated that cohorts of 50–100 samples are adequate for identifying common driver mutations (BRAF, NRAS, KIT) with frequencies > 5% [10]. Third, our sample size provides approximately 80% power to detect variants present in ≥ 8% of cases (α = 0.05). Post-hoc power analysis for associations between molecular subtypes and clinicopathological parameters indicated adequate power (> 0.70) for moderate to large effect sizes. We acknowledge that detection of rare mutations (< 5% frequency) may be limited by sample size, which represents an inherent limitation of this study.

Tissue processing and DNA extraction

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks underwent systematic processing for molecular genetic analysis. Six serial sections of 10-µm thickness were cut from each selected tissue block, with careful attention to tumor content and tissue quality. Deparaffinization was performed using standard xylene and ethanol protocols, followed by macrodissection of tumor areas under microscopic guidance using marked H-E-stained reference slides to ensure optimal tumor cellularity of ≥ 50%. DNA extraction was performed using the Maxwell 16 FFPE Plus Tissue LEV DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Mannheim, Germany) on the Maxwell 16 automated extraction system following manufacturer specifications. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using real-time quantitative PCR-based methods to ensure adequate template availability for subsequent sequencing analysis.

Targeted next-generation sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

To characterize the genetic landscape of melanomagenesis, we employed a customized GeneReadDNAseq Targeted Panel V2 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) targeting 17 genes known to be relevant in melanoma development. The targeted gene panel included BRAF, CDK4, CDKN2A, GNA11, GNAQ, HRAS, IDH1, KIT, KNSTRN, KRAS, NRAS, OXA1L, PDGFRA, PIK3CA, PTEN, RAC1, and TP53, representing key pathways involved in melanomagenesis including MAPK signaling, cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and novel pathways recently implicated in melanoma development. The targeted panel did not include NF1, which represents a recognized limitation. NF1 is frequently mutated in melanoma, particularly in wild-type cases lacking BRAF or NRAS mutations. The omission of NF1 was due to technical constraints of the sequencing platform available during the study period (2016–2017). Consequently, some cases classified as “wild-type” may harbor NF1 mutations. Literature suggests NF1 mutations occur in approximately 10–15% of melanomas [16]. This limitation is considered when interpreting the wild-type category and is discussed in the limitations section.

Multiplex PCR-based target enrichment was performed using 10 ng of extracted DNA per reaction, with amplification conducted using the GeneReadDNAseq Panel PCR Kit V2 according to established protocols. Sequencing libraries were prepared following the GeneReadDNASeq Gene Panel Handbook specifications (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Targeted next-generation sequencing of FFPE melanoma specimens was performed using protocols validated for clinical diagnostic applications [15]. Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform with target coverage ≥ 200× per amplicon.

Variant calling and filtering

Sequence alignment and annotation were performed using modified bioinformatics pipelines based on standard protocols. Given the use of FFPE tissue, which introduces sequencing artifacts including C > T transitions due to formalin-induced deamination, we implemented stringent variant filtering criteria: (1) minimum read depth ≥ 50×; (2) variant allele frequency (VAF) ≥ 5%; (3) minimum 5 variant-supporting reads; (4) strand bias filtering (Fisher’s exact test P > 0.05); (5) exclusion of variants in homopolymer regions > 6 bp; (6) heightened quality requirements (Q > 30) for C > T and G > A transitions; and (7) manual inspection using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) to exclude artifacts. BAM files were visualized and analyzed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer to ensure data quality and accuracy of variant calls. A stringent 5% cutoff for variant calling was applied, with results interpreted only when sequencing coverage exceeded 200× to ensure reliability of mutation detection.

Somatic versus germline variant discrimination

This study utilized tumor-only sequencing without matched normal tissue. To distinguish somatic from germline variants, we employed a multi-tiered filtering strategy: (1) variants were filtered against population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, ExAC) with variants > 0.1% minor allele frequency excluded; (2) variants were cross-referenced with COSMIC and melanoma-specific databases to prioritize known somatic alterations; (3) VAF patterns were evaluated, as germline variants typically exhibit ~ 50% or ~ 100% VAF while somatic mutations show variable VAF; and (4) all candidate driver mutations underwent manual review using IGV. We acknowledge that without matched normal tissue, definitive somatic/germline discrimination is limited. However, all clinically relevant driver mutations reported (BRAFV600E, NRASQ61 mutations, KIT exon 11/13 mutations) have well-established somatic roles in melanoma pathogenesis.

Molecular subtype classification

Melanoma cases were classified into molecular subtypes based on driver mutations according to hierarchical criteria: (1) BRAF-mutant: samples harboring BRAF mutations (V600E, V600K, or other V600 variants); (2) NRAS-mutant: samples with NRAS mutations (predominantly Q61 codon) in the absence of BRAF mutations; (3) KIT-mutant: samples with KIT mutations in the absence of BRAF or NRAS mutations; (4) Wild-type: samples lacking mutations in BRAF, NRAS, and KIT. This category may include cases with mutations in other genes (TP53, CDKN2A) or cases potentially harboring NF1 mutations not covered by our panel. In cases with multiple concurrent mutations (n = 5), samples were classified by the dominant driver based on VAF and biological hierarchy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation with ranges. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Age comparisons across histological subtypes were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Associations between mutation status and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test for comparisons with expected cell frequencies less than 5, or Chi-square test when all expected frequencies exceeded 5. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Clinical characteristics and histological distribution

The study cohort comprised 55 patients with malignant melanoma, including 24 females (43.7%) and 31 males (56.3%), showing no significant gender-related differences in disease presentation (χ²=1.01; p = 0.680). Patient ages ranged from 23 to 88 years with a mean age of 67.2 years, reflecting the typical demographic distribution of melanoma patients. Notably, patients with melanoma of unknown primary demonstrated significantly younger age at presentation compared to other histological subtypes (F = 5.13; p = 0.009), suggesting potential differences in underlying biology or genetic susceptibility. The histological distribution of melanoma subtypes revealed important insights into melanomagenesis patterns within our patient population (Table 1). Nodular melanoma represented the most common histological subtype with 22 cases (40.0%), followed by mucosal melanomas with 12 cases (21.8%), superficial spreading melanoma with 10 cases (18.2%), and melanoma of unknown primary with 9 cases (16.4%). Single cases of acral lentiginous melanoma and lentigo maligna melanoma were identified, each representing 1.8% of the total cohort. The mucosal melanomas were distributed across various anatomical sites including nasal passage (3 cases), anorectal region (3 cases), conjunctiva (2 cases), and single cases in duodenum, oral cavity, urethra, and vagina. Melanomas of unknown primary most commonly presented as lymph node metastases (4 cases), followed by brain metastases (2 cases), liver metastases (2 cases), and one submandibular mass.

Genetic landscape and mutation profile

The genetic analysis revealed diversity in the molecular mechanisms underlying melanomagenesis, with 33 of 55 cases (60.0%) harboring detectable mutations across the analyzed gene panel. This mutation frequency demonstrates the genetic architecture that characterizes melanoma development and supports the concept that melanomagenesis involves multiple distinct oncogenetic pathways (Table 2). BRAF mutations were identified in 13 cases (23.6% of total cohort), representing the most frequently altered gene in our analysis. The vast majority of BRAF mutations involved the classical V600E substitution (12 cases, 92.3% of BRAF mutations), with a single case harboring the V600K variant (7.7% of BRAF mutations). NRAS mutations were detected in 4 cases (7.3% of total cohort), demonstrating classical hotspot alterations that are characteristic of melanomagenesis, with variants including p.Q61K (2 cases), p.Q61R (1 case), and p.Q61L (1 case). KIT mutations were observed in 6 cases (10.9% of total cohort), showing diverse mutational patterns including p.R791K, p.S854F, p.D816Y, p.L576P, and p.K642E (2 cases). Beyond these well-established melanoma genes, our analysis revealed important insights into the broader genetic landscape of melanomagenesis. TP53 mutations were identified in 3 cases (5.5%), KNSTRN mutations in 2 cases (3.6%), and single cases each of KRAS, PIK3CA, CDKN2A, OXA1L, and RAC1 mutations. Most notably, we identified a novel OXA1L frameshift mutation (p.A54Sfs*100) in 1 case (1.8%), representing the first reported instance of this genetic alteration in melanoma and suggesting previously unrecognized mechanisms contributing to melanomagenesis.

Genetic alterations stratified by tumor stage and anatomical site

To account for the genetic heterogeneity associated with melanoma origin and progression patterns, we performed additional stratification analyses by tumor stage and anatomical site (Table 3).Among the 66 specimens analyzed, 49 were from primary tumors and 17 from metastatic sites (11 patients with paired primary-metastasis samples, 6 patients with metastasis only). BRAF mutations were detected in 10 of 49 primary tumors (20.4%) compared to 4 of 17 metastatic samples (23.5%), showing no significant difference in mutation frequency between primary and metastatic sites (P = 0.785, Fisher’s exact test). NRAS mutations occurred in 3 of 49 primary tumors (6.1%) versus 1 of 17 metastases (5.9%), again with no significant difference (P = 1.000). Wild-type status was observed in 18 of 49 primary tumors (36.7%) and 7 of 17 metastases (41.2%). The comparable mutation profiles between primary and metastatic sites suggest that driver mutations are typically acquired early in melanomagenesis and maintained during metastatic progression.

Stratification by anatomical site revealed distinct genetic patterns between cutaneous and mucosal melanomas (Table 4). Among 43 cutaneous melanomas (including nodular, superficial spreading, acral lentiginous, and lentigo maligna subtypes), BRAF mutations were detected in 12 cases (27.9%), while mucosal melanomas (n = 12) harbored BRAF mutations in only 2 cases (16.7%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.478, Fisher’s exact test). In contrast, KIT mutations were significantly enriched in mucosal melanomas (4 of 12 cases, 33.3%) compared to cutaneous melanomas (1 of 43 cases, 2.3%) (P = 0.003, Fisher’s exact test), consistent with established literature demonstrating higher KIT mutation prevalence in mucosal sites. Wild-type status was more common in mucosal melanomas (6 of 12 cases, 50.0%) compared to cutaneous melanomas (12 of 43 cases, 27.9%) (P = 0.157, Fisher’s exact test), potentially reflecting different oncogenic mechanisms or the absence of NF1 from our sequencing panel, as NF1 mutations are known to be enriched in sun-damaged cutaneous melanomas.

Histological subtype-specific patterns and population-specific genetic findings

Analysis of genetic alterations across different histological subtypes revealed important insights into the diverse pathways underlying melanomagenesis (Table 5). Nodular melanomas demonstrated the highest frequency of BRAF mutations, with 8 of 22 cases (36.4%) harboring these alterations, supporting the central role of MAPK pathway activation in this aggressive melanoma subtype. Notably, superficial spreading melanomas revealed a striking pattern that represents a key novel finding of this study: none of the 10 superficial spreading melanoma cases demonstrated BRAF mutations (0.0%), representing a significant departure from established literature patterns where this histological subtype typically shows the highest frequency of BRAF alterations (50–70% in Caucasian populations). This absence of BRAF mutations in superficial spreading melanoma suggests potential population-specific genetic mechanisms underlying melanomagenesis in the Turkish population and warrants further investigation in larger cohorts. Mucosal melanomas showed characteristic patterns consistent with their unique anatomical locations and environmental exposures, with 6 of 12 cases (50.0%) remaining wild-type, reflecting the known lower frequency of classical melanoma mutations in mucosal sites. Melanomas of unknown primary demonstrated genetic patterns consistent with cutaneous origin, with 4 of 9 cases (44.4%) harboring BRAF mutations, supporting the hypothesis that these tumors arise from regressed or unrecognized primary cutaneous melanomas (Table 6).

Genetic interactions and multiple concurrent mutations

Our analysis revealed that melanomagenesis can involve genetic interactions, with 5 cases demonstrating multiple concurrent mutations that suggest cooperative effects in tumor development and progression (Table 7). Temporal evolution and novel discoveries

Our analysis provided insights into the temporal evolution of genetic alterations during melanomagenesis through examination of sequential tissue samples from select patients. One patient with rectal mucosal melanoma demonstrated clear evidence of genetic evolution during melanomagenesis, with the initial biopsy showing wild-type status across all analyzed genes, while a subsequent biopsy obtained one year later revealed acquisition of a PIK3CA (p.E545K) mutation. Another case involved a submandibular melanoma of unknown primary where initial tissue demonstrated BRAF (p.V600E), TP53 (p.A159V), and KNSTRN (p.Y31D) mutations, while subsequent lymph node metastasis retained only the BRAF (p.V600E) mutation, suggesting selective pressure during metastatic dissemination where certain genetic alterations may confer advantages for survival and growth in specific metastatic microenvironments. Our genetic analysis revealed several novel findings that expand current understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying melanomagenesis. The identification of an OXA1L frameshift mutation (p.A54Sfs*100) in an anorectal mucosal melanoma represents the first reported instance of this genetic alteration in melanoma literature. The OXA1L gene encodes a protein involved in oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial function, suggesting that alterations in cellular metabolism may contribute to melanomagenesis through previously unrecognized mechanisms. Additionally, our identification of KNSTRN mutations in melanoma cases represents an important finding, as these mutations have been primarily associated with squamous cell carcinoma and other malignancies, suggesting potential shared genetic mechanisms between different cancer types.

Discussion

The genetic analysis presented in this study reveals substantial diversity in the molecular mechanisms underlying melanomagenesis, with important implications for understanding melanoma development across different populations and anatomical sites. Our findings demonstrate that melanomagenesis involves genetic alterations extending well beyond traditionally studied BRAF and NRAS mutations, encompassing a broad spectrum of genetic changes that exhibit population-specific patterns and novel mechanisms of tumor development. The observed mutation frequency of 60.0% in our cohort supports the concept that melanomagenesis represents a highly mutagenic process involving multiple genetic pathways, consistent with recent large-scale genomic studies revealing extensive genetic heterogeneity characteristic of melanoma development [8]. The diversity of genetic alterations identified in our analysis, including novel mutations and genetic interactions, suggests that melanomagenesis proceeds through multiple distinct pathways that vary based on anatomical location, environmental exposures, and genetic background factors.

Population-specific genetic patterns and the role of UV exposure

The most striking finding of this study relates to population-specific patterns of genetic alterations that diverge substantially from established paradigms derived predominantly from Caucasian populations. Most notably, the complete absence of BRAF mutations in superficial spreading melanomas (0 of 10 cases, 0.0%) represents a significant departure from Western cohorts where this histological subtype demonstrates BRAF mutation frequencies of 50–70% [6, 9]. This finding, combined with the enrichment of BRAF mutations in nodular melanomas (8 of 22 cases, 36.4%) and the high proportion of wild-type melanomas (40.0%), suggests that melanomagenesis in the Turkish population may proceed through distinct genetic pathways compared to those characterized in European, North American, and Australian populations.

The relationship between ultraviolet radiation exposure patterns and melanoma genetics has been well established in Western populations, where superficial spreading melanomas typically arise in individuals with intermittent intense sun exposure and demonstrate high BRAF mutation frequencies, while nodular melanomas show more variable UV-associated mutation patterns [6, 9]. The absence of BRAF mutations in superficial spreading melanomas in our cohort challenges this established paradigm and suggests alternative mechanisms of melanomagenesis in the Turkish population. Several hypotheses may explain these population-specific patterns. First, genetic ancestry variations may influence susceptibility to UV-induced mutagenesis through polymorphisms in DNA repair genes, particularly those involved in nucleotide excision repair and base excision repair pathways, which could alter the mutagenic signature of UV exposure in Turkish individuals compared to fair-skinned Northern European populations [17, 18]. Second, the geographic location of Turkey (latitude 36–42°N) results in different UV radiation intensity and spectral distribution compared to Northern Europe, Australia, or North America, potentially creating distinct mutagenic profiles [17]. Third, cultural and behavioral factors including traditional clothing patterns, occupational exposures, and sun protection practices may result in different patterns of UV exposure, considering the balance between chronic cumulative exposure versus intermittent intense exposure, compared to Western populations where intermittent recreational sun exposure is a major risk factor for BRAF-mutant melanomas [6, 9]. Fourth, skin phototype distribution in the Turkish population, with higher frequencies of Type III-IV skin (olive to moderate brown) compared to predominantly Type I-II skin (pale white to white) in Northern European populations, may alter both the quantity of UV radiation reaching melanocytes and the resulting mutational spectrum [17].

The predominance of nodular melanoma in our cohort (40.0% versus the typical 18–20% reported in Western populations) further supports population-specific differences in melanomagenesis pathways [6, 9]. Nodular melanomas are generally associated with chronic cumulative sun damage rather than intermittent intense exposure [19], and the high frequency of BRAF mutations in nodular melanomas in our cohort (36.4%) contrasts with patterns typically observed in Western populations where nodular melanomas show lower BRAF mutation rates. This suggests that in the Turkish population, nodular melanomas may arise through a distinct pathway involving chronic UV exposure that generates BRAF mutations, while superficial spreading melanomas may develop through BRAF-independent mechanisms possibly related to different UV exposure patterns or genetic susceptibility factors. The substantial proportion of wild-type melanomas (40.0%) in our cohort exceeds typical frequencies reported in Western studies and may reflect either the absence of NF1 from our sequencing panel, true differences in oncogenic pathways, or distinct mechanisms of melanomagenesis in populations with different UV exposure histories and genetic backgrounds.

Clinical implications for precision medicine

These observations have immediate and important clinical implications for precision medicine approaches in melanoma treatment. Therapeutic strategies developed and validated predominantly in Western populations may require adaptation for optimal efficacy in diverse ethnic groups. The high frequency of BRAF mutations in nodular melanomas (36.4%) suggests these cases may be particularly suitable for BRAF-targeted therapies [5], while the absence of BRAF mutations in superficial spreading melanomas indicates that alternative therapeutic approaches, including immunotherapy or other targeted agents, may be necessary for this histological subtype in our population. The substantial proportion of wild-type melanomas (40.0%) emphasizes the need for therapeutic strategies extending beyond MAPK pathway inhibitors, with immunotherapy approaches such as checkpoint blockade potentially playing a more prominent role in treatment algorithms for Turkish melanoma patients compared to populations with higher BRAF mutation frequencies [5, 10]. Furthermore, risk stratification models and screening protocols developed based on Western genetic patterns may require modification to reflect population-specific genetic architectures, potentially affecting recommendations for genetic testing, surveillance intervals, and preventive strategies [6, 10]. Clinical trial design and interpretation must also consider population-specific genetic landscapes, as efficacy data from predominantly Western cohorts may not directly translate to populations with different mutational profiles [10].

Novel genetic alterations and mechanistic insights

Our identification of novel genetic alterations provides insights into previously unrecognized mechanisms contributing to melanomagenesis. The discovery of an OXA1L frameshift mutation (p.A54Sfs*100) in an anorectal mucosal melanoma represents the first reported instance of this genetic alteration in melanoma literature. While this observation is based on a single case and requires validation in larger cohorts, it potentially expands the mutational landscape of melanomagenesis. OXA1L encodes a protein involved in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and inner membrane protein insertion, suggesting that alterations in cellular metabolism may contribute to melanomagenesis through mechanisms distinct from traditional oncogenic pathways. This observation is consistent with emerging evidence that metabolic reprogramming represents an important component of melanoma development and progression [2, 8]. The role of mitochondrial dysfunction in promoting tumorigenesis through altered cellular energetics, increased reactive oxygen species production, and modified apoptotic sensitivity may represent important mechanisms underlying melanomagenesis that merit further investigation. However, given the limited sample size and absence of functional validation, the pathogenic role of this variant remains uncertain and requires experimental verification to establish biological significance.

Similarly, our identification of KNSTRN mutations in 2 melanoma cases (3.6%) represents a notable finding, as KNSTRN mutations have been primarily associated with squamous cell carcinoma and other malignancies, suggesting potential shared genetic mechanisms between different cancer types. KNSTRN encodes kinetochore-localized astrin/SPAG5 binding protein, with roles in chromosome segregation and mitotic spindle checkpoint function. The presence of KNSTRN mutations in melanoma suggests that alterations in mitotic fidelity and chromosomal stability may contribute to melanomagenesis, potentially through mechanisms involving chromosomal instability and aneuploidy that promote tumor evolution and therapeutic resistance. The identification of KNSTRN mutations in both a melanoma of unknown primary and superficial spreading melanoma suggests these alterations may occur across different melanoma subtypes and potentially represent cooperating events with other oncogenic drivers.

KIT mutations in mucosal and sun-protected melanomas

Our observation of KIT mutations in 10.9% of the total cohort, with significant enrichment in mucosal melanomas (33.3%) compared to cutaneous melanomas (2.3%, P = 0.003), is consistent with established patterns of KIT alterations in specific melanoma subtypes. Importantly, KIT mutations occur predominantly in melanoma subtypes arising from sun-protected anatomical sites, including mucosal surfaces, acral locations, and chronically sun-damaged skin, representing distinct pathways of melanomagenesis independent of intermittent UV exposure [7]. Curtin et al. [7] demonstrated KIT mutations and/or copy number increases in 39% of mucosal melanomas, 36% of acral melanomas, and 28% of melanomas on chronically sun-damaged skin, while finding no KIT alterations in melanomas on skin without chronic sun damage. The somewhat lower frequency of KIT mutations in our mucosal melanoma cohort compared to some published series may reflect variations in cohort composition, as KIT mutation frequencies vary by specific mucosal anatomical location, technical differences in mutation detection methodologies, or true population-specific differences. The enrichment of KIT mutations in sun-protected sites reinforces the concept that UV exposure patterns play a determining role in the genetic pathways of melanomagenesis, with different anatomical locations and exposure patterns leading to distinct mutational profiles [7, 8, 20].

The therapeutic implications of KIT mutations in melanoma are particularly significant, as the majority of KIT mutations identified in melanoma occur at sites that demonstrate responsiveness to imatinib therapy in other malignancies, suggesting immediate therapeutic opportunities for patients harboring these alterations [7]. Several clinical trials have demonstrated variable responses to KIT inhibitors in melanoma patients with KIT mutations, with response rates and durability varying based on specific mutation location and type [21]. The identification of KIT mutations in our mucosal melanoma cases suggests these patients may benefit from KIT inhibitor therapy, though the optimal therapeutic approach may require consideration of mutation-specific sensitivity patterns. Recent case reports continue to expand the anatomical spectrum of KIT-driven melanomas, including rare sites such as the gastroesophageal junction [21], emphasizing the importance of comprehensive molecular profiling across all melanoma subtypes regardless of anatomical location.

Genetic interactions and cooperative mechanisms

The analysis revealed that melanomagenesis can involve genetic interactions, with multiple concurrent mutations observed in 5 cases (9.1%) providing important insights into cooperative effects driving tumor development and progression beyond single-gene alterations. The identification of a conjunctival mucosal melanoma harboring concurrent KIT mutations (p.R791K and p.S854F), RAC1 mutation (p.P29S), and TP53 mutation (p.P278S) represents a particularly striking example of genetic complexity in melanomagenesis. This combination suggests that tumor development in this anatomical site may require coordinated alterations affecting receptor tyrosine kinase signaling (through KIT activation), cytoskeletal regulation and cell migration (through RAC1 alteration), and cell cycle checkpoint mechanisms (through TP53 inactivation). The presence of dual KIT mutations in this case is particularly intriguing, potentially representing either compound heterozygosity or sequential acquisition of mutations in the same allele, with possible implications for KIT inhibitor sensitivity. The cooperative effects of these mutations may contribute to the aggressive behavior characteristic of mucosal melanomas and suggest potential targets for combination therapeutic approaches [20]. However, the functional significance of these co-occurring mutations and whether they represent true biological cooperativity, passenger events, or sequential acquisition during tumor evolution remains to be established through functional validation studies.

Similarly, the concurrent presence of NRAS and CDKN2A mutations in an anorectal mucosal melanoma demonstrates the potential for coordinated oncogene activation and tumor suppressor inactivation in driving melanomagenesis. This pattern may be particularly important in mucosal sites where chronic inflammation and exposure to various carcinogens create selective pressures for cells with enhanced proliferative capacity and reduced growth control mechanisms [2]. The combination of activated RAS signaling through NRAS mutation with loss of cell cycle regulation through CDKN2A inactivation represents a mechanistically rational combination for promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation characteristic of aggressive melanomas. Additional cases demonstrated combinations of BRAF mutations with TP53 and KNSTRN alterations, as well as KRAS with KNSTRN mutations, suggesting that multiple distinct pathways of genetic cooperation may contribute to melanomagenesis across different tumor contexts [2, 8].

Temporal evolution of genetic alterations

Our observations regarding temporal evolution of genetic alterations during melanomagenesis, though based on a limited number of paired samples (n = 11), provide important insights into the dynamic nature of tumor genetics with implications for therapeutic monitoring and resistance mechanisms. The documented acquisition of a PIK3CA mutation in a rectal mucosal melanoma over a one-year interval, progressing from wild-type status to harboring this alteration, suggests that melanomagenesis represents an ongoing process continuing throughout tumor progression rather than a static genetic profile established at initiation [2]. This observation supports the concept that genetic characterization should ideally be performed at multiple time points during disease progression, particularly when considering therapeutic interventions or investigating resistance mechanisms. The acquisition of PIK3CA mutations, which activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, may represent a mechanism of therapeutic resistance or disease progression independent of the original oncogenic drivers, potentially conferring growth advantages in the evolving tumor microenvironment [2, 8].

Similarly, the selective retention of BRAF mutation while losing TP53 and KNSTRN mutations during metastatic progression in a melanoma of unknown primary suggests that different genetic alterations may confer distinct advantages at different stages of tumor evolution, with some mutations being essential for primary tumor development while others may be dispensable or even disadvantageous during metastatic dissemination to specific anatomical sites [8]. This pattern of genetic evolution, where certain alterations are selectively retained while others are lost during metastatic progression, may reflect the differential selective pressures encountered in primary versus metastatic microenvironments, with BRAF mutations potentially providing essential proliferative signals across multiple tissue contexts while cooperating mutations may be context-dependent. However, the small number of paired samples limits rigorous statistical analysis and definitive conclusions regarding the frequency and patterns of genetic evolution during melanoma progression. Future studies with larger series of matched primary-metastatic samples, ideally from multiple metastatic sites within individual patients, would provide valuable insights into the rules governing genetic evolution during melanomagenesis and metastatic dissemination [8].

Study limitations

This study has several important limitations that merit consideration. First, the modest sample size (n = 55 patients, 66 specimens) limits statistical power for detecting rare mutations (< 5% frequency) and subtle genotype-phenotype associations, particularly for small histological subtype groups. Post-hoc power analysis indicates adequate power (> 0.70) for common driver mutations but limited ability to detect small effects. Second, the targeted panel did not include NF1, a tumor suppressor mutated in approximately 10–15% of melanomas [6, 8]. Consequently, some cases classified as “wild-type” (40.0%) may harbor NF1 mutations, potentially overestimating the true triple-negative proportion. Third, tumor-only sequencing without matched normal tissue limits definitive somatic/germline discrimination despite rigorous bioinformatic filtering. However, all clinically relevant driver mutations reported have well-established somatic roles validated in paired sequencing studies [5–7]. Fourth, FFPE tissue introduces potential sequencing artifacts, particularly C > T transitions from formalin-induced deamination, which may persist despite stringent filtering. Fifth, variant allele frequency interpretation is constrained by unknown tumor purity and intratumoral heterogeneity. Sixth, the retrospective single-institution design spanning 16 years introduces potential temporal and selection biases, and the single-center nature limits generalizability to broader Turkish or Middle Eastern populations. Seventh, absence of survival data precludes prognostic analyses correlating genetic alterations with clinical outcomes. Eighth, heterogeneous melanoma subtypes with small subgroup sizes limit statistical comparisons between groups. Ninth, and critically important for interpreting our findings, we lack detailed UV exposure histories for individual patients, including data on cumulative lifetime sun exposure, patterns of intermittent versus chronic exposure, sunburn history, occupational outdoor exposure, use of sun protection measures, and indoor tanning practices [17, 18].This absence of quantitative UV exposure data limits our ability to definitively establish whether the population-specific genetic patterns observed reflect differences in UV exposure patterns, genetic susceptibility to UV-induced mutagenesis, gene-environment interactions, or combinations of these factors. Future studies incorporating validated sun exposure questionnaires and potentially UV dosimetry would be valuable for clarifying the relative contributions of environmental and genetic factors to population-specific melanomagenesis patterns [17–19]. Finally, novel variants such as the OXA1L mutation require functional validation to establish pathogenic significance, and the small number of paired samples (n = 11) limits definitive conclusions regarding temporal genetic evolution. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into melanomagenesis in an understudied population, identifying patterns that merit further investigation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts with detailed genomic profiling (including NF1), matched tumor-normal sequencing, quantitative environmental assessments, and functional validation.

Conclusions

This analysis of 55 melanoma cases from a Turkish population demonstrates significant genetic diversity in melanomagenesis with notable population-specific deviations from established Western mutation paradigms. The complete absence of BRAF mutations in superficial spreading melanomas (0.0%) contrasts sharply with the 50–70% frequency reported in Caucasian populations [6, 9], while the enrichment of BRAF mutations in nodular melanomas (36.4%) and substantial wild-type proportion (40.0%) suggest genetic pathways underlying melanomagenesis vary substantially across populations. The significant enrichment of KIT mutations in mucosal melanomas (33.3%) is consistent with established anatomical patterns [7], reinforcing distinct molecular pathways in different melanoma subtypes. The identification of a novel OXA1L frameshift mutation, while based on a single case requiring validation, potentially expands the mutational spectrum of melanoma to include alterations in mitochondrial metabolism genes, suggesting previously unrecognized mechanisms contributing to tumor development [2].These population-specific patterns carry important clinical implications for precision medicine, as therapeutic strategies, risk stratification models, and screening protocols developed predominantly in Western populations may require adaptation for optimal application in diverse ethnic groups [5, 10, 20].The relationship between UV exposure patterns, genetic susceptibility, and melanoma genetics appears to differ substantially between Turkish and Western populations [6, 17, 20], though the absence of detailed UV exposure data in our cohort limits definitive mechanistic conclusions. The results emphasize the need for larger, multi-institutional studies incorporating detailed genomic profiling (including NF1), matched tumor-normal sequencing to establish somatic origin of variants, quantitative environmental exposure assessments to clarify gene-environment interactions, and functional validation of novel variants to characterize population-specific melanomagenesis mechanisms [8, 17, 18]. Such studies will be essential for optimizing therapeutic approaches, developing appropriate screening strategies, and advancing precision medicine applications globally across ethnically diverse melanoma patient populations.
