Evidence for endogenous rhythmicity in cirral activity of the intertidal barnacle Fistulobalanus albicostatus
Authors: Aoi Katayama, Hiroki Takekata, Itsuki Kunita, Akihiro Takemura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost intertidal invertebrates align feeding and reproduction with the tidal cycle. However, it is unknown to what extent biological clocks are involved in these activities. We focused on the internal and external regulation of cirral activity in Fistulobalanus albicostatus, an intertidal barnacle. Barnacles were subjected to a light–dark cycle (LD) for 14 days, constant darkness (DD) for 14 days, or constant light (LL) for 13 days, while monitoring the changes in cirral activity under laboratory conditions. LD individuals showed a major peak in activity repeating in 24 h, while DD and LL caused two peaks per day repeating at approximately 12 h. The 24 h day-active or day-inactive rhythm under LD often disappeared or showed no clear circadian component after transition to DD. Rayleigh’s test showed that activity onset was significantly clustered around specific tidal phases in most experiments, indicating a stable phase-locked circatidal rhythm. Additionally, the timing of collection from the field appeared to influence the response of the cirral activity to the light treatments. These differences in activity may be linked to tidal seasonality. We conclude that F. albicostatus possesses a plastic circatidal rhythm, and that this behavioural flexibility aids adaptation to the demanding intertidal environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27889-8.Subject terms: Ocean sciences, Marine biology, Animal behaviour

Introduction

Most organisms in nature exhibit rhythmicity in their behaviour, which is entrained by cyclic changes in environmental factors in their habitat1. Entrainment to environmental factors plays an important role in ensuring the success of reproduction and feeding. However, some rhythmicity in reproductive and feeding activities is repeated even under constant conditions, these are referred to as free-running rhythms, and are controlled by endogenous clock systems. For instance, circadian rhythms are driven by a well-known endogenous clock2. In this case, the day-night cycle in the habitat produces potent zeitgebers that synchronize the circadian system.

The tide also causes rhythmic changes, with its effects stretching beyond the intertidal zone. An endogenous clock corresponding to tidal cycles (circatidal rhythms) has been reported in many invertebrates, including crustaceans, insects, molluscs, annelids, acoels, echinoderms, and cnidarians3–6. Some of the crustaceans known to have circatidal rhythms include, circatidal rhythms have been confirmed in the cumacean Dimorphostylis asiatica7, the fiddler crabs Minuca pugnax8, Leptuca pugilator and Minuca minax9, the shore crab Carcinus maenas10, the horseshoe crab Limulus polyphemus11,12, and the soldier crab Mictris guinotae13. The identity of the key zeitgebers responsible for entraining circatidal rhythms remains debated since tidal cycles involve simultaneous fluctuations in temperature, salinity, and hydrostatic pressure14,15. Experiments controlling environmental factors have revealed that D. asiatica exhibits both circatidal and circadian rhythms, with activity peaks occurring twice daily in synchrony with water pressure, and also once daily in synchrony with light7,15. Similarly, the intertidal isopod Excirolana chiltoni exhibits a bimodal daily swimming rhythm that is likely entrained by wave-induced mechanical stimulation14. Therefore, it is hypothesised that the activities of invertebrates adapted to the intertidal zone are integrally and endogenously entrained by several zeitgebers, although the zeitgebers seem to differ among species7,15.

Barnacles like Fistulobalanus albicostatus (Pilsbry, 1916) may be an ideal species to further clarify this hypothesis since they are commonly found in intertidal zones16 and, as such, are constantly exposed to drastic changes in both light (photoperiod, light intensity, and light spectrum) and tidal (temperature, pressure, and current) conditions. Barnacles are sessile crustaceans comprised of a soft body, including cirri for capturing food, a digestive tract, and sexual organs, contained within a calcareous exoskeleton, and are found worldwide in both shallow and deeper waters17. The cirri are paired thoracic appendages that protrude through the opercular opening and rhythmically beat to collect suspended particles from the surrounding water. Mating, as well as cirral activity which refers to the repeated extension and beating of the cirri for feeding, occur only in conjunction with the opening of the opercular plates18,19,20–22, and as such, these activities only occur when the soft body is flooded. It is proposed that exposure to tidal cycles has driven the evolution of circatidal rhythms in intertidal barnacles, enabling them to adapt to their fluctuating environment. Previous studies have shown that environmental cues such as light intensity, shadow, and photoperiod strongly influence barnacle activity by affecting the timing of cirral activity and opercular opening23–25. However, few studies have verified the intrinsic rhythms of barnacles. This study aimed to clarify the presence, or absence, of circadian and circatidal rhythms in F. albicostatus by monitoring the changes in cirral activity under laboratory conditions and several light regimes, including light-dark cycles (12-h light and 12-h darkness; LD), constant light (LL), and constant darkness (DD). Studies of rhythmic activities in terrestrial and semi-terrestrial organisms have previously used visual observations, time-lapse images, and, more recently, infrared blocking devices6,13,26. For instance, in the amphipod Parhyale hawaiensis, infrared blocking devices were shown to be an efficient tool for monitoring locomotor activity and characterize circatidal rhythms27. However, this technique is not suitable for monitoring cirral activity in Fistulobalanus albicostatus since infrared rays are attenuated in water and this sessile species exhibits only small-scale cirral movements, which do not interrupt the infrared beam. To address this issue, we developed a novel program based on average image difference to similarly automate the analysis of cirral activity in F. albicostatus.

To confirm the presence of a free-running rhythm that would indicate the activity of internal clocks, individuals were kept under constant darkness (DD) or constant light (LL) conditions. In seven of the individuals kept under DD conditions for 14 days (Exp. 2, n = 8), a clear bimodal daily activity pattern was detected over the entire experimental period (Fig. 2). This experiment was conducted in two separate trials (Exp. 2a and Exp. 2b, n = 4 in each). The free-running period (τ) determined by chi-square periodogram analysis was 11.40 ± 0.00 h (n = 2) and 12.20 ± 0.28 h (n = 2) in Exp. 2a, and 12.27 ± 0.15 h (n = 3) in Exp. 2b, with an overall mean of 12.00 ± 0.40 h (n = 7) (Fig. 2, right panels). The one remaining individual kept in DD was arhythmic. Furthermore, all individuals kept under LL conditions (Exp. 3, n = 5) for 13 days exhibited a clear bimodal daily activity pattern in their cirral activities (Fig. 3) that persisted throughout the experimental period, with a free-running period (τ) of 11.80 ± 0.10 h (mean ± SD). This free-running period of approximately 12 h is close to the tidal cycle which oscillates over a 12.4 h cycle, and was consistently observed in experiments regardless of the individuals measured and the time of the year (Figs. 2 and 3). Although a 12 h periodicity can also arise from bimodal circadian activity, the alignment of activity onset with predicted tidal phases, and its persistence under DD and LL, strongly suggests a circatidal origin. Rayleigh’s test revealed that activity onset was significantly clustered around specific tidal phases in most experiments, indicating stable phase-locked circatidal rhythms. High vector strength (R) and low circular standard deviation (circular SD) were observed, particularly in Exp. 4 (Table 3).

The final experiment (Exp. 4) kept individuals (n = 8) under LD conditions for 14 days before transiting to DD conditions for a further 17 days (Fig. 4). Under LD conditions, all individuals showed cirral activity during the photophase, suggesting the presence of an endogenous circadian rhythm, which was not clearly detected following transition to DD conditions (Fig. 4A,B; ii and iii). Among them, six individuals that exhibited an endogenous circatidal rhythm were analysed. For each of these individuals, statistical analysis (ANCOVA) comparing odd and even cycles supported the disappearance of the daily rhythm approximately 24 h after the transition to DD conditions (Fig. 4B; p > 0.05).

We identified several patterns in the cirral activity of barnacles kept under LD conditions. When these patterns were expressed as a ratio of daytime cirral activity to total cirral activity, all individuals in Exp. 1 exceeded a ratio of 0.75, while most individuals in Exp. 4 had a ratio below 0.75 (Fig. 5). Here, a ratio above 0.75 was considered indicative of predominantly diurnal activity. The individuals in Exp.1 were kept under LD conditions and showed a clear day-active and night-inactive rhythm (Fig. 6A). By contrast, the individuals in Exp. 4 (transitioned from LD to DD conditions) could be divided into two groups based on their activity patterns; one group with increased activity during the photophase (active type, Fig. 6B) similar to that seen in Exp. 1 individuals, and another with inhibited activity during the photophase (inactive type, Fig. 6C). When the cirral activity was compared among all three groups (Fig. 7), a statistical difference was only observed between the individuals in Exp. 1 and the inactive group from Exp. 4 (p < 0.05). It should be noted that the individuals in Exp. 1 were collected in March, whereas those in Exp. 4 were collected in October.

Discussion

This study showed that F. albicostatus exhibits daily rhythms of cirral activity when exposed to a 12 h:12 h light-dark cycle (Figs. 1 and 4). However, since the clear daily rhythm in cirral activity was not maintained when F. albicostatus was kept under constant darkness or constant light conditions, this suggests that circadian control does not play a major role in this activity. This hypothesis was supported by the changes in cirral activity in individuals transitioned from LD to DD (Fig. 4), where the circadian rhythm was no longer observed under DD. Therefore, the daily rhythm observed under LD may partly have a masking effect, and light may play a role in inducing cirral activity. All individuals analyzed in Experiment 4 exhibited either day-active or day-inactive patterns during the LD period. This suggests that the inter-individual variation observed after the transition to DD does not stem from the absence of daily rhythmicity under LD, but rather from differences in how each individual’s rhythm responded to the removal of the light cue. Interestingly, individuals kept under constant conditions, LL and DD, showed a major free-running rhythm, estimated to be approximately 12.0 h, which is approximately equivalent to the tidal cycle. This finding is supported by circular statistical analysis (Table 3), which showed significant clustering of activity onset around specific tidal phases in most experiments. High R and low circular SD, particularly in Exp. 4, indicate stable phase-locked entrainment to the tidal cycle. These results indicate that F. albicostatus possesses a circatidal rhythm in its cirral activity, with the secondary peak at an interval of approximately 22 to 24 h under LL and DD conditions potentially corresponding to a rhythm with a period approximately double that of the circatidal cycle. Nevertheless, the presence of a weak circadian component cannot be entirely ruled out. If such a component exists, it may be masked or phase-locked to the dominant tidal oscillator, resulting in no significant 24 h peak under DD. While under LL conditions, strong light masking, together with shortening of the circadian free-running period according to Aschoff’s law, could bias the second harmonic of this weak component toward the tidal range. This alternative scenario could explain subtle differences observed between DD and LL without contradicting the conclusion that the tidal oscillator predominates. Several studies have shown that crustacean species inhabiting the sublittoral line or intertidal zone exhibit endogenous rhythms in their activities8,9,13,15,28. For example, D. asiatica, a species found in the sublittoral line, exhibited both a circadian and circatidal rhythm in its swimming activity when kept under DD15. Locomotor activities that persisted within the tidal cycle were also observed in the fiddler crab U. pugnax, and the isopod E. chiltoni under constant light conditions, although the circadian rhythm was not examined in either species8,28. Similarly, the existence of circatidal rhythms was confirmed in the mangrove cricket A. asahinai and the American horseshoe crab L. polyphemus, in which locomotor activity was entrained to inundation stimuli and water pressure, respectively29,30. These results suggest that the presentation of endogenous rhythms and zeitgebers differs among species. In addition to the above-mentioned species, the present study revealed that F. albicostatus had a circatidal rhythm, with a day-active and night-inactive pattern synchronised to the light-dark cycle. The acquisition of a circadian system may be a disadvantage for sessile barnacles in the intertidal zone because of the difficulty in responding to seasonal changes in ebb/flood fluctuations (Fig. 8). It should be noted that water temperature has also been reported to act as a zeitgeber of circatidal rhythms in some taxa31. The importance of water temperature and the ebb/flood cycles in the oscillation of circatidal rhythm in barnacles cannot be neglected since the continuous flood and temperature conditions allow cirral activity to occur during the experiments. Taken together with the potential effects of light, these findings highlight the need to consider multiple environmental cues when interpreting circatidal rhythms in barnacles.

We considered the adaptive significance of the circatidal rhythm in relation to the day-night cycle. In this regard, the importance of the rising tide after dry-out is emphasized because barnacles are filter feeders that efficiently perform feeding and respiration during the rising tide. The endogenous rhythm observed in this study was in close temporal agreement with the timing of tidal immersion at the collection site (Tables 2 and 3), suggesting that synchronizing cirral activity with the rising tide enhances feeding efficiency in the intertidal environment. Although we did not directly measure immersion timing or feeding rates, this correspondence indicates a potential adaptive advantage of maintaining such a rhythm. Similar changes in behavioural activities have been reported in intertidal species including the high-shore limpet Cellana grata32.

Seasonal changes in environmental factors may also influence cirral activity in F. albicostatus. Differences in the cirral activities of individuals collected in March (used in Exp. 1) and October (used in Exp. 4) was observed in this study. All individuals collected in March showed day-active and night-inactive rhythm. On the other hand, two types of cirral activity was observed in individuals collected in October; the active type, which showed a rhythm similar to the March individuals, with a day-active and night-inactive rhythm, and the inactive type, with inhibited activity during the photophase (Fig. 6). This difference in the cirral activity between March (spring) and October (autumn) may be due to the seasonality of tidal fluctuations. Barnacles at the sampling site were dried out even in the neap tide in March but not in October (Fig. 8). In addition, cirral activity is influenced by the interaction between photophase and tidal height because it increases more during the daytime rising tide during the long-day period than during the daytime rising tide during the short-day period (Fig. 7). This suggests that both light conditions and tidal height interactively shape activity patterns. On the other hand, involvement in the reproductive state may be eliminated because this species is able to reproduce throughout the year33. The difference in behavioural patterns between experiments 1 and 4 may reflect seasonal or physiological differences at the time of collection, as well as inter-individual variability. The limited sample size in each experiment is a consequence of the technical constraints associated with long-term recording and maintenance of barnacles under controlled laboratory conditions, as well as a desire to minimise the impact on the sampling site ecosystem. While this limitation may have restricted the ability to capture the full range of variation in behaviour, the diversity of activity rhythms that were observed under each experimental condition are fully outlined in Tables 1 and 2.

In conclusion, we observed that the cirral activity of F. albicostatus has at least two rhythms, an endogenous rhythm (circatidal rhythm) entrained to the tide and a direct response to the day-night cycle (masking effect). In addition, we found seasonal differences in the cirral activity – day-active and day-inactive types – which coincided with the seasonal changes in tide and light. These findings provide new insights into the rhythmic behaviour of intertidal barnacles; however, the potential multifunctional roles of this rhythm (e.g19,34), and the mechanisms underlying individual variability remain unclear. The ongoing aim of this complementary study is to explore the behavioural rhythms of marine organisms by considering the evolutionary background of intertidal barnacles. Adult barnacles are sessile and inhabit marginal environments, understanding the behavioural rhythms they exhibit will help in understanding the ecological and evolutionary adaptation that has allowed them to survive in such a rapidly changing habitat.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and experimental design

Adult F. albicostatus (barnacles with a basal diameter of > 1.5 mm) were collected with the attached rocks or artifacts from the coast of Yoshinoura Park Beach (26°15’N, 127°47’E), Nakagusuku, Okinawa, Japan, in 2020, 2021 and 2023 (Tables 1 and 2). All individuals were collected during low tide, when this species is exposed in the field. They were transferred to a laboratory at the Senbaru Campus of the University of the Ryukyus and housed in glass tanks (18 L capacity) with aerated seawater at 25 °C in a temperature- and light-controlled incubator (MIR-154-PJ, PHC, Tokyo, Japan). Each glass tank had a lid and was covered with plastic wrap. A fluorescent bulb (15 W) and infrared light (SA6-60-C-IR) were used as the light sources during the photophase and scotophase, respectively. The total luminous flux was set to 750 lm during the photophase. Cirral activity was recorded for 1 min every 6 min using a USB camera (KL36IR, ELP, Shenzhen, China). Following the precedent set in previous studies11,28, no feeding or water replacement was performed during the experimental period.

Four different experiments to monitor the changes in cirral activity were conducted altering the light regimes between each, they were as follows: Exp. 1, 14 days of a day-night cycle (LD conditions, light on at 07:00, light-off at 19:00, n = 5); Exp. 2, 14 days of constant darkness (DD, n = 8); Exp. 3, 13 days of constant light (LL, n = 5); and Exp. 4, 14 days of LD conditions followed by 17 days of DD conditions (n = 8). The number of samples taken from the field was minimized to lessen the impact on the environment and the ecosystem of the sampling site. Different individuals collected from the mid‑intertidal zone (standard tidal level; 120–140 cm) at different sampling dates were used for all experiments. Details of the sampling conditions of each experiment are outlined in Tables 1 and 2.Tidal data and sunrise/sunset times were obtained from the Japan Meteorological Agency and the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan, respectively 35,36.

Activity analysis

A measurement of the number of cirral activities performed by each individual was analysed using several programs including TMPGEnc Video Mastering Works 7 (Pegasys, Tokyo, Japan), a custom macro in ImageJ (Supplementary Data 3), and a custom Python script (Supplementary Data 4). Based on the differences in the total pixel values recorded in the serial photos, the cirral activities were detected and counted automatically (See Supplementary Data 1, 2, 3, and 4 for more details). The number of detected activities was plotted as a double plotted actogram and rhythmicity was determined using the chi-square test (p < 0.05) in Actogram J37. Major and secondary free-running periods were determined by chi-square periodogram analysis (p < 0.05) using ActogramJ37. Period values represent the mean ± SD of clustered peaks within ± 0.5 h. Singleton peaks (n = 1) were excluded. Major peaks were assigned based on clusters with n ≥ 2, with ∼12 h rhythms prioritized under DD and LL conditions, and 24 h rhythms prioritized under LD conditions. When multiple clusters were detected, all were reported as major peaks. To quantify the phase relationship between activity onset and the tidal cycle, onset times were converted to tidal phase angles, assuming one tidal cycle of 12.4 h = 360°. Mean phase angle (ψ), circular standard deviation (circular SD), and vector strength (R) were calculated using circular statistics in R (version 4.4.1; package circular, version 0.5.2). The Rayleigh test was applied to assess the uniformity of the phase distribution. Analyses were conducted using only individuals that exhibited significant rhythmicity based on periodogram analysis. Differences between the odd- and even-numbered cycles was examined using ANCOVA, assuming a linear regression of the activity levels. Statistical analyses were performed using standard spreadsheet software, with the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity confirmed prior to applying the test. Following the method of Takekata et al.38, the circadian influence was inferred when the regression slopes or mean activities differed significantly between the two cycle types (p < 0.05). For these analyses, the starting point of the data was aligned with the onset of the DD period, and the ANCOVA was performed on individual-level activity data. Specifically, when activity is not influenced by circadian rhythms, the regression lines of odd and even circatidal cycles show no significant differences in elevation or slope, whereas significant differences indicate an interaction between circadian and circatidal rhythms. Comparison of cirral activities in individuals between Exp.1 and 4 was carried out using a one-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey-Kramer method, performed in R (version 4.4.1). The assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were confirmed prior to applying the test.
