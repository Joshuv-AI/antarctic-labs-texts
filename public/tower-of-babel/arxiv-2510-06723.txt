Alexander Falk, Andreas Habring, Christoph Griesbacher, Thomas Pock

Abstract
We present a novel method for drawing samples from Gibbs distributions with densities of the form π ⁡ ( x ) ∝ exp ⁡ ( − U ⁡ ( x ) ) \pi(x)\propto\exp(-U(x)) . The method accelerates the unadjusted Langevin algorithm by introducing an inertia term similar to Polyak’s heavy ball method, together with a corresponding noise rescaling. Interpreting the scheme as a discretization of kinetic Langevin dynamics, we prove ergodicity (in continuous and discrete time) for twice continuously differentiable, strongly convex, and L L -smooth potentials and bound the bias of the discretization to the target in Wasserstein-2 distance. In particular, the presented proofs allow for smaller friction parameters in the kinetic Langevin diffusion compared to existing literature. Moreover, we show the close ties of the proposed method to the over-relaxed Gibbs sampler. The scheme is tested in an extensive set of numerical experiments covering simple toy examples, total variation image denoising, and the complex task of maximum likelihood learning of an energy-based model for molecular structure generation. The experimental results confirm the acceleration provided by the proposed scheme even beyond the strongly convex and L L -smooth setting.

Splitting methods

As their name already suggests,
splitting methods
rely on the observation that Langevin dynamics
eq.

5
can be decomposed into multiple subdynamics that can be simulated separately
[
8
]
,
[
34
, Section 7.3.1]
.
The overall update can be separated into three individual propagators:
First, a position update, also referred to as positional drift, is obtained by only modifying the position variable via
A_{\Delta t}(x,v)=(x+{\Delta t}v,v)
.
Second, the velocity update (often called impulse or kick) reads as
B_{\Delta t}(x,v)=(x,v-{\Delta t}\theta\nabla U(x))
.
Third, the stochastic update of the velocity corresponds to an OUprocess
O_{\Delta t}(x,v)=(x,V)
with
V\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon{\Delta t}}v,\theta(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\varepsilon{\Delta t}})\id_{d})
.
By concatenating these elementary steps in different orders, one can construct a myriad of splitting schemes.
Using the shorthand notation A, B, and O for the position drift, potential kick, and OUupdate, respectively, the simplest schemes correspond to permutations of A, B, and O.
Each letter represents one propagator acting on the position-velocity phase space, and one full simulation step is completed once all three propagators have been applied. Such schemes are referred to in the literature as first-order methods because they provide weak first-order accuracy
[
35
]
.
Higher-order methods can be obtained by employing symmetric splittings, such as the sequence of updates ABOBA. In these schemes, propagators appearing twice are applied with half-time steps, which improves accuracy while maintaining a computational cost comparable to that of the simpler first-order splittings.

In this work, we shall focus on two splitting schemes in particular: On the one hand, we have OBA, which is closely related to our method ILA(see
remark

3
) and updates
(X^{k},V^{k})
according to

\begin{cases}V^{k+\frac{1}{2}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon{\Delta t}}V^{k}+\sqrt{{\theta}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\varepsilon{\Delta t}})}N^{k},\\
V^{k+1}=V^{k+\frac{1}{2}}-{\Delta t}{\theta}\nabla U(X^{k}),\\
X^{k+1}=X^{k}+{\Delta t}V^{k+1},\end{cases}\quad N^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d}).

(10)

On the other hand, as a representative of second-order splittings, we consider BAOAB
[
33
]
, which produces updates via

\begin{cases}V^{k+\frac{1}{3}}=V^{k}-\frac{{\Delta t}}{2}\theta\nabla U(X^{k}),\\
X^{k+\frac{1}{2}}=X^{k}+\frac{{\Delta t}}{2}V^{k+\frac{1}{3}},\\
V^{k+\frac{2}{3}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon{\Delta t}}V^{k+\frac{1}{3}}+\sqrt{\theta(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\varepsilon{\Delta t}})}N^{k},\\
X^{k+1}=X^{k+\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{{\Delta t}}{2}V^{k+\frac{2}{3}},\\
V^{k+1}=V^{k+\frac{2}{3}}-\frac{{\Delta t}}{2}\nabla\theta U(X^{k+1}),\end{cases}\quad N^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id_{d}).

(11)

Note that, despite the two-fold occurrence of the B update, BAOAB only requires one gradient evaluation per iteration as the
n
-fold application of the update can be simplified as
(\mathrm{BAOAB})^{n}=\mathrm{BAOA}(\mathrm{BBAOA})^{n-1}\mathrm{B}
, where the two gradient evaluations within BB are at the same
x

[
35
]
.
Further, in
[
33
]
it has been put forward that the BAOAB dynamics converge to

2

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

For completeness, we also shall mention the broad family of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)samplers
[
41

42
]
which
target as well distributions of the form

Each iteration of the HMCalgorithm consists of three distinct steps: First, the velocity/momentum

is sampled from the true target which is simply a Gaussian

\begin{cases}V^{k+\frac{1}{2}}=V^{k}-\frac{{\Delta t}}{2}\nabla U(X^{k}),\\
X^{k+1}=X^{k}+\frac{{\Delta t}}{\theta}V^{k},\\
V^{k+1}=V^{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{{\Delta t}}{2}\nabla U(X^{k+1}).\end{cases}

(12)

Finally, the resulting pair of position and velocity variable is accepted based on an Metropolis-Hastings acceptance criterion
[
42
]
.
Omitting the Metropolis correction leads to the
unadjusted
HMCsampler.
A further generalization replaces the full velocity refresh with the partial update
\bar{O}_{\Delta t}(x,v)=\bigl(x,\,\eta({\Delta t})v+\sqrt{1-\eta({\Delta t})^{2}}\,V\bigr),\,V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\theta}}I_{d})

Notably, choosing

Similar to discretizations of the kinetic Langevin diffusion, HMCyields faster mixing as the simulation of Hamiltonian dynamics leads to larger steps. A main drawback of HMC, however, is the need for delicate parameter finetuning, in particular, the discretization step size and number of iterations within the leap-frog integrator

4

As announced, the first step is to establish that ILAindeed is a discretization of the kinetic Langevin dynamics.

Lemma 2
.

Let
{\Delta t}>0
denote the time discretization,
\varepsilon>0
the friction parameter, and
\theta^{-1}>0
the particle mass. Setting
\tau=\theta{\Delta t}^{2}
,
\beta=1-\varepsilon{\Delta t}
, the update rule of ILAin
algorithm

1
is a discretization of the kinetic Langevin dynamics
eq.

5
.

Proof.

In order to provide more insight, we will not only show the stated result, but also derive how the specific parameter choices for
\sigma
and
\beta
are derived starting from the initial scheme
eq.

4
. Let us introduce the velocity
V^{k}=\tfrac{1}{{\Delta t}}(X^{k}-X^{k-1})
. Then
eq.

4
is equivalent to

\begin{cases}X^{k+1}=X^{k}+\beta{\Delta t}V^{k}-{\Delta t}^{2}{\theta}\nabla U(X^{k})+\sigma N^{k},\\
V^{k+1}=\beta V^{k}-{\Delta t}{\theta}\nabla U(X^{k})+\frac{\sigma}{{\Delta t}}N^{k},\end{cases}

(17)

or, by flipping the order of the updates and inserting
V^{k+1}
,

\begin{cases}V^{k+1}=\beta V^{k}-{\Delta t}{\theta}\nabla U(X^{k})+\frac{\sigma}{{\Delta t}}N^{k},\\
X^{k+1}=X^{k}+{\Delta t}V^{k+1}.\end{cases}

(18)

In order to obtain a diffusion process as
{\Delta t}\rightarrow 0
for the velocity update, we require
\beta-1=\mathcal{O}({\Delta t})
and
\frac{\sigma}{{\Delta t}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{{\Delta t}})
. In particular, to obtain the kinetic Langevin dynamics, we set
\beta=1-\varepsilon{\Delta t}
for some friction parameter
\varepsilon>0
and
\sigma=\sqrt{2\theta\varepsilon{\Delta t}^{3}}
leading to

\begin{cases}V^{k+1}=V^{k}-\varepsilon{\Delta t}V^{k}-{\Delta t}{\theta}\nabla U(X^{k})+\sqrt{2{\theta}\varepsilon{\Delta t}}N^{k},\\
X^{k+1}=X^{k}+{\Delta t}V^{k+1}.\end{cases}

(ILA)

It is apparent that the first line is the EMdiscretization of the velocity dynamics in
eq.

5
and the second line is a discretization of the respective position dynamics utilizing the already updated velocity.

That is, we find that ILAcorresponds to a discretization of
eq.

5
akin to the Gauss-Seidel iteration, inserting already the new velocity
V^{k+1}
into the position update.
For the following analysis, we will rely on the representation
eq.

ILA
instead of the one in
algorithm

1

Two-block Gibbs sampler

Since
p(z)
is normal, its conditionals are normal as well
[
6
, Section 3.2]
.
In particular, the updates in lines
2
and
3
of
algorithm

2
take on the form

\begin{cases}X^{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=Y^{k}],\Sigma_{X|Y}\bigr),\\
Y^{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=X^{k+1}],\Sigma_{Y|X}\bigr),\end{cases}

(40)

where
\Sigma_{X|Y},\Sigma_{Y|X}
denote the respective conditional covariance matrices which are, indeed, constant across iterations
[
6
, Section 3.2.4]
.
As shown in
[
22
]
, Gibbs sampling is deeply connected to matrix splitting methods for solving linear equations.
In particular, basic Gibbs sampling corresponds to a stochastic version of the Gauss-Seidel method
[
57
]
.
It is, therefore, a natural question whether acceleration techniques for solving linear systems can be adapted to Gibbs sampling.
This question has already been answered positively in
[
1
,
22
,
41
]
.
Specifically, we may apply successive over-relaxation (SOR)to the Gibbs sampler, leading to the updates

\begin{cases}X^{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl((1-\omega)X^{k}+\omega\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=Y^{k}],\omega(2-\omega)\Sigma_{X|Y}\bigr),\\
Y^{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl((1-\omega)Y^{k}+\omega\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=X^{k+1}],\omega(2-\omega)\Sigma_{Y|X}\bigr),\end{cases}

(41)

where the parameter
\omega>0
controls the degree of over-relaxation, with larger values typically associated with accelerated convergence.

While the elaboration to this point was restricted to the Gaussian setting, we would like to adapt this acceleration technique to ULA.
Recall that the standard ULAupdate reads as

X^{k+1}=X^{k}-\tau\nabla U(X^{k})+\sqrt{2\tau}N^{k},\quad N^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id).

(42)

In order to obtain an algorithm resembling the structure of the Gibbs sampler, we introduce an auxiliary variable
Y^{k}
and consider the split update

\begin{cases}Y^{k+1}=X^{k}-\tau\nabla U(X^{k})+\sqrt{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}N^{k}_{1},\\
X^{k+1}=Y^{k+1}+\sqrt{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}N^{k}_{2},\end{cases}

(43)

where
(N_{i}^{k})_{i,k}
are i.i.d. standard normal and we require
\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}=\tau
.
It is easy to see that the sequence
(X^{k})_{k}
in
eq.

43
is equal in distribution to the one generated by ULA.
Note that, in general, there may not exist a joint density
p(x,y)
for which
eq.

43
constitutes its Gibbs sampler (
cf.

[
2
]
).
Nonetheless, we ignore this for now and apply the SORover-relaxation to
eq.

43
, leading to the scheme

\begin{cases}Y^{k+1}=(1-\omega)Y^{k}+\omega(X^{k}-\tau\nabla U(X^{k}))+\sqrt{\omega(2-\omega)2\sigma_{1}^{2}}N^{k}_{1},\\
X^{k+1}=(1-\omega)X^{k}+\omega Y^{k+1}+\sqrt{\omega(2-\omega)2\sigma_{2}^{2}}N^{k}_{2}.\end{cases}

(44)

Let us now reduce this scheme to an iteration solely on
X^{k}
again.
Inserting the update rule of
Y^{k}
into the second line yields

\displaystyle X^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\omega)X^{k}+\omega(1-\omega)Y^{k}+\omega^{2}(X^{k}-\tau\nabla U(X^{k}))

(45)

\displaystyle+\omega\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\sigma_{1}^{2}}N_{1}^{k}+\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\sigma_{2}^{2}}N_{2}^{k}.

Additionally, expressing
Y^{k}=\omega^{-1}\bigl(X^{k}-(1-\omega)X^{k-1}-\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\sigma_{2}^{2}}N_{2}^{k-1}\bigr)
in terms of the iterates
X^{k}
,
X^{k-1}
and the old noise
N_{2}^{k-1}
, yields

\displaystyle X^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\omega)X^{k}+(1-\omega)\Bigl(X^{k}-(1-\omega)X^{k-1}-\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\sigma_{2}^{2}}N_{2}^{k-1}\Bigr)

(46)

\displaystyle+\omega^{2}\bigl(X^{k}-\tau\nabla U(X^{k})\bigr)+\omega\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\sigma_{1}^{2}}N_{1}^{k}+\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\sigma_{2}^{2}}N_{2}^{k}.

Now, choosing
\sigma_{2}=0
and
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\tau
and rearranging terms leads us to

\displaystyle X^{k+1}=X^{k}+(1-\omega)^{2}(X^{k}-X^{k-1})-\omega^{2}\tau\nabla U(X^{k})+\omega\sqrt{2\omega(2-\omega)\tau}N_{1}^{k}.

(47)

Introducing a new parameter
\beta=(1-\omega)^{2}
and a new step size variable
\gamma=\omega^{2}\tau
and noting that
\omega(2-\omega)=1-(1-2\omega+\omega^{2})=1-\beta
we arrive at

\displaystyle X^{k+1}=X^{k}+\beta(X^{k}-X^{k-1})-\gamma\nabla U(X^{k})+\sqrt{2\gamma(1-\beta)}N_{1}^{k}

(48)

which is precisely our sampling scheme ILA.
While we want to emphasize once more that this derivation is not theoretically well-grounded, as
eq.

43
is not the Gibbs sampler of a normal distribution as required in
[
22
]
, we nonetheless point out that, perhaps surprisingly, the derivation leads precisely to the required trade-off between inertia and randomness rendering
eq.

48
a discretization of kinetic Langevin dynamics (
cf.

lemma

2
).

Smooth Bivariate Laplace

We consider a smooth approximation of a bivariate Laplace distribution with the associated potential function

U\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{J}\varphi_{\delta}(a_{j}^{\top}x-b_{j}),

(49)

where

As a final experiment, we apply ILAto the challenging task of maximum likelihood learning of a Lipschitz-constrained EBM.
Specifically, we address the problem of
molecular structure generation

[
4
]
, a fundamental problem in computational chemistry and structural biology.
The goal is to predict energetically favorable 3D configurations for a given set of atoms.
EBMsallow for a natural treatment of this problem by directly parameterizing the molecular potential energy surface (PES).
Such energy landscapes are known to be highly multi-modal, with many shallow local minima, and physically valid conformations occupy only a small fraction of the configuration space
[
3
]
.
As EBMtraining via contrastive divergence
[
29
]
crucially depends on the quality of samples drawn from the model, this task serves as a challenging benchmark for the sampler’s ability to traverse complex, multi-modal potential functions.
In the following, we will demonstrate the ability of ILAto improve mixing and convergence in such multi-modal settings, and benchmark it against the predominant ULAalgorithm.

For structure generation we are interested in learning the parameters
\psi
of a conditional Gibbs distribution
\pi(x\mid a)\propto\exp\bigl(-E_{\psi}(x\mid a)\bigr)
,
where
x=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times 3}
denotes the 3D coordinates of the
V
atoms, and
a=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{V}\in\mathcal{V}^{V}
specifies their atomic types drawn from the set of elements
\mathcal{V}
.
The potential energy function that we model using a neural network with parameters
\psi
is denoted via
E_{\psi}(x\mid a)\in\mathbb{R}
.

We parameterize the energy function
E_{\psi}
with a three-layer equivariant graph neural network (EGNN)
[
55
]
with embedding dimension
128
.
The final energy is obtained by summing the per-atom outputs of the last layer.
To better leverage our theoretical results regarding valid momentum parameters, we apply spectral normalization
[
39
]
to all linear layers of the EGNN, effectively upper-bounding the network’s Lipschitz constant with
L\leq 1
.
Conveniently, this addition is also known to increase training stability
[
15
]
.
The training data consists of a subset of the QM9 dataset
[
50
]
, a popular dataset for the molecule generation task.
To reduce the computational workload, we restricted the dataset to molecules of size
V=19
.
The resulting dataset contains
18\,146
molecular structures, which we split into
90\%
training and
10\%
test data.
Bond types are not provided during training, meaning the network only receives atom types and inter-atomic distances as input.
As the primary evaluation criterion, we calculate how well the model captures the chemically plausible bond length distribution.
Therefore, we compute the pairwise distance distribution of atoms
\pi_{r}^{m}
of our model at iteration
m
and compare it to the data distribution.
As we are mainly interested in accurate modeling of bond lengths, we restrict the considered pairwise distances to a range from
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}
and compare to the ground-truth distribution
\pi_{r}
using the Wasserstein-2 distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\pi_{r}^{m},\pi_{r})
.
More details about the training procedure of the EBMcan be found in
appendix

B
.

Figure
8
summarizes the quantitative results for molecular structure generation.
The left plot reports the Wasserstein-2 distance between the bond length distributions of the test set and model samples during training, using a fixed budget of
K=400
Langevin steps.
While both samplers initially improve, ULArapidly saturates at a higher distance value, indicating that its chains fail to mix once the energy landscape becomes increasingly rugged through contrastive divergence updates.
In contrast, ILAmaintains stable exploration, traversing shallow minima and continuing to provide diverse and informative negative samples.
This allows the learned energy function to improve throughout training, yielding substantially lower
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-distances at the same computational cost.

We next analyze how the computational budget influences model quality.
The right plot shows the final model quality over the available computational budget, where each point corresponds to the best checkpoint of an independent run.
Increasing the number of Langevin steps improves both methods, yet ILA exhibits a much steeper initial gain and reaches its asymptotic accuracy after only a fraction of the iterations required by ULA.
With only
100
inner-loop steps, ILAalready matches the final performance of ULA, corresponding to a roughly
16
-fold reduction in computational cost, and converges fully at around
400
steps.
The addition of momentum substantially enhances sampling efficiency, particularly in curvature-constrained energy landscapes, such as in our case, where spectral normalization bounds the gradient norm and thus limits local curvature.
This reduces the need for long inner-loop simulations and effectively mitigates one of the primary computational bottlenecks in maximum-likelihood training of energy-based models.

5
Conclusion

In this article, we introduced ILA, an algorithm for sampling from Gibbs distributions that resembles the heavy ball method from optimization.
Using its relation to kinetic Langevin dynamics, the proposed method was analyzed theoretically, providing proofs of its ergodicity in continuous and discrete time as well as a bound on its asymptotic bias to the target distribution in terms of the step size.
In particular, in our proofs, we obtain improved constants compared to the available literature on discretizations of kinetic Langevin dynamics.
We showed that the proposed method is closely related to the over-relaxed Gibbs sampler.
Lastly, the practical relevance of ILAis confirmed by extensive numerical experiments within and beyond the setting covered by our theory.
In particular, the experiments confirm (i) the acceleration provided by including momentum and (ii) the improved convergence rates for the proposed method in comparison to the literature.

Limitations and future work

There are several potential research avenues we would like to explore.
Firstly, we aim to extend the convergence proofs of ILA(respectively, discretizations of kinetic Langevin dynamics in general) to settings beyond strongly log-concave and
L
-smooth densities (
cf.

[
21
,
56
]
).
Additionally, we would like to analyze a similar scheme that resembles Nesterov’s gradient descent.
A key ingredient in generalizing results will be to rely on Lyapunov drift conditions.
Moreover, we plan to combine ILAwith other sampling strategies such as annealing/tempering.
