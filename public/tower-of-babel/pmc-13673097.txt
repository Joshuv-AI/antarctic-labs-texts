Comments on “Homologous and heterologous booster of COVID-19 vaccines: A systematic review and meta-analysis”
Authors: Farhad Dadgar, Mehdi Rostami
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the systematic review and meta-analysis by Pial et al. comparing homologous and heterologous COVID-19 vaccine booster regimens [1]. This is an important topic, particularly in the context of waning immunity, changing vaccine availability, and the emergence of immune-evasive SARS-CoV-2 variants. The authors suggest that heterologous boosting may offer immunological advantages. However, several methodological issues should be considered when interpreting the clinical applicability of these findings.

First, the included studies were characterized by substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Homologous and heterologous booster strategies are not single interventions, but encompass multiple combinations of priming vaccines, booster platforms, dosing intervals, age groups, and background epidemiological settings [2]. In addition, studies may have assessed immune responses at different post-booster time points and during different variant periods. Pooling such diverse comparisons may produce a summary estimate that is difficult to apply to any specific vaccine sequence or population.

Second, the evidence base appears to rely heavily on immunogenicity outcomes rather than clinical endpoints. Neutralizing antibody titers and cellular immune responses are useful indicators of vaccine-induced immunity, but they do not always translate directly into proportional reductions in infection, hospitalization, or death, particularly when circulating variants change. Therefore, conclusions regarding the superiority of heterologous boosting should be distinguished from evidence of improved clinical protection [3].

Third, prior SARS-CoV-2 infection is an important potential confounder. Previous infection substantially modifies baseline immunity and post-booster immune responses [4]. If included studies differed in how they excluded, measured, or stratified participants by prior infection status, pooled estimates may partly reflect differences in baseline serostatus rather than the independent effect of homologous or heterologous boosting. This issue is particularly relevant for observational studies, where individuals receiving different booster regimens may also differ by age, comorbidity, exposure risk, and access to vaccines.

Fourth, follow-up duration is critical. Many booster studies report immune responses shortly after vaccination, often within weeks [5]. However, the durability of any early immunological advantage of heterologous boosting is uncertain. A higher peak antibody response may not necessarily imply sustained superiority over several months, nor does it fully characterize protection against severe disease. Longer follow-up is needed to determine whether differences between booster strategies persist and remain clinically meaningful.

Finally, the broad category of “heterologous boosting” may obscure important differences between specific vaccine combinations. For example, viral-vector-to-mRNA, inactivated-to-mRNA, mRNA-to-mRNA, and protein-subunit-based booster strategies may have different immunogenicity, safety, and practical implications. A single pooled comparison of heterologous versus homologous boosting may therefore lose clinically relevant granularity. Subgroup analyses or network meta-analytic approaches may better inform decisions about specific booster sequences.

In summary, the review by Pial et al. addresses an important question and provides a useful overview of the available evidence. However, heterogeneity in vaccine platforms, booster combinations, dosing intervals, variant periods, prior infection status, and outcome definitions limits the interpretation of a single pooled estimate. While heterologous boosting may generate higher short-term immune responses in some settings, its comparative clinical benefit, durability, and applicability to specific vaccine sequences require cautious interpretation. Future evidence syntheses would be strengthened by separating immunogenicity from clinical outcomes, stratifying by prior infection and variant period, and evaluating specific booster combinations rather than treating heterologous boosting as a uniform strategy.
