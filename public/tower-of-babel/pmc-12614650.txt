Rationale for preoperative planning in the supine position based on variability in the joint line convergence angle before and after open-wedge high tibial osteotomy
Authors: Sayako Sakai, Shinichi Kuriyama, Takahiro Maeda, Yusuke Yamawaki, Yugo Morita, Kohei Nishitani, Shinichiro Nakamura, Shuichi Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsIn advanced medial knee osteoarthritis, lower limb alignment is assessed using weightbearing imaging, which reflects the joint line convergence angle (JLCA). However, successful lateral load transfer with open-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) can reduce the JLCA by decreasing the lateral and increasing the medial joint space on weightbearing imaging, making the correction less predictable. This study aimed to demonstrate that OWHTO planning using preoperative supine imaging yields more reproducible correction than weightbearing imaging and to identify radiological predictors of excessive JLCA change (> 2°).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 115 knees of 96 patients who underwent OWHTO planned using supine imaging. Radiological parameters, including the hip-knee-ankle angle (HKAA) and arithmetic HKAA, were measured using preoperative and postoperative long-leg weightbearing radiographs. Knees were categorized into three groups by ΔJLCA: ΔJLCA < ˗2° (decreased); ˗2° ≤ ΔJLCA ≤ 2° (unchanged), and ΔJLCA > 2° (increased). Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were used to determine radiological factors associated with ΔJLCA.ResultsThe unchanged JLCA group comprised 84.3% of knees, with similar preoperative weightbearing and supine JLCAs (2.6° and 2.5°, respectively). The postoperative weightbearing JLCA was 2.5°. The decreased JLCA group included 11.3% of knees, and the postoperative weightbearing JLCA (mean 2.6°) was closer to the preoperative supine (mean 4.1°) than the weightbearing JLCA (mean 5.5°). Preoperative arithmetic HKAAs were identical in the unchanged and decreased JLCA groups (3.9° varus). The increased JLCA group comprised only 4.3% of knees. Preoperative weightbearing JLCA > 4.9° and varus HKAA > 8.8° were significantly associated with decreased JLCA, with areas under the curve of 0.86 and 0.72, respectively.ConclusionPostoperative weightbearing alignment following OWHTO is better predicted by preoperative supine than weightbearing imaging. Furthermore, large preoperative weightbearing JLCA (> 4.9°) and severe varus HKAA (> 8.8°) are associated with decreased postoperative JLCA.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(11):1446–1455.Keywords: Open-wedge high tibial osteotomy, Preoperative planning, Supine position, Joint line convergence angle, Arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle, Radiographic factor, open-wedge high tibial osteotomy, Knees, Hip-knee-ankle angle, radiographs, joint space, logistic regression analysis, medial knee osteoarthritis, limb alignment, Medial proximal tibial angle, WBLR

Introduction

Achieving proper coronal alignment correction during open-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) is crucial for favourable postoperative outcomes.1-7 However, even when the bony alignment is precisely corrected as planned preoperatively, individual variability in soft-tissue laxity makes the correction less predictable. The joint line convergence angle (JLCA) reflects mediolateral soft-tissue laxity around the knee joint. Measuring lower-limb alignment using weightbearing imaging incorporates the large JLCA commonly observed in advanced medial knee osteoarthritis (OA).8-11 Successful lateral load transfer following OWHTO can reduce the JLCA by increasing the medial and decreasing the lateral joint space on weightbearing imaging,12-14 potentially resulting in unexpected overcorrection.

Ogata et al15 confirmed the rationale for planning closed-wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO) using preoperative supine imaging, focusing on the ‘condylar-plateau angle’, now referred to as JLCA. Reportedly, the preoperative condylar-plateau angle was larger on a standing radiograph than on the supine radiograph, and postoperative condylar-plateau angle on a standing radiograph was similar to the preoperative angle measured in the supine position. Furthermore, the larger preoperative condylar-plateau angle on the standing radiograph exhibited a greater decrease postoperatively. However, the reproducibility of correction and the factors predicting the extent of JLCA change (ΔJLCA) when OWHTO is planned using supine imaging remain unclear.

This study aimed to demonstrate that OWHTO planning using preoperative supine imaging yields more reproducible correction than weightbearing imaging, with a focus on ΔJLCA, and to identify preoperative radiological predictors of excessive ΔJLCA following OWHTO planned on supine imaging. We hypothesized that OWHTO planned using preoperative supine imaging would result in reproducible correction without excessive ΔJLCA and that an excessive ΔJLCA due to OWHTO would be more likely to occur in knees with greater varus alignment with a larger preoperative JLCA.

Methods

Patients

Following approval from the ethics committee of Kyoto University, Japan (approval no: R0987), 124 consecutive knees of 105 patients with medial knee OA or spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee who underwent OWHTO between September 2014 and April 2022 were retrospectively enrolled. The specific indications for OWHTO included the absence of lateral tibiofemoral or patellofemoral joint symptoms, aged ≤ 80 years with a relatively high activity level, and a flexion contracture of < 10°. No patients were excluded because of obesity. Nine knees were excluded because of previous ligament reconstruction (one knee), post-traumatic knee OA (five knees), and unavailable postoperative radiographs (three knees). Additional procedures for articular cartilage and meniscus surgeries were not considered reasons for exclusion. Finally, 115 knees of 96 patients met the inclusion criteria (Table I).

Preoperative planning

3D planning was performed using preoperative CT scans of the entire affected limb in the supine position, as previously described,16 using a planning software (3D Template; Kyocera, Japan) (Figure 1). This software enables free delineation of planes (such as the osteotomy plane) on multiplanar reconstruction CT images with arbitrary axial settings. The correction angle and opening width were adjusted to align the mechanical axis to a point 60.0% to 62.5% from the medial edge of the tibial plateau on the coronal plane, following the Miniaci method.17,18 This ‘planned correction angle’ was applied as a preoperative parameter in the analysis. The ‘actual correction angle’ was obtained postoperatively from surgical records to assess its concordance with the planned angle.

Images used for preoperative 3D planning showing the planned correction angle (arrows). The hinge line was set parallel to the posterior tibial slope on the sagittal plane: A) 15 mm distal to the lateral tibial plateau; and B) 5 mm medial to the lateral tibial cortex. C) The osteotomy surface was defined as starting from the medial tibial cortex 35 mm distal to the medial tibial plateau.

Surgical techniques

Biplane OWHTO was performed using 3D planning with fluoroscopic assistance. The anterior portion of the proximal attachment of the superficial medial collateral ligament was minimally dissected to open the gap, and the pes anserinus was preserved. The osteotomy gap was filled with two β-tricalcium phosphate wedge spacers (Osferion60, Olympus Terumo Biomaterials, Japan) and was fixed using a titanium locking plate (TomoFix; DePuy Synthes, USA) or TriS plate (Olympus Terumo Biomaterials, Japan). Range of motion exercises and partial weightbearing were allowed one-week postoperatively, and full weightbearing was permitted at three weeks.

Radiological parameters

A long-leg weightbearing anteroposterior radiograph was used to measure the radiological parameters preoperatively and after four weeks to within one-year postoperatively (Figure 2). Combined bone- and soft-tissue-related parameters included the weightbearing line ratio (WBLR) and hip-knee-ankle angle (HKAA; positive values representing valgus alignment). Bone-related parameters included the medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) and mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (mLDFA). The soft-tissue-related parameter was JLCA (weightbearing JLCA). A positive JLCA value represented the medial apex, indicating wider lateral joint space than the medial joint space. JLCA was also measured using non-weightbearing knee anteroposterior radiographs (supine JLCA). In addition, the arithmetic HKAA (aHKAA) was calculated according to the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee classification: ‘aHKAA = MPTA ˗ mLDFA’.19-23 The aHKAA enables the evaluation of bony alignment without exaggeration of varus alignment due to an increased JLCA associated with OA progression.20 Positive aHKAA values reflect valgus alignment. Finally, the preoperative to postoperative change in weightbearing JLCA was calculated: ‘ΔJLCA = postoperative-operative JLCA ˗ preoperative JLCA’, with a positive value indicating the larger postoperative than preoperative JLCA. The intra- and inter-rater reliabilities were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) by two authors (SS, SK) with a six-month interval between measurements.

Pre- and postoperative radiological measurements using a long-leg weightbearing anteroposterior radiograph. a) Weightbearing line ratio was the percentage of the intersection of the weightbearing line and the tibial plateau (the medial and lateral edges were defined as 0% and 100%, respectively). b) Hip-knee-ankle angle was defined as the acute angle between the femoral and tibial mechanical axes. A positive value represents a valgus alignment. c) Medial proximal tibial angle was defined as the medial angle between the tibial mechanical axis and a line tangent to the tibial plateau. Mechanical lateral distal femoral angle was defined as the lateral angle between the femoral mechanical axis and a line tangent to the distal femur. d) Joint line convergence angle was defined as the angle between the line tangent to the articular surface of the distal femur and a line tangent to the articular surface of the proximal tibia. A positive value represents a medial apex, implying that the lateral joint space is wider than the medial joint space.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical outcome was routinely assessed preoperatively and one-year postoperatively using the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS),24 starting from the case operated on 22 May 2018. The total score and the scores of the five subscales — symptoms, pain, activities of daily living, sports and recreational activities, and quality of life — were evaluated.

Data analysis

As previous studies reported a mean absolute ΔJLCA within 2°,8,14,25,26 knees were classified into three groups according to ΔJLCA: the decreased JLCA group (ΔJLCA < ˗2°), unchanged JLCA group (˗2° ≤ ΔJLCA ≤ 2°), and increased JLCA group (ΔJLCA > 2°). We investigated the associations between patient demographic details, preoperative and postoperative radiological parameters, and ΔJLCA by group. Preoperative and postoperative KOOS were also evaluated for each group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software v. 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Normality and homoscedasticity were examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Differences between preoperative and postoperative values were compared using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Differences between the decreased and unchanged JLCA groups were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Preoperative parameters with a significant difference between the decreased and unchanged JLCA groups were included in binary logistic regression analysis to determine factors associated with ΔJLCA < ˗2°. The area under the curve (AUC) was determined using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the optimal cutoff values were selected using the Youden index. Statistical significance level was set at 0.05. A priori sample size estimation was performed to detect a meaningful difference between the two groups as previously described,9 using G*power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany), with an α error of 0.05 and power (1 ˗ β) of 0.8. The required sample size was nine and 65 knees in the decreased and unchanged JLCA groups, respectively.

Discussion

This study clarified the rationale for non-weightbearing planning of OWHTO, with a focus on ΔJLCA, and identified preoperative radiological predictors of ΔJLCA following OWHTO planned based on preoperative supine imaging. Weightbearing planning results in 10% overcorrection of WBLR following OWHTO compared with non-weightbearing planning.10 A small preoperative WBLR is identified as a risk factor for discrepancies in planned correction angle between planning using weightbearing and non-weightbearing imaging.11 However, predicting JLCA changes during preoperative planning remains a challenge.4

Using non-weightbearing planning, 84.3% of knees (97 knees) were included in the unchanged JLCA group. Preoperative weightbearing or supine JLCAs in the unchanged JLCA group were similar (2.6° and 2.5°, respectively) (Table III, Figure 4), reflecting that planning using either position is acceptable. The mean postoperative WBLR was 57.0%, with a target coronal alignment of approximately 60%. A 1° valgus correction corresponds to a 4% lateral shift in WBLR.18 Therefore, the correction error in the unchanged JLCA group in this study was maintained at approximately 1° using non-weightbearing planning.

The decreased JLCA group comprised 11.3% of all the knees (13 knees). Preoperative HKAA was more varus in the decreased JLCA group than in the unchanged JLCA group, likely due to larger JLCA rather than bony morphology, as preoperative aHKAAs were identical at ˗3.9° for both groups and preoperative weightbearing JLCAs were 5.5° and 2.6° in the decreased and unchanged JLCA groups, respectively (Table III). Medial knee OA results in JLCA of ≥ 4°9,11,27,28 because of lateral soft-tissue elongation29 and medial articular cartilage wear.30,31 In the decreased JLCA group, preoperative weightbearing JLCA was reduced in the supine position and further decreased postoperatively under weightbearing position (Figure 4). This may be attributed to a persistently large preoperative lateral joint space that remained evident even in the supine position.

In the decreased JLCA group, the preoperative large weightbearing JLCA was reduced postoperatively to approximately 2.5°, the same level observed postoperatively in the unchanged JLCA group (Figure 4). Both groups also showed comparable postoperative HKAAs (Table IV). Therefore, the postoperative weightbearing JLCA averaged approximately 2.5° in 95.4% of the study population (110 knees). In the unchanged JLCA group, postoperative weightbearing JLCA, as well as preoperative weightbearing and supine JLCAs, clustered around 2.5°. The finding that the mean postoperative weightbearing JLCA remained at 2.5° following OWHTO should be considered during the preoperative planning. Postoperative weightbearing JLCA between 1.5° and 3.9° following OWHTO reduces the risk for OA progression of both operated and non-operated knees.32 Non-weightbearing planning may offer a more reproducible correction by minimizing ΔJLCA and achieving appropriate postoperative JLCA. In the present study, OWHTO was planned using preoperative supine CT images rather than supine radiographs. A previous study reported an equivalent coronal correction angle using either supine CT images or radiographs.16 Therefore, our results might be applicable to OWHTO planned using a supine radiograph.

This study also identified preoperative radiological predictors for ΔJLCA < ˗2°. Knees with a large weightbearing JLCA > 4.9° as well as a small HKAA < ˗8.8° exhibited a change in weightbearing JLCA of over 2° in the valgus direction. Na et al8 showed that a preoperative JLCA of at least 4.0° in the standing position was the cutoff for predicting ΔJLCA < ˗2°, which supports our results. Meanwhile, preoperative bony factors, including aHKAA, were not associated with ΔJLCA < ˗2° in this study.

Five knees (4.3%) were included in the increased JLCA group in this study. These cases had an almost parallel weightbearing JLCA of 0.7° and milder varus WBLR and HKAA than the other two groups preoperatively, although their preoperative aHKAA did not differ significantly (Table III). In contrast, the preoperative JLCA indicated more lateral joint openings in the supine position than in the weightbearing position. This may be attributed to residual medial articular cartilage and meniscal stuffing in the medial joint space, as well as a medial-lateral tissue flexibility similar to that of normal knees with a JLCA of 0° to 2°.13,25,27,30 The elevated medial joint line without preoperative medial laxity might have increased JLCA postoperatively. Even in this group, postoperative weightbearing JLCA was closer to the preoperative supine than the weightbearing JLCA (Figure 4). Therefore, in knees with slight or moderate deformities, planning OWHTO in the supine position is still effective for reproducible correction. However, the increased JLCA group exhibited slight postoperative overcorrection despite non-weightbearing planning (Table IV), although the error was approximately 1° (4%) from the target alignment of around 60%. Therefore, excessive correction should be avoided for relatively mild osteoarthritic knees.

Although the number of KOOS assessments was insufficient for statistical analysis, relatively high scores were observed in the decreased JLCA group, specifically in the preoperative and postoperative symptoms and pain subscales (Table VI). This is consistent with a previous finding that a decrease of > 1° in JLCA following OWHTO was associated with better knee function and reduced pain.33

The present study also has some limitations. First, this study was based on radiological values for a target postoperative WBLR of 60% to 62.5% using preoperative non-weightbearing planning. Therefore, our results may not be applicable to OWHTO using weightbearing planning. Second, stress radiography was not performed, and actual soft-tissue laxity was not measured in this study. We only examined the 2D coronal plane and did not capture 3D alignment changes. Third, knees with JLCA > 2° were excluded from the analyses, and the factors associated with JLCA > 2° were not determined. However, only five of the 115 knees demonstrated an increase in JLCA > 2°, and in most of these, weightbearing JLCA remained unchanged following OWHTO. Fourth, the coronal alignment was measured using radiographs, and the results may have been influenced by measurement errors, such as rotational differences. Lastly, clinical outcomes of a limited number of patients were not assessed statistically.

In conclusion, postoperative weightbearing alignment following OWHTO incorporating JLCA is more accurately predicted using preoperative supine imaging than weightbearing imaging. Planning OWHTO using preoperative supine imaging provides reproducible correction in most OWHTO cases. A greater preoperative weightbearing JLCA (> 4.9°) and severe varus HKAA (> 8.8°) are associated with decreased JLCA following OWHTO planned using preoperative supine imaging.

Take home message

- Preoperative non-weightbearing planning predicts postoperative weightbearing alignment following open-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) more accurately than weightbearing planning for the majority of patients, because the postoperative joint line convergence angle (JLCA) in the weight-bearing position, which was around 2.5°, was closer to the preoperative JLCA in the supine position than that in the weightbearing position.

- A greater preoperative weightbearing JLCA (> 4.9°) and severe varus hip-knee-ankle angle (> 8.8°) are associated with decreased JLCA following OWHTO planned using preoperative supine imaging.
