Genomic resources to advance seed coat color and patterning genetics and breeding in common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.)
Authors: Jayanta Roy, Rian Lee, Caroline Osborne, Phillip N Miklas, Juan M Osorno, Phillip E McClean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeed coat color and patterning are key quality traits in common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) that define market classes and strongly influence consumer preference and market value. These traits are controlled by a complex network of major genes (sometimes with epistatic interactions), which complicates the recovery of desired market class phenotypes following inter‐market class hybridization. Although many of the underlying loci have been genetically mapped, diagnostic, high‐throughput molecular markers for efficient allele tracking across the Middle American and Andean gene pools remain limited. In this study, we developed and validated 24 gene‐specific PCR Allele Competitive Extension (PACE) markers targeting seven major seed coat color genes (G, B, V, J, Rk, T, and Z) and two patterning genes (CPi and CSt ), together with a previously reported marker associated with the postharvest seed coat darkening locus (Psd ). An additional PACE marker targeting the Phaseolin (Phs) locus was developed to distinguish Middle American (S‐type) and Andean (T‐type) gene pools, providing a complementary tool for assessing genetic background alongside seed coat‐specific loci. Marker performance was evaluated across three diverse panels, revealing high diagnostic accuracy for most loci (90%–100%). However, for loci such as J, V, Rk, T, and Z, allele‐specific markers or marker combinations were required to capture full allelic diversity. Haplotype analysis further revealed substantial allelic diversity across market classes and identified background‐specific interactions. Collectively, these results provide a comprehensive set of high‐resolution, gene‐anchored PACE markers for seed coat color, patterning, and gene pool classification in common bean. These markers enable rapid and precise allele tracking in breeding populations and germplasm collections, facilitating marker‐assisted selection for market class‐specific seed coat traits and accelerating genetic improvement.

INTRODUCTION

Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is the most important legume species for direct human consumption worldwide. Bean seeds are rich in many essential nutrients and represent a major dietary protein and micronutrient source in many countries across Africa and Latin America (Broughton et al., 2003; Uebersax et al., 2022). Beyond their use as mature dry seeds, common beans are also consumed as vegetables, with their immature seeds and succulent pods harvested and marketed as snap beans. Based on domestication history and population structure, common bean is divided into two major gene pools, Middle American and Andean (Mamidi et al., 2011). These gene pools are differentiated by morphological traits, agronomic adaptation, and biochemical markers such as phaseolin, the primary seed storage protein (Gepts & Bliss, 1986). Each gene pool is further subdivided into distinct races, and within each race, multiple market classes are defined by characteristic seed size, color, pattern, and agronomic traits (Singh, 1999). The Middle American gene pool includes the races Durango/Jalisco, Mesoamerica, and Guatemala, whereas the Andean gene pool is represented by the races Nueva Granada, Peru, and Chile. The pinto, medium red, great northern (GN), and pink bean market classes are grouped within races Durango/Jalisco, while black and navy beans belong to race Mesoamerica, and climbing bean types are associated with race Guatemala. In the Andean gene pool, kidney, bush cranberry, and most snap beans belong to race Nueva Granada. Race Peru includes climbing and yellow‐seeded bean types such as Mayocoba and Canario, while race Chile encompasses vine‐type cranberry beans and several localized market classes such as Coscorron and Tortola, which are predominantly cultivated in Chile (Kelly, 2010).

The extensive diversity of common bean seed types makes this species unique among major food legumes. Seed coat colors range from white, yellow, tan, brown, red, purple, and black to numerous intermediate shades, accompanied by variation in seed size and shape. In addition to solid colors, seeds of many market classes exhibit distinctive patterns such as mottling (speckles or spots), striping, and localized color variation around the hilum. Seed coat color and pattern, together with seed size and shape, are key traits that define distinct market classes and strongly influence consumer acceptance and regional preference. These preferences vary widely across continents and countries, which makes breeding efforts and progress even more challenging (Beaver & Osorno, 2009). Even in some cases, preferred market classes vary among regions within the same country and it is closely tied to their local history and culture (Singh, 1999). For example, pinto is the leading market class in the United States, followed by black and navy beans (Davis & Weber, 2026). In Brazil, multiple market classes, including carioca, cream, and black beans, dominate production depending on regional demand (Blair et al., 2013). In Mexico, medium‐sized light‐colored beans (e.g., bayos, pintos, and flor de mayo) and small black beans are preferred, depending on geographic region and consumer choices. In Africa, Asia, and Europe, large‐seeded beans with diverse colors and patterns, excluding black beans, are more popular (Singh, 1999). Given this diversity, dry bean breeders must develop cultivars that not only deliver improved agronomic performance but also maintain the highly specific seed coat color and pattern phenotypes required for each preferred market class within each region. Therefore, a detailed understanding of seed coat color and patterning genetics and genomics is critical for improving breeding efficiency, particularly when introgressing valuable traits from outside the target market class. Consequently, improved knowledge of the genetic control of seed coat traits, together with diagnostic molecular markers, can enhance selection accuracy and accelerate recovery of desired market class appearance in segregating populations (M. J. Bassett, 2007).

Classical and molecular studies have identified a suite of major color genes (P, C, R, J, G, B, V, and Rk) and patterning genes (T, Z, J, Bip, and Fib) that collectively determine the remarkable diversity of seed coat phenotypes. A comprehensive overview of common bean seed coat color and pattern genetics by M. J. Bassett (2007) reviews the inheritance and complex interactions among these loci. The master regulator of seed coat color expression is the P (=Pigment; Emerson, 1909) gene, and the dominant allele is required for the expression of any color (McClean et al., 2018). In the presence of the “ground factor” P gene and the two basic color genes C and J, a series of color‐modifying genes, including G, B, V, and Rk, interact to produce a wide spectrum of seed coat colors. The C locus is tightly linked (almost unbreakable) to the dominant R gene, which conditions oxblood red seed coats, and is known to carry a series of linked genes forming the so‐called “complex C locus.” Importantly, this complex includes independent subloci (M. J. Bassett, 2007; Prakken, 1977) that control the dominant cranberry striping (CSt ) (M. J. Bassett, 2007; Prakken, 1974) and dominant pinto pattern (CPi ) (Prakken, 1977). Three color‐modifying genes, G (Lundberg & Åkerman, 1917), B (Johannsen, 1909), and V (Johannsen, 1909), interact with C and J in various allelic combinations to generate seed coat colors ranging from yellow to black (Lamprecht, 1932; Prakken, 1970). Seed coat phenotypes in common bean can generally be classified as either solid‐colored or partly colored, where color is restricted to a specific region of the seed coat and the remainder remains white (M. J. Bassett, 2007; McClean et al., 2024). Genotypes carrying dominant P, T, and C alleles typically exhibit solid coloration, while all partially colored types require the homozygous recessive tt genotype at the T (=Total) locus. The extent and distribution of pigmentation in partly colored seed coat is controlled by interactions between tt and alleles at additional loci, including Z (=Zonal; Tschermak, 1912), Bip (=Bipunctata; Lamprecht, 1932), J (=Joker; Lamprecht, 1932), and Fib (=Fibula; M. J. Bassett, 2001) genes (McClean et al., 2024).

Seed coat color in common bean is largely determined by flavonoid composition and concentration, which vary markedly among market classes (Lin et al., 2008; Rodríguez Madrera et al., 2020), and several seed coat color genes encode regulators or enzymes within the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway (McClean et al., 2022; Roy, Sreedasyam, et al., 2025), linking seed color to nutritional properties and consumer preference (Beninger & Hosfield, 2003). Most seed coat color and patterning genes have been mapped in the common bean genome using bi‐parental linkage mapping and genome‐wide association studies (GWAS). To date, only the P gene has been cloned, although strong candidate genes have been identified for several other major loci. The P gene, orthologous to Arabidopsis TT8, encodes a bHLH transcription factor and is located on chromosome Pv07 (gene model: Phvul.007G171333) (McClean et al., 2018). An alternative recessive allele of this P locus, denoted as Psd , controls the slow‐darkening (SD) trait, which reduces postharvest seed coat darkening (Islam et al., 2020; Junk‐Knievel et al., 2008). The V (=Violet; “violet factor”) gene on Pv06 (gene model: Phvul.006G018800) exhibits pleiotropic effects on both flower and seed color (McClean et al., 2022). The B (=Bronze; Lamprecht, 1932; greenish‑brown factor) locus maps to Pv02 (McClean, 2002; Osborne et al., 2026), while the G (=Gelb‐braun; Lamprecht, 1932; “yellow‐brown factor”) locus maps to Pv04 (McClean, 2002). Another seed coat color‐modifying locus, Rk (=Red kidney; Smith, 1939), located on Pv02 (gene model: PvUI111.02G023800), contributes light red tinges to the seed coat (M. J. Bassett & Miklas, 2003; Osborne et al., 2026). The complex C locus on Pv08 contains multiple tightly linked patterning genes, including CSt , CPi , and the closely linked Gy gene controlling greenish‑yellow seed coat color (M. J. Bassett, 2007; McClean, 2002; Osborne et al., 2026; Roy, Oladzad, et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2019). Additional loci influencing partly colored patterns include T (Pv09; gene model: Pv5‐593.09G047300) (Brady et al., 1998; McClean et al., 2024; Parker et al., 2024), Z (Pv03; Pv5‐593.03G127600) (McClean et al., 2024; Parker et al., 2024), J (Pv10; Phvul.010G130600) (Erfatpour & Pauls, 2020), Bip (Pv10) (McClean, 2002; Parker et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2019), and Fib (unmapped).

Breeding cultivars with improved disease resistance, yield, seed quality, plant architecture, and nutritional quality often requires introgression of favorable alleles from donor parents outside the target market class or even from different gene pools and races. Such hybridization results in segregating populations with extensive variation in seed coat color and pattern due to their complex inheritance. As a result, breeders restrict their crosses mostly within races or market classes to preserve commercially desirable seed traits. Consequently, selection of breeding lines with preferred market class seed types is difficult but can be improved with reliable molecular markers. Despite decades of genetic mapping, the practical application of gene‐based markers in breeding remains constrained by the limited availability of high‑throughput, diagnostic markers suitable for routine selection. Early gel‑based markers such as Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA (RAPD) and Sequence‐Tagged Site (STS) were valuable but lacked robustness and scalability. In contrast, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‑based platforms such as PCR Allele Competitive Extension (PACE) offer high accuracy, throughput, and cost‑effectiveness, making them well‐suited for modern breeding programs. The increasing availability of resequencing data and high‑quality reference genomes provides new opportunities to develop allele‑specific markers tightly linked to key seed coat traits.

The objective of this study was to develop user‐friendly, diagnostic PACE markers for major seed coat color and patterning loci and to evaluate their allelic distributions across a broad collection of genotypes representing diverse market classes, including genetic stocks, the Middle American Diversity Panel (MDP), and the Andean Diversity Panel (ADP).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

Seed stocks designated with a PI (Plant Introduction) number, carrying specific alleles for flower color, seed coat color, and patterning used for the genetic component, were obtained from the USDA National Plant Germplasm System (USDA‐NPGS; https://www.ars‐grin.gov/Collections#plant‐germplasm). These genetic stocks represent a large set of backcross introgression lines developed by Dr. Mark J. Bassett, University of Florida, for classical genetic analysis of seed coat color and patterning in common bean (Table S1). The introgression lines were developed using 5‐593 (PI 608674) as the recurrent parent and donor parents carrying specific alleles that affect flower color and seed coat traits. Genotype 5‐593 is a black‐seeded, purple‐flowered line, selected from a genetic stock originally developed by Dr. Jim Beaver (University of Puerto Rico). This genotype is dominant for nearly all known seed coat color and pattern genes but two of the genes. The seed coat color and pattern genotype of 5‐593 is T Z Bip Fib P [C r] Gy J G B V Rk sal (M. J. Bassett, 2007; McClean et al., 2024). Numerous recessive alleles governing seed coat color and pattern genes were introgressed into 5‐593 genetic background through repeated backcrossing to a level of BC3. In this study, we included 53 backcross introgression lines, each carrying specific recessive alleles with known genotypic combinations (see M. J. Bassett [2007] for detailed genetic descriptions). Historically, these lines were used as genetic testers in crosses with stocks of unknown genotypes to determine allelic states at major color and pattern loci. In addition, we included 18 donor parents used to develop these introgression lines. Throughout this study, the combined set of 71 genotypes (53 introgression lines and 18 donor parents) is collectively referred to as Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines (Table S1).

Two additional diversity panels, representing a wide collection of genotypes from the Middle American and Andean gene pools, were also included in this study. The MDP ([Moghaddam et al., 2016]; n = 315) comprises multiple market classes, including pinto (n = 104), black (n = 45), small red (n = 33), pink (n = 24), GN (n = 50), navy (n = 46), and others (n = 13) (Figure 1; Table S2). The ADP ([Cichy et al., 2015]; n = 278) includes genotypes from several important market classes such as light red kidney (LRK; n = 35), dark red kidney (DRK; n = 20), cranberry (n = 40), yellow (n = 30), red (n = 42), purple speckled (n = 32), red mottled (n = 18), and others (n = 62) (Figure 2; Table S3). Together, these panels capture extensive phenotypic and genetic diversity across both major gene pools and provide a robust framework for evaluating allelic variation at seed coat color and patterning loci.

Sequencing, read alignment, and marker development strategies

A total of 39 selected genotypes that primarily include Bassett's genetic stocks were whole‐genome resequenced at an average depth ranging from 11x to 63x (McClean et al., 2024; Osborne et al., 2026). Sequencing was performed using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 paired‐end technology (2 bp× 150 bp) at the HudsonAlpha Institute for Biotechnology. High‐quality reads were aligned to the P. vulgaris G19833 v2.1 (Schmutz et al., 2014; https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Pvulgaris_v2_1), UI111 v1.1 (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/info/PvulgarisUI111_v1_1), and 5‐593 v1.1 (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Pvulgaris5_593_v1_1) reference genomes using BWA‐MEM with default parameters (Li, 2013). The resulting alignment files were sorted and indexed using SAMtools v1.15.1 (Li et al., 2009), and duplicate reads were filtered using Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard).

We employed several complementary strategies to develop markers linked to seed coat color and patterning genes. First, we compiled information from previously identified loci and cloned candidate genes associated with specific seed coat color and pattern traits from published literature. Details on traits, chromosomes, physical position, and candidate genes are summarized in Table 1. Second, introgression lines carrying specific recessive alleles, along with corresponding donor parents, were selected for comparative analysis. Genotype 5‐593, which carries dominant alleles for most color and patterning genes, served as a dominant allele reference when appropriate. Importantly, a high‐quality reference genome for 5‐593 is publicly available (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Pvulgaris5_593_v1_1). Third, whole‐genome resequenced genotypes representing contrasting allelic states (dominant vs. recessive) were examined to identify polymorphisms. Fourth, the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV; Robinson et al., 2011) was used to visualize and identify sequence variation, including SNPs and INDELs (insertions and deletions), between genotypes with contrasting allelic states within the relevant genomic interval and candidate gene regions.

Seed coat color and pattern gene‐specific PACE marker development

BAM (binary alignment map) files from genotypes carrying dominant and recessive alleles were examined in IGV to identify the putative mutations within the genomic regions corresponding to specific seed coat color and patterning genes. To develop robust PACE markers, priority was given to missense (non‐synonymous) SNPs and INDELs, as these variants are more likely to have functional consequences and therefore serve as reliable diagnostic markers. For each selected polymorphism, allele‐specific forward primers and a common reverse primer were designed by the 3CR Bioscience (https://3crbio.com/) primer development service. Primer sequences and additional details for all developed PACE markers used in the study are provided in Table S4.

Genotyping reactions were performed in a total volume of 8 µL, consisting of 2 µL genomic DNA (approximately 10 ng/uL), 0.15 µL of primer mix (12 µM of each allele‐specific forward primer and 30 µM of the common reverse primer), 4 µL of 3CR Bioscience PACE Master Mix Standard Rox, and 1.85 µL H2O. PCR amplification was conducted using the following cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 94°C for 15 min, 10 touchdown cycles at 94°C for 20 s and 65°C for 1 min with a 0.8°C reduction per cycle, followed by 40 amplification cycles at 94°C for 20 s and 57°C for 1 min. Endpoint fluorescence was measured after PCR using a CFX Opus 96 Real‐time PCR System (Bio‐Rad, Inc.). Allele calling was performed using CFX Maestro v2.3 software, which distinguishes homozygous and heterozygous genotypes by clustering fluorescence intensities for the two allele‑specific dyes.

Nomenclature and design of PACE markers

PACE markers were named using a standardized format designed to encode essential genomic and allelic information, ensuring clarity, reproducibility, and ease of interpretation across breeding and genomics applications. Each marker name includes the gene symbol, marker type, variant class, source genotype, chromosome, physical position, and reference genome. The naming structure is as follows: Gene–PACE–VariantType–SourceGenotype–Chr–Position‐ReferenceGenome, where Gene: gene symbol associated with the targeted allele (dominant or recessive); PACE: indicates the marker was developed using the PACE genotyping platform; VariantType: SNP or INDEL (INDELs include size and type [e.g., 39bp‑del]); SourceGenotype: genotype from which the target allele was identified; Chr: chromosome number; Position: physical coordinate (bp) of the variant; ReferenceGenome: genome assembly used to determine the physical position (e.g., G19833v2, UI111v1, and 5‑593v1).

Let us take the example of t‑PACE‑39bp‑del‑Earliwax‑09‑10713827‐5‑593. This marker targets the recessive t allele, is based on a 39‑bp deletion identified in Earliwax genotype, and is located on chromosome Pv09 at 10,713,827 bp in the 5‑593 v1.1 reference genome.

Pinto pattern (CPi ) gene

The CPi gene was mapped to chromosome Pv08 by genome‐wide association analysis, and the gene model PvUI111.08G040900 was identified within the peak GWAS interval (Roy, Oladzad, et al., 2025). A PACE marker for the CPi locus was developed using a non‑synonymous SNP located at Pv08:3,360,423 bp within this gene model (Table 2 and Table S4).

Striping pattern (CSt ) gene

Two missense SNPs located at Pv08:3,258,164 bp and Pv08:3,258,731 bp were identified within the gene model PvUI111.08G040100 for the cranberry striping (CSt ) locus, based on GWAS peak intervals reported by Roy, Oladzad, et al. (2025). Both SNPs were converted into PACE markers and evaluated for their ability to track the CSt allele in this study (Table 2 and Table S4).

T gene

Two PACE markers were developed for the T (Pv5‐593.09G047300) gene based on two independent deletions previously reported in Earliwax (1‑bp deletion) and 65‑73 (39‑bp deletion) (McClean et al., 2024; Parker et al., 2024). The 1‐bp deletion is at position Pv09:10,713,827 bp, while the 39‐bp deletion begins at position Pv09:10,713,101 bp in the 5‐593 reference genome. Each deletion was converted into a diagnostic PACE marker to track the recessive t allele (Table 2 and Table S4).

Z gene

McClean et al. (2024) identified two mutations in the candidate Z gene (Pv5‐593.03G127600) in Earliwax (homozygous for recessive z) relative to 5‐593 (homozygous dominant Z). The first mutation is a non‐synonymous SNP at Pv03:34,523,093 bp, also reported by Parker et al. (2024). The second mutation is a deletion spanning Pv03:34,522,869–34,522,880 bp, relative to the 5‐593 reference genome assembly. Two PACE markers were developed to target these respective mutations to distinguish Z and z alleles (Table 2 and Table S4).

J gene

The candidate gene for the J locus, Phvul.010G130600, encodes an anthocyanin regulatory C1 protein and was previously identified as the gene controlling the non‑darkening (ND) seed coat phenotype (Erfatpour & Pauls, 2020). A single‑nucleotide deletion in the non‑darkening genotype “Wit‑rood boontje (PI 439540)” relative to regular‑darkening and ND genotypes was reported as a putative causal variant for the non‑darkening trait (Erfatpour & Pauls, 2020). Using this variant, located at Pv10:41,185,069 bp in the G19833 v2 reference genome, we developed a PACE marker to track the non‐darkening allele.

Comparison of the 5‑593 genotype, which carries the dominant J allele, with Bassett's introgression line V0919 (PI 527820), which carries the recessive j allele, revealed a unique missense SNP at Pv10:43,652,450 bp in the 5‑593 reference genome ortholog Pv5‑593.10G132900. This SNP was also converted into a PACE marker to track allelic variation at the J locus associated with corona color and seed coat shininess versus dullness (Table 2 and Table S4).

Slow‐darkening (Psd )

An alternate recessive allele of the master regulatory P gene, known as Psd , controls the slow‐darkening (SD) trait in common bean. A PACE marker linked to the Psd allele (Alvares et al., 2019) has previously been developed. This marker was applied to the diverse MDP and ADP genotypes to determine the allelic distribution of the SD trait across the studied genotypes.

Rk gene

The Rk locus controls the expression of reddish seed coat colors through a series of recessive alleles (M. J. Bassett & Miklas, 2003; Osborne et al., 2026). Allele‐specific polymorphisms within the candidate gene PvUI111.02G213800 were used to develop PACE markers corresponding to different market classes (Osborne et al., 2026). A missense SNP at position Pv02:39,518,531 bp was used to develop a marker diagnostic for both pink and small red (rkp ) classes. A second missense SNP at Pv02:39,518,891 bp was associated with both light red kidney (rk) and dark red kidney (rkd ) classes. A third missense SNP at Pv02:39,519,517 bp was specific to the dark red kidney (rkd ) genotypes. Each SNP was converted into a PACE marker to distinguish allelic variants at the Rk locus (Table 2 and Table S4).

G gene

The “yellow‐brown factor” G gene was previously mapped to Pv04 and shown to co‐segregate with three STS markers (OAP3S1400, OAP7S850, and OU14S850) (McClean et al., 2002). BLASTn analysis of these markers against the 5‐593 genome placed the G locus within a heterochromatic interval of 25.2–35.9 Mb on Pv04. Four PACE markers spanning this interval were designed to evaluate their utility for indirect selection of G alleles (Table 2 and Table S4).

B gene

The B locus mapped in the genomic interval of 49.3–49.4 Mb (UI111 reference) on Pv02 using GWAS (Osborne et al., 2026). Two PACE markers targeting non‐synonymous variants in the gene models PvUI111.02G3139 and PvUI111.02G3141 were developed, linked to the B‐gene (Table 2 and Table S4).

V gene

McClean et al. (2022) identified Phvul.006G018800, encoding flavonoid 3′5′ hydroxylase (F3′5′H), as the candidate gene for the V locus. Nine distinct haplotypes associated with recessive v alleles were identified across genotypes, including V0491, M0056, UI111, Mex235, G2858, G5686, and MDRK. To capture full allelic diversity at the V locus, six PACE markers were developed targeting haplotype‐specific polymorphisms (Table 2 and Table S4).

Phaseolin (Phs) locus—Gene pool classification

Phaseolin is encoded at the Phs locus on chromosome Pv07 within a cluster of six cupin‑superfamily genes in the G19833 v2 reference (Gepts et al., 1993). Historically, gel‑based PCR assays that amplify a region containing a 15‑bp tandem repeat have been used to distinguish Andean (T‐type) from Middle American (S‐type) phaseolin (Kami et al., 1995). BLASTn analysis of the primer sequence from this marker revealed two genomic locations in Pv07 with 100% identity, corresponding to the Phvul.007G059700 and Phvul.007G059800 gene models, both of which annotated as cupin‐family proteins. To develop a rapid, high‐throughput alternative, we designed a PACE assay that targets a missense SNP in the Phvul.007G059800 gene model at Pv07:5,144,769 bp, a variant tightly associated with S versus T phaseolin classes (Table 2 and Table S4).

Validation of the Phs PACE marker was performed in multiple germplasm sets. In addition to the MDP and ADP, the PACE marker was evaluated in a diverse panel of 268 lines comprising 74 wild accessions, 114 landraces, and 75 cultivated genotypes representing both Middle American and Andean gene pools (Table S5). Gene pool assignments for the diverse panel were obtained from population structure analyses reported by Schmutz et al. (2014) and Celebioglu et al. (2026). Population structure information for the ADP was compiled from previously published sources (Cichy et al., 2015; Soler‐Garzón et al., 2024).

PACE marker performance and application

The developed PACE markers targeting seed coat color and patterning genes were evaluated across three diverse germplasm panels described above: the Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, MDP, and ADP. Marker performance was first assessed in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, as this panel contains well‑defined genotypes with known dominant and recessive allelic combinations. This allowed us to confirm whether each PACE marker accurately detected and distinguished the expected allelic states for its target gene. The wild‐landrace‐cultivated diversity panel was used only to evaluate the efficiency of the Phs PACE marker in distinguishing genotypes from the Middle American and Andean gene pools (Table S5).

Markers that successfully discriminated the defined allelic states in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines were subsequently evaluated in the MDP and ADP to determine allelic distributions across a broader, more genetically diverse set of market classes. Although the complete allelic states of seed coat color and patterning genes are not fully characterized for all market classes represented in these panels, a subset of market classes has well‑established allelic states based on prior genetic studies. These known market classes, along with several genotypes with clearly defined allelic combinations, were used as an additional benchmark to verify marker accuracy and consistency. This approach ensured that each marker performed reliably in both controlled genetic backgrounds and diverse breeding germplasm.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Seed coat color and patterning gene‐specific PACE marker development

In this study, we developed molecular markers linked to seven major seed coat color genes (G, B, V, J, Rk, T, and Z) and two patterning genes (CPi and CSt ) that collectively govern the pigmentation and pattern diversity of economically important common bean market classes across the world. Additionally, a PACE marker targeting the Phs locus was developed to distinguish genotypes belonging to the Middle American and Andean gene pools. A total of 25 gene‑specific PACE markers were designed across these 10 loci. For loci with strong candidate genes (T, Z, J, V, and Rk), markers were developed within the gene models, targeting non‐synonymous SNPs and INDELs to maximize the likelihood of functional association with phenotypic variation, identified through comparative analysis of multiple dominant and recessive allele source genotypes (Tables 1 and 2, and Table S4). This strategy enabled us to capture haplotype‑specific polymorphisms and to represent the maximum allelic diversity present across market classes. For loci defined by linkage mapping or GWAS intervals (G, B, CPi , and CSt ), we targeted the gene model surrounding the peak genomic regions and developed multiple PACE markers spanning the associated intervals to increase the probability of detecting causal or tightly linked variants. The performance of all 25 PACE markers was evaluated, and the allelic states for each marker were determined in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, MDP, and ADP. In addition, the previously reported marker PvbHLH12804 (Alvares et al., 2019), linked to Psd locus, was incorporated to assess the prevalence of the SD trait within these collections.

Complex C locus

The C locus has historically been interpreted either as a single locus with a multiple‑allelic series or as a cluster of very tightly linked genes forming a “complex C locus” that governs diverse seed coat patterns such as marbling, striping, red coloration, and light mottling (M. J. Bassett, 2007). Although recombination among these pattern phenotypes has not been conclusively demonstrated, the tightly linked complex C locus model proposed by Prakken (1974) is generally favored. Until the underlying multiple‐allelic series or a set of tightly linked pattern genes is fully resolved, CPi (Prakken, 1977) and CSt (M. J. Bassett, 2007; Prakken, 1974) can be used as gene symbols representing genes within the complex C locus that condition the pinto mottling and cranberry striping patterns, respectively. Diagnostic PACE markers for both CPi and CSt facilitate tracking of the dominant patterning alleles.

Pinto patterning (CPi ) gene

Prakken (1977) first described the pinto pattern and designated the gene symbol CPi . In this study, CPi and Cpi are used to denote the pinto‐ and non‐pinto patterned alleles, respectively. Although inheritance was not formally tested, breeder experience and recent genetic analysis suggest that pinto patterning is dominant over solid seed coat color (Roy, Oladzad, et al., 2025). Pinto is the most important dry bean market class in the United States, and genetic improvement often requires inter‑market class crosses. Therefore, the availability of a diagnostic molecular marker associated with CPi would greatly enhance selection efficiency in breeding populations.

No genetic stocks in the Bassett's introgression lines were specifically developed for the CPi and CSt loci. Genotyping with the CPi‐PACE‐snp‐UI111‐Pv08‐3360423‐UI111 marker revealed that the recessive Cpi allele predominated (91.5%) in Bassett's genetic introgression lines (Table S1). Only six genetic stocks carried the dominant CPi allele; however, all were white‐seeded and none exhibited pinto patterning, suggesting that CPi expression may be masked by recessive p.

Across the MDP, all pinto genotypes carried the dominant CPi allele, whereas small red, black, and pink market class beans carried the non‐pinto Cpi allele (Table 3 and Tables S2 and S6). These results indicate that the CPi‐PACE‐snp‐UI111‐Pv08‐3360423‐UI111 marker reliably differentiates pinto patterned genotypes from non‐pinto patterned genotypes and therefore can be effectively implemented in breeding programs to recover either the pinto pattern or the non‐pinto phenotype depending on selection objectives. The robustness of this marker was further demonstrated by clear and well‐defined allele clusters in the discrimination analysis, with distinct separation of CPi (pinto patterned seed coat) and Cpi (non‐pinto patterned seed coat) genotypes and minimal overlap (Figure 3). Similar clustering patterns were observed for the remaining loci evaluated in this study. In contrast, GN and navy beans displayed dominant, recessive, and heterozygous CPi alleles, despite the absence of pinto patterning. This is expected because these white‐seeded market classes (GN and navy) carry the homozygous recessive p allele, which masks the expression of all other seed coat color and patterning genes (Bassett, 2007; McClean et al., 2018), including CPi .

Cranberry striping (CSt ) gene

The first genetic analysis of dominant seed coat patterning involving cranberry striping was reported by Tjebbes and Kooiman (1919) using the striped Kievitsboon cranberry bean. They observed that the striping pattern was dominant over solid color and assigned the symbol S to the dominant striping phenotype. Subsequently, Lamprecht (1932) described additional dominant patterning genes responsible for marbled, speckled, and other seed coat patterns, highlighting the complexity of dominant seed coat patterning inheritance in common bean. Later, Prakken (1974) revised the nomenclature and assigned the allele symbol Cst to the dominant red striping phenotype. M. J. Bassett (2007) subsequently used St and st to denote the presence and absence of the stripe pattern phenotype, respectively (see M. J. Bassett, 2007; tab. 8.2, p. 250). To facilitate marker interpretation in the present study and maintain consistency with C‐locus nomenclature, we use CSt and Cst to designate the cranberry red striping and non‐cranberry striping phenotype, respectively.

In this study, the cranberry striping CSt‐PACE‐snp‐ADP0678‐Pv08‐3258164‐UI111 marker demonstrated 95% diagnostic accuracy (38 out of 40 cranberry genotypes in the panel) for detecting the dominant CSt ( ADP0678 ) allele among cranberry market class genotypes in the ADP. Two cranberry genotypes, ADP0470 (PI 527508) and USCR‐CBB‐20, did not carry the dominant allele according to marker data (Table S3). Examination of Germplasm Resources Information Network (GRIN) descriptors data and available seed samples indicated that PI 527508 exhibits irregularly distributed stripes and spots rather than continuous cranberry striping, potentially explaining the lack of marker detection. In contrast, the discrepant result observed for USCR‐CBB‐20 may reflect introgression effects, as this line was developed through a modified backcross breeding scheme involving multiple market classes in its pedigree (Miklas et al., 2011). Across ADP, the dominant CSt allele was relatively rare, with a frequency of 17.7%, compared to the recessive Cst allele, which accounted for 82.3% of genotypes (Tables 4 and 5, and Tables S3 and S7). As expected, the marker CSt‐PACE‐snp‐ADP0678‐Pv08‐3258164‐UI111 consistently detected the recessive Cst allele in both Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines (Table S1) and MDP (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6), indicating complete diagnostic accuracy for identifying the non‐striped allele in these materials.

A second marker, CSt‐PACE‐snp‐ADP0678‐Pv08‐3258731‐UI111, tracked the dominant CSt ( ADP0678 ) allele with 92% accuracy among cranberry market class genotypes and collectively represented approximately 20.1% of the dominant CSt alleles in the ADP (Table 4 and Table S3). In Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, six lines carrying a dominant CSt allele were detected despite the absence of cranberry striping phenotypes (Table S1). Moreover, in the MDP, this marker detected nearly equal frequencies of dominant and recessive alleles (Tables S2 and S6), suggesting reduced reliability in this population. Since a dominant‐acting CSt allele would not be expected at such frequency without corresponding phenotypic expression, this pattern indicates that CSt‐PACE‐snp‐ADP0678‐Pv08‐3258731‐UI111 is not suitable for the MDP, potentially due to differences in linkage phase, background‐specific recombination, or interaction with additional patterning loci.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the CSt‐PACE‐snp‐ADP0678‐Pv08‐3258164‐UI111 marker is a highly reliable diagnostic tool for tracking cranberry striping inheritance as well as for identifying the recessive non‐striped allele across diverse genetic backgrounds.

Partly colored genes: T and Z

T gene (partly colored seed coat patterning)

Partly colored seed coat types in common bean are genetically distinct from patterned seed coat types, as pigmentation is restricted to a defined zone of the seed coat while the remaining area remains white (M. J. Bassett, 2007; McClean et al., 2024). The T locus functions as a key “ground factor” controlling partly colored seed coats (M. J. Bassett, 2007), and expression of this phenotype requires a homozygous recessive genotype (tt). The size, position, and configuration of the colored region conditioned by t can be further modified by additional partly colored pattern genes such as Z, J, Bip, and Fib (M. J. Bassett, 2007), indicating that partly colored phenotypes arise from both the T locus and interacting modifier loci.

To evaluate marker utility for tracking the recessive t allele, two developed PACE markers (t‐PACE‐39bp‐del‐65–73‐Pv09‐10713101‐5‐593 and t‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐Earliwax‐Pv09‐10713827‐5‐593) were tested on Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines. All introgression lines carrying the homozygous recessive t allele were accurately detected by the appropriate marker, depending on which recessive t allele had been introgressed from the donor parent (Table 5 and Table S1). This confirms that the PACE assays effectively differentiate recessive t alleles associated with partly colored seed coat phenotypes.

In the ADP, all genotypes carried the dominant T allele, as detected by both PACE markers, consistent with the absence of partly colored phenotypes in this panel (Tables 4 and 5, and Tables S3 and S7). Similarly, genotypes in the MDP predominantly carried the dominant T allele based on the Earliwax‐based PACE marker. Notably, only a single genotype, “Orca,” carried the recessive t65–73 allele detected by the 39‐bp deletion marker. Orca was the only partly colored genotype within the studied MDP, and its accurate identification demonstrates the high diagnostic effectiveness (100%) of the T‐gene PACE markers for detecting partly colored seed coat inheritance in this germplasm set (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6).

However, the two T‐PACE markers did not detect the partly colored Jacob's Cattle genotype in the ADP. This discrepancy is explained by the presence of a unique 14‐Mb inversion in the untranslated region (UTR) region of the T locus reported by Parker et al. (2024), which might disrupt marker binding and prevent detection of this specific recessive t allele.

Taken together, results across Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, ADP, and MDP indicate that the two PACE markers capture complementary components of T allelic variation, and therefore both assays should be deployed together to recover the full spectrum of recessive t allelic diversity present in breeding materials. This combined marker strategy will improve selection efficiency for partly colored seed coat phenotypes and facilitate introgression of the t allele into diverse genetic backgrounds.

Z gene (hilum ring color expression)

Gene Z, also called the hilum ring factor, controls both ring color and the extent of the seed coat patterns. The Z gene only affects seed coat patterns when paired with tt. Inheritance and expression of color in the hilum ring, the specialized layer of cells bordering the hilum, is primarily controlled by the Z and J genes. A two‐factor interaction governs this trait, where the recessive z allele is dependent on the presence of j for expression of loss of color in the hilum ring (M. J. Bassett, 1996; Prakken, 1970). This interaction highlights the genetic complexity of hilum ring pigmentation and suggests that marker‐based selection for hilum ring traits must account for potential epistatic interactions among these loci.

Two PACE markers were developed to track the Z locus using both a SNP‐based variant (z‐PACE‐snp‐Earliwax‐Pv03‐34523093‐5‐593) and an indel‐based variant (z‐PACE‐12bp‐del‐Earliwax‐Pv03‐34522869‐5‐593). Both markers were completely diagnostic in detecting the recessive z allele in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines known to carry the homozygous recessive z genotype, confirming their effectiveness for marker‐assisted tracking of z in controlled genetic backgrounds (Table 5 and Table S1).

Across the MDP, the dominant Z allele predominated, with 97.5% of genotypes carrying the dominant allele based on z‐PACE‐snp‐Earliwax‐Pv03‐34523093‐5‐593 and 92.1% based on z‐PACE‐12bp‐del‐Earliwax‐Pv03‐34522869‐5‐593 (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6). In contrast, the ADP showed a high frequency (96.3%) of the recessive z allele, particularly when assessed using the indel‐based PACE marker, while the SNP‐based PACE marker detected the recessive allele in 55.9% of genotypes (Tables 4 and 5, and Tables S3 and S7). The discrepancy between the two assays in ADP suggests that these markers may be capturing different z‐linked haplotypes, or that one marker may have reduced diagnostic performance across broader germplasm diversity due to recombination or allele heterogeneity at the Z locus.

Overall, these results indicate that both PACE markers are effective for identifying recessive z alleles in introgression materials, while marker performance may vary across diverse panels. Therefore, the combined use of both PACE markers may provide a more comprehensive assessment of Z allelic diversity, particularly when screening genetically diverse breeding populations.

J gene (seed coat color development, shininess versus dullness, and non‐darkening trait)

The J gene is involved in the regulation of multiple traits, including mature seed coat color development, brown hilum ring color, seed coat shininess, and postharvest darkening (M. J. Bassett, 1996; Prakken, 1970). A recessive allele at the J locus results in a dull seed coat phenotype, caused by reduced or absent color expression in the corona and altered immature seed coat pigmentation (M. J. Bassett, 2007). Common bean often experiences darkening of the seed coat after harvesting, a phenomenon known as postharvest darkening. Darkened seeds are undesirable for both producers and consumers, as they often need to be sold at discounted prices, require longer cooking times, and have low iron (Fe) bioavailability (Wiesinger et al., 2021). “Non‐darkening” is one of the seed coat darkening phenotypes controlled by the J locus (Erfatpour & Pauls, 2020). The genotype with J present postharvest darkening, and the homozygous recessive jj genotype, is considered the primary genetic factors underlying the non‐darkening trait in common bean, making this locus highly relevant for market classes where color stability is a key quality trait.

The PACE marker j‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐witroodboonje‐Pv10‐41185069‐G19833 successfully tracked the recessive jWRB in two USDA‐NPGS accessions labeled as “Wit‐rood boon” (PI 431611) and “Witrood boontje” (PI 439540). As expected, none of the Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines carried the recessive jWRB allele (Table 5 and Table S1). Similarly, the MDP showed 100% presence of the dominant J allele, indicating the absence of non‐darkening genotypes in this diversity panel (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6). In the ADP, only a single genotype labeled as “Witrood” (ADP0101) carried the recessive jWRB allele, while all remaining genotypes carried the dominant J allele (Table S3).

The second PACE marker, j‐PACE‐snp‐V0919‐Pv10‐43652450‐5‐593, effectively tracked the recessive jV0553 allele in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines known to carry the recessive j allele, confirming its utility for MAS at the J locus (Table 5 and Table S1). Across both the ADP and MDP, the dominant J allele was highly prevalent, with approximately 98% of genotypes carrying the dominant allele, while only about 2% carried the recessive j allele (Tables 3 and 4, and Tables S2, S3, S6, and S7).

To the best of our knowledge, Bassett's introgression lines carrying recessive j alleles were developed for seeds with a non‐shiny dark purple phenotype with a near white corona. Moreover, none of the genotypes in the MDP is known to carry the non‐darkening trait. Therefore, the absence of the recessive jWRB allele in these panels highlights the presence of multiple functional alleles at the J locus, each contributing to different aspects of seed coat phenotypes.

Previous QTL (quantitative trait locus) analysis in a biparental population derived from Ervilha (ADP0512; non‑darkening) × PI 527538 (ADP0468; regular darkening) identified a QTL overlapping with the J locus associated with postharvest darkening (A. Bassett et al., 2021). Marker analysis showed that Ervilha carried the jV0553 allele but not the jWRB allele, while PI 527538 carried the dominant J allele as expected (Table S3). The absence of jWRB in Ervilha suggests that additional J‑locus haplotypes or interactions between jV0553 and other loci may contribute to the non‑darkening phenotype in different market class backgrounds. However, further genetic and functional validation will be required to resolve these mechanisms.

The successful identification of the jV0553 allele in the introgression lines further supports this allelic diversity. Collectively, these results indicate that recessive j alleles are relatively rare in the evaluated germplasm sets but exhibit functional diversity. The developed J‐allele‐specific PACE markers provide reliable tools for identifying and selecting non‐darkening and other J‐locus associated pigmentation alleles when present, supporting their application in MAS for seed quality traits.

Slow‐darkening (Psd )

Postharvest darkening of common bean seeds, particularly in light‐colored seed coats, is an undesirable trait affecting both market value and consumer preference. The SD phenotype, conditioned by the recessive Psd allele, reduces the rate of seed coat darkening and has become increasingly important to breeders, growers, and the bean industry (Islam et al., 2020; Junk‐Knievel et al., 2008).

The Psd ‐linked PACE marker was evaluated across Bassett's introgression lines, as well as the MDP and ADP, to assess allelic distribution. As expected, no genotypes carrying the Psd allele were identified in either the introgression lines or the ADP. In contrast, three MDP genotypes known to have the slow darkening trait (CDC WM‐2, ICB‐12, and SDIP‐1) were identified as carriers of the Psd allele. The remaining ∼99% of genotypes in the MDP lacked the SD‐associated allele, indicating that the Psd allele is rare within this germplasm set. These results are consistent with the fact that Psd allele is largely confined to germplasm specifically selected for the SD trait. The low frequency of the Psd allele across the MDP and complete absence in the ADP suggest limited historical incorporation of the SD trait into these panels. This highlights a clear opportunity for targeted introgression of Psd into elite germplasm to improve seed coat quality and enhance market acceptance.

Interestingly, Sadohara et al. (2022) identified significant SNPs on Pv01, Pv04, Pv07, and Pv10 associated with postharvest seed coat darkening (SD or non‐darkening phenotypes) in a yellow bean collection from the ADP. The significant SNP on Pv07 was located approximately 395 kb from the P locus. Given the absence of the P sd allele in the ADP examined in this study, these results suggest that additional loci may contribute to postharvest darkening phenotypes in Andean germplasm. Further genetic validation will be required to determine whether these loci represent alternative mechanisms controlling seed coat darkening independent of the P sd allele at the P locus.

Color‐modifying genes (G, B, V, and Rk)

The G, B, V, and Rk genes are classified as intensifying or modifying genes because of their interactions with the seed coat “ground factor” P gene (M. J. Bassett, 2007; Prakken, 1970). Among these, G, B, and V interact in eight allelic combinations that collectively determine seed coat color variation ranging from yellow to black (Lamprecht, 1932; Prakken, 1970). For instance, genotypes carrying a dominant V allele in combination with a dominant B allele, or with both dominant G and B alleles, typically express black seed coat color (Bassett, 2007; McClean et al., 2022). In contrast, solid‐colored market classes such as pink and small red are generally associated with the G b v rkp Cpi genotypic combination, whereas pinto beans are commonly represented by the G B v Rk CPi genotype (Osborne et al., 2026). Because these loci act in combination, robust diagnostic markers for V, B, and G are essential for efficient marker‐assisted selection (MAS) and for tracking allele introgression across diverse market class backgrounds.

G gene

Four PACE markers were developed to target the G locus, and their diagnostic performance was evaluated across diverse genetic backgrounds. All four markers showed complete diagnostic accuracy in detecting the homozygous recessive g allele in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines known to carry recessive g, confirming that these assays reliably track the G locus in controlled genetic backgrounds (Table 5 and Table S1).

Across the MDP, the dominant G allele was consistently detected across the evaluated market classes, indicating that it is widespread in this germplasm set (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6). In contrast, the recessive g allele predominated in the ADP, with allele frequencies ranging from 78.1% to 83.7% depending on the used markers (Tables 4 and 5, and Tables S3 and S7). This result demonstrates a high prevalence of recessive g in the ADP diversity panel. Furthermore, within each ADP market class, the recessive g allele was the major allele, although a small proportion of genotypes carrying the dominant G allele was also observed, suggesting allelic heterogeneity at the G locus across diverse market class backgrounds.

The genomic interval containing the G locus is relatively large due to its location in a heterochromatic region where recombination is strongly suppressed. Despite this, all four PACE markers exhibited high diagnostic accuracy, likely because reduced recombination in this region preserves linkage between the markers and the underlying causal variant (s). These results indicate that the developed G‑locus markers are robust tools for distinguishing dominant and recessive alleles across diverse germplasm and can be reliably deployed in breeding programs to track G‑locus inheritance.

B gene

Two PACE markers, b‐PACE‐snp‐V0722‐Pv02‐49324548‐UI111 and b‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐V0722‐Pv02‐49331915‐UI111, were developed to target the B locus using missense and frameshift mutations located in the gene models PvUI111.02G313900 and PvUI111.02G314100, respectively. Both markers successfully traced the homozygous recessive b allele in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines known to carry recessive b, confirming their effectiveness for marker‐assisted tracking of the B locus (Table 5 and Table S1).

Across the MDP, the dominant B allele predominated, with a frequency of 71% (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6). In contrast, the ADP was largely enriched for the recessive b allele, with approximately 71% of genotypes carrying recessive b (Tables 4 and 5, and Tables S3 and S7). Within the MDP, pink and small red beans are generally expected to carry recessive b, whereas pinto beans are typically associated with the dominant B allele. Consistent with these expectations, both B‐PACE markers were fully diagnostic for pink beans, accurately detecting the recessive b allele in all genotypes. However, small red beans showed 85% recessive b alleles, indicating either unexpected allelic heterogeneity within this market class or the presence of additional genetic factors influencing seed coat color classification. In other market classes, dominant B alleles were prevalent, including pinto (96.1%), black (83.3%), GN (91.5%), and navy (68.9%), demonstrating that the dominant B allele is widespread among several major market classes represented in MDP (Table 3 and Tables S2 and S6).

In the ADP, the recessive b allele predominated across most market classes, with frequencies ranging from 87.0% to 95.7%, depending on the marker (Table 4 and Tables S3 and S7). LRK genotypes are typically known to carry the recessive b, and both markers consistently detected the recessive allele in all LRK entries. Similar enrichment for recessive b was also observed among DRK, purple speckled, red mottled, red, small red, and yellow beans, supporting the broad distribution of recessive b across these market classes.

Notably, cranberry beans showed contrasting results depending on the marker used. The marker b‐PACE‐snp‐V0722‐Pv02‐49324548‐UI111 detected nearly equal proportions of dominant and recessive alleles within cranberry genotypes, whereas b‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐V0722‐Pv02‐49331915‐UI111 indicated that all cranberry genotypes carried the recessive b allele (Table 4 and Tables S3 and S7). When evaluated against known genotypic combinations for cranberry bean entries, both markers correctly identified the expected recessive b allele for the corresponding genotypes. Therefore, the discrepancy between the two assays likely reflects haplotype‐level variation or allele heterogeneity at the B locus within cranberry germplasm, rather than a failure of either marker. As a result, it is difficult to recommend a single B‐PACE marker as universally optimal without prior knowledge of the specific b allele background, and using both markers together may provide the most reliable coverage of B allelic diversity across diverse market classes.

V gene

Based on the allelic diversity reported at the V locus (McClean et al., 2022), six allele‐specific PACE markers were developed and evaluated across Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, MDP, and ADP. When tested on Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines carrying known recessive alleles, two markers, v‐PACE‐snp‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567680‐5‐593 and v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593, generated consistent results and detected the majority of the known recessive v alleles. The remaining three recessive v alleles were detected using v‐PACE‐1bp‐ins‐G19833‐Pv06‐12567771‐5‐593, which overlapped in detection with v‐PACE‐snp‐MDRK‐Pv06‐12564033‐5‐593, and additionally identified several recessive v alleles in introgression lines with undetermined donor origin. In contrast, two markers, v‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐UI111‐Pv06‐12568021‐5‐593 and v‐PACE‐15bp‐del‐G28‐Pv06‐12567686‐5‐593, did not detect any recessive v alleles in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, suggesting that these markers may not target the recessive alleles present in the introgression materials used in this study (Table 5 and Table S1).

In the MDP, all six V‐allele‐specific PACE markers consistently detected the dominant V allele in black and navy market class genotypes (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6). This indicates that any of the evaluated V locus markers can be reliably used to track the dominant V allele in these market classes. In contrast, pinto, pink, and small red beans are expected to predominantly carry recessive v alleles, and marker performance differed among these groups. For pinto beans, the marker v‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐UI111‐Pv06‐12568021‐5‐593 was sufficient to detect the recessive v allele. However, this marker detected only approximately 92% and 67% of recessive v alleles in pink and small red beans, respectively. The remaining recessive v alleles in these market classes were identified using v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593 and v‐PACE‐15bp‐del‐G28‐Pv06‐12567686‐5‐593, indicating the presence of multiple recessive v haplotypes. Therefore, while v‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐UI111‐Pv06‐12568021‐5‐593 provides effective coverage for pinto germplasm, combining it with either v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593 or v‐PACE‐15bp‐del‐G28‐Pv06‐12567686‐5‐593 is necessary to capture the full recessive v allelic diversity in pink and small red beans. GN genotypes showed both dominant and recessive alleles at the V locus, reflecting increased allelic heterogeneity in this market class. For GN beans, a two‐marker strategy using v‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐UI111‐Pv06‐12568021‐5‐593 together with v‐PACE‐snp‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567680‐5‐593 or v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593, would provide the most comprehensive detection of V‐locus allelic diversity (Tables 3 and 5, and Tables S2 and S6).

The distribution of V locus alleles in the ADP varied substantially depending on the specific PACE marker used, indicating allelic heterogeneity and/or haplotype divergence across market classes. According to M. J. Bassett (2007), LRK cultivars are expected to carry recessive v (or vlae ) alleles. In agreement with this expectation, multiple markers were required to detect all recessive v alleles in the LRK genetic background. Specifically, the two markers v‐PACE‐snp‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567680‐5‐593 and v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593 produced similar results and detected recessive v alleles in 16 LRK genotypes, whereas v‐PACE‐snp‐MDRK‐Pv06‐12564033‐5‐593 detected recessive v alleles in an additional 18 LRK genotypes. In contrast, v‐PACE‐1bp‐del‐UI111‐Pv06‐12568021‐5‐593 detected only a single LRK genotype carrying a recessive v allele, further supporting that no single marker is sufficient to capture all recessive v haplotypes present in this market class (Table S3). Similarly, for DRK genotypes, a combination of markers was necessary to recover full V allelic diversity. The most informative marker sets included v‐PACE‐snp‐MDRK‐Pv06‐12564033‐5‐593 together with either v‐PACE‐snp‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567680‐5‐593 or v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593, yielding comparable results. Different marker combinations were also required to identify recessive v alleles in additional ADP market classes. For example, v‐PACE‐snp‐MDRK‐Pv06‐12564033‐5‐593 combined with either v‐PACE‐snp‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567680‐5‐593 or v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593 was most effective for cranberry, purple speckled, and yellow beans. For red mottled and red beans, the best coverage required a three‐marker combination consisting of v‐PACE‐snp‐MDRK‐Pv06‐12564033‐5‐593, v‐PACE‐15bp‐del‐G28‐Pv06‐12567686‐5‐593, and either v‐PACE‐snp‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567680‐5‐593 or v‐PACE‐14bp‐del‐M0056‐Pv06‐12567943‐5‐593 (Tables 4 and 5, and Tables S3 and S7). Collectively, these results demonstrate that while V‐locus PACE markers are highly effective for tracking dominant and recessive alleles, marker performance is strongly market class dependent, and a multi‐marker strategy is required to ensure complete detection of recessive v haplotypes across genetically diverse germplasm sets.

The V locus has well‐known pleiotropic effects on flower color, where the dominant V allele produces purple flowers and several recessive alleles condition pink or white flowers (Lamprecht, 1936; McClean et al., 2022). Although we attempted to capture all haplotype variation and independent mutations present within the V gene, this study was limited in its ability to develop a definitive marker for the recessive pleiotropic vlae allele associated with pink (rose‑purple) flowers and the dark corona on seed coats (M. J. Bassett, 1995). Additional allelic diversity and structural complexity at the V locus likely contributed to this limitation.

Red kidney color (Rk) gene

The Rk gene is responsible for red kidney color in common bean and confers red seed coat color phenotypes in a recessive manner observed in LRKs, DRKs, pink, and small red beans (M. J. Bassett & Miklas, 2003). Three recessive alleles at the Rk locus, rk, rkp , and rkd , give various hues of red. The rk allele conditions the pink/testaceous color found in LRKs, the rkd allele produces the garnet brown color of DRKs, and the rkp allele confers the pale pink and red colors found in pink and small red beans, respectively. Although both pink and small red beans carry the rkp allele, their phenotypic expression differs due to the interaction with the C locus. Specifically, pink beans carry the cucu genotype, whereas small red beans require a C_ genotype at the C locus to express their characteristic seed coat color (M. J. Bassett & Miklas, 2003; Osborne et al., 2026).

Three PACE markers targeting market class‐specific alleles at the Rk locus (Osborne et al., 2026) were evaluated in this study. All PACE markers accurately traced their respective Rk allele in Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines (Table S1). The rkp‐PACE‐snp‐PinkFloyd‐Pv02‐39518531‐UI111 marker, designed to detect rkp allele associated with pink and small red beans, was fully diagnostic in both ADP and MDP, with few exceptions (Tables 3, 4, 5 and Tables S2, S3, S6, and S7). Minor discrepancies in small red beans may reflect introgression from other market classes or interaction with the dominant C locus. For example, the small red cultivar Rio Rojo was derived from a cross between Buster (pinto) and VAX 3 (small red), the latter being a backcross‐derived line with interspecific ancestry (Osorno et al., 2013; Singh et al., 2001), which may contribute to allelic complexity at the rkp allele. The rk‐PACE‐snp‐CELRK&Montcalm‐Pv02‐39518891‐UI111 marker, derived from a SNP shared by LRK and DRK genotypes, successfully identified both market classes in the ADP and differentiated them from other market classes in the MDP. Similarly, rkd‐PACE‐snp‐Montcalm‐Pv02‐39519517‐UI111 marker specifically tracked the rkd alleles in DRK genotypes and showed distinct allelic patterns for other market classes across both ADP and MDP (Tables 3, 4, 5 and Tables S2, S3, S6, and S7). It is important to note that a few discrepancies in marker‐based classification, particularly within the ADP, may reflect misclassification of seed types and inter‐market and gene pool introgression or hybridization rather than marker failure. As with any market class‑specific marker, interpretation should consider the possibility of mislabeled accessions or background‑specific interactions that may influence phenotype.

PACE marker for classifying Middle American versus Andean genotypes at the Phs locus

Two independent domestication events gave rise to the highly differentiated Middle American and Andean gene pools of P. vulgaris (Schmutz et al., 2014). Historically, phaseolin type has been a reliable biochemical indicator to distinguish the two domestication‑derived gene pools: S‑type (originally described in Sanilac) phaseolin predominates in cultivars derived from the Middle American center of origin, whereas T‑type (originally described in Tendergreen) predominates in cultivars derived from the Andean origin (Gepts & Bliss, 1986). Both S and T phaseolin families include two subfamilies, α and β. Sequence analyses of genomic and cDNA clones have shown that a 15‑bp direct repeat is uniquely present in T α‑phaseolin gene and is absent from S α‑phaseolin gene. This structural difference underlies historical gel‑based PCR diagnostics that separate Andean and Middle American genotypes (Kami et al., 1995). To provide a rapid, high‑throughput alternative, we developed a PACE assay targeting a missense SNP in Phvul.007G059800 (Pv07:5,144,769 bp) within the Phs cupin gene cluster.

Across all evaluated panels, the Phs‐PACE‐snp‐UI111‐Pv07‐5144769‐G19833 marker showed high accordance with gene pool classifications. In the wild‐landrace‐cultivated diversity panel, the marker identified Middle American genotypes with ∼91% accuracy (153 of 169) and Andean genotypes with ∼93% accuracy (92 of 99) (Table 5 and Table S5). The marker was fully diagnostic in the MDP (Table 5 and Table S2). In the ADP, all 15 genotypes previously assigned to the Middle American gene pool were correctly identified; the marker tracked the Andean allele with ∼92% accuracy (237/258) (Table 5 and Table S3). Most discrepancies were rare and were likely attributable to inter‐gene pool introgression or known pedigree admixture. For example, the DRK cultivar Montcalm, which carries both GN 1 (Middle American) and DRK (Andean) ancestry, displays the Middle American allele despite Andean parentage. Pedigree analyses of several misclassified genotypes (Red Hawk, Redcoat, Rosie, Talon, USWK‐CBB‐17, and USCR‐CBB‐20) revealed Montcalm‐related ancestry or other introgression events (Kelly et al., 1998; Miklas et al., 2006, 2011; Osorno et al., 2017), supporting introgression as the likely cause (Johns et al., 1997; Kami et al., 1995). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the Phs PACE marker provides a robust, high‐throughput molecular tool for gene pool classification while also revealing introgression patterns that may complicate strict gene pool assignments in elite breeding germplasm.

Marker validation and diagnostic accuracy

The diagnostic performance of the developed 25 PACE markers was validated across three complementary germplasm panels: classical Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines and broad diversity panels from both Middle American and Andean gene pools (MDP and ADP) (Table 5). An additional panel with diverse wild, landrace, and cultivated genotypes from two gene pools was tested for the diagnostic accuracy of the Phs locus marker. In the Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines panel, which contains introgression lines with known allelic states, all gene‐specific markers correctly tracked the expected dominant and recessive alleles, confirming their robustness for detecting target loci. In the MDP and ADP, most markers consistently demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (90%–100%) for market class‐specific phenotypes, particularly for CSt , T, Z, J, G, B, V, Rk, and Phs loci. For patterning loci within the complex C locus, diagnostic accuracy varied by marker and population, reflecting the region's underlying genetic complexity. The CPi‐PACE‐snp‐8‐3360423‐UI111 and CSt‐PACE‐snp‐ADP0678‐Pv08‐3258164‐UI111 markers were fully diagnostic in panels where the corresponding phenotypes were well represented, while secondary markers showed reduced accuracy in certain backgrounds, likely due to differences in linkage phase or local haplotype structure.

The reduced accuracy observed for some marker assays is consistent with the complex evolutionary history of several major seed coat color genes. Multiple studies have shown that loci such as P, V, T, and Z have undergone repeated independent mutational events, with 2–10 reported loss‐of‐function alleles identified at individual loci (McClean et al., 2018, 2022, 2024; Parker et al., 2024). Consequently, a single marker may not capture all functional allelic variants present across diverse germplasm, reducing its diagnostic power in broader populations. Furthermore, many seed coat color and pattern phenotypes likely originated prior to domestication and have been maintained and diversified throughout the evolutionary history of wild and cultivated common bean (McClean et al., 2018; Parker et al., 2024). Pattern classes such as spots, stripes, speckled, and mottles have therefore had extensive opportunities to accumulate genetic variation within and between the Andean and Middle American gene pools. This long history of diversification may explain the occasional misclassification observed for the pinto and stripe marker assays, particularly when markers developed in one gene pool are applied to germplasm from the alternate gene pool.

Overall, the validation results highlight that the majority of the developed PACE markers are reliable diagnostic tools for both genetic studies and breeding applications. Concomitantly, they emphasize the importance of validating marker performance across diverse genetic backgrounds, especially for loci characterized by multiple functional alleles, complex haplotype structures, or independent mutational origins prior to deployment.

Haplotype diversity and allelic variation

Analysis of allelic states across the Bassett's genetic stock introgression lines, MDP, and ADP revealed substantial haplotype diversity at several seed coat color and patterning loci, reflecting the broad genetic and market‑class diversity represented in these populations. By targeting multiple non‐synonymous variants and haplotype‐specific polymorphisms for loci such as J, Rk, and V, the marker set captures a broad spectrum of allelic diversity across the gene pools and exhibits clear haplotype structure corresponding to known dominant and recessive alleles. This approach enabled distinction among closely related alleles (e.g., rk, rkp , and rkd at the Rk locus) and facilitated tracking of market class‐specific haplotypes. In contrast, loci such as CPi displayed background‑dependent allelic variation, with market classes such as GN and navy exhibiting unexpected allele combinations. This likely reflects epistatic masking by the recessive p locus, allowing dominant CPi alleles to persist without phenotypic expression and underscoring the need to consider genetic context when deploying patterning markers in breeding populations. The observed allelic variation also reflects historical domestication and breeding trajectories that shaped seed coat color and patterning diversity in common bean. These findings emphasize the importance of capturing haplotype‑level information when developing diagnostic markers and highlight the genetic complexity underlying seed coat traits in common bean.

Integration with market class breeding and implications for MAS

Inter‐market class crossing within and across gene pools and races has long been used to introduce economically important traits such as agronomic performance, disease resistance, plant architecture, and quality attributes while broadening the genetic base of common bean. However, despite the extensive genetic variability within the species, specific market class improvement through inter‐market class hybridization is often constrained due to its strict requirement to maintain market class‐specific seed traits such as shape, size, coat color, and pattern (McClean et al., 1993). The suite of gene‑specific PACE markers developed in this study provides dry bean breeders with high‑resolution practical tools for tracking seed coat color and patterning alleles during inter‑ and intra‑market class breeding. Because many economically important market classes, such as pinto, pink, black, red kidney, and cranberry, require precise recovery of both color and pattern phenotypes, these markers enable efficient early‑generation selection for the desired allelic combinations that define each market class. The ability to distinguish recessive alleles at loci such as Rk, V, and B, or dominant patterning alleles at CPi and CSt , allows breeders to fix target phenotypes more effectively while minimizing the segregation load typically associated with inter‑market class crosses. By enabling genotype‐based selection, these markers shorten breeding cycles, decrease the number of segregating population evaluations, and reduce reliance on phenotypic evaluation, which is often confounded by epistatic interactions and environmental effects. Recent breeding successes highlight the practical utility of MAS for seed coat traits.

A recent example illustrating the value of marker‐assisted selection is the successful introgression of the SD postharvest quality trait from an SD pinto cultivar into regular darkening pink beans, resulting in the development of the first SD pink lines (Osborne et al., 2026). This achievement relied on coordinated use of rkp (pink seed coat), Cpi (non‐pinto pattern), and a pre‐existing Psd (SD trait) PACE marker. Similarly, the dry bean genomics and breeding programs at North Dakota State University are implementing MAS to transfer white mold (WM) resistance QTL (WM2.2a, WM5.4, and WM7.5) from black bean sources (Oladzad et al., 2023; Roy et al., 2023) into susceptible commercial pinto cultivars. Recovery of the pinto market class phenotype is facilitated by selecting individuals homozygous for the dominant CPi allele in combination with recessive v alleles; where color and pattern markers are essential for preserving market class identity in complex trait introgression (current breeding efforts).

Collectively, these examples underscore the critical role of seed coat color and pattern markers in the design of breeding strategies. This comprehensive marker development effort provides a set of high‑resolution, gene‑anchored tools for tracking seed coat color and patterning alleles in breeding populations and diversity panels, enabling more precise characterization of allelic variation and more efficient marker‑assisted selection for market class‐specific seed coat traits. Together, these PACE markers provide a robust framework for accelerating the development of market class true cultivars, improving selection accuracy, and facilitating the pyramiding of quality and disease resistance traits across diverse breeding pipelines.

LIMITATIONS AND CONSIDERATIONS

Despite the comprehensive marker development effort, several limitations should be considered. The P locus, a master regulator of seed‑coat and flower pigmentation, exhibits extensive allelic diversity. At least 10 independent loss‑of‑function p mutations have been reported (McClean et al., 2018), each resulting in complete loss of pigmentation. The presence of multiple independent mutations at the P locus precludes the development of a single robust marker capable of distinguishing pigmentation versus non‐pigmentation phenotypes across diverse germplasm. Although a recessive P‑locus marker (Psd ) associated with the SD trait has been developed previously, markers for the recessive pgri allele responsible for grayish white seed coats (in the presence of V) and white flowers are still lacking. Similarly, functional markers for the recessive vlae allele that conditions pink flowers and the dark corona on seed coats remain to be developed. In addition, loci such as Bip and Fib, which influence the spatial distribution of pigmentation in partially colored seed coats, were not addressed in this study.

Although the PACE markers developed here showed strong diagnostic performance across multiple germplasm panels, several considerations should guide their application. For some loci (e.g., G, B, CPi , and CSt ), markers were developed within linkage or GWAS intervals rather than confirmed causal genes, which may limit their diagnostic precision in genetically diverse backgrounds. Moreover, the Rk is the only locus with well‐defined true functional alleles directly associated with distinct phenotypes (e.g., rkp for pink, rk for LRK, and rkd for DRK). While multiple markers were developed for loci such as T, Z, J, G, B, and V to capture known haplotypic variation or independent mutations, additional undiscovered diversity may exist that is not fully represented by the current marker set.

Additionally, occasional discrepancies between marker genotype and expected phenotype may reflect phenotypic misclassification of market classes, historical or background‐specific introgression events, or epistatic interactions among seed coat trait loci rather than marker failure. Taken together, these considerations highlight the importance of validating marker accuracy within targeted breeding populations and genetic backgrounds prior to large‐scale deployment. Furthermore, these limitations highlight the inherent complexity of seed coat genetics and the continued necessity of integrating molecular marker data with phenotypic evaluation.
