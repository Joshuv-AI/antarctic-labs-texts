Parents’ and caregivers’ views on pediatric meningococcal disease and serogroup B vaccination
Authors: Oscar Herrera-Restrepo, Vasudha Gupta, Sophie Jacobovitz, Tosin Olaiya, Elise Kuylen, Karam Dahyaleh, Lynn Kwock, Thatiana Pinto, Sofia B. Pinto, Suzanne Staebler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the United States (US), the highest incidence of invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is among infants aged ≤ 1 year (y). While most IMD cases in US infants are caused by meningococcal serogroup B (MenB), there is no MenB vaccine approved or recommended for US children < 10 y. This study sought to understand general attitudes toward vaccination, knowledge of IMD, and MenB vaccine preferences among parents/caregivers of children ≤ 18 months. An online survey was conducted in November–December 2024 among US parents/caregivers of children ≤ 18 months. Survey responses were analyzed descriptively. Overall, 1,051 parents/caregivers participated in the study. Most parents’/caregivers’ children had received all age-appropriate immunizations (73.4%). Of the 266 respondents (25.3%) whose children had received some or no immunizations, 59.8% reported concerns regarding potential vaccine side effects. Most parents/caregivers were familiar with IMD, but knowledge gaps exist regarding its risk and presentation in infants. Two-thirds of parents/caregivers (66.3%) would accept a MenB vaccine for their child if it were included in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention immunization schedule as optional and recommended by a healthcare provider (HCP); acceptance increased to 72.1% following receipt of IMD information. Perceived barriers to uptake of a potential MenB vaccine focused on HCP-driven communication and guidance. Most US parents/caregivers understood the importance of vaccination and indicated acceptance of a potential MenB vaccine for their child ≤ 18 months. This study highlights the critical role of HCPs in addressing concerns and knowledge gaps to promote IMD prevention in US children ≤ 18 months.

Introduction

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is an uncommon but serious infectious disease caused by Neisseria meningitidis that may result in death or long-term sequelae.1,2 In the overall population of the United States (US), there were 209–437 annual cases of IMD from 2015–2023 (incidence rate: 0.06–0.13 cases per 100,000 population).3–11 IMD incidence was highest among infants (< 1 y old; 0.33–1.14 cases per 100,000 population) compared to all other age groups and the overall population.3–11 Most cases of IMD in US infants are caused by meningococcal serogroup B (MenB).12–14 While vaccines against MenB are available in the US, they are not Food and Drug Administration-approved for children < 10 y old.14,15 However, MenB vaccination for infants is included in the national immunization programs of several countries.16 A recent US-based clinical trial studied the safety and immunogenicity of MenB vaccination in infants, though results were not available at the time of this study.17

Previous research has described knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding MenB vaccination in parents/caregivers of US adolescents, but these findings are not necessarily generalizable to parents/caregivers of infants.18,19 This study surveyed US parents/caregivers of children ≤ 18 months old to understand their general attitudes toward vaccination, knowledge of IMD, and preferences regarding a hypothetical MenB vaccine for children ≤ 18 months old (Table 1). Methods

Study design and recruitment

This was a US-based, cross-sectional, observational study, to capture parents’/caregivers’ KAP regarding general vaccination, IMD, and acceptance of potential infant MenB vaccination. Respondents completed an online survey using the Confirmit Horizons survey platform and were compensated for their time upon survey completion.

Parents/caregivers of children ≤ 18 months old were recruited from a representative sample of the US population, via a US consumer marketing panel (ROI Rocket), and screened for inclusion starting on November 14, 2024, until at least 1,000 parents/caregivers completed the survey (December 6, 2024). The marketing panel maintains large, first-party panels of parents/caregivers with pre-profiled demographic information. Demographic quotas were applied to guide recruitment toward the study’s target population (Figure 1); parent/caregiver inclusion and exclusion criteria applied during recruitment are also indicated in Figure 1. Study design.

A sample size of at least 1,000 parents/caregivers was sought based on a feasibility analysis as well as resource and timeline limitations, split by a mother/father quota of 800 (±5%)/200 (±20%) as informed by current literature indicating that more mothers than fathers tend to participate in surveys regarding healthcare decisions for their children,21–23 which likely reflects differences in healthcare decision-making between mothers and fathers.24 Of the target participants, quotas were also established for specific age ranges of both parents/caregivers and their children. The distributions were informed by ROI Rocket demographics and developed with the goal of representing parental attitudes throughout the first 18 months of a child’s life, as views on vaccination may change alongside distinct healthcare experiences.

Survey design

The de novo study survey was adapted from previous KAP survey literature related to vaccine perceptions,19 and designed based on expertise within the research team and a pre-testing phase with five parents/caregivers. The survey administrators did not release names of respondents to the survey sponsor and the survey sponsor’s name was not shared with the respondents. All data were reported on an aggregated, anonymized basis. The screening questionnaire included five questions, and the final survey had 28 questions (Supplementary File 1). Certain questions were posed before and after providing additional information on IMD to examine the impact of learning about IMD on responses. Most questions were closed-ended (e.g., multiple choice, 5-point Likert scales) with text fields used to clarify specific responses. For ease of understanding, IMD was referred to as “meningitis” throughout the survey.

Data analysis

Surveys were administered through a secure electronic data capture that prevented duplicate submissions. Only completed surveys were analyzed, and missing data were summarized descriptively as a separate category where applicable. All analyses were descriptive and performed using SAS® version 9.4 or later (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), Q Research Software (version 5.16.4.0), or Microsoft Excel. Responses to closed-ended questions were primarily summarized as the proportion of parents/caregivers selecting each response option. Responses to open-ended questions were manually coded into thematic categories (e.g., reasons for the decision to receive some recommended vaccines or none, important factors when deciding to vaccinate with a hypothetical MenB vaccine, factors influencing the decision not to vaccinate with a hypothetical MenB vaccine, barriers to accessing hypothetical MenB vaccination) for quantitative analysis using a predefined thematic framework. As additional responses were analyzed, nets and sub-nets were developed and refined, with the codebook iteratively evolving to reflect emerging patterns in the data. Certain outcomes were stratified by parents’/caregivers’ health literacy (measured according to their confidence in completing medical forms independently25) or separately, their highest level of education attained. Stratified results based on health literacy were aggregated and are presented as “higher” and “lower” health literacy for simplicity and to avoid small sample sizes. Responses recorded on Likert scales from “Strongly agree” to “Strongly disagree” are aggregated here as “agree” (“Strongly agree”/“agree”) and “disagree” (“Strongly disagree”/“disagree”); responses of “Likely” and “Very likely” are aggregated as “likely”/“very likely.”

Results

Parent/caregiver characteristics

A total of 64,883 parents/caregivers were invited to participate in the study, of whom 2,761 initiated the survey. After the exclusion criteria were applied, 1,051 parents/caregivers completed the survey and were included in the study (Supplementary File 2, Figure 1). Per established quotas, most parents/caregivers were mothers (77.2%), aged 26 to ≤ 35 y old (56.8%), and made most of the healthcare decisions for their child compared with other family members (74.7%). Most parents/caregivers self-reported being “very” (68.6%) or “quite a bit” (20.6%) confident filling out medical forms independently and hereafter are aggregated as parents/caregivers with “higher” health literacy. Conversely, 7.5%, 2.6%, and 0.7% reported being “somewhat,” “a little bit,” or “not at all” confident filling out medical forms independently, respectively, and hereafter are aggregated as parents/caregivers with “lower” health literacy. The highest proportions of parents/caregivers resided in the South (39.5%) and lived in suburban areas (44.0%). Most parents/caregivers identified as White or Caucasian (71.4%) and the largest proportion had a 4 y college degree (27.6%). Most did not know anyone who had been diagnosed with and/or treated for IMD (76.8%).

In alignment with established quotas, most children about whom the survey was completed were 6 to < 12 months old (45.0%). Parents’/caregivers’ general vaccination attitudes and practices

Most parents/caregivers agreed that vaccines are necessary to prevent infections (74.3%) and that vaccines work (70.9%), although 51.4% were worried about side effects. Over one-third of parents/caregivers (36.5%) agreed that there are too many vaccines recommended by HCPs for children ≤ 18 months old. Most parents/caregivers agreed that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines set the standard for children’s vaccination in the US (66.8%) and that they generally follow their child’s HCP’s recommendations for vaccines (77.2%). Over a quarter of all respondents (28.5%) agreed with the statement “I don’t trust vaccine manufacturers” (Figure 2). Parents’/caregivers’ general vaccine attitudes and practices.

The proportion of parents/caregivers who agreed that they follow their child’s HCP’s recommendations tended to increase with parent/caregiver education (lowest: 54.5% [vocational or technical school degree]; highest: 89.9% [postgraduate degree]; Supplementary File 2, Table 1). Moreover, parents/caregivers with higher health literacy more often followed their child’s HCP’s recommendations (80.1%) than those with lower health literacy (53.1%; Supplementary File 2, Table 2). Parents/caregivers with lower health literacy more commonly reported not trusting vaccine manufacturers (38.1%) than those with higher health literacy (27.4%).

Most parents/caregivers (73.4%) reported their child had received all recommended age-appropriate vaccines. Among the 266 (25.3%) respondents who indicated their child had received some or none of these vaccines, the top factors contributing to their vaccination decision included concerns about side effects (59.8%) or the safety of vaccine ingredients (54.1%), having heard negative vaccine experiences from friends or family (43.2%), not trusting vaccine manufacturers (42.9%), and/or negative attitudes about vaccines as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic (34.6%).

Most respondents (63.7%) reported their child’s HCP initiated vaccination conversations. Regarding the type of recommendation provided by their child’s HCP, 61.0% of parents/caregivers preferred a collaborative discussion, while 27.7% preferred a strong, direct recommendation. Most respondents preferred their child’s HCP spend time discussing the benefits and risks of each vaccine (58.1%), while 33.0% preferred to independently look up information about vaccines after listening to information their child’s HCP provided. Around one-third of respondents (30.2%) preferred for their child’s HCP to provide an alternative vaccination schedule to limit the number of vaccines received at each visit. Lastly, when asked about sources of information that help inform vaccination decisions, parents/caregivers selected hospital, HCP, institution, or healthcare websites (59.8%), researching about the vaccine (e.g., medical or CDC guidelines, National Institutes of Health [NIH], PubMed; 57.7%), educational pamphlets provided by their child’s HCP (44.3%), friends or family members (38.3%), and/or using online search engines (35.9%).

Parents’/caregivers’ IMD and MenB vaccine knowledge and attitudes

Of the nine IMD knowledge questions parents/caregivers were asked, 65.1% of respondents correctly answered ≥ 5. Most parents/caregivers correctly identified possible IMD symptoms (73.4%) and that IMD can be fatal (74.8%). However, knowledge gaps were observed regarding IMD transmission and diagnosis. Additionally, 23.6% of parents/caregivers correctly identified that adults are not at the highest risk for IMD. When asked if skin rash is the most common symptom of children ≤ 18 months old, 11.9% correctly identified this statement as false, while 52.3% did not know if the statement was true or false (Figure 3A). Parents’/caregivers’ IMD and vaccine knowledge and attitudes.

Parents/caregivers were asked about their likelihood of accepting a MenB vaccine for their child if it were included in the CDC vaccination schedule for children ≤ 18 months old as an optional vaccine and recommended by their child’s HCP. The survey described the vaccine as optional to reflect the non-routine nature of existing MenB vaccine recommendations for individuals 16–23 y old under shared clinical decision-making with terminology that is more widely understood. Parents/caregivers were asked this question twice: before and after reading a series of facts about IMD. The percentage of respondents who indicated they would be “likely”/“very likely” to accept the MenB vaccine for their child rose from 66.3% to 72.1% before and after receiving information about IMD, respectively (Figure 3B). Among all respondents, 75.3% were interested in learning more about IMD to make an educated decision about vaccinating their child with a potential MenB vaccine.

While 49.4% of parents/caregivers responded that their child was healthy and not at risk of getting IMD, 88.0% agreed that they would be concerned about IMD if their child contracted it. Over half (54.4%) agreed that a MenB vaccine is necessary to protect their child against IMD.

Prior to receiving information about IMD, a greater proportion of parents/caregivers who had higher education reported that they were “likely”/“very likely” to accept the MenB vaccine compared to parents/caregivers with lower education (lowest: 54.5% [vocational or technical school degree]; highest: 81.0% [postgraduate degree]; Supplementary File 2, Table 1). After receiving information about IMD, the proportion of parents/caregivers who reported that they were “likely”/“very likely” to accept the MenB vaccine increased in all education levels except one (some high school/no degree: −2.7%); the increase was smaller in those with higher education (lowest: +3.4% [postgraduate degree]; highest: +12.1% [vocational or technical school degree]). Additionally, a greater proportion of respondents with higher health literacy reported being “likely”/“very likely” to accept the MenB vaccine than those with lower health literacy prior to receiving information about IMD (69.9% vs. 36.3%, respectively; Supplementary File 2, Table 2). However, the higher and lower health literacy subgroups reported similar increases in acceptance after receiving information about IMD (+6.0% vs. +4.4%, respectively). Overall, parents/caregivers with lower health literacy were less interested in learning more about IMD compared with respondents with higher health literacy (41.6% vs. 79.3%, respectively).

When parents/caregivers were asked to identify the factors that would be important considerations when deciding to vaccinate their child with a potential MenB vaccine, the most frequently selected responses included: general information from their child’s HCP about the vaccine (52.4%); the risks of getting IMD if their child does not get the vaccine (51.0%); and/or information from their child’s HCP demonstrating the vaccine’s safety (50.6%). The most common factors parents/caregivers indicated would influence their decision not to vaccinate their child with a potential MenB vaccine included concerns about the side effects of a newly recommended vaccine (46.0%) and/or not knowing enough about the vaccine (39.5%).

Discussion

This study identified US parents’/caregivers’ KAP regarding general vaccination, IMD, and acceptance of a potential MenB vaccine for their child ≤ 18 months old. Most parents/caregivers agreed that vaccines work (70.9%) and are necessary to prevent infections (74.3%), indicating alignment with national policies and resources that emphasize to parents how infant vaccinations promote health and reduce the risk of serious and fatal diseases.26 Nevertheless, given persistent vaccine hesitancy among parents/caregivers,27,28 understanding vaccine- and disease-related KAP continues to be important for the protection of infant health.

Many parents/caregivers (66.3% before and 72.1% after receiving IMD information) indicated they would accept a MenB vaccine for their child if it were included in the CDC vaccination schedule as optional and recommended by their child’s HCP. Notably, for 52.4% of parents/caregivers, vaccine information from their child’s HCP was a top factor shaping their decision to vaccinate, demonstrating reliance on HCPs for informed vaccination guidance. Similar findings have been reported among parents of older adolescents for MenB vaccination,19 as well as for immunization decisions more broadly,29–31 reinforcing the role of HCPs in educating their patients and/or their parents/caregivers about vaccination. While parents/caregivers indicated high acceptance of a potential MenB vaccine (up to 72.1%), and 54.4% of parents/caregivers agreed that a MenB vaccine was necessary to protect their child against IMD, 49.4% did not believe their child was at risk for IMD. Given the relatively low incidence of IMD, the high proportion (76.8%) of parents/caregivers who did not know someone who had contracted IMD, and identified knowledge gaps regarding the risk and clinical presentation of IMD in infants, HCPs may need to develop strategies to effectively communicate IMD’s risk and severity.32,33 Findings from the present study on the influence of parent/caregiver perception of disease risk on vaccine decision-making may support the facilitation of more effective communication surrounding IMD prevention to audiences with varying perceptions of risk.34

Regarding current vaccination practices, 73.4% of parents/caregivers reported their child had received all recommended age-appropriate vaccines. This proportion is higher than findings from the 2011–2021 National Immunization Survey analyses, which reported that 52.2–59.4% of US children had received all Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices-recommended vaccinations by 19 months.35 However, the present study may overestimate actual vaccines received as vaccine uptake was reported by parents/caregivers. Despite high adherence to vaccination recommendations in this survey, 28.5% of parents/caregivers reported distrust in vaccine manufacturers and 51.4% revealed concerns with side effects, highlighting the need for targeted communication on vaccine safety.

Targeted communication initiatives are important in light of previous research demonstrating that parents’ vaccine-related safety concerns are frequently based on information obtained through the media (social media and traditional outlets), often focusing on rare and unexpected side effects and potentially presented in a way to attract viewers.36 In this study, receipt of information about IMD led to a greater proportion of parents/caregivers indicating MenB vaccine acceptance across almost all subgroups. Additionally, targeted communication may address the differences in vaccine attitudes and practices by parent/caregiver health literacy and education level that were identified in this study and were consistent with findings from previous studies.37–40 Therefore, the provision of tailored, IMD-related information from trusted health information sources may offer a promising approach to reaching parents/caregivers of various educational backgrounds and improving IMD prevention efforts.

Perceived barriers reported by parents/caregivers in this study to potential MenB vaccination focused heavily on the role of conversations with and information provided by HCPs. These findings align with prior studies suggesting that strengthening the HCP-patient relationship and HCPs providing clear guidance may be key to improving vaccine uptake and mitigating parental hesitancy.41,42 There is an opportunity for HCPs to address parents’/caregivers’ concerns by providing information that includes available evidence supporting the safety of MenB vaccination for infants and younger children from other countries where MenB vaccines have been routinely recommended.43–50

Most parents/caregivers (75.3%) indicated interest in learning more about IMD to make an educated vaccination decision, highlighting the potential for effective educational initiatives to shape parent/caregiver vaccine decision-making. Furthermore, when parents/caregivers received facts about IMD, the percentage who felt neutral decreased from 20.3% to 16.3% and more parents/caregivers became likely to accept MenB vaccination. These findings, together with previous research regarding other vaccines,51,52 indicate that education about IMD may be particularly meaningful for those unsure about vaccination.

Some parents/caregivers (36.5%) felt there are too many vaccines recommended for children ≤ 18 months old and 30.2% wanted an alternative schedule to limit the number of vaccines received at each HCP visit. A similar study found that 51.2% of HCPs felt the vaccination schedule for this age group is very crowded.53 In both studies, concern over a crowded schedule was coupled with acceptance of a potential MenB vaccine for children ≤ 18 months old, suggesting that these concerns may ultimately not preclude a parent/caregiver from choosing to vaccinate their child against MenB. Overall, the results presented here and from previous research demonstrate the importance of education on the value of current vaccination schedules and safety/efficacy of included vaccines for relevant stakeholders (e.g., parents/caregivers, HCPs, and policy makers). Future research is warranted to explore the underlying drivers of KAP differences based on parent/caregiver demographics, inform efforts to reach populations experiencing barriers to immunization, and improve vaccine acceptance.

Limitations

Potential limitations of this study include its descriptive nature and that it was not powered for statistical significance; therefore, findings must be interpreted in the context of relative observations among the study population. For example, no statistical comparisons were performed across subgroups or before and after parents/caregivers received information about IMD. Utilizing a US consumer marketing panel to identify parents/caregivers to complete the survey may have introduced bias toward greater participation of health-engaged parents. While the study aimed to obtain a representative sample of US parents/caregivers, survey participation was voluntary and internet-based, potentially introducing self-selection bias. Respondents may differ from the broader US parent/caregiver population, including potentially greater engagement with health-related topics or higher digital literacy. Parents/caregivers who are more knowledgeable about or hold more positive attitudes toward vaccines for children ≤ 18 months old may have been more likely to participate, potentially limiting generalizability of the results. The consumer panel that was used to identify participants was representative of US parents and caregivers; however, the results of this study should be interpreted as descriptive of only the surveyed population.

Stratification by some demographics resulted in small subgroup sizes, limiting the interpretability of subgroup results. To mitigate the impact of small sample sizes, results for select subgroups, such as health literacy, were presented in aggregated form. However, this approach may hinder more detailed understanding of heterogeneity across categories within these subgroups.

As this study was reliant on self-reporting, social desirability bias may have been present. To reduce this bias, respondents were informed about the purpose of the study and were reminded that the data would be anonymized and not disclosed to the study sponsor except in aggregated or non-identified form. Phrasing of questions was also neutral where possible to prevent leading respondents to a particular response. It is also possible that while completing the survey, participants could have looked up answers to knowledge questions inflating their real knowledge; to mitigate this risk, the survey included a prompt asking respondents to answer based on their current knowledge. In addition, recall bias may have led to participants misreporting past experiences or attitudes, potentially biasing their self-reported responses.

Following three overarching knowledge questions about meningitis, the survey defined meningitis and noted that meningococcal meningitis would simply be referred to as ‘meningitis’ for accessibility and ease of understanding. However, while IMD typically results in meningitis, it can also result in other manifestations such as septicemia; therefore, this choice of language may have simplified the disease or impacted the perceived risk and seriousness of the disease and led to an incomplete representation of parents’/caregivers’ knowledge of IMD and potential vaccine acceptance.

This survey assessed the willingness of parents/caregivers to accept a hypothetical MenB vaccine rather than actual vaccine uptake behaviors. Therefore, indicated vaccine acceptance measured in this study may not be reflective of real-life vaccine acceptance. Additional factors impacting vaccine uptake may include costs, time, and school policy contexts.19

Finally, this survey inquired about KAP pertaining to a vaccine that is not currently approved. It is possible that parent/caregiver views may have been different if the vaccine was already included in the CDC pediatric immunization schedule.

Conclusions

Most parents/caregivers understood the importance of vaccinations and reported that their child had received all age-appropriate immunizations. Overall, most parents/caregivers would accept a MenB vaccine for their child if it were included in the CDC immunization schedule as an optional vaccine and recommended by an HCP. Among parents/caregivers, the most commonly perceived barriers to MenB vaccination were related to information and recommendations from HCPs. Notably, attitudes toward and acceptance of potential MenB vaccination became more positive with receipt of information about IMD. These findings suggest that HCP-driven education for parents/caregivers may be important to mitigate concerns and address knowledge gaps about IMD and potential MenB vaccines.
