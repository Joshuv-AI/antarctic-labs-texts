Minimally invasive circumferential fixation using TIFI and INFIX for unstable pelvic ring injuries: a prospective evaluation of clinical and radiological outcomes
Authors: Mahmoud Fahmy, Fouad Zamel Sadek, Sherif Ahmed khaled, Mohamed Salah Said, Elsayed Kassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of minimally invasive circumferential fixation using combined trans-iliac internal fixation (TIFI) and anterior internal fixation (INFIX) in unstable pelvic ring injuries.MethodsA prospective study was conducted between 2022 and 2023 including 30 patients with unilateral unstable pelvic fractures (Tile B2 and C1). Patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months (mean 22 ± 4.7 months). Functional outcomes were assessed using the Majeed Score and Pelvic Outcome Score. Radiological assessment included displacement measurements, reduction quality, maintenance of reduction, and time to union.ResultsThirty patients (mean age 32.2 ± 14.9 years) were included. Mean operative time was 103.5 ± 10.5 min, blood loss 335 ± 65.8 mL, and fluoroscopy time 52 ± 14.7 s. Pelvic displacement improved from 15.8 ± 4.6 mm preoperatively to 3.6 ± 2.4 mm postoperatively, with a mean correction of 12.2 ± 4.5 mm and minimal loss of reduction (0.3 ± 0.8 mm). At one year, mean Majeed and Pelvic Outcome scores were 89.1 ± 5.6 and 34.6 ± 2.6, respectively. Mean time to union was 13.4 ± 2.1 weeks, with 90% excellent–good reductions (Matta criteria) and 100% union. Complications included superficial infection (13.3%), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve irritation (13.3%), and asymptomatic heterotopic ossification (3.3%).ConclusionCircumferential fixation using TIFI and INFIX achieved reliable reduction, maintained alignment until union, and was associated with favorable clinical and radiological outcomes in selected unstable pelvic ring injuries. The present findings demonstrate the feasibility and satisfactory performance of this technique; however, comparative studies are required to determine its relative advantages compared with other fixation strategies.

Introduction

Unstable pelvic fractures are severe injuries resulting from high-energy trauma and remain challenging because of the complex three-dimensional pelvic anatomy and the need to restore load-bearing stability [1–5]. Achieving stable fixation while minimizing surgical morbidity is essential for early mobilization and functional recovery [5–10].

Although open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) remains an established treatment, it is associated with extensive soft-tissue dissection, increased blood loss, and wound complications [11–15]. Consequently, minimally invasive surgical (MIS) techniques have gained popularity because they reduce soft-tissue disruption while providing adequate biomechanical stability [16–20].

Percutaneous iliosacral screw fixation is widely used for posterior pelvic ring stabilization; however, sacral dysmorphism, fracture comminution, narrow osseous corridors, and the risk of neurovascular injury may complicate safe screw placement in selected patients [20–25]. Alternative MIS strategies have therefore been developed to address these challenges [26–33].

Fragility fractures of the pelvis (FFP) represent an increasingly important problem in the aging population. Although many FFPs are stable and can be managed conservatively, advanced fracture patterns may be mechanically unstable and associated with persistent pain, impaired mobility, and functional decline, sometimes requiring surgical stabilization [11, 30]. The geriatric population presents additional challenges related to osteoporosis, compromised bone quality, comorbidities, and increased susceptibility to surgical morbidity. Importantly, recent evidence suggests that circumferential 360° stabilization is not necessarily required for all unstable FFPs, with isolated posterior stabilization providing satisfactory outcomes with a lower operative burden in selected elderly patients [11]. Therefore, fixation strategies for geriatric FFP should be individualized according to fracture morphology, instability, bone quality, and the patient’s clinical condition.

Previous clinical studies have demonstrated encouraging results with minimally invasive pelvic-ring fixation, particularly INFIX and percutaneous posterior fixation. A systematic review and meta-analysis of INFIX reported excellent or good radiological reduction in approximately 91.4% of patients [31]. More recent comparative studies have reported broadly comparable functional and radiological outcomes between minimally invasive posterior fixation and iliosacral screw fixation, while minimally invasive anterior fixation has demonstrated potential reductions in operative time and blood loss compared with open plating [32–34]. However, most available clinical evidence evaluates anterior or posterior fixation separately, and evidence specifically evaluating combined circumferential TIFI–INFIX fixation remains limited.

From a biomechanical perspective, adequate stabilization of unstable pelvic-ring injuries requires resistance to both rotational and vertical displacement. Experimental studies have demonstrated that minimally invasive anterior and posterior fixation constructs can provide substantial mechanical stability in unstable pelvic-ring patterns. Becker et al. demonstrated that a modified less invasive anterior subcutaneous fixator provided improved stabilization of unstable Tile-C pelvic-ring injuries, while Kußmaul et al. reported that different minimally invasive fixation strategies provided comparable mechanical stability in Type C pelvic-ring injuries [28, 29]. These findings support the concept that appropriately configured minimally invasive constructs can provide sufficient stability while limiting the soft-tissue disruption associated with extensive open fixation.

Circumferential fixation combining trans-iliac internal fixation (TIFI) and anterior internal fixation (INFIX) provides stabilization of both the posterior and anterior pelvic ring through a minimally invasive approach [20–34]. Despite encouraging biomechanical and early clinical results, evidence regarding reduction quality, maintenance of alignment, fracture union, functional recovery, and complications remains limited.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of circumferential fixation using combined TIFI and INFIX in patients with unstable pelvic ring injuries. We hypothesized that this construct would provide reliable stabilization, maintain reduction until union, and result in favorable functional and radiological outcomes with acceptable morbidity.

Patients and methods

This prospective clinical study was conducted at a University Hospital between March 2022 and August 2023 after institutional review board approval, with written informed consent obtained from all patients. The study evaluated clinical and radiological outcomes of circumferential fixation using combined trans-iliac internal fixation (TIFI) and anterior internal fixation (INFIX) for unstable pelvic ring injuries.

Patients aged ≥ 16 years with rotationally and/or vertically unstable unilateral pelvic fractures (Tile B2 or C1) were included. Unilateral injuries were selected to ensure cohort homogeneity and reduce confounding from bilateral patterns. LC1 injuries were included only when associated with instability features, including complete sacral fracture involvement, displacement > 10 mm, progression under examination under anesthesia, or vertical instability. Exclusion criteria included symphyseal diastasis, crescent fractures, acetabular involvement, open fractures, bilateral injuries, and skeletal immaturity.

All patients underwent standardized ATLS assessment with full neurological and vascular examination. Radiological evaluation included pelvic radiographs (AP, inlet, outlet, oblique views) and CT scans with multiplanar and 3D reconstructions. Fractures were classified using Tile, Young–Burgess, and Denis systems.

Preoperative displacement was measured using standardized pelvic radiographs and CT scans with multiplanar and three-dimensional reconstructions. Postoperative reduction was assessed using standardized AP, inlet, and outlet pelvic radiographs. Vertical displacement was assessed on outlet views, while residual displacement was assessed using the available standardized radiographic views. Measurements were independently performed by two blinded observers, with maximal displacement recorded in the relevant planes for multiplanar deformities.

Posterior fixation (TIFI) was performed with the patient in the prone position. Reduction of the posterior pelvic ring was initially facilitated by appropriate positioning and longitudinal traction applied to the lower extremity. When residual displacement persisted, percutaneous reduction clamps were introduced through small additional incisions and used to manipulate and compress the iliac bone until satisfactory alignment was achieved. Reduction was assessed fluoroscopically using anteroposterior, inlet, and outlet views before definitive tightening of the TIFI construct. Bilateral iliac screws were then connected with a subfascial rod to maintain the reduction. The patient was subsequently repositioned supine for anterior INFIX fixation using bilateral supra-acetabular screws and a subcutaneous connecting rod. [Fig. 1].

All patients received standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis for 24 h, while thromboprophylaxis was continued for 28 days postoperatively. Toe-touch weight bearing was initiated on postoperative day 1 to facilitate early mobilization, followed by progressive weight bearing as tolerated. Full weight bearing was permitted according to pain, clinical condition, and radiographic assessment, with no predefined prolonged period of restricted or non-weight bearing. Follow-up was at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months. Implant removal was performed after confirmed union when indicated.

Operative time, blood loss, fluoroscopy exposure, and complications were recorded. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Majeed and Pelvic Outcome Scores, while radiological outcomes included Matta grading, maintenance of reduction, and time to union. Fracture union was assessed on serial AP, inlet, and outlet pelvic radiographs obtained during follow-up. Union was defined by bridging callus across the fracture site with progressive disappearance of the fracture line, maintenance of pelvic alignment and absence of implant-related mechanical failure, together with clinical resolution of symptoms attributable to the fracture. Non-union was defined as persistent fracture lines without progressive radiological healing at 6 months, accompanied by persistent clinical symptoms suggestive of failure of healing.

Statistical Analysis: Continuous variables were assessed for distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Variables demonstrating an approximately normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Thirty skeletally mature patients with unilateral unstable pelvic ring fractures (Tile B2 and C1) were prospectively enrolled and followed for 12–28 months (mean 22 ± 4.7 months). The mean age was 32.2 ± 14.9 years, and 63.3% were female. All injuries resulted from high-energy trauma, including falls from height (63.3%) and road traffic accidents (36.7%). Associated injuries were present in 56.7% of patients, while 43.3% had isolated pelvic fractures. Fracture patterns included 24 lateral compression (LC1) and six vertical shear injuries, with Denis type 1 and type 2 sacral fractures accounting for 40% and 60% of cases, respectively (Table 1).

Patient demographics, injury characteristics, radiological, operative, functional outcomes, and statistical analysis

The mean interval from injury to surgery was 7.3 ± 3.3 days and the mean hospital stay was 14.4 ± 5.0 days. Twenty-eight patients (93.3%) were neurologically intact at presentation, whereas two patients (6.7%) presented with L5–S1 nerve root deficits, both of which resolved within 3–6 months without surgical decompression. No patient developed a new postoperative neurological deficit, perineal paresthesia, reduction in sphincter tone, urinary dysfunction, or other clinical evidence of sacral plexus injury during follow-up.

Mean preoperative pelvic displacement was 15.8 ± 4.6 mm, including a mean vertical displacement of 11.4 ± 3.8 mm and posterior ring displacement of 14.7 ± 4.2 mm. Following fixation, residual displacement decreased to 3.6 ± 2.4 mm, corresponding to a mean correction of 12.2 ± 4.5 mm. Reduction was maintained throughout follow-up, with a mean loss of reduction of only 0.3 ± 0.8 mm.

Mean technical operative time was 78.6 ± 8.9 min, increasing to 103.5 ± 10.5 min including repositioning. Mean fluoroscopy time was 52 ± 14.7 s and blood loss was 335 ± 65.8 mL.

At one year, the mean Majeed score was 89.1 ± 5.6 and the Pelvic Outcome Score was 34.6 ± 2.6. Fracture union occurred at a mean of 13.4 ± 2.1 weeks. According to Matta criteria, reduction was excellent in 50%, good in 40%, and fair in 10% of patients. All fractures united without implant loosening, fixation failure, or non-union [Figs. 2,3 and 4 are representative case examples]. All INFIX and TIFI implants were routinely removed after confirmed fracture union at a mean of 6 months postoperatively, with no observed adverse effect on functional or radiological outcomes following implant removal.

Complications included four superficial posterior wound infections (13.3%), four cases of transient lateral femoral cutaneous nerve irritation (13.3%), and one asymptomatic heterotopic ossification (3.3%).

Discussion

This prospective study found that minimally invasive circumferential fixation using a combined TIFI–INFIX construct was associated with favorable clinical and radiological outcomes in selected unstable pelvic fractures (Tile B2 and C1) [1–3]. All fractures achieved union, with favorable functional outcomes (mean Majeed score 89.1 and Pelvic Outcome Score 34.6) and excellent-to-good Matta reductions in 90% of patients. The technique was associated with an acceptable complication profile, with no deep infections, implant failures, or reoperations observed during follow-up, and permitted early mobilization.

Importantly, the cohort represented genuinely unstable injuries rather than minimally displaced fractures. Mean preoperative displacement was 15.8 ± 4.6 mm, exceeding accepted surgical thresholds. Fixation reduced displacement to 3.6 ± 2.4 mm (mean correction 12.2 ± 4.5 mm) with maintained reduction throughout follow-up and no clinically relevant secondary displacement. The performance of circumferential TIFI–INFIX fixation in vertically unstable injuries warrants further investigation. In the present series, six patients (20%) had Tile C1 injuries with vertical instability. All achieved fracture union and maintained satisfactory reduction during follow-up, with no implant loosening or fixation failure. However, the limited number of vertically unstable cases substantially restricts the strength of any conclusions regarding this subgroup. Vertical instability places greater demands on posterior fixation because the construct must resist both rotational and cranio-caudal displacement. Although the present clinical results suggest that TIFI–INFIX can provide adequate stabilization in selected vertically unstable injuries, the study was not designed or powered to evaluate this subgroup separately. These findings should therefore be regarded as preliminary, and larger comparative studies specifically addressing vertically unstable pelvic ring injuries are required before the reliability of this construct in such patterns can be established.

When compared with published clinical series, the outcomes observed in the present cohort appear broadly consistent with those reported for contemporary minimally invasive pelvic-ring fixation techniques. In our series, the mean total operative time, including patient repositioning, was 103.5 ± 10.5 min and mean blood loss was 335 ± 65.8 mL. These perioperative results are within the range reported for minimally invasive fixation strategies. Huang et al. reported significantly shorter operative time and lower blood loss with INFIX compared with anterior plating in patients with Tile C pelvic fractures undergoing combined anterior and posterior fixation, while postoperative Majeed and Matta scores were comparable between groups [32]. Similarly, comparative studies of minimally invasive posterior fixation have reported broadly comparable clinical and radiological outcomes between TIFI or ilioiliac fixation and iliosacral screw fixation, suggesting that fracture morphology and the technical feasibility of safe screw placement may influence the choice of posterior fixation method [33, 34]. The functional and radiological outcomes in our cohort were also comparable with pooled results from the INFIX literature. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled mean Majeed score of approximately 86.5 and excellent-to-good reduction in approximately 91.4% of patients, compared with a mean Majeed score of 89.1 ± 5.6 and excellent-to-good Matta reduction in 90% of our patients [31]. The incidence of transient lateral femoral cutaneous nerve irritation in our series (13.3%) was lower than the pooled rate of approximately 28% reported in the same meta-analysis [31]. These comparisons suggest that the clinical, radiological, and perioperative outcomes observed with the combined TIFI–INFIX construct are broadly comparable with those reported for established minimally invasive fixation strategies. However, these are indirect comparisons, and differences in fracture pattern, injury severity, patient characteristics, fixation constructs, and outcome definitions preclude direct statistical comparison. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that TIFI–INFIX is superior to anterior plating, iliosacral screw fixation, or other minimally invasive techniques.

Alternative techniques also have limitations. Iliosacral screws are technically demanding in sacral dysmorphism or narrow corridors and carry neurovascular risk [8–10]. External fixation is associated with pin-site infection and patient discomfort [1, 5–20]. Reported failures of percutaneous posterior fixation include loss of reduction due to poor screw purchase, sacral dysmorphism, or early mechanical overload [8, 9]. Thus, TIFI–INFIX should be considered a complementary rather than replacement technique.

Biomechanically, TIFI stabilizes posterior rotational and vertical forces while INFIX maintains anterior ring alignment, forming a circumferential construct that minimizes soft-tissue disruption [4, 13, 14]. Radiologically, mean correction of 12.2 mm and maintained alignment confirms construct stability.

Functionally, patients achieved excellent outcomes (Majeed 89.1; POS 34.6), with 50% excellent, 40% good, and 10% fair Matta results [3, 14, 15]. Complications were acceptable, including superficial infection and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve irritation (13.3% each) and asymptomatic heterotopic ossification (3.3%), consistent with reported minimally invasive pelvic fixation techniques [21, 22, 25]. Neurological deficits in Denis II fractures resolved completely [25].

Taken together, the available literature supports the feasibility of minimally invasive fixation strategies for unstable pelvic-ring injuries, with potential advantages in operative morbidity and functional recovery particularly where iliosacral screw fixation is technically challenging. Nevertheless, the evidence remains heterogeneous, and most published studies are retrospective, non-randomized, or involve relatively small cohorts. Therefore, the favorable results observed in our series should be interpreted as evidence of satisfactory clinical and radiological performance rather than proof of superiority over established fixation strategies.

The present study has several strengths, including its prospective design, standardized surgical technique, predefined clinical and radiological assessments, and relatively complete follow-up. However, the major limitation is the absence of a concurrent comparison group. Consequently, the present study cannot determine whether TIFI–INFIX is superior, equivalent, or inferior to established strategies such as iliosacral screw fixation, anterior plating, external fixation, or other minimally invasive constructs. Although comparisons with published studies provide useful clinical context, differences in patient selection, fracture morphology, injury severity, fixation strategy, and outcome assessment preclude direct statistical comparison across studies. Additional limitations include the relatively small sample size, single-center design, and restriction to selected unilateral Tile B2 and C1 injuries, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other pelvic-ring fracture patterns, bilateral injuries, or geriatric fragility fractures. Furthermore, the study was not powered to identify uncommon complications or to determine predictors of treatment failure. A further limitation is that postoperative reduction was assessed primarily using standardized pelvic radiographs rather than routine postoperative CT, which may limit the precision of three-dimensional assessment of residual displacement. Future comparative studies should incorporate standardized postoperative CT-based measurements to improve the accuracy and reproducibility of radiological outcome assessment.

Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as a prospective evaluation of the feasibility and clinical performance of the TIFI–INFIX construct rather than evidence of comparative superiority. Future multicenter prospective comparative studies or randomized trials directly comparing TIFI–INFIX with established posterior and anterior fixation strategies are warranted.

Conclusion

Minimally invasive circumferential fixation using TIFI and INFIX is a safe, effective, and reproducible technique for selected unstable [Tile B2/C1] pelvic ring injuries. It achieved excellent functional outcomes, reliable union, and durable maintenance of reduction, with mean pelvic displacement improving from 15.8 mm preoperatively to 3.6 mm postoperatively and remaining stable throughout follow-up.

These results support TIFI–INFIX as a valuable minimally invasive option, particularly in cases where iliosacral screw fixation is technically challenging due to sacral dysmorphism, narrow osseous corridors, or complex posterior ring patterns. Rather than replacing established methods, it should be considered a complementary reconstructive strategy.

However, the absence of a comparative control group precludes conclusions regarding superiority over iliosacral screws or ORIF. Larger multicenter comparative studies are required to define its optimal indications and long-term outcomes.
