Barriers to abortion access for young southerners: a qualitative analysis of case notes from ARC-Southeast
Authors: Ci’erra Larsen, Oriaku Njoku, Katie Labgold, Jalessah Jackson, Roger Rochat, Whitney Smith Rice
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung people in the U.S. Southeast navigate a constellation of legal, systemic and social barriers for abortion care. We identified and described barriers to abortion access experienced by young Southerners who sought abortion funding and the context they navigate to do so.MethodsWe conducted a secondary qualitative data analysis of case notes from people ages 21 and under who resided in six Southeastern states and contacted ARC-Southeast for support between January 2017 and May 2021 (n = 2,278). Nine-hundred and fifty information rich case notes were selected for thematic analysis.ResultsThe findings revealed young Southerners encounter a multitude of barriers addressed by abortion funds within four primary categories: structural barriers, financial barriers, personal barriers, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Case notes also described the context that youth seeking abortion care navigate, which are scarcely documented in abortion access literature, such as pre-existing conditions, intimate partner violence, and immigration status. We conceptualized these findings into a framework synthesizing the challenges, impacts, and opportunities for abortion provision in the U.S. Southeast.ConclusionsEach category of barrier contributed to constrained bodily autonomy and delayed abortion care. Abortion funds are uniquely situated among other key actors (e.g., health care providers, legal experts, other advocates, policymakers) and equipped to mitigate barriers that could otherwise deter and delay youth from obtaining abortion care and facilitate young people’s dignity and respect. This research highlights areas where such community-led and youth-centered efforts could be bolstered to address inequities in abortion access and other intersecting health and social systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25039-3.Keywords: Reproductive justice, Abortion, Pregnancy termination, Access to health, U.S. state laws, Sexual and reproductive health

Introduction

Following the 2022 United States (U.S.) Supreme Court Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision, near-total and early gestational duration abortion bans proliferated, concentrated in part in the U.S. Southeast (defined herein as Alabama, Florida, Georgia, Mississippi, Tennessee, and South Carolina) [1, 2]. These historically extreme gestational limits added to (or superseded) a slew of existing bans in the same states, including bans on use of state funding to cover all or most abortions for Medicaid beneficiaries, bans on private coverage of abortion in the Affordable Care Act’s Health Insurance Marketplace, and requirements that people seeking an abortion wait 24–48 h between pre-abortion counseling and getting an abortion. Young people in the Southeast are among social groups whose reproductive autonomy is disproportionately constrained by these policy changes, given pre-existing minor specific policies, the intersection of abortion stigma and infantilization, and the greater logistical complexity that young people navigate through to obtain abortion care as compared to adults, among other challenges [3–7].

All six U.S. Southeast states have parental involvement laws such as parental notification requirements, parental consent requirements, or both, with exceptions for medical emergencies [8]. Proponents of parental involvement laws suggest that these laws protect parent rights and enable parent awareness and child harm prevention. In contrast, these restrictions can have negative consequences for young people, such as family violence, coercion to continue an unwanted pregnancy, and delays in abortion care, which increase the likelihood of health complications [9, 10]. Minor-specific policy-level barriers to abortion access can be characterized as forms of structural-level abortion stigma [11].

Young people are subject to abortion stigma at other socioecological levels. At the societal-level, stereotypes about people seeking abortion invoke youth (i.e., teens and young adults) “not-yet” ready for parenthood [12]. Counterintuitively, state-mandated institutional-level hurdles -- from paperwork to communication needs -- on provider and patient sides inordinately affect teen pathways to abortion care relative to other health care [13]. At the interpersonal and individual levels most discussed in the literature, some young people experience or anticipate adverse responses (e.g., emotional abuse, shame, etc.) to disclosing an abortion or abortion need to providers, teachers, parents, partners, or others in their lives, although they may require and desire emotional and material support in their abortion care process [6].

Compounded with these multi-level abortion care access challenges, young people are less likely to know that they are pregnant as early as older individuals, which can contribute to delays in care seeking [14]. Later abortion care is more expensive than earlier abortion care, and for young people, paying the cost of an abortion may be complicated by greater likelihood of uninsurance, nonuse of insurance to avoid disclosure, and relatedly, greater likelihood to need support with the cost of obtaining an abortion—again furthering the likelihood of care delay as well [15–18]. Young people are additionally more likely than adults to report being driven to an abortion facility by someone else, suggesting a greater need for logistical support. Despite the constellation of legal, social and health difficulties impeding young people’s ability to exercise reproductive autonomy, there is limited documentation of youth experiences seeking abortion care [6, 7, 19–28].

Abortion funds, organizations that provide financial and logistical support to persons seeking abortion care, have historically played a key role in supporting communities disproportionately affected by state-level abortion restrictions to navigate multifaceted barriers to accessing abortion care [19, 29–31]. Access Reproductive Care-Southeast (ARC-Southeast) is an abortion fund providing funding and practical support (i.e., childcare, travel, and other logistical needs) for abortion services in the six U.S. Southeastern states since 2016 [32]. ARC-Southeast was among the first abortion funds to leverage case note data to understand barriers people encounter when accessing abortion care and contribute to the scarce research engaging abortion funds to understand abortion access [19]. Rice et al. (2021) found between 2017 and 2019 an estimated 1 in 10 of ARC-Southeast’s cases were people under the age of 22 years [19].

Therefore, an analysis of ARC-Southeast case data offers an opportunity to address a gap in the literature around young peoples’ experiences along the pathway to abortion care. Our study aims to: (1) identify barriers to abortion access experienced by young people in the abortion policy-restrictive U.S. Southeast who sought abortion funding from ARC-Southeast between January 2017 and May 2021, and (2) describe the context in which young people in the U.S. Southeast received abortion funding from ARC-Southeast between January 2017 and May 2021.

Methods

ARC-Southeast and the Center for Reproductive Health Research in the U.S. Southeast at Emory University partnered to conduct a qualitative analysis of ARC-Southeast’s case notes to achieve the study aims. Case notes are administrative data collected by ARC-Southeast staff in Salesforce during the abortion funding request intake process. A case is created for each distinct person requesting abortion funding (referred to by ARC-Southeast as “callers”) to document demographic information and link relevant case notes. Case notes consist of comments with information about the caller, such as the circumstances underlying their request, the funding requested, any practical support requests, and funding provided (Fig. 1). In cases involving minors who require judicial bypass - a judge order that allows a minor to obtain abortion care without parental notification or consent, corresponding case notes also contain record of coordination with regional reproductive health, rights, and justice organizations to schedule judicial bypass hearings.

ARC-Southeast Abortion Fund Healthline caller intake process utilized by ARC-Southeast Healthline staff during the data collection process between 2017 and 2021

The research team extracted a dataset containing cases (n = 2,029), and a separate dataset with case notes (n = 2,278) from January 2017 and May 2021 involving people under the age of 22 years who resided in the six U.S. Southeast states where ARC-Southeast serves. Case notes were de-identified and unlinked from demographic information assigned to cases to protect callers’ anonymity. Cases including at least 1 case note (n = 1,879) and occurring within a subset of years 2019–2021 (n = 1,043) were included in a preliminary qualitative analysis (Fig. 2). From there, included case notes required assignment of at least one deductive or inductive code, indicating that relevant information about barriers to abortion access were detailed in the note (n = 950; Table A1). Thematic analysis was deployed to systematically identify and organize themes across the data in the textual analysis software MAXQDA [33, 34]. The lead author led the following sequential steps: (1) developed initial deductive and inductive codes and code definitions, informed by conceptually relevant literature on young people’s abortion experiences and preliminary reading of the 1,043 case notes included in the aforementioned preliminary analysis; (2) created memos to initially summarize codes and representative case notes, and inform updated code definitions where needed; (3) engaged other research team members in review of key preliminary codes, code definitions, and representative case notes to discuss and identify resolution for any discordance in the data and as a form of peer debriefing for quality control; (4) applied codes to the final subset of 950 selected case notes for analysis; and (5) presented data to ARC-Southeast staff beyond the research team to assess whether the themes and codes were reflective of their experiences with youth callers [35]. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Emory University (IRB 00114890).

Results

Sample demographics

Among a total of 2,029 ARC-Southeast abortion fund callers aged 10–21 years, 84% were adults (aged 18–21 years) and 16% were minors (aged 17 years or under) (Table 1). The racial-ethnic composition of this group of callers was 53% Non-Hispanic Black, 12% Non-Hispanic White, and 3% Hispanic. Most cases were opened in 2019 (36%) and 2020 (27%) with the fewer cases opened in 2017 (7%), 2018 (12%), and 2021 (17%). About half of callers were residents of Georgia (46%) and over half attended a clinic in Georgia (56%). Most callers obtained a procedural abortion (61%) and fewer received a medication abortion (36%).

Conceptual framework: challenges, impacts and opportunities

The following interrelated and contextual barriers to abortion access for young Southerners emerged as themes in our analysis: structural, personal, financial, and the COVID-19 pandemic. We adapted a conceptual framework developed by Chowdhary (2022) to summarize key study findings regarding barriers to abortion access, their consequences, and opportunities to improve abortion care provision (Fig. 3) [36].

Conceptual framework summarizing the challenges affecting youth who seek abortion support in the U.S. Southeast between 2017 and 2021, the associated impacts, and potential opportunities to mitigate them

Structural barriers

Federal and state-level abortion restrictions, including parental involvement laws, insurance coverage bans, limits to gestation, and counseling and waiting period mandates, affected young Southerners’ ability to autonomously decide to have an abortion, pay for an abortion, and obtain timely abortion care.

Parental involvement laws

Case notes involving a judicial bypass described that affected callers were adamant about not involving a parent or guardian due to fear of consequences of disclosing their pregnancy and abortion decision. ARC-Southeast coordinated judicial bypass hearings for youth in the state where abortion care was provided, which occasionally resulted in out-of-state travel for judicial bypass proceedings. Case notes attributed avoidance of the judicial bypass process to the complexities of the legal proceedings and the challenges involved in gathering necessary legal documents. Some youth chose to delay care until their 18th birthday or attempted to receive a parent’s approval despite concerns for retaliation.

Insurance coverage bans

Many case notes mentioned that youth were insured through their parents’ insurance or under Medicaid, which largely does not provide abortion coverage in their states. Case notes described caller ineligibility for support from or limited financial assistance through a national abortion fund that ARC-Southeast collaborates with to provide funding assistance, based upon verified household income and insurance coverage. These exclusion criteria restricted funding options for some youth who wanted to fund their abortion without parental involvement. Case notes also documented ARC-Southeast support with Medicaid registration prior to uninsured callers’ abortion care appointments so that the cost of abortion care could be reduced via clinic discounts for Medicaid recipients.

Limits to gestation

Several state policies limiting gestational duration changed during or just prior to the study period, including a 24-week ban in Florida (June 2014) and Tennessee (March 2017), a 22-week ban in Alabama (May 2019), Georgia (May 2012), and South Carolina (May 2016), and a 20-week ban in Mississippi (July 2014) [19, 37]. Notably, in 2019 several states enacted 6-week abortion bans (Georgia and Mississippi) and Alabama enacted a total ban [38]. Notes from cases involving youth who misestimated their gestational duration noted caller surprise with their state gestational duration limit and with the cost of abortion care. Some case notes described clients obtaining ultrasounds that revealed a gestational duration later than the clinic’s service limit. In these situations, some clinics offered a refund. Callers then paid for another ultrasound at a clinic that could provide services.

When unable to access in-state abortion care under gestational limits, most young Southerners were able to travel for abortion care among the states where ARC-Southeast provided funding. Those who traveled farther distances for care frequently requested practical support. The distance individuals traveled for care was also influenced by caller preferences for lower costs and closer proximity. Callers who traveled out-of-state due to their state gestational limit experienced further barriers to care due to the need for coordination of extended lodging, transportation, time off from work or school, and concealment from parents.

Counseling and waiting period mandates

Many people contacting ARC-Southeast for support lacked funds for abortion care following use of most or all their funds for the first appointment designated for mandatory counseling. Case notes often mentioned that callers paid out-of-pocket costs for the ultrasound at their first appointment. This cost burden was amplified by the requirements in most of callers’ states that required them to wait between 24 and 48 h after their initial counseling visit for an abortion procedure. Case notes documented discussions around waiting periods referring to the challenges of scheduling, travel, and lodging to accommodate multiple appointments.

Personal barriers

Case notes captured examples of intrapersonal, interpersonal, and intersectional experiences of barriers to abortion access encountered by young Southerners.

Social support

Degree and source of social support varied across case notes. Various people provided callers with financial and logistical support during the study period. Mothers of callers were the most common supporters involved in the intake process, funding, and logistical assistance, regardless of the ranging levels of support they offered. Youth were asked to provide informed consent to proceed with the intake and to share any information with a parent or guardian. Case notes described that mothers supported youth during intake to prevent conflict, manage stigma, or mitigate trauma by protecting their children from other family members or abusive partners who threatened their child’s physical and psychological safety. Partners were the next most frequently mentioned source of social support involved during caller intake.

Some mothers and partners involved with intake did not provide financial or logistical support because they did not have funds or disagreed with the abortion decision. One caller’s partner refused to support the abortion because he desired for the pregnancy to be carried to term. To conceal her decision, she asked her social support for money for “bills.” Another case note involving a caller who chose not to seek support from their mother, stating “it’s not her problem.”

Concealment

Case notes included instances where youth explicitly described concealing their abortion decision to prevent retaliation from abusive partners and parents, as well as to preserve their privacy. Case notes also documented callers’ description of exposure to anti-abortion rhetoric from partners and family, due to which they anticipated negative consequences of abortion disclosure such as being kicked out of the house, spiritual condemnation, life endangerment, interference with the abortion process, and negative familial perceptions.

More than half of case notes that referenced concealment of an abortion highlighted safety concerns, including intrapersonal violence and homelessness. Case notes described that callers who were in abusive relationships or attempting to escape domestic violence concealed their abortion decision to avoid reproductive coercion from partners. For example, a young person who was unhoused and experiencing intimate partner violence expressed fear that their partner would coerce them into continuing an unwanted pregnancy. Callers perceived and realized safety-related consequences imposed by parents for becoming pregnant and having an abortion. Case notes detailed youth fear that parents would “kick them out” of the house if they were to either learn of the pregnancy, continue an unplanned pregnancy, or terminate their pregnancy. Unhoused youth were also prevalent in the case notes.

Violence

Various forms of violence against young abortion seekers were noted. More than half of case notes coded for sexual assault and rape were minors. Most rape and sexual assault case notes were handled by the callers’ mother or another guardian. In other cases, pregnancy disclosure threatened callers’ safety, and callers managed the intake process in isolation in attempt to ensure concealment from potential perpetrators of violence.

One case note detailed a domestic violence survivor who recently escaped their husband and was challenged to hide her daughter’s pregnancy to prevent retaliation from her husband. Case notes also described intimate partner violence including emotional and verbal abuse, stealthing—nonconsensual condom removal, and forced penetrative acts. Moreover, experiences of unhoused callers intersected with youth experiences of sexual violence. In one case note, a young person became unhoused as the consequence for disclosing a pregnancy that resulted from rape. In another, a young person experiencing homelessness became a victim of rape.

Parenting

About half of the case notes from callers who were parents and requested abortion fund support gave birth within the last year. These callers were concerned about supporting another child amid current challenges to afford infant care. One case note described a young parent who could not afford an abortion because they needed diapers and formula for their newborn. Case notes also indicated that mothers of multiple children, including the youth whose intake process they supported, were unable to afford the abortion procedure due to other competing childcare expenses.

Pregnancy morbidity and pre-existing conditions

Case notes mentioned that past experiences with pregnancy morbidity and loss, and presence of pre-existing conditions, influenced the callers’ emotional, physical, and mental capacity to continue a pregnancy and the availability of adequate medical care. Previous experiences with pregnancy morbidity, such as COVID-19 infection and chronic health conditions, as well as pregnancy and child loss, contributed to caller decisions to terminate their pregnancies. Pregnancies complicated chronic disease management and vice versa. For example, pregnancy disrupted blood transfusions or complicated cancer treatments. In contrast, chronic conditions compromised reproductive health care scheduling due to flare-ups, and chronic conditions limited or created volatility of financial resources (i.e., unemployed for medical reasons).

Youth with various health conditions resulting from injury or with chronic conditions experienced abortion care delays due to abortion providers hesitation or discomfort performing more complex procedures. For example, a severe injury caused by gun violence delayed care for a young person because there were limited providers who would provide abortion care. Moreover, the ability status of callers prompted additional support during intake to ensure speech and learning delays do not interrupt compassionate and comprehensive care.

Case notes also included a few instances where callers provided direct support to parents, grandparents, and children with health conditions or impairments. Youth who were caregivers had limited funding opportunities as they were not able to work while providing care or financially supporting family. Furthermore, disability insurance and other welfare benefits did not cover the cost of abortion care.

Student status

Case notes show that youth aged 21 and under are commonly pursuing education and often experience missed instruction time and scheduling conflicts to access state policy-constrained abortion care. For callers in grades 1–12, obtaining parental consent, scheduling abortion care appointments and attending them was difficult due to conflicting school hours and scheduled extracurricular activities. Those pursuing higher education faced pressure related to academics, work, and athletic demands. For instance, a young person had to reschedule their procedural abortion appointment because they did not want the timing to fall during midterm exams.

Language

Interpreters were not available daily for languages outside of Spanish and English. Callers who spoke another language had to either find someone they trusted who spoke English or Spanish or contact ARC-Southeast for support when contracted interpreters were available.

Immigration status

Political rhetoric during the study period ignited fear among immigrant families seeking abortion care. Immigration status was a barrier for undocumented individuals and mixed-status families. Case notes documented one parent worried that abortion funds and clinics would ask for their immigration status and report their information to the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, which they anticipated would result in deportation and family separation.

Abortion type

Case notes referenced personal preferences for an abortion method that were influenced by factors such as cost, timing, and privacy. Medication abortion was often preferred by callers who were concerned about receiving confidential and timely care. For instance, one young person requested a medication abortion to simulate a miscarriage because family members insisted that they continue the pregnancy. Callers whose priority was to conceal their pregnancy and were looking to travel less than an hour from home or in state typically did not prefer procedural abortion. Many callers planned cover stories to avoid detection, often involving telling their parents they were at a friend’s house while making arrangements for the procedural abortion. Among youth who preferred procedural abortions, the most important factor was affordability.

Financial barriers

Callers who sought funding navigated unstable employment opportunities, with part-time work and unemployment most commonly referenced in the case notes. Youth often relied on volatile employment opportunities with inconsistent wages, which made it harder to plan and pay the full cost of abortion care. Unemployed callers and their families experienced barriers to pay for any abortion expenses. Unforeseen circumstances, such as theft, loss of a guardian, car trouble, and housing damage, also affected youth and their financial resources. Case notes documented recurring expenses (i.e., rent, utilities) often competed with the cost of abortion care.

Youth explicitly asked ARC-Southeast staff to fund an amount such that they could discreetly ask for financial support for the remaining balance within their social network (specifically family members) without raising “red flags”. Youth were concerned that parents who monitor their finances might ask questions and force them to disclose their abortion decision.

COVID

Case notes described that COVID-19 related unexpected layoffs, reductions in work hours, clinic closures and client isolation periods due to exposure in the workplace made it difficult to access care. Youth also encountered unexpected changes in living situations as universities closed, directly impacting access to care. Some young people were sources of family financial support, and the pandemic offered an opportunity to provide increased support. For example, one young person cited sending remittances to family abroad who were struggling to support themselves during the pandemic.

Discussion

Our study findings provide evidence of the structural, financial, personal, and pandemic-related barriers to abortion access experienced by young people in the U.S. Southeast. This study contributes to a dearth of literature documenting strategies that youth and their support systems use to circumvent the unique constellation of obstacles that youth face in navigating abortion access barriers. The data reported were collected and analyzed prior to the Dobbs decision and resulting state legal changes, yet many of the difficulties reported and needs to alleviate them have only exacerbated [39].

Consistent with previous studies of youth abortion access, delays in obtaining care were observed due to parental involvement laws, limits to gestation, and counseling and mandatory waiting mandates [20–26, 29, 40]. Our data also provided further evidence of coercion by parents and partners influencing young people’s abortion decision making [27, 39, 41]. Case notes indicated that among youth who required assistance from parents or partners during abortion fund intake, there was concern about significant consequences if their pregnancy or abortion decision was disclosed to an unsupportive social network. These consequences included but were not limited to those documented in the literature, such as homelessness, life endangerment, reproductive coercion, and debt [42–44]. The overall limited financial stability of many young people stifled their access to abortion care and escalated financial, food, and housing insecurity for both the youth and their families.

We identified three important limitations to this study. First, ARC-Southeast did not intend to collect the administrative data analyzed here for research purposes. Case note management changed over time (i.e., documentation increased) and seldom included direct quotes from callers. Secondly, case notes did not exclusively capture the experiences of youth. In some cases, intake interactions were a reflection of conversation with another individual who supported the caller. Finally, the analysis of case notes rather than cases limited our reporting of individual profiles and identification of callers who accessed services from ARC-Southeast more than once. Previous data analysis showed 4% of all callers between 2016 and 2019 were potential repeat callers, which suggests that most cases in the dataset represent distinct individuals [19].

Despite these limitations, we present qualitative evidence of the barriers that young people in abortion restrictive states navigate when seeking abortion care. The abortion fund data reflected in our findings offer insights into several southeastern U.S. states that are underrepresented in existing literature. These data highlight the impacts of widening disparities for young abortion seekers, particularly those who are most marginalized, including minors, people of color, and individuals with low incomes amid increasing restrictions. Our data also captured effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and found evidence that the pandemic amplified existing barriers to abortion access in the U.S. Southeast. Our analysis of case notes revealed directions and considerations for future community-led and youth-centered reproductive justice research and advocacy around experiences of homelessness, intrapersonal violence, pre-existing conditions, and immigration concerns [45]. Additionally, this research is among few studies that involved abortion funds as community-based partners, contributing their insights throughout the research process.

Conclusions

Young Southerners who contact abortion funds experience a number of barriers that impede their bodily autonomy and delay abortion care. Reproductive health, rights, and justice organizations, healthcare providers, policy makers, and advocates may integrate these findings into opportunities to mitigate if not eliminate barriers for young abortion seekers. For example, advocates in Georgia have proposed “The Reproductive Freedom Act,” that urges lawmakers to guarantee safe, legal, and affordable abortions to youth without barriers or fear by providing additional options to navigate parental notification laws [46]. Additionally, governmental efforts have recognized and resourced abortion funds’ knowledge and skills in supporting abortion care in abortion restrictive environments. The City of Atlanta allocated $300,000 to support the efforts of ARC-Southeast in 2022 [47]. These efforts required coordination among reproductive health, rights, and justice organizations, healthcare providers, policy makers, and advocates. This research highlights key areas where community-led youth-centered efforts could be bolstered to address systemic and policy intensified barriers to abortion access and other intersecting health and social inequities.
