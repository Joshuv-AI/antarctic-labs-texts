Factors influencing Major lower extremity amputations in Johannesburg
Authors: M AbuOwn, ATO Abdool-Carrim, I Cassimjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMajor lower extremity amputations (MLEA) are common and place an undue burden on society. They result in major morbidity as well as increase mortality for individuals. Little is known in South Africa about the primary causes of amputations. Understanding this will hopefully lead to prevention of amputations.AimThe aim was to provide insights into the underlying causes, demographic distribution, risk factors for amputations, prior revascularisation attempts as well as to assess the quality of the amputations in the vascular unit at a major tertiary referral hospital vascular unit in Johannesburg (South Africa).MethodsClinical records of the vascular unit at CMJAH, theatre records, and NHLS laboratory results were retrospectively studied to identify all adult patients who underwent MLEA in 2017 and 2018.ResultsA total of 178 patients (64.04% male, 35.96% female) with a mean age of 62.48 years (± 12.19) were studied, with the majority being of African origin (57.86%). The leading cause of amputation was chronic limb-threatening ischemia (73.60%), followed by acute limb ischemia (ALI) (11.12%) and trauma (5.06%). Amputations were performed above the knee in 55% of cases and below the knee in 45%. The rate of conversion from below-knee to above-knee amputation was 12%. Black African patients were more likely to undergo above-knee amputations and were younger at the time of amputation compared to white patients. Among those with peripheral artery disease (PAD) who required amputation, only 30% had undergone a previous revascularization attempt. In the subgroup of patients presenting with ALI (n = 40), only 11 had attempted revascularization. Of those who had prior revascularization, the primary reasons for subsequent amputation were failed revascularisation or ongoing sepsis.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that patients referred to a tertiary vascular referral centre in Johannesburg, are more likely to receive an amputation a decade earlier in life for CLTI, with only 30% receiving an attempt at revascularisation. Furthermore, they are more likely to receive an AKA, and both the level of amputation and lack of revascularisation probably reflect the advanced stage of disease presentation. This study emphasises the importance of improving access to medical care and strengthening referral patterns in order to facilitate earlier referral when limbs are still potentially salvageable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-03896-2.

Introduction

Amputations are often viewed as treatment failure. The last two decades have seen a marked increase in endovascular interventions, however, it is still not clear if they result in a conclusive improvement in amputation-free survival (AFS) [1]. Within the context of South Africa, it is not known if patients receiving amputations have had a previous attempt at revascularisation, nor is the underlying pathology resulting in the amputations known.

Amputations are a worldwide phenomenon and, in the USA, approximately 2 million people are living without a limb. Approximately 158 000 amputations are performed in the USA each year, of which 54% are due to vascular disease (diabetes mellitus or peripheral vascular disease), 45% due to trauma, and approximately 2% due to cancer [2]. In 2015, Diabetes UK reported that more than 135 amputations are performed as a complication of Diabetes Mellitus (DM) every week [3]. In the South African province of Kwazulu-Natal (population: 11 million), approximately 2400 amputations are performed annually in the public sector [4]. Notably, there is only one state run vascular surgical centre in this province.

Major lower extremity amputation (MLEA) is associated with significant short and long-term mortality. A 2013 Dutch study quoted 30-day mortality of 22%, 44% at 1 year, and 77% at 5 years [5]. A study from Switzerland undertaken between 2000 and 2017 with A total of 312 patients (65.7% male) with a mean age of 73.3 ± 11 years underwent 338 major amputations, and reported 1-year mortality of 35–48% [6]. South Africa (higher-middle income) and many other lower-middle-income countries have no accurate national data on mortality rates after amputation. Furthermore, the epidemiology and risk factors for MLEA are not clearly defined either. It is assumed that these are similar to Western populations.

The rate of amputations in diabetics is reported to be an indication of a health system’s performance and has been studied by the OECD [7]. The multinational study of Behrendt et al. demonstrated decreasing major amputation rates in almost every country, although they noted strong differences among countries, with a mean incidence of major amputation varying between 7.2 and 41.4/100,000 [8]. Declining amputation rates have been reported in England [9] and in the USA over a 15-year period, the lower limb amputation rate has decreased by 45% among the Medicare patient population. During this period, endovascular procedures have increased (both diagnostic and therapeutic) and preventive care and wound management have improved [10]. However, some countries such as Germany reported an increase in both revascularisation and amputation rate. This may be a result of an increased prevalence of the leading risk factors for amputations such as PAD and DM. Rates of major amputation in diabetics remain six times higher than in non-diabetics [11]. Furthermore, DM has reached epidemic proportions, with an estimated 32% increase globally [12].

The aim of this study was to provide insights into the demographic distribution, risk factors for amputations, and prior revascularisation attempts and to assess the quality of the amputations in the vascular unit at a major tertiary referral hospital vascular unit in Johannesburg (South Africa).

Methods

Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of the Witwatersrand (M180916), the Chief executive officer (CEO) of Charlotte-Maxeke Johannesburg academic hospital, and the National health laboratory service (NHLS). This was a retrospective observational study based on a two-year audit of patients’ records of the weekly morbidity and mortality (M&M) meetings of the department of surgery. The M&M data are stored on the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform. Additional information was obtained from theatre records and patients’ discharge summaries. Data retrieved included demographic information, co-morbidities, cause and level of amputation, a prior attempt of revascularisation, and overall outcome including if there’s a conversion from BKA to AKA. The records of all patients who were operated on at CMJAH between the 1st of June 2017 and the 31st of December 2018, were reviewed.

Statistical analyses

Data were entered into a spreadsheet. Statistical analyses were performed using appropriate software, where p < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. The preoperative and postoperative factors related to MLEA were described using the frequency distribution for categorical variables and the mean ± standard error (SE) of the mean for continuous variables. Then, data were examined using Pearson’s χ [2], the test of association for categorical variables, and Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. If a significant (p < 0.05) relationship was found, then a post hoc residual analysis for categorical variables and a Fisher’s least significant difference test for continuous variables were applied to determine the exact significance between groups for each variable. Figures were created with GrapPad Prism verision 9.0.

Results

In this study, 178 patients had a major lower extremity limb amputation (MLEA) at the Charlotte-Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital (CMJAH) vascular surgery department between January 2017 and December 2018. Demographically, there was an almost 2/3rds ratio of males to females (114:64, Fig. 1a), with African males constituting the largest group (65 out of 178), followed by white males (43 out of 178). The mean age of patients was 62.48 years (SD 12.19), with most patients between the ages of 50 and 80 (78.09%)( Fig. 1b). There were 24 patients (13.48%) below the age of 50, of which 8 (30%) were secondary to trauma, 3(12.5%) were as a result of HIV, 3 of these 8 (12.5%) amputations were diabetics with sepsis and the rest (10 patients, 41.66%) were secondary to chronic limb threatening ischaemia or acute limb ischaemia (Fig. 1b). For the entire cohort, the underlying cause for the amputations was CLTI in 54.49% of patients, followed by acute limb ischaemia (16.85%). Other reasons for amputation were, HIV in 5.62%, malignancies in 1.12%, and trauma in 2.81%. (Fig. 1c).

Level of the amputation and conversion to AKA

An AKA was performed in 55%, and a BKA accounted for 45% of all the amputations respectively (Fig. 2a). This is a ratio of 1.2 (AKA to BKA). The conversion incidence of BKA to AKA was 12.34% (Fig. 2b). There was no significant association between the level of amputation and the sex, but older patients were more likely to get an above knee amputation (60 vs. 64 years, p < 0.026) (Fig. 2c). There was no significant association between the level of amputation and varying comorbidities. However, we found that black Africans were not likely to get a primary amputation at a younger age and even those that were revascularised were amputated at a younger age.

Revascularisation

Our data demonstrated that only one-third (29.78%) of patients with CLTI or ALI had a prior attempt at revascularisation and that two-thirds (70.22%) had a primary amputation. The results did not demonstrate a significant association between HIV status, sex, ethnicity and comorbidities (Fig. 3a). In those that received revascularisation, the reasons for amputation were ongoing sepsis (28%), a thrombosed graft (45%), or a failed endovascular procedure (26%) (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Our study reviewed 178 patients that underwent a major lower extremity limb amputation (MLEA) at the Charlotte-Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital (CMJAH) vascular surgery department between January 2017 and December 2018. We retrospectively examined their clinical characteristics and indications for amputation. Alarmingly, our study found that almost 70% (125/178) of the patients’ required a primary MLEA without a prior attempt at revascularisation. The CMJAH vascular unit is a regional referral unit.

In the literature there is a paucity of data on amputations in higher-middle and lower-middle income countries. This is therefore an important study aimed at identifying the main reasons for MLEA. This study also assists in understanding the referral patterns to a specialised vascular unit in the public sector. South Africa has a two-tiered health care system, where most of the more than sixty million population are served by a public health care system; and where there are only 18 full-time vascular surgeons (VS). This represents a ratio of one vascular surgeon per three million which far exceeds the ratio in the private sector or the average in other OECD countries where the ratio is one per one hundred thousand [13].

The major cause for MLEA was CLTI, followed by ALI, and then trauma. In our study, the racial demographics of patients receiving an MLEA were 58% black African and 37% white South African. While it appears that more black people undergo amputations, the numbers reflect the demographics as the population of South Africa is 79% black African and only 10% white. Additionally, most patients presenting to the public sector are black Africans [14]. International literature reports that the MLEA rate is higher for black patients when compared to whites, and in some areas, it is as much as 5-fold higher [10]. In comparison, our data would suggest a higher rate of amputations in the white population presenting to CMJAH. There are two possible explanations for this. The first is that the CMJAH referral pattern may represent a sample bias as referral patterns are grouped into referring clusters, and as South Africa had previously segregated living areas, the referral pattern may represent an area serving a previously white-dominated referral pattern. The second is that the rate of atherosclerosis in black Africans is lower than in whites [15] in South Africa, although that is now changing with urbanisation. Although the rates of amputations are higher in white South Africans, the age of amputation is lower in Black South Africans (61 vs. 64 years). This is in keeping with the international literature which suggests a lower age of amputation in black populations [10], and may reflect poorer management of underlying precipitating conditions for an amputation.

With regards to primary amputation, 70% of our patients had a primary amputation with no prior attempt at revascularisation. When compared to data from developed countries, this is high. A study that examined Medicare data between 2003 and 2006 reported that 54% of patients with CLTI who underwent major lower extremity amputations had no attempt at revascularisation in the year preceding the amputation [16]. The British numbers were slightly higher with primary amputation rates at 56.7% in a study done over a similar period [17]. When we compared racial demographics between revascularised and non-revascularised patients, they were similar. The rate of primary amputations may be a surrogate for advanced disease presentation and may reflect poor referral patterns. In patients who were previously revascularised, failed revascularisation and ongoing sepsis were the major cause of amputations.

The male predominance of 64.04% in our study is consistent with other studies [18–20] in the literature and likely reflects the male predominance of PAD [15]. However, when comparing to international data, our cohort of patients was amputated almost a decade earlier (mean age = 62), than patients internationally 70 to73 years [18, 20, 21]. Lower socio-economic status, lack of access to medical care and poor patient follow-up may all play a role in this. We did not find any racial differences in the ages of amputations in the cohort, including those that had a primary amputation. One would assume that the ages of amputations of white patients would be similar to those in Europe; however, this was not the case. We considered that HIV may have played a role in the earlier presentation of patients with PAD, however, our cohort had low rates of HIV and was therefore not a causative factor.

The BKA to AKA ratio was 1:1.2 in our cohort, which is higher than the reported literature [20, 22, 23]. The ratio of BKA to AKA is a surrogate marker for the quality of a vascular surgical service. Recent data from the British National Vascular Registry reported that approximately two-thirds of MLEA performed between 2014 and 2016 were BKAs [24]. This is the ratio that would indicate a good quality service. Even in underdeveloped countries such as Jordan, the reported ratio is 3:1 [10]. We assume that the ratio observed in our study reflects delayed referrals, lack of access to vascular services and inadequate clinical assessment during early disease stages. Therefore, many of our patients presented with advanced disease that rendered limb salvage attempts futile [25].

The early revision rate of BKAs to AKAs in our study was 12.43% which is comparable to the rates reported by Aulivola et al. and Dillingham et al. (9.4%) [18, 26]. However, our revision rates were lower than those reported by Lim et. al(17.6%) [21], and almost the same revision rate was reported by Cruz et al. (12%) [20]. Our similar rate of revision may be confounded by the high number of primary AKAs. In 2016, The Vascular Society of Great Britain and Ireland Amputation Quality Improvement Framework recommended that MLEA should be performed using a planned operating list in the presence of a consultant vascular surgeon. A significant decrease in revision rates from 28.6% in 2012 to 5.0% in 2017 was reported in the study done in Jordan when amputation was done by a vascular surgeon [27].

This study was conducted at the Charlotte-Maxeke Johannesburg academic hospital, which is 1 of 2 state hospitals with a vascular surgical unit in Johannesburg. They serve a population of an estimated 8 million people within the greater Johannesburg metropolitan area, as well as referrals from neighbouring provinces where there are no vascular surgeons in state practice. This is thus a limitation of this study and must be taken in this context as these outcomes are not generalisable. The parallel private health care system is juxtaposed to the state system and is a modern well capacitated system with good referral systems and funded through health insurance but caters for only 15% of the population.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that patients referred to a major tertiary vascular referral centre in Johannesburg, South Africa, are more likely to receive an amputation a decade earlier for CLTI, with only 30% receiving an attempt at revascularisation. Furthermore, they are more likely to receive an AKA, and both the level of amputation and lack of revascularisation probably reflect the advanced stage of disease presentation. This study emphasises the importance of improving awareness around PAD, improving access to medical care and better referral patterns.
