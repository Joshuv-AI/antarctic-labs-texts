Glucagon-like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists and Risk of Tuberculosis in Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Kuang-Ming Liao, Jheng-Yan Wu, Chih-Cheng Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis remains a leading cause of illness and death worldwide, and individuals with type 2 diabetes have an increased risk of developing this infection. However, whether different glucose-lowering medications influence this risk is unclear. Here we show that use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists is associated with a lower risk of tuberculosis compared with several commonly prescribed alternatives. We conducted a multicenter cohort study using TriNetX electronic health records from 143 healthcare organizations across multiple countries between 2017–2025. Patients receiving glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists were compared with those receiving sulfonylureas, metformin, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. Propensity score matching and time-to-event analyses were applied over up to 5 years of follow-up. Use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists is consistently associated with reduced tuberculosis risk compared with other agents. Incidence rates per 1,000 person-years ae lower than with sulfonylureas (0.68 vs 1.67; hazard ratio 0.53, 95% confidence interval 0.47–0.59), metformin (0.65 vs 1.31; 0.60, 0.51–0.70), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (0.73 vs 1.71; 0.49, 0.43–0.56) and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (0.89 vs 1.02; 0.82, 0.72–0.92). These findings suggest that this class of medications may provide additional protection against tuberculosis in patients with type 2 diabetes.Subject terms: Outcomes research, Respiratory tract diseases, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

In 2023, the global burden of tuberculosis (TB) rose to an estimated 10.8 million cases (134 per 100,000 population)1,2. TB has reemerged as the leading cause of death from an infectious disease globally, surpassing COVID-192. Notably, 8.2 million new TB cases were reported in 2023, the highest annual figure since the World Health Organization (WHO) began systematic monitoring in 19952,3. These data underscore the continued urgency of TB prevention, particularly among populations with heightened susceptibility to infection. One such high-risk group is individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D), a chronic metabolic disease whose global prevalence is rising rapidly4. T2D could be associated with a two- to threefold increased risk of active TB, likely mediated by chronic hyperglycemia-induced immune dysfunction and microvascular complications4,5. As the dual epidemics of TB and T2D increasingly intersect, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, it becomes critical to understand not only the pathophysiologic link between the two diseases but also the impact of T2D treatments. Specifically, there is growing interest in whether different classes of glucose-lowering therapies might influence TB susceptibility, either by attenuating or amplifying risk. Clarifying these relationships is essential for guiding both clinical decision-making and public health strategies aimed at protecting this high-risk population.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are a newer class of glucose-lowering agents that have demonstrated multifaceted benefits beyond glycemic control, including cardiovascular and renal protection, weight loss, and potential anti-inflammatory effects6,7. Preclinical and translational studies have suggested that GLP-1 signaling may modulate innate and adaptive immune responses8–10, raising the possibility that GLP-1RAs could influence host susceptibility to infections11, including TB. Yet, despite these immunomodulatory properties, clinical evidence regarding the relationship between GLP-1RA use and TB risk remains limited. In contrast, other commonly used glucose-lowering agents, such as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP-4is), sulfonylureas, and metformin, have been more extensively evaluated in this context, with mixed findings12–15. Given the rising global prevalence of T2D, the increasing adoption of GLP-1RAs in clinical practice, and the persistent burden of TB, clarifying the impact of GLP-1RA therapy on TB risk is essential to inform evidence-based treatment decisions and guide public health strategies.

To address this important knowledge gap, we conducted a large, retrospective cohort study to evaluate the risk of incident TB among patients with T2D initiating GLP-1RA therapy, compared with those receiving SGLT2is, DPP-4is, sulfonylureas, or metformin. This study aimed to determine whether GLP-1RA use is associated with a differential risk of TB and to provide real-world evidence to inform clinical decision-making and public health strategies in this vulnerable population.

Results

Patient selection

We utilized the TriNetX global federated health research network, comprising data from 143 HCOs and encompassing a total of 153,884,610 individuals as of August 7, 2025. Within this network, we identified 117,081,180 individuals who had accessed health care services at least once between January 1, 2017, and June 30, 2025. Of these, 7,297,342 were diagnosed with T2D. After applying exclusion criteria and PSM, three comparative cohorts were constructed based on exposure to GLP-1RA versus SU, metformin, DPP-4i, or SGLT2i. Specifically, the GLP-1RA versus SU cohort included 613,371 patients per group; the GLP-1RA versus metformin cohort included 359,310 patients per group; the GLP-1RA versus DPP-4i cohort included 533,713 patients per group and the GLP-1RA versus SGLT2i cohort included 574,060 patients per group. The median duration of follow-up differed across comparison groups, with median follow-up times of 868 vs 772 days for GLP-1RA versus SGLT2is, 778 vs 1789 days for GLP-1RA versus SU, 784 vs 1595 days for GLP-1RA versus DPP-4is, and 767 vs 1442 days for GLP-1RA versus metformin (Fig. 1).

Primary outcomes

GLP-1RA vs Sulfonylureas

Compared with SU, GLP-1RA use was associated with a significantly lower risk of TB, with rates of 0.68 and 1.67‰, respectively (HR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.47–0.59; E-value, 3.2 [95% LCL, 2.8], Table 2). In addition, the Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrates a significantly lower cumulative incidence of TB in the GLP-1RA group compared with the SU group over the follow-up period (log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 2A).

GLP-1RA vs Metformin

Compared with metformin, GLP-1RA use was linked to a significantly reduced risk of TB, with incidence rates of 0.65 and 1.31‰, respectively (HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.51–0.70; E-value, 2.7 [95% LCL, 2.2]; Table 2). Furthermore, the Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significantly lower cumulative incidence of TB among GLP-1RA users compared with those receiving metformin during the follow-up period (log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

Subgroup analyses comparing GLP-1RA use with metformin demonstrated consistent protective associations across most patient characteristics (Fig. 4). Statistically significant risk reductions were observed across age groups, sex, underlying conditions, HbA1c levels, and BMI categories, with HRs ranging from 0.54 to 0.74. However, the association was not statistically significant among patients with CKD (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.60–1.10), though the point estimate remained consistent with a protective effect.

GLP-1RA vs DPP-4i

Compared to DPP-4i, the use of GLP-1RA was associated with a significantly lower risk of TB, with rates of 0.73 and 1.71‰, respectively (HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.43–0.56; E-value, 3.5 [95% LCL, 3.0]; Table 2). In addition, Kaplan–Meier estimates showed a significantly reduced cumulative incidence of TB in the GLP-1RA group versus the DPP-4i group over the follow-up period (log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 2C).

Stratified analyses revealed consistent TB risk reduction with GLP-1RA versus DPP-4i across all patient subgroups. HRs ranged from 0.51 to 0.81 across all strata, and the protective effect remained statistically significant across all subgroups except in patients with neoplasms (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.62–1.05, Fig. 5).

GLP-1RA vs SGLT2is

Relative to SGLT2i, the administration of GLP-1RA was significantly associated with a lower risk of TB, with rates of 0.89 and 1.02‰, respectively (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.72–0.92; E-value, 1.7 [95% LCL, 1.4]; Table 2). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier estimates indicated a significantly decreased cumulative incidence of TB in the GLP-1RA group compared to the SGLT2i group throughout the follow-up period (log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 2D).

Stratified analyses of GLP-1RA versus SGLT2 inhibitors revealed significant protective associations in younger adults (HR, 0.68), males (HR, 0.76), and patients without CKD (HR, 0.71) or COPD (HR, 0.82), but not in other subgroups (Fig. 6).

Secondary outcomes

Compared with SU, GLP-1RA use was associated with significantly lower risks of pulmonary TB (HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.43–0.55) and extrapulmonary TB (HR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.51–0.81). Compared with metformin, GLP-1RA use was associated with a lower risk of pulmonary TB (HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.43–0.63), whereas the risk of extrapulmonary TB was not significantly different (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.65–1.23). Compared with DPP-4i, GLP-1RA use was associated with significantly lower risks of both pulmonary TB (HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.43–0.56) and extrapulmonary TB (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.50–0.83). Compared with SGLT2i, GLP-1RA use was associated with a lower risk of pulmonary TB (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.69–0.91), but the difference in risk for extrapulmonary TB was not statistically significant (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.63–1.04)(Tables S5 and S6).

Sensitivity analysis

In the US network cohort, GLP-1RA use remained associated with a lower risk of TB compared with SU (HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.60–0.76), metformin (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.60–0.81), and DPP-4is (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.76–0.97). However, this association was attenuated and not statistically significant when compared with SGLT2is (HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.83–1.06) (Table S7).

In the exposure-restricted sensitivity analysis, the association between GLP-1RA use and reduced TB risk remained consistent across all comparisons. Compared with SU, metformin, DPP-4is, and SGLT2is, GLP-1RA use was associated with a lower risk of TB, with HRs ranging from 0.56 to 0.70 (Table S8). In negative control outcome analyses using skin cancer, no associations were observed between GLP-1RA use and the control outcome across all comparisons (Table S9).

Discussion

This retrospective study used a multi-institutional research database (TriNetX) to evaluate the relationship between GLP-1RAs and the risk of developing tuberculosis in individuals with diabetes. Our findings demonstrated that the use of GLP-1RAs was associated with a significantly reduced risk of TB when compared with other commonly prescribed antidiabetic medications, including sulfonylureas, metformin, DPP-4is, and SGLT2is. More specifically, when compared directly with DPP-4i use, GLP-1RA therapy was linked to substantially lower risks of both respiratory TB and extrapulmonary TB affecting other organs. Furthermore, in comparison with SGLT2i use, treatment with GLP-1RAs was associated with a markedly lower risk of respiratory TB. These results suggest a potentially protective role of GLP-1RAs against different forms of TB in diabetic populations, highlighting the need for further mechanistic studies and clinical validation.

Patients with diabetes mellitus have a markedly increased risk of developing active tuberculosis due to combined defects in adaptive and innate immunity and the effects of chronic hyperglycemia16,17. Diabetes is associated with reduced T-lymphocyte number and function, particularly impaired Th1 responses and production of cytokines, and macrophage activation is also abnormal16. Innate immune defenses are also compromised, as diabetic macrophages and neutrophils exhibit reduced phagocytosis, chemotaxis, and reactive oxygen species production, limiting intracellular killing of M. tuberculosis18,19. Additionally, impaired monocyte migration—unimproved by insulin therapy - further weakens granuloma formation20. Chronic hyperglycemia exacerbates these defects by impairing the respiratory burst and promoting oxidative stress and advanced glycation end-product formation, resulting in dysfunctional immune signaling and sustained susceptibility to TB18–21.

GLP-1 receptors (GLP-1R) are expressed on multiple immune cells, including macrophages, monocytes, and B and T lymphocytes, indicating a role in immune regulation20. Disruption of GLP-1R signaling impairs lymphocyte proliferation, while GLP-1R activation increases intracellular cAMP, promoting peripheral T-cell proliferation and regulating Treg activity, thereby supporting adaptive immunity and immune tolerance22.

Preclinical studies suggest that GLP-1RA exerts immunomodulatory effects in diabetes-related immune dysfunction23. In obese, high-fat diet–induced diabetic mice, GLP-1RA outperformed insulin in restoring host defense against infection by enhancing neutrophil phagocytosis, migration, and bacterial clearance24. Prolonged GLP-1RA treatment further improved infection resistance by correcting hyperglycemia, increasing neutrophil counts, and restoring innate immune competence25. These improvements indicate that GLP-1RA may provide a superior anti-infective effect compared with conventional therapy, not only through glycemic control but also by directly enhancing immune cell function. Taken together, their study findings suggest that GLP-1RA holds promise as a therapeutic agent capable of addressing both metabolic and immunological dysfunctions in T2D, thereby reducing the risk and severity of infections.

A major strength of our study lies in the use of the TriNetX global health research network, which aggregates de-identified electronic health records from many participating HCOs across multiple countries. This provides access to a very large and diverse patient population, enabling the analysis of real-world clinical outcomes in routine practice. Another important strength is the longitudinal nature of the TriNetX database, which enables assessment of temporal relationships between exposures and outcomes over extended follow-up periods.

The risk of long-term infections increases as HbA1c levels rise. When compared to individuals without T2D, those with T2D - whether optimally controlled or poorly controlled - showed significantly higher risks of hospitalization due to infection26. Furthermore, the relationship between TB and T2D appears to be bidirectional. The likelihood of developing TB is heightened among individuals with T2D, while the risk of developing T2D is also elevated in those with a history of TB disease27. This suggests that T2D requires more intensive management, and the use of GLP-1RA may not only help in controlling T2D but could potentially reduce the risk of developing TB as well. The prevalence of a positive test for Mycobacterium tuberculosis varies according to an individual’s diabetes status, even when stratified by race and ethnicity. Notably, previous studies have reported that the positive correlation between diabetes and a positive test for Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection is particularly pronounced among Hispanic and Asian adults. As such, Hispanic and Asian adults with diabetes may particularly benefit from proactive screening and treatment for tuberculosis infection28. In addition, African individuals with T2D also exhibit a high prevalence of TB infection, particularly among older adults or those with poorly controlled T2D29. These populations require more intensive monitoring and intervention to manage the risk of TB.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the database lacked information on several clinically relevant TB variables, including contact history, latent infection status, radiographic findings, and disease severity, limiting our ability to assess whether GLP-1RA effects differ across TB exposure patterns or disease phenotypes. Additionally, the TriNetX database does not capture medication dosage or adherence data, preventing assessment of dose-response relationships and the potential impact of treatment compliance on the observed associations. Future studies incorporating these clinical variables are warranted. Second, although we adjusted for several available proxies of socioeconomic and lifestyle status, direct measures of income, insurance coverage, or out-of-pocket payment were not available in the TriNetX database. Residual confounding related to unmeasured socioeconomic factors, therefore, cannot be excluded. Third, we acknowledge that the relatively early divergence of survival curves observed in Fig. 2 requires careful interpretation. While proportional hazards testing did not indicate significant violations (p > 0.05), and the early separation may reflect biologically plausible immune-modulatory effects of GLP-1RAs, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of unmeasured time-related confounding factors that may have contributed to this pattern. Forth, we acknowledge that TB epidemiology varies substantially across countries, and GLP-1RA adoption differs by region, which could introduce geographical confounding that was not explicitly adjusted for in our PSM. To address this limitation, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to the US network in TriNetX (Table S7), where TB epidemiology and drug availability are more uniform. In this US-restricted analysis, GLP-1RA use remained associated with a lower risk of tuberculosis compared with SU, metformin, and DPP-4i, though the association was attenuated and not statistically significant when compared with SGLT2is. This attenuation may reflect lower event rates in the US setting, reduced statistical power, and/or greater clinical comparability between newer drug classes, leading to smaller differences in TB risk. Fifth, although ethnicity-specific BMI thresholds may be particularly relevant for Asian populations, individuals classified as Asian comprised a small proportion of the study cohort, and BMI was primarily modeled as a continuous variable to mitigate potential bias related to population-specific cut-offs. Finally, we note that one of our sensitivity analyses, which required patients to have at least one prescription of the index drug during years 1–5 following the index date while having no exposure to comparator drugs during follow-up, inadvertently introduced immortal time bias by conditioning on future exposure information.

We acknowledge that an ideal assessment of sustained drug exposure would involve time-dependent modeling with censoring at treatment discontinuation or crossover. However, due to limitations of the TriNetX platform, we were unable to implement such analyses. The platform does not support time-varying exposure definitions with appropriate dynamic censoring, which are necessary to avoid biases such as immortal time bias. As a result, our study relied on an intention-to-treat active comparator new-user design, which defines exposure at baseline and follows patients regardless of subsequent treatment changes. While this approach mitigates certain biases, it does not fully account for variations in treatment over time.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence suggesting that the use of GLP-1RAs is associated with a significantly reduced risk of tuberculosis among individuals with T2D. Compared with other commonly prescribed antidiabetic agents, including sulfonylureas, metformin, DPP-4is, and SGLT2is, GLP-1RAs consistently showed a protective association. These findings suggest that beyond their established metabolic benefits, GLP-1RAs may confer additional advantages in lowering infection risk. To validate these observations, future research should prioritize mechanistic studies, prospective clinical investigations, and systematic analyses of tuberculosis events reported as adverse events in randomized controlled trials.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from TriNetX, a global federated health research network comprising electronic health records from approximately 153 million patients across 143 healthcare organizations (HCOs). The database provides comprehensive clinical information, including diagnostic codes, medical procedures, medication prescriptions, laboratory results, and genomic profiles. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chi Mei Hospital (IRB No. 11402-E02), and informed consent was waived, given the retrospective nature of the research and the use of de-identified, aggregated data. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study design

The study population comprised new users of the agents of interest, and individuals with any previous or concurrent exposure to these medications before the index date were excluded. Patients were assigned to treatment groups according to the first antidiabetic drug class initiated during the study period (intention-to-treat approach), and the index date was defined as the date of first prescription (Fig. S1). Detailed definitions and coding algorithms for all variables are provided in Table S130,31.

Covariates and propensity score matching

Baseline covariates were assessed during the 12-month lookback window (day −365 to day −1). Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression to estimate the probability of receiving the comparator treatment given baseline characteristics. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching was performed with a greedy algorithm and a caliper width of 0.1 times the pooled standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Individuals in the smaller treatment group were paired with their closest counterparts from the larger group. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values below 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance32.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a comprehensive set of baseline characteristics to enhance comparability between treatment groups. Demographic variables included age (mean and standard deviation), sex, and race (White, Black or African American, Asian, other, and unknown). Clinical variables comprised body mass index (BMI, modeled as mean with standard deviation and categorized as <20 and ≥25 kg/m²) and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c, modeled as mean with standard deviation and categorized as ≥9%). Comorbidities included hypertension, dyslipidemia, malnutrition, chronic lower respiratory diseases, chronic ischemic heart disease, neoplasms, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular diseases, rheumatoid arthritis with rheumatoid factor, arthropathic psoriasis, dependence on renal dialysis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome, and pneumoconiosis due to other dust containing silica. Diabetes-related complications were also included, comprising kidney complications, ophthalmic complications, neurological complications, and circulatory complications. To address potential confounding related to socioeconomic position and lifestyle, available proxy variables recorded in the TriNetX database were incorporated into the propensity score model. These included housing and economic circumstances, employment and unemployment status, education and literacy, occupational exposure to risk factors, alcohol-related disorders, and nicotine dependence. Baseline medications encompassed statins, systemic corticosteroids, inhaled corticosteroids, tumor necrosis factor-alpha inhibitors, and interleukin inhibitors. Detailed definitions and coding algorithms for all covariates are provided in Table S2.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome was the incidence of TB. Secondary outcomes were pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB. Follow-up commenced the day after the index date and continued until the earliest of the following: occurrence of an outcome event, last recorded clinical encounter, death, or five years after the index date. Comprehensive definitions and coding algorithms for all outcomes are presented in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables as counts with corresponding percentages. PSM was applied before conducting the primary and subgroup analyses to reduce confounding and improve comparability between treatment groups. Time-to-event outcomes were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to compare event-free survival across groups.

Stratified analyses were prespecified according to age (18–64 years and ≥65 years), sex, and selected clinical characteristics, including the presence or absence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, and neoplastic disorders. Additional stratifications were based on glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 9% or <9%) and BMI (≥30 kg/m² or <30 kg/m²). To evaluate the robustness of the findings, E-values were calculated to determine the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding necessary to negate the observed associations33.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings under alternative exposure definitions and geographic restrictions. First, an exploratory sensitivity analysis was performed to approximate an on-treatment framework, in which patients were required to have at least one prescription for the index drug during the first 1–5 years after the index date and no exposure to comparator antidiabetic drug classes during follow-up. Second, to address potential geographic heterogeneity in TB epidemiology and drug adoption patterns, analyses were repeated in a cohort restricted to the US network within TriNetX. Additionally, a negative control outcome analysis using skin cancer was conducted to evaluate the presence of residual confounding unrelated to TB risk. All statistical analyses were performed using the TriNetX analytics platform.
