Sensory Sensitivity Over Time After Lower Limb Amputation and Its Association with Phantom Limb Pain
Authors: Jana Wagner, Angela Knaf-Serian, Gregor Reiter, Jamila Andoh, Herta Flor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPhantom limb pain (PLP) is a common complication after amputation, yet its underlying mechanisms and longitudinal course remain incompletely understood. Alterations in peripheral sensory processing, as assessed using Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST), have been associated with PLP; however, most existing studies are cross-sectional, and little is known about how sensory function changes over time. To address this research question, the present longitudinal study aimed to characterize changes in sensory sensitivity during the first year after amputation and to examine their association with the presence and intensity of PLP.MethodsThis study adopted a longitudinal within-subject design, examining 11 persons with unilateral lower limb amputation (mean age 47 years, n = 7 men, n = 4 women) within the first six months after amputation (T1) and at a follow-up assessment at least six months later (T2). To control for potential day-to-day variability in sensory assessments, we additionally collected data from 11 age- and sex-matched control subjects without amputation at both measurements. Sensory function was assessed using the standardized DFNS QST protocol. Outcome measures included warm detection threshold (WDT), heat pain threshold (HPT), mechanical detection threshold (MDT), mechanical pain threshold (MPT), pressure pain threshold (PPT), wind-up ratio (WUR), mechanical pain sensitivity (MPS) and the presence of mechanical allodynia (DMA). Measurements were conducted at the residual limb and the homologous contralateral body site. Group comparisons were conducted using paired and independent t-tests or their non-parametric equivalents (Wilcoxon signed-rank and Mann–Whitney U-tests), and correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.ResultsPLP prevalence increased in our cohort from T1 to T2, with three additional amputees classified as having PLP at T2. At T1, amputees with PLP showed a significantly higher WDT and MDT at the residual limb compared to the contralateral body site (resp. p = 0.026, 95% CI [0.08, 0.68]); (p = 0.003, 95% CI [0.37, 0.92]). At T2, MDT at the residual limb in the PLP+ group was significantly higher than in non-amputated controls (p = 0.049, 95% CI [−0.91, −0.002]). Furthermore, within the PLP+ group, higher PLP intensity was positively correlated with MDT at the residual limb at T2 (p = 0.638, p = 0.047). In addition, signs of central sensitization were observed in amputees with PLP compared to Controls and included allodynia at T1 and T2 (resp. T1: p = 0.010, 95% CI [−0.31, −0.06]; T2: p = 0.043, 95% CI [−0.14, −0.01]) as well as a higher wind-up ratio in amputees with PLP compared to amputees without PLP at T2 (p = 0.037, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.5]) indicating enhanced temporal summation of pain.ConclusionThese findings suggest that PLP is associated with dynamic alterations in peripheral sensory processing at the residual limb and increasing signs of central sensitization over time. Together, the results support the notion that PLP is not solely a consequence of peripheral deafferentation but may reflect an interaction between evolving sensory abnormalities and centrally mediated pain amplification processes.

Introduction

Amputations of limbs may become necessary due to trauma, vascular disease, infection or other severe medical conditions. Following amputation, individuals may experience several forms of pain, including nociceptive pain related to tissue injury and neuropathic pain resulting from peripheral nerve injury and maladaptive central nervous system processes.1 Phantom limb pain (PLP) is a neuropathic pain condition perceived in the missing part of an amputated limb,2 and affects approximately 50–80% of individuals after amputation.3 Patients commonly described PLP as a pulsating, electric, cramping and stabbing sensations of varying intensity and duration.4,5 PLP should be distinguished from residual limb pain (RLP), which originates from the remaining limb tissue and may involve different underlying mechanisms.6

Current evidence suggests that both peripheral and central nervous system mechanisms contribute to PLP, although their relative contributions remain incompletely understood.7,8 Proposed peripheral mechanisms include spontaneous ectopic activity of injured afferent nerve fibers, neuroma formation, altered dorsal root ganglion excitability, and sympathetic dysregulation.3 Central mechanisms involve maladaptive neuroplasticity and altered sensory processing, including cortical reorganization within the primary somatosensory cortex.9 However, the relationship between altered sensory processing and the development or persistence of PLP remains unclear.

Several therapeutic approaches have been explored for PLP, including pharmacological treatment, prosthesis use, mirror therapy, cognitive-behavioral interventions, targeted muscle reinnervation and virtual or augmented reality.10–15 Despite the availability of these approaches, treatment outcomes remain variable, and many patients report only partial or limited relief.16 This highlights the clinical need for improved understanding of PLP mechanisms and better stratification of patients early after amputation. In this context, identifying early changes in sensory processing during the initial months following amputation may be important for recognizing individuals at higher risk and for developing mechanism-based preventive and personalized treatment strategies.

PLP can emerge immediately after amputation or develop months to years later.17 Importantly, the first year after amputation appears to represent a critical period during which substantial sensory and neuroplastic changes occur. Nevertheless, the clinical course of PLP is highly heterogeneous, with some individuals reporting symptom improvement over time while others experience persistent or worsening pain.18,19 This heterogeneity highlights the need for longitudinal approaches that can characterize evolving sensory profiles after amputation.

Previous studies from our group and others have contributed to understanding the neural and clinical correlates of PLP using cross-sectional and neuroimaging approaches. Two large-scale survey studies20,21 provided important epidemiological insights, showing that PLP is linked to altered or impaired body representation and that both painful and non-painful phantom phenomena remain highly prevalent in amputees.

Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) studies have linked phantom limb pain to preserved or reorganized activity in the deafferented sensorimotor cortex, with pain intensity related to the extent of this cortical representation.22,23

The German Research Network on Neuropathic Pain (DFNS) published a standardized protocol for QST in 200624 that can be used to examine the perception of different painful and non-painful stimuli. The results of the QST profile can help to detect sensitivity losses and gains of different nerve fibers.25 Neuropathic pain conditions have been shown to have altered sensory profiles compared with those of healthy controls.26 After amputation, some patients reported a side differentiated perception of thermal27 or pain stimuli28 between the residual limb and the homologous body site. However, less is known on how different measures of sensory sensitivity vary over time and how they may relate to PLP. A previous study observed an association between the intensity of PLP and reduced thermal pain thresholds in individuals who underwent an upper limb amputation 5 to 55 years ago.28 Another study combined lower and upper limb amputees and found no significant difference between amputees with and without PLP and no significant relationship between sensory measures and PLP.27,29 Differences in outcomes between these studies might be related to the heterogeneous samples used, including both leg and arm amputees and/or participants tested at different times after amputation.28,30 In another study, Hunter, Katz and Davis29 recruited a homogenous sample of 12 participants with unilateral upper limb amputation with assessments performed within 6 months after amputation, in which only one participant had no PLP. The authors reported inconclusive results with high variability in sensory measures between participants and no relationship with PLP. This variability may be partially explained by alterations in the anatomical, physiological and vascular conditions of the amputated limb.29

Although these studies provided important insights into predictors and mechanisms of PLP, longitudinal changes in sensory function have not yet been systematically examined within individuals. This presents an opportunity to better understand the development of sensory phenotypes and their association with PLP over time.31,32

Investigating longitudinal sensory changes may improve a mechanistic understanding of the development of PLP and help identify sensory phenotypes associated with increased risk or persistence of pain. Longitudinal assessments may distinguish transient consequences of peripheral deafferentation from persistent maladaptive sensory alterations related to chronicity of PLP, thereby clarifying the respective contributions of peripheral sensory loss and central sensitization.

Therefore, the present longitudinal study examined changes in sensory sensitivity during the first year after lower-limb amputation and explored their relationship with the presence and intensity of PLP.

Current evidence suggests that neuropathic pain consists of distinct sensory phenotypes that may require different diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. However, longitudinal data on the progression of these phenotypes in PLP remain incomplete but could support the development of targeted early interventions. By providing a clearer picture of how sensory profiles change over time, this study may support earlier identification of patients at risk for persistent pain and enable more individualized sensory assessment. These findings have the potential to inform more precise and effective treatment strategies, ultimately improving pain management outcomes for patients with PLP.33

In this longitudinal study, we recruited lower-limb amputees shortly after amputation and followed them over time to examine changes in sensory function using the standardized QST protocol. Assessments were conducted within 6 months post-amputation (T1) and again at least 6 months later (T2). By comparing participants with and without PLP, we aimed to identify sensory parameters associated with PLP intensity and persistence, with the goal of improving clinical monitoring strategies.

Based on current models of neuropathic pain and central sensitization, we hypothesized that greater PLP intensity would be associated with altered sensory processing, including sensory loss, hyperalgesia, and markers of central sensitization identified through QST. We further hypothesized that amputees with PLP would show progressive increases in sensory markers of central sensitization from T1 to T2, reflecting maladaptive neuroplastic changes over time. In contrast, amputees without PLP were expected to exhibit mainly mild mechanical and thermal hyposensitivity at the residual limb due to peripheral nerve loss, without evidence of temporal summation, allodynia, or increased pain sensitivity, and with relatively stable sensory profiles across time. Finally, we expected non-amputated controls to show stable sensory function between T1 and T2 without significant longitudinal changes. As the primary focus of the study was PLP, we did not include RLP in this study.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

All participants were recruited in Germany and were residing there at the time of the study. They were included following major unilateral lower‑limb amputation.

Both male and female participants without age restriction were eligible. Amputees with and without PLP were included and stratified post hoc according to PLP status. Eleven age- and sex-matched non-amputated controls were included to account for measurement variability across time points.

Exclusion criteria comprised bilateral limb amputation, upper-extremity amputation, severe renal or hepatic disease, pregnancy, history of epilepsy, seizures or migraines, suicidality or risk to others, and limited legal capacity. During the COVID-19 pandemic, additional exclusion criteria included acute respiratory symptoms or known exposure to infected individuals.

At baseline, all participants underwent structured screening for physical and psychological suitability for QST assessment. We also screened the participating amputees for medication use as a potential confounding factor (see Table 1). Additionally, we examined participants for other potential confounders, such as diabetes, diabetic neuropathy, coexisting neuropathic pain syndromes, or mental disorders; none of these conditions were present in our cohort. Individuals without amputation, matched for sex and age at the T1 time point, were also recruited to control for inter-individual variability in sensory thresholds.

For both T1 and T2, we assessed PLP using a separate version of the Pain Severity subscale of the German version of the West Haven-Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory (MPI).34,35 The MPI is a 22-item questionnaire that uses a 7-point numeric rating scale for each item ranging from 0 to 6. The Pain Severity subscale is calculated as the average value of three items assessing (a) the intensity of momentary pain, (b) the average pain during the last week and (c) the intensity of pain-related suffering.28 Amputees with a Phantom Limb Pain Severity score ≥ 1 were assigned to the PLP group (PLP+) and the others to the non PLP group (PLP-).

Ethical committee approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Medical Faculty Mannheim, Heidelberg University (approval number: 2014–596N-MA). The study was conducted at the Central Institute of Mental Health in Mannheim, Germany. The recruitment period lasted from 2020 until 2023 and involved multiple sources, including the existing PHANTOMMIND database,36 digital platforms, and collaboration with the BG Klinik Ludwigshafen, Hospital of the German Federal Statutory Accident Insurance (“Deutsche Gesetzliche Unfallversicherung”), affiliated with the University of Heidelberg. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study commencement in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted within Collaborative Research Center 1158, project B07 and registered with the German Clinical Studies Register (DRKS00008842). It was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies (see Supplementary Information 1).

Sensory Testing

We used the standardized QST protocol, which provides a comprehensive assessment of somatosensory function.24,37

The DFNS protocol has established age- and sex-matched reference values and has shown good test–retest and interobserver reliability when performed by trained examiners.38 In addition, QST has demonstrated construct and clinical validity for characterizing sensory loss, hyperalgesia, allodynia, and mechanisms of neuropathic and other chronic pain conditions.26,37 Measurements were performed at the residual limb and the homologous contralateral body site.

To minimize observer bias and ensure consistency across the longitudinal assessment, all QST measurements were performed by trained examiners following a standardized operating procedure, enabling a thorough assessment of eight sensory measures of thermal and mechanical sensitivity. We measured warm detection threshold (WDT), heat pain threshold (HPT), mechanical detection threshold (MDT), mechanical pain threshold (MPT), wind-up ratio (WUR), mechanical pain sensitivity (MPS), dynamic mechanical allodynia (DMA) and pressure pain threshold (PPT). Lower detection thresholds indicate increased sensitivity to the respective stimulus (ie, better detection at lower intensities), whereas lower pain thresholds reflect increased pain sensitivity. Conversely, higher thresholds indicate reduced sensitivity or hypoesthesia. Positive signs of sensory gain (eg, mechanical allodynia, wind‑up) are indicated by positive values or suprathreshold responses, whereas negative values typically indicate sensory loss or hypoesthesia, depending on the parameter.

Due to the specialized nature of the clinical population and the longitudinal study design, a formal a priori sample size calculation could not be performed. Instead, we used a convenience sample of eligible participants recruited during the study period. The final sample size is comparable to previous longitudinal and QST studies in post-amputation populations.29,39,40

QST was performed at an area at the residual limb approximately 5 cm proximal to the residual limb and a homologous site on the intact leg. The testing area at the residual limb was chosen to be easily accessible and free of scar tissue. In non-amputated controls we also used QST at the left thenar to compare our data with the DFNS-data. Participants were instructed to close their eyes during sensory testing and focus on the procedure to enhance their attention to the sensations.

Thermal Thresholds

WDT and HPT were measured using a Medoc Thermal Sensory Analyzer system (TSA 2001-II; Medoc, Ramat Yishai, Israel) with a skin contact area of 9.61 cm2 attached to the testing area. Participants were asked to report the first sensation of warmth to detect the WDT. The HPT was determined as the first painful heat sensation participants reported. The WDT and the HPT were measured in three consecutive runs and the arithmetic mean for each threshold was calculated.

Mechanical Thresholds

The MDT was examined using von Frey filaments (Optihair2-Set, MRC Systems GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). It was calculated using the “method of limits”,41,42 the final threshold was the geometric mean of five series of ascending and descending stimulus intensities.

The MPT was measured with a PinPricks-Set (MRC Systems GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). The stimulation was applied perpendicular to the skin at a 90° angle. The MPT was also calculated using the “method of limits”,41,42 the final threshold was the geometric mean of five series of ascending and descending stimulus intensities.

Wind-Up Ratio

Pain summation over time was induced with PinPrick series (MRC Systems GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). Pain perception was recorded on a numerical rating scale (NRS) from 0 (“no pain, no sensation”) to 100 (“most intense pain imaginable”). First, a single stimulus was applied and rated on the NRS. Afterwards, a series of 10 stimuli was applied within a 1 cm2 area at a frequency of approximately 1 Hz to achieve a wind-up effect43 and the NRS rating was recorded. Five runs of single stimuli and series of stimuli were examined, for each the arithmetic mean was calculated. The WUR was determined by the ratio of the values of the series and the single stimuli.

Mechanical pain sensitivity and dynamic mechanical allodynia: MPS was tested using the same weighted PinPrick stimuli (MRC Systems GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) as the ones used for MPT. To obtain a stimulus-response-function, these seven PinPrick stimuli were applied in a balanced order, five times each, and participants were asked to give a pain rating for each stimulus on a 0 to 100 NRS.

Stimulus-response-functions for DMA were determined using a set of three light tactile stimulators: a cotton wisp (∼3 mN), a cotton wool tip (∼100 mN), and a standardized brush (∼200-400 mN). The three tactile stimuli were applied five times each with a single stroke of approximately 1–2 cm in length over the skin. They were intermingled with the PinPrick stimuli in balanced order and subjects were asked to give a rating on the same scale as the one used for PinPrick stimuli.44

Pressure Pain Threshold

PPT was measured using a manual dynamometer with the TSA II algomed program of the MEDOC software (Ramat Yishai, Israel). Pressure was increased steadily at 0.5 kg/cm2 per second, participants were asked to report the first painful pressure sensation they felt. We assessed the PPT three times, and a mean value from the three readings was calculated. Noteworthy, the PPT was included in the study protocol later, so PPT was measured in N = 4 amputees at T1 and in N = 5 amputees at T2.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0). All parameters were tested for normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. No systematic outliers were found, so the entire dataset was used.

For WDT, the difference between the detected threshold temperature and the baseline temperature of 32°C was calculated and used for further analysis.24 Following the method of Bartlett,45 a constant of 0.1 was added to each NRS value for MPS and DMA to prevent the loss of values with NRS = 0.

Data of the WDT, MDT, MPT, WUR und PPT were log-transformed as recommended by the standardized protocol.24

Based on our hypotheses and sample size, we assessed group differences between amputees with PLP, amputees without PLP and matched controls. This calculation was performed for both time points separately using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney-U-tests, depending on normal distribution. Changes in QST variables between T1 and T2 and between the residual limb and the contralateral body site were analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon-tests, applied separately to amputees with and without PLP. Unless otherwise described, a two-tailed test was used with a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05). Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene´s test. Additionally, the association between PLP intensity and QST variables was analyzed using Spearman correlations.

We also verified whether the QST data measured on the non-amputated controls were representative of healthy individuals by comparing the measured thenar values with the standardized thenar values of the DFNS subjects. For this purpose, the test values of the measured control subjects were Z-transformed using the published DFNS norms for healthy subjects.37 Categorization into men and women as well as subjects younger and older than 40 years was performed. The Z-values of the non-amputated control subjects were interpreted based on the 95% confidence interval. The mean value of healthy controls 0 ± 0.25 and the standard deviation of healthy controls 1 ± 0.1 was supposed.46

Results

Clinical Characteristics and PLP Prevalence

This study adopted a longitudinal design examining 11 unilateral lower-limb amputees (n = 4 right-sided and n = 7 left-sided amputations). The causes of amputation were traumatic (n = 5), vascular (n = 4), and osteomyelitis (n = 2). Participants were assessed at two time points separated by a mean interval of 11 months (SD = 3.65 months, range 6–19 months). T1 was conducted between 6 weeks and 6 months after amputation, and T2 at least 6 months after the first assessment. Measurement timing was individually adjusted to avoid confounding by postoperative wound pain. For T1, data of N = 11 amputees consisting of n = 7 men and n = 4 women were collected. The average age was 47.18 years (SD = 11.26 years) with an age range from 28 to 68 years. Due to a dropout, the T2 dataset consisted of N = 10 subjects with n = 7 men and n = 3 women. One woman withdrew consent because of worsening health problems. The average age was 46.7 years (SD = 11.75 years).

The cohort of non-amputated control subjects comprised N = 11 for both times of measurement with n = 7 men and n = 4 women. The average age was 47.55 years (SD = 11.32 years) with an age range from 27 to 67 years. There were no significant differences in age and gender between amputees and non-amputated controls at both time points. Comparable to the amputee group, the T2 measurement was conducted M = 11 months (SD = 4.07 months, range from 6 to 21 months) after T1.

The presence of PLP changes during the time of our longitudinal study. At T1, out of N = 11 amputees, n = 5 felt PLP and n = 6 did not. At T2, out of N = 10 amputees, n = 7 suffered from PLP and n = 3 did not. The prevalence of PLP increased over the year, with n = 3 developing PLP and n = 1 reporting remission.

Peripheral Sensory Function

At T1, amputees with PLP showed a significantly higher WDT at the residual limb compared to the contralateral body site (p = 0.026, 95% CI [0.08, 0.68]). Comparison with Non-Amputated Controls and Normative Data

Control participants showed no detectable side differences at either measurement point, indicating low intra-individual variability across repeated measurements. Moreover, we compared the measured thenar values of the non-amputated controls with the DFNS reference values for the hand to ensure consistency with normative QST profiles, reported in the DFNS-protocol. Z-standardization demonstrated that all thenar values of non-amputated controls fell within the 95% confidence interval of the DFNS reference values (Z-mean < 1.93). None of the QST measures in non-amputated controls changed significantly between T1 and T2.

Discussion

We examined longitudinal changes in sensory profiles after amputation and their relationship to phantom limb pain (PLP) using quantitative sensory testing (QST). Our findings suggest that PLP is associated with dynamic alterations in both peripheral sensory processing and central pain modulation, rather than representing a static consequence of amputation. Specifically, sensory abnormalities appeared to shift from predominantly peripheral disturbances shortly after amputation toward increasingly centrally mediated mechanisms over time.

Early Peripheral Sensory Dysfunction

Shortly after amputation (T1), amputees with PLP demonstrated clear signs of peripheral sensory dysfunction at the residual limb, characterized by elevated warm and mechanical detection thresholds compared with the contralateral homologous site. According to the DFNS framework, such side differences indicate impairment of the peripheral sensory nervous system. This pattern reflects early hyposensitivity at the residual limb in individuals who develop PLP.

Similar elevations in warm detection thresholds have been reported previously, whereas findings regarding mechanical sensitivity have been heterogeneous, ranging from hypoesthesia to hyperesthesia or absent side differences across studies. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design, patient characteristics, or time since amputation.

Evolution of Sensory Changes Over Time

Over time, the initially observed side differences diminished and were no longer statistically significant at follow-up (T2). Importantly, this reduction in asymmetry should not necessarily be interpreted as complete sensory recovery. While peripheral adaptation may contribute, bilateral changes in central sensory representation may also reduce side-to-side differences over time. This interpretation is consistent with theories emphasizing cortical and subcortical reorganization in PLP, suggesting that altered sensory processing may extend beyond the residual limb to involve broader body representations and the intact side.9

When directly comparing amputees with and without PLP, a temporal shift in sensory abnormalities became apparent. The PLP group showed a tendency toward greater thermal hyposensitivity early after amputation and greater mechanical hyposensitivity at follow-up, suggesting a transition from predominantly thermal to mechanical sensory dysfunction over time.

Although thermal thresholds were not associated with PLP intensity, mechanical detection thresholds positively correlated with pain intensity at follow-up. This finding suggests that greater mechanical hyposensitivity may be linked to more severe PLP in the longer term. Similar sensory profiles have been described in other neuropathic pain conditions and may reflect processes such as deafferentation and maladaptive cortical reorganization.47,48

Mechanical Hyperalgesia and Impaired Sensory Discrimination

In addition to detection deficits, amputees with PLP exhibited a reduced range between mechanical detection and pain thresholds. They required stronger stimuli to detect touch but experienced pain at lower stimulus intensities, indicating impaired tactile discrimination combined with mechanical hyperalgesia. This narrowing of the perceptual window may reflect dysfunctional sensory gain mechanisms characteristic of neuropathic pain states.

Clinically, this finding may have implications for prosthesis use. Adequate sensory feedback is necessary to detect pressure or prosthetic misfit, whereas increased pain sensitivity may reduce prosthesis tolerance and comfort.

Differences Between Amputees with and without PLP

Amputees without PLP demonstrated a different sensory profile. At T1, they showed lower pressure pain thresholds compared with non-amputated controls, suggesting transient hypersensitivity potentially related to surgical trauma, peripheral sensitization, or early healing processes. This effect was no longer present at T2, indicating sensory adaptation over time.

In contrast, amputees with PLP continued to show altered sensory processing at follow-up, suggesting more persistent mechanisms that may increasingly involve central pain modulation.

Markers of Central Sensitization

Markers of central sensitization further supported this distinction. The wind-up ratio, reflecting temporal summation of pain, increased significantly over time in the PLP group and was higher than in the non-PLP group at follow-up. This finding suggests increasing amplification of nociceptive input within the central nervous system, a hallmark of neuropathic pain and a known risk factor for chronic pain development.

Additionally, allodynia persisted in the PLP group at both time points, whereas it was only observed transiently in amputees without PLP. The transient allodynia in the non-PLP group may reflect heightened peripheral sensitivity during postoperative healing, whereas its persistence in PLP suggests sustained central sensitization and ongoing dysfunction of sensory processing pathways.

Overall Interpretation

Taken together, the longitudinal changes observed over approximately 11 months suggest that PLP is not solely a consequence of peripheral nerve injury but rather emerges from an interaction between evolving peripheral sensory deficits and increasingly prominent central sensitization processes. Early after amputation, peripheral sensory disturbances appear to dominate, whereas over time, central mechanisms such as enhanced temporal summation and persistent allodynia become more pronounced and increasingly differentiate individuals with PLP from those without PLP.

These findings are consistent with our previous work demonstrating that pre-amputation pain and psychological factors contribute to the development of PLP.49 The present results extend this perspective by showing that sensory processing continues to evolve after amputation and may contribute to the persistence and chronification of PLP. Overall, PLP should be understood as a dynamic and multifactorial pain condition involving interacting peripheral and central mechanisms, rather than a static consequence of deafferentation alone.

This study is clinically relevant for researchers, clinicians, and patients alike, as it aligns with the growing shift toward patient stratification and personalized management of neuropathic pain.33 By longitudinally characterizing sensory changes following lower-limb amputation and examining their relationship with phantom limb pain (PLP), the study addresses an important research question in understanding how sensory phenotypes develop and evolve over time.

Non-Amputated Controls

As expected, non-amputated subjects showed symmetrical and unchanged QST variables during the observation period. The comparison of the non-amputated controls’ QST data with the DFNS values confirmed that their sensory profile matches that of healthy individuals of the DFNS protocol.

Real-World Limitations

Recruiting persons with amputations in suitable physical and mental conditions at both measurement times was difficult and several practical challenges like the Covid-19 pandemic influenced the data collection progress. This contributed to the relatively small sample size and the variable interval between follow-up assessments. Given the longitudinal design and the clinical challenges associated with repeatedly assessing individuals with recent lower limb amputation, we prioritized complete follow-up assessments and observed only one dropout at T2.

General Limitations

Due to the small sample size (N = 11) and one dropout at T2, we used more conservative statistical methods (paired and between-group comparisons) which may be more robust when sample sizes are very small. This approach is consistent with recommendations to use less assumption-dependent methods when sample sizes are very small.50

Accordingly, the present findings should be considered as exploratory and interpreted with caution, particularly in light of the limited statistical power. Future research with larger cohorts are needed to confirm the replicability and generalizability of these findings. Studies with larger samples could use mixed model approaches and analysis of etiologically relevant subgroups.

A further consideration in longitudinal QST studies is the potential influence of measurement variability over time. To minimize this, all examiners underwent training and adhered to standardized procedures, as consistency and reliability across time points are essential for longitudinal assessments.38 Notably, in the non-amputated control cohort, no significant differences were detected between T1 and T2, suggesting minimal measurement variability in our protocol.

We focused exclusively on PLP using the MPI questionnaire and did not include other clinical conditions, such as phantom limb sensations or RLP. Accordingly, our findings apply specifically to PLP, and no conclusions can be drawn regarding other clinical conditions.

Importantly, prior studies suggest associations between telescoping, RLP intensity, and PLP modulation, likely involving cortical reorganization.20,21 Future research should longitudinally examine these relationships and their links to sensory profiles, cortical plasticity, and pain chronification.

In amputees, the testing area at the residual limb was individually determined based on the amputation height and to ensure the absence of hypertrophic scar tissue near the testing region. Therefore, the testing areas at the residual limb can differ between amputees. However, the testing areas were controlled by matching each test site to the corresponding contralateral body site and by ensuring it was comparable to the sites in non-amputated controls.

Some of the amputees with PLP were under pain medication and persistent drug effects can therefore not be excluded. Especially at T2, some subjects had engaged in various pain management therapies, including medication, physiotherapy and mirror therapy (albeit unsuccessful). We did not categorize the amputees from medication intake or other pain management therapies since these differed highly between the participants. Therefore, we did not carry out further analysis concerning medication or other pain management strategies. But with the MPI questionnaire we recognized that some amputees suffered from PLP despite these treatments.

In addition, other potential confounding factors such as lifestyle variables (eg, physical activity, diet), socioeconomic status, which may have influenced the observed outcomes, were not comprehensively captured. Future studies should aim to systematically collect such data and control for these variables to allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of potential confounding effects.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study suggests that phantom limb pain (PLP) is associated with dynamic changes in sensory processing after amputation, involving both peripheral sensory dysfunction and central sensitization. Early after amputation, amputees with PLP showed predominantly peripheral sensory abnormalities, whereas over time, markers of central sensitization such as enhanced temporal summation and persistent allodynia became more prominent.

These findings indicate that PLP is not solely a consequence of peripheral deafferentation, but rather reflects an evolving interaction between peripheral and central pain mechanisms. Improved understanding of these processes may support earlier identification and targeted treatment of individuals at risk for persistent PLP.

Future Directions

Future research should aim to replicate these findings in larger longitudinal cohorts to allow more robust statistical modelling of within-subject sensory changes over time. In particular, studies with sufficient power should investigate whether distinct sensory phenotypes can be identified and whether these phenotypes predict the development, persistence, or remission of PLP.

Furthermore, multimodal approaches combining QST with neuroimaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging or electroencephalography may help clarify how peripheral sensory alterations relate to central nervous system reorganization and pain processing in PLP.10,33,48

Finally, integrating longitudinal sensory profiling into clinical follow-up after amputation may help identify individuals at risk for persistent or increasing pain, but this clinical applicability requires validation in prospective, larger-scale studies.26,42
