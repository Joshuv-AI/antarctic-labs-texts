Dual-phase T1-mapping (DHR-MAP) for non-invasive assessment of advanced liver fibrosis and moderate hepatitis: a single-center validation study

Introduction

Chronic liver diseases (CLDs) represent a significant global health burden, with liver fibrosis and necroinflammation being the central pathological processes driving progression to cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 2]. While these processes are distinct pathophysiological entities, their clinical discrimination remains a fundamental challenge in hepatology, with profound implications for treatment selection and disease monitoring.

Liver biopsy has historically served as the reference standard for fibrosis staging and inflammation grading, but its invasiveness and sampling variability have motivated the development of non-invasive tests (NITs) [3, 4]. Among these, magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) has established itself as a robust tool for fibrosis assessment by measuring tissue stiffness as a surrogate of fibrotic deposition [5, 6]. However, MRE’s diagnostic accuracy is compromised in conditions such as severe steatosis, biliary obstruction, and hepatic congestion [7, 8], and it cannot reliably differentiate inflammation-driven stiffness from pure fibrosis [9].

Complementing imaging techniques, serum biomarkers such as the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index provide accessible alternatives but face their own limitations. Although widely used for fibrosis screening, FIB-4 incorporates inflammatory markers (transaminases) and thus inherently conflates fibrotic and inflammatory processes. This conceptual ambiguity has led to inconsistent performance across patient populations and disease stages, creating what we term the “biomarker paradox”—where tools developed for one purpose may primarily reflect different pathophysiological processes [10–15].

The fundamental challenge

We hypothesized that the primary limitation of current NITs lies not in their technical accuracy, but in the incomplete understanding of their pathophysiological specificity. The clinical community lacks a coherent framework that distinguishes which biomarkers reflect predominantly fibrotic structural changes, inflammatory activity, or an inseparable combination thereof.

Our approach addresses this fundamental gap by leveraging the unique properties of gadobenate dimeglumine (Gd-BOPTA)-enhanced MRI. We propose that the ratio of hepatobiliary phase to delayed phase T1 values (DHR-MAP) quantitatively captures the balance between contrast uptake dysfunction and drainage impairment—processes differentially affected by fibrosis and inflammation [16]. This mechanistic premise suggests that DHR-MAP may provide complementary information to MRE’s stiffness measurement and FIB-4’s inflammatory profile.

Therefore, we designed this study to fundamentally redefine the pathophysiological basis of established and emerging biomarkers and translate these mechanistic insights into clinical practice.

The primary aims of this study were to:

▯ Definitively establish the pathophysiological specificity of MRE, FIB-4, and DHR-MAP through rigorous statistical and mediation analyses that control for confounding between fibrosis and inflammation.

Novel contribution

We propose that only through such a foundational redefinition of biomarker roles can an optimally integrated diagnostic framework be constructed—one that moves beyond correlative approximation to establish a new standard of mechanism-based precision in hepatology.

Materials and methods

This study was designed to first redefine the pathophysiological basis of key non-invasive biomarkers and then translate these mechanistic insights into an integrated diagnostic strategy. The methodological framework, detailed below, was constructed specifically to resolve the biomarker paradox and validate a complementary two-step workflow.

Study population and cohort construction

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (Ethics Approval: II2024-228-01) with a waiver of informed consent, as the research involved no more than minimal risk and utilized pre-existing, anonymized clinical and imaging data collected between March 2022 and June 2023. From 2,180 consecutive adult patients undergoing abdominal MRI (March 2022–June 2023), we applied rigorous exclusion criteria to ensure data quality and technical adequacy for precise biomarker characterization.

Key exclusion criteria

MRE failure/suboptimal quality (n = 25), liver transplantation (n = 5), portal vein tumor thrombus (n = 44), missing serology (n = 223), large focal lesions (n = 76), timing deviations (DLP > 1 min: n = 44; HBP > 10 min: n = 212), and lack of biopsy reference (n = 1,452).

This highly selective process yielded two analysis cohorts that represent only 4.5% of the original screening population, introducing significant selection bias that must be considered when interpreting results: the advanced fibrosis cohort (n = 99, for ≥ F3 vs. F0-F2 analysis) and the moderate hepatitis cohort (a subset of n = 73 with available METAVIR inflammation grading, for ≥ G2 vs. G0-G1 analysis) (Fig. 1).

Pathophysiological basis of DHR-MAP for fibrosis and hepatitis assessment

The DHR-MAP (Hepatobiliary-to-Delayed Phase T1 Ratio) metric was designed to capture the distinct pathophysiological processes underlying liver fibrosis and hepatitis through gadobenate dimeglumine (Gd-BOPTA) pharmacokinetics (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In the delayed phase (5-minute post-injection), elevated portal pressure from fibrotic architectural changes impairs contrast drainage, leading to prolonged parenchymal retention and consequently reduced T1 values.

Conversely, in the hepatobiliary phase (120-minute post-injection), the loss of functional hepatocytes in both fibrotic and inflamed regions reduces contrast uptake via organic anion transporting polypeptide transporters, resulting in elevated T1 values.

The ratio of hepatobiliary phase to delayed phase T1 values (DHR-MAP = HBP-MAP / DLP-MAP) intrinsically normalizes for patient-specific variables and amplifies the signal difference between healthy and diseased tissue. This dual-phase approach enables DHR-MAP to simultaneously capture both the structural consequences of fibrosis (delayed phase retention) and the functional impairment of hepatitis (hepatobiliary phase uptake reduction).

Core Innovation: The DHR-MAP metric was explicitly engineered to disentangle the conflation of fibrosis and inflammation by quantifying their distinct functional and structural impacts on contrast kinetics.

MRI acquisition protocol

All examinations were performed on a single 3.0T scanner (United Imaging Healthcare) with a standard abdominal coil after ≥ 4 h of fasting.

Dual-phase T1-mapping acquisition

Sequences: 3D gradient-echo with B1 inhomogeneity correction using a dual flip-angle method (3° and 15°). T1 maps were calculated using the variable flip angle (VFA) method with B1 correction based on the actual flip angle imaging (AFI) technique. The B1 map was acquired using a pre-saturation turboFLASH sequence with two different saturation delay times (TR1/TR2 = 30/100 ms) to estimate B1 inhomogeneity. Corrected flip angles were then used in the VFA T1 calculation algorithm to minimize errors from B1 field variations.

Phases & Timing: 5-minute delayed phase (DLP-MAP) and 120-minute hepatobiliary phase (HBP-MAP). Each acquisition: 14 s.

Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE)

Sequence: 2D spin-echo echo-planar imaging with 60-Hz external driver.

Parameters: TR/TE: 1000.2/44.6 ms; PI: 2; FOV: 420 × 420 mm²; Matrix: 96 × 96; Slice thickness: 8 mm.

Analysis: Stiffness maps generated using the Mayo Clinic algorithm (kPa).

Contrast agent administration

Gadobenate Dimeglumine (Gd-BOPTA) was administered intravenously at 0.75 mmol/kg, followed by a saline flush. The arterial phase was triggered via bolus-tracking, with subsequent phases acquired at the standardized 5-minute (delayed) and 120-minute (hepatobiliary) time points.Gd-BOPTA has an established safety profile similar to other gadolinium-based contrast agents, with rare serious adverse events (incidence < 0.1%). Contraindications include severe renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²) due to risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, and known hypersensitivity to gadolinium chelates. No serious adverse events related to contrast administration occurred in this stud.

Image analysis

All image analyses were performed independently by two abdominal radiologists (with 10 and 17 years of experience, blinded to patient information and pathological results).

T1-mapping analysis

Readers placed freehand irregular-shaped regions of interest (ROIs) conforming to the liver parenchyma contour on the DLP-MAP and HBP-MAP images at three levels: the liver hilum, and 2–4 cm above and below it. ROIs were carefully drawn to encompass the liver parenchyma while excluding major blood vessels, bile ducts, focal lesions, and artifacts near the liver edge. The mean T1 values from the three slices were averaged to obtain single DLP-MAP and HBP-MAP values for each patient.

The DHR-MAP metric was then calculated as: DHR-MAP = HBP-MAP / DLP-MAP (Fig. 8) [17].

MRE analysis

Readers reviewed stiffness maps, wave images, and confidence maps to ensure quality. Freehand ROIs conforming to the liver parenchyma contour were manually drawn on all four stiffness map slices, carefully excluding liver edges, large vessels, and focal lesions. The final liver stiffness measurement (LSM) was calculated as a pixel-weighted mean of stiffness values from all slices (Fig. 8).

Management of MRE confounding factors in ROI placement

To assess the real-world, pathophysiological relationship between DHR-MAP, MRE, and FIB-4 without introducing additional selection bias, we intentionally did not exclude patients based on known MRE confounders such as ascites or iron overload. This approach preserves the natural spectrum of chronic liver disease (CLD) in our cohort, allowing the biomarker correlations to reflect their true biological and clinical interplay. The observed lower sensitivity of MRE in this unselected cohort likely, in part, reflects the impact of these confounders, providing a more realistic performance estimate for clinical practice.

To ensure measurement accuracy within the analyzed ROIs while maintaining the cohort’s natural distribution, we implemented the following quality control measures during ROI placement:

Ascites: While patients with ascites were included in the cohort, ROIs were carefully placed in parenchymal regions distant from ascitic fluid to minimize shear wave attenuation effects on the local stiffness measurement.

Histopathological analysis

Liver biopsy specimens were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and stained with Hematoxylin & Eosin and Sirius Red. All biopsies were evaluated by a single, dedicated hepatopathologist (with 6 years of experience in liver pathology) who was blinded to all clinical and radiological data. Biopsy samples were considered adequate if the core length was ≥ 1.5 cm and contained ≥ 10 complete portal tracts.

Fibrosis and hepatitis activity were staged according to the METAVIR scoring system: Fibrosis: F0 (none), F1 (mild), F2 (significant), F3 (advanced), F4 (cirrhosis); Hepatitis Activity: G0 (none), G1 (mild), G2 (moderate), G3 (severe), G4 (very severe). For this study, patients were dichotomized into clinically relevant groups: advanced fibrosis (F3-F4 vs. F0-F2) and moderate hepatitis (G2-G4 vs. G0-G1).

Statistical analysis: a framework for biomarker redefinition and validation

Analyses used SPSS (v26.0), R (v4.2.3), and Python (v3.11.4). Our statistical framework was purpose-built to test our primary hypothesis of biomarker specificity.

Establishing pathophysiological specificity

Partial Correlation Analysis: Used to isolate the independent association of each biomarker with fibrosis and inflammation by controlling for confounding variables (inflammation grade, sex, BMI for fibrosis; fibrosis stage, sex, BMI for hepatitis).

Hierarchical Regression: Stepwise models determined the independent predictive value of each biomarker after controlling for baseline variables.

Mediation Analysis: Elucidated causal pathways between fibrosis and inflammation to establish whether the biomarkers act as mediators or mere reflectors.

All tests in this family were corrected for multiple comparisons using the hierarchical Holm-Bonferroni method .

Diagnostic performance and two-step strategy validation

ROC Analysis & Delong’s Test: Evaluated diagnostic performance for dichotomized outcomes and compared AUCs to assess complementarity. Pairwise comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure.

Cross-Validation: 5-fold cross-validation with a fixed random seed and a 20% test set ratio was employed to provide robust, generalized performance estimates and mitigate overfitting.

Error Reduction Analysis: The net error reduction of the two-step strategy was quantitatively compared against single-modality approaches.

Reproducibility and additional analyses

Inter-reader agreement for MRE and DHR-MAP was assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC).

Statistical power and multiple comparisons control

Given the sample size, we performed post-hoc power analyses using G*Power 3.1. To control Type I error inflation from multiple testing, we applied hierarchical Holm-Bonferroni correction within logical families of tests .

Statistical Context & Power: Post-hoc power analysis revealed adequate power (~ 85%) for DHR-MAP and MRE associations with fibrosis, but limited power (~ 65%) for the FIB-4 association with hepatitis. After hierarchical multiple comparisons correction (Supplementary Table 3), the marginal association between FIB-4 and hepatitis became non-significant, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and validation in larger studies.

Key findings from hierarchical regression

For Fibrosis Prediction: Adding FIB-4 to a baseline model provided negligible improvement (ΔR²=0.006, Holm-corrected p = 0.414), while subsequent inclusion of DHR-MAP (ΔR²=0.07, Holm-corrected p = 0.036) and MRE (ΔR²=0.05, Holm-corrected p = 0.075) significantly enhanced predictive power.

For Hepatitis Grading: Only FIB-4 showed marginal predictive value (ΔR²=0.036, Holm-corrected p = 0.052, FDR-corrected q = 0.104), while DHR-MAP (ΔR²=0.011, p = 0.286) and MRE (ΔR²=0.000, p = 0.985) added no significant explanatory power after correction.

These results quantitatively establish that DHR-MAP and MRE are independent fibrosis predictors, while FIB-4 is the only biomarker with potential for inflammation assessment, confirming their complementary rather than redundant roles.

The significant direct effect (0.685, 79.2% of total effect) confirms that established fibrosis creates a pro-inflammatory microenvironment through mechanical stress, hypoxia, and altered cytokine signaling

Key mechanistic insights

The fibrosis→hepatitis pathway demonstrated significant DHR-MAP mediation (21.7%, Holm-corrected p = 0.048, FDR-corrected q = 0.048), establishing DHR-MAP as a fibrosis-specific biomarker that captures the functional consequences of fibrotic architectural changes on inflammatory activity.

The hepatitis→fibrosis pathway showed no significant mediation through either FIB-4 (8.9%, p = 0.188) or DHR-MAP (7.7%, p = 0.596) after correction, confirming that FIB-4 reflects inflammatory processes rather than mediating fibrogenesis.

Mediation analysis provides the final mechanistic validation: DHR-MAP detects established fibrotic changes and their consequences, while FIB-4 reflects inflammatory activity, conclusively resolving their pathophysiological roles.

Comparative performance against established benchmarks

When benchmarked against established non-invasive tests in external validation cohorts (Supplementary Table 1), our models demonstrated a balanced diagnostic profile.

For Advanced Fibrosis (≥ F3): The DHR-MAP + FIB-4 model achieved an AUC of 0.758 and a PPV of 0.700 in the independent test set, while MRE alone demonstrated an AUC of 0.879 with perfect specificity (1.000). Published benchmarks for FIB-4 alone report lower PPV (0.161–0.289). Our two-step approach addresses the critical limitation of low PPV in serum biomarkers by incorporating imaging confirmation.

For Moderate Hepatitis (≥ G2): FIB-4 alone in our test set showed perfect sensitivity (1.000) but very low specificity (0.143). The DHR-MAP + FIB-4 model provided substantially improved specificity (0.857) and PPV (0.667), addressing the common trade-off in conventional biomarkers that often prioritize sensitivity at the expense of specificity.

The sequential application of biomarkers in our two-step strategy transforms their individual limitations into collective diagnostic power, achieving substantial error reduction in this cohort (88.5% for fibrosis, 30.4% for hepatitis).

Context

These performance characteristics and the observed error reduction demonstrate proof-of-concept for the integrative approach, but the absolute performance metrics require validation in larger, prospective cohorts.

Discussion

A pathophysiological re-evaluation of biomarkers and a novel integrative diagnostic framework for chronic liver disease

This study provides a fundamental re-evaluation of established non-invasive biomarkers in chronic liver disease (CLD) through a pathophysiology-focused lens. By employing rigorous statistical controls for confounding and multiple comparisons, we resolve a key diagnostic paradox and propose a novel two-step integrative strategy. Our findings challenge the conventional classification of the FIB-4 index, reaffirm the context-dependent utility of MRE, introduce DHR-MAP as a fibrosis-specific functional imaging biomarker, and translate these insights into a sequential diagnostic framework designed to leverage complementary biomarker strengths.

Re-defining the FIB-4 index: from a fibrosis marker to an inflammation-predominant signal

Our analysis reveals a critical paradox surrounding the FIB-4 index. Despite its widespread use for fibrosis screening, our data demonstrate no statistically independent association between FIB-4 and histological fibrosis stage after controlling for concurrent inflammatory activity (partial correlation corrected p = 0.414). Conversely, its association with hepatitis grade, while marginal and not surviving stringent family-wise error correction in our critically underpowered sample, aligns with its fundamental biological composition.

The biological rationale for FIB-4 as a marker of inflammatory activity is compelling. The index directly incorporates AST and ALT, which are enzymes released upon hepatocellular injury and are established serum hallmarks of necroinflammation [11–15]. Therefore, the diagnostic signal of FIB-4 likely reflects this inflammatory component more than fibrotic structural change. This explains its historical validation in cohorts like hepatitis C, where fibrosis and inflammation progression are tightly coupled [19–21], and its variable performance in contemporary, etiologically-mixed CLD populations where this coupling may be less consistent.

Thus, we propose a reconceptualization: FIB-4 is best understood as a sensitive, inflammation-predominant biomarker. Its clinical utility is not diminished but strategically refocused. This redefinition forms the cornerstone of our two-step strategy, where FIB-4’s high sensitivity for detecting inflammatory activity is leveraged for initial screening, while its lack of fibrosis-specificity is compensated for by subsequent confirmatory tests.

MRE in clinical context: validated authority with acknowledged real-world constraints

Our findings strongly reaffirm magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) as the non-invasive gold standard for fibrosis assessment, demonstrating excellent specificity (1.000) and high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.879) [5, 6, 8, 22]. However, the observed sensitivity (58.3%) in our unselected cohort was lower than reported in idealized studies [23, 24], providing a crucial real-world performance estimate.

This apparent discrepancy underscores the impact of clinical confounders. In contrast to highly selective trial cohorts, we intentionally included patients with conditions known to affect MRE accuracy (e.g., ascites, iron overload) to preserve the natural disease spectrum, as detailed in the Methods. This approach likely yields a more generalizable, albeit conservative, estimate of MRE’s sensitivity in routine practice [9, 25–27]. The result reinforces the principle that MRE’s supreme specificity makes it an ideal confirmatory tool within an integrated diagnostic pathway, rather than a flawless standalone test [28–33].

DHR-MAP: a novel dual-phase biomarker targeting fibrosis-specific pathophysiology

The introduced DHR-MAP metric is engineered to disentangle fibrotic from inflammatory processes by exploiting the distinct pharmacokinetics of gadobenate dimeglumine (Gd-BOPTA) across two temporal phases. The 5-minute delayed phase (DLP-MAP) primarily reflects contrast retention within the expanding fibrotic interstitium due to impaired drainage. In contrast, the 120-minute hepatobiliary phase (HBP-MAP) quantifies hepatocellular uptake dysfunction via OATP transporters. The ratio (DHR-MAP = HBP-MAP/DLP-MAP) intrinsically amplifies the signal difference between diseased and healthy tissue, rendering it a sensitive and specific marker for the structural consequences of fibrosis.

This dual-phase mechanism offers a distinct advantage over single-phase approaches or agents with very high hepatobiliary uptake (e.g., Gd-EOB-DTPA), as it provides a balanced dynamic range to capture both vascular-interstitial and hepatocellular functional impairments across the fibrosis spectrum [10, 16, 34–36].

A novel Two-Step diagnostic strategy: translating pathophysiological insights into clinical logic

The proposed two-step strategy is a direct translation of the redefined biomarker roles into a practical diagnostic algorithm designed to minimize overall error.

For advanced fibrosis (≥ F3)

Step 1 (Screening)

The combined DHR-MAP + FIB-4 model provides high sensitivity (0.778 in cross-validation) to reliably “rule out” disease.

Step 2 (Confirmation)

Positive screenings are confirmed with MRE, which provides exceptional specificity (1.000) to “rule in” fibrosis and mitigate false positives from the sensitive first step.

For moderate hepatitis (≥ G2)

Step 1 (Screening)

FIB-4 alone, leveraging its inflammation-sensitive biology, is used for high-sensitivity screening.

Step 2 (Refinement)

Positive cases are further evaluated with the DHR-MAP + FIB-4 model, which adds morphological context to improve specificity and reduce false-positive referrals.

This sequential, complementary application addresses the core weakness of each standalone biomarker. In our cohort, it achieved a substantial 88.5% net error reduction for fibrosis and a 30.4% net error reduction for hepatitis compared to single-modality approaches (Table 6). The perfect sensitivity values observed in the full cohort for some models are likely optimistic due to overfitting in a small sample; the cross-validated test set metrics (Table 5) provide a more realistic performance estimate, and these results must be interpreted with caution pending external validation.

Limitations and future directions

The primary limitation of this study is its critically insufficient sample size, which constrains statistical power and generalizability. The marginal association between FIB-4 and hepatitis, which became non-significant after multiple comparisons correction, exemplifies the risk of Type II errors in this setting. Post-hoc power analysis confirms limited power for detecting moderate effects (Supplementary Table 3). The severe selection bias introduced by the 95.5% exclusion rate further limits applicability to broader CLD populations.

Furthermore, developing and testing multivariable models in a small sample carries a high risk of overfitting. While we employed 5-fold cross-validation and rigorous statistical corrections to mitigate this, the performance metrics—particularly the perfect sensitivities noted in the initial analysis—should be viewed as exploratory and require validation in larger, independent cohorts. The single-center, retrospective design and the protocol-specific use of Gd-BOPTA also limit immediate generalizability.

Consequently, the redefinition of FIB-4 and the performance claims of the two-step strategy are presented as a mechanistically plausible hypothesis and a proof-of-concept framework. Future work must involve prospective, multicenter validation in larger and more diverse cohorts. Investigations into abbreviated protocols, alternative contrast agents, and automated analysis pipelines are essential next steps for clinical translation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study challenges conventional biomarker classifications by proposing a pathophysiology-driven re-evaluation. We hypothesize that the FIB-4 index primarily reflects inflammatory activity, validate MRE’s role within its real-world constraints, and introduce DHR-MAP as a novel fibrosis-specific imaging biomarker. Synthesizing these insights, we propose a novel two-step diagnostic strategy that strategically sequences biomarkers to leverage their complementary strengths. This integrative framework demonstrates the potential for significant diagnostic error reduction and represents a move towards more precise, mechanism-based assessment in chronic liver disease, pending definitive validation in appropriately powered prospective studies.
