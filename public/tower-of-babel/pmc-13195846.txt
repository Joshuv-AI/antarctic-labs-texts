Dyslipidemia is associated with abdominal fat depots but not with bone mineral density and muscle fat infiltration
Authors: Yuhang Yang, Yunjie Zhang, Jianrong Ji, Chao Song, Shiping Wang, Zaiqiang Liu, Xiaopeng Li, Yuanjiang Min, Xihao Tang, Jianxun Jiang, Guixuan Li, Mengyuan Fan, Qinfei Yang, Shizheng Hui, Jun Yuan, Guang Yang, Yandong Liu, Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDyslipidemia (DLP) is linked to adverse abdominal fat distribution; however, its relationship with bone mineral density (BMD) and muscle fat infiltration (MFI) remains unclear. This study aimed to assess, using quantitative computed tomography (QCT), whether DLP is associated with abdominal fat depots, and if it independently correlates with BMD or MFI.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included participants aged ≥ 40 years from a health check-up cohort. Demographic data and fasting blood lipid measurements were collected for analysis. QCT imaging at the L2 vertebral level was employed to assess lumbar BMD at L1-L2, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), visceral adipose tissue (VAT), liver fat fraction (LFF), and paravertebral extensor muscle fat fraction (MFF). Participants were categorized into DLP and non-DLP groups based on lipid profiles or the use of lipid-lowering medication. Sex-specific analyses were conducted in both male and female participants, with adjustment for age, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference (WC); menstrual status was additionally included as a covariate in analyses of female participants.ResultsThe study included 2,115 participants: 1,426 males (53.3 ± 2.9 years; 48.7% DLP) and 689 females (48.9 ± 2.5 years; 29.0% DLP). Participants in the DLP group were younger on average (males: 52.6 ± 2.4 vs. 53.9 ± 3.1 years; females: 47.8 ± 1.9 vs. 49.4 ± 2.6 years), and had higher weight, BMI, and WC. However, increases in weight and WC were statistically significant only in males. The proportion of postmenopausal women was higher in the non-DLP group (32.5%). Age was the main factor influencing BMD, MFF, and VAT area in males, and BMD and LFF in females. Unadjusted comparisons revealed higher MFF in males and greater BMD, LFF, and SAT areas in females with DLP. After adjusting for confounders, especially age, differences in BMD and MFF were no longer significant in either sex. In fully adjusted models, no significant differences in body composition parameters were observed in males. In females, however, the DLP group had significantly higher LFF (P < 0.001), SAT (P = 0.012), and VAT areas (P = 0.022).ConclusionThis study confirms a sex-specific association between DLP and abdominal fat depots, with females showing higher liver fat and larger subcutaneous and visceral adipose areas. Critically, it demonstrates that DLP is not independently associated with BMD or MFI, as these parameters were primarily influenced by age. This dissociation underscores distinct pathophysiological pathways connecting DLP to different body composition compartments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-02899-1.Keywords: Dyslipidemia, Adipose Tissue, Bone Density, Muscular Atrophy, Body Composition Tomography

Introduction

Obesity can induce various chronic diseases and has become a major global health issue, with adipose tissue (AT) playing a crucial role in this process [1, 2]. AT consists primarily of adipocytes (fat cells) distributed throughout the body. Based on anatomical location, AT can be divided into subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and visceral adipose tissue (VAT). Additionally, adipocytes and lipids may accumulate in non-ATs or organs. Fat deposition in VAT and non-ATs or organs is defined as ectopic lipid deposition. Severe ectopic lipid deposition can cause tissue degeneration and organ dysfunction, leading to disease [3]. Fat distribution patterns vary, and specific distribution patterns may influence disease progression [4, 5].

Dyslipidemia (DLP), defined by elevated triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and/or reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), significantly contributes to the development of cardiovascular and metabolic disorders [6]. Blood lipid parameters effectively reflect lipid metabolic status within the body. Abnormal lipid profiles in DLP represent disrupted lipid metabolism, thus signifying a heightened risk of chronic diseases [7–9]. The pathogenesis of DLP is closely linked to AT distribution, particularly fat accumulation in specific abdominal depots. Extensive evidence shows that individuals with DLP are susceptible to VAT expansion and hepatic steatosis, forms of ectopic fat deposition that promote systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [10, 11]. The association between DLP and these abdominal fat depots is fundamental to understanding metabolic syndrome.

In contrast, the relationships between DLP and other ectopic fat deposits, particularly within bone and skeletal muscle, remain unclear and less explored. The influence of circulating lipids on bone mineral density (BMD) is complex, with studies reporting inconsistent results, positive, negative, or null associations, likely moderated by factors such as gender and age [12–15]. Additionally, the potential link between DLP and muscle fat infiltration (MFI), known as myosteatosis, which impairs muscle quality and function [16], has not been thoroughly investigated.

This knowledge gap may stem from methodological limitations. Common body composition techniques, such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), cannot quantify specific ectopic fat depots within muscle or bone marrow [17, 18]. Quantitative magnetic resonance imaging (QMRI) accurately distinguishes between SAT and VAT and measures fat fractions in the liver and muscles [17]. However, QMRI is costly, time-consuming, and has limited accessibility, hindering its application in large-sample studies. Furthermore, as a technique primarily used for soft tissue composition, QMRI cannot measure BMD. Quantitative computed tomography (QCT) is a reliable alternative, encompassing all relevant parameters. QCT accurately measures BMD and abdominal total soft tissue (TST), differentiating its subcomponents into SAT, VAT, and lean tissue (LT), and quantifies fat fractions in organs such as the liver and skeletal muscles, specifically liver fat fraction (LFF) and muscle fat fraction (MFF) [19, 20]. Routine chest CT enables comprehensive body composition analysis from a single scan, allowing concurrent assessment of multiple parameters [21].

Therefore, this study aimed to delineate the specificity of associations between DLP and various body composition parameters measured by QCT. It focused on links between DLP and BMD or MFI, while evaluating its relationship with abdominal adiposity. The findings may provide deeper insight into the interplay between lipid and musculoskeletal metabolism. Given established sexual dimorphism in lipid metabolism [22], all analyses were stratified by sex.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study population was recruited from an affiliated community of an enterprise in Anning City, Yunnan Province, China. Annual health check-ups were conducted by Anning First People’s Hospital between January and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) active employees of the enterprise; (2) age ≥ 40 years; and (3) voluntary consent to undergo chest CT and blood tests. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pregnancy; (2) inability to cooperate with examinations for various reasons; and (3) refusal to undergo chest CT or blood tests.

The current investigation forms part of a nationwide multicenter research initiative spearheaded by Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University, with collaborative participation from Anning First People’s Hospital. Institutional Review Board approval for the study protocol and informed consent forms was obtained from Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval no. K202216801).

Demographic data

Each subject had a unique personal identification number and electronic medical record containing basic information. Participants were instructed to fast overnight, and examinations were performed in the morning. Subjects wore lightweight clothing and stood barefoot for the during assessments. Uniformly trained medical staff measured height, weight, and waist circumference (WC) twice, and recorded the average values in medical record system. The Body Mass Index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated by weight divided by height2. Menstrual status of female subjects was also recorded. Demographic data were extracted collectively from the medical record system.

Lipid profile testing

Venous blood was collected from subjects after overnight fasting. TG levels were measured using the GPO-PAP method, TC by the enzymatic method, HDL-C by the direct method-catalase removal method, and LDL-C by the direct method-surfactant removal method. Reference values for lipid parameters were obtained from the database of the Clinical Laboratory Department of Anning First People’s Hospital. DLP was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, TC ≥ 5.18 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 3.37 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.16 mmol/L for males and < 1.29 mmol/L for females, or receiving lipid-lowering medication.

CT Scanning and QCT measurements

Trained radiologic technologists performed chest CT scans from the lung apices to the inferior margin of the L3 vertebra. All scans were conducted using a TOSHIBA Alexion scanner with standardized settings: table height fixed at 90 cm; tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 100 mAs; slice thickness, 1 mm; field of view (FOV), 500 mm; convolution kernel, FC18. Mindways QCT Pro software (v5.0) was used to measure BMD, SAT area, VAT area, LT area, LFF, and MFF (Fig. 1), collectively referred to as body composition parameters.

All QCT measurements were performed by 11 trained radiologists. Prior to formal analysis, a set of 20 images were measured collaboratively by the radiologists, achieving excellent intra-observer (intra-class correlation coefficients [ICC]: 0.923–0.989, P < 0.001) and inter-observer (ICC: 0.903–0.958, P < 0.001) agreement.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted separately for male and female groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical data are expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Comparisons between groups utilized Student’s t-test for variables with normal distribution and the Mann-Whitney U test for those with non-normal distribution. Chi-square tests were applied to assess differences in categorical variables. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for relevant covariates. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess correlations between body composition parameters and age, BMI, and WC. All continuous variables were standardized prior to analysis. The Correlation strength was defined by absolute correlation coefficients (R): extremely strong (0.8 < R ≤ 1), strong (0.6 < R ≤ 0.8), moderate (0.4 < R ≤ 0.6), weak (0.2 < R ≤ 0.4), and extremely weak (0 < R ≤ 0.2).

All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.2) and the Free Statistics analysis platform (Version 2.0).

Results

A total of 2,162 subjects participated in the annual physical examination, of whom 17 declined chest CT screening. Consequently, 2,145 sets of CT images were initially collected. However, 27 sets were excluded due to excessive noise or artifacts. Ultimately, CT images of 2,118 subjects were analyzed (Fig. 2). Additionally, three subjects did not complete lipid profile testing. Therefore, the final study population included 2,115 subjects, comprising 1,426 males (mean age: 53.3 ± 2.9 years, range: 48–63 years), with 695 individuals (48.7%) in the DLP group, and 689 females (mean age: 48.9 ± 2.5 years, range: 43–61 years), with 200 individuals (29.0%) in the DLP group.

In the non-DLP group (n = 1220), females accounted for 40.1% (n = 489) and males 59.9% (n = 731). In contrast, the DLP group (n = 895) had fewer females (22.3%, n = 200) and more males (77.7%, n = 695). The sex distribution significantly differed between groups (P < 0.001).

Effects of Age, BMI, and WC on body composition parameters

In males, age exhibited a negative association with BMD and positive associations with MFF and VAT area (all P < 0.05). Among females, age was negatively linked to BMD (P < 0.001) and positively related to LFF (P = 0.034). BMI was positively associated with most body composition measures in both sexes. Notably, MFF showed no significant relationship with BMI in either sex, and LT area displayed no significant correlation with BMI among females. WC demonstrated positive associations with the majority of the examined parameters; however, it negatively correlated with BMD in either sex and lacked a significant correlation with MFF among females (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study provides clear evidence supporting a sex-specific dissociation between DLP and various body composition parameters. The findings confirm the core hypothesis that DLP is significantly associated with adverse abdominal fat distribution in women but not with BMD or MFI in either sex. The pathophysiology of DLP is complex, with sex hormones exerting significant effects on lipid metabolism regulation. Consequently, notable disparities exist in fat distribution patterns and lipid profiles between males and females [22, 26, 27]. Moreover, fat distribution and serum lipid profiles in females significantly change during the perimenopausal and postmenopausal periods [27, 28]. Since estrogen favors subcutaneous fat storage, premenopausal females predominantly accumulate SAT, whereas males typically accumulate VAT [29]. With aging and declining estrogen levels post-menopause, females shift toward a visceral fat distribution, while males also progressively accumulate VAT with advancing age [29, 30]. Therefore, this study analyzed results separately for males and females and adjusted specifically for menstrual status in females. Fat distribution patterns relative to sex and age observed in this study align with previous findings [26–30]: females had greater SAT areas, while males had greater VAT areas (Tables 1 and 2). In males, VAT positively correlated with age; a similar trend of VAT increasing with age was observed in females (Fig. 3).

The most significant finding in females was a strong, independent association between DLP and a phenotype of centralized and ectopic fat accumulation, characterized by larger VAT and SAT areas and higher LFF. This aligns with the well-established pathophysiological model, where lipid overflow leads to VAT expansion and hepatic steatosis, which subsequently exacerbate DLP through increased free fatty acid flux and altered lipoprotein metabolism [10, 11]. The persistence of this association after adjusting for menopausal status underscores DLP as a key correlate of adverse fat distribution in women.

The data indicate no independent association between DLP and either BMD or MFI. The initial, unadjusted differences observed in male MFF and female BMD were entirely due to confounding by age. This null finding is important for several reasons. First, it helps resolve inconsistencies in the literature regarding lipids and BMD, suggesting that previously reported associations may have been confounded by age or other variables. In this study, the DLP group was younger than the non-DLP group. Indeed, the relationship between serum lipid levels and age is not always linear. Gobal et al. found that TG levels in males peak between 40 and 50 years of age and subsequently decline [26]. Similarly, TC levels in elderly individuals may decrease with age [31]. However, these decreasing trends might reflect cohort effects, generational differences, and environmental factors rather than truly indicating lipid-age relationships [32]. Second, it suggests that bone loss and myosteatosis in this middle-aged population are primarily driven by factors other than DLP, with aging as the most prominent factor. BMD in both sexes and MFF in males showed significant correlations with age (Fig. 3). Unadjusted comparisons between DLP and non-DLP groups revealed differences in male MFF and BMD. However, these differences were predominantly attributed to age disparities. After age adjustment, intergroup differences disappeared. Previous studies have similarly indicated that increases in bone marrow and intramuscular fat, affecting BMD and MFF, respectively, are primarily influenced by aging [20]. Aging shifts bone remodeling toward resorption and promotes pathological fat infiltration in muscle and bone marrow [33, 34]. Age-related decline in sex hormones, particularly estrogen, exacerbate these changes [33]. Therefore, significant age differences between groups introduce age as a strong confounder, potentially obscuring or exaggerating true associations between DLP and body composition. The metabolic disturbances of DLP appear specifically linked to abdominal and liver fat depots without directly affecting bone marrow or skeletal muscle fat accumulation.

Subjects in the DLP group had higher weight, BMI, and WC. Excessive caloric intake is primarily responsible for increased weight, BMI, and WC, resulting in fat accumulation in the body. Initially, this manifests as increased SAT. Once SAT storage capacity is exceeded, lipids begin accumulating as VAT. Generally, SAT accumulation is considered relatively benign, although its metabolic implications depend on the region of accumulation. For instance, SAT in thighs, buttocks, and lower abdomen is associated with lower serum TG and reduced hypo-HDL cholesterolemia risk [35]. Conversely, trunk or abdominal SAT accumulation may increase metabolic risks [35]. Unlike SAT, excessive VAT accumulation is categorized as ectopic lipid deposition and considered pathological. Excess VAT releases various adipokines, pro-inflammatory cytokines, glycerol, lactic acid, and free fatty acids into the hepatic portal system. This influx can disrupt hepatic lipid and glucose metabolism, contributing to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), DLP, cardiovascular diseases, insulin resistance, and diabetes mellitus [11, 35–38]. These mechanisms explain why female subjects in the DLP group in this study exhibited significantly higher LFF, SAT, and VAT areas than the non-DLP group. However, fat distribution differences between male groups were not statistically significant. This result may reflect sex-related differences in fat metabolism.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it included 2,115 participants, representing a relatively large sample size, and conducted sex-specific analyses, enhancing the representativeness of the results. Second, most large-scale studies on body composition primarily utilize DXA or BIA devices. However, this study employed chest CT images to measure BMD and abdominal fat distribution, a method capable of accurately distinguishing specific body composition compartments [18]. Furthermore, this study investigated the relationships between BMD, MFF, and DLP for the first time, finding no significant associations between DLP and these parameters.

This study also has limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits the analysis of causal relationships. Second, as a single-center study, the participants’ ages were predominantly between 40 and 60 years. Therefore, these findings require validation in populations from other regions and broader age ranges. The notable sex differences, particularly the lack of significant associations in males, warrant further investigation. This suggests that the relationship between DLP and fat distribution is stronger in women, potentially due to estrogen’s protective role in lipid metabolism. The loss of this protective effect during menopause may increase women’s susceptibility to DLP-related fat redistribution. In men, the relationship between DLP and body composition might be weaker or obscured by other lifestyle factors. Additionally, the study adjusted for relatively few confounding variables and did not consider participants’ medical history, dietary habits, physical activity levels, smoking, or alcohol consumption. Future studies should address these factors.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates a clear dissociation: DLP is significantly associated with abdominal-centric adiposity in women but not directly related to bone or muscle integrity as reflected by BMD and MFF. Clinicians managing female patients with DLP should emphasize the importance of addressing central obesity. Future research should focus on longitudinally exploring these associations and the mechanisms behind pronounced sex differences. Additionally, the null findings suggest that factors other than serum lipid levels primarily drive myosteatosis and osteoporosis in dyslipidemic individuals.
