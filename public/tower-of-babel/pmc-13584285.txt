What do we talk about when we talk about ecological determinants of health? A systematic review
Authors: Predrag Duric, Biljana Panin, Smiljana Rajcevic, Jelena Djekic Malbasa, Danica Mulic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing attention to political and commercial determinants of health may have unintentionally overshadowed ecological determinants, while persistent conceptual ambiguity between ecological determinants of health (EclDoH) and environmental determinants of health (EnvDoH) may limit clarity for research, policy, and practice. This systematic review aimed to synthesise how EclDoH are defined and conceptualised in the academic literature, identify the components described, and examine their relationships with other determinants of health, particularly environmental determinants, at a global level.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Peer-reviewed publications explicitly addressing EclDoH and providing definitions, components, or relationships with other determinants were eligible; non-English and non–peer-reviewed publications were excluded. A comprehensive search of Scopus, PubMed, and EBSCOhost (including CINAHL, MEDLINE and PsycInfo databases) was undertaken for peer-reviewed publications available up to 16 September 2025, with additional studies identified through citation chaining and targeted Google Scholar searches. As the aim was to synthesise definitions, conceptualisations, and relationships, formal risk-of-bias tools were not applied. Definitions and relationships were synthesised narratively, while components were analysed using an inductive thematic approach. The study was funded by the Provincial Secretariat for Higher Education and ScientiicResearch of the Autonomous Province of Vojvodina, Republic of Serbia.ResultsA total of 100 publications were included, predominantly from high-income countries, particularly Canada (24%), the United States (13%), and Australia (12%). Just over half were original research articles, with the remainder comprising reviews, commentaries, book chapters, and conference papers. Only 16 publications (16%) explicitly defined EclDoH, most commonly framing them as systems and processes related to ecosystems, nature, and the non-human environment, often emphasising human–non-human interdependence. Components were identified in 94 publications and grouped into seven domains: physical environment, climate and weather, ecosystem, natural disasters, housing and urbanisation, food, and social environment, with the physical environment most frequently reported. Relationships with other determinants were described in 31 publications: 10 distinguished EclDoH from environmental determinants, 8 treated them as synonymous, and the remainder described interactions, particularly with social determinants of health.DiscussionThis review has found there is an absence of conceptual consensus on ecological determinants of health. This may limit comparability across studies and constrain effective research, advocacy, policymaking, and education. Greater conceptual clarity is essential to strengthen public health responses in the context of accelerating ecological change. Limitations: only English-language publications were included, and grey literature was notexamined. Introduction

Half a century after its publication, the Lalonde Report (1974) is considered a pivotal shift in public health thinking, moving away from a purely medical and technology-driven model toward an approach that emphasised social determinants of health [1]. This perspective was reinforced by the first International Conference on Health Promotion, Ottawa (1986), which produced the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion (1986) and reframed health as a positive resource shaped by interconnected social, political, and environmental determinants, rather than individual behaviour alone [2].

In the decades that followed, international public health agendas increasingly focused on the social determinants of health (SDoH), particularly through the World Health Organization’s Global Commission on Social Determinants of Health and its report Closing the Gap in a Generation [3], as well as the WHO’s Conceptual Framework for Action on the Social Determinants of Health [4]. The SDoH refer to “the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age that shape health” [4] and are widely valued for shifting focus away from individual behaviours and purely biomedical explanations toward broader social inequalities and health justice [5]. Although social determinants of health were initially defined more broadly, in practice they are often operationalised as economic stability, education access and quality, healthcare access and quality, neighbourhood and built environment, and social and community context [6].

While these initiatives advanced population health and equity, critiques highlighted the scope and limitations of the SDoH framework [7–10] and the need to extend determinants-based approaches to include political and commercial determinants of health [11]. The framework has been criticised for insufficiently addressing politics and power and for becoming increasingly depoliticised, with a shift toward downstream and individualised determinants [10]. It may also reinforce existing inequalities by promoting standardised, expert-driven approaches that fail to produce deep structural transformation; as Yates-Doerr argues, “My concern is that the desire to address the roots of a pre-given problem imagined to begin at a measurable point and to then advance to a predictable (i.e., determinant) place, sets us on a path toward prescriptive solutions that often do not result in the deep structural transformation they claim to inspire” [12]. In practice, SDH applications can generate long lists of determinants that overwhelm decision-makers, obscure priorities, and allow prior assumptions to guide interventions [11]. Additional limitations include the conflation of social and economic factors, the tendency for adoption by elite organisations to result in incremental rather than transformative change, and the misleading distinction between “medical” and “non-medical” factors, which obscures how social, economic, and political forces shape healthcare systems themselves [11].

Within this evolving landscape, the Canadian Public Health Association’s discussion paper Global Change and Public Health: Addressing the Ecological Determinants of Health (CPHA, 2015) advanced the concept of ecological determinants of health (EclDoH) [13]. The paper emphasised the implications of global ecological change for population health and equity. While developed for the Canadian context, its importance extends globally, particularly given the absence of a comparable international framework. It defines these determinants as “the ecosystem-based ‘goods and services’ that we get from nature” [13]. The document categorises determinants into three groups: essential for life (oxygen, water, food); vital ecological processes and natural resources (ozone layer, nutrient cycles, fertile soil, freshwater, marine systems); and determinants crucial for human culture and civilisation (construction materials, energy, stable global climate).

Despite its significance, the broader uptake and influence of EclDoH in the international literature remain unclear. Increasing attention to political and commercial determinants of health may have inadvertently overshadowed ecological determinants, while ongoing conceptual ambiguity between EclDoH and EnvDoH continues to constrain research, policy, and practice [14].

As described by Lisitza and Wolbring, these conceptual gaps have real consequences: the EcoHealth community is unable to fully identify and integrate the multiple determinants that affect the health of humans, animals, and the environment [14]. This limitation acts as a barrier to understanding and improving interactions at the human–animal–environment interface. A lack of conceptual clarity also contributes to reduced understanding, limited enthusiasm, and insufficient funding for EcoHealth initiatives. Consequently, EcoHealth’s capacity to improve health and well-being across humans, animals, and ecosystems is constrained [14]. These challenges underscore the importance of explicitly identifying and integrating different types of determinants of health, and of applying them in ways that meaningfully inform research, policy, and practice. Without such clarity, complex health problems may not be addressed effectively, and the potential for EcoHealth to serve as a model for integrated health approaches is diminished.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review of peer-reviewed literature following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Our objectives were:

to synthesise how ecological determinants of health are defined in the literature;

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review to examine how EclDoH have been conceptualised, following PRISMA 2020 guidelines [15]. The review team included a lead reviewer, three secondary reviewers, and an independent arbitrating reviewer, all of whom participated in each stage of the review.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were peer-reviewed publications addressing human health or related topics, including empirical studies, editorials, commentaries, opinion pieces, letters, conference papers, and book chapters. Inclusion criteria were:

Explicitly addressing EclDoH or related topics such as public health, planetary health, global health, or mental health.

Explicitly addressing EclDoH or related topics such as public health, planetary health, global health, or mental health.

Providing a definition, describing components, or examining relationships with environmental determinants or other determinants.

Providing a definition, describing components, or examining relationships with environmental determinants or other determinants.

Studies were excluded if EclDoH could not be clearly distinguished as a distinct determinant. Non-English publications and non-peer-reviewed sources were also excluded (Supplementary File B).

As the aim was to capture definitions, components, and relationships, non-empirical literature was included to identify conceptual insights in editorials, commentaries, opinion pieces, letters, and book chapters.

Information sources and search strategy

The search strategy was developed iteratively and informed by this aim. Given the conceptual and heterogeneous nature of the literature, we did not adopt a formal search framework such as PICO or SPIDER, which are more commonly applied to intervention-based or empirical qualitative research. Instead, we used a focused, concept-led approach, selecting search terms that explicitly captured ecological determinants of health across Scopus, PubMed and EBSCOhost, using title, abstract and keyword fields as appropriate. This approach is consistent with previous work in related areas, where reviews have sought to synthesise definitions and conceptualisations rather than evaluate interventions. For example, the systematic review of commercial determinants of health and definitions of global health similarly addressed variation in how key concepts are understood across the literature [16, 17].

We systematically searched Scopus, PubMed, and EBSCOhost (including CINAHL, MEDLINE and PsycInfo databases) for publications available up to 16 September 2025 using predefined search terms related to EclDoH. Full search strategies are provided in Supplementary File A. Search results were imported into Rayyan both to remove duplicates and for screening. Reference lists of included studies and targeted Google Scholar searches were also examined to identify additional publications (Supplementary File C).

Study selection

Four reviewers independently screened each titles and abstracts, and each record was double-screened. For publications without abstracts (e.g., editorials, correspondence, book chapters), executive summaries or tables of contents were used. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a reviewer acting as an independent arbitrator was consulted if consensus could not be reached. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed, and authors were contacted when full texts were inaccessible.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted using a standardised template in Microsoft Excel, capturing: author(s), year, publication type, study design, country of first or corresponding author, definitions, components, and relationships with other determinants (Supplementary File D). Four reviewers independently and systematically reviewed each full-text article to identify and manually extracted verbatim definitions, descriptions of components, and analyses of relationships with environmental, social, or other determinants.

An inductive thematic analysis [18] was then conducted to identify patterns and develop thematic categories. We adopted an inductive approach to thematic analysis to allow themes to emerge from the data rather than applying predefined categories. This was considered appropriate given the conceptual and heterogeneous nature of the literature, where definitions and components are not standardised. The iterative, ‘bottom-up’ process supported a more in-depth exploration of patterns across studies while retaining flexibility in relation to theoretical frameworks. This approach is consistent with previous conceptual systematic reviews, including those by de Lacy-Vawdon and Livingstone [16] and Salm et al. [17], which similarly used iterative coding and abstraction to develop higher-level thematic categories. Themes were generated based on frequency, consistency, and conceptual clarity across studies. Relationships between EclDoH and other determinants were mapped to explore conceptual linkages. Quantitative summaries (counts of studies reporting each component) were used to support qualitative synthesis where appropriate. EclDoH definitions and the relationships were analysed narratively.

To increase inter-rater reliability, at the beginning each of the first 20 full text were checked by each of the reviewers independently; discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The template was iteratively refined, allowing the development of main domains, themes, and components. All analyses and reporting were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 checklist.

Quality assessment

A formal methodological quality or risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted, as the aim of this review was to synthesise definitions, conceptualisations, and relationships rather than to evaluate the effectiveness or outcomes of empirical studies. Conventional risk-of-bias tools are designed for assessing methodological quality in empirical research and were therefore not appropriate for this type of conceptual analysis.

Instead, we appraised the type and source of publications, the nature of evidence, and the use of theoretical or conceptual frameworks. Observations on evidence quality are reported in the Results section.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this study.

The study was not registered in any registry. It is part of a series of systematic reviews that aim to identify definitions, components, and relationships among different determinants of health.

Results

Overview of included publications

Database searches identified 609 records, of which 239 were retained for full-text assessment. Following application of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 85 publications met eligibility requirements. An additional 15 publications were identified through citation chaining and targeted Google Scholar searches. In total, 100 publications were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1, Supplementary File E).

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection

The temporal distribution shows a clear increase in publications over time. Slightly more than half (n = 55) were published between 2020 and September 2025, with 37 publications (37%) from 2010 to 2019. Earlier publications were few, with three in the 2000s and five prior to 2000 (Supplementary File F).

Geographic distribution

Most publications originated from high-income countries. Based on first or corresponding author affiliation, Canada accounted for 24% (n = 24), the United States 13% (n = 13), Australia 12% (n = 12), and the United Kingdom 8% (n = 8), together representing 57% of the total. The remaining 43% (n = 43) were from other countries, most commonly Asia (n = 21), Europe (n = 9), and Latin America (n = 7) (Supplementary File G).

Publication characteristics and quality assessment

In total, 100 publications were included in the review, comprising 93 journal articles, 5 book chapters, and 2 conference papers(see Supplementary File G). The journal articles comprised research articles, review articles, commentaries, editorials, essays, letters, perspectives, short communications, and viewpoints.

Among the 53 research articles, study designs varied and included cohort studies, cross-sectional analyses and surveys, longitudinal follow-up studies, modelling studies, regression analyses, qualitative studies, secondary data analyses, ecological studies, seroprevalence studies, and others. Nine literature reviews were included, including five systematic, two scoping, one narrative and one rapid review.

With the exception of one study, all literature reviews focused on EclDoH. Almalki and Karunamoorthi reviewed the impact of bio-geo-ecological determinants on mosquito fauna in Saudi Arabia [19]; Bennett et al. synthesised existing evidence on the social and ecological determinants of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Africa [20]; Mahmoudi Nezhad et al. examined the prevalence, incidence, and ecological determinants of diabetic retinopathy in Iran [21]; and Tajvar et al. conducted a systematic review of individual and ecological determinants of maternal mortality globally, stratified by country income level [22]. DeWinter et al. undertook a scoping review on ecological determinants of the leishmaniasis vector [23], while Moutinho et al. identified and described methodological approaches used to investigate the distribution and ecological determinants of mosquito-borne infections in Europe [24]. Rahman and Willott employed a narrative review approach to explore links between social, economic, and ecological factors and socioeconomic inequalities in tuberculosis burden [25].

The included texts addressed a wide range of topics, including environment, health promotion, Indigenous health, equity, planetary health, globalisation, mental health, infectious diseases, nutrition, and early warning systems. However, many of the articles focused on a single disease, most often a communicable disease (e.g. Zika, dengue, Chagas disease, leishmaniasis, leptospirosis, malaria). Other studies focused on non-communicable diseases (e.g. asthma, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, dental health) and unhealthy behaviours (e.g. sedentary behaviour). While some studies identified multiple ecological determinants of a specific disease or its agent (e.g. Moutinho et al., who systematically described a range of biotic and abiotic factors associated with mosquito-borne diseases [24]; Mahfouz et al., 1997, who identified ecological determinants associated with parasitic infections among pre-school children [26]; or DeWinter et al., 2024, who described ecological determinants of the leishmaniasis vector [23]), they generally did not extend this analysis to broader conceptualisation or framing of these factors. Notable exceptions include the study by Gislason et al., which examined the interplay between social and ecological determinants of mental health among children and youth in the context of climate change [27], and the commentary by Elton, which reconsidered the concept of food swamps through the lens of EclDoH [28].

In contrast, a smaller number of publications explicitly engaged with determinants of health (e.g. Lisitza and Wollbring, 2018; Hancock et al., 2022; Muntaner and Benach, 2023) [14, 29, 30], or more specifically with ecological determinants of health (e.g. Pasek and Saklak, 2017; Schultz, 2019; Parkes et al., 2020; Vold et al., 2022; Buse et al., 2023) [31–35]. These publications offered more direct insight into how EclDoH are currently conceptualised and positioned in relation to other determinants of health. Most of them proposed definitions of EclDoH and situated them alongside other determinants of health. In addition, several publications focusing on health promotion [29, 36], environment [37], globalisation [38], the Human Genome Organisation [39], EcoHealth [40], and the Mandala of Health [41] provided further contributions to the understanding of EclDoH.

Fourteen publications either framed their analyses using existing frameworks, proposed new frameworks, or based their reflections on established frameworks. Lisitza and Wolbring used CPHA eco-social framework to structure their analysis and subsequently proposed a new analytic framework for the determinants of health and EcoHealth [14]. Langmaid et al. applied elements of 21st-century socio-ecological thinking within a revised socio-ecological framework for health promotion [41]. Gislason et al. used an eco-social lens to develop a framework for understanding the interplay between social and ecological determinants of mental health among children and youth [27]. Dickin and Schuster-Wallace applied the Water-Associated Disease Index (WADI) conceptual framework to dengue at a regional level in Pernambuco State, Brazil [42]. Huynen et al. presented a conceptual framework for the health implications of globalisation, explicitly illustrating how globalisation influences institutional, economic, socio-cultural, and ecological determinants of population health [38]. Kathe and Wani applied a theoretical framework based on the County Health Rankings model to examine associations between COVID-19 case fatality rates and county-level ecological factors [43]. Oswald and Langmaid demonstrated how EclDoH can be incorporated into health promotion approaches targeting youth mental health [44].

It is also important to note that several publication types not typically prioritised in systematic reviews made valuable contributions to this review. For example, commentaries by Elton, Parkes et al., and Oswald and Langmaid, as well as a letter by Buse, offered important theoretical perspectives and, in some cases, explicitly defined EclDoH and positioned them in relation to other determinants of health [28, 33, 35, 44, 45]. Particularly relevant is the study by Lisitza and Wolbring, who employed a qualitative research design involving seven semi-structured interviews to explore the perspectives of a subset of the Canadian EcoHealth community on EcoHealth and determinants of health, including social, ecological, and environmental [14].

A substantial number of reviewed studies were excluded from the analysis because they focused on “socio-ecological determinants.” While some authors define socio-ecological determinants as the social, political, and economic power structures shaping equitable resource distribution, including ecosystem services [46], the majority use the term to denote determinants conceptualised through socio-ecological models [47, 48], covering nested levels from the individual to macro-systems.

Definitions consistently conceptualised EclDoH as foundational influences on health, encompassing natural processes, ecosystem goods and services, renewable resources, Earth systems, and environmental conditions shaping interdependence between humans, other life forms, and non-human nature (Table 1).

Components of ecological determinants

Most publications (n = 94) described at least one component of EclDoH (Supplementary File H), resulting in 86 distinct components. Conceptually similar components were merged (e.g., waste and sanitation with hygiene). Components per publication ranged from 1 to 21, with the highest reported by Moutinho et al. [24].

Components were grouped into themes, aggregated into seven broader domains: ecosystem; physical environment; climate and weather; natural disasters; housing and urbanisation; food; and social environment (Table 2). Each component was counted once per publication, so frequencies reflect the number of publications reporting it rather than its relative importance.

Physical environment was the most frequently reported domain (f = 166), followed by climate and weather (f = 94) and ecosystem (f = 79). Within physical environment, the most frequent themes were water (f = 39), land and soil (f = 34), and air/oxygen (f = 23). Within climate and weather and ecosystem, common themes included climate and weather (f = 41), ecosystem (f = 26), animals (f = 24), and vegetation (f = 23). For social environment, health and wellbeing was most frequent.

At the component level, water (including access, surface water bodies, and pollution; f = 31), climate change (f = 21), and air/oxygen (presence and pollution; f = 19) were the most frequently identified.

Relationships with other determinants

In 31 publications, we identified relationships between EclDoH and other determinants, both those explicitly described and those inferred from the text (Supplementary File I). Ten publications conceptualised EclDoH as distinct from environmental determinants, while eight treated them as synonymous. One described EnvDoH as a subset of EclDoH. Publications distinguishing EclDoH from EnvDoH were predominantly Canadian (6 of 10), whereas three of eight publications treating them as synonymous included Canadian authors.

Relationships with social determinants of health were discussed in 12 publications. One defined EclDoH as a component of SDoH, two treated them as equivalent, and the remaining described interactive or bidirectional relationships. In one publication, climate determinants were conceptualised as distinct from EclDoH.

Discussion

Ecological determinants of health represent one of several groups of health determinants that have gained prominence over the past decade. Their emergence reflects both a critical reassessment of the strengths and limitations of research and policy responses focused on social determinants, which rose to prominence earlier, and growing recognition of the limitations inherent in approaches that prioritise a single group of determinants. Political, commercial, and digital determinants have also increasingly been acknowledged as important influences on population health [11, 52].

Despite growing interest, our findings indicate that there is no shared global understanding of these determinants. With the notable exception of Canada, authors from other countries predominantly equate them with environmental determinants. Moreover, only a small proportion of publications explicitly defined them. In those studies, they are generally conceptualised as systems and processes related to ecosystems, nature, or the non-human environment, sometimes subdivided into physical, biological, and psychosocial components. Most definitions emphasise interactions between humans and non-human nature, including negative consequences of these interactions for ecosystems.

In an era characterised by an overabundance of information, precision and coherence in communication are particularly important [53, 54]. This applies not only to dialogue with policymakers, but also within the scientific community, educational contexts, and public engagement [55, 56]. Clear and consistent terminology supports trust, facilitates discussion, and enables consensus-building [57]. Divergent conceptualisations and definitions risk generating confusion, impeding effective communication, and limiting comparability of research findings [17, 58].

The majority of definitions identified in the reviewed publications originate from Canadian authors and reflect the CPHA approach. Restricting EclDoH to ecosystem-based “goods and services” derived from nature—including those essential for life (e.g., oxygen, water, and food), critical ecological processes and natural resources (e.g., the ozone layer, nutrient cycles, fertile soils, freshwater, and marine systems), as well as determinants fundamental to human culture and civilisation (e.g., construction materials, energy, and a stable global climate)—renders the concept sufficiently specific and delineates clear boundaries in relation to other determinants of health [13]. EclDoH are subject to global ecological change, which is an inherent aspect of the Earth’s geological and biotic evolution; however, contemporary concern arises from the unprecedented rate and scale of declines in ecological functioning attributable to human activity over the past century—particularly in the last 50 years—manifested through climate change, ecotoxicity, resource depletion, biodiversity loss, and marine degradation, thereby highlighting the pressures imposed on EclDoH by other, often social, political, and commercial, determinants of health [13].

Determinants not classified as ecological by CPHA, including natural disasters, non-human living systems (animals, vegetation, microorganisms), social environment, urbanisation, and housing, were frequently included, reflecting the enduring influence of socio-ecological health models and broader definitions [47, 48]. This holistic perspective contrasts with approaches that analytically separate ecological, social, and biological determinants, even while recognising their interdependence. The CPHA framework frames social and human factors as driving forces shaping ecological determinants rather than determinants themselves [13].

The literature prioritises harmful effects of human and societal activities on ecosystems and fundamental determinants such as water, air, soil, and biodiversity. Frequently examined issues include pollution, land degradation, deforestation, habitat destruction, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion. Some studies focus on human interactions with plants, animals, and microorganisms, particularly regarding disease emergence (e.g., zoonoses) and habitats favouring disease vectors [23, 59]. While this focus is necessary, it may obscure the direct positive contributions of ecosystem-based goods and services [60]. Emphasising their role could enhance awareness at global and community levels and support balanced public health strategies.

Our review highlights relationships with other determinants. Non-Canadian publications largely treat ecological determinants as equivalent to environmental determinants, while Canadian frameworks distinguish them [14, 26, 32, 61–63]. Social factors such as housing and urbanisation influence ecological determinants while remaining classified as social determinants. Conceptual slippage persists, with some studies equating ecological and social determinants or conflating ecological and environmental determinants [64, 65]. This underscores the lack of a precise definition and the absence of empirical research examining their interactions.

This study has some limitations. Only English-language publications were included, which may have excluded relevant research from non-English-speaking contexts; three such publications were identified but not reviewed in full. Grey literature was not examined, which could contain valuable insights outside the academic sphere. Finally, although the screeners conducted thorough evaluations, some EclDoH may have been missed in the literature reviewed. Our study focused on publications in which authors explicitly defined EclDoH or described their components and interactions with other determinants. We also included studies thataddressed these concepts more implicitly; however, such instances were more dificult to capture. Consequently, studies that addressed these concepts more implicitly, as well as broader discussions on the influence of ecology and the environment across determinants of health when not explicitly framed as EclDoH, may not have been captured, potentially overlooking important contextual factors. Future research exploring definitions and relationships among ecological, environmental, and other determinants would help build consensus and strengthen comprehensive public health frameworks.

Conclusion

At a time when protecting nature and its resources is a central priority for public health and planetary well-being, focused attention to ecological determinants of health—the ecosystem-based goods and services provided by nature—remains important. Achieving this requires international consensus on what constitutes EclDoH, what they include, and how they relate to other determinants of health. The 2015 CPHA Discussion Paper provided a useful starting point. Yet, a decade later, progress toward a shared global understanding appears limited. Many authors continue to subsume a wide range of psychosocial, biological, and physical factors under the EclDoH label.

The CPHA proposal to establish a United Nations Commission on the Ecological Determinants of Health is therefore noteworthy. Global consensus on EclDoH may support more effective research, advocacy, policymaking, education, and for building public trust— however we have found that there are substantial discrepancies in how this term is defined in the literature.
