A Fast Coordinate Descent Method for High-Dimensional Non-Negative Least Squares using a Unified Sparse Regression Framework
James Yang, Trevor Hastie

Abstract
We develop theoretical results that establish a connection across various regression methods such as the non-negative least squares, bounded variable least squares, simplex constrained least squares, and lasso. In particular, we show in general that a polyhedron constrained least squares problem admits a locally unique sparse solution in high dimensions. We demonstrate the power of our result by concretely quantifying the sparsity level for the aforementioned methods. Furthermore, we propose a novel coordinate descent based solver for NNLS in high dimensions using our theoretical result as motivation. We show through simulated data and a real data example that our solver achieves at least a 5x speed-up from the state-of-the-art solvers.



1
Introduction

Sparse regression methods have grown increasingly popular recently
with the prevalance of high-dimensional data and large computing resource.
Notably, the lasso
[
Tibshirani, 1996
]
is widely used
for feature selection as it often sets many coefficients exactly to zero.
It is particularly useful in ultra high-dimensional settings such as in genomics

[
He et al., 2022
;
Qian et al., 2020
;
Li et al., 2020
]
where feature selection is especially meaningful for interpretability.
The non-negative least squares (NNLS)
[
Lawson and Hanson, 1995
]

is also a reliable tool for many practitioners across diverse fields
as it often provides a physically meaningful and interpretable solution
due to its empirical sparsity

[
Li and Speed, 2000
;
Slawski and Hein, 2013
;
Chou et al., 2022
]
.
Some theoretical work exists around understanding this behavior

[
Bruckstein et al., 2008
;
Wang and Tang, 2009
;
Wang et al., 2011
]
.
A natural extension of NNLS is the bounded variable least squares (BVLS)
[
Stark and Parker, 1995
]
,
which extends the non-negativity constraint to any box constraint.
Similar sparsity occurs in high dimensions where many coefficients lie at their respective boundaries.
The simplex constrained least squares (SLS)
is at the heart of synthetic controls in causal inference
[
Abadie, 2021
]

where the potential outcome of no intervention for the treatment group is estimated
using a weighted average of the donor pool’s outcomes.

Abadie [2021]
notes that we often observe sparsity in the weighted average,
which is desirable for interpretability among practitioners
especially when the donor pool size is large.
Indeed, we see in
Lei and Sudijono [2024]
[Table 2]
that many real data examples have more donors than the pre-intervention time periods,
which guarantees the existence of a sparse solution per our results in
Section

3
.
Finally, the inequality simplex constrained least squares (ISLS) is commonly seen in portfolio optimization,
e.g., a long-only portfolio under a Markowitz model
with a leverage constraint so that the asset weights
w

satisfy
w\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}w_{i}\leq C
for some
C>0
.
The model may be in a high-dimensional setting if the covariance matrix of the assets
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

admits a low-rank representation
\Sigma=FF^{\top}
where
F\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
and
k<p
.
In this case, we often observe near zero weights for many assets
using a standard interior point solver.

While these methods may seem disparate at first glance,
they share a deep connection through the more general problem of polyhedron constrained least squares.
Our first contribution is a theoretical result in
Section

3
that establishes
the existence of a “sparse” solution to any polyhedron constrained least squares problem,
thereby unifying our understanding of sparse solutions to the aforementioned methods.
The notion of sparsity is extended to mean that the solution

binds
many linear inequality constraints as equality constraints in the polyhedron
(see
Definition

3.1
).
Moreover, we quantify the number of such binding constraints
and show the
local uniqueness property
, which states that
such sparse solutions are unique within the affine subspace defined by the binding constraints.
We apply our results to NNLS, BVLS, SLS, ISLS, lasso, and box constrained lasso to show that
the promised sparsity is at least
p-\rank(X)+O(1)
where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the feature matrix.
Lastly, we argue that the local uniqueness property helps explain
the success of many active-set optimizers for these problems.
Our second contribution is a computational result in
Section

4
that
uses the previous result as motivation to derive
a coordinate descent based solver for BVLS and NNLS with screening and an active-set strategy.
We show in
Section

5
that our solver
performs competitively in terms of accuracy and speed in high-dimensional settings.

For any two vectors

to be the element-wise

The

.
For a matrix

Given a set

The positive and negative parts of a value

3
Theoretical results

3.1
Background

We first begin with the definition of a polyhedron.
Note that some authors define a polyhedron more generally
and refer to
Definition

3.1
specifically as a
convex polyhedron
.
Since we always work with convex polyhedra in this paper, we simplify the terminology.

Definition 3.1

(Polyhedron)
.

\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a
polyhedron
if
there exists
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m

such that
\mathcal{P}\equiv\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:Ax\leq b\right\}
.
We say that the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is
represented by
A
and
b
.
An element
x\in\mathcal{P}

binds the
i
th constraint
if
a_{i}^{\top}x=b_{i}
.
Moreover, we say
x\in\mathcal{P}

binds
k
constraints
if there exists
S\subseteq[m]
of size
k

such that
x
binds the
i
th constraint for every
i\in S
.
We say a constraint
a_{i}
is non-zero if
a_{i}\neq\mathbf{0}
.

It is sometimes useful to identify a polyhedron
with a set of “non-trivial” inequality constraints
and a set of equality constraints that are always satisfied by its elements.
Concretely, it is useful to represent a polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{-S}x\leq b_{-S},\,A_{S}x=b_{S}\right\}

(1)

where
S:=\left\{i\in[m]:a_{i}^{\top}x=b_{i},\,\forall x\in\mathcal{P}\right\}

is the set of equality constraints.

Lemma

A.2
shows that

\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{-S}x\leq b_{-S}\right\}
has a non-empty interior,
which is a way to express
that this set of inequality constraints is “non-trivial.”
Moreover, the affine space
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{S}x=b_{S}\right\}
is uniquely determined.
This motivates our definition of a dimension of a polyhedron.

Definition 3.2

(Dimension of a polyhedron)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a non-empty polyhedron represented by
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
S:=\left\{i\in[m]:a_{i}^{\top}x=b_{i},\,\forall x\in\mathcal{P}\right\}
.
We call
S
the
maximal affine constraints of
\mathcal{P}
.
We define the
dimension of a polyhedron
\mathcal{P}

denoted as
\dim(\mathcal{P})
to be
p-\rank(A_{S})
.

Intuitively,
p-\rank(A_{S})
is the degrees of freedom in representing
an element
x\in\mathcal{P}
, and hence, the effective dimension of the polyhedron.
Since the affine space determined by
S
is uniquely determined,

\dim(\mathcal{P})
is well-defined.

Next, we extend the notion of a
hull
in
Definition

3.3
.

Definition 3.3

(Hull)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be any matrix and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be any set.
The
\mathcal{P}
-hull
of
Q
is defined to be

\displaystyle\hull(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x=Qw,\,w\in\mathcal{P}\right\}

(2)

If
\mathcal{P}
is empty, we take
\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
An element of
\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})
is called a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q
.
We say that
x\in\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})
is a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q
with
w\in\mathcal{P}

if
x=Qw
.

Definition

3.3
is a bona fide extension of commonly used hulls.
For example, a convex hull of a set of points
Q
is
\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})

where
\mathcal{P}
is the unit simplex; a conical hull of
Q
is where
\mathcal{P}
is the positive orthant.
This extension will become useful as we will soon discuss the
\mathcal{P}
-hull of
Q

when
\mathcal{P}
is a polyhedron.
As a first result, we show in
Lemma

A.3

that if
\mathcal{P}
is a polyhedron, then
\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})
is also a polyhedron.

3.2
Main results

In this section, we discuss our main results for showing the
existence of a sparse polyhedral regression solution in a high-dimensional setting
and the local uniqueness property of such solutions.
We first give a high-level description of our results.
Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a polyhedron represented by
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We will show that there always exists a solution to
the polyhedral regression

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

(3)

that binds many constraints of
A
.
Moreover, we will show the local uniqueness property
that such a solution is unique when restricted
to the affine subspace defined by the binding constraints.

We first restrict our attention to the simple case of a polyhedron

\mathcal{P}
with a non-empty interior.
Hence, the maximal affine constraints
S
is empty.
Suppose that
x\in\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})
so that
x=Qw
for some
w\in\mathcal{P}
.
Our goal is to show that we can find a (possibly different)
\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{P}

that binds at least a certain number of constraints of
A

while reproducing
x
, i.e.,
x=Q\tilde{w}
.

Lemma

3.4
formalizes this claim
and quantifies the number of binding constraints.
Moreover, it also shows that the binding constraints form a full row rank sub-matrix of
A
,
a fact that will become useful when we prove the local uniqueness property in
Theorem

3.8
.

:
Visualization of the main proof idea of
Lemma

3.4
.
The polyhedron
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
(with a non-empty interior) is given by the shaded blue region
defined by the constraints
a_{i}
(red arrows).
Suppose
w\in\mathcal{P}
(blue dot) is such that
x=Qw
.
If
v\neq\mathbf{0}\in\nullspace(Q)
,
then any point along the line
\mathcal{L}:=\left\{w+tv:t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

(dotted black line) reproduces
x
.
As long as the direction
v
is not orthogonal to every constraint,
i.e.,
Av\neq\mathbf{0}
,
then there always exists an intersection point
\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{P}

by
Lemma

A.4
.
The left plot shows the case of a polytope when
Av\neq\mathbf{0}

with the intersection point
\tilde{w}
denoted by a green star.
On the other hand, the right plot shows the opposite case

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be any matrix
and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be any polyhedron represented by

A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with a non-empty interior.
Suppose that
\nullspace(Q)\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\subseteq\nullspace(A)^{c}
.
If
x
is in the
\mathcal{P}
-hull of
Q
,
then
x
can be written as a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q

with an element
w
that binds at least

\min(\rank(A),(p-n)_{+})
constraints of
A

that form a full row rank sub-matrix of
A
.

We give a proof of
Lemma

3.4
in

Section

B.1
.
Note that
Lemma

3.4
is only interesting
in the high-dimensional setting of
p>n
.
The main idea of the proof is to consider a solution
w\in\mathcal{P}

such that
x=Qw
and move
w
along a direction in the null space of
Q

until a boundary of
\mathcal{P}
is hit.
Once we hit a boundary, we may restrict ourselves to that facet of the polyhedron
and repeat the process in this lower-dimensional polyhedron.
The reader should imagine “peeling” off the facet,
discarding the rest of the polyhedron,
and viewing this facet in a lower-dimensional space.
Each time we restrict to the lower-dimensional space,
we remove a degree of freedom from both
Q
and
\mathcal{P}
.
Since
p>n
, we can then guarantee that at least

p-n
degrees of freedom can be removed,
so long as there are enough degrees of freedom, i.e.,
\rank(A)
, to remove from
\mathcal{P}
.
With this intuition in mind, it is no surprise that
Lemma

3.4
holds.

We now discuss a few technical aspects of
Lemma

3.4
.
The hypothesis that
\nullspace(Q)\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\subseteq\nullspace(A)^{c}

guarantees that moving along
any
direction in
\nullspace(Q)

will hit a boundary of
\mathcal{P}

with the help of
Lemma

A.4
.
This condition may seem convoluted at first,
but it has a meaningful geometrical interpretation.
In words, any non-zero vector
v\in\nullspace(Q)

must not be orthogonal to every constraint of
A
, i.e.,
Av\neq\mathbf{0}
.
We give a visualization in
Figure

1
.
We show in the left plot a case of a polytope where
any direction
v
(not just in
\nullspace(Q)
)
satisfies
Av\neq\mathbf{0}
.
On the other hand, the right plot depicts the case of a single half-space
where our condition may fail (specifically along the direction parallel to the boundary).
In the same vein,
the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
has a non-empty interior is also crucial,
since otherwise, the line
\mathcal{L}=\left\{w+tv:t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

may only cross at
w

while satisfying
Av\neq\mathbf{0}

for every
v\in\nullspace(Q)

(imagine
\mathcal{P}
on the xy-plane and a line piercing through
w
not parallel to the z-axis).

We now remove the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
has a non-empty interior
while also strengthening the conclusion of
Lemma

3.4
.
We show in
Theorem

3.5
our main theoretical result,
which is an extension of
Lemma

3.4
to the most general case.

Theorem 3.5

(Existence of a sparse representation)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be any matrix
and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be any polyhedron represented by
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

with
S
as its maximal affine constraints.
Further let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\dim(\mathcal{P})}
be any full column rank matrix
such that
A_{S}V=\mathbf{0}
.
Suppose that
\nullspace(QV)\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\subseteq\nullspace(A_{-S}V)^{c}
.
If
x
is in the
\mathcal{P}
-hull of
Q
,
then
x
can be written as a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q

with an element
w
that binds at least
\min(\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S}),\dim(\mathcal{P})-\rank(QV))

constraints of
A_{-S}
that form a full row rank sub-matrix.

We give a proof of
Theorem

3.5
in

Section

B.2
.
We first explain the assumptions of the theorem.
As explained previously, a polyhedron may have an empty interior,
and therefore,
Lemma

3.4
cannot be applied directly.
The remedy is the same as in the previous discussion;
we first find the subspace in which the polyhedron lies (i.e., given by
S
) and
remove the degrees of freedom from
Q
and
\mathcal{P}
to restrict to a lower-dimensional space.
We then invoke
Lemma

3.4
on the lower-dimensional space
where the polyhedron is guaranteed to have a non-empty interior by
Lemma

A.5
.
The proof reveals that the new set of points is precisely
QV
and
the new polyhedron is represented by the constraint matrix
A_{-S}V
.
Moreover, we show in the proof that
\rank(A_{-S}V)=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})
.
Lastly, we note that
\dim(\mathcal{P})-\rank(QV)
has been improved from
\dim(\mathcal{P})-n
.
We argue that without loss of generality we may assume

QV
to be full row rank since otherwise we can perform a QR-decomposition
and use the “
R
” matrix in place of
QV
.

We provide a few consequences of
Theorem

3.5
.
The first consequence is
Corollary

3.6
, which shows that the famous
Carathéodory’s Theorem is an immediate corollary.
Secondly, we can finally establish the existence of a
“sparse” solution for polyhedral regression.
This is formally stated in
Corollary

3.7
.

Corollary 3.6

(Carathéodory’s Theorem)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be any matrix.
If
x
is a convex combination of the columns of
Q
,
then it can be written as a convex combination of
at most
n+1
columns of
Q
.
Equivalently, if
x
is a conical combination of the columns of
Q
,
then it can be written as a conical combination of
at most
n
columns of
Q
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
p>n
.

We first consider the case of convex combinations.
Define the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
represented by

\displaystyle A:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\\
-I\end{bmatrix},\quad b:=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}

(4)

Then,
S:=\left\{1\right\}
forms the maximal affine constraints.
It is clear that
\dim(\mathcal{P})=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})=p-1
.
Then, for the matrix
V
in
Theorem

3.5
,
which is injective, we have that

\nullspace(QV)\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}=\nullspace(V)^{c}=\nullspace(A_{-S}V)^{c}
.
By definition,
the convex hull of
Q
is equivalent to the
\mathcal{P}
-hull of
Q
.
Then, if
x
is a convex combination of
Q
,
i.e., a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q
,
then by
Theorem

3.5
,
it is a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q
with
w

that binds at least
p-1-\rank(QV)
constraints of
A_{-S}
.
In other words,
w\in\mathcal{P}
contains at most
\rank(QV)+1\leq n+1

positive values so that
x=Qw
is a convex combination of at most
n+1
columns of
Q
.

The second case of conical combinations is easier.
Define the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
represented by

\displaystyle A:=-I,\quad b:=\mathbf{0}

(5)

It is clear that
\dim(\mathcal{P})=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})=p
.
Since the maximal affine constraints is empty
and
A
is invertible, the conditions of
Theorem

3.5
are satisfied.
If
x
is a conical combination of
Q
,
i.e., a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q
,
then by
Theorem

3.5
,
it is a
\mathcal{P}
-combination of
Q
with
w

that binds at least
p-\rank(Q)
constraints of
A
.
In other words,
w\in\mathcal{P}
contains at most
\rank(Q)\leq n

positive values so that
x=Qw
is a conical combination of at most
n
columns of
Q
.
∎

Corollary 3.7

(Polyhedral regression)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be a feature matrix
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a response vector.
Suppose
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a polyhedron
represented by
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

that satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

3.5

with
Q\equiv X
.
Let
S
be the maximal affine constraints of
\mathcal{P}

and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\dim(\mathcal{P})}
such that
A_{S}V=\mathbf{0}
.
Consider the polyhedral regression given by
3
.
Then, there exists a solution
\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

that binds at least
\min(\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S}),\dim(\mathcal{P})-\rank(XV))
constraints of
A_{-S}

that form a full row rank sub-matrix.

Proof.

Note that any solution
\beta
for
3

must satisfy

\displaystyle\Pi_{\hull(X,\mathcal{P})}(y)=X\beta

(6)

where the projection exists since
\hull(X,\mathcal{P})
is closed and convex,
which follows directly from
Lemma

A.3
.
By
Theorem

3.5
,
since
\Pi_{\hull(X,\mathcal{P})}(y)\in\hull(X,\mathcal{P})
,
we have the desired claim.
∎

We conclude this section with a final result on

the local uniqueness property
of polyhedrons in
Theorem

3.8
.
The proof is provided in
Section

B.3
.

Theorem 3.8

(Local uniqueness property)
.

Consider the setting of
Theorem

3.5
.
Suppose that

\displaystyle\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})\geq\dim(\mathcal{P})-\rank(QV)

Let
w
be the sparse representation that binds at least
\dim(\mathcal{P})-\rank(QV)
constraints of
A_{-S}
.
Let
T
be the set of indices such that
(A_{-S})_{T}
denotes the binding constraints.
Define
\mathcal{T}:=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:(A_{-S})_{T}w=(b_{-S})_{T}\right\}
.
Then,
w
is the unique element in
\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{T}
such that
x=Qw
.

The local uniqueness property says that
the sparse solution
w
given by
Theorem

3.5

must be unique in a sufficiently restricted subspace.
The condition for
Theorem

3.8
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\rank(QV)+\rank(A)\geq p

(7)

This mild condition formalizes the idea that there must be enough degrees of freedom
in the set of points
QV
and the constraint matrix
A
combined
for the existence of a solution in such a sufficiently restricted subspace.
In many practical settings, this condition will hold true
as we often have
\rank(A)\geq p
.

The local uniqueness property offers a way of
concretely understanding active-set optimization algorithms.
Active-set strategies typicially employ heuristics
to first enforce many constraints and simplify an optimization problem
to a lower-dimensional problem.
If the binding constraints are correctly guessed,
then the solution to the lower-dimensional problem is optimal for the original problem.
These algorithms have found a place in the optimization literature
as sometimes the state-of-the-art methods for various polyhedral regression problems
such as the non-negative least squares and the lasso

[
Friedman et al., 2010
;
Yang and Hastie, 2024
;
Lawson and Hanson, 1995
;
Myre et al., 2017
;
Stark and Parker, 1995
]
.

Theorem

3.8
suggests that
if these strategies have an accurate way of
guessing the binding constraints of an optimal solution,
then the solution is unambiguous in the restricted subspace.
Hence, any optimizer should, in principle, quickly converge to the optimum.
In
Section

4
, we will utilize this fact
to derive a new coordinate descent based solver for
BVLS and NNLS that works especially well in high-dimensional settings.

3.3
Applications on sparse regression models

In this section, we use our main results from
Section

3.2

to unify many popular sparse regression models
such as NNLS, BVLS, SLS, ISLS, lasso, and box constrained lasso
under the umbrella of polyhedral regression.
In all these examples, we establish the existence of a sparse solution.
In the case of lasso, we rediscover a well-known result
that there exists a solution with at most
\rank(X)
non-zero entries
[
Tibshirani, 2012
]
.
For all other models, we show an analogous result,
which we believe is novel and does not exist in the literature
to the best of our knowledge.

3.3.1
Non-negative least squares

The non-negative least squares (NNLS) problem is given by

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta\geq\mathbf{0}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

(8)

Using
Corollary

3.7
,
we now show in
Corollary

3.9

that there
always
exists a solution with at most
\rank(X)
positive entries.

Corollary 3.9

(Existence of a sparse NNLS solution)
.

There exists an NNLS solution with at most
\rank(X)
positive values.

Proof.

The polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is given by the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
,
which can be represented by
A=-I
and
b=\mathbf{0}
.
Since
A
is invertible and the maximal affine constraint set is empty,
the conditions of
Theorem

3.5
are trivially satisfied.
Moreover,
\dim(\mathcal{P})=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})=p
.
By
Corollary

3.7
,
there exists a solution
\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

that binds at least
p-\rank(X)
constraints of
A
.
That is,
\hat{\beta}
has at most
\rank(X)
positive values.
∎

3.3.2
Bounded variable least squares

The bounded variable least squares (BVLS) problem is given by

\displaystyle\minimize_{\ell\leq\beta\leq u}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

(9)

which can be thought of as an extension of NNLS.
Similar to NNLS, we show in
Corollary

3.10

the existence of a sparse solution to BVLS.

Corollary 3.10

(Existence of a sparse BVLS solution)
.

There exists a BVLS solution with at most
\rank(X)
non-boundary values.

Proof.

The polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is given by

\displaystyle A:=\begin{bmatrix}-I\\
I\end{bmatrix},\quad b:=\begin{bmatrix}-\ell\\
u\end{bmatrix}

(10)

Since
A
has full column rank and the maximal affine constraint set is empty,
the conditions of
Theorem

3.5
are trivially satisfied.
Moreover
\dim(\mathcal{P})=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})=p
.
By
Corollary

3.7
, there exists a solution

\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
that binds at least
p-\rank(X)
constraints of
A
.
Note that a coefficient is either non-binding or exactly one of the lower or upper bounds is binding.
Hence, we indeed have that
\hat{\beta}
has at most
\rank(X)
non-binding entries.
∎

3.3.3
Simplex constrained least squares

The simplex constrained least squares (SLS) is given by

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta\geq\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}^{\top}\beta=C}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

(11)

and the inequality version (ISLS) is given by

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta\geq\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}^{\top}\beta\leq C}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

(12)

for some
C\geq 0
.
We show in
Corollary

3.11
the existence of a sparse solution to both problems.

Corollary 3.11

(Existence of a sparse ISLS/SLS solution)
.

There exists an ISLS solution with at most
\rank(X)
positive values,
and an SLS solution with at most
\rank(X)+1
positive values.

Proof.

We first consider the ISLS problem.
The polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is represented by

\displaystyle A:=\begin{bmatrix}-I\\
1^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad b:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
C\end{bmatrix}

(13)

Since
A
has full column rank and the maximal affine constraint set is empty,
the conditions of
Theorem

3.5
are trivially satisfied.
Moreover,
\dim(\mathcal{P})=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})=p
.
By
Corollary

3.7
, there exists a solution

\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
that binds at least
p-\rank(X)
constraints of
A
.
If
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\hat{\beta}<C
, then we have the desired claim.
Otherwise, there can be at most
\rank(X)+1
positive values in
\hat{\beta}
.
Without loss of generality, suppose
\hat{\beta}
has exactly
\rank(X)+1
positive values.
Let
\mathcal{A}:=\left\{j\in[p]:\hat{\beta}_{j}>0\right\}
.
Note that
X_{\cdot\mathcal{A}}
must have a non-trivial null space
since
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|=\rank(X)+1
by construction.
Consider any
v\neq\mathbf{0}\in\nullspace(X_{\cdot\mathcal{A}})
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
\mathbf{1}^{\top}v\leq 0
.
Then,
v
must have some strictly negative entry since otherwise
\mathbf{1}^{\top}v\leq 0

implies that
v\equiv\mathbf{0}
, which is a contradiction.
Then,
\mathbf{1}^{\top}(\hat{\beta}_{\mathcal{A}}+tv)\leq C
for all
t\geq 0

and there must exist a smallest
t
(in magnitude) such that

\hat{\beta}_{\mathcal{A}}+tv
contains a zero while remaining in
\mathcal{P}
.
Moreover,
X_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{\beta}_{\mathcal{A}}+tv)=X_{\mathcal{A}}\hat{\beta}_{\mathcal{A}}
by construction
so that the objective remains the same.
We have then constructed a possibly different optimal solution
with at most
\rank(X)
positive values.

For the SLS problem,
the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}
is the same as in the ISLS case
but the last constraint is always binding.
Since
A_{-S}\equiv-I
is invertible,
the conditions of
Theorem

3.5
are trivially satisfied.
Moreover,
\dim(\mathcal{P})=\rank(A)-\rank(A_{S})=p-1
.
Let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\dim(\mathcal{P})}
such that
A_{S}V=\mathbf{0}
.
By
Corollary

3.7
,
there exists a solution
\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

that binds at least
p-1-\rank(XV)\geq p-1-\rank(X)
constraints of
A_{-S}
.
That is,
\hat{\beta}
has at most
\rank(X)+1
positive values.
∎

3.3.4
Lasso

The lasso problem is given by

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\beta\right\|_{1}

(14)

for a regularization parameter
\lambda\geq 0
.
A perhaps interesting fact is that despite
14

being an unconstrained problem, one can show that
there exists some
C\geq 0
such that
14
is equivalent to the constrained problem

[
Hastie et al., 2001
]

\displaystyle\minimize_{\left\|\beta\right\|_{1}\leq C}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

(15)

When viewed this way, it becomes clearer why the lasso
exhibits very similar behavior to other polyhedral regression models such as NNLS.
Namely, the constraint
\left\|\beta\right\|_{1}\leq C
defines a polytope.
In
Corollary

3.12
,
we will use
15
to rediscover
the result that there exists a lasso solution with at most
\rank(X)
non-zero values.
As a novel extension, we show in
Corollary

3.13

that a similar result holds for the box constrained lasso problem given by

\displaystyle\minimize_{\ell\leq\beta\leq u}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\beta\right\|_{1}

(16)

Corollary 3.12

(Existence of a sparse lasso solution)
.

There exists a lasso solution with at most
\rank(X)
non-zero values.

Proof.

We use the constrained form
15
.
For any given lasso solution, it must lie in one of the
2^{p}

disjoint partitions of the
\ell_{1}
-ball given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{j}:=\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:AD^{j}\beta\leq b\right\},\quad A:=\begin{bmatrix}-I\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad b:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
C\end{bmatrix}

(17)

where
D^{j}
enumerates all diagonal sign matrices.
Hence, it suffices to show that the least squares
constrained to any one of the
\mathcal{P}^{j}
polyhedrons
admits a solution with at most
\rank(X)
non-zero values.
Now, the least squares constrained to
\mathcal{P}^{j}
can be reparametrized as

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-XD^{j}\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\subjto

\displaystyle\quad\beta\geq\mathbf{0},\quad\mathbf{1}^{\top}\beta\leq C

which is an ISLS problem.
By
Corollary

3.11
, there exists a solution
\hat{\beta}

with at most
\rank(XD^{j})\equiv\rank(X)
(since
D^{j}
is invertible) positive values.
Then, the original solution is given by
D^{j}\hat{\beta}
,
which does not change the number of non-zero values.
∎

Corollary 3.13

(Existence of a sparse box constrained lasso solution)
.

There exists a box constrained lasso solution
with at most
\rank(X)
non-zero, non-boundary values.

Proof.

Consider the proof setup as in
Corollary

3.12
.
The only change is in
\mathcal{P}^{j}
, which is now represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{j}:=\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:AD^{j}\beta\leq b\right\},\quad A:=\begin{bmatrix}-I\\
I\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad b:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\tilde{u}^{j}\\
C\end{bmatrix}

(18)

where
\tilde{u}^{j}_{k}=-\ell_{k}
if
D^{j}_{kk}=-1

and otherwise
u_{k}
.
The
\mathcal{P}^{j}
constrained least squares problem can be reparametrized as

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-XD^{j}\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\subjto

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{0}\leq\beta\leq\tilde{u}^{j},\quad\mathbf{1}^{\top}\beta\leq C

By
Corollary

3.7
,
there exists a solution
\hat{\beta}
that binds at most

p-\rank(XD^{j})\equiv p-\rank(X)
constraints.
Note that
\beta_{k}
is either non-binding or binding the lower or upper bound, exclusively.
If
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\beta<C
, then we have our desired claim.
Otherwise, we may use the same proof as in
Corollary

3.11
for this case.
∎

4
Coordinate descent for BVLS and NNLS

Consider the usual regression setup where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}

and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the feature matrix and response vector, respectively.
Let
\ell\leq u
be vectors in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{p}

denoting our lower and upper bounds (which may not be all finite) for the coefficients.
In this section, we discuss our algorithm for solving BVLS given by
9
.
Note that BVLS is an extension of NNLS
where
\ell\equiv\mathbf{0}
and
u
infinite.
Our proposed algorithm is specifically designed to tackle
high-dimensional settings where
p>n
.
Although our only use-case of our method is in solving NNLS,
the algorithm extends naturally to the case of BVLS, so we present the more general algorithm.
While BVLS may be an ill-posed problem if
p>n
,
there nonetheless always exists a solution to
9

since the objective is closed, convex, and proper and the constraint is convex.

The most popular optimizer for NNLS is an active-set strategy
first developed by Lawson and Hanson (LH-NNLS)
[
Lawson and Hanson, 1995
]
.
They propose an algorithm that searches for
the next variable to be added as a candidate non-zero variable.
Then, the unconstrained least squares solution is obtained for the set of non-zero variables.
A safety measure is put in place in case a variable is selected incorrectly.
Moreover, the algorithm is guaranteed to converge in finite number of steps.
Despite the simplicity of this algorithm,
it is now considered an out-dated algorithm for the scale of problems we see today.
Since then, many similar algorithms improve upon LH-NNLS
while adhering to the active-set strategy
[
Bro and De Jong, 1997
;
Van Benthem and Keenan, 2004
]
.
For example,
the FNNLS method
[
Bro and De Jong, 1997
]
is a direct modification of LH-NNLS
designed to cache certain quantities for reuse during subsequent similar computations.
Other methods such as the TNT-NN method
[
Myre et al., 2017
]

further improve LH-NNLS by solving
the least squares solution using a left-preconditioned conjugate gradient descent normal residual routine.
We found one reference that uses coordinate descent to solve NNLS
[
Franc et al., 2005
]
;
however, it does not incorporate clever screening or active-set strategies,
which is a large part of our method.

Our algorithm for solving
9

is based on the coordinate descent algorithm
[
Tseng, 2001
]
.
Indeed,
letting
\mathbbm{1}_{\beta_{i}\in C}

to be defined as
0
whenever
x\in C
and
+\infty
otherwise,
we may rewrite
9
as the following unconstrained problem:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}_{\ell_{i}\leq\beta_{i}\leq u_{i}}

where the objective is composed of a differentiable convex loss
and a non-differentiable separable convex penalty.
In this setting,
Tseng [2001]
provides a convergence guarantee.
We choose coordinate descent since it is the state-of-the-art method for fitting sparse regression models
such as the lasso and group lasso
[
Yang and Hastie, 2024
;
Friedman et al., 2010
]
.
With the help of
Corollary

3.10
,
we are assured that there must exist a sparse solution
with few coefficients in the interior of the box constraint.
Hence, we expect coordinate descent to efficiently find a sparse solution,
though no such guarantees exist.
Indeed, we observe in some cases, especially when
y
is perfectly reproducible,
that the solution contains more than
\rank(X)
non-binding entries.
However, in the cases when
y
is not perfectly reproducible,
we often observe a sparse solution.

The vanilla coordinate descent algorithm
simply cycles through each coefficient
\beta_{k}

and performs a
coordinate update
on
\beta_{k}
,
that is, optimize over the
k
th coordinate while holding all others fixed.
The separability and simplicity of the box constraints
allow us to implement an efficient closed-form coordinate update.
While it is possible in principle to solve BVLS by cycling over all
p
variables,
it generally suffers from a long computation time when

p>n
(underdetermined system) for two main reasons.
First, due to the non-uniqueness of the solution (in general)
and collinearity of
X
,
the iterates tend to wander for a long period of time before converging to a solution
if the algorithm is not well warm-started.
Secondly, the sheer computational complexity increases as
p
grows
since there are more variables to cycle over.
Before addressing these issues in full detail,
we give a high-level summary of our method.
First, we identify a well-chosen (and small) subset of features
S

that most require adjustments to their coefficients.
In the process, we check the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
to verify whether the current
\beta
is optimal.
If the KKT check does not pass, we solve the BVLS problem only on
S
.
This procedure is repeated until the KKT conditions are satisfied or
S\equiv[p]
.
We summarize the pseudocode in
Algorithm

1

and explain the steps in detail below.
We note that this type of active-set strategy is ubiquitous not only in
the BVLS and NNLS literature
[
Lawson and Hanson, 1995
;
Stark and Parker, 1995
;
Myre et al., 2017
]
,
but also in lasso type regression literature
[
Friedman et al., 2010
;
Zou and Hastie, 2005
;
Yang and Hastie, 2024
]
.

We first discuss how to choose the subset of features
S
.
Let
f(\beta)=\left\|y-X\beta\right\|_{2}^{2}/2
be the objective of BVLS.
Given an initial value
\beta
, we compute the gradient

\nabla f(\beta)=-X^{\top}(y-X\beta)
.
The KKT conditions for optimality state that
\beta
is optimal
if and only if

\displaystyle\forall i\in[p],\quad\partial_{i}f(\beta)\in\begin{cases}[0,\infty),&\beta_{i}=\ell_{i}\\
0,&\beta_{i}\in(\ell_{i},u_{i})\\
(-\infty,0],&\beta_{i}=u_{i}\end{cases}

(19)

Hence, it is intuitive to first order the coefficients by their violation of the KKT conditions.
Namely, we compute the
violations

\displaystyle\forall i\in[p],\quad\delta_{i}:=(\partial_{i}f(\beta))_{-}\mathbbm{1}_{\beta_{i}<u_{i}}+(\partial_{i}f(\beta))_{+}\mathbbm{1}_{\beta_{i}>\ell_{i}}

(20)

and sort in decreasing order from the most violating feature to the least.
We say that feature
i
is
violating
if
\delta_{i}>0
.
While this heuristic provides a reasonable way to determine the order of features
to include in the set
S
, it is not clear thus far
how many
to pick.
Fortunately,
Corollary

3.10
shows that
there always exists a solution with
\rank(X)

entries in the interior of their respective bounds.
A simple rule that works well in practice
is to batch at most
\rank(X)
most violating features at a time.
If
\rank(X)
is not known, one can approximate it with
\min(n,p)
.
In a sense, this batching creates a “path” of coefficients
similar to a penalized regression coefficient path (e.g., lasso) as the penalization is varied.

In the process,
we check the KKT conditions on
S^{c}
to ensure that we have found an optimal solution.
We simply check that
\nabla f(\beta)
satisfies
19
,
or equivalently, the violations are
0
.
For numerical stability, we relax the check with a tolerance that scales
with
X
and
y
.
If the check passes, we have found an optimal solution.
Otherwise, we continue the algorithm with the augmented
S
.
Since
S
is always strictly augmented,
we have guaranteed convergence via the coordinate descent algorithm
in the worst case that
S\equiv[p]
.
In practice, we rarely reach the worst case especially when
p\gg n
.

Given a subset of features
S
, we next solve BVLS only iterating over
S
.
We discuss a few optimizations to aid in faster convergence.
We keep track of the active set,
which contains the variables in
S
that are in the interior of their bounds.
We first perform one cycle over
S

and collect the active set in the process.
If we converged, we are done.
Otherwise, we cycle over only the active set until convergence.
We then remove variables in the active set that are at the boundary
and repeat the entire procedure.
This strategy significantly improves the performance of the algorithm
as
S
tends to be sufficiently larger than the active set.

We set the initial value
\beta

with
\beta_{i}=\ell_{i}
if
\left|\ell_{i}\right|<\left|u_{i}\right|

and otherwise
u_{i}
.
This way,
\beta
is always initialized at some vertex of the box
and is closest to zero entry-wise.
Since collinearity is inevitable in high-dimensional settings,
it is beneficial to initialize
\beta
close to
\mathbf{0}

to mimic the shrinkage in penalized regression.

.
Suppose we observe a response vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where each
y_{i}

are noisy observations of the mean function

\displaystyle f(t;\beta,\tau):=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\beta_{k}\phi(t-\tau_{k})

The coefficients
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
are assumed to be non-negative
and
\phi(\cdot)
is the mean-zero Gaussian density with a user-specified standard deviation.
In practice, the user defines
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
as a dense gridding
of an interval of interest, and the goal is to identify

\beta\geq\mathbf{0}
that best reconstructs
y
.
The positive entries of
\beta
then correspond to the important spike points in
\tau
.
The feature matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is then given by

\displaystyle X:=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(t_{1}-\tau_{1})&\dots&\phi(t_{1}-\tau_{p})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi(t_{n}-\tau_{1})&\dots&\phi(t_{1}-\tau_{p})\end{bmatrix}

where
y_{i}=f(t_{i};\beta,\tau)+\varepsilon_{i}
for some noise
\varepsilon_{i}
.
We use NNLS to solve for
\beta
by regressing
y
on
X
(given the non-negativity constraint).

Figure 3
:
Plot of the carbon monoxide level
y
within the first two weeks of measurement
and the NNLS predictions
\hat{y}
using adelie, MOSEK, and FNNLS.
We see that NNLS is able to reproduce
y
fairly well and identify the spikes.
The red “x”’s mark the top 30 spike locations
\tau_{k}
with the largest coefficients
\beta_{k}

using our method.
These spike locations align well with the spikes observed in the data.
All methods produce nearly identical fits.

We use the Air Quality dataset from UCI
[
Vito, 2008
]
to apply the spike deconvolution method.
This dataset consists of hourly averaged responses from 5 metal oxide chemical sensors
in an Air Quality Checmical Multisensor Device located in a significantly polluted area of an Italian city.
We use only two weeks worth of data from March 10, 2004 to March 24, 2004.
Our goal is to detect the spikes of carbon monoxide levels (mg/
m^{3}
).
After removing missing data, there are
n=304
samples.
The time stamps are put relative to the start date and normalized to the unit interval.
We let
p=1000
and set
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
equally spaced on the interval
[0,1]
.
The standard deviation in
\phi
is defined as
2 times the median (normalized) time difference in the
n
samples,
as similarly done in
Slawski and Hein [2013]
.
We solve the NNLS problem using our method, which takes 0.026 seconds.
For a comparison, we also solve it using MOSEK and FNNLS
[
Bro and De Jong, 1997
]
,
which take 0.12 seconds and 1.17 seconds, respectively.
In
Figure

3
, we show the response
y
along with the predictions
X\hat{\beta}

where
\hat{\beta}
is obtained via our method, MOSEK, and FNNLS.
We see that the fits are nearly identical and reproduce
y
fairly well.
Although all methods produce a feasible solution,
our method and FNNLS are the only ones that output sparse solutions.
FNNLS is the most accurate method, but it is the most time consuming.
MOSEK is only slightly less accurate despite being about 10x faster,
however, it outputs a dense solution, which is not as easily interpretable.
We see our method as a bridge between the two methods;
that is, we provide a sparse solution with a similar accuracy as MOSEK and at a fast speed.
Using our method, we identify
225
spikes for this example.

6
Discussion

We develop a framework for understanding sparse solutions to any polyhedron constrained least squares problem.
In particular, we establish a connection between popular regression methods
such as NNLS, BVLS, SLS, ISLS, and the lasso that they all admit a locally unique sparse solution in high dimensions.
We leverage this theory to derive an efficient coordinate descent based solver for NNLS,
demonstrating that our solver tends to quickly find such sparse solutions
in both simulated data settings and real data examples.

Our implementation is included in the Python package
adelie
,
which is available under the MIT license from PyPI
(
https://pypi.org/project/adelie
).
The user can install the latest version by running

pip install adelie
in the terminal.

Lemma A.1

(Interior of a polyhedron)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a polyhedron represented by
A
and
b
.
Then, the interior of
\mathcal{P}
is given by
\interior(\mathcal{P})=\left\{x\in\mathcal{P}:Ax<b\right\}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
\mathcal{P}
is non-empty.
Suppose
x\in\interior(\mathcal{P})
.
Then, there exists
B_{r}(x)
, a ball of radius
r
centered at
x
, for some
r>0

such that
B_{r}(x)\subseteq\interior(\mathcal{P})
.
Suppose
x
binds a constraint
a_{i}
for some
i\in[m]
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
a_{i}\neq\mathbf{0}

since it may be removed from the constraints without changing
\mathcal{P}
.
Consider
w:=x+\epsilon a_{i}
for any
\epsilon>0
.
Then,
a_{i}^{\top}w=a_{i}^{\top}x+\epsilon\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=b_{i}+\epsilon\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}>b_{i}

so that
w\notin\mathcal{P}
.
In particular, if
\epsilon<r/\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}
,
then
w\in B_{r}(x)
, which is a contradiction.

Conversely, suppose
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
Ax<b
.
Consider
\varphi
defined by

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle:=\max_{i\in[m]}\max_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}\left\{a_{i}^{\top}(x+tv)-b_{i}\right\}

(21)

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in[m]}\left\{a_{i}^{\top}x+t\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}-b_{i}\right\}

(22)

which identifies the constraint closest to the boundary
on the ball centered at
x
with radius
t
.
Note that
\varphi
is continuous,
\varphi(0)<0
, and
\varphi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
Hence, there exists
t^{\star}>0
such that
\varphi(t^{\star})=0
.
Note also that
\varphi
is strictly increasing,
so this implies that for any
t\in[0,t^{\star})
,

\varphi(t)<0
.
In other words,
B_{t}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{P}
so that
x
is an interior point.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a non-empty polyhedron represented by
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Define
S:=\left\{i\in[m]:a_{i}^{\top}x=b_{i},\,\forall x\in\mathcal{P}\right\}

as the set of constraints that are always binding.
Then, the polyhedron represented by
A_{-S}
and
b_{-S}
has a non-empty interior
and the affine space
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{S}x=b_{S}\}
is unique.
Consequently, any non-empty polyhedron can be decomposed as
an intersection of a polyhedron with a non-empty interior and a unique affine space.

Proof.

We first show that the polyhedron represented by
A_{-S}
and
b_{-S}
has a non-empty interior.
If
\mathcal{P}
has a non-empty interior, we are done.
Suppose
\mathcal{P}
has an empty interior.
We claim that there exists a constraint
a_{i^{\star}}
such that

a_{i^{\star}}^{\top}x=b_{i^{\star}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{P}
.
Suppose not so that for every constraint
a_{i}
, there exists
x_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
a_{i}^{\top}x_{i}<b_{i}
.
We show that there exists a convex combination of
\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
such that
Ax<b
,
which will establish a contradiction by
Lemma

A.1
.
Indeed, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{m}
strictly positive and
\sum_{k}\theta_{k}=1
,

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\top}\sum_{k}\theta_{k}x_{k}

\displaystyle<b_{i},\quad\forall i\in[m]

(23)

since for every
i\in[m]
,
a_{i}^{\top}x_{i}<b_{i}

while
a_{i}^{\top}x_{k}\leq b_{i}
for all
k\neq i
.
This completes the proof for showing there exists a constraint

a_{i^{\star}}
such that
a_{i^{\star}}^{\top}x=b_{i^{\star}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{P}
.
Once we identify the constraint
a_{i^{\star}}
,
we remove it and repeat the procedure on the polyhedron

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{-i^{\star}}x\leq b_{-i^{\star}}\right\}

until
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
has a non-empty interior.
Clearly, this process must terminate in finite steps.

We now show that the affine space determined by
S
is unique.
Suppose there exists a different affine space that contains
\mathcal{P}
.
Then,
\mathcal{P}
must lie in a strictly lower-dimensional subspace,
which implies that
\mathcal{P}
has an empty interior.
This contradicts the fact that we have terminated our procedure
when we reached
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
with a non-empty interior.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be any matrix and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a polyhedron.
Then,
\hull(Q,\mathcal{P})
is a polyhedron.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
\mathcal{P}
is non-empty.
By the polyhedron decomposition theorem,

\mathcal{P}
can be represented as a Minkowski sum of a cone and a polytope.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\cone(B_{\mathcal{C}})
and
\mathcal{V}=\conv(B_{\mathcal{V}})
denote the cone and the polytope
generated by
B_{\mathcal{C}}
and
B_{\mathcal{V}}
, respectively.
Then,
Q(\mathcal{P})=\cone(QB_{\mathcal{C}})+\conv(QB_{\mathcal{V}})
.
By the polyhedron decomposition theorem again,

Q(\mathcal{P})
is a polyhedron.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a polyhedron represented by

A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with a non-empty interior.
Suppose
y
is any element in the interior of
\mathcal{P}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
any vector.
Then,
v\notin\nullspace(A)
if and only if
there exists a point
w=y+tv
for some
t\neq 0
such that

w
lies in
\mathcal{P}
and binds at least one constraint of
A
.

Proof.

Since
y
is in the interior of
\mathcal{P}
,
we must have that
Ay<b
by
Lemma

A.1
.

Suppose
v\notin\nullspace(A)
so that
Av\neq\mathbf{0}
.
Then, there exists
i\in[m]
such that
a_{i}^{\top}v\neq 0
.
For any constraint
a_{i}
not orthogonal to
v
,

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\top}(y+tv)=b_{i}\iff t=\frac{b_{i}-a_{i}^{\top}y}{a_{i}^{\top}v}

(24)

Label the solution for
t
in
24

as
t_{i}^{\star}
for each
i
such that
a_{i}^{\top}v\neq 0
.
Then, we simply choose the
t_{i}^{\star}
that is smallest in magnitude,
that is,

\displaystyle i^{\star}:=\argmin_{i:a_{i}^{\top}v\neq 0}\left|t_{i}^{\star}\right|,\quad t^{\star}:=t_{i^{\star}}^{\star}

(25)

Note that since each
t_{i}^{\star}\neq 0
,
we also have that
t^{\star}\neq 0
.
With this choice of
t^{\star}
,
we have the guarantee that
w:=y+t^{\star}v

binds the
i^{\star}
th constraint
and is the first binding constraint so that
w
remains in
\mathcal{P}
.

Conversely, suppose
v\in\nullspace(A)
so that
Av=\mathbf{0}
.
Then, for any point
w=y+tv
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
we have that
Aw=Ay<b
.
Hence, every such point
w
does not bind any constraint.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a non-empty polyhedron
represented by
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

with
S
as its maximal affine constraints.
Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be any vector satisfying
A_{S}\xi=b_{S}

and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\dim(\mathcal{P})}
be any full column rank matrix
such that
A_{S}V\equiv\mathbf{0}
.
Define the following quantities

\displaystyle A^{\prime}:=A_{-S}V\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-\left|S\right|)\times\dim(\mathcal{P})},\quad b^{\prime}:=b_{-S}-A_{-S}\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m-\left|S\right|}

(26)

and let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\mathcal{P})}
be the polyhedron
represented by
26
.
Then, every
x\in\mathcal{P}
can be represented uniquely as
x=\xi+Vx^{\prime}
with
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
has a non-empty interior.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
\mathcal{P}\equiv\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{-S}x\leq b_{-S},\,A_{S}x=b_{S}\right\}
.
First, it is a standard linear algebra fact that
any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
A_{S}x=b_{S}
can be
uniquely identified with the low-rank representation

x=\xi+Vx^{\prime}
for some
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\mathcal{P})}
.
Then, we have the first claim by simple algebra that

\displaystyle A_{-S}x-b_{-S}=A^{\prime}x^{\prime}-b^{\prime}

(27)

We now show that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
has a non-empty interior.
Since
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:A_{-S}x\leq b_{-S}\right\}
has a non-empty interior by
Lemma

A.2
,
there exists
x\in\mathcal{P}
such that
A_{-S}x<b_{-S}

by
Lemma

A.1
.
By
27
, we must have that
A^{\prime}x^{\prime}<b^{\prime}
.
Another application of
Lemma

A.1
shows that
\interior(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
is non-empty.
∎

and it is clear that
\rank(AV^{\perp})=\rank(A_{S})
.
Moreover, since
A_{S}V=\mathbf{0}
,

Next, we show that

(34)

which is a contradiction.
