Pan-cancer 8q24 amplification predicts primary immunotherapy resistance and therapeutic vulnerabilities
Authors: Changyuan Chen, Shuye Liu, Jingxiu Feng, Zhihong Luo, Yun Bi, Qiuxia Huang, Yue Zhang, Hang Yu, Junwen Wu, Haolong Yu, Rumin Liu, Jintong Hu, Ling Ye, Yiyi Zhu, Ying He, Lang Chen, Lijun Lu, Ke Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune checkpoint blockade has transformed cancer therapy, yet most patients do not respond and reliable predictive biomarkers are lacking. Amplification of chromosomal region 8q24.21 is recurrent across cancer types, but its role in anti-tumor immunity is unknown. Through pan-cancer profiling of 10,809 tumor samples and validation in five independent immunotherapy cohorts (over 800 patients), we show that 8q24 amplification occurs in approximately 10% of human cancers and promises an immune-depleted microenvironment with reduced T cell infiltration and impaired antigen presentation. 8q24 amplification robustly predicted primary immunotherapy resistance and poor survival. A composite score integrating 8q24 status, tumor mutational burden, and PD-L1 expression effectively identified non-responders. Prognosis-driven and cell-line screening revealed 8q24-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities, providing a framework for targeted combination strategies in this genomically defined patient subset.Subject Areas: therapeutics, cancer

Introduction

Through releasing the natural brakes of the immune system, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) boosts immune cells to recognize and attack tumors, bringing about clinical benefits for some patients.1 However, a majority of patients are resistant to ICB, particularly when it comes to monotherapy.2,3 In consequence, Solving the problem of resistance to ICB is urgent. A major contributor to ICB unresponsiveness is the elimination of immune cells within cancers, which are known as “immune-depleted” cancers.4,5 This phenotype is attributed to multiple factors containing reduction in tumor antigen expression level, antigen presentation defect, impaired T cell cytotoxic activity, absence of T cell trafficking, and increase in immune evasion signaling.2,3 In addition, suppressive immune cell infiltration like cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)6,7 can result in difficulty of CD8+ T cells to penetrate into tumor bed. A higher level of tumor somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs) was also associated with a worse response to ICB.8 Nevertheless, no comprehensive study has examined the association between somatic copy number amplification and ICB response, especially when considering large-scale cancer cohorts and multiple ICB clinical trials.

Additionally, ICB can cause various immune related adverse reactions.9 Due to the limited ICB response in solid tumors, distinguishing a non-responder before ICB is essential for selecting an appropriate treatment plan for individual patients and avoiding serious immune related adverse reactions. Current biomarkers for predicting ICB response rates encompass tumor mutation burden (TMB),10,11 PD-L1 level,11,12,13 and high microsatellite instability (MSI-H).14,15 Whereas, no strong correlations were observed between either tumor-cell PD-L1 level or TMB and prognosis in several ICB cohorts.16,17 To distinguish non-responders from responders in patients bearing NSCLC malignancies, patient stratification only by PD-L1 expression or TMB level has poor performance.18 When it comes to identifying patients who could derive durable clinical benefit (DCB), a combination of TMB and PD-L1 level showed enhanced yet not optimal performance, and it was not effective to distinguish patients that achieve no clinical benefit,18 emphasizing the necessity of more powerful methods for discovering additional biomarkers.

High-level amplification of the 8q24.21 chromosomal region (8q24 amplification)19,20 has been associated with oncogenesis21 because of the amplification of oncogene MYC in the 8q24 locus. However, the function of 8q24 amplification in regulating tumor immune-milieu and ICB response has not been thoroughly examined, particularly in multiple large-scale ICB clinical trials.

Herein, we conduct comprehensive immunogenomic analysis on cancer patients from TCGA studies and ICB cohorts of different tumor types and demonstrate the widespread correlation between 8q24 amplification and immune-depleted tumor phenotypes as well as primary resistance to ICB. In light of these observations, prognosis-driven screening and cell line library screening were performed to identify a list of 8q24 amplification-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities, facilitating the development of combination therapies.

Results

Overview of workflow and datasets

The design of our study is schematically outlined in Figure 1. Our integrative analysis followed a four-stage pipeline (Figure 1A): 8q24 amplification characterization; tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) analysis; validation in ICB cohorts; and discovery of therapeutic vulnerabilities. To execute this workflow, we utilized three major datasets. The discovery cohort consisted of 10,809 tumor samples across 33 cancer types from TCGA program (Figure 1B). For clinical validation, we integrated 5 independent ICB cohorts totaling >800 patients treated with anti-PD-1/L1 monotherapy (Figure 1C). These cohorts spanned multiple solid tumors, including melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and urothelial carcinoma. To identify 8q24 amplification-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities, we utilized a cancer cell line dataset encompassing 996 cancer cell lines (Figure 1D).

8q24 amplification is frequently observed in pan-cancer and associated with lower survival rates

First, we interrogated genomic data from TCGA program to profile genome-wide SCNAs in 33 TCGA studies (n = 10,809 patients, Tables S1 and S2). We observed two genes (MYC [10.54%] and PVT1 [10.47%]) aligning to the 8q24.21 chromosomal segment had the highest frequency of amplification (Figure 2A). Apart from high-level amplification (8q24 amplification), low-level amplification (8q24 gain) resulting from gain of MYC and PVT1 was noted in 35.32% and 35.19% of tumors, respectively (Figure 2B). With MYC/PVT1 amplification, bulk tissues of tumors showed significantly increased average gene expression levels (vs. WT/gain). (Figure 2C). On 8q24.21, MYC and PVT1 were only 53 kb apart and frequently co-amplified in human tumors (Figure 2D). MYC and PVT1 co-amplification was observed in about 10.2% of tumors, while MYC amplification with PVT1 wild type/gain and PVT1 amplification with MYC wildtype/gain were found both in 0.2% of tumors (Figure 2D). The following analyses focused on 18 tumor types with frequent 8q24 amplification (Figure 2F and Table S3). The frequency of 8q24 amplification changed significantly among molecular subtypes in 7 of these 18 tumor types (Figure 2G).

Next, we examined the significance of 8q24 amplification as a prognostic factor across pan-cancers (Figures 2E–2I). Patients with amplification of both MYC and PVT1 exhibited statistically significant lower overall survival (OS) time (Figures 2E–2H). No significant distinction was found in the OS time between patients with amplification of both MYC and PVT1 and those with amplification of either gene (Figure 2E). When divided by molecular subtypes, the distinctions in OS remained significant among different tumor types (Figures 2I and S2).

The genetic alteration profiling of 8q24 and other oncogenic signaling pathways

Given that the clinical safety and efficacy of agents directly targeting MYC is yet to be fully substantiated,22,23 we next profiled the genetic alterations across 18 TCGA tumor types with frequent 8q24 amplification to identify alternative therapeutic vulnerabilities for patients with 8q24 amplification (Figure S3 and Table S4). To this end, we utilized the genomic data to systematically analyze patterns of co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity between 8q24 amplification and genetic alterations in other oncogenic signaling pathways. It was found a significant pattern of co-occurrence between 8q24 amplification and targetable oncogenes, including ERBB2 (q = 0.002), MET (q = 0.002), and CDK6 (q = 0.005) (Figure S3), suggesting 8q24-amplified patients can be treated with their corresponding inhibitors. Unfortunately, the co-occurrence occurs too infrequently to support broad clinical application (Figure S3). Importantly, we observed extremely low levels of TMB and MSI in patients with 8q24 amplification (Figure S3), suggesting that this group of patients with 8q24 amplification may respond poorly to ICB. Hence, it is very crucial to examine whether patients with 8q24 amplification can benefit from ICB and identify more 8q24 amplification-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities.22,23

8q24 amplification is related to immune-depleted tumor phenotypes across TCGA tumors

Next, we evaluated 8q24 amplification’s immunomodulating impact on tumor microenvironment. We conducted immune cell estimation of the TCGA transcriptomic data utilizing CIBERSORT,24 MCP-counter,25 quanTIseq,26 and EPIC27 to assess the impact of 8q24 amplification on immune cell abundance. We found that among most of 8q24-amplification cancer types (vs. 8q24-WT), the abundance of T cells, B cells, DC-cells, NK-cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, Tfh cells, memory B cells decreased significantly (Figures 3A and 3B). These significant alterations were also observed among molecular subtypes (Figures 3A and 3B). Consistent with this finding, in a subset of 8q24-amplification cancers, the abundance and variety of TCR and B cell receptor (BCR) repertoires were much lower (Figure 3C). Furthermore, we noticed higher proportions of CAFs in STAD_MSI, HNSC, LUAD, BRCA_Her2, and OV (Figure 3A). Conversely, in STAD_GS, HNSC_HPV+, BRCA_LumB, UCEC_CN_HIGH, READ_CIN, and PRAD_TMPRSS2_ERG_fusion, we noticed distinctive characteristics with elevated fraction of regulatory T cells, B cells, monocytes, and macrophages in 8q24-amplification cancers (Figure 3A). These findings suggest that the immunomodulating impact of 8q24 amplification on tumor microenvironment differed among different tumor types.

Based on published research, the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) distribution pattern is a critical pathological characteristic of cancers with prognostic significance.28 High levels of spatial diversity among tumors of TCGA studies were discovered in a pan-cancer investigation of TIL patterns determined by processing pathological images of tumors.29 We reviewed the TIL distribution patterns described by a published research29 to investigate the impact of 8q24 amplification on TIL distribution patterns. We focused on 10 types of tumors (STAD, PAAD, LUAD, LUSC, UCEC, BLCA, SKCM, BRCA, PRAD, and READ) with frequent 8q24 amplification. In general, we found that 8q24-amplified tumors had lower TIL density than 8q24-WT tumors. For instance, there was a tendency toward a lower fraction of the “brisk diffuse” pattern (containing diffusely dispersed TILs within the cancer) in 8q24-amplified cancers (vs. 8q24-WT tumors), whereas the fraction of “brisk band-like” pattern (with TILs mainly restricted to the invasive edge of the cancer) was higher in 8q24-amplified cancers (vs. 8q24-WT cancers), especially in BLCA, LUAD, and UCEC (Figures 3D and S4). It’s interesting to note that gradient shifts in TIL distribution patterns which matched to gradually amplified 8q24 (from WT to gain eventually to amplification) (Figure 3D), which supports the immunoregulating interaction between 8q24 amplification and the landscape of TIL distribution.

To further understand the biological processes associated with the cold immune phenotypes in cancers with 8q24 amplification, single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was employed for functional enrichment analyses of tumors in TCGA, focusing on 39 curated immune-related gene sets (Tables S7 and S8). Compared to 8q24-WT tumors, 8q24-amplified tumors demonstrated a significant decrease in a number of immune-related pathways including antigen processing and presentation, BCR/TCR signaling, interferon alpha/beta/gamma-mediated immune response, CTLs pathway (Figure S5 and Table S8).

Lastly, in order to gain insight into the possible regulatory mechanisms underlying immune-depleted cancer phenotypes in 8q24-amplification cancers, we also examined the expression of a collection of immunomodulating genes encompassing genes governing immune evasion, cytotoxic activity, antigen presentation, the genes controlling immune cells recruitment, and immune checkpoints (Table S5). 28 genes of these immunomodulating gene candidates displayed statistically significant variations in expression levels in at least one cancer type or molecular subtype between 8q24-amplified and 8q24-WT cancers (Figure 3E). Across a variety of cancer types or subtypes, we observed that 8q24-amplified cancers exhibited enhanced (vs. 8q24-WT cancers) expressions of 7 immune suppressive genes, including BTLA, IL10, KIR2DL1, KIR2DL3, PVR, VEGFA, and ARG1. IL-10 is a vital anti-inflammatory factor in suppressing T cell activation and cytotoxic activity.30 When PVR expressed on cancer cells contacts with immune cell (such as T cells and NK-cells) receptors, an inhibitory signal is sent out.31 The overexpression of BTLA exacerbates immune evasion by hampering immune cell function, especially T cells.32 The immune suppressive effect of VEGFA on T cells was emphasized in a number of researches.33,34,35 On the contrary, 8q24-amplified tumors displayed significantly reduced expression of genes governing T cell and NK-cell cytotoxic activities (e.g., PRF1, GZMA), antigen-presenting (e.g., HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DRB5), as well as immune cell recruitment (e.g., CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL2, and CCL5) among a variety of tumor types or molecular subtypes (Figure 3E). Within them, CXCL9 and CXCL10 are essential for T cell trafficking to the tumor bed,36,37 and CX3CL1 is involved in the maintenance of cytotoxic T cells.38 Taken collectively, downregulation of the immunomodulating genes controlling T cell and NK-cell cytotoxic activities, antigen presentation and immune cell trafficking, combined with upregulation of the genes regulating immune evasion can collaboratively result in immune-depleted tumor phenotypes in 8q24-amplified cancers.

8q24 amplification is related to a lower response to anti-PD-1/L1 monotherapy

Considering the evidence that immune-depleted cancers are likely to be resistant to ICB, we thus assumed that cases with tumors carrying 8q24 amplification could exhibit poor response rates to ICB. To assess how 8q24 amplification affected therapeutic outcomes in ICB-treated patients, we conducted comprehensive studies of the clinical and immunogenomic dataset of patients receiving ICB (monotherapy) across 5 solid tumor clinical trials (>800 patients) (Table S6).

First, we reviewed the clinical information of an NSCLC cohort.18 193 patients out of 240 patients receiving anti-PD-1/L1 monotherapy and with clinical and genomic data available were chosen for further analysis. (Figure 4A). Genomic data from the MSK-IMPACT panel was used to identify the status of 8q24.39 It was shown that a gradient decline in the DCB rates matched to lower TMB (from high to low) and gradually amplified copy number of 8q24 (from WT to amplification) (Figure 4B). Similarly, none of the patients with 8q24 amplification and low PD-L1 level had DCB, while patients with wildtype 8q24 and low PD-L1 level exhibited a 20% rate of DCB and patients with high PD-L1 level displayed a 34% rate of DCB (Figure 4D). These findings indicate that 8q24 amplification could be utilized as a biomarker synergizing with TMB/PD-L1, especially in distinguishing NSCLC patients that are not expected to respond well to PD-1/L1 monotherapy. Consistent with this, we demonstrated that patients with low levels of TMB/PD-L1 combined with 8q24 amplification, had a worse PFS (Figures 4C–4E), implying the potential prognostic significance of the combination of TMB/PD-L1 and 8q24 for patients receiving PD-1/L1 monotherapy.

We next assessed the effect of 8q24 amplification on ICB response in 3 melanoma cohorts40,41,42 (Figures 4F–4I and Table S6). 8q24 status was determined by expression levels of MYC and PVT1: cancers with expression levels of both MYC and PVT1 under the medians were categorized as “lo_lo”, and cancers with expression levels of both genes above the medians were categorized as “hi_hi”. For all 3 cohorts, patients were receiving anti-PD-1 monotherapy and with transcriptomic and clinical data available were chosen. In order to enhance the statistical power, we assessed the effect of 8q24 amplification on ICB response in melanoma patients by integrating these 3 datasets. In this combined melanoma cohort (n = 159), it was shown that the presence of low TMB combined with 8q24 amplification (the hi_hi group) was related to a significantly lower rate of CR/PR: patients with low TMB and 8q24 amplification had a 22% rate of CR/PR (vs. a 46% rate of CR/PR in patients with high TMB and wild type 8q24 (the lo_lo group) (Figure 4G). Additionally, we demonstrated that patients with low levels of TMB combined with 8q24 amplification, had worse PFS and OS (Figures 4H and 4I).

Eventually, we further evaluated the significance of the combination of 8q24 and other factors (PD-L1/TMB) as a biomarker in the IMvigor210 metastatic urothelial cancer cohort. PD-L1 staining and transcriptomic data of the pre-treatment patients with clinical data accessible (n = 298) were retrieved from the published research.16 8q24 status was determined depending on expression levels of MYC/PVT1, as previously mentioned in the combined melanoma cohorts. We initially determined whether variations of MYC/PVT1 expression were related to immune/tumor cell PD-L1 level, immune phenotypes, and immune cell infiltration. It was observed that tumors with high MYC expression (Q4) (Figure 5A) had significantly higher tumor cell PD-L1 levels, whereas changes in MYC expression did not significantly affect immune cell PD-L1 levels or immune phenotypes, in addition, it was found that no significant distinction in the immune cell abundance derived by CIBERSORT and MCP-counter between tumors with high MYC/PVT1 expression (hi_hi) and tumors with low MYC/PVT1 expression (lo_lo) (Figure 5B).

Next, we determined the impact of MYC/PVT1 expression level on OS following atezolizumab treatment. High MYC/PVT1 expression was associated with a decreased OS time in patients receiving atezolizumab (Figure 5C). By performing multivariable Cox regression analysis, it was revealed that 8q24 amplification was a robust predictive indicator of reduced OS time, independent of additional factors like TMB or PD-L1 level on immune cells (Figure 5D).

Further, we evaluated whether there was a synergy between 8q24 and PD-L1/TMB in finding patients who did not respond to atezolizumab (Figure 5E). It was displayed a gradient decrease in the rate of CR/PR, corresponding to lower immune cell PD-L1 level (from ≥5% to <5%) and higher expression of MYC/PVT1 (from lo_lo to hi_hi): the highest rate of CR/PR (39%) was seen in patients with high immune cell PD-L1 level (≥5%) and low MYC/PVT1 expression (lo_lo), and those with high immune cell PD-L1 level plus high MYC/PVT1 expression or those with low immune cell PD-L1 level plus low MYC/PVT1 expression had 27% and 20% rate of CR/PR, respectively, whereas those with low immune cell PD-L1 level and high MYC/PVT1 expression displayed the worst rate of CR/PR (7%, 4/53). Similarly, patients with lower TMB level and higher MYC/PVT1 expression had a significantly lower rate of CR/PR: cases with low TMB and high MYC/PVT1 expression had a 12% rate of CR/PR (vs. a 50% rate of CR/PR in cases with high TMB and low MYC/PVT1 expression).

To assess the translational value of the observations, a prognostic model based on logistic regression with these 3 variables (8q24, immune cell PD-L1 level, and TMB) was developed. The performance of the combination of all these 3 variables was superior to TMB, PD-L1, and their combination (Figure 5F). We established a “benefit score” based on the predicted probabilities and defined fixed risk-stratification cut-offs using tertiles in the training set (low benefit: <0.1266, n = 49; median benefit: 0.1266–0.2660, n = 48; high benefit: >0.2660, n = 49). It was shown that patients with a low benefit score had only 6% rate of CR/PR, while 16% of patients with a median benefit score and 53% of patients with a high benefit score derived CR/PR, respectively (Figure 5G). With the help of this model, we were able to classify patients into 3 groups: a “low benefit score” group consisting of patients who were almost all unresponsive patients (CR/PR: 6%), a “median benefit score” group with a rate of CR/PR that was three times higher (CR/PR: 16%) than that of the “low benefit score” group, and a “high benefit score” group with a rate of CR/PR that was approximately nine times (CR/PR: 53%) higher than that of the “low benefit score” group, comprised of patients who received the highest clinical benefit following atezolizumab treatment. To validate the clinical generalizability of our model and avoid overfitting, we applied these exact fixed cut-offs, derived from the training set, to a pooled independent validation cohort (RIAZ, LIU, and MSK, total N = 153) without any further optimization. The response rate increased progressively across the low (20.8%), intermediate (31.3%), and high (38.6%) groups (Cochran-Armitage trend test p = 0.05), demonstrating that this model provides a robust and standardized framework for predicting ICB response across diverse clinical contexts. (Figure S6).

In line with this, it was revealed that patients with high MYC/PVT1 expression had lower OS time, demonstrating that the combination of 8q24 and PD-L1 or TMB can more effectively classify patients (Figure 5E). Consistently, patients with a lower benefit score had poor clinical outcome, indicating the prognostic value of this benefit score (Figure 5H).

Overall, 8q24 amplification is linked to a lower ICB response rate and a poor clinical outcome, as shown in our analyses of five cohorts spanning a range of solid cancer types, emphasizing the critical need to find additional potential therapeutic vulnerabilities, which is discussed in the section that follows.

Prognosis-driven screening for therapeutic vulnerabilities

We next integrated four ICB cohorts using the ComBat algorithm, including the IMvigor210 metastatic urothelial carcinoma cohort from Mariathasan et al. and three metastatic melanoma cohorts from Riaz et al., Liu et al., and Hugo et al. (Figure 6A and Table S6). As the same method described previously, tumors with both MYC and PVT1 expression above the cohort-specific median were classified as “hi_hi,” while those with both below the median were classified as “lo_lo.” All patients included in the analysis were ICB-naïve and had response data available from pre-treatment tumor biopsies. We then performed gene-wise univariate Cox regression analysis within the “hi_hi” and “lo_lo” subgroups, respectively (Figure 6B). By this method, we aimed to identify “context-dependent” genes significantly associated with poor prognosis specifically in the context of high MYC/PVT1 expression, which representing 8q24 amplification.

To systematically quantify context-dependent prognostic effects, we developed a vulnerability index (VI) based on a Cox proportional hazards (PH) model with an explicit interaction term:

Within this framework, the vulnerability index (VI=exp(βinteraction)) for a given gene is mathematically equivalent to the ratio of its hazard ratios between the “hi_hi” and “lo_lo” subgroups. A VI > 1 indicates that the adverse prognostic association of a gene is specifically amplified in the 8q24-high context. Genes were retained as candidate vulnerabilities only if they satisfied all three criteria simultaneously: (1) the interaction term was significant (P_interaction <0.05), confirming that the gene-expression–hazard association differs between the 8q24-high (hi_hi) and 8q24-low (lo_lo) subgroups; (2) within the hi_hi subgroup, the gene-expression effect estimated from the same interaction Cox model was adverse (HR_hi_hi > 1) and significant (P_hi_hi < 0.05); and (3) within the lo_lo subgroup, the corresponding gene-expression effect was not significant (P_lo_lo ≥ 0.05), supporting 8q24-context specificity rather than a ubiquitous prognostic association. Applying this three-criterion filter to a genome-wide scan of 16,938 genes, we identified a final list of genes whose high expression is strongly and selectively associated with worse survival outcomes in the hi_hi patient subgroup, representing candidate therapeutic vulnerabilities specific to 8q24-amplified tumors, including OASL,43 LGALS4,44 AGT,45 APOA2,46 FGFR4,47 TRIM6,48 ARG1,49 and APOC150 (Figure 6C). 8q24 amplification-specific significantly worse prognostic effects of these genes were further supported by a unified Cox PH model with a gene-expression×8q24-status interaction term (Figure 6D), highlighting them as potential context-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities in 8q24-amplified patients treated with ICB.

Cell library screening for therapeutic vulnerabilities

We further leveraged a list of 996 cancer cell lines to identify more 8q24 amplification-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities (Table S9). Consistent with genomic data from patients, MYC and PVT1 had the highest frequency of amplification across cancer cell lines (Figure 7A). The status of 8q24 was determined by the status of MYC/PVT1 (Figure 7B). In brief, the cell line with diploidy MYC/PVT1 were identified as “8q24-WT”, cell lines with gain of MYC/PVT1 were identified as “8q24-gain”, and cell lines had high-level amplification of either MYC or PVT1 were identified as “8q24-amplification”. Next, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR differential-dependency screen between “8q24-amplification” cell lines and “8q24-WT” cell lines to identify 8q24 amplification-specific vulnerabilities (Table S10). We further reviewed efficacy targets of approved drugs as well as clinical-phase drug candidates (Table S11), and ultimately yielded 105 druggable 8q24 amplification-specific vulnerabilities (Figure 7C).

Discussion

8q24 amplification is among the most frequently occurring somatic copy-number alterations (SCNAs) across human tumors.19,20 Nevertheless, the clinical implications of 8q24 amplification remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding its impact on the microenvironment and response to ICB. In this study, we comprehensively examined 8q24 amplification across a range of tumor types using large-scale data from the TCGA program and five ICB cohorts. Through integrating immunogenomic, transcriptomic, and clinical datasets, we were able to decipher how 8q24 amplification impacts the landscape of tumor immunity and the effectiveness of ICB. It was revealed that 8q24 amplification is linked to an “immune-depleted” tumor phenotype, primary resistance to ICB, and worse prognosis. A major hurdle to the effectiveness of current tumor therapies is primary resistance to ICB,3 and deciphering the underlying molecular determinants may facilitate the development of actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities to improve clinical outcomes.

We show that 8q24-amplified tumors were immune-depleted, displaying significantly lower TIL densities, decreased infiltration of both innate (NK cells) and adaptive (T and B cells) immune populations, altered TIL distribution patterns, reduced TCR/BCR diversity, and impaired antigen presentation. The “immune-depleted” phenotypes observed in 8q24-amplification tumors were probably triggered by both reduced factors governing T cell and NK-cell cytotoxic activity, antigen presentation, and immune-cell trafficking, and the overexpression of genes controlling immune evasion. For instance, expression of genes involved in cytotoxic activity (e.g., PRF1 and GZMA), antigen presentation (e.g., HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DRB5), and immune-cell trafficking (e.g., CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL2, and CCL5) was significantly reduced, whereas immune-evasion genes such as BTLA, IL10, PVR (CD155), VEGFA, and ARG1 were increased in 8q24-amplified tumors. Taken collectively, these diverse mechanisms reflect a close connection between 8q24 amplification and adverse tumor immune-microenvironment reprogramming.

Importantly, clinical benefit from ICB is governed by complex and dynamic interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME), rather than by lymphocyte abundance alone. Tumor-intrinsic programs can shape antigen availability and immune priming, remodel cytokine and chemokine networks that control immune-cell trafficking, and promote stromal, vascular, and myeloid suppressive states that constrain effector function. For example, approaches that triggers immunogenic cell death—including pyroptosis-like inflammatory cell death—has been proposed to convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” lesions by enhancing dendritic-cell activation and T cell infiltration, providing a mechanistic rationale for synergy with ICB.57 Pan-cancer analyses further link cell-death-associated tumor states to TME immune features and immunotherapy responsiveness.58 Moreover, multi-omic studies indicate that specific tumor-intrinsic regulators can be inversely associated with antitumor immune programs and immune-cell infiltration within the TME59. Correlative studies also suggest that additional tumor molecular programs, including glycosylation-related signatures, track with immune phenotypes and potential immunotherapy response.60 In this broader framework, our data support a model in which 8q24 amplification promotes primary ICB resistance through coordinated, tumor-intrinsic TME reprogramming—characterized by concurrent attenuation of antigen presentation and immune-cell recruitment programs and enrichment of immunosuppressive/vascular-myeloid circuits—thereby limiting immune priming, infiltration, and effector function.

Another key observation is a strong correlation between 8q24 amplification and primary resistance to ICB. Even though we used different approaches to determine 8q24 status, the comprehensive analyses of the clinical and immunogenomic data from five ICB cohorts treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy consistently demonstrate a strong association between 8q24 amplification and lower ICB response. Large-scale cohorts including the combined melanoma cohorts, NSCLC, and metastatic urothelial cancer showed a significant drop in ICB response. Importantly, 8q24 amplification might act as a biomarker synergizing with TMB and PD-L1 level to distinguish patients who are likely to be resistant to ICB as well as those who could benefit from ICB. More notably, finding potential non-responders before ICB can facilitate more effective treatments in these patients by combining additional potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

To identify therapeutic vulnerabilities for 8q24-amplified patients, we developed “vulnerability index” and identified a list of candidates including OASL,43 LGALS4,44 AGT,45 APOA2,46 FGFR4,47 TRIM6,48 ARG1,49 and APOC1.50 We further performed a genome-wide CRISPR differential-dependency screen and identified 105 druggable 8q24 amplification-specific vulnerabilities, whose immune-related relevance was supported by convergent evidence from multiple independent pan-cancer immune co-culture CRISPR screening datasets, including ADAM10, MMP1/MMP2, PGF, CD44, SQLE, and ERBB2. From a translational perspective, the nominated vulnerabilities span clinically established and emerging targets. For ERBB2/HER2, HER2-directed therapy has already been evaluated in combination with PD-1 blockade; for example, pembrolizumab plus trastuzumab and chemotherapy improved clinical outcomes in HER2-positive gastric/GEJ adenocarcinoma in KEYNOTE-811.61,62 In contrast, ARG1 represents an emerging immunometabolic target: small-molecule arginase inhibitors (e.g., INCB001158) have entered early-phase clinical testing, including in combination with pembrolizumab, with pharmacodynamic activity reported but limited efficacy to date.63 For FGFR4, FGFR4-selective inhibitors have also been explored together with ICB in early-phase trials; for example, roblitinib (FGF401) has been tested in combination with the anti-PD-1 antibody spartalizumab,64 and fisogatinib (BLU-554) has been evaluated with the anti-PD-L1 antibody sugemalimab.65 Importantly, these results support the premise that applying our framework to guide therapy in molecularly stratified patients, such as those defined by 8q24 amplification, may enrich for subgroups with differential drug sensitivity and thereby improve clinical benefit relative to unselected populations.

Lastly, it is noteworthy that our study was intended to characterize the molecular consequences and clinical implications of 8q24 amplification, not to identify the optimal pan-cancer SCNA associated with ICB resistance. In conclusion, our findings indicate that 8q24 amplification is a promising pan-cancer indicator of an immune-depleted phenotype and is associated with primary resistance to ICB.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, our analyses are retrospective and integrate heterogeneous public cohorts; therefore, residual confounding due to between-cohort differences in clinical context, patient composition, and profiling/processing pipelines may remain. Although we harmonized data processing and aligned samples across datasets, some between-cohort differences may persist. Second, 8q24 status was not uniformly ascertained across cohorts. When copy number variation (CNV) data were unavailable, we used an expression-based surrogate (MYC/PVT1 “hi_hi”). This strategy is supported by TCGA data showing (1) stepwise increases of MYC and PVT1 expression across CNV states (Figure 2C) and (2) consistent positive correlations between continuous CNV and mRNA expression across cancer types, with good discriminatory performance for stringent amplification (Figure S7). Nevertheless, because MYC/PVT1 expression can also be influenced by copy-number-independent mechanisms (e.g., transcriptional activation or post-transcriptional regulation), the surrogate may misclassify 8q24 status and could bias effect estimates toward or away from the null. Finally, translation to clinical use will require additional validation. Although we applied fixed cutoffs defined in the IMvigor210 training cohort unchanged to external cohorts, prospective studies and context-specific calibration may be needed prior to implementation. Moreover, because immune relevance was supported in part by pan-cancer CRISPR immune-screen datasets that were not stratified by 8q24 status, validation in 8q24-stratified, immunocompetent models will be important to establish causality and therapeutic utility.

Experimental model and study participant details

Public datasets, case selection criteria and data processing

TCGA studies

The genomic and transcriptomic data of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) studies were available from the NCI Cancer Genomic Data Commons (NCI-GDC: https://gdc.cancer.gov). The clinical and immunogenomic data of TCGA cases were obtained from TCGA pan-cancer studies.66,75 The cases with clinical data unavailable were eliminated. The status of MYC/PVT1 was determined by the gene-level copy number data derived by GISTIC2.0 algorithm.76 The status of 8q24 was determined by the status of MYC/PVT1. In brief, the cancers with diploidy and wildtype 8q24 were identified as “8q24-WT”, cancers with gain of MYC/PVT1 were identified as “8q24-gain”, and cancers had high-level amplification of either MYC or PVT1 were identified as “8q24-amplification”. The TIL distribution patterns were obtained from Saltz et al.29

Public data of anti-PD-1/L1 cohorts

The MSKCC advanced NSCLC cohort

The genomic, PD-L1 expression, and clinical data of n=240 non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients were obtained from the study by Rizvi et al.18 Among these patients, we selected for those who received anti-PD-1/L1 monotherapy and had available clinical response data, resulting in a final cohort of n=193 patients for subsequent analysis. Somatic mutations and copy number alterations were identified using the targeted MSK-IMPACT sequencing panel, with the 8q24 status determined by the gene-level copy number provided in the original study on cBioPortal.39 For tumor mutation burden (TMB) analysis, tumors with TMB greater than the cohort-specific median were categorized into the “TMB-high” group, and those with TMB less than the median were categorized into the “TMB-low” group. Efficacy was assessed by RECIST 1.1, and durable clinical benefit (DCB) was defined by the original study as partial response or stable disease that lasted over six months.

The metastatic melanoma cohort from Liu et al.

The clinical and bulk RNA-seq data for n = 144 patients with metastatic melanoma were downloaded from the study by Liu et al.40 After further excluding patients with mixed response or tumors with ultra-high mutation burden, ipilimumab-naïve tumor specimens collected prior to pembrolizumab or nivolumab monotherapy (n = 109) were selected for further analysis. The 8q24 status was inferred based on the transcriptional expression of both MYC and PVT1: tumors with expression levels of both genes below the cohort median were classified as “lo_lo”, and tumors with expression of both genes above the median were classified as “hi_hi”. The best overall response rate (RECIST 1.1) was then compared between these two groups.

The unresectable or advanced melanoma cohort from Riaz et al.

The clinical, response, and RNA-seq data of an unresectable or advanced melanoma cohort were downloaded from Riaz et al.42 (GSE91061). This dataset initially contained 109 samples from 65 patients. From these, the 51 pre-nivolumab biopsies (from 51 unique patients) were selected. We then excluded patients who had progressed on prior ipilimumab therapy and those lacking definitive response data, resulting in a final cohort (n=24) for downstream analyses. The 8q24 status was inferred based on the transcriptional expression levels of both MYC and PVT1 as described above. Tumor response for patients was defined by RECIST v1.1 criteria by the original study.

The metastatic melanoma cohort from Hugo et al.

A metastatic melanoma cohort treated with anti-PD-1 monotherapy (n=26) was curated from the study by Hugo et al.41 From the larger initial dataset, we first selected only those patients for whom pre-treatment tumor biopsies and corresponding bulk RNA-seq data were available. After further excluding cases with ambiguous or un-evaluable clinical response data, a final cohort of 26 patients was established. The 8q24 status was inferred from the transcriptional expression levels of both MYC and PVT1, dichotomizing tumors into “lo_lo” (both genes below median expression) and “hi_hi” (both genes above median expression) groups. Clinical response was defined by RECIST v1.1 criteria, and response rates were compared between the two groups.

The IMvigor210 metastatic urothelial cancer cohort

The clinical, bulk RNA-seq, and immune profiling data for the IMvigor210 trial cohort (n = 298) were downloaded from Mariathasan et al.16 via the provided link (http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies). The 8q24 status was inferred based on the transcriptional expression levels of both MYC and PVT1. The tumors with high (above the cohort median) expression of both MYC and PVT1 were identified as the “hi_hi” group, and those with low (below the cohort median) expression of both genes were identified as the “lo_lo” group for subsequent analysis. Available data on PD-L1 protein expression, immune phenotypes, and TMB were integrated into the analysis.

Method details

Definition of 8q24 status and molecular subtypes

The molecular status of the 8q24 locus was determined using either copy number variation (CNV) or transcriptional data, depending on the availability of genomic profiling. In cohorts with CNV data (TCGA, MSKCC, and DepMap), the status was determined by the gene-level copy number of MYC and PVT1 derived from the GISTIC 2.0 algorithm. Cancers were classified as “8q24-WT” (diploid), “8q24-gain” (low-level gain), or “8q24-amplification” (high-level amplification of either MYC or PVT1).

In cohorts lacking genomic copy number data (melanoma and IMvigor210 cohorts), 8q24 status was inferred based on the transcriptional expression of both MYC and PVT1. Tumors with expression levels of both genes below the cohort median were classified as “lo_lo” (8q24-low), and those with expression of both genes above the median were classified as “hi_hi” (8q24-high).

Limitation of mRNA-based 8q24 status inference

In cohorts lacking genomic copy number data (melanoma and IMvigor210 cohorts), 8q24 status was inferred based on combined mRNA expression of MYC and PVT1. This approach is supported by the strong correlation between copy number status and mRNA expression observed in TCGA cohorts, where tumors with copy number amplification exhibit significantly higher expression levels. However, we acknowledge that transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation independent of copy number alterations may contribute to expression variability and could affect classification accuracy in some samples. Despite this limitation, the consistent associations between high MYC/PVT1 expression (hi_hi) and reduced ICB response across multiple independent cohorts support the validity of this surrogate approach.

Analysis of bulk RNA-seq data

Immune cell estimation and immune feature analysis

To quantify the abundance and composition of the tumor immune infiltrate, we utilized a suite of four immune cell estimation algorithms. First, the R package MCP-counter25 was used to infer the absolute abundance scores for eight major immune cell types (T cells, CD8 T cells, B lineage, etc.), as well as for endothelial cells and fibroblasts. In parallel, the CIBERSORT algorithm24 was applied to estimate the relative proportions of 22 distinct immune cell subpopulations. To ensure the robustness of our findings, these estimations were further complemented by applying the quanTIseq26 and EPIC27 algorithms. For the TCGA cohorts, pre-computed T-cell receptor (TCR) and B-cell receptor (BCR) diversity metrics, specifically richness and Shannon entropy, were downloaded from a recent TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas study.66 The resulting abundance scores and fractions for each immune cell type were then compared between the 8q24-amplification and 8q24-WT groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. We applied the Benjamini-Hochberg method to correct for multiple comparisons, and the false discovery rate (FDR q-values) was calculated for each test.

Pathway enrichment analysis

For pathway analysis, curated gene sets representing canonical biological and immune signaling pathways were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB: http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp), including the Hallmark, KEGG, PID, BIOCARTA and Reactome collections. Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to calculate an enrichment score for each pathway in each sample, implemented via the GSVA R software package. The resulting pathway enrichment scores were then compared between the 8q24-amplification and 8q24-WT groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with FDR correction for multiple hypotheses.

Cell line data and therapeutic vulnerability screening

Firstly, we utilized gene-level copy number and CRISPR-Cas9 gene effect scores in the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) portal (depmap.org/portal/).77 Cell lines were stratified as “8q24-amplification” (absolute copy number of MYC/PVT1 ≥ 4) and “8q24-diploid” (absolute copy number of MYC/PVT1 = 2). A genome-wide CRISPR differential-dependency screen was performed to identify sensitivity to 8q24 amplification (8q24 amplification-specific vulnerability). We then collected data from 7 published CRISPR/Cas9 screening studies that assessed the individual effect of each gene knockout on tumor immunity, including Freeman 2019, Kearney 2018, Manguso 2017, Pan 2018, Patel 2017, Vredevoogd 2019, and Lawson 2020 (Tables S12 and S13). These seven CRISPR cohorts are pan-cancer immune co-culture screening datasets and are not 8q24-stratified; we used them as orthogonal evidence to prioritize immune-relevant candidates rather than as direct validation in 8q24-amplified models We utilized these data to identify genes that are more likely to modulate lymphocyte-mediated cancer killing and influence immunotherapy response across different datasets. Genes were totally ranked by the mean Z-scores across 17 datasets. The lower z scores indicate better immune response after gene knockout. Top-ranked genes with lower z scores were characterized as immune resistant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Survival analysis

Survival analyses were conducted to evaluate the prognostic significance of 8q24 status. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to plot survival curves for visual comparison between stratified patient groups (e.g., 8q24-amplification vs. 8q24-WT; hi_hi vs. lo_lo), and the statistical significance of any observed differences was calculated using the log-rank test. The survival data for TCGA patients were downloaded from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas study,66 while for other public datasets, survival data were curated from their corresponding publications. Patients for whom survival data were not available were excluded from all survival analyses. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models were utilized to calculate Hazard Ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) while adjusting for potential confounders.

To provide an interpretable effect-size summary, we defined a “Vulnerability Index” (VI) for each gene as VI = exp(β_interaction), where β_interaction is the coefficient of the interaction term. This VI quantifies the multiplicative modification of the gene-expression hazard ratio in the hi_hi group relative to the lo_lo group. VI > 1 indicates that the adverse prognostic effect of high gene expression is amplified in the 8q24-amplified (hi_hi) context, whereas VI < 1 suggests an attenuated or reversed effect.

Genes with significant interaction terms (P_interaction < 0.05) and high VI values were prioritized as potential 8q24-context-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities. The candidates identified through this framework were further supported by stratified continuous Cox proportional hazards analyses within the MYC/PVT1 hi_hi and lo_lo groups.
