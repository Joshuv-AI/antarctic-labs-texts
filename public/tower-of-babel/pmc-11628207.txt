Short‐ and Long‐Term Outcomes of Prenatally Identified Congenital Aqueductal Stenosis by Fetal MRI
Authors: Noah J. Smith, Usha D. Nagaraj, Beth M. Kline‐Fath, Karin S. Bierbrauer, Mounira Habli, Charu Venkatesan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveProviding accurate prenatal prognostication for expectant parents is challenging due to limited literature on factors impacting outcomes in children with congenital aqueductal stenosis (CAS). This study stratified CAS patients into isolated or complex categories (presence of additional intra‐ or extra‐cranial anomalies or genetic syndromes) and evaluated both short‐ and long‐term outcomes. Additionally, the role of ventricular rupture was assessed.MethodsThis was a single center retrospective‐cohort study of CAS patients who underwent fetal MRI over a 10‐year period.ResultsOf 140 patients with CAS, 107 (76%) were complex and 33 (24%) were isolated. There were no differences in the size of ventricular enlargement or incidence of ventricular rupture between the two groups. 14 pregnancies were terminated, 9 experienced fetal demise/stillbirth, and there were 21 post‐natal deaths. Outcomes at the time of hospital discharge were available for 86 patients and long‐term follow‐up data for 64. CSF diversion (via ventriculoperitoneal shunt) was performed in 95% of complex and 71% of isolated CAS patients. Acutely, no differences were noted in seizures (complex: 10%; isolated: 18%) or respiratory support but there was an increased risk for feeding support. Risks for non‐ambulatory status (complex: 32% vs. isolated: 0%), epilepsy (complex: 56% vs. isolated: 19%) and long‐term gastrostomy tube assisted feeding (complex: 25.5% vs. isolated: 0%) were significantly greater with complex CAS. The presence of rupture did not impact clinical outcome.ConclusionPoor clinical outcome was associated with complex CAS. Ventricular rupture alone did not portend a poor outcome. Prenatal counseling can tailor prognostication by CAS type.

Introduction

Congenital aqueductal stenosis (CAS) is a cause of severe prenatal ventriculomegaly and has a reported incidence of 0.5–1 per 1000 births [1, 2]. Putative underlying etiologies include identified and presumed genetic disorders and mechanical obstruction related to posthemorrhagic insult [1, 3]. Although CAS can be accurately diagnosed via ultrasound and/or fetal MRI [4, 5], there are challenges in providing accurate prognostication and counseling to expectant parents due to limited literature on factors impacting developmental outcome in affected children.

Studies have reported varied outcomes with CAS. Reported rates for termination of pregnancy and fetal deaths range from 5% to 48% [2, 6, 7]. Postnatal mortality rates range from 34% to 68% [2, 6]. Variable rates of typical development have been reported ranging from 4% to 63% [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. A complication of severe ventriculomegaly is ventricular rupture where CSF accumulation and the resultant pressure leads to a dehiscence or diverticular outpouching of the cortical mantle [13]. The risk of ventricular rupture increases with increasing ventricular size and has been noted in 60% of patients with prenatal diagnosis of CAS and severe ventriculomegaly [14]. However, little is known about the neurodevelopmental outcome in children with prenatal ventricular rupture.

The objective of this single center retrospective study was to evaluate short‐ and long‐term outcomes in children with CAS diagnosed by fetal MRI and determine which factors, including ventricular rupture, impact outcome. Short‐term outcome measures were those at the time of discharge including feeding and respiratory support, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion and seizures. Long‐term outcomes included ambulation status, long‐term feeding and respiratory support, epilepsy, and degree of speech impairment.

Methods

This was an IRB‐approved (IRB#:2023‐0070) retrospective cohort study of 140 CAS patients who underwent fetal MRI at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC) between 2003 and 2023. The requirement for informed consent was waived. Patients were identified by Illuminate Insight software (version 3.5, Softeck Solutions) search. Patients were referred to the Fetal Care Center at CCHMC after abnormal ultrasound findings. They underwent both fetal MRI and ultrasound studies at CCHMC. We chose to analyze fetal MRI scans for this study as they have greater sensitivity for detecting subtle structural anomalies, particularly in the posterior fossa [15]. CAS was characterized by imaging the hallmark of ventriculomegaly of the lateral and third ventricles with a normal size fourth ventricle. Ventricular rupture was characterized by dehiscence or diverticular outpouching of the cortical mantle. MRI scans were retrospectively reviewed by the study radiologists, and patients not diagnosed as having CAS were excluded. Quantitative image analysis was performed on fetal MRI scans: ventricular measurements (transverse atrial diameter) were obtained in fetuses without ventricular rupture and measurements/location of rupture were obtained in fetuses with ventricular rupture. Quantitative analysis was not performed if prenatal imaging was of inadequate quality to preclude accurate analysis. Imaging features in a smaller subset of patients (n = 30) was analyzed previously [14]. All fetuses in our study were scanned using an Ingenia 1.5T (Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) or a 1.5T Signa HDxt (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin) system. A routinely implemented fetal brain MRI protocol was used where the majority of scan time was focused on acquiring T2‐single shot fast spin echo and BTFE/FIESTA images of the brain in three orthogonal planes, repeated as many times as needed at the radiologists' direction. Postnatal imaging was done using CT and MRI. MRI included sagittal and axial T1, axial fast spin echoproton density and T2, gradient echo blood sequence, and diffusion‐weighted sequences using 1.5 or 3 T magnets. Ventriculomegaly was assessed in fetuses by measuring the transverse atrial diameter [16]. Patient charts were reviewed for clinical and demographic information. Patients were classified as having isolated CAS if no other intracranial or body anomalies or genetic abnormalities were identified on prenatal or postnatal testing and clinical evaluation. Genetic testing included noninvasive prenatal testing (cell free DNA), chromosomes, microarray and rarely gene panels or whole exome/genome sequencing.

Electronic medical records including clinic notes were reviewed to gather demographic and clinical outcome data (motor, speech, feeding and respiratory status, seizures, CSF diversion). For long‐term outcomes, the last clinic visit that detailed their clinical status was used. Decisions regarding CSF diversion were made by neurosurgeons caring for the patient. There was no uniform set of institutional guidelines directing this aspect of care.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive quantitative analyses were performed for demographic information. The relationship between ventriculomegaly/ventricular rupture severity, as determined by fetal MRI, was analyzed for each response of interest. Outcomes that yielded non‐parametric data (e.g., ambulatory status) were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlations, while dichotomous responses were interpreted using Mann–Whitney U tests, where averages were compared across groups. Dichotomous outcomes were seizures in the NICU (presence vs. absence), epilepsy diagnosis (presence vs. absence), surgical intervention (CSF diversion vs. no intervention), respiratory support (unassisted breathing vs. support), feeding support (solely oral feeds vs. any degree of aid), and speech (age‐appropriate vs. delay/deficit). Mann–Whitney U Tests were also used to compare the differences in average outcomes for the same non‐parametric, numerical outcomes between independent variable groups (complex CAS vs. isolated CAS; ventricular rupture vs. no ventricular rupture). The relationships between the independent variable groups and dichotomous outcomes were analyzed using odds ratios. Depending on the sample sizes, p‐values were calculated using chi‐square (expected cell frequency > 5) and Fisher's exact tests (expected cell frequency < 5). Apart from demise, the other outcomes were limited to those who survived. Results were considered statistically significant if the p‐value was less than 0.05. Multinomial logistic regression was also performed to analyze the degree to which seizures in the NICU, complex CAS status, and ventricular rupture status influenced future epilepsy diagnoses. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Maternal and Child Demographics

140 patients (complex: 107 [76%] and isolated: 33 [24%]) were identified with a prenatal diagnosis of CAS (Figure 1). 51 (36.4%) had ventricular rupture. No significant differences were found in maternal age at time of birth or in comorbidities (Table 1). 14 pregnancies (10%) were terminated, 9 patients (6.4%) experienced fetal demise/stillbirth, and there were 21 post‐natal deaths (15%) (Figure 1). One patient had isolated CAS and died at 16 days of life; details were not available about this patient's clinical course. 20 patients with complex CAS died postnatally; 14 died during the NICU stay and 6 died later (range: 0 days–12 years of age). Information regarding the cause of death in the neonatal period was available for 5/14 patients; 3 died from complications related to cardiac disease, 1 developed apnea related to severe brain anomalies and 1 died from procedure complications. Cause of death was available for 3/6 patients who died later: these included sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), cardiopulmonary complications, and sepsis.

Prenatal MRI Features

Fetal MRI was obtained at an average gestational age of 25.6 ± 0.38 weeks. Quantitative analysis of fetal ventriculomegaly and ventricular rupture, including location and size of rupture, was performed only for those who had fetal imaging of sufficient quality to allow accurate measurements (Table 2; Figures 2 and 3). Ventricular rupture typically involved more than one brain region in both complex and isolated cases (Figure 3). There were no statistically significant differences in measurements of ventriculomegaly or rupture size between the complex and isolated groups (Table 2). Correlation between radiological data and outcome showed that those who received CSF diversion also had, on average, much more severe ventriculomegaly. Patients undergoing CSF diversion had an average right and left transverse atrial diameter of 25.0 ± 1.35 mm and 24.8 ± 1.32 mm, respectively. Those not receiving surgical intervention had an average right and left transverse atrial diameter of 18.8 ± 1.70 mm and 19.0 ± 1.41 mm, respectively. Additionally, right‐sided rupture diameter was found to be negatively correlated with motor outcome (ρ = −0.77, 95% CI [−0.96, −0.12], p = 0.027), suggesting less severe impairment for those with larger rupture sizes. This was not replicated with the left‐sided rupture diameters (ρ = 0.36, 95% CI [−0.78, 0.95], p = 0.553). Analysis of post‐natal imaging showed that ventricular rupture persisted postnatally in all cases. In patients without ventricular rupture noted prenatally, postnatal imaging noted rupture in 9 of 32 patients (28%) with syndromic CAS and 1 of 14 (7%) with isolated CAS.

Identification of Other Anomalies

107 patients were classified as having complex CAS based on the presence of additional intracranial or body anomalies or genetic abnormalities identified on prenatal or postnatal testing and post‐natal clinical and laboratory evaluation. Pre‐and post‐natal genetic testing practices varied and depended on testing availability at the time of consultation and patient preference. Prenatally, 60 patients underwent amniocentesis with testing including fluorescence in situ hybridization, chromosomes, and/or microarray; 39 patients underwent noninvasive prenatal testing, 4 had panels that included L1CAM testing, and 1 patient had whole genome sequencing. Postnatally, 2 patients had chromosome testing, 20 patients had microarray, 4 had whole exome sequencing, and 15 had gene panel testing. While conditions known to be associated with congenital aqueductal stenosis such as Gomez‐Lopez‐Hernandez Syndrome (n = 2), L1CAM—X‐Linked Hydrocephalus (n = 4), Goldenhar syndrome (n = 1) and Walker‐Warburg Syndrome (n = 1) were found, patients with conditions not clearly associated with CAS such as Trisomy 21 (n = 4), Turner syndrome (n = 1), 22q11 syndrome (n = 1) and SCN3A‐related neurodevelopmental disorder (n = 1) were also identified. Other brain abnormalities present included craniosynostosis (n = 3), Dandy‐Walker malformation (n = 2), Chiari II malformation (n = 6), absent septum pellucidum (n = 8), lobar holoprosencephaly (n = 3), rhombencephalosynapsis (n = 24), encephalocele (n = 3), cystic encephalomalacia (n = 2), cortical migrational anomalies and basal ganglia and cerebellar anomalies followed by cardiovascular, ophthalmologic and head and neck anomalies (Table 3).

Outcomes: Discharge From Hospital

Short‐term outcomes at the time of discharge from the hospital were assessed in 86 patients; 69 had complex CAS and 17 had isolated CAS (Table 4). 38 patients had ventricular rupture. CSF diversion (via ventriculoperitoneal shunt) was performed in 95% of complex and 71% of isolated CAS patients. There was an increased risk (OR = 8.19, 95% CI [1.19, 39.00], p = 0.008) of requiring diversion for complex CAS patients compared with isolated CAS patients. Data regarding CSF diversion were lacking in 7 patients. Two patients (ages 1 and 5 years at time of study) with ventricular rupture did not undergo CSF diversion, demonstrating stable imaging and no developmental impairment (Figure 4). There were six patients without rupture (2 complex and 4 isolated CAS; age range: 19 months–9 years) who did not receive CSF diversion. Those with ventricular rupture received surgical intervention significantly earlier (DOL 4.85 ± 0.86) than those without rupture (DOL 61.73 ± 20.02), (p = 0.008). There was no difference between the complex and isolated groups in terms of timing of surgery (complex: DOL 29.80 ± 10.14 vs. isolated: DOL 58.17 ± 27.79 (p = 0.348)). Seizures were noted in the acute period but no differences in incidence were noted among groups (OR = 0.86, 95% CI [0.21, 3.57], p = 0.840). There was an increased risk of feeding support with complex CAS. No differences were noted between rupture versus non‐rupture groups.

Long Term Outcomes

Long‐term outcomes were available for 64 patients (Table 5). Three patients (2 with complex CAS and 2 with ventricular rupture) were excluded from analysis of motor and language function because of their young age (9–13 months of age). The average age for the 61 patients analyzed for motor and speech function was 7.59 ± 0.69 years. The evaluation of motor function revealed a significant difference between patients with complex versus isolated CAS. Among the patients with isolated CAS, 93% were ambulatory, compared to only 44% of patients with complex CAS. Furthermore, none of the patients with isolated CAS were non‐ambulatory, whereas 32% of those with complex CAS were non‐ambulatory. The risk of speech impairment was not significantly increased for those with complex CAS (OR = 1.55, 95% CI [0.46, 5.28], p = 0.483). There was a 5‐fold increased risk for gastrostomy assisted feeding for complex versus isolated CAS patients (OR = 5.56, 95% CI [1.14, 27.10], p = 0.034). There were only 4 patients requiring tracheostomy support and all were in the complex group. There was a higher risk of epilepsy in the complex (56%) compared to the isolated (19%) CAS cohort (OR = 4.80, 95% CI [1.19, 19.42], p = 0.020). Of the six patients who experienced seizures during the initial hospitalization and had data on an epilepsy diagnosis, 5 (83.3%) were eventually diagnosed with epilepsy. The presence of ventricular rupture did not increase the risk of motor or speech deficits, need of feeding assistance, or epilepsy.

Discussion

Congenital aqueductal stenosis can occur in isolation or in the presence of additional intracranial, extracranial, or genetic anomalies. Severe ventriculomegaly can be associated with ventricular rupture, characterized by dehiscence or diverticular outpouchings of the cortical mantle [13]. While it may seem intuitive that the presence of additional anomalies would portend poorer outcome, there are no studies to date that have compared outcome in patients with isolated versus complex CAS. Furthermore, the contribution of ventricular rupture in outcome has not been studied. Our study finds that long‐term motor function is significantly impaired in patients with complex compared with isolated CAS. Ambulation was normal in 93% of patients with isolated CAS versus 44% of patients with complex CAS. Although there was a relatively small risk of seizures in the neonatal period in both types of CAS, the risk of developing long‐term epilepsy was significantly higher in complex (56%) than in isolated (19%) CAS patients. There was an increased risk of needing feeding support with complex CAS. There was an increased risk of needing respiratory support at the time of discharge with ventricular rupture, but only 4 patients (all complex CAS) required long term tracheostomy support. The presence of ventricular rupture did not impact any of the other outcomes assessed.

Quantitative analysis of ventriculomegaly and location and size of ventricular rupture showed no significant differences between isolated and complex groups. Not surprisingly, patients who did not undergo CSF diversion had smaller measurements of transverse atrial diameter, albeit still falling into the category of severe ventriculomegaly. An interesting finding is that right‐sided ventricular rupture was correlated with better motor outcome leading to speculation whether rupture may perhaps exert a protective effect on the cortical mantle. Etiology of this possible protective effect is unclear. It is interesting to note that ventricular rupture did not negatively impact outcome. Given that these patients underwent CSF diversion beforehand (average DOL 4), earlier surgical intervention may have a beneficial effect.

Our rates for fetal and post‐natal demise (6.4% and 15%, respectively) are lower than those reported in other studies where mortality rates range from 29% to 68% [2, 6]. Termination rates vary by region of practice and are impacted by many factors including timing of imaging and diagnosis, personal belief systems and local laws and regulations. Given that our study exclusively examined patients undergoing fetal MRI, which was obtained at an average gestational age of 25.6 weeks, we may not be fully capturing the entire population with this diagnosis who may have elected termination based on ultrasound results (and prior to referral to Fetal Care Center). Of note, our study examined hydrocephalus secondary to CAS, whereas most studies in the literature examined the outcome of congenital hydrocephalus of all etiologies (e.g., [2, 6, 7, 8]).

Most patients required CSF diversion and those with ventricular rupture received surgical intervention earlier (average: 4 days of life) than those without rupture (average: 61 days). There was an increased risk of complex patients requiring CSF diversion compared with isolated CAS patients, but there were no differences in the timing of surgery. This contrasts with an earlier study that found that patients with CAS accompanied by rhombencephalosynapsis (RES) underwent CSF diversion much earlier at an average of 6 versus 55 days for those with CAS without RES [17]. This may reflect an earlier intervention with RES specifically. Alternatively, decisions about the timing of surgeries are at the discretion of practicing neurosurgeons. With increasing interest in surgical interventions such as endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) with or without choroid plexus cauterization that typically are not performed until infancy with a mean age of 3 months [18, 19, 20, 21], consulting neurosurgeons may elect to monitor conservatively and choose to place a ventriculoperiotoneal shunt if they note signs of clinical instability. Interestingly, a minority of patients did not require a CSF diversion procedure. With these patients, it is possible that there is enough CSF flow through existing or alternative channels to prevent the development of hydrocephalus.

Our study found that seizures in the neonatal/infancy period occurred in 10% of complex and 17% of isolated CAS patients. One study that included only 9 children with CAS found that none developed seizures during the early period [22]. Consistent with other studies that have identified an almost 50% risk for epilepsy [11, 23], we found that long term risk for epilepsy was high, particularly in patients with complex CAS. The risk for epilepsy in isolated CAS patients was lower and consistent with a previous study that found that older age of onset, and absence of CNS malformations were correlated with seizure remission [23].

Our results showed increased risk for needing feeding support with complex CAS. This finding is consistent with a prior study showing that 53% of CAS patients with accompanying RES required feeding assistance versus 20% in isolated CAS [17]. Patients with complex CAS were at a much greater risk for being non‐ambulatory. Importantly, ventricular rupture did not impact motor outcome. A study evaluating outcome in isolated CAS noted worse outcomes (68% were developmentally delayed and 9.8% had cerebral palsy) [11] but the exact definitions used when assessing developmental delay and cerebral palsy are unclear. Speech was impaired in the majority of patients with CAS. However, this was more difficult to assess retrospectively in our study through clinic notes since patients had no formal testing.

Our study found a range of genetic anomalies and syndromes associated with CAS, which is consistent with previously reported literature [3, 24, 25]. Outcome can be informed by the underlying genetic diagnosis or syndrome. Very few patients in our study had testing with whole genome or exome sequencing. Genetic testing has changed over the years and in the timeframe covered by this retrospective study. Reporting of genetic anomalies associated with CAS will continue to expand as more extensive genetic analysis will be more readily available in the future. Ideally, genetic testing with whole exome/genome sequencing would be optimal for this population.

Our study has several limitations, including those inherent with the retrospective design and risk of selection bias. We recognize that patients grouped within the complex category are heterogeneous and have diverse underlying etiologies; outcomes will vary depending on the underlying etiology. Ideally, it would be optimal to group patients by diagnosis within the complex CAS phenotype and report on their outcomes. However, given the rarity of these conditions, comparisons between each subgroup would require a larger number of patients with these rare diagnoses that is beyond the scope of this paper. We feel that future efforts toward the creation of national and international registries would be beneficial for generating phenotype‐ and genotype‐specific longitudinal natural history studies. We were limited in our ability to obtain standardized developmental testing; clinical outcomes in our study were extrapolated from clinician notes. However, we obtained data about specific functional activities, including modes of feeding and ambulation status that are relevant and sought by families in fetal counseling meetings. Certain functions such as speech assessment were more difficult to evaluate from chart review due to variability in documentation and lack of standard evaluation strategies. Follow‐up attrition may lead to over‐representation of more severe phenotypes in surviving infants as those who have mild or no neurological deficits are also less likely to seek post‐natal care. However, given that the majority of patients had surgical CSF diversion, they likely continued seeking medical care. Our hospital is a large referral center and many patients travel from other states for fetal consultation; therefore, patients may have assumed post‐natal care in their home state. Long‐term outcome studies of congenital hydrocephalus following patients into adulthood are sparse, particularly as we consider evolving and improving medical management [10, 26]. One study which examined adult outcomes in patients who had been treated between 1971 and 1987 for congenital hydrocephalus before 12 months of life found that 44.6% were able to live an independent life while 20% required professional care institutions [10]. Future research should engage in larger multi‐center and global studies to generate prospective standardized outcome measures that are inclusive of resource‐limited and resource‐generous settings.

Our study will assist with prenatal counseling of CAS. Based on our results, we recommend comprehensive fetal imaging evaluation of brain structures but also the entire fetus to exclude additional anomalies. Fetal echocardiogram may also be useful given our finding that post‐natal demise in a subset of patients was due to cardiac etiology. Genetic evaluation, pre‐or post‐natally, can provide additional insights into whether the findings are isolated or complex. Prenatal counseling should emphasize that ventricular rupture alone does not portend a poor prognosis. There is a higher risk for epilepsy and poor motor outcome with complex CAS. The advancing and evolving landscape of genetic testing will hopefully also advance basic science research regarding the pathophysiology of CAS, leading to novel treatments.
