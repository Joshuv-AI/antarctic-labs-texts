Communities of Practice in Emergency Mental Health Care for Involuntary Patients in Common Law Countries: A Scoping Review of Evidence Gaps
Authors: Alan R Clough, Veronica Graham, Janet Catterall, Richard Stone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency departments (EDs) play a critical role in managing individuals experiencing mental health crises who are involuntarily transported under mental health legislation. Although collaborative models such as co-responder teams and diversion services have shown operational benefits, their implementation is inconsistent and rarely embedded within systems that support sustained practice development. Communities of Practice (CoPs), which emphasise ongoing social learning and shared practice, may offer a framework to address this gap.ObjectiveTo map the scope of published research on involuntary mental health presentations to EDs and to assess whether the evidence supports the existence, implementation, or potential value of CoP approaches in this context.Eligibility Criteria and Information SourcesPeer-reviewed studies published in English from 2008 onwards were included if they examined responses to involuntary mental health presentations in EDs within common law countries (Australia, Canada, Aotearoa New Zealand, the United Kingdom, and the United States). Searches were conducted in Medline, PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar, supplemented by targeted grey literature searches and an AI-assisted search tool.MethodsFollowing Joanna Briggs Institute scoping review methodology, studies were screened and selected by two independent reviewers. Data extraction and synthesis were conducted using a structured framework, with studies analysed for evidence of CoP-consistent elements such as collaborative learning, interagency communication, and shared practice development.ResultsSixty-five studies met inclusion criteria (40 quantitative retrospective, 11 quantitative prospective, 10 qualitative, and 4 mixed methods), predominantly from the United States (n=30) and Australia (n=23). Only one study explicitly evaluated a CoP, and no sustained CoP structures were identified. While many studies described collaborative practices consistent with CoP principles—such as interagency coordination, shared training, and improved communication—these were typically operational and episodic rather than embedded in ongoing systems of shared learning and practice development.ConclusionDespite the growth of research and the demonstrated benefits of collaborative service models, there is little evidence of formalised CoP approaches in emergency mental health care for involuntary patients. This absence suggests a gap in sustained collaborative learning mechanisms that may limit system level improvement. CoPs may offer a theoretically coherent framework to address this gap. Future research should examine the feasibility, implementation, and impact of CoPs, particularly through qualitative and mixed-methods approaches.

Introduction

Emergency departments (EDs) are critical points of care for individuals experiencing acute mental health crises, including those involuntarily transported from the community by police or ambulance services under mental health legislation,1,2 circumstances that involve some of the most vulnerable members of the community and require cohesive, collaborative responses from multidisciplinary teams.3,4 However, systemic fragmentation, role ambiguity, and inconsistent training across professional groups reportedly undermines the quality and safety of care provided in these high-stakes presentations,3–7 prompting the development of collaborative service models such as co-responder teams and liaison psychiatry services across common law jurisdictions including Canada, Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand (AoNZ), the United States (US) and the United Kingdom (UK).3,4 While these models have demonstrated promising outcomes such as reductions in involuntary detention, improved patient flow, and enhanced interagency coordination,3 evaluations consistently highlight systemic constraints that limit their sustainability and effectiveness, including variability in implementation, lack of standardised protocols, and inadequate integration of staff and service‑user perspectives.3,6,8

Absent from our preliminary scan of the literature is a systematic examination of “communities of practice” (CoPs) in these emergency contexts involving involuntary patients. CoPs are commonly understood as collaborative learning arrangements in which members of a multidisciplinary group engage in shared reflection, knowledge exchange, and the co-development of practice in response to real-world challenges,5,9 and are increasingly regarded as a promising, practice‑led approach to workforce development and service integration, particularly in emergency department settings characterised by high staff turnover, diverse professional cultures, and rapid, high‑stakes decision‑making.10–13 Given the acknowledged limitations of existing collaborative service models in embedding sustainable practice change, and the limited evidence examining structured collaborative learning mechanisms in emergency mental health responses, a scoping review was warranted. Preliminary searches of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and JBI Evidence Synthesis identified no current or ongoing systematic or scoping reviews addressing the application or evaluation of CoPs in emergency psychiatric care involving involuntary patients, nor any relevant registered protocols, leading to registration of the present review with the Open Science Framework.14

Accordingly, this review aimed first to map the scope of peer-reviewed and relevant grey literature describing responses arising in emergency departments where involuntary transport for mental health care is authorised, incorporating both staff and consumer perspectives. Second, it aimed to identify and examine evidence supporting the potential use of CoPs to address challenges within the emergency care service ecosystem involving police, ambulance, and mental health services.

Communities of practice (CoPs) are understood as policies and practices that bring together a community of people within a defined domain who meet or communicate regularly to promote shared learning, experience, and the development of skills and knowledge around common challenges.15 In the present context, the domain is state-authorised involuntary transport from the community to emergency departments for the management and treatment of mental health and behavioural disturbance. CoPs promote social learning and mutual understanding to address shared challenges that may not be fully captured by formal policy or guidelines, through processes that Lave and Wenger16 described as legitimate peripheral participation, whereby learning occurs through social embedding within a culture of practice.17,18 Such socially embedded learning can generate emergent effects that are difficult to prescribe in advance but may include enhanced interagency communication and improvements in the quality of interpersonal interactions that support more dignified, destigmatising patient experiences. CoPs are also characterised by relationships that promote accountability, cooperation, and collaborative problem‑solving, as articulated by White et al,19 and it was these characteristics and emergent properties that we sought to identify in the published literature as indicators of the actual use of CoPs in emergency mental health care contexts. We therefore sought published reports of these characteristics and properties as indications of actual use of CoPs.

This review focuses on research conducted in English-speaking common-law countries, where health law systems are grounded in broadly comparable legal and human rights principles. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities entered into force in 2008,20 providing an ethically and legally coherent timeframe for examining emergency mental health responses involving involuntary care in these jurisdictions.

Types of Sources

To capture the scope of formal studies on the topic of involuntary transport, both quantitative and qualitative original research and all methodological approaches and study designs were considered. The websites of national Colleges of Emergency Medicine and Emergency Nursing in each jurisdiction were examined, where these existed, to source relevant guidelines for practice in the grey literature.

Methods

The review was conducted following the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and in accordance with an a priori protocol registered with OSF.14,23

Search Strategy

The medical databases Medline, Pubmed, Embase and PsycINFO were searched for the period from 2008 to 2025, using combinations of keywords and database-specific subject headings supported by AI-supported pilot searches to optimise the conventional search.24 Full search strategies are provided in Appendix 1.

EndNote version 20 was used to record the date, search and number of records retrieved for each search in each database.

Google Scholar was also searched using simple keyword searches. Ranked by relevance, the first 100 records returned were examined and those that appeared to fit the inclusion criteria were evaluated.

The AI-assisted search agent tool Undermind was used to discover relevant peer reviewed papers that may have been missed in the conventional database searches.25–27 Prompts and reports can be found in Appendix 2.

Grey literature was also sought using a site-delimited Google search for the exact phrase “communities of practice” across the websites of a jurisdiction’s professional and policy authorities, namely the national College of Emergency Medicine and College of Emergency Nursing, where these existed.

Evidence Selection

All identified citations were imported into Zotero (version 7.0.15) reference management system and duplicates removed. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (VG and JC) against the inclusion criteria, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements at each stage were resolved through discussion and, where required, consultation with a third reviewer (AC).

In addition to database searches, an AI-assisted search tool (Undermind) was used to identify potentially relevant studies not captured through conventional methods. Studies identified through this process were assessed against the same inclusion criteria.

The final set of included studies comprised 65 peer-reviewed articles. Data Extraction

A data extraction tool adapted from the Joanna Briggs Institute template was used (Appendix 3).23 Authors VG, JC and AC first piloted the tool with a convenience sample of the seed studies retrieved in pilot searches to ensure consistency of interpretation. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (VG and JC).

Extracted data included study characteristics (eg. authorship, year, country), study design, population, intervention, and outcomes. Studies were classified by methodological type as quantitative retrospective, quantitative prospective, qualitative, or mixed methods.

In addition to descriptive summarisation, an interpretive analysis was undertaken to assess the extent to which the included studies demonstrated elements consistent with Communities of Practice (CoPs). For the purposes of this review, CoPs were understood as collaborative learning arrangements characterised by sustained interaction, shared knowledge exchange, and the co-development of practice within and across professional groups.

A structured analytical framework was applied to each study to identify the presence of CoP-consistent elements, including: Interagency collaboration and coordination;Shared learning or training activities;Communication and information exchange across roles or services; andPractices supporting reflection, workforce development, or service improvement

Studies were examined for explicit or implicit evidence of these elements and for any reported effects on processes or outcomes. Coding was conducted independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Given that most included studies did not explicitly adopt a CoP framework, identification of CoP-consistent elements required interpretive judgement. The analysis therefore focused not only on the presence of collaborative practices, but also on whether these reflected sustained, system-level approaches to shared learning, as distinct from isolated or operational forms of collaboration.

Findings are presented both descriptively by study type and thematically in relation to their relevance to CoP concepts, with emphasis on the extent to which existing evidence supports, approximates, or fails to demonstrate CoP-based approaches.

Research Outputs Over Time and by Jurisdiction

Figure 2 shows that the trend in the number of studies meeting inclusion criteria was for a steady increase over time, accelerating after 2014–2016. No publications were identified for 2008–9, whereas during the period from 2017 to 2022, inclusive, there was an average of seven publications per year, including eight in 2020, the largest number published in any calendar year. More studies may well have been published after June 2025 but not included in our review, making it likely that the overall trend for a steady increase in research output since 2008 has continued.

Figure 3 shows that almost half of the publications included in our review came from the US (n=30), a third from Australia (n=23) with the remainder from Canada (n=8), the UK (n=3) and AoNZ (n=1).

Quantitative Retrospective Studies (n=40)

The quantitative retrospective studies all interrogated service system records such as clinical charts and hospital, police or ambulance electronic databases (Table 1). Twenty-two studies contained comparative elements while the remaining 18 were purely descriptive in nature. These studies primarily characterise service use and clinical outcomes providing only indirect insight into collaborative practices relevant to CoPs.

Patient characteristics considered included: age; sex; diagnosis (particularly major psychiatric disorder, substance misuse disorder); intoxication; pain; and insurance status (a particular focus in the United States). Specific behaviours examined included suicidality and threats of harm to self or others.33,45,46,48,49,53,57 Individual studies considered homicidal intent,48 gun-related violence after involuntary admission,45 intentional foreign body ingestion,33 pain43, and effects of mandated alcohol treatment.41 Only three studies considered paediatric or adolescent minors,32,44,55 one specifically considered older adults42 and two considered ethnic minorities.38,54

Generally, the quantitative retrospective studies reported that most patients transported involuntarily are discharged home, with a small proportion of service users accounting for a disproportionate number of instances of involuntary transport. The US, Australian and UK studies found that around 5–10% of patients accounted for around one quarter of service use.31,35

The more commonly examined outcomes were length of stay31,32,36,38,44,46,47,53,59,61,63,66 use of seclusion67 or physical or chemical restraint,35,38,67 the proportion of involuntary patients discharged home without admission to hospital and frequency of involuntary admission to psychiatric ward or facility.31,35–37,44,50,56,59,61,66,67 Severity of disorder, night or weekend admission with lack of access to a mental health specialist, violence or the threat of violence occasioning sedation, and intoxication all increased wait-times and length of stay.36,45,46,50,52,53,64,66,67 Diversion directly to specialised psychiatric facilities was found to be feasible with favourable treatment outcomes where such services were available.11,50,56 Police co-responder models and field triage reduced involuntary detention, arrest rates29 and wait-times.58 Treatment by the appropriate mental health specialist and advanced care plans also reduced wait-times.41,44,62

A commonly recurring theme in the 40 studies was a focus on police transport.11,29–31,34,36,39,40,58,64–67 These studies generally reported that patients transported by police are more likely to be male,67 and to exhibit violent behaviour64 or suicidal ideation.35 Among six Australian studies altogether, two reported that police and ambulance transport episodes had increased over time in Queensland.34,65 Two studies compared patients transported by police with other involuntary patients, finding more frequent use of seclusion64 and greater length of stay36 in police-transported patients, both outcomes attributed to the nature of the behaviours (eg. suicidality, threats of violence) requiring police intervention. Police-issued records were found to be more detailed and compliant with regulations than for their ambulance or clinician counterparts.60

Quantitative Prospective Studies (n=11)

As for the quantitative retrospective studies, the prospective studies interrogated service system records such as clinical charts and hospital, police or ambulance electronic databases (Table 2). Seven studies evaluated pre-post intervention effects,68–71,74,75,77 and one the effects of physical restraint on follow-up compliance.78 The remainder compared different patient group characteristics76 or exposure to treatment or intervention.72,73 These studies more frequently evaluate interventions, particularly co-responder and diversion models, and therefore provide more direct evidence of structured interagency collaboration.

Five of the 11 prospective studies focused on street triage or diversion models.68–71,74 Childs and colleagues specifically examined effects on youth populations with compelling findings of 85% de-escalation with co-response compared to 17% for police-only responses, enabling young people to remain in the community with safety plans with reduced likelihood of further involuntary commitment within one year.68 Co-response models in AoNZ, the US and Australia supported rapid response, reduced involuntary transport and subsequent ED and mental health unit admissions and did not increase the use of force69 or the risk of re-presentation in the two weeks following initial contact.74 Morabito & Savage noted the co-responder approach extended reach by connecting individuals to services previously unknown to them, potentially, reducing the need for future calls.71 Hoffman and colleagues’ Canadian study also demonstrated that reduced police transport in the absence of co-response was achieved with the use of a screening tool supported by appropriate training.70

Five quantitative prospective studies considered a range of topics with a common theme of improving patient compliance and reducing involuntary transport. Topics covered included: the effects of regional specialist psychiatric services on boarding;75 aggression and substance use disorder;77 use of physical restraint;78 ambulance-initiated transport;76 and, telehealth in rural populations.72 Ambulance certified involuntary transport was found to be a poor predictor of psychiatric admission.76 Currier et al reported that experiencing physical restraint reduced the likelihood of patients attending their first outpatient psychiatric appointment78 Chaput et al observed that the decision to apply involuntary holds was most often determined by poor impulse control and suicidal ideation in the patient despite a patient’s untreated alcohol dependence.77 Zeller et al’s study of direct diversion to a psychiatric facility reduced wait times and admission to EDs, and improved patient experience.75 In a high-risk rural Canadian context, Fairchild et al reported a telemental health service was a cost-effective means to access immediate and intensive psychiatric care for suicide threat.72

Bhalla & Danovitch’s study, the only study to feature an evaluation of a CoP in our literature search and selection, found that after hospital-based clinicians were trained and certified to write a hold, two-thirds of patients committed on pilot holds were transferred to psychiatric hospitals within 24 hours, without distressing patients.73 Further evaluations of the appropriateness of holds and patient experience are pending at time of writing.

Qualitative Studies (n=10)

Of the ten qualitative studies included in our review six were carried out in Australia,81–83,85,86,88 two in Canada,79,87 one each in the US80 and the UK84 (Table 3). Eight of the ten qualitative studies employed interviews with patients and/or providers and there were two case studies79,80 (Table 3). One case study considered the potential benefits of brief, involuntary admission to stabilise substance use disorder79 and the other considered the potential consequences of pathologising Black children’s developmentally appropriate stress/distress that might compound differential treatment.80 These studies provide insight into relational, organisational, and experiential dimensions of care highlighting the importance of communication, trust, and shared understanding across roles.

Two of eight qualitative studies, focused primarily on staff perceptions of an aspect of involuntary care, one exploring clinician perceptions of barriers to using mental health legislation in EDs88 and the other exploring “Assistants in Nursing” (AIN) self-perception.85 Jelinek et al highlighted that ED environment, resources, communication, relationships and staff understanding or ability to apply legislation were often beyond role descriptions for staff.88 Gerace et al explored the AIN role in supporting patient trust, comfort and emotional state.85 Both studies spoke to the need for professional supports like refresher training, formal supervision and debriefing—practices legitimising participation and collaboration in shared practice beyond the strict clinical role definition.

Studies from the UK, Australia and Canada highlighted the importance of patient experience, citing positive professional-patient relationships, and the need for a therapeutic, structured treatment setting to support patients’ sense of autonomy and perception of being helped.84,86,87 Studies exploring challenges in police transport co-response models highlighted concerns for confidentiality, coordination,83 resourcing and training.86 Both patients and staff viewed mental health liaison nurses as supporting internal (eg. bed managers) and interagency (eg. referral services) communication and workflow in Jelinek et al’s Australian study.88

Mixed-Methods Studies (n=4)

Four articles from Canada (n=2) and the US (n=2) employed mixed methods, and featured qualitative interviews of patient and/or provider perspectives with street triage or co-responder models with police as the focus89–92 (Table 4).

The few mixed-methods studies were published recently, and—though more explicit about both measured efficacy and effects on patient experience—lacked robust designs and did not promote sophisticated practice responses to enhance system functionality. However, these studies offer partial integration of outcome and experiential data, providing limited but relevant insight into both effectiveness and collaborative processes without examining the development of sustained collaborative learning systems characteristic of CoPs.

Synthesis

Across all study types, collaborative practices, particularly interagency coordination, shared training, and co-response models, are not prominent and are typically inferred as operational or programmatic responses rather than as components of sustained systems of shared learning. While many studies report elements consistent with CoP principles, the deliberate establishment of ongoing, adaptive communities of practice is lacking. The current evidence base we depict here is characterised by repeated description of service challenges and interventions, with limited attention to how collaborative practice can be developed, embedded, and sustained over time.

Discussion

Research interest in the effectiveness and appropriateness of involuntary transport to EDs for patients requiring urgent mental health care has grown substantially since 2008. However, the evidence base remains heavily weighted towards quantitative analyses of routinely collected service data. These studies repeatedly characterise patterns of service use, patient characteristics, and system pressures, while providing limited insight into how collaborative practices are developed, sustained, or improved over time. Most of the 65 studies included in this review were published in the US (n=30) or Australia (n=23), with most (n=47) published from 2016 onwards. The vast majority have been quantitative studies (n=51, 40 retrospective and 11 prospective) interrogating service system records such as clinical charts and hospital, police or ambulance electronic databases. Fourteen were qualitative or mixed methods studies. Irrespective of study type the most common focus among the studies reviewed was the efficacy of police co-responder models. Other interventions, such as the role of an Assistant in Nursing, Mental Health Liaison Services or targeted training, have also been assessed.

Patient experience was a topic in several quantitative prospective studies, and in most of the qualitative studies albeit, understandably, not as a primary topic in the quantitative retrospective studies. Topics that received little or no attention among the studies reviewed included sexuality, gender identity, neurodiversity and indigeneity. Studies specifically considering paediatric involuntary emergency mental health transport and treatment were few—all published in the US by one research group.

Interventions that were effective in reducing overall transport to EDs and reducing wait times were co-responder models enabling street triage11,29,50,56,58,64,68,69,71,74 and direct transfer to psychiatric facilities.56,75 Co-response models generally reported no ill-effects or unintended outcomes on patients, and use of force, arrest rates and re-transport in the weeks following, where reported, were low.29,50,68 Though the benefits seem clear, such models pose policy and resourcing challenges due in large part to the complexity of interagency autonomy, communication, and cooperation.83,87

No studies examined in any detail the phenomenon of multiple episodes for the same individual. Studies from the US,50 UK51 and Australia35 reported that perhaps as few as 5% of patients account for around one fifth to one quarter of involuntary admissions to EDs.

The imbalance in the evidence base reflects the underlying field rather than the authors’ editorial emphasis. Quantitative studies—often based on secondary analyses of system-generated data—predominate; whereas, qualitative and mixed-methods research is comparatively scarce. This imbalance is not unexpected, given the practical and ethical challenges of conducting qualitative research in acute mental health crisis settings, particularly involving involuntary patients. However, this gap has important implications, as understanding patient experience, interprofessional relationships, and the dynamics of collaborative practice is central to the development of CoP-based approaches.

Evidence of Actual or Potential Use of CoP

A Community of Practice (CoP) is typically defined as a group of people who share a common interest or domain and engage in ongoing interaction to share knowledge, solve problems, and improve practice related to that domain. According to seminal work by Wenger et al,15 a CoP involves three key elements: a shared domain of interest, a community of members who interact and build relationships around that domain, and a shared practice including resources, tools, experiences, and standards developed over time through collaboration and mutual engagement. CoPs emphasise continuous learning, collective problem-solving, identity formation within the group, and the development of a shared repertoire of knowledge and approaches relevant to their focus area. They often include members from different backgrounds who learn through social participation and exchange, moving beyond mere individual expertise to collective development of knowledge and practice.

In the context of emergency and crisis response, many of the included studies examined elements consistent with CoP characteristics. These include elements which enhance and legitimise professional participation in shared processes, such as: collaboration among professionals from diverse services authorised by government to intervene (emergency services, police, ambulance, mental health teams); information and data sharing and knowledge exchange as a key enabler; shared training protocols and resources; interdisciplinary or interagency cooperation; and an emphasis on effective partnerships and communication.

However, while the papers reviewed implicitly depict features of collaborative and integrated practice, they typically do not explicitly frame their work or findings in terms of a CoP. They tend rather to describe operational or procedural cooperation, protocols, or partnership models rather than highlighting the ongoing social learning, identity formation, mutual engagement, and development of shared repertoires that are the hallmark of the CoP notion. In other words, although the described interdisciplinary efforts and partnerships contain components of CoP theory, the studies lack evidence of a formal or intentional establishment of a CoP with its continuous, evolving knowledge-sharing and collective learning culture. For this scoping review, aiming to assess the presence or impact of CoPs in emergency mental health crisis contexts, these papers indicate promising collaborative elements but do not provide direct empirical support or conceptual framing confirming an actual CoP structure or process at work. This gap is a key finding of this review.

Although a formal CoP around emergency services, involving police, ambulance, mental health, and hospital teams could offer a useful framework to enhance the collaboration, shared learning, and coordination required, the current literature only documents related but distinct phenomena such as interagency protocols, co-responder initiatives, and partnership-based approaches rather than explicitly identified or studied CoPs. While co-responder, and similar diversion models demonstrate measurable improvements in outcomes, they are typically implemented as discrete interventions rather than as components of sustained systems of shared learning. This distinction is important, as it may explain why successful models are not consistently embedded or scaled across settings. CoPs offer a framework for understanding how collaborative practice can be developed as an ongoing, adaptive process rather than a series of isolated initiatives. CoPs emphasise sustained social learning mechanisms and shared identity that can lead to deeper practice improvements beyond standard operational collaboration. None of the papers included in this review offer these elements.

Limitations

Although this scoping review employed a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases and sources, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, while both peer-reviewed and grey literature sources were searched to inform contextual understanding, only peer-reviewed studies meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the final synthesis. Grey literature searches focused on emergency medicine discipline peak bodies where these existed in the jurisdictions but yielded no materials meeting inclusion criteria. It is nonetheless possible that relevant policy or practice-based insights were not captured from other source materials, eg. health department policies and programs in the jurisdictions.

Second, the inclusion of studies identified through an AI-assisted search tool (Undermind) may have introduced selection bias. Although these studies were assessed against the same inclusion criteria as those identified through conventional database searches, the potential for systematic differences in retrieval cannot be entirely excluded.

Third, the review was limited to English-language publications from five common law jurisdictions. While this provided a coherent legal and policy context, it limits the generalisability of findings to other health systems and legal frameworks, particularly in non-English speaking countries and countries where legal systems are based on an inquisitorial tradition.

Fourth, consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal critical appraisal of included studies was undertaken. As a result, the review maps the extent and nature of the evidence rather than assessing the quality or strength of individual studies.

Fifth, the analysis of CoP-consistent elements required interpretive judgement, as most included studies did not explicitly adopt a CoP framework. Although a structured analytical approach was used, the identification of CoP-relevant practices is necessarily subjective and may be influenced by the conceptual lens applied.

Finally, the evidence base itself is characterised by a predominance of quantitative studies, many of which are based on secondary analyses of routinely collected data. This limits the depth of insight into patient experience, interprofessional relationships, and the social learning processes central to CoP development. The relative scarcity of qualitative and mixed-methods research in this field represents an important gap in the literature rather than a limitation of the review alone.

Conclusion

Research on involuntary mental health presentations to emergency departments has increased steadily since 2008 across common law jurisdictions, with a strong focus on service utilisation, patient characteristics, and the effectiveness of specific interventions. This evidence base is dominated by quantitative studies, many of which rely on secondary analyses of routinely collected data, and therefore provides limited insight into patient experience, interprofessional relationships, and the processes through which collaborative practice is developed and sustained.

While a range of collaborative models—including co-responder approaches, diversion pathways, and liaison services—have demonstrated improvements in operational outcomes, these are typically implemented as discrete interventions rather than as components of sustained systems of shared learning. Across the literature, elements consistent with CoPs, such as interagency communication, shared training, and coordination, are frequently described but not conceptualised or evaluated as part of an ongoing, adaptive community of practice.

The absence of explicitly defined or sustained CoP approaches represents a gap in the current evidence base. This gap is significant, as it suggests that existing models may lack the mechanisms required to support continuous learning, integration across services, and long-term improvement in practice.

Addressing this gap will require research that moves beyond descriptive, quantitative studies to examine how collaborative practice is developed, embedded, and maintained over time. In particular, there is a need to balance the preponderance of quantitative studies with qualitative and mixed-methods research that captures patient experience, clinician perspectives, and the social and organisational dynamics that underpin effective collaboration.

Communities of Practice offer a theoretically coherent framework for understanding and supporting these processes. However, their feasibility, implementation, and impact in the context of emergency mental health care for involuntary patients remain largely unexplored. Future research should therefore focus on the development and evaluation of CoP-based approaches, including their potential to improve patient experience, support workforce capability, and strengthen interagency coordination in this complex and enduring area of care.
