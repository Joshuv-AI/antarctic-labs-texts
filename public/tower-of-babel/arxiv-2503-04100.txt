Abstract
. We show how to improve the discrepancy of an iid sample by moving only a few points. Specifically, modifying O ⁡ ( m ) O(m) sample points on average reduces the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance to the population distribution to 1 / m 1/m .



1. Main results

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be an iid sample from a distribution on
\mathbb{R}
with cumulative distribution function
F(x)
. The empirical distribution function of the sample is defined as

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}

Define the sample discrepancy
D_{n}
as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between the empirical and population distributions:

D_{n}=\sup_{x}\lvert F_{n}(x)-F(x)\rvert.

Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality (DKW) implies that
D_{n}=O(1/\sqrt{n})
with high probability
[
2
,
3
]
. Our first result shows that the discrepancy can be significantly reduced by carefully changing a small fraction of the sample:

Theorem 1

(One-sample discrepancy)
.

Given iid observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from a distribution with cumulative distribution function
F(x)
and a number
m\in(0,n]
, one can replace at most
Cm
observations on average so that

(1.1)

D_{n}=\sup_{x}\lvert F_{n}(x)-F(x)\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{m},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Note that the replacements are done via a constructive procedure (Algorithm 1) that has an explicit time complexity of
O\bigl(n\ln(n)\ln(m)\bigr)
. See §
5
.

Next, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be two independent samples from the same distribution, each consisting of
n
independent observations. Define their empirical distribution functions as:

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}},\quad G_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{i}\leqslant x\}},

and consider the two-sample discrepancy

D_{n,n}=\sup_{x}\lvert F_{n}(x)-G_{n}(x)\rvert.

Our second result shows that the discrepancy can be significantly reduced by changing a small fraction of observations in one sample:

Theorem 2

(Two-sample discrepancy)
.

Given iid observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
from the same distribution and a number
m>0
, one can replace at most
Cm
observations on average in
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
so that

\sup_{x}\lvert F_{n}(x)-G_{n}(x)\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{m},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Our results above are sharp, as the following matching lower bound shows:

Proposition 1

(Lower bound)
.

Given iid observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and a number
m\geqslant\sqrt{n}
, achieving a discrepancy of
c/m
requires replacing at least
cm
observations on average. Here
c>0
is an absolute constant.

The constraint
m\geqslant\sqrt{n}
is explained by the fact that the discrepancy is always
O(1/\sqrt{n})
with high probabillity by the DKW inequality, even without replacing any points.

Remark 1

(Removing instead of replacing)
.

Our work was inspired by the recent result of D. Bilyk and S. Steinerberger
[
1
]
, which achieves a discrepancy of
O(\ln(n)/m)
by
removing
at most
m
points from the original sample. As shown in
[
1
]
, this result is optimal in terms of the number of removed points.

2. Proof of Theorem
1

Our argument will be based on DKW inequality
[
2
]
:

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}\left\{D_{n}>\dfrac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}\leqslant 2e^{-2t^{2}},\quad t\geqslant 0

where the optimal constant
2
in front of the exponent was obtained by P. Massart
[
3
]
.

To begin with, let us show that it suffices to consider the case where
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
follow the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. This step is standard:

Lemma 1
.

If Theorem
1
holds for the uniform distribution
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, then it holds for all distributions.

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable with cumulative distribution function
F(x)
, which may not be continuous or strictly increasing. Consider the generalized inverse of
F
:

F^{-1}(u)=\inf\left\{x\,:\,F(x)\geqslant u\right\}.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
u\in[0,1]
, we have

(2.2)

\{F^{-1}(u)\leqslant x\}\quad\text{iff}\quad\{u\leqslant F(x)\}.

If
X
is
F
-distrbuted, then
so is
F^{-1}(U)
for a uniform random variable
U
, as follows from (
2.2
):

\mathbb{P}\{F^{-1}(U)\leqslant x\}=\mathbb{P}\{U\leqslant F(x)\}=F(x).

Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
X_{i}=F^{-1}(U_{i})
, where
U_{i}
are independent uniform random variables. By (
2.2
), for each
x
,

\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}=\#\{U_{i}\leqslant F(x)\}.

Setting
u=F(x)
, we find:

\left\lvert\frac{\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}}{n}-F(x)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{\#\{U_{i}\leqslant u\}}{n}-u\right\rvert,

and the lemma follows. ∎

Throughout, let us assume that the sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
is drawn from the uniform distribution
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
. For convenience, let us also assume that
n
is odd.
If
n
is even, one may simply discard one observation and reintroduce it later; since a single point contributes negligibly to the discrepancy, this does not affect the overall result.

Our goal is to modify the sample by replacing, on average, only
O(m)
observations so that the new sample has discrepancy
O(1/m)
.

Introduce a uniform grid:

Y_{k}=\frac{k}{n},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n-1.

Let us use the following measure of discrepancy. For each (closed) interval
I\subset[0,1]
, define:

d_{Y}(I)=\sup_{J}\left\lvert\frac{\#\{X_{i}\in J\}}{n}-\frac{\#\{Y_{k}\in J\}}{n}\right\rvert,

where the supremum is taken over all intervals
J\subset I
sharing the left endpoint with
I
. Note that for each
J
,

\left\lvert\frac{\#\{X_{i}\in J\}}{n}-\lvert J\rvert\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert\frac{\#\{X_{i}\in J\}}{n}-\frac{\#\{Y_{k}\in J\}}{n}\right\rvert+\frac{1}{n},

where
\lvert J\rvert
denotes the length of
J
.

We shall say that an interval
I
is

acceptable
if

d_{Y}(I)\leqslant\dfrac{1}{m}.

We shall say that an interval
I
is

equalized
if

\#\{X_{i}\in I\}=\#\{Y_{i}\in I\}.

Our goal to arrange that
[0,1]

is acceptable so that

D_{n}\leqslant d_{Y}([0,1])+\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\frac{2}{m},

while keeping the number of replaced points

O(m)
on average. To do so, let us arrange the replacements as follows.

Algorithm 1:

•

Start with
I=[0,1]
.

•

If the current interval
I
is
not acceptable
, divide it into two equal halves,
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
. Then randomly and independently select the appropriate number of sample points in one half and relocate them to random positions in the other half (chosen independently and uniformly) so that the two halves become equalized.

•

Apply the same procedure recursively to
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.

Since we begin with
I=[0,1]
, the endpoints of all intervals produced in this recursion are dyadic (i.e., of the form
2^{-j}
). Moreover, with
n
odd, no grid point
Y_{k}
lies in more than one such interval, and almost surely none of the sample points
X_{i}
equals one of these dyadic endpoints. It is clear that Algorithm 1 terminates after a finite number of steps. Let us now verify that the final outcome makes
[0,1]
acceptable.

Lemma 2
.

At the end of Algorithm 1, the interval
[0,1]
is acceptable.

Proof.

When the algorithm stops, it generates a partition of
[0,1]
into equalized, acceptable intervals. For any
x\in[0,1]
, let
I
be the unique interval (generated during the algorithm) that contains
x
, and let
y
denote the left endpoint of
I
. We calculate:

\left|\frac{\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}}{n}-\frac{\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant x\}}{n}\right|\leqslant\left|\frac{\#\{X_{i}\leqslant y\}}{n}-\frac{\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant y\}}{n}\right|+\left|\frac{\#\{X_{i}\in[y,x]\}}{n}-\frac{\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant[y,x]\}}{n}\right|

Since
[0,y]
is a disjoint union of back-to-back equalized intervals, it tself is equalized, so the first term on the right hand side is zero. Since
I
is acceptable, the second term is at most
1/m
. Consequently,

\left|\frac{\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}}{n}-\frac{\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant x\}}{n}\right|\leqslant 0+\frac{1}{m}\leqslant\frac{1}{m},

and the lemma follows. ∎

Let
M_{1}
denote the total number of points moved by Algorithm 1. We shall show that
M_{1}
is
O(m)
on average. To bound the expected total number of replaced points, we compare Algorithm 1 to the following alternative algorithm:

Algorithm 2:

•

Start with
I=[0,1]
.

•

Divide
I
into two equal halves,
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
. Then randomly and independently select the appropriate number of sample points in one half and relocate them to random positions in the other half (chosen independently and uniformly) so that the two halves become equalized.

•

Repeat the previous step recursively for both
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.

Thus, the alternative algorithm is identical to Algorithm 1 except it recursively divides
each
encountered interval, regardless of whether it is acceptable or not. Algorithm 2 terminates after a finite number of steps.

Lemma 3
.

Let
M_{1}
denote the total number of points moved by Algorithm 1. Let
M_{2}
denote the total number of points moved by Algorithm 2
in those intervals that are unacceptable
.
If both algorithms are applied to the same input sample using the same random choices, then

M_{1}\leqslant M_{2}.

Proof.

Up until an acceptable interval, both algorithms perform the same replacements. However, when an acceptable interval is encountered, Algorithm 1 stops processing that interval while Algorithm 2 continues to subdivide and perform additional relocations. ∎

At every stage in Algorithm 2, the interval
[0,1]
is partitioned into equalized subintervals. Within each such interval, the points
X_{i}
remain independent and uniformly distributed. With this understood,
we claim:

Lemma 4
.

Let
I
be an interval of length
|I|=1/k
encountered in Algorithm 2, and define:

\delta(I)=d_{Y}(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}.

Then for each
\lambda\geqslant 0
,

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}\left\{\delta(I)>\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n\,k}}\right\}\leqslant C_{1}e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}

for some absolute constant
C_{1}>0
. In particular,

(2.4)

\E\delta(I)\leqslant\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{n\,k}}

for some absolute constant
C_{2}>0
.

Proof.

Let
n_{0}
be the number of sample points
X_{i}
in
I
when it is processed. Since
I
is equalized,
n_{0}
is the same as the number of grid points
0,\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots,\frac{n-1}{n}
in
I
.

If
k>n
, the interval
I
has length
1/k<1/n
and so
n_{0}\leqslant 1
. With either zero or one sample point, the discrepancy trivially satisfies
d_{Y}(I)\leqslant 1/n\leqslant 1/m
, so
I
is automatically acceptable.

Consider the case where
k<n
now. Then

(2.5)

n_{0}\leqslant n/k+1\leqslant 2n/k.

Without loss of generality, assume that
I=[0,1/k]
. DKW inequality (
2.1
) for
n_{0}
iid points sampled from the uniform distribution on
[0,1/k]
implies that with probability at least
1-2e^{-2t^{2}}
, the following ‘‘good event’’ holds:

\left|\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}-n_{0}\,x\,k\right|\leqslant t\sqrt{n_{0}}\quad\text{for any }x\in[0,1/k].

On the other hand,

\left|\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}-n_{0}\,x\,k\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{n0}}}{{\geqslant}}\left|\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}-n\,x\right|-\left|k\,x\right|\geqslant\left|\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}-n\,x\right|-1.

Then, on the good event, we have

\left\lvert\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}-n\,x\right\rvert\leqslant t\sqrt{n_{0}}+1.

On the other hand, the grid points
Y_{i}
trivially satisfy

\left\lvert\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant x\}-n\,x\right\rvert\leqslant 1.

Since these two bounds hold for all
x\in I
, combining them gives

\displaystyle n\,d_{Y}(I)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in I}\left\lvert\#\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}-\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant x\}\right\rvert\leqslant t\sqrt{n_{0}}+2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{n0}}}{{\leqslant}}t\sqrt{2n/k}+2

\displaystyle\leqslant t\sqrt{2n/k}+2\sqrt{n/k}\quad\text{(by the assumption $k<n$)}

\displaystyle=(t\sqrt{2}+2)\sqrt{n/k}=\lambda\sqrt{n/k},

where we can assume that
\lambda\geqslant 2
to ensure that
t\geqslant 0
(otherwise we can make the bound (
2.3
) trivial by adjusting
C_{1}
.)

Summarizing, we showed that if the good event occurs, then
d_{Y}(I)\leqslant\lambda/\sqrt{nk}
, and the good event occurs with probability at least

1-2e^{-2\,t^{2}}=1-2e^{-(\lambda-2)^{2}}\geqslant 1-C_{1}e^{-\lambda^{2}/2}

for some
C_{1}
. This completes the proof. ∎

Even without any replacements, the DKW inequality implies that the discrepancy of the original sample is
O(n^{-1/2})
. Hence, we may assume
m\geqslant\sqrt{n}
. For simplicity, assume also that there exists a dyadic (i.e., of the form
2^{-j}
)
integer
k_{0}
such that

\sqrt{nk_{0}}=m.

It then suffices to show that

\E M_{2}=O\bigl(\sqrt{n\,k_{0}}\bigr),

since, by Lemma
3
, the bound on
\E M_{1}
then follows.

For each interval
I
encountered in Algorithm 2, let

m(I)=\#\{\text{points moved between
$I_{1}$ and $I_{2}$ by Algorithm 2}\}.

Lemma 5
.

For each interval
I
encountered in Algorithm 2,

m(I)\leqslant 2\,n\,d_{Y}(I)+1.

Proof.

Since
I
is equalized, it follows
that

m(I)=\left|\#\{X_{i}\in I_{1}\}-\#\{X_{i}\in I_{2}\}\right|=\left|2\left(\#\{X_{i}\in I_{1}\}-\#\{Y_{i}\in I_{1}\}\right)+\#\{Y_{i}\in I_{1}\}-\#\{Y_{i}\in I_{2}\}\right|.

Consequently,

m(I)\leqslant 2\,n\,d_{Y}(I_{1})+\left|\#\{Y_{i}\in I_{1}\}-\#\{Y_{i}\in I_{2}\}\right|=2\,n\,d_{Y}(I)+1,

since
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
have the same length. ∎

Each interval
I
in Algorithm 2 has length
|I|=1/k
for some dyadic integer
k\in\{1,2,4,\ldots\}
. Hence, the total number of replacements is given by

M_{2}=\sum_{k=1,2,4,\ldots}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|I|=1/k\end{subarray}}m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{ is unacceptable}\}}.

For each unacceptable
I
, we have
nd_{Y}(I)>n/m\geqslant 1
, so Lemma
5
gives
m(I)\leqslant 3\,n\,d_{Y}(I)
. Then, for each
I
with
|I|=1/k
, we have

\mathbb{E}[m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{ is unacceptable}\}}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[3\,n\,d_{Y}(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}]=3\,n\,\mathbb{E}[\delta(I)]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{E}}}{{\leqslant}}3\,C_{2}\sqrt{n/k}.

It is now convenient to treat the cases
k\leqslant k_{0}
and
k>k_{0}
separately. First consider the case when
k\leqslant k_{0}
. Since the algorithm encounters at most
k

intervals of length
1/k
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k\leqslant k_{0}}\sum_{|I|=1/k}m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}\right]\leqslant\sum_{k\leqslant k_{0}}3\,C_{2}\sqrt{nk}=3\,C_{2}\sqrt{n}\sum_{k\leqslant k_{0}}\sqrt{k}=O(\sqrt{nk_{0}}),

where the latter equality follows since
the sum is over dyadic values of
k
. Next, consider the case when
k>k_{0}
. Since

m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}\leqslant 3\,n\,d_{Y}(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}=3\,n\,\delta(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}},

it follows from Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
that for each

I
with
|I|=1/k
,

\mathbb{E}[m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}]\leqslant 3\,n\mathbb{E}[\delta(I)^{2}]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}]^{1/2}.

From the tail bound (
2.3
), we find for each
I
with
|I|=1/k
,

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}[\delta(I)^{2}]\leqslant\frac{C_{3}^{2}}{nk}

for some constant
C_{3}>0
. On the other hand, for
k>k_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}]^{1/2}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\delta(I)>\frac{1}{\sqrt{nk_{0}}}\right\}^{1/2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{tail}}}{{\leqslant}}C_{1}e^{-(k/k_{0})/2}.

Consequently, for each
k>k_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}[m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}]\leqslant 3C_{3}\sqrt{n/k}\cdot C_{1}e^{-(k/k_{0})/2}.

Since the algorithm encounters at most
k
intervals of length
1/k
, summing over all of them and then over numbers
k>k_{0}
that are powers of
2
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k>k_{0}}\sum_{|I|=1/k}m(I)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{I\text{{ is unacceptable}}\}}\right]\leqslant 3C_{1}C_{3}\sqrt{n}\sum_{k>k_{0}}\sqrt{k}e^{-(k/k_{0})/2}=O(\sqrt{nk_{0}}).

Hence, adding the contributions from both ranges of
k
, we find:

\E M_{2}=O(\sqrt{nk_{0}})+O(\sqrt{nk_{0}})=O(\sqrt{nk_{0}}),

and the proof of Theorem
1
is complete.

3. Proof of Theorem
2

The proof is analogous to that of the one-sample case. In this two-sample setting, the points
Y_{i}
denote the second independent sample drawn uniformly from
[0,1]
, instead of a fixed uniform grid. One obtains an analogue of Lemma
4
by using
DKW inequality along with the the triangle inequality,

\left|\#\{X_{i}\leqslant I\}-\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant I\}\right|\leqslant\left|\#\{X_{i}\leqslant I\}-|I|\cdot n\right|+\left|\#\{Y_{i}\leqslant I\}-|I|\cdot n\right|\quad\text{for each $I\subset[0,1]$,}

and the rest of the proof follows that of
Theorem
1
.

4. Proof of Proposition
1

One can never achieve discrepancy less than
1/n
by replacing any number of observations. Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that
c/m\geqslant 1/n
, so
\sqrt{n}\leqslant m\leqslant cn
. Thus, there exists
1\leqslant k\leqslant c^{2}n
such that

\sqrt{nk}=m.

Assume for simplicity of exposition that
k
is an integer. Partition the interval
[0,1]
into
k
subintervals
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{k}
of equal length
1/k
. For each
I_{j}
,
set:

\nu_{j}=\#\{X_{i}\in I_{j}\}.

We shall say that
I_{j}
is
regular
if

\nu_{j}\leqslant n/k+0.5\sqrt{n/k}.

In order to achieve discrepancy
D_{n}<1/(4m)
, all intervals
I_{j}
must be regular. Indeed, by triangle inequality we have

\left\lvert\frac{\#\{X_{i}\in I_{j}\}}{n}-\lvert I_{j}\rvert\right\rvert\leqslant 2D_{n}

so if
I_{j}
is irregular then we would have

2D_{n}\geqslant\frac{\nu_{j}}{n}-\frac{1}{k}\geqslant\frac{0.5}{\sqrt{nk}}=\frac{1}{2m}.

Note that
\nu_{j}
is a binomial random variable with mean
n/k\geqslant c^{-2}\geqslant 100
, if we choose
c<0.1
. Call an interval
I_{j}
is
dense
if

\nu_{j}>n/k+\sqrt{n/k}.

Standard results (e.g., via the Berry–Esseen theorem or direct lower bounds for the binomial tail) imply that

\mathbb{P}\{\text{$I_{j}$ is dense}\}>\frac{1}{100}.

Thus, the expected number of dense intervals is at least
k/100
. To achieve the discrepancy
D_{n}<1/(4m)
, we must make each interval regular, and this requires moving at least
0.5\sqrt{n/k}
points out of each dense interval. Thus, achieving
D_{n}<1/(4m)
requires moving, on average, at least

\frac{k}{100}\cdot 0.5\sqrt{n/k}=\frac{\sqrt{nk}}{200}=\frac{m}{200}

points, and the proposition follows if we choose
c<1/200
.

5. Complexity of Algorithm 1

The time complexity of Algorithm 1 is
O\left(n\ln(n)\ln(m)\right)
.
To check this, observe that for each

equalized
interval
I
with
|I|\leqslant 1/m
,

d_{Y}(I)\leqslant\left|\frac{\#\{X_{i}\in I\}}{n}\right|=\left|\frac{\#\{Y_{i}\in I\}}{n}\right|\leqslant|I|+\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\frac{2}{m}.

Thus, every interval
I
of length at most
1/m
is acceptable (after a suitable adjustment of the constant in the definition of acceptability). Consequently, the recursion stops after at most

O(\ln{m})
steps. On each step, computing the empirical distribution function requires
O(n\ln n)
operations, and the replacement of the sample points is done in linear time. Combining these yields the overall time complexity.
