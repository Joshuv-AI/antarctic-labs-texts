ECG Sex Index in children and adolescents
Authors: Tolga Hayit, Ibrahim Karabayir, David Herrington, Robert L Davis, Oguz Akbilgic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrocardiographic sex index (ESI) was developed in adults to capture continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) features associated with biological sex, but its behaviour in children, whose ECG morphology changes substantially through growth and puberty, has not been characterized. We applied the adult-derived ESI model to 61 930 paediatric ECGs from the University of Tennessee Health Science Center clinical population (ages 0–18 years). ESI values were examined by single-year age and stratified by sex and race. Age-related patterns were summarized using mean estimates with 95% confidence intervals, distributional analyses across broader age groups, and the prevalence of extreme sex-typical values (ESI ≥0.8 in males; ≤0.2 in females). In early childhood, ESI values were tightly clustered around 0.5 in both sexes. Beginning in late childhood and becoming more pronounced through adolescence, ESI increased in males and decreased in females, producing a widening separation that plateaued in mid-to-late adolescence, a pattern that was consistent across racial subgroups. Distributional analyses showed parallel shifts in ESI distributions, with older males enriched for higher values and older females enriched for lower values, and threshold-based analyses revealed similar divergence, with the proportions of males with ESI ≥0.8 and females with ESI ≤0.2 both increasing sharply during adolescence. Although derived from adult data, ESI thus exhibits age and sex-dependent patterns in children that align with known developmental trajectories of ECG morphology, indicating that ESI tracks physiological maturation across childhood and adolescence and providing a foundation for evaluating its clinical and biological significance in younger populations.Keywords: ECG Sex Index, Electrocardiogram, Artificial intelligence, Biological sex, Heart maturation

Introduction

Human sexual development is a dynamic process shaped by anatomical, physiological, and hormonal changes across the life course rather than a static binary state.1,2,3 Before adulthood, the transient hormonal activation of infancy (mini-puberty), the quiescent prepubertal period, and the reactivation of puberty constitute salient phases that reflect coordinated changes in the reproductive axis.1,3,4,5 Despite this continuity, many paediatric and epidemiologic studies still model sex as a binary variable, which may not capture stage-specific biology during childhood and adolescence.3,5 Because these developmental processes influence multiple organ systems, tools that capture subtle physiologic variation across age are needed.

Electrocardiogram (ECG) encodes rich information about cardiac structure and function and frequently reveal sex-related differences in autonomic and repolarization phenotypes.6,7 Importantly, ECGs also capture information about a wide range of clinical and physiologic traits that extend beyond the cardiovascular system. Application of artificial intelligence (AI) to ECG data has shown that suitably trained deep learning models can extract such latent signals at scale, including for arrhythmia detection,8 prediction of mortality,9 and cardiovascular risk stratification across diverse settings.10,11,12,13 Recent paediatric work further indicates that electrocardiogram-based artificial intelligence (ECG-AI) approaches are feasible and informative in childhood cohorts, for example, among survivors of childhood cancer who face elevated late cardiotoxicity risk.11,14,15,16

Previously developed, the electrocardiographic sex index (ESI)17 is a continuous, data-derived index produced by applying a deep learning convolutional neural network—originally trained on adult 12-lead ECGs—to standard 10-second ECG waveform data, then rank-normalizing the model's output scores to a [0, 1] range. ESI values near 1.0 reflect ECG features strongly associated with male sex, while values near 0.0 reflect features associated with female sex, and intermediate values capture graded states between these poles. Crucially, ESI is not a binary classification but a continuous representation that can vary within an individual over time, making it well-suited to capturing physiological transitions such as puberty. In adults, ESI has demonstrated strong discrimination between biological sexes and has provided prognostic information beyond a binary sex variable across several cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular outcomes.17,18 However, because the original model was derived from adults, understanding its behaviour in younger populations is a necessary next step.

The present study extends the ESI framework to children from infancy through Tanner stages 4–5 without retraining the model.19,20 Our goal is to quantify age-specific discrimination and to characterize index trajectories across childhood and adolescence, while comparing paediatric patterns with adult benchmarks.17 This continuous approach is intended to better reflect the graded and stage-specific nature of development and to offer a practical covariate for studies in which detailed staging or hormone measurements may not be available.3,5

We hypothesized that clinically relevant but not directly measured developmental sex differences can be captured by a continuous ECG-derived index in paediatric populations and that index behaviour across age would be consistent with established developmental physiology. We also sought to provide context for potential clinical applications while avoiding claims that require paediatric outcome data, which are beyond the scope of the present analysis.

Methods

Data sources

Two institutional ECG repositories were utilized. The Wake Forest Baptist Health archive had been used to develop the adult sex-classification model and ESI as reported previously,17 and paediatric ECGs from University of Tennessee Health Science Center (UTHSC) were used for the present analyses.

Wake Forest Baptist Health (WF): ECG repository at Wake Forest Baptist Health (Winston-Salem, NC) was used to develop a convolutional neural network-based ECG-AI model to detect sex from standard 12-lead ECGs. The repository consists of standard 10-second, 12-lead resting ECGs linked to demographics and longitudinal outcomes, totalling 3 573 844 ECGs from 754 761 patients in the parent resource. Further details of the source systems and linkage are available in the prior publication.17

University of Tennessee Health Science Center: To evaluate performance in a paediatric cohort, a second dataset from UTHSC was used. The de-identified paediatric dataset comprising 61 930 resting 12-lead ECGs from 37 234 unique patients aged 0–17, recorded between 1997-04-02 and 2020-11-10. No diagnosis-based exclusions were applied for congenital heart disease, rhythm disturbances, or conduction abnormalities. To characterize potential pathological confounding, we used linked ICD-9/10 diagnosis codes to flag a prespecified set of ECG-relevant conditions; the code set is provided in the Supplementary material (Supplementary material online, Table S4 and S5).

ECG data preparation

Wake Forest ECGs were stored in the GE MUSE system and exported as Extensible Markup Language (XML) files (with base64-encoded waveform data). We used a customized Python parser to decode 12-lead waveforms into NumPy arrays and wrote encounter-level metadata to comma-separated values (CSV). Recordings sampled at 500 Hz were down-sampled to 250 Hz so that each ECG could be represented in the same input format used in the original ESI pipeline17; each ECG was represented as a 2500 × 12 matrix (rows: milliseconds; columns: per-lead microvolts). UTHSC ECGs were exported from the institutional ECG archive in dicom format and processed with the same parser and down-sampling procedure to ensure identical input format across sites.13,17 No other ECG pre-processing steps were applied. The model was applied to the raw 12-lead waveform matrices; no engineered ECG measurements were used as inputs.

ESI derivation

We used the same convolutional neural network from our prior ESI study,17 adapted from a ResNet design.21 That adult sex-classification model was trained on 10 s, 12-lead ECG time series with patient-wise splits at Wake Forest (80% training, 10% validation, 10% holdout). The final model was selected by validation Area Under the Curve (AUC) and evaluated on the Wake Forest holdout and an external PhysioNet cohort without additional tuning.22

In the present study we applied this previously trained model to each paediatric ECG without retraining and derived ESI exactly as in our original study: model outputs were rank ordered and mapped into [0, 1] interval, where ESI values closer to 0 suggest strong confidence about model prediction of female and ESI closer to 1 suggest stronger confidence to male sex.17 No paediatric specific calibration or thresholding was introduced. For full architectural and optimization details, see prior publication.17

Subgroup analyses

Primary subgroups were specified a priori. Analyses were carried out on the combined holdout dataset drawn from both Wake Forest and UTHSC. Age was the main stratifier. We computed ESI summaries by sex and AUC with confidence intervals in 1-year bins from 0 to 17. Site comparisons contrasted Wake Forest and UTHSC using the same code with no recalibration. Race and ethnicity were analysed as recorded in the EHR. When comparing AUCs between subgroups, we used the DeLong test.23

Results

Cohort characteristics

Derivation cohort (Wake Forest, adults): The ECG-AI sex-classification model was developed using the Wake Forest Baptist Health ECG repository as previously reported. The repository includes 3 573 844 resting 12-lead, 10-s ECGs from 754 761 patients, with 75% White, 17% Black, and 51% female; mean age was 61 years [standard deviation (SD) 17]. For ECG-level counts, the previous study reported 183 126 male ECGs (51.3%) and 174 173 female ECGs (48.7%).17

Evaluation cohort (UTHSC, paediatrics 0–17 years): The paediatric dataset comprised 61 930 ECGs from 37 234 unique patients. Median age was 10.0 years (interquartile range 3.0–15.0). At the ECG level, 30 835 studies were from males and 31 095 from females (49.8% male, 50.2% female). At the patient level, 1435 of 37 234 children (3.9%) had at least one ICD-9/10 code in the prespecified ECG-relevant set (see Supplementary Material online, Tables S4 and S5), whereas 35 799 (%96.1) had no such codes. Race and ethnicity recorded in the EHR were 57.2% Black or African American, 36.5% White, and 6.3% other or unknown. A total of 26.8% of patients contributed more than one ECG. Performance of an ECG-AI sex-classification model

The previously trained adult sex-classification model was applied to paediatric ECGs from UTHSC without retraining. Across the paediatric range, discrimination increased with age. Using 1-year bins, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) ranged from 0.587 in the 0–1 year bin (confidence interval 0.575–0.598; n = 9293 ECGs) to 0.970 in the 17–18 year bin (confidence interval 0.966–0.974; n = 5568). The ECG-count weighted average AUC across all bins was 0.781. The evaluation set comprised 61 930 ECGs from 37 234 children, with 30 835 male ECGs and 31 095 female ECGs (49.8% male, 50.2% female).

To contextualize paediatric performance, we compared site- and race-specific AUCs with the adult Wake Forest (WF) test set. Table 2 shows higher AUCs in adults than in paediatrics overall: WF 0.947 vs. UTHSC 0.814. Table 2 also shows a similar ordering across race subgroups: White 0.948 vs. 0.807, Black 0.939 vs. 0.827. These differences are consistent with age-related maturation and the absence of paediatric retraining.

To complement these single-year estimates, we examined the distribution of ESI across three broader paediatric age bands (0–6, 6–12, and 12–18 years) separately in males and females. In early childhood, both sexes showed ESI values concentrated around 0.5 with substantial overlap between age groups. With increasing age, the distribution in males shifted towards higher values, with the 12–18-year group enriched for ESI values close to 1.0. In contrast, the distribution in females shifted towards lower values, and the 12–18-year group showed a greater concentration of ESI values near 0.0. These age and sex-related shifts in the full distributions parallel the divergence in mean ESI summarized in Supplementary material online, Table S3 and provide a graphical depiction of the widening separation between male and female ESI across childhood and adolescence.

To further assess the biological relevance of ESI, we quantified the percentage of children exhibiting strongly sex-typical values across single-year age bins (ESI ≥0.8 in males and ESI ≤ 0.2 in females; Figure 6). In early childhood, these extreme values were uncommon in both sexes, consistent with the narrow distributions centred near 0.5. Beginning in late childhood and continuing through adolescence, the percentage of males with ESI ≥0.8 increased sharply, whereas the percentage of females with ESI ≤0.2 rose in parallel. By mid-to-late adolescence, a substantial fraction of both sexes demonstrated these strongly sex-typical patterns. This threshold-based analysis mirrors the continuous trends observed in the mean and distributional summaries and is consistent with ESI reflecting biologically driven maturation of sexually dimorphic ECG features.

Age-dependent prevalence of extreme ESI values (ESI ≥ 0.8 in males; ≤ 0.2 in females).

Assessing heart rate as a confounding factor. Heart rate is a major physiological confounder in paediatric cohorts, decreasing significantly with age. In this subsection, we assessed correlation of ESI and hear rate. We also assess whether heart rate-adjusted ESI still could detect biological sex.

Heart rate and ESI showed an extremely weak positive Pearson Correlation of 0.019 yet it was significant (P < 0.01). On the other hand, heart rate-alone did not show any value in discriminating biological sex, AUC of 0.487 (0.481–0.493), which was 0.814 () when using ESI to classify biological sex. When we adjusted ESI with heart rate withing a logistic regression framework, we obtained OR of 0.996 (0.995–0.997) and 37.182 (34.553–40.012) for heart rate and ESI, respectively.

Discussion

Our findings show that an ECG-derived ESI trained in adults generalizes to children and adolescents with performance that rises steadily as individuals approach biological adulthood and it is independent from heart rate. In single-year age bins, AUC increased from 0.587 at ages 0–1 to 0.970 at ages 17–18, with a count-weighted mean of 0.781 and a significant DeLong difference between the youngest and oldest bins. Race-stratified analyses in the paediatric cohort followed the same increasing pattern, and adult performance remained higher than paediatric performance overall, which is consistent with maturation effects. In early childhood, mean ESI values in males and females clustered near 0.5. They then diverged during late childhood and adolescence. Male ESI increased and female ESI decreased before plateauing in late adolescence. The same pattern was observed in race-specific summaries.

These age-dependent ECG patterns parallel established endocrinologic trajectories that are continuous and stage specific rather than stepwise. The hypothalamic pituitary gonadal axis shows a transient activation in early infancy (mini-puberty), relative suppression through mid-childhood, and reactivation with the onset of puberty. Hormone levels reach pubertal ranges during the first months of life. They then decline and rise again in adolescence.2,3,5 Continuous paediatric reference frameworks show that sex steroids and related biomarkers change smoothly with age and differ by sex across childhood and adolescence. This supports age and sex specific reference intervals rather than adult carry-overs.3,5 These reference models also emphasize puberty, body mass index, and oral contraceptive use as covariates that shift endocrine levels. This is consistent with a physiological substrate for the observed ESI divergence during maturation.5

Sex linked ECG differences reported in the literature, including variation in heart rate dynamics and ventricular repolarization, widen with sexual maturation and are plausibly hormone linked. This aligns with the paediatric ESI trajectory that we observe.6

Consistent with the ESI trajectories reported here, a large contemporary digital cohort of healthy North American children demonstrated that standard ECG measurements—including QTc in lead II and R wave amplitude in V6—showed minimal sex differences in early childhood but became significantly sex-differentiated beginning at or around the pubertal age range, with girls exhibiting longer QTc and boys exhibiting greater V6 amplitude from approximately age 12 years onward.24 This alignment between measurement-based sex differences in conventional ECG parameters and the AI-derived continuous ESI trajectory supports the interpretation that ESI tracks the same underlying biological signal—the progressive electrophysiological maturation driven by gonadal hormone activation during puberty. In keeping with this, we observe increasing separation in ESI during adolescence. For example, long QT sex differences are minimal at birth and emerge after puberty. Post pubertal females typically show longer baseline corrected QT interval (QTc) than males. This pattern points to endocrine modulation of cardiac electrophysiology in adolescence.7 Taken together, these data suggest that the index captures a developmentally graded electrophysiologic signal rather than a static label.

Linking to our prior adult study, ESI was defined as a continuous representation of sex based on rank normalization of ECG-AI outputs. In adults, ESI and related discordance measures were associated with one-year risk for all-cause mortality, heart failure, and progression to kidney failure. In several analyses they provided information beyond a binary sex variable.17 The paediatric results reported here, with rising AUCs and widening ESI separation with age, indicate that the same adult-trained construct tracks developmental biology in earlier life stages without paediatric retraining.

Beyond discrimination metrics, the distribution of ESI itself changes in a manner consistent with biological maturation. In early childhood, male and female ESI values cluster near 0.5 and then separate progressively from late childhood through adolescence, with widening male-female gaps that plateau in late adolescence; this pattern is also evident across race strata. The age-dependent shift in ESI aligns with continuous paediatric hormone references and with established sex differences in autonomic tone and ventricular repolarization, supporting the interpretation of ESI as a biologically grounded maturity signal rather than a purely statistical construct.3,5,6,7

Several implications follow. First, the upward trajectory in discrimination likely reflects increasing separation of sex-linked ECG features as endocrine axes mature. The concordance with continuous paediatric hormone references argues for using ESI as a graded covariate in developmental research rather than relying only on a binary sex term.3,5 Second, where Tanner staging or direct hormone measurements are unavailable, ESI may serve as a practical proxy for maturation when modelling phenotypes that are sensitive to sex and puberty, such as autonomic function or repolarization indices with known sex and age dependence.6,7 In longitudinal paediatric settings, ESI could be evaluated as an age-aware covariate to contextualize ECG-based phenotypes that evolve through puberty. Examples include follow-up cohorts with treatment exposures such as childhood cancer survivors, and studies where endocrine dysregulation or altered pubertal timing is a concern. Third, because ESI improved risk modelling in adults, prospective paediatric studies can now test whether age-aware ESI relates to outcomes that emerge during or after adolescence, while recognizing that such prognostic links have not yet been established in children.17

Potential clinical utility in paediatric cardio-oncology. Cardiotoxic cancer therapies, including anthracyclines and chest-directed radiation, are associated with long-term risk of cardiomyopathy, and this risk is greater when exposure occurs before completion of cardiac growth and maturation.11,25,26 In this context, ESI measured at the time of treatment may provide a quantitative ECG-based marker of developmental cardiac maturity, distinguishing children whose electrophysiologic phenotype is more ‘adult-like’ from those with less mature profiles. This raises the hypothesis that children with lower-maturity ESI values at the time of cardiotoxic exposure may face higher long-term risk of cardiomyopathy and heart failure in adulthood. Longitudinal survivorship cohorts with serial ECGs and outcome follow-up could directly test whether ESI at exposure or changes in ESI over time modify treatment-associated cardiac risk.

Should paediatric-specific ESIs be derived? An important open question is whether paediatric-specific ESI models should be developed. Paediatric-trained models may improve discrimination in early childhood and reduce domain shift between adult and paediatric ECG morphology. However, age-specific training may also embed developmental stage into the representation itself, potentially limiting interpretability across the life course. By contrast, applying a single adult-trained model provides a stable reference frame across ages and enables evaluation of how ‘adult-like’ ECG phenotypes emerge during development. Future work should directly compare adult-trained, paediatric-trained, and age-calibrated ESI models in outcome-linked paediatric cohorts to determine whether paediatric-specific training improves prognostic utility without sacrificing interpretability across developmental stages.

Limitations

Although we examined performance in children with ICD-9/10 codes for ECG-relevant conditions, diagnosis codes are an imperfect proxy for disease severity, timing, and ECG phenotype. Residual confounding by unmeasured cardiac or systemic conditions may influence ESI values and discrimination, particularly in younger age groups where congenital or structural abnormalities are more prevalent among those receiving ECGs.

The ESI model was trained exclusively on adult ECGs and applied to paediatric data without recalibration or fine-tuning. Domain shift between adult and paediatric ECG morphology likely contributes to lower discrimination in early childhood and may introduce systematic miscalibration of ESI values at younger ages. Paediatric-specific training or age-aware recalibration may improve performance and should be explored in future work. However, we also note that we have specifically chosen to not-calibrate our original adult driven model for paediatric cohort to be able to better understand at what age children’s ESI represent and adult ESI.

Our paediatric cohort was derived from a clinical ECG archive rather than a population-based sample. Children undergoing ECG testing are more likely to have symptoms, comorbidities, or clinical indications than the general paediatric population. This may limit the generalizability of ESI distributions and discrimination metrics to healthy community samples and could bias age-specific patterns if indications for ECG testing vary by age or sex.

We used chronological age as a proxy for biological maturation; however, pubertal timing varies widely between individuals. The absence of Tanner staging, sex steroid measurements, and growth metrics (e.g. height velocity) limits our ability to directly link ESI trajectories to pubertal stage or endocrine status. As a result, the observed divergence of ESI during adolescence should be interpreted as correlational with age-related maturation rather than a direct measure of pubertal development.

In addition, adulthood has both legal and biological meanings. Statutory age thresholds vary across jurisdictions, whereas biological adulthood reflects physical development and sexual maturity. Our analyses use age as a proxy for biological maturation rather than a legal status. These factors argue for follow up studies that incorporate Tanner staging, hormone measurements, and longitudinal outcomes to examine mechanism and clinical utility within paediatric populations.3

Conclusion

Building on our prior work, we applied an adult trained ECG-AI model to paediatric ECGs to derive a continuous ESI that tracks development from infancy through Tanner stages 4–5. The index generalized to children and adolescents, with discrimination that rose steadily across age and patterns consistent with known endocrine maturation. These results support using ESI as a graded covariate for studies and models that require attention to developmental sex effects, and suggest potential utility in clinical decision making. Future work should test prognostic value in paediatric cohorts and integrate staging and hormone measures to clarify mechanism and clinical impact.
