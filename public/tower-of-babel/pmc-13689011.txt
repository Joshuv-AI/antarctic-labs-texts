To become or not to become: uncovering the influence of exposure to positive and negative role models in professional identity development
Authors: Houra Heidari, Elaheh Mohammadi, Fatemeh Ebrahimpour, Elizabeth K Kachur, Mahboobeh Khabaz Mafinejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRole models are crucial in shaping and developing students’ professional identities. This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of medical students encountering both positive and negative role models and their impact on the process of professional identity growth and development at Tehran University of Medical Sciences.MethodsThis study was carried out using the Colaizzi descriptive phenomenological method. Sixteen medical students from the undergraduate medical curriculum from 2023 to 2024 constituted participants. The data were collected through semi-structured interviews. Data trustworthiness was assured using Guba and Lincoln’s criteria.ResultsThe data were categorized into six main themes. (1) Enhancing Students’ Professional Insight and Vision, (2) Improving Students’ Professional Performance and Behavior through Positive Reinforcement, (3) Instilling Positive Feelings and Affections in Students, (4) Triggering Negative Reactions and Feelings in Students5) Changing Students’ Avoidance Performance, and 6) Stimulating Students’ Reflections on Their Professional Beliefs and Views.ConclusionThe study results indicate that medical students pattern their performance, behaviors, feelings, and perspectives, which positively contributes to developing their professional identity through positive role models. Furthermore, facing negative role models through reflection, they think about behaviors and feelings that lead them away from professional norms and avoid internalizing them in their professional value system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-10004-4.Keywords: Medical student, Role model, Professional identity, Qualitative research

Background

In recent years, medical education and the necessity of training competent graduates have gained increasing importance. However, current educational programs predominantly focus on achieving measurable competencies, often paying insufficient attention to the development of professional identity [1]. Although acquiring competencies is critical, this approach limits opportunities for students to reflect on who they are becoming within the professional community, a reflection that is essential for holistic professional growth [2].

Professional identity in medicine is conceptualized as a dynamic, multidimensional, self-directed process wherein individuals internalize the values, norms, and behaviors characteristic of the medical profession [2–8], According to Lave and Wenger’s “Community of Practice” theory [5, 9–15], this identity formation occurs through sustained participation and interaction within professional communities [9, 13, 15, 16]. Although prior research has explored professional identity development among managers [17], instructors [18], physicians [19], medical and nursing students [20–22], the lived experiences of medical students encountering both positive and negative role models integrated within authentic clinical settings have not been systematically investigated.

Role modeling that plays a significant role [23], rooted in socialization theory and central to Bandura’s social learning framework [24], is a critical mechanism through which professional behaviors and attitudes are transmitted [25] even with just observation [26]. Positive role models foster the internalization of professional values and ethical practices [26–29], whereas negative role models may undermine professional growth by reinforcing unprofessional behaviors [26–29], and adversely influence medical student’s perceptions of professionalism and shape their developing professional identities [30]. Exploring how medical students interpret and integrate these dual influences is essential to understanding the complexities of identity development in clinical education.

Given that professional identity formation is an evolving, experience-driven phenomenon, qualitative inquiry, particularly approaches focused on capturing lived experiences, is well-suited to address this gap [31]. Moreover, most studies have focused on explaining the role of positive role models, and there is no available study that elucidates the role of negative role models and both simultaneously. Therefore, given the importance of this issue, the knowledge gap in this area, and the lack of studies, a deeper understanding of these lived experiences may help health professions design clinical learning environments that foster professional identity development while minimizing the influence of negative role modeling. This study thus employs a qualitative phenomenological design to explore how encounters with positive and negative role models influence medical students’ emerging professional identities in real workplace clinical contexts.

Materials and methods

Study design

We used a descriptive qualitative study to understand the medical students’ lived experiences with positive and negative role models in their professional identity development. In this study, the ontological stance is based on constructivism, assuming that professional identity is socially constructed and shaped by multiple realities [32]. The epistemological stance is interpretivism, emphasizing that knowledge emerges through interpreting individuals’ lived experiences [33]. Accordingly, using a phenomenological approach and semi-structured interviews is appropriate for deeply exploring and understanding students’ subjective meanings [34].

Therefore, this study was conducted in the teaching hospitals of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, from winter 2024 to spring 2024.

Context

The undergraduate medical curriculum at Tehran University of Medical Sciences is a 7-year program divided into pre-clinical and clinical phases. During the pre-clinical phase, students spend two years learning theoretical basic sciences and one year studying pathophysiology. Students also undergo an educational program that includes “early clinical exposure” and “medical semiology” to prepare them to take patient histories and perform physical examinations. After completion, they enter the clinical phase of the program. In this phase, students participate in multiple clinical rotations at affiliated teaching and educational hospitals.

Participants

We employed purposive sampling to invite participants [35], who would be able to provide extensive information about their professional identity and exposure to role modeling during their clinical phase. Participants were undergraduate medical students in their fourth to seventh years of study who had completed at least 6 months of the clinical phase and had interacted with both positive and negative clinical role models. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was employed to recruit participants with diverse educational experiences. Variation was sought in terms of gender and academic year to ensure a broad range of perspectives regarding professional identity formation in clinical settings. Sixteen students participated in the study, including nine males and seven females, with a mean age of 24 ± 2.3 years.

Data collection

After obtaining the participants’ consent, this study conducted the interviews individually by the first researcher (HH). In this context, participants had the option to assist in flexibility both in person and virtually. The interviews took between 45 and 90 min. The interview guide and questions were compiled by MKM, FE, and HH (Appendix 1). In addition, semi-structured interviews with several open-ended questions were used. Participants’ consent to record the audio was obtained for both in-person and virtual sessions, and to maintain confidentiality, each participant was assigned a specific number. The interviews began with two general questions about the participants’ lived experiences related to our objectives, followed by interview questions. These questions were developed by the research team and subsequently reviewed by three experts in medical education with experience in qualitative research. Based on their feedback, necessary revisions were made: “Have you ever had professors who made you think, ‘I want to become a doctor like them in the future’?” and " Have you ever had professors who made you think, ‘I don’t want to become a doctor like them in the future’?“, “Please describe your experiences.“, “Did these behaviors hurt or help you shape your feelings and behaviors as a doctor?” “Please explain what you were thinking at that moment. How did you feel?“, “Apart from your professors, did other medical professionals act as role models for you?” During the interviews, probing and reflective questions (e.g., “Can you explain that further?“) were used to encourage participants to elaborate on their lived experiences. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. After each Interview, the data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed to identify emerging codes and thems. By the fourteenth interview, no new codes or concepts were observed. To further ensure the sufficiency and richness of the data (information power), two additional interviews (15th and 16th) were conducted, confirming that no new significant information emerged [36].

Reflexivity statement

This study was part of the first author’s master’s thesis in medical education. With a background in health sciences, the researcher was familiar with the clinical context but was not a physician. Participants were recruited voluntarily from teaching hospitals without prior acquaintance, minimizing bias. Interviews were conducted under MKM supervision, and bracketing was practiced throughout to manage personal assumptions. Other members of the research team (EM, FE, EKC, MKM) had experience in conducting qualitative studies. The research team reviewed and approved the entire process to ensure rigor and consistency.

Data analysis

The study data were analyzed using the Colaizzi descriptive phenomenological method (Fig. 1), with MAXQDA (version 2020) for coding and interview transcripts. In accordance with Colaizzi’s seven-step approach, the interviews were transcribed by (HH). By identifying meaningful phrases, each concept related to the research aim was assigned a code. Participants were invited to review the interpretations and offer clarifications or additions. The research team critically considered all participants’ feedback; where participants’ perspectives aligned with the emerging analysis, modifications were made. In instances where discrepancies arose, the research team (HH) and (MKM) maintained the integrity of the analysis and formed sub-themes and then main themes based on the theoretical framework and the broader data context [36]. An international expert in the field of professional identity was consulted for the final check of the themes and sub-themes (EKK). Additionally, according to Colaizzi’s sixth step, the first researcher (HH) and (FE), presented the overall narrative of the experienced phenomenon to describe the fundamental structure (Appendix 2).

Trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of these research data was assured by using Guba and Lincoln’s criteria [37–39]. To ensure the credibility and dependability of the data, the researcher engaged with it, spent sufficient time collecting, analyzing, and immersing in it. Preliminary findings, including extracted codes, were shared with all participants and an expert researcher in qualitative studies for feedback [36]. To establish the confirmability of the findings, the researcher fully described all stages of the research, including data collection, analysis, and development of themes and sub-themes, so that others could confirm the findings by reading them. A peer examination was also used to assess the transferability of the findings. The researcher provided a comprehensive description of the research context to enable readers to determine the transferability of the findings. Peer debriefing was conducted throughout the analytical process. The research team reviewed coding decisions and thematic interpretations, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved.

Ethical considerations

Approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1402.619). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their information and that each interview would be coded to maintain anonymity. Participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time. Also, in this study, all procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

This study collected data through individual interviews with 16 medical student participants. The demographic characteristics of the participants are listed in the table below (Table 1). A total of 820 primary codes were derived from the analysis of interviews. After eliciting and merging similar codes, the total number of codes was reduced to 240; these are presented solely to illustrate the progression of the qualitative analytical process and its abstraction. Also, these are descriptive and should not be interpreted as quantitative findings or statistical evidence. Ultimately, these were categorized into six main themes and 14 sub-themes and were formed based on the effects of exposure to positive and negative role models on students’ professional identity development (Fig. 2). Main themes regarding positive role models included: 1) ‘’Enhancing Students’ Professional Insight and Vision, 2) ‘’ Improving Students’ Professional Performance and Behavior through Positive Reinforcement, and 3) " Instilling Positive Feelings and Affections in Students. Main themes regarding negative role models included: 4) “Triggering Negative Reactions and Feelings in Students, 5)” Changing Students’ Avoidance Performance, and 6) “Stimulating Students’ Reflection on Their Professional Beliefs and Views (Appendix 3).

Theme one: Enhancing students’ professional insight and vision

This theme pertains to the changes and impacts on students’ understanding of situations from encountering positive role models in developing their professional identity.

Sub-theme one: Norms related to the nature of the field

Students’ perception of their field is reinforced by exposure to the medical field, especially when interacting with role models. Their initial perception of the sacredness of the medicine that formed before entering clinical practice reinforced to continue. In this regard, one participant stated:

“When I saw the selflessness and dedication of the positive role models I described to you and saw that for them, medicine meant sacred service and helping people, not just exploiting their title and position, I realized that the initial ethical integrity I felt about medicine was still valid. I was glad I didn’t give up my beliefs because of negative role models.” (Participant 7, Female, 6th Year)

Sub-theme two: Norms related to professional performance

From the students’ perspective, medicine is a field where satisfaction and achievement require growth in various aspects of professional performance. It is crucial to train competent graduates with a desirable professional identity. Students acknowledged the importance of improving their professional performance after observing their positive role models managing challenging situations; in this regard, they stated that:

“During a clinical round, a professor was verbally abused by a patient—a situation that would upset anyone. However, the professor controlled the situation professionally, and the patient eventually apologized and complied with the doctor’s orders. This taught me the importance of maintaining professionalism and patience. It also made me want to develop the ability to manage such situations effectively.” (Participant 11, Male, 4th Year)

Sub-theme three: Norms related to personal performance

Medicine is considered a sacred field by students. To achieve the goals of this ethical integrity, which include serving patients and managing their physical, mental, and emotional conditions, it depends on professional performance and personal characteristics. The understanding students gain from encountering positive role models related to their traits influences their growth and development in professional identity. If a doctor is satisfied with their professional performance, they can instill a positive sense of success in others. One participant stated that:

“I noticed that if a doctor can establish proper and effective communication with patients and students, it can transform a mundane, clinical encounter into something that heals, educates, and brings personal satisfaction. This satisfaction from one’s performance is crucial for success. I learned that whatever I do, I should feel it wholeheartedly and be satisfied with it before expecting others to be.” (Participant 10, Male, 5th Year)

Theme two: Improving students’ professional performance and behavior through positive reinforcement

In this context, students realized the importance of professional performance in providing services and developing their personal qualities through interactions with positive role models.

Sub-theme one: Behavioral Changes Related to Clinical Service Provision

Students view the improvement of their performance in clinical service provision, influenced by positive role models, as a factor contributing to their professional identity development. This includes providing better services, for instance, by collaborating and consulting with experts when necessary to improve interpersonal relationships. One participant mentioned:

“There was a case where a patient had undergone multiple surgeries, the wound became infected, requiring another surgery. The previous surgeon had not consulted, but our professor despite his experience consulted with two other attending physicians, discussed the patient’s clinical condition, and they reached a consensus. The patient improved and discharged. The professor said that whenever we had even the slightest doubt, we should consult to provide the best possible care. It was at that moment that I truly understood the importance of consultation. From then on, even for the smallest tasks, we made it a point to consult with others. This was a professional behavior on the part of the professor that helped me improve the quality of care I provided to my patient” (Participant 9 - Male − 6th Year)

Sub-theme two: Behavioral changes related to personal development

Students expressed that their professional identity development was influenced by changes in their clinical service provision and related to their personal growth and enhancement. For instance, a professional must dedicate sufficient time to keep up with the latest knowledge. Regarding being an up-to-date doctor, one participant believed that:

“During my time in clinical settings, I internalized through the guidance of positive role models that being a professional in my field requires a continuous pursuit of up-to-date medical knowledge, rather than relying solely on university coursework.” (Participant 2 - Female − 7th Year)

Theme three: Instilling positive feelings and affections in students

Students experience various feelings and Affections when exposed to different situations and role models, which can impact their performance.

Sub-theme one: Positive feelings and affections instilled from interactions with patients and their families

One key factor in professional identity development is instilling feelings and affection through interactions with role models. These experiences can significantly impact students. When students engage with these role models, they often feel happiness, satisfaction, usefulness, surprise, and a sense of value. One of the participants expressed his sense of value as follows:

“I undoubtedly felt a profound sense of worth at that moment, thanks to the instructor who allowed me, for the first time, to independently complete the entire process of patient examination and history-taking under their supervision. Unlike some other instructors, they did not belittle me for being a junior student with limited experience, but instead instilled in me a sense of value within the community and a deeper appreciation for understanding and addressing patients’ suffering.” (Participant 12 - Female − 4th Year)

Sub-theme two: Positive feelings and affections instilled by educational interactions

Students’ interactions with positive role models and their observations of their behavior and performance in educational settings stimulate their emotions in ways that contribute to the growth and development of their professional identity. One example of this that is considered a platform for growth and development is the role models’ determination to achieve their professional goals despite numerous obstacles. One participant reflected:

“The first thing I felt when encountering the professor was a sense of pride. Despite all the problems and unfortunate events in his life, these people fought for his professional goals and remained strong amid life’s turmoil. The professor was so determined to achieve his goals that he is now unique in their field and specialty.” (Participant 9 - Male – 6th Year)

Theme four: Triggering negative reactions and feelings in students

Similar to encounters with positive role models, interactions with negative role models also trigger negative feelings and reactions from students.

Sub-theme one: Arousal of negative feelings and reactions resulting from interactions with patients and families

Experiencing feelings such as anger, frustration, and disgust from a role model’s behavior toward a patient and their family can lead students to understand that the situation could have been managed differently, avoiding these negative feelings and prompting them to avoid similar behaviors. One participant described his reaction to a lack of empathy from a negative role model with a patient:

“In that situation, I undoubtedly felt anger, frustration, and even resentment toward the instructor who showed no concern for the patient’s suffering or the emotional distress of the patient’s family. There was a complete lack of empathy, as he neither placed himself in the family’s position nor made any effort to acknowledge their pain. He also ignored our questions and paid no attention to us as students.“(Participant 8 - Female – 6th Year)

Sub-theme two: Arousal of negative feelings and reactions resulting from interactions in the professional community

Students also experienced negative feelings due to interactions with negative role models within the community of practice. These feelings and experiences led them to avoid repeating such behaviors and conditions. One participant described his feelings about a negative role model’s failure to establish effective communication with students:

“Similarly, seeing that we were standing there without receiving any explanation or answers to our questions as if we were invisible made me feel ignored. The instructor seemed unable to communicate effectively with us or even with other staff members, which led to feelings of frustration, uselessness, worthlessness, and a sense of being invalidated.” (Participant 13 - Male – 4th Year)

Theme five: Changing students’ avoidance performance

Based on students’ experiences with negative role models, they admitted to changing their behavior.

Sub-theme one: Changing avoidance performance related to professional behaviors

After encountering the performance and behaviors of negative role models in the professional environment and the consequences of these behaviors, students try to avoid repeating such actions and to change them in their professional practice. Regarding this, one participant described his effort to avoid shirking responsibilities:

“To be honest, we had many professors. I remember that they were supposed to come to the clinic but didn’t, even though it was their duty to come and teach. Week after week, they sent a newly graduated or resident instead. We inferred they worked in private practice during that time, which felt like neglecting their duties, seeing how avoiding responsibility led to patient dissatisfaction and lost learning opportunities, I decided to either fully commit to one workplace or organize my schedule carefully to meet all responsibilities and maintain the satisfaction of both patients and colleagues

“(Participant 1 - Male – 7th Year).

Sub-theme two: Changing avoidant performance associated with clinical service provision

Through their clinical experiences, medical students recognized the need to alter their behaviors and avoid repeating harmful practices observed by role models. One participant described her effort to prevent normalizing critical patient conditions after observing such behavior from negative role models:

“I learned always to pay attention to the patients’ companions because they are in critical condition. Even though we see these things often and might consider them normal. We need to communicate with each patient and their companions in a manner that is appropriate to their situation to ensure they feel at ease and satisfied with our care. I learned this from observing that professor, who misbehaved with all patients and their companions. “(Participant 16 - Female – 5th Year)

Theme six: Stimulating students’ reflections on their professional beliefs and views

Reflection on behaviors, beliefs, and views can be an effective stimulus for avoiding incorrect practices and misconceptions about the profession and personal future.

Sub-theme one: Stimulating reflections on previous beliefs

Due to interacting with negative role models and the situations they encountered, students reflecting on their previous views about the profession and the medical field plays a role in their professional identity development. Regarding the correction of prior misconceptions, one participant said:

“When I saw that professor, I realized that it is possible to be a compassionate doctor. We always had professors who said doctors had to be cold-hearted to survive, but this professor changed that perspective for me, and I corrected the wrong view I had learned from the previous professor” (Participant 3 - Male – 5th Year)

Sub-theme two: Stimulating reflections on current beliefs/views

Students reflect on and reconsider their beliefs when exposed to extreme, negative views of the medical profession and field presented by negative role models. In such moments, they decide not to accept these views. One participant discussed his rejection of extreme views:

“One of our professors was very harsh in consulting a patient with a heart problem, accusing the patient of wanting to take the surgery away from me and then taking me to court and complaining about me. They kept saying it’s terrible for a doctor to go to court. This created a negative atmosphere for the patient and me. They were disrespectful and instilled their own extreme beliefs in us. This created frightening beliefs in us. But I realized such a possibility doesn’t exist, so I discarded that view” (Participant 10 - Male – 5th Year)

Sub-theme three: Stimulating reflections for changing future beliefs/views

Some students reported that their exposure to negative role models and their behaviors and views changed their perspectives on their future careers. They decided to avoid having negative opinions about their profession in the future. For instance, one participant discussed her decision to prevent discrimination based on social status:

“Unfortunately, some professors exhibited discrimination in their behavior towards patients based on their social status and repeatedly did this. This view is not good, favoring one person over another just because of their job. What happens to humanity? I hope other students avoid such views” (Participant 5 - Female – 6th Year)

Discussion

The findings of this study provide meaningful insights into how medical students experience the influence of role models on their professional identity development. Encounters with positive role models were found to enhance students’ understanding of the importance of aligning personal and professional behaviors, emphasizing that becoming a doctor entails more than acquiring competencies [40], which requires embodying the norms and values of the medical profession according to the Communities of Practice theory, which emphasizes that professional learning occurs through participation in real clinical communities. Moreover, learning environments and communities are essential for professional identity formation, as learners construct their professional values and behaviors through every day clinical experiences, interactions with role models, and participation in the healthcare community [41].

Through positive reinforcement, Positive role models also contributed to improvements in students’ professional performance and behavior. Students internalized behaviors that led to satisfaction among service recipients and were motivated to refine their future professional conduct accordingly. These observations strengthened the feedback loop between behavior and reflection, encouraging students to envision and strive for successful careers [18].

Another critical dimension highlighted was the role of positive emotions instilled through encounters with exemplary role models. Students sought to recreate the positive emotional states associated with professionalism [43], understanding that motivation and enthusiasm are foundational pillars for professional growth and long-term commitment to the medical field [44]. In contrast, encounters with negative role models activated different mechanisms [45]. Students’ experiences of negative feelings prompted conscious avoidance strategies; they recognized that imitating unprofessional behaviors yielded no benefits, either clinically or professionally. Thus, students intentionally internalized the opposite behaviors from the negative role models during the impressionability process [46]. Aligning with Bandura’s social learning theory, which emphasizes the influence of environmental factors on professional development and proposes that learning occurs not only through imitation but also through observing the consequences of others’ behavior [47]. Moreover, negative role models prompted students to reflect on their existing professional beliefs and values. Confronting unethical behaviors encouraged critical examination and, when necessary, revision of previously held views [35, 48, 49]. This reflective process, consistent with Kegan’s constructivist theory, plays a vital role in fostering professional identity development, recognizing that growth occurs at different paces for different individuals [35, 48–50]. In addition, the reflective process facilitates professional identity by promoting self-awareness, critical reflection, and the reconstruction of professional roles among health professions [51].

Taken together, these themes illustrate a dynamic and reciprocal relationship between emotional responses, reflective practices, and identity construction. Positive role models inspire students to internalize desired attributes, whereas negative role models catalyze reflective rejection of undesired traits, both ultimately contributing to professional identity formation.

Educational and research implications

These findings underscore the need for curriculum reforms that deliberately cultivate positive role-modeling environments and foster students’ reflective capacities. Faculty development programs emphasizing role modeling awareness, alongside student empowerment initiatives aimed at strengthening critical reflection skills in the face of conflicting professional values by observing negative role models, are vital strategies to support intentional, resilient professional identity development among future physicians. The results can help policymakers improve the criteria for selecting and evaluating teachers, emphasizing characteristics that contribute to the positive formation of students’ professional identities.

Future research directions

This study can serve as a foundation for further research on the role of teacher-student relationships in the formation of professional identity. Moreover, future studies may delve into how role models shape professional identity among medical students, not only in terms of outcomes but also in the process. In addition, designing and testing reflective training programs can help students critically navigate negative modeling, offering practical strategies to support identity development in real clinical settings.

Strength and limitations

Interviews were conducted with students who were exposed to role models, which can enrich the information received beyond the mere understanding of their views. Although the sample size (n = 16) was sufficient for a phenomenological study and to achieve information power and data sufficiency, the findings should be interpreted in light of the specific educational and cultural context in which the study was conducted. As with most qualitative studies, the aim was to provide an in-depth understanding of participants’ lived experiences rather than achieve statistical generalization. Moreover, some participants were initially reluctant to discuss their experiences with negative role models because of concerns about confidentiality and possible consequences. To enhance the trustworthiness of the findings despite these challenges, established qualitative strategies were employed throughout the study, as described in the Trustworthiness section. In addition, future studies involving multiple medical schools and diverse cultural contexts may further extend the transferability of these findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicated that medical students pattern their actions, behaviors, feelings, and viewpoints in ways that positively strengthen and develop their professional identity when explaining their experiences with positive and negative role models. Emotional engagement is evident in students’ interactions with both positive and negative role models during the development of their professional identity; however, divergences in approach arise in other aspects of this process. Students adopt any action, belief, behavior, or norm that leads to success in their profession, patient satisfaction, and the achievement of their goals to grow and develop their identity. They inherently reflect on their encounters with negative role models to find incorrect paths and act contrary to those models. They contemplate behaviors and feelings that deviate from professional norms and strive not to internalize them within their professional values.
