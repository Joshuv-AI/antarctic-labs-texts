The Aronson-Bénilan estimate for a Lagrangian particle discretization of the Porous Medium Equation
Marco Di Francesco, Daniel Matthes

Abstract
. We consider a nearest neighbor, Lagrangian particle discretization of the one dimensional porous medium equation. We prove that the particle model satisfies a discrete analog of the celebrated Aronson-Bénilan estimate, which we use to prove a growth estimate for the evolution of the support and an L ∞ L^{\infty} decay estimate which are both known to hold in the continuum. These estimates are uniform with respect to the number of particles. We also prove convergence of the scheme towards the solution to the porous medium equation in the full generality of L 1 L^{1} initial data.

1.
Introduction

The porous medium equation

(1)

\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta\rho^{m}\,,\qquad m>1

is a classical model describing the filtration of an isentropic gas through a porous medium
[
5
]
, or the evolution of temperature in radiating media
[
33
]
. More recently, it has been used in population dynamics as well, to describe phenomena of nonlinear diffusion or local repulsion between interacting agents e.g. in swarms
[
22
]
. In (
1
),
\rho=\rho(t,x)
represents the (nonnegative) density of a continuum medium, depending on a space variable
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and time
t\geq 0
.
Unlike its
“linear closest relative”
, the heat equation, which features solutions supported on the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
regardless of the initial datum, the porous medium equation features a finite-speed growth of the support, a property that makes it more preferable to its linear counterpart in many applications. The mathematical theory of the porous medium equation (
1
) has seen numerous and significant contributions since the 1980s. Far from claiming to be exhaustive, we cite here
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
9
,
17
,
28
,
29
,
25
,
11
,
24
]
and refer to the monographs
[
32
]
and
[
31
]
for a more comprehensive analysis.

Mostly motivated by applications in numerical analysis and sampling methods, but also by the need of detecting (
1
) as a
“good approximation”
of a discrete system of interacting agents in some contexts, increasing attention has recently been devoted to the approximation of nonlinear transport/diffusion models via (a suitable combination/interpolation of) finitely many
moving particles
, obeying a suitable system of ODEs. Such a system is typically obtained as the discretization of the Lagrangian formulation of the underlying model. More specifically for (
1
) regarded as a continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho v)=0\,,\qquad v=-\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla\rho^{m-1}\,,

the moving particles
[0,+\infty)\ni t\mapsto x(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
should somehow satisfy a law that approximates

(2)

\dot{x}(t)=v(x(t),t)\,.

The initial data for the particles’ positions are computed after a
sampling
, or
atomization
of the initial datum
\overline{\rho}
of the Cauchy problem for (
1
). Based on the positions of the particles at time
t>0
, an approximate moving density is constructed, with the goal of proving that it is close to the solutions to (
1
) with
\overline{\rho}
as initial condition in a suitable topology. Since the approximation is constructed by the law (
2
) following the path of
“material particles”
(with a terminology borrowed from classical fluid mechanics), this whole procedure is also referred to as
Lagrangian
particles approximation. As particles obey a deterministic law, the above approximation procedure is often called Deterministic Particle Approximation (DPA).

A common DPA for (
1
) is obtained by suitably convolving the above velocity field
v
, for example by considering
v_{\varepsilon}=\frac{m}{m-1}\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho^{m-1}
with
K_{\varepsilon}
a suitable symmetric, often smooth kernel, and
\varepsilon>0
a scaling parameter vanishing as the number of particles tends to infinity, with
K_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\delta_{0}
in some measure sense as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, see for example
[
23
]
for the stochastic case,
[
10
,
8
,
15
]
for the fully deterministic one. This method, often referred to as
“blob method”
, while it has the clear advantage of easing the definition of the ODE system for the moving particles (in that the velocity field may be calculated pointwise even if
\rho
is discontinuous, provided
K_{\varepsilon}
is smooth enough), essentially turns the diffusion operator in (
1
) into a nonlocal transport operator, thus drastically changing the structure of the particles’ dynamics in the case of initial data with
“high concentrations”
. Such an issue is reflected in the particles ODEs of a blob method, in which every particles actually interacts with any other particle, although with weights that vanish in the limit for particles very far from each other.

A perhaps more natural way of reproducing (
1
) at the level of Lagrangian particles and maintaining, at the same time, the features of a local model, is to restrict to
nearest neighbor
interactions, that is, to allow every particle to interact only with
“nearby”
particles. Clearly, the feasibility of this method is highly dependent on the spatial dimension. In one space dimension, taking
N
ordered particles with positions
x^{(N)}_{0}<x^{(N)}_{1}<\ldots<x^{(N)}_{N}
, the nearest neighbor particles of a given
x^{(N)}_{i}
are naturally identified with
x^{(N)}_{i-1}
and
x^{(N)}_{i+1}
. Following the seminal idea of
[
27
]
, in which a discrete analog of the velocity in (
2
) was identified (and referred to as
“osmotic velocity”
) for the linear diffusion equation, a nearest neighbor interaction approximation for the one-dimensional (
1
) was considered in
[
20
]
, which investigated the approximation problem mainly from a numerical point of view. The corresponding particle system reads

(3)

\dot{x}^{(N)}_{i}(t)=-N(R^{(N)}_{i+1}(t)^{m}-R^{(N)}_{i}(t)^{m})\,,\qquad i\in\{0,\ldots,N\}

where

R^{(N)}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{N(x^{(N)}_{i}(t)-x^{(N)}_{i-1}(t))}\,,\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,.

Here,
x^{(N)}_{i}(t)
with
i\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
are
N+1
ordered particles, and
R^{(N)}_{0}(t)=R^{(N)}_{N+1}(t)=0
by convention. We shall provide an informal justification for (
3
) in Subsections
2.1
and
2.5
. In this paper, as well as in other papers on similar subjects (see e.g.
[
16
]
for scalar conservation laws), the following discrete reconstruction of the density

(4)

\rho^{(N)}(t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}R^{(N)}_{i}(t)\mathbf{1}_{[x^{(N)}_{i-1}(t),x^{(N)}_{i}(t))}(x)

is used for comparison with the solution
\rho
to the continuum model (
1
).

Rigorous results on the convergence of
\rho^{N}
towards weak solutions to

(5)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=(\rho^{m})_{xx}

were obtained for the first time in
[
21
]
for a more general class of
1d
models which includes (
1
) as a special case. Further results have been obtained in
[
19
,
14
,
13
]
for aggregation-diffusion equations. While the above particle scheme (
3
) has the advantage of reproducing, at the discrete particle level, the variational formulation of (
1
) in the Wasserstein gradient flow setting of
[
24
,
1
]
, so far the identification of the limit as a weak solution to (
1
) has been possible only in frameworks in which, simplistically speaking, the solution to (
1
) is far from zero. Such an issue is due to the difficulty encountered so far in controlling the speed of propagation of the support of the approximating density reconstruction
\rho^{N}
defined in (
4
)
uniformly w.r.t. the number of particles.

Partly related to that, the particle scheme (
3
) has never been proven, so far, to satisfy a discrete counterpart of any of the
regularizing effects
that characterize the porous medium equation, the most celebrated one being the Aronson-Bénilan fundamental estimate
[
2
]
(see also
[
32
, Proposition 9.4]
), which in the one-dimensional case states that, for a given nonnegative initial condition in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, at any
t>0
the profile
\rho(t,\cdot)
solving (
5
) is positive and smooth inside its support,
and satisfies there

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{xx}\left(\frac{\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)\geq-\frac{1}{m(m+1)t}.

The estimate (
6
) is one of the key tools in the analysis of non-negative finite mass solutions
\rho

to (
5
). In fact, an existence theory of
L^{1}
solutions to (
5
) can be carried out mostly from (
6
), which provides higher order estimates needed to ensure compactness. The inequality (
6
) is sharp, and is saturated for the self-similar solution
\hat{\rho}
to (
5
),
given by

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t,x)=t^{-1/(m+1)}B\big(t^{-1/(m+1)}x\big)\quad\text{with}\quad B(\xi)=\left(C_{m}^{m-1}-\frac{m-1}{2m(m+1)}\xi^{2}\right)_{+}^{1/(m-1)},

where
C_{m}>0
is a mass parameter. On grounds of (
6
) and elementary properties like mass conservation alone,
one can easily deduce a variety of qualitative features of solutions. One, which applies for compactly supported initial data, is the upper bound on the measure of the support at time
t>0

(8)

\mathrm{meas}(\mathrm{supp}(\rho(\cdot,t)))\leq a+bt^{\frac{1}{m+1}}

for some constants
a,b>0
depending on the initial support and on
m
, see for example
[
30
]
. Another one is the uniform in time decay of
\rho(t,\cdot)
,

(9)

\displaystyle\max_{x}\rho(t,x)\leq C_{m}t^{-1/(m+1)}\quad\text{for all $t>0$},

where
C_{m}
is the mass parameter from (
7
) above, see
[
32
, Section 9.4]
. Both bounds (
8
) and (
9
) are sharp, as they are saturated by the self-similar solution
\hat{\rho}
in (
7
).

The main goal of the paper at hand is to replicate the Aronson-Bénilan estimate (
6
), as well as its consequences (
8
) and (
9
),
for the discrete density
\rho^{N}
defined in (
4
) out of the
particle discretization (
3
) of the PME (
5
).
Moreover, we use our discrete version of (
6
) to show rigorous convergence of the density (
4
) constructed from the scheme (
3
) towards the weak solution to (
5
) in the full generality of
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
initial data. The main conclusion of this paper is that a discrete version of (
6
) alone is enough to prove convergence of the scheme (
3
) towards (
5
) as well as to replicate qualitative properties such as (
8
) and (
9
). The concept of solution to (
5
) we consider here is that of distributional solutions in
L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,+\infty)\,;\,L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}))
with distributional initial trace in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, for which uniqueness holds, see
[
25
]
.

The paper is structured as follows.

•

In Section
2
we introduce the main concepts, provide a heuristic derivation of the Aronson-Bénilan estimate (
6
), set up our particle scheme and our main assumptions, and state our results in Subsection
2.6
. Our main results are collected in Theorems
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
below.

•

In Section
3
we prove some basic properties of the particle scheme and provide the rigorous proof of the discrete Aronson-Bénilan estimate (that is, Theorem
1
).

•

In Section

2
on the uniform growth estimate of the support.

•

In Section

we prove the uniform

decay property of Theorem

.

•

Finally, in Section

4
.

We conclude this introductory section setting some ground rules on notations. All
N
-depending approximating quantities will carry their dependence on
N
in different ways:

•

N
-depending
spaces
of vectors/functions will be denoted with
N
as a subscript, with the only exception of the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

•

N
-depending
maps
defined on linear spaces, or functional spaces, are denoted with
N
as a superscript (e.g. a sampling map
\mathbf{S}^{N}
).

•

Finite dimensional vectors, for example in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
, are denoted in general as
X=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{N})
, with upper case letters to denoted the vector, lower case ones to denote their components. No dependence on the dimension is encoded in the notation. However, when dealing with
solutions to a particle scheme
, their dependence on
N
is (often) emphasized with the superscript notation
X^{(N)}
.

2.
Preliminary concepts and statement of the main results

2.1.
Probability measures, pseudo-inverses, Lagrangian formulation

Let
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})
be the set of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
. For a given
\rho\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})
, we define the cumulative distribution function of
\rho
, denoted by
F_{\rho}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow[0,1]
, as

F_{\rho}(x)=\rho((-\infty,x])\,,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,

and its right-continuous pseudo inverse
X_{\rho}:(0,1)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

(10)

X_{\rho}(z)=\inf\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\,F_{\rho}(x)>z\right\}\,.

Given

\mathcal{K}=\left\{X:(0,1)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\,\hbox{$X$ is right continuous and non-decreasing}\right\}

the map
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})\rightarrow\mathcal{K}
defined by

(11)

\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})\ni\rho\mapsto\mathcal{T}[\rho]=X_{\rho}\in\mathcal{K}

is a bijection. Assuming the necessary regularity for a solution
\rho
to (
5
), a Lagrangian formulation satisfied by the function

(12)

X(t,z)=X_{\rho(t,\cdot)}(z)=\mathcal{T}[\rho(t,\cdot)](z)\,,

can be recovered (see e.g.
[
12
]
), namely

(13)

\partial_{t}X(z,t)=-\partial_{z}\left(\frac{1}{\partial_{z}X(z,t)}\right)\,.

More precisely, (
13
) follows rigorously by assuming
\rho
solves (
5
), with
\rho
smooth on its support and and the set
\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\,\rho(x,t)>0\right\}
being a connected interval for all
t>0
.

For future use, we recall that the
1
-Wasserstein distance between two probability measures
\rho,\eta\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})
can be computed as

(14)

d_{1}(\rho,\eta)=\|\mathcal{T}[\rho]-\mathcal{T}[\eta]\|_{L^{1}((0,1))}\,.

2.2.
Lagrangian version of the Aronson-Bénilan estimate

The Lagrangian formulation (
13
) of (
5
) appears to be particularly well-adapted to proving, at least formally, the estimate (
6
). Assuming the necessary regularity on a solution
\rho
to (
5
) and unit mass for simplicity, let
X
be defined as in (
12
). Let

(15)

R(t,z)=\rho(t,X(t,z))=\frac{1}{\partial_{z}X(t,z)}\,,

which is finite for all
(t,z)\in(0,+\infty)\times(0,1)
due to the regularity of
\rho
and assuming that the support of
\rho(\cdot,t)
is a connected interval. Formally, thanks to (
13
),
R
satisfies

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R(t,z)=R(t,z)^{2}\partial^{2}_{zz}(R(t,z)^{m}).

Now rewrite the second derivative on the left-hand side of (
6
) with the help of (
15
). Since

\displaystyle\frac{m\rho^{m-1}(t,X(t,z))}{m-1}=\frac{mR^{m-1}(t,z)}{m-1},

we obtain for the first derivative

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)_{x}\circ X\,X_{z}=mR^{m-2}R_{z}\,,

which implies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)_{x}\circ X=(R^{m})_{z}.

Accordingly, for the second derivative we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)_{xx}\circ XX_{z}=(R^{m})_{zz}\,,

which implies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)_{xx}\circ X=R(R^{m})_{zz}.

Thus, the quantity of interest in (
6
) in Lagrangian variables is

(17)

\displaystyle Z(t,z):=R(t,z)(R^{m}(t,z))_{zz}.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a sufficiently smooth solution to the Lagrangian formulation (
16
) of the porous medium equation. Then the function
Z
defined in (
17
) satisfies

(18)

\displaystyle Z(t,z)\geq-\frac{1}{(m+1)t}.

The meaning of
“sufficiently smooth”
above is intentionally vague. For example, the role of boundary conditions is neglected. In any case, the proof below is just for illustration of the principle, which will be made fully rigorous for spatially discrete solutions later.

Proof.

For the time derivative of
Z
, we obtain by means of (
16
) the expression

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Z

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}R\,(R^{m})_{zz}+mR(R^{m-1}\,\partial_{t}R)_{zz}

\displaystyle=\big[R(R^{m})_{zz}\big]^{2}+mR\big(R^{m+1}(R^{m})_{zz}\big)_{zz}

\displaystyle=Z^{2}+mR(R^{m}Z)_{zz}

\displaystyle=(m+1)Z^{2}+2mR(R^{m-1})_{z}Z_{z}+mR^{m}Z_{zz}.

Now, at some fixed time
t>0
, consider a minimal point
\xi^{*}
of
Z
. Then
Z_{z}(t,\xi^{*})=0
and
Z_{zz}(t,\xi^{*})\geq 0
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Z(t,\xi^{*})\geq(m+1)Z(t,\xi^{*})^{2}.

In other words, the minimum
f(t):=\min_{\xi}Z(t,\xi)
of
Z
satisfies the ODE

(19)

\displaystyle\dot{f}\geq(m+1)f^{2},

which implies, with a simple ODE argument after separating the variables
f
and
t
,

(20)

\displaystyle f(t)\geq-\frac{1}{(m+1)t},

which yields (
18
).
∎

2.3.
Main assumptions and definition of the particle scheme

Throughout the whole paper we shall assume the initial condition
\overline{\rho}
satisfies

(21)

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}).

Fix a number
N\in\mathbb{N}
of intervals, each carrying a mass of
1/N
.
Elements
X
in the convex cone

\mathcal{K}_{N}=\left\{X=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\,:\,\,x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}\ \text{for all $i=0,\ldots,N-1$}\right\}\,,

determine the
N+1
end points of the intervals.
The total length
x_{N}-x_{0}
of the collection of intervals is denoted by

(22)

\mathcal{L}^{N}[X]=x_{N}-x_{0}\,.

We think as the mass portions on
1/N
as uniformly distributed on each interval, i.e.,
the interval
(x_{i-1},x_{i})
carries a constant spatial density of
1/[N(x_{i}-x_{i-1})]
.
These
N
density values are summarized by
the
reconstruction map

\mathcal{R}^{N}:\mathcal{K}_{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{N}[X]=\big(\mathcal{R}^{N}_{1}[X],\ldots,\mathcal{R}^{N}_{N}[X]\big)

\displaystyle\ \text{with}\quad\mathcal{R}^{N}_{i}[X]=\frac{1}{N(x_{i}-x_{i-1})}\,,\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,.

where
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{N}
denotes the cone of
N
-vectors with non-negative entries.
Alternatively, the mass distribution can be reconstructed
as a piecewise constant interpolation
\mathfrak{R}^{N}:\mathcal{K}_{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})
:

(23)

\mathfrak{R}^{N}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{R}^{N}_{i}[X]\mathbf{1}_{[x_{i-1},x_{i})}\,.

For later reference, we further define
\mathcal{D}^{N}:\mathcal{K}_{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{N}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{N}[X]=\big(\mathcal{D}^{N}_{1}[X],\ldots,\mathcal{D}^{N}_{N}[X]\big)\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{D}^{N}_{i}[X]=(x_{i}-x_{i-1}),\,\quad X=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{K}_{N}\,,

which implies
\mathcal{R}^{N}_{i}[X]=1/(N\,\mathcal{D}^{N}_{i}[X])
.

By a
sampling map
, we mean an association of any absolutely continuous probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}

to an ordered
(N+1)
-vector of nonoverlapping particle positions,

(24)

\mathbf{S}^{N}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{N}^{\circ}\,,\quad\mathbf{S}^{N}[\rho]=\left(\mathbf{S}^{N}_{0}[\rho],\ldots,\mathbf{S}^{N}_{N}[\rho]\right)

with the approximation property

(25)

\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\varphi\left(\mathbf{S}^{N}_{i}[\rho]\right)\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\rho(x)dx\qquad\hbox{as $N\rightarrow+\infty$, for all $\varphi\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})$}\,.

In some cases we shall also require the additional assumption

(26)

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{N}[\mathbf{S}^{N}[\rho]]^{2}}{N}=0\,,\qquad\hbox{for all $\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$}\,.

There are a variety of possible sampling maps with the above properties.
A frequently used one, which we denote by
\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*}
,
applies to compactly supported measures
\rho\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and is defined as follows.
First set

(27)

\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*,0}[\rho]=\inf{\mathrm{supp}(\rho)},

and then recursively for
i=1,\ldots,N
:

(28)

\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*,i}[\rho]=\inf\left\{x\geq\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*,i-1}[\rho]\,:\,\,\int_{\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*,i-1}[\rho]}^{x}d\rho\geq\frac{1}{N}\right\}\,.

We observe that
\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*}[\rho]\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ}
and that
\mathbf{S}^{N}_{*,N}[\rho]=\sup\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
.
We shall refer to it as
support preserving sampling
.

We are now ready to define our approximating scheme.
Let
\overline{\rho}
satisfy (
21
) and assume
\mathbf{S}^{N}
is a given sampling map.
Set

(29)

\overline{X}^{(N)}=(\overline{x}^{(N)}_{0},\ldots,\overline{x}^{(N)}_{N})=\mathbf{S}^{N}[\overline{\rho}]\in\mathcal{K}_{N}

and define the curve

[0,+\infty)\ni t\mapsto X^{(N)}(t)=(x^{(N)}_{0}(t),\ldots,x^{(N)}_{N}(t))\in\mathcal{K}_{N}

as the unique, local-in-time solution to the Cauchy problem

(30)

\begin{cases}\dot{x}^{(N)}_{i}(t)=-N(R^{(N)}_{i+1}(t)^{m}-R^{(N)}_{i}(t)^{m})&i=1,\ldots,N-1\\
\dot{x}^{(N)}_{0}(t)=-NR^{(N)}_{1}(t)^{m}&\\
\dot{x}^{(N)}_{N}(t)=NR^{(N)}_{N}(t)^{m}&\\
x^{(N)}(0)=\overline{X}^{(N)}\,,&\end{cases}

where we denoted
R^{(N)}(t)=(R^{(N)}_{1}(t),\ldots,R^{(N)}_{N}(t))
the vector
of mass densities on the time-dependent intervals,

(31)

R^{(N)}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{R}_{i}[X^{(N)}(t)]=\frac{1}{N(x^{(N)}_{i}(t)-x^{(N)}_{i-1}(t))}\,,\qquad i=1,\ldots,N\,.

With the convention that

(32)

\displaystyle R^{(N)}_{0}(t)=R^{(N)}_{N+1}(t)=0,

the first evolution equation in (
30
) actually holds for all
N+1
points, i.e., from
i=0
to
i=N
.
We will also use the vector
d^{(N)}(t)=(d^{(N)}_{1}(t),\ldots,d^{(N)}_{N}(t))
of interval lengths, given by

(33)

d^{(N)}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{D}^{N}_{i}[X^{(N)}(t)]=(x^{(N)}_{i}(t)-x^{(N)}_{i-1}(t))\,,\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,,

We finally introduce the quantity that is supposed to approximate the solution
\rho
to (
5
) for large
N
, namely

(34)

\rho^{(N)}(t,x)=\mathfrak{R}^{N}[X(t)]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}R^{(N)}_{i}(t)\mathbf{1}_{[x^{(N)}_{i-1}(t),x^{(N)}_{i}(t))}(x)\,.

2.4.
Finite difference operators

For vectors
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, we define
the forward/backward difference quotients
\operatorname{D}^{+}F
/
\operatorname{D}^{-}F
and the discrete Laplacian
\Delta F
in the usual way,

\displaystyle\operatorname{D}^{+}_{k}F=N(F_{k+1}-F_{k}),\quad\operatorname{D}^{-}_{k}F=N(F_{k}-F_{k-1}),\quad\Delta_{k}F=N^{2}(F_{k+1}+F_{k-1}-2F_{k})=\operatorname{D}^{-}_{k}\operatorname{D}^{+}F,

for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
where
F_{j}
for indices
j
outside of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
is interpreted as zero
(so, for example,
\operatorname{D}^{-}_{1}F=NF_{1}
,
\Delta_{N}F=N^{2}(F_{N-1}-2F_{N})
,
\Delta_{0}F=N^{2}F_{1}
).
This definition automatically implies the following summation by parts rule for arbitrary
F,G\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
:

(35)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}G_{k}\Delta_{k}F=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\operatorname{D}^{+}_{k}F\operatorname{D}^{+}_{k}G.

We also introduce the notation

F^{\alpha}=(F_{0}^{\alpha},\ldots,F_{N-1}^{\alpha})

for
\alpha>0
and for vectors
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{N}
.

We observe that, with the above notation and the convention (
32
),
the evolution equations in (
30

\dot{x}^{(N)}_{i}(t)=-\operatorname{D}^{+}_{i}\mathcal{R}^{N}[X^{(N)}(t)]^{m}=-\operatorname{D}^{+}_{i}R^{(N)}(t)^{m}\,,\qquad i\in\{0,\ldots,N\}

3.
Well posedness of the scheme and proof of Theorem
1

In this section and in the next two, we will often lighten the notation and suppress the superscript
(N)
for the solution to the particle scheme (
30
) and their related variables
R_{k},d_{k},Z_{k}
. We will maintain the superscript in the statements of all the results for clarity. The ODE system (
30
) has a unique local-in-time solution, which may be proven to be global.

Proposition 2

(Global Existence and Discrete Minimum Principle)
.

Let
\overline{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\overline{X}^{(N)}
be defined by (
29
), with
\mathbf{S}^{N}
satisfying (
25
) and (
24
). Then, the Cauchy problem (
30
) with initial datum
\overline{X}^{(N)}
has a unique, global-in-time solution. Moreover, the estimate

(43)

d^{(N)}_{i}(t)\geq\overline{d}^{(N)}=\min\left\{d^{(N)}_{i}[\overline{X}^{(N)}]\,:\,\,i=1,\ldots,N\right\}

holds for all
t\geq 0
, with
d^{(N)}_{i}
defined in (
33
).

Proof.

The assumption (
24
) ensures
\overline{x}_{i}<\overline{x}_{i+1}
for all
i=0,\ldots,N-1
. Hence, from the classical Cauchy-Lipschitz theory, a unique solution
[0,T)\ni t\mapsto X(t)
exists for a sufficiently small
T>0
. We prove (
43
) by a contradiction argument. Let
t^{*}\geq 0
be the first time at which

\min_{i=1,\ldots,N}d_{i}(t^{*})=\overline{d}

and

\min_{i=1,\ldots,N}d_{i}(t^{*})<\overline{d}\qquad\hbox{on the interval $(t^{*},t^{*}+\varepsilon)$}

for some
\varepsilon>0
. By possibly choosing a smaller
\varepsilon
, there exists an index
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
such that

d_{i_{0}}(t)=\min_{i=1,\ldots,N}d_{i}(t)

for
t\in[t^{*},t^{*}+\varepsilon)
, which implies for
t\in(t^{*},t^{*}+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle d_{i_{0}}(t)=\overline{d}+\int_{t^{*}}^{t}\left(\dot{x}_{i_{0}}(s)-\dot{x}_{i_{0}-1}(s)\right)ds=\overline{d}-N\int_{t^{*}}^{t}\left(R_{i_{0}+1}(s)^{m}+R_{i_{0}-1}(s)^{m}-2R_{i_{0}}(s)^{m}\right)ds,

using the convention (
32
).
Now, for
t\in[t^{*},t^{*}+\varepsilon)
we have

R_{i_{0}}(t)=\max_{i=1,\ldots,N}R_{i}(t)\geq\max\left\{R_{i_{0}-1}(t),R_{i_{0}+1}(t)\right\}\,,

which implies

d_{i_{0}}(t)\geq\overline{d}\qquad\hbox{on $t\in(t^{*},t^{*}+\varepsilon)$}\,,

which is a contradiction. We have therefore proven (
43
) on the short-time existence interval
[0,T)
. Global existence then follows by a standard continuation argument, and (
43
) follows for all times.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
2
, the approximated density
\rho^{(N)}
in (
34
)
is defined globally in time. From (
30
) we easily obtain the ODE system for the discrete densities
R^{(N)}_{k}(t)=R_{k}(t)

(44)

\dot{R}_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}N^{2}R_{1}(t)^{2}(R_{2}^{m}(t)-2R_{1}^{m}(t))&\hbox{if $i=1$}\\
N^{2}R_{i}(t)^{2}\left(R_{i+1}^{m}(t)+R_{i-1}^{m}(t)-2R_{i}^{m}(t)\right)&\hbox{if $i\in\{2,\ldots,N-1$\lx@text@rbrace}\\
N^{2}R_{N}(t)^{2}(R_{N-1}^{m}(t)-2R_{N}^{m}(t))&\hbox{if $i=N$}\end{cases}

which may be re-written as

(45)

\dot{R}_{i}(t)=R_{i}(t)^{2}\Delta_{i}[R^{m}(t)]=R_{i}(t)Z_{i}(t)\,,\qquad i\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\,.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The inequality (
38
) follows from Lemma
1
.
Differentiating
Z_{k}(t)
in time, recalling the convention (
32
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle=\dot{R}_{k}\,\Delta_{k}R^{m}+N^{2}mR_{k}\big(R_{k+1}^{m-1}\dot{R}_{k+1}+R_{k-1}^{m-1}\dot{R}_{k-1}-2R_{k}^{m-1}\dot{R}_{k})

\displaystyle=\big[R_{k}\Delta R_{k}^{m}\big]^{2}+N^{2}mR_{k}\big(R_{k+1}^{m+1}\Delta_{k+1}R^{m}+R_{k-1}^{m+1}\Delta_{k-1}R^{m}-2R_{k}^{m+1}\Delta_{k}R^{m}\big)

\displaystyle=Z_{k}^{2}+N^{2}mR_{k}\big(R_{k+1}^{m}Z_{k+1}+R_{k-1}^{m}Z_{k-1}-2R_{k}^{m}Z_{k}\big)\,.

Fix
t>0
and assume that
Z_{k}(t)
attains its minimal value at
k=k^{*}
on the interval
[t,t+\varepsilon)
. In particular,
Z_{k^{*}+1}(t)\geq Z_{k^{*}}(t)
and
Z_{k^{*}-1}(t)\geq Z_{k^{*}}(t)
. Consequently, for all
k=0,\ldots,N-1
,

\displaystyle\dot{Z}_{k^{*}}(t)

\displaystyle\geq Z_{k^{*}}(t)^{2}+N^{2}mR_{k^{*}}(t)\big(R_{k^{*}+1}(t)^{m}+R_{k^{*}-1}(t)^{m}-2R_{k^{*}}(t)^{m}\big)Z_{k^{*}}(t)

\displaystyle=Z_{k^{*}}(t)^{2}+mR_{k^{*}}(t)\big(\Delta_{k^{*}}R(t)^{m}\big)Z_{k^{*}}(t)

\displaystyle=(m+1)Z_{k^{*}}(t)^{2}.

Hence, the function

[0,+\infty)\ni t\mapsto Z(t)=\min_{k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}}Z_{k}(t)

satisfies the differential inequality

(46)

\dot{Z}(t)\geq(m+1)Z(t)^{2}\,.

A simple comparison argument for ODEs shows that
Z(t)\geq y(t)
,
where

y(t)=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{y(0)}-(m+1)t}\,.

is the unique solution to the Cauchy problem

\dot{y}(t)=(m+1)y(t)^{2}\,,\qquad y(0)=\overline{Z}\,.

Since
y(0)<0
we get the desired inequality (
39
).
∎

4.
Uniform growth estimate of the support

In this section, we apply Theorem
1
to detect a uniform bound for the distance

\mathcal{L}^{N}[X^{(N)}(t)]=x^{(N)}_{N}(t)-x^{(N)}_{0}(t)

with respect to the number of particles
N
for some time
t\geq 0
. The sampling map
\mathcal{S}^{N}
for the initial condition
\overline{\rho}
is kept general and satisfying (
25
) and (
24
).

Proposition 3
.

For all
t\geq 0
we have

(47)

\mathcal{L}^{N}[X^{(N)}(t)]\leq\mathcal{L}^{N}[X^{(N)}(0)](1+|\overline{Z}^{(N)}|(m+1)t)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}\,.

Proof.

The ODE (
45
) and Theorem
1
imply for
i=0,\ldots,N-1

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{i}(t)\geq-\frac{R_{i}(t)}{|\overline{Z}|^{-1}+(m+1)t}\,.

By a comparison argument for ODEs,
R_{i}(t)\geq u(t)
with
u(t)
being the only solution to the Cauchy problem

\dot{u}(t)=-\frac{u(t)}{|\overline{Z}|^{-1}+(m+1)t}\,,\qquad u(0)=R_{i}(0)\,,

which has the explicit solution

u(t)=R_{i}(0)(1+|\overline{Z}|(m+1)t)^{-\frac{1}{m+1}}

which in turns implies for all
t\geq 0
and
i=0,\ldots,N-1

R_{i}(t)\geq R_{i}(0)(1+|\overline{Z}|(m+1)t)^{-\frac{1}{m+1}}\,.

Hence, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{N}[X(t)]=x_{N}(t)-x_{0}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(x_{i+1}(t)-x_{i}(t))=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}d_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\ =\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{NR_{i}(t)}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{R_{i}(0)}(1+|\overline{Z}|(m+1)t)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}d_{i}(0)(1+|\overline{Z}|(m+1)t)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}

\displaystyle\ =\mathcal{L}^{N}[X(0)](1+|\overline{Z}|(m+1)t)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}\,.

∎

The estimate in Proposition
3
is uniform with respect to
N
only under the condition that the initial quantity
\overline{Z}^{(N)}
is uniformly bounded from below with respect to
N
, which might not be the case if we are sampling an initial condition the space derivative of which has decreasing jumps. Our next goal is therefore to extend the above estimate to any initial datum satisfying (
21
). For a given particle trajectory
X^{(N)}(t)=(x^{(N)}_{0}(t),\ldots,x^{(N)}_{N}(t))
we define

\mathcal{X}_{X^{(N)}}:[0,1)\times[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\mathcal{X}_{X^{(N)}}(z,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x^{(N)}_{i}(t)\mathbf{1}_{[i/N,(i+1)/N)}(z)\,.

Given two particles trajectories
X^{(N)}(t)=(x^{(N)}_{0}(t),\ldots,x^{(N)}_{N}(t))
and
Y^{(N)}(t)=(y^{(N)}_{0}(t),\ldots,y^{(N)}_{N}(t))
and
h\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

d_{h}(X^{(N)}(t),Y^{(N)}(t))=\|\mathcal{X}_{X^{(N)}}(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{X}_{Y^{(N)}}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2h}((0,1))}

with the convention

d_{\infty}(X^{(N)}(t),Y^{(N)}(t))=\|\mathcal{X}_{X^{(N)}}(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{X}_{Y^{(N)}}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1))}\,.

We prove the following result, which is a continuous-in-time version of a similar result in
[
20
]
. Although our result differs slightly from the result in
[
20
]
, the main idea behind the proof is the same.

Proposition 4
.

The quantities
d_{h}(X^{(N)}(t),Y^{(N)}(t))
for
h\in\mathbb{N}
and
d_{\infty}(X^{(N)}(t),Y^{(N)}(t))
are non increasing in time.

Proof.

For
h
finite we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}d_{h}(X(t),Y(t))^{2h}=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathcal{X}_{X}(z,t)-\mathcal{X}_{Y}(z,t)\right)^{2h}dz=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right)^{2h}

\displaystyle\ =\frac{2h}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t))^{2h-1}(\dot{x}_{i}(t)-\dot{y}_{i}(t))

\displaystyle\ =-2h\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t))^{2h-1}\left(\left(R_{i+1}(t)^{m}-R_{i}(t)^{m}\right)-\left(S_{i+1}(t)^{m}-S_{i}(t)^{m}\right)\right)

where we are using the notation
R_{i}(t)=\mathcal{R}_{i}[X(t)]
and
S_{i}(t)=\mathcal{R}_{i}[Y(t)]
,
and adopt the convention (
32
). We now use summation by parts and get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}d_{h}(X(t),Y(t))^{2h}

\displaystyle\ =-2h\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t))^{2h-1}\left(R_{i+1}(t)^{m}-S_{i+1}(t)^{m}\right)+2h\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t))^{2h-1}\left(R_{i}(t)^{m}-S_{i}(t)^{m}\right)

\displaystyle\ =-2h\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left[(x_{i-1}(t)-y_{i-1}(t))^{2h-1}-(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t))^{2h-1}\right]\left(R_{i}(t)^{m}-S_{i}(t)^{m}\right)\,.

We claim that the last right-hand side above is non positive. Assume first
R_{i}(t)\leq S_{i}(t)
. This implies

x_{i}(t)-x_{i-1}(t)\geq y_{i}(t)-y_{i-1}(t)

which in turns implies

x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t)\geq x_{i-1}(t)-y_{i-1}(t)

and therefore

-\left[(x_{i-1}(t)-y_{i-1}(t))^{2h-1}-(x_{i}(t)-y_{i}(t))^{2h-1}\right]\left(R_{i}(t)^{m}-S_{i}(t)^{m}\right)\geq 0\,.

Similarly, if
R_{i}(t)\geq S_{i}(t)
then

x_{i}(t)-x_{i-1}(t)\leq y_{i}(t)-y_{i-1}(t)

and the claim follows. This proves the statement for
h
finite. The estimate for
d_{\infty}
follows by a standard argument in which
h\rightarrow+\infty
. We omit the details.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The idea for the remaining part of the proof is to construct for a given
N\geq 4
an
N
-particle measure
— defined in terms of its point configuration
\bar{Y}^{(N)}
and density mapping
\bar{S}^{(N)}
—
of arbitrarily short support for which
the corresponding expression
\bar{Z}
appearing in (
47
)
has a universal lower bound
-\Lambda
, i.e.,

(48)

\displaystyle S^{(N)}_{k}\Delta_{k}\big[\big(\bar{S}^{(N)}\big)^{m}\big]\geq-\Lambda\quad\text{for $k=1,\ldots,N$}.

For that construction, consider the function
f:(0,1)\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by

\displaystyle f(z)=\big(z(1-z)\big)^{-1/m},

which — recalling
m>1
— has finite integral,

\displaystyle\ell:=\int_{0}^{1}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

Depending on two parameters
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\beta>0
at our disposal,
define
\bar{S}^{(N)}
and
\bar{Y}^{(N)}
by

\displaystyle\bar{S}^{(N)}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\beta f\left(\frac{k-1/2}{N}\right)}\quad\text{for $k=1,2,\ldots,N$},

\displaystyle\bar{y}^{(N)}_{j}

\displaystyle:=\alpha+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{\bar{S}^{(N)}_{k}}\quad\text{for $j=0,1,\ldots,N$};

we further set
\bar{S}^{(N)}_{0}:=0
and
\bar{S}^{(N)}_{N+1}:=0
.
By convexity of
f
, the support length
\mathcal{L}[\bar{Y}^{(N)}]=\bar{y}^{(N)}_{N}-\bar{y}^{(N)}_{0}

can be estimated as follows:

(49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\bar{Y}^{(N)}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\bar{S}^{(N)}_{k}}=\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}f\left(\frac{k-1/2}{N}\right)\leq\beta\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{(k-1)/N}^{k/N}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\beta\ell.

A direct computation — using that
f(z)^{-m}=z(1-z)/\beta^{m}
— yields that

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\big[\big(\bar{S}^{(N)}\big)^{m}\big]=-\frac{2}{\beta^{m}}\quad\text{for $k=2,3,\ldots,N-1$},

as well as

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}\big[\big(\bar{S}^{(N)}\big)^{m}\big]=\Delta_{N}\big[\big(\bar{S}^{(N)}\big)^{m}\big]=\frac{2N-7}{2\beta^{m}}>0,

since we assume
N\geq 4
.
Thus

\displaystyle\min_{k=1,\ldots,N}S^{(N)}_{k}\Delta_{k}\big[\big(\bar{S}^{(N)}\big)^{m}\big]\geq-\frac{2}{\beta^{m}}\max_{k=2,\ldots,N-1}S^{(N)}_{k}\geq-\frac{2}{\beta^{m+1}}\max_{z\in(0,1)}\frac{1}{f(z)}\geq-\frac{2}{4^{1/m}\beta^{m+1}},

which imples that (
48
) holds with

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{1}{4^{1/m-1/2}\beta^{m+1}}.

Now let
Y^{(N)}(t)
be the solution to the evolution equation (
30
) with initial condition
\bar{Y}^{(N)}
.
Estimate (
47
) on the support length
in combination with the elementary inequality (
70
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big[Y^{(N)}(t)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}\big[\bar{Y}^{(N)}\big]\big(1+(m+1)\Lambda t\big)^{1/(m+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\beta\ell\Big(1+\big[(m+1)\Lambda t\big]^{1/(m+1)}\Big)\leq\beta\ell+\frac{\ell}{4^{(1/m-1/2)/(m+1)}}\big[(m+1)t\big]^{1/(m+1)}.

In short, with
B:=4^{-(1/m-1/2)/(m+1)}(m+1)^{1/(m+1)}\ell
, we have

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big[Y^{(N)}(t)\big]\leq\beta\ell+Bt^{1/(m+1)}.

To finish the proof, choose
\alpha:=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{x}^{(N)}_{0}+\bar{x}^{(N)}_{N})
as the center of
\bar{X}^{(N)}
’s support,
and let
\beta>0
be sufficiently small such that
\bar{Y}^{(N)}
’s support length
is smaller than half of that of
\bar{X}^{(N)}
,
i.e.,
\beta\ell<\frac{1}{2}\big(\bar{x}^{(N)}_{N}-\bar{x}^{(N)}_{0}\big)
.
This choice is made such that

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\big(\bar{X}^{(N)},\bar{Y}^{(N)}\big)=\max_{k}\big|\bar{x}^{(N)}_{k}-\bar{y}^{(N)}_{k}\big|\leq\max_{k}\big|\bar{x}^{(N)}_{k}-\alpha|+\max_{k}\big|\bar{y}^{(N)}_{k}-\alpha\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}[\bar{X}^{(N)}]+\beta\ell.

By contractivity in
d_{\infty}
, see Proposition
4
, it follows that at any time
t>0
,

\displaystyle\big|x^{(N)}_{0}(t)-y^{(N)}_{0}(t)\big|+\big|x^{(N)}_{N}(t)-y^{(N)}_{N}(t)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2d_{\infty}\big(X^{(N)}(t),Y^{(N)}(t)\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2d_{\infty}\big(\bar{X}^{(N)},\bar{Y}^{(N)}\big)\leq\mathcal{L}[\bar{X}^{(N)}]+2\beta\ell.

In combination with (
50
), we thus obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[X^{(N)}(t)]

\displaystyle\leq\big|x^{(N)}_{0}(t)-y^{(N)}_{0}(t)\big|+\big|x^{(N)}_{N}(t)-y^{(N)}_{N}(t)\big|+\mathcal{L}\big[Y^{(N)}(t)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}[\bar{X}^{(N)}]+2\beta\ell+\beta\ell+Bt^{1/(m+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}[\bar{X}^{(N)}]+Bt^{1/(m+1)}+3\beta\ell.

The construction above works for any sufficiently small choice of
\beta>0
.
This implies the claimed estimate (
40
).
∎

5.
Uniform
L^{\infty}
decay

In this section we prove the uniform
L^{\infty}
-decay estimate of Theorem
3
.
For the reader’s convenience, we first discuss the derivation of (
41
) in the continuum setting and motivate our choice of the strategy of the proof in the discrete setting.

The classical proof of the sharp form (
41
) of the decay estimate at a given time
t>0

is to maximize the supremum of the spatial profile
x\mapsto U(x):=\rho(t;x)
under the constraint of given mass,
subject to the estimate (
6
).
In other words, due to the translation invariance one may assume without restriction that the maximum of
U
is attained at
x=0
. Then,
one seeks to find
U(0)

subject to

\displaystyle(U^{m-1})^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{m-1}{m(m+1)t}\quad\text{on its support $B$, and}\quad\int_{B}U\,\mathrm{d}x=M.

It is easily seen that an optimizing profile (actually: the unique one up to translations)
is indeed given by
U(x)=\hat{\rho}(t;x)
, the corresponding profile of the self-similar solution from (
7
).
Since
\hat{\rho}
is a solution, the sharpness of the estimate follows automatically.

Translating this strategy to the discrete setting is not straightforward,
since dealing with the discrete counterpart of the self-similar profile is not immediate.
Instead, we shall provide a different proof, which leads to the same decay estimate, but with a sub-optimal constant.
The advantage of this proof is that it is sufficiently robust to be directly rephrased for the Lagrangian discretization. We sketch below the proof of the continuum case for convenience.

Let
\rho
be a compactly supported unit mass solution to (
5
).
First observe that, by the estimate (
6
) and integration by parts,

(51)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{m(m+1)t}&=\frac{1}{m(m+1)t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(-\frac{\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)_{xx}\rho\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\rho^{m-1}}{m-1}\right)_{x}\rho_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{4}{m^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\rho^{m/2}\big)_{x}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

With that at hand, we find that for any
\bar{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(52)

\begin{split}\frac{\rho(\bar{x})^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}{\frac{m+1}{2}}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\bar{x}}\rho^{(m-1)/2}\rho_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\bar{x}}^{\infty}\rho^{(m-1)/2}\rho_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{m}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\bar{x}}\rho^{1/2}\big(\rho^{m/2}\big)_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\bar{x}}^{\infty}\rho^{1/2}\big(\rho^{m/2}\big)_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\rho^{m/2}\big)_{x}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Combining the two estimates (
51
) and (
52
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup\rho\leq\left[\frac{m+1}{2m}\left(\frac{m}{4(m+1)t}\right)^{1/2}\right]^{2/(m+1)}=\left(\frac{m+1}{16m\,t}\right)^{1/(m+1)}.

We now adopt a discrete version of the above strategy to prove Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Since by (
39
), the upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(m+1)t}\geq-R_{k}\Delta_{k}(R^{m})

holds at every
k=1,2,\ldots,N
,
the same upper bound is true also for the average of the right-hand side.
In combination with the summation by parts rule (
35
) and the elementary inequality (
71
),
we thus obtain

(53)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{(m+1)t}&\geq-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}R_{k}\Delta_{k}(R^{m})\\
&\qquad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\operatorname{D}^{+}_{k}R\,\operatorname{D}^{+}_{k}(R^{m})\geq\frac{4m}{(m+1)^{2}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\big(\operatorname{D}^{+}_{k}R^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\big)^{2}.\end{split}

Now, on the other hand, we have for every index
k
:

\displaystyle R_{k}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(R_{j}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}-R_{j-1}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\big)+\sum_{j=k}^{N}\big(R_{j}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}-R_{j+1}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\big|\operatorname{D}^{+}_{j}R^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\big(\operatorname{D}^{+}_{j}R^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\big)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where we have used again Jensen’s inequality for sums in the last estimate.
Bounding the right-hand side above further in terms using (
53
) yields the result.
∎

•

6.2.
Limit equation

Throughout this section, let

Appendix A
A technical lemma

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We compute

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N+1}\Delta_{k}F=N\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left((\operatorname{D}^{+}F)_{k}-(\operatorname{D}^{+}F)_{k-1}\right)+N^{2}F_{1}+N^{2}F_{N}

\displaystyle\ =-N(\operatorname{D}^{+}F)_{0}+N(\operatorname{D}^{+}F)_{N}+N^{2}F_{1}+N^{2}F_{N}=0.

Hence, either
\Delta_{k}F=0
for all

or there exists at least one index

such that
\Delta_{k}F<0
. The former case implies in particular
\Delta_{0}F=N^{2}F_{1}=0
, which in turns implies

from

Lemma 8
.

Let
\rho_{n}\rightarrow\rho
in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then, for all
\gamma>1
there holds

(68)

\left\|\rho^{\gamma}\right\|_{\text{TV}}\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left\|\rho^{\gamma}_{n}\right\|_{\text{TV}}

Proof.

Let
\rho_{n_{k}}
be a subsequence of
\rho_{n}
such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\left\|\rho^{\gamma}_{n_{k}}\right\|_{\text{TV}}=\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left\|\rho^{\gamma}_{n}\right\|_{\text{TV}}\,.

Without restriction, due to the convergence of
\rho_{n_{k}}
to
\rho
in

and to the classical Fisher-Riesz theorem (see
[
7
, Theorem 4.9]
), we may assume that
\rho_{n_{k}}\rightarrow\rho
almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Helly’s Theorem (see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 2.3]
) implies that, up to extracting a further subsequence we keep denoting by
\rho_{n_{k}}
for simplicity,

(69)

\rho_{n_{k}}^{\gamma}\rightarrow\eta\qquad\hbox{almost everywhere on ${\mathbb{R}}$ and in $L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}})$}\,,

where have also used once again Fisher-Riesz theorem and a standard diagonal procedure. The above implies

Lemma 9
.

Assume the sequence
(\rho^{n})_{n}
is compact in
L^{1}(\Omega)
for any
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}
compact and satisfies
\rho_{n}\geq 0

\mathcal{L}^{1}
-almost everywhere. Assume
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\rho^{n}(x)dx
is uniformly bounded. Then
\rho^{n}
is compact in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

By choosing an increasing sequence of intervals
\Omega_{k}=[-k,k]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, we construct a subsequence of
\rho^{n}
which converges
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-almost everywhere and strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega_{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
to some locally integrable function
\rho\geq 0
. By Fatou’s lemma we infer that
\rho\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\rho(x)dx\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\rho^{n}(x)dx<+\infty\,.

Then, for a given
k>0
, we denote by
\rho^{n}
the above subsequence and compute

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx=\int_{-k}^{k}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-k,k]}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle\ =\int_{-k}^{k}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-k,k]}\frac{1}{|x|}|x|\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle\ \leq\int_{-k}^{k}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx+\frac{1}{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\left|(\rho^{n}(x)+\rho(x)\right)dx\,.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
R=C/\varepsilon
where
C>0
is an upper bound for

\left\{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|x|\rho(x)dx\right\}\cup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\rho^{n}(x)dx\,:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

We get

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{-[R]-1}^{[R]+1}\left|\rho^{n}(x)-\rho(x)\right|dx+\varepsilon=\varepsilon

and the statement follows from the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.
∎

Appendix C
Elementary inequalities

Lemma 10
.

For any
a>0
and
m>0
, there holds

(70)

(1+a)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}\leq 1+a^{\frac{1}{m+1}}

Proof.

Given
f:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

f(a)=1+a^{\frac{1}{m+1}}-(1+a)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}\,,

the statement follows from
f(0)=0
and

f^{\prime}(a)=\frac{1}{m+1}a^{-\frac{m}{m+1}}\left(1-\left(\frac{a}{1+a}\right)^{\frac{m}{m+1}}\right)\geq 0\,.

∎

Lemma 11
.

For any
p>0
, the following inequality holds for all
y,z\geq 0
:

(71)

\displaystyle(z-y)(z^{p}-y^{p})\geq\frac{4p}{(p+1)^{2}}\big(z^{(p+1)/2}-y^{(p+1)/2}\big)^{2}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
z>y\geq 0
.
Divide (
71
) by
p(z-y)^{2}
to obtain the following equivalent form:

\displaystyle\frac{z^{p}-y^{p}}{p(z-y)}\geq\left(\frac{z^{(p+1)/2}-y^{(p+1)/2}}{\frac{p+1}{2}\,(z-y)}\right)^{2}.

Now rewrite both sides as averaged integrals:

\displaystyle\fint_{y}^{z}\zeta^{p-1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\geq\left(\fint_{y}^{z}\zeta^{(p-1)/2}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\right)^{2}.

This is a direct consequence of Jensen’s inequality.
∎
