Responses of China’s dry grassland low-growth to temperature- and water-related extremes
Authors: Shuo Zhou, Zhongxu Zhao, Lei Wang, Deliang Chen, Xiuping Li, Dongliang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGrasslands in arid and semi-arid regions are highly vulnerable to climate change. While extreme climate events (ECEs) affect vegetation, the relative impacts of temperature-related (heat/cold) versus water-related (drought/wetness) extremes remain unclear. Using coincidence analysis and structural equation modeling across China’s drylands, we identified distinct spatial responses: extreme heat suppressed temperate grasslands, whereas extreme cold suppressed alpine grasslands. Extreme drought offsets the positive effect of extreme heat on alpine vegetation. These impacts reflect differing sensitivities to ECEs. Greenness (NDVI) and productivity (GPP) responses were generally consistent, except on the southern Tibetan Plateau, where heat extremes affected NDVI-based low-growth extremes while cold extremes impacted GPP-based low-growth extremes. Our findings highlight the divergent pathways of ECE impacts, providing crucial insights for ecosystem management under climate change.

Introduction

Anthropogenic activities not only drive gradual changes in the global mean climate state but also alter the variability of temperature and precipitation.1 Enhanced variability intensifies thermal and hydrological fluctuations, significantly increasing the frequency and magnitude of extreme climate events (ECEs)‒including extreme heat, cold, drought, and wetness.2,3 Notably, water-constrained arid and semi-arid regions show heightened vulnerability to hydrothermal anomalies.4 Observational data show that Mongolia’s arid zone exhibits increasing trends in both precipitation intensity and consecutive dry days,5 whereas the central and eastern Tibetan Plateau experiences rising frequency and intensity of warm days.6 Concurrently, its southern region shows increased extreme cold events.7 Multiple lines of evidence‒remote sensing, tree-ring data, and manipulative experiments demonstrate that ECEs impair vegetation growth, alter phenology, and reduce productivity.8,9 Grassland ecosystems, characterized by lower structural complexity and biodiversity, exhibit heightened susceptibility to ECEs.10 Therefore, deciphering grassland ecosystems’ response mechanisms to ECEs has emerged as a critical frontier in ecological research.11 Specifically, temperature-related extremes (extreme heat and cold) modulate growing-season length. Beyond physiological thresholds, they cause delayed onset or premature termination of the growing season.12 Water-related extremes (extreme drought and wetness) alter soil moisture dynamics through irregular rainfall patterns (e.g., intense rainfall followed by dry spells), directly affecting grassland productivity.13,14 Understanding these responses is critical for predicting ecosystem resilience under future climate scenarios.

Vegetation growth dynamics are co-regulated by both water and temperature,15 with this phenomenon being particularly pronounced in arid and semi-arid regions. It has been demonstrated that precipitation can influence the temperature dependence of vegetation green-up in arid and semi-arid regions, whereas in humid regions, green-up is primarily governed by temperature.16 In assessing the impacts of ECEs, the comprehensive consideration of hydrothermal factors has become a research consensus. The Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), as a representative metric, offers the advantage of simultaneously accounting for the combined effects of precipitation and temperature changes on drought conditions.17 Nevertheless, ecosystem responses to hydrothermal extreme events often exhibit complex, non-additive effects: the 2023 “high temperature-drought” compound event in southwestern China caused vegetation productivity to reach its lowest level in two decades18; meanwhile, it has been shown that while drought alone reduced alpine meadow aboveground net primary productivity by 42%, a “warm-dry” treatment resulted in only a 26% reduction, indicating that warming can mitigate the negative effects of drought.19 Moreover, it has been found that under conditions of sufficient moisture, extreme heat could enhance vegetation growth in Northeast Asia. These differential responses underscore the complexity of interactions between extreme hydrothermal conditions across different ecosystems. Therefore, it is essential to comprehensively examine the combined effects of various ECEs and their interactions on vegetation growth.

NDVI and GPP are two representative indices commonly used to assess vegetation greenness and productivity. NDVI, which reflects vegetation greenness through remote sensing and spectral measurements, is widely used in studies to assess vegetation response to climate change.20 GPP, representing carbon flux from photosynthesis, serves as a key indicator of vegetation productivity and carbon sequestration capacity.21,22,23 For instance, NDVI has been applied to analyze the sensitivity of grassland phenology on the Tibetan Plateau to ECEs.24,25 A connection between vegetation growth and carbon sequestration capacity has been studied, with variations in vegetation growth serving as a key factor influencing the ability of vegetation to sequester carbon.13,26,27 However, the similarities and differences in how NDVI and GPP respond to ECEs remain unclear.

The sparse coverage of in situ measurements limits their utility for assessing large-scale vegetation responses to ECEs.28,29 Coincidence analysis addresses this spatial gap and accommodates complex response dynamics by identifying co-occurring vegetation loss events using widely available gridded data. A major strength of this method lies in its operational simplicity, combined with its ability to provide a clear and intuitive picture of where ECEs coincide with grassland degradation, directly showcasing their immediate disruptive impact on vegetation growth patterns across various spatial scales.30,31

China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands, covering approximately 34.1% of the country’s dryland regions, are essential for maintaining the ecosystem and supporting local livelihoods.32 (Figure 1) However, climate change has intensified fluctuations in temperature and precipitation, leading to an increase in the frequency and severity of ECEs, particularly extreme heat and drought.33,34 These changes have likely resulted in a significant decline in the resistance of grassland ecosystems to such events, increasing their vulnerability and posing challenges for local environmental management.35,36 Therefore, understanding how China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands respond to different ECEs is crucial for effective management and adaptation strategies.Figure 1The study area covers China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands(A) The distribution and area of four grassland ecosystems; the long-term mean, (B) normalized difference vegetation index, and (C) gross primary productivity from 2000 to 2022; and the spatial patterns of (D) the aridity index and (E) elevation.

Here, we aim to answer three key questions: 1. What are the spatial patterns of the impacts of ECEs on China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands? 2. How do temperature-related and water-related extreme events interact to affect grassland ecosystems in these regions? 3. Are there differences in the responses of vegetation greenness and productivity to various ECEs in China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands? Specifically, we first quantify low-growth events in grasslands caused by different ECEs using coincidence analysis, based on multiple vegetation, meteorological, and hydrological datasets (Table 1). Results

Analysis of coincidence rates between low-growth extremes and ECEs

We calculated the coincidence rates between NDVI-based and GPP-based low-growth extremes and ECEs, ranging from 0 to 1. Higher rates indicate stronger impacts of ECEs on vegetation anomalies. In most study areas, both NDVI-based and GPP-based low-growth extremes showed high coincidence rates with all ECEs, with values ranging between 0.4 and 0.6 (Figures 2A and 2B). The spatial distribution of ECEs driving low-growth extremes showed distinct clustering patterns (Figures 2C and 2D). Extreme cold and wet conditions primarily affected alpine grasslands and meadows, while extreme heat and droughts predominantly influenced vegetation growth in temperate grasslands and desert steppes. We also analyzed the coincidence rates across four grassland ecosystems (Figures 2E and 2F). Compared to NDVI-based low-growth extremes, which showed rates between 0.3 and 0.6, the coincidence rates for GPP-based low-growth extremes exhibited greater variation, particularly in temperate desert steppes and alpine meadows. This variation highlights significant differences in GPP responses to ECEs within the same grassland ecosystems. In terms of affected areas, extreme cold accounted for the largest proportion of NDVI-based low-growth extremes (41%), followed by extreme heat (38%), extreme droughts (20%), and extreme wetness (3%). For GPP-based low-growth extremes, extreme cold covered the majority of the area (54%), followed by extreme wetness (20%), extreme heat (19%), and extreme droughts (2.8%).

Sensitivity of vegetation growth to ECEs

The sensitivities of NDVI standard anomalies to extreme heat and extreme cold exhibited distinct spatial asymmetry (Figure 3). In particular, extreme heat enhanced vegetation growth in alpine steppe and alpine meadow regions, while it inhibited grassland growth in temperate steppe and temperate desert steppe areas. The sensitivities of NDVI standard anomalies to extreme drought and extreme wetness showed spatially discrete patterns. Further evaluation of grassland sensitivity to different ECEs showed that, across all grassland types, NDVI and GPP were most sensitive to extreme drought (with most pixel values ranging between −6 and 6), followed by extreme wetness (−3 to 3), while extreme heat and extreme cold showed much weaker responses (within −0.2 to 0.2) (Figures 3 and 4). In alpine regions, GPP generally showed stronger sensitivity to temperature-related extremes than NDVI. For extreme heat, the sensitivity of GPP was 0.07 (alpine steppe) and 0.1 (alpine meadow), compared to 0.01 and 0.2 for NDVI. Similarly, GPP responses to extreme cold were −0.1 (alpine steppe) and −0.12 (alpine meadow), while NDVI values were −0.05 and −0.04 (Figure S6).

Path analysis of the impacts of ECEs on low-growth extremes

Extreme heat substantially increased the occurrence of extreme drought across all grassland types, with the highest path coefficient (0.46) in the temperate steppe region. The relationship between extreme cold and extreme wetness was significant only in the temperate steppe and temperate desert steppe regions (p < 0.05), with path coefficients of 0.31 and 0.38, respectively. However, this relationship was weak in alpine steppe and alpine meadow (path coefficients: −0.12 and −0.10). These results suggest that extreme cold is typically associated with extreme wetness in temperate regions, whereas no strong direct linkage exists between these variables in alpine regions. The correlation between low-growth extremes, indicated by the NDVI and GPP, was particularly significant in the temperate steppe and temperate desert steppe regions, with both coefficients reaching 0.95 (p < 0.05), while in alpine regions, the coefficients were lower, with values of 0.39 for alpine steppe and 0.50 for alpine meadows, respectively (p < 0.05). The effects of ECEs on vegetation growth varied by grassland type: Extreme heat enhanced GPP in alpine meadows (−0.32) but inhibited the NDVI and GPP in temperate regions. Extreme cold significantly reduced the NDVI and GPP in alpine regions (p < 0.05), and extreme drought notably inhibited the NDVI in alpine grasslands. The direct and indirect effects of ECEs on the NDVI (Figure 6) and GPP (Figure 7) varied across different grassland types. When the signs of the direct and indirect effects were the same, this was termed an enhancing effect; otherwise, it was termed an offset effect. For NDVI-based low-growth extremes, extreme drought offset the positive effects of extreme heat on vegetation growth in alpine meadow and alpine steppe regions, with indirect coefficients of 0.215 and 0.118, respectively. This offset effect, albeit weaker (0.02), also appeared in the temperate desert steppe region. The interaction between extreme wetness and extreme cold had a complex impact on the NDVI. Extreme wetness offset the positive effect of extreme cold on the NDVI in the temperate steppe region but enhanced the negative impact of extreme cold on the NDVI in the temperate desert steppe region. The indirect effects on the NDVI in alpine meadow and alpine steppe environments were weakly negative. In addition, in these grassland types, extreme drought and NDVI-based low-growth extremes offset the negative effects of extreme heat on GPP, while extreme wetness and NDVI-based low-growth extremes offset the negative effects of extreme cold. Discussion

Diverse impacts of different ECEs on various grassland types

In this study, we show that the spatial heterogeneity of ECEs impacts greenness and productivity in the arid and semi-arid grassland of China. However, the extent and direction of these effects vary significantly depending on the type of ECE.

The effects of temperature-related ECEs on grassland vegetation vary across temperature zones. Extreme heat and drought primarily suppress vegetation growth in temperate regions. This response is associated with the occurrence of compound heat-drought events, characterized by increased vapor pressure deficit and reduced soil moisture, which intensify water stress in these regions.37,38 Additionally, spring warming advances the start of the growing season, further depleting soil moisture and exacerbating its negative impact on vegetation growth.39,40,41

Our results show that extreme heat can significantly enhance vegetation growth in alpine meadows (Figure 5D), while extreme cold primarily inhibits vegetation growth in both alpine meadows and alpine steppes (Figures 5C and 5D) This contrasting response aligns with the differential sensitivity of vegetation to various temperature stresses: Extreme heat mainly enhances vegetation greenness and productivity in alpine regions (Figures 3A and 4A), whereas extreme cold shows inhibitory effects (Figures 3B and 4B). These findings are consistent with previous research showing that warming can prolong the growing season42 and increase carbon storage in alpine ecosystems.43 Notably, we found that extreme drought offsets the positive effects of extreme heat on vegetation growth. This phenomenon may be regulated by differential responses of biodiversity to temperature and water availability variations.44 Controlled experiments on moisture and temperature revealed opposing effects of warming and drought on alpine meadow biodiversity (warming increases species diversity while drought reduces it), consequently leading to contrasting effects of warming and drought on aboveground net primary productivity.19

Our results show that grasslands in these regions of China are more sensitive to water-related extreme events, particularly extreme drought, than to temperature-related extreme events. This is reflected in the higher sensitivity values and more spatially heterogeneous distribution observed across most areas (Figures 3C, 3D, 4C, and 4D). However, the area of grassland affected by extreme drought is smaller than that influenced by temperature-related extreme events (Figures 2C and 2D). This can be attributed to the distinct spatiotemporal scales of atmospheric and soil droughts. Typically, atmospheric drought, characterized by high vapor pressure deficit and low precipitation, spreads faster than soil drought, which often exhibits a time lag.45,46 As a result, vegetation responses to these two types of drought vary in timing.47,48,49 It has been emphasized that average precipitation has a greater impact on vegetation growth than extreme precipitation due to the cumulative effect of consistent rainfall.50 Moreover, ecosystem biodiversity contributes to the stability of ecosystems experiencing water-related extreme events.51 For example, in temperate steppe environments, grasses and forbs exhibit differing responses to extreme precipitation, with heavy rainfall benefiting grass growth but negatively affecting forbs. This asynchronous response among sub-dominant species enhances ecosystem stability under conditions of extreme drought and/or extreme wetness.52

The responses of temperate and alpine grasslands to extreme wetness also differ markedly (Figures 6 and 7). In temperate grasslands, extreme wetness promotes vegetation growth due to increased precipitation and soil moisture, which delays senescence and fosters development.53,54,55,56 In contrast, in alpine regions, where seasonally frozen ground and low-permeability permafrost are common, excess precipitation leads to surface water accumulation, which affects runoff and drainage patterns. This water accumulation creates anoxic conditions that hinder vegetation growth in alpine grasslands.30,57,58

Diverse effects of ECEs on grassland vegetation greenness and productivity

The correlation between NDVI and GPP, which represent vegetation greenness and productivity, respectively, is well-established.59,60,61 However, this relationship varies across grassland types. Specifically, the correlation is notably stronger in temperate grasslands (temperate steppe: 0.95; temperate desert steppe: 0.95) than in alpine ecosystems (alpine steppe: 0.39; alpine meadow: 0.5) (Figure 5). Two primary mechanisms may explain this divergence. First, inherent differences in light-use efficiency exist among dominant plant species across these ecosystems.62 Second, vegetation requires time to adjust its carbon assimilation processes to environmental changes, leading to a delayed GPP response to climatic factors that drive the observed seasonal decoupling between NDVI and GPP in northern ecosystems.63 In addition, the sensitivity to temperature-related extreme events is more pronounced in alpine regions compared with temperate regions (Figures 3 and 4). Given the current limited understanding of species-specific light-use efficiencies in these ecosystems, further research is necessary.

In most arid and semi-arid grasslands of China, the effects of ECEs on vegetation greenness and productivity are generally consistent, with coincidence rates typically ranging from 0.4 to 0.5. However, alpine steppe grasslands on the northern Tibetan Plateau present a notable exception to this pattern (Figures 2A and 2B). Despite increased vegetation greenness owing to recent human activities, productivity in this region remains remarkably low.64,65,66

In the southern Tibetan Plateau, vegetation greenness and productivity show distinct responses to primary ECEs. Our results show that extreme heat primarily affects NDVI-based low-growth extremes (Figure 2C). Notably, while NDVI shows negative sensitivity to extreme heat (inhibiting greening), GPP shows positive sensitivity (enhancing productivity) (Figures 3A and 4A). This apparent discrepancy can be explained through the following mechanisms: First, extreme heat directly damages vegetation structure, causing visible injury such as leaf yellowing, thereby reducing NDVI values.67 However, plant photosynthesis follows a temperature optimum response curve.68 Considering the region’s low baseline temperatures, the extreme heat thresholds identified in this study likely remain within or below the photosynthetic optimum for alpine grasslands. In fact, moderate warming has been shown to enhance photosynthesis in alpine meadows,69 explaining the positive GPP response to heat. In contrast, extreme cold primarily influences GPP-based low-growth extremes (Figure 2D), a phenomenon closely linked to phenological shifts. It has been found that the start of the growing season advanced by 8.3 ± 2.0 days during 2000–2022.42 This extended growing season increases exposure to frost events in early spring,70 particularly for vulnerable young shoots with low cold resistance.71 Frost damages plant tissues and severely reduces carbon assimilation efficiency.72 A single frost event can decrease productivity in high-altitude forests by 7%–14%.73 Additionally, extreme wetness in parts of the southeastern region also contributes to GPP-based low-growth extremes (Figure 2D). The mechanism involves excessive moisture disrupting abscisic acid-mediated stomatal regulation, thereby impeding oxygen diffusion and root respiration, ultimately limiting productivity.74

Limitations of the study

In this study, we analyzed the coincidence of ECEs with vegetation growth anomalies (detected via NDVI and GPP metrics) and assessed the direct and indirect impacts of temperature- and water-related ECEs on vegetation in China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands. Despite these findings, three uncertainties remain to be addressed in future research. First, vegetation responses to ECEs may emerge weeks or months after the events. By not accounting for these delayed responses, our study may have underestimated the magnitude of ECE-driven vegetation anomalies. Future research will employ time-lagged correlation analysis to systematically quantify the delayed and cumulative effects of ECEs. Additionally, we did not account for the temporal variations in the impact of ECEs on grassland vegetation, including seasonal fluctuations, which could provide deeper insights into the contributions of snow cover and permafrost to these effects. Future research should integrate high-resolution snow data to better understand the role of snow-related factors in shaping vegetation responses to ECEs. Lastly, our results suggest that the responses of different sub-species within a single ecosystem may influence ecosystem stability. Therefore, future research should emphasize long-term, detailed in situ investigations to explore the varying responses of different sub-species to ECEs in the arid and semi-arid grasslands of China.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study did not use experimental models, human participants, animals, plants, microbe strains, or cell lines. All analyses were conducted using publicly available remote sensing and climate datasets as described in the method details section.

Method details

Study area

The arid and semi-arid grassland regions in China extend from 30° to 50° N and 75° to 125° E, primarily encompassing most western provinces (Figure 1). This region includes two major topographical zones: the Inner Mongolia Plateau and Tibetan Plateau (Figure 1E). Meteorological factors, including air temperature, precipitation, and evapotranspiration, exhibit distinct longitudinal gradients; specifically, precipitation and temperature both decrease moving from east to west. The aridity index of the region, defined as the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration,82 is generally < 0.65, with evapotranspiration exceeding precipitation (Figure 1D). Grasslands are the dominant vegetation type, displaying a gradient from temperate steppe and temperate desert steppe in the east to alpine meadow and alpine steppe in the west, covering 25%, 12%, 27%, and 36% of the area, respectively (Figure 1A). The NDVI and GPP also display distinct longitudinal gradients, with higher values in temperate grasslands and lower values in alpine grasslands (Figures 1B and 1C).

Datasets

To comprehensively assess the ecological impacts of ECEs in China’s arid and semi-arid grasslands, we utilized a range of datasets spanning the 2000‒2022 growing season (April‒September). As shown in Table 1, our study incorporated vegetation indicators (NDVI and GPP), climatic variables (monthly maximum, minimum, mean temperatures, monthly precipitation, vapor pressure deficit, 0‒7 cm soil moisture), and grassland type distribution data (temperate steppe, temperate desert steppe, alpine meadow alpine steppe). All data processing was conducted using Google Earth Engine,79,81 with final output datasets resampled to a uniform 500m resolution. The NDVI values were extracted from the 16-day composite MOD13A1 product, with pixels affected by clouds, shadows, water and aerosols excluded.75 The GPP values were obtained from the 8-day composite MOD17A2 based on the radiation-use efficiency concept.76 Monthly composites for both NDVI and GPP were generated through averaging pixels within each month.83

Definition of ECEs

To define four types of ECEs (extreme heat, cold, drought, and wetness) and low-growth extremes, we used the standardized deviation (STD)-based method that is commonly used in ecological studies.84 First, we removed the long-term trend of each variable to eliminate natural growth trends and intra-annual variations.85 Specifically, we calculated the trend at the annual scale and then replicated it for each month. This trend was then subtracted from the monthly time series. Finally, we calculated the standardized anomalies for each variables using the following formula:(Equation 1)λi,j=Xi,j−mean(Xi)std(Xi),

Considering that both the monthly average and extreme temperatures (maximum and minimum) are known to influence grassland ecosystem growth, it was essential to incorporate these variables when evaluating the effects of temperature-related extreme events.86,87 Therefore, extreme heat was identified when either the monthly average or maximum temperature exceeded on standard deviation from the mean, while extreme cold was defined when either the monthly average or minimum temperature fell down one standard deviation from the mean. Extreme drought was defined as conditions where either monthly precipitation or soil moisture (0‒7 cm) fell at least one standard deviation below the long-term average, or atmospheric vapor pressure deficit exceeded one standard deviation above the mean. The inclusion of these three indicators‒precipitation, soil moisture, and vapor pressure deficit was essential because extreme values of each represent different forms of drought, specifically atmospheric drought and soil drought.88,89,90 Similarly, extreme wetness was recognized when either monthly precipitation or soil moisture (0‒7 cm) exceeded on standard deviation above the mean. In terms of vegetation indices, our focus was on identifying low-growth extremes. Values of NDVI and GPP falling below one standard deviation from their respective means were classified as low-growth extremes. In statistical analyses, events exceeding beyond ±1 standard deviation are frequently classified as extremes.24,25 We also applied a two-standard deviation threshold, and the results showed spatial patterns similar to those derived from the one standard deviation threshold (Figures S3–S5).

Event coincidence analysis

Event coincidence analysis was conducted to quantify the co-occurrence between low-growth extremes and four types of extreme climate events (extreme heat, cold and drought and wetness) during 2000‒2022. We calculated single-ECE coincidence rates (Equation.2) and all-ECE coincidence rates (Equation.3). And then we compared the magnitudes of single-ECE coincidence rates at the pixel scale to identify the ECE that exerted the greatest influence on vegetation growth from 2000 to 2022.(Equation 2)CRi=N(VI∩ECEi)N(ECEi)(Equation 3)CRall=N(VI∩ECEall)N(ECEall)

Where N(VI∩ECEi) represents the count of low-growth extremes co-occurring with ECEi (i = heat, cold, drought and wetness), N(ECEi) represents the total number of ECEi; N(VI∩ECEall) represents the count of low-growth extremes co-occurring with all ECE types, and N(ECEall) refers the total number of all ECE types. CRi and CRall are coincidence rates.

To verify that the connection between low-growth extremes and ECEs was not a random occurrence, we conducted a bootstrap experiment: while preserving the sequence of low-growth extremes, we randomly shuffled the ECEs sequence with replacement and calculated the coincidence rate of low-growth extremes with ECEs in randomized time series. This process was repeated 1000 times. Based on the bootstrap p test (comparing the coincidence rate of the original ECEs sequence with that of shuffled sequences by calculating the proportion of instances where the former exceeded the latter out of 1000 total iterations), regions with p-values below 0.01 were identified. Our results showed that in nearly all regions, the coincidence rate derived from the original (unshuffled) ECEs sequence was significantly higher than those from the shuffled sequences (Figure S2). This provides evidence that the link between low-growth extremes and ECEs did not occur randomly.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sensitivity analysis

For sensitivity analysis, the values of ECEs were first converted to absolute values, because different ECEs may have opposing signs. Then, the annual average of ECEs was calculated at each pixel during the growing season (April‒September) for the period 2000‒2022. Finally, the vegetation standard anomalies were divided by the extreme climate standard anomalies to obtain the sensitivity of vegetation growth to ECEs, calculated as follows:(Equation 4)Scli=SAviSAcli,where, Scli represents the sensitivity of vegetation to ECEs, SAvi denotes vegetation standard anomalies, and SAcli denotes extreme climate standard anomalies. The results are regarded as the deviation in vegetation growth caused by ECEs. A positive value indicates that ECEs enhance vegetation growth, while a negative value indicates that ECEs inhibit vegetation growth.

Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM)

We employed structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine causal relationships among multiple variables using multiple linear regression, a method widely applied in ecological studies.80,91 Unlike traditional SEM based on maximum likelihood estimation, partial least squares SEM (PLS-SEM) requires neither a large sample size nor specific distributional assumptions and is robust when handling missing data.92

First, for each of the four grassland types, we calculated monthly anomaly values (including monthly average temperature, monthly maximum temperature, monthly minimum temperature, monthly precipitation, vapor pressure deficit, soil moisture, NDVI, and GPP) that exceeded ± 1 standard deviation, then performed regional averaging. This process yielded a dataset with 138 observations per variable, which was subsequently standardized. As shown in (Figure S1), the observed values were used to generate six latent variables. We hypothesized that: (1) extreme heat may induce drought by suppressing rainfall and amplifying evapotranspiration89; (2) extreme cold could increase precipitation by enhancing moisture transport and reducing evapotranspiration through reinforced land-atmosphere pressure gradients93; (3) NDVI-based low-growth extremes are functionally linked to GPP-based low-growth extremes. The analysis was conducted using the ‘PiecewiseSEM’ R package. We performed PLS-SEM with 1000 bootstraps to validate path coefficient estimates and coefficients of determination. The six latent variables were modeled using linear equations, where direct effects were quantified as path coefficients, and indirect effects were computed as the product of the path coefficients β along mediated pathways (e.g., β1×β2 for Extreme heat → Extreme drought → NDVI‒based low‒growth extremes). Model performance was assessed using the Goodness-of-Fit (GoF) index (ranging from 0 to 1), which integrates composite reliability (commonality) and explained variance to evaluate predictive capability. Higher GoF values indicate better model fit and greater explanatory power.94
