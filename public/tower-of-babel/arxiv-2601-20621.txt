Saturation of algebraic surfaces
Agnieszka Bodzenta, Tomasz Pełka, Dario Weißmann

Abstract
. The saturation of an algebraic surface is the maximal open embedding with complement of dimension zero. For schemes, it was introduced by the first named author and A. Bondal [ 5 ] , who proved that the saturation of a surface X X can be recovered from the category of reflexive sheaves on X X . In this article, we extend these results to algebraic spaces. Furthermore, we address the question of A. Bondal whether every saturated surface X X is proper over its affinisation X aff = Spec ⁡ H 0 ​ ( X , 𝒪 X ) X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=\mathop{\textrm{Spec}}\nolimits H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X}) . We prove that passing from schemes to algebraic spaces guarantees that this property holds whenever X aff X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits} is non-trivial. Finally, we give some characterisation of saturated surfaces depending on dim X aff \dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits} .

1.
Introduction

In this article we address the question of characterising saturated surfaces, i.e. those to which one cannot
“add more closed points”
. More precisely, we say that a surface
X
is
saturated
if any open embedding
X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow Y
whose image has complement of dimension zero is an isomorphism. We call such an embedding
X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow Y

a
big open
embedding.

In the category of schemes, saturated surfaces were first introduced
in the article
[
5
]
by the first named author and A. Bondal.
It was also proved that if
X
is proper over its affinisation

\mathop{\textrm{Spec}}\nolimits H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
,
then it is saturated
[
5
, Theorem 2.12]
,
but the converse is false
[
5
, Example 2.13]
.
The original motivation for this paper was the question by A. Bondal whether
the converse holds when, instead of schemes, one considers algebraic spaces.
We prove that it holds in most cases (Theorem
C
)
and study the case when it does not
(Theorem
D
(c)
).

Therefore, from now on by a
surface
we mean an irreducible, separated, normal
algebraic space
of finite type and dimension two over
\mathop{\textrm{Spec}}\nolimits{k}
, where
{k}
is a fixed algebraically closed field. We consider the category
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
of surfaces and big open embeddings.
We say that a morphism
\eta_{X}\colon X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
in
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
is the
saturation
of
X
if
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
contains all closed points that can be added to
X
, that is, for any
j\in\mathop{\textrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits}(X,Y)
there exists a unique
j^{\prime}\in\mathop{\textrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits}(Y,X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits})
such that
j^{\prime}\circ j=\eta_{X}
. We say that
X
is
saturated
if
\eta_{X}
is an isomorphism, equivalently, any big open embedding
X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow Y
is an isomorphism, see Definition
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
.

Note that (unless
{k}
is an algebraic closure of a finite field, see Remark
5.14
) this definition is more restrictive than the one given in
[
5
]
, where instead of
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
one considers its full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
of schemes, see Example
6.4
. To avoid the risk of confusion, we call the objects of
{\mathcal{D}}

schematic surfaces
, and say that
X\in{\mathcal{D}}
is
scheme-saturated
if it is saturated in the weaker sense of
[
5
]
. Our first result shows that the saturation exists, has categorical properties analogous to
[
5
, Theorem 2.10]
, and is functorial with respect to proper surjective morphisms.
Interestingly, the last statement fails for scheme-saturation, see Example
3.16
.

[

]
.

Theorem B
.

Let
X
be a normal saturated surface.
Then the saturation
\eta_{X}\colon X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}

can be recovered from the category of reflexive sheaves on
X
,
as described in Theorem

.

In fact, Theorem
B
improves
[

]
, as it recovers not only the surface

, but also the morphism

. The proof relies on the results of
[

]
combined with the methods of
[

]
.

Next, we consider the
affinisation
of a surface
X
,
i.e. the universal morphism to an affine scheme

X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\coloneqq\mathop{\textrm{Spec}}\nolimits H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
.
We refer to Section
4.1
for details.
Here we note that since
X
is a surface,
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is a scheme of finite type and dimension at most two
[
29
, Corollary 6.2]
.
It is easy to see (Proposition
4.3
)
that if
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is proper, then
X
is saturated.
Our next result is a partial converse to this statement,
which answers the question of A. Bondal stated at the beginning of the introduction.

Theorem C

(Theorem
4.10
)
.

Let
X
be a normal surface such that
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}>0
.
Then
X
is saturated if and only if the affinisation morphism

X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is proper.

We emphasise that Theorem
C

requires passing to the category of algebraic spaces.
Indeed, Example
6.4
shows that there exist scheme-saturated surfaces
X

with
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=0,1,2
which are non-proper over their affinisations.
On the other hand, since
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is dominant (Lemma
4.2
),
Theorem
C
implies that if
X
is saturated,
then
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is surjective.
This weaker property still holds in the category of schemes
(Proposition
4.12
).

The assumption
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}>0
in Theorem
C
is necessary.
Indeed, Examples
6.1
and
6.3
(b)
show that,
if
{k}
is not an algebraic closure of a finite field,
then there do exist non-proper, saturated surfaces with trivial affinisations.
In fact, Proposition
5.13
shows that
they can be constructed in any class of birational equivalence,
but still are somewhat peculiar.

Our next goal is to describe saturated
surfaces according to
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
. The result, summarised in Theorem

D
below, is stated in terms of the boundary
divisor of a normal compactification of
X
.
We use the
\mathbb{Q}
-valued intersection product of Weil divisors,
defined on any proper normal surface as in
[
26
]
,
see Remark
4.5
.
We say that a Weil divisor
D
with irreducible
components
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
is negative (semi-)definite if the
intersection matrix
[D_{i}\cdot D_{j}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}
is so.

Theorem D
.

Let
X
be an open subspace of a proper normal surface
\overline{X}
,
and let
D=\overline{X}\setminus X
.
Then
X
is saturated if and only if
D
is a Weil divisor
without a negative definite connected component
(see Proposition
4.8
).
Furthermore, if
X
is saturated and non-proper, then the following hold.

(a)

\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=2
if and only if
D
is not negative semi-definite (see Proposition
5.4
(a)
).

(b)

\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=1
if and only if there is a proper morphism from
\overline{X}
to a smooth curve, such that
D
is the union of some of its fibres (see Proposition
5.4
(c)
).

(c)

\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=0
if and only if
D
is negative semi-definite and
X
contains only finitely many proper irreducible curves, or equivalently, at most one proper reduced Weil divisor which is not negative definite (see Theorem
5.12
).

Theorem
D
(c)
characterises the saturated surfaces which are non-proper over their affinisations, see Theorem
C
.
For a more detailed result and discussion see Section
5.3
.

As shown by Examples
6.1
(c)
and
6.3
,
the cases
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=1
and
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=0
cannot be distinguished
by numerical properties of
X
and
D
alone. For instance, in Example
6.3
the boundary
D
is the proper transform of a planar cubic blown up in
9
points, and we can have
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=1
or
0
depending on the position of those points. In fact, this example is a slight modification of the classical construction of Hironaka
[
14
, Example V.7.3]
, showing that, after blowing up
D
once more and contracting its proper transform, the resulting algebraic space can be a scheme or not, see Lemma
6.2
. Our results, given in Theorem
D
(c)
and Section
5.3
, are analogous to the contractibility criterion
[
29
, Theorem 3.3]
, see Remark
5.10
.

Structure of the paper.

2
contains preliminary facts that are used throughout the paper. In Section

We show in Theorem

that

admits the left adjoint

hence the notation

.
This proves Theorem

(b)
.
In Section

we describe how

is determined by

and the category
\mathop{\textrm{Ref}}\nolimits(X)

,

.
Finally, in Section

we prove Theorem

(c)
,
in particular, we show that saturatedness is invariant under proper surjective morphisms.
Using the Stein factorization, we reduce this statement to the cases
of finite and proper birational morphisms,
which are settled in
the Propositions
3.17
and
3.15
,
respectively.
We note that the latter holds for the scheme-saturation as well,

3.16
.

Section
4
concerns the affinisation of saturated surfaces.
We discuss its basic properties in Section
4.1
.
In Section
4.2
we prove Theorem
C
,
namely that a saturated surface is proper over its affinisation
as long as the latter is non-trivial.
In Proposition
4.4
, we use Chow’s lemma,
resolution of surface singularities,
and Theorem
A
(c)

to reduce the problem to the smooth setting.
In Proposition
4.8
,

,

Finally, in Section

).

In Section
5

describing boundary divisors of

to give a numerical condition characterising

\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\leqslant 1

).

We conclude our discussion with a list of examples in Section

In Example

Notation

We work over an algebraically closed field
{k}
. A
curve
is a separated integral scheme of dimension
1
and of finite type over
{k}
.
A
surface
is an irreducible, normal,
separated algebraic space of dimension two and of finite type over
{k}
.
A
schematic
surface is a surface which is also a scheme. By a
divisor
on a surface we always mean a Weil divisor, and we often identify reduced divisors with their supports.

An open embedding of surfaces is
big
if its image has codimension at least two.
We denote by
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
(resp.
{\mathcal{D}}
) the category of surfaces (resp. schematic
surfaces) and big open embeddings.
For a surface
X
, we denote by
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sch}}\nolimits}
its schematic locus, and by
\eta_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
and
\alpha_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}

the saturation and affinisation morphisms, see Definition
3.1

and Section
4.1
, respectively.

2.
Gluing surfaces

2.1.
Algebraic spaces

We say that an open subspace
U\subset X
of an algebraic space is
big

if the complement
X\setminus U
has codimension at least two in
X
.
We say that
j\colon U\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X
is a
big open embedding

if it is an open embedding with big image.
For an algebraic space
X
we denote by
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sch}}\nolimits}
the
schematic locus
of
X
,
i.e. the set of geometric points in
X
that admit an affine open neighbourhood,
see
[
30
,
Tag 03JG
]
for details.
By
[
30
,
Tag 0ADD
]
,
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sch}}\nolimits}\subseteq X
is a big open subspace.
If
X
is normal, then the smooth locus of
X
is a big open subspace as well.

A morphism
Y\longrightarrow X
of normal algebraic spaces is a
cover
if it is
finite, surjective, and generically étale.
A cover
Y\longrightarrow X
is
Galois
if the function field extension

\kappa(Y)/\kappa(X)
is Galois, where
\kappa(X)
is the field of rational functions,
see
[
30
,
Tag 0END
]
.
We refer to the group
\mathop{\textrm{Gal}}\nolimits(\kappa(Y)/\kappa(X))

as the Galois group of the cover
X\longrightarrow Y
.

Lemma 2.1

(Galois covers)
.

Let
Y\longrightarrow X
be a Galois cover of surfaces with Galois group
G
.

(a)

The group
G
acts on
Y
over
X
and every automorphism of
Y
over
X
arises this way.

(b)

The surface
X
is the coarse quotient of
Y
by
G
, in the sense that it is universal among
G
-equivariant morphisms
Y\longrightarrow Z
to a space
Z
with the trivial
G
-action and geometric points of
X
correspond to
G
-orbits of geometric points of
Y
.

(a)
Since
Y\longrightarrow X
is Galois,
Y
is the integral closure of
X
in
\kappa(Y)
, hence
(a)
follows from the universal property of normalization
[
30
,
Tag 0823
]
.

(b)
The result of Keel–Mori
[
17
]
shows that there exists a coarse moduli space
of
[Y/G]
, call it
\widetilde{X}
. Since
Y
is a normal and separated algebraic space, so is
\widetilde{X}
. The action of
G
on

is generically free. We conclude that
\kappa(\widetilde{X})=\kappa(X)
, so the natural morphism
\widetilde{X}\longrightarrow X
is birational.
Since

and

are finite and surjective, so is
\widetilde{X}\longrightarrow X
.
Thus,
\widetilde{X}\longrightarrow X
is a finite birational morphism
of normal separated algebraic spaces, hence
an isomorphism by the Zariski’s Main Theorem
[
30
,
Tag 082K
]
.

2.2.
Pushout lemmas

To prove the existence of saturation, we need to
“glue”
to
X
the
missing points of its saturated model. To do this, we first gather useful
technical results on gluing of algebraic spaces. Recall that
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
is the
category of surfaces with big open embeddings, and
{\mathcal{D}}
is its full subcategory
of schematic surfaces.

Lemma 2.4

(Pushouts in
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
)
.

Consider big open embeddings
X_{0}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X_{1}
,
X_{0}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X_{2}
of surfaces.
Then there exists a pushout
X=X_{1}\sqcup_{X_{0}}X_{2}

in the category of separated algebraic spaces.
Moreover,
X
is a surface
which is schematic if
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}
are schematic,
and the natural maps
X_{1}\longrightarrow X
and
X_{2}\longrightarrow X
are big open embeddings of surfaces.

In particular, the categories
\mathop{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{alg}}}\nolimits
and
{\mathcal{D}}
admit pushouts.

By Lemma

, the surfaces

4.
Affinisation of saturated surfaces

In this section we prove Theorem
C
,
which implies that a saturated surface is proper over its affinisation,
provided the latter is non-trivial.
To do this, we start by recalling the definition of affinisation and its basic properties.
Next, we prove that a surface is saturated if and only if the boundary of its
compactification is a divisor with no negative definite connected components
(Proposition
4.8
, cf.
[
5
, Theorem 2.14]
),
and use it to prove the main result (Theorem
4.10
).
We comment on the surjectivity of the affinisation morphism
(Proposition
4.12
)
and its relation to the Iitaka fibration associated to the boundary divisor
(Remark
4.13
).

4.1.
The affinisation of an algebraic space

We now recall basic properties of the affinisation. For a more detailed discussion in the setting of locally ringed spaces, we refer to
[
12
, Proposition 3.4]
.

The affinisation functor maps a surface
X
to its
affinisation

X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}:=\mathop{\textrm{Spec}}\nolimits H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
, and a morphism
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
of surfaces to a morphism
f^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\colon X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\longrightarrow Y^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
of affine schemes induced by the morphism
f^{\sharp}\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}\longrightarrow f_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}

of sheaves on
Y
.
By
[
30
,
Tag 081X
]
, every surface has an
affinisation morphism

\alpha_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
which satisfies the following universal property: every morphism from
X
to an affine scheme factors uniquely through
\alpha_{X}
. It follows that for every morphism of surfaces
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
, the morphisms
\alpha_{Y}\circ f
and
f^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\circ\alpha_{X}
from
X
to
Y^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
coincide, that is, the affinisation morphism induces a natural transformation.

Let
X
be a surface. By
[
29
, Corollary 6.2]
the ring
H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
is a finitely generated algebra of dimension at most
2
, hence
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is an affine variety. We note that this no longer holds in higher dimension, see
[
7
, Example 3.2.3]
or
[
32
]
for an explicit example of dimension
3
.

Since
X
is normal, by the universal property of the normalisation so is
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
.
If
\iota\colon U\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X
is a big open embedding,
then
\iota^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\colon U^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is an isomorphism
by Hartogs’s lemma.
Hence the affinisations of
U
and
X
coincide,
i.e.,
\alpha_{U}=\alpha_{X}\circ\iota
.
In particular, the affinisation of
X
coincides
with the affinisation of its schematic locus
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sch}}\nolimits}
, and of its saturated model
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X
be a proper birational morphism of surfaces.
Then the composition
\alpha_{X}\circ\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is the affinisation morphism
\alpha_{Y}
of
Y
.

As geometric fibres of a birational morphism are connected,
[
30
,
Tag 0A1B
]
implies that
\pi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
. Hence,
\pi^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is the identity morphism. Since the affinisation morphism is a natural transformation, we conclude that
\alpha_{Y}\circ\pi=\pi^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\circ\alpha_{X}=\alpha_{X}
.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
be a surface. Then the affinisation morphism
\alpha_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is dominant. Moreover, if
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\leqslant 1
, then
\alpha_{X}
is surjective.

If
\alpha_{X}
is not dominant, then its image is contained in a proper closed subspace
Z
of
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
, which is affine since
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is; contrary to the universal property of
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
. If
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\leqslant 1
, then since
\alpha_{X}
is dominant, its image is affine, hence equal to
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
by the universal property.

∎

Proposition 4.3

(cf.
[
5
, Theorem 2.12]
)
.

Let
X
be a surface such that the affinisation morphism

is proper.
Then

Since
\eta_{X}\colon X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
is a big open embedding, we have
\alpha_{X}=\alpha_{X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}}\circ\eta_{X}
. By assumption,
\alpha_{X}
is proper, hence so is

[

,

]
. Thus,

In order to reduce our setting to complements of divisors in smooth projective surfaces,
we use the following consequence of Chow’s lemma and resolution of surface singularities.

Proposition 4.4

(Resolution of singularities)
.

Let

(a)

.

. We now prove part

By the Nagata compactification theorem,

.
Applying Chow’s lemma

Remark 4.5

(Intersection form)
.

Proposition

(a)
allows to define a
\mathbb{Q}
-valued intersection product for proper Weil divisors on a normal surface
X
as in
[
26
]
. Namely, let
\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X
be as in

(a)
, and let

be its exceptional divisor, with components

. On
\overline{Y}
we have the standard intersection product, and the intersection matrix
[E_{i}\cdot E_{j}]
of

is negative definite. Hence we can define a pullback of a proper Weil divisor

as the
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
\pi^{*}D=\pi^{-1}_{*}D+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}E_{i}
, where
\pi^{-1}_{*}D
is the proper transform of
D
, and

are unique rational numbers such that
\pi^{*}D\cdot E_{i}=0
for all

. Now if
D^{\prime}
is another proper divisor on
X
, we put
D\cdot D^{\prime}=\pi^{*}D\cdot\pi^{*}D^{\prime}
.
One readily checks, see
[
26
]
,
that this definition is compatible with the projection formula
and, in particular, does not depend on
\pi
.

We say that a proper Weil divisor on a normal surface is
negative

(
semi
-)
definite
if its intersection matrix is so.
We summarize basic properties of the intersection form
used throughout the paper.
They are well known,
see e.g.
[
11
, Examples 7.1.16, 8.3.11]
.

is smooth.
Clearly, if
\pi^{*}D
is negative (semi-)definite, then so is
D=\pi_{*}\pi^{*}D
.
Assume that

is negative (semi-)definite
and let

be a Weil divisor with support contained in
(\pi^{*}D)_{\textnormal{red}}
.
Then

, where
P\coloneqq\pi^{*}\pi_{*}G

is a pullback of a divisor with support in
D
, and

has support in the exceptional divisor of
\pi
.
Then
P\cdot E=0
and
E^{2}\leqslant 0
, with strict inequality if
E\neq 0
.
It follows that
G^{2}\leqslant P^{2}\leqslant 0
,
so
\pi^{*}D
is negative semi-definite.
Moreover, if
D
is negative definite and
G\neq 0
,
then either

, so
G^{2}<0
, or
P=0
and
E\neq 0
, so

, as needed.

Finally, assume that
D
is effective, and write
\pi^{*}D=D^{\prime}+E
, where
D^{\prime}
is the proper transform of

, and
E=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}E_{i}
, where

are all exceptional curves in the preimage of

. We need to show that

for all

. We have
0\leqslant D^{\prime}\cdot E_{i}=(\pi^{*}D-E)\cdot E_{i}=-E\cdot E_{i}
, and the inequality is strict for at least one

in each connected component of
\bigcup_{i}E_{i}
.
Hence by
[
20
, Lemma 3.41]
, we have

for all
i
, as needed.

(b)
We argue as in
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
. We can assume that
D
is reduced. By Proposition
4.4
(a)
, there exists a proper birational morphism
\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X
such that
Y
is smooth. By
(a)
, the preimage of
D
is negative definite.
By Artin’s contractibility criterion
[

, Corollary 6.12(b)]
,
there is a proper birational morphism of surfaces

which contracts

to points and is an isomorphism on

. By Lemma

below,

factors as

, where

is the required proper birational morphism.

(c)
Like before, let
\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X
be a proper birational morphism such that

is smooth projective. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the subspace of
\operatorname{NS}_{\mathbb{Q}}(Y)
spanned by the exceptional curves of
\pi
. Then
(\mathcal{E},\cdot)
is negative definite. The pullback

gives an embedding
(\operatorname{NS}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X),\cdot)\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow(\operatorname{NS}_{\mathbb{Q}}(Y),\cdot)
, such that

.
The Hodge Index Theorem asserts that the intersection form on

has signature

which gives the required signature

\operatorname{NS}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)
.
For the last statement, note that if

then

is effective by

,
so for an ample divisor

on

, as needed.

∎

In the above proof we used the following known result on uniqueness of contractions, cf.

.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
D
be a proper divisor on a surface
Y
. Let
\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X
be a proper birational morphism of surfaces which maps
D
to a point and is an isomorphism on
Y\setminus D
. Let
\varphi\colon Y\longrightarrow X^{\prime}
be any proper morphism of separated algebraic spaces of finite type over
{k}
, such that
\varphi(D)
is a point. Then there is a unique proper morphism
\sigma\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\prime}
such that
\varphi=\sigma\circ\pi
.

Since
\pi\colon Y\longrightarrow X
and
\varphi\colon Y\longrightarrow X^{\prime}
are proper,
the image
Z
of
(\pi,\varphi)\colon Y\longrightarrow X\times X^{\prime}
is closed
and the projections
p\colon Z\longrightarrow X
and
p^{\prime}\colon Z\longrightarrow X^{\prime}
are proper
[
30
,
Tag 0AGD
]
.
Since
Y
is integral, so is
Z
. For
x\in X
we have
p^{-1}(x)=\{x\}\times\varphi(\pi^{-1}(x))
, which is a point,
hence
p
is one-to-one. Since
\pi=p\circ(\pi,\varphi)\colon Y\longrightarrow Z\longrightarrow X
is birational and
Z
is integral,
p\colon X\longrightarrow Z
is birational, too.
Thus
p\colon Z\longrightarrow X
is a proper, birational, one-to-one morphism of normal algebraic spaces, hence an isomorphism by the Zariski’s Main Theorem.
Now
p^{\prime}\circ p^{-1}\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\prime}
is the required morphism
\sigma
. It is unique as
X^{\prime}
is separated and
\sigma=\varphi\circ\pi^{-1}
on
\pi(Y\setminus D)
.

∎

We can now prove the following analogue of

.

Proposition 4.8

(Criterion for saturatedness, see Theorem
D
)
.

Let

be an open embedding of a surface
X

into a proper surface
\overline{X}
, and let

.
Then

is saturated if and only if
D

is a divisor with no negative definite connected components.

Assume that

is saturated. Let
C
be a connected component of

which is negative definite, or a point.
By Artin’s criterion, see Lemma
4.6
(b)
, there is a proper birational morphism

of algebraic spaces which maps

to a point and is an isomorphism on

. Put

. Then the restriction
\pi|_{X}\colon X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow W
is an open embedding such that the complement of its image is the point

, so

is not saturated.

Assume now that

is a divisor with no negative definite connected components. We apply Proposition

(a)
to the saturation

. Let

(a)
, and let

, we have

. Since

is an open subspace of
\overline{X}
,

is an open subspace of

, and since

is proper,

is closed in

. Hence

is a union of connected components of

. But the latter are not negative definite, so they cannot be contracted. Thus

, so

∎

Remark 4.9

(Constructing
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
from
\overline{X}
)
.

Proposition

provides an easy recipe to construct the saturation of

from any compactification
\overline{X}
of

. Indeed, split
\overline{X}\setminus X
into the divisorial part

and zero-dimensional part

, let

Theorem 4.10

(Theorem
C
)
.

Let
X
be a normal surface with
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}>0
.
Then
X
is saturated if and only if the affinisation morphism

X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is proper.

Remark 4.11

(Necessity of the assumptions in Theorem
4.10
)
.

(a)

Examples
6.1
and
6.3
(b)
show that the assumption
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}>0
is necessary, i.e. there exist non-proper saturated surfaces with trivial affinisations.

(b)

Example
6.4
shows that if
X
is only scheme-saturated, then the affinisation morphism
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
can be non-proper regardless of the dimension of
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
.

If
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is proper,
then
X
is saturated by Proposition
4.3
.
Assume that
X
is saturated.
By Proposition
4.4

we can assume that
X
is an open subspace of a smooth projective surface
\overline{X}
,
and the complement
D\coloneqq\overline{X}\setminus X
is a divisor.
Embed
X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
as a closed subspace of some affine space
\mathbb{A}^{N}
,
let
\overline{Y}
be its closure in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
,
and let
B
be the scheme-theoretic intersection of
\overline{Y}

with the hyperplane at infinity,
so
B
is an ample divisor with support equal to
\overline{Y}\setminus X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
.
Blowing up over
D
we can assume that the affinisation morphism

\alpha\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
extends to a proper morphism

\overline{\alpha}\colon\overline{X}\longrightarrow\overline{Y}
.
Since
\alpha_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}={\mathcal{O}}_{X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}}
, the morphism
\alpha
has connected fibres.
Replacing
\overline{Y}
by its normalization,
we get a proper morphism
\overline{\alpha}\colon\overline{X}\longrightarrow\overline{Y}
with connected fibres, such that
\overline{\alpha}|_{X}=\alpha
and
\overline{Y}\setminus X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
supports an ample divisor
B
.
The pullback
A
of the ample divisor

is a divisor whose support is contained in
D
.

Assume
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=2
. Since
B
is ample, we have
0<B^{2}=A^{2}
,
and the support of
B
is connected
[
14
, Corollary III.7.9]
,
hence the same holds for
A
.
We claim that
D
is connected.
Suppose the contrary, so
D
has a connected component
D_{0}
which is disjoint
from the support of
A
.
By the Hodge Index Theorem,
D_{0}
is negative definite.
Since
X
is saturated, we get a contradiction
by Proposition
4.8
.
Therefore,
D
is connected,
so the implication (b)
\implies
(a) of
[
29
, Proposition 3.2]

shows that the affinisation morphism
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is proper, as needed.

Assume
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=1
. Then
\overline{\alpha}\colon\overline{X}\longrightarrow\overline{Y}

is a morphism onto a smooth curve, and
A
is a sum of its fibres.
In particular,
A^{2}=0
.
Suppose
D
has an irreducible component
H
such that

\overline{\alpha}|_{H}\colon H\longrightarrow\overline{Y}
is surjective.
Then
H\cdot A>0
, so we can choose an integer
m\gg 0
such that
H\cdot(H+mA)>0
.
We have
A\cdot(H+mA)=A\cdot H>0
, so
(H+mA)^{2}>0
.
Thus
H+mA
is nef and big, see
[
23
, Theorem 2.2.16]
.
This means that the rational map
\varphi
induced by some multiple of
|H+mA|

is birational onto its image.
Since
X
is disjoint from the support of
H+mA
,
the map
\varphi
restricts to a birational morphism
X\longrightarrow Y
to an affine surface
Y
.
By the universal property of the affinisation,
\varphi
factors through

X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
, a contradiction as
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=1
.
Thus,
D
is contained in the fibres of
\overline{\alpha}
.

If a connected component of
D
is properly contained in a fibre,
then it is negative definite by Zariski’s lemma,
see Lemma
5.2
(c)
,
which is impossible by Proposition
4.8
.
Hence, every fibre of
\overline{\alpha}
which meets

is contained in
X
.
Thus, we have
X=\overline{\alpha}^{-1}(\alpha(X))=\overline{\alpha}^{-1}(X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits})

since
\alpha(X)=X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
by Lemma
4.2
.
We conclude that
\alpha\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is a base change of the proper morphism

\overline{\alpha}\colon\overline{X}\longrightarrow\overline{Y}
along the inclusion

X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\overline{Y}
, hence
\alpha
is proper, as claimed.

∎

4.3.
Surjectivity of the affinisation morphism of saturated surfaces

Theorem
4.10
and Lemma

imply that if

is saturated, then the affinisation

is surjective. It turns out that this weaker property holds also for scheme-saturated surfaces.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let

be a scheme-saturated surface. Then

is surjective.

If
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}\leqslant 1
, then
X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is surjective by Lemma
4.2
.
Assume
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=2
.
Since
X
is scheme-saturated, Lemma
3.12

implies that the image of
X
in its saturation

coincides with the schematic locus of
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
.
Because
X
is a big open subspace of
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
,
the affinisations of

and
X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}
agree, i.e. we have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X=(X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits})^{\mathop{\textrm{sch}}\nolimits}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=(X^{\mathop{\textrm{sat}}\nolimits})^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits},}

where the slant arrows are the affinisation morphisms. By Theorem

, the affinisation morphism

is a proper morphism. Since

, it is birational. Hence any fibre

of

is either a point in the schematic locus

, or a divisor, which meets the big open subspace

, as needed.

∎

Remark 4.13

(The affinisation is the restriction of the Iitaka fibration)
.

Assume that
X
is the complement of a reduced divisor

on a smooth projective surface
\overline{X}
(recall that we can always pass to this setting via Proposition

).
Then
[

, Proposition 10.1]
shows that

is equal to the Iitaka dimension
\kappa(\overline{X},D)
,
that is, the maximum of the dimensions of the images of the maps
\varphi_{m}
induced by the linear systems
|mD|
, for all

.

We claim that the affinisation morphism
\alpha\colon X\longrightarrow X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}
is the restriction of the Iitaka fibration
\varphi_{\infty}\colon\overline{X}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N}
for

, which by definition is a common birational model of the maps
\varphi_{m}
for all
m\gg 0
, see
[
16
, Theorems 8.6, 10.3]
or
[
23
, Theorem 2.1.33]
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

has no negative definite connected components, i.e.

is saturated, see Proposition
4.8
. Indeed, let
C
be a negative definite connected component of

. For every

we have

, so

lies in the fixed part of

, hence the Iitaka fibrations of

and

coincide. In turn, since

is a big open subspace of the algebraic space obtained from

by contracting

, we see that the affinisations of

and

coincide, as needed.

If

, then both

and

are constant. Assume

. The Iitaka fibration

is regular on

factors through

from

More generally, if

5.
Saturated open subspaces

In this section we prove Theorem
D
, characterising saturated surfaces
X
in terms of the boundary divisors
D
of their compactifications, according to the dimension of their affinisations, or equivalently, to the Iitaka dimension of
D
, see Remark
4.13
or
[
16
, §8.3c]
. We pay special attention to the case
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=0
and
D\neq 0
, which, by Theorem
C
, corresponds to saturated surfaces which are non-proper over their affinisations. We describe these surfaces in Section
5.3
.

5.1.
Numerical properties of boundaries of saturated surfaces

To state our result in a convenient way, we pose a definition which abstracts numerical properties of a fibre of a fibration, and list its basic consequences.
Here by a
fibration
we mean a morphism with connected fibres from a surface onto a smooth curve.
We say that a divisor
F
is
supported on
a reduced divisor
D
, or that
D

supports

F
, if
F_{\textnormal{red}}=D
, i.e. the support of
F
equals
D
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a non-zero, proper, reduced Weil divisor on a normal surface is

of fibre type
if it is connected and negative semi-definite,
but not negative definite (see Remark
4.5
).
We say that a divisor of fibre type is a
false fibre

if it does not support a fibre of a fibration.

Zariski’s lemma
[
4
, II.8.2]
implies that a fibre of a proper fibration is supported on a divisor of fibre type, in particular, has the following numerical properties.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
D
be a divisor of fibre type on a surface
X
. Then the following hold.

(a)

There is a non-zero divisor
F
with support contained in
D
, such that
F^{2}=0
.

(b)

We have
F_{\textnormal{red}}=D
(i.e.
F
is supported on the entire
D
),
F\cdot C=0
for every irreducible component
C
of
D
, and replacing
F
by
-F
if needed, we can assume that
F
is effective.

(c)

Every non-zero divisor with support properly contained in
D
is negative definite.

(d)

The divisor
F
as in
(a)
is unique up to taking multiples.

(e)

Let
Y\longrightarrow X
be a proper birational morphism of surfaces. Then
\pi^{-1}(D)
is of fibre type, and it is a false fibre if and only if
D
is
a false fibre.

(f)

Let
X\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\overline{X}
be an open embedding of surfaces. If
D
is a fibre of a fibration
f\colon X\longrightarrow B
, then
f
extends to a fibration
\overline{f}\colon\overline{X}\longrightarrow\overline{B}
for some open embedding
B\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow\overline{B}
. In particular, a false fibre on
\overline{X}
which is contained in
X
is also a false fibre on
X
.

(a)
This follows from directly from Definition
5.1
.

(b)
Choose one divisor
F
as in
(a)
.
Write
F=F_{+}-F_{-}
, where
F_{+}
,

F_{-}
are effective and have no common components.
Replacing
F
by
-F
if needed, we can assume
F_{+}\neq 0
.
We have
F_{+}\cdot F_{-}\geqslant 0
and
F_{\pm}^{2}\leqslant 0
since
D

is negative semi-definite.
Hence
0=F^{2}=F_{+}^{2}-2F_{+}\cdot F_{-}+F_{-}^{2}\leqslant 0
, so
F_{+}^{2}=0
.
Suppose
D
has an irreducible component
C
such that
F_{+}\cdot C\neq 0
.
Then we have
(mF_{+}+C)^{2}=mF_{+}\cdot C+C^{2}>0
for
m\gg 0
or
m\ll 0
,
contrary to the fact that
D
is negative semi-definite.
Since
D
is connected, we get

(F_{+})_{\textnormal{red}}=D
and
F_{+}\cdot C=0
for all
C
, as needed.

(c)

Suppose
Z\subsetneq D
is not negative definite.
Since
D
is of fibre type,
there is a non-zero divisor
F_{Z}
such that
(F_{Z})_{\textnormal{red}}\subseteq Z
and
F_{Z}^{2}=0
, so
(F_{Z})_{\textnormal{red}}=D
by
(b)
, a contradiction.

(d)
Suppose the contrary,
so there are divisors
F,F^{\prime}
as in
(a)

and integers
a,b>0
such that the divisor
aF-bF^{\prime}
is non-zero
and has support strictly contained in
D
.
Part
(c)
implies that
0>(aF-bF^{\prime})^{2}=-2abF\cdot F^{\prime}
,
so
F\cdot F^{\prime}>0
. But then
(F+F^{\prime})^{2}=2F\cdot F^{\prime}>0
,
a contradiction with the fact that
D
is negative semi-definite.

(e)
The fact that
\pi^{-1}(D)
is of fibre type follows from Lemma
4.6
(a)
.
If
D
supports a fibre of a fibration
X\longrightarrow B
, then
\pi^{-1}(D)
supports a fibre of the fibration
Y\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow B
.
Conversely, assume that
\pi^{-1}(D)
supports a fibre
F
of a fibration
f\colon Y\longrightarrow B
.
We claim that the exceptional divisor of
\pi
is contained in the fibres of
f
.
Suppose the contrary, so for some exceptional curve
H
, the restriction
f|_{H}\colon H\longrightarrow B
is dominant.
Since
H
is proper,
f|_{H}\colon H\longrightarrow B
is surjective, so
H
meets the fibre
F
.
It follows that the point
\pi(H)
lies on
\pi(F_{\textnormal{red}})=D
, so
H\subseteq\pi^{-1}(D)=F_{\textnormal{red}}
, a contradiction.
The above claim and Lemma
4.7
show that
f
descends to a fibration of
X
with one of the fibres supported on
D
, as needed.

(f)
Replacing
\overline{X}
by its normal compactification, we can assume that
\overline{X}
is proper. Let
\overline{B}
be a normal compactification of
B
. By the Nagata compactification theorem, the composition of
f\colon X\longrightarrow B
with the open embedding

5.2.
Bounding false fibres

Our next goal is to improve Proposition
5.4
with some criteria
allowing to distinguish case
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=1
from
\dim X^{\mathop{\textrm{aff}}\nolimits}=0
; or
equivalently, a false fibre (Definition
5.1
) from a genuine
fibre of a fibration. Examples
6.1
(c)
and

6.3
show that purely numerical criterion does not exist. Our
criterion, see Theorem

D
(c)
, is similar to the contractibility criterion

[
29
, 3.3]
, see Remark
5.10
.
It is based on the main result of this section, Corollary
5.8
,
which implies that any disjoint collection of false fibres consists of at most two elements.

Its proof uses the notion of the Albanese variety, which we now briefly recall.
For a smooth projective variety
X
, its
Albanese variety
is an Abelian
variety
\operatorname{Alb}(X)
together with the
Albanese morphism

a_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)
such that the image

a_{X}(X)
generates
\operatorname{Alb}(X)
as a group, and for
any morphism
f\colon X\longrightarrow A
to an abelian variety
we have
f=h\circ a_{X}+c
for a unique homomorphism
h\colon\operatorname{Alb}(X)\longrightarrow A

and a unique constant
c\in A
.
For the existence and basic properties of
\operatorname{Alb}(X)

see e.g.
[
21
, §IV]
.
By Theorem VI.1 loc. cit, the dual to
\operatorname{Alb}(X)
is the
Picard variety
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}_{\textnormal{red}}(X)
parametrising line bundles
which are algebraically equivalent to
0
, modulo linear equivalence.
By
[
9
, 9.6.3]
(see also
[
11
, 19.3.1]
for the case
{k}=\mathbb{C}
),
every numerically trivial divisor on
X

has a multiple which corresponds to an algebraically trivial line bundle,
so its linear equivalence class falls in
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)
.
If
X
is a smooth projective curve,
then
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)\cong\operatorname{Alb}(X)
is isomorphic to the Jacobian of
X
,
in particular
\dim\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)
is equal to the genus
g(X)
of
X

[
21
, Theorem VI.3]
.
If
X
is a smooth projective surface, then
\dim\operatorname{Pic}_{\textnormal{red}}^{0}(X)
is called the

irregularity
of
X
and is denoted by
q(X)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
D
be a false fibre on a smooth projective surface
X
,
such that all irreducible components
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
of
D
are smooth.
Then the restriction homomorphism

\operatorname{Pic}_{\textnormal{red}}^{0}(X)\longrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D_{i})

has finite kernel.

The restriction homomorphism
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}_{\textnormal{red}}(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D_{i})
is dual to the homomorphism

\operatorname{Alb}(D_{i})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)
induced by the inclusion
D_{i}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X
.
Therefore, by
[
21
, Proposition V.2]

it is enough to prove that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Alb}(D_{i})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)
is surjective.
Denote the image of this homomorphism by
A
,
and let
h\colon X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)/A
be the composition of the Albanese morphism

a_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)
with the quotient
\operatorname{Alb}(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)/A
.
Since
a_{X}(X)
generates
\operatorname{Alb}(X)
, its image
h(X)
generates
\operatorname{Alb}(X)/A
.
Thus, it is enough to prove that
h(X)
is a point.

The restriction
a_{X}|_{D_{i}}\colon D_{i}\lhook\joinrel\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)
factors as the composition of the Albanese morphism
D_{i}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(D_{i})
, the above homomorphism
\operatorname{Alb}(D_{i})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)
, and a translation. Therefore,
a_{X}(D_{i})\subseteq\operatorname{Alb}(X)
is a coset of
\operatorname{Alb}(D_{i})
, so
h(D_{i})
is a point. Since
D
is connected, it follows that
h\colon X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Alb}(X)/A
maps
D
to a point.

Because
D
is not negative definite,
we have
\dim h(X)\leqslant 1
.
If
\dim h(X)=1
, then the Stein factorisation of
h
gives a fibration of
X

with one of its fibres supported on
D
,
contrary to the definition of a false fibre.
Thus,
\dim h(X)=0
, as needed.

.

Remark 5.7

Let

Remark 5.9

(Characterization of false fibres)
.

Let
D
be a divisor of fibre type on a proper surface
X
. Corollary
5.8
implies that
D
is a false fibre if and only if there exists at most one divisor of fibre type which is disjoint from
D
.

Remark 5.10

(Comparison with
[
29
, 3.3]
)
.

Let
D
be a connected reduced divisor on a projective surface
X
. If
D
is negative definite, then
[
29
, Theorem 3.3]
asserts that
D
supports a fibre of a projective birational morphism
X\longrightarrow Y
if and only if the complement
X\setminus D
contains an effective divisor
R
such that
R^{2}>0
.

In turn, if
D
is of fibre type, then Remark
5.9
asserts that
D
supports a fibre of a projective fibration
X\longrightarrow Y
if and only if
X\setminus D
contains an effective divisor
R
such that
R^{2}=0
and

has non-connected support.

In the Minimal Model Program, morphisms

as above are called

and

contractions, respectively, see
[

, Definition 1.5]
. In each case, if

exists, then the required

can be obtained as a pullback of a very ample divisor on

. Thus both criteria can be informally summarized as follows:
a contraction of

exists if and only if

contains an effective divisor which is a candidate for a pullback of a very ample one.

5.3.

With a description of false fibres at hand, we can now complete the proof of our last main result, Theorem
