Resistance pathways and next-generation treatments in chronic lymphocytic leukemia
Authors: Rawane Bellemrrabet, Ezzahra Rachid, Meriem Lahmouad, Youssef Aboussaleh, Waleed Al Abdulmonem, Abdelhakim Bouyahya, Oualid Abboussi, Loubna Amahdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is still the most common form of lymphoid malignancy, however, despite numerous developments in therapeutic approaches over the last ten years, CLL continues to pose important clinical challenges. The majority of currently available treatments target B-cell receptor signaling through either targeting Bruton Tyrosine Kinase (BTK) or B-cell Lymphoma 2 (BCL-2), while Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase (PI3K) inhibitors play a more limited role in current clinical practice. These agents have significantly changed the course of CLL disease; nevertheless, drug resistance, especially to covalent BTK inhibitors and venetoclax, remains an important problem that has led to the development of several new approaches: targeting BTK via a noncovalent approach, BTK degradation, Chimeric Antigen Receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, and bispecific antibodies. Moreover, novel discoveries concerning the microenvironment of CLL have been reported, revealing how components such as neutrophil reprogramming and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) can promote cancer cell survival and lead to resistance to treatment. Thus, from the discussion above, it is clear that the resistance problem has become complicated and complex and requires the targeting of not only cancer cells but also the environment surrounding them. Future treatments will likely involve combination therapy based on predictive biomarkers.Keywords: Chronic lymphocytic leukemia, Signaling pathways, Targeted therapies, Bruton's tyrosine kinase, Drug resistance

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is the most common type of leukemia worldwide. It involves the slow accumulation of mature Cluster of Differentiation 5 positive (CD5+) B lymphocytes in the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and lymphoid tissues [1]. CLL mostly occurs in older patients, with a diagnosis typically being made at the age of 70 years through routine blood tests. Symptoms are nonspecific, and include fatigue, night sweats, fever, and recurrent infections [2].

Clinically, the diagnosis of CLL is made on the basis of persistent lymphocytosis greater than 5 × 10⁹/L for a period of three months or more, supported by specific cell morphology and immunophenotyping, often established using the Matutes score [2]. The treatment of this disease is increasingly informed by the genetic makeup of the patient; for example, Tumor Protein 53 (TP53) mutations, including 17p deletion (del17p), predict a poor response to standard chemotherapy, whereas somatic hypermutation of Immunoglobulin Heavy Chain Variable region (IGHV) implies a better prognosis [3, 4].

Traditionally, the approach involves the application of chemoimmunotherapy protocols, primarily the Fludarabine+Cyclophosphamide+Rituximab (FCR) regimen, which is effective but is associated with complications such as infections and neutropenia [5]. The introduction of targeted therapy has changed treatment protocols, especially inhibitors of B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathway, such as Tyrosine Kinase (BTK), Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase (PI3K), and B-cell Lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) inhibitors. Compared with chemoimmunotherapy, targeted therapy is safer and more effective, especially for high-risk patients [6].

However, drug resistance in CLL is still a major challenge, commonly resulting from mutations in the BTK pathway, activation of alternate survival pathways, or other signaling pathway components, which reduce treatment efficacy. In light of the developments discussed above, it is important to note that CLL is still complicated due to various resistance mechanisms and treatment toxicities. This underscores the importance of understanding molecular mechanisms, including signaling pathways, microenvironmental factors, and genetic drivers that support leukemic cell survival [7].

The present literature review is based on a comprehensive analysis of peer-reviewed papers available in PubMed databases during 2007–2025. The inclusion criteria for scientific sources included the important of studies for the biology of CLL, signaling pathways and mechanisms of resistance. Original research papers and reviews with high impact factors were considered for the analysis. This literature review is organized as follows. First, we present the biological and genetic background of CLL, followed by an overview of key signaling pathways relevant for CLL progression. We then discuss current therapeutic strategies and emerging treatments, before focusing on the major mechanisms of resistance and their clinical implications. Overall, this review provides a comprehensive and updated overview of resistance mechanisms in CLL, while also highlighting potential combinatorial therapeutic strategies and novel biomarkers that may personalize treatment approaches.

Biological basis and pathogenesis of chronic lymphocytic leukemia

Cellular origin and genomic subtypes of CLL

The development of CLL is mostly attributed to the progressive accumulation of genetic and epigenetic mutations that occur early in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). Early mutations are thought to be responsible for the initiation of leukemic transformation, as suggested by rare cases in which premalignant clones are transplanted into allogeneic patients undergoing HSC transplantation, indicating that early progenitor cells can already initiate disease lesions. Recurrent mutations in genes such as Neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 1 (NOTCH1), Splicing Factor 3B Subunit 1 (SF3B1), B-Raf Proto-Oncogene (BRAF), TP53, Exportin1 (XPO1), Mediator Complex Subunit 12 (MED12), Nuclear Factor Kappa B Inhibitor Epsilon (NFKBIE), and Early Growth Response 2 (EGR2) have been identified in purified HSCs from CLL patients, supporting the idea that early leukemogenic events may arise in the bone marrow [8, 9]. In addition, non-coding mutations affecting regulatory regions of key genes have been identified. Notably, mutations in the 3′ untranslated region of NOTCH1 are predicted to disrupt the PEST domain, resulting in increased protein stability and sustained NOTCH signaling. Furthermore, alterations in enhancer regions such as those affecting Paired Box 5 (PAX5) and deregulation of microRNA clusters particularly miR-15a and miR-16-1, further contribute to transcriptional dysregulation in CLL [10].

CLL may also derive from mature B lymphocytes expressing CD5+, typically associated with the innate-like B1 subset producing natural Immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies. This development origin has led to numerous immunophenotypic and functional features of CLL cells [11].

Clinically and molecularly, CLL may be divided into two categories, depending upon whether the IGHV region is mutated or unmutated. Mutated IGHV (IGHV-M) represents leukemic cells that have already been subjected to somatic hypermutation and, therefore, suggests a germinal center origin as well as antigen exposure and usually has a benign course. Unmutated IGHV (IGHV-UM) B-CLL, which is derived from naive/pre-germinal center B cells, usually has a poor prognosis [12].

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) has improved our knowledge regarding the heterogeneity of CLL, with the discovery of an overall mutation burden of approximately 0.9 mutations per megabase, which includes point mutations, copy number variations, and structural variants. Interestingly, IGHV-M patients appear to have more mutations than IGHV-UM patients do, indicating different evolution pathways for each subtype [13]. SF3B1 mutations occur in 10–15% of cases, resulting in defects in RNA splicing, DNA damage repair, and telomere biology [14, 15]. NOTCH1 mutations, which are found in 10% of patients, maintain oncogenic pathway and MYC Proto-Oncogene (MYC) overexpression and are often accompanied by Trisomy 12, which associated with more aggressive disease phenotype [16, 17]. Moreover, mutations in inflammatory and NF-κB signaling regulatory factors, such as NFKBIE, TNF Receptor Associated Factor 3 (TRAF3), Baculoviral IAP Repeat Containing 3 (BIRC3), and Myeloid Differentiation Primary Response 88 (MYD88), result in constitutive activation or dysregulation of the expression of NF-κB, which increases the transcription of pro-survival and anti-apoptosis genes, contributing to CLL pathogenesis. Mutations in Protection of Telomeres 1 (POT1), which are relatively prevalent among IGHV-UM CLL patients (3–7%), impair telomere protection and cause chromosomal instability [18].

Cytogenetic abnormalities contribute to increased heterogeneity. The most common mutation is 13q14 deletion (del13q14), which is observed in approximately 60% of patients and is usually linked to the IGHV-M phenotype and favorable prognosis through deletion of the miR-15/16 cluster, which suppresses the expression of BCL2 [2, 4]. In contrast, 11q22-23 deletion (Del11q), which targets the ATM and BIRC3 genes and is detected in approximately 15% of patients, highly correlated with IGHV-UM and poor outcomes. The deletion 17p13 (Del17p13), which occurs in around 10% of cases and targets TP53, suggests chemotherapy-refractory disease and reduced survival, especially among IGHV-UM patients [3, 19]. Trisomy 12, present in nearly 16% of cases and linked with NOTCH1 or TP53 mutations, serves as an intermediate risk marker [17].

Epigenomic analyses have demonstrated that IGHV-UM CLL cells display a more open chromatin landscape enriched for transcription factor families, including NFAT (e.g. NFATC1), FOX (e.g. FOXO1/FOXO3) and TCF/LEF (e.g. LEF1) [20]. These factors likely sustain transcriptional programs critical for cell survival and proliferation, representing novel potential therapeutic targets (Table 1) [5, 21].

Main genetic abnormalities in CLL influencing disease behavior and treatment sensitivity

Genetic lesions driving CLL: frequency and clinical impact

Clinical variability in CLL is based on the occurrence of genetic and cytogenetic aberrations, which affect key biological pathways differently. In addition to being prognostic factors, these aberrations play important roles in determining how patients respond to therapy and the disease progression rate. Genetic aberrations affect cellular processes such as apoptosis and DNA repair, resulting in the disruption of signaling networks critical for leukemic cell survival.

Among these genetic abnormalities, del13q14 is the most common alteration, occurring in approximately 60% of patients. The deletion affects the Deleted in Lymphocytic Leukemia 2 (DLEU2) gene, resulting in decreased production of the microRNAs miR-15 and miR-16. As these molecules regulate the activity of BCL2, which prevents cell death, the deficiency of these molecules results in an increased level of BCL2, which in turn prevents the premature death of leukemic cells. Clinically, this mutation is usually associated with slower disease progression and relatively favorable prognosis [4].

Conversely, del17p13 or TP53 mutation results in a significantly worse outcome. TP53 functions as a critical tumor suppressor gene, playing roles in DNA repair and cell cycle control. Disruption of its function causes the accumulation of genetic abnormalities and reduced sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs. Moreover, del17p occurs in approximately 10% of cases, more specifically among patients with unmutated IGHV, and has been strongly linked to poor prognosis and drug resistance [3].

Similarly, del11q affects the Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) gene, an important mediator of the DNA damage response. Patients with this deletion often present bulky lymphadenopathy and have an unfavorable course of disease. Moreover, trisomy 12 also stands out among the recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities observed alongside mutations in the NOTCH1 gene, as both genetic defects promote proliferation and survival of leukemia cells. Splicing machinery mutations, such as alterations in the SF3B1 gene, lead to impaired RNA maturation, increasing genome instability, and correlate with poor prognosis [14, 17].

Nevertheless, these genetic abnormalities rarely occur in isolation. In CLL, progression usually occurs through the simultaneous acquisition of various mutations, which affects the overall behavior of the disease. This clonal evolution process contributes to treatment resistance and underscores the critical importance of obtaining a detailed genetic profile to guide clinical decision-making (Fig. 1). Advances in next-generation sequencing technologies allow the detection of even sub-clonal mutations despite their relatively low prevalence, providing a better understanding of disease evolutionary trajectories over time [2].

A deep understanding of all these genetic alterations, along with their impact on biology, will be needed to improve prognostic modeling and develop more personalized therapies for CLL.

Microenvironmental interactions fueling CLL progression

CLL cells are constantly moving between peripheral blood and secondary lymphoid organs, preferentially migrating toward specialized proliferation centers known as pseudo-follicles. These niches actively promote the proliferation of leukemic cells through providing microenvironmental signals that promote the survival and proliferation of CLL cells. The process of migration is further enhanced as the follicular dendritic cells within these structures produce chemokines such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) and CXCL13, setting up concentration gradients of the chemokines via their receptors CXCR4 and CXCR5; beyond facilitating migration, this chemokine receptor axis amplifies proliferation and survival signals, thereby sustaining leukemic growth [22].

In addition to niche-dependent trafficking, the wider tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a major role in modulating the functions of CLL cells by supplying pro-survival cues and remodeling the immune environment to sustain leukemic growth. Nurse-like cells (NLCs), derived from circulating monocyte precursors, play a key role in reciprocal interactions with CLL cells that trigger BCR and Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathways, which in turn promote proliferation and anti-apoptotic properties [23]. Similarly, bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) provide supportive functions; contact-dependent interactions inhibit spontaneous and drug-induced apoptosis through the Protein Kinase B (AKT) and NF-κB signaling pathways, whereas soluble factors such as B-cell Activating Factor (BAFF) and A Proliferation-Inducing Ligand (APRIL) enhance this mechanism [8].

Conversely, CLL cells are known to facilitate an immunosuppressive TME through the secretion of cytokines such as Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-10 (IL-10), and Transforming Growth Factor beta (TGF-β), which contribute to the exhaustion of CD4 + T cells and suppress antitumor immunity [24]. Experimental evidence has revealed metabolic interactions and immune checkpoint feedback mechanisms, which have been shown to create positive feedback loops, thereby improving cell survival and drug resistance [25]. CLL cells exhibit an increased reliance on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and glycolysis pathways for generating energy and maintaining survival signals. In addition, the induction of immune checkpoint receptors such as PD-1 and PD-L1 has been shown to impair immune surveillance and facilitate immune escape. This process has been elucidated through functional and mechanistic studies that illustrate the interaction between metabolic reprogramming and immune checkpoint activation in maintaining the survival of leukemia cells in the context of therapeutic intervention [26]. Disrupting these TME dependencies offers the possibility of restoring immune surveillance to enhance CLL treatment, which can be achieved by combining BCR inhibitors with agents targeting stromal crosstalk or chemokine signaling.

Clinical evolution and preleukemic conditions

CLL originates from monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis (MBL), an asymptomatic precancerous condition characterized by the presence of clonal B cells in the blood whose number does not reach the diagnostic criterion of 5,000 cells per microliter. The incidence of MBL increases with age and is associated with the gradual development of genetic and epigenetic changes that lead to clonal expansion; early detection of these alterations enhances the prediction of disease progression and patient stratification [27].

As the disease progresses, leukemia B-cells invade the bone marrow and spread to lymph nodes and the spleen, causing lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and hepatomegaly. This stage is usually accompanied by B symptoms, which include fatigue, night sweats, fever, unintentional weight loss, and early satiety, indicating a high tumor load and systemic inflammation [28].

Moreover, immunosuppression becomes more pronounced, with hypogammaglobulinemia predisposing patients to infections, which are the leading cause of death in CLL patients. T-cell exhaustion and increased expression of immune checkpoint molecules contribute to reduced antitumor immunity and autoimmune phenomena such as hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia [11].

In addition to physical effects, the long-term nature of CLL causes many mental stress and financial burdens, profoundly impacting quality of life. Therefore, appropriate management also requires psychological and social support. In conclusion, CLL develops from asymptomatic MBL proliferation to leukemia where there is a disturbance in immune function, with microenvironment interactions guiding personalized care [29].

Treatments, mechanisms, and therapeutic resistance

For many years, the treatment of CLL has been exclusively dependent on standard chemotherapy involving mainly purine analogs and alkylator agents. The use of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab led to a revolutionary change in CLL management; their combination with chemotherapy led to better therapeutic results from the early 2000 s onward. Targeted therapies have had a significant effect on treatment during the past decade; they offer superior efficacy especially among high-risk patients with TP53 mutations or del17p [30].

Monoclonal antibody treatment in CLL

Despite the lack of curability offered by standard chemoimmunotherapy for most patients, the development of next-generation anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies has revolutionized the treatment paradigm. Compared with those of rituximab, newer molecules such as obinutuzumab and ofatumumab exhibit improved biological efficacy and higher clinical response rates in specific patient populations [31]. Obinutuzumab is a humanized glycoengineered type II anti-CD20 antibody that is designed to improve antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by enhancing interactions with the FcγRIIIa receptor expressed on Natural Killer cells (NK) and macrophages. In addition to the increased recruitment of immune effector cells, B cells death can indeed occur via lysosomal involvement and actin-dependent cytoskeletal reorganization mechanisms, which are absent in first-generation antibodies, thereby leading to superior in vivo depletion of B cells [31, 32]. Compared with rituximab therapy paired with chlorambucil treatment, combination therapy results in significant improvements in progression-free and overall survival [33].

BTK, PI3K, and BCL2 inhibitors

The development of therapies that target survival pathways such as B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling and apoptosis modulation has revolutionized the treatment strategy of CLL [34]. Drugs such as ibrutinib act by forming irreversible covalent bonds with the cysteine at position 481 of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK), blocking the cascade of phosphorylation reactions that regulate leukemic B-cell activation, growth, and survival (Fig. 2) [35]. In addition, ibrutinib blocks the signaling of chemokine receptors, which are essential for CLL cell migration and support from the tumor microenvironment. It is approved for relapsed/refractory CLL and as frontline therapy for cases with TP53 disruptions [36].

Second-generation covalent BTK inhibitors, acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, improve kinase selectivity while reducing off-target toxicities, maintaining clinical efficacy [37]. Mutations at Cysteine 481 (Cys481) represent a common mechanism of resistance to covalent agents, in this setting, non-covalent BTK inhibitors like pirtobrutinib and nemtabrutinib bind independently of Cys481, restoring pathway inhibition in patients with multiple relapses. Recent data from the phase 1/2 BRUIN study (NCT03740529) of pirtobrutinib in previously treated B-cell malignancies showed an overall response rate of 73.3% in patients with relapsed/refractory CLL/SLL previously exposed to a covalent BTK inhibitor; this increased to 82.2% when partial response with lymphocytosis was included. Meanwhile, BTK degraders such as NX-5948 act by inducing ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of both wild type and mutant BTK; early-stage studies show significant BTK reduction and preliminary anti-leukemic activity, supporting ongoing development [38, 39].

PI3K inhibitors such as idelalisib and duvelisib have demonstrated clinical activity in relapsed/refractory CLL by targeting key survival pathways and promoting leukemic cell apoptosis. Idelalisib, a selective PI3Kδ inhibitor, suppresses AKT signaling, whereas duvelisib, a dual PI3Kδ/γ inhibitor, exerts both direct cytotoxic effects and microenvironmental modulation (Fig. 2). However, their clinical use has been substantially limited by an unfavorable toxicity profile, including immune-mediated adverse events such as diarrhea/colitis, hepatitis, and pneumonitis, as well as serious infections and cytopenia [38]. Accordingly, recent guidelines and expert recommendations consider PI3K inhibitors as non-preferred or carefully selected options, generally reserved for patients in whom BTK- and BCL2-directed strategies are not feasible [40, 41].

Complementing these advances, BCL2 inhibition with venetoclax exploits CLL cells’ profound BCL-2 dependence by displacing pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 Interacting Mediator of Cell Death and Bcl-2 Antagonist/Killer (BIM/BAK), rapidly activating mitochondrial apoptosis (Fig. 2). Notably, this approach elicits deep, swift responses even in high-risk subsets [42, 43]. Despite distinctive risks such as tumor lysis syndrome and cytopenias that demand careful dose ramp-up and monitoring, venetoclax plus obinutuzumab has established fixed duration frontline standards per French Innovative Leukemia Organization (FILO) 2023 guidelines (Table 2) [44]. Importantly, next-generation BCL-2 inhibitors such as lisaftoclax are in advanced clinical development and may help address this limitation, as recent phase 1b/2 data showed that lisaftoclax can be administered with a shorter 5–7-day ramp-up, with a low rate of tumor lysis syndrome and encouraging efficacy in CLL [45]. In addition, acquired resistance resulting from BTK/PLCG2 mutations, BCL2 alterations, or microenvironmental evasion highlights the need for sequential combinatorial treatments to address CLL’s dynamic resistance landscape.

Emerging cellular and immunotherapeutic modalities

In addition to small-molecule inhibitors, cellular immunotherapies such as Chimeric Antigen Receptor T (CAR-T) cells have shown promise in treating heavily pre-treated or resistant cases of CLL. CD19-targeted CAR-T cells are known to achieve profound and occasionally long-lasting remission even when BTK inhibitors and BCL2 inhibitors fail. Despite their promising nature, these approaches have not been found to have consistently life-changing efficacy in CLL as seen in B-cell lymphomas, achieving an overall response rate between 40 and 60%, and showing low persistence. These impressive, but inconsistent-results, indicate severe immune-related toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and Immune Effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome (ICANS), which demand rigorous patient selection, specialized management, and close monitoring. However, ongoing CAR-T engineering improvements, such as optimized co-stimulatory domains (4-1BB vs. CD28), humanized constructs, and streamlined manufacturing, may increase persistence, reduce exhaustion, and enhance safety while maintaining antileukemic effectiveness in the near future [38].

Moreover, there are bispecific antibodies capable of engaging T-cells as an out-of-the-box treatment, thus avoiding the complex logistics and technical issues involved in CAR-T-cell treatment. Drugs such as epcoritamab and mosunetuzumab, which are both CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies, involve T cells to kill CLL cells through binding to CD19 or CD20, bypassing personalized cell production. Early-phase clinical trials have shown encouraging response rates (ORRs of 50%−70%) alongside favorable and better toxicity profiles, with mostly low-grade CRS. This profile expands the indications to fragile, multiple relapsed cases who are unable to be treated with CAR-T cells. The fast-immune reprogramming that is inherent to the action of bispecific antibodies makes them very useful in the initial treatment of high-risk or refractory cases, where they may be used even as an outpatient option and in combination with targeted agents to enhance responses and overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment [46]. Notably, the efficacy of both CAR-T-cell therapy and T-cell–targeting bispecific antibodies could be influenced by the profound impairment in T-cell functionality observed in CLL patients, which includes exhaustion, proliferation failure, and faulty immune synapse formation. Since both CAR-T cells and bispecific antibodies rely heavily on functional competent T cells, such an immune environment might influence the response to these treatments, limit CAR-T-cell persistence, and potentially reduce bispecific antibody activity [47].

Mechanisms of resistance to targeted therapies

Despite these developments, drug resistance limits the curative capacity of CLL treatments via various overlapping mechanisms. Resistance to covalent BTK inhibitors caused mainly by mutations in the kinase domain of BTK, such as the C481S mutation, which blocks irreversible binding. Similarly, the activation of mutations in Phospholipase C Gamma 2 (PLCG2) allows independent signaling of the BCR pathway regardless of BTK. In addition, deletions of 8p reduce the level of expression of TNF-Related Apoptosis-Inducing Ligand (TRAIL) death receptors, hence increasing the resistance of cells to therapy by improving their survival rate [48–50]. CLL cells also activate alternative survival pathways like the PI3K/AKT, Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK), and NF-κB to avoid being affected. Tumor microenvironment further promotes this phenomenon via paracrine cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-10, causing cellular reprogramming into drug tolerance. In advanced cases, genetic evolution, for example, Richter transformation, plays an additional role in refractoriness and aggression, underscoring resistance as an adaptive response rather than a sequence of discrete incidents [51–53].

PI3K inhibitor resistance has a multifaceted nature. It has been demonstrated that activation of the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor pathway restores Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling, bypassing PI3K inhibition [54, 55]. This is supported by epigenetic modulation and immune evasion mechanisms, which play a role in lowering sensitivity, highlighting broader cellular plasticity. Similarly, venetoclax resistance involves genetic, proteomic, and metabolic changes. Mutations in BCL2, such as Gly101Val, interfere with binding and inhibit apoptosis [56]. On the other hand, the induction of Myeloid Cell Leukemia 1 (MCL1) results in the binding of BIM, which bypasses the action of BCL2, while metabolic switching promotes oxidative phosphorylation and AMP-activated protein kinase/protein kinase A (AMPK/PKA) signaling pathways. Notably, emerging data have revealed that mitochondrial dynamics are essential players in venetoclax tolerance [57].

However, advancements in the uses of BTK, PI3K, and BCL2 inhibitors, combined with the increased knowledge on BCR signaling and apoptosis pathways, have made it possible to improve the understanding of CLL biology. Furthermore, the development of non-covalent BTK inhibitors and degraders has helped broaden therapeutic perspectives, thereby enabling an effective approach to overcoming resistance and designing the rational sequencing of targeted drugs. Notably, the use of immunotherapies such as CAR-T and bispecific antibodies can help overcome kinase- and BCL2-driven resistance and result in deep remission, on the basis of the early evidence available. Overall, the resistance of LLC results from a complex interplay between genetic, metabolic, and microenvironmental factors, which necessitates strategies targeting multiple pathways to ensure future success [33].

Signaling pathways involved in CLL

An increasing body of scientific evidence has established that aberrant intracellular signaling plays a key role in the development and progression of CLL, as does therapeutic resistance. A better understanding of these signaling pathways is no longer optional but rather necessary for developing innovative, mechanism-based therapies that exploit CLL cell biological vulnerabilities.

NOTCH signaling pathway

NOTCH signaling pathway is a conserved cell-cell communication system that controls cell fate, differentiation, and survival across tissues. During physiological processes, the activation of NOTCH occurs via ligand-induced proteolytic cleavage. This releases the intracellular NOTCH domain (ICN), which subsequently migrates to the nucleus where it regulates gene expression controlled by the Proline (P), Glutamic acid (E), Serine (S), Threonine (T)-rich domain (PEST) [58].

Mutations that activate NOTCH1 (10–15% of cases) and occasionally NOTCH2 have been implicated in CLL, leading to alterations in this mechanism, which is associated with more aggressive disease, such as a poor survival rate, resistance to chemotherapy, and a greater likelihood of Richter transformation [59]. The involvement of NOTCH1 mutations in cancers was first demonstrated in seminal experimental studies [60]. This model has been enhanced through recent research studies that have identified other mutations that contribute to NOTCH signaling, including non-coding alterations in regulatory regions such as the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of NOTCH1. These mutations lead to truncation of the PEST domain, resulting in the stabilization of the ICN and hence the activation of the target genes. According to functional assays, NOTCH1 mutations play a major part role in the transcription programs of proliferation, survival, and resistance to apoptosis in CLL cells [61, 62].

For instance, NOTCH alterations are also accompanied by trisomy 12 and increased CD49d expression, indicating the existence of proliferating cell clusters linked to unfavorable outcomes. Accordingly, crosstalk between NOTCH and BCR pathways is well known, whereby NOTCH1 mutant clones are found within distinct BCR sub-clusters and synergy is achieved in pre-clinical settings. Under therapeutic pressure, NOTCH-mediated signaling can promote adaptive reprogramming through maintaining alternate transcriptional programs to compensate for the blockage of certain pathways. In this context, the NOTCH pathway becomes an integral part of the resistance network and cannot be considered an isolated signaling axis. Indeed, there is a basis for combinatorial treatment strategies; however, NOTCH blockers and γ-secretase inhibitors face challenges because of the essential of these pathways in normal tissues [63].

B cell receptor (BCR) pathway: BTK and PI3K

BCR signaling pathway acts as an important hub for facilitating CLL survival, proliferation, and evasion mechanisms through sustained activation [64]. In CLL, BCR signaling activation involves two pathways, namely antigen-dependent activation and antigen-independent (tonic) stimulation pathways, which utilize enzymes like LYN Proto-Oncogene (LYN) and Spleen Tyrosine Kinase (SYK) [65]. Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Activation Motif (ITAM) phosphorylation strengthens the signaling cascade through SYK and B-cell Linker Protein (BLNK), followed by the activation of BTK and downstream pathways, such as PKC, MAPK, and NF-κB [34]. In parallel, BCR signaling activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, which also promotes the survival and proliferation of leukemic cells [66].

Therapeutically, BCR targeting revolutionized CLL: BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib) and PI3Kδ agents (idelalisib, duvelisib) block key nodes, yielding durable remissions in high-risk cases (Fig. 3) [67]. However, drug resistance continues to pose a major challenge in the management of this disorder, largely due to the emergence of BTK and PLCG2 mutations that bypass signaling through these molecules. Moreover, the prolonged response to BTK inhibition is compromised by both toxicity and drug resistance due to mutations enabling signaling [48, 52]. Notably, BCR signaling occurs in an interconnected network of survival pathways. This means that the activation of alternative pathways such as those involving the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, as well as microenvironmental niche support, highlights the adaptable network nature of CLL and necessitates multi-target combinations for sustained efficacy [51].

WNT/β-catenin pathway

WNT pathway regulates proliferation, survival, and differentiation through both canonical β-catenin-dependent and non-canonical β-catenin-independent pathways. In CLL various proteins associated with the WNT pathway are activated, contributing to leukemic cell maintenance and disease progression. CLL cells display marked overexpression of several WNT ligands, including Wnt-3, Wnt-5b, Wnt-6, Wnt-10a, and Wnt-14, compared with normal B lymphocytes, resulting in sustained autocrine and paracrine WNT signaling [68, 69].

CLL cells promote leukemogenesis through activation of transcriptional programs driven by β-catenin and the transcription factor LEF-1, which is markedly overexpressed compared with normal B cells. This leads to the upregulation of oncogenic targets such as Cyclin D1 (CCND1), MYC, and Axis Inhibition Protein 2 (AXIN2), promoting leukemic growth and persistence. A major amplifier of this network is Receptor Tyrosine Kinase-Like Orphan Receptor 1 (ROR1), which is highly expressed in CLL and binds to non-canonical Wnt-5a, activating NF-κB signaling and increasing resistance to apoptosis, making ROR1 an appealing target for therapy [68].

Additional regulators of WNT signaling are frequently altered in CLL. Aberrant expression and epigenetic silencing of inhibitory molecules such as secreted Frizzled-related proteins (sFRPs) and WNT inhibitory factor-1 (WIF1), as well as deregulation of Low-density Lipoprotein Receptor-Related Protein 5/6 (LRP5/6) co-receptors, further disrupt the physiological balance of this pathway [70, 71]. In addition, constitutive PI3K/AKT signaling in CLL inhibits GSK-3β activity, leading to β-catenin stabilization and nuclear accumulation. Functional studies targeting Lymphoid Enhancer-Binding Factor 1 (LEF-1) consistently demonstrate growth arrest and apoptosis in CLL cells, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of this pathway (Fig. 4) [71].

These findings highlight the potential of targeting components such as ROR1 or LEF-1 as part of combinatorial therapeutic strategies. However, the precise contribution of WNT signaling to treatment resistance in CLL remains to be fully elucidated.

XPO1/exportin-1 pathway

Exportin-1 (XPO1), also known as Chromosome Region Maintenance 1 (CRM1), is responsible for transporting hundreds of cargo proteins and several RNA species from the nucleus to the cytoplasm. Under physiological conditions, XPO1 recognizes leucine-rich nuclear export signals (NES) on cargo proteins, forms an export complex with RanGTP, and mediates their translocation through the nuclear pore complex, thereby contributing to the proper spatial regulation of proteins involved in cell-cycle control, stress responses, and survival. In addition to the recurring mutation status of XPO1 in CLL, findings also support the notion that XPO1 alterations lead to increased B-cell receptor signaling and a shorter time to first treatment, supporting their biological relevance in disease progression. Targeted therapy involving XPO1 inhibitors, like selinexor, can have preclinical pro-apoptotic effects in CLL and possibly interfere with BCR signaling in cases of ibrutinib resistance. Preliminary clinical experience with XPO1-targeted combinations demonstrates that this approach is achievable, highlighting XPO1 as a promising therapeutic target that deserves further investigation in CLL [72, 73].

Signaling pathways involved in treatment resistance in CLL

Treatment resistance in CLL is due to the considerable heterogeneity of tumors along with a supportive microenvironment. These mechanisms facilitate clonal selection under therapeutic pressure, such as BTKi and venetoclax. Current research suggests that BCR signaling, followed by BCL2 and microenvironment pathways, with mutations in BTK, PLCG2, BCL2, and TP53 is associated with the development of the disease at approximately 24–48 months [74, 75]. The microenvironment, consisting of stromal cells, cytokines, and chemokines, protects leukemic cells, impedes drug penetration, and suppresses antitumor immunity, thereby enhancing intrinsic resistance mechanisms such as efflux pumps and target mutations [76].

BCR signaling: the most implicated pathway

The BCR pathway dominates resistance mechanisms to BTKi, with mutations in the BTK gene such as C481S/R and T474I found in 65–85% of relapse cases under these therapies. These mutations directly prevent inhibitors from binding to their target proteins [74, 75]. Simultaneously, gain-of-function variants of PLCG2, like S707F, activate bypass signaling downstream of the BCR pathway, restoring signaling independently of BTK inhibition and supporting clonal persistence [75]. CD44, a multifunctional surface glycoprotein regulated by alternative splicing and post-translational modifications, including glycosylation, plays a central role in organizing membrane signaling platforms. It recruits Lyn and Ras-GTPase kinases to activate the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways, thereby amplifying the activity of survival kinases like Proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase Src (SRC), SYK, and AKT [77]. In murine models of CLL, deleting CD44 reduces MCL1 expression, an important anti-apoptotic protein leading to increased cell death, tumor regression, and longer survival [78]. Its expression is dynamically regulated by external signals: stromal cytokines and BCR/ZAP70 activation via ERK increase its levels, while it cooperates with co-receptors such as CD38, CD49d, and Matrix Metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) to strengthen the adhesion of leukemic cells to protective microenvironmental niches [76, 78]. Because of this integration, dual BTK/CD44 blockade is prioritized to target proliferation at the nodal level and to break BCR-ME resistance loops [79].

BCL2 pathway and apoptotic remodeling

Second in impact on resistance, alterations in the BCL2 family counteract venetoclax effects through direct mutations in the BCL2 gene, compensatory overexpression of BCL-XL and MCL1 (alternative anti-apoptotic proteins), and loss of TP53 function. These changes often emerge in selected subclones under therapeutic pressure [75, 80]. The CD40/CD40L interaction, initiated by activated T lymphocytes in lymphoid tissues, induces survivin expression (a caspase-3/7 inhibitor essential for mitosis and apoptotic resistance) while activating the mTORC1 pathway; this activation promotes metabolic remodeling with a simultaneous increase in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and aerobic glycolysis in pseudo-follicles, unlike the classical Warburg effect (where OXPHOS decreases in favor of glycolysis). Here, the OXPHOS/glycolysis coupling supports mitochondrial biogenesis and the rapid proliferation of resistant clones [81, 82]. This survivin/BCL2/mTOR synergy enables robust apoptotic escape and persistent resistance to venetoclax, with survivin stabilizing mitochondria and inhibiting transition pore opening (mPTPs) to preserve OXPHOS while facilitating glycolysis through indirect transcriptional regulation [82]. Overexpressed specifically in proliferative compartments (pseudo-follicles and bone marrow), survivin maintains the stem properties of leukemic cells (self-renewal, quiescence) and confers multi-drug resistance to chemotherapies and targeted treatments; its protein and transcriptional levels increase under therapy (notably venetoclax and BTKi) via clonal selection and persistent CD40 signals, reflecting the inherent heterogeneity of CLL [75, 82].

Chemokine and microenvironmental adhesion axes

The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, ranked third in impact, regulates the homing of leukemic cells to protective niches such as bone marrow and lymphoid tissues by activating the convergent PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, and Src/ABL pathways. These signals phosphorylate and inactivate the pro-apoptotic protein BAD (via serine 136), while also inducing the transcriptional upregulation of BCL2 and survivin, thereby promoting survival, migration, and niche retention. This phenomenon is closely related to invasion and a higher risk of disease progression and therapy failure [75, 76]. CD44 strengthens this pathway through its ability to bind hyaluronic acid (HA), which is one of the main elements of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in CLL microenvironments. This binding maintains the signaling complex in the cell membrane, facilitates adherence to fibroblastic and osteoblastic components by the action of RhoA/ROCK and cytoskeletal reorganization, and acts synergistically with CXCR4 for efficient chemotaxis and protection against apoptosis in the microenvironment [76, 78]. CXCR4 antagonists, such as plerixafor (AMD3100) or the monoclonal antibody ulocuplumab, disrupt this homing process by mobilizing leukemic cells into the peripheral circulation. This mobilization exposes resistant clones to improved therapy penetration and restores their sensitivity, with a demonstrated synergistic effect when used with venetoclax or BTKi in preclinical models [75, 83]. Meanwhile, CD40 provides variable immune support depending on the help provided by activated CD4⁺ T lymphocytes. In lymphoid tissues, the interaction of CD40 with CD40L remodels clonal metabolism by activating mTORC1, promoting long-term persistence. The CD40-induced OXPHOS metabolic pathway, which involves mitochondrial biogenesis and coupling of OXPHOS with glycolysis, ranks fourth in overall impact on resistance [81, 84].

Interconnections and therapeutic perspectives

These signaling pathways functionally intersect: the BCR/CD44 cascade activates AKT/ERK, converging with CXCL12/CXCR4 signals (sharing PI3K/AKT/MAPK pathways), while CD40/CD40L enhances mTORC1 and BCL-2 via survivin. This creates an integrated network where blockade of one pathway allows for compensatory escapes, thus requiring multi-target combinations (BTKi, CXCR4 antagonists and mTOR inhibitors) to delay clonal evolution and prevent relapses, rather than sequential monotherapies [75, 80]. Hierarchically, the BCR pathway (BTK/PLCG2/CD44) dominates, accounting for about 70% of BTKi failures (mainly C481S mutations), followed by BCL2/apoptotic pathways (mTOR/CD40-bound survivin, venetoclax resistance), CXCL12/CXCR4 and ME (chemotaxis and nested retention supporting subclones), and metabolic pathways (increased OXPHOS and glycolysis via CD40/mTOR) (Fig. 5) [77, 85, 86]. Single-cell and spatial omics techniques (scRNA-seq, CyTOF, multiplex imaging) enable detailed resolution of resistant subclones, their heterogeneity, and their dynamic ME interactions, guiding the discovery of predictive biomarkers (CXCR4-high/CD44-high score for early relapse) and patient stratification [76, 82]. Importantly, these high-resolution approaches have strong implications for measurable residual disease (MRD), which has become a key clinical and translational endpoint in CLL management. Beyond conventional MRD quantification, single-cell technologies may help characterize the biological nature of residual disease by identifying rare resistant subclones, transcriptionally distinct residual populations, and microenvironmental programs associated with persistence. In the future, such combined strategies will provide better insights into MRD, improve relapse prediction, and support more precise MRD-guided treatment adaptation [87, 88]. Pharmacological modulation of CXCR4 (using AMD3100) and CD40 (with selective agonist/inhibitor antibodies) sensitizes niche-resident cells by exposing them to circulation and disrupting immune/stromal support [79]. Preclinical studies utilizing concurrent inhibition of CD44, CXCL12, and CD40 pathways have revealed synergistic effects with greater than an 80% decrease in clonal survival and a threefold reduction in the PDX relapse interval [89].

Finally, omics technologies, particularly spatial transcriptomics, drive faster identification of subtle weaknesses by combining intrinsically targeted tumors (BTK/BCL2), ME interference (CXCR4/CD44), and immune reactivation (anti-PD1 after ME) to bypass adaptive resistance and optimize clinical results for CLL [75, 76].

Conclusion

This review shows that therapeutic resistance in CLL is due to a complex signaling network of pathways such as BCR, BCL-2, CXCL12/CXCR4, and CD40/mTOR. Targeting just one pathway may result in escape through compensation. However, the details of these interactions and their exact sequence mostly remain unknown despite existing research [75]. Additionally, even with progress in targeted therapies, clonal diversity and evolution during treatment continue to pose major challenges: resistant subclones either already exist or develop in protective niches (bone marrow, pseudo-follicles), yet detecting them early remains difficult with standard clinical tools [76]. Nonetheless, hope exists in technological advances such as single-cell/spatial omics (scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics), which are already generating large volumes of data that identify predictive biomarkers (high expression of CXCR4 and CD44), and Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning (AI/ML), which is rapidly advancing toward dependable clinical interpretation [77, 89]. Therapeutically, preclinical studies show promising results for the synergistic effects of multi-target combinations (non-covalent BTK inhibitors, AMD3100 and mTOR inhibitors, > 80% clonal decline in PDX), despite challenges related to toxicity and patient variability [89].

Therefore, it is necessary to first develop standardized omics platforms for routine clinical use (liquid ME biomarkers), second, initiate phase II trials of rational combinations (third-generation BTKi, CXCR4 inhibitors, and mTOR inhibitors), and third, heavily invest in AI to model clonal evolution in real time. Dynamic monitoring (circulating tumor DNA, immune phenotyping) will make these adaptive strategies feasible despite logistical challenges. In addition, MRD should be recognized as a key biomarker in CLL, as persistent or re-emergent detectable MRD may reflect the survival of resistant subclones and is associated with a higher risk of relapse, particularly in time-limited targeted treatment strategies.

In conclusion, despite the challenges and complexities, the potential for success depends on defining a clear strategy that integrates: omics, artificial intelligence, and rational combinations of therapeutic approaches, all with the goal of achieving prolonged remission of CLL or even a cure [75].
