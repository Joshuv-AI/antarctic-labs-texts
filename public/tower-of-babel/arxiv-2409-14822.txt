Shannon Bounds for Quadratic Rate-Distortion Problems
Michael Gastpar, Erixhen Sula

Abstract
The Shannon lower bound has been the subject of several important contributions by Berger. This paper surveys Shannon bounds on rate-distortion problems under mean-squared error distortion with a particular emphasis on Berger’s techniques. Moreover, as a new result, the Gray-Wyner network is added to the canon of settings for which such bounds are known. In the Shannon bounding technique, elegant lower bounds are expressed in terms of the source entropy power. Moreover, there is often a complementary upper bound that involves the source variance in such a way that the bounds coincide in the special case of Gaussian statistics. Such pairs of bounds are sometimes referred to as Shannon bounds. The present paper puts Berger’s work on many aspects of this problem in the context of more recent developments, encompassing indirect and remote source coding such as the CEO problem, originally proposed by Berger, as well as the Gray-Wyner network as a new contribution.

I
Introduction

Rate-distortion theory was initiated by Shannon
[
1
]
. Berger
[
2
]
developed the first full and still authoritative account on this important topic.
Rate-distortion theory develops the fundamental limits of lossy data compression.
In the classic data compression problem, a single underlying source sequence needs to be compressed. From the compressed representation, a decoder produces an approximate version of the source sequence. The quality of the approximate version is assessed via a distortion measure.
This leads to a trade-off between the rate of the compressed representation and the incurred distortion.
The corresponding fundamental limit is referred to as the rate-distortion function. The foundational result of rate-distortion theory shows that the rate-distortion function can be expressed as a constrained information minimization problem, see, e.g.,
[
3
, Thm.10.2.1]
.
This is a convex optimization problem.
It can be tackled numerically
[
4
,
5
]
, but it
rarely admits explicit closed-form solutions. Notable special cases for which such solutions are known include the case of Gaussian statistics when the distortion measure is the mean-squared error, and the binary case subject to Hamming distortion.
Beyond such special cases, however, one needs to either resort to numerical answers or to bounds.
The focus of the present paper concerns bounds to the rate-distortion function of a particular type.
Specifically, early on in the development of rate-distortion theory, Shannon devised an elegant way of bounding the rate-distortion function via the entropy of the source
[
1
]
.
This argument works for all so-called
difference distortion measures
but has been most appreciated in the special case of quadratic distortion, that is, the mean-squared error.
Part of the reason may be that in this special case, the Shannon lower bound can often be complemented by a corresponding upper bound of exactly the same shape, but with the source variance in place of the source entropy power.
Such pairs of bounds are sometimes referred to as
Shannon bounds.

In the simplest case, discussed in
Section

III
below, these bounds take the following shape:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen}{\Delta}}\leq R_{X}(\Delta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\Delta}}.

(1)

The main features of Shannon bounds can be summarized as follows:

1.

They are automatically tight in the case of Gaussian statistics.

2.

Worst-case property: When fixing only source variances, Shannon bounds directly imply that Gaussian statistics are worst.

3.

Best-case property: When fixing only source entropies, Shannon bounds directly imply that Gaussian statistics are best.

4.

The gap between the upper and the lower bound is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the source distribution and a Gaussian distribution with the same variance.

I-A

Contributions

Berger, in his seminal book
[
2
, Section 4.3]
, provides an excellent account of the pioneering work on Shannon bounds along with some key extensions.
In the present paper, we review Berger’s many contributions to the topic and put them in the context of more recent developments.
In particular, for point-to-point problems, we encompass the conditional rate-distortion function, the Wyner-Ziv rate-distortion function, and the remote (or indirect) rate-distortion function. We show how these results are established via the techniques of Berger, most specifically, his elegant variational formula for the rate-distortion function. The latter lends itself beautifully to the development of lower bounds.

The last two sections of the paper concern multi-terminal (network) rate-distortion problems. Berger
[
6
]
made foundational contributions to this important topic. A new technical contribution of our work is a pair of Shannon bounds for the so-called Gray-Wyner network given in
7
. We then end our discussion with a special case of the CEO problem. This source coding network was introduced by Berger
[
7
]
.

The new result,
7
, may share its apparent shape with classic results such as the one in
Equation

1
. However, it is important to note that the techniques to establish these two lower bounds, respectively, are vastly different. The lower bound in
Equation

1
can be established via elementary facts about entropy and a judicious maximum entropy argument. By contrast, the lower bound in our new result (
7
) relies on techniques including Brascamp-Lieb-type inequalities (such as
1
below).

I-B

Related Work

The Shannon lower bound has been the subject of a steady trickle of work.
The main focus of this paper is on the case of
scalar
sources. The Shannon lower bound has also been extended to the case of vector sources by Yamada, Tazaki, and Gray
[
8
]
. Further extensions concern Markov sources, where a notable result is due to Avram and Berger
[
9
]
, establishing the critical distortion below which the Shannon lower bound is tight.

Berger discusses the advantages of the Shannon lower bound in his book
[
2
, Section 4.3.4]
, pointing out that the bound is interesting in the low-distortion regime.
This angle has been the subject of intense studies over the years.
Special scrutiny was reserved for the
asymptotic
tightness of the Shannon lower bound, notably in the work of Linder and Zamir
[
10
]
and Koch
[
11
]
. A finite block-length refinement of the Shannon lower bound was developed by Kostina
[
12
]
.

The Shannon lower bound also plays a role in a string of interesting applications. For example, Feng and Effros, in
[
13
]
and Weissman and Ordentlich, in
[
14
]
, establish theorems for sources for which the Shannon lower bound is tight. Zamir, Kochman, and Erez
[
15
]
leverage the Shannon lower bound in an application involving predictive coding. The Shannon lower bound also plays a pivotal role in Rose’s mapping approach
[
16
]
. Nayak and Tuncel
[
17
]
leverage Shannon lower bounds in the context of successive coding.
The Shannon lower bound has appeared in multiterminal problems, e.g., in the work of Zamir and Berger
[
18
]
.

Our focus in this paper is on quadratic rate-distortion problems. However, the rationale of the Shannon lower bound easily extends to difference distortion measures, which is perhaps most obvious from Berger’s proof in
[
2
, Section 4.3.4]
.
Several results are known for difference distortion measures beyond the mean-squared error.
Berger
[
19
]
pitches the Shannon lower bound (applied in the case of binary sources and Hamming distortion) against an improved bound.
Bounds akin to the Shannon lower bound can also be derived for distortion measures that are not difference distortion measures, see e.g., Linder and Zamir in
[
20
]
and with Zeger in
[
21
]
.
Shannon lower bounds for special classes of sources are given by Koliander
et al.
in
[
22
]
.

I-C

Notation

Throughout this paper, we will use
\log(\cdot)
to denote the natural logarithm, and for any non-negative real number
x,
we use the shorthand
\log^{+}(x)=\max(0,\log(x)).

For a continuous real-valued random variable
X,
the variance is denoted by
\mathrm{Var}(X),
the (differential) entropy by
h(X),
and the entropy power as

\displaystyle\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2h(X)}}{2\pi e},

(2)

and we recall that for Gaussian random variables
X,
we have that
\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen=\mathrm{Var}(X).

For two jointly continuous real-valued random variables
X
and
W,
we will use the following standard notation

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X|W=w)

\displaystyle={\mathbb E}\left[\left.\left(X-{\mathbb E}[X|W]\right)^{2}\right|W=w\right],

(3)

and we denote the expected value of this quantity over
W
by the semi-standard notation

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X|W)

\displaystyle={\mathbb E}_{X,W}\left[\left(X-{\mathbb E}[X|W]\right)^{2}\right],

(4)

which is the minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) in estimating
X
from
W.

Moreover, we will use the notation

\displaystyle\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X|W\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2h(X|W)}}{2\pi e},

(5)

where one can again see that when
X
and
W
are jointly Gaussian, then
\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X|W\right\rparen=\mathrm{Var}(X|W)=\sigma_{X}^{2}(1-\rho^{2}),

where
\rho
denotes the correlation coefficient between
X
and
W.

Moreover, we also use
\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X,Y\right\rparen
to denote the entropy power of the vector
(X,Y)
, that is,

\displaystyle\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X,Y\right\rparen=\frac{1}{2\pi e}e^{h(X,Y)}.

(6)

When
X
and
Y
are jointly Gaussian with covariance matrix
\Sigma,
then
\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen X,Y\right\rparen=\det\Sigma,
which is the product of the eigenvalues of
\Sigma.

To see this, recall that in this case,
h(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e)^{2}\det\Sigma.

It is important to recall that this is
not
the expected value of the squared
L_{2}
-norm of the random vector
(X,Y),
which is
\Tr\Sigma,
and thus the sum of the eigenvalues of
\Sigma.

II
MMSE Estimation

A first statement akin to the Shannon lower bound can be made about the classic problem of estimation subject to the mean-squared error criterion.
Let us define the minimum mean-squared error as

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle={\mathbb E}\left[\left(X-{\mathbb E}[X|Y]\right)^{2}\right].

(7)

Then, whenever
\Delta_{0}\leq\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen,
we have (letting
\rho
denote the correlation coefficient between
X
and
Y
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X|Y\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{0}\leq\sigma_{X}^{2}(1-\rho^{2}).

(8)

where we have equality throughout if and only if
X
and
Y
are jointly Gaussian.
This result can be found, e.g,, in
[
3
, p.255]
.

While these bounds can be useful, we may have a preference for an even more explicit pair of bounds that we discuss next.
In this case, we specifically study the case where
Y
is an observation of
X
subject to
additive
(independent) noise, that is,

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=X+Z.

(9)

For this case, the bounds take the following more explicit form, see e.g.
[
23
, Eqn.(1.34)]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen\operatorname{N}\left\lparen Z\right\rparen}{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen Y\right\rparen}\leq\Delta_{0}\leq\frac{\sigma_{X}^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{2}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}.

(10)

A key feature of the bounds in
Equation

8
and in
Equation

10
is that their shape is identical;
and that variance terms in the upper bound are systematically replaced by the corresponding entropy powers to obtain the lower bound.
Therefore, in the special case of Gaussian statistics, these bounds are equal and thus tight.
We will refer to such bounds as Shannon bounds.

III
The Quadratic Rate-Distortion Function

In
[
2
]
, Berger makes several intricate and deep contributions to rate-distortion theory in the special case of so-called difference distortion measures.
This designates the case where the value of
d(x,y)
only depends on the difference between
x
and
y
(and thus, requires
x
and
y
to take value in a group).
In this paper, we restrict attention to mean-squared error distortion, which is of course a special case of a difference distortion measure.
Another important example is the class of modulo-additive distortion measures over a finite group.

III-A

The Scalar Case

Consider the classic problem of compressing a continuous-amplitude source in a lossy fashion. Let the source be modeled by a sequence of independent and identically distributed continuous random variables, as described in detail, e.g., in
[
2
]
and
[
3
, Chapter 10]
. From the compressed representation, the original source sequence needs to be reconstructed to within a prescribed distortion level
\Delta.
How many bits of coding rate are required to attain this goal?
The answer to this question is the rate-distortion function. A careful discussion of this can be found, e.g., in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
.
For the case where the distortion measure is the mean-squared error, the (so-called quadratic) rate-distortion function is given by

\displaystyle R_{X}(\Delta):=\inf_{p(\hat{x}|x):\mathbb{E}[(X-\hat{X})^{2}]\leq\Delta}I(X;\hat{X}).

(11)

The specific goal of the present paper is to discuss the optimization problem stated in
Equation

11
.
For most distributions
p_{X}(x),
this problem cannot be solved in closed form.
It can be solved numerically, but this shall not be of particular interest here.
Instead, our interest is in
bounds
on the rate-distortion function in
Equation

11
.
More specifically, we are interested in the following type of bounds, originally due to Shannon and further developed by Berger.

Theorem 1
.

For a continuous random variable
X
and mean-squared error distortion
d(x,\hat{x})=(x-\hat{x})^{2},
the rate-distortion function is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen}{\Delta}}\leq R_{X}(\Delta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\Delta}}.

(12)

Needless to say, this basic version of the Shannon bound is a classic exercise in many books and classes on information theory.
Its proof is usually accomplished leveraging the fact that conditioning reduces entropy together with a maximum entropy argument.
Berger, in his book
[
2
, Sec.4.3]
, provides a rather different but equally interesting proof approach.
For completeness, we include a brief sketch of Berger’s argument of the lower bound in this theorem in Appendix
A
.

To interpret
1
, we note that the gap between the upper and the lower bound in
Equation

12
can be expressed in an instructive fashion as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\Delta}}-\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen}{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=D(f\|g),

(13)

for
\Delta\leq\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X\right\rparen,

where
D(\cdot\|\cdot)
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence,
f
denotes the distribution of the random variable
X,
and
g
denotes a Gaussian distribution with the same variance as
X.

This shows that the bounds in
1
are interesting for distributions that are close to Gaussian.

III-B

The Vector Case

Let
(X_{1},X_{2})
be distributed with full-rank covariance matrix
\Sigma.

Suppose that this vector needs to be compressed to yield lossy descriptions at mean-squared error distortion
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2},
respectively.
For this problem, the corresponding rate-distortion function is given by

\displaystyle R_{(X_{1},X_{2})}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}p(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2}|x_{1},x_{2}):\\
\mathbb{E}[(X_{1}-\hat{X}_{1})^{2}]\leq\Delta_{1},\\
\mathbb{E}[(X_{2}-\hat{X}_{2})^{2}]\leq\Delta_{2}\end{subarray}}I(X_{1},X_{2};\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2}).

(14)

For this problem, the Shannon lower bound is well known, see e.g.
[
18
]
, and given by the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

For a continuous random vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
with full-rank covariance matrix
\Sigma
and separate (coordinate-wise) mean-squared error distortion criteria, the rate-distortion function is bounded by

\displaystyle\min_{D}\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}\frac{\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen X_{1},X_{2}\right\rparen}{\det D}

\displaystyle\leq R_{(X_{1},X_{2})}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})\leq\min_{D}\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}\frac{\det\Sigma}{\det D}

(15)

where the minimum is over all matrices
D
that satisfy
0\preccurlyeq D\preccurlyeq\Sigma
(i.e., in semi-definite ordering) and
D_{ii}\leq\Delta_{i}.

As a side remark, we note that this theorem can be extended to a vector of arbitrary length.

A simple extension of the lossy source coding problem addressed by
2
is the case where a distortion constraint is only imposed on the sum, rather than individually on the components.
The corresponding rate-distortion function can be expressed simply by taking the definition in
Equation

14
and adding an outer minimization over all non-negative pairs
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
whose
sum
is at most the desired sum distortion level.
This leads to an allocation problem known as
(reverse) water-filling
in the information-theoretic literature, see e.g.
[
3
, Section 10.3.3]
.

2
also extends to this case by adding, both in the upper and in the lower bound, an outer optimization.

In this section, we consider two basic rate-distortion scenarios with side information, namely, the conditional rate-distortion function and the Wyner-Ziv problem.

For both cases, one can give bounds of the type considered in the present paper.

The conditional rate-distortion problem is illustrated in

Specifically, let the source be modeled by a sequence of independent and identically distributed pairs of jointly continuous random variables,

From the compressed representation together with the side information sequence

How many bits of coding rate are required to attain this goal?
The answer to this question is the conditional rate-distortion function. A careful discussion of this can be found in
[

For the case where the distortion measure is the mean-squared error, the conditional rate-distortion function is given by

\displaystyle R_{X|W}(\Delta):=\inf_{p(\hat{x}|x,w):\mathbb{E}[(X-\hat{X})^{2}]\leq\Delta}I(X;\hat{X}|W).

An interesting variant of the conditional rate-distortion function has been widely studied in the literature. In this variant, called the

only the decoder has access to the side information. This is illustrated in

Specifically, let the source be modeled by a sequence of independent and identically distributed pairs of jointly continuous random variables,

From the compressed representation together with the side information sequence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}\leq R_{X|W}^{{\mathrm{WZ}}}(\Delta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\sigma_{X}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}{\Delta}},

The problem of remote source coding was originally introduced in by Dobrushin and Tsybakov
[
27
]
.
It is the subject of an in-depth discussion in Berger’s book on rate-distortion theory
[
2
, Section 3.5]
.
The setting is illustrated in
Figure

3
.
Specifically, let the source be modeled by a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables
X.

The source is first passed through a memoryless channel to form the observed sequence
Y.
Observing only the sequence
Y,
the encoder produces a compressed representation.
From the compressed representation, the original source sequence needs to be reconstructed to within a prescribed distortion level
\Delta.
How many bits of coding rate are required to attain this goal?
The answer to this question is the remote rate-distortion function. A careful discussion of this can be found in
[
2
, Section 3.5]
.
For the case where the distortion measure is the mean-squared error, the remote rate-distortion function is given by

\displaystyle R_{X}^{R}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p(\hat{x}|y):{\mathbb E}[d(X,\hat{X})]\leq\Delta}I(Y;\hat{X}).

(20)

Berger, in
[
2
, Section 3.5]
, shows a general technique to reduce the remote to a regular rate-distortion function,
albeit with a different distortion measure.
Indeed, the main vehicle is to introduce what Berger refers to as the
modified
distortion measure:
the original
d(x,\hat{x})

is converted into a measure expressed
in terms of the remote source observation
y
as

d^{*}(y,\hat{x})={\mathbb E}_{X|y}\left[d(X,\hat{x})\right]
.
This important insight shows that we might equivalently write the remote rate-distortion function as

\displaystyle R_{X}^{R}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=R_{Y}^{*}(\Delta)=\inf_{p(\hat{x}|y):{\mathbb E}[d^{*}(Y,\hat{X})]\leq\Delta}I(Y;\hat{X}),

(21)

which we recognize to be a regular rate-distortion function for the source
Y,
subject to a distortion criterion based on
d^{*}(y,\hat{x}),
hence to asterisk in the superscript of
R_{Y}^{*}(\Delta).

For the purpose of our consideration, we can further simplify (leveraging the well-known orthogonality property of conditional expectation)

\displaystyle d^{*}(y,\hat{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb E}_{X|y}[(X-v(y))^{2}]+(v(y)-\hat{x})^{2},

(22)

where we use the shorthand
v(y)={\mathbb E}[X|Y=y]
for ease of notation. Therefore, we can rewrite

\displaystyle{\mathbb E}[d^{*}(Y,\hat{X})]

\displaystyle=\Delta_{0}+{\mathbb E}[(V(Y)-\hat{X})^{2}],

(23)

where
\Delta_{0}
denotes the minimum mean-squared error as defined in
Equation

7
.
Observing that
\hat{X}
is a dummy variable and leveraging the data processing inequality, Berger’s elegant decomposition can be simplified to the following form:

\displaystyle R_{X}^{R}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=R_{V}(\Delta-\Delta_{0})=\inf_{p(\hat{v}|v):{\mathbb E}[(V-\hat{V})^{2}]\leq\Delta-\Delta_{0}}I(V;\hat{V}),

(24)

which is the regular quadratic rate-distortion function for the random variable
V={\mathbb E}[X|Y].

Therefore,
1
now directly implies the following well known bounds.

Theorem 5
.

The remote rate-distortion function subject to mean-squared error satisfies the following lower and upper bounds, using the shorthand
V={\mathbb E}[X|Y]:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen V\right\rparen}{\Delta-\Delta_{0}}\leq R_{X}^{R}(\Delta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}\frac{\sigma_{V}^{2}}{\Delta-\Delta_{0}},

(25)

for
\Delta>\Delta_{0},
where
\Delta_{0}={\mathbb E}\left[\left(X-V\right)^{2}\right].

As a side remark, we point out that a hand-tailored argument leading to
Equation

24
for the special case of the mean-squared error was also given by Wolf and Ziv
[

]
, see e.g.
[

, Eqn.(42)]
.

It is instructive to consider the special case where the noisy source observation is obtained by adding independent noise to the original source. That is, let

where

This case is well understood in the literature, see e.g.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen X_{1},X_{2}\right\rparen}{\sigma^{2}(1-|\rho|)\left(2\Delta e^{R_{p}}+\sigma^{2}(|\rho|-1)\right)}}\leq R_{c}(\Delta,R_{p})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}{(1-|\rho|)\left(2\Delta e^{R_{p}}+\sigma^{2}(|\rho|-1)\right)}}

A detailed proof is included in Appendices

VIII
Discussion and Open Problems

The main purpose of this paper is to survey bounds of the Shannon type on quadratic rate-distortion problems and to put these bounds in the context of Berger’s work on this topic. Specifically, the paper includes Shannon bounds for the conditional rate-distortion function, the Wyner-Ziv rate-distortion function, and the remote (or indirect) rate-distortion function. Proofs are given following Berger’s techniques. Moreover, we also present a new pair of Shannon bounds for the Gray-Wyner network, and we include similar bounds for a special case of the CEO problem.

There are many open problems of interest.
In the present paper, we have focused on quadratic rate-distortion problems,
i.e.,
when the distortion measure is the mean-squared error.
For the standard direct rate-distortion problem, Berger
[
2
, Section 4.3.1]
gives a much more general result, considering general difference distortion measures, that is, if the distortion measure is of the form
d(x,\hat{x})=\rho(x-\hat{x})
for some function
\rho(\cdot).

Clearly, it would be of interest to extend the theorems surveyed in this paper beyond the quadratic case.
Another avenue of research comes out of Berger’s variational formula for the rate-distortion function. In the present paper, we have seen how it can be leveraged directly in cases including the conditional rate-distortion function or the remote rate-distortion function. An extension to source coding networks would be of great interest.
For the networks discussed in this survey, an important avenue for future research would be to extend the bounds for the AWGN CEO problem to cases where the observation noise is not Gaussian.
Finally, bounds akin to the ones discussed here ought to be available for the standard distributed lossy source coding problem spearheaded by Berger and Tung
[
6
,
36
]
.

Appendix A
Berger’s variational theorem

To establish the Shannon lower bound,
Berger uses a proof technique that lends itself to further generalization.
Specifically, Berger establishes the following alternative form of the rate-distortion function
R_{X}(\Delta)
defined in
Equation

11
:

Theorem 9

(Theorem 4.2.3 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda_{s}
be the set of all nonnegative functions
\lambda(x)
satisfying

\displaystyle\int\lambda(x)p(x)e^{sd(x,y)}dx

\displaystyle\leq 1,

(36)

for all values of
y.
Then,

\displaystyle R_{X}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\leq 0,\lambda(x)\in{\Lambda}_{s}}\left(s\Delta+\int p(x)\log\lambda(x)dx\right).

(37)

Using this theorem, it is possible to give lower bounds to the rate-distortion function simply by selecting a valid function
\lambda(x)
and a non-positive real number
s.

Specifically, we may choose (see
[
2
, Sec.4.3.1]

\displaystyle\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{p(x)}.

(38)

Then, for quadratic distortion
d(x,y)=(x-y)^{2},
we select
K=\sqrt{\frac{-s}{\pi}},

which can be verified to satisfy the constraint of the theorem for all negative numbers

Moreover, we select
s=-\frac{1}{2\Delta}.

Plugging in directly leads to the lower bound formula given in
1
.

Appendix B
Proofs of

To establish the lower bound, we offer a proof based on Berger’s variational theorem as discussed in Appendix

To leverage this theorem, we start by observing that we can write

\displaystyle R_{X|W}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\min_{\Delta_{W}:{\mathbb E}_{W}[\Delta_{W}]\leq\Delta}{\mathbb E}_{W}[R_{X|W}(\Delta_{W})].

(39)

For a fixed realization
W=w,
the expression inside the last expectation is merely a standard rate-distortion function (concerning the conditional distribution
p(x|w)
).
Hence, using
9
, we can write

\displaystyle R_{X|W}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\min_{\Delta_{W}:{\mathbb E}_{W}[\Delta_{W}]\leq\Delta}{\mathbb E}_{W}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{W}\leq 0,\\
\lambda_{W}(x)\in{\Lambda}_{s_{W}}(W)\end{subarray}}\left(s_{W}\Delta_{W}+\int p(x|W)\log\lambda_{W}(x)dx\right)\right],

(40)

where
\Lambda_{s}(w)
is the set of all nonnegative functions

satisfying

\displaystyle\int\lambda_{w}(x)p(x|w)e^{sd(x,y)}dx

\displaystyle\leq 1,

(41)

for all values of
y.

Now, selecting

we get the lower bound

\displaystyle R_{X|W}(\Delta)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{s\leq 0}\min_{\Delta_{W}:{\mathbb E}_{W}[\Delta_{W}]\leq\Delta}{\mathbb E}_{W}\left[s\Delta_{W}+\sup_{\lambda_{W}(x)\in{\Lambda}_{s}(W)}\int p(x|W)\log\lambda_{W}(x)dx\right]

(42)

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\leq 0}\min_{\Delta_{W}:{\mathbb E}_{W}[\Delta_{W}]\leq\Delta}\Biggl\{s{\mathbb E}_{W}[\Delta_{W}]+{\mathbb E}_{W}\left[\sup_{\lambda_{W}(x)\in{\Lambda}_{s}(W)}\int p(x|W)\log\lambda_{W}(x)dx\right]\Biggr\}

(43)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{s\leq 0}s\Delta+{\mathbb E}_{W}\left[\sup_{\lambda_{W}(x)\in{\Lambda}_{s}(W)}\int p(x|W)\log\lambda_{W}(x)dx\right].

(44)

To obtain a lower bound, it now suffices to choose
s
and
\lambda_{w}(x)
wisely.
Following the proceedings in Appendix
A
, we select

\displaystyle\lambda_{w}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{p(x|w)},

(45)

and we select
K=\sqrt{\frac{-s}{\pi}}.

For this choice, recalling that we consider quadratic distortion
d(x,y)=(x-y)^{2},
it is straightforward to verify that
\lambda_{w}(x)\in\Lambda_{s}(w)
for every

Finally, we select
s=-\frac{1}{2\Delta}

We also note that an alternative way of proving the lower bound leverages

Combining this with

For the upper bound, we start by leveraging the following simple facts (established for example in

\displaystyle R_{X|W}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=R_{X-{\mathbb E}[X|W]|W}(\Delta)

(46)

\displaystyle\leq R_{X-{\mathbb E}[X|W]}(\Delta)

(47)

At this point, we can again leverage

. The only remaining argument is to find the variance of the random variable

Since the mean of this random variable vanishes, the variance is simply

{\mathbb E}[\left(X-{\mathbb E}[X|W]\right)^{2}],

which, in the notation used in the present paper, we choose to denote as

Hence,

\displaystyle R_{X|W}(\Delta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X|W)}{\Delta}},

which completes the proof for the conditional rate-distortion function.

For the upper bound in
4
, it suffices to plug in a judicious choice of auxiliary
U

and upper bound the resulting mutual information and distortion.
Recall that
U
must be chosen to satisfy the Markov constraint
W-X-U.

Hence, let us choose
U=\tilde{\rho}X+\sqrt{1-\tilde{\rho}^{2}}Z,
where
Z
is a zero-mean Gaussian of variance
\sigma_{X}^{2}
independent of
X
and
W.
Note that with this choice, the correlation coefficient between
X
and
U
is precisely given by
\tilde{\rho}.

For this choice of the auxiliary, we can upper bound the incurred distortion by analyzing
the optimal
linear
estimator
\hat{X}_{lin}(U,W)
of
X
given both
U
and
W.

This distortion is well known and can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\hat{X})^{2}]}{\sigma_{X}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\hat{X}_{lin}(U,W))^{2}]}{\sigma_{X}^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma+\tilde{\gamma}},

(49)

where
\gamma=\rho^{2}/(1-\rho^{2})
and
\tilde{\gamma}=\tilde{\rho}^{2}/(1-\tilde{\rho}^{2}).
This can be seen for example by maximum ratio combining.

gives the condition
\tilde{\gamma}=\frac{\sigma_{X}^{2}}{\Delta}-1-\gamma.

Moreover, for this
U,
we observe by standard arguments that

\displaystyle I(X;U|W)

\displaystyle=h(U|W)-h(U|X).

(50)

By our construction, the second term is merely the entropy of a Gaussian random variable.
For the first term,
from the conditional maximum entropy theorem (see e.g.
[

(U,W)
and
(U^{*},W^{*})

(U^{*},W^{*})
are jointly Gaussian. For jointly Gaussian random variables, entropy formulas are of course well known and allow us to write

\displaystyle I(X;U|W)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+(1-\rho^{2})\tilde{\gamma}\right).

(51)

Combining gives the claimed upper bound.

Appendix C
Proof of
7
, Part I : Lower Bound

This result constitutes a novel contribution of the present paper, having previously appeared in the conference version only
[
25
]
.
Therefore, a detailed proof is included.
Without loss of generality, in the proof, we assume that
0\leq\rho\leq 1
(that is, non-negative correlation) to avoid notational clutter.
Let us start from
R_{c}(R_{p})
that we defined in (
31
),

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})

\displaystyle=\inf_{W:R_{X_{1}|W}(\Delta)+R_{X_{2}|W}(\Delta)\leq R_{p}}I(X_{1},X_{2};W).

(52)

The first step is to relax the constraint.
Using the lower bound given in
3
,
we know that any
W
for which we have
R_{X_{1}|W}(\Delta)+R_{X_{2}|W}(\Delta)\leq R_{p}

must also satisfy
\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{1}|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}+\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{2}|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}\leq R_{p}.

Therefore, we can infer that

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{W:\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{1}|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}+\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{2}|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}\leq R_{p}}I(X_{1},X_{2};W).

(53)

From weak duality, this can be further lower bounded by

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{W}I(X_{1},X_{2};W)+\frac{\nu}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{1}|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\nu\left(\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{2}|W\right\rparen}{\Delta}}-R_{p}\right).

(54)

To continue, let us now consider the case where the distortion satisfies
\Delta\leq\min\{\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{1}|W\right\rparen,\operatorname{N}\left\lparen X_{2}|W\right\rparen\},

noting that the other cases are simple to handle and much less interesting.
In this case, the two
\log^{+}(\cdot)
in the previous expression can be replaced by
\log(\cdot)
and combined to yield the following quite canonical form:

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})\geq h(X_{1},X_{2})-\nu R_{p}-\nu\log(2\pi e\Delta)

\displaystyle+\nu\cdot\inf_{W}\left\{h(X_{1}|W)+h(X_{2}|W)-\frac{1}{\nu}h(X_{1},X_{2}|W)\right\},

(55)

where
\nu
is the Lagrangian parameter that must satisfy
\nu\geq 0
. At this stage, we prefer to rewrite this in terms of normalized random variables
\tilde{X}_{1}=X_{1}/\sqrt{\sigma^{2}}
and
\tilde{X}_{2}=X_{2}/\sqrt{\sigma^{2}},
leading to

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})\geq h(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2})-\nu R_{p}-\nu\log(2\pi e\Delta)+\nu\log\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle+\nu\cdot\inf_{W}\left\{h(\tilde{X}_{1}|W)+h(\tilde{X}_{2}|W)-\frac{1}{\nu}h(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}|W)\right\}.

(56)

Observe that the covariance matrix of the normalized random variables
(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2})
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}.

(57)

To continue from here, we use the following fact, which is
[
34
, Theorem 8]
:

Lemma 1
.

Let
K\succeq 0
and let
(X,Y)\sim p_{X,Y}
with covariance matrix
K.

For all
0<\lambda<1
, there exists a
0\preceq K^{\prime}\preceq K
and
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,K^{\prime})
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{W}\left\{h(X|W)+h(Y|W)-(1+\lambda)h(X,Y|W)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\geq h(X^{\prime})+h(Y^{\prime})-(1+\lambda)h(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}).

(58)

With this lemma, we thus have, for
\frac{1}{2}\leq\nu\leq 1,

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})

\displaystyle\geq h(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2})-\nu R_{p}-\nu\log{(2\pi e\frac{\Delta}{\sigma^{2}})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\nu\cdot\hskip-20.00003pt\min_{0\preceq K^{\prime}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}}h(X^{\prime})+h(Y^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\nu}h(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})

(59)

This minimum can be further lower bounded using
[
34
, Lemma 13]
, which says:

Lemma 2
.

For
\lambda\leq\rho
, the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\min_{K^{\prime}:0\preceq K^{\prime}\preceq\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}}h(X^{\prime})+h(Y^{\prime})-(1+\lambda)h(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\geq\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{2}}}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log{(2\pi e)^{2}\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}(1+\lambda)}{1-\lambda}},

(60)

where
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,K^{\prime}).

Hence, we find for
\frac{1}{\nu}-1\leq\rho,

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log{(2\pi e)^{2}\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen}-\nu R_{p}-\nu\log{(2\pi e\frac{\Delta}{\sigma^{2}})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{\nu}{2}\log{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2\nu-1}}-\frac{1-\nu}{2}\log{(2\pi e)^{2}\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}}{2\nu-1}}.

(61)

The final step of the proof is to maximize this expression over all choices of
\nu
in the interval for which our lower bound holds, that is,
in the interval
1\geq\nu\geq\frac{1}{1+\rho}.

To do so, consider the function

\displaystyle\ell(\nu)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\log{(2\pi e)^{2}\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen}-\nu R_{p}-\nu\log{(2\pi e\frac{\Delta}{\sigma^{2}})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{\nu}{2}\log{\frac{\nu^{2}}{2\nu-1}}-\frac{1-\nu}{2}\log{(2\pi e)^{2}\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}}{2\nu-1}},

(62)

for
1\geq\nu\geq\frac{1}{1+\rho}.

To goal is to maximize the function, i.e., to solve

\max_{1\geq\nu\geq\frac{1}{1+\rho}}\ell(\nu)
.
For notational compactness, let us define
\tilde{\Delta}=\frac{\Delta}{\sigma^{2}}.

Note that the function
\ell
is concave since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial\nu^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\nu(2\nu-1)}<0,

(63)

and by studying the monotonicity

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\nu}=\log{\frac{\nu(1-\rho)}{(2\nu-1)\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}}},

(64)

its maximal value occurs when the derivative vanishes, that is, when
\nu_{*}=\frac{\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}}{2\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}-1+\rho}.
Substituting for the optimal
\nu_{*}
we get

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})

\displaystyle\geq\ell\left(\frac{\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}}{2\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}-1+\rho}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}\frac{\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen}{(1-\rho)\left(2\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}-1+\rho\right)},

(66)

for
1\geq\nu_{*}\geq\frac{1}{1+\rho}
, which means the bound is valid for the range
1-\rho\leq\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}\leq 1
.

We now separately also consider the case where
\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}\leq 1-\rho
. In this case note that
\nu(1-\rho)\geq\nu\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}\geq(2\nu-1)\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}
for
\nu\leq 1
. This implies
\frac{\nu(1-\rho)}{(2\nu-1)\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}}\geq 1
, thus we have
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\nu}\geq 0
. Since the function is concave and increasing the maximum is attained at
\nu_{*}=1
, thus the maximum is attained at the boundary, and we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})\geq\ell\left(1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}\frac{\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen}{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}e^{2R_{p}}},

(67)

and this bound is valid for
\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}\leq 1-\rho
.
Combining, we thus get

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen}{(1-\rho)\left(2\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}+\rho-1\right)}},&\mbox{ if }1-\rho\leq\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}\leq 1\\
\frac{1}{2}\log^{+}{\frac{\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen}{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}e^{2R_{p}}}},&\mbox{ if }\tilde{\Delta}e^{R_{p}}\leq 1-\rho.\end{array}\right.

The claimed lower bound is now obtained by recalling that
\tilde{\Delta}=\Delta/\sigma^{2}
and that
\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2}\right\rparen=\operatorname{N}^{2}\left\lparen X_{1},X_{2}\right\rparen/\sigma^{2}.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
7
, Part II : Upper Bound

For the upper bound, as is typical for the Shannon bounds studied in this paper, the technique consists in selecting auxiliaries according to (conditional) Gaussian distributions.
For the Gray-Wyner network, two different cases need to be considered: the auxiliary could be a scalar (one-dimensional) random variable, or it could be a two-dimensional random vector.
We separately optimize each case, and then select the better of the two depending on the target distortion
\Delta.
Intuitively, when the target distortion is large, then a one-dimensional auxiliary is better, and when the distortion is small, we need to consider two-dimensional auxiliaries. In the sequel, the optimizations are carried out in detail.
To keep notation simple, we write the proof assuming that the variances of
X
and
Y
are equal to 1.
Clearly, if a distortion
\Delta
is attainable for sources of variance 1, then a distortion of
\Delta\sigma^{2}
is attainable for sources of variance
\sigma^{2}.

D-A

W
is a scalar random variable

We managed to show in Theorem
3
that
R_{X_{1}|W}(\Delta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X_{1}|W)]}{\Delta}}
and in order to satisfy the constraint in (
31
) we need to finally show that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X_{1}|W)]\leq\Delta e^{R_{p}}
. We construct
W
as

\displaystyle W=\alpha(X_{1}+X_{2})+N,

(70)

where
N
is independent of
(X_{1},X_{2})
and
N\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{2\Delta e^{R_{p}}+\rho-1}{1+\rho}\right)
and we choose
\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{1-\Delta e^{R_{p}}}}{1+\rho}
, thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X_{1}|W)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(X_{1})-\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X_{1}|W])

(71)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}^{2}[X_{1}|W]]

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]-\frac{\mathbb{E}^{2}[W\mathbb{E}[X_{1}|W]]}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}

(73)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]-\mathbb{E}^{2}[X_{1}W]}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}

(74)

\displaystyle=\Delta e^{R_{p}}

(75)

where
Equation

73
follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and
Equation

75
follows from
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W]=\sqrt{1-\Delta e^{R_{p}}}
.
The same arguments can be applied to bound
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(X_{2}|W)],
and thus,
R_{X_{2}|W}(\Delta).

Thus,

\displaystyle R_{c}(R_{p})\leq I(X_{1},X_{2};W)

\displaystyle=h(W)-h(N)

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(W)}{\mathrm{Var}(N)}}

(77)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{1+\rho}{2\Delta e^{R_{p}}+\rho-1}},

(78)

where
Equation

77
follows from
h(W)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log{(2\pi e\mathrm{Var}(W))}
and
Equation

78
follows from
\mathrm{Var}(W)=1
and
\mathrm{Var}(N)=\frac{2\Delta e^{R_{p}}+\rho-1}{1+\rho}
. Note that
Equation

78
corresponds to the upper bound in
Equation

32
.

D-B

W
is a two-dimensional random vector

We construct
W=(W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha X_{1}+\beta X_{2}+N_{1},

(79)

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle=\beta X_{1}+\alpha X_{2}+N_{2},

(80)

where
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are independent zero-mean Gaussians of variance
\Delta e^{R_{p}},

independent of
(X_{1},X_{2}).

Moreover, the constants
\alpha
and
\beta
are selected to satisfy

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\Delta e^{R_{p}}}{1-\rho^{2}},

(81)

\displaystyle 2\alpha\beta

\displaystyle=\rho\frac{\Delta e^{R_{p}}}{1-\rho^{2}}.

(82)

With these choices, we have
{\mathbb E}[W_{1}^{2}]={\mathbb E}[W_{2}^{2}]=1
and
{\mathbb E}[W_{1}W_{2}]=\rho
.
This permits to upper bound the minimum mean-squared error by the mean-squared error of the optimal
linear
estimator as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X_{1}|W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta e^{R_{p}}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(X_{1}|W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{1}]\\
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{2}]\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{2}]&\mathbb{E}[W_{1}W_{2}]\\
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}W_{2}]&\mathbb{E}[W_{2}^{2}]\\
\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{1}]\\
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{2}]\\
\end{bmatrix}

(84)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]-

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{1}]\\
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{2}]\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{2}]&\mathbb{E}[W_{1}W_{2}]\\
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}W_{2}]&\mathbb{E}[W_{2}^{2}]\\

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}W_{2}]\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=1-\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\beta\rho\\

\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\

\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha+\beta\rho\\
\beta+\alpha\rho\\

\displaystyle=1-(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})-2\alpha\beta\rho

(87)
