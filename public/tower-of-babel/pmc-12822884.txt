The impact of educational intervention based on the constructs of the developed Pender health promotion model on medication adherence and adherence to a low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled hypertension: A randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Saeid Bashirian, Majid Barati, Erfan Ayubi, Saman Talebi, Mostafa Jiba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Uncontrolled hypertension is a critical preventable risk factor for cardiovascular events. This study evaluated the impact of educational interventions based on the constructs of the enhanced Pender health promotion model (EPHPM) on medication adherence and low-salt diet adoption in patients with uncontrolled hypertension in Ahvaz, Iran.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The present study is a randomized clinical trial conducted from April to October 2024 among 130 patients with uncontrolled hypertension, who were covered by the health centers of East, West, and Karun districts in the city of Ahvaz. Participants were selected from men and women aged 18–70 years who had electronic health records in the Sib system and did not have controlled blood pressure (blood pressure <140/90 mmHg). They were chosen using simple random sampling and divided into two groups: the intervention group (65 participants) and the control group (65 participants). In the intervention group, educational content was delivered through group training sessions, group discussion sessions, and face-to-face question-and-answer sessions, as well as in group meetings (17–23 participants). For the delivery of educational content (short videos and text messages) during the follow-up phase, participants in the intervention group were divided into two groups: WhatsApp (40 participants) and Eita (25 participants). During the period between the educational sessions and the post-test, necessary reminders and follow-ups regarding medication adherence and the consumption of healthy, low-salt foods were conducted based on the constructs of the EPHPM. Data were collected using a researcher-developed questionnaire in two stages: pre-test and post-test (3 months after the intervention). Independent t-test, paired t-test, Chi-square test, and analysis of variance were used to analyze the data.RESULTS:The findings of the study revealed that, after the educational intervention, a significant difference was observed in the mean scores of the following constructs between the intervention and control groups regarding adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet: perceived susceptibility (P ˂ 0.001, MD = 1.835), perceived severity (P ˂ 0.001, MD = 1.993), perceived barriers (P ˂ 0.001, MD = 1.061), perceived self-efficacy (P ˂ 0.045, MD = 2.790), interpersonal influences (P ˂ 0.001, MD = 1.611), and commitment to action (P ˂ 0.001, MD = 3.143).CONCLUSION:The educational intervention based on the constructs of the EPHPM led to the promotion of treatment adherence, the adoption of a healthy, low-salt diet, and blood pressure control among patients with hypertension.

Introduction

Uncontrolled hypertension is one of the leading preventable causes of heart attacks and strokes.[1] Only one in four people with high blood pressure manages to keep their blood pressure under control.[2] This condition represents a major public health challenge, resulting in millions of adult deaths worldwide each year.[3] Uncontrolled blood pressure is one of the main factors contributing to the high rate of stroke and mortality in society.[4] However, 50% of patients with hypertension do not have their blood pressure adequately controlled.[5] The rate of blood pressure control among treated patients is approximately 50% in the United States[6] and 11% in China.[7] In Iran, the rate of blood pressure control among hypertensive patients receiving pharmacological treatment stands at 38.6%.[8] Studies have demonstrated that despite the availability of effective antihypertensive medications and easy access to these drugs, many patients undergoing treatment still have uncontrolled blood pressure.[1] Hypertension is a chronic condition that necessitates lifestyle modifications and appropriate pharmacological treatment.[9,10]

Evaluation of self-care behaviors in patients with high blood pressure can contribute to better blood pressure control.[11] The World Health Organization recognizes self-care behaviors as a fundamental approach to controlling high blood pressure. Adherence to medication; engaging in regular physical activity; following a healthy, low-salt diet; maintaining a healthy body weight; and avoiding tobacco and alcohol consumption are all effective measures for controlling high blood pressure.[12] Interventions such as behavior and lifestyle modifications, including improving dietary habits, increasing physical activity, and quitting tobacco and alcohol, are also effective in controlling high blood pressure.[13]

Medication adherence and a low-salt diet are two crucial factors in controlling high blood pressure.[14,15] Health education-based interventions play a vital role in enhancing health literacy among patients with hypertension. Therefore, intervention programs are necessary to reinforce self-care, promote healthy behaviors, and effectively manage uncontrolled blood pressure.[16,17]

The use of health-focused models to identify factors related to health problems in hypertensive patients can be extremely valuable. The Pender health promotion model is one of the most comprehensive and predictive frameworks designed to encourage health-promoting behaviors. This model is effective in achieving lifestyle behavior changes and reinforcing systems for hypertension management.[18] Therefore, considering the role of the Pender health promotion model in facilitating behavior change and the critical importance of adopting low-salt diets and adhering to medication for managing hypertension, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of educational interventions based on the constructs of the Pender model on blood pressure control among patients in the city of Ahvaz [Figure 1].

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The present study is a randomized clinical trial conducted from April to October 2024 among patients with uncontrolled hypertension covered by health centers in the East, West, and Karun districts of Ahvaz.

Study participants and sampling

The sample size was calculated based on a quasi-experimental study by Esmaeili et al.[19] and the constructs of the Pender health promotion model. In that study, the mean (SD) self-care scores post-intervention were 192.14 (14.51) in the intervention group (n = 43) and 155.31 (26.43) in the control group (n = 43), with a significant difference (P < 0.001). To ensure adequate power, assuming a 10-point difference, 90% power, and 1% type I error, the minimum sample size was estimated at 65 participants per group. Ultimately, 130 willing individuals were selected via simple random sampling and allocated equally into intervention and control groups. Due to the loss of three participants during the study (incomplete questionnaires and absence from more than one training session), the final sample size decreased to 127 participants (63 in the intervention group and 64 in the control group) [Figure 2].

Sampling method and participants’ follow-up

The inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: physician-diagnosed hypertension at least 1 year prior, literacy (ability to read and write), age 18–70 years, active record in health centers/posts/houses, no prior participation in similar educational programs, systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg despite antihypertensive medication, and written consent. Exclusion criteria included incomplete questionnaire responses post-intervention, missing more than one educational session, and refusal to have BP measured after the intervention. Using a multistage random sampling method, a total of 130 patients (65 in the intervention group and 65 in the control group) with uncontrolled hypertension were selected to participate. Specifically, from the East and West health centers, two urban comprehensive health centers and one rural comprehensive health center were selected, while from the Karun health center, one urban and one rural comprehensive health center were chosen. Subsequently, from each urban comprehensive health center, four health posts (2 for the intervention group and 2 for the control group, totaling 20 posts), and from the rural centers, four health houses (2 intervention and 2 control, totaling 12 houses) were randomly selected.

Randomization and participant recruitment

After randomly assigning health posts and health houses into intervention and control groups, patients with uncontrolled blood pressure (BP ≥140/90 mmHg) whose electronic records were available in the SIB system were randomly allocated to either group. Participants were then invited to join the study via phone calls. To assign individuals, patients’ names were coded numerically and placed in an opaque box. Sample size was determined based on the number of uncontrolled hypertensive patients in each health post and health house. After completing these steps, a total of 20 health posts (10 intervention and 10 control) and 12 health houses (6 intervention and 6 control) with their respective patient numbers and codes were assigned with the assistance of an independent person.

Intervention design

Initially, in both groups before the intervention, blood pressure measurements (twice with a 5-minute interval) and body mass index (BMI) measurements were conducted. Subsequently, the pre-test questionnaire was completed in writing. With the assistance of professors, health education specialists, physicians (general practitioners and internists), and nutrition experts, educational content tailored to the characteristics of the target population and based on the constructs of the enhanced Pender health promotion model (EPHPM), which are effective in controlling high blood pressure, was created and implemented in the intervention group. The educational content included the following topics: perceived sensitivity and severity (providing statistics and information about the consequences of high blood pressure, complications of high blood pressure, etc.), drug therapy, family support, benefits of controlling high blood pressure, barriers to controlling high blood pressure, self-efficacy in controlling high blood pressure, the role of situational influences, commitment to action, and the role of nutrition in controlling high blood pressure. The educational content in the intervention group was delivered through, group training sessions (17–23 participants), group discussion sessions (17–23 participants), question-and-answer sessions, and a face-to-face meeting in groups of 2–3 people. The group discussion sessions (3 sessions) were conducted over a 1-month period in the intervention group. For the delivery of educational content (short videos and text messages) during the follow-up phase, participants in the intervention group were divided into two groups: WhatsApp (40 participants) and Eita (25 participants). To reinforce the educational content provided to the intervention group, necessary reminders and follow-ups regarding medication adherence and the consumption of healthy, low-salt foods were conducted based on the constructs of the developed Pender model. This was achieved by sending educational content (short videos and text messages) weekly through social media platforms. Additionally, questions from the intervention group were answered by an internal medicine specialist, a general practitioner, and a nutrition expert via these platforms. For each participant in the intervention group, a healthy, low-salt meal plan was prepared by a nutrition expert based on anthropometric data (height, age, gender, and BMI) and provided to them. Furthermore, an educational booklet containing the educational content was prepared in Word format and shared with the intervention group through WhatsApp and Eita.

During the intervention phase, blood pressure was measured monthly for both groups (intervention and control). The post-test was conducted 3 months after the educational sessions and the delivery of educational content short videos and text messages [Table 1]. In the post-test phase, blood pressure (measured twice with a 5-minute interval) and BMI were measured, and the post-test questionnaire was completed by the intervention group (63 participants) and the control group (64 participants). To prevent the spread of information and the influence of the two groups on each other, participants in the intervention and control groups were selected from different locations. Control group participants were not given information about which group (control or intervention) they belonged to. In the final phase, the educational program was evaluated (effect and outcome) based on the impact of the constructs of the EPHPM model on controlling high blood pressure in both groups (intervention and control). After the educational intervention, systolic and diastolic blood pressure of both the intervention and control groups were assessed at follow-up 1 month, 2 months, and 3 months after the intervention.

Validity and reliability of the questionnaire

After an extensive search and numerous studies, 134 items were written on the basis of the constructs of the developed Pender model. In the qualitative validation stage, 43 items from 134 items were eliminated after qualitative interviews were conducted with four health professionals. The questions were corrected at this stage from the aspects of writing and wording. For quantitative validity, the remaining 91 items were assessed by 10 health professionals in terms of the content validity index (CVI) and content validity ratio (CVR). The CVI (≤0.8) and CVR (≤0.75) were considered acceptable; 13 items were removed, and a total of 77items remained.[20]

To assess the reliability of the questionnaire, it was completed by 25 participants in the first phase and completed again by the same participants 15 days later in the second phase. The ICC for the entire questionnaire was 0.87. The correlation coefficient before and after for each of the structures related to the questions of the section ‘Healthy and low-salt diet’ (perceived sensitivity (before = 0.86, after = 0.82), perceived severity (before = 0.90, after = 0.75), perceived benefit (before = 0.72, after = 0.85), perceived barriers (before = 0.78, after = 0.76), perceived self-efficacy (before = 0.90, after = 0, 91), activity related affect (before = 0.76, after = 0.71), interpersonal influences (before = 0.74, after = 0.79), situational influences (before = 0.74, after = 0.71), commitment to plan (before = 0.85, after = 0.87) and diet compliance behavior (before = 0.76, after = 0.70) and the constructs related to medication adherence questions (perceived sensitivity (before = 0.89, after = 0.87), perceived severity (before = 0.88, after = 0.87), perceived benefit (before = 0.89, after = 0.77) and perceived barriers (before = 0.89, after = 0.92), perceived self-efficacy (before = 0.89, after = 0.86), activity related affect (before = 0.70, after = 0.71), interpersonal influences (before = 0.90, after = 0.85), situational influences (before = 0.71, after = 0.75), commitment to plan (before = 0.92, after = 0.91), and medication adherence behavior (before = 0.87, after = 0.89) calculated.

Data analysis

For data analysis, all collected information was entered into SPSS version 21. To compare quantitative and qualitative variables before and after the educational intervention, the following statistical tests were used: independent t-test and paired t-test for comparing means, Chi-square test for comparing categorical variables, and analysis of variance for assessing differences among groups. These tests were applied to evaluate the effectiveness of the educational intervention and to determine significant changes in the studied variables.

Ethics consideration

The Ethics Committee of Hamedan University of Medical Sciences approved the research (Ref. No.: IR.UMSHA.REC.1402.391), and the study was conducted under the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants were reminded about the confidentiality of the information of the studied community, the observance of trust in the resources, and the anonymity of the participants. Written informed consent was obtained from the individuals for this study.

Results

Twenty-nine participants (41.6%) in the intervention group and 27 participants (44.6%) in the control group reported irregular medication use. The findings in Table 1 reveal that the two groups were comparable in terms of demographic variables, including gender, education level, ethnicity, employment status, marital status, type of insurance coverage, family history of hypertension in first-degree relatives, history of drug use, and history of antihypertensive medication use. Both groups exhibited maximum variance, and no statistically significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups (P > 0.05). The predominant ethnicities in both groups were Arab and Bakhtiari. Regarding marital status, 54 participants (83.1%) in the intervention group and 58 participants (89.2%) in the control group were married.

The analysis of intragroup and intergroup relationships based on the constructs of the developed Pender model in the study participants [Table 2] showed that there was no significant difference in the mean scores of the constructs of the developed Pender model regarding adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet between the intervention and control groups before the educational intervention (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of educational interventions, based on the constructs of the EPHPM, on medication adherence and compliance with a healthy, low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. The results showed that, before the intervention, there were no significant differences between the two groups in the mean scores of the model’s constructs related to adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet and treatment compliance. However, 3 months after the intervention, a significant difference was observed between the groups.

Health education interventions based on behavior change models effectively control blood pressure in patients with hypertension. Their use in educational programs is thus essential.[21] Educational interventions have been shown to be highly effective in promoting adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet and medication adherence in patients with hypertension.[22] Health professionals should strengthen educational interventions to promote medication adherence and lifestyle modification among patients with hypertension.[23] The primary strategy for managing hypertension is lifestyle modification, which includes adopting a healthy, low-salt diet and medication adherence.[24] Reducing salt intake in daily diets is an effective and cost-efficient method for controlling high blood pressure and reducing cardiovascular diseases in human populations.[25] Numerous studies have demonstrated that adherence to a healthy diet, such as consuming fruits, vegetables, and low-salt foods, limiting salt intake, and medication adherence, is effective in controlling high blood pressure (both systolic and diastolic).[26,27,28,29]

The findings of the current study revealed that after the educational intervention, the constructs of perceived susceptibility (P < 0.001), perceived severity (P < 0.001), perceived barriers (P < 0.001), perceived self-efficacy (P < 0.001), interpersonal influences (P < 0.001), and commitment to action (P < 0.001) related to adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet, and constructs of perceived susceptibility (P < 0.001), perceived barriers (P < 0.001), perceived self-efficacy (P < 0.001), interpersonal influences (P < 0.001), commitment to action (P < 0.001), and medication adherence behavior (P < 0.001) were significantly improved in patients with uncontrolled hypertension in the intervention group. Additionally, the mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the intervention group significantly decreased compared to the control group after the implementation of the educational programs. These results highlight the effectiveness of the educational intervention in enhancing health-promoting behaviors and improving hypertension management.

Educational interventions increase perceived susceptibility and severity among patients with hypertension in the intervention group.[30] Yazdanpanah et al.[31] demonstrated in their study that when patients perceive their susceptibility to the consequences of high blood pressure, their adherence to treatment increases, which aligns with the findings of the present study. Other studies have shown that educational interventions in patients with hypertension lead to an increase in perceived sensitivity, perceived severity, and a decrease in systolic and diastolic blood pressure, which are consistent with the results of the present study.[22,32,33] Naeemi et al. and Yazdanpanah et al. showed in their studies that perceived severity significantly affects treatment adherence in patients; these findings were inconsistent with the results of the present study.[31,33] After educational interventions, patients with uncontrolled hypertension came to believe that if they did not follow a healthy, low-salt diet and did not take blood pressure medications regularly and on time, they would be at risk of high blood pressure and, as a result, exposed to physical, psychological, social, and economic complications resulting from it.

Self-efficacy refers to a person’s ability and belief in managing blood pressure through healthy behaviors.[34,35,36] Higher self-efficacy enables patients to overcome barriers to treatment adherence and follow a healthy, low-salt diet.[37,38] In this study, the mean score of the perceived barriers constructs in patients with high blood pressure increased significantly after the educational intervention, meaning that patients gained the ability to overcome barriers to consuming low-salt foods and taking medications regularly and on time. Hypertensive patients undergoing pharmacological treatment who receive support from their family members exhibit higher self-efficacy, which leads to greater treatment adherence compared to those without social support.[39] Interpersonal influences can increase willingness to act and subsequently improve health-promoting behaviors. Therefore, the stronger one’s commitment to a healthy behavior, the more likely it is that that behavior will be maintained over time.[40] Rezaeemanesh et al. reported significant differences in the mean scores of perceived self-efficacy, perceived barriers, interpersonal influences, and commitment to action after educational intervention in the intervention group, which were consistent with the results of the present study.[41,42] Khorashadizadeh et al.[43] demonstrated that the constructs of interpersonal influences and self-efficacy were effective in improving treatment adherence in diabetic patients after the educational intervention, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. The construct of commitment to action was identified as one of the effective factors in treatment adherence among patients with uncontrolled hypertension after the educational intervention. A study by Loykaew et al.[44] demonstrated that commitment to action is one of the most effective constructs in promoting health-oriented behaviors. These findings highlight the importance of addressing psychological and social factors, such as self-efficacy, interpersonal influences, and commitment to action, in designing educational interventions to improve treatment adherence and adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet, in patients with chronic conditions like hypertension.

The findings of the present study showed that after the educational intervention, no significant difference was observed between the scores of the constructs of perceived benefits, behavior-related emotions, and situational influences in patients with uncontrolled hypertension in the intervention and control groups. Mirkarimi et al.[17] reported in their study perceived benefits, affects related to behavior, and situational influences were the most important predictors of healthy eating behavior in patients with hypertension, which was inconsistent with the findings of the present study.

Numerous studies, such as those by Zenoozadeh et al.[45] and Drevenhorn et al.,[46] have shown that the constructs of the Pender model significantly enhance self-care behaviors and treatment adherence after educational interventions. Educational interventions based on behavioral models play a significant role in improving treatment adherence and adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet among patients with chronic conditions, such as hypertension.[17,31] The Pender health promotion model is effective in encouraging patients to take their antihypertensive medications as prescribed by their physicians. Therefore, educational interventions designed based on this model are effective in controlling high blood pressure.[47] In this study, both systolic and diastolic blood pressure significantly decreased after the educational intervention. Therefore, it can be concluded that, in addition to the role of the individual and their surroundings in adhering to a healthy, low-salt diet and medication adherence, the constructs of perceived susceptibility and perceived severity, when added to the Pender model, can enhance self-care behaviors and treatment adherence by increasing patients’ understanding and belief in the complications of hypertension. Given that this is the first study to investigate educational interventions for adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet and medication adherence using the constructs of the developed Pender model in patients with uncontrolled hypertension, the results of this study can provide valuable practical information for researchers, policymakers, decision-makers, and healthcare providers working in the field of uncontrolled hypertension.

The present study contained limitations, such as lack of cooperation from some comprehensive health center staff with the researcher in gathering information. In addition, we could not educate families and healthcare workers givers due to cost and facilities limitations, and some comprehensive health centers lack suitable meeting rooms for conducting group discussion sessions.

Conclusion

There are several challenges in the prevention and treatment of hypertension, including the lack of health literacy among healthcare providers and patients, inadequate patient understanding of the importance of taking medication regularly and on time, and fear of side effects of blood pressure medications.[48] When patients’ health literacy is low, they perform poorly in self-management and hypertension control programs.[49] Given the limited health literacy of patients with hypertension in Iran, the use of educational interventions based on the constructs of the Pender model, which was developed to promote self-management of hypertension among these patients, may be effective. The results of the current study demonstrated that education based on the constructs of the developed Pender model in the intervention group led to the improvement of treatment adherence and adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet in patients with hypertension. As a result, after the implementation of the educational intervention, systolic and diastolic blood pressure significantly decreased in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Therefore, it is recommended that the developed Pender model be utilized in future educational programs for controlling high blood pressure in patients.
