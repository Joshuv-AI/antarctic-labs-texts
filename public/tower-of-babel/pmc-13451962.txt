Isolated Testicular Tuberculosis Mimicking a Testicular Tumor: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Tian-peng Zhan, Xiao-shan Li, Pan-feng Shang, Nikhat Kaura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTesticular tuberculosis is a rare form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB). Due to the nonspecific nature of its clinical manifestations and imaging features, it is often misdiagnosed as a malignant testicular tumor, leading to unnecessary radical orchiectomy. Accurate preoperative diagnosis is essential for organ preservation and appropriate antituberculous therapy.CaseA 59‑year‑old male patient was admitted with a 4‑day history of left testicular enlargement. Pre‑admission ultrasonography suggested “a solid mass in the left testicle, suspected seminoma.” Physical examination revealed enlargement of the left testicle with moderate consistency. Scrotal ultrasound, contrast‑enhanced ultrasound, and enhanced CT all supported the diagnosis of testicular malignancy. The patient underwent left radical orchiectomy. However, postoperative pathology revealed extensive acute and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, caseous necrosis, and granulomatous inflammation within the testicular tissue, consistent with tuberculous pathology. The final pathological diagnosis was “left testicular tuberculosis.”ConclusionTesticular tuberculosis is an important differential diagnosis for testicular tumors. Despite advances in modern imaging techniques, atypical presentation remains highly prone to misdiagnosis. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for testicular tuberculosis in patients with atypical clinical and imaging findings of testicular masses. When feasible, obtaining tissue samples for histopathological and molecular examination preoperatively or intraoperatively may be crucial in avoiding unnecessary orchiectomy and achieving organ preservation.Keywords: case report, differential diagnosis, misdiagnosis, seminoma, testicular tumor, testicular tuberculosis

1. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and remains one of the world’s deadliest single infectious diseases. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), despite global progress in TB control, millions of new cases emerge annually. The emergence of drug‐resistant TB poses an even more severe challenge to global public health [1]. TB most commonly affects the lungs, forming pulmonary tuberculosis. However, the bacillus can spread through the bloodstream or lymphatic system to any part of the body, leading to extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) [2]. Genitourinary tuberculosis (GUTB) is the second most common form of EPTB after lymph node tuberculosis [3]. Within the male reproductive system, TB most commonly affects the epididymis, followed by the seminal vesicles and prostate [4]. Testicular tuberculosis is relatively uncommon. Traditionally, it was believed that testicular tuberculosis typically resulted from direct spread from epididymal tuberculosis, forming tuberculous epididymo‐orchitis. However, increasing evidence suggests that isolated testicular tuberculosis—a primary testicular lesion without epididymal involvement—may arise directly through hematogenous dissemination [5, 6]. Its clinical presentation often involves a painless or mildly painful testicular mass, which highly overlaps with the typical symptoms of testicular malignancies, making it a “masquerader” in clinical practice [5, 7]. Given that the high cure rate for testicular tumors relies on timely radical orchiectomy, clinicians often prioritize malignancy when encountering solid testicular masses. This tendency may lead to misdiagnosis and unnecessary organ removal in some testicular tuberculosis patients.

In recent years, advances in imaging techniques—particularly multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—and molecular diagnostics have enhanced our understanding and diagnostic capabilities regarding testicular tuberculosis. Nevertheless, the diagnostic complexity and challenges persist. By reporting a rare case, this study aims to systematically explore the diagnostic dilemmas, imaging characteristics, recent advances in molecular diagnostics, and future therapeutic strategies for testicular tuberculosis. The patient presented with clinical manifestations and imaging findings highly suggestive of seminoma but was ultimately pathologically confirmed to have solitary testicular tuberculosis. The goal is to enhance clinicians’ awareness of this condition and to reduce misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.

2. Case Presentation

A 59‐year‐old male patient presented with an unexplained enlargement of the left testicle 4 days prior to admission. The affected testicle was soft in consistency with mild discomfort and a sensation of heaviness, which persisted even when lying down. He sought care at a local hospital where ultrasound revealed a solid mass in the left testicle, highly suggestive of seminoma; right epididymal congestion; and bilateral hydrocele. He was referred to our hospital for further management. During the course of illness, the patient was alert and oriented, with adequate appetite and sleep. There was no documented history of fever or no significant recent weight change. The patient has borderline hypertension without oral medication. Diagnosed with benign prostatic hyperplasia in 2020, he has been treated with finasteride and red light therapy with acceptable results. He denies a history of diabetes or coronary heart disease. The patient denies smoking or alcohol consumption and has no family history of TB. Admission physical examination revealed T 36.5°C, P 98 bpm, R 21 bpm, BP 147/99 mmHg, and weight 92 kg. Physical development was normal with no other abnormalities. No tenderness or percussion pain in bilateral renal areas. No tenderness along bilateral ureteral courses. No tenderness or mass in the suprapubic bladder region. Male pubic hair distribution, adult‐type penis, and no redness or purulent discharge at the external urethral orifice. Scrotal skin normal. The left testis showed increased volume, larger than the right, with firm consistency and no tenderness. The right testis was soft with mild tenderness. Preliminary diagnosis: “Malignant testicular tumor.”

3. Laboratory Findings

Laboratory findings were as follows: WBC 12.17 × 109/L (3.5–9.5 × 109/L), RBC 5.25 × 1012/L (4.3–5.8 × 1012/L), Hb 149 g/L (130–175 g/L), PTL 229 × 109/L (125–350 × 109/L), neutrophil percentage 89.40 (40%–75%), lymphocyte percentage 5.80 (20%–50%), monocyte percentage 4.60 (3%–10%), eosinophil percentage 0.00 (0.4%–8%), and basophil percentage 0.20 (0%–1%). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 8 U/L (9–50 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 16 U/L (15–40 U/L), AST/ALT 2.00, total bilirubin 22.7 μmol/L (≤26 μmol/L), direct bilirubin 4.3 μmol/L (≤4 μmol/L), indirect bilirubin 18.4 μmol/L (≤22 μmol/L), urea 8.53 mmol/L (3.1–8.0 mmol/L), creatinine 97.8 μmol/L (57–97 μmol/L). Alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) 7.83 ng/mL (<7 ng/mL). Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) <1.00 mIU/mL (0.00–10.00 mIU/mL). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 224 U/L (120–250 U/L). Hepatitis B surface antibody, hepatitis B e antibody, and hepatitis B core antibody were positive. Hepatitis C antibody, syphilis‐specific antibody, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antigen/antibody were all negative. Chest X‐ray showed no abnormalities. Scrotal ultrasound revealed a hypoechoic lesion measuring ~2.1 × 2.4 × 1.8 cm at the inferior pole of the left testis. The lesion had clear margins and heterogeneous internal echoes, with abundant blood flow signals detectable within (Figure 1A). Testicular contrast‐enhanced ultrasound revealed a hypoechoic lesion at the inferior pole of the left testis with a malignant enhancement pattern, highly suggestive of seminoma (Figure 1B, C). CT revealed a roughly circular mixed‐density lesion at the inferior pole of the left testis, measuring ~23 × 22 mm. The lesion exhibited a relatively regular shape with distinct margins. The solid component demonstrated mild enhancement on contrast‐enhanced imaging, while the cystic low‐density area within showed no enhancement (Figure 1D). No definite enlarged retroperitoneal lymph nodes.

(A) Scrotal ultrasound; (B, C) contrast‐enhanced ultrasound; (D) CT.

Based on the patient’s medical history, laboratory tests, and ancillary examinations, a definitive diagnosis of “malignant testicular tumor” was established. Surgical indications were clear with no significant contraindications. A unilateral orchiectomy was performed electively under combined intravenous and inhalation anesthesia, proceeding uneventfully. Intraoperative findings: the left testis was irregular in shape, and the epididymis was grossly normal. However, postoperative pathological examination revealed marked proliferation of fibrous tissue surrounding the epididymis and seminiferous tubules of the testis. This tissue contained extensive infiltration of acute and chronic inflammatory cells, with evidence of caseous necrosis and granulomatous inflammation. No granulomatous inflammation was observed at the vascular stumps of the vas deferens and the spermatic cord. Morphologically, the pathological changes were primarily suggestive of TB (Figure 2). A limitation of this case is that Ziehl–Neelsen staining, PCR, and culture were not performed; the diagnosis was based solely on histomorphology.

4. Discussion

The diagnostic and therapeutic process of this case highlights the significant diagnostic challenges posed by testicular tuberculosis as a “masquerader” of testicular tumors in clinical practice. The patient presented with the classic chief complaint of “testicular enlargement.” Combined with imaging studies, all evidence pointed toward a malignant testicular tumor, yet the final pathological diagnosis was testicular tuberculosis. The primary cause of misdiagnosis lies in the diverse and nonspecific clinical manifestations of testicular tuberculosis. It can present as an acute, subacute, or chronic process. The acute phase may present with sudden testicular enlargement, pain, and fever, mimicking acute orchitis or testicular torsion. However, subacute or chronic onset is more common, presenting as a painless or mildly painful testicular mass or diffuse induration lasting weeks to months, accompanied by a sensation of heaviness [5, 8]. The patient in this case presented with mild pain and a sensation of heaviness, which is nearly indistinguishable from the typical presentation of testicular tumors—a painless mass. Literature reports indicate that ~50% of testicular tuberculosis patients present with painless masses, leading clinicians to primarily consider the possibility of a tumor [9].

Imaging studies are crucial for preoperative assessment of testicular mass characteristics. Ultrasound is the preferred imaging modality for evaluating testicular lesions. Typical ultrasound findings for testicular tuberculosis often originate in the epididymis, presenting as heterogeneous enlargement and hypoechoic areas, potentially accompanied by calcifications, abscess formation, or cutaneous fistulae. When inflammation extends to the testis, it may present as diffuse, heterogeneous hypoechoic enlargement; focal, multiple small nodular hypoechoic areas; or a single, larger nodular hypoechoic mass [7]. In this case, ultrasound revealed an isolated hypoechoic mass at the inferior pole of the left testis, exhibiting internal echoic heterogeneity and rich blood flow. This presentation of an isolated hypoechoic mass closely resembles the typical ultrasonic appearance of seminoma. Seminoma typically manifests as a well‐defined, regular‐shaped, homogeneous hypoechoic mass with abundant internal vascularity [10]. Ultrasound contrast imaging provides richer hemodynamic information by observing the real‐time perfusion pattern of contrast agent within tissues. Malignant tumors typically exhibit a “rapid high enhancement with slightly faster washout” pattern due to neovascularization. The contrast imaging results in this case matched this malignant enhancement pattern, serving as a key factor contributing to the high preoperative diagnostic confidence for seminoma. However, it must be recognized that severe inflammatory lesions, such as tuberculous granulomas, can also exhibit rapid arterial phase enhancement due to their highly vascularized granulation tissue [11]. Therefore, the malignant enhancement pattern demonstrated by contrast‐enhanced ultrasound cannot completely rule out inflammatory lesions.

This case did not undergo an MRI examination, but retrospective analysis suggests that MRI might provide additional valuable differential diagnostic information. A recent study systematically described the MRI features of epididymal/testicular tuberculosis [4]. Typical tuberculous lesions often exhibit heterogeneous low signal intensity or isointense to hypointense signal on T2‐weighted images (T2WI), reflecting fibrosis and cellular density within the granulomatous tissue. A ring of lower T2 signal intensity surrounding the lesion is frequently visible, representing the fibrous capsule. The center of caseous necrosis may show isointense or slightly hyperintense signal on T1‐weighted images (T1WI), with variable signal intensity on T2WI. Postcontrast enhancement is typically absent or limited to a ring‐like pattern. In contrast, seminoma exhibits relatively characteristic MRI features. It typically demonstrates uniform isointense signal on T1WI, characteristic uniform low signal on T2WI, and uniform marked enhancement after contrast administration. Fine, low‐signal‐intensity fibrous septa are often visible within the tumor, dividing it into lobular segments—a key feature of seminoma [12]. Although atypical cases exist, MRI frequently provides critical clues for differentiation. For example, a uniformly low signal on T2WI strongly suggests seminoma, whereas a testicular mass with marked epididymal enlargement, heterogeneous signal intensity, and no central enhancement is more suggestive of TB. Diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) can also be helpful; both tumors and abscesses typically show high signal intensity centrally, but combining ADC values and enhancement patterns aids in differentiation [9]. Studies indicate mMRI achieves up to 95% accuracy in preoperatively distinguishing seminoma from nonseminoma tumors, and its value in differentiating tumors from inflammatory lesions is increasingly recognized [12].

When clinical presentation and imaging fail to establish a definitive diagnosis, obtaining tissue or pathogenetic evidence may become the ultimate means of confirmation. Can a preoperative pathological diagnosis be obtained to avoid unnecessary surgery? This raises a longstanding controversy: preoperative biopsy of testicular masses. Traditionally, percutaneous needle biopsy of masses suspected of testicular cancer was considered contraindicated due to concerns about needle‐track implantation. However, this notion is being challenged. Studies indicate that the risk of needle‐track implantation is extremely low when strict technical protocols are followed, such as using an inguinal approach and applying short clamping of the spermatic cord [9]. For patients with atypical imaging findings, the value of percutaneous fine‐needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) or core needle biopsy (CNB) is particularly significant. FNAC yields cytological specimens that, when combined with acid‐fast staining or culture, may directly diagnose TB. CNB, meanwhile, obtains tissue sections for histopathological examination, offering higher diagnostic value. In recent years, molecular diagnostic technologies, particularly the WHO‐recommended Xpert MTB/RIF test, have revolutionized rapid diagnosis of EPTB. Xpert MTB/RIF is an automated real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) system capable of simultaneously detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA and rifampicin resistance within 2 h [9]. This technology exhibits high diagnostic sensitivity for sputum samples and shows promising applicability in extrapulmonary specimens (e.g., urine, cerebrospinal fluid, tissue fluid). For GBTB, studies have explored the diagnostic value of Xpert MTB/RIF in urine samples [13]. A dedicated study evaluating Xpert MTB/RIF’s accuracy in diagnosing epididymal tuberculosis found high sensitivity and specificity in detecting the pathogen from FNAC specimens [14]. Another study further confirmed that ultrasound‐guided needle biopsy combined with GeneXpert MTB/RIF testing enables safe, rapid, and accurate diagnosis of epididymal tuberculosis [15].

Furthermore, intraoperative frozen section biopsy (FSB) must be considered in cases with a high suspicion of testicular tuberculosis. Intraoperative FSB is a valuable tool that allows rapid histopathological assessment while preserving the testis if benign or infectious etiology is confirmed. Compared with FNAC, FSB provides superior tissue architecture and higher diagnostic accuracy, whereas FNAC often yields limited cellular material and may miss granulomatous inflammation or caseous necrosis. CNB offers better tissue preservation than FNAC but carries non‑negligible risks of hematoma, infection, and theoretical tumor seeding in cases of unsuspected malignancy. A limitation of this case is that intraoperative FSB was not performed, and future similar cases may benefit from this approach.

5. Conclusion

Testicular tuberculosis represents a rare yet significant differential diagnosis of testicular tumors. Urologists encountering solid testicular masses must maintain a broad diagnostic mindset, avoiding a narrow focus on neoplastic possibilities alone. To prevent unnecessary orchiectomies in future clinical practice, we advocate a multimodal, individualized diagnostic strategy. This involves heightening clinical vigilance for testicular tuberculosis, fully leveraging advanced imaging techniques such as mMRI, and employing pre‑ or intraoperative molecular diagnostic techniques with both courage and caution in selected high‑risk or atypical cases. Through such a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic approach, we aim to strike a better balance between organ preservation and disease eradication, thereby improving the prognosis and quality of life for patients with testicular tuberculosis.
