Association between gut microbiota and acute upper respiratory tract infection: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yuexing Li, Guanglei Chen, Ke Chen, Wen Li, Yunzhi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting specific gut microbiota (GM) species to prevent and treat acute upper respiratory tract infection (AURTI) has attracted researchers’ attention, but the relationship between the two is unclear. Based on the summary data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on GM and five types of AURTIs (acute nasopharyngitis (common cold), acute pharyngitis, acute sinusitis, acute upper respiratory infections, and acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites), we performed two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) to assess the causal relationship. Through inverse variance weighting (IVW) method, we found that 33 potential microbial taxa can influence the occurrence of AURTI. Sensitivity analysis showed no potential horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity bias. We further employed multivariable Mendelian randomization to investigate the impact of potential interference factors on the significant associations previously identified, considering aspects such as comorbidities associated with AURTI, seasonal variations, pathogen specificity, and history of antibiotic allergies. Ultimately, 11 microbial taxa remained significantly associated. This study provides robust evidence for a causal relationship between GM and five types of AURTIs, thereby offering a foundation for the development of microbiota-targeted therapies and related probiotic interventions aimed at AURTI.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Acute upper respiratory tract infection, Mendelian randomization studySubject terms: Microbiology, Microbiota

Introduction

The upper respiratory tract (URT), serving as the conduit connecting the external environment to the lower respiratory tract (LRT), is susceptible to colonization by various microorganisms1. When the URT is infected by bacteria or viruses, causing pharyngitis and rhinitis, it is collectively called upper respiratory tract infection (URTI)2. URTI represents the most prevalent type of respiratory tract infection (RTI) and is an important risk factor for triggering lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI), potentially leading to severe complications. Acute upper respiratory tract infection (AURTI) is one of the most common illnesses in children3. In fact, AURTI is a common disease, regardless of region, age, and gender4. Currently, the most used drugs for AURTI are antibiotics, but antibiotic misuse has become an important cause of antibiotic resistance in microorganisms5. In the United States, over 2 million cases of antibiotic-resistant infections occur annually, resulting in at least 23,000 deaths6. Therefore, the quest for safer and more effective treatment methods has become a critical aspect of protecting the health of AURTI patients.

The microbiota refers to the specific microorganisms that inhabit the mucous membranes and skin surface of the host. These species are numerous and they maintain a dynamic balance with the host, influenced by genes, age, sex, and lifestyle. In most cases this relationship is symbiotic, but many factors may lead to disturbances in this interaction7. The gut has been colonized by a plethora of microorganisms since birth, gradually establishing a stable colony through solid food consumption8. Specific gut microbiota (GM) species have the capacity to produce metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate), tryptophan, and bile acids. These metabolites not only play a crucial role in regulation of food digestion and intestinal endocrine function, but also affect the regulation of T helper (Th)1, Th2, Th17, regulatory T cells (Tregs) and other immune cells, aiding in maintaining both gut and systemic immune homeostasis9. Therefore, the interaction of GM and its metabolites with the immune system of the intestine constitutes the unique intestinal regional immune system, which plays an important role in maintaining human health10. When alterations in the intestinal microenvironment occur due to changes in dietary structure and environmental factors, they not only affect the progression of intestinal diseases but also exert influence on remote organs such as the respiratory system11.

Like the GM, many microorganisms reside in URT, particularly in the oral, nasal cavity, and nasopharyngeal mucosa, and can enter LRT through aspiration12,13. The core genera of URT microbiota in healthy adults include Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Corynebacterium, Prevotella, Veillonella, Propionibacterium, and Fusobacterium. These microorganisms can help the host resist the development of lung infections by mechanisms such as direct microbiome competition with pathogens and indirect immune modulation13. The microbial imbalance in the microenvironment of both the gastrointestinal tract and URT disrupts immune homeostasis, and is considered an important factor in the frequent occurrence of RTI. The shaping of the immune system by the GM and the disruption of URT microbial composition by GM translocating into the airways can influence the occurrence of RTI14. In a study of male C57BL/6 mice transplanted with the fecal microbiota of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients, Wang et al. found that the mice exhibited weight loss, heightened lung inflammation, increased IL-1β and TNF-α concentrations, elevated T lymphocyte count, and reduced B lymphocyte count in the bloodstream15. Simultaneously, as our understanding of the gut-lung axis has advanced, microbial modulation therapies have been extensively studied. It has been verified that the long-term use of probiotics can effectively reduce the risk of RTI16. In a study involving 6950 subjects, Zhao et al. found that probiotics reduced the number of participants diagnosed with AURTI and may reduce the number of participants prescribed antibiotics for AURTI17.

The GM plays a critical role in respiratory health; however, the causal relationship between the GM and AURTI remains unclear. The human body is a complex system, and both the GM and AURTI are influenced by various factors, including underlying diseases, medications, seasonal variations, and specific pathogens. The extent to which these factors affect the causal relationship between the GM and AURTI has yet to be determined. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical analysis method based on Mendelian laws of inheritance and instrumental variables (IVs), using genetic data as a mediator to explore the causal relationship between exposure risk factors and outcomes. MR can effectively reduce the interference of confounding factors on results. Multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR), an extension of MR, allows the assessment of the direct effects of multiple exposures simultaneously. In this study, we employed bidirectional two-sample MR and MVMR to explore the potential causal relationships between the GM and five types of AURTIs. Furthermore, we examined the influence of interference factors on these causal effects, aiming to identify specific pathogenic microbial taxa and beneficial bacteria, thus advancing the development of precision medicine in the treatment of AURTI.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used GWAS summary statistics to perform bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to assess the potential causal relationship between 211 bacterial taxonomic units as exposure factors and five types of AURTI as outcomes. The AURTI categories included acute nasopharyngitis (common cold), acute pharyngitis, acute sinusitis, acute upper respiratory infections, and acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites. In addition, we incorporated nine conditions (diabetes, pulmonary heart disease, coronary atherosclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, vitamin D levels, influenza, COVID-19, and antibiotic allergy history) as covariates to evaluate the robustness of these causal relationships using MVMR. The study workflow is depicted in Fig. 1. Throughout the entire analysis process, strict adherence to the MRSTROBE guidelines was maintained18.

Data sources

The GM data used in the study were acquired from MiBioGen consortium. The MiBioGen database is one of the most comprehensive databases for host-genetic-microbiome association studies to date. The database includes genome-wide genotype and 16S microbiome sequencing data from 18,340 participants, involving 24 population sequences in the United States, Canada, Israel, South Korea, Germany, Denmark, the Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden, Finland, and the United Kingdom. European ancestry (16 cohorts, N = 13,266) made up the largest proportion of all participants19.

The data related to the five AURTIs were obtained from publicly available data in FinnGen database. The FinnGen study, which combines genotype data from Finnish biobanks and digital health record data from Finnish health registries, provides an important aid in the study of genetic variation associated with the disease trajectories in an isolated population. It has more than 500,000 participants. Diagnosis of AURTI is based on the International Classification of Diseases code J10. The study samples were limited to people of European origin, including 3,956 cases of acute nasopharyngitis (common cold), 4,747 cases of acute pharyngitis, 20,520 cases of acute sinusitis, 69,111 cases of acute upper respiratory infections, and 36,445 cases of acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites, with each case having a control sample size of 308,166 cases (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S00).

Selection of instrumental variables

In our study, 211 bacterial taxonomic units were analyzed at five taxonomic levels (phylum, order, order, family, and genus) to clarify the causal relationship between the GM and the five AURTIs.

When using the GM as the exposure and the five AURTIs as the outcome, we imposed the following requirements on the instrumental variables(IVs): (a) the significance threshold at the genome-wide level of the IVs associated with the GM should satisfy P < 1 × 10–5; (b) to satisfy the conditions of the MR analyses, we conducted a European 1,000-person based Genome Project linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis, which requires R2 < 0.001 and LD = 10000 kb for IVs; (c) to prevent allelic effects on the causal relationship between the GM and the five AURTIs, we used the F-statistic to assessed the strength of genetic variants as IVs. F-statistics ≤ 10 are considered as weak IVs, which may bias the analysis results. On the contrary, F-statistic > 10 indicates that the IVs are stronger, so we excluded IVs with an F-statistic ≤ 10.

When performing the reverse MR analysis, the IVs of five AURTIs met the following conditions:(1) acute nasopharyngitis (common cold): p < 5 × 10–6 , R2 < 0.001, LD = 10000 kb; (2) acute pharyngitis: p < 5 × 10–6, R2 < 0.001, LD = 10000 kb; (3) acute sinusitis: P < 5 × 10–8, R2 < 0.001, LD = 10000 kb; (4) acute upper respiratory infections: P < 5 × 10–8, R2 < 0.001, LD = 10000 kb; (5) acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites: P < 5 × 10–6, R2 < 0.001, LD = 10000 kb, and similarly, we respectively excluded IVs with F-statistics ≤ 10.

For the MVMR analysis, the following conditions were required to meet the criteria of P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, R2 < 0.001, LD = 10,000 kb, and F > 10: type 1 diabetes mellitus (diabetic ketoacidosis), cor pulmonale, coronary atherosclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels, influenza, and COVID-19. Meanwhile, for personal history of allergy to other antibiotic agents, the criteria were P < 1 × 10⁻5, R2 < 0.001, LD = 10,000 kb, and F > 10.

Statistical analyses

During the MR analyses, we mainly used R (version 4.3.1) with the help of the "Two Sample MR" R package (version 0.5.7) and “Mendelian Randomization” R package (version 0.9.0) .

We first used the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method to validate all IVs and generated a weighted total effect by judging the size of the p-value, When P < 0.05, it is defined as a causal relationship between exposure and outcome. To ensure the stability of IVW results, we supplemented the use of MR-Egger, weighted median (WM), and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO). The MR-Egger employs directed pleiotropy tests, causal effect tests, and causal effect estimates to evaluate whether genetic variants exhibit pleiotropy for the outcome that differ on average from zero. Additionally, it provides a consistent estimation of causal effects under the weaker assumption21. WM enables precise estimates of causality. The MR-PRESSO test removes outlier IVs to detect and correct outliers. The results are more reliable when the effect size of the supplementary method is consistent with IVW and P < 0.05. We performed a sensitivity analysis using the MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test to test for gene pleiotropy, and Cochran’s Q to detect the heterogeneity of IVs. Pleiotropy and heterogeneity were considered to exist when P < 0.05. subsequently, we performed a reverse MR analysis to assess whether there was a reverse causal relationship between AURTI and GM.

Finally, in the MVMR analysis, we applied the multivariable IVW method to assess the validity of all IVs. A result with MVMR_P < 0.05 was considered to indicate that the causal effect of the bacterial taxa on AURTIs was robust and not influenced by interference factors.

Results

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) selection

Through multiple quality control steps, we finally selected 2,818 SNPs associated with 211 bacterial species as IVs. The F-statistics for the SNPs ranged from 14.58 to 88.42, with a mean of 21.69, which all exceeded the threshold of 10, showing that bias is less likely to occur (Supplementary Table S1).

Forward MR analysis

Acute nasopharyngitis (common cold)

According to the IVW results, one family and five genera had causal relationships with acute nasopharyngitis. As shown in Fig. 2a, Gordonibacter and Escherichia/Shigella increased the risk of acute nasopharyngitis. Conversely, family Clostridiales vadinBB60 group, and Ruminococcus gauvreauii group showed a negative correlation with the risk of acute nasopharyngitis. The MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global test indicated no pleiotropy (P > 0.05), and the results of Cochran’s Q test showed no heterogeneity (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3a).

Acute pharyngitis

In this result, the genetically predicted relative abundance of Holdemanella and Turicibacter was positively correlated with the risk of acute pharyngitis. Phylum Actinobacteria, family Peptostreptococcaceae, family Rhodospirillaceae, order Rhodospirillales, and Eubacterium oxidoreducens group reduced the risk of acute pharyngitis (Fig. 2b). The causal associations between Holdemanella (IVW: OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.02–1.36, p = 0.03), Eubacterium oxidoreducens group (IVW: OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.61–0.91, p = 0.00), and acute pharyngitis were verified in WM and MR-PRESSO. MR-Egger intercept, MR-PRESSO global tests, and Cochran’s Q test showed that there was no horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity (P > 0.05), with the exception of Actinobacteria (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3b).

Acute sinusitis

In this study, eleven microbiota showed causal relationships with acute sinusitis. An increase in the abundance of the Eubacterium fissicatena group, Actinomyces, Butyricimonas, Prevotella 9, and Ruminococcaceae UCG 009 could lead to the development of acute sinusitis. The abundance of phylum Verrucomicrobia, Family XIII AD3011 group, Eubacterium xylanophilum group, Ruminococcus torques group, Dorea, and Subdoligranulum was associated with a reduced risk of acute sinusitis (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S3). IVW results of Butyricimonas (IVW: OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.04–1.31, p = 0.01), Eubacterium xylanophilum group (IVW: OR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.80–0.98, p = 0.02), Ruminococcus torques group (IVW: OR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.64–0.92, p = 0.00), Dorea (IVW: OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.76–0.99, p = 0.03), and Verrucomicrobia (IVW: OR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.82–0.98, p = 0.02) were consistent with WM and MR-PRESSO test. Sensitivity analysis did not detect horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3c).

Acute upper respiratory infections

We found that higher genetically predicted levels of Enterorhabdus and Eubacterium fissicatena group were associated with an increase in acute upper respiratory infections, while order Desulfovibrionales, order Bifidobacteriales , Ruminococcaceae UCG 011, Clostridium sensus tricto 1, Ruminococcus gnavus group, Eubacterium xylanophilum group, family Lactobacillaceae, family Desulfovibrionaceae, family Defluviitaleaceae, family Bifidobacteriaceae were associated with a decrease in disease (Fig. 2d). Eubacterium fissicatena group (IVW: OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.01–1.09, p = 0.02), Enterorhabdus (IVW: OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.02–1.17, p = 0.01), Ruminococcaceae UCG 011 (IVW: OR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.93–1.00, p = 0.04), and Eubacterium xylanophilum group (IVW: OR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.88–1.00, p = 0.04) passed both WM and MR-PRESSO tests. The MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global tests showed no horizontal pleiotropy (p > 0.05), and Cochran’s Q tests did not reveal significant heterogeneity (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3d).

Acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites

In this study, as shown in Fig. 2e, a higher level of Oxalobacter gave rise to the disease development. In contrast, higher levels of Bifidobacteriales, Clostridium sensu stricto 1, Ruminococcus gnavus group, Lactobacillaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae reduced the risk of disease. Among them, the causal relationships between Bifidobacteriales (IVW: OR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.83–0.97, p = 0.01), Bifidobacteriaceae (IVW: OR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.83–0.97, p = 0.01) and acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites were confirmed by WM and MR-PRESSO. According to the sensitivity analysis, no heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were found (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3e).

Reverse MR analysis

We performed a reverse MR analysis of the causal relationship of AURTIs with the GM. In this study, acute sinusitis had the reverse causal relationships with class Alphaproteobacteria (forward: OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.01–1.27, p = 0.03; reverse: BETA = 0.19, 95% CI = 0.03–0.35, p = 0.02), family Actinomycetaceae (forward: OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.00–1.28, p = 0.04; reverse: BETA = 0.20, 95% CI = 0.01–0.40, p = 0.04), and order Actinomycetales (forward: OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.00–1.28, p = 0.04; reverse: BETA = 0.20, 95% CI = 0.01–0.40, p = 0.04) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S3). No pleiotropy with heterogeneity was detected using MR-Egger intercepts, MR-PRESSO global tests and Cochran’s Q (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S10).

Multivariable MR analysis

In the MVMR analysis, we examined how interference factors influenced the causal relationship between GM and AURTI. The results indicated that neither coronary atherosclerosis nor COVID-19 altered the causal effect of Escherichia/Shigella in increasing the risk of acute nasopharyngitis (common cold). Similarly, the impact of Turicibacter, Rhodospirillaceae, Rhodospirillales, and Actinobacteria on acute pharyngitis was not affected by diabetes, cor pulmonale, coronary atherosclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, or COVID-19. Furthermore, the causal relationships between Verrucomicrobia and Family XIII AD3011 group and acute sinusitis remained unaffected by cor pulmonale, systemic lupus erythematosus, influenza, and COVID-19. Additionally, the associations between acute upper respiratory infections, Acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites, and Clostridium sensu stricto 1 were not influenced by rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels, or COVID-19 (Table 2).

Discussion

Currently, based on the microbiota-gut-lung axis, research exploring the impact of GM on diseases, predominantly focuses on lower respiratory diseases. In fact, the URT and the gastrointestinal tract are exposed organs that can come into direct contact with external materials, giving them an advantage in being colonized by microorganisms. Meanwhile, digestive system organs such as the pharynx are also part of the URT, which strengthens the connection between GM and URTIs. In this study, we used publicly available genomic databases to explore the bidirectional potential causality between 211 bacterial taxa and five AURTIs. We identified 33 GM taxa that have a causal relationship with AURTI, of which 11 taxa are unaffected by potential interferences and demonstrate stable causal effects with AURTI.

In our study, the Escherichia/Shigella and Gordonibacter were identified as potential risk factors for an increase in acute nasopharyngitis. Acute nasopharyngitis refers to acute inflammation of the mucosa, submucosa and lymphoid tissue of the nasopharynx which usually occurs in association with the common cold. Researchers have observed an increase in the abundance of the Escherichia/Shigella in cold-exposed pigs, which was accompanied by elevated levels of inflammation, cell apoptosis, mitophagy, and mitochondrial fusion in the colon22. The increase of Escherichia/Shigella in the lungs could also induce pulmonary tissue inflammation via activating NLRP3 inflammasome to promote IL-1β release23. These findings suggest that Escherichia/Shigella has the possibility of affecting remote organs by damaging the intestinal barrier and disrupting intestinal and systemic immune homeostasis. On the other hand, Gordonibacter is a member of the family Eggerthellaceae and the class Coriobacteriia, and it can produce urolithin through polyphenol metabolism. In contrast to our results, urolithin is generally considered to have anti-inflammatory properties. For example, researchers have found a lower abundance of Gordonibacter in the colonic tissue of mice with COPD, while the abundance of Gordonibacter was positively correlated with the body weight and lung function of mice, and negatively correlated with the Th17/Treg ratio and pro-inflammatory cytokines24.

Furthermore, we found that an increase in the abundance of Turicibacter and Holdemanella were associated with an increased risk of acute pharyngitis. Streptococcus pyogenes infection (group A streptococcus) is the main cause of acute pharyngitis in children, causing throat pain, and inflammation of the pharynx and surrounding tissues25. Chung Y et al. observed a significant increase of Turicibacter in the colorectal cancer mouse models26. Li H et al. observed an increase of Turicibacter in colitis mice, along with an increase in the histological scores of the colon tissues and dysregulation of the Th17/Treg balance27. Th17, Treg, and other T cell subgroups are considered crucial for the early immune response following infection with Streptococcus pyogenes28. Similarly, the pathogenicity of Holdemanella is also related to its increase in host inflammation. It has been reported that Holdemanella might aggravate allergic asthma by promoting Th2 inflammation29. A more balanced Th1/Th2-type response is an important evaluation indicator for vaccine development for group A Streptococcus30. However, whether the aforementioned situations have a causal relationship has not been confirmed. At the same time, researchers still have differing opinions on the Holdemanella. Researchers isolated the Holdemanella from metabolically healthy individuals and found that Holdemanella can improve diabetes through the glucagon-like peptide 1 signaling pathway31. Xiang N et al. found that the effect of Euphorbia humifusa-Derived Polysaccharides on improving colitis might be related to its significant increase in Holdemanella levels32.

In our results, the increased abundance of Eubacterium fissicatena group, Actinomyces, Butyricimonas, Prevotella9, Ruminococcaceae UCG 009 would lead to an increased risk of acute sinusitis and might serve as potential risk indicators for assessment of acute sinusitis. It has been reported that the content of Eubacterium fissicatena group increased in the colon region and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of colitis mice, which might be related to the occurrence of pulmonary complications33. The enrichment of Eubacterium fissicatena group was also observed in the intestinal tissue in the early stage of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), which could further cause intestinal inflammation and metabolic disorders34. Besides, Actinomyces is a recognized pathogen, present on the mucosal surfaces of the oropharynx and intestines, and it can invade during host immune reduction or mucosal injury, becoming the pathogenic factor of paranasal sinus actinomycosis35. When bacteria invade the body, the process of causing acute inflammation of the mucous membrane of the sinus cavity is called acute sinusitis. Researchers have obtained Actinomyces cardiffensis strains from antral washout specimens from patients with sinusitis and found Actinomyces nasicola strains in pus collected from the sinuses36. In this study, Butyricimonas and genus Prevotella9 increased the risk of acute sinusitis. However, in an observational study, Butyricimonas was found to exert anti-inflammatory effects through butyrate, thereby alleviating the level of airway inflammation in allergic asthma models37. The abundance of Prevotella was reduced in feces of influenza patients38. Meanwhile, genus Prevotella9 levels in the nasal cavities of patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis were positively correlated with lung function, and some members of the Prevotella were related to lung function improvement39.

Subsequently, in our analysis using acute upper respiratory infections and acute upper respiratory infections of multiple and unspecified sites as outcomes, we observed that the genetically predicted relative abundances of Enterorhabdus, Eubacterium fissicatena group, and Oxalobacter were associated with the increased risk of AURTI. Enterorhabdus is an opportunistic pathogen closely related to inflammatory diseases. In a gene sequencing study of mice with liver fibrosis, it was found that the relative abundance of Enterorhabdus increased when carbon tetrachloride induced liver injury, and it could affect the disease process through the regulation of metabolism-related genes and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway40.

Additionally, in the results of the univariable MR study, 67% of the microbial taxa exhibited protective effects in AURTI. Among these, six GM taxa passed the MVMR assessment, demonstrating causal effects on AURTIs that are unaffected by various confounding factors, including comorbidities, influenza virus, adenovirus, and COVID-19, thereby exhibiting stable protective effects. The Rhodospirillaceae, classified within the Rhodospirillales, primarily participates in the decomposition of organic matter and photosynthesis. Although research on the impact of the Rhodospirillaceae on diseases is limited, its presence has been identified in the lesions of patients with atherosclerosis and pulmonary diseases, which provides the possibility that Rhodospirillaceae may intervene in the disease progression through various mechanisms41,42.The Actinobacteria is one of the most diverse bacterial groups in nature. In addition to containing the pathogenic Actinomyces, it is also a significant source of various pharmaceutical compounds, including antibiotics, anticancer agents, and immunosuppressants, due to its capacity to produce multiple secondary metabolites43,44. The Family XIII AD3011 group and the Verrucomicrobia represent relatively new bacterial classifications that have not been extensively studied. Bloemendaal et al. discovered an increase in the relative abundance of the Family XIII AD3011 group in the feces of subjects consuming probiotics, while its abundance decreased in healthy males who smoke and consume alcohol, suggesting that the Family XIII AD3011 group may be associated with a healthy gut microbiome45.The Akkermansia muciniphila, a member of the Verrucomicrobia, has been shown to influence tumor progression by modulating the immune microenvironment in colorectal cancer46. Furthermore, our study revealed that the Clostridium sensu stricto 1 is negatively correlated with disease incidence. This finding is consistent with observations made by Ni et al. in rats with NAFLD47. The protective effects of Clostridium sensu stricto 1 may be related to its impact on the production of SCFAs, such as propionate and butyrate48,49.

Although there is sufficient evidence to support that certain GM species influence the occurrence and development of AURTI, their mechanisms are still lacking verification. In fact, the importance of maintaining intestinal regional immune homeostasis in this process has been widely recognized. The incomplete development of the infant GM could impair immune homeostasis, leading to increased susceptibility to lung infections50. GM are typically divided into three types: beneficial, harmful, and opportunistic pathogens, among which a dynamic balance is maintained. An increase in harmful or opportunistic bacteria, and depletion of beneficial microbiota can all lead to the destruction. Mechanistically, the metabolites, bacteriocins, and other pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) secreted by GM, are recognized by pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptor (TLR), activating the regional immune system in the gut, which is an important link between GM and their involvement in URTI10. Excessive lipopolysaccharide produced by opportunistic pathogens activates the TLR/MyD88 signaling pathway, which not only destroys the intestinal barrier by affecting the tight connections of intestinal epithelial cells, but also activates multiple inflammatory response pathways, triggering a series of immune cascade reactions. SCFAs play a pivotal role in the protective function of beneficial bacteria such as Ruminococcus. They can provide energy for intestinal epithelial cells to maintain mucosal integrity by being converted into acetyl Coenzyme A and entering the citric acid cycle51. SCFAs also participate in regulating local and distant immune homeostasis. Many types of cells, including intestinal epithelial cells, Tregs, and B cells, all express G protein-coupled receptors (GPRs) that can recognize SCFAs. When acetate is recognized by GPR43, it activates NLRP3 and enhances the production of type I interferon, helping to control viral infections52. Additionally, SCFAs absorbed by the cells can inhibit the activity of histone deacetylases (HDAC) to remove the inhibitory effect on cell differentiation, and promote the production of Tregs, Bregs, and macrophages with anti-inflammatory effects51. Therefore, in terms of treatment, some scholars have proposed to use the competition between beneficial and harmful bacteria to control the abundance of specific GM species, thereby achieving the purpose of controlling the disease7. In addition, GM also migrate to URT via esophageal reflux or through damaged gut barriers, interacting with microbes colonized in URT to influence disease progression. Research has reported that in the context of SARS-CoV-2 and other viral infection, there are connections and interactions between the microbiome of the gut and oral pharynx53. At the same time, the presence of Helicobacter pylori has been found in the nasopharynges of patients with chronic rhinosinusitis and pharyngitis and can affect the disease progression54.

In summary, we identified causal relationships between 33 GM taxa and AURTI. However, given the complexity of these diseases, both GM and AURTI are subject to the influence of numerous potential ‌interference factors. We incorporated nine ‌interference factors that may affect both exposure and outcome into MVMR analysis, enhancing the robustness of our findings to some extent. Nevertheless, our study has some limitations. First, in terms of sample selection, to include more data and increase the reliability of the results, we chose the public genetic databases to obtain samples, which made it impossible for us to conduct more detailed research on the high-risk groups for AURTIs. At the same time, the study participants were mainly European populations, and we did not verify the specific mechanisms of GM involvement in URTI through clinical, animal, and cellular sites, which limits the applicability of the results to the general population. Furthermore, we selected specific acute upper respiratory tract diseases, as well as the more broadly defined acute upper respiratory infections. However, these diseases are intertwined in disease locations, which might lead to the risk of non-differential mismatch in the results.

Conclusions

In this study, we initially employed univariable MR to identify causal relationships between 33 GM taxa and five types of AURTI. Among these, 11 bacterial taxa demonstrated stable causal associations with AURTI, independent of potential ‌interference factors such as comorbidities, seasonal variation, specific pathogens, and antibiotic allergy history. Specifically, Escherichia/Shigella, Holdemanella, Turicibacter, Actinomyces, and Butyricimonas were found to significantly increase the risk of AURTI, while Rhodospirillaceae, Rhodospirillales, Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Family XIII AD3011 group, and Clostridium sensu stricto 1 exhibited consistent protective effects against AURTI. Based on these findings, we aim to conduct further experimental studies to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these relationships, with a focus on the identified bacterial taxa. This work will provide a stronger foundation for the development of microbiota-based immunomodulatory therapies and probiotic interventions targeting AURTI.
