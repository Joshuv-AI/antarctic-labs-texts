Analyzing URA Geometry for Enhanced Spatial Multiplexing and Extended Near-Field Coverage
Ahmed Hussain, Asmaa Abdallah, Abdulkadir Celik, Ahmed M. Eltawil

Abstract
With the deployment of large antenna arrays at high-frequency bands, future wireless communication systems are likely to operate in the radiative near-field. Unlike far-field beam steering, near-field beams can be focused within a spatial region of finite depth, enabling spatial multiplexing in both the angular and range dimensions. This paper derives the beamdepth for a generalized uniform rectangular array (URA) and investigates how array geometry influences the near-field beamdepth and the limits where near-field beamfocusing is achievable. To characterize the near-field boundary in terms of beamfocusing and spatial multiplexing gains, we define the effective beamfocusing Rayleigh distance (EBRD) for a generalized URA. Our analysis reveals that while a square URA achieves the narrowest beamdepth, the EBRD is maximized for a wide or tall URA. However, despite its narrow beamdepth, a square URA may experience a reduction in multiuser sum rate due to its severely constrained EBRD. Simulation results confirm that a wide or tall URA achieves a sum rate of 3.5 × 3.5\times more than that of a square URA, benefiting from the extended EBRD and improved spatial multiplexing capabilities.

Introduction

Massive

At higher carrier frequencies, the reduced wavelength enables the deployment of massive antenna arrays within a confined space. The electromagnetic field surrounding an antenna array is generally divided into three zones: the reactive near-field, the radiative near-field, and the far-field. Extending from the reactive near-field up to the Rayleigh distance, the radiative near-field (also called the Fresnel region) is dominated by spherical wave propagation. Notably, the Rayleigh distance, which marks the boundary between the radiative near-field and the far-field, increases significantly with a larger array aperture and a shorter wavelength
[
3
]
. As a result, upcoming wireless technologies are expected to operate predominantly in the radiative near-field.

Recent studies highlight that considering near-field propagation effects can markedly boost communication capacity
[
4
]
. As wireless systems transition from the far-field to the near-field regime, MIMO technology experiences a significant enhancement in spatial multiplexing gains
[
5
]
. Furthermore, the near-field
LoS
(

) channel, influenced by spherical wave propagation, can foster a rich scattering environment, thereby increasing single-user capacity. Although spherical wavefront propagation contributes to capacity improvements, an in-depth analysis is crucial to better understand the distinct electromagnetic properties of the near-field and further optimize communication performance.

This focusing ability requires compensating for phase shifts arising from the varying distances between antenna elements and the targeted focal point, leading to spherical equiphase surfaces converging at the intended location. The presence of a finite beamdepth also enables the formation of multiple beams at different ranges within the same angular direction, improving spatial multiplexing. Notably, the

Recent studies distinguish between

Given these considerations, further research is required to identify array geometries that achieve narrow beamdepth while extending the

, enabling more users to operate within the effective near-field region. This work addresses these gaps by analyzing near-field beamfocusing for an

URA

However, despite its narrower beamdepth, a square URA may suffer from a reduced multiuser sum rate due to its significantly constrained EBRD. Simulation results demonstrate that a wide or tall URA achieves a sum rate of
8.2\,\mathrm{bps/Hz}
, compared to only

III-B

Impact of Spatial Focus Region and Array Geometry

Beamdepth plays a crucial role in enhancing spatial multiplexing gains within the
EBRD
region. A narrow beamdepth in the near-field minimizes mutual interference, allowing more users to be served in the same angular direction. In the subsequent analysis, we explore how the spatial focus region and array geometry influence beamdepth and
EBRD

III-B
1
Spatial Focus Region

As the beam deviates from the boresight, the angular beamwidth expands. Similarly, beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
increases as the spatial focus region moves further from the array, exhibiting a quadratic dependence on the focus distance

\eta\gg 1
), the azimuth angle
\varphi
predominantly influences beamdepth, whereas for taller arrays (
\eta\ll 1
), the elevation angle
\theta
plays a more significant role. In general, beamdepth increases as either the azimuth or elevation angle deviates from the boresight. For instance, Fig.
3

5\pi/6
, beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
also increases.

III-B
2
Array Geometry

We aim to determine the optimal width-to-height ratio
\eta
for a
URA
that minimizes the beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
in (
9
), given a fixed number of antenna elements
N_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BS}}}
. The parameter
\eta
not only appears explicitly in (
9
) but also influences other critical factors, such as the Rayleigh distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}
and the coefficient
\alpha_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{3dB}}}
.

For a fixed number of antennas
N_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BS}}}
, the Rayleigh distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}=\frac{2D^{2}}{\lambda}
increases as the URA becomes more elongated, either in height or width, due to the corresponding increase in aperture length
D
. We simulate a range of tall and wide array configurations by varying
\eta
from
2^{-6}
to
2^{6}
, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. The results indicate that the Rayleigh distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}
reaches its minimum when
\eta=1
, corresponding to a square
URA
.

Furthermore, the factor
\alpha_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{3dB}}}
, as defined in (
10
), is influenced by
\eta
. The gain function
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{URA}}}
in (
8
) exhibits symmetry with respect to
\eta
, satisfying the relation
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{URA}}}(\eta)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.5}{URA}}}(1/\eta)
. Consequently,
\alpha_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{3dB}}}
is also symmetric around
\eta=1
, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. Additionally, the dependence of beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
on
\eta
, given in (
9
), can be approximated by
\frac{\eta^{2}+1}{\eta}
, which is also depicted in Fig.
4
. Among different array configurations, the combined factor
\alpha_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{3dB}}}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}+1}{\eta}\right)
reaches its highest value for a square
URA
compared to more elongated structures.

While the combined factor
\alpha_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{3dB}}}\left(\frac{\eta^{2}+1}{\eta}\right)
is maximized for a square
URA
, the Rayleigh distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}
is at its lowest in this configuration. Among these two factors,
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}
has a greater influence on beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
due to its larger magnitude. As a result, the beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
is minimized for a square
URA
and becomes larger for other array geometries.

III-B
3
Limits of Beamfocusing

The
EBRD
represents the boundary beyond which near-field beamfocusing is no longer achievable. As formulated in (
12
), its value is determined by the Rayleigh distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}
and the focus direction. For a given array, increasing the aperture size
D
—either by adding more antenna elements
N_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BS}}}
or enlarging the inter-element spacing
d
—results in an extended
EBRD
. Moreover, deploying widely spaced sub-arrays offers a means to achieve a large
EBRD
without requiring a significant increase in the number of antennas
[
19
]
.

Since a square
URA
yields the smallest Rayleigh distance
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}
, it also results in the minimum
EBRD
, which increases as the array shape transitions toward a
ULA
. This pattern is illustrated in Fig.
5
, where the
EBRD
is plotted against the azimuth angle
\varphi
for different values of
\eta
and elevation angles
\theta
. For wide arrays, variations in the
EBRD
are primarily influenced by the azimuth angle, whereas for tall arrays, the elevation angle plays a dominant role. Regardless of the configuration, the
EBRD
reaches its maximum at the boresight direction (
\varphi=0,\theta=\pi/2
) and decreases toward the endfire directions.

Among various array configurations, the maximum
EBRD
occurs when
\eta=0.004
, whereas the minimum
EBRD
is observed for a square
URA
(
\eta=1
). For tall
URA
s with
\eta\ll 1
, such as
\eta=0.016
and
\eta=0.004
, the
EBRD
exhibits minimal variation with respect to the azimuth angle
\varphi
. However, as the elevation angle shifts away from the boresight (
\theta=\pi/2
), the
EBRD
progressively decreases.

To summarize, the square
URA
yields the smallest beamdepth
r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BD}}}
, which facilitates superior spatial multiplexing capabilities. Nevertheless, its effective near-field coverage, as determined by the
EBRD
, is very limited. On the other hand, wide or tall
URA
configurations offer a larger
EBRD
, albeit at the expense of increased beamdepth relative to the square configuration. A detailed numerical evaluation of the multiuser capacity is presented in Section
IV
to determine the array geometry that achieves the highest sum rate.

IV
Numerical Results

We conduct Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the achievable sum rate across different array configurations. The simulation setup considers a downlink communication system, where a
BS
serves
M_{\mathrm{UE}}
users. The
BS
employs
N_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BS}}}
antenna elements and
N_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RF}}}=4
RF chains, utilizing hybrid precoding. To estimate the channel, the
BS
performs beam training through beam sweeping. Each
UE
reports the index of the codeword that provides the highest gain, enabling the
BS
to select the optimal codeword from the polar codebook for analog precoding, represented by
\mathbf{W}
. Finally, the
BS
applies zero-forcing for digital precoding. We compute the overall
SE
(
SE
) using the following expression

R=\sum_{m}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{p_{m}\left|\mathbf{h}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{f}_{m}\right|^{2}}{\sigma_{m}^{2}+\sum_{l\neq m}p_{l}\left|\mathbf{h}_{m}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{f}_{l}\right|^{2}}\right),

(15)

where
p_{m}
denotes power allocated to the
{m}^{\text{th}}
user and
\sigma_{m}^{2}
denotes the noise variance. Furthermore,
f_{m}
represents the
{m}^{\text{th}}
column of the digital precoder.

The finite beamdepth remains achievable only within the
EBRD
region, constraining the spatial multiplexing gains in the near-field domain defined by the
EBRD
. To illustrate this effect, we examine a
64\times 8

URA
serving five users positioned along the boresight direction
(\varphi=0,\theta=\pi/2)
. These users are distributed across three distinct regions: the
EBRD
region
[1.2D,r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{EBRD}}}]
, the extended near-field beyond
EBRD

[r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{EBRD}}},r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}]
, and the far-field region
[r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}},100r_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{RD}}}]
. We evaluate performance using both polar and
DFT
(
DFT
) codebooks across these regions. In the polar codebook, codewords are sampled across the EBRD region as described in
[
13
]
, whereas in the DFT codebook, codewords are uniformly sampled across the angular region.
The spectral efficiency results, presented in Fig.
6
, show that within the
EBRD
region, the polar codebook achieves a spectral efficiency of
5.8
bps/Hz, significantly surpassing the
DFT
codebook, which only reaches
2
bps/Hz. However, beyond the
EBRD
, in both the extended near-field and far-field regions, the spectral efficiency of both codebooks remains comparable. These findings highlight the
EBRD
as a critical threshold distinguishing the radiative near-field from the far-field in terms of achievable multiplexing gains.

The array geometry plays a crucial role in determining beamdepth and the boundaries of the
EBRD
. A square
URA

(\eta=1)
achieves the smallest beamdepth, which enhances spatial multiplexing. However, this configuration also results in a more constrained
EBRD
, potentially limiting overall system capacity when compared to wider or taller array structures. To demonstrate this effect, we analyze two configurations: a square
URA
with dimensions
[32\times 32]

(\eta=1)
and a wide
URA
with dimensions
[128\times 8]

(\eta=16)
. Both arrays contain the same number of antenna elements,
N_{\mathrm{\scalebox{0.6}{BS}}}=1024
, and serve six users aligned along the boresight direction. Fig.
7
presents the
SE
as a function of
SNR
(
SNR
) for these configurations. The results indicate that the wide
URA

(\eta=16)
consistently outperforms the square
URA

(\eta=1)
across all
SNR
levels, demonstrating that wider array designs offer enhanced
SE
. At high
SNR
, the wide
URA
attains a
SE
of
8.2\,\mathrm{bps/Hz}
, significantly outperforming the square
URA
, which achieves only
2.3\,\mathrm{bps/Hz}
.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we have analyzed the beamdepth and
EBRD
for a generalized
URA
. Specifically, we have derived the beamdepth by evaluating the normalized array gain at the
3\,\mathrm{dB}
points and introduced the
EBRD
to define the near-field region where beamfocusing is achievable. Our analysis has demonstrated that the beamdepth was narrowest at boresight and increased at off-boresight angles, while the
EBRD
was maximized at boresight and decreased at off-boresight angles. To assess the impact of array geometry, we have shown that the beamdepth is minimized for a square
URA
, whereas the
EBRD
is maximized for wide or tall
URA
configurations.
