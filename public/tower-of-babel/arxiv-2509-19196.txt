A novel generalized inner product-based wave scattering from an underwater source in a compressible ocean
R. Pethiyagoda 1 S. Das 2,3 B. Wilks 1,4 M. H. Meylan 1

Abstract
Motivated by applications to underwater explosions and volcanic eruptions, this paper considers the evolution of an initial pressure disturbance in the ocean, including effects due to the dynamic and static compression of water and the free surface. In order to solve the equations of motion of a linear compressible ocean, a special inner product is introduced, which allows us to apply self-adjoint operator theory. What results is a Hilbert space in which the acoustic-gravity modes are orthogonal in the generalised sense. This allows the time-domain evolution of the free surface and subsurface pressure field resulting from an initial disturbance to be calculated. Our simulations show initial radial propagation of the pressure pulse and subsequent reflection from the water surface and the rigid ocean floor, eventually leading to horizontal propagation away from the source point. The solutions with and without the inclusion of the static compression are compared, and the effect of static compression is shown to be small but not negligible.



1
Introduction

The evolution of the pressure field from an initial disturbance in the ocean has been of significant interest to the scientific community for a long time, primarily due to the potential impact of underwater nuclear bomb testing, underwater volcanic eruptions, seismic events, and the associated tsunami hazards. There also exists significant monitoring of pressure; for example, the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban-Treaty Organization (CTBTO) monitors pressure signals from hydrophone stations to detect possible nuclear tests. Similar devices also exist for detecting tsunami waves. Accurate modelling of the pressure signatures allows information about the event to be determined, for example, an attempt has been made to estimate the crash site location of Malaysian Airlines Flight 370 (MH370) using an inverse problem
(
Kadri 2024
)
. The accuracy of such inverse problems depends on the accurate mathematical analysis of the associated wave evolution problem. Obviously, the short–time behaviour of such an event may be highly nonlinear and involves the formation of a cavity and subsequent expansion at the initial state. However, after some time (and some distance), the pressure wave inside the water column can be accurately modelled with a linearised water wave theory that accounts for the effect of dynamic compression (we use this term to distinguish the effect of the local compressibility from the static compression which is the accumulation of compression due to the weight of the overlying water column. In this work, we consider the linear problem and solve the initial value problem, including the effect of the free surface. The solution relies on the use of a special Hilbert space. We also extend this formulation to the more challenging case where we include the effect of static compression.

Many energy-conserving linear wave problems can be recast as an abstract wave equation

\partial_{t}^{2}u+\mathcal{A}u=0
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a positive self-adjoint operator in a Hilbert space
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. For example, choosing
\mathcal{A}=-c^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}
gives the classical wave equation with wave speed
c
. More complicated forms of
\mathcal{A}
appear in various scenarios such as hydroelasticity
(
Meylan 2002
)
.
While the use of the spectral theorem to obtain the solution as an expansion over the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
was long known when
\mathcal{A}
has a discrete spectrum (Fourier/Sturm Liouville) the case for when
\mathcal{A}
has a continuous spectrum began being worked out for the Schrödinger equation with the advent of quantum mechanics
(
Povzner 1953
;
Ikebe 1960
)
.
The theory, which has been extended to the classical wave equation
(
Wilcox 1975
)
and to hydrodynamics for the case of incompressible water wave scattering
(
Hazard & Loret 2007
;
Hazard & Meylan 2008
)
, is known as a generalised eigenfunction expansion. The theoretical foundation is given by a Gelfand triple, also known as a rigged Hilbert space, which is a mathematical structure used to extend the usual Hilbert space framework to include more generalised functions (like distributions). This is because we need to deal with eigenfunctions that are not square-integrable (i.e., not in the Hilbert space) but are still useful and meaningful in a generalised sense.
In general, this theory has been developed in isolation from computation, despite the fact that the simplest example of this structure is the Fourier transform, and its computation by the discrete Fourier transform (often implemented using the fast Fourier transform) is one of the most ubiquitous algorithms.

It turns out that the theory of generalised eigenfunctions and spectral solutions for continuous spectra can be obtained numerically by applying quadrature to the integrals over the continuous spectrum. This idea was in the work of
Hazard & Meylan 2008
;
Meylan 2009
;
Meylan & Eatock Taylor 2009
;
Peter & Meylan 2010
, but appears explicitly in
Wilks et al. 2024
;
Wilks et al. 2025
. As far as we are aware, this work is the first application of the theory to compressible water wave scattering. A key advantage of the numerical spectral technique is that the generalised eigenfunctions, which satisfy
\mathcal{A}\hat{u}=-\omega^{2}\hat{u}
, are precisely the time harmonic solutions for the angular frequency
\omega
. These single-frequency scattering solutions can be found numerically for a wide range of problems, e.g. variable bathymetry, which would be a relatively straightforward extension of the present work. We note that the ease of determining the spectrum stands in contrast with a more numerical method, in which the operator
\mathcal{A}
is approximated by a matrix and the spectrum of this matrix is sought. In this case, very many numerical problems arise, especially because the eigenvalues of the underlying operator are continuous. Further problems can arise from the fact that it may be difficult to preserve the self-adjoint property of the matrix if the inner product is nontrivial (as in the case with the current problem).

The inclusion of the compressibility of water is an essential part of our work. The introduction of realistic but slight water compressibility produces acoustic-gravity waves (AGW) - hydroacoustic waves influenced by gravity at low frequency, which act as analogous propagating modes in acoustic/electromagnetic waveguides. These modes capture the effect of the boundary condition of the free surface and play a pivotal role in the accurate calculation of the pressure of the water column. It is worth mentioning that at the limit of infinite sound speed in the water, the first AGW mode transforms into the incompressible wave mode under realistic parameter choices. Following the devastating aftermath of the
2004
Indian Ocean tsunami, a renewed interest in the application of AGW in tsunami detection mechanisms (through modelling of surface wave propagation) has grown through the works of
(

;

computed the far-field profiles of the tsunami and AGW modes. Subsequently, there has been a surge in understanding different properties of these AGW modes (e.g., two interacting gravity waves producing

(

)
, triad resonances
(

;
Kadri & Akylas 2016
)
), impact on different oceanic processes (deep-water transport
(

)
, ice shelf breaking
(

)
, wave dissipation on sea ice
(

)
), impact of seafloor characteristics (porous
(

)
and viscous
(

The static compression of the ocean is due to the effect of the slight compressibility of water, causing an increase in the density of the deep ocean. It is only significant for waves which penetrate the deep ocean, and even then, its effect is small. The initial account of static compression can be found through the pioneering work of
Longuet-Higgins 1950
. However, this quantity is rarely included in the mathematical formulation, citing its presumably insignificant impact on the wave propagation until the work of
Abdolali & Kirby 2017
, who illustrated the amplification of tsunami phase speed at a higher ocean depth induced by static compression. A few contemporary works retained static compression in the mathematical treatment of physical problems to study the behaviour of the pure-gravity mode
(

;

;

;

to match the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha‘apai (HTHH) volcanic eruption-induced tsunami data using the resonance between the acoustic waves and the surface gravity wave. The quantification of the effect of static compression is entangled with the varying sound speed along the water depth, and the above literature makes use of the Eulerian approach of solution based on the approximation of the vertical sound profile.
Michele & Renzi 2020
used a perturbation technique to derive an analytical expression correct up to third order that governs the evolution of the hydroacoustic waves. Recently,
Andrade et al. 2025

(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
, and it has been demonstrated that other models in the literature can be derived from their general proposed model. In the context of tsunami wave generation,
Das & Meylan 2023a
solved for the time-dependent evolution due to impulsive forcing from the sea floor, including the effect of static compression. The eigenfunction matching required a special inner product, which is generalised here. This novel method was later extended to include more realistic geometries (a step
(

)
, a trench
(
Pethiyagoda et al. 2024b
)
, a generic ocean floor
(
Pethiyagoda et al. 2025a

), three-dimensional solutions
(

)
, and surface disturbance-induced wave propagation models
(
Pethiyagoda et al. 2025b

Pethiyagoda et al. 2024a
)
that augment the works where static compression is dropped
(
Renzi & Dias 2014
)
and ocean compressibility is ignored
(
Liu & Higuera 2022
)
. We note here that there are ways in which the current work could be extended to include other effects which are non-standard beyond the static compression we consider here. Two examples are the effect of the bottom elasticity
(
Eyov et al. 2013
;

;
Kadri 2019

and more complex static compression using sound speed vertical profile
(

The properties of the operator involved in this class of problems, where ocean water compressibility (including static compression) is involved, are not well studied in the literature. We provide a mathematical structure for the operators involved in a rigid and flat ocean floor via a special inner product. The manuscript is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the two-dimensional mathematical formulation of the physical problem where static compression is ignored, followed by the solution method that involves deriving a generic two-dimensional inner product in §3. The time domain simulations are detailed in §4. The problem is much simpler if static compression is ignored, and for many scenarios, only this solution will be required, and this is the motivation for giving this in detail. In §5, we extend the formulation to static compression and calculate the time-domain simulations of the flow field in this case. We note that the inclusion of static compression complicates the derivation, but the numerical solution takes a similar computational time to the solution without compression. The manuscript concludes with a brief summary in §6.

Mathematical formulation without static compression

We construct the physical problem in a homogeneous ocean water environment where the standard density variance due to the water column’s own weight is ignored, leading to ocean compressibility without static compression (only the dynamic compression is included). (23a)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{c^{2}}{g}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{n}^{\pm}(x,0;k)\overline{\varphi_{p}^{\pm}(x,0;\tilde{k})}\,{\rm d}x,

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{k^{2}-\tilde{k}^{2}}{\mu_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\mu}_{p}^{2}}\right)e^{\mp{\rm i}(k-\tilde{k})x}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\left(\frac{k^{2}-\tilde{k}^{2}}{\mu_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\mu}_{p}^{2}}\right)\delta(k-\tilde{k}),

(23b)

where
\delta(\kappa)
is the Dirac delta. Finally, in the case of
n=p
, we can recover the normalisation constant by taking limits,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\tilde{k}}\langle\phi_{n}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,k),\phi_{p}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,\tilde{k})\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\left(\frac{\tilde{k}}{\mu_{n}\partial_{k}\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\right)\delta(k-\tilde{k})\delta_{np},

where

\partial_{k}\mu_{n}=\frac{2\dfrac{c^{2}}{g}k}{\mu_{n}h{\rm sech}^{2}{\mu_{n}h}+\tanh{\mu_{n}h}+2\dfrac{c^{2}}{g}\mu_{n}}.

For simplicity, we write

\lim_{k\rightarrow\tilde{k}}\langle\phi_{n}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,k),\phi_{p}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,\tilde{k})\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=D_{p}(k)\delta(k-\tilde{k})\delta_{np},

where

D_{p}(k)=\left(\frac{h}{2}{\rm sech}^{2}{\mu_{p}h}+\frac{\tanh{\mu_{p}h}}{2\mu_{p}}+\frac{c^{2}}{g}\right).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{n}^{\pm},\phi_{p}^{\mp}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\left(\frac{k^{2}-\tilde{k}^{2}}{\mu_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\mu}_{p}^{2}}\right)\delta(k+\tilde{k})=0,

(23c)

since
k
and
\tilde{k}
are both positive real numbers.

5
Time-domain simulation

We will now compute time-domain solutions of the internal pressure

P(x,z,t)=-\rho\Phi_{t}(x,z,t),

(24)

and free-surface

\eta(x,t)=-\frac{1}{g}\Phi_{t}(x,0,t)=\frac{1}{\rho g}P(x,0,t).

(25)

Noting that when computing the pressure over the full
z
-domain, we recover the surface as a byproduct.

5.1
Numerical evaluation

When evaluating (
24
), care must be taken due to the potentially highly oscillatory nature of the integrand (for large
x
or
t
). We reformulate the integrals into the form

\displaystyle I_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pm_{1}}\sum_{\pm_{2}}\pm_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n\pm_{1}}^{(1)}e^{{\rm i}(\pm_{1}kx\pm_{2}\omega_{n}t)}\,{\rm d}k,

\displaystyle I_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pm_{1}}\sum_{\pm_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n\pm_{1}}^{(2)}e^{{\rm i}(\pm_{1}kx\pm_{2}\omega_{n}t)}\,{\rm d}k,

where

g_{n\pm}^{(1)}=\frac{\omega_{n}\langle\Phi_{0}(x,z),\phi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}f_{n}(z)}{2D_{p}}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{n\pm}^{(2)}=\frac{\langle P_{0}(x,z),\phi_{n}^{\pm}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}f_{n}(z)}{2D_{p}}.

Here, the subscripts of
\pm
indicate whether the signs are chosen independently (different subscripts) or must be consistent (same subscript). This formulation allows us to implement a Filon-type quadrature method
(
Dahlquist & Björck 2008
;
Filon 1930
)
detailed in Appendix
A
. Further efficiency can be gained by recognising that

g_{+}e^{{\rm i}(kx+\omega_{n}t)}=\overline{g_{-}e^{-{\rm i}(kx+\omega_{n}t)}},

halving the number of terms in the summations that must be computed.

6
Inclusion of static compression

In this section, we include (mild) density variance of the ocean water, the effect which is known in the literature as static compression, into the mathematical formulation, and develop the solution using a similar approach to that used in the previous section. The rigorous derivation of the governing equations can be found in the works of
Longuet-Higgins 1950
,
Miyoshi 1954
, and
Sells 1965
. Note that in the subsequent mathematical treatment, the density
\rho
represents its value at the surface, which is treated as the baseline density. The hydrostatic density is
\rho e^{-\gamma z}

(
Abdolali & Kirby 2017
)
, where the effect of the variance in the density away from the surface is reflected through the term
\gamma
, which depends on the speed of sound in the water and is defined by
\gamma=g/c^{2}
.
The effect of static compression is small for realistic oceans, as we shall see, and it significantly complicates the formulation. We believe that including it is worthwhile, first because it allows accurate calculation of its effect and second because it highlights how powerful the special inner product-based method is and how it can be extended to highly non-trivial cases. We note that the complexity arises in the theory, but that the computational difficulty is not greatly increased.

6.1
Mathematical Solution with Static Compression

The modified IBVP is given by

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Phi_{tt}+\gamma\Phi_{z},\quad

\displaystyle(x,z)\in\Omega,

(27a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{tt}+g\Phi_{z}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle z=0,

(27b)

\displaystyle\Phi_{z}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle z=-h,

(27c)

\displaystyle\Phi_{x}

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle|x|\to\infty,

(27d)

subject to the initial conditions

\displaystyle\Phi(x,z,0)

\displaystyle=0,

(28)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(x,z,0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{P_{0}(x,z)}{\rho e^{-\gamma z}}.

(29)

In order to identify an appropriate inner product, we apply Green’s second identity to the functions
\hat{\Phi}=e^{-\gamma z/2}\Phi
and
\hat{\Psi}=e^{-\gamma z/2}\Psi
, where
\Phi
and
\Psi
satisfy (
27
), in the form

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{2}\hat{\Phi}\,\hat{\Psi}-\hat{\Phi}\,\nabla^{2}\hat{\Psi}\right){\rm d}V=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{n}\,\hat{\Psi}-\hat{\Phi}\,\hat{\Psi}_{n}\right){\rm d}S,

Taking
\Omega
to be the region
(-L,L)\times(-h,0)
gives, in the limit as
L\to\infty

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}e^{-\gamma z}\left[\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi-\gamma\Phi_{z}\right)\Psi-\Phi\left(\nabla^{2}\Psi-\gamma\Psi_{z}\right)\right]{\rm d}V=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left.\left(\Phi_{z}\,\Psi-\Phi\,\Psi_{z}\right)\right|_{z=0}{\rm d}x,

where we have used (
27c
) and (
27d
). Subsequently

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}e^{-\gamma z}\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi-\gamma\Phi_{z}\right)\Psi{\rm d}V-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left.\Phi_{z}\,\Psi\right|_{z=0}{\rm d}x=\iint_{\Omega}e^{-\gamma z}\Phi\left(\nabla^{2}\Psi-\gamma\Psi_{z}\right){\rm d}V

\displaystyle\hskip 216.81pt-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left.\Phi\,\Psi_{z}\right|_{z=0}{\rm d}x.

(30a)

Note that the combination of (
27a
) and (
27b
) yields
\Phi_{z}=-\frac{c^{2}}{g}(\nabla^{2}\Phi-\gamma\Phi_{z})
on
z=0
, which further modifies the above expression into

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}e^{-\gamma z}\mathcal{A}\Phi\Psi{\rm d}V+\frac{c^{2}}{g}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left.\mathcal{A}\Phi\,\Psi\right|_{z=0}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega}e^{-\gamma z}\Phi\mathcal{A}\Psi{\rm d}V+\frac{c^{2}}{g}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left.\Phi\,\mathcal{A}\Psi\right|_{z=0}{\rm d}x,

(30b)

where
\mathcal{A}=\nabla^{2}-\gamma\partial_{z}
. Consequently, this operator is symmetric provided we choose the inner product to be

\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=\int_{\Omega}e^{-\gamma z}\Phi(x,z)\overline{\Psi(x,z)}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+\frac{c^{2}}{g}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(x,0)\overline{\Psi(x,0)}\,{\rm d}x.

(31)

In a way similar to what is mentioned in §
3
, we expand the time domain solution in the generalised eigenfunctions of the underlying differential operator. These eigenfunctions satisfy

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\phi+\gamma\phi_{z},\quad

\displaystyle(x,z)\in\Omega,

(32)

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}\phi+g\phi_{z}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle z=0,

(33)

\displaystyle\phi_{z}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle z=-h,

(34)

and are of the form

\phi_{n}^{\pm}(x,z;k)=e^{{\rm i}kx}f_{n}(z;k).

(35)

In the case of static compression, the depth-dependent functions
f_{n}(z,k)
are more complicated. They satisfy the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z;k)-\gamma f_{n}^{\prime}(z;k)

\displaystyle=\left(\mu_{n}^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\right)f_{n}(z;k),

(36a)

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\prime}(-h;k)

\displaystyle=0,

(36b)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0;k)

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\left(k^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\mu_{n}^{2}\right)f(0;k),

(36c)

where the prime denotes differentiation with respect to
z
. For each
k
, the discrete spectrum of eigenvalues
\mu_{n}
of (
36
) satisfy the following dispersion relation

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{g}\left(k^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\mu_{n}^{2}\right)\left(\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\right)\tanh{\mu_{n}h}\right)=\mu_{n}\displaystyle\left[1-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}\right]\tanh{\mu_{n}h}.

(37)

The solutions to (
37
) are taken so that
\mu_{0}(k)\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu_{n}(k)
are purely imaginary with positive and increasing imaginary part. The angular frequency associated with the mode
\phi_{n}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot;k)
is

\omega_{n}(k)=c\sqrt{k^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\mu_{n}^{2}.}

(38)

Without loss of generality, we set
f(0)=1
, yielding

\displaystyle f_{n}(z;k)=

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle e^{(\gamma z/2)}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\sinh{\mu_{n}(z+h)}-\cosh{\mu_{n}(z+h)}\right)}{\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\sinh{(\mu_{n}h)}-\cosh{(\mu_{n}h)}}.

(39)

As in §
3
, the spectral theorem gives a time domain solution of the form

\Phi(x,z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(A_{n}^{-}(k)\phi_{n}^{-}(x,z;k)+A_{n}^{+}(k)\phi_{n}^{+}(x,z;k)\right)\cos(\omega_{n}(k)t){\rm d}k\\
+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(B_{n}^{-}(k)\phi_{n}^{-}(x,z;k)+B_{n}^{+}(k)\phi_{n}^{+}(x,z;k)\right)\frac{\sin(\omega_{n}(k)t)}{\omega_{n}(k)}{\rm d}k,

(40)

where the coefficient functions
A_{n}^{\pm}
and
B_{n}^{\pm}
are to be determined from the initial conditions. With orthonormality conditions again being of the form (
14
), albeit with different normalisation functions
D_{p}(k)
, the coefficients are recovered as

A_{p}^{\pm}(k)=\frac{\langle\Phi_{0}(x,z),\phi_{p}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,k)\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}}{D_{p}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{p}^{\pm}(k)=-\frac{\langle P_{0}(x,z)/\rho e^{-\gamma z},\phi_{p}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,k)\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}}{\displaystyle D_{p}},

(41)

which is precisely in the form of (
20
). To finalise the mathematical solution in the case of static compression, it remains to compute the normalisation functions
D_{p}(k)
. This is done in Appendix
B
.

7
Conclusion

Under the assumptions of linearised water wave theory, this paper has considered the IBVP governing the evolution of a pressure disturbance in a two-dimensional ocean with a free surface, including the effect of compression. The problem was solved mathematically using self-adjoint operator theory by introducing an appropriate inner product. We presented two versions of the theory; in the first, we ignored the effect of static compression and included only the dynamic compression, while the second included both dynamic and static compression. Time-domain simulations showed the propagation of the pressure wave away from the initial source, reflecting off the free surface and the rigid ocean floor, and the onset of surface gravity waves propagating away from the source. In particular, a near reversal of phase is observed upon reflection from the surface, while the opposite pattern occurs in the reflection process from the rigid ocean floor. This indicates the fact that the free surface is approximately a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary. The corresponding time-domain simulations for the model incorporating static compression show that the effect of static compression is minor. While the present work deals with the canonical problem of uniform water depth, the theory considered here can be extended to tackle more realistic variable bathymetry using step-approximation, and it is equally applicable in modelling three-dimensional waves, albeit with increased computational requirements.

Appendix A
Filon-type quadrature

Following the method of
Filon 1930
, we compute an integral of the form

I=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)e^{{\rm i}\psi(x)}\,dx,

where
f(x)
and
\psi^{\prime}(x)
are slowly varying functions, by splitting the domain of integration at the points
x_{0}=a,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=b
and approximating the functions
f(x)
and
\psi(x)
by piecewise linear functions

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}\left(f_{i}+\frac{f_{i+1}-f_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}(x-x_{i})\right)e^{{\rm i}\left(\psi_{i}+\frac{\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}}(x-x_{i})\right)}dx,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Delta x_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\left(f_{i}+(f_{i+1}-f_{i})s\right)e^{{\rm i}\left(\psi_{i}+(\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i})s\right)}ds,\quad\mbox{where }s=\frac{x-x_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}},

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Delta x_{i}\left[-\frac{{\rm i}}{\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i}}\left(f_{i+1}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i+1}}-f_{i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}\right)+\frac{f_{i+1}-f_{i}}{(\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i})^{2}}\left(e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i+1}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Delta x_{i}\left[\left(\frac{{\rm i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}}{\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i}}-\frac{e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i+1}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}}{(\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i})^{2}}\right)f_{i}+\left(-\frac{{\rm i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i+1}}}{\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i}}+\frac{e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i+1}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}}{(\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i})^{2}}\right)f_{i+1}\right],

\displaystyle=\Delta x_{0}\left(\frac{{\rm i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{0}}}{\psi_{1}-\psi_{0}}-\frac{e^{{\rm i}\psi_{1}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{0}}}{(\psi_{1}-\psi_{0})^{2}}\right)f_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left[\Delta x_{i-1}\left(-\frac{{\rm i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}}{\psi_{i}-\psi_{i-1}}+\frac{e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i-1}}}{(\psi_{i}-\psi_{i-1})^{2}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\Delta x_{i}\left(\frac{{\rm i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}}{\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i}}-\frac{e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i+1}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{i}}}{(\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i})^{2}}\right)\right]f_{i}+\Delta x_{N-1}\left(-\frac{{\rm i}e^{{\rm i}\psi_{N}}}{\psi_{N}-\psi_{N-1}}+\frac{e^{{\rm i}\psi_{N}}-e^{{\rm i}\psi_{N-1}}}{(\psi_{N}-\psi_{N-1})^{2}}\right)f_{N}.

The result is a weighting scheme for quadrature integration that can account for a rapidly changing phase function.

Appendix B
Normalisation coefficients in the case of static compression

In this section, we compute the normalisation coefficients
D_{p}(k)
needed in the solution in the case of static compression, as discussed in §
6
. We begin by introducing a new inner product over the vertical ordinate

\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{h}=\int_{-h}^{0}e^{-\gamma z}u_{1}(z)\overline{u_{2}(z)}\,{\rm d}z+\frac{c^{2}}{g}u_{1}(0)\overline{u_{2}(0)},

(43)

and seek to compute
\langle f_{n}(\cdot;k),f_{m}(\cdot;\tilde{k})\rangle_{h}
. Following some tedious calculations, we find

\displaystyle\left(\mu_{n}^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\right)\int_{-h}^{0}e^{-\gamma z}f_{n}(z;k)f_{m}(z;\tilde{k})\,{\rm d}z=\left[e^{-\gamma z}f_{n}^{\prime}(z;k)f_{m}(z;\tilde{k})\right]_{-h}^{0}-\left[e^{-\gamma z}f_{n}(z;k)f_{m}^{\prime}(z;\tilde{k})\right]_{-h}^{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt+\int_{-h}^{0}e^{-\gamma z}f_{n}(z;k)\left(\tilde{\mu}_{m}^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\right)f_{m}(z;\tilde{k})\,{\rm d}z,

(44a)

which yields

\displaystyle\int_{-h}^{0}e^{-\gamma z}f_{n}(z;k)f_{m}(z;\tilde{k})\,{\rm d}z=\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}(k)-\omega_{m}^{2}(\tilde{k})}{g\left(\mu_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}^{2}\right)}.

(44b)

Now, using (
43
),

\displaystyle\langle f_{n}(z;k),f_{m}(z;\tilde{k})\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}(k)-\omega_{m}^{2}(\tilde{k})}{g\left(\mu_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}^{2}\right)}f_{n}(0;k)f_{m}(0;\tilde{k})+\frac{c^{2}}{g},

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\frac{k^{2}-\tilde{k}^{2}}{\mu_{n}^{2}-\tilde{\mu}_{m}^{2}}.

(45a)

When
n=m
, the above inner product reduces to

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\tilde{k}}\langle f_{m}(z;k),f_{m}(z;\tilde{k})\rangle_{h}=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\frac{\tilde{k}}{\tilde{\mu}_{m}\partial_{k}\tilde{\mu}_{m}},

(45b)

where

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\mu_{n}=\frac{\displaystyle 2\frac{c^{2}}{g}\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{\tanh{\mu_{n}h}}{\mu_{n}}\right)k}{\mathcal{A}(\mu_{n},k)\tanh{\mu_{n}h}+\mathcal{B}(\mu_{n},k)\mathrm{sech}^{2}{\mu_{n}h}+2\dfrac{c^{2}}{g}\mu_{n}},

(45c)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mu_{n},k)=\left(1-\frac{\gamma c^{2}}{2g}\right)+\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\gamma c^{2}}{2g}\left(\left(\frac{k}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mu_{n},k)=\mu_{n}h\left[1-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\gamma c^{2}}{2g}\left(\left(\frac{k}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{2\mu_{n}}\right)^{2}-1\right)\right].

Consequently, the inner product
\langle\phi_{n}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,k),\phi_{p}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,\tilde{k})\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}
is computed as

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{n}^{\pm},\phi_{p}^{\pm}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-h}^{0}\phi_{n}^{\pm}(x,z;k)\overline{\phi_{p}^{\pm}(x,z;\tilde{k})}\,{\rm d}z\,{\rm d}x+\frac{c^{2}}{g}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{n}^{\pm}(x,0;k)\overline{\phi_{p}^{\pm}(x,0;\tilde{k})}\,{\rm d}x,

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle f_{n}(z;k),f_{p}(z;\tilde{k})\rangle_{h}e^{\mp{\rm i}(k-\tilde{k})x}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{c^{2}}{g}\frac{\tilde{k}}{\tilde{\mu}_{n}\partial_{k}\tilde{\mu}_{n}}\delta(k-\tilde{k})\delta_{np}.

(46a)

Note that in the case of counter-propagating waves, we compute
\langle\phi_{n}^{\pm}(\cdot,\cdot,k),\phi_{p}^{\mp}(\cdot,\cdot,\tilde{k})\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=0
, which is analogous to (
23c
).
