1
Introduction

The
generalized Ramsey number

f(K_{n},K_{p},q)
, introduced by Erdős and Shelah
[
15
]
, is the minimum number of colors needed to edge-color the complete graph on
n
vertices so that every copy of
K_{p}
receives at least
q
colors.
In the special case where
q=2
, determining the generalized Ramsey numbers is equivalent to finding the diagonal Ramsey numbers
R(p,p)
. The problem can be further extended by replacing
K_{n}
and
K_{p}
by a hypergraph
G
and a subhypergraph
H
, respectively. Generalized Ramsey numbers have been studied extensively, see e.g.,
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
16
,
23
,
24
]
.

Recently, a variant of this problem has been introduced (see
[
1
,
7
,
9
,
25
]
), asking for edge-colorings of
G
in which every copy
C
of
H
intersects some color class in an odd number of edges. In this case, we say that
C

has an odd color class
. The minimum number of colors needed to produce such a coloring is called the
odd Ramsey number
,
r_{odd}(G,H)
.

The study of
r_{odd}(K_{n},H)
was initially motivated by a work of Alon
[
1
]
on bounding the size of
graph-codes
.
Given a family
\mathcal{H}
of graphs on vertex set
[n]
, an
\mathcal{H}
-(graph)-code
is a collection of graphs
\mathcal{F}
on vertex set
[n]
such that the symmetric difference of any two members in
{\mathcal{F}}
is not a graph in
\mathcal{H}
. We denote the maximum possible cardinality of an
\mathcal{H}
-code by
D_{\mathcal{H}}(n)
, and the maximum possible density of an
\mathcal{H}
-code by

d_{\mathcal{H}}(n)=\displaystyle\frac{D_{\mathcal{H}}(n)}{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}.

In
[
25
]
, Versteegen showed that
d_{H}(n)\geq r_{odd}(K_{n},H)^{-(e(H)/2+1)}
. Thus, an upper bound on
r_{odd}(K_{n},H)
implies a lower bound for
d_{H}(n)
.
In this context, Versteegen
[
25
]
obtained lower bounds on
r_{odd}(K_{n},H)
, when
H
is a
K_{4}
-free graph with an even number of edges. To this end, he used a modification of the explicit coloring in
[
11
]
.
In
[
10
]
, Boyadzhiyska, Das, Lesgourgues, and Petrova established a connection between odd Ramsey numbers and results from coding theory. They showed
r_{odd}(K_{n},K_{t,n-t})\in\{n-1,n\}
, and

(1+o(1))\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)^{1/\lceil s/2\rceil}\leq r_{odd}\left(K_{n},K_{s,t}\right)\leq O\left(n^{\frac{2s+2t-4}{st}}\right),

where the upper bound is a direct corollary of the bound on
f\left(K_{n},K_{s,t},\frac{st}{2}+1\right)
from
[
2
]
.

In
[
1
]
it was observed that a coloring in
[
11
]
modifying constructions in
[
13
,
23
]
implies
r_{odd}(K_{n},K_{4})\leq n^{o(1)}
.
Bennett, Heath, and Zerbib
[
9
]
, and independently Ge, Xu, and Zhang
[
17
]
, then proved
r_{odd}(K_{n},K_{5})\leq n^{o(1)}
. The next non-trivial case,
r_{odd}(K_{n},K_{8})\leq n^{o(1)}
, was proven recently by Yip
[
26
]
. All these results were obtained by providing an explicit (deterministic) edge-coloring of
K_{n}
.

For host graphs other than
K_{n}
, not much is known yet. In
[
9
]
it was shown that
r_{odd}(K_{n,n},K_{2,2})=\frac{1}{2}n+o(n)
. The proof used a probabilistic coloring technique that utilized the
conflict-free hypergraph matching method
(see below).
Joos and Mubayi
[
20
]
used this technique to obtain an upper bound on the generalized Ramsey number
f(K_{n},C_{4},3)
.
A similar approach has been used by various authors to obtain upper bounds on other generalized Ramsey numbers,
see e.g.,
[
4
,
9
,
22
,
19
]
.

In this paper, we generalize the result
r_{odd}(K_{n,n},K_{2,2})=\frac{1}{2}n+o(n)
from
[
9
]
in two different ways.

Theorem 1
.

For any integer
t\geq 2
, we have

r_{odd}\left(K_{n,n},K_{2,t}\right)=\frac{n}{t}+o(n).

Theorem 2
.

For any integer
k\geq 2
, we have

r_{odd}\left({\mathcal{K}}^{(k)}_{n,\dots,n},{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\dots,1,2,2}\right)=\frac{n}{2}+o(n).

The proofs of the upper bounds in both theorems use the
Tripartite Matching Theorem
, a new version of the conflict-free hypergraph matching method recently introduced by Joos, Mubayi, and Smith
[
21
]
. Theorem
2
is the first result studying odd Ramsey numbers for hypergraphs.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we outline the Tripartite Matching Theorem
[
21
]
. The proof of Theorem
1
is then given in Section
3
, and the proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
4
.

2
The Tripartite Matching Theorem

The conflict-free hypergraph matching method
is a powerful probabilistic tool that, given some conditions, guarantees the existence of a matching avoiding certain negative constraints (conflicts) in a hypergraph.
The method was introduced by Glock, Joos, Kim, Kühn, and Lichev
[
18
]
and independently by Delcourt and Postle
[
14
]
, and it has already seen many applications, including bounds for odd Ramsey and generalized Ramsey numbers (see e.g.,
[
4
,
7
,
9
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
).

In this paper, we use a new version of the method, the Tripartite Matching Theorem, which was proven recently by Joos, Mubayi, and Smith
[
21
]
(Theorem
3
below). Very recently Bal and Bennett improved the upper bound on
f(K_{n},C_{4},3)
using the Tripartite Matching Theorem
[
3
]
. The key difference between the original method and the new version involves properties of the matching obtained by the theorem. In the original method, there is a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
and a
conflict system

{\mathcal{C}}
for
{\mathcal{H}}
, where
V({\mathcal{C}})=E({\mathcal{H}})
. The edges of
{\mathcal{C}}
correspond to
conflicts
, namely sets of edges in
{\mathcal{H}}
that should not be included together in the resulting matching. As long as some conditions on the degrees in
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
hold, the method guarantees the existence of an
almost perfect matching
of
{\mathcal{H}}
that does not contain any of the conflicts in
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Applying this method to edge-coloring, one defines a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
in which an almost perfect matching corresponds to an edge-coloring of a dense subgraph of
G
with
k
colors. The conflicts in
{\mathcal{C}}
are defined to correspond to such colorings that would not satisfy the odd- or generalized- Ramsey requirements. Thus, if the conditions of the theorem hold, one obtains a matching corresponding to a coloring of most of the edges in
G
. The edges that remain uncolored must be colored in a second step, usually by a random coloring with a new set of colors of size
o(k)
; then it must be shown that this can be done in a way that preserves the odd- or generalized-Ramsey properties required.

In the Tripartite Matching Theorem, the method was modified to produce a matching that corresponds to a coloring of
all
the edges in
G
in one step.
This was done by introducing a second conflict system
\mathcal{D}
in addition to
{\mathcal{C}}
. In this version, a matching in
{\mathcal{H}}
contains two types of edges: the first type corresponds to a coloring of the majority of the edges in
G
much like the initial version, and the second type corresponds to a coloring of the rest of the edges. The conflict system
{\mathcal{C}}
consists of conflicts containing only edges of the first type, while the new conflict system
\mathcal{D}
consists of conflicts containing both types of edges. This replaces the need to have a second coloring step.

We now state the Tripartite Matching Theorem.
Given a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
, and a vertex
v\in V({\mathcal{H}})
, the
degree

\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(v)
of
v
is the number of edges in
{\mathcal{H}}
containing
v
.
The maximum degree and minimum degree of
{\mathcal{H}}
are denoted by
\Delta({\mathcal{H}})
and
\delta({\mathcal{H}})
, respectively. For
j\geq 2
,
\Delta_{j}({\mathcal{H}})
denotes the maximum
j
-codegree, that is, the maximum number of edges in
{\mathcal{H}}
containing a particular set of
j
vertices, over all vertex subsets of size
j
. Given a subset of vertices
X\subseteq V(\mathcal{H})
,
\delta_{X}(\mathcal{H})
and
\Delta_{X}(\mathcal{H})
denote the minimum and maximum degree in
\mathcal{H}
of any vertex from
X
, respectively. An
X
-perfect matching
in
\mathcal{H}
is a matching which saturates every vertex in
X
.

For a hypergraph
{\mathcal{C}}
and an integer
k
, let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(k)}
be the set of edges in
{\mathcal{C}}
of size
k
. For a vertex
u\in V({\mathcal{C}})
, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{u}
denote the hypergraph
\{C\setminus\{u\}\colon C\in E({\mathcal{C}}),u\in C\}
. Given a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
, a hypergraph
{\mathcal{C}}
is a
conflict system
for
{\mathcal{H}}
if
V({\mathcal{C}})=E({\mathcal{H}})
. A set of edges
E\subset{\mathcal{H}}
is
{\mathcal{C}}
-free
if
E
contains no subset
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

Assume
\ell\geq 3
,
d>0
,
p\geq 1
,
q\geq 0
, and
r\geq 1
are integers, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
P,Q,R
be disjoint sets such that
d^{\varepsilon}\leq|P|\leq|P\cup Q|\leq\exp(d^{\varepsilon^{3}})
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
be a
(p+q)
-uniform hypergraph on vertex set
P\cup Q
whose every edge consists of
p
vertices from
P
and
q
vertices from
Q
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
be a
(1+r)
-uniform hypergraph on vertex set
P\cup R
whose edges consist of one vertex from
P
and
r
vertices from
R
. Finally, let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conflict system for
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}
be a conflict system for
{\mathcal{H}}:={\mathcal{H}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
.

Assume
{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies the following degree conditions:

(H1)

(1-d^{-\varepsilon})d\leq\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})\leq\Delta({\mathcal{H}}_{1})\leq d
(if
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
satisfies this condition we say that
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is
essentially
d
-regular)
;

(H2)

\Delta_{2}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})\leq d^{1-\varepsilon}
;

(H3)

\Delta_{R}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})\leq d^{\varepsilon^{4}}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})
;

(H4)

d(x,v)\leq d^{-\varepsilon}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})
for each
x\in P
and
v\in R
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies the following conditions, we say
{\mathcal{C}}
is
(d,\ell,\varepsilon)
-bounded
:

(C1)

3\leq|C|\leq\ell
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
;

(C2)

\Delta({\mathcal{C}}^{(j)})\leq\ell d^{j-1}
for all
3\leq j\leq\ell
;

(C3)

\Delta_{j^{\prime}}({\mathcal{C}}^{(j)})\leq d^{j-j^{\prime}-\varepsilon}
for all
3\leq j\leq\ell
and
2\leq j^{\prime}\leq j-1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}
denote the set of conflicts in
\mathcal{D}
consisting of
j_{1}
edges from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
j_{2}
edges from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Similarly, let
\Delta_{j^{\prime}_{1},j^{\prime}_{2}}(\mathcal{D})
denote the maximum degree among sets of edges
F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
consisting of
|F_{1}|=j^{\prime}_{1}
edges from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
|F_{2}|=j^{\prime}_{2}
edges from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Lastly, for
x\in P
, let
\mathcal{D}_{x}
denote the set of conflicts in
\mathcal{D}
containing
x
in their
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
-part, and
\mathcal{D}_{x,y}
denote the set of conflicts containing both
x
and
y
in their
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
-part.

If
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the following conditions for all
x,y\in P
and
0\leq j_{1}\leq\ell,2\leq j_{2}\leq\ell
, we say that
\mathcal{D}
is
(d,\ell,\varepsilon)
-simply-bounded
:

(D1)

2\leq|D\cap{\mathcal{H}}_{2}|\leq|D|\leq\ell
for each conflict
D\in\mathcal{D}
;

(D2)

|\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}|\leq d^{j_{1}+\varepsilon^{4}}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})^{j_{2}}
;

(D3)

\Delta_{j^{\prime},0}(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(j_{1},j_{2})})\leq d^{j_{1}-j^{\prime}-\varepsilon}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})^{j_{2}}
for each
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j_{1}
;

(D4)

|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}|\leq d^{j_{1}-\varepsilon}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})^{j_{2}}
.

Assuming that
\mathcal{C}
is
(d,\ell,\varepsilon)
-bounded and
\mathcal{D}
is
(d,\ell,\varepsilon)
-simply-bounded, Joos et al. proved the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(
[
21
]
)
.

For
p+q=k\geq 2
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists
d_{0}
such that given the above setup, the following holds for all
d\geq d_{0}
: there exists a
P
-perfect matching
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
which contains none of the conflicts from
{\mathcal{C}}\cup\mathcal{D}
. Furthermore, at most
d^{-\varepsilon^{4}}|P|
vertices of
P
belong to an edge in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\cap{\mathcal{M}}
.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

We start by showing that

r_{odd}\left(K_{n,n},K_{2,t}\right)>\frac{n}{t}
. Indeed, let the partite sets of
K_{n,n}
be
X
and
Y
, and suppose the edges are colored by at most
\frac{n}{t}
colors. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, every vertex
x\in X
has at least
\frac{n}{t}{t\choose 2}
pairs of vertices
u,v\in Y
so that
xu,xv
are colored by the same color. It follows that there are at least
\frac{n^{2}}{t}{t\choose 2}>{n\choose 2}(t-1)
pairs
u,v\in Y
so that there exists some
x\in X
with the edges
xu,xv
having the same color. Since there are
{n\choose 2}
pairs of vertices in
Y
, by the pigeonhole principle, there must exist at least
t
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\in X
and a pair vertices
u,v\in Y
, so that
x_{i}u,x_{i}v
has the same color
i
for all
i\in[t]
. But this gives a copy of
K_{t,2}
that has no odd color class.

Let
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
be disjoint sets of
\lceil\frac{n}{t}\rceil
and
\lceil n^{\delta}\rceil
colors, respectively, where
\delta<1
will be determined later in the proof.
To prove
r_{odd}\left(K_{n,n},K_{2,t}\right)\leq\frac{n}{t}+o(n)
, it suffices to find an edge-coloring of
K_{n,n}
with colors taken from the set
N_{1}\cup N_{2}
, such that each copy of
K_{2,t}
has an odd color class.

Let
K_{n,n}
have partite sets
X
and
Y
. Let
U=\binom{X\cup Y}{2}
and
V=\cup_{i\in N_{1}}V_{i}
, where each
V_{i}
is a copy of
X\cup Y
. We denote the copy of
v\in X\cup Y
in
V_{i}
by
v^{i}
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
be the
(2(t+1)^{2})
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
U\cup V
, whose edges are defined as follows: for every
i\in N_{1}
, for every copy
S^{\prime}
of
K_{t+1,t+1}
in
K_{n,n}
, and for every perfect matching
M
in
S^{\prime}
, let
S=S^{\prime}-M
, and define an edge
e_{S,i}
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
as

e_{S,i}=\{v^{i}\colon v\in V(S)\}\cup\{xy\in E(S)\}\cup\left\{xx^{\prime}\in\binom{V(S)\cap X}{2}\right\}\cup\left\{yy^{\prime}\in\binom{V(S)\cap Y}{2}\right\}.

Note that each
e_{S,i}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
corresponds to a copy
S
of the graph
K_{t+1,t+1}-(t+1)K_{2}
in
k_{n,n}
, whose all edges are colored by the color
i\in N_{1}
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
be the 3-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
E(K_{n,n})\cup W
, where
W=\cup_{i\in N_{2}}W_{i}
and each
W_{i}
is a copy of
X\cup Y
. We denote the copy of
w\in X\cup Y
in
W_{i}
as
w^{i}
.
For each
xy\in E(K_{n,n})
and each
i\in N_{2}
, let
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
contain the edge

e_{xy,i}=\left\{xy,x^{i},y^{i}\right\}.

Note that every edge
e_{xy,i}
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
corresponds to an edge
xy\in K_{n,n}
that is colored by the color
i\in N_{2}
.
Further, a matching in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
corresponds to a set of edge-disjoint monochromatic copies of
K_{t+1,t+1}-(t+1)K_{2}
in
K_{n,n}
, such that no vertex of
K_{n,n}
appears in two copies of
K_{t+1,t+1}-M
of the same color. A matching in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
corresponds to a set of edges in
K_{n,n}
that are properly colored. A matching in
{\mathcal{H}}:={\mathcal{H}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is the union of two such matchings, and it corresponds to a well-defined (partial) edge-coloring of
K_{n,n}
(that is, no edge gets more than one color).

To align our notation with those of Theorem
3
, define
P:=U
,
Q:=V
, and
R:=W
. For the rest of the proof, we refer to the edges of
{\mathcal{H}}
as
tiles
, to the edges of
K_{n,n}
as
edges
, and the edges of the complement of
K_{n,n}
as
non-edges
. Note that every tile of
cH_{1}
contains both edges and non-edges.

Let
d=\frac{n^{2t+1}}{t!}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2t+2})
, and
\delta\in(1-(2t+1)\varepsilon^{4},1)
.

Claim 4
.

Conditions
(H1)
-
(H4)
hold,
and
d^{\varepsilon}\leq|P|\leq|P\cup Q|\leq\exp(d^{\varepsilon^{3}})
for sufficiently large
n
.

Proof.

We first show that the hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is essentially
d
-regular for
d=\frac{n^{2t+1}}{t!}
. To see this we show that for any vertex
u\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
, we have

(1-d^{-\varepsilon})d=d-d^{1-\varepsilon}\leq d-O\left(n^{2t+1-\varepsilon(2t+1)}\right)\leq d-O\left(n^{2t+1-1}\right)=d-O(n^{2t})\leq\deg_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}(u)\leq d.

First, suppose
u\in V_{i}
for some
i\in N_{1}
. There are
\frac{n^{2t+1}}{t!}-O(n^{2t-1})
many tiles in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
containing
u
since there are
\binom{n}{t+1}
ways to choose the vertices in the vertex part not containing
u
,
\binom{n-1}{t}
ways to choose the vertices in the same vertex part as
u
, and
(t+1)!
ways to choose which perfect matching to remove. If instead
u=xy
for
x\in X,y\in Y
, then there are
\binom{n-1}{t}
ways to choose the remaining vertices in each vertex part, there are
t\cdot t!
ways to choose a matching to remove (we know
xy
is not in the matching), and
\lceil\frac{n}{t}\rceil
ways to choose the color. Similarly, if
u=xx^{\prime}
for
x,x^{\prime}\in X
(or
yy^{\prime}
for
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
), there are
\binom{n-2}{t-1}
ways to choose the remaining vertices of the same vertex part,
\binom{n}{t+1}
ways to choose the vertices of the other vertex part,
(t+1)!
ways to choose the matching, and
\lceil\frac{n}{t}\rceil
ways to choose the color. Thus, in each case, we have
\deg_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}(u)=\frac{n^{2t+1}}{t!}-O(n^{2t})
, satisfying
(H1)
.

Furthermore, we have
\Delta_{2}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})\leq d^{1-\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2t+2})
, since each pair of vertices
u,v\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
is contained in at most
O(n^{2t})
tiles in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. To see this, given
u,v\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
, then either at least two vertices and a color of the copy of
K_{t+1,t+1}
are given, or at least three of the vertices of a copy of
K_{t+1,t+1}
are given. Hence, there are at most
O(n^{2t})
tiles containing both
u,v
, satisfying
(H2)
.

Now consider some
w^{i}\in R
. Then
\deg_{R}(w^{i})=n
since
w
has exactly
n
neighbors in
K_{n,n}
. This implies that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2t+2})
and
\delta\in(1-(2t+1)\varepsilon^{4},1)
we have
n\leq n^{(2t+1)\varepsilon^{4}}\cdot n^{\delta}
. Thus,
\Delta_{R}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\leq d^{\varepsilon^{4}}\delta_{P}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
and condition
(H3)
is satisfied.

To check
(H4)
, fix
v\in R
, say
v=w^{i}
, where
i\in N_{2}
. Now fix
x\in P
where
x=z_{1}z_{2}
for some
z_{1},z_{2}\in X\cup Y
. If
z_{1}z_{2}\notin E(K_{n,n})
then it cannot appear in a hyperedge with
w^{i}
. Similarly, if
w\notin\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
then
z_{1}z_{2}
also cannot appear in a hyperedge with
w^{i}
. Finally if without loss of generality,
w=z_{1}
, then

•

Let

\{e_{S,i},e_{S^{\prime},i},e_{T,j}\}
. If
e_{S,i},e_{S^{\prime},i}
are the fixed tiles, then we know two
k
-tuples of the form
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
and
\{x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{k}\}
in
e_{T,j}
. Hence, there are at most
\Delta_{2}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})=O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\leq d^{1-\varepsilon}
ways to choose
e_{T,j}
to be in the conflict. If, without loss of generality,
e_{S,i}
and
e_{T,j}
are fixed, then we know two
k
-tuples in
e_{S^{\prime},i}
, and there are
O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)
ways to choose
e_{S^{\prime},i}
to be in the conflict.

Similarly, the number of conflicts in
{\mathcal{C}}^{(4)}
containing two fixed vertices is
O(d^{2-\varepsilon})
. Observe that a conflict in
{\mathcal{C}}^{(4)}
has the form
\{e_{S,i},e_{S^{\prime},i},e_{T,j},e_{T^{\prime},j}\}
. If
e_{S,i}
and
e_{S^{\prime},i}
are fixed, then there are
O(d)
ways to choose
e_{T,j}
and
O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)
ways to choose
e_{T^{\prime},j}
. If
e_{S,i}
and
e_{T,j}
are fixed, then there are
O(d)
ways to choose
e_{S^{\prime},i}
(since we know a
k
-tuple of the form
\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},i\right\}
in
e_{S^{\prime},i}
), and
O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)
ways to choose
e_{T^{\prime},j}
(since we know two
k
-tuples, one of the form
\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right\}
and one of the form
\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},j\right\}
, in
e_{T^{\prime},j}
). Lastly, the number of conflicts in
{\mathcal{C}}^{(4)}
containing three fixed vertices is
O(d^{1-\varepsilon})
. Without loss of generality, suppose
e_{S,i}
,
e_{S^{\prime},i}
, and
e_{T,j}
are fixed. Then there are
O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)
ways to choose
e_{T^{\prime},j}
to form the conflict since we know two
k
-tuples, of the form
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
and
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},j\}
, in
e_{T^{\prime},j}
. Hence,
(C3)
is satisfied.

Thus,
{\mathcal{C}}
is a
(d,O(1),\varepsilon)
-bounded conflict system for
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
for all
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{k^{2}+2}\right)
.

4.2
The conflict system
\mathcal{D}

Let
V(\mathcal{D})=E({\mathcal{H}})
, and let the edges of
\mathcal{D}
correspond to copies of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\dots,1,2,2}
in
{\mathcal{K}}_{n,\dots,n}^{(k)}
colored as in
Fig.

4
. Note that the coloring of the graph in
Fig.

4
is again the only coloring of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\dots,1,2,2}
which has no odd color class since we are using a matching of
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
to color
{\mathcal{K}}^{(k)}_{n,\dots,n}
. To see this, observe that any two edges corresponding to two tiles in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
cannot overlap in
k-1
graph vertices, since then those edges would not be in a matching of
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
(and thus not in a matching of
{\mathcal{H}}
). Again, we will call these edges of
\mathcal{D}

conflicts
, and without loss of generality, they correspond to sets of one or two monochromatic copies
S,S^{\prime}
which are each transversals of a copy of
{\mathcal{K}}_{k+1,\dots,k+1}^{(k)}
in
{\mathcal{K}}_{n,\dots,n}^{(k)}
, and two or four copies
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3},T_{4}
which are each edges in
{\mathcal{K}}_{n,\dots,n}^{(k)}
. A conflict of the first type with only two tiles coming from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
we call
type 1
, and a conflict of the second type with four tiles coming from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
we call
type 2
. Note that type 1 conflicts can be of size three or four, while type 2 conflicts are of size four. See
Fig.

5
for an example.

Figure 5
:
The bad colorings of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\dots,1,2,2}
in
{\mathcal{K}}_{n,n,...,n}^{(k)}
which correspond with the different types of conflicts in
\mathcal{D}
(type 1 on the left and type 2 on the right). Note that two edges of the same color from
N_{1}
can come from the same tile in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
.

Our above definition of the conflicts in
\mathcal{D}
implies that any conflict has at least two, and no more than four, tiles in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Hence, the property
(D1)
is satisfied.

We next show
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}\right|\leq d^{j_{1}+\varepsilon^{4}}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})^{j_{2}}
for all
j_{1}\in[0,O(1)],j_{2}\in[2,O(1)]
. To this end, first, observe that
\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})=n^{\delta}
. To see this, a vertex in
P
that is also in
V({\mathcal{H}}_{2})
is an edge
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}\}
of
{\mathcal{K}}_{n,\dots,n}^{(k)}
, and there are
n^{\delta}
ways to choose a color for the edge; tiles in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
correspond to colored edges, so the observation follows. Now, consider
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(1,2)}
, which contains conflicts only of type 1. If
x\in P
is in some tile in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
that is in a conflict in
\mathcal{D}
, then there are
\frac{n}{2}
ways to choose the color of the tile from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, then
d
ways to choose that tile, and
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of
x
and the other tile in the conflict. Hence,
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(1,2)}\right|\leq O(n^{1+\delta}d)\leq d^{1+\varepsilon^{4}}n^{2\delta}
by our choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
(since
1-\frac{k^{2}+1}{(k^{2}+2)^{4}}<\delta)
. Next, consider
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}
, which again contains conflicts only of type 1. Again, we fix
x\in P
in some tile in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
that is in a conflict in
\mathcal{D}
. There are
O(n)
ways to choose the color of the two tiles coming from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, then
d
ways to choose the first tile coming from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, then
d
ways to choose the second tile coming from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, and
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of the tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
(all edges in the tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
are fixed by the tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
). Hence,
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}\right|\leq O(n^{1+\delta}d^{2})\leq d^{2+\varepsilon^{4}}n^{2\delta}
by our choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
(since
1-\frac{k^{2}+1}{(k^{2}+2)^{4}}<\delta)
. Now, consider
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(0,4)}
, which contains only conflicts of type 2. Note there are
O(n^{2})
ways to choose the two remaining graph vertices and
(n^{\delta})^{2}
ways to choose the two colors of the tiles. Hence,
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(0,4)}\right|\leq O(n^{2+2\delta})\leq d^{\varepsilon^{4}}n^{4\delta}
by our choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
(since
1-\frac{k^{2}+1}{2(k^{2}+2)^{4}}<\delta)
. Finally, note that for all other
j_{1},j_{2}
,
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}
is empty and hence the bound holds trivially. Thus,
(D2)
is satisfied.

Next, we will show
\Delta_{j^{\prime},0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}\right)\leq d^{j_{1}-j^{\prime}-\varepsilon}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})^{j_{2}}
for each
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j_{1}
. Note that in our case, as we observed above,
j_{1}\in\{0,1,2\}
. If
j_{1}=0
, then
j^{\prime}=0
, and
\Delta_{0,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(0,4)}\right)
= 0 since in
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(0,4)}
, there are no conflicts that contain zero tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and zero tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. If
j_{1}=1
, then
j_{2}=2
and
j^{\prime}=0
or
j^{\prime}=1
. Note that again we have
\Delta_{0,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(1,2)}\right)=0
. We also have
\Delta_{1,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(1,2)}\right)=O(n^{\delta})
(since for a fixed tile from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
in a conflict in
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(1,2)}
, there are
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of the tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
in the conflict, but the graph edges in the tile are already determined by the fixed tile from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
). Hence,
\Delta_{1,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(1,2)}\right)=O(n^{\delta})\leq d^{-\varepsilon}n^{2\delta}
for our particular choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
(since
\frac{k^{2}+1}{k^{2}+2}<\delta
). If
j_{1}=2
, then
j_{2}=2
again, and
j^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}
. We have
\Delta_{0,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}\right)=0
. Similarly, we have
\Delta_{1,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}\right)=O(d\cdot n^{\delta})
since for a given tile in
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}
from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, it must appear in a conflict with an
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tile containing
x
, so there are
d
ways to choose the other
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
tile and
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles (the graph edges corresponding to the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles are fixed by the
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
tiles and
x\in P
). Hence,
\Delta_{1,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}\right)=O(d\cdot n^{\delta})\leq d^{1-\varepsilon}n^{2\delta}
for our particular choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
(since
\frac{k^{2}+1}{k^{2}+2}<\delta
). Lastly, we have
\Delta_{2,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}\right)=O(n^{\delta})
since for two given tiles in
\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}
from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
to appear together in a conflict, there are
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles (the graph edges corresponding to the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles are fixed by the
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
tiles). Hence,
\Delta_{2,0}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x}^{(2,2)}\right)=O(n^{\delta})\leq d^{-\varepsilon}n^{2\delta}
for our particular choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
(since
\frac{k^{2}+1}{k^{2}+2}<\delta
). Thus,
(D3)
is satisfied.

Finally, we will show
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(j_{1},j_{2})}\right|\leq d^{j_{1}-\varepsilon}\delta_{P}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})^{j_{2}}
for
x,y\in P
in the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
part of the conflicts from
\mathcal{D}
. First observe that
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(0,4)}\right|=O(n^{1+2\delta})
since for any fixed
x,y
, there are
O(n)
ways to choose the last graph vertex in a graph edge that corresponds to the other two
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles in a conflict, and
n^{2\delta}
ways to choose the colors of the tiles. Hence,
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(0,4)}\right|=O(n^{1+2\delta})\leq d^{-\varepsilon}n^{4\delta}
since
\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{k^{2}+1}{k^{2}+2})<\delta
. Next observe that
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(1,2)}\right|=O\left(\frac{d}{n}\cdot n^{\delta}\right)
since for any fixed
x,y
, there are
O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)
ways to choose the tile from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
(we know two graph edges in the tile, they are fixed by
x,y
), and
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles. Hence,
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(1,2)}\right|=O\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\leq d^{1-\varepsilon}n^{2\delta}
since
\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k^{2}+1}{k^{2}+2}-1\right)<\delta
. Lastly, observe that
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(2,2)}\right|=O\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\cdot n^{\delta}\right)
since for any fixed
x,y
, there are
O\left(d\cdot\frac{d}{n}\right)
ways to choose the tiles from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
(we know a graph edge in one tile, it is fixed by
x,y
, and after choosing one tile from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, we know a graph edge and a color of the last tile), and
n^{\delta}
ways to choose the color of the
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
tiles. Hence,
\left|\mathcal{D}_{x,y}^{(2,2)}\right|=O\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\cdot n^{\delta}\right)\leq d^{2-\varepsilon}n^{2\delta}
since
\frac{k^{2}+1}{k^{2}+2}-1<\delta
. Thus,
(D4)
is satisfied. Thus,
\mathcal{D}
is a
(d,O(1),\varepsilon)
-simply-bounded conflict system for
{\mathcal{H}}
for all
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{k^{2}+2}\right)
.

By
Theorem

3
, there exists a
P
-perfect matching
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
which contains none of the conflicts from
{\mathcal{C}}\cup\mathcal{D}
. This matching corresponds to a well-defined edge-coloring of all the edges of
{\mathcal{K}}^{(k)}_{n,\dots,n}
, using
N_{1}+N_{2}=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+\lceil n^{\delta}\rceil
colors, in which every copy of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\dots,1,2,2}
has an odd color class. Hence, the upper bound in
Theorem

2
holds.
