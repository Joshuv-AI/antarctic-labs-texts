Immunomodulatory effects of PACAP sequence modifications in juvenile white shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei
Authors: Jesús Luis Betancourt, Tania Rodríguez-Ramos, Laura Rivera, Yamila Carpio, Mario Pablo Estrada, Laida Ramos, Brian Dixon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP) is a highly conserved multifunctional neuropeptide belonging to the secretin/glucagon/vasoactive intestinal peptide superfamily. In recent years, the role of PACAP in immunoregulation has been partially elucidated in mammals and teleost fishes, demonstrating that PACAP modulates both innate and acquired immunity. However, in invertebrates, little is known about its immunostimulant and antimicrobial properties. In previous research, studies performed in Litopenaeus (Penaeus) vannamei have revealed that Clarias gariepinus PACAP-38 improves immune functions like lectins, superoxide dismutase and nitric oxide synthase derived metabolites in postlarvae shrimp. In this study, the immunomodulatory effects of C. gariepinus PACAP-38 and four PACAP sequence modified peptides in juvenile L. vannamei were evaluated. The effect of several administrations of PACAP and its variants on shrimp immune effectors, as well as the effect of different doses was examined. Results revealed that PACAP can influence hemolymph immune parameters, and immune-related gene mRNA levels in the hemocytes, gill and hepatopancreas with different effects depending on the modification of the peptide sequence, the dose and the tissue assessed. Altogether, these findings provide valuable insights regarding the role of PACAP in crustacean innate immunity and highlight the need for further research into its potential application as an alternative to antibiotics in aquaculture.Keywords: Litopenaeus vannamei, Crustaceans, Innate immunity, Antimicrobial peptides, PACAP

Introduction

Innate immunity is the first line of defense against infections and the type of immunity available for invertebrates [1]. Crustaceans are frequently exposed to both opportunistic and obligate pathogens in their environment; nevertheless, their immune system effectively eliminates most microbes that manage to penetrate their defenses [2]. However, the stress produced by high population densities and abiotic changes in aquaculture facilities produces a disruption of the balance between host and pathogens, leading to disease. Outbreaks in aquaculture, a key source of for human consumption, cause severe economic losses to the industry [3]. Consequently, farmers are making use of antibiotics as a prophylactic treatment worldwide. Nonetheless, overuse and excess drugs released into the environment has led to microbial resistance in both animal and human pathogens, which has driven the search for novel molecules with antibacterial and immunostimulatory activities [[4], [5], [6]].

To address this problem researchers are studying antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). AMPs are small amino acid sequences with cationic and amphipathic properties, thus allowing them to interact with the bacterial cell wall [7,8]. These molecules can be classified into two types based on their mode of action: membrane-disruptive and non-membrane-disruptive AMPs. Therefore, some AMPs cause membrane disruption, leading to lysis or leakage, whereas others bypass the membrane and act on intracellular structures, interfering with internal functions rather than directly compromising the membrane [[7], [8], [9]]. In either case, these peptides disrupt the cell by disturbing the cytoplasmic membrane or targeting intracellular structures, ultimately leading to cell death. AMPs can be found in all kingdoms of life and exhibit an extraordinary structural and functional diversity, thus making them attractive alternatives to antibiotics [7,10].

In this context, scientists have made significant progress in testing Pituitary Adenylate Cyclase-Activating Polypeptide (PACAP) in aquatic organisms as a potential candidate for replacing conventional antibiotics. PACAP is a highly conserved multifunctional AMP that belongs to the secretin/glucagon/vasoactive intestinal peptide superfamily [11]. This peptide has been shown to stimulate the adaptive immune response in teleost fish [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]], as well as the innate immune axis in shrimp [17]. Additionally, in its immunomodulatory role, PACAP acts as an AMP, killing a wide spectrum of Gram-negative bacteria such as Flavobacterium psychrophilum [13] and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [12,18], which are one of the most common pathogens in fish aquaculture. Most studies on fish have used rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) as a model [13,15]. However, little is known about the effects of PACAP on invertebrates like shrimp and crayfish. Studies performed by Lugo et al. [17] in Litopenaeus (Penaeus) vannamei revealed that Clarias gariepinus PACAP-38 improves immune parameters such as total hemocyte count and hemagglutinating, superoxide dismutase and nitric oxide synthase activities in postlarvae shrimp. Nevertheless, further research is needed to address the influence of PACAP on immune gene expression and molecular mechanisms of action.

In this study, we evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of C. gariepinus PACAP-38 and four PACAP-38 modified sequences in juvenile shrimp L. vannamei. Bioassays were carried out to evaluate the influence of several administrations of PACAP-38 and its variants on shrimp immune effectors, as well as the effect of different doses. Immune-related gene modulation and some hemolymph immune parameters were measured. PACAP analogs influenced hemolymph immune parameters and regulated immune-related gene expression with effects depending on the modification of the peptide sequence, dose and tissue assessed. Overall, these findings offer valuable perspective regarding the role of PACAP in crustacean immunology and underscore the need for further research to explore its potential as an alternative to antibiotics in aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Animals

Juvenile shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei, with a body weight of 7.74 ± 1.95 g, were kindly provided by the Yaguacam broodstock (Cienfuegos, Cuba) and transported to the Marine Research Center, University of Havana (CIM.UH). Shrimp were fed twice a day ad libitum and acclimated for a minimum of one week in 500 L tanks with recirculating natural seawater (28.56 ± 0.22 °C, 35.7 ± 0.07 PPS salinity, 7.81 ± 0.05 pH, and 5.26 ± 0.25 mg/L dissolved oxygen) under 12 light/12 dark hours photoperiod. During the experimental period, debris and feces were removed daily. For the experiments, 10–15 animals were randomly distributed in 70 L tanks under the above conditions. The animal experiments were conducted in accordance with guidelines of the Ethics Committee of the Marine Research Center.

Peptide synthesis

For this bioassay, the native and modified peptide sequences were named as shown in Table 1. All peptides were synthesized by Bio Basic Canada Inc., with a minimum of 95% purity, using the sequence of C. gariepinus PACAP-38 (PACAP 1) as a template, introducing chemical modifications (PACAP 2–4) to make them exopeptidase-resistant, therefore increasing their half-life. It is important to highlight that these alterations on C. gariepinus PACAP-38 sequence were implemented according to the design by [18]. PACAP 5 (scrambled sequence) was designed to be used as a negative control peptide. This sequence was scrambled by randomizing four amino acid blocks of PACAP 1.

Experimental design

To test the differential effects of the original C. gariepinus PACAP-38 and the modified PACAP sequences as well as their dose-dependent effects, four injections of each dose of each peptide were administrated to animals in each group, at the coxa of the third pair of pereopods alternating one pereopod of the pair every 48 h over one week. The doses assessed consisted in 1 μg and 10 μg of each peptide which correspond to around 0.1 and 1 μg per g of body weight, respectively. A Crustacean Saline Solution (CSS, NaCl 450 mM, KCl 10 mM at pH 7.3) and a Non-Injection (NI) group were also assessed as negative control groups. In total, 12 experimental groups (one group per each dose of PACAP plus CSS and NI control groups) were included. Each experimental group contained 12–15 shrimp.

Sampling and plasma preparation

Tissue samples were collected 24 hours after the last injection and stored as described below. Hemolymph was withdrawn using a 1 mL syringe and a 20-G needle from the ventral sinus of the cephalothorax into a pre-cooled and sterile anticoagulant solution (AC: NaCl 450 mM, KCl 10 mM and EDTA 20 mM at pH 7.3) in a ratio 1:3 v/v according to [19]. After collection, 25 μL hemolymph was mixed with 75 μL Alsever's solution (113 mM glucose, 27.2 mM sodium citrate, 2.8 mM citric acid, 71.9 mM sodium chloride and 4% formalin at pH 7.3) and refrigerated at 4 °C for measuring the total hemocyte count (THC). The remaining hemolymph was immediately centrifuged (800 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Following centrifugation, the supernatant was frozen at −20 °C for measurement of the plasma immune response assays and the remaining hemocyte pellet was mixed with 300 μL of RNA later and stored for 24 hours at 4 °C, after which it was stored at −20 °C. Furthermore, tissue samples of gill and hepatopancreas were cut to ≤0.3 cm in any single dimension and kept in 1.5 mL tubes filled with RNAlater following the manufacturer's protocol.

Hemolymph immune parameter assays

For measuring THC, 10 μL of hemocyte suspension was added to a Neubauer hemocytometer in duplicate and counts were carried out using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). The hemagglutination activity (HA) assay was performed according to [20] modified by [21]. The total protein concentration of the plasma was measured according to the Bradford (1976) method [22] using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as standard. To determine the plasma-derived phenoloxidase (PO) activity, 30 μL plasma and 250 μL L-DOPA (3 mg/ml in ultrapure water, Sigma) were added into a 96-well microplate (in duplicate). Afterward, the mixture was incubated for 80 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark after which the absorbance was measured at 490 nm against a blank consisting of AC solution. One unit of PO activity was defined as an increase of 0.001 in OD490nm min−1 mg−1 protein in the plasma at RT [23]. The concentration of total nitric oxide synthase (NOS) derived metabolites (NO2- + NO3-), in the plasma, was determined with a Nitrite/Nitrate Assay Kit (Sigma) following the manufacturer's recommendations.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from gills and hepatopancreas with Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) and from hemocytes with a RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the instructions of each manufacturer. Hemocyte RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) to remove any contaminating genomic DNA and resuspended in commercial RNAase-free water. While the RNA from the gills and hepatopancreas was treated with Ambion DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States), cleaned up using Norgen RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Canada), and, finally, resuspended in commercial RNase-free water. Total RNA purity and quantity was assessed using a Take3 plate on a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek Instruments, United States); afterward, the samples were stored at -80 °C until further use. cDNA was synthesized from 250 ng (gill and hepatopancreas) or 125 ng (hemocytes) of total RNA, respectively, using the qScript™ cDNA SuperMix (Quanta Biosciences, United States) and following the manufacturer's guidelines. No template controls consisting of only RNA resuspended in DEPC-water were included for each cDNA synthesis reaction. Afterward, the cDNA was stored at -20 °C until subsequent use as template in qPCR reaction.

qRT-PCR

qPCR reactions were performed on a LightCycler® 480 II (Roche, Switzerland) with a 10 μL final volume consisting of: 2.5 μL of cDNA (2.5 ng/μl), 2.5 μL of Primer Mix (1 µM) and 5 μL 2x WISENT ADVANCED™ qPCR Master Mix (Wisent, Canada). All samples were run in triplicate, and each experimental plate included a calibrator and a no template control. Cycling conditions for all qPCR reactions was as follows: pre-incubation at 95 °C for 10 mins, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 5 s, and extension at 72 °C for 8 s. The Livak (2 –ΔΔCT) method [24] was used to analyze gene expression relative to the elongation factor 1 alpha gene (EF-1α) and the NI control group. Primer efficiency was between 85% and 100% (data not shown) for all primer sets listed in Table 2.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were executed using Statistica version 12 (StatSoft Inc., United States) and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5.1) was used to generate the graphs. Firstly, normality and homogeneity were tested by Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Levene's tests, respectively. Then, a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test (gene expression data) or Fisher's LSD test (hemolymph metabolites data) was used to determine statistical differences between groups (p < 0.05).

Hemolymph immunological indicators

The immunostimulatory properties of the original PACAP-38 and its modified sequences as well as their dose-dependent effects are shown in Fig. 1. As can be seen (Fig. 1A), PACAP 1, PACAP 3 and PACAP 4 treated groups showed a dramatic decrease of THC at 1 µg dose compared to the NI control group. At the same time, a decline of the THC was found for PACAP 4 and PACAP 5 treated groups at 10 µg dose compared to the NI control group.

As shown in Fig. 1B HA increased for the PACAP 2 (1 µg) and PACAP 4 (1 µg) groups compared to NI and CSS control groups. The concentration of proteins in the hemolymph dropped for 10 µg dose PACAP 1, PACAP 2, PACAP 3 and PACAP 5 treated animals relative to the NI control group (Fig. 1C). Moreover, this decline was noticeable for PACAP 1 (1µg) as it also declined relative to the NI groups. PO activity (Fig.1D) remained constant for most experimental groups with exception of PACAP 1 (1 µg), PACAP 2 (10 µg) and PACAP 3 (10 µg) groups which showed a 2-to-3-fold increase compared to the NI control group. Similarly, the concentration of total NOS derived metabolites (Fig.1E) stayed the same for most groups, except the CSS control group which exhibited a significantly higher concentration of metabolites compared to all groups excluding PACAP 3 (10 µg) and the NI group. Moreover, PACAP 2 (1 µg) exhibited low values of NOS derived metabolites in comparison to the CSS and NI control groups and the PACAP 3 (10 µg) treated group.

Differential immune gene expression analysis

Fig. 2 illustrates the differential effects of the PACAP's treatments on gene expression in the gill of L. vannamei. As can been seen (Fig. 2A), the ProPO transcripts remained the same for all experimental groups. Notably, the PACAP 3 group at 10 µg showed a significant increase in alpha-2-macroglobulin gene expression compared to the NI control, with a log2 fold change of approximately 1 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Regarding the NOS gene expression, there was a downregulation for the CSS group compared to most experimental groups including the NI control and the PACAP 2 and 4 groups at 1 µg dose (p < 0.05); as well as the PACAP 1 to 4 groups at 10 µg dose (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). It is worth to highly that the transcript levels of NOS in the CSS group exhibited a downregulation of approximately 60% compared to the PACAP 3 group at 10 µg dose (p < 0.0001). There were no significant changes in the lysozyme gene expression level in the gills (Fig. 2D). Heat shock protein 70 (HSP-70) transcript levels (Fig. 2F), dropped after treatment with PACAP 5 (10 µg) in comparison to most experimental groups and there was a rise in HSP-70 gene expression for the PACAP 3 (10 µg) group in contrast to the CSS group. Furthermore, the PACAP 5 10 µg dose group showed a downregulation of crustin gene expression compared to PACAP 3 (10 µg) (Fig. 2E).

Discussion

Antimicrobial peptides, like PACAP, are one of the most promising alternatives to antibiotics in the ongoing battle between the aquaculture industry and infectious agents. The uniqueness of AMPs’ mode of action combined with their natural origins and typically immunomodulatory effects on host cells has increase the interest over the years in studying these peptides [10]. Given this background, in the present work, we studied the effect of C. gariepinus PACAP-38, three exopeptidase-resistant PACAP-38 analogs and one scrambled PACAP-38 sequence as immunostimulants in juvenile shrimp L. vannamei.

Previously, Lugo et al. [17] carried out an in vivo experiment using immersion baths to deliver C. gariepinus PACAP to postlarvae shrimp. Therefore, to confirm the viable use of C. gariepinus PACAP as a treatment for shrimp, cDNA encoding the mature L. vannamei PACAP was isolated and was shown to possess a high degree of primary sequence similarity to C. gariepinus PACAP (94%) as well as to other invertebrates’ species (92–97%). Consequently, there is clear evolutionary conservation of PACAP sequence across organisms, which supports the use of C. gariepinus PACAP in shrimp. In this context, the immune assays performed by Lugo and co-workers demonstrated that PACAP improves several immune parameters in shrimp hemolymph. Moreover, this peptide was also suggested to stimulate growth performance as observed by increases in size, body weight and total protein concentration [17]. Consequently, the present study proposed to continue examining the effects of PACAP on shrimp.

It is worth noting that the chemical modifications present on the modified C. gariepinus PACAP-38 (PACAP 2–4) sequences were designed as per Starr et al. [18]. Since Human PACAP-38 has proved to be rapidly cleaved and inactivated by aminodipeptidase DPP IV, Starr and co-workers designed and tested different chemical modifications to this peptide's sequence to possibly make it exopeptidase-resistant, therefore increasing its half-life and extend its effects. Consequently, the three modifications (Table 1) chosen for our study were the best candidates for making PACAP exopeptidase-resistant and maintaining or increasing antimicrobial activity.

An overall look to the results could suggests that the shrimp experience a depletion of some immunological parameters caused by the stress associated with captivity and handling. It is important to highlight that samples, excluding the NI control group, were taken 24 hours after the last injection. Consequently, the shrimp could be still recovering from the stress associated with the handling originated by four injections over one week, especially the last injection, and the additional time in captivity. Moreover, the time of emersion during the injection procedure could be a factor contributing to the exhaustion of the animals. Although little is known about the crustacean neuroendocrine-stress-immunity axis [32], parameters such as THC, PO activity and proteins levels could be useful indicators; as long as fluctuation related to moult cycle, reproductive status and biomineralization are consider it. In this context, during the experiment a decrease in the THC and protein levels was reported for some experimental groups compared to the NI control group. However, no differences were recorded between the CSS and the NI controls groups. Therefore, it is challenging to conclude whether the observed decrease in certain immune markers is attributable to stress-induced deterioration, as previously mentioned, or to peptide overdose and/or chemical modifications within the peptide analogs. Future studies should investigate this issue by measuring more robust physiological stress indicators, such as L-lactate concentrations in the hemolymph [33].

Also, the observed decrease in the circulating hemocytes, for some experimental groups, could be explained by the migration of the hemocytes to the site of injections. As described by Burge et al. [34], shrimp may exhibit a 'wounding response' following injection, characterized by a decrease in THC and upregulation of genes such as lysozyme at the injection site. Therefore, this decline in circulating hemocytes is likely due to their recruitment to the site of the wound. Additionally, this response appears to be part of a broader systemic mobilization of immune components, which in the case of a non-sterile wound, would play a role in detecting and abolishing pathogens [34]. In crustaceans, numerous studies have shown that the number of freely circulating hemocytes is influenced by abiotic changes, moulting cycles, physic-chemical stress, and infections [35,36]. Further studies should consider sampling the injection site for gene expression analysis to provide stronger support for this claim.

In this context, a study conducted in the crayfish Orconectes propinquus showed that a 12 µg dose (around 0.5 µg per g of body weight) of C. gariepinus PACAP-38 was able to increase the HA as well as the NO metabolites production during the first 24- and 6-hours post-treatment, respectively [37]. Even though, in the present study was not detected any variation of these immune parameters in the animals treated with the original sequence of C. gariepinus PACAP-38 (named PACAP 1) and that the immune systems of both species can present significant differences, for further studies should be consider including early sampling time points. Nonetheless, treatments such as PACAP 2 and 4 at 1 µg exhibited an increase of the HA. In this line, Monod et al. [37] proved the safety of the PACAP's treatment using a primary O. propinquus hemocytes cell culture model where no reduction of the cell viability or membrane stability disturbance was detected; thus, supporting the use of PACAP as a harmless drug treatment in crustacean.

PACAP's effects on the immune parameters measured in the hemolymph and the gene expression levels at the hemocytes, gill and hepatopancreas were dependent on the PACAP's variant used and the dose assessed. Briefly, PACAP 3 at 10 µg dose proved to be the most promising treatment overall. As was described in the Results section, PACAP 3 (10 µg) triggered a minor rise in the PO activity as well as a slight increase in the transcript levels of alpha-2-macroglobulin, NOS and HSP-70 at the gill. Nonetheless, other treatments also showed interesting results such as PACAP 2 (1 µg) and PACAP 4 (1 µg), which seemed to be the best treatments stimulating the HA activity, and PACAP 4 (10 µg) which upregulated lysozyme gene expression at the hepatopancreas.

Overall, the data presented in this paper indicate that PACAP modulate the expression of several immune-related genes and improve some hemolymph immune parameters in shrimp L. vannamei., The data presented here suggest that PACAP 3 at a dose of 10 µg dose is the best candidate for further studies testing PACAP's effects against live pathogen challenges, as well as its molecular and neuroendocrine mechanism of action. Altogether, these findings suggest that PACAP may be a candidate for use as an immunostimulant in aquaculture.
