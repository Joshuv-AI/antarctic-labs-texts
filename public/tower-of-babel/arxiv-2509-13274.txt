Spectral properties of random perturbations of non-normal Toeplitz matrices
Anirban Basak ∗

Abstract
. Spectral properties of Toeplitz operators and their finite truncations have long been central in operator theory. In the finite dimensional, non-normal setting, the spectrum is notoriously unstable under perturbations. Random perturbations provide a natural framework for studying this instability and identifying spectral features that emerge in typical noisy situations. This article surveys recent advances on the spectral behavior of (polynomially vanishing) random perturbations of Toeplitz matrices, focusing mostly on the limiting spectral distribution, the distribution of outliers, and localization of eigenvectors, and highlight the major techniques used to study these problems. We complement the survey with new results on the limiting spectral distribution of polynomially vanishing random perturbation of Toeplitz matrices with continuous symbols, on the limiting spectral distribution of finitely banded Toeplitz matrices under exponentially and super-exponentially vanishing random perturbations, and on the complete localization of outlier eigenvectors of randomly perturbed Jordan blocks.



1.
Introduction

Instability and insufficiency of the spectrum

Early usages of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions date back to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, primarily in the context of understanding physical problems involving vibrations and heat dissiptaions. Since then eigenvalues and eigenfunctions have been widely used to successfully study complex systems arising in varied areas, including, but not limited to, quantum mechanics, neuroscience and medical imaging, structural engineering, data science and machine learning, and even in the PageRank algorithm. A closer inspection reveals that such successes are largely obtained when operators under consideration are normal or are nearly normal.

Let us make a quick detour and recall the definition of a normal operator: A bounded linear operator
T
on a complex Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
is said to be normal if it commutes with its Hermitian adjoint
T^{*}
. That is,
TT^{*}=T^{*}T
. Otherwise, it is said to be non-normal.

Non-normal operators arise naturally in many real-world systems that are non-conservative, dissipative, or open, i.e. where energy is not preserved. For example, open quantum systems, shear flows in fluid dynamics, linearized models of weather systems and climate dynamics, and neural network models with asymmetric connectivity. Compared to normal operators, the eigenvalues of non-normal operators pose additional challenges: (i) eigenvalue analysis in many applications turns out to be insufficient and therefore can be misleading, and (ii) eigenvalues are sensitive to perturbations and often yielding unreliable results. Let us illustrate these two features through the following two simple examples.

Pseudospectrum

The phenomena noted in Examples
1.1
and
1.2
cannot be explained by analyzing the eigenvalues of relevant matrices. Notice that both these examples involve non-normal matrices. Instead of the spectrum, the correct object to look at for such matrices is the ‘pseudospectrum’. The earliest definition of the pseudospectrum can be traced back Varah’s Ph.D. thesis in
1967

[
54
]
. Seminal contributions by Lloyd N. Trefethen and collaborators in the nineties popularized the concept of the pseudospectrum. We refer the reader to
[
51
, Chapter 6]
for a brief historical account of the advancement of this area.

From a mathematical perspective a key difference between a normal and a non-normal operator is that the former admits a spectral theorem while the latter does not. In fact, from the spectral theorem it follows that for any bounded normal operator
T
,

(1.2)

\|(z-T)^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{{\rm dist}(z,\sigma(T))},\qquad\text{ for all }z\notin\sigma(T),

(see Section
1.1
for the notational conventions adopted in this article) where

\rho(T):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:(\lambda-T)^{-1}\text{ is a densely defined bounded linear operator on }\mathscr{H}\}

is the resolvent set of
T
, and
\sigma(T):=\mathbb{C}\backslash\rho(T)
is the spectrum of
T
. In contrast, for a non-normal operator the
lhs
of (
1.2
) can only be lower bounded by its
rhs
. Moreover, for a
z
of distance order one from
\sigma(T)
, for a non-normal operator
T
, one can have both
\|(z-T)^{-1}\|\asymp 1
and
\|(z-T)^{-1}\|\gg 1
, commonly known in the literature as ‘zone of spectral stability’ and ‘zone of spectral instability’, respectively. The notion of pseudospectrum is essentially an attempt to characterize and quantify these zones.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
M\in{\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, the set of all
N\times N
matrices with possibly complex valued entries. Let
\|\cdot\|
be any norm on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
and for any
B\in{\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, by a slight abuse of notation, we write
\|B\|
to denote the operator norm of
B
induced by the chosen norm on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. Fix any
\varepsilon>0
.
The
\varepsilon
-pseudospectrum of
M
is defined as

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\|(z-M)^{-1}\|>\varepsilon^{-1}\right\}.

In this article, we only discuss the notion of pseudospectrum for finite dimensional matrices. It has a natural extension for bounded linear operators on Banach spaces, see
[
51
, Chapter 4]
.

The following result provides two alternate characterizations of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)
.

Proposition 1.3

(
[
51
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
M
,
\varepsilon
, and the norm
\|\cdot\|
be as in Definition
1.1
. Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)

\displaystyle=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\exists\,E\in{\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\text{ with }\|E\|<\varepsilon\text{ such that }z\in\sigma(M+E)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\exists\,v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\text{ with }\|v\|=1\text{ such that }\|(z-M)v\|<\varepsilon\right\}.

It is trivial to note that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)\supset\sigma(M)
. Proposition
1.3
additionally shows that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)
consists of regions in the complex plane that can be reached by the eigenvalues of
M
perturbed by some matrix of operator norm at most
\varepsilon
. The following result captures a key difference between the pseudospectra of normal and non-normal matrices.

Proposition 1.4

(
[
51
, Theorems 2.2 and 2.3]
)
.

Let
M
,
\varepsilon
, and the norm
\|\cdot\|
be as in Definition
1.1
. The following properties of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)
hold.

(i)

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)\supset\sigma(M)+\mathbb{D}(0,\varepsilon)
.

(ii)

If
M
is normal and
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}
, the standard Euclidean norm then

(1.3)

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)=\sigma(M)+\mathbb{D}(0,\varepsilon),\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

Conversely, if
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}
and (
1.3
) holds then
M
must be a normal matrix.

(iii)

Let
M
be diagonalizable. That is, there exists a diagonal matrix
D
and a non-singular matrix
V
such that
M=VDV^{-1}
. For matrix
A
, let
\kappa(A)
be its condition number. That is,
\kappa(A)=\|A\|\cdot\|A^{-1}\|
, for
A
invertible and
\kappa(A)=\infty
otherwise. If
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}
then

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(M)\subset\sigma(M)+\mathbb{D}(0,\varepsilon\kappa(V)),\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0.

The key takeaway from Proposition
1.4
is that for a normal matrix its
\varepsilon
-pseudospectrum, when the chosen norm is the standard Euclidean norm, is simply the
\varepsilon
-fattening of its spectrum. In contrast, the
\varepsilon
-pseudospectrum, even for a small
\varepsilon
, of a non-normal matrix can be quite far from its spectrum.

Random perturbations of non-normal operators

One notes from Proposition
1.3
that pseudospectra describe the worst case behavior under a perturbation of a given size. To determine the pseudospectral level lines one essentially needs to choose a fine meshed grid and compute the norm of the resolvent at each of the grid points, making it computationally expensive and not scalable for large matrices.
On the other hand, random perturbations reflect the typical case behavior. Most physical or biological systems are not adversarially perturbed – they are simply noisy. Random perturbations model those more realistically. For example, see Figures
4
and
5
.
Further, in many applications eigenvalue clouds resulting from random perturbations tend to align along pseudospectral level lines revealing pseudospectral boundary statistically (e.g., compare Figures
5
and
6
).
Hence, even without determining the pseudospectral level lines, which involves heavy computations, one can identify salient characteristics of the underlying operator, such as pseudospectral bulges. These advantages of typical perturbation over a worst case perturbation analysis has led to a significant surge in the last twenty years in studying spectral features of random perturbations of non-normal matrices and operators.

While the focus of this article is on the spectral properties of random perturbations of large non-normal matrices, there is a substantial and growing body of work regarding the same for non-normal operators. We refer the reader to the recent survey article
[
56
]
for a brief overview of results in that direction.

Returning to the limiting spectral properties of random perturbation of large finite dimensional matrices perhaps one of the earliest works was done by Śniady in
[
44
]
, where the problem was addressed from a different perspective. To describe Śniday’s perspective we need a couple of definitions. The first one is a notion of convergence borrowed from the free probability theory.

Definition 1.2

(Convergence in
*
-moments)
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\phi)
be a non commutative
W^{*}
probability space. That is,
\mathscr{A}
is a von Neumann algebra (cf.
[
5
, Definition 5.2.21]
) and
\phi
is a normal faithful tracial state (cf.
[
5
, Definitions 5.2.9 and 5.2.26]
). Let
{\bm{x}}\in\mathscr{A}
and
\{A_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of matrices with
A_{N}\in{\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. The sequence of matrices
\{A_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to converge to
{\bm{x}}
in
*
-moments if

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\tr P(A_{N},A_{N}^{*})=\phi(P({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{*}))
, for every bivariate non-commutative polynomial
P
.

The next notion generalizes the notion of spectral measure for non-normal operators.

Definition 1.3

(Brown measure)
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\phi)
be as above. For any bounded self-adjoint element
{\bm{h}}\in\mathscr{A}
, we let
{\bm{\nu}}_{{\bm{h}}}
to be its spectral measure, i.e. the unique probability measure compactly supported on reals given by
\phi({\bm{h}}^{n})=\int t^{n}d{\bm{\nu}}_{{\bm{h}}}(t)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For any bounded operator
{\bm{x}}\in\mathscr{A}
we define its Brown measure
{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{x}}}
to be the probability measure on
\mathbb{C}
given by

{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{x}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta_{z}\int\log(t)\,{\bm{\nu}}_{|{\bm{x}}-z|}dt
, where the equality is in the sense of distribution,
|{\bm{h}_{0}}|:=\sqrt{{\bm{h}}_{0}^{*}{\bm{h}}_{0}}
for any operator
{\bm{h}}_{0}
, and
\Delta_{z}:=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial({\rm Re}\,z)^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial({\rm Im}\,z)^{2}}
, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, is the two dimensional Laplacian.

It can be checked that for
\mathscr{A}={\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
and
\phi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{N}\tr(\cdot)
, the Brown measure of any
A_{N}\in{\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is
L_{A_{N}}
, the empirical measure of its eigenvalues. Furthermore, if
\{A_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of normal matrices, then the convergence to a bounded normal operator
{\bm{x}}
in
*
-moments is sufficient for their respective Brown measures to converge. This fails as soon as
\{A_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are non-normal. For example, it can be checked that
\{J_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
{\bm{u}}
, the Haar unitary operator (i.e.
\phi({\bm{u}}^{n})=\phi(({\bm{u}}^{*})^{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
), in
*
-moments, while Brown measures of
J_{N}
and
{\bm{u}}
are Dirac measure at zero and the uniform measure on the unit circle on
\mathbb{C}
, respectively. Śniday asked whether one could randomly perturb
\{A_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
so that the empirical measure of the eigenvalues of randomly perturbed
A_{N}
converge to the Brown measure of the limiting operator
{\bm{x}}
(in the sense of
*
-moment convergence). It was answered affirmatively in
[
44
]
, and such a phenomena is termed in the literature as ‘random regularization of Brown measure’.

The proof in
[
44
]
uses a soft analysis (stochastic calculus and a diagonal subsequence extraction argument) and it does not shed any light on the strength of the random perturbation needed to achieve this regularization. The work
[
33
]
shows that if one is able to find a deterministic perturbation, say
\widetilde{E}_{N}
, so that
L_{\widetilde{A}_{N}}
, where
\widetilde{A}_{N}:=A_{N}+\widetilde{E}_{N}
, converges to
{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{x}}}
then any polynomially vanishing Gaussian perturbation of
A_{N}
will have the same limiting spectral distribution (
lsd
). Therefore, in light of the discussion in Example
1.5
, the
lsd
of any polynomially vanishing Gaussian perturbation of
J_{N}
is the law of
{\bm{u}}
. In a follow up work
[
58
]
the assumption on the distribution of the entries of the perturbation was relaxed.
On the other hand, relying on a completely different approach
[
21
]
provides quantitative bounds on the number of eigenvalues for Gaussian random perturbations of Jordan blocks in a given region in the complex plane.
Let us also mention in passing that there are examples of sequences of deterministic matrices such that no polynomially vanishing Gaussian perturbation regularizes the Brown measure, see
[
27
]
.

Following
[
21
,
27
,
33
,
58
]
a substantial body of research has emerged that explores the spectral properties of polynomially vanishing random perturbations of Toeplitz and related matrices. This article aims to provide a comprehensive account of the current state of the art, elucidating the central ideas that underpin this growing literature, while also augmenting the existing body of results with some extensions, including results on
lsd
under exponentially small random perturbation and complete localization of eigenvectors corresponding to outlier eigenvalues. Section
2
introduces necessary terminologies, followed by results on the limiting spectral distribution, limiting outlier distribution, and localization of eigenvectors, while the latter sections are devoted for
highlighting the key approaches and strategies in addressing these problems and for the proofs of the new results.

Notational conventions

We use the following standard asymptotic notation: For two sequences of nonnegative reals

and

we write

,

, and

to denote

a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
,

, and

are used. We further write

.

For a matrix

. We use

. For a vector

. Unless otherwise mentioned, for any vector

we further let
\|v\|
and

be its Euclidean and

norms, respectively.

For two sets

and

we write
S_{1}\pm S_{2}
to denote their Minkowski sum and difference. That is,
S_{1}\pm S_{2}:=\{s_{1}\pm s_{2};s_{1}\in S_{1},s_{2}\in S_{2}\}
. For any

and

we let

. For

and

we write

to denote the open disk in the complex plane of radius

centered at

.

2.
Random perturbations of non-normal Toeplitz matrices

We begin this section with definitions of infinite dimensional Toeplitz and Laurent operators and some useful spectral properties of these operators.

Definition 2.1
.

For any map
{\bm{a}}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
we write
\|{\bm{a}}\|_{\infty,\mathbb{S}^{1}}
to denote the standard essential supremum norm with respect to the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We let
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1}):=\{{\bm{a}}:\|{\bm{a}}\|_{\infty,\mathbb{S}^{1}}<\infty\}
. For any
{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
we define
L({\bm{a}}):\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\mapsto\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the Laurent operator with symbol
{\bm{a}}
, given by
\langle\mathfrak{e}_{i},L({\bm{a}})\mathfrak{e}_{j}\rangle=a_{i-j}
, where
\{\mathfrak{e}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
are canonical basis vectors in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Euclidean inner product on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
over the complex field, and
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are Fourier coefficients of
{\bm{a}}
.
That is,

a_{n}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\bm{a}}(e^{{\rm i}\theta})e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}d\theta.

For
{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
we define
T({\bm{a}}):\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\mapsto\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, the Toeplitz operator with symbol
{\bm{a}}
given by
\langle\mathfrak{e}_{i},T({\bm{a}})\mathfrak{e}_{j}\rangle=a_{i-j}
, where by a slight abuse of notation we now let
\{\mathfrak{e}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
to be the canonical basis vectors in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to be the Euclidean inner product on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
over the complex field.

It is well known that for
{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
the operators
L({\bm{a}})
and
T({\bm{a}})
are bounded linear operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

[

, Theorems 1.1 and 1.9]
). The following is a classical result regarding the spectra of infinite dimensional Laurent and Toeplitz operators.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
16
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.17]
)
.

Let
{\bm{a}}\in C(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. For any
w\in\mathbb{C}\backslash{\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
we write
{\rm wind}({\bm{a}},w)
to denote the winding number of the closed curve
{\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
around
w
. Then
\sigma(L({\bm{a}}))={\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and

\sigma(T({\bm{a}}))={\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\cup\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}\backslash{{\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}:{\rm wind}({\bm{a}},w)\neq 0\right\}.

Next we define finite dimensional Toeplitz matrices.

Definition 2.2
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
let
\Pi_{N}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{N}
be the canonical projection map. For
{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
we set
T_{N}({\bm{a}})
to be the restriction of
\Pi_{N}T({\bm{a}})\Pi_{N}
to the image of
\Pi_{N}
, the
N\times N
dimensional Toeplitz matrix with symbol
{\bm{a}}
. Therefore,

T_{N}({\bm{a}}):=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\cdots&\cdots&a_{-(N-1)}\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{-1}&\ddots&&\vdots\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a_{-1}&a_{-2}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{-1}\\
a_{N-1}&\cdots&\cdots&a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

For most of this article we will consider the case when the symbol
{\bm{a}}
is a Laurent polynomial. That is,

(2.1)

{\bm{a}}(\lambda)=\sum_{n=-N_{-}}^{N_{+}}a_{k}\lambda^{k},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},

some integers
N_{\pm}
such that
-N_{-}\leq N_{+}
. Without loss of generality we further assume
|N_{+}|+|N_{-}|>0
(otherwise the matrix is a constant multiple of identity and hence normal) and
\min\{|a_{N_{-}}|,|a_{N_{+}}|\}>0
. Notice that for such a symbol
{\bm{a}}(\cdot)
the corresponding Toeplitz matrix
T_{N}({\bm{a}})
is finitely banded. That is, there exists a finite band around the main diagonal such that outside that band the entries of
T_{N}({\bm{a}})
are all zero.

Next we move on to describe the limit of
L_{T_{N}({\bm{a}})}
for
{\bm{a}}
a Laurent polynomial. We need a few standard definitions and some notation. Let
\{\nu_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
. We say that
\nu_{N}
converges weakly to
\nu

\nu_{N}\Longrightarrow\nu

\int f(x)d\nu_{N}(x)\to\int f(x)d\nu(x)
, as
N\to\infty
, for every bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. If
\nu_{N}
’s are random, e.g. the empirical measure of eigenvalues of random matrices, and
\int f(x)d\nu_{N}(x)\to\int f(x)d\nu(x)
in probability, as
N\to\infty
, for any
f
as above, then
\nu_{N}
is said to converge weakly, in probability, to
\nu
, as
N\to\infty
.

We also need the following useful notion of
\log
-potential of a probability measure.

Definition 2.3

(Log-potential)
.

Let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
be the set of probability measure on
\mathbb{C}
that integrates the
\log|\cdot|
in a neighborhood of infinity. For

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}:\mathbb{C}\mapsto[-\infty,\infty)
as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(z):=\int\log|z-x|d\mu(x),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The usefulness of
\log
-potentials of probability measures is captured in the lemma below.

Lemma 2.2
.

(i) Let
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of probability measures in
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
such that

(2.2)

\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{N}}(z)\to\mathcal{L}(z),\quad\text{ as }N\to\infty,

for Lebesgue a.e. every
z\in\mathbb{C}
and some
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{C}\mapsto[-\infty,\infty)
, and

\sup_{N}\int|\lambda|^{2}d\mu_{N}(\lambda)<\infty.

Then, there exists some
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(z)=\mathcal{L}(z)
for Lebesgue a.e.
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
\mu=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta\mathcal{L}
in the sense of distribution, where
\Delta
denotes the two dimensional Laplacian, and
\mu_{N}\Longrightarrow\mu
, as
N\to\infty
.

(ii) Let
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random probability measures in
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
such that (
2.2
) holds in probability for Lebesgue a.e. every

and

(2.3)

\lim_{K\to\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\int|\lambda|^{2}d\mu_{N}(\lambda)\leq K\right)=1.

Then,
\mu_{N}\Longrightarrow\mu
, in probability, as
N\to\infty
, where
\mu
is as given in part (i).

Lemma

[

, and Vitali’s convergence theorem. We leave the details to the reader.

Lemma

, where

is an

, for some

be as in (

Proposition 2.3

(

Unlike the normal case, where Szegő’s first limit theorem (see

2.1.
Limiting spectral distribution under random perturbation

To study the limiting spectral distribution of Toeplitz matrices under additive random perturbations, as needed, we will impose several conditions on the noise matrix. Unless otherwise stated, it will be implicitly assumed that the entries of the noise matrix
E_{N}
are jointly independent with zero mean. We work with various additional assumptions on the entries of
E_{N}
which we state below.

Assumption 2.4

(Bound on expected Hilbert-Schmidt norm)
.

\mathbb{E}\|E_{N}\|_{{\rm HS}}^{2}\lesssim N^{2}
.

Assumption 2.5

(Bound on expected spectral norm)
.

\mathbb{E}\|E_{N}\|\lesssim N^{1/2}
.

Assumption 2.6

(Bound on the smallest singular value)
.

For any
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
, there exists a
\beta=\beta(\kappa)\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon_{N}(\kappa)\ll 1
so that for any fixed deterministic matrix
\mathcal{M}_{N}
with
\|\mathcal{M}_{N}\|\lesssim N^{\kappa}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(s_{\min}(E_{N}+\mathcal{M}_{N})\leq N^{-\beta}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{N}(\kappa).

Assumption 2.7

(Anti concentration bound)
.

For any complex valued random variable
X
and
\varepsilon>0
we define its Lévy concentration function as follows:

(2.5)

{\mathfrak{L}(X,\varepsilon):=\sup_{w\in C}\mathbb{P}(|X-w|\leq\varepsilon).}

There exists
\upeta>0
and some absolute constant
C<\infty
such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\max_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathfrak{L}(E_{N}(i,j),\varepsilon)\leq C\varepsilon^{\upeta},

for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, where
\{E_{N}(i,j)\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
are the entries of
E_{N}
.

Assumption 2.8

(Bounds on moments)
.

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\max_{i,j\in[N]}\mathbb{E}[|E_{N}(i,j)|^{h}]<\infty
for all
h\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assumption
2.4
holds as soon as the entries of
E_{N}
have a uniformly bounded second moment, while by
[
36
]
, Assumption
2.5
holds under a finite fourth moment assumption. By
[
46
, Theorem 2.1]
, Assumption
2.6
holds whenever the entries of
E_{N}
are i.i.d. (complex or real) with common
N
-independent distribution having a finite variance. On the other hand, it is straightforward to note that an existence of uniformly bounded densities of the entries of
E_{N}
yield Assumption
2.7
.

Our goal is to understand spectral properties of
T_{N}({\bm{a}})+\updelta_{N}E_{N}
. If the coupling constant
\updelta_{N}\gg N^{-1/2}
, as

(2.6)

\|T_{N}({\bm{a}})\|\leq\|T({\bm{a}})\|=\|{\bm{a}}\|_{\infty,\mathbb{S}^{1}},\qquad\text{ for any }{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

(see
[
16
, Eqn. (1.14)]
), using Weyl’s inequality for the singular values (e.g.
[
13
, Eqn. (1.6)]
), bounds on the smallest singular value (
[
13
, Lemma 4.12]
) and the intermediate singular values (
[
13
, Lemma 4.11]
), followed by an application of the standard replacement principle
[
47
, Theorem 2.1]
, one finds that for any symbol
{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, the
lsd
of
T_{N}({\bm{a}})+\updelta_{N}E_{N}
, after a proper normalization, is the celebrated circular law.

The rest of the ranges for
\updelta_{N}
can be broadly split into two sub cases: (i)
\updelta_{N}\ll N^{-1/2}
– microscopic perturbation (under Assumption
2.5
the perturbation
\updelta_{N}E_{N}
is of vanishing spectral norm), and (ii)
\updelta_{N}\asymp N^{-1/2}
– macroscopic perturbation.

First we consider the case of microscopic perturbation. Below is a result on the
lsd
of random perturbations of Toeplitz matrices, which is a culmination of several works.

Theorem 2.9

(Polynomially vanishing perturbation)
.

Let
{\bm{a}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
be such that
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|na_{n}|<\infty
. Assume that
E_{N}
satisfies Assumptions
2.4
and
2.6
. Let
\updelta_{N}\ll 1
be such that
\log(\updelta_{N}^{-1}N^{-1/2})\gg\log\log N
and
\updelta_{N}\geq N^{-L}
. Then
L_{T_{N}({\bm{a}})+\updelta_{N}E_{N}}\Longrightarrow{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{a}}({\bm{u}})}
, in probability, as
N\to\infty
.

Recall that
{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{a}}({{\bm{u}}})}
is the Brown measure of the operator
{\bm{a}}({\bm{u}})
, where
{\bm{u}}
is the Haar unitary operator. In other words,
{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{a}}({\bm{u}})}
is push forward of the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
by the symbol
{\bm{a}}
.

It should be mentioned that

Remark 2.13

In this section we discuss localization properties of eigenvectors corresponding to ‘bulk’ eigenvalues of randomly perturbed finitely banded Toeplitz matrices. Unlike the Wigner setting or the circular law limit setting, here we term an eigenvalue to be a bulk eigenvalue if it avoids neighborhoods of some ‘bad’ points, defined below, determined by the symbol.

Definition 2.5

{\bm{a}}

To ensure that there cannot be too many eigenvalues of

By

\|(v_{i}^{N})_{[\ell,\ell^{\prime}]}\|^{2}{\bf 1}(d(\lambda_{i}^{N})>0)+\|(v_{i}^{N})_{[N-\ell^{\prime},N-\ell]}\|^{2}{\bf 1}(d(\lambda_{i}^{N})<0)\geq c_{1}(\ell^{\prime}-\ell)\log N/N.

(iv)

There exist
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0
so that with
|{\rm supp}_{\mu_{1}}(v)|:=\min\{|I|:\|v_{I}\|>1-\mu_{1}\}
, for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left|\left\{i\in[N]:|{\rm supp}_{\mu_{1}}(v_{i}^{N}))|<\mu_{2}N/\log N\right\}\right|\leq\mu.

Let us explain the consequences of Theorem
2.21
. Its first part shows that indeed most of the eigenvalues are at a distance of order
\log N/N
from
{\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Theorem
2.21
(ii)-(iii) show that for all eigenvalues in the good region, the corresponding eigenvectors localize at scale
N/\log N
, and for most eigenvalues, this is the scale at which the eigenvectors spread out. The last part consolidates on the first three parts to claim that for most eigenvectors one needs at least an order of
N/\log N
many sites to accumulate a non-negligible
\ell^{2}
mass. Let us also add that the restriction
\gamma>1
is sharp both for the location of the eigenvalues with respect to the symbol curve, and for the localization of the eigenvectors. See Figure
7
for the contrasting behavior of eigenvectors in the two regimes:
\gamma>1
and
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
. Establishing the delocalization properties of eigenvectors for
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
is a work in progress
[
9
]
.

It was predicted in

that eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues that are at a distance of order greater than

To keep the exposition short we only consider the Jordan block setting below. The same strategy can be used to treat the finitely banded setting.

Theorem 2.22

(Complete localization of eigenvectors for outlier eigenvalues)
.

Let
{\bm{a}}(\zeta)=\zeta^{-1}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma>1/2
. Let
E_{N}
satisfy Assumption
2.5
. There exists some constant
\widehat{c}_{0}(\gamma)>0
with

and

so that the following event holds with probability

: For any

such that

,
\|v_{i}^{N}\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon^{1/2}/2
and
\|(v_{i}^{N})_{[\ell,N]}\|\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{\ell-1}
for all

Open problems

There are several interesting directions one can pursue. For example, it would be interesting to investigate finer details of the spectrum (such as local laws and eigenvalue spacing) in the setting of Theorem
2.10
. Further, there have been no work addressing the microscopic behavior of eigenvalues (e.g.
k
point correlation function). It would be of interest to see if such behaviors depend on
\gamma
or not, where
\updelta_{N}=N^{-\gamma}
.

Although we do not discuss it here, applying Fejér’s theorem and approximating
{\bm{a}}\in C(\mathbb{S}^{1})
by Laurent polynomials, one can extend Theorem
2.18
for continuous symbols. More challenging will be to find an analog of Theorem
2.19
beyond the finitely banded setup. It would be also interesting to understand the support of the
lsd
when the perturbation is exponentially vanishing, investigate whether there would be outliers, and if so to identify their limit distribution.

Regarding properties of eigenvectors much is yet to be understood beside those in
[
8
,
9
]
. We list a couple of unexplored directions. Based on analogies with pseudoeigenvectors (see
[
50
]
) it is expected that the eigenvectors of randomly perturbed finitely banded twisted Toeplitz matrices should localize, but unlike
[
8
]
the localization site would depend on the location of the corresponding eigenvalues. On the other hand, for random bidiagonal matrices a more intricate behavior of the eigenvectors are expected. Eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues that are in the so called ‘Hatano-Nelson wing’ are expected to be localized, while those corresponding to the eigenvalues in the ‘bubble’ are believed to be delocalized.

3.
Proofs of limiting laws of eigenvalues

First we consider the case of super-exponential perturbation. The result hinges on the following bounds on the determinant of a Toeplitz matrix. To state the result, we need to separate out a collection of bad
z
’s that are of zero Lebesgue measure.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\bm{a}}
be as in (
2.1
) and
\Gamma({\bm{a}})
be the set of
z
’s such that
\zeta_{j}(z)/\zeta_{k}(z)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
for some
j\neq k
. Recall
\mathcal{B}({\bm{a}})
from Definition
2.5
. Set
\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}}):=\mathcal{B}({\bm{a}})\cup\Gamma({\bm{a}})\cup\{a_{0}\}
.

It is well known that
\Gamma({\bm{a}})
is a union of finitely many analytic curves (cf.
[
15
, pp. 265-266]
) and thus has a zero Lebesgue measure. Therefore,
\mathcal{B}({\bm{a}})
being a finite set,
\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
has a zero Lebesgue measure.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\bm{a}}
be as in (
2.1
) and
z\notin\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
. Then, there exist some
\varepsilon_{0}(z)>0
such that

\varepsilon_{0}(z)\cdot|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{+}}|\zeta_{j}(z)|^{N}\leq|\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z}))|\leq\varepsilon_{0}(z)^{-1}\cdot|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{+}}|\zeta_{j}(z)|^{N}.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward consequence of Widom’s formula
[
15
, Theorem 2.8]
. Indeed, by Widom’s formula, for any
z\notin\mathcal{B}({\bm{a}})
we have

(3.1)

\det T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})=\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in\binom{[N_{+}+N_{-}]}{N_{+}}}C_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot a_{N_{+}}^{N}(-1)^{NN_{+}}\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}}\zeta_{\ell}(z)^{N},\qquad\text{ where }\quad C_{\mathcal{I}}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{I}\\
k\notin\mathcal{I}\end{subarray}}\frac{\zeta_{j}(z)}{\zeta_{j}(z)-\zeta_{k}(z)}.

Next note that for any
z\notin\mathcal{B}({\bm{a}})\cup\{a_{0}\}
(and thus none of the roots are zero) there exists some
\varepsilon(z)>0
such that

\varepsilon(z)\leq\inf_{\mathcal{I}}|C_{\mathcal{I}}|\leq\sup_{\mathcal{I}}|C_{\mathcal{I}}|\leq\varepsilon(z)^{-1}.

On the other hand,
z\notin\Gamma({\bm{a}})
implies

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[N_{+}]\\
k\notin[N_{+}]\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\zeta_{j}(z)}{\zeta_{k}(z)}\right|>1.

Plugging the last two estimates in (
3.1
) we obtain the desired upper and lower bounds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.12
.

Fix any
z\notin\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
. By the lower bound in Lemma
3.1
the matrix
T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})
is invertible for any such
z
. Therefore

(3.2)

\frac{1}{N}\log|\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})+\updelta_{N}E_{N})|-\frac{1}{N}\log|\det T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})|=\frac{1}{N}\log|\det({\rm Id}_{N}+\updelta_{N}T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{-1}E_{N})|\\
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|1+\updelta_{N}\lambda_{i}(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{-1}E_{N})|.

Since
E_{N}
satisfies Assumption
2.4
, by Markov’s inequality
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{N})\ll 1
, where
\Omega_{N}:=\{\|E_{N}\|\geq N^{1+c}\}
and
c>0
. Recall (
2.6
). Applying the lower bound from Lemma
3.1
yet again, the fact
N^{-1}\log(1/\updelta_{N})\gg 1
, and Weyl’s inequality we deduce that

\updelta_{N}\max_{i=1}^{N}|\lambda_{i}(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{-1}E_{N})|\leq\updelta_{N}\|T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{-1}\|\cdot\|E_{N}\|\leq\updelta_{N}N^{1+c}\cdot\|{\bm{a}}_{z}\|_{\infty,\mathbb{S}^{1}}^{N-1}\cdot C(z)^{N}\ll 1,

on the event
\Omega_{N}^{c}
, for some
C(z)<\infty
. Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{L_{T_{N}({\bm{a}})+\updelta_{N}E_{N}}}\to\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{a}}}
, in probability, as
N\to\infty
, for Lebesgue a.e.
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence, upon applying Lemma
2.2
(ii) the proof completes.
∎

3.1.
Deterministic equivalence

Given a deterministic matrix this approach attempts to approximate the
\log
-determinant of
M_{N}+\updelta_{N}E_{N}
by the empirical average of the
\log
-singular values of
M_{N}
that are not ‘too small’. Therefore, applying this deterministic equivalence with
M_{N}
replaced by
M_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N}
one can approximately express the
\log
-potential of
L_{M_{N}+\updelta_{N}E_{N}}
at a
z
by the empirical average of the
\log
-singular values of
M_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N}
truncated at some small value. The latter being deterministic maybe easier to handle. This approach has been successfully employed in
[
6
]
to study the
lsd
of twisted Toeplitz and random bidiagonal matrices. The result below is taken from
[
57
]
, which unlike
[
6
]
allows for generic random perturbations.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
57
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
M_{N}
be a deterministic
N\times N
matrix with
\|M_{N}\|\lesssim N^{\kappa_{1}}
and
E_{N}
be a random matrix satisfying Assumptions
2.5
and
2.6
. Fix
L>0
and
N^{-L}\lesssim\alpha\leq 1
. Assume that

\left|\{j:s_{j}(M_{N})\leq\alpha\}\right|=:M\ll\frac{N}{\log N}.

Then, for any
C<\infty
with
N^{-C}\leq\updelta_{N}\ll N^{-1/2}
, provided
\alpha\gg\updelta_{N}N^{1/2}
, there exists some constant
C^{\prime}<\infty
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\log|\det(M_{N}+\updelta_{N}E_{N})|-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j:s_{j}(M_{N})>\alpha}\log s_{j}(M_{N})\right|=o(1)\right)=1-o(1).

3.1.1.
Grushin problem and the proof of Theorem
3.2

The Grushin problem is a simple algebraic tool used often to study spectral problems where one replaces the operator in context by a suitable operator on an enlarged domain that is ‘well’ invertible. The origin of this construction goes back to
[
32
]
where it was used to study hypoelliptic operators. It has also been quite useful in bifurcation
theory, numerical analysis, and for treatments of spectral problems arising in
electromagnetism and quantum mechanics. See also the review paper
[
45
]
for various uses of the Grushin problem.

Let
A=A_{N}
be a complex
N\times N
-matrix with singular values
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\cdots\leq t_{N}
, corresponding right singular vectors
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, and left singular vectors
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
(note that these two sets of vectors are two orthonormal bases in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
). That is,

(3.3)

A^{*}f_{i}=t_{i}e_{i},\quad Ae_{i}=t_{i}f_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

Fix
\alpha>0
‘small’ and let
M>0
be the number of singular values
t_{i}\in[0,\alpha]
, i.e.

(3.4)

0\leq t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{M}\leq\alpha<t_{M+1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{N}.

Let
\delta_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq M
, denote an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{M}
.
Set

(3.5)

R_{+}:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{i}e_{i}^{*}\quad\text{ and }\quad R_{-}:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}f_{i}\delta_{i}^{*}.

Then the Grushin problem for the unperturbed operator is defined as follows:

(3.6)

{\mathcal{P}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&R_{-}\\
R_{+}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{M}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{M}.}

It is not difficult to check that the map
\mathcal{P}
is bijective. It further follows that

(3.7)

\mathcal{P}^{-1}=:\mathcal{E}=\begin{pmatrix}E&E_{+}\\
E_{-}&E_{-+}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where

(3.8)

\begin{split}&E:=\sum_{i=M+1}^{N}\frac{1}{t_{i}}e_{i}f_{i}^{*},\quad E_{+}:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}e_{i}\delta_{i}^{*},\quad E_{-}:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{i}f_{i}^{*},\quad\text{ and }\quad E_{-+}:=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}t_{j}\delta_{j}\delta_{j}^{*}.\end{split}

From (
3.4
) and (
3.8
) it follows that we have the following norm estimates:

(3.9)

\|E\|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha},\quad\|E_{\pm}\|=1,\quad\text{ and }\quad\|E_{-+}\|\leq\alpha.

Further, using that
\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
and
\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are orthonormal bases in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, we find

\mathcal{E}^{*}\mathcal{E}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum\limits_{j=M+1}^{N}\frac{1}{t_{j}}f_{j}f_{j}^{*}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}f_{j}f_{j}^{*}&-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}f_{j}\delta_{j}^{*}\\
-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}\delta_{j}f_{j}^{*}&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}(1+t_{j}^{2})\delta_{j}\delta_{j}^{*}\\
\end{pmatrix},

and subsequently using the formula for the determinant of a block matrix we derive

(3.10)

|\det\mathcal{P}|=\prod_{j=M+1}^{N}t_{j}.

Next, as we work with a perturbation of a given matrix we need to extend the notion of Grushin problem to such setting. Namely, we set

(3.11)

A^{\delta}:=A+\delta Q,\quad\delta\geq 0,

where
Q
is a complex
N\times N
-matrix (eventually, random).
Let
R_{\pm}
be
as in (
3.5
), and set

(3.12)

\mathcal{P}^{\delta}:=\begin{pmatrix}A^{\delta}&R_{-}\\
R_{+}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{M}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{M}.

Applying
\mathcal{E}
(see (
3.7
)) from
the right to (
3.12
) yields

\mathcal{P}^{\delta}\mathcal{E}={\rm Id}_{N+M}+\begin{pmatrix}\delta QE&\delta QE_{+}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Now under the additional assumption that

2\delta\|Q\|\alpha^{-1}\leq 1,

upon applying the bound (
3.9
) we find that
({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)
is invertible. It is then
straightforward to check that
\mathcal{P}^{\delta}
is invertible, with
inverse

(3.13)

(\mathcal{P}^{\delta})^{-1}=:\mathcal{E}^{\delta}=\begin{pmatrix}E^{\delta}&E^{\delta}_{+}\\
E^{\delta}_{-}&E^{\delta}_{-+}\end{pmatrix},

where

(3.14)

E^{\delta}=E({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)^{-1},E_{-}^{\delta}=E_{-}({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)^{-1},E^{\delta}_{-+}=E_{-+}-E_{-}({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)^{-1}\delta QE_{+},

and

(3.15)

E^{\delta}_{+}=E_{+}-E({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)^{-1}\delta QE_{+}.

The following norm estimates are then easy to obtain:

(3.16)

\begin{split}&\|E^{\delta}\|=\|E({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)^{-1}\|\leq 2\|E\|\leq 2\alpha^{-1},\\
&\|E_{-+}^{\delta}-E_{-+}\|=\|E_{-}({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE)^{-1}\delta QE_{+}\|\leq 2\|\delta Q\|\leq\alpha.\\
\end{split}

To conclude the proof we notice that
\frac{d}{d\delta}\log\det\mathcal{P}^{\delta}=\tr(\mathcal{E}^{\delta}\frac{d}{d\delta}\mathcal{P}^{\delta})
and
{\rm Re}\log\det\mathcal{P}^{\delta}=\log|\det\mathcal{P}^{\delta}|
. Therefore, noting that
\mathcal{P}^{0}=\mathcal{P}
and using (
3.15
),

\left|\log|\det\mathcal{P}^{\delta}|-\log|\det\mathcal{P}^{0}|\right|=\left|{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\delta}\tr(\mathcal{E}^{\tau}\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathcal{P}^{\tau})d\tau\right|=\left|{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\delta}\tr(E^{\tau}Q)d\tau\right|\leq 2\alpha^{-1}\delta N\|Q\|.

On the other hand, using the Schur complement formula we find

\log|\det A^{\delta}|=\log|\det\mathcal{P}^{\delta}|+\log|\det E_{-+}^{\delta}|.

The last two observations together with (
3.10
) yield

\left|\frac{1}{N}\log|\det(M_{N}+\updelta_{N}Q)|-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j:s_{j}(M_{N})>\alpha}\log s_{j}(M_{N})\right|\leq 2\alpha^{-1}\updelta_{N}\|Q\|+\frac{1}{N}\left|\log|\det E_{-+}^{\delta}|\right|=o(1),

on the event

\mathcal{T}_{N}:=\{\|Q\|\leq\mathfrak{c}_{N}(\alpha)\cdot N^{1/2}\}\cap\{\log|\det E_{-+}^{\delta}|\leq M|\log(2\alpha)|\}\cap\{\log|\det E_{-+}^{\delta}|\geq-{\beta M\log N}\},

where
\mathfrak{c}_{N}(\alpha):=\alpha N^{-1/2}/\updelta_{N}
, and
\beta=\beta(\kappa+\gamma)
of Assumption
2.6
. From
[
55
, Lemma 18]
we also have that
s_{\min}(A^{\delta})\leq s_{\min}(E_{-+}^{\delta})
. Therefore, from the assumptions on
\updelta_{N}
,
\alpha
, and
M
, Assumptions
2.5
and
2.6
, and upon recalling (
3.9
) and (
3.16
) it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{N})\ll 1
completing the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Although the formulation is different from (
3.6
),
[
42
,
43
]
rely on Grushin problems to derive
lsd
of randomly perturbed Toeplitz matrices. In particular,
[

3.2.
Determinant expansion

At a high level this approach relies on expanding the determinant of
T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})+\updelta_{N}E_{N}
, expressing it as a sum of homogeneous polynomials in the entries of
E_{N}
of degrees varying from zero to
N
, and identifying the dominant term in that expression. This approach has been useful in studying the limiting spectral distribution, locating the zone of no outliers, and the limiting outlier distribution in the complement of that zone, for polynomially randomly perturbed finitely banded Toeplitz matrices (see
[
7
,
10
]
). Beyond these settings this general idea of determinant expansion (together with a joint combinatorial central limit theorem) has been fruitful in deriving convergence of the spectral radius of various non-Hermitian random matrix ensembles, e.g.
[
14
,
19
,
20
,
34
,
41
]
.

In this section we explain the determinant expansion approach in somewhat detail and later apply them to derive
lsd
under an exponentially small vanishing perturbation. The starting point of this approach is the following simple identity: Let
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
be two
N\times N
matrices. Then

(3.17)

\det(A_{N}+B_{N})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X,Y\subset[N]\\
|X|=|Y|\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\sgn(\sigma_{X})\sgn(\sigma_{Y})}\det(A_{N}[{X}^{c};{Y}^{c}])\det(B_{N}[X;Y]),

where
{X}^{c}:=[N]\backslash X
,
{Y}^{c}:=[N]\backslash Y
and
\sigma_{Z}
for
Z\in\{X,Y\}
is the permutation on
[N]
which places all the elements of
Z
before all the elements of
{Z}^{c}
, but preserves the order of elements within the two sets. Its proof follows straight from the definition of the determinant,
see e.g.
[
39
]
. Applying (
3.17
) in our setting we notice that

\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})+\delta_{N}E_{N})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{\det}_{k}(z),

where
{\det}_{0}(z):=\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z}))=\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{t})
and

(3.18)

{\det}_{k}(z):=\updelta_{N}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X,Y\subset[N]\\
|X|=|Y|=k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\sgn(\sigma_{X})\sgn(\sigma_{Y})}\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{t}[{X}^{c};{Y}^{c}])\cdot\det(E_{N}^{t}[X;Y]),\qquad k\in[N].

Identifying the order of magnitude of
{\det}_{k}(\cdot)
requires the same for
\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})[{X}^{c};{Y}^{c}])
for all
X,Y\subset[N]
. For certain choices of
X
and
Y
this sub matrix is still a Toeplitz matrix, and hence one can either use Widom’s formula
[
15
, Theorem 2.8]
(in the case of no double roots of the associated symbol) or Trench’s formula
[
15
, Theorem 2.10]
(in the case of a double root). For arbitrary choices of
X
and
Y
one can only express the determinant as some Schur polynomial (see
[
1
]
) and latter is not easy to work with.

To tackle this issue the following two stage approach was adopted in
[
9
,
10
]
. First let us assume that
T_{N}({\bm{a}})
is a lower triangular matrix, i.e.
N_{-}=0
in (
2.1
). In this case, recalling that
\{\zeta_{j}(z)\}_{j=1}^{N_{+}}
are the roots of the polynomial
\zeta\mapsto{\bm{a}}_{z}(\zeta)
we note that

(3.19)

T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{t}={\bm{a}}_{z}(J_{N})=a_{N_{+}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{N_{+}}(J_{N}-\zeta_{j}(z)).

Hence, if one is able to determine minors of bidiagonal matrices then one can hope to use Cauchy-Binet theorem to determine the order of magnitude of minors of
T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})
. The following lemma provides the necessary estimate on minors of a bidiagonal matrix.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
27
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

Let
A_{N}=J_{N}+\mathfrak{z}\operatorname{Id}_{N}
,
\mathfrak{z}\in\mathbb{C}
,
X=\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{k}\}\subset[N]
, and
Y=\{y_{1}<y_{2}<\ldots<y_{k}\}\subset[N]
. Then, with

y_{k+1}=\infty
,

\det(A_{N}[{X}^{c};{Y}^{c}])=\mathfrak{z}^{y_{1}-1}\cdot\left(\prod_{i=2}^{k}\mathfrak{z}^{y_{i}-x_{i-1}-1}\right)\cdot\mathfrak{z}^{N-x_{k}}{\bf 1}\left\{y_{i}\leq x_{i}<y_{i+1},i\in[k]\right\}.

When
T_{N}({\bm{a}})
is non-triangular the representation (
3.19
) no longer holds. Nevertheless, one notes that in such cases
T_{N}({\bm{a}})
can still be viewed as a sub matrix of a triangular Toeplitz matrix and therefore one can obtain an analog of (
3.19
). To explain the idea more clearly let us introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.2

(Toeplitz with a shifted symbol)
.

Let
{\bm{a}}
be as in (
2.1
).
For
n>N_{+}+N_{-}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
and

\bar{N}_{+},\bar{N}_{-}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\bar{N}_{+}+\bar{N}_{-}=N_{+}+N_{-}:=\widetilde{N}
,
let
T_{n}({\bm{a}},z;\bar{N}_{+})
denote the
n\times n
Toeplitz matrix with symbol
\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{z,\bar{N}_{+}}
, where
\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{z,\bar{N}_{+}}(\lambda):=\sum_{j=-N_{-}}^{N_{+}}a^{\prime}_{j}\lambda^{-(j+\bar{N}_{+}-N_{+})},

and
a^{\prime}_{j}:=a_{j}-z\delta_{j,0}
, for
j=-N_{-},-N_{-}+1,\ldots,N_{+}
.

From Definition
3.2

it follows that

(3.20)

{T_{\widehat{N}_{-}}({\bm{a}},z;\widetilde{N})}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{-N_{-}}&\cdots&a_{0}-z&\cdots&a_{N_{+}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&a_{-N_{-}}&&a_{0}-z&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&&\ddots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&&\ddots&&a_{N_{+}}\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\ddots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&\ddots&\ddots&&a_{0}-z\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&a_{-N_{-}}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\widehat{N}_{-}:=N+N_{-}.

Therefore,

T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{t}=T_{N}({\bm{a}},z;N_{+})=T_{\widehat{N}_{-}}({\bm{a}},z;\widetilde{N})[[N];[\widehat{N}_{-}]\backslash[N_{-}]].

Recalling (
2.4
), letting
\eta_{j}(z):=-\zeta_{j}(z)
for
j\in[\widetilde{N}]
, and setting
\widehat{\bm{a}}_{z}(\zeta)=\zeta^{N_{-}}{\bm{a}}_{z}(\zeta)
we note that

{T_{\widehat{N}_{-}}({\bm{a}},z;\widetilde{N})}=\widehat{\bm{a}}_{z}(J_{\widehat{N}_{-}})=a_{N_{+}}{\prod_{j=1}^{\widetilde{N}}}(J_{\widehat{N}_{-}}+\eta_{j}(z){\rm Id}_{\widehat{N}_{-}}).

Using Cauchy-Binet theorem we obtain from (
3.18
)

(3.21)

\displaystyle{\det}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\updelta_{N}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X,Y\subset[N]\\
|X|=|Y|=k\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=2}^{\widetilde{N}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{i}\subset[\widehat{N}_{-}]\\
|X_{i}|=k+N_{-}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\sgn(\sigma_{X})\sgn(\sigma_{Y})}a_{N_{+}}^{N-k}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{d}\det\left((J_{\widehat{N}_{-}}+\eta_{i}(z)\operatorname{Id}_{\widehat{N}_{-}})[\check{X}_{i};\check{X}_{i+1}]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\det(E_{N}^{t}[X;Y]),

where

(3.22)

X_{1}:=X_{1}(X):=X\cup[\widehat{N}_{-}]\backslash[N],\qquad X_{\widetilde{N}+1}:=X_{\widetilde{N}+1}(Y):=(Y+N_{-})\cup[N_{-}],

and
\check{Z}:=[\widehat{N}_{-}]\backslash Z
for any set
Z\subset[\widehat{N}_{-}]
. Note the notational difference between
\check{Z}
and
Z^{c}
. The former is used to write the complement of
Z
when viewed as a subset of
[\widehat{N}_{-}]
, where for the latter
Z
is viewed as a subset of
[N]
.

Lemma
3.4
allows one to evaluate each term in the summand in the
rhs
of (
3.21
). However, to obtain any meaningful and usable bound we need some further preprocessing. The idea is to express the sums in (
3.21
) over
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\widetilde{N}+1}
as an iterated sums, where the inner sum will be over the choices of
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\widetilde{N}+1}
such that the product of the determinants of the bidiagonal matrices is constant and the outer sum will be over all possible values of that product of the determinants. This requires a further set of notation.

We let

(3.23)

X_{i}:=\left\{x_{i,1}<x_{i,2}<\cdots<x_{i,k+N_{-}}\right\},\quad i\in[\widetilde{N}+1];\quad\quad\mathcal{X}_{k}:=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{\widetilde{N}+1}).

Next for any
{\bm{\ell}}:=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{\widetilde{N}})
with
0\leq\ell_{i}\leq\widehat{N}_{-}
for

i\in[\widetilde{N}]
, and
k\in[N]
, we define

L_{{\bm{\ell}},k}:=\{\mathcal{X}_{k}:\,1\leq x_{i+1,1}\leq x_{i,1}<x_{i+1,2}\leq x_{i,2}<\cdots<x_{i+1,k+N_{-}}\leq x_{i,k+N_{-}}\leq\widehat{N}_{-},\\
\text{ and }\,x_{i+1,1}+\sum_{j=2}^{k+N_{-}}(x_{i+1,j}-x_{i,j-1})+(\widehat{N}_{-}-x_{i,k+d_{2}})=\ell_{i}+k+N_{-},\text{ for all }i\in[\widetilde{N}]\}.

Note that Lemma
3.4
implies that the summand in (
3.21
) is non-zero only when
\mathcal{X}_{k}\in L_{{\bm{\ell}},k}
for some
{\bm{\ell}}
and in that case

(3.24)

a_{N_{+}}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{\widetilde{N}}\det\left((J_{\widehat{N}_{-}}+\eta_{i}(z)\operatorname{Id}_{\widehat{N}_{-}})[\check{X}_{i};\check{X}_{i+1}]\right)=a_{N_{+}}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{\widetilde{N}}\lambda_{i}(z)^{\ell_{i}}=:{\bm{\eta}}(z,{\bm{\ell}}).

Furthermore, the restriction (
3.22
) and the fact that the outer sum in (
3.21
) is over
X,Y\subset[N]
implies that the summand in (
3.21
) vanishes unless
\mathcal{X}_{k}\in\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}
, where

(3.25)

\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}:=\{\mathcal{X}_{k}\in L_{{\bm{\ell}},k}:x_{1,k+j}=N+j;\ j\in[N_{-}]\quad\text{ and }\quad x_{\widetilde{N}+1,j}=j;\ j\in[N_{-}]\}.

Thus, setting

(3.26)

\mathbb{X}:=\mathbb{X}(X_{1}):=X_{1}\cap[N]\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{Y}:=\mathbb{Y}(X_{\widetilde{N}+1}):=(X_{\widetilde{N}+1}-N_{-})\cap[N],

and

(3.27)

Q_{{\bm{\ell}},k}:=\sum_{\mathcal{X}_{k}\in\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}}(-1)^{\sgn(\sigma_{\mathbb{X}})\sgn(\sigma_{\mathbb{Y}})}\det(E_{N}^{t}[\mathbb{X};\mathbb{Y}]),

we deduce

(3.28)

{\det}_{k}(z)=\updelta_{N}^{k}a_{N_{+}}^{-k}\sum_{{\bm{\ell}}:\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing}{\bm{\eta}}(z,{\bm{\ell}})\cdot Q_{{\bm{\ell}},k}.

Having obtained the above usable decomposition of
{\det}_{k}(\cdot)
the strategy would be to identify an index
k(z)\in[N]
such that
{\det}_{k(z)}(z)
is the dominant term in the expansion of
\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})+\updelta_{N}E_{N})
, i.e.
{\det}_{k}(z)\ll{\det}_{k(z)}(z)
for
k\neq k(z)
. This strategy will be implemented in two steps. In the first step, using a second moment bound one identifies an upper bound on the negligible terms. This bound needs to be tight upto some small polynomial in
N
factors in the case of sub exponential decay of
\updelta_{N}
, while in the case of exponential decay although one has more room, as we will see below for an
O(1)
many choices of
k
the correct exponential rate of decay of magnitude of
{\det}_{k}
compared to that of the dominant term needs to be identified.
In the second step one uses the anti-concentration property of the entries of
E_{N}
, as stated in Assumption
2.7
, to obtain a lower bound on the dominant term.

Step I (Upper bound):

For ease in writing we will assume that the entries
E_{N}
have unit variances. The reader may note that the same proof works under the uniformly bounded variance assumption. As the entries of
E_{N}
are independent with zero mean it is straightforward to see that

\mathbb{E}\left[\det(E_{N}[\mathbb{X}_{*};\mathbb{Y}_{*}])\cdot\overline{\det(E_{N}[\mathbb{X}^{\prime};\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}])}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k!&\mbox{if }\mathbb{X}_{*}=\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\text{ and }\mathbb{Y}_{*}=\mathbb{Y}^{\prime},\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

for any collection of subsets
\mathbb{X}_{*},\mathbb{Y}_{*},\mathbb{X}^{\prime},\mathbb{Y}^{\prime}\subset[N]
, each of cardinality
k
. Hence, we deduce that

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Q_{{\bm{\ell}},k}|^{2}]=\Var(Q_{{\bm{\ell}},k})

\displaystyle\,=k!\cdot\mathfrak{N}_{{\bm{\ell}},k},

where

(3.30)

\mathfrak{N}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}:=\left|\left\{\mathcal{X}_{k}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{\widetilde{N}+1}),\mathcal{X}_{k}^{\prime}=(X_{1}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{\widetilde{N}+1}^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}:X_{1}=X_{1}^{\prime},X_{\widetilde{N}+1}=X_{\widetilde{N}+1}^{\prime}\right\}\right|.

Using the above notation and applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we find

(3.31)

\mathbb{E}|{\det}_{k}(z)|^{2}\leq\left|\frac{\updelta_{N}}{a_{N_{+}}}\right|^{2k}\left(\sum_{{\bm{\ell}}:\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing}|{\bm{\eta}}(z,{\bm{\ell}})|\cdot(\mathbb{E}|Q_{{\bm{\ell}},k}|^{2})^{1/2}\right)^{2}\leq\left|\frac{\updelta_{N}}{a_{N_{+}}}\right|^{2k}\left(\sum_{{\bm{\ell}}:\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing}|{\bm{\eta}}(z,{\bm{\ell}})|\cdot(k!\mathfrak{N}_{{\bm{\ell}},k})^{1/2}\right)^{2}.

To bound the
rhs
of (
3.31
) we again need two different approaches. For
k\geq|d|
, where
d=d(z)={\rm wind}({\bm{a}},z)
, we use the following bound on
\mathfrak{N}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}
. Recall that for
z\in\mathbb{C}
the notation
m_{+}=m_{+}(z)
denotes the number of roots of the polynomial
\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{N_{-}}{\bm{a}}_{z}(\zeta)
that are outside
\overline{\mathbb{D}(0,1)}
.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
10
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{Z}
. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|d|\leq k\leq N
, and
{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{d})
such that
0\leq\ell_{i}\leq\widehat{N}_{-}
for all
i\in[\widetilde{N}]
.
Then

(3.32)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}

\displaystyle\,\leq\binom{\widehat{N}_{-}}{k-|d|}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m_{+}}\binom{\hat{\ell}_{i}-1}{k+N_{-}-1}^{2}\cdot\prod_{i=m_{+}+1}^{\widetilde{N}}\binom{\hat{\ell}_{i}+k+N_{-}}{k+N_{-}}^{2},

where

(3.33)

\hat{\ell}_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ell_{i}&\mbox{ if }i>m_{+},\\
\widehat{N}_{-}-\ell_{i}&\mbox{ if }i\leq m_{+}.\end{array}\right.

To treat the case
k<|d|
we will show that the summand in the
rhs
of (
3.31
) is either zero or exponentially small compared the dominant term, which in turn will yield that
{\det}_{k}(z)
, for such a
k
, is exponentially small compared to the dominant term.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
for some
d
such that
|d|\geq 2
. Fix any
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k<|d|
. Fix
{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{\widetilde{N}})
with
0\leq\ell_{i}\leq\widehat{N}_{-}
for
i\in[\widetilde{N}]
.

(i)

Let
d>0
. Then
\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing
implies
\sum_{i=m_{+}+1}^{\widetilde{N}}\ell_{i}\geq(d-k)\widehat{N}_{-}-\widetilde{N}^{2}
.

(ii)

Let
d<0
. Then
\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing
implies
\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}\ell_{i}\leq(m_{+}+k+d)\widehat{N}_{-}+\widetilde{N}^{2}
.

The proof of Lemma
3.6
is a refinement of that of
[
10
, Lemma 2.9]
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Consider first
the case
d=N_{+}-m_{+}>0
. For
k<d
,
s\in[d-k]
, and any
\mathcal{X}_{k}\in\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\subset L_{{\bm{\ell}},k}
the indices
\{x_{m_{+}+s+i+1,j},i,j\in[k+N_{-}]\}
are well defined.
On the other hand observe that

(3.34)

x_{m_{+}+s+1,1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k+N_{-}-1}(x_{m_{+}+s+j+1,j+1}-x_{m_{+}+s+j,j})+(\widehat{N}_{-}-x_{m_{+}+s+k+N_{-},k+N_{-}})=\widehat{N}_{-}.

Adding both sides of (
3.34
) for
s=1,2,\ldots,d-k
, we see that the
rhs
is
(d-k)\widehat{N}_{-}
, while recalling the definition of
L_{{\bm{\ell}},k}
, noting that each of the summand in (
3.34
) is nonnegative, and rearranging the sum we see that the sum of the
lhs
is bounded above by
\sum_{i=m_{+}+1}^{\widetilde{N}}(\ell_{i}+k+N_{-})
. The desired bound for
d>0
is now immediate.

Next we consider the case
d<0
. Recalling (
3.33
) and using the observation

x_{i+1,1}+\sum_{j=2}^{k+N_{-}}(x_{i+1,j}-x_{i,j-1})+\sum_{j=1}^{k+N_{-}}(x_{i,j}-x_{i+1,j})+(\widehat{N}_{-}-x_{i,k+N_{-}})=\widehat{N},

we see that

(3.35)

\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}\widehat{\ell}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{k+N_{-}}(x_{i,j}-x_{i+1,j}+1)\geq\sum_{j=k+1}^{|d|}(x_{1,j}-x_{m_{+}+1,j}+1).

Now, for any
\mathcal{X}_{k}\subset\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}
we have
x_{1,k+j}=N+j
for
j\in[N_{-}]
. Further
x_{\widetilde{N}+1,\ell}=\ell
for
\ell\in[N_{-}]
. Since
x_{i+1,\ell}\leq x_{i,\ell}<x_{i+1,\ell+1}
for any
\ell\in[k+N_{-}-1]
, and
\{x_{i,\ell}\}
are integers, using induction, we obtain that
x_{m_{+}+1,\ell}=\ell
for any
\ell\in[|d|]
. The last two observations together implies that
x_{1,j}-x_{m_{+}+1,j}=N
for
j=k+1,\ldots,|d|
. Plugging this estimate in the
rhs
of (
3.35
) the desired bound is now immediate.
∎

we have the following upper bound.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
{\bm{a}}
be as in (
2.1
) and
\updelta_{N}=r^{N}
for some
r\in(0,1)
. Assume that the entries of
E_{N}
are centered and have unit variance.

(i)

Let
z\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then, there exists some constant
\bar{C}(z)<\infty
so that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|d|\leq k\leq N
, any
\varepsilon>0
, and all large
N
,

\mathbb{E}|{\det}_{k}(z)|^{2}\leq\left(r^{2|d|N}|a_{N_{+}}|^{2N}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{+}}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{2N}\right)\cdot\bar{C}(z)\cdot(r(1+\varepsilon))^{2(k-|d|)N}.

(ii)

Recall the definition of
\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
from Definition
3.1
. Let
z\in\mathcal{S}_{d}\backslash\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
for some
d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
d\neq 0
. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k<|d|
and all large

,

(3.36)

\mathbb{E}|{\det}_{k}(z)|^{2}\leq\left(r^{2kN}|a_{N_{+}}|^{2N}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{+}-k{\bf 1}(d>0)+k{\bf 1}(d<0)}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{2N}\right)\cdot\bar{C}^{\prime}(z)\cdot N^{2k+4\widetilde{N}}.

Notice the difference in the upper bound in (
3.36
) for
d>0
and
d<0
.

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

As
z\notin{\bm{a}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
there exists some
\varepsilon_{0}(z)>0
such that

|\zeta_{m_{+}}(z)|^{-1}\wedge|\zeta_{m_{+}+1}(z)|\leq 1-\varepsilon_{0}(z).

Recalling (
3.24
), and applying the combinatorial identity

\sum_{s\geq m}\binom{s-1}{m-1}\uplambda^{s-m}=(1-\uplambda)^{-m},\qquad\text{ for }\uplambda\in(0,1)\text{ and }m\in\mathbb{N},

together with (
3.31
) and Lemma
3.5
we obtain

(3.37)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}|{\det}_{k}(z)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m_{+}}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{-\widehat{N}_{-}}\cdot|a_{N_{+}}|^{-N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,r^{kN}|a_{N_{+}}|^{-k}\widehat{N}^{\frac{k-|d|}{2}}_{-}k^{\frac{|d|}{2}}\sum_{{\bm{\ell}}:\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{+}}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{-\widehat{\ell}_{i}}\prod_{j=m_{+}+1}^{\widetilde{N}}|\zeta_{j}(z)|^{\widehat{\ell}_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m_{+}}\binom{\hat{\ell}_{i}-1}{k+N_{-}-1}\prod_{i=m_{+}+1}^{\widetilde{N}}\binom{\hat{\ell}_{i}+k+N_{-}}{k+N_{-}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,r^{kN}N^{\frac{k-|d|}{2}}k^{\frac{|d|}{2}}C(z)^{k},

for some
C(z)<\infty
. This completes the proof of part (i).

Turning to prove part (ii) we first consider the case
d>0
. Recalling (
3.24
), as
\ell_{i}\leq\widehat{N}_{-}
and
|\zeta_{m_{+}+1}(z)|<1
, applying Lemma
3.6
(i) we note that for any
{\bm{\ell}}
such that
\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing
, as
z\notin\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
, we have

(3.38)

|{\bm{\eta}}(z,{\bm{\ell}})|\leq|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{+}}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{\widehat{N}_{-}}\cdot\prod_{j=m_{+}+1}^{\widetilde{N}}|\zeta_{j}(z)|^{\ell_{j}}\leq C^{\prime}(z)\cdot|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{+}-k}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{\widehat{N}_{-}},

for some constant
C^{\prime}(z)<\infty
. The desired bound for
d>0
now follows upon using the trivial bound
\mathfrak{N}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\leq N^{2k}
and noting that the number of possible choices for
\ell_{i}
is at most
\widehat{N}_{-}+1
for each
i\in[\widetilde{N}]
, and then plugging them in (
3.31
). For
d<0
applying Lemma
3.6
(ii) for any
{\bm{\ell}}
such that
\mathfrak{L}_{{\bm{\ell}},k}\neq\varnothing
, and arguing analogously as in (
3.38
) we have

|{\bm{\eta}}(z,{\bm{\ell}})|\leq|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{+}}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{\ell_{i}}\leq C^{\prime}(z)\cdot|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{+}+k}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{\widehat{N}_{-}}.

Now proceeding similarly as in the proof for the case
d>0
, the proof for
d<0
also completes.
∎

Step II (Lower bound):
The lower bound relies on the following anti-concentration bound.

Lemma 3.8

(
[
10
, Proposition 4.1]
)
.

Fix
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a sequence of
independent (possibly complex valued) random variables
such that

\max_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{L}(U_{i},\varepsilon)\leq C\varepsilon^{\upeta},

where
C,\varepsilon
, and
\upeta
are as in Assumption
2.7
(recall (
2.5
)). Let
P_{k}(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n})
be a
homogenous polynomial of degree
k
such that the degree of
each variable is at most one. That is,

P_{k}(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n}):=\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in\binom{[n]}{k}}a(\mathcal{I})\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}U_{i},

for some collection of complex valued coefficients
\{a(\mathcal{I});\,\mathcal{I}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\}
. Assume that there exists an

\mathcal{I}_{0}
such that
|a(\mathcal{I}_{0})|\geq c_{\star}
for some absolute constant
c_{\star}>0
. Then for
\varepsilon\in(0,{e^{-1}}]
sufficiently small and some large
\bar{C}<\infty
we have

(3.39)

\mathbb{P}\left(|P_{k}(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n})|\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq\bar{C}(c_{\star}\wedge 1)^{-\upeta}\varepsilon^{\upeta}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)^{k-1}.

At a high level, the proof of Lemma
3.8
relies on the observation that if
U
has a good anti-concentration property, characterized by an upper bound on its Lévy concentration function, then one can derive anti-concentration property of the random variable
AU+V
, where
\{A,V\}
is jointly independent of
U
, on the event that
A
is bounded below. Since the degree of each variable is at most one in
P_{k}(\cdot)
and at least one coefficient is bounded below the idea can propagated to obtain the bound (
3.39
).

Building on Lemmas
3.1
and
3.8
we derive the following lower bound on
{\det}_{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k<|d|
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{Z}
and
z\in\mathcal{S}_{d}\backslash\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
. Then for any
\delta>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that
k\leq d
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|{\det}_{k}(z)|\leq N^{-\delta}\cdot r^{kN}|a_{N_{+}}|^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{+}-k{\bf 1}(d>0)+k{\bf 1}(d<0)}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{N}\right)\ll 1.

Proof.

The case
d=0
is immediate from Lemma
3.1
. Consider the case
d>0
. Set
\mathbb{X}_{k}:=[N]\backslash[k]
and
\mathbb{Y}_{k}:=[k]
. Let
{\bm{a}}_{k,z}(\zeta):=\zeta^{-k}{\bm{a}}_{z}(\zeta)
. Then
T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})[\mathbb{Y}^{c}_{k};\mathbb{X}^{c}_{k}]=T_{N-k}({\bm{a}}_{k,z})
. As
z\notin\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
, applying Lemma
3.1
for the symbol
{\bm{a}}_{k,z}(\zeta)
we get

|\det(T_{N}({\bm{a}}_{z})^{t}[\mathbb{X}^{c}_{k};\mathbb{Y}^{c}_{k}])|\gtrsim|a_{N_{+}}|^{N-k}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{+}-k}|\zeta_{i}(z)|^{N-k}.

The desired bound now follows upon recalling (
3.18
) and applying Lemma
3.8
.

For
d<0
setting
\mathbb{Y}_{k}:=[N]\backslash[k]
and
\mathbb{X}_{k}:=[k]
, and repeating the same argument as above the proof completes.
∎

Combining Lemmas
3.7
and

we now complete the proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

As the entries of
E_{N}
have uniformly bounded second moments, by (
2.6
) and Weyl’s inequality, it follows that (
2.3
) holds for
\mu_{N}=L_{T_{N}({\bm{a}})+r^{N}E_{N}}
. Hence, by Lemma
2.2
(ii) it suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}_{L_{T_{N}({\bm{a}})+r^{N}E_{N}}}(z)\to\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{a}},r}(z)
, in probability, as
N\to\infty
, for Lebesgue a.e.
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

To this end, fix any
z\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\backslash\widehat{\Gamma}({\bm{a}})
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
such that
r_{\varepsilon}:=r(1+\varepsilon)<1
. By Lemma
3.7
(i) it follows that

(3.40)

\mathbb{P}\left(|{\det}_{k}(z)|\geq r_{\varepsilon}^{kN/3}\cdot\exp(N\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{a}},r}(z))\right)\leq r_{\varepsilon}^{kN},\quad\text{ for }k\in[N]\text{ and all large }N.

Therefore, by a union bound
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{N}^{c})\leq r_{\varepsilon}^{N/2}
, for all large
N
, where

\Omega_{N}:=\left\{{\sum_{k=1}^{N}|{\det}_{k}(z)|}\leq r_{\varepsilon}^{N/6}\cdot\exp(N\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{a}},r}(z))\right\}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
3.1
,
|{\det}_{0}(z)|\gtrsim\exp(N\mathcal{L}_{{\bm{a}},r}(z))
. Therefore, as
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{N}^{c})\ll 1
, using both the lower and upper bounds from Lemma
3.1
we deduce that

(3.41)

\widetilde{\varepsilon}>0
, where in the last step we set
\alpha_{0}=3\theta+\widetilde{\varepsilon}
.
By a similar argument we also find

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\|E(z)({\rm Id}_{N}+\delta QE(z))^{-1}\delta QE_{+}(z)\|\geq N^{-\widetilde{\varepsilon}}\right)\leq 2N^{-\widetilde{\varepsilon}}.

The last two probability bounds together with (
4.3
), upon setting
\theta=\widetilde{\varepsilon}
, as
\{e_{i}(z)\}_{i=1}^{N}
is an orthonormal basis, yield that on an event with probability at least
1-o(1)
for any eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}(0,1-\varepsilon)
of
J_{N}+N^{-\gamma}E_{N}
, there exists some
\theta_{\lambda}\in(0,2\pi]
such that

\|v_{\lambda}-e^{{\rm i}\theta_{\lambda}}e_{1}(z_{\lambda})\|_{\infty}\leq\|v_{\lambda}-e^{{\rm i}\theta_{\lambda}}e_{1}(z_{\lambda})\|=O(N^{-\widetilde{\varepsilon}}),

where
z_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
satisfying
|\lambda-z_{\lambda}|\leq N^{-\theta}
. To complete the proof it suffices to show that

(4.10)

\|e_{1}(z)-e^{{\rm i}\widehat{\theta}_{z}}\mathfrak{z}_{z}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e_{1}(z)-e^{{\rm i}\widehat{\theta}_{z}}\mathfrak{z}_{z}\|\leq 2\varepsilon^{-1/2}(1-\varepsilon)^{N/2}\qquad\text{ for all }z\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta},

where
\widehat{\mathfrak{z}}_{z}:=(1,z,z^{2},\ldots,z^{N-1})^{t}
,
\mathfrak{z}_{z}:=\widehat{\mathfrak{z}}_{z}/\|\widehat{\mathfrak{z}}_{z}\|
, and some phase
\widehat{\theta}_{z}\in(0,2\pi]
. Turning to prove (
4.10
), recalling (
3.3
), as
\|\mathfrak{z}_{z}\|=1
, and
\{e_{i}(z)\}_{i\in[N]}
forms an orthonormal basis we deduce that

(4.11)

J_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}(z)f_{i}(z)e_{i}(z)^{*},\quad\mathfrak{z}_{z}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}(z)e_{i}(z),\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\beta_{i}(z)|^{2}=1,

for some coefficients
\{\beta_{i}(z)\}_{i\in[N]}
. Observe that
\|(J_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N})\mathfrak{z}_{z}\|\leq|z|^{N}/\|\widehat{\mathfrak{z}}_{z}\|\leq|z|^{N}
(this yields the desired bound on
s_{\min}
of (
4.7
)). Hence, from (
4.11
), as
\{f_{i}(z)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are orthonormal, using the bound
t_{2}(z)\geq\varepsilon
(see (
4.7
)) we further derive that
\sum_{i=2}^{N}|\beta_{i}(z)|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)^{N}
. This immediately yields (
4.10
).

It remains to prove the right most inequality in (
4.7
). To this end, denote
C_{N}
to be the circulant matrix whose first row is same as that of
J_{N}
. Set
\mathcal{R}_{n}\subset{\rm Mat}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be the set of matrices of rank at most
n
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By
[
15
, Proposition 9.5]
, and as
C_{N}
is a rank one perturbation of
J_{N}
, we find

s_{2}(J_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N})=\min\{\|J_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N}-A\|:A\in\mathcal{R}_{N-2}\}\\
\geq\min\{\|C_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N}-A\|:A\in\mathcal{R}_{N-1}\}=s_{\min}(C_{N}-z{\rm Id}_{N})\geq\varepsilon,

completing the proof of (
4.7
) and of the theorem.
∎
