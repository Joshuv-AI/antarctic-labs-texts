Non-ergosterol lipid remodeling governs amphotericin B susceptibility via TOR–calcineurin signaling in Candida glabrata☆
Authors: Mohit Kumar, Sonam Kumari, Mohd Wasi, Basharat Ali, Praveen Kumar, Garvit Sharma, Ashutosh Singh, Alessandra da Silva Dantas, Neil AR Gow, Naseem A Gaur, Rajendra Prasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the molecular determinants of antifungal drug susceptibility is essential for addressing rising resistance in fungal pathogens. Here, we uncover a central role for sphingolipid (SL) metabolism in modulating susceptibility to amphotericin B (AmB) in Candida glabrata. Through targeted lipidomics, we show that C. glabrata lacks the glucosylceramide biosynthetic branch and is enriched in long-chain phytoceramides. Deleting the inositol phosphosphingolipid phospholipase C gene (CgISC1) resulted in elevated AmB sensitivity, independent of ergosterol biosynthesis, and was associated with altered SL profiles and disrupted ceramide chain-length ratios. We further demonstrate that this phenotype is regulated by the TOR and calcineurin signaling pathways. Pharmacological inhibition and genetic interaction studies reveal that TOR-dependent regulators (CgORM2) and calcineurin effectors, including the CK2 subunit CgCKBs, modulate SL biosynthesis. Deletion of CgCKB2 in the Cgisc1Δ background reverses AmB susceptibility, whereas deletion of CgCKB1 in Cgisc1Δ cells further increases susceptibility to AmB. These findings define a signaling lipid regulator that governs membrane composition and AmB sensitivity, providing a framework for targeting sphingolipid pathways to potentiate antifungal therapies.Keywords: Candida glabrata, CgIsc1, Sphingolipids, TOR pathway, Calcineurin, Amphotericin B

Introduction

Most strains of Candida glabrata (now renamed Nakaseomyces glabratus), a major cause of fungal infections, are inherently resistant to azoles, and some are also resistant to echinocandins and flucytosine (Edlind and Katiyar, 2010; Pristov and Ghannoum, 2019). For multidrug-resistant strains, the polyene macrolide antifungal amphotericin B (AmB) is the only remaining treatment option (Healey and Perlin, 2018). A major advantage of using AmB is the low incidence of natural resistance in this yeast's populations. This is because amphotericin resistance normally imparts a high fitness cost on the organism. However, the emergence of AmB-resistant isolates in C. glabrata (Ahmad et al., 2019; Hull et al., 2012; Khan et al., 2008) and other yeasts such as Candida auris (now renamed Candidozyma auris) (Fenton and John, 2024) is a concern as the possibility exists that multidrug-resistant strains of these yeasts may be virtually untreatable. The rise of AmB resistance in these and other fungal pathogens necessitates deeper investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying it.

AmB exerts its antifungal effect by binding or sequestering ergosterol in membranes, disrupting membrane function, causing leakage of intracellular ions, and ultimately leading to cell death (Anderson et al., 2014; Gray et al., 2012; Kristanc et al., 2019). However, recent studies have demonstrated that pore formation is not required for AmB action and that AmB instead forms extracellular head-to-tail homodimer complexes that act as ergosterol sponges, extracting ergosterol from the cell membrane (Anderson et al., 2014; Palacios et al., 2007; Zielińska et al., 2016). Fungicidal activity of AmB also appears to be mediated by several other factors, including fungal growth phase, redox homeostasis, and various metabolic conditions (Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, oxidative cellular damage and cell wall modulation have been proposed to affect AmB susceptibility (Gonzalez-Jimenez et al., 2023; Mesa-Arango et al., 2016). Despite over six decades of research, the precise mechanism of its fungicidal activity remains unresolved.

Sphingolipids (SL) and ergosterol are crucial for membrane structural integrity and cellular function. Ergosterol regulates membrane fluidity and permeability, and its depletion affects specific SL composition, possibly compensating for its loss (Jordá and Puig, 2020). This interplay suggests a possible mechanism for AmB resistance via SL regulation. Studies of SL mutants indicate that deletion of the FEN1 and SUR4 genes, which encode SL intermediates, led to increased AmB sensitivity in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, independent of membrane ergosterol content (Sharma et al., 2014). Similarly, deletion of orthologs of these genes in Candida albicans exhibited the same phenotype, again highlighting a strong correlation between SL and AmB resistance (Madaan and Bari, 2023; Sharma et al., 2014). The involvement of SL in AmB susceptibility was also demonstrated in a study in which partial inhibition of the SL biosynthetic pathway with myriocin (MYR) increased the AmB sensitivity of various yeast species. This heightened susceptibility was reversed upon supplementation with the SL intermediate, phytosphingosine (PHS), confirming the role of SL in modulating AmB sensitivity (Sharma et al., 2014).

Hassoun et al. investigated the evolutionary dynamics of C. glabrata colonizing the gastrointestinal tract of immunocompetent mice during caspofungin therapy. Mutations were identified in the drug target gene FKS2 and the FEN1 gene, which encodes a fatty acid elongase involved in SL biosynthesis (Hassoun et al., 2024). In C. albicans, the TOR cascade was implicated in azole susceptibility (Khandelwal et al., 2018). A study from our group has also shown cross-talk among azole susceptibility, TOR, and calcineurin signaling, as well as the modulation of azole sensitivity by SL in C. glabrata (Kumari et al., 2022). Although specific studies in C. glabrata AmB susceptibility are currently lacking, the interplay between SL metabolism and TOR signaling observed in other fungal species suggests a possible regulatory network influencing AmB susceptibility.

The present study aims to understand AmB susceptibility and its association with SL and the signaling cascades. The deletion analysis of 14 key non-essential genes showed that five mutants in the SL biosynthesis pathway, namely Cgsur1Δ, Cgcsg2Δ, Cgsur2Δ, Cgisc1Δ and Cglcb5Δ, displayed increased susceptibility to AmB. Unique among these, the AmB susceptibility of Cgisc1Δ- a phospholipase C that hydrolyzes inositol phosphosphingolipids, to generate ceramide, was found to be independent of ergosterol levels. Further analysis demonstrated regulation of SL biosynthesis via the TOR and calcineurin signaling cascades. Supporting this, mutants of the downstream effector kinase encoding genes (CgORM1, CgORM2, CgYPK1, CgNPR1, CgCKB1, CgCKB2, and CgCNB1) were also involved in AmB sensitivity. Together, our study suggests that AmB susceptibility may be regulated by cross-talk between SL and TOR/calcineurin signaling via CgIsc1 and provides a mechanism linking SL to AmB sensitivity in C. glabrata.

Results

Sphingolipidomics of Candida glabrata and pathway analysis

The high throughput SL analysis of wild type (WT) C. glabrata cells showed the presence of all major SL classes, including long-chain bases, sphingosine (SPH), dihydrosphingosine (DHS) along with sphingosine-1-phosphate (SPH1P), dihydrosphingosine-1-phosphate (DHS1P), phytosphingosine (PHS), phytosphingosine-1-phosphate (PHS1P), dihydroceramide (dhCer), and phytoceramide (PCer) (Fig. 1a). Among these molecular species, Cer(d18:0/28:0) and Cer(t18:0/28:0) were the most abundant dhCer and PCer structures, respectively, in C. glabrata (Supplementary Fig. 1). Other SL intermediates, including Cer, hydroxylated ceramide (α-OHCer), hydroxylated phytoceramide (α-OHPCer), and low levels of inositolphosphorylceramide (IPC), were also detected (Supplementary File 1). Interestingly, S. cerevisiae and C. albicans are rich in C24 and C26 fatty acyl SL intermediates (Ejsing et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2012), while C. glabrata predominantly has C26 and C28 fatty acyls. Notably, unlike C. albicans, and similar to S. cerevisiae, C. glabrata lacks the GlcCer branch of the SL biosynthetic pathway.

Deletion of SL biosynthesis genes alters antifungal susceptibility

The sphingolipidomic analysis of C. glabrata, together with comparative gene homology analysis with S. cerevisiae and C. albicans, identified 28 SL pathway genes. This analysis revealed high conservation between the SL biosynthetic pathways of S. cerevisiae and C. glabrata. To investigate the role of these genes, 14 non-essential genes were deleted as described in the methods section. None of the single deleted strains displayed a growth defect under non-stressed conditions (Supplementary Table 1). All the mutants were tested for azoles and polyenes susceptibility, wherein Cgsur2Δ and Cglcb5Δ showed susceptibility towards all tested azoles (Fig. 1b), while Cgsur1Δ, Cgcsg2Δ, Cgsur2Δ and Cglcb5Δ exhibited susceptibility to both polyenes and at least one of the azoles. Only Cgisc1Δ showed high susceptibility exclusively towards polyenes (Fig. 1b). While ergosterol content is a contributing factor for azole and polyene susceptibility, the associated SL alterations also play a significant role in membrane integrity and azole susceptibility (Gao et al., 2018).

Cgisc1p exhibits ergosterol-independent AmB susceptibility

Ergosterol levels were measured in mutants exhibiting heightened amphotericin B (AmB) susceptibility. Among these, Cgsur1Δ, Cgcsg2Δ, Cgsur2Δ, and Cglcb5Δ had reduced ergosterol content, whereas Cgisc1Δ maintained ergosterol at wild-type levels (Fig. 2a). This observation aligns with no significant change in transcript levels of key ergosterol biosynthesis genes (CgERG2, CgERG3, CgERG4, CgERG6, and CgERG11) in Cgisc1Δ (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that increased AmB sensitivity is independent of ergosterol biosynthesis defects. Analysis of the Cgisc1Δ mutant revealed no significant changes in the major phospholipid classes, except for phosphatidic acid (PA) (Supplementary File 2). We hypothesized that SLs may play a unique role in modulating AmB susceptibility. Supporting this, partial inhibition of SL biosynthesis using a non-toxic concentration of myriocin (MYR) in the growth medium of Cgisc1Δ led to a reduction in AmB susceptibility (0.8 μg/ml) (Fig. 2b). This phenotype was reversed by adding exogenous phytosphingosine (PHS) alongside AmB and MYR. Notably, MYR or PHS treatment had minimal effects on the other mutants tested (Fig. 2b). No change was found in membrane permeability and membrane potential after AmB treatment in Cgisc1Δ (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results support the hypothesis that AmB susceptibility in Cgisc1Δ is linked directly to altered SL homeostasis.

Cgisc1Δ-mediated alterations in SL levels lead to increased polyene susceptibility

To further assess the correlation between SL and AmB susceptibility, the expression levels of SL pathway genes CgSUR2, CgLAG1, CgLAC1, CgLCB3, CgLCB4, CgLCB5, CgYSR3, CgSCS7, and CgDPL1 under varied conditions were compared between Cgisc1Δ and WT (Fig. 2c). We observed increased expression of all tested SL biosynthesis genes in Cgisc1Δ compared to WT. However, exposure to AmB significantly reduced the expression of these genes, whereas supplementation with a sub-lethal concentration of MYR with AmB restored their expression. Supplementation of PHS, a key intermediate in SL biosynthesis, along with AmB and MYR, mirrored the AmB treated expression levels. For example, in the Cgisc1Δ mutant, CgLAC1 and CgLAG1 were markedly upregulated compared with the WT. AmB treatment partially attenuated the elevated expression, whereas co-treatment with MYR and AmB further increased transcription of both genes. Remarkably, subsequent supplementation with PHS reversed this effect, restoring gene expression to levels comparable to those observed with AmB treatment alone (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 4). Changes in the SL profile closely paralleled this transcriptional response. Specifically, AmB treatment increased the accumulation of SL intermediates in the Cgisc1Δ mutant relative to WT, whereas co-treatment of AmB and MYR normalized their levels to those in untreated cells. Replenishment of PHS with AmB and MYR restored the accumulation of these intermediates, reproducing the lipid profile observed under AmB treatment alone. (Fig. 3a). Similar changes in the concentrations of other SL intermediates were also observed (Supplementary File 3).

The modulation of SL in Cgisc1Δ cells in response to AmB, MYR, and PHS treatments not only correlated well with changes in SL biosynthesis pathway gene expression at intermediate levels but was also reflected at the level of the SL metabolome. The ratio of 28PCer to 26PCer appeared to be a critical determinant for maintenance of SL homeostasis in C. glabrata. In the WT strain grown in YPD, this ratio was approximately 2.06. However, in the absence of CgISC1, the ratio dropped to 1.3, primarily due to a decrease in 28PCer (from 45 Mole% to 36 Mole%) and a concomitant increase in 26PCer (from 21 Mole% to 28 Mole%). Upon AmB treatment, the 28PCer/26PCer ratio shifts further: in WT, it increases from 2.0 to 2.5, while in Cgisc1Δ, it declines from 1.3 to 1.03 (Supplementary File 3). Co-treatment with AmB and MYR restored SL content and ratios to similar levels observed in YPD-grown cells. However, adding PHS along with AmB and MYR led to a pronounced decline in the 28PCer/26PCer ratio, dropping to 0.71 in WT and 0.3 in Cgisc1Δ, likely due to the exogenous PHS supplementation. These data underscore the importance of intermediate SL species, particularly the balance between 28PCer and 26PCer, in modulating AmB susceptibility in the Cgisc1Δ mutant (Fig. 3b).

AmB susceptibility and SL regulation by Isc1 are mediated via the TOR signaling pathway

Previous studies have suggested that the TOR signaling cascade may influence SL regulation and antifungal resistance (Khandelwal et al., 2018; Kumari et al., 2022). Inhibition of TOR kinase has been shown to enhance the efficacy of antifungal agents in S. cerevisiae and C. albicans (Shekhar-Guturja et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2021), indicating that TOR suppression might play a key role in this synergistic effect. Our group recently demonstrated that TOR kinase suppression is linked to azole resistance in C. glabrata (Kumari et al., 2022). We therefore explored whether the TOR signaling cascade could also impact AmB susceptibility, manifested by Cgisc1Δ. In this context, screening Cgisc1Δ in the presence of rapamycin (Rapa) revealed a significant reduction in growth. This growth retardation was rescued in the revertant Cgisc1ΔR, containing a copy of the CgISC1 gene (Fig. 4a). The involvement of the TOR pathway was further confirmed by elevated transcript levels of the CgKNS1 and CgNPR1 genes in the presence of Rapa, which are negatively regulated by the CgTor1 kinase (Fig. 4b). In addition, increased expression of CgLCB1 and CgLCB2 (Serine Palmitoyl Co-A transferases (SPT)) transcripts and reduced transcription of CgORM2 (Fig. 4b) were observed in Cgisc1Δ, which are regulated by downstream kinases of the TOR pathway. A significant accumulation of SL coincided with the elevated expression of these downstream kinases (supplementary file 4). To further dissect the role of the TOR cascade, we constructed double knockout mutants of the TOR pathway downstream regulators (Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm1Δ, Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm2Δ, Cgisc1Δ/Cgypk1Δ and Cgisc1Δ/Cgnpr1Δ) in a Cgisc1Δ background. Single-gene deletions and double knockouts did not exhibit growth defects under the influence of AmB, except Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm2Δ, where growth was significantly reduced in the presence of AmB, implying a functional relationship between CgOrm2 and CgIsc1 (Fig. 4c).

Orm2 negatively regulates SL biosynthesis by modulating the phosphorylation/dephosphorylation of the SPT enzyme that catalyzes the first step of SL biosynthesis (Xie et al., 2024). The yeast Orm proteins form a stable complex with SPT enzymes that regulate SL metabolism. In response to low SL, Orm inhibition is relieved by its phosphorylation by TOR2/Ypk1 (Shimobayashi et al., 2013). We measured the protein expression level of HA-tagged CgOrm2 in the Cgisc1Δ strain. We found reduced expression of CgOrm2, indicating a limited capacity to inhibit the SPT enzyme, which may contribute to the elevated SL levels in the Cgisc1Δ strain. Upon treatment with AmB, CgOrm2 protein levels decreased in the WT strain but increased in Cgisc1Δ (Fig. 4d), suggesting that CgIsc1 plays a critical role in AmB susceptibility, potentially through regulation of CgOrm protein expression.

Additionally, Orms can regulate SL at the ceramide level (Ren et al., 2024). PCer is the predominant SL in C. glabrata, and it may serve as a regulator of AmB susceptibility. Our recent report showed increased PCer levels in AmB-resistant C. auris isolates (Kumar et al., 2021). The lipidomic analysis in Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm2Δ revealed modulation of total PCer and its subspecies (Supplementary File 4). For example, 26-PCer was significantly reduced, while 28-PCer levels were substantially higher compared to Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm2Δ than in Cgisc1Δ (Fig. 4e), while Cgorm2Δ alone was not significantly affected. These data suggest that CgIsc1 may regulate SL metabolism via the TOR pathway, thereby influencing AmB susceptibility (Fig. 4f).

Calcineurin regulates SL homeostasis and AmB susceptibility in Cgisc1Δ

Treatment with the calcineurin inhibitor FK520 significantly reduced the growth of Cgisc1Δ cells (Fig. 5a). Similarly, geldanamycin which primarily inhibits Heat Shock Protein 90 (Hsp90), a key regulator of the calcineurin signaling pathway also suppressed the growth of Cgisc1Δ cells (Fig. 5b). The inhibition of CgHsp90 was further supported by a decrease in its transcript levels in Cgisc1Δ cells (Fig. 5c). Additionally, the upregulation of CgKNS1 in Cgisc1Δ cells further supports the involvement of calcineurin signaling in the regulation of AmB susceptibility (Fig.5b). Transcript levels of the calcineurin catalytic subunit (CgCNA1), regulatory subunit (CgCNB1), and its downstream transcription factor CgCRZ1 were all reduced, indicating suppression of the calcineurin pathway and its downstream effectors in Cgisc1Δ cells (Fig. 5c).

Calcineurin-Hsp90 stability is regulated by casein kinase (CK2), which is activated by Kns1 through phosphorylation of its regulatory domain (Ckb1/2), leading to further phosphorylation of Hsp90 (Diezmann et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2015). To understand the role of CK2 and ultimately calcineurin suppression on AmB susceptibility, we created the double knockout mutants of CgCKB1, CgCKB2, and CgCNB1 in the background of Cgisc1Δ. We observed no growth defect in Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb1Δ, Cgisc1Δ/ Cgcnb1Δ, but a significant growth reduction in Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ. In the presence of AmB, while Cgckb1Δ, Cgckb2Δ, Cgcnb1Δ and double mutants Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb1Δ and Cgisc1Δ/Cgcnb1Δ did not exhibit a growth defect, the growth rate was enhanced in Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ. In contrast, the Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb1Δ mutant displayed reduced growth in the presence of AmB, implying increased susceptibility to this polyene (Fig. 5d). These results indicate that CgCkb2 is important for AmB susceptibility in the Cgisc1Δ strain. Furthermore, higher levels of HA-tagged CgCkb1 and HA-tagged CgCkb2 protein expression were observed in Cgisc1Δ (Fig. 5e), supporting our previous results that CK2 proteins may be elevated and active in this strain, thereby contributing to suppression of the calcineurin pathway. Addition of AmB reduced the protein expression of both CgCkb1 and CgCkb2 in the Cgisc1Δ background. This suggests that both CK regulatory factors are also important for AmB susceptibility in the Cgisc1Δ mutant.

We assessed the impact of calcineurin effectors on SL by measuring PCer and its subspecies in CKB regulatory subunit mutants (Supplementary Files 5 and 6). The levels of 24PCer were elevated in the Cgckb1Δ and Cgckb2Δ compared to the Cgisc1Δ strain, whereas the Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ mutant exhibited reduced levels of 24PCer relative to the Cgckb2Δ. In contrast, the levels of 26PCer and 28PCer were lower in the Cgckb1Δ and Cgckb2Δ than in the Cgisc1Δ. Interestingly, the Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ mutant displayed levels of 26PCer and 28PCer similar to those observed in the wild-type strain and helped maintain a 26/28 PCer ratio (Fig. 5f). The change in PCer in Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ might explain enhanced growth in the presence of AmB. This suggests that modulation of the PCer chain-length distribution, driven by the combined effect of regulatory domain deletions in CgISC1 and CgCK2, may attenuate AmB-induced membrane perturbation. Our findings reveal a complex regulatory relationship between CgIsc1 and calcineurin/CKb2 signaling to influence SL remodeling, ultimately impacting AmB susceptibility. These results suggest that activated CK2 and suppressed calcineurin are more prominent in AmB sensitivity in a Cgisc1Δ background with impaired SL metabolism (Fig. 5g).

Discussion

Among the limited antifungal agents available to clinicians, AmB remains a critical, broad-spectrum, life-saving option, especially for severe fungal infections, including those caused by Candida species. Despite over 60 years of clinical use, the detailed AmB mechanism of action remains incompletely understood, but is assumed to act directly and indirectly through ergosterol binding (Cavassin et al., 2021). In this study, we demonstrate that AmB susceptibility in C. glabrata is governed by SL biosynthesis, which is regulated by TOR and calcineurin signaling pathways, independently of ergosterol biosynthesis.

Our data show that CgIsc1 hydrolyzes complex SL to generate Cer and inositol phosphate, key regulators of AmB susceptibility in C. glabrata. In this organism, 26- and 28‑carbon SL intermediates are prominent, whilst in S. cerevisiae and C. albicans, 24- and 26‑carbon SL intermediates are more common (Ejsing et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2012), highlighting a distinct shift in SL species composition in these distantly related species. Notably, despite sharing a close evolutionary relationship with S. cerevisiae, C. glabrata synthesizes distinct SL fatty acyl chains. This divergence probably arises from a combination of selective gene loss, differences in very-long-chain fatty acid elongases or ceramide synthases, and evolutionary adaptation to its host environment (Gao et al., 2018; Kumar et al., 2019; Roetzer et al., 2011; Usmani et al., 2023).

This conclusion is based on an analysis of 14 single-deletion mutants with targeted defects in SL biosynthesis. While several mutants in the SL biosynthetic pathway exhibited altered susceptibility to azoles and polyenes, only the Cgisc1Δ mutant displayed a unique susceptibility to AmB. This increased susceptibility was not associated with changes in ergosterol content (Supplementary File 2), suggesting an alternative aspect of AmB action. Further, neither altered phospholipid homeostasis, ROS production, nor membrane diffusion and potential could account for the AmB susceptibility of the Cgisc1Δ mutant.

Previous studies have suggested that modifications in the SL pathway alter antifungal susceptibility. For example, deletion of IPT1 in both C. albicans and C. glabrata has significantly enhanced azole sensitivity (Prasad et al., 2005; Shahi et al., 2022). Similarly, deletion of CaFEN1 and CaFEN12 in C. albicans increased susceptibility to AmB (Alfatah et al., 2017). Consistent with these findings, deleting five SL metabolic genes in C. glabrata enhanced susceptibility to AmB. Four of these mutants (Cgsur1Δ, Cgcsg2Δ, Cgsur2Δ, and Cglcb5Δ) also exhibited altered ergosterol levels.

In contrast, the AmB sensitivity of Cgisc1Δ appears to result from disruption of SL metabolism. This was supported in a rescue experiment using MYR and PHS supplementation. These results, along with observed changes in the expression of SL metabolic genes and corresponding shifts in SL intermediates in rescue experiments, highlight the central role of SL homeostasis in mediating AmB sensitivity.

SL biosynthesis is tightly regulated. SPT (Serine Palmitoyl-CoA synthase) is a rate-limiting enzyme that Orms negatively regulate (Han et al., 2019). Cer levels influence a critical branching point in the SL pathway, and Cer formation serves as a substrate for downstream steps. The TOR signaling pathway regulates both Orms and Cer levels. Tor1 interacts with Orm1/2 to regulate SPT activity, and Tor2 and Ypk1 regulate Cer levels (Ren et al., 2024; Roelants et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2024). Tor2-activated Ypk1 also inhibits Ysp2, thereby blocking retrograde sterol transport from the plasma membrane to the ER, increasing ergosterol accessibility to polyenes. In our analysis of the Cgisc1Δ mutant, we observed suppressed TOR kinase activity, including hypersensitivity to Rapa and upregulation of TOR-responsive genes CgKNS1 and CgNPR1. Additionally, the observation of increased expression of CgLCB1 and CgLCB2, both of which are regulated by Orms, further supports the involvement of the TOR pathway in determining AmB sensitivity. The Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm2Δ exhibited significant growth defects in the presence of AmB, suggesting a functional link between CgOrm2 and CgIsc1. Reduced HA-tagged CgOrm2 levels in the Cgisc1Δmutant and its differential AmB response suggested that CgIsc1 may modulate Orm2 expression, impacting SL homeostasis. Lipidomics of Cgisc1Δ/Cgorm2Δ revealed altered PCer profiles correlating with AmB susceptibility. These findings indicate that the CgIsc1/TOR/Orm2/SL signaling pathway may regulate AmB susceptibility in C. glabrata.

SL regulation is also influenced by the calcineurin pathway, which negatively regulates Cer synthases (Aronova et al., 2008). Overexpression of CgKNS1 in Cgisc1Δ also elevated CK2 complex expression, thereby suppressing the calcineurin pathway. Calcineurin activity in the Cgisc1Δ mutant was reduced, and the HSP90-calcineurin complex was destabilized. This likely inactivated calcineurin, thereby preventing its inhibitory control of Cer synthases and contributing to elevated Cer levels in the Cgisc1Δ strain. Furthermore, the Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ double mutant exhibited enhanced growth in the presence of AmB, while the Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb1Δstrain showed reduced growth, implying increased susceptibility. An elevated expression of HA-tagged CgCkb1 and CgCkb2 in the Cgisc1Δ mutant supports the concept of CK2 hyperactivation, which may suppress calcineurin signaling to mediate polyene susceptibility via SL intermediate remodeling. Lipidomic analysis revealed alterations in specific PCer subspecies, and this PCer remodeling likely underlies the reduced AmB susceptibility of Cgisc1Δ/Cgckb2Δ, suggesting a complex interplay between CgIsc1 and CKB2/calcineurin signaling in regulating SL and AmB susceptibility.

These results suggest that CgIsc1 functions as a key regulator of SL homeostasis in C. glabrata, potentially influencing AmB susceptibility by modulating the TOR and calcineurin signaling pathways (Fig. 6). This study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying AmB activity and resistance and highlights an interplay between ergosterol and SL biosynthesis in regulating susceptibility to AmB. These findings inform future research strategies and possible combination therapies to improve antifungal effectiveness and mitigate drug resistance in fungal pathogens.

Proposed mechanism underpinning CgIsc1-mediated AmB resistance through modulating the SLs pathway as well as the TOR and calcineurin signaling cascade: AmB exerts its antifungal effect in CgISC1-deleted cells primarily by targeting the cell membrane and disrupting its integrity, rather than by altering overall cellular ergosterol levels. Cells lacking CgISC1 exhibit SLs imbalance, which directly contributes to the increased susceptibility of these cells to AmB. This imbalance is governed by two interconnected signaling cascades that controls sphingolipid biosynthesis. Intracellularly, the TOR1/2 complex acts as a master regulator of sphingolipid synthesis, activating downstream kinases Npr1 and Ypk1/2, which in turn activate the Orm1/2 complex a negative regulator that directly inhibits serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) and thereby modulates sphingolipid content. In parallel, a second cascade links TOR1/2 to calcineurin signaling through Kns1 and Ck1/2. Calcineurin, whose stability is maintained by Hsp90 and which can be pharmacologically inhibited by FK520 or Hsp90 inhibitor geldanamycin, directly represses ceramide synthase activity and further regulates sphingolipid levels. It is proposed that CgIsc1-mediated AmB susceptibility is not governed by ergosterol, but rather emerges from the cross-regulatory interplay between the sphingolipid biosynthetic pathway and the TOR–calcineurin signaling. Dash lines show data from the literature.

Material and methods

Yeast strains and plasmids

C. glabrata BG14 strain was used and modified for the studies. Both cloning and expression plasmids used are mentioned in Supplementary Table 2. C. glabrata strains were usually grown in YPD media unless specified. Plasmids were maintained for construction and propagation in the Escherichia coli DH5α strain. E. coli strains were maintained in LB media with Ampicillin (100 μg/ml). Bacterial culturing was performed at 37 °C for 14–16 h with continuous shaking at 150 rpm. 15% glycerol was used for long-term storage of the bacterial culture.

Culture media, chemicals, antifungal drugs

All media components and chemicals were of molecular biology grade and were purchased from commercial sources. Growth media, yeast extract, peptone, dextrose (YPD) media, Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, and LB agar were purchased from Himedia, India.

Construction of C. glabrata knockout

A fusion PCR-mediated homologous recombination-based strategy was used to disrupt C. glabrata ORFs with a cassette containing a dominant selection marker, the NAT1 Gene, which encodes nourseothricin acetyltransferase and confers resistance to nourseothricin, as described earlier (Kumari et al., 2022). Briefly, the gene's 5′ and 3’ UTR flanking regions were PCR-amplified from wild-type (WT) genomic DNA. PCR amplified the selection marker NAT1 gene with FRT sites in two halves from plasmid pRK625. The details of plasmids used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Each UTR was fused to one-half of the NAT1 gene, and the resulting fusion products were co-transformed into the wild-type strain by electroporation. The transformants were screened on YPD agar containing 200 μg/ml nourseothricin. Gene deletion was confirmed by diagnostic PCR using genomic DNA. The selection marker was recycled with the Flipase gene induction for the second deletion. Then, after removing the selection marker and the Flipase plasmid, the second deletion was performed using the same selection cassette. Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Serial dilution spot assay

C. glabrata mutants were phenotypically characterized by a serial dilution spot assay. Briefly, the overnight-grown cells were normalized to an OD600 of 0.1 and were further serially diluted 10-fold in 0.9% NaCl five times. 3 μl of serially diluted culture were then spotted on test plates. Plates were incubated at 30 °C (unless otherwise specified) for 24–48 h, and growth was recorded by capturing images.

Minimum inhibitory concentration detection assay

Overnight-grown cells were resuspended in 0.9% saline to an OD600 of 0.1. Then, 100 μl of cells was added to 10 ml of YPD medium, with the final OD600 of the cells at 0.001 or 104 cells/ml. Dilutions of drugs/chemicals were prepared by serial dilution in 96-well plates, and 100 μl of 0.001 OD600 cells were inoculated in each well. Growth differences were recorded following Incubation of the plates for 48 h at 30 °C.

Time course growth analysis

Time-course growth curve analysis of C. glabrata mutant cells was performed in 96-well plate format using a liquid-handling system (Tecon, Austria). For growth curve generation, cells were first grown overnight in the appropriate medium, then sub-cultured into a test medium with an initial OD600 of 0.2. Growth was monitored by measuring absorbance at 600 nm at 30-min intervals for 24 h.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). Briefly, overnight-grown cultures of C. glabrata were diluted to an OD600 of 0.1 and grown for 4 h at 30 °C in YPD under normal conditions and with transient exposure to AmB (1 μg/ml) for 1 h. Total RNA was extracted using the manufacturer's protocol. RNA samples were quantified using a nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and 5 μg of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using the Revert Aid H minus first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific, Lithuania). The quantitative gene expression profile was evaluated using the DyNAmo Flash SYBR Green qPCR kit (Thermo Scientific, Lithuania) and gene-specific primers, including CgRND5.8 for normalization, in a CFX96™ real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). The expression levels of the tested genes were measured by comparing their Ct values with those of CgRND5.8. Comparative expression profiles were analyzed using the 2-∆∆Ct method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). qRT-PCR was performed in biological duplicates with technical triplicate.

Membrane potential detection by DiBAC4(3)

The membrane potential was measured under two conditions: normal growth and transient treatment with AmB (1 μg/ml). Staining of the strains was performed as previously described (Teixeira-Santos et al., 2012). Briefly, log-phase growing cells (1 × 107 cells) of mutants were suspended in MOPS buffer (pH 7.0). AmB (0.8 mlg/ml) treatment is given for 1 h in both WT and Cgisc1Δ, subsequently cells were washed and suspended in MOPS buffer. DiBAC4(3) was added to the cells at a final concentration of 8 μg/ml, and the cells were incubated at room temperature with shaking for 45 min in dark. After Incubation, samples were washed twice in MOPS buffer (pH 7.0) and then maintained on ice. Cells was then analyzed with FACSCalibur flow cytometer with at least 10,000 events at florescence intensity of 530 nm. Data were processed using FlowJo v10.3 software.

ROS level measurement

The oxidant-sensitive probe DCFDA was used to measure endogenous ROS with and without AmB treatment. Cells were set to an OD600 of 0.1 in YPD medium and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 4 h, with AmB treatment applied during the final 2 h in the treated group. After treatment, DCFDA was added to a final concentration of 2 μM, and cultures were incubated for another 2 h. Cells were then analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer with excitation at 495 nm and emission at 529 nm (FITC-A filter). Data were processed using FlowJo v10.3 software.

NPN-based membrane permeabilization assay

N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN) is a hydrophobic dye that exhibits low fluorescence in aqueous solution but increases fluorescence upon binding to phospholipids, indicating disruption of fungal membrane permeability. The assay was performed to check the membrane permeability. Briefly, strains were grown overnight at 30 °C at 200 rpm. Cells were washed 2–3 times with milliQ water (MQ) to remove the YPD broth, and 1 × 105 cells were maintained in MQ. NPN solution with a final concentration of 10 μM was added to the cells and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Cell fluorescence was measured at 5 min intervals until saturation was reached. NPN-labeled cells were treated with the cell-perturbing agent SDS at a sub-MIC concentration (0.01%). The fluorescence readings were taken every 5 min, and the change in fluorescence intensity was measured with excitation at 350 nm and emission at 420 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer.

Lipid extraction method

Lipid extraction was performed by the Folch method (Folch et al., 1957). Briefly, 50 OD600 (∼5 × 107) cells of secondary culture (0.8–1.0 OD600) were taken for lipid extraction. The cells were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature and washed twice with sterile water. After washing, the cells were disrupted using 50 mg of 0.5 mm glass beads. After centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5 min, lipids were extracted using 9 ml of chloroform: methanol (2:1), and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The upper aqueous layer was discarded, and 2 ml of 0.9% NaCl was added to the sample. Samples were vortexed vigorously and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 2 min. The lower layer was taken, and the sample was dried using nitrogen gas. The sample was stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

Analysis of lipids

A high-throughput LC-MS/MS was used for the analysis of lipids. Data collection and processing were carried out as described previously (Kumar et al., 2021; Shahi et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2017).

Sphingolipid extraction and analysis

Sphingolipid extraction involved suspending approximately 50 OD600 cells in 1.5 ml of Mandala buffer, supplemented with internal standards (C17 ceramide and C17 sphingosine). Glass beads were added, and cells were disrupted using a FastPrep®(MP Biomedicals). After Incubation at 60 °C with periodic vortexing and sonication, cell debris was removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant was dried using N2 gas. Bligh and Dyer's extraction was then performed by dissolving the pellet in chloroform: methanol (1:2, v/v) and incubating it at 37 °C. Phase separation was achieved by adding chloroform and water, and the hydrophobic layer was collected and dried. Mild alkaline hydrolysis was then performed, and the hydrophobic layer was dried and stored at −20 °C.

For sphingolipidomic mass spectrometry analysis, extracted lipids were suspended in methanol containing 1 mM ammonium formate +0.2% formic acid. Samples were delivered to an HPLC system fitted with a C8 column. A two-buffer mobile-phase system was used, and the internal-standard normalization method was used to quantify SL species. The lipid data were normalized to per mg of protein, and mole% was calculated (Kumar et al., 2021). All the raw chromatograms are listed in Supplementary File 7.

Ergosterol analysis

For free sterol analysis, the base-hydrolyzed lipid extract was derivatized with N, O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide with trimethylsilylchlorosilane (BSTFA/TMCS, Sigma, USA) and analyzed on a DB5-MS column (30 m × 0.2 mm × 0.20 μm) as described previously (Singh et al., 2012). The retention time and mass spectral patterns of external standards were used to identify sterol species. All the raw chromatograms are listed in Supplementary File 8.

Total protein extraction

For protein extraction, Cells were pelleted at 4000 rpm for 5 min and washed with ice-cold water. The pellet was then resuspended in 250–500 μl of homogenization buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 2 mM EDTA, 1 mM phenylmethylsulphonyl fluoride (PMSF), 10 mM sodium fluoride, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Glass beads (0.5 mm size) were added in equal amounts to the suspension. Cells were lysed mechanically using a bead-beating homogenizer, Fast Prep®) (MP Biomedicals, at maximum speed for 60 s, repeated five times with intermittent cooling on ice. After lysis, the lysate was collected by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The protein supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube, and protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kit. Protein samples were stored at −20 °C until further use.

Western blotting

For western blotting, 30 μg of total protein was separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel in Tris-Glycine-SDS buffer at 70–100 V for 2–3 h using a MINI PROTEAN® 3 electrophoresis unit. Following separation, proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane using a Bio-Rad Mini Trans-Blot unit in Tris-Glycine transfer buffer at 4 °C. Before transfer, the PVDF membrane was activated in 100% methanol and washed in transfer buffer. Transfer time and current settings were adjusted based on protein size. After transfer, membranes were blocked in 0.1% Tween-20, 5% fat-free skimmed milk in 1× TBS for 1 h at room temperature with shaking. Membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies (Anti-HA and GAPDH) diluted in blocking buffer for 3–4 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed thrice with 1× TBS-T for 10 min each. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for 60 min at room temperature with gentle shaking, followed by three washes with 1× TBST. To visualize proteins, membranes were treated with the HRP substrate ECL plus, and chemiluminescent signals were captured using the Chemidoc XRS plus Bio-Rad.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in biological triplicate, and all lipid datasets are presented as mean ± standard error (n = 3). Statistical significance was determined using Student's t-test, and p-values <0.05 were considered significant.
