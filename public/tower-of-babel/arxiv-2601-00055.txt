Abstract
Red supergiants (RSGs) are important for our understanding of supernova progenitors, stellar populations, stellar evolution, mass loss and dust production. Extragalactic surveys of RSGs have a long history in the Local Group, but few studies exist beyond that due to the limited resolution and sensitivity of ground-based and previous space-based infrared observatories. Here we demonstrate the combined power of HST and JWST to push systematic searches of RSGs out to ∼ \sim 20 Mpc. We introduce a catalog of 97057 RSGs —the largest single-survey release of RSGs— with masses ≳ \gtrsim 10 M ⊙ in 19 galaxies from the PHANGS HST+JWST Treasury program. We use HST F814W and JWST F200W photometry to select stars as RSGs based on predicted colors and magnitudes from PARSEC isochrones. The spatial distribution of our recovered RSGs follow the familiar pattern of mostly being concentrated in active star-forming regions such as spiral arms and central starburst rings. The RSG number density on kpc-scales is strongly correlated ( r s r_{s} ∼ \sim 0.82) with local star-formation rate density ( Σ S ​ F ​ R \Sigma_{SFR} ) traced by extinction-corrected far-ultraviolet (FUV) from GALEX+WISE , and weakly correlated ( r s r_{s} ∼ \sim 0.57) with the total stellar mass density ( Σ ∗ \Sigma_{*} ), traced by near-infrared emission from WISE+Spitzer . The number of RSGs per mass of stellar populations with ages 6-30 Myr (the likely age range of RSGs > > 10M ⊙ ) is ∼ \sim 1 per 10 3.77±0.27 M ⊙ , assuming constant star-formation rates from FUV+W4. Our sample will be a useful resource for tracking progenitors and feedback sites of future supernovae in PHANGS,age-dating stellar populations, and more.

Introduction

Red supergiants (RSGs) typically refer to K or M-type supergiants that have evolved from stars with zero-age main-sequence (ZAMS) masses
>
8 M
⊙

(
E. M. Levesque 2018
)
. Studies of RSGs are essential for many areas of astrophysics. They have been confirmed as progenitors of various subtypes of Type II supernovae (SNe)
(
S. J. Smartt 2009
;
S. J. Smartt 2015
)
, making the understanding of their pre-SN evolution vital. In stellar evolution, RSGs are important laboratories for understanding mass-loss mechanisms in cool supergiants
(
A. Maeder 1981
;
C. Chiosi & A. Maeder 1986
;
J. T. van Loon et al. 2005
;
E. R. Beasor et al. 2020

, binary evolution models
(
K. F. Neugent et al. 2019
;
L. R. Patrick et al. 2020
;
K. F. Neugent et al. 2020
;
L. R. Patrick et al. 2022

(
A. Maeder et al. 1980
;
P. Massey 2003
;
J. J. Eldridge et al. 2017
;
P. Massey et al. 2021a
)
. For stellar feedback studies, RSGs serve as markers of future Type II SNe in nearby galaxies, enabling spatially-resolved studies of their host stellar populations
(
J. P. Anderson & P. A. James 2008
;
T. Kangas et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
and future SN feedback sites
(
N. Mayker Chen et al. 2023a
;
S. K. Sarbadhicary et al. 2023
;
N. Mayker Chen et al. 2024a
)
.

Many of these topics have greatly benefited from extragalactic surveys of RSGs. Such studies were pioneered using ground-based photometric surveys of Local Group galaxies

Surveys of RSGs in galaxies beyond the Local Group have been attempted out to distances of

The superior angular resolution of HST and JWST allows identification of RSGs in galaxies out to
\gtrsim 20
Mpc. This capability has been demonstrated in identification of the progenitors of individual core-collapse SNe

II.1
The PHANGS-HST and JWST data

We draw our targets from the PHANGS sample which consists of 90 galaxies that were observed with the Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA)
(
A. K. Leroy et al. 2021
)
. A subset of about 74 of these galaxies have been systematically observed with other telescopes, including HST
(38 published, see
J. C. Lee et al. 2022
;
D. Maschmann et al. 2024

and JWST

J. C. Lee et al. 2023
;
R. Chown et al. 2025
;
O. V. Egorov et al. 2025

. In this paper, we work with the first 19 of these 74 galaxies observed by the PHANGS JWST Cycle 1 Treasury Survey
(
J. C. Lee et al. 2023
)
because of their high-quality astrometric alignment in all filters
(
T. G. Williams et al. 2024
)
, making them ideal for our first attempt at multiband photometry and census of supergiants.

From the NUV to the MIR, the combined HST and JWST data offer 13 wide/medium filters spanning 0.25-21 µm. These filters include the HST F275W(
NUV

II.2
Star-formation tracers from PHANGS MegaTables

PHANGS provides a number of supporting datasets which we can use to understand the statistics of the recovered RSG population. In this paper we use the PHANGS MegaTables constructed by
J. Sun et al. 2022
;
J. Sun et al. 2023
, which estimate relevant star-formation and ISM measurements from multi-wavelength maps in hexagonal bins of
\sim
1.95 kpc
2
(the large bins are necessitated by the limiting resolution of the WISE 22
\mu
m maps used for attenuation correction). While these bins may smooth over finer sub-kpc scale spatial correlations between RSGs and star-forming regions, we believe the MegaTables are a good starting point as all the SFR measurements and their inter-correlations have been carefully calibrated and vetted by
J. Sun et al. 2023
. The MegaTable apertures are large enough to avoid low number statistics at low SFRs, and to treat the RSGs and other star-formation within the apertures as approximately co-eval, even after some stellar drifting and mixing. We refer the reader to
J. Sun et al. 2022
and
J. Sun et al. 2023
for details on how the tables were constructed.

The PHANGS MegaTables provide several star-formation rate (SFR) and stellar mass tracers on 1.5 kpc scales that can be useful for assessing the recovered RSG population, including: SFRs from
GALEX
far-ultraviolet (FUV, 154 nm) and near-ultraviolet (NUV,231 nm) with
WISE
W4 (22
\mu
m)-based extinction correction based on the prescriptions of
A. K. Leroy et al. 2019
and
F. Belfiore et al. 2023
, H
\alpha
-based SFRs from the ESO Wide Field Imager with W4-based extinction correction
(
D. Calzetti et al. 2007
;
E. J. Murphy et al. 2011
;
F. Belfiore et al. 2023
)
, MUSE H
\alpha
-based SFRs with Balmer-decrement correction
(
B. Groves et al. 2023
)
, and the
WISE W1
(3.4
\mu
m) +
Spitzer IRAC
(3.6
\mu
m) based stellar mass maps using a mass-to-light ratio based on
A. K. Leroy et al. 2019

In Section
IV

F. Belfiore et al. 2023
-based FUV+W4 hybrid SFRs and Balmer-decremented-corrected MUSE H
\alpha

⊙

\sim 1

III.1
PSF photometry & point source selection with DOLPHOT

We carry out multiband PSF photometry with
DOLPHOT
, which is designed to operate on HST
(
A. E. Dolphin 2000
;
A. Dolphin 2016
)
and JWST data
(
D. R. Weisz et al. 2024
)
. PSF and pixel maps of the HST WFC3/UVIS, ACS/WFC and NIRCam were downloaded from the
DOLPHOT
website. Prior to photometry, the
nircammask
,
wfc3mask
and
acsmask
tasks were used to mask out bad or saturated pixels, and make minor adjustments to the FITS headers to aid the
DOLPHOT
routine. For each dither, a background sky map is generated with the
calcsky
package. With this setup, the
DOLPHOT
photometry routine was run on the individual dither images, with a mosaicked F814W image of each galaxy being used as reference for alignment. We chose F814W as the reference image as it is roughly the midpoint of the full NUV-to-NIR range spanned by our filters, and is therefore most likely to have enough stars in common with images taken using other filters for accurate astrometric alignment, PSF and aperture corrections.

The routine
DOLPHOT
iteratively detects sources, records their pixel locations and aperture-corrected fluxes using the procedure in
A. E. Dolphin 2000
and several user-defined parameters. The key
DOLPHOT
parameters used in this paper are summarized in Table
1
, mostly based on recommendations by the JWST Resolved Stellar Populations ERS program
(
D. R. Weisz et al. 2024
)
.

We then optimize our run to select point sources, which describes our RSGs as opposed to slightly extended objects like clusters
(
D. A. Thilker et al. 2022
, see discussion in Section 4 of)
. We will use the F814W (0.8
\mu
m) and F200W (2
\mu
m) filters for the selection of RSGs from the
DOLPHOT
catalog. These filters are close to the peak of the RSG SEDs typically occurring around
\sim 1

\mu
m, making them the most suitable among our available filters for identifying RSGs (Figure
1
). We first need to obtain a photometric sample of stars that is mostly uncompromised by crowding, and reject stars of poor or dubious photometric quality.
DOLPHOT
records quantities such as photometry signal-to-noise, crowding, roundness, and goodness-of-fit
\chi^{2}
in each band
(see
A. E. Dolphin 2000
, for definitions)
, and we again follow
D. R. Weisz et al. 2024
to set thresholds to select a candidate star catalog (or ‘good stars’):

1.

The detection signal-to-noise in both F814W and F200W filters should be greater than 5:

S2N_WFC3_F814W
\geq
5 & S2N_NIRCAM_F200W
\geq
5

2.

The
crowding
parameter, which informs how much brighter a star would have been in the absence of nearby fitted stars, should be less than 0.2 mag in both F814W and F200W:

Crowd_WFC3_F814W
\leq
0.2 & Crowd_NIRCAM_F200W
\leq
0.2

3.

The
sharp
-squared parameter should be less than 0.02 in both filters. For a perfectly fit star,
sharp
=0. For overly sharp sources (e.g. cosmic rays),
sharp
>
0, while for blended sources,
sharp
<
0. As per convention, we use the squared value of this parameter:

Sharp_WFC3_F814W
2

\leq
0.02 & Sharp_NIRCAM_F200W
2

\leq
0.02

For the remainder of this paper, we will discuss fluxes in vegamag. Analysis in the subsequent sections will use stars brighter than the 5
\sigma
completeness limit from the
DOLPHOT
good-star selection above. Uncertainties in the
DOLPHOT
photometry are
\lesssim
0.22 mag in the F814W and F200W filters. Absolute magnitudes were obtained assuming distances to galaxies from
G. S. Anand et al. 2021
, and corrected for extinction in each filter due to foreground Milky Way dust using

, and the default circumstellar dust composition of 60% Silicate and 40% AlO

(
M. A. T. Groenewegen 2006
;

. PARSEC provides several other options for AGB dust composition with variations in the C, Si, and AlO

We define the RSG selection region as the thick dashed polygon in Figure
1
. The design of the region is intended to minimize contamination from hotter yellow supergiants and cooler AGBs/super-AGBs, and can be explained as follows:

1.

The blue end of the polygon is at F814W
-
F200W=2 mag, in order to restrict to stars cooler than 4000 K, appropriate for K/M spectral type expected for RSGs
(
E. M. Levesque et al. 2005
;
E. M. Levesque 2018
)
. The color boundary should also exclude any Milky Way foreground dwarfs in the direction of our galaxies, based on experiments with the TRILEGAL code
(
L. Girardi et al. 2005
;
E. Vanhollebeke et al. 2009
)
.

2.

Between F814W
-
F200W=2
-
4 mag, the F200W brightness limit is drawn diagonally from
-
8.2 mag to
-
10 mag to form a wedge, which allows inclusion of the lower mass (
<
10 M
⊙
) RSGs with up to moderate levels of ISM extinction (
A_{V}\lesssim 1.5
). However, as one can tell from Figure
1
, the selection of these 8-10 M
⊙
will be incomplete as the wedge is about 0.8 mag brighter than the base of the 8 M
⊙
RSG branch at solar metallicity. This was however a choice made to reduce contamination with sub-solar metallicity AGBs (compare grey points in the left and middle panels, Figure
1
). For the purpose of this paper, we consider our RSG sample to be effectively complete above 10 M
⊙
.

3.

At F814W
-
F200W
\geq
4 mag, we include all stars brighter than F200W=
-10
mag as RSGs, since AGB contamination above this brightness is expected to be minimal based on PARSEC predictions for up to [M/H]=
-
1. The box is also allowed to span redward in F814W
-
F200W up to the 5
\sigma

DOLPHOT
completeness limit (black dashed line, third panel, Figure
1
) in order to be as complete as possible to highly attenuated RSGs, especially above 20 M
⊙
, by either interstellar or circumstellar dust. The latter is expected in a minority of RSGs, typically the most evolved ones, produced by pulsational mass-loss
(
G. Meynet et al. 2015
, e.g.)
.

4.

To enable some discussion about the statistics of older vs. younger RSGs, we define a sub-population of ‘young’ RSGs, mostly with masses above 14 M
⊙
, with the thin dashed polygon region in Figure
1
. We refer to these RSGs in the discussion about spatial distribution in Section
III.4
.

V
Conclusion

We have recovered a catalog of 97057 RSGs—the largest single survey of red-supergiants (RSGs) —in a sample of 19 galaxies with stellar masses between
10^{9}
and
10^{11}
M
⊙
from the PHANGS-HST and PHANGS-JWST surveys. We derived the catalog from
DOLPHOT
PSF photometry in the filters closest to the peak wavelength of RSGs—UVIS/WFC3 F814W and NIRCAM F200W bands. RSGs were selected from the predicted positions of stars cooler than 4000 K and above
>
10 M
⊙
in F814W
-
F200W, as predicted by stellar isochrone models from PARSEC. The region was designed to minimize contamination with AGB stars, foreground dwarfs, and hotter supergiants.

We mainly carry out a zeroth order analysis of the RSG population in this paper, focusing on the number and spatial statistics, as well as correlation with kpc-scale measurements of star-formation rates from the PHANGS MegaTables from
J. Sun 2022
. We find that:

•

The number of RSGs recovered in these galaxies ranges from a few hundred (e.g 453 in IC5332) to over 10
4
(NGC 4303).

•

The distribution of the RSGs closely trace prominent sites of recent star-formation in galaxies, such as central starburst rings, and spiral arms. Younger (
>
14 M
⊙
) RSGs are more strongly spatially correlated with high star-formation sites, such as central starburst rings, than the total RSG sample.

•

The RSG number density is strongly correlated with the FUV+W4 based star-formation rates, with a Spearman correlation coefficient
r_{s}
=0.82. Similarly strong correlation is obtained with SFRs based on H
\alpha
, and only a weak correlation (
r_{s}
=0.57) is obtained with stellar mass.

•

We find a consistent production rate of 10
{}^{3.6}\pm 0.27
RSG per (M
⊙
yr
-1
), or 1 RSG per 10
3.77±0.27
M
⊙
of stars formed between 6-30 Myr in the 19 galaxies, assuming the average star-formation rate during this period given by FUV+W4 based SFRs. The observed rates are slightly lower than predictions of 1 RSG per 10
3.5±0.08
M
⊙
from stellar population models, and could be due to assumptions about star-formation histories, incompleteness in high extinction/crowding regions, and physical factors such as binary evolution.

The paper breaks new ground by pushing systematic searches of RSGs in a large sample of galaxies with distances up to 20 Mpc by leveraging the combined power of HST and JWST NIRCam, a feat that was impossible with previous observatories. Our catalog will be published with the journal, and serve as a valuable resource for characterizing progenitors of future Type II supernovae in the PHANGS sample
(
S. D. Van Dyk et al. 2023
;
C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025
, e.g)
, age-dating and obtaining census of star-formation in the 6-30 Myr range
(
J. J. Eldridge et al. 2020
;
S. Hannon et al. 2022
, e.g)
, and studying correlations of future supernova sites with star clusters, associations and molecular clouds
(
T. Kangas et al. 2017
;
S. K. Sarbadhicary et al. 2023
;
N. Mayker Chen et al. 2023b
;
N. Mayker Chen et al. 2024b
, e.g)
.

In upcoming papers, we will continue refining the catalog with artificial star tests for more systematically characterizing the completeness in different regions of the galaxies, and estimating bolometric properties and line-of-sight extinction
(
K. D. Gordon et al. 2016
;
C. W. Lindberg et al. 2025
)
. Our catalog can be cross-matched with MIRI data for investigation on dust production in RSGs, expanding upon the works by
H. Hassani et al. 2025
and
D. Maschmann et al. 2025
. Future observations using JWST filters near 1
\mu
m, such as F090W and F115W can significantly improve the RSG selection at lower masses (
\lesssim
10 M
⊙
) by not only being higher-resolution blue filters than F814W, but also less sensitive to extinction.
