The influence of exercise duration on inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress in ischemic stroke rats
Authors: Jin Yoo, Eunjae Jang, Jinsu Hwang, Dohee Kim, Jiyun Choi, Sujeong Jang, Han-Seong Jeong, Daeyeol Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke, one of the world’s leading fatal diseases, has a high incidence and recurrence, leading to severe mortality and disability. In this study, we investigated whether treadmill exercise is an important treatment to prevent recurrence and improve functional impairment following an ischemic stroke. Experimental cerebral ischemia by occluding the middle cerebral artery was induced in rats, and the effect of 10- or 30-min training for two weeks was evaluated. To assess for motor function improvement, behavioral tests including the elevated body swing test were conducted. The expressions of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and apoptosis markers were investigated by Western blotting analysis and immunohistochemistry. In both exercise groups (10 and 30 min), motor function improved compared to the non-exercise group. TTC staining demonstrated that the brain infarct volume also decreased after exercise. Further examination of the signaling pathway showed that the expression of ER stress-related proteins, such as IRE1-α, JNK, ERK, and p38 MAPK, decreased significantly in the exercise groups. The pro-apoptotic genes (Bax and pro-caspase3) of the apoptosis signaling mechanism, also decreased in the exercise groups. Interestingly, the level of neuronal markers (NeuN, SYP, and NFH) increased in the exercise groups. Our results suggest that exercise has a beneficial effect following ischemic stroke. In particular, exercise used in short- or long-term training could regulate the signaling mechanisms, such as ER stress, apoptosis, and neuronal cell death protection.Keywords: Apoptosis, Endoplasmic reticulum stress, Exercise, Ischemic stroke

INTRODUCTION

Stroke, a serious manifestation of cerebrovascular disease, is the leading cause of death in the world, second only to cancer [1]. Around 14 million individuals are diagnosed with stroke each year, and one in six people will experience one in their lifetime [2]. Strokes can be categorized as ischemic and hemorrhagic; ischemic strokes account for more than 85% of all strokes [3]. Ischemic stroke is one of the main causes of death and the main cause of permanent disability worldwide [2,4]. Ischemic stroke occurs when platelet coagulation or blood clots block the blood flow to the brain [5]. In addition, a temporary or permanent migration of an embolism that forms in the artery and travels to the brain can also narrow the arterial lumen and limit the blood supply [6,7]. In the brain, disrupted blood flow results in a cascade of molecular reactions that results in cell death. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress is one cell death mechanism [8-10].

The ER is essential for lipid and protein biogenesis in cellular homeostasis [11,12]. ER stress occurs in the presence of prone protein aggregate, when glucose deprivation limits protein glycosylation and hypoxia reduce the production of disulfide bonds, as well as in response to other physiological and pathological situations [13-15]. During stress conditions, when unfolded proteins accumulate in the ER, the dissociation of glucose regulated protein 78 (GRP78) from stress sensor proteins occurs, leading to their activation and an increase in folding capacity [16,17]. Inositol requiring enzyme 1-α (IRE1α), the most widely preserved mediator of the unfolded protein response, is increased in different tissues as a reaction to ER stress. IRE1α comprises the endoribonuclease and serine/threonine kinase domains [18]. Activated IRE1α binds to TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2) and initiates a cascade involving apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). This results in the activation of pro-apoptotic B-cell leukemia/lymphoma 2 protein (Bcl-2)-interacting mediator of cell death (Bim) and inhibition of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 [19]. p38 MAPK phosphorylates and activates C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), leading to increased expression of Bim and Death receptor 5 (DR5), and promoting apoptosis [20]. Additionally, the pro-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 homologues antagonist/killer (Bak) and Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), known to counteract the ER Ca2+-modulating functions of B-cell lymphoma-extra large (Bcl-xL) and Bcl-2 at the ER membrane, interact with IRE1α, leading to its pathological, pro-apoptotic activation [21]. Cerebral ischemia is a pathogenic ER stressor that can stop protein translation and cause apoptosis. In other words, ER is involved in cerebral ischemia [22,23].

Many pathological conditions, such as stroke and neurodegeneration, are characterized by neuronal loss [24]. In the initial stages, stroke induces cellular injury and mortality, resulting in readily observable neurological deficits [25]. The loss of neurons following a stroke is one of the most direct consequences of damage resulting from ischemia and infarction [26]. After a stroke, neuronal markers can become damaged. This phenomenon has been extensively researched [27-29].

To facilitate neurological recovery following a stroke, various techniques, such as medications, exercises, and brain stimulations, are employed [30]. Following a stroke, individuals must engage in a level of physical activity comparable to those of the general population to preserve physical functions and capacities [31]. Quality of life, cognitive function, walking speed and endurance, balance, mobility, and other health outcomes have all been shown to improve significantly when individuals engage in aerobic exercises after a stroke [32,33]. Generally, exercise induces increased capillary volume in the motor cortex and consequently, enhances cerebral blood flow under normal conditions [34]. This indicates that angiogenesis is promoted through exercise. Exercising after a stroke can also attenuate inflammatory processes, reduce apoptotic marker expression, facilitate brain angiogenesis, enhance the expression of growth factors, and improve the activation of affected muscles [35]. Therefore, exercise conditioning is thought to be beneficial for reducing the negative effects of ischemia and improving a variety of cognitive functions, including motor functions [36,37]. Previous studies suggest that treadmill training promotes functional over ground mobility in individuals with chronic stroke and stable hemiparesis [38]. Moreover, in metabolic and chronic disease models, exercise training has been shown to reduce ER stress markers, ER stress-mediated inflammation, and apoptosis [39]. Furthermore, regular exercise can interrupt ER stress [40]. Importantly, treadmill exercising after middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAo) increases neuronal markers [22].

In our previous study, we confirmed that post-stroke exercise reduces cell apoptosis [41]. To date, the mechanism of whether exercise reduces ER stress-related factors after stroke is unknown. This study aimed to demonstrate the effects of aerobic exercise in the MCAo rat models. To assess the benefit exercise, we investigated different durations of exercise training in ischemic stroke rat models. In particular, we investigated the regulation of apoptosis and neuronal recovery following reduced ER stress.

METHODS

Animals

All animal experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the Chonnam National University (committee: Chonnam National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, approval number: CNU IACUC-H-2022-36, date of approval: 4 May 2022). Twenty-four male Sprague–Dawley rats (weight, 280–320 g; age, 8 weeks; sex, male) were purchased from Damul Science. Subjects were maintained in an animal facility under constant environmental conditions (room temperature, 20°C ± 2°C; relative humidity, 50% ± 10%; and a 12 h light–dark cycle). Surviving animals from the control (sham, n = 12) and MCAo groups (n = 52; 39 remained after exclusion) with comparable behavioral scores and body weights were randomly allocated to the MCAo or MCAo with exercise groups (n = 13 per group). They were assigned to the sham-surgery (Sham, n = 12), ischemia group (MCAo, n = 13), ischemia and 10 min of exercise (MCAo + 10Ex, n = 13), ischemia and 30 min exercise group (MCAo + 30Ex, n = 13). Behavioral assessments were carried out on five occasions across a 14-day period following MCAo, with 13 rats assigned to each group. Brain tissues were subsequently designated for analysis, with three allocated for protein quantification, three for RNA analysis, three for triphenly tetrzolium chloride (TTC) staining, and four for immunohistochemical evaluation. Upon completion of the 14-day experimental period, animals were euthanized and tissue samples were harvested for immunohistochemistry (IHC), Western blotting, and PCR analyses. Every effort was made to lessen the suffering of the animals.

Ischemic stroke animal model

To study ischemic stroke in rats, we developed MCAo model in accordance with previous reports [42-44]. For the surgery, we anesthetized the animals with an intraperitoneal injection of pentothal sodium (50 mg/kg, JW Pharmaceuticals). Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane USP (Troikaa Pharmaceuticals Ltd.) during the operation. The common carotid artery (CCA), internal carotid artery (ICA), and external carotid artery were exposed by making an incision in the center of the neck with surgical scissors. To obstruct the MCA's origin, a blunt tip filament (L.D-403556PK10; Doccol Corporation) was advanced to the ICA from the left CCA. The filament was secured by a knot and the animal was allowed to wake up from anesthesia. After 45 min of occlusion, during which time the rat was kept in a heated cage (25°C), it was re-anesthetized to enable filament withdrawal and proper closure of the incision, allowing for reperfusion. The rats in the Sham group were subjected to the same procedure, but no incision was made in the CCA and no filament was inserted.

Exercise training

To assess whether exercise training is beneficial after an ischemic stroke, the animals underwent treadmill training, which was explained in our previous study [41]. Before surgical procedure, all rats underwent pre-training with 6 m/min for 10 min on the treadmill (Treadmill, Version 10, B.S. Technolab Inc.) for two days. The main exercise program conducted with on the treadmill for 10 min (MCAo + 10Ex) or 30 min (MCAo + 30Ex) at a pace of 12 m/min, five times per week, for two weeks. During the training period, the sham and MCAo groups did not do any exercise.

Elevated body swing test (EBST)

The EBST, also known as ‘the tail suspension test’, indicates asymmetrical motor behavior and laminarization of the lesion [43]. All animals underwent EBST. EBST was performed by following the original description by Borlongan et al. [45] with modification [43]. Each rat was put in a transparent cage and allowed to attain a neutral position, with all four paws on the ground. It was then elevated by the base of the tail, to approximately 10 cm from the bottom of the cage. The direction of the first body swing, i.e., a > 10° bending of the upper body out of the vertical axis to either side, was recorded. The animal was placed back on the ground of the cage before attempting another swing. The procedure was repeated 10 times and the proportion of right- and left-side swings were calculated. The overall number of swings made to the left side was divided by the total number of swings [35].

TTC staining

TTC staining was used to measure the cerebral infarct size. Under anesthesia, the brain was dissected, cooled in ice water for five minutes, and sliced into 2 mm slices using a rat brain matrix (RBM-4000; ASI Instrument Inc.). The slices were soaked for 30 min in 2% TTC solution (Sigma Chemical Co.) at 37°C for 30 min and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Chuncheon Bioindustry Foundation) overnight [46]. All the brain tissues were photographed by a camera and the infarct volume was calculated using ImageJ software.

Perfusion and tissue collection

Two weeks after surgery, the rats were injected with an over-dose of sodium pentothal (75 mg/kg) and perfused transcardially, followed by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, LPS Solution Co. Ltd.) or 4% PFA. The brain from the rats perfused with PBS were removed and stored at –80°C for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blotting analysis. The brains perfused with PFA were removed and post-fixed in the same fixative for 24 h. Then, they were placed in a 30% sucrose solution for the IHC [47].

qPCR assay

After cardiac perfusion using PBS, the brain was dissected out and sonicated using Trizol (Takara Bio Co.) as following the protocol. For qPCR analyses, cDNA was synthesized according to manufacturer’s instructions with a PCR instrument (LightCycler 480 II; Roche Holding AG) and SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara Bio Co.) at the suggested annealing temperature 60°C [48]. All primers listed in Table 1 were purchased from Bioneer and CosmoGenetech [49].

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

TUNEL staining was performed on prepared tissue sections using the DeadEnd Colorimetric TUNEL System (Promega Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [41]. Stained sections were imaged using a light microscope (ZEISS Axio Vert.A1; Carl Zeiss) at 40× magnification. TUNEL-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Western blotting analysis

A tissue lysate buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors was added and sonicated as described in our previous study [47]. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of proteins (15 μg) were separated on 7 or 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies such as GRP78 (1:1,000), p-IRE1-α (1:1,000), IRE1-α (1:1,000), p-JNK (1:1,000), JNK (1:1,000), p-ERK (1:1,000), ERK (1:1,000), p-p38 MAPK (1:1,000), p38 MAPK (1:1,000), Bax (1:1,000), pro-caspase-3 (1:2,000), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1,000), Bcl-2 (1:500), Bcl-xL (1:1,000), NeuN (1:1,000), and synaptophysin (SYP, 1:1,000) overnight at 4°C. The membranes were then incubated with the secondary and developed with Immobilon Crescendo Western HRP Substrate (Merck Millipore). The signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Merck Millipore) and a luminescent image analyzer (LAS 4000; GE Healthcare). All antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology and Santa Cruz Biotechnology [50].

IHC

For the histological estimations, the tissue from the sucrose solution were removed and wrapped in tissue paper for a few seconds. The blocks were made in a cryomold (Sungwon Medical Company) with cryo-embedding medium (FSC-22; Leica Biosystems), which was arranged by three brains in parallel. The brain was sectioned coronally in 20 parallel series of 10-μm sections on the charged slides (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.) using Cryostat (Leica CM1860; Leica Biosystems). The sections were incubated with anti-NeuN (1:800, Cell Signaling), anti-SYP (1:200, Cell Signaling), and anti-Neurofilament-H (NFH, 1:100, Cell Signaling) antibodies diluted with 10% normal goat serum and placed at room temperature for 1.5 h. The samples were incubated with a biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature for one hour. The slides were treated by avidin-binding complex-elite (Vector Laboratories Inc.), and then, incubated in a diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.) until the color of the tissue turned brown. Finally, the prepared slides were examined under a microscope (ZEISS Axio Vert.A1; Carl Zeiss) [49]. The immunohistochemical results were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. For NeuN staining, double-stained nuclei were counted manually and the mean number of NeuN-positive cells was calculated. For SYP and NFH, the positive staining intensity was quantified by measuring the DAB-positive area fraction using ImageJ threshold analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 5.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc.). All data expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Difference in EBST was assessed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA with significance levels of *p < 0.05. If mean values between populations showed statistically significant changes, they were analyzed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests. TTC staining, TUNEL assay, Western blotting analysis, qPCR, and IHC data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc tests (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 compared with the MCAo group; ###p < 0.001 compared with the MCAo + 10Ex group).

RESULTS

The effects of exercise training on infarct size after stroke

After establishing a rat MCAo model, the rats in the exercise groups underwent exercise training for two weeks. After performing whole-brain examination following brain extraction, it was observed that the left hemisphere exhibited ischemic lesions in the MCAo group compared to the sham group (Fig. 1A). The size of cerebral infarction in each group after MCAo was measured using TTC staining. The results showed that the infarct size in the MCAo, MCAo + 10Ex, and MCAo + 30Ex groups were 20.06 ± 2.04, 15.88 ± 1.73, and 11.58 ± 2.13 (mean ± SEM, % of total size), respectively. The cerebral infarction volume gradually decreased in the exercise groups (Fig. 1B, C). These findings imply that, when compared to the MCAo group, exercise significantly reduced infarct size and brain damage.

The effect of exercise training on asymmetrical motor behavior

EBST testing was conducted at 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, and 14 days after surgery, EBST tests were performed. Following the exercise training, a left swing was greater in both MCAo + 10Ex or MCAo + 30Ex groups than in the MCAo group. The left swing was significantly higher in both the MCAo + 10Ex or MCAo + 30Ex groups than in the MCAo group at day 10 (*p < 0.05 compared with MCAo). These results demonstrated that MCAo with treadmill exercise increased asymmetrical motor behaviors related to MCAo without exercise (Fig. 2). Additionally, we performed cylinder and rotarod tests to further evaluate motor function; however, the results showed no significant differences between groups, even though a trend toward improved motor function in the exercise groups compared with those MCAo group (Supplementary Fig. 1).

We investigated the gene expression of apoptotic markers using qPCR analyses. The expression of pro-apoptotic marker, Bax, was substantially increased in the MCAo group compared with the exercise groups (Fig. 3). However, the levels of caspase-9 and Bcl-xl did not change in the exercise groups compared with those of the MCAo group.

The effect of exercise training on apoptotic cell death

To assess cell death following ischemic stroke, apoptotic cells were quantified using a TUNEL assay. The number of apoptotic cells was 100 ± 18.78, 237.9 ± 13.97, 162.1 ± 9.28, and 164 ± 6.59 (mean % ± SEM %) in the sham, MCAo, MCAo + 10Ex, and MCAo + 30Ex groups, respectively (***p < 0.001 compared with MCAo; Fig. 4). Ischemic injury markedly increased the number of apoptotic cells, whereas treadmill exercise significantly reduced apoptosis in ischemic rats. These findings suggest that exercise attenuates apoptotic cell death following ischemic injury.

The effect of treadmill exercise on ER-stress signaling

We investigated the protein expression of ER stress-related and apoptosis markers, including GRP-78, p-IRE1-α, IRE1-α, p-JNK, JNK, p-ERK, ERK, p-p38 MAPK, p38 MAPK, Bax, pro-caspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, Bcl-2, and Bcl-xl, using Western blotting analysis (Fig. 5). The ER stress marker, GRP78 showed a decreasing tendency in the exercise groups compared with the MCAo group, although this change did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5A). In the both exercise groups, the expression of the ER stress pathway markers, IRE1-a, JNK, ERK, and p38 MAPK, decreased relative to the MCAo group (Fig. 5B–E). Notably, p-ERK levels were more effectively suppressed in the MCAo + 10Ex than in the MCAo + 30Ex (Fig. 5D), indicating a duration-dependent modulation of ER-stress signaling.

Regarding apoptosis-related markers, Bax expression was significantly elevated in the MCAo group and was attenuated by treadmill exercise (Fig. 5F). In contrast, the level of pro-caspase-3 was reduced in the MCAo group and showed a recovery trend in the exercise groups (Fig. 5G). To directly confirm the occurrence of apoptosis, we further analyzed cleaved-caspase-3 expression. Cleaved-caspase-3 levels were markedly increased in the MCAo group compared with the sham group, whereas both exercise groups exhibited a significant reduction in cleaved-caspase-3 expression (Fig. 5H), indicating that treadmill exercise effectively suppressed apoptosis following ischemic injury. Meanwhile, the anti-apoptotic markers Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl did not show significant differences among the groups (Fig. 5I, J). All uncropped gel images were shown in the supplementary data (Supplementary Fig. 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that short-duration treadmill exercise attenuates ER stress and apoptosis-related signaling after MCAo, with some molecular changes showing trends rather than statistically robust effects.

The effect of treadmill exercise on protective neuronal markers

Neuronal markers (NeuN, SYP, and NFH) were evaluated by IHC (Fig. 6). The number of NeuN-positive cells was 50.18 ± 1.46, 23.36 ± 1.67, 41.77 ± 1.36, and 41.15 ± 2.845 (mean ± standard error, NeuN-positive cells) in the sham, MCAo, MCAo + 10Ex, and MCAo + 30Ex groups, respectively. The number of NeuN-positive cells was increased in the exercise groups. In contrast, the number of NeuN-positive cells decreased in the MCAo group (Fig. 6A, B). While the Neuronal markers, SYP and NFH, exhibited detectable staining in both the MCAo and MCAo + Ex groups (10- and 30-min), a notably higher staining intensity was observed in the MCAo + Ex groups compared to the MCAo group (Fig. 6A, C, D). The SYP- or NFH-positive cells in the other groups were calculated and normalized to Sham group. These results indicated that both of the exercise groups promoted the expression of NeuN, SYP and NFH after ischemic injury.

DISCUSSION

We demonstrated that in ischemic stroke-induced rats, both 10-min and 30-min exercise sessions led to a reduction in the size of cerebral infarction and improved in asymmetrical behaviors. Furthermore, ER stress or apoptosis-related markers (GRP78, p-IRE1-α, IRE1-α, p-JNK, JNK, p-ERK, ERK, p-p38 MAPK, p38 MAPK, Bax, pro-caspase-3, and cleaved-caspase-3) showed a decreased trend after both 10-min and 30-min exercise sessions, whereas the anti-apoptotic or neural markers (Bcl-2, Bcl-xl, NeuN, SYP, GAP-43, and NFH) tended to increased. Our findings suggest that both short-term and long-term exercise training may be beneficial after an ischemic stroke.

Following a stroke, patients commonly experience significant behavioral and functional impairment [51]. To date, effective exercise therapy methods for improving these behavioral deficits have not been firmly established. To reduce cell death and improve functional recovery, swift recovery of the cerebral blood flow in the ischemic brain is critical [52]. The increased blood flow and endothelium vasorelaxation of the brain by exercise result in infarct size reduction and less severe functional defects [53,54]. Exercise has been recognized as an effective rehabilitation strategy for promoting motor and cognitive functional recovery following stroke, notwithstanding the incompletely understood complexities associated with exercise-induced repair of cerebral ischemic injury [22]. Aerobic exercise is known to augment the restoration of impaired memory function in rat post-stroke [55]. In addition, after a stroke, isometric exercise may also improve behavioral function and reduce cerebral infarct volume [45]. While numerous studies have evidenced the effectiveness of exercise therapy in improving behavioral function post-stroke, there remains a need to develop a broader range of exercise therapy methods.

Cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury serves as a trigger for inducing and initiating ER stress [56]. Excessive ER stress induces neuronal apoptosis, thereby exacerbating the neuronal damage incurred during cerebral ischemia [52]. Under these conditions, GRP78 functions as a sensor and triggers the activation of PERK and IRE1α, subsequently leading to the activation of downstream signaling molecules including eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha, X-box binding protein 1 spliced form, and JNK [57]. Furthermore, JNK activation occurs through the dimerization of IRE1, mediated by TRAF2-ASK1 signaling, ultimately leading to apoptosis induction [52]. In MCAo mice, there is an increase in ER stress factors and associated apoptotic markers, which can be mitigated through various methods aimed at reducing ER stress [58]. Exercise after stroke has been reported to reduce ER stress factors and cell death, and the most previous studies have employed 30-min exercise protocols to demonstrate these effects [36]. In the present study, both 10- and 30-min sessions alleviated ER-stress-related signaling, with the 30-min exercise showing a more pronounced suppression of p-ERK and p-p38 MAPK. Therefore, short-duration exercise might more effectively suppress early ER-stress signaling, such as ERK or p38 MAPK, than prolonged exercise.

Initiating aerobic exercise training shortly after stroke surgery can alleviate neuronal apoptosis, reduce motor function impairment, and suppress the activation of astrocytes and microglia [38]. In this study, we found that following two weeks of exercise therapy after an induced ischemic stroke, the exercise groups showed a trend toward decreased expression of GRP78, although this change did not have significant difference. Subsequently, downstream factors of the IRE1-α pathway, including JNK, p38 MAPK, and ERK, were significantly reduced in the exercise group. Furthermore, ER stress-related pro-apoptotic factor such as Bax was decreased following both 10- and 30-min exercise sessions. Although pro-caspase-3 level showed a recovery trend in the exercise groups compared with the MCAo group, additional analysis revealed that cleaved-caspase-s, a direct executioner marker of apoptosis, was markedly increased after MCAo and significantly reduced by exercise. In contrast, anti-apoptotic factors such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl did not exhibit significant changes among the groups. Consistent with these molecular findings, TUNEL staining further demonstrated a significant reduction in apoptotic cells in both exercise groups. Interestingly, although both exercise durations reduced TUNEL-positive cells, the magnitude of apoptosis suppression was comparable between the 10- and 30-min exercise groups, suggesting that even short-duration exercise is sufficient to confer anti-apoptotic effects during the early post-ischemic phase. These results demonstrate that both 10- and 30-min exercise sessions positively impact ER stress after stroke. Interestingly, short-duration exercise appeared to more effectively suppressed early ER stress signaling, whereas longer exercise might promote adaptive recovery through gradual modulation of neuronal cell survival. This pattern suggests a time-dependent transition from acute ER stress inhibition to adaptive signaling that supports cellular survival and metabolic restoration. In other words, brief exercise bouts may primarily act as early-phase protectors against ER stress, while prolonged sessions contribute to sustained recovery and remodeling processes in the post-ischemic brain.

Ischemic strokes lead to brain infarctions, accompanied by cerebral tissue necrosis and focal neuronal injury [26]. Our findings demonstrated a significant increase in neural progenitor cell and neuron counts in both the control and exercise groups. However, the apoptotic cell count decreased solely in the exercise group [59]. This study also observed an increase in neuronal markers, NeuN, GAP-43, SYP and NFH, in the exercise group. Taken together, our findings suggest that exercise may prevent neuronal damage following an ischemic stroke.

The present study has several limitations. First, we did not consider the time effects, such as long-term exercise, because the patient with stroke had difficulties with exercising continuously. Our results support the functional improvement of mild-stroke after exercise. Second, we utilized animal models, so that clinical research studies are warranted in the future. Finally, we only considered the effect of aerobic exercise. We expect that different outcomes may arise from other forms of exercise.

Post-stroke exercise can reduce and regulate ER stress-related and apoptotic factors. In the 10-min exercise group, the downstream factors of ER stress, such as JNK, ERK, and p38 MAPK, decreased, while in the 30-min exercise group, apoptotic factors, such as Bax and Caspase-3, decreased. Our results suggest that treadmill exercise reduces ER stress and apoptosis factors post-stroke while increasing neuronal markers. In the future, further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved in modifying ER stress, apoptosis, and neuronal markers during exercise therapy.
