Near Optimal Code Construction for the Adversarial Torn Paper Channel With Edit Errors
Maria Abu-Sini, Reinhard Heckel

Abstract
Motivated by DNA storage systems and 3D fingerprinting, this work studies the adversarial torn paper channel with edit errors. This channel first applies at most t e t_{e} edit errors (i.e., insertions, deletions, and substitutions) to the transmitted word and then breaks it into t + 1 t+1 fragments at arbitrary positions. In this paper, we construct a near optimal error correcting code for this channel, which will be referred to as a t t -breaks t e t_{e} -edit-errors resilient code. This code enables reconstructing the transmitted codeword from the t + 1 t+1 noisy fragments. Moreover, we study list decoding of the torn paper channel by deriving bounds on the size of the list (of codewords) obtained from cutting a codeword of a t t -breaks resilient code t ′ t^{\prime} times, where t ′ > t t^{\prime}>t .

3.2
Key Ideas Behind the Proofs of Theorems
1
and
3

To demonstrate the key ideas behind our proofs, we recall first how a codeword
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in{\cal C}^{b}_{t}
is reconstructed in the paper
[
42
]
from its error-free fragments
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t+1}
. To reconstruct
{\boldsymbol{c}}
we iteratively go over all levels, and for each level
\ell
we recover its hash values
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{N^{\ell}}
. Once a hash value
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{i}
is known, we search for a fragment
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}
that has length-
\left|{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\ell}_{i}\right|
substring with hash value
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{i}
. Then we affix
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}
to align its substring on the position of block
{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\ell}_{i}
. The hash values are chosen wisely so that every two substrings of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
have distinct hash values, hence the affixing procedure recovers
{\boldsymbol{c}}
correctly.

Next, assume the fragments
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t+1}
are obtained from at most
t_{e}
edit errors. Then, the affixing procedure explained above is not guaranteed to succeed anymore, for an error in fragment
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}
might result in substring
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}
with hash value
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{1}
, though
{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\ell}_{1}
is not a block of
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}
. Furthermore, two errors in
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}
might result in two consecutive non-overlapping substrings
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}
that have the hash values
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\ell}_{N^{\ell}}
, respectively. In this case, it is not clear where to affix
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{j}
, at the beginning or at the end of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. We resolve this, in Subsection
3.3
, using the idea of finding
longest matching
from the papers
[
6
,
15
]
, and
[
18
]
. Indeed this technique gives a near optimal

-breaks
t_{e}
-edit-errors resilient code. However, it results in decoding run-time complexity
\Omega\left((t+1)!\right)
as it requires going over all possible fragments’ concatenations.
Therefore, in the case of substitutions solely, as stated in Theorem
3
, we optimize the decoder and avoid going over all
(t+1)!
concatenations. The optimized decoder is provided in the proof of Theorem
3
in Section

. In a future work, we plan to optimize the decoder in the presence of insertions and deletions too.

In Theorem

\left\{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in{\cal C}:S\in B^{b}_{t^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}\right)\right\}

may be generated. This section investigates the maximum possible size of such a list, defined by

L\left(t,t^{\prime},n\right)\triangleq\max_{{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:B^{b}_{t}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}\right)\cap B^{b}_{t}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}^{\prime}\right)=\emptyset}\left|B^{b}_{t^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}\right)\cap B^{b}_{t^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{c}}^{\prime}\right)\right|.

Notice that
L\left(t,t^{\prime},n\right)\leqslant\left(t^{\prime}+1\right)!
, for the codewords are obtained by some concatenations of the
t^{\prime}+1
fragments. Theorem
13
improves this observation.

The key idea behind Theorem
13
is that a potential codeword
{\boldsymbol{x}}
may be obtained by some concatenation of the fragments, such as
{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i_{1}}|{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i_{2}}|\cdots|{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i_{t+1}}
. Thus, assume we have a decoder
{\cal D}_{{\cal C}}
of the code
{\cal C}
, which gets a set of
t+1
fragments and finds whether there exists a codeword corresponding to the
t+1
fragments. If there exists a suitable codeword,
{\cal D}
outputs it as well. Then, we can recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
by executing
{\cal D}
on the set of fragments

\left\{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i_{1}}|\cdots|{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i_{t^{\prime}-t+1}}\right\}\cup\left\{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i_{j}}:t^{\prime}-t+2\leqslant j\leqslant t^{\prime}+1\right\}.
Similarly,
{\boldsymbol{c}}
may be obtained from concatenating the second or the third length-
\left(t^{\prime}-t+1\right)
sequence of fragments instead. This intuition may be developed to derive Theorem
13
.

Theorem 13

.

For every
t,t^{\prime},n\in\mathbb{N}
where
t<t^{\prime}
, it holds that

L\left(t,t^{\prime},n\right)\leqslant\binom{t^{\prime}+1}{t^{\prime}-t+1}\frac{\left(t^{\prime}-t+1\right)!}{t+1}.

By Theorem
13
, if
t^{\prime}=t+c
for some constant
c
, i.e., only few more breakings happened compared to the correcting capability of
{\cal C}
, then
L\left(t,t^{\prime},n\right)=O\left(t^{c-1}\right)
.
Lastly, Theorem
14
establishes a lower bound on
L\left(t,t^{\prime},n\right)
.

Theorem 14

.

For
t,t^{\prime},n
such that
t^{\prime}\geqslant\max\left\{t,6\right\}
and

n\geqslant\left(t^{\prime}+1\right)\left(3+2\left\lceil\log\left(2\left(t^{\prime}+1\right)\right)\right\rceil\right),

it holds that
L\left(t,t^{\prime},n\right)\geqslant t^{\prime}+1
.
