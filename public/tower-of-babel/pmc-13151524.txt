Cardiopulmonary exercise testing before lung resection surgery: still indicated? Evaluating predictive utility using machine learning
Authors: Ákos Filakovszky, Kristian Brat, Thomas Tschoellitsch, Stepan Bartos, Andrej Mazur, Jens Meier, Lyle Olson, Ivan Cundrle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleDespite significant advances in patient care and outcomes, criteria for cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) in risk stratification guidelines for lung resection have not been updated in over a decade. We hypothesised that CPET no longer holds additional predictive value for postoperative complications.MethodsIn this secondary analysis, we included lung resection candidates from two prospective, multicentre studies eligible for CPET and assessed with preoperative pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and arterial blood gas analysis. Postoperative pulmonary (PPCs) and cardiovascular complications (PCCs) were documented during hospitalisation. We trained five types of machine learning models applying nested cross-validation to predict complications and compared predictive performance based on four metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC).ResultsA total of 497 patients were included. PPCs developed in 71 (14%) patients. Adding CPET parameters to PFTs and baseline clinical data did not improve the ability of models to predict PPCs in unselected patients (AUC-ROC=0.72–0.78; p=0.47), nor in those meeting American College of Chest Physicians (ACCPs) (n=236; AUC-ROC=0.64–0.78; p=0.70) or European Respiratory Society/European Society of Thoracic Surgery (ERS/ESTS) criteria (n=168; AUC-ROC=0.59–0.76; p=0.92). PCCs developed in 90 (18%) patients. CPET parameters likewise did not improve model performance for the prediction of PCCs in unselected patients (AUC-ROC=0.65–0.73; p=0.96), nor in the ACCP (AUC-ROC=0.61–0.73; p=0.82) or ERS/ESTS subgroups (AUC-ROC=0.62–0.69; p=0.87).ConclusionsIn contemporary surgical practice, CPET did not improve the predictive performance of machine learning models for PPCs or PCCs in patients with an indication based on established guidelines or in those without. The role of CPET in preoperative risk stratification for lung resection should be re-evaluated.Trial registration numberNCT03498352, NCT04826575.

Introduction

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) complements conventional ergometry by acquiring the respiratory parameters of patients to evaluate functional reserves. CPET is part of both the European Respiratory Society/European Society of Thoracic Surgery (ERS/ESTS) and American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) guidelines in which oxygen consumption (VO2) at peak exercise is used for perioperative morbidity and mortality risk stratification of lung resection candidates.1 2 However, CPET is costly, not always available and burdened by low patient compliance.3

Over the last decade, multiple advances have been made in thoracic surgery, including the introduction of video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS),4 the growing adoption of smaller anatomical (segmentectomy)5 and extra-anatomical (wedge) resections,6 the early recovery after thoracic surgery bundle7 and new anaesthesia techniques,8 which have led to improved patient care and outcomes.9 As a result, the role of peak VO2 in preoperative assessment has been called into question, while the ventilatory efficiency slope (VE/VCO2 slope) has been recently recognised as an independent predictor of postoperative pulmonary (PPCs)1012 and cardiovascular complications (PCCs).12 Furthermore, the partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) at rest has been suggested as a surrogate for the VE/VCO2 slope.11

Significant improvements in patient outcomes and care stand in contrast to the outdated guidelines (eg, considering only major anatomical resections and peak VO2 for risk stratification), which have not been updated since publication in 2009 and 2013.1 2 Lower postoperative morbidity and mortality, as well as new alternatives (especially the VE/VCO2 slope) for the prediction of postoperative complications in lung resection candidates, may have altered the role of CPET in preoperative evaluation. We hypothesised that, in the current surgical era, the added value of CPET for the prediction of postoperative complications is limited compared with baseline clinical data paired with pulmonary function tests (PFTs).

Physiological and pathophysiological processes, including those contributing to the occurrence of complications, are often complex, multifactorial and nonlinear.13 Supervised machine learning methods are characterised by the ability to learn from complex patterns and produce models that can predict complications.14 Accordingly, we aimed to use machine learning models to evaluate the predictive value of basic clinical data combined with PFTs compared with the same with the inclusion of CPET parameters in (a) unselected patients and (b) patients for whom CPET was indicated before lung resection surgery according to ACCP (predicted postoperative forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) or diffusing lung capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO)<30%) or ERS/ESTS (FEV1 or DLCO<80%) guidelines.

Methods

Patients

This is a secondary analysis of two prospective, multicentre studies conducted at medical facilities in the Czech Republic (St. Anne’s University Hospital, Brno, and University Hospital, Brno). Included individuals were adult patients undergoing lung resection surgery due to the presence of a proven or highly suspected lung tumour who were also capable of undergoing CPET. The exclusion criteria were the presence of an inoperable tumour (lung resection not performed) and/or a contraindication for lung resection due to a low peak predicted postoperative VO2 (<35% or <10 mL/kg/min).2

Pulmonary function tests

All patients underwent PFTs following ERS and American Thoracic Society (ATS) standards.15 Spirometry was performed using the ZAN100 device (nSpire Health, Longmont, USA), and measurements of diffusing lung capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) were obtained using the PowerCube Diffusion+equipment (Ganshorn Medizin Electronic, Niederlauer, Germany). Analysed spirometry measurements were forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC), both recorded as a percentage of the predicted value.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

Before surgery, all patients completed a symptom-limited CPET.11 The examination was conducted using an electronically braked bicycle ergometer (Ergoline, Ergometrics 800, Bitz, Germany) equipped with a 12-channel electrocardiograph unit (AT-104, Schiller AG, Baar, Switzerland). The difference in heart rate between peak exercise and rest (∆HR) was included as a measure of chronotropic response. Exhaled gases and ventilatory volumes were analysed using the PowerCube-Ergo cardiopulmonary exercise equipment (Ganshorn Medizin Electronic, Niederlauer, Germany). The following CPET parameters obtained at peak exercise were included in our analysis: sex-adjusted oxygen uptake (VO2), calculated by normalising peak VO2 using sex-specific thresholds16; carbon dioxide output (VCO2), respiratory exchange ratio (RER); tidal volume (VT); breathing frequency (fb); minute ventilation (VE); ventilatory efficiency slope (VE/VCO2 slope); dead space to tidal volume ratio (VD/VT) and partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2). Resting PETCO2 was included as part of our extended analysis. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analyses were carried out with the ABL90 Flex Plus analyser (Radiometer Medical ApS, Copenhagen, Denmark) at the conclusion of the resting period and during peak exercise. Analysed parameters included partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) and pH.

Pulmonary postoperative complications

Pulmonary complications were defined based on STEP-COMPAC17 and according to prior studies in thoracic surgery.1011 1820 They included: pneumonia (identified by the presence of chest X-ray infiltrates along with typical symptoms: fever, leucocytosis/leucopenia and purulent sputum), atelectasis (determined by chest X-ray and confirmed from bronchoscopy), respiratory failure (requiring either non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation) and adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) identified by bilateral chest X-ray infiltrates and arterial partial pressure of oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen ratio below 300 mm Hg21). Complications were prospectively assessed within 30 days of hospital care after surgery. In addition, the duration of stay in the intensive care unit and the overall length of stay in the hospital were recorded.

Postoperative cardiovascular complications

Cardiovascular complications were defined based on STEP-COMPAQ22 and previous studies.12 23 They included: newly developed arrhythmias, such as atrial fibrillation and supraventricular tachycardia lasting for at least 10 s;24 pulmonary oedema (determined from chest X-ray findings and clinical signs); hypotension (indicated by administration of catecholamines and/or parenteral fluids exceeding 200 mL/hour); myocardial infarction or myocardial lesion (diagnosed from coronary angiography and/or presence of new ECG abnormalities accompanied by pathological troponin levels in the blood); heart failure (identified by elevated serum brain natriuretic peptide levels and/or administration of inotropic agents25); pulmonary embolism (confirmed by CT-angiography or the presence of highly suspicious signs of right heart dysfunction on echocardiography); stroke (characterised by new neurological deficit and CT evidence of brain infarction of either ischaemic or haemorrhagic origin26) and cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

Statistics

To evaluate the predictive value of CPET parameters, we devised two profiles common in clinical practice: First, the ‘Standard’ profile included patients’ demographic and basic clinical data, together with ABGs and PFTs. Second, the ‘Standard+CPET’ profile included all variables from the ‘Standard’ profile extended by CPET parameters. We provide a detailed overview of the included variables for both profiles in online supplemental table E1.

We trained five different types of machine learning models (support vector machine,27 logistic regression, neural network,28 random forest,29 XGBoost30) in a fivefold nested cross-validation on the ‘Standard’ and ‘Standard+CPET’ profiles to predict PPCs and PCCs. We selected five established model types to ensure that our results are representative across multiple methods. In each of the five outer-loop iterations of the nested cross-validation, one fold served as test data (20%) while the remaining folds formed the training data (80%). All preprocessing steps (including missing value imputation) were performed based on information from the training data of the respective iteration to avoid any test data leakage.

We conducted an exhaustive grid search, which constituted the inner loop of the nested cross-validation, across extensive hyperparameter spaces to find the optimal hyperparameter configuration for each model. We detailed the explored hyperparameters in the online supplemental appendix. Optimal hyperparameter selection was based on the F1-score to account for class imbalance.

To evaluate predictive performance, we used the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), the area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), the F1-score and the Net Reclassification Index (NRI),31 all computed for each model and fold. We also evaluated predictive performance in selected patients for whom CPET was indicated according to either ACCP or ERS/ESTS guidelines.1 2 For each metric, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to assess whether the ‘Standard+CPET’ profile resulted in a statistically significant improvement in predictive performance compared with the ‘Standard’ profile. We used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test because it is a non-parametric method that does not assume normality and is suitable for comparing paired observations, such as the AUC-ROC obtained from the two profiles. A p<0.05 was considered significant. The hypothesis tests were carried out using the SciPy library32 (V.1.11.2, SciPy developers, 2023) in Python (V.3.11.0, Python Software Foundation, 2022). We provided additional metrics for each model type in online supplemental table E2 and table E16.

To assess the importance of the variables included in the ‘Standard+CPET’ profile for the predictions of the best-performing model type (based on the highest average values across performance metrics for PPC and PCC predictions), we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).33 The SHAP value indicates the importance of a variable by quantifying the extent of its contribution to individual model predictions. This means that a variable with a high SHAP value (whether positive or negative) has a great influence on the predictions obtained. A detailed description of how we performed the SHAP analysis is found in the online supplemental appendix.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 497 patients were included. Postoperative complications occurred in 132 (26.6%) patients. General patient characteristics are summarised in table 1. Lobectomy via thoracotomy was more frequent, and the median surgery time was longer in patients who developed PPCs or PCCs. Both length of stay at the hospital and the intensive care unit, as well as 30-day and 90-day mortality, were higher in patients with postoperative complications. PFT and ABG data are summarised in table 2. Resting ventilatory and CPET parameters are given in table 3. At peak exercise, VO2, VCO2, RER and PETCO2 were lower, and fb, VD/VT ratio and VE/VCO2 slope were higher in patients with either postoperative complication. At rest, PETCO2 was also lower in patients with postoperative complications. Likewise, chronotropic response (table 4) was impaired in patients who developed postoperative complications, with a lower peak heart rate and a smaller heart rate increase from rest to peak exercise (ie, ∆HR). CPET was indicated in 168 patients according to ACCP criteria1 and in 236 patients according to ERS/ESTS guidelines,2 forming the respective subgroups.

Prediction of PPCs

PPCs developed in 71 (14%) patients. Figure 1 summarises predictive performance (AUC-ROC, AUC-PR and F1-score), while figure 2 presents the performance differences between the ‘Standard’ and ‘Standard+CPET’ profiles, along with NRI. The ‘Standard+CPET’ profile did not yield a statistically significant improvement in predictive performance based on any of the metrics assessed (p=0.41–0.84). Similarly, when we only considered patients for whom CPET was indicated according to ACCP or ERS/ESTS guidelines,1 2 the inclusion of CPET parameters did not significantly improve model performance for the prediction of PPCs over the ‘Standard’ profile, with p=0.44–0.79 for ACCP and p=0.28–1.00 for ERS/ESTS (figure 2).

Prediction of PCCs

PCCs developed in 90 patients (18%). CPET parameters did not result in a significant improvement (p=0.96–0.98, figure 2) in any performance metric for the prediction of PCCs (figure 1). There was also no significant improvement for the prediction of PCCs in patients selected for CPET according to ACCP (p=0.59–0.89) or ERS/ESTS (p=0.32–0.93) criteria. These findings suggest that models could not predict PCCs more reliably in unselected patients or patients selected according to the current guidelines,1 2 even when CPET parameters were included.

Variable importance for prediction

For PPCs, SHAP analysis of the logistic regression models identified the type of surgical procedure as the strongest predictor, followed by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and male sex. CPET parameters such as the VE/VCO2 slope and sex-adjusted peak VO2 were also among the most important variables. For PCCs, in addition to age and surgical procedure, the CPET parameter VE/VCO2 slope ranked in the top half of the most important predictors. Resting PETCO2 (not yet routinely used but shown previously to predict postoperative complications similar to the VE/VCO2 slope11) was among the most important variables for the prediction of both postoperative complications. Discussion

The major finding of this study was that the inclusion of CPET parameters in machine learning models for the prediction of PPCs and PCCs in lung resection candidates did not yield a significant improvement in predictive performance, as measured by AUC-ROC, PR-AUC, F1-score and NRI, in either unselected patients or patients for whom CPET was indicated according to previously established ACCP1 or ERS/ESTS2 guidelines.

Our results are based on the evaluation of five different types of machine learning models: We evaluated algorithms that differ in the way they extract patterns and make predictions, as well as nested cross-validation, to ensure that our results are robust.34 In unselected patients, these models were able to predict PPCs with moderate to good ability (mean AUC-ROC ranging from 0.72 to 0.78) and PCCs with moderate ability (mean AUC-ROC ranging from 0.64 to 0.73) based on both ‘Standard’ and ‘Standard+CPET’ clinical profiles.

We acknowledge that an improvement in the ability to predict postoperative complications from CPET parameters might not be detectable in a population that underwent CPET regardless of a guideline-based indication to do so. Accordingly, we also analysed patients for whom CPET was indicated according to ACCP or ERS/ESTS criteria1 2 but again found no significant improvement in predictive performance.

This lack of improvement may not be due to CPET itself but may instead be attributable to CPET indication criteria: These have not been updated since publication, despite advances in thoracic surgery that have led to improved patient outcomes in recent decades.479 Indeed, CPET-derived parameters (such as the VE/VCO2 slope and sex-specific thresholds for peak VO2) have been shown to be associated with PPCs (the major source of postoperative morbidity in lung resection surgery) in multiple studies.

Brunelli et al10 found the VE/VCO2 slope to have a high predictive utility for PPCs, and Brat et al11 confirmed this result in a recent prospective multicentre study. Rushwan et al came to the same conclusion in an independent retrospective analysis and also recommended that the VE/VCO2 slope be included in a new functional algorithm to assess fitness for surgery.35 In our study, the VE/VCO2 slope ranked in the top half of variables contributing to the prediction of PPCs as well as PCCs, as determined by SHAP analysis (figure 3).

Recently, Alfitian et al have found sex-specific peak VO2 thresholds to improve risk prediction for postoperative complications. Such thresholds were not considered by previous studies and may help explain the poor prognostic utility of peak VO2 observed to date.16 In our SHAP analysis, sex-adjusted peak VO2 was in the upper half of the variables for predicting PPCs, further supporting the relevance of these sex-specific thresholds.

Taken together, these observations and our finding that the inclusion of CPET parameters did not significantly improve predictive model performance in either an unselected population or a population selected according to current guidelines suggest that new criteria for deciding when to perform preoperative CPET assessment may be needed: Currently, the indication to perform CPET depends on patient PFTs (DLCO and FEV1) according to both the ACCP and ERS/ESTS guidelines.

The predictive value of PFTs for morbidity (and mortality) has previously been the subject of several studies: With the advent of VATS, Berry et al found that although PFTs remain a significant predictor for complications following thoracotomy, the predictive value for VATS is limited.36 More recently, Dankert et al reported that PFTs did not provide added value for risk assessment in patients with COPD.37

Consistent with these findings, our SHAP analysis showed that PFTs contributed unevenly to the prediction of postoperative complications. While FEV1 ranked among the top half of predictors for PPCs, it did not do so for PCCs. FVC did not appear in the top half of predictors for either outcome. Similarly, DLCO did not rank among the most important variables for predicting either type of postoperative complication.

Consequently, a novel parameter, potentially obtained at rest, may be helpful to select patients for CPET. In a previous study,11 resting PETCO2 was found to be a promising surrogate for the VE/VCO2 slope to predict PPCs. The close association of resting PETCO2 and the VE/VCO2 slope may be attributable to shared underlying physiologic determinants.11 In our SHAP analysis, resting PETCO2 was more important for the prediction of both types of postoperative complications than PFTs. Therefore, we suggest resting PETCO2 may serve as a better indicator for CPET than PFTs. However, subsequent studies will be needed to determine an optimal resting PETCO2 threshold, which might indicate a need for further assessment by CPET.

Limitations

Consistent with other studies,10 12 36 PPCs and PCCs occurred in approximately 14% and 18% of patients, respectively, constituting a clear minority. As class imbalance can bias models toward the majority class, we addressed this by balancing outcome weights, using the F1-score for tuning model hyperparameters and applying unsupervised outlier detection to reduce noise.

Being aware that the dataset size might represent a limitation, we employed nested cross-validation to obtain robust and unbiased estimates of predictive performance. Additionally, we performed a data ablation analysis, reducing the dataset size from 100% to 60%. Predictive performance remained stable down to 65%, with no apparent upward trend and a decline at 60%, suggesting that dataset size was unlikely to be a major limiting factor. We provided details in the online supplemental appendix and figure E3. Nevertheless, we note that the number of patients meeting ACCP or ERS/ESTS criteria may affect the interpretability of subgroup-specific results.

Turning to model types, on the one hand, we included methods with few parameters to estimate (logistic regression and support vector machine). On the other hand, we also included two tree-based ensemble algorithms (random forest and XGBoost), which work well even on small tabular datasets.38

Considering the inherent correlation between certain PFTs and CPET parameters, we applied principal component analysis and derived principal components capturing 80%, 90% and 95% of the variance, replacing the original variables. Even so, none of the performance metrics indicated a significant improvement with the inclusion of CPET parameters (p>0.05). We described this analysis further in the online supplemental appendix and presented results in online supplemental figure E4–E9.

Pre-CPET ABGs at rest were not obtained in 84 (20%) patients, and ABGs at peak exercise in 91 patients (23%) due to refusal or invalid samples. We also did not obtain a measurement of DLCO in 25 (5%) patients. Heart rate measurements were missing in 10 patients (2%).

We compensated for missing values using a regression-based imputation method. To assess the impact of imputation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis, which showed minimal differences across performance metrics, not exceeding 0.05 points, with most differences being close to zero. We detailed all preprocessing and optimisation steps in the online supplemental appendix and also presented the results of this analysis in online supplemental figure E10–E13.

Patients with a predicted peak postoperative VO2 of less than 35% or 10 mL/kg/min were excluded following current guidelines.2 This exclusion could affect the predictive value of VO2, but we only encountered six such patients (1%). Hence, the potential impact on model performance is most likely negligible. Grading of complication severity was not performed.

Low exercise capacity may have influenced the prognostic value of (sex-adjusted) peak VO2, as RER≥1.1 was not achieved at peak exercise in 228 patients (45.8%). However, Chase et al demonstrated that the VE/VCO2 slope retains prognostic utility regardless of exercise intensity.39 Their finding is consistent with its placement among the top predictors in our SHAP analysis. We also found that 112 patients (23%) were unable to increase their heart rate by more than 40 bpm during exercise, indicating chronotropic incompetence. This condition is frequently associated with beta-blocker therapy,40 which was present in 149 patients (30%) and may have affected the prognostic value of the chronotropic response.

Conclusions

According to current guidelines, for lung resection candidates either with or without an indication for CPET, exercise ventilatory parameters and chronotropic response did not lead to a significant performance improvement of machine learning models for the prediction of PPCs or PCCs in patients undergoing lung resection surgery. Updated guidelines should consider re-evaluating CPET indication criteria for preoperative assessment of lung resection candidates.
