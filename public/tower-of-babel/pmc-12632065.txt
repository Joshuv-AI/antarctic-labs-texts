The tale of two assumptions: incorporating healthcare-seeking behaviour in epidemic forecasting
Authors: Marie Varughese, Weston Roda, Donglin Han, Xuyuan Wang, Michael Y. Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundModelling efforts during the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of incorporating human behaviour into mathematical models and the challenges of making accurate forecasts. Case detection is affected by different healthcare-seeking behaviors, including visiting physicians to seek help, which can impact the number of laboratory tests performed and the number of cases identified by surveillance systems throughout an epidemic. Mathematical models for forecasting epidemics of respiratory viruses such as influenza and COVID-19 generally assume a constant rate of case detection, and only a few studies have previously used time-dependent rates.PurposeThis study aims to compare constant and time-dependent case detection rate approaches for the forecast and retrospective fitting of seasonal influenza data.MethodsAn age-stratified Susceptible-Infected-Removed (SIR) model that incorporates case detection for influenza is formulated. Influenza case data and case detection rates for the 2016–2019 seasons in Alberta, Canada, are used for model training. The model fitting results are compared for the constant and time-dependent case detection assumptions. The model forecasting results using partial-season data are compared to the data for the remainder of the season for validation.ResultsWhile both constant and time-dependent case detection rate assumptions allowed an accurate retrospective fitting to the case data of an entire season, the forecasting performance showed a significant difference between the two assumptions. Models with a time-dependent case detection rate accurately predicted the influenza peak time four weeks before the actual peak occurred. The average total infections per case detected, an estimate that includes both under-ascertainment and underreporting, also showed a significant difference between the two assumptions.ConclusionThe incorporation of healthcare-seeking behaviour in mathematical modelling helps quantify the dynamic process of how infections are detected by surveillance systems. This is an important consideration for influenza forecasting. Since not all individuals engage in healthcare-seeking behaviour and only a fraction of those who do seek help may get tested, a proportion of infections remain undetected by the surveillance system. The retrospective forecasting results highlight that a time-dependent case detection rate is more representative of changes in healthcare-seeking behaviour during the influenza seasons than a constant case detection rate. This approach provides a reliable solution for improving forecasts of seasonal influenza and may be adaptable to other respiratory viral infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11940-0.

Background

Mathematical modelling efforts during the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of incorporating human behaviour into mathematical models of infectious diseases and many challenges in making accurate forecasts. Human behaviour implemented in mathematical models for COVID-19 mainly focused on its influence of transmission and mobility [1]. Among behavioural-informed mathematical models for respiratory virus epidemics, the precision of model predictions remains mixed in terms of performance [2]. For respiratory viruses such as influenza and COVID-19, where only a fraction of infections are detected through testing by surveillance systems (also called laboratory-confirmed cases), case detection is an important modelling consideration, which can be impacted by healthcare-seeking behaviour. healthcare-seeking behaviour is broadly defined as any appropriate remedy-finding activity carried out by people who suspect that they may have a health problem or illness [3]. In the modelling context, healthcare-seeking behaviours related to case detection include accessing healthcare providers (in person e.g., primary care or emergency department, online, and by telephone) and/or pharmacies. The linkage of health seeking behaviour to case detection is further complicated by under-reporting; not all health visits related to influenza-like-illness (ILI) will result in a laboratory test. Dynamic change in healthcare-seeking behaviour occurs during the course of an epidemic and is likely to be influenced by the severity of the epidemic, and behavioral changes, in turn, can have a strong influence on temporal changes in case detection within surveillance systems [4].

The incorporation of a case detection rate in mathematical modelling is important for respiratory viral infections such as influenza and COVID-19 where the reported case numbers are much lower than the number of infections. Case detection for influenza and COVID-19 has generally assumed a constant rate or constant fraction in mathematical models [5–9], while only a few studies have adopted time-dependent rates [10, 11] or used non-constant functions related to healthcare-seeking behaviour [4, 12, 13]. The temporal changes of case detection may play an important role in mitigating the impact of parameter nonidentifiability, a key challenge to making accurate forecasts. Nonidentifiability refers to that, for certain model calibration processes, multiple combinations of parameter values can produce the best fit between the model output and the data (e.g. laboratory-confirmed cases), but these different combinations of parameter values can produce significantly different predictions of unobserved model outputs (e.g. total number of infections) [14]. Nonidentifiability can occur in the calibration of a simple SIR model with a constant case detection rate using the data of laboratory-confirmed cases [14]. We believe that a time-dependent case detection rate can help mitigate the impact of nonidentifiability and improve the forecasting accuracy. Other approaches to mitigate the impacts of nonidentifiability include finding additional data sources for model calibration and using simpler models to avoid overparameterization.

Case detection within surveillance systems can subsequently be impacted by under-ascertainment (people who are sick and decide not to access healthcare providers) and underreporting (people who access healthcare providers, but are not tested) [15]. In this study, the average total infections per laboratory-confirmed case detected is an estimate that describes both under-ascertainment and underreporting. (Note: for simplicity, the term under-ascertainment will also include underreporting in this study). Estimation of total infections and under-ascertainment are important objectives of retrospective data analysis and epidemic forecasting. However, these estimates are challenging to validate without additional independent data sources. In addition, disease dynamics related to total infections, transmission rates, severity, and counter-factual scenarios can all be affected by assumptions on the case detection rate [10]. Epidemiological, statistical, machine learning, and mathematical methods are the main approaches used to understand and estimate under-ascertainment. [10, 11, 16–20].

This study aims to retrospectively (i.e. counterfactual analysis) and prospectively (i.e. forecasting) compare a constant and a time-dependent case detection assumption in forecasting of respiratory virus epidemics using mathematical models. A model of the Susceptible-Infected-Removed (SIR) type for seasonal influenza dynamics in Alberta between 2016 and 2019 is used as an example for the comparison.

Methods

Study design

The study aims to use an age-stratified (0 to 18 years, 19 to 64 years, and 65 years and older) SIR model for influenza using case data for the following seasons 2016–2017, 2017–2018 and 2018–2019. Influenza case data is defined as all cases testing positive through laboratory tests. Historical case detection data informed by physician visits and antiviral dispensation related to influenza (analogous to ILI) from 2011 to 2019 are used to help inform the case detection rate (CDR). For both retrospective and prospective (or retrospective forecasting) comparisons, a constant CDR and a time-dependent CDR will be used, while the rest of the model assumptions remain the same. Retrospective analysis of a season will use all available historical data up to the influenza season of the analysis inclusive (e.g., retrospective analysis of the 2017–2018 influenza season used data up to and including 2017–2018). This analysis will assess epidemiological quantities such as the epidemic size (proportion of infected population) and the average total infections per case detected (analogous to under-ascertainment ratios) for both case detection assumptions. Prospective analysis will: 1) use historical case detection data for all seasons prior to the one for retrospective forecasting (e.g., the 2017–2018 season forecast only used case detection data for seasons between 2011–2012 and 2016–2017) and 2) include a fraction of influenza case data from the forecast season for model calibration and the data after the calibration period for assessing the forecasting precision of the peak timing and magnitude under the two case detection assumptions.

Model description

The main purpose of the influenza model is to make forecasts of cases and validate the predictions using surveillance data. This requires that the model includes key processes that capture the complexity of the transmission dynamics of influenza while excluding non-essential processes to avoid overparameterization. The three age groups were chosen to minimize overfitting and were based on age-specific rates of cases and severity [21]. Infected people in compartment (I) are not identified by a positive laboratory test and unknown to the surveillance system. Removed is defined as those people who are no longer infectious with influenza through natural recovery and/or people who are confirmed with influenza infection by laboratory tests (detected or confirmed cases). Incorporating healthcare-seeking behavioural change in the case detection rate (CDR)

The CDR is the rate of removal of infected from compartment I to R by a positive laboratory test. In the Canadian public health systems, a laboratory test is prescribed by physicians which requires a visit to a family doctor’s office or within a hospital setting. Data on the weekly number of physician visits for related to influenza analogous to ILI will capture the type of healthcare-seeking behaviours that directly impact the case-detection, as well as the changes in the healthcare-seeking behaviours during the influenza season. For completeness of the health visit data, we also included data on the weekly number of pharmacy claims of prescribed antiviral drugs for influenza.

For estimating ILI visits, the International Classification of Diseases 9 (ICD9) codes 487 and 488 [27] and health service code 13.59A (intramuscular or subcutaneous injection) [28] were used for physician visits related to influenza. To account for unrecorded physician visits, antiviral prescriptions related to influenza were also included using Drug Identification Numbers ‘02304848’, ‘02241472’, ‘02245549’, ‘02304856’, ‘02381842’, ‘02457989’ (subset of the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code of J05AH02) [29, 30].

Data

The influenza model uses data obtained from Alberta data sources (i.e., Analytics and Performance Reporting Branch, Alberta Health, Canada). Overall historical seasonal patterns of influenza can be observed using publicly available data [21]. We used data from three influenza seasons were obtained: 2016–17 (first wave: influenza A(H3N2)), 2017–18 (first wave: Influenza A(H3N2)), and 2018–19 (first wave: Influenza A(H1N1)) [31, 32]. Influenza confirmed case counts stratified by age group were obtained from the Communicable Disease Reporting and Provincial Surveillance Information Systems (CDRS & PSI). Confirmed cases from the 2016–17 and 2018–19 influenza seasons were restricted to the first 25 weeks for retrospective model fitting since these seasons also involved the circulation of influenza B (2016–17) and H3N2 (2018–19)) subtypes. As the model can only describe the dominant peak, later activity from additional subtypes that were not aligned to the first dominant peak was excluded. Therefore, parameter estimation from retrospective model fitting for the 2016–17 and 2018–19 influenza seasons only included confirmed case data up to week 25.

The Pharmaceutical Information Network (PIN) and the Alberta Health Physician Claims Data were used to obtain information on antivirals and influenza-associated claims. Population estimates were obtained using the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan (AHCIP) Adjusted Mid-Year Population Registry. Data management was performed using SAS 9.4 and Microsoft Excel.

Parameter estimation and model-fitting method

The data and algorithm used for model fitting were identical for the models using a constant and a time-dependent CDR assumption. The initial size of the susceptible population at the beginning of each influenza season was estimated from the AHCIP population registry. Contact rates were estimated using the POLYMOD data [23] recalibrated to the Alberta population [22, 23] (see Table S1). The infectious period was set as one week (γ = 1).

The remaining model parameters were estimated from the fitting of model outcomes to the data. The model fitting for each influenza season was completed using the Bayesian inference-based Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method incorporating the Diffusive Nested Sampling (DNS) algorithm “MatlabDiffNestAlg” [33]. The DNS algorithm is capable of reliably estimating parameters in a high-dimensional space and is also capable of effectively sampling multimodal distributions [34, 35]. The MATLAB implementation of the DNS algorithm, “MatlabDiffNestAlg”, was used with the following settings: five particles; the number of samples needed above the current likelihood cut off to create another level was 1000 with at least 1500 samples per level overall; The probability of observing these data is assumed to follow a negative binomial distribution with a mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu^{j}_{i}$$\end{document}) and variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu^{j}_{i}/p^{j}$$\end{document}) for the jth dataset, the corresponding weekly time points i(week 1 to week 52), and the probability of success per trial pj [36].

Retrospective analysis and prospective forecasting

In the retrospective analysis, the influenza model was fitted to weekly new laboratory-confirmed influenza case data for each of the three influenza seasons: 2016–2017 (25 weeks, see the Data section), 2017–2018 (52 weeks) and 2018–2019 (25 weeks, see the Data section) and included \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\rho}(t)$$\end{document} from the historical seasons up to the analysis season inclusive. For prospective forecasting in a season, the model was fitted to weekly new lab-confirmed case data up to four and two weeks before the actual observed peak. The number of weekly data points used for the two weeks prior to the actual observed peak forecasting is the following: 16 weeks (2016–2017), 14 weeks (2017–2018), and 12 weeks (2018–2019). The number of weekly data points used for the four weeks prior to the actual observed peak forecasting is the following: 14 weeks (2016–2017), 12 weeks (2017–2018), and 10 weeks (2018–2019). All historical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\rho}(t)$$\end{document} prior to the forecasting season were used. This approach simulates the realistic forecasting scenario where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\rho}(t)$$\end{document} data for the forecasting season will not be available. For example, the retrospective analysis of the 2017–18 influenza season included historical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\rho}(t)$$\end{document} data between 2011–12 and 2017–18, as well as case data for the 2017–18 season. Prospective model training for forecasting included \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\rho}(t)$$\end{document} data between the 2011–12 and 2016–17 seasons and partial case data for the 2017–18 season.

Assessment of case detection assumptions

Overall assessment of case detection assumptions included using a goodness-of-fit test, and comparisons of the estimated epidemic size, total infections per case detected, and predictive accuracy.

The goodness of fit of the model to the data used for the fitting was assessed using the Bayesian p-value [37, 38]. The Bayesian p-value quantifies the discrepancies between the data and the model, thus gauging whether these discrepancies could have happened by chance. The χ2 test is used as the discrepancy measure for the Bayesian p-value test as it is a commonly used discrepancy measure for the Bayesian p-value tests. The χ2 discrepancy is the sum of squares of standardized residuals of the data with respect to their true unknown expectations and is a function of the data, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D = \{D_1, \dots, D_m \}$$\end{document}, as well as the unknown θ, for the corresponding weekly time points i(week 1 to week 52) and jth dataset: 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi^2(D, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{j = 1}^{m} \sum_{i = 1}^{n_j} \frac{(d_i^j - \textrm{E}[d_i^j, \boldsymbol{\theta}])^2}{\textrm{Var}[d_i^j, \boldsymbol{\theta}]}.$$\end{document}

The Bayesian p-value, pB, is defined as the probability that the replicated data could be more extreme than the observed data: 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_B = P( \chi^2(\tilde{D}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \geq \chi^2(D, \boldsymbol{\theta}) | D),$$\end{document}

where the probability is evaluated over the posterior distribution of θ and the posterior predictive distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{D}$$\end{document}. If the model predictions fit the data, ideally pB will be close to 0.5. If pB is between 0.05 and 0.95, then the p-value is considered to be in a reasonable range and there is no evidence against the null hypothesis that the model predictions fit the data used for the fitting

The predictive accuracy of the model was assessed by using the posterior predictive distribution, data that was not used for the fitting of the model, and the Weighted Interval Score (WIS) [39–41]. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{D}$$\end{document} be future responses of interest (replicated data) for the dataset D. The posterior predictive distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{D}$$\end{document} is given by 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\tilde{D} | D) = \int_{\Omega} P(\tilde{D} | \boldsymbol{\theta}) P(\boldsymbol{\theta} | D) d \boldsymbol{\theta}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\boldsymbol{\theta} | D)$$\end{document} is the posterior distribution and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\tilde{D} | \boldsymbol{\theta})$$\end{document} is the same probability model for the data specified in the Bayesian framework, which in this study is a negative binomial distribution [38]. The posterior predictive mean is found by taking the mean of the replicated data values produced by the posterior predictive distribution. The 50%, 80%, and 95% prediction interval is found by taking the 25.0 and 75.0, 10.0 and 90.0, and 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles, respectively of the replicated data values generated by the posterior predictive distribution.

The WIS and log transformed WIS was estimated using 11 interval scored (K = 11) for α = 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, … , 0.9 [39]. This was estimated for forecasts two and four weeks prior to the peak and by CDR assumption at 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20 weeks from the start of the 2016–2017, 2017–2018, and 2018–2019 influenza seasons.

The general temporal pattern of changes in healthcare-seeking behaviour, as shown in Fig. 2, appears to increase initially, reach a long plateau, and then decline toward the end of season. This temporal pattern is common across age groups and influenza seasons in Alberta from 2011 to 2019. From data provided by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (see Figure S1), a similar pattern is observed in the fraction of hospital or physician visits for influenza-like-illness (ILI) in every US influenza season from 2016 to 2023 (see Figure S1 and [42]). The observed time dependency of CDR (Fig. 2 and Figures S1 to S4) may be due to changes in awareness of influenza and/or the proportion of people with influenza-like symptoms seeking medical care during an influenza season. The observed plateau could indicate a saturation rate of infected people being detected. Compared to the time-dependent CDR assumption, the constant-rate assumption would lead to an overestimation of case detection at the start and end of an influenza season and an underestimation during the peak period. The use of constant and time-dependent case detection assumptions in the influenza model has significant differences in the estimation of the total number of people infected, the under-ascertainment ratio (or average total infections per case detected) and making accurate forecasts.

Retrospective (or counter factual) analysis

A SIR model has multiple inputs and outputs. Key outputs of the SIR model used in this study include the number of new confirmed cases, epidemic size (proportion of the infected population) and average total infections per case detected (a measure to describe under-ascertainment). For each influenza season (2016–2017, 2017–2018, 2018–2019), the SIR model was fit simultaneously to the age-stratified number of newly confirmed cases. The results of the model fitting for each of the influenza seasons and by age group are shown in Figures S5, S6 and S7. For each influenza season, the total observed number of new confirmed cases for the three age groups (all combined age cases) and the corresponding totals from the calibrated model solutions are shown in Fig. 3. Our retrospective analysis showed that the SIR model using a constant or time-dependent CDR both fit the observed number of new confirmed cases well, as demonstrated by the estimated Bayesian goodness of fit, or Bayesian p-value, for the SIR model-fitting results in Table 1. The forecasting results of the peak time and magnitude of new confirmed cases show better performance consistently by models using the time-dependent CDR assumption. For forecasts providing a four-week lead time before the actual peak time, models using a time-dependent CDR assumption accurately forecasted the influenza peak time within a one-week window of the true peak time (2016–17, 2018–19) or two-week window of the true peak time (2017–18). In contrast, models that used constant CDR predicted peak times that were approximately six weeks after the true peak time for the 2016–17 and 2017–18 influenza seasons. While the mean peak-case numbers were generally comparable for models using both CDR assumptions, a greater 95% prediction interval was observed for models using a constant CDR for the 2016–17 and 2017–18 seasons (the maximum upper 95% prediction interval value was approximately 4.0 and 1.3 times higher compared to models using time-dependent CDR, respectively). The predicted peak time and magnitude were comparable for the 2018–19 influenza season using both forms of CDR, but the maximum upper 95% prediction interval was approximately 1.5 times higher compared to models using time-dependent CDR. Prospective forecasting by age groups at four weeks (Figures S8–S10) and at two weeks (Figures S11–S14) before the actual peak week of cases show similar results. For influenza seasons 2016–2017 and 2017–2018, the absolute and log transformed WIS was generally lower using the time-dependent compared to the constant CDR assumption (all age forecasts). An exception was week 18, where results were inconsistent based on the spread and distance of the data to the mean without additional context of the accuracy of the actual observed and forecasted peak (see Fig. 4 and Table 3). For influenza seasons 2018–2019, WIS results were inconsistent between CDR assumptions due to comparable forecasting results.

Discussion

Quantification of changes in human behaviour during epidemics was identified as one of several challenges in incorporating behaviour dynamics into infectious disease models [46, 47], and it was a central theme of a recent National Science Foundation funding initiative on Incorporating Human Behaviours in Epidemiological Models (IHBEM) [48]. Several modelling studies focused efforts on forecasting influenza by modifying transmission rates over time [8, 49–52]. In our study, a SIR mathematical model with three age groups for seasonal influenza focused on case detection and compared two different approaches: one assumed a constant CDR and the other assumed a time-dependent CDR assumption that incorporated healthcare-seeking behaviour, using public health data from three influenza seasons: 2016–2017, 2017–2018, and 2018–2019. The use of a constant CDR is a common practice in the literature on mathematical modelling of epidemics, and particularly for models of respiratory viral infections [5–9].

In comparing the two approaches, we used the same mathematical model structures, prior distributions for parameter estimation, dataset, and algorithm for model training to produce an accurate evaluation of the differences between important model outcomes under the two assumptions of the CDR. Although the results showed that there was a good fit of the model output to the epidemic curve of the newly confirmed cases under both CDR assumptions (Fig. 3), there were significant differences between the estimated proportion of the infected population, under-ascertainment ratios (Table 2) and the precision in the predicted peak times (Fig. 4). For three consecutive influenza seasons in Alberta, mathematical models with time-dependent CDR generally resulted in a smaller infected population, lower under-ascertainment ratios, and accurate peak time forecasts four weeks prior to the actual peak. In comparison, models with constant CDR had higher estimates of the proportion of the infected population and missed the actual peak time by several weeks in the forecast. This was further supported from WIS estimates for the 2016–2017 and 2017–2018 influenza seasons (see Table 3) and with the log10 transformed WIS estimates generally lower in forecasts using time-dependent CDR for greater than ‘4-week ahead’ forecasts (i.e., ‘4-week ahead’ analogous to forecasts 4 weeks into the future) (see Figure S15). In addition, the log10 transformed WIS estimates were also comparable to existing literature [41].

The challenges of respiratory virus modelling and forecasting including data considerations have been described in the literature [47, 53–56]. The results produced by models with constant CDR in this study are consistent with the recent results of Castro et al. (2020) [53] and Wilke et al. (2020) [54]. The analysis and results of Castro et al. (2020) [53] and Wilke et al. (2020) [54] demonstrated that it is extremely difficult or even impossible to accurately predict peak time and other key epidemic indicators using standard mathematical models with constant rate parameters.

The results of our study indicate that the incorporation of a time-dependent CDR is an important and useful consideration for modelling infectious diseases. It is particularly vital to incorporate a time-dependent CDR for modelling respiratory viral infections, since the case data reported to surveillance systems do not include those that are undetected. The case detection rate is significantly affected by temporal patterns of healthcare-seeking behaviour and behavioural changes during the respiratory viral seasons. Our study explicitly compared the impact of using a model with constant or time-dependent CDR. The overall consistent performance of making accurate forecasts using a time-dependent CDR assumption may indicate a more reasonable estimate of the proportion of the population that is infected. Accurate estimation of the infected proportion is crucial for quantifying under-ascertainment of disease, estimating the cost-effectiveness of interventions (e.g. vaccinations), health system planning, and pandemic preparedness policies.

Our approach of using time-dependent CDR has been developed and applied to seasonal influenza forecasting since 2015. This study is further supported by several recent studies that have used a time-dependent ascertainment rate in mathematical models for COVID-19 [10, 13, 57, 58]. Přibylová et al. (2023) presented a method that used a time-dependent ascertainment rate for COVID-19 models and reported that its performance in forecasting hospitalizations was better than using a constant rate [58]. The results in [58] show evidence that the time-dependent approach in our study can be adapted to the modelling of other respiratory viral infections.

Several studies examining healthcare-seeking behaviours related to respiratory viral infections have described time-dependent factors related to case detection such as testing probability [4] and percentage of people with ILI who sought healthcare [59]. Other studies have described factors associated with increased healthcare-seeking behaviour such as comorbidity [60–63], severity of symptoms [60, 63, 64], gender, [60, 61, 63], having a personal doctor [62, 64], and older adults (65 years and older) [62].

The temporal patterns of the time-dependent CDR in Fig. 2 has a common rise-plateau-decline shape. This shape can be influenced by several characteristics of healthcare-seeking behaviour and the health system such as the following: a) not all people with influenza-like-illness will see a healthcare provider, b) not all provider visits for influenza-like-illness will be tested, c) increased awareness [60] about the respiratory virus season could impact how people access the health system, and d) saturation of health resources can impact how cases are reported to surveillance systems. A constant CDR would average out the temporal changes of detecting laboratory-confirmed cases that are influenced by temporal changes in healthcare-seeking behaviour and testing during the respiratory virus season (see Fig. 5). Further research is needed to provide additional insights to temporal patterns related to ILI and healthcare-seeking behaviour associated with influenza and other respiratory viruses.

In our study, both retrospective and prospective analysis of the 2018–19 influenza season in Alberta showed relatively similar results for models using either a constant or a time-dependent CDR, which was also further supported by the WIS results (see Table 3). This is likely due to the narrow shape of the first influenza A (H1N1) wave, which may have diminished the time-dependent effects of the CDR (see Fig. 3).

A limitation of our study is that the influenza model is suitable for capturing only the dominant influenza strain during an influenza season. This simplification is reasonable for jurisdictions like Alberta, where the influenza seasons typically have a large wave of a dominant influenza strain, followed by a much smaller wave of a different influenza strain or subtype [32]. The simplified model produced an accurate prediction of the peak time and the peak case number for the dominant large wave, which has the greatest impact on health systems. This model also did not include seasonality and assumed a constant transmission rate given our late-August/early-September start and the short duration of a single influenza wave. Our constant transmission assumption did not greatly impact forecasting results since we obtained log10 transformed WIS estimates comparable to existing literature [41]. Towers and Feng (2009) successfully used a time-dependent transmission rate with seasonality in a SIR model to predict the two waves in the 2009 pandemic H1N1 influenza [51]. As future extensions of this work, incorporating time-varying transmission rates into our framework could improve our predictions in Alberta and other jurisdictions including forecasting multiple influenza waves within a season.

Our definition of healthcare-seeking behaviour focused primarily on seeking medical care and subsequent impacts of laboratory testing and assumed that behaviour changes of individuals in I(t), those infected with other respiratory viruses, and noninfected individuals are similar. However, this assumption may be impacted if behaviours are different across other co-circulating respiratory viruses (e.g., COVID-19 and Respiratory Syncytial Virus). Further information can help identify whether other factors should be considered.

Although this study is limited to the influenza seasons of the population in Alberta, all public health data used in our model calibration are available from public health agencies in other jurisdictions. For example, the CDC provides data on the fraction of hospital or physician visits for influenza-like illness (ILI) for the influenza seasons in the United States from 2016 to 2023 (see Figure S1 and [42]), at both national and state levels, which can be used to inform \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\rho}(t)$$\end{document}. Weekly new hospital admissions and death are other available data sources for model training. If hospital admission data is acquired independently of that of laboratory confirmed cases, it can be used to enhance the model training. In Alberta, new hospital admissions and deaths due to influenza is a subset of the lab confirmed influenza cases and we can use historical weekly case hospitalization and death rates to forecast weekly hospital admissions and deaths, respectively. As future extensions of this study, we can use the hospital admission data, which would require incorporating considerations of how cases are triaged as hospitalized into the model. Our study is a case study with a limited scope for proof of principle. We believe that the modelling approach in our study can be adapted to other jurisdictions where influenza seasons have characteristics similar to those in Alberta.

Conclusions

Healthcare-seeking behaviour, captured in the denominator of the crude CDR through health administrative data on physician visits and antiviral dispensations, is an important factor in shaping the temporal dynamics of seasonal respiratory viral infections. This study uniquely compares two different case detection rate assumptions in a seasonal influenza SIR epidemic model. The time-dependent approach in our model resulted in accurate predictions of the influenza peak time four weeks in advance, which can provide significant lead time for health system planning. Given recent work that demonstrated the near impossibility of precisely predicting the peak time of an epidemic using standard epidemic models [53, 54], our paper describes a methodological approach using health administrative data with an epidemic model that helps address challenges in epidemic forecasting.
