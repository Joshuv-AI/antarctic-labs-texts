Determinants of undergraduate bioscience engagement: Validating attendance barriers and classroom spatial behaviour across institutions
Authors: Nigel Page, Rachel J Hunt, Lauren J Albee, Daniel Berwick, Alison Snape
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudent engagement with in‐person teaching remains a key challenge in undergraduate bioscience education, particularly in large‐cohort and commuter‐dominated contexts. This study evaluates a dual‐mechanism framework encompassing macro‐level structural determinants influencing attendance and micro‐level affective determinants influencing in‐class spatial behaviour. Survey data were collected from 891 undergraduate bioscience students across three London universities. Attendance barriers were assessed using ranked‐choice responses across 15 pedagogic factors and analysed using weighted priority scores. Seating behaviour was captured through self‐reported habitual classroom positioning. Non‐parametric analyses examined variation across institutional, commuting, demographic, and health‐related variables. Consistent with the hypothesis, attendance was primarily associated with macro‐level academic and structural factors, with workload management and reliance on recorded teaching emerging as the highest‐priority barriers across all institutions. Institutional differences reflected variation in lecture‐capture provision and teaching practices. Journey length was associated with academic readiness barriers, particularly for medium‐distance commuters, while demographic characteristics showed limited independent influence once structural factors were considered. In contrast, seating behaviour was strongly associated with micro‐level affective factors, particularly social anxiety and sensitivity to crowding. Students reporting these factors tended to select peripheral or low‐density areas, with minimal influence from institutional or commuting variables. These findings support a dual‐dimensional model in which attendance is shaped by structural conditions, while spatial behaviour reflects sensory and emotional factors, with implications for lecture‐capture, timetabling and inclusive learning environments in bioscience teaching.Keywords: attendance, bioscience education, classroom behaviour, learning spaces, lecture capture, student engagement

In large‐cohort bioscience teaching environments, traditional lectures typically serve as the primary vehicle for introducing conceptually complex material that underpins subsequent laboratory and independent study. Optimising learning outcomes requires a clear understanding of how undergraduate cohorts navigate on‐campus education. This dynamic encompasses two overlapping behavioural domains: the multiple factors influencing student attendance decisions, and the spatial positioning strategies deployed by students within the physical teaching environment once present.

While in‐person attendance remains a widely used proxy for academic engagement and is positively associated with superior academic outcomes [1], tracking patterns of attendance in higher education has become increasingly complex, particularly in the post‐COVID‐19 context [2]. Rather than functioning as a direct indicator of student motivation or commitment to study, modern attendance patterns are shaped by a fluid set of strategic and contextual behaviours rather than a simple binary presence or absence. Contemporary undergraduates continuously execute cost–benefit analyses, actively balancing academic workload pressures against commuting barriers, digital learning architectures, and institutional expectations of flexibility.

A growing body of literature on awarding and participation gaps underscores the necessity of isolating systemic structural influences from individualised student variables [3, 4]. Disparities in engagement often trace back to macro‐level institutional structures, including curriculum design, assessment load, timetabling practices, and access to digital resources. The impact of these structural elements can vary across different student groups. For example, they are particularly relevant for commuting students, for whom travel time, cost, associated fatigue, and fragmented timetables can constrain available time and academic energy [5, 6, 7]. Accordingly, demographic variables, including ethnicity, were included in the present study to determine whether engagement patterns were independently associated with demographic characteristics or were better explained by underlying factors.

Within these institutional boundaries, students experience a variety of internal attendance barriers that shape their decisions around physical presence [1, 8, 9]. These barriers often manifest as responses to academic pressure, such as perceived lack of preparation, mismatches in session pace, and the deliberate prioritisation of independent catch‐up study over live lecture attendance. The increasing ubiquity of institutional lecture capture technology has fundamentally transformed this dynamic. Digital repositories permit students to weigh the pedagogical and interactive benefits of live lecture halls against the convenience of remote, self‐paced playback and the immediate mitigation of travel frictions [10, 11, 12, 13].

However, understanding whether students attend only partially captures engagement; equally important is how they position themselves within the learning space once present. Physical attendance constitutes only one dimension of undergraduate engagement. Once inside the lecture theatre, students must navigate the physical and social environment of the classroom, where seating choice impacts cognitive comfort, social proximity and visibility [14, 15]. Spatial positioning represents a behavioural mechanism through which students actively regulate their level of interaction, with some positions promoting high visibility and direct connection with academic staff, whereas others afford a strategic buffer, reduced interaction, and enhanced autonomy over the immediate sensory environment.

While some studies have considered demographic factors, there is limited consistent evidence linking seating behaviour to characteristics such as age, gender or ethnicity. Instead, spatial positioning appears to be more strongly associated with academic performance and motivational factors [14, 16, 17]. Furthermore, factors such as social anxiety and sensitivity to crowding exert significant influence on how students occupy learning spaces [18, 19].

Taken together, this landscape suggests that distinct dimensions of student engagement are governed by fundamentally separate behavioural alignments. Despite these parallel strands of research, few studies have evaluated attendance barriers and classroom spatial behaviour concurrently, or examined how institutional, structural, demographic and health‐related factors intersect across both domains. Indeed, while previous bioscience education research has separately evaluated how physical lecture theatre spatiality impacts peer dynamics and attainment [18] and how digital lecture‐capture repositories influence individual study habits [20], there remains a critical gap in understanding how these physical and digital realities actively interact. This literature gap is acutely apparent in commuter‐dominated contexts, where structural strains like journey lengths intersect with immediate, room‐level psychological pressures like social anxiety. Examining these domains side‐by‐side provides a valuable opportunity to differentiate engagement challenges that are structurally imposed from those that are intrinsically affective.

The present study addresses this gap through a multi‐institutional, hypothesis‐driven investigation of undergraduate bioscience students (N = 891) across three London universities featuring contrasting commuting profiles and teaching infrastructures. The study builds upon earlier work examining external structural factors shaping student engagement that included travel, timetabling, and employment [13], to explicitly contrast external engagement choices against behaviours manifested within the immediate teaching environment.

In this study, we apply a dual‐mechanism framework to test the following hypotheses: (a) macro‐level structural attendance determinants: undergraduate attendance choices are primarily dictated by systematic, structural, and logistical barriers and will operate uniformly across student demographic profiles; and (b) micro‐level affective spatial determinants: in‐class spatial positioning (seating location choice) is fundamentally associated with localised, micro‐level psychosocial and sensory‐regulation conditions (such as social anxiety and crowd sensitivity) and operates independently of macro‐level commuting constraints or institutional campus settings.

By validating these parallel behavioural pathways within a large‐scale, multi‐campus cohort, this study aims to isolate systemic variables from localised institutional cultures. In doing so, we seek to contribute a generalisable framework to guide predictive curriculum design, strategic lecture‐capture policymaking, and inclusive learning‐space architecture within modern bioscience education.

Methods

Research design and participants

A mixed‐methods study [21] was conducted across three London‐based universities: Kingston University London (KUL), King's College London (KCL), and St George's, University of London (SGUL; now part of City St George's, University of London). The study was quantitatively driven, with survey data providing the primary analytic strand to systematically test a dual‐mechanism model of engagement, and qualitative focus groups used to support interpretation of the findings rather than to generate parallel analyses.

An online questionnaire was administered between December 2022 and February 2023. Participants were undergraduate students across all year groups enrolled on bioscience‐related programmes and were recruited via institutionally approved communication channels, including course announcements and email distribution lists. A total of 891 students completed the survey, drawn from diverse undergraduate bioscience programmes. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and based on informed consent.

Data collection

The questionnaire contained 15 possible pedagogic and learning‐related attendance barriers covering workload management, preparedness, learning preferences, lecture capture, and peer and social factors. These attendance barriers were treated as independent categorical reasons for non‐attendance rather than as a psychometric scale. Students were asked to select up to three reasons that best explained occasions of non‐attendance and to rank these selections in order of importance (first, second and third). This format enabled analysis of the relative prioritisation of barriers.

Seating behaviour was assessed using a single item asking students where they ‘always or usually’ sit during on‐campus sessions, with seven response categories capturing habitual spatial positioning (e.g. front, middle, back, side/exit, low‐density areas, no typical location or wherever friends sit).

Demographic variables included gender identity, age group, ethnicity, year of study, journey length to campus, and fee status (Home/Overseas). Health‐related experiences were captured using a multi‐response checklist with an open‐ended free‐text field. Responses were grouped into seven categories: physical ill health, mental ill health, tiredness/fatigue, social anxiety/crowd sensitivity, medical appointments, disability and COVID‐related concern. All variables were coded as binary indicators (presence/absence) to evaluate their respective interactions with macro‐level barriers versus room‐level positioning.

Statistical analysis

Attendance barriers were ranked by respondents and assigned weights (3 = first, 2 = second, 1 = third; 0 = not selected/not applicable). Where duplicate rankings occurred, only the highest‐ranked response was retained. Weighted totals were converted to Percent of Maximum (POM) scores (0–100%) to indicate relative prioritisation rather than intensity. Sensitivity analyses using alternative approaches (unweighted frequencies and inclusion of non‐unique selections) produced consistent rankings, indicating robustness.

Inferential analyses were performed on raw priority scores to statistically validate the hypotheses. Given the ordinal and non‐normally distributed data, institutional comparisons were conducted using Kruskal–Wallis tests, with Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc comparisons. Differences associated with binary predictors (e.g. health‐related variables) were examined using Mann–Whitney U‐tests. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Q = 0.05).

Seating behaviours were analysed using chi‐square tests of independence with standardised residuals and Cramér's V used to assess association strength. Analyses were conducted at both aggregate and institution‐specific levels.

Effect sizes were reported using epsilon squared (ε2), rank biserial correlation (r) and Cramér's V. ‘Prefer not to say’ responses were excluded from percentage denominators. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 unless adjusted by correction procedures. Data preparation and visualisation were conducted in Microsoft Excel (v16.0), and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v31.0).

Qualitative phase and ethics

Focus groups were conducted between March and April 2023 using a semi‐structured protocol exploring learning environment experiences. Groups comprised 4–18 students and were conducted either in person or online. Participants were recruited following the survey phase through institutionally approved invitations. Participants were from the same cohort of students who had previously been invited to complete the questionnaire. Focus groups were used to support interpretation of quantitative findings and further contextualise the validated behavioural mechanisms. Participants reviewed preliminary survey results and provided reflections. Prior to participation, students were reminded of the confidential nature of the discussions and asked not to disclose information shared by other participants outside the session. Sessions were audio recorded, transcribed, anonymised, and analysed using inductive thematic analysis [22]. Following anonymisation, coding was undertaken by researchers who had not facilitated the focus groups. Themes were coded and grouped into higher‐order categories relating to structural, pedagogic, and psychosocial influences.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kingston University's Research Ethics Committee (Reference: 3134‐SADRAS‐02) (November 2022) and the study methodologies conformed to the standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent, and data were handled in accordance with GDPR requirements.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 891 undergraduate bioscience students participated across three institutions: KCL (n = 370), KUL (n = 353), and SGUL (n = 168). The sample was predominantly female (74.2%), aged 18–21 (85.0%), and identified as Asian (46.7%), White (26.0%), Mixed/other (15.8%), and Black (11.5%). Most participants were classified as Home students (82.7%). Journey lengths to campus were categorised as Short (32.3%), Medium (45.2%), and Long (22.5%). Institutional differences in attendance barriers

To investigate potential learning‐related and pedagogical attendance barriers, students were asked to select and rank up to three barriers for non‐attendance in order of priority (first, second, and third). Institutional differences in the prioritisation of these attendance barriers were examined using Kruskal–Wallis tests on the priority scores (0–3). Several high‐priority barriers showed statistically significant differences between institutions, with the largest effect observed for the availability of recordings (ɛ2 = 0.07), reflecting the absence of institutional lecture capture provision at KUL at the time.

Importantly, while statistically significant differences between institutions were detected for several attendance barriers, the relative prioritisation of key barriers was largely stable (Table 2). Institutional effects generally reflected differences in magnitude rather than qualitative shifts in which barriers were most prominent. This consistency aligns with the descriptive Percent of Maximum (POM) comparisons, which showed a similar rank ordering of primary barriers across all institutions.

Significant institutional effects were observed for being behind with studies, sessions being recorded, and academic preparedness (all P < 0.001), as well as lecturer‐related factors (P = 0.013). Post hoc Dunn tests indicated that these differences were driven by specific institution contrasts rather than consistent gradients across all three groups. For example, prioritisation of being behind with studies was significantly higher at SGUL compared with both KCL and KUL, whereas prioritisation of sessions being recorded was significantly lower at KUL than at the other institutions. This pattern in lecture‐capture appears to correspond to differences in provision across the institutions at the time of data collection, with KCL and SGUL operating institution‐wide lecture‐capture systems, whereas KUL did not have a universal lecture‐capture policy. Despite these differences, the overall ranking of high‐priority barriers was consistent across institutions, validating that academic backlog, access to recorded sessions, and session pace remained universally prominent.

The attendance barriers related to social and contextual factors did not differ significantly between institutions; however, social familiarity (not knowing anyone attending) showed a significant institutional effect (P = 0.005), primarily driven by higher prioritisation at KCL compared to KUL. In contrast, other moderate‐to‐low priority barriers including preference for independent study (P = 0.396), session interactivity (P = 0.382), peer attendance (P = 0.375), and confidence in group work (P = 0.347) did not differ significantly between institutions (P > 0.05).

Preference for independent study followed a linear pattern (Long > Medium > Short), with long‐journey students showing significantly higher prioritisation than both other groups (P = 0.048, ɛ2 = 0.010). This pattern corresponded to higher prioritisation of independent study among students with longer commutes. While short‐journey students (0–30 min) showed higher mean scores for lecturer‐related dissatisfaction and certain social barriers (e.g. friends not attending), these did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (P = 0.074).

No significant differences were observed for motivation, perceived session pace, or reliance on recorded teaching (P > 0.05), indicating broadly similar patterns across journey groups. Institution‐specific analyses showed that preparation‐related effects were consistent across sites. Reliance on recorded teaching varied in magnitude between institutions but was highest among long‐journey students in all cases.

Overall, journey length was associated with differences in the intensity of barrier prioritisation rather than qualitative differences in barrier type.

The interplay of demographics and commute

Ethnicity was significantly associated with journey length (χ2(2, N = 840) = 50.32, P < 0.001), with minority ethnic students more frequently represented in medium‐ and long‐journey cohorts. White students were more likely to reside within 30 min of campus. Once stratified by journey length, ethnicity showed limited independent association with attendance‐barrier profiles. Mean priority scores indicated that the primary drivers Behind/Catching Up and Sessions Recorded remained consistent across ethnic groups within journey‐length categories.

Preference for independent study increased with travel time across all ethnic groups. Stratified analyses did not identify statistically significant ethnicity‐based differences for primary attendance barriers after adjustment. The only notable exception was Confidence in Group Work, where differences were observed within the long‐journey cohort (P = 0.0007). Specifically, White students with long commutes were the most likely to report group work anxiety as a barrier (POM = 12.22%), whereas its relevance dropped to near‐zero for Asian (1.06%) and Black students (0.00%). Overall, attendance‐barrier profiles were broadly consistent across ethnic groups once accounting for journey length.

Additional demographic predictors

Attendance‐barrier profiles were largely consistent across gender, age group, and Home/Overseas status, and year of study. Academically oriented barriers and reliance on recorded teaching showed the highest levels of endorsement across all groups. Residency status (Home/Overseas) was initially associated with subject enjoyment (P = 0.050); however, this association appeared to be explained by differences in journey length, with overseas students reporting shorter commutes (χ2(2, N = 891) = 72.24, P < 0.001). Once stratified, no significant differences remained, validating the primacy of the structural commute variable.

Gender, age and year of study showed independent associations not explained by commute patterns. Female students showed greater sensitivity to session pace (P = 0.020), while younger students (18–21) prioritised social factors, such as friends not attending (P = 0.022), more than older students. Furthermore, academic progression from foundation to final year introduced a distinct increase in concerns regarding catching up (P = 0.008), whereas early‐stage confidence in understanding the subject declined (P < 0.001). Aside from these specific differences, attendance barriers were broadly consistent across demographic groups.

Health‐related attendance barriers

Of the seven self‐reported health‐related factors analysed, each coded as a binary indicator (present/absent), two (social anxiety/crowding sensitivity and tiredness/fatigue) showed significant associations with specific attendance barriers following Benjamini–Hochberg correction (P < 0.05, Table 4, Fig. 2). These factors were small but consistent. Students reporting social anxiety and crowding sensitivity showed lower confidence in group work (P = 0.037, r = −0.09) and higher prioritisation of independent study (P = 0.043). Students reporting tiredness/fatigue showed higher endorsement of not knowing anyone attending (P = 0.043). Other health factors, including mental health, physical health, disability, and COVID‐related concerns, were not significantly associated with specific barriers. Overall, health‐related factors were associated with specific social and attendance‐related barriers rather than general attendance patterns.

Summary of attendance barrier patterns

Across analyses, attendance barriers were most frequently associated with academic workload and access to recorded teaching, with similar ranking patterns observed across institutions and demographic groups. Journey length and institutional context showed the strongest associations with variation in barrier prioritisation, particularly for academically oriented barriers. Increasing year of study introduced developmental shifts, marked by increasing catch‐up pressures and a decline in confidence. Health‐related factors were associated with selected social and attendance‐related barriers. Gender, ethnicity, and residency status showed limited independent effects once structural factors were considered. While minor differences were observed for gender (sensitivity to session pace) and age (social motivation), overall patterns of attendance barriers were consistent across demographic groups (Table 5).

Overview of seating behaviours across institutions

Students reported their usual seating location within on‐campus teaching sessions by selecting one of seven predefined categories across the three participating institutions (Table 6). The most frequently selected positions were the middle of the room (32.0%), front seating (16.5%), and social seating (‘wherever my friends sit’; 15.9%). A smaller proportion consistently selected back‐row seating or reported no typical location, while peripheral seating (side or exit‐adjacent) was selected by 5.6% of participants. Distributions were similar across institutions.

Institutional comparison of seating behaviour

A chi‐square test of independence showed no statistically significant association between institution and seating behaviour (χ2(12) = 16.52, P = 0.168), with a negligible effect size (V = 0.096).

Demographic predictors

Ethnicity, journey length, year of study and Home/Overseas status were not significantly associated with seating behaviour (P > 0.05), and effect sizes were very small (Cramér's V ≤ 0.10). Seating distributions were broadly similar across ethnic groups, commute length categories, and fee‐status groups, although isolated institution‐specific associations were observed. Crucially, the lack of association with commute length indicates that macro‐level logistical factors did not significantly predict in‐class spatial positioning within this dataset.

Gender and age showed statistically significant associations with seating choice. Gender differences were distinct (χ2(6) = 27.29, P < 0.001, V = 0.177): female students were significantly more likely to occupy central or front‐row seating (50.7% combined). Conversely, male students were more likely to select peripheral or low‐density locations, specifically showing a three‐fold higher tendency than females to seek out the ‘section with the fewest people’ (8.3% vs 2.8%).

Age also showed a significant association (V = 0.147), driven by the marked over‐representation of older students (26 and over) in front‐row seating; while only 13.9% of the youngest cohort (21 and under) sat at the front, 63.6% of students aged 26+ occupied these seats. These patterns showed some institutional variation and were not uniformly replicated across all institutions.

Institution‐specific analyses identified different patterns of association across sites. At KCL, seating behaviour was associated with gender and residency status, whereas at KUL, associations were observed with ethnicity and age. These patterns were not consistently observed across all institutions.

Health‐related predictors of seating behaviour

Health‐related factors showed the strongest associations with seating behaviour. Social anxiety/sensitivity to crowding (χ2(6) = 69.07, P < 0.001, V = 0.278) showed the largest effect on seating choice, with an effect size nearly double those of demographic factors such as age or gender. This factor was associated with a dramatic shift from central to peripheral locations: students sensitive to social anxiety/crowding were five‐and‐a‐half times more likely to select a seat in a section with the ‘fewest people’ (~ 12.7% vs. ~ 2.3%) and twice as likely to sit in the back row (~ 20.5% vs. ~ 10.6%) compared to the general population. Mental ill health also showed a highly significant association with seating choice (χ2(6) = 27.00, P < 0.001, V = 0.174), specifically driving a preference for ‘Side or Exit’ seating (~ 9.4% vs ~ 4.2%) and the ‘Section with the fewest people’ (~ 8.2% vs ~ 2.8%).

In contrast, physical ill health, tiredness/fatigue, medical appointments, and COVID‐related worry showed no significant associations with seating location (P > 0.05, V < 0.11). Associations were stronger for psychological and sensory factors than for physical or logistical health factors. Finally, disability occupied a borderline statistical position (χ2(6) = 12.54, P = 0.051), suggesting a non‐significant trend toward peripheral seating while otherwise mirroring the ‘middle‐row’ dominance of the holistic sample.

Summary of seating behaviour patterns

Across aggregate and institution‐level analyses, seating behaviour showed no significant association with institutional context, ethnicity, journey length, fee status or year of study (V < 0.10, P > 0.05). Gender and age were associated with statistically significant differences, although effect sizes were small to moderate (V < 0.15) and not consistently observed across all institutions. In contrast, health‐related factors, particularly social anxiety and sensitivity to crowding, showed the strongest associations with seating behaviour (V ≈ 0.28). Qualitative analysis of student attendance and engagement

Qualitative analysis identified four themes that describe how students navigate logistical constraints, pedagogical value, and academic workload when deciding whether to attend on‐campus teaching.

Commuting demands and timetabling friction

Students frequently described attendance decisions as contingent on the perceived proportionality between travel effort and session duration. Medium‐length commutes were often characterised as too long to feel convenient, yet not long enough to justify full disengagement from campus. One student described this calculus as follows: ‘If I'm coming in just for an hour, is it worth the 45‐minute train ride and the money?’ [P7].

Fragmented timetables further reduced willingness to attend, particularly when sessions were separated by long gaps. As one participant noted, ‘You've got three‐hour lectures and then five hours gap. You're just wasting that time’ [P3].

Use of recorded teaching for academic pacing

Students described recorded teaching as a routine component of their study practices, particularly where live delivery was perceived as too fast. Participants highlighted the ability to pause, replay, and selectively review content as a key advantage: ‘I can replay that bit over and over again until I actually understand the concept’ [P12].

Several students also reported adapting their engagement strategies over time, particularly in later years of study, using recordings to manage workload more efficiently. One participant explained: ‘I watch it at 1.5 or two times speed because now I'm better at identifying what's important’ [P1].

Perceived value of different teaching formats

Participants distinguished between teaching sessions they perceived as more or less valuable to attend in person. Interactive sessions were reported as easier to sustain attention in, with quizzes or breaks between content segments helping to maintain engagement: ‘It makes you stay awake’ [P9].

Attendance was also influenced by proximity to assessment. One student noted increased attendance at lectures directly linked to upcoming coursework: ‘That's why everyone goes to that one’ [P4]. In contrast, seminars and tutorials were frequently described as offering opportunities for collaborative discussion: ‘You absorb knowledge from other people as well’ [P10].

Managing the experience of ‘being behind’

A recurring feature of the data was the experience of cumulative academic pressure associated with clustered deadlines. Students described periods of low assessment demand followed by simultaneous submissions, leading them to prioritise completing existing work over attending new sessions. One participant commented: ‘Nothing is assigned for months and then everything comes at once’ [P14].

Some students expressed a preference for regular, low‐stakes assessment structures that would help regulate engagement with content across the semester. As one participant suggested, ‘Constant quizzes every week… involving active recall’ [P13].

Discussion

This study examined two complementary dimensions of student engagement: attendance barriers influencing attendance decisions and classroom seating behaviour reflecting in‐class spatial behaviour. By analysing these domains in parallel, the findings indicate two analytically distinct patterns of engagement behaviour. Student attendance decisions were primarily associated with academic workload pressures and structural conditions, whereas seating behaviour was more strongly associated with sensory and emotional factors. Across both domains, demographic characteristics showed limited independent influence once structural and health‐related factors were considered.

Consistent with prior research, the most prominent reasons for non‐attendance were academic workload pressure and the availability of recorded lectures. These findings indicate that students prioritised ‘catching up’ and reliance on recordings as a substitute for in‐person teaching, reflecting the influence of structural academic pressures on attendance decisions. While lecture‐capture is widely valued for flexibility and convenience [11], it has also been associated with changes in attendance behaviour that may not support academic outcomes. This aligns with evidence that lecture‐capture availability can reduce attendance by encouraging substitution of live sessions [10] and encouraging students to skip more classes and postpone catching up [20, 23].

A crucial contextual factor is the institutional divergence in recording policies. KCL and SGUL operated institution‐wide lecture‐capture systems, while KUL did not. This pattern is consistent with institutional differences in lecture‐capture provision, suggesting that access to recorded teaching is a significant structural determinant of attendance decisions within this cohort. While recorded teaching may function as a simple substitute for attendance, the present findings support earlier studies in suggesting a more nuanced interpretation [11, 20]. The prominence of both recorded teaching and ‘being behind with studies’ as attendance‐related barriers indicates that students may use lecture capture strategically to manage academic workload, assessment pressures, commuting demands, and competing commitments. Consequently, reliance on recorded lectures should not necessarily be interpreted as disengagement from learning but may instead represent an alternative form of engagement through self‐paced study. The present study did not examine academic performance or patterns of lecture‐capture usage directly and therefore cannot determine whether the use of recorded teaching primarily reflects attendance substitution, workload management, or a combination of both.

The influence of journey length on barriers demonstrates that commuter‐related logistical constraints function as macro‐level structural determinants shaping attendance decisions [5, 6]. Students with medium‐length journeys (31–90 min) reported the highest academic strain, consistent with a ‘squeezed middle’ pattern. This finding suggests that commuter experience is not necessarily linear, with students experiencing moderate travel times potentially occupying a particularly challenging position. Such journeys may be sufficiently long to disrupt study routines and preparation, yet not long enough to prompt the adaptive behaviours or scheduling strategies often adopted by students facing more substantial commuting demands. While students with longer commutes (90+ minutes) may be more likely to rely on remote learning approaches, those with medium‐length journeys may be less able to balance commuting demands with pre‐session preparation. High‐ranking barriers such as ‘feeling behind’ are therefore consistent with structural constraints rather than individual factors, reflecting curriculum designs that may not fully account for the time demands associated with urban commuting.

Importantly, demographic characteristics (ethnicity, gender, age, year of study, Home/Overseas status) showed minimal or no independent influence on attendance barriers once structural factors such as journey length were accounted for. Ethnicity and fee status functioned largely as structural proxies for journey length; once travel time was controlled for, ethnic differences in attendance barriers were not observed. This aligns with research demonstrating that attendance patterns are shaped more strongly by institutional context and structural pressures than by demographic identity [24]. These findings contribute to wider discussions surrounding awarding and participation gaps by suggesting that apparent demographic differences may, in part, reflect exposure to unequal structural conditions rather than inherent differences between student groups [3, 4].

Health‐related effects were selectively but consistently linked to a limited number of barriers. Social anxiety, crowding, and fatigue were associated with increased reporting of social and interaction‐related attendance barriers, particularly reduced confidence in group work and peer‐related uncertainty. Prior research indicates that anxiety is a significant factor in higher‐education learning [25] and may be heightened by active learning demands [26], supporting the patterns observed here.

In contrast to attendance barriers, seating behaviour was primarily associated with health factors rather than academic or structural variables. Students reporting social anxiety or crowding sensitivity consistently selected peripheral, low‐density, or exit‐adjacent seating. This is consistent with previous evidence that students with anxiety preferentially select spatial positions that minimise sensory exposure, social evaluation, and visual intensity [18, 19, 27]. Such behaviours are likely individual‐level strategies to maintain perceived control and reduce social density within the learning environment [15, 28].

Demographic associations with seating behaviour were limited, with modest effects observed for age and gender and no consistent associations for ethnicity. Previous findings have suggested links between seating location and outcomes such as academic performance and motivation [14, 16, 29]; however, these outcomes were not examined in the present study, which instead identified stronger associations with psychological and sensory factors. This expands upon the seat‐mapping paradigms of Smith et al. [18], confirming that student spatial behaviour is deeply governed by micro‐level affective and sensory‐regulation choices rather than macro‐level logistics.

No significant institutional differences were observed in seating behaviour, despite clear institutional effects on attendance barriers. While institutional factors (such as lecture‐capture provision) were associated with attendance patterns, seating choice appeared to reflect responses to the immediate social and sensory environment. These findings indicate that attendance decisions are more sensitive to institutional context, whereas seating behaviour is more strongly associated with social and sensory conditions within the learning environment.

Collectively, these findings provide support for a dual‐dimensional view of student engagement, in which attendance and in‐class spatial behaviour reflect distinct but complementary aspects of interaction with the learning environment.

This distinction suggests that students engage with different aspects of the learning environment through distinct behavioural responses. Attendance decisions appear to reflect responses to external academic and structural conditions, whereas seating behaviour reflects how students manage the immediate social and sensory environment of the classroom.

Health‐related factors were the only variables showing associations in both domains, indicating that some students may adopt both behavioural (e.g. selective attendance) and spatial (e.g. peripheral seating) strategies to manage their learning experience. This highlights the importance of considering both structural and sensory dimensions when interpreting student behaviour. The qualitative findings further strengthen these interpretations by demonstrating that students consistently describe attendance decisions in relation to workload management, commuting demands, academic pacing, and the perceived value of different teaching formats. The recurrence of these themes across focus groups drawn from multiple institutions supports the broader transferability of the proposed dual‐dimensional framework beyond individual campus contexts. Implications

The findings of this study have several implications for bioscience teaching, particularly in large‐cohort and commuter‐dominated contexts.

First, the strong influence of academic workload and lecture‐capture availability on attendance highlights the importance of clearly communicating the role of recorded teaching. These findings suggest that institutional communication around lecture‐capture provision should emphasise its role as a supplementary resource rather than a substitute for in‐person engagement, given its observed association with attendance decision‐making. This may include aligning recorded material with preparatory or revision activities rather than positioning it as an equivalent alternative to live teaching. At the same time, recorded teaching may also function as a strategic mechanism through which students manage academic workload, assessment pressures, commuting demands, and competing commitments. Institutions should therefore consider how lecture‐capture provision supports flexible engagement while maintaining the distinctive benefits of live teaching. This consideration is particularly important given increasing recognition that student engagement cannot be understood solely through physical attendance [1]. For some students, including those managing health conditions, neurodiverse learning needs, caring responsibilities, financial pressures, or substantial commuting demands, recorded teaching may facilitate alternative forms of engagement and participation. Institutions should therefore seek to balance the benefits of in‐person learning with the inclusive opportunities afforded by flexible and digitally supported approaches.

Second, the association between journey length and academic readiness barriers emphasises the importance of timetabling and curriculum design that account for commuting constraints. In particular, the elevated strain observed among medium‐distance commuters suggests value in more consolidated timetables, reducing isolated or short‐duration sessions that require disproportionate travel effort relative to teaching time. Such approaches may help students in managing preparation and engagement with core content. More broadly, these findings support growing recognition that commuter experience should be considered within institutional approaches to student engagement, retention and success [7].

Third, the strong relationship between seating behaviour and social anxiety or crowding sensitivity indicates a role for learning‐space design in supporting inclusive engagement. Practical considerations include the availability of low‐density seating areas, flexible seating arrangements, and accessible exit‐adjacent locations, allowing students to manage comfort and participation within the lecture environment without disengaging entirely [28, 29]. These findings suggest that inclusive learning environments should not only focus on curriculum and pedagogy, but also consider how physical learning spaces accommodate diverse sensory and social needs. More broadly, these findings align with growing recognition that physical learning environments contribute not only to academic participation but also to student wellbeing, belonging, and psychological comfort [15].

Finally, the limited independent influence of demographic characteristics across both attendance and seating domains suggests that structural and environmental factors play a more consistent role in shaping engagement patterns than demographic categorisation alone. Interventions may therefore be most effective when focused on teaching practices, timetabling, digital provision, and learning environments that support a broad range of student needs, rather than targeting specific demographic groups.

Collectively, these findings have relevance beyond bioscience education and contribute to wider discussions within UK higher education concerning attendance, commuter support, inclusive practice, digitally supported learning, and how student engagement is conceptualised and measured. The proposed dual‐dimensional framework suggests that attendance decisions and in‐class participation behaviours may be driven by distinct structural and affective mechanisms. As institutions continue to expand blended and flexible models of delivery, recognising these different dimensions of engagement may support more effective policy development, curriculum design and student‐support strategies across a range of disciplinary and institutional settings.

Limitations

Attendance barriers were measured using ranked self‐report items, capturing relative priority rather than absolute frequency or magnitude. As a result, lower‐ranked barriers should be interpreted as less influential in decision‐making at the time of response rather than absent. Furthermore, rankings do not reflect variation in the absolute strength of barriers between students; for example, one student's lower‐ranked barrier may have a greater overall impact on attendance than another's highest‐ranked barrier, potentially leading to misrepresentation of the overall significance of specific barriers. In addition, the cross‐sectional design limits causal interpretation; for example, feeling ‘behind’ may act as both a cause and a consequence of non‐attendance. Longitudinal approaches may help clarify the temporal relationships between these factors. Furthermore, the study did not examine relationships between attendance barriers, seating behaviour, and academic attainment. Consequently, while factors associated with engagement could be identified, their direct relationship with educational outcomes could not be determined, particularly within the anonymous design of the study. Future research linking engagement behaviours with academic performance may help clarify these relationships.

Seating behaviours were self‐reported as habitual behaviour rather than directly observed [18], which may introduce recall or reporting bias. While seating behaviour provided insight into how students interact with the physical learning environment, the study did not directly assess cognitive engagement, classroom participation, or academic performance. Consequently, seating location should be interpreted as a behavioural indicator of spatial interaction with the learning environment rather than a direct measure of student engagement. In addition, health‐related indicators were coded as binary variables and may mask variation in severity or intensity that could influence engagement behaviour.

While demographic characteristics showed limited independent associations once structural factors were considered, this should not be interpreted as indicating demographic irrelevance. Rather, it suggests that demographic differences may operate indirectly through structural factors, particularly commuting patterns, which were a primary focus of this study.

Finally, the sample was predominantly composed of female students aged 18–21 reflecting typical enrolment patterns within UK undergraduate bioscience programmes. While this may limit generalisability to more diverse cohorts, the sample remains representative of the disciplinary context examined.

Conclusion

This study identifies and provides validation for a dual‐dimensional pattern of student engagement in bioscience education. Through the integration of quantitative findings and qualitative perspectives, we found that attendance decisions were associated with structural and academic factors, whereas in‐class spatial behaviour was more closely linked to sensory and emotional influences. Considering both dimensions highlights how students respond differently to the demands of the learning environment. These findings support a model in which student engagement is governed by distinct structural and affective mechanisms, with implications for curriculum design, lecture‐capture policy, and inclusive learning environments in large‐cohort bioscience education.
