Face perception and impact of face masks at 6 months post-term age in preterm and term infants (The BabyFace Study)
Authors: Celina Brunsch, Eri Nanizawa, Srikar Vallabhapurapu, Naomi Keurentjes, Kato van Lint, Aniek Sijbring, David Walker, Robin Laycock, Flora Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic drastically changed face-to-face interactions, as face masks cover facial zones and affect face perception. Preterm birth is associated with altered face perception, which is important in socio-cognitive development. Use of face masks in neonatal units could further impede face perception development. We assessed the impact of face masks on face perception, in preterm (23–30 weeks gestation at birth, n = 24) and term infants (n = 24) at 6 months’ post-term age, using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). The infants viewed alternating 5-s trials with images of full or masked happy faces, interspersed with 9-15 s baseline trials. Changes in oxy- and deoxy-haemoglobin concentration (ΔHbO and ΔHbR, µM), were measured using multichannel fNIRS covering inferior-frontal, temporo-parietal, and lateral occipital regions. Term infants showed higher ΔHbO than preterm infants for full faces in the right inferior-frontal region. The ΔHbO was higher for full than masked faces in the right temporo-parietal region for term infants, but not preterm infants. These findings reveal lower brain activation in preterm compared to term infants in the inferior-frontal region which is important for face and emotion processing. Preterm infants also show no differential brain response between full vs masked faces, reflecting altered face perception at 6 months’ post-term age.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48027-y.

Introduction

Preterm birth is the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality1. Preterm infants are known to be at high risk of socio-cognitive dysfunction which disrupts their understanding and response to social information, leading to reduced social engagement, attention2 and prosocial behaviour like cooperation and sharing3,4. However, socio-cognitive assessment remains limited in early follow-up during infancy. Preterm children also have increased incidence of neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), both of which involve socio-cognitive dysfunction and are often characterised by atypical neural responses to faces, and manifested as altered social skills including impaired eye-contact and difficulty in reading facial expressions5–7. The nonverbal cues from faces facilitate social interactions by allowing for inferences about emotions and intentions8,9. Accordingly, face perception is a particularly relevant skill that, if impaired, might contribute to socio-cognitive difficulties in preterm infants10.

While fetuses and newborns show an innate preference for faces or face-like patterns11,12 that is likely driven by subcortical networks, continued exposure to faces after birth is thought to drive development of cortical networks at around 2 months of age13–15. The cortical face perception network includes the inferior occipital gyrus for early perception of faces, the lateral fusiform gyrus for invariant aspects of faces, and the superior temporal sulcus for dynamic features16–18. This is supported by the “social brain” areas for higher-order social and affective processing, including the orbitofrontal cortex, the inferior frontal gyrus, the middle and superior temporal gyrus, and the temporo-parietal junction19–21. Cortical functional responses to human faces are attenuated in social brain areas in 4–6 month-old infants who later develop ASD, compared to control infants19,22.

Face perception has been observed to be altered in preterm infants. Unlike term infants who within 48 h after birth demonstrate preferential looking at natural vs distorted face images, preterm infants do not differentiate between these stimuli23. Furthermore, while term infants at 6–10 months of age show stronger neural activation when viewing photos of their own mother than for photos of unknown women, preterm infants show the opposite pattern24. Compared to term infants, preterm infants at 7–9 months’ post-term age take longer to first look at a face amongst pictures of different objects, and have reduced preference for looking at the eyes relative to the mouth6,25. During the Covid-19 pandemic, mandatory mask-wearing in most neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) brought an additional challenge on face perception in NICU patients. Face perception relies on recognition of the characteristic inverted triangular configuration between the eyes, nose and mouth12. If this pattern is distorted or obscured (as in masked or inverted faces)18, preferential looking and face perception may be altered even at one month of age 12,26. Thus, this ‘face deprivation’ in preterm infants may further impede their development of face perception. Currently, the literature regarding the consequences of mask wearing for socio-cognitive development is largely limited to behavioral studies with conflicting results27–30. For instance, in a study of term and preterm infants at 3 months post-term, reactive social smiles were displayed towards adult faces regardless of whether they were presented in full or covered by a mask28. In a study of 6-to-14-month-old term infants, the infants’ reaction when looking at smiling faces was similar when viewing full and masked faces up to 10 months; only at 14 months were infants more likely to respond positively to a smile on the full face29. Contrastingly, another study demonstrated that infants aged 6–9 months memorized full faces better than masked ones, with increased looking time at the eye region in masked faces30. Such increased looking time at masked faces was confirmed in another study of infants aged 12 months27. To our knowledge, the neural processing of masked faces has not been reported in infants and further work on this topic is encouraged9.

Our study aimed to assess the impact of face masks on face perception, using functional near infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), in term and preterm infants at the post-term age of 6 months. Given the novelty of the masked face paradigm in infant fNIRS studies, the overall research aim was to characterize patterns of neural responses in term and preterm infants. We hypothesized that, in frontal, temporo-parietal, and occipital regions linked to the social brain17,19,31 the haemodynamic response to photos of faces, with or without face masks, would be decreased in preterm compared to term infants. Additionally, we hypothesized a diminished haemodynamic response in these brain regions to masked faces compared to full faces in term infants, but no differential responses in preterm infants23,24,32.

Results

Cohort and participant characteristics

Between November 2022 and May 2024, 51 infants (25 term, 26 preterm) were consented for this study. Of those, 1 term infant withdrew from the study, 2 preterm infants were excluded as 1 had a technical error with the data recording and 1 had insufficient valid trials for both full face and masked face conditions. The final cohort consisted of 48 infants, with 24 in each group. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. In addition, 1 term and 1 preterm infant had insufficient valid trials for the masked face condition and full face condition respectively, but were retained for analyses where they had valid data (Fig. 1). The preterm infants had mean (range) Apgar scores of 6 (3-9) at 1 min and 8 (5-10) at 5 min after birth. All preterm infants required respiratory support at birth due to prematurity, but none required cardiovascular resuscitation. Preterm infants had a significantly lower gestational age and birth weight (but not birth weight z-score) than term infants. Mean duration of hospitalization for the preterm infants was 113 days (range: 53–209 days) after birth. The post-term equivalent age at the time of the study was not significantly different between the groups. The head circumference at time of study was smaller in the preterm than term group. Out of the 24 preterm infants, 13 infants had a grade 1 and 1 infant had a grade 2 intraventricular haemorrhage on cranial ultrasound. Eighteen infants had retinopathy of prematurity with stage 1 in 6 infants, stage 2 in 11 infants and stage 3 in 1 infant. Two infants received laser therapy. All infants at follow-up had regressed retinopathy with normal vision. Differences between infant groups and study conditions

The analyses across hemispheres, ROIs and time-windows were not treated as independent exploratory tests, but as a structured and meaningful examination of the same neural response, across spatial (ROI), temporal (time window), and hemispheric dimensions. Hence, we decided to accept a type I error rate of 5% for each of these comparisons and refrain from correcting for multiple testing, although we do report which significant effects would be lost after false discovery rate (FDR) correction for transparency.

The ∆HbO differences between infant groups and study conditions per ROI are shown in Fig. 5 (full statistical results are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Term infants showed a higher change in oxyhaemoglobin concentration (∆HbO) than preterm infants in the right inferior-frontal region (IF), being marginally significant in the first time window and significant in the second time window for the full face condition (non-parametric, p = 0.051 and 0.019, respectively; significance would be lost if FDR is applied to correct for multiple comparisons); and in the left IF region during the second time window (F(1,46) = 4.662, pgroup = 0.036, main effect) while follow-up analyses showed no group difference within each condition. In the right temporo-parietal (TP) region, planned comparisons revealed that term infants had a significantly higher ∆HbO for full faces compared to masked faces during the first time window (p = 0.048, t = 2.038; significance would be lost if FDR is applied), whereas this condition difference did not reach significance in preterm infants (p = 0.836, t = 0.209). However, the group x condition interaction was not significant (p = 0.193), and therefore we cannot conclude that the groups differed significantly in their sensitivity to full vs. masked faces in this ROI. Preterm infants did not show differential responses between full vs masked faces in all ROIs. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing the impact of preterm birth and face masks on face perception in infants, using fNIRS. Term infants showed higher functional activation than preterm infants in the right IF region when viewing full faces, and in the left IF region for full and masked faces. Term infants showed higher functional activation in the right TP region for full faces compared to masked faces, while preterm infants did not show this difference in any ROI.

The findings confirm our hypothesis of a decreased haemodynamic response during face perception in preterm infants compared to term infants, specifically as a main effect in the left IF and for full faces in the right IF region. The IF region is a key region for higher-order face processing functions such as recognizing emotions 18,33, particularly in children. Previous fNIRS studies suggest that emotion processing and activation of the medial prefrontal cortex are further moderated by infant temperament and mother-infant interaction34,35, both of which may be influenced by preterm birth and prolonged NICU admission. In functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies on mentalizing and face processing, children and adolescents showed increased IF activity compared to adults36,37. Previous literature supports the right-hemisphere dominance in face-selective activations relative to objects, including the right inferior-frontal gyrus38,39 where neural activation is driven by the eye region40. Developmentally, face perception networks in the right hemisphere mature earlier than those in the left16. The left IF region plays an important role in judging emotions based on the eye region35. For example, adults with lesions in the left IF region perform worse during the “Reading the mind in the eyes”-test, an emotion recognition task using eye photographs41. The IF activation during this task is stronger in children than in adults33. The smaller haemodynamic response in preterm compared to term infants in bilateral IF regions could indicate altered face perception related to delayed maturation of the frontal cortical networks, as demonstrated in functional connectivity studies in preterm infants at term-equivalent age42. Our results indicate that the altered frontal neural response in preterm infants persists at 6 months of post-term age.

We observed more preterm than term infants with a negative haemodynamic response during this visual task. A negative haemodynamic response occurs if the functional hyperemia in response to neural activity is absent or insufficient, resulting in reduced HbO and increased deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) on fNIRS43, or a negative blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal with fMRI44,45. Previous studies in preterm infants have reported negative haemodynamic responses following auditory46, visual47, olfactory48, or somatosensory49 stimuli, suggesting the cerebral oxygen extraction exceeds the functional increase in cerebral blood flow when neurovascular coupling is immature43. In our case, the higher proportion of preterm infants with a negative haemodynamic response in the left IF could be another indication of the immature face processing network.

We did not find differences in peak latencies between term and preterm infants. In fMRI studies using somatosensory stimuli, haemodynamic response magnitude and speed show a maturational increase when comparing preterm, term and adult cohorts50. These differences, however, were described at term-equivalent age, and may have disappeared in our cohort at 6 months post-term age.

Term infants showed higher neural activation in the right TP region when viewing full faces compared to masked faces. This result comes from a priori planned analyses, and should be interpreted with caution as the omnibus effect of condition did not reach statistical significance. Our TP channels capture the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS)51, which is implicated in the visual analysis of changeable aspects of faces, including eye gaze and expression, to derive mental states and intentions52,53, and is important for social communication. pSTS activity was observed in studies of both children and adults on mentalizing and eye gaze perception54,55. Our findings in term infants suggest that full faces may enhance mentalizing and social perception more than masked faces which provide reduced social and emotional cues.

Contrary to our results, a study in adults showed stronger neural activation on fMRI to masked faces compared to full faces in the right occipito-temporal area18, suggesting more neural resources are implemented to decipher the information behind masks. However, these adult participants were explicitly tasked with recognising the emotions of the faces, which placed different neural demands compared to our free-viewing task.

Unlike term infants, preterm infants did not demonstrate different functional responses to full vs masked faces. Given the limited sample size and known variability in infant fNIRS data, our findings should be considered preliminary and warrant replication in larger, confirmatory studies. Possible explanations include an inability to process full and masked faces differently due to developmental delay, similar to previous research which shows that preterm infants, unlike the term infants, did not distinguish between natural and distorted faces or non-faces in early postnatal days23, as well as at 8 months of age in electroencephalographic studies32. Additionally, a recent systematic review suggested poorer facial identity and expression processing in preterm infants, potentially explained by structural alterations and delayed maturation, as revealed on neuroimaging56. Term infants appear to focus more on key facial features such as the eyes and mouth, whereas preterm infants display more scattered scanning with less fixation on socially informative features such as the eyes6,57, which may underlie the less differentiated neural responses to full vs masked faces. Another possible explanation may be the higher exposure to masked faces58 in our preterm infants who were hospitalised when face masks were mandatory in the NICU following the COVID-19 pandemic. In monkeys, face deprivation after birth diminishes the preferential looking at faces and prevents face selectivity developing in STS, indicating the importance of face exposure59. A study in adults found differences in the event-related potentials when viewing full faces vs masked faces, but the effect was less in participants with high exposure to masks, suggesting that the brain adapts to partial face deprivation60. Similarly, an infant study proposed that experience with masked faces may lead to visual adaptations to better accommodate for impoverished or incomplete faces61.

Since face-to-face interaction was drastically changed during the COVID-19 pandemic, several studies in adults have described the neural impact of face masks. Concerns regarding potential negative effects of masks in infants and their human connection and attachment have been raised58, but the neural impact remains unexplored. Our study addresses this question and included term infants to describe the normal functional response to face perception at this age and for comparison with preterm infants. We excluded infants with significant brain injury to reduce heterogeneity of our study population, therefore our results more likely represent differences originating from prematurity itself. Future studies including infants with significant brain lesions would verify the impact of preterm brain injury on neural activation. Furthermore, the present study does not differentiate altered neural responses to faces due to prematurity, or due to prolonged exposure to masked faces in the NICU. Therefore, whether the group differences between term and preterm infants are associated with innate differences in face processing, different exposure to NICU admission and face masks, or developmental delay or deficit remains to be determined in future studies. Notably, preterm infants have demonstrated altered haemodynamic responses to various somatosensory stimuli when compared to term infants13,43, likely due to differences in brain development and also in both intra- and extra-uterine environmental exposure. fNIRS is non-invasive and well-tolerated in infants and allows longitudinal studies to examine the development of face perception, which we have planned at 12 and 24 months post-term age. To address the clinical implication and significance of our findings, neurodevelopmental assessment at 2 years of age will be performed. The result will reveal whether the functional response to face perception at early infancy is related to socio-cognitive deficits in preterm infants, and its potential as early marker of neurodevelopmental outcomes. Current early follow-up after preterm birth focuses on motor development, such as the Hammersmith Neonatal and Infant Neurological Examination62 and General Movements assessment63, to screen for motor disorders such as cerebral palsy at 3–4 months of post-term age. In contrast, early socio-cognitive assessment remains limited and may be overlooked until a later age. Although there are social-emotional and cognitive measures in the Bayley Scales of Infant Development, its validity and reliability in 6-month-old infants is suboptimal 64. fNIRS, with its ease of use, may be suitable for screening in infants.

We acknowledge several limitations. Our sample size is small but comparable to other infant fNIRS studies65 and adult studies of face masks18,60. Data loss is a well-known challenge in infant fNIRS research due to motion artifacts and inattention. To minimize this, we optimized motion artifact detection and correction, followed by manual artifact-screening cross-checked by at least 2 researchers. We did not compare preterm infants with term infants with similar postnatal exposure to face masks in the NICU, as such comparisons would have been confounded by morbidities (often congenital anomalies) that require NICU admission after term birth. In the present study, we used static non-social stimuli with pictures of houses as baseline control. These were selected rather than a fixation cross as commonly used in adult studies to maintain the infant’s attention. However, inclusion of a non-social condition, separate to the baseline, could allow assessment of functional responses to social vs non-social visual stimuli in our cohort. We refrained from correcting for multiple testing, but we do report which significant effects would be lost after FDR correction for transparency. The results from these planned comparisons should be interpreted with particular caution and need confirmation in larger trials. Lastly, we focused on three ROI’s specific to our task paradigm and age group, based on existing literature. Deeper regions such as the fusiform face area could not be studied with this technique.

In conclusion, at 6 months of (post-term) age, term infants show a higher haemodynamic response to full happy faces than preterm infants in the IF region, an important social brain area that is involved in higher-order face perception functions such as emotion recognition. This may suggest delayed or altered face perception development in preterm infants. Term infants showed a higher haemodynamic response to full faces compared to masked faces in the right TP region important for mentalizing and social communication, reflecting the impact of reduced social and emotional cues with masked faces. Such differences were not observed in preterm infants, possibly due to delayed face perception and/or habituation to masked faces during their NICU stay. Future research should confirm whether these differences persist and associate with socio-cognitive assessment of these infants.

Methods

Participants

We prospectively included preterm infants born between 23–30 weeks of gestational age and admitted to Monash Newborn between November 2022 and May 2024, when mask-wearing was mandatory for all staff, parents and visitors at all times. Exclusion criteria were major genetic, congenital, or intracranial (including Grade 3 or 4 intraventricular haemorrhage) abnormalities or evident visual impairment. We also excluded children who lived at > 40 km from Monash Newborn. The control group consisted of healthy term-born infants (≥ 37 + 0 weeks of gestation), recruited from the postnatal ward, or via staff members who volunteered to participate. We collected the sex, birth weight, gestational age and head circumference of all infants.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Monash Health Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC no RES-21–0000-778A), and written informed consent was obtained from the parents. All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental design

Infants were studied at 6 months of post-term equivalent age. The cortical hemodynamic response was measured using a multi-channel fNIRS device (BabyBrite, Artinis Medical Systems, Elst, The Netherlands) with near-infrared light emitters using two different wavelengths (760 and 850 nm) and data sampling rate of 25 or 50 Hz. Infants wore a head cap holding the fNIRS optode array of 18 channels (9 channels on each hemisphere, total of 10 transmitters and 8 receivers, Fig. 3) with a source-detector separation of 20 mm. The optode array captured the IF, TP, and lateral occipital regions (LO), which were previously found to show activation in response to visual social stimuli17,19,31. The optode array with the respective channels over the right hemisphere and their corresponding ROIs is depicted in Fig. 4. The head cap was positioned with transmitters 4 and 10 above the pre-auricular point (T4 position in the 10–20 system), based on scalp co-registration with the corresponding anatomical regions in an MRI study51. Photos were also taken to record the channel location.

Studies were scheduled in alignment with the infants’ most wakeful periods according to the parents. During the study, the infants sat on their parent’s lap in a dimly lit room (Fig. 6). The visual stimuli (15.5 cm face height) were presented on a 31.5-inch monitor screen (1920 × 1080 resolution). The infants sat at a 60 cm distance from the monitor (and consequently stimuli were ~ 15° visual angle). Parents were instructed to have minimal interaction with the infant during the experiment. A video camera placed on top of the screen recorded the child’s face and eye movements.Fig. 6Photo showing the study setup, taken and used with parental permission.

Visual stimuli

The visual task consisted of 2 conditions – full face and masked face – and was created using OpenSesame66 (Fig. 7). The video comprised alternating 5-s experimental trials displaying sequences of 5 different photos with each face presented for 800 ms with a 200 ms inter-stimulus interval (i.e., images cycled at 1 Hz). The faces showed models expressing either full happy faces, or happy faces with a medical face mask placed over the mouth and nose region, and were randomly selected from 15 male and 15 female actors taken from the NimStim Set of Facial Expressions67. We only used images of happy faces to avoid the possible confounding effects of face emotions10,32. Also, young infants are likely most exposed to happy faces compared to other face emotions; and the face mask covers important features to convey a ‘happy face’. The experimental trials were interspersed with baseline trials of at least 9 s displaying colored pictures of different houses each for 1-3 s. To capture the infant’s attention during the baseline trials, non-social sounds (e.g. doorbell, car horns) were randomly played after every 1–2 house pictures. The duration of each baseline trial was controlled by the experimenter, who initiated the next experimental trial when the infant was attending the screen. Data processing

fNIRS data were recorded using Oxysoft software (version 4.0, Artinis Medical Systems, Elst, The Netherlands, www.artinis.com). This software was coupled to OpenSesame for synchronization of trial markers in the data.

First, we scored the infants’ looking times offline based on video recordings of the infants’ face. We discarded invalid trials during which the infant looked at the screen for less than 60% of the trial duration. From the video recordings, 30% were cross-checked between 2 researchers to ensure adequate inter-observer agreement, and the agreement in trial inclusion based on infant looking time was 100%. fNIRS data were processed using Homer368 (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts) with a processing stream adapted from the Spline and Wavelet interpolation approach described by Di Lorenzo et al.69. Channels with very high or low optical intensities were discarded (“enPruneChannels” function, intensity threshold 5e-03 to 9e-01 based on manufacturer recommendations for the device). The raw data were then converted to optical densities, and motion artifact was detected (“MotionArtifactByChannel” function, amplitude 0.40, standard deviation threshold 15.5), and corrected using cubic spline interpolation (“MotionCorrectSpline”, p = 0.99), and wavelet filtering (“MotionCorrectWavelet”, iqr = 0.80). The optical density data were bandpass-filtered (“BandpassFilt”, hpf = 0.03 Hz, lpf = 1.00 Hz) and converted to ∆HbO and ∆HbR (µM) concentration using the modified Beer-Lambert law70 with a differential pathlength factor of 5.1 for infants71. We further visually inspected the data channel-by-channel for any artifacts that survived the correction steps within a time frame of -2 to + 8 s from the stimulus onset. We excluded trials with remaining artifacts defined as sudden large changes in the HbO signal (variations of > 1 µM within ≤ 3 s) that were not in line with the variability of surrounding data. Trials were also excluded from further analysis if there was artifact in ≥ 50% (9/18) of the channels. Channels were excluded if < 3 artifact-free trials were left per condition.

Lastly, the processed data were block-averaged for -2 to + 16 s from the stimulus onset and baseline-corrected to the arbitrary zero of the 2 s pre-stimulus to yield the final haemodynamic response.

Statistical analysis

Based on previous fNIRS studies using social visual stimuli in term infants19 which showed peak ΔHbO responses of ~ 0.5 ± 0.3 µM, we estimated that a group size of n = 23 infants per group would allow us to detect a 50% difference in ∆HbO in the preterm compared to term infants (α = 0.05, power = 0.8). This estimation also matches the sample sizes typically employed in infant fNIRS studies65. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad version 10.2.2, Boston, MA, USA). Infant characteristics were compared using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test between the term and preterm groups. To investigate differences in the timing of the response across experimental conditions, two time windows were selected between 2–7 and 8–14 s post-stimulus onset, based on visual inspection to best capture the maximum concentration changes across individuals and in accordance with previous infant studies17,22,31. For each infant, the maximum increase in HbO and decrease in HbR were calculated per channel per experimental condition in each time window.

Preliminary channel-by-channel analyses were conducted using data averaged for each infant group, to test for channel activation in each experimental condition. Data was tested for normality with the D’Agostino-Pearson test. A significant channel activation was defined as a significant increase in HbO and/or decrease in HbR from the 2-s baseline average immediately before onset of the experimental stimuli, using a paired t-test or Wilcoxon test depending on normality. To adjust for multiple comparisons across the investigated channels, p-values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure75.

Subsequent analyses used the peak ∆HbO averaged within each ROI to assess differences between infant groups (term vs preterm) and study conditions (full vs masked face) in each time window. We primarily focus on the ∆HbO, as this measure has a higher signal-to-noise ratio and is more consistent than ∆HbR76, particularly in infant research. For each infant, the peak ∆HbO across the channels was averaged for each ROI per study condition. Group-averaged data was tested for normality with the D’Agostino-Pearson test. For each ROI, data were analysed using a mixed-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML), with the fixed effects being infant group and study condition, and treating participants as a random intercept. This approach is equivalent to a repeated-measures ANOVA but allows inclusion of participants with missing observations. In addition to testing fixed effects, a priori planned contrasts to test our hypotheses were applied to examine estimated marginal means across groups and conditions, using the Fisher’s Least Significant Difference test. Our hypotheses were formulated at the functional level predicting different responses between term and preterm infants, based on prior infant studies. Regions of interest were defined a priori based on previous literature of infant face processing in relation to the social brain, to assess region-specific responses. However, the precise spatial and temporal localization as well as hemispheric lateralization of such effects is not yet established72–74. Accordingly, we conducted planned (a priori) contrasts within all ROIs and time-windows to examine whether the predicted response differentiations were expressed across different spatial and temporal components, rather than to test independent region- or time-specific hypotheses. For ROI data which was non-parametric, the Wilcoxon test was used to compare conditions within each infant group, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare infant groups for each condition.

The time taken to reach the peak ∆HbO (∆HbO peak latency, s) over the entire period of 2–14 s post stimulus onset was calculated for each channel. The ∆HbO peak latencies across channels were averaged for each ROI, and analysed using mixed-effects models with infant group and study condition as fixed effects, and participant as a random effect.

The number of infants with a positive, negative, or no haemodynamic response were calculated per channel and condition using the following criteria: A positive haemodynamic response was defined as an increase of ∆HbO > 0.15 µM or a decrease of ∆HbR < -0.15 µM from the baseline. If these criteria were not fulfilled, the minimum ∆HbO + /- maximum ∆HbR were calculated, with a negative haemodynamic response defined as a minimum ∆HbO < -0.15 µM or a maximum ∆HbR > 0.15 µM. Otherwise, no haemodynamic response was noted. These criteria were based on visual examination of our group-averaged ∆HbO and ∆HbR data per channel, and informed by previous studies demonstrating negative (inverted) haemodynamic responses to visual stimuli in infants43,77. The proportions of infants with a negative haemodynamic response were calculated per ROI and condition, and compared between infant groups using a chi-square test. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered significant for all statistical tests.
