The relationship between postoperative shoulder balance and coronal sacral slanting in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis patients with double structural curves
Authors: Shuxin Zheng, Aqib Rasool, Zhengxiao Lai, Junhu Li, Qiujiang Li, Jianzhi Wang, Zhipeng Deng, Huiliang Yang, Lei Wang, Yueming Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS) is a complex spinal deformity where surgical correction aims to restore both spinal and shoulder balance. Postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI) is a common complication, leading to patient dissatisfaction. While numerous factors contribute to PSI, the role of coronal sacral slanting, particularly in Lenke type 3 and 6 patients, is not well understood. This study investigated the relationship between postoperative shoulder balance and coronal sacral slanting in these patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 231 AIS patients (102 males, 129 females) with Lenke type 3 and 6 curves who underwent posterior fusion and had a minimum of 24 months of follow-up. Patients were divided into a postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI) group (n = 61) and a postoperative shoulder balance (PSB) group (n = 170). They were also subdivided into three groups according to coronal sacral slanting: left-sided (> 3°), non-slanting (0°–3°), and right-sided (> 3°). Radiographic parameters, including clavicle angle (CA), coracoid height difference (CHD), T1 tilt, radiographic shoulder height (RSH), and distal vertebral wedge angle (DWA), were measured at various follow-up intervals.ResultsAt final follow-up, the PSI group showed significantly worse outcomes in all shoulder parameters (RSH, CA, T1 Tilt, and CHD) and a larger sacral slanting angle compared to the PSB group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were found in main curve correction rates between the two groups. In the sacral slanting subgroups, while preoperative shoulder parameters and main curve correction rates were similar, the postoperative DWA was significantly different among the groups (P < 0.001). The right-side slanting group demonstrated a significant increase in DWA and the largest improvement in RSH, with a mean change of -7.26 mm, suggesting adaptive changes.ConclusionDue to the observational nature of this study, a direct causal relationship cannot be definitively established; however, our findings indicate that right-side sacral slanting is significantly associated with a better long-term recovery of postoperative shoulder balance. This process may be mediated by an adaptive increase in the distal wedge angle. These insights may help surgeons better predict and manage postoperative shoulder imbalance in Lenke type 3 and 6 AIS patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03824-0.

Introduction

Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS) is a complex three-dimensional spinal deformity that affects 2–3% of adolescents worldwide [1, 2]. Surgical correction is often necessary for severe curves to prevent progression, improve cosmetic appearance, and restore spinal balance [3]. While significant advancements in surgical techniques have led to high rates of coronal and sagittal plane correction, achieving and maintaining postoperative spinal balance remains a key challenge for surgeons. One of the most critical and aesthetically important components of this balance is shoulder balance [4–6]. Postoperative shoulder imbalance is a common complication, occurring in up to 40% of patients with Lenke 1 and 2 curves [7, 8]. It can lead to patient dissatisfaction and, in some cases, require revision surgery. Factors contributing to postoperative shoulder imbalance are multifactorial, including the type of curve, the selection of fusion levels, and the degree of correction of the main curves [9, 10]. However, recent literature highlights that the global coronal alignment of the spine is intricately linked to pelvic and sacral parameters. The sacrum acts as the absolute foundation of the spinal column. Coronal sacral slanting, representing the tilt of this foundation, is a crucial but frequently underappreciated parameter that influences the baseline scoliotic deformity and postoperative rebalancing.

Historically, research on PSI has predominantly focused on Lenke 1 and 2 curves [11–13]. However, Lenke 3 and 6 curves present a uniquely complex biomechanical challenge due to the presence of double structural curves. In these patients, both the main thoracic and the structural lumbar curves require surgical correction [14], leaving the unfused lumbosacral fractional curve and the sacral-pelvic foundation to govern subsequent global compensation. We hypothesize that coronal sacral slanting initiates a ‘bottom-up’ compensatory cascade in these specific curve types. We postulate that the degree and direction of sacral slanting drive adaptive remodeling in the unfused distal segments—specifically reflected in dynamic changes of the distal wedge angle (DWA). This adaptation ultimately propagates cranially, influencing the long-term restoration of the shoulder girdle. Furthermore, Lenke 4 curves (triple major) were excluded due to the extreme complexity of cervicothoracic compensation, which warrants independent study. Lenke 5 curves were also excluded because surgical fusion is typically limited to the distal segments, historically showing limited long-term impact on global shoulder balance.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between postoperative shoulder balance and coronal sacral slanting in AIS patients with Lenke 3 and 6 curves. By elucidating this sacral-DWA-shoulder coupling mechanism, we hope to provide surgeons with valuable prognostic indicators to optimize intraoperative decision-making and better predict postoperative shoulder outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary medical center using anonymized clinical and radiographic data retrieved from an institutional database. According to the policy of the local Ethics Committee, formal ethical approval was waived because the study involved no intervention and posed minimal risk to participants. This was a retrospective cohort study that consecutively retrieved 289 patients with AIS from an institutional database from June 2015 to June 2023. Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) Lenke 3 and Lenke 6; (2) Undergoing posterior correction surgery; (3) A minimum postoperative follow-up of 24 months. Patients with scoliosis caused by neurofibromatosis, instrumentation failure, infection, or those with any prior spinal surgical interventions were excluded. Patients who were lost to follow-up due to an inability to establish contact were also excluded. A total of 231 patients who met these criteria were enrolled, consisting of 102 males and 129 females. All cases were performed by two senior surgeons. A random subset of 30 patients was selected for reliability analysis; two observers measured all parameters independently, and one observer repeated the measurements one week later for intra-observer assessment.

Surgical technique

Patients were positioned prone on a Jackson table with a headrest and an open frame to suspend the chest and abdomen. This positioning was utilized to minimize intra-abdominal pressure and reduce intraoperative blood loss. A midline incision was made along the spinal axis, and the subcutaneous tissue and fascia were incised layer by layer with meticulous hemostasis. The paraspinal muscles were then subperiosteally dissected to expose the laminae, transverse processes, and facet joints bilaterally, from the most proximal to the most distal planned screw insertion levels. The selection of fusion levels followed a standardized institutional protocol. The general principles for determining fusion levels were as follows: the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) was primarily selected as the upper end vertebra (UEV) of the main thoracic curve, and the Lowest Instrumented Vertebra (LIV) was generally defined as the lower end vertebra (LEV) of the structural lumbar curve [15, 16]. Nevertheless, the precise fusion levels were individualized and fine-tuned based on each patient’s specific characteristics, including curve flexibility, rotational deformity, and baseline shoulder parameters. Under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, pedicle screws were accurately inserted into each targeted vertebral segment. All screw placements were confirmed to be in good position, without evidence of perforation into the spinal canal or neurovascular structures. Appropriately pre-contoured rods were then seated into the screw heads. The spinal deformity was corrected segment by segment. To address the three-dimensional deformity, global rod derotation was performed, accompanied by Direct Vertebral Rotation (DVR) at the apical and periapical vertebrae to maximally correct the rotational component. This was supplemented by translation, convex compression, and concave distraction to achieve optimal coronal and sagittal realignment. Care was taken during the apical derotation maneuvers to avoid over-correction, which could inadvertently shift the upper thoracic spine and adversely affect postoperative shoulder balance. Laminectomy or decortication of the facet joints was performed at the fusion levels to enhance the surface area for bone fusion. Autologous or allogeneic bone graft material was then placed over the laminae and between the transverse processes. The surgical site was thoroughly irrigated with copious amounts of normal saline to ensure no bone fragments or debris remained. A negative pressure drain was placed to facilitate drainage of postoperative fluid and prevent hematoma formation. The incision was closed in layers. Postoperatively, patients were instructed to wear a Thoracolumbosacral Orthosis brace for six months.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and Risser sign (graded from 0 to V based on the ossification of the iliac apophysis) [17], were systematically collected from the institutional database. Slanting angles were sub-classified using a 3° cutoff (left-side > 3°, non-slanting 0°–3°, right-side > 3°). This 3° threshold was established based on standard margins for radiographic measurement error and represents the minimal clinically notable magnitude for functional pelvic/sacral obliquity [18]. Full-spine anteroposterior radiographs were taken for all patients. Each radiograph was acquired in a natural standing position with the patient’s eyes looking straight ahead, 24 h after brace removal. Patients were positioned with a line connecting the toes of both feet parallel to the X-ray cassette. The hands were allowed to hang naturally in an anterior-posterior position.

Anteroposterior radiographs were taken preoperatively, at 1 week, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively to measure the following parameters. Radiographic shoulder balance was evaluated using radiographic shoulder height (RSH), clavicle angle (CA), coracoid height difference (CHD), and T1 tilt. RSH was defined as the vertical difference in soft-tissue shadow height between the left and right acromioclavicular regions on standing anteroposterior radiographs. CA was defined as the angle between a horizontal reference line and a line connecting the highest points of both clavicles. CHD was defined as the vertical height difference between the superior borders of the bilateral coracoid processes. T1 tilt was defined as the angle between the superior endplate of T1 and the horizontal plane. Coronal sacral slanting was defined as the angle between the superior endplate of S1 and a horizontal reference line. Proximal wedge angle (PWA) and distal wedge angle (DWA) were also recorded. All parameters are illustrated in Fig. 1. Patients were categorized into the postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI) group or postoperative shoulder balance (PSB) group according to the final follow-up radiographic shoulder height (RSH). An RSH of < 10 mm was defined as postoperative shoulder balance, whereas an RSH of ≥ 10 mm was defined as postoperative shoulder imbalance. All assessments were performed by two orthopedic surgeons specializing in spinal surgery. All radiographic parameters were measured again by the same surgeons one week later, and the average of all measurements was used for the final analysis to eliminate measurement errors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows (version 22.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Comparisons of continuous variables between the PSB and PSI groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, as the data were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square (χ2 ) test. To identify independent predictors of postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI), a multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. Variables that demonstrated statistical significance in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05), including preoperative RSH and T1 tilt, were entered as covariates. Additionally, based on our subgroup analysis findings suggesting a directional influence of sacral slanting, a categorical variable for “Right-sided Sacral Slanting” (Yes vs. No) was explicitly included in the model to evaluate its independent effect. The results were reported as Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare changes in RSH and DWA among the different slanting angle subgroups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Radiographic measurement reliability

The intra-observer and inter-observer reliability assessments demonstrated excellent agreement for all evaluated radiographic parameters. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) values for key parameters (RSH, CA, T1 Tilt, SS, DWA) were all greater than 0.85, indicating a high level of reproducibility for the measurements used in this study (Table S1).

Demographic data

Table 1 presents the demographic, clinical, and radiological data of the patients included in this study. The patients were divided into two groups: the PSI group (n = 61) and the PSB group (n = 170). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, gender, BMI, Risser sign, or follow-up time. Preoperative Cobb angles for the main thoracic, proximal thoracic, and thoracolumbar curves did not show significant differences between the groups. The mean main thoracic Cobb angle was 56.32 ± 4.78° in the PSI group and 38.50 ± 6.22° in the PSB group. The mean thoracolumbar Cobb angle was 41.35 ± 5.88° and 52.43 ± 5.29° for the PSI and PSB groups, respectively. Notably, the groups showed significant differences in several preoperative shoulder parameters: The PSI group had a mean RSH of 25.54 ± 9.88 mm, which was significantly greater than the 5.36 ± 8.28 mm in the PSB group. The mean clavicle angle was 9.52 ± 2.85° in the PSI group compared to 3.32 ± 1.82° in the PSB group. The PSI group demonstrated a mean T1 tilt of 12.42 ± 2.15°, which was substantially larger than the 7.98 ± 2.94° observed in the PSB group. The mean coracoid height difference was also significantly different, with the PSI group having a mean of 24.72 ± 7.24 mm versus 3.91 ± 2.46 mm in the PSB group. No significant differences were found in trunk shift or sacral slanting between the two groups.

Comparison of the PSI and PSB groups

Final follow-up radiographic measurements in Table 2 showed significant statistical differences between the two groups in RSH (Radiographic Shoulder Height), CA (Clavicle Angle), T1 Tilt, CHD (Coracoid Height Difference), and SS (Sacral Slanting). There were no significant differences between the PSI and PSB groups in the final follow-up measurements of proximal thoracic, main thoracic, and thoracolumbar Cobb angles. Both groups also demonstrated similar main thoracic and thoracolumbar curve correction rates, with the PSI group showing a correction rate of 75.25% for the main thoracic curve and 72.10% for the thoracolumbar curve. The PSB group had correction rates of 73.30% and 69.71%, respectively. Proximal and distal wedge angles were also similar between the groups. During our follow-up, we observed that the PSI group experienced a rebound increase in RSH between three and six months post-surgery, which then gradually stabilized and showed an overall decrease. Conversely, the RSH in the PSB group gradually increased throughout the follow-up period. There was a significant difference in RSH between the two groups at every follow-up time point. The results are presented in Table 3 and Fig. 2.

Comparison of the coronal sacral slanting subgroups

Table 4 compares the radiographic parameters among three groups categorized by the degree of coronal sacral slanting (left-side > 3°, 0°–3°, and right-side > 3°). We found that the correction rates for both the main thoracic and thoracolumbar curves were not significantly different across the three groups. The preoperative values for shoulder parameters also showed no significant differences among the groups. However, we did observe significant differences in the postoperative DWA among the groups (p < 0.001). This difference persisted at the last follow-up. Additionally, the right-side slanting group showed a greater increase in DWA. This group also had the largest change in RSH with an average change of − 7.26 mm. As demonstrated in Table 5 and Fig. 3, this suggests that as the DWA increased, the shoulders of patients in the right-side slanting group were moving toward a more balanced position. These findings suggest that while sacral slanting does not influence the overall correction of the main curves, it is significantly correlated with changes in the DWA and has a notable impact on the long-term changes in RSH.

Multivariate analysis of predictors for postoperative shoulder imbalance

A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the independent risk factors for PSI. The model included preoperative RSH and preoperative T1 tilt, which showed significant differences in the univariate comparison, along with the specific categorical variable for right-sided sacral slanting derived from the subgroup analysis. The results, summarized in Table 6, demonstrated that Preoperative Radiographic Shoulder Height (RSH) was a significant independent risk factor for PSI (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.28–1.90, P < 0.001). This indicates that for every unit increase in preoperative shoulder height difference, the risk of developing postoperative imbalance increases significantly. Notably, after adjusting for preoperative shoulder deformity, Right-sided Sacral Slanting was identified as a significant independent protective factor against PSI (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.65, P = 0.049). Patients with a right-sided sacral slant were approximately 68% less likely to develop postoperative shoulder imbalance compared to those with left-sided or negligible slanting. Preoperative T1 tilt did not maintain statistical significance in the multivariate model (P = 0.785), likely due to its strong correlation with RSH.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between postoperative shoulder balance and coronal sacral slanting in patients with Lenke 3 and 6 adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). Our findings demonstrate a significant link between preoperative shoulder parameters and the final postoperative outcome. Specifically, patients who achieved postoperative shoulder balance (PSB group) had a significantly more balanced shoulder at baseline compared to those who developed postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI group). This suggests that preoperative shoulder asymmetry is a critical risk factor for persistent postoperative imbalance, and our results support the growing body of evidence that the outcome of shoulder balance is heavily influenced by preoperative status. Despite similar main curve correction rates between the PSI and PSB groups, the significant differences in postoperative shoulder parameters persisted, highlighting that correcting the primary spinal deformity does not automatically resolve pre-existing shoulder asymmetry [19, 20].

This study breaks from previous research by focusing on Lenke 3 and 6 curves, a group for which the relationship between sacral parameters and shoulder balance has not been extensively studied. Prior literature has predominantly concentrated on Lenke 1 and 2 curves, often under the assumption that these are the most prone to postoperative shoulder imbalance [7, 12]. Our analysis of the coronal sacral slanting subgroups revealed a novel finding: a positive correlation between right-side sacral slanting and long-term improvement in shoulder balance. While a direct causal relationship cannot be established in a retrospective study, our data suggest a compensatory mechanism is at play. We observed a significant increase in the distal vertebral wedge angle (DWA) in the right-side slanting group, coinciding with the largest improvement in radiographic shoulder height (RSH). This finding suggests that an adaptive change in the distal spine, specifically an increase in DWA, may be a biomechanical response that compensates for the right-sided sacral slanting, thereby helping to restore coronal spinal and shoulder balance. As shown in Figs. 5 and 6, this compensatory mechanism has not been previously described in the context of postoperative shoulder balance in this patient population.

The clinical implications of these findings are significant. Our results underscore the importance of a comprehensive preoperative evaluation that extends beyond the primary scoliotic curves to include sacral and pelvic parameters. These findings may have implications for preoperative assessment and surgical planning. If a right-sided sacral slant is identified preoperatively, surgeons might tolerate a mild immediate postoperative shoulder asymmetry, anticipating the dynamic DWA adaptation to facilitate spontaneous shoulder leveling over time. Conversely, in patients lacking this protective right-sided slant (i.e., left-sided or non-slanting), surgeons cannot rely on this distal compensatory mechanism. In such cases, surgeons may need to pay closer attention to intraoperative rod contouring, UIV leveling, and curve translation to achieve immediate and definitive shoulder balance on the operating table. Furthermore, our observation of a dynamic change in RSH, characterized by a rebound increase followed by gradual improvement provide valuable insight for patient counseling, helping to manage expectations regarding the postoperative recovery timeline for shoulder balance.

Mechanistically, a rightward sacral slant may necessitate a greater compensatory wedge in the fractional lumbosacral curve (increasing DWA), which effectively helps to depress the elevated right shoulder or elevate the left shoulder, verifying the ‘coupling’ effect between the pelvic base and the shoulder girdle. The significant postoperative increase in DWA observed in right-sided sacral slanting patients, together with the largest decrease in radiographic shoulder height (RSH), suggests a dynamic compensatory mechanism. This adaptation likely reflects remodeling of the unfused lumbosacral region to mitigate residual shoulder height differences. Yamauchi et al. [21]previously reported that sacral wedging may represent long-term adaptive remodeling in AIS, supporting our hypothesis that sacral morphology participates actively in coronal rebalancing. The lack of differences in curve correction also reinforces that achieving radiographic shoulder balance depends on more than intraoperative correction parameters alone.

In addition to baseline anatomical parameters such as coronal sacral slanting, intraoperative surgical techniques also play a crucial role in determining final postoperative shoulder balance. Previous literature has indicated that aggressive correction maneuvers, particularly powerful direct vertebral rotation (DVR) applied at the main thoracic apex, can inadvertently translate the upper thoracic segments and alter the T1 tilt, potentially exacerbating postoperative shoulder imbalance. In the present study, to minimize the bias introduced by technical variations, all surgical procedures were performed by two senior surgeons utilizing a standardized segment-by-segment correction strategy. Care was consistently taken during apical derotation to balance curve correction with the preservation of global coronal alignment. Nevertheless, the nuanced biomechanical impact of specific derotation forces on shoulder kinematics remains an important consideration that warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces potential selection bias. Second, as an observational study, causal inferences regarding the interplay between sacral slanting and shoulder balance cannot be definitively drawn and must be interpreted with caution. Third, the sample size of the specific sacral slanting subgroups remains relatively small. Finally, AIS is fundamentally a three-dimensional deformity. This study primarily relied on coronal plane measurements and did not fully capture the intricate multi-planar interactions involving axial vertebral rotation and sagittal spinopelvic parameters (e.g., pelvic incidence, sacral slope). Future prospective studies incorporating 3D imaging systems are essential to holistically validate the biomechanical coupling between the sacral foundation and global spinal balance. Future research should include larger, multicenter prospective studies to confirm our findings and to explore the biomechanical mechanisms in greater detail. Advanced imaging techniques and motion analysis could also be used to further elucidate the relationship between sacral slanting, DWA, and long-term shoulder balance.

In conclusion, our study provides new evidence on the role of coronal sacral slanting in postoperative shoulder balance for Lenke 3 and 6 AIS patients. The potential protective role of right-side sacral slanting regarding shoulder balance, likely associated with an adaptive increase in DWA, offers a new perspective for preoperative planning and patient management.

Conclusion

Because of the retrospective and observational design of this study, causal inferences should be made with caution. Nevertheless, right-sided sacral slanting was associated with more favorable long-term recovery of postoperative shoulder balance in Lenke 3 and 6 AIS. This pattern was accompanied by greater longitudinal changes in DWA, suggesting a possible relationship between distal coronal compensation and postoperative shoulder rebalancing.
