Evaluating the role of resistive index in pediatric Hashimoto’s thyroiditis diagnosis and the impact of vitamin D treatment on resistive index
Authors: Sultan Kaba, Murat Doğan, Sercan Özkaçmaz, Sinan Kılıç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThyroid ultrasonography combined with color Doppler imaging represents one of the most reliable and non-invasive tools for assessing patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Growing evidence suggests that vitamin D deficiency contributes to the pathophysiology of this autoimmune disorder and may influence its progression toward hypothyroidism. This study aimed to explore the role of ultrasonography and color Doppler evaluation in children with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and to determine the effect of vitamin D supplementation on resistive index values, as well as on serum thyroid hormone and antibody levels.Materials and methodsEighty-four children were enrolled in this study, consisting of 45 diagnosed with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and 39 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. In all participants, serum concentrations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, thyroid hormones, and thyroid antibodies were analyzed. Both groups underwent grayscale ultrasonography and color Doppler evaluation of the thyroid gland. The patient group received oral vitamin D supplementation at approximately 2,000 IU daily. After two months of treatment, all biochemical and sonographic assessments were repeated.ResultsPatients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis demonstrated significantly higher grayscale ultrasonography grades compared with controls. Free thyroxine and vitamin D levels were notably lower among patients. Following vitamin D supplementation, anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody titers decreased significantly, accompanied by a statistically meaningful reduction in resistive index values in the right, left, and overall thyroid lobes.ConclusionAlthough the resistive index alone does not appear to be a reliable diagnostic indicator for Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, vitamin D supplementation was associated with reductions in both resistive index values and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody levels. Further controlled trials including untreated patients are warranted to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06330-z.Keywords: Hashimoto's thyroiditis, Vitamin d supplementation, Resistive index, Thyroid doppler sonography, Thyroid antibodies

Introduction

Vitamin D plays a fundamental role in maintaining calcium–phosphorus balance and skeletal integrity. Beyond its classical effects on bone metabolism, evidence accumulated in the past decade has highlighted its broader biological influence, including regulatory actions on immune function and potential anti-neoplastic properties [1]. Deficiency or dysregulation of vitamin D metabolism has been associated with several endocrine disorders such as primary hyperparathyroidism, both type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, autoimmune thyroid and adrenal diseases, and polycystic ovary syndrome [2–6].

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT), a major subtype of autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD), arises from immune-mediated destruction of thyroid tissue involving both humoral and cell-mediated mechanisms [7]. It frequently coexists with other autoimmune conditions and is characterized by the presence of circulating antibodies against thyroid-specific antigens, most notably anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) and anti-thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) antibodies [8]. Among these, anti-TPO antibodies are considered highly sensitive markers for diagnosing AITD (including HT), for monitoring therapeutic response, and for identifying individuals at elevated risk [9]. Although no current therapy can completely halt the autoimmune cascade in AITD, L-thyroxine replacement has been shown to produce modest reductions in circulating autoantibody titers [10].

Selenium supplementation has also demonstrated immune-modulating properties by lowering TPO-Ab concentrations and attenuating thyroidal inflammation in patients with elevated antibody levels [11]. A growing body of evidence suggests an association between low serum vitamin D and the development of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Polymorphisms in the vitamin D receptor (VDR) gene, as well as alterations in the vitamin D–binding protein and its genetic variants, may impair vitamin D metabolism and function, thereby contributing to disease susceptibility [12–16].

The immunoregulatory effects of vitamin D are thought to arise from its ability to suppress activated T lymphocytes, enhance macrophage phagocytic capacity, and reduce the production of autoantibodies directed against thyroid antigens [5, 17, 18]. Despite growing interest in the immunological implications of vitamin D supplementation in autoimmune thyroid disorders, available data—particularly in pediatric populations—remain limited.

Color-flow Doppler (CFD) ultrasonography provides a non-invasive means of assessing vascular alterations and perfusion in the thyroid gland [19]. Hemodynamic parameters such as resistive index (RI), pulsatility index (PI), and peak systolic velocity can be derived from Doppler studies of the thyroid parenchyma or inferior thyroid artery. However, discrepancies in measurement techniques and interpretation have resulted in inconsistent findings across studies [20–25], and standardized reference values for these indices have yet to be established.

Based on these observations, the present study hypothesizes that vitamin D supplementation in euthyroid children with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis leads to a decrease in thyroid autoantibody levels and alters vascular resistance parameters as measured by color-flow Doppler ultrasonography.

Materials and methods

Study Design and patients

This study was conducted at the Yuzuncu Yıl University School of Medicine between January 2012 and March 2015. Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (Date: January 01, 2012, No: B-30.2YYU.0.01.00.00/39). The study included 45 children who presented to the Pediatric Endocrinology Department with isolated Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and 39 healthy controls.

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was diagnosed based on serological positivity for anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) above 35 IU/mL and/or anti-thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) above 45 IU/mL. In addition to seropositivity, at least one of the following criteria was required to confirm the diagnosis: abnormal thyroid function, thyroid gland enlargement on physical examination, or morphological changes on thyroid ultrasonography, such as hypoechogenicity and heterogeneous parenchymal texture [26]. This multimodal diagnostic approach was adopted in accordance with current recommendations and reflects the need for both functional and structural assessment in autoimmune thyroiditis.

The study population consisted of children with a confirmed diagnosis of HT who were euthyroid at the time of evaluation. Inclusion criteria were as follows: evidence of thyroid enlargement or characteristic morphological alterations on ultrasonographic imaging, elevated serum anti-TPO levels exceeding 100 IU/mL, and normal levels of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (fT3), and free thyroxine (fT4), without the use of L-thyroxine therapy. Participants were also required to be free from medications that could influence vitamin D metabolism, peripheral conversion of T4 to T3, or thyroid autoimmunity. Furthermore, iodine status was confirmed to be within normal limits, as indicated by urinary iodine concentrations between 100 and 200 µg/L.

In all participants, the following baseline measurements were taken: urinary iodine levels, serum vitamin D [25(OH)D3], TSH, fT4, anti-TPO, anti-Tg, calcium, phosphorus, and alkaline phosphatase levels.

Thyroid volume and echogenicity were assessed via ultrasonography, and RI was evaluated by color Doppler ultrasonography.

Regardless of their initial vitamin D status, all patients with HT (including those with vitamin D levels below 20 ng/mL) received oral vitamin D supplementation (15 drops; ~2,000 IU/day). Monthly follow-up visits were scheduled to ensure compliance. At the end of the second month, blood samples were collected again from the patient group to reassess serum 25(OH)D3, TSH, fT4, anti-TPO, anti-Tg, calcium, phosphorus, and alkaline phosphatase levels. Thyroid ultrasonography and Doppler assessments were also repeated. All ultrasonographic evaluations were performed by a single radiologist.

Laboratory Tests

Fasting blood samples were collected between 8:00 and 9:00 AM. Electrolyte profiles were analyzed in the hospital’s Biochemistry Laboratory using the Bayer Opera Autoanalyzer and Biotrol kits. Serum 25(OH)D3 levels were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; HP Agilent 1200 series, Chrome Systems kit, Germany). Serum fT4, TSH, anti-TPO, and anti-Tg were measured via chemiluminescence immunoassay. Urinary iodine levels were determined using the Sandell-Kolthoff method, which is based on the spectrophotometric measurement of the arsenic acid–ceric acid reduction reaction (16 JPEM).

Sonography Evaluation

All ultrasonographic examinations were conducted by a single radiologist using a Philips HD-11™ unit with a 7.5 MHz transducer (Bothell, Washington, USA). Gray-scale ultrasonography parameters included echogenicity and thyroid size. Total thyroid volume for each patient was compared to upper limit values for specific age groups defined by the World Health Organization; thyroid volumes exceeding these limits were considered indicative of thyromegaly [27]. Thyroid echogenicity was classified using the Sostre and Reyes system [28] (Table 3), in which Grade 0 indicates normal echogenicity. RI was measured at a central point with visible vascularity in each thyroid lobe, and a mean RI value was calculated for each group based on individual lobe values.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted utilizing SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Distribution analysis was performed to differentiate between parametric and non-parametric characteristics. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the distributional properties of the data. Parametric characteristics were appraised using methods such as the t-test, while non-parametric traits were evaluated employing techniques like the Mann-Whitney U test. Pearson’s chi-squared test was utilized for proportional comparisons between groups, and correlation analysis was executed utilizing the Pearson correlation analysis method. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

The study demonstrated that both the mean thyroid volume and the parenchymal echogenicity grades were markedly higher in children with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) compared with the control group. These results reinforce the diagnostic importance of ultrasonographic findings in HT, aligning with previously published literature.

Earlier research has suggested that the rise in intrathyroidal vascularity and flow velocity primarily reflects spontaneous hyperthyroid activity rather than hyperthyroidism secondary to exogenous thyroid hormone administration or thyroid gland destruction. Interestingly, such vascular alterations have also been observed in HT patients who present with hypothyroidism [29]. Nevertheless, there are currently no established consensus guidelines defining the optimal approach for assessing these Doppler parameters [30, 31].

Mahmutyazıcıoğlu and Turgut compared 20 children with endemic goiter to 20 healthy controls and found mean resistive index (RI) values of 0.58 ± 0.05 in the affected group and 0.70 ± 0.05 among controls [32]. Although their measurements were obtained from thyroid arteries rather than from the thyroid gland itself, their findings were comparable to the mean RI observed in our study (0.57 ± 0.05). In our cohort, mean RI values were 0.57 ± 0.06 in the HT group and 0.55 ± 0.05 in controls, showing no statistically significant difference. This lack of significance may be explained by the fact that all HT patients included in our study were euthyroid. Bogazzi et al. reported that increases in vascularity and flow velocity were evident only in hypothyroid, not euthyroid, patients [29]. Conversely, Mahmutyazıcıoğlu and Turgut noted elevated peak systolic velocity but reduced RI values in children with endemic goiter [32].

Sarikaya and colleagues, referencing the RI values defined by Mahmutyazıcıoğlu and Turgut [32] as the normative range, emphasized that RI may offer superior diagnostic value compared with grayscale ultrasonography. In their work, no correlation was found between grayscale findings and RI values [32]. However, their conclusion of reduced RI in HT was derived through indirect comparison with previous studies rather than an internal control group. Furthermore, because the study employed a retrospective design and multiple radiologists were involved in image interpretation, inter-observer variability could not be excluded. In contrast, the current study minimized this limitation by having all examinations evaluated by a single radiologist, thereby ensuring greater consistency in subjective interpretation.

Given that HT can present across a spectrum of thyroid functional states (hyperthyroid, hypothyroid, or euthyroid) the diagnostic value of color-flow Doppler (CFD) should ideally be assessed in euthyroid patients as well.

Combination therapies incorporating vitamin D repletion have been shown to confer benefits across a range of chronic conditions, including asthma, type 1 diabetes mellitus, essential hypertension, hepatitis, malignancies, and cardiac failure [33–39]. However, data specifically exploring the impact of vitamin D therapy in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis remain limited. While prophylactic levothyroxine (LT4) administration has been reported to slightly reduce circulating antibody titers in euthyroid individuals, our study design deliberately excluded LT4-treated subjects to isolate the effects of vitamin D supplementation.

Following vitamin D supplementation, the mean serum vitamin D level increased significantly to 30.5 ± 10.6 ng/dL. Concurrently, anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) antibody titers declined significantly, while TSH and anti-thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) antibody levels exhibited modest, non-significant decreases. CFD analysis revealed a significant post-treatment reduction in RI values. The children in our cohort may have experienced prolonged subclinical effects of vitamin D deficiency, and we hypothesize that sustained maintenance of adequate vitamin D levels might lead to further reductions in anti-TPO and TSH concentrations over time. Because all patients were euthyroid, a pronounced decline in TSH was not anticipated.

Although TSH values remained relatively stable, the reduction in RI observed in this study is noteworthy. Bogazzi et al. proposed that thyroid stimulation mediated by TSH or TSH receptor antibodies contributes to enhanced thyroidal blood flow. Our findings suggest that vitamin D supplementation, even without TSH suppression, may reduce thyroid vascularity by attenuating local inflammation and tissue injury through its immunomodulatory effects [40, 41].

Despite these statistically significant reductions in RI, their direct clinical significance remains uncertain. Whether RI truly reflects inflammatory activity, predicts disease progression, or correlates with clinical outcomes in pediatric HT remains unclear. Therefore, while RI may provide valuable insight into thyroid vascular characteristics, its role as a dependable biomarker for disease monitoring requires further validation. Larger-scale, long-term studies incorporating clinical endpoints are necessary to determine whether changes in RI carry prognostic relevance.

Medication adherence was not objectively monitored in this study; however, families were thoroughly informed about the importance of compliance and verbally confirmed that the patients adhered to prescribed regimens. Because vitamin D was administered in liquid form, adherence problems were assumed to be minimal [42].

The relatively short two-month follow-up period represents another limitation. Such a duration may not be sufficient to fully capture the long-term immunological or vascular effects of vitamin D therapy. Thus, the observed declines in RI and anti-TPO antibody levels should be interpreted as short-term responses. Longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm whether these improvements persist and to clarify their clinical implications.

All patients in our study were euthyroid, which may have limited the ability to detect more substantial differences in vascular parameters such as RI between groups, thereby restricting the generalizability of our findings to other thyroid functional states. Furthermore, all participants received vitamin D supplementation irrespective of baseline vitamin D levels. This approach was guided by regional factors (Van, Turkey), has a cold continental climate, and vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent among local children. Consequently, including an untreated HT control group was not feasible. While this limits the capacity to assess the isolated effect of vitamin D on vascular and immunologic outcomes, it accurately reflects the real-world necessity of supplementation in this population. Future research should consider subgroup analyses comparing vitamin D–deficient and sufficient patients to better delineate the dependency of these effects on baseline status.

It is also important to recognize that reductions in RI and antibody levels may partly represent natural fluctuations in disease activity rather than a direct pharmacologic effect. Although potential confounders were minimized by selecting euthyroid patients not receiving LT4, the absence of a placebo arm prevents definitive causal inference. These results should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials are essential to verify these findings and determine whether vitamin D truly modifies thyroid vascularity and autoimmunity in HT.

Future investigations incorporating patients with different thyroid functional states and appropriate untreated control groups will be necessary to elucidate these associations more comprehensively.

Conclusions

In conclusion, RI measurement was not found to be a useful diagnostic tool in pediatric patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Vitamin D supplementation led to a reduction in thyroid blood flow and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody levels. However, since all patients in this study received vitamin D, the independent effect of supplementation could not be fully assessed. Further controlled studies including untreated comparison groups are warranted to clarify the role of vitamin D in thyroid vascularization and autoimmunity.
