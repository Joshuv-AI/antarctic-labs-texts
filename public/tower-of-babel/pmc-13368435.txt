Prehospital whole blood after SWiFT and TOWAR: not superior, not settled
Authors: Daniele Prati, Luca Barcella, Chiara Novelli, Irene Cuppari
Published: 2026

The debate over whether whole blood (WB) is superior to component therapy for resuscitation of traumatic hemorrhage has long been fueled by observational data, military experience, and physiological rationale, but remained largely unanswered by high-quality randomized evidence1–3. Two trials published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2026 - SWiFT (Study of Whole Blood in Frontline Trauma)4 and TOWAR (Type O Whole Blood and Assessment of Age during Prehospital Resuscitation)5 – now provide phase 3 RCT-level data in a field that had previously relied almost exclusively on retrospective cohorts and a single underpowered pilot trial6. Their convergent findings deserve careful interpretation, not only for what they demonstrate, but equally for what they leave unresolved.

SWiFT and TOWAR differ substantially in design, population, and primary endpoint (Table I). SWiFT was a pragmatic, individually randomized, superiority trial across 10 UK air ambulance services, testing prehospital transfusion of up to 2 units of leukocyte-depleted platelet-rich group O WB against standard care (red cells plus plasma), with a composite primary outcome of death or massive transfusion within 24 hours. TOWAR used a cluster-randomized design across 44 US air medical bases, comparing low-titer group O WB against as-indicated component therapy, with 30-day all-cause mortality as the primary endpoint.

The primary results are unambiguous: WB was not superior to standard component therapy in either trial. In SWiFT, the composite outcome occurred in 48.7% of WB recipients versus 47.7% of controls (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.80–1.31; p=0.84). In TOWAR, 30-day mortality was 25.9% versus 20.5% in the component group (adjusted OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.87–1.76; p=0.24). A critical interpretive point follows directly: both SWiFT and TOWAR were superiority trials. Failure to demonstrate superiority does not establish equivalence or non-inferiority. Their confidence intervals remain compatible with modest benefit or modest harm, a range of uncertainty that only a non-inferiority design with pre-specified margins could resolve. Notably, prothrombin time on hospital arrival was more frequently prolonged in WB recipients in SWiFT (40.7% vs 30.5%, RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.10–1.56)4, plausibly reflecting older plasma in WB units and documented storage-related coagulation factor decline7,8, though without apparent clinical consequence. The absence of a comparable coagulation signal in TOWAR may not simply reflect product equivalence: because 31.6% of patients assigned to WB also received blood components, either alone or in addition to WB, product-specific hemostatic differences may have been diluted. Secondary outcomes, subgroup analyses, and safety profiles were broadly consistent across trials (Table I).

The convergence of two independent phase 3 RCTs strengthens the inference that, within the tested prehospital strategies, WB has not demonstrated clinically meaningful superiority over component therapy in civilian trauma. A fundamental methodological caveat applies to both: randomizing patients to WB only in the prehospital phase, while allowing unrestricted component use thereafter, is analogous to evaluating only the loading phase of a drug intervention whose putative benefit may depend on sustained exposure. In TOWAR, a substantial proportion of patients assigned to WB also received blood components, blurring the distinction between trial arms5. If WB confers hemostatic advantages, those would need to be sustained throughout resuscitation to translate into survival benefit. The broader question-whether WB-based resuscitation sustained through the in-hospital phase is superior to component-based care-is being directly addressed by TROOP, a phase III Bayesian non-inferiority/superiority trial designed to enroll 1,100 patients across 15 US level I trauma centers, with 6-hour mortality as the primary endpoint9.

Both trial groups acknowledge the logistic appeal of WB: a single bag simplifies prehospital administration, shortens delivery time, and reduces the risk of errors under operational stress10. These advantages are genuine. However, the framing of WB as “logistically simpler” conceals a structural problem: the dual-inventory challenge. Adopting WB for prehospital emergency use adds a parallel supply chain. WB requires separate donor screening for low anti-A and anti-B titers11, dedicated collection pathways, cold storage logistics, and carries meaningful wastage risk given its 21-day shelf life and unpredictable demand. Prehospital WB programmes also tie up group O red cells -and, where O RhD-negative units are used, the scarcest inventory of all-in products reserved for unpredictable emergency use, potentially disrupting stock management in systems already operating with narrow margins, a risk that would be further amplified if WB use were extended to the in-hospital phase. This tension is particularly acute in Europe, where regulatory constraints are stringent and blood services face demographic donor pressure12–15. The logistic simplicity of WB in the field is real; the complexity it introduces into the supplying blood service is equally real and systematically underreported.

In our view, the different strategies for the treatment of post-traumatic hemorrhage must be carefully evaluated for their overall impact on improving the quality of treatment, not only in terms of efficacy but also in terms of sustainability, accessibility, resilience, and appropriateness. A carefully assessed alternative to WB could be reconstituted whole blood (RWB), which, being assembled within the blood bank infrastructure from standard, licensed components, can combine the logistical advantage of a single-bag product with established regulatory pathways, although introducing this approach into clinical practice would require substantial product development and regulatory work12,15. A more immediately implementable strategy could consist of integrating standard component therapy with products such as cold-stored platelets, fibrinogen concentrate, or lyophilized cryoprecipitate, approaches that address hemostatic gaps without introducing a parallel WB supply chain. The priority is not to replace one rigid framework with another, but to design strategies that reconcile clinical urgency with the real constraints of blood service organization.

SWiFT and TOWAR are landmark contributions, but their convergent finding of non-superiority does not establish equivalence, and proceeding as if it did carries real risks. The experience of COVID-19 convalescent plasma offers a cautionary analogy: blood services worldwide-including in Italy-mobilized rapidly, investing substantial logistic and organizational capacity in a biologically plausible product, before randomized trials largely failed to confirm the benefits suggested by observational data16,17. These considerations raise questions we believe deserve open debate: what non-inferiority margin for WB would be clinically and ethically acceptable, and who should define it? Should thresholds differ between military and civilian contexts, or between health systems with different supply constraints? We hope this commentary will prompt responses and the methodological discussion these trials deserve. What neither transfusion medicine nor trauma policy can afford is to interpret “not superior in the first two prehospital units” as a sufficient basis for large-scale implementation before these harder questions have been answered.
