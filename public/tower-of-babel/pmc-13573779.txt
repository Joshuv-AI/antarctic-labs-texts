Response to “Methodological Caveats in Evaluating Perioperative Esketamine for Postoperative Pain Sensitivity: A Comment on the Study by Chen et al” [Response to Letter]
Authors: Yanqin Wu, Yu Chen
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao and Dr. Guan for their thoughtful comments and constructive discussion regarding our recently published randomized clinical trial entitled “Effect of Subanesthetic Doses of Esketamine on Early Postoperative Pain Sensitivity in Patients Undergoing Salpingectomy: A Randomized Clinical Trial”.1,2

We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify several methodological considerations raised in their Letter to the Editor. Their comments highlight important issues regarding the interpretation of perioperative esketamine studies, particularly the influence of opioid background regimens, assessment of postoperative pain sensitivity, and mechanistic interpretation of biomarker findings.

We have carefully considered these valuable comments and provide our responses below.

Comment 1: Non-equivalent postoperative opioid regimens and potential confounding effects.

Response: We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao et al for their insightful comments regarding the difference in postoperative sufentanil doses between groups and its potential impact on the interpretation of our findings.

We appreciate that the difference in postoperative sufentanil background between groups represents an important methodological consideration that deserves careful interpretation. In our study, the esketamine group received PCIA containing esketamine (1 mg/kg) plus sufentanil (1 μg/kg), whereas the control group received sufentanil alone (2 μg/kg). This design was adopted to compare two clinically applicable multimodal analgesic strategies rather than to isolate the pharmacological effect of esketamine under identical opioid exposure conditions.

The higher sufentanil dose in the control group was selected based on previous clinical experience and published perioperative esketamine-containing analgesic regimens suggesting an opioid-sparing role of esketamine, with the aim of maintaining comparable postoperative analgesic efficacy between groups.3,4 Importantly, intraoperative sufentanil consumption was comparable between groups, indicating that the perioperative opioid exposure before PCIA initiation was well balanced, as reported in Table 1 of the original article.

We acknowledge that differential postoperative opioid exposure may theoretically introduce confounding effects, including the potential influence of opioid-induced hyperalgesia on postoperative pain sensitivity. Therefore, the present study cannot completely distinguish the independent pharmacological effect of esketamine from the combined effect of esketamine administration and reduced opioid exposure.

However, several findings support the clinical relevance of our observations. First, despite receiving a lower sufentanil dose in the PCIA regimen, patients in the esketamine group did not demonstrate inferior analgesic outcomes, with no significant differences observed in PCA demands or rescue analgesia requirements between groups, as reported in Table 2 of the original article. Second, the primary objective of our study was to evaluate whether an esketamine-containing multimodal analgesic strategy could modulate early postoperative pain sensitivity in a clinically relevant setting, rather than to determine the isolated effect of esketamine alone.

We agree that future randomized controlled trials using identical background opioid regimens, with opioid consumption prespecified as a co-primary endpoint, will be valuable to further clarify the independent contribution of esketamine to postoperative pain sensitivity modulation.

Comment 2: Regarding the repeated use of PSQ as an outcome measure during the acute postoperative period.

Response: We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao et al for their thoughtful comments regarding the application of the Pain Sensitivity Questionnaire (PSQ) as a repeated outcome measure during the early postoperative period.

We appreciate the opportunity to clarify that the PSQ was originally developed and validated as a measure of trait-like perceived pain sensitivity rather than an objective assessment of experimentally induced hyperalgesia or short-term alterations in nociceptive processing. Therefore, postoperative changes in PSQ scores should be interpreted within the appropriate clinical context.5

Importantly, our study did not aim to quantify experimentally induced hyperalgesia or sensory thresholds. Rather, we aimed to evaluate longitudinal changes in patients’ perceived pain sensitivity during early postoperative recovery. The PSQ was selected because it provides a standardized patient-reported assessment of pain sensitivity and captures an aspect of pain perception that is clinically relevant to postoperative recovery. Furthermore, preoperative PSQ assessments were performed before surgery, allowing evaluation of within-patient postoperative changes and trajectories rather than relying solely on postoperative absolute scores.6

We acknowledge that postoperative PSQ scores may be influenced by dynamic factors, including recent pain experience, emotional state, and recovery trajectory. Accordingly, we have interpreted our findings cautiously and consider the observed differences as changes in perceived postoperative pain sensitivity rather than definitive evidence of altered sensory processing or physiological hyperalgesia.

Future studies incorporating objective psychophysical assessments, including quantitative sensory testing (QST) and temporal summation measurements, together with patient-reported outcomes, will be valuable to further characterize the effects of esketamine on postoperative pain sensitivity and to clarify the relationship between subjective pain perception and underlying sensory mechanisms.

Comment 3: Differential attrition and potential bias arising from per-protocol analysis.

Response: We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao et al for their thoughtful comments regarding the unequal post-randomization attrition between groups and the potential impact on the robustness of our findings.

We agree that the asymmetric exclusion of participants after randomization, with all four exclusions occurring in the control group, represents an important methodological consideration. Unequal attrition may potentially introduce bias, particularly when reasons for missing data may be associated with postoperative clinical outcomes.

To address this concern, we performed an intention-to-treat (ITT) sensitivity analysis including all randomized participants. The ITT analysis yielded results that were generally consistent with the primary per-protocol analysis, with the direction and overall pattern of treatment effects remaining unchanged. These findings provide additional support for the robustness of our primary observations, although we acknowledge that the magnitude of the effect estimates was slightly attenuated in the ITT analysis.

Regarding the handling of missing data, we acknowledge that the use of last observation carried forward (LOCF) imputation has limitations, particularly when missingness may not be completely random. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. More advanced approaches, such as multiple imputation or pattern-mixture models, may provide more robust methods for handling missing data in future studies with larger sample sizes.

We have also acknowledged this issue as a limitation of the current study. Future randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, prespecified missing-data strategies, and robust sensitivity analyses will be valuable to further confirm the reliability of the observed effects of esketamine on postoperative pain sensitivity.

Comment 4: Regarding the interpretation of BDNF changes as a potential mechanism underlying the analgesic effect of esketamine.

Response: We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao et al for their insightful comments regarding the interpretation of serum BDNF changes and their potential relationship with the observed effects of esketamine on postoperative pain sensitivity.

We appreciate this important point and agree that the observed increase in peripheral serum BDNF levels should not be interpreted as direct evidence of a causal mechanism underlying the effects of esketamine. In the present study, the association between higher BDNF levels and reduced postoperative pain sensitivity represents a biological correlation rather than a confirmed mechanistic pathway.

Our intention in measuring BDNF was to explore a potential neurobiological correlate associated with the observed clinical effects of esketamine, based on previous evidence suggesting that BDNF is involved in synaptic plasticity, nociceptive processing, and emotional regulation. However, we acknowledge that peripheral BDNF concentrations may not fully reflect central nervous system BDNF activity due to differences in compartmental regulation and limited blood–brain barrier permeability.

Furthermore, we agree that formal mediation analysis would be required to determine whether BDNF acts as a mediator between esketamine administration and changes in postoperative pain sensitivity. The current study was not specifically designed to address mechanistic mediation, and therefore causal inference regarding the role of BDNF cannot be established.

Accordingly, during revision of the manuscript, we have adjusted our interpretation of these findings and now characterize BDNF as a potential biomarker associated with the response to esketamine rather than a confirmed mechanistic driver underlying its effects. Future studies incorporating mediation analyses, longitudinal mechanistic assessments, and central nervous system biomarkers or experimental approaches will be needed to further elucidate the role of BDNF in esketamine-induced modulation of postoperative pain sensitivity.

Conclusion

We sincerely thank Dr. Zhao et al for their valuable comments and constructive suggestions. Their insights have encouraged us to further consider the methodological limitations and interpretation of our findings, and have helped improve the scientific rigor of our manuscript.

We believe that these discussions will contribute to the ongoing refinement of perioperative esketamine research. Future studies incorporating standardized opioid regimens, objective pain assessment tools, and mechanistic investigations will be important to further clarify the role of esketamine in postoperative pain modulation.

Once again, we appreciate the opportunity for this valuable academic discussion.
