Shift Angle and Medial Retraction of the Deep Layer on Magnetic Resonance Imaging Are Associated With Intraoperative Reparability in Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Hideya Yoshimura, Hiroko Ueki, Naoko Araya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine whether magnetic resonance imaging‐based measurements of the deep layer, specifically the shift angle and medial retraction, can predict intraoperative reparability of large‐to‐massive rotator cuff tears.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients who underwent arthroscopic repair of large‐to‐massive rotator cuff tears between April 2015 and September 2019, with a minimum follow‐up of 12 months. The shift angle was defined as the anteroposterior extent of the deep‐layer tear on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging and medial retraction as the distance from the lateral edge of the deep‐layer stump to the cartilage margins. Inter‐ and intraobserver reliabilities and predictive accuracy for reparability were assessed.ResultsA total of 177 shoulders were analyzed, of which 149 were reparable and 28 were irreparable. The irreparable group showed larger shift angles (114.3° ± 14.5° vs 82.6° ± 24.0°, P < .001) and greater medial retraction (33.9 ± 5.2 vs 25.5 ± 6.1 mm, P < .001) than the reparable group. Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed good diagnostic performance for predicting irreparability for both the shift angle (area under the curve, 0.861; cutoff, 98.0°) and medial retraction (area under the curve, 0.870; cutoff, 29.8 mm), comparable to or better than superficial tear‐size measurements. Measurement reliability for both parameters was substantial to excellent (interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.862 and 0.769; intraobserver intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.896 and 0.889, respectively).ConclusionsMagnetic resonance imaging‐based assessment of the deep layer using the shift angle and medial retraction showed good diagnostic performance and substantial reliability for predicting intraoperative reparability.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.

Rotator cuff tears are a common cause of shoulder pain and functional limitation, and arthroscopic repair is widely performed to restore function and relieve symptoms in patients with persistent dysfunction or risk of progression. Successful outcomes are closely associated with the anatomical restoration of the torn tendon, particularly in large‐to‐massive tears in which tendon retraction, muscle degeneration, and delamination are frequently observed. 1 , 2

Among these, delaminated tears, defined as a separation between the superficial bursal layer and the deep articular or capsular layer, have traditionally been regarded as technically demanding and sometimes associated with poorer healing. 3 , 4 , 5 However, recent biomechanical and clinical studies have shown that anatomical repair of the deep layer, even in delaminated tears, can restore the superior stability of the humeral head and improve outcomes. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

Anatomical investigations by Nimura, Mochizuki, and others have identified the deep layer as corresponding to the superior joint capsule, which inserts at the posterior margin of the supraspinatus (SSP) footprint with a consistent width of approximately 4.4 mm. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The superior capsule and articular‐side SSP tendon are anatomically distinct but showed substantial continuity near the footprint. This structure contributes to superior glenohumeral joint stability, and its restoration has become a key principle in modern repair strategies, including superior capsular reconstruction. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Recent clinical reports have further shown that double‐layer or separate deep‐layer repairs reduce retear rates compared with repairs limited to the superficial bursal layer. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 17 Collectively, these findings highlight the biomechanical and clinical importance of the deep layer in achieving durable healing of rotator cuff repairs.

In this context, preoperative assessment of the deep‐layer tear repairability is essential for guiding surgical decision‐making and predicting the likelihood of successful repair. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the standard tool for assessing rotator cuff tears. Established MRI‐based predictors of reparability include the tear size in the anteroposterior (AP) and mediolateral (ML) dimensions, fatty degeneration of the SSP and infraspinatus (ISP), and the tangent sign. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 However, these parameters are primarily derived from the superficial layer, whereas the deep layer has received little attention in preoperative imaging assessments. Visualization and quantification of the deep layer on MRI remain challenging, and no previous studies have systematically examined whether MRI findings of the deep layer itself can predict intraoperative reparability in large‐to‐massive tears. This discrepancy between imaging practice and current surgical concepts motivated the present investigation.

On proton‐density‐weighted MRI, the deep layer can be identified as a low‐signal band immediately beneath the SSP tendon, 32 , 33 corresponding in location and thickness to the superior capsule described in cadaveric studies. 14 , 15 , 18 , 19 Based on these anatomical correlations, we speculated that the extent of AP involvement (shift angle) and degree of medial displacement (medial retraction) of the deep layer observed on MRI would serve as complementary parameters within a broader preoperative assessment, rather than as stand‐alone or definitive predictors. 31

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine whether MRI‐based measurements of the deep layer, specifically the shift angle and medial retraction, can predict intraoperative reparability of large‐to‐massive rotator cuff tears. We hypothesized that larger shift angles and greater medial retraction of the deep layer on preoperative MRI would be associated with irreparable tears.

METHODS

Study Population

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair for large‐to‐massive full‐thickness tears between April 2015 and September 2019. All surgeries were performed by a single experienced shoulder surgeon (H.Y.) at our institution. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Kawaguchi Kogyo General Hospital (approval number: KKH‐2006‐05), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for the use of clinical and imaging data was obtained from all participants.

Patients were included if intraoperative assessment confirmed a large‐to‐massive full‐thickness rotator cuff tear, with tear size determined intraoperatively using a calibrated probe. Large‐to‐massive tears were defined as an AP or ML dimension ≥ 3 cm, according to the criteria of Cofield et al. 34 Eligible patients were required to have undergone arthroscopic repair by a single experienced shoulder surgeon (H.Y.) during the study period and to have complete preoperative MRI available for evaluation.

Patients with isolated subscapularis (SSC) tears, partial‐thickness tears, or small‐to‐medium full‐thickness tears were excluded. Patients were also excluded if they had undergone prior surgery on the affected shoulder, exhibited severe glenohumeral stiffness or pseudoparalysis, or showed severe osteoarthritis, defined as Hamada grade ≥3 for cuff tear arthropathy or Samilson‐Prieto grade ≥3 for primary osteoarthritis. Patients aged 65 years or older with Goutallier grade 4 fatty degeneration were also excluded, as such cases are generally considered candidates for reverse shoulder arthroplasty. In contrast, patients younger than 65 years with Goutallier grade 4 fatty degeneration but without osteoarthritic change, and with Hamada grade II without pseudoparalysis or severe functional impairment, were included, as arthroscopic repair is commonly considered in this population.

MRI Protocol

MRI was performed using a 1.5‐T scanner (Avanto; Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). The preoperative MRI protocol included the following sequences and parameters.1. Fat‐suppressed proton density‐weighted images were acquired in oblique coronal and sagittal planes (repetition time/echo time, 2300/25 ms; field of view, 160 mm; matrix, 320 × 240; slice thickness, 4 mm with a 20% interslice gap; echo train length, 6; and bandwidth, 140 Hz/pixel).2. T2‐weighted turbo spin‐echo images were acquired in oblique coronal and sagittal planes (repetition time/echo time, 4800/110 ms; field of view, 160 mm; matrix, 320 × 224; slice thickness, 4 mm with a 20% gap; echo train length, 14; and bandwidth, 150 Hz/pixel).

MRI Evaluation for the Extent of Deep‐Layer Tear

On oblique coronal and sagittal proton‐density‐weighted MRI, the superficial and deep layers were identified as 2 distinct low‐signal intensity bands (Figure 1). In shoulders with rotator cuff tears, the most anterior attachment of the deep layer was identified on oblique sagittal images. On the slice intersecting the articular cartilage margin of the humeral head, a circle was fitted to match the contour of the articular surface. From the center of this fitted circle, 2 lines were drawn: one to the posterior edge of the long head of the biceps (LHB) tendon and the other to the most anterior point of the deep‐layer attachment. If the LHB was torn and no intra‐articular portion was visible, the reference point was placed at the posteromedial margin of the intertubercular groove. On MRI, this landmark appears as an angulated contour point at the cartilage‐groove junction. The angle formed between these lines was defined as the shift angle, representing the AP extent of deep‐layer involvement.

Tear Length of the Superficial Layer

Tear length was quantified in both the ML and AP planes using digital calipers integrated into the picture archiving and communication system; measurements were performed using T2‐weighted oblique coronal and sagittal MR images, respectively. In the coronal plane, ML tear length was defined as the distance from the medial margin of the tendon defect to the lateral cortex of the greater tuberosity. In the sagittal plane, AP length was determined from the posterior to the anterior margins of the torn tendon, following previously published protocols. 27 , 31

Tangent Sign

The tangent sign was determined on the sagittal Y‐view image, based on the method described by Rulewicz et al. The sign was interpreted as positive when the SSP muscle belly remained entirely below the reference line connecting the superior margins of the coracoid process and scapular spine. 23 , 25 , 35

Fatty Degeneration

The extent of fatty degeneration in the rotator cuff muscles, including the SSP and ISP, was graded using the Fuchs‐modified Goutallier system on sagittal T2‐weighted MRI, with classifications ranging from grade 0 (normal) to grade 4 (severe fatty infiltration, >50%). 30 , 36

Measurement of the Acromiohumeral Interval

The acromiohumeral interval (AHI) was measured on oblique coronal T2‐weighted images according to the method described previously. 22 , 28 , 30 The AHI was defined as the minimum perpendicular distance between the inferior acromial surface and the superior aspect of the humeral head on the slice showing the greatest humeral head diameter.

Surgical Procedure

The surgical indication was persistent shoulder pain and dysfunction refractory to nonoperative management, including physiotherapy and subacromial injections.

All procedures were performed by a single experienced shoulder surgeon (H.Y.) under general anesthesia combined with an interscalene nerve block in the beach‐chair position. Arthroscopy was performed via the posterolateral viewing portal, with the anterior, anterolateral, and posterior portals used as working portals. Subacromial decompression was performed as needed, followed by debridement of degenerative tissue. Adhesions around the rotator cuff were carefully released to improve mobility. Tear size was measured intraoperatively and classified according to Cofield's definition. 34 Delamination was assessed intraoperatively using arthroscopy, and a delaminated tear was defined by the presence of horizontal cleavage ≥5 mm between the deep and superficial layers, according to previous reports. 6 Reparable SSC tears, when present, were anatomically repaired to restore force couples and facilitate reduction of the posterosuperior tear. When limited excursion of the deep layer was identified, selective release of the coracohumeral ligament, which is anatomically continuous with the deep layer, was performed to facilitate mobilization. Beyond this maneuver, only limited dissection was performed, and unnecessary separation along the deep‐superficial interlayer plane was avoided. Lesions of the LHB were treated with tenotomy or tenodesis when indicated.

The deep layer of the rotator cuff was identified and sutured to the margin of the humeral head cartilage whenever reduction was possible. The superficial layer was subsequently repaired using a suture‐bridge technique. Reparability of both layers was documented. Tears in which <50% of the humeral footprint could be covered after mobilization were classified as irreparable, in accordance with previous reports. 27 , 30 Irreparable tears underwent partial repair or fascia lata autograft augmentation, including superior capsule reconstruction.

Postoperative Structural Integrity

As an ancillary postoperative outcome, structural integrity at 12 months was evaluated in the reparable group. Retear was assessed on T2‐weighted oblique coronal images using the Sugaya classification, with types 4 and 5 defined as full‐thickness retears. 1 The retear rate was reported descriptively because postoperative integrity was not a primary endpoint, and no inferential statistical analysis was performed.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20.0; IBM, Armonk, NY) and R software (version 2.12; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐tailed P value of <.05. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and compared using the Student's t test or Mann‐Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 or Fisher's exact test. Correlations among MRI‐derived parameters were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman coefficients as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess associations with reparability.

In accordance with the Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines, sex‐disaggregated subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential sex differences in demographic variables, MRI parameters, and reparability.

As an exploratory analysis, all MRI‐derived structural and degenerative parameters were simultaneously entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to evaluate their independent contributions. Because these variables are expected to display substantial multicollinearity, the multivariable model was interpreted as supportive rather than predictive.

Receiver operating characteristic curves were used to assess the discriminative ability of the continuous predictors of irreparability. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) served as an indicator of diagnostic performance, with cutoff points determined based on the Youden index. The AUC interpretations were as follows: 0.5 (noninformative), 0.5 to 0.7 (low accuracy), 0.7 to 0.9 (moderate), 0.9 to 1.0 (high accuracy), and 1.0 (perfect).

To evaluate reliability, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for interobserver agreement, and Cohen's kappa statistics were used for intraobserver reproducibility. Kappa values were interpreted as excellent (0.81‐1.00), substantial (0.61‐0.80), moderate (0.41‐0.60), fair (0.21‐0.40), and slight (<0.20).

RESULTS

Patient Enrollment and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 432 shoulders met the initial inclusion criteria. Of these, 60 had partial‐thickness tears, 185 had small‐to‐medium full‐thickness tears, and 10 had isolated SSC tears. After applying the predefined exclusion criteria, 177 shoulders with large‐to‐massive full‐thickness rotator cuff tears were included in the final cohort. The study population comprised 109 men and 68 women, with a mean age of 65.5 ± 9.8 years (range, 21‐86 years). The skewed sex distribution reflects the real‐world profile of patients undergoing rotator cuff repair, with a predominance of male patients. Primary repair was achieved in 149 shoulders, whereas 28 tears were deemed irreparable. Baseline demographic and imaging data are shown in Table 1.

Comparison Between Reparable and Irreparable Tears

Patients with irreparable tears had a significantly longer duration of symptoms than those with reparable tears (31.4 ± 39.4 vs 14.6 ± 22.5 months, P = .035). No significant between‐group differences were observed in age (66.0 ± 9.9 vs 62.9 ± 9.1 years, P = .105), sex distribution (93:56 vs 16:12, P = .753), body mass index (P = .523), dominant‐side involvement (P = .916), acromioclavicular joint degeneration (P = .896), smoking history (14.8% vs 17.9%, P = .896), or the frequency of concomitant LHB lesions (P = .580) or SSC repair (P = .870). Diabetes mellitus was more frequent in the reparable group (17.4% vs 0%, P = .036).

Deep‐layer parameters differed significantly between groups. The shift angle was significantly larger in the irreparable group (114.3° ± 14.5°) than that in the reparable group (82.6° ± 24.0°) (P < .001). Medial retraction of the deep layer was also significantly greater in the irreparable group (22.7 ± 7.5 vs 13.1 ± 11.4 mm, P < .001).

Superficial‐layer tear dimensions were significantly larger in irreparable tears in both the AP direction (40.9 ± 9.3 vs 28.8 ± 13.0 mm, P < .001) and the ML direction (45.4 ± 7.1 vs 31.4 ± 13.2 mm, P < .001). Irreparable tears also showed higher grades of SSP and ISP fatty degeneration (P < .001 for both), a higher prevalence of positive tangent signs (85.7% vs 41.6%, P < .001), and a significantly smaller AHI (5.7 ± 1.8 vs 7.0 ± 2.2 mm, P = .004) (Table 1).

Sex‐Disaggregated Analysis

Reparability did not differ significantly by sex (85.3% in male patients vs 82.4% in female patients, P = .753). Female patients were significantly older (68.2 ± 9.2 vs 63.8 ± 9.8 years, P = .003) and showed more advanced SSP (P < .001) and ISP (P = .002) fatty degeneration, as well as a higher prevalence of positive tangent signs (P = .001). Deep‐layer parameters, including shift angle (P = .204) and medial retraction (P = .516), did not differ significantly by sex.

Correlation

Both deep‐layer parameters showed moderate‐to‐strong correlations with AP/ML tear size and fatty degeneration, whereas correlations with demographic or clinical variables were weak (r < 0.20). Medial retraction showed the strongest structural associations, especially with ML and AP tear size (r = 0.76 and 0.68) (Table 2).

Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated With Reparability

In the univariable logistic regression analyses, all MRI‐derived parameters—including shift angle, medial retraction, superficial tear size, and SSP/ISP fatty degeneration—were significantly associated with reparability (all P < .001). These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to determine their independent contributions. In the multivariable analysis including all MRI‐derived parameters, only medial retraction (odds ratio 0.78; P = .018) and SSP fatty degeneration (odds ratio 0.39; P = .012) remained independently associated with reparability. Shift angle, superficial tear dimensions, and ISP fatty degeneration did not retain significance after adjustment, likely because of substantial multicollinearity among structural and degenerative variables (Table 3).

Prediction of Intraoperative Reparability

The shift angle of the deep rotator cuff layer is a key imaging parameter for predicting intraoperative reparability. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed strong diagnostic accuracy (Figure 3), with an AUC of 0.861 (95% confidence interval, 0.799‐0.921). The optimal cutoff point, determined using the maximum Youden index, was 98.0°, yielding a sensitivity of 0.862 and a specificity of 0.709. Medial retraction of the deep layer showed the highest predictive performance among all evaluated variables, with an AUC of 0.870 (95% confidence interval, 0.799‐0.941). The optimal threshold for medial retraction was 29.8 mm, achieving a sensitivity of 0.931 and a specificity of 0.709. Tear size in the superficial layer also showed predictive relevance. The AP tear dimension produced an AUC of 0.839, with an optimal cutoff of 28.8 mm (sensitivity 0.931, specificity 0.696), whereas the ML dimension showed an AUC of 0.848, with an optimal cutoff of 33.3 mm (sensitivity 0.828, specificity 0.784).

Although the tangent sign showed limited discriminative ability (AUC = 0.704), deep‐layer parameters such as shift angle and medial retraction consistently showed superior predictive value and may serve as robust preoperative indicators of reparability in large‐to‐massive rotator cuff tears.

Interobserver and Intraobserver Reliability of Each Parameter

The ICC for the interobserver reliability of the shift angle was 0.862, and that for intraobserver reliability was 0.896, indicating excellent reproducibility. Similarly, the inter‐ and intraobserver ICCs for medial retraction were 0.769 and 0.889, respectively. The AP tear size also showed acceptable reliability, with inter‐ and intraobserver ICCs of 0.760 and 0.830, respectively. The ML tear size showed inter‐ and intraobserver ICCs of 0.783 and 0.824, reflecting good measurement consistency for both parameters (Table 4).

Postoperative Outcome

Among the reparable cases (n = 149), 145 patients (97.3%) completed postoperative MRI follow‐up. Based on T2‐weighted imaging classified according to Sugaya, 20 of 145 shoulders showed type 4 or 5 changes, indicating full‐thickness retear (13.8%, 95% confidence interval 8.7‐20.4). This postoperative structural outcome is presented for descriptive purposes and was not included in the primary analyses focusing on intraoperative reparability.

DISCUSSION

The present study showed that MRI‐based evaluation of the deep layer, quantified using the shift angle and medial retraction, is strongly associated with intraoperative reparability in large‐to‐massive rotator cuff tears. These parameters showed substantial‐to‐excellent reliability and diagnostic performance comparable to established predictors, such as superficial tear size and fatty degeneration, suggesting that deep‐layer‐specific assessment may enhance preoperative planning. Superficial‐layer degeneration likely reflects chronic bursal‐side wear and tendon thinning, whereas deep‐layer displacement reflects structural compromise at the articular side and capsulotendinous junction. These processes may not progress in parallel, particularly in delaminated tears, which may explain why certain conventional markers (e.g., tangent sign) do not fully capture deep‐layer pathology.

Anatomical and radiologic studies have emphasized that the deep layer, which is structurally continuous with the superior capsular complex, contributes to glenohumeral stability and repair integrity. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 In our cohort, the low‐signal band visualized on proton density‐weighted MRI corresponded closely to the capsulotendinous deep layer adjacent to the SSP footprint, consistent with prior imaging investigations. 32 , 33 Because MRI cannot always distinguish a purely capsular lesion from an articular‐side tendon lesion, the “deep layer” evaluated in this study is best conceptualized as a capsulotendinous complex. Although some surgeons distinguish between the capsule and tendon, histologic evidence indicates that these components form an integrated functional unit in the context of rotator cuff tears. 13 , 14 , 18 , 19

Prior MRI‐based studies have identified several established predictors of irreparability, including large AP/ML tear size, advanced tendon retraction, high‐grade fatty degeneration, a positive tangent sign, and a reduced AHI, highlighting the multifactorial nature of reparability assessment. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In the present study, both deep‐layer parameters showed strong correlations with AP/ML tear size and with ISP fatty degeneration, reflecting progressive structural deterioration as tears enlarge. Nevertheless, AP/ML tear size showed comparable predictive performance, suggesting that deep‐layer parameters should be regarded as complementary, anatomically grounded measures rather than replacements for conventional predictors.

Importantly, multivariable logistic regression identified medial retraction and SSP fatty degeneration as independent predictors of irreparability. This finding underscores the combined influence of structural shortening and biological degeneration on tendon mobilization and is consistent with previous reports emphasizing the central roles of retraction and fatty infiltration in determining reparability in large‐to‐massive tears. 23

These observations highlight the value of multidimensional deep‐layer assessment. The shift angle provides a geometrically normalized representation of AP deep‐layer displacement, whereas medial retraction captures medial advancement of articular‐side fibers. Together, they offer a reproducible 2‐dimensional characterization of deep‐layer morphology that is grounded in anatomical structure. Additionally, no significant sex‐related differences in MRI parameters or reparability were observed in this cohort.

Although postoperative integrity was not a primary endpoint, the 13.8% retear rate at 12 months among reparable cases was comparable to previously reported outcomes for arthroscopic repair of large‐to‐massive tears. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 37 , 38 This suggests that cases deemed reparable according to the MRI‐based criteria used in this study achieved acceptable healing profiles; however, these observations should be interpreted descriptively.

Our findings further support the importance of accurate assessment and adequate restoration of the deep layer during repair. Previous studies have shown that insufficient reapproximation of articular‐side fibers may compromise postoperative healing, 8 , 9 , 37 and reports of dual‐layer repair techniques have shown reduced retear rates when the deep layer is anatomically restored. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Consistent with this concept, Mihata et al. reported that reinforcement of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair with superior capsular reconstruction significantly improved cuff integrity in large‐to‐massive tears, 21 , 22 underscoring the biomechanical and clinical importance of restoring deep‐layer structures. Although superficial tear size and muscle degeneration remain essential components of reparability assessment, 25 deep‐layer parameters provide mechanistically meaningful information that broadens the understanding of structural failure patterns.

Clinically, characterization of deep‐layer displacement may assist surgeons in anticipating the need for selective release of the surrounding tissue, estimating repair tension, and assessing the likelihood of achieving anatomical footprint coverage. Although these parameters do not replace comprehensive evaluation of tear morphology, they offer reliable, standardized metrics that may be particularly useful when reparability is uncertain.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection bias because only patients who underwent arthroscopic surgery with available preoperative MRI data were included. Second, our institutional criteria excluded patients aged ≥65 years with grade 4 fatty degeneration who were considered candidates for reverse shoulder arthroplasty; therefore, the study population may not fully represent the entire spectrum of large‐to‐massive rotator cuff tears. Third, the modest sample size, sex distribution, and relatively small number of irreparable cases may have reduced statistical power and generalizability. Fourth, although the deep layer was identified according to established anatomical landmarks, the absence of universally accepted MRI criteria for distinguishing the deep and superficial layers may limit reproducibility across institutions. Fifth, all MRI examinations were performed using a single 1.5‐T scanner with a uniform imaging protocol at a single institution, which may limit applicability of the study findings to other imaging systems or clinical settings. Sixth, although inter‐ and intraobserver reliability were substantial, all MRI measurements were performed by orthopaedic surgeons without independent validation by a musculoskeletal radiologist. Finally, postoperative structural integrity was reported descriptively and was not included in inferential analyses; therefore, the relation between deep‐layer parameters and long‐term clinical outcomes could not be determined.

CONCLUSIONS

MRI‐based assessment of the deep layer using the shift angle and medial retraction showed good diagnostic performance and substantial reliability for predicting intraoperative reparability.
