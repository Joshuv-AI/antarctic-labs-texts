Data-driven Analysis of First-Order Methods via Distributionally Robust Optimization
Jisun Park, Vinit Ranjan, Bartolomeo Stellato

Abstract
We consider the problem of analyzing the probabilistic performance of first-order methods when solving convex optimization problems drawn from an unknown distribution only accessible through samples. By combining performance estimation (PEP) and Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization (DRO), we formulate the analysis as a tractable semidefinite program. Our approach unifies worst-case and average-case analyses by incorporating data-driven information from the observed convergence of first-order methods on a limited number of problem instances. This yields probabilistic, data-driven performance guarantees in terms of the expectation or conditional value-at-risk of the selected performance metric. Experiments on smooth convex minimization, logistics regression, and Lasso show that our method significantly reduces the conservatism of classical worst-case bounds and narrows the gap between theoretical and empirical performance.

Introduction

First-order methods have gained significant attention over the past decade due to their low computational cost per iteration, modest memory requirements, and ability to warm-start from previous solutions
[
4
,
60
]
.
These advantages make them particularly valuable for large-scale problems in data science and machine learning, as well as embedded optimization applications in engineering and optimal control.

These algorithms have been around since the 1950s
[
23
]
, and have been extended to handle nondifferentiable functions and constraints via proximal operators
[
54
]
, and composite functions via operator splitting techniques such as the alternating directions method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
10
]
, or primal-dual hybrid gradient method (PDHG)
[
12
]
.
Recently, several general-purpose solvers based on first-order methods appeared in the literature, including PDLP
[
1
,
2
]
for linear programs (LPs), OSQP
[
67
]
for quadratic programs (QPs), and SCS
[
52
,
51
]
and COSMO
[
25
]
for semidefinite programs (SDPs). Despite these advances, the behavior of these algorithms are still highly sensitive to data, often exhibiting slow convergence for badly-scaled problems.

To design faster and more reliable first-order methods, a central question is to understand the accuracy of the solutions returned.
In most convergence analyses, this translates into estimating the worst-case accuracy after a certain number of iterations for a given function class.
While most of these results focus on asymptotic convergence rates
[
6
,
49
]
, a recent line of work called the performance estimation problem (PEP)
[
21
,
69
]
, focuses on constructing finite-step guarantees by solving a semidefinite program (SDP).
By defining the appropriate constraints,
i.e.
,
interpolation conditions
, this technique allows us to represent various popular convex function classes (
e.g.
, strongly convex, smooth, Lipschitz, indicators) and virtually all first-order methods used in practice.
This approach has led to several new results in the optimization community including tighter convergence rate proofs
[
68
,
73
]
, and even faster optimization algorithms
[
35
,
55
,
32

However, the practical convergence behavior of these schemes can be very different from the best known classical or computer-assisted worst-case bounds.
In some cases, first-order methods might converge significantly faster, as outlined in the following example.

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we develop a data-driven performance estimation framework for analyzing the convergence of deterministic first-order methods over a distribution of convex optimization problems.
By combining
a priori
information on the function classes considered, and
a posteriori
information from observing the algorithm trajectories, our data-driven technique narrows the gap between theoretical and empirical performance.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We combine the performance estimation framework (PEP) with data-driven Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization (DRO) to analyze performance of a first-order method over a distribution of convex optimization problems.
To achieve this, we directly formulate the analysis as a tractable conic program that depends on the observed trajectories of the algorithm on a finite set of problem instances.

•

We establish finite-sample performance guarantees on unseen problem instances in terms of the average value, conditional value-at-risk, or worst-case value on optimality measures such as
function-value gap and distance-to-solution.

•

Numerical experiments on quadratic minimization, logistic regression, and Lasso show that our approach significantly reduces the pessimism of state-of-the-art computer-assisted worst-case bounds.
Specifically, our bounds capture convergence behaviors that the worst-case analysis is not able to guarantee, such as point convergence for smooth convex functions.
In addition, we provide numerical evidence to conjecture faster probabilistic rates of convergence by fitting the bounds obtained by our procedure.

1.2
Related works

Classical convergence analysis and algorithm design

First-order methods have been widely adopted to solve large-scale optimization problems.
Unfortunately, they can require a large number of iterations to reach high-quality solutions, especially for badly-scaled problems.
This led to a significant amount of effort to analyze the worst-case performance of first-order methods under different assumptions on the problem classes involved.

The convergence behavior depends critically on problem structure.
Gradient descent exhibit fast linear convergence
[
43
]
or even super-linear convergence
[
71
]
when the objective function is strongly convex or satisfies error bound conditions.
Without strong convexity, it converges sublinearly
[
11
]
.
Similar sublinear rates hold for other methods, including the proximal point method
[
29
,
30
]
, the iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (ISTA), primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG)
[
12
]
, and the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) when applied to non-smooth convex optimization.

To mitigate this slow convergence behavior, Nesterov introduced the famous fast gradient method (FGM)
[
48
]
which employs an auxiliary iterate sequence to improve worst-case convergence rates.
Several extensions build on this concept: the optimized gradient method (OGM) for smooth convex minimization
[
35
]
,
the fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (FISTA) for composite optimization
[
5
]
, the accelerated proximal point method for monotone inclusion, the Halpern iteration for fixed-point problems
[
34
,
44
]
, and the extra anchored gradient methods for minimax optimization
[
79
]
.
Most of these acceleration schemes are
optimal
, in the sense that their convergence rate matches the fastest achievable rate among first-order methods
[
47
]
.

Nevertheless, convergence behavior of worst-case optimal methods in practice may be significantly worse when considering a specific distribution of problem instances.
Non-accelerated algorithms can outperform accelerated variants
[
55
]
on certain problem examples, suggesting that worst-case optimality does not always translate to practical efficiency.
To remedy this, we aim to develop a probabilistic performance analysis framework that takes into account a specific distribution of optimization problems.

Computer-assisted performance analysis

Several recent approaches in worst-case analysis use numerical solvers, by formulating an optimization problem whose objective is the worst-case convergence rate over a specific problem class.
The integral-quadratic constraint (IQC) framework uses control theory to find convergence rates for strongly-convex minimization
[
42
,
80
]
, using semidefinite programming (SDP) techniques.
For non-asymptotic convergence rates,
performance estimation problem
(PEP) also uses SDP tools to formulate an optimization problem whose optimal value gives finite-step worst-case bounds
[
21
,
70
]
.
The PEP framework can also be used to search for optimal step sizes, leading to the discovery of the worst-case optimal first-order methods including OGM
[
35
]
and OGM-G
[
36
]
for smooth convex minimization, OptISTA
[
32
]
for composite minimization, and APPM for monotone inclusions
[
34
,
44
]
.
For parametric linear and convex quadratic problems, we can formulate a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) to exactly calculate the worst-case convergence bounds for first-order methods
[
58
,
57
]
, called the verification problem (VP).
Although these approaches use numerical solvers and obtain non-analytical numerical values as results, Lyapunov analysis enables the reconstruction of human-interpretable proofs for certain problem classes
[
3
,
73
]
.
Unfortunately, these computer-assisted techniques provide bounds only for worst-case scenarios.
In this paper, we extend the PEP framework to automatically construct probabilistic performance guarantees of first-order methods.

Probabilistic convergence analysis for deterministic algorithms

Several works move away from classical worst-case analysis to study the average-case performance of an algorithm on a certain distribution of optimization problems.
Notable examples include smoothed analysis for the simplex method solving linear programs under spherically-symmetric distributions
[
65
,
7
,
66
]
and sorting algorithms under uniform distribution
[
38
,
33
,
75
]
.
More recent works study the average-case performance of first-order methods solving regression tasks with Gaussian-distributed features.
Such setups include applying gradient descent for strongly convex least-squares problems
[
56
,
63
,
53
]
, smooth convex quadratic optimization
[
16
]
,
accelerated gradient methods for bilinear optimization
[
19
]
and Hamiltonian gradient descent for distributed optimization
[
50
]
.
Other than the average-cases analysis, the high-probability convergence bounds with Gaussian distributions have been established for PDHG applied to linear programs (LPs)
[
78
]
.
Probabilistic approaches have also been combined with learning-to-optimize frameworks
[
15
]
, obtaining probabilistic guarantees analogous to generalization bounds in statistical learning theory
[
61
,
62
,
31
]
.
Importantly, most of these probabilistic convergence results consider performance metrics for which we already know the worst-case convergence. For example, convergence in function-value gap or norm of the gradient for smooth convex minimization.
However, in practice, we may observe stronger convergence results, such as point convergence, for specific distributions of problem instances.
In addition, most of this literature focuses on either average-case or high-probability convergence, without providing systematic ways to quantify such bounds for specific quantiles.

In this paper, we provide such a systematic framework, estimating the quantile information by conditional value-at-risk (
\mathbf{CVaR}
) of selected performance metric.
Moreover, we show that our approach can capture the phenomenon which is yet to be proven, namely the non-asymptotic rate to point convergence of algorithm iterates.

Distributionally robust optimization (DRO)

Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) is a popular approach to model optimization problems affected by random uncertainty, without having full knowledge on the underlying distribution.
DRO constructs an
ambiguity set
,
i.e.
, the set of distributions containing the true distribution with high probability, using only partial information about the uncertainty
[
13
,
40
]
.
Common ambiguity sets are based on either
a priori
information, such as moments (
e.g.
, Chebyshev ambiguity set, Gelbrich ambiguity set, Markov ambiguity set)
[
18
,
26
,
81
]
, or
a posteriori
information, such as sample realizations.
The latter type of ambiguity set corresponds to a ball around the empirical distribution of samples, with respect to the KL-divergence or the Wasserstein distance
[
77
,
46
]
.
By formulating a robust optimization problem over this ambiguity set, DRO computes a solution and a worst-case bound on the true objective, that holds with high confidence.
In this paper, we construct a PEP formulation to analyze the performance of deterministic first-order methods applied to solving a distribution of optimization problems, using data-driven Wasserstein DRO.
Specifically, we model the randomness in the problem instance realizations as uncertainty in the PEP problem formulation.
Thanks to the flexibility of Wasserstein DRO
[
39
,
24
]
, we obtain bounds on the true probabilistic performance without requiring full knowledge of the underlying distribution of problem instances, but only sample realizations of the algorithm iterate trajectories.

Stochastic analysis of first-order methods

Consider a minimization problem

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&f(x),\end{array}

Probabilistic performance analysis

Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of all probability distributions supported on

.
We model the distribution of the optimization problem instances and the initial iterates as
{(f,x^{0})\sim{\mathbf{P}}}
, where
{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
.
To derive probabilistic convergence guarantees with respect to
{\mathbf{P}}
, we define risk measures of
\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})
as follows.

The
Value-at-Risk
(
\mathbf{VaR}
) with quantile parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]

\mathbf{VaR}_{\alpha}(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0}))=\mathop{\rm argmin}\left\{\tau\in{\mathbf{R}}\mid{\mathbf{P}}(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})\leq\tau)\geq 1-\alpha\right\},

and is exactly the

\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})
over
(f,x^{0})\sim{\mathbf{P}}
.
Unfortunately, computing the
\mathbf{VaR}
is intractable
[
74
]
.
Instead, a tractable approximation of the
\mathbf{VaR}
is the

(
\mathbf{CVaR}
), defined as

\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0}))=\inf_{t\in{\mathbf{R}}}\,\left(t+\alpha^{-1}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(f,x^{0})\sim{\mathbf{P}}}(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})-t)_{+}\right),

where
(z)_{+}=\max\{z,0\}
.
According to
[
64
, Theorem 6.2]
, the
\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}
can be interpreted as the conditional expectation of

exceeding its

\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0}))=\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(f,x^{0})\sim{\mathbf{P}}}\Bigl(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})\;\Big|\;\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})\geq\mathbf{VaR}_{\alpha}\left(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})\right)\Big.\Bigr).

Consequently,
\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}
upper-bounds
\mathbf{VaR}_{\alpha}
.
By varying the quantile parameter
\alpha

we can interpolate between the average case (
\alpha=1

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\phi^{K}(f,x^{0}))=\sup_{(f,x^{0})\in\mathcal{H}}\,\phi^{K}(f,x^{0})

3
Convex formulation of the data-driven performance estimation problem

3.1
Finite-dimensional function representation via interpolation conditions

We obtain finite-dimensional representations of
(f,x^{0})\in\mathcal{H}
by evaluating the function
f
and its (sub)gradient only at the points encountered by the algorithm.
This is the only function information required to compute the performance metrics in Table
1
.

Suppose we are given a sequence of vectors
z^{k}=(x^{k},g^{k},f^{k})\in{\mathbf{R}}^{2d+1}
for
k\in I
,
where
I
is an arbitrary index set.
We say that
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in I}
is
\mathcal{F}
-interpolable
if there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{F}
such that

f^{k}=f(x^{k}),\qquad g^{k}\in\partial f(x^{k}),\qquad\forall k\in I.

For each of the function classes in Table
2
, their interpolability corresponds to a set of necessary and sufficient quadratic inequalities in the entries of the tuples
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in I}
, often referred to as the
interpolation conditions

[
21
,
69
,
70
,
59
]
.
In general, these inequalities are nonconvex, as shown in the following example.

Running example

(Interpolation condition of
\mathcal{F}_{0,L}
)
.

A set of tuples
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in I}
is
\mathcal{F}_{0,L}
-interpolable if and only if the following inequality holds for any pair
(i,j)\in I\times I

[
70
, Theorem 4]
:

f^{j}-f^{i}+(g^{j})^{T}(x^{i}-x^{j})+\frac{1}{2L}\big\|g^{i}-g^{j}\big\|^{2}\leq 0.

This is a nonconvex quadratic inequality, due to the bilinear term
(g^{j})^{T}(x^{i}-x^{j})
.

In this paper, we consider tuples coming from the algorithm iterations and optimality conditions, which correspond to the index set
I=\{\star,0,1,\dots,K\}
, where the
\star
index corresponds to the tuple at optimality.
Note that, without loss of generality, we can set
g^{\star}=0
and
f^{\star}=0
, because of translation invariance of the function classes considered
[
70
]
.

Semidefinite variable lifting

Adopting the strategy used in the PEP framework
[
21
,
69
,
70
]
, we lift the algorithm iterates to a higher dimensional space where the interpolation conditions can be represented as a set of convex constraints.
Given a set of tuples
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in I}
with
z^{k}=(x^{k},g^{k},f^{k})
, define the symmetric positive semidefinite
Gram matrix

G\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}
and the function-value vector
F\in{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
as

G=P^{T}P,\qquad F=\left(f^{0}-f^{\star},\dots,f^{K}-f^{\star}\right),

(3)

where

P=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
(x^{0}-x^{\star})&g^{0}&\cdots&g^{K}\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d\times(K+2)}.

(4)

It is well-known that the interpolation conditions for the function classes in Table
2
can be written as a set of linear matrix inequalities (LMIs) in terms of
G
and
F

[
70
]
.
For simplicity, we write such inequalities in terms of the inner product
\big\langle\cdot,\cdot\big\rangle
defined as

\big\langle(X,Y),(G,F)\big\rangle=\mathop{\bf tr}(X^{T}G)+Y^{T}F,

for any
(X,Y),\,(G,F)\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
.
Similarly, the performance metrics in Table

From Gram matrix distances to function distances

We now discuss how the distance between functions and the distance between lifted variable representations are related.

For notational simplicity, let
Z=(G,F)
be the pair of Gram matrix and function-value vector.
Define the distance between two proper, lower-semicontinuous, and convex functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
with compact domain
\mathcal{D}\subseteq{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
as

\big\|f_{1}-f_{2}\big\|=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\,|f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)|+\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\,\big\|\nabla f_{1}(x)-\nabla f_{2}(x)\big\|.

(5)

In functional analysis, this defines a complete normed space called the
Sobolev space
.
The classes of function
\mathcal{F}
we consider are closed subspaces of such a Sobolev space, as shown in Theorem
4
in Appendix
A
, and therefore complete normed spaces.
Define the distance over
\mathcal{H}
as

\|(f_{1},x_{1}^{0})-(f_{2},x_{2}^{0})\|=\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|+\|x_{1}^{0}-x_{2}^{0}\|,

for any
(f_{1},x_{1}^{0})
and
(f_{2},x_{2}^{0})
in
\mathcal{H}
,
and the distance over
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
as

\big\|Z_{1}-Z_{2}\big\|=\sqrt{\big\|G_{1}-G_{2}\big\|_{F}^{2}+\big\|F_{1}-F_{2}\big\|^{2}},

(6)

for any
Z_{1}=(G_{1},F_{1})
and
Z_{2}=(G_{2},F_{2})
in
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
, where
\big\|\cdot\big\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.

We claim that, according to these distances, whenever the pairs of function and initial iterate are close to each other, the corresponding Gram matrices and function-value vectors are close as well.
See Appendix
A
for the proof.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
L=\sup_{(f,x)\in\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{D}}\textnormal{Lip}(\nabla f)<\infty
.
Given the iteration number

there exists

such that

\big\|Z_{1}-Z_{2}\big\|\leq C_{K}\|(f_{1},x_{1}^{0})-(f_{2},x_{2}^{0})\|,

for any

and any closed, convex, and proper functions

with

,
g_{1}^{\star}=g_{2}^{\star}=0
, and

.

3.2
Approximating the expectation from data

After the variable lifting from Section

\mathop{\bf E{}}_{Z\sim{\mathbf{P}}_{Z}}\left(\ell(Z)\right),

[

39

40
]

Specifically, we incorporate

Distributionally-robust optimization

Consider a set
\mathcal{P}
of probability distributions supported on
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}

If

upper-bounds the true expectation (

\inf_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\,\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(f,x^{0})\sim{\mathbf{P}}}\left(\ell(f,x^{0})\right)=\inf_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\,\underset{Z\sim{\mathbf{P}}_{Z}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\left(\ell(Z)\right)\leq\begin{array}[t]{ll}\underset{\mathbf{Q}_{Z}\in\mathcal{P}}{\sup}&\underset{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}{\inf}\,\underset{Z\sim\mathbf{Q}_{Z}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\left(\ell(Z)\right).\end{array}

(8)

Our goal is to construct an

that is guaranteed to contain

with high probability, using only partial information about

obtained from samples

Consider the empirical distribution

with respect to the type-

\displaystyle W({\mathbf{P}}_{Z},\mathbf{Q}_{Z})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi({\mathbf{P}}_{Z},\mathbf{Q}_{Z})}\,\left(\int_{\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}\big\|Z_{1}-Z_{2}\big\|\,\pi(dZ_{1},dZ_{2})\right),

is a norm over
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
and

and

with marginals

Assume that the true distribution

\mathop{\bf E{}}_{Z\sim{\mathbf{P}}_{Z}}\,[\exp\left(\big\|Z\big\|^{a}\right)]<\infty

\varepsilon_{N}(\beta)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}N}\right)^{\left((K+2)(K+3)/2+(K+1)\right)^{-1}}&\textnormal{ if }N\geq\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}}\\

3.3
Tractable convex formulation using duality theory

We now derive a convex formulation of the upper bound in (
8
).
Note that the support set
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
of the distributions considered is compact, as the algorithm trajectories are constructed from compact initial iterate sets.

Assumption 2
.

\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
is a compact set in
{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
.

This is not only required to obtain our next result, but also implies the light-tailed property discussed in Section
3.2
which gives finite-sample guarantee (
10
) of Wasserstein DRO.
We now present our main result, which is a convex formulation of the upper bound in (
8
), whose proof is deferred to Appendix
C
.

Theorem 2
.

Given samples
(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,N
, consider the set
\mathcal{L}
of loss functions
\ell\colon\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbf{R}}
parametrized as

\mathcal{L}=\left\{\ell(G,F)=\max_{1\leq j\leq J}\,\ell^{j}(G,F)\;\middle|\;\ell^{j}(G,F)=\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),(G,F)\big\rangle+c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t,~t\in{\mathbf{R}}\right\},

with
A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}
,
b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
, and
c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}
.
Under Assumption
1
, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{lll}\underset{\mathbf{Q}_{Z}\in B_{{\varepsilon}}}{\sup}&\underset{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}{\inf}&\underset{(G,F)\sim\mathbf{Q}_{Z}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\,\left(\ell(G,F)\right)\end{array}=\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\|(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\|\leq\varepsilon,\end{array}

which is also equivalent to

\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{j}-\big\langle(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j}),\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{j})-(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})+\tau_{i}^{j}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\|(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\hfill i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J.\end{array}

(DRO-PEP)

Here, the primal variables are
(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in\Gamma
and
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
where

\Gamma=\left\{(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{N\times J}\;\left|\;\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}=1/N,\qquad i=1,\dots,N\\
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}=0\end{cases}\right.\right\},

and dual variables are
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
t\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\tau_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
, and
y_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
.

Remark 2

(Problem complexity)
.

The maximization problem of Theorem

DRO-PEP
) is a convex conic program.
Still, there is a trade-off between the problem complexity of (
DRO-PEP
), which depends on the numbers of iterations
K
and samples
N
, and the confidence
1-\beta
for which the inequality (
8
) is valid.

Remark 3

(Constructing a worst-case distribution)
.

According to
[
46
, Corollary 4.6]
, a worst-case distribution achieving the optimal value of (

) is a weighted sum of Dirac distributions.
Let
(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in\Gamma
and
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})
for

and

be optimal solutions to the maximization problem of Theorem

For each tuple

).

, we get

\sup_{\mathbf{Q}\in B_{{\varepsilon}}}\mathop{\bf E{}}(\phi^{K}(G,F))=\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\tau_{i}-\big\langle(X_{i},Y_{i}),\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i})-(X_{i},Y_{i})+\tau_{i}(e_{1}e_{1}^{T},0)-(0,e_{2})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{3}\times\{0\}\\
&\big\|(X_{i},Y_{i})\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\hfill i=1,\dots,N,\end{array}

with
variables
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\tau_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
(X_{i},Y_{i})\in{\mathbf{S}}^{3}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{2}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
, and
y_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{6}
.

3.4
Bridging worst-case and average-case analyses

The radius
\varepsilon
of Wasserstein ambiguity set
B_{{\varepsilon}}
controls the trade-off between a priori and a posteriori information.
When
\varepsilon
gets larger, the upper bounds in Corollary
2.1
approach the worst-case convergence bound, which only uses a priori information,
i.e.
, function classes and initial conditions.
On the other hand, when
\varepsilon
gets smaller, the bounds from Corollary
2.1
approach the a posteriori empirical performance,
i.e.
, the sampled average-case and
\mathbf{CVaR}
-case losses for the observed iterate trajectories.
The following theorem states the behavior of the problem (
DRO-PEP
) on its extreme cases:
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\varepsilon\to\infty
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the setup of Corollary
2.1
.

•

When
\varepsilon\to 0
,
the optimal values of (
DRO-PEP
) converge respectively to the in-sample average-case performance
(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi^{K}(\widehat{Z}_{i})
and the in-sample
\mathbf{CVaR}
-performance
\inf_{t\in{\mathbf{R}}}\big(t+(\alpha^{-1}/N){\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}(\phi^{K}(\widehat{Z}_{i})-t)_{+}\big)

which is the optimal value of

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&(\alpha^{-1}/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\phi^{K}(\widehat{Z}_{i})\\
\textnormal{subject to}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}=\alpha,\end{array}

with variables
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]
.

•

Under Assumption
2
, there exists
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon\geq\bar{\varepsilon}
, the optimal values of (
DRO-PEP
) all equal the worst-case performance:

\underset{Z\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}{\sup}\phi^{K}(Z)=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&-c_{0}\tau\\
\textnormal{subject to}&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y)+\tau(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\},\end{array}

with variables
\tau\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
and
y\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
.
This is exactly the dual of worst-case PEP
[
70
, Section 3.4]
.

Proof.

First, consider the case when
\varepsilon\to 0
, where the maximization problem of Theorem
2
has a unique feasible point
Z_{i}=\widehat{Z}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,N
.
Then, the objective value of (
DRO-PEP
) in case of
J=1
will be the in-sample average
(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi^{K}(\widehat{Z}_{i})
.
Similarly, the objective value of (
DRO-PEP
) in case of
J=2
converges to
\max\,\{(\alpha^{-1}/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\phi^{K}{(\widehat{Z}_{i})}\mid(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}=\alpha,\,\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]\}
, which is exactly the
\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\phi^{K}(Z))
of empirical distribution
\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{Z}
.

Under Assumption
2
, there exists a worst-case instance
\bar{Z}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
such that
\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}\phi^{K}(Z)=\phi^{K}(\bar{Z})
.
Let
\bar{\varepsilon}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|\bar{Z}-\widehat{Z}_{i}\|
.
Then for any
\varepsilon\geq\bar{\varepsilon}
,
\bar{Z}
is a feasible point of maximization problem in Theorem
2
since

{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}}\alpha_{i}^{j}\|\bar{Z}-\widehat{Z}_{i}\|=(1/N){\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\|\bar{Z}-\widehat{Z}_{i}\|\leq\bar{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon,

for any
(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in\Gamma
.
Therefore, we can say that
\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(G,F)\sim\delta_{(\widehat{G},\widehat{F})}}(\phi^{K}(G,F))=\phi^{K}(\widehat{G},\widehat{F})
lower-bounds and upper-bounds the worst-case expectations in Corollary
2.1
at the same time, which concludes the proof.
∎

DRO-PEP
) interpolates the probabilistic performance of empirical distribution and the worst-case convergence bound, as Theorem
3
suggests.

3.5
Wasserstein distance with relative error

In this section, we introduce how we choose the norm over
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
, in order to give equal importance to each iteration along the trajectory.
Specifically, the norm described in (
6
) overly weights earlier iterations because they tend to have larger entries,
e.g.
, gradient norm or suboptimality gap.
To address this issue, consider the scaled norm
{\|\cdot\|_{D}}
over
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
defined as

\big\|(G,F)\big\|_{D}=\sqrt{D^{1/2}(G,F)D^{1/2}}=\big\|{(D_{G}^{1/2}GD_{G}^{1/2},D_{F}F)}\big\|,

with a pair of positive definite diagonal matrices
D=(D_{G},D_{F})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{++}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{S}}_{++}^{K+1}
referred to as a
preconditioner
.
An effective way to assign higher weight to later iterations is to construct a preconditioner that is inversely proportional to the observed values of the entries in the diagonal of the samples
\widehat{G}_{i}
and the entries of
\widehat{F}_{i}
.

We use the averaged value of sampled gradient magnitude and function-value gap for every
k
-th iterate as normalizing factor for
g^{k}
and
f^{k}-f^{\star}
, respectively.
Furthermore, in order for the quantity
\big\|(G_{1},F_{1})-(G_{2},F_{2})\big\|_{D}
to be similar for any choice of total iteration number
K
, we further normalize
D
by the number of its diagonal components.
Therefore, we use the following form of the preconditioner
D=(D_{G},D_{F})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{++}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{S}}_{++}^{K+1}
defined as

\displaystyle D_{G}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{K+2}\mathop{\bf diag}\left({\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\big\|\hat{x}_{i}^{0}-\hat{x}_{i}^{\star}\big\|,\;{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\big\|\hat{g}_{i}^{0}\big\|,\;\cdots,\;{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\big\|\hat{g}_{i}^{K}\big\|\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle D_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{K+1}\mathop{\bf diag}\left({\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}(\hat{f}_{i}^{0}-\hat{f}_{i}^{\star}),\cdots,{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}(\hat{f}_{i}^{K}-\hat{f}_{i}^{\star})\right)^{-1}.

Corollary 3.1
.

Consider the setup of Theorem
2
with the Wasserstein ambiguity set
B_{{\varepsilon}}
defined with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{D}
over
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
.
The convex reformulation is given by (
DRO-PEP
) with the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
replaced by
\|\cdot\|_{D^{-1}}
.

Proof.

Since the dual norm
\big\|\cdot\big\|_{D*}
of
\big\|\cdot\big\|_{D}
is evaluated as

\displaystyle\big\|(X,Y)\big\|_{D*}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\big\|(G,F)\big\|_{D}\leq 1}\big\langle(X,Y),(G,F)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|D^{1/2}(G,F)D^{1/2}\|\leq 1}\big\langle(D_{G}^{-1/2}XD_{G}^{-1/2},D_{F}^{-1}Y),D^{1/2}(G,F)D^{1/2}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\|\left(D_{G}^{-1/2}XD_{G}^{-1/2},\,D_{F}^{-1}Y\right)\big\|=\big\|(X,Y)\big\|_{D^{-1}},

where
D^{-1}=(D_{G}^{-1},D_{F}^{-1})
, we can replace the dual norm constraint
\|(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\|_{*}\leq\lambda
in (
DRO-PEP
) by
\|(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\|_{D^{-1}}\leq\lambda
.
∎

3.6
Data-driven convergence rates

Thus far, we have constructed numeric bounds for fixed values of iteration number
K
.
We now turn into extrapolating these bounds to larger values of
K
.
Specifically, we propose a method to fit curves that upper bound the numeric values obtained by solving (
DRO-PEP
), obtaining data-driven convergence rates.

We say an algorithm has a
sublinear
convergence rate if for some
\gamma>0
, the performance metric as a function of
K
decreases as
O(K^{-\gamma})
.
In contrast, we say it has a
linear
convergence rate if for some
\rho\in(0,1)
, the metric decreases as
O(\rho^{K})
.
The following example for gradient descent on different function classes highlights the difference.

Running example

(Gradient descent rates)
.

Consider gradient descent iterations
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-(1/L)\nabla f(x^{k})
.
When
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,L}
, the following worst-case convergence bound holds for the function-value gap
[
21
,
37
]
:

f(x^{K})-f^{\star}\leq\dfrac{L\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}}{2(2K+1)},

i.e.
a sublinear convergence rate with
\gamma=1
.
Instead, if we consider the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
with
\mu>0
, then the following worst-case convergence bound holds with a different initial condition
[
9
, Chapter 9.3]
:

f(x^{K})-f^{\star}\leq(1-\mu/L)^{K}(f(x^{0})-f^{\star}),

i.e.
a linear convergence rate with
\rho=1-\mu/L
.

To fit these curves, we use a constrained least-squares procedure.
Given pairs
\{(K,\phi_{K})\}_{K=1}^{{K_{\max}}}
, where
\phi_{K}
is the optimal value of (
DRO-PEP
) with iteration number
K
, our goal is to approximately fit constants
C>0
,
\gamma\geq 0
, and
\rho\in(0,1]
to the form
\phi_{K}\approx C\rho^{K}(K+1)^{-\gamma}
.
Note that we use
(K+1)
for sublinear terms in order to eliminate the effect of
\gamma
when
K=0
.
Taking the log transform of both sides gives the fit

\log\phi_{K}\approx\log C+K\log\rho-\gamma\log(K+1).

(11)

Let
H\in{\mathbf{R}}^{{K_{\max}}\times 3}
be the matrix whose entries, for every row
K
, are
H_{K1}=1
,
H_{K2}=-\log(K+1)
and
H_{K3}=K
, and also let
h\in{\mathbf{R}}^{{K_{\max}}}
be the vector with entries
h_{K}=\log\phi_{K}
, for
K=1,\cdots,{K_{\max}}
.
Our goal is to fit (
11
) in a least-squares sense; however, we make two modifications.
First, in order to maintain the bounds as in (
8
) with high confidence (
10
), we constrain our fitted curve to upper-bound the observed
\phi_{K}
for each
K
.
Second, since
\phi_{K}
tends to decrease with
K
, we normalize the contribution to the least-squares objective via the diagonal matrix
W\in{\mathbf{S}}^{{K_{\max}}\times{K_{\max}}}
with each diagonal entry taking into account the magnitude of
\phi_{K}
, by setting
W_{KK}=1+\log(\phi_{0}/\phi_{K})
.
Overall, we solve the following constrained, weighted least-squares problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&\|W(Hx-h)\|^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&Hx\geq h.\end{array}

(12)

From the optimal solution
x^{\star}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{3}
to (
12
), we recover
\rho=e^{x^{\star}_{1}}
,
\gamma=x^{\star}_{2}
, and
C=e^{x^{\star}_{3}}
.
Additionally, we may enforce either
x_{1}=0
or
x_{2}=1
if we decide to isolate either a sublinear or linear fit, respectively.

4.1
Unconstrained quadratic minimization

For this experiment, we restrict ourselves to the class of
L
-smooth
\mu
-strongly convex
quadratic
functions,
which is

f(x)=(1/2)x^{T}Qx,

where
Q\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{d\times d}
is a positive semidefinite matrix whose eigenvalues are contained in
[\mu,L]
.
For scalability reasons, we solve (
DRO-PEP
) with the function class
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
of
L
-smooth
\mu
-strongly convex functions, while we make sure to compute the worst-case performance in PEPit
[
8
]
with the tightest function class of convex quadratic functions
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu,L}
.

Algorithms

We compare gradient descent with step size
\eta>0
,

x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\eta\nabla f(x^{k}),\qquad k=0,1,\dots,

(GD)

and Nesterov’s fast gradient method
[
48
]
[
17
, Section B.1.3]
,

\begin{array}[t]{ll}x^{k+1}&=y^{k}-\eta\nabla f(y^{k})\\
y^{k+1}&=x^{k+1}+\beta_{k}(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\qquad k=0,1,\dots,\end{array}

(FGM)

where
\eta\in(0,1/L]
.
The initial iterate
x^{0}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
satisfies the condition
{\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|\leq r}
, and
y^{0}=x^{0}
.
Unless otherwise stated, we recursively define the momentum coefficient
\beta_{k}
as
[
17
, Section B.1.3]

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\alpha_{k+2}&\leftarrow\;\text{Solve }(\alpha_{k+2}-\alpha_{k+1})^{2}-\alpha_{k+2}-q\alpha_{k+2}^{2}=0\\
\beta_{k}&\,=\;\dfrac{(\alpha_{k+2}-\alpha_{k+1})(\alpha_{k+1}(1-q)-\alpha_{k}-1)}{\alpha_{k+2}(2q\alpha_{k+1}+1)-q\alpha^{2}_{k+1}},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,\end{array}

where
q=\mu/L
,
\alpha_{0}=0
, and
\alpha_{1}=(1-q)^{-1}
.
Note that the quadratic equation for
\alpha_{k+2}\geq 0
always has a closed-form solution.

Problem distribution

We sample values of
Q
, whose eigenvalues are all in
[\mu,L]\subseteq{\mathbf{R}}
, to match existing results on average-case analysis
[
63
,
16
,
53
]
.
Let
n=(\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu})^{2}/(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^{2}
and
\sigma=(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})/2
.
Define
A\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d\times nd}
whose entries are i.i.d. Gaussian with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
In the regime of
d\to\infty
, the average value of the eigenvalues of
Q=(1/d)A^{T}A\in{\mathbf{R}}^{nd\times nd}
follows the
Marchenko-Pastur (MP) distribution

[
45
]

\mu_{\text{MP}}
whose probability density function is

d\mu_{\text{MP}}(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{(L-\lambda)(\lambda-\mu)}}{2\pi n\sigma^{2}\lambda}1_{\lambda\in[\mu,L]}d\lambda,

with
1_{[\mu,L]}
being the 0-1 indicator function of the interval
[\mu,L]
.
In practice, we use sufficiently large
d
and rejection sampling to ensure all sample values of
Q
have eigenvalues in our predefined range
[\mu,L]
.

Problem setup

Consider the initial condition
\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|\leq r
.
We set
d=300
,
L=25
,
r=10
,
\eta=1/L
, and sample

In both cases, we used the distance-to-solution

as the performance metric.
Later, we also consider the function-value gap
f(x^{K})-f^{\star}
to compare our bounds to known results
[
16
,
53
]
for this specific distribution.

Comparison with known average-case rates

As discussed in Section
2.1
, probabilistic analysis can be challenging as it requires full knowledge of the underlying distribution
{\mathbf{P}}
.
One such analysis derives the average-case convergence rate in terms of function-value gap
\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(f,x^{0})\sim{\mathbf{P}}}(f(x^{K})-f^{\star})
, when the non-strongly convex quadratic objective function follows the MP distribution and the initial iterate satisfies
\mathop{\bf E{}}_{x^{0}}\big((x^{0}-x^{\star})(x^{0}-x^{\star})^{T}\big)=r^{2}I

[
16
,
53
]
.
In such cases, the average-case convergence rate of GD is of order
O(K^{-1.5})
and that of FGM with momentum coefficient
\beta_{k}=k/(k+3)
is of order
O(K^{-3}\log K)
.

We numerically compare our fitted (
DRO-PEP
) rates in terms of function-value gap with known rates
[
16
,
53
]
.
For our curve-fitting procedure, we fix
\rho=1
and consider an extra
\log(K+1)
-term to the fit in Section
3.6
to match the logarithmic term in the average-case rate of FGM.
That is, we consider the following fit:

\log\phi_{K}\approx-\gamma\log(K+1)+\omega\log\big(\log(K+1)\big)+\log C,

where
\gamma\geq 0
,
\omega\geq 0

Algorithms

As the objective function (

[
54

[
5

Given parameter
\delta>0

\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(v)=\min\left\{v+\delta,\,\max\left\{v-\delta,\,0\right\}\right\}

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a probabilistic performance estimation framework to quantify the convergence behavior of deterministic first-order methods applied on a distribution of convex optimization problems.
By combining performance estimation problem (PEP) and distributionally robust optimization (DRO) techniques, we derive data-driven probabilistic performance guarantees that only require iterate trajectories from a finite set of sampled function instances.
By fitting the resulting bounds, we also construct data-driven convergence rates that consistently improve worst-case analysis.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to apply PEP to establish probabilistic convergence rates.
A key limitation of our approach is the scalability of (
DRO-PEP
), whose dimension is proportional to the number of samples and the square of the number of iterations.
One way to address this issue, is to adopt scenario reduction techniques, such as mean robust optimization
[
76
]
, that cluster the data points together, effectively reducing the size of the DRO problem.

Theorem 4

Therefore, the Sobolev norm (

Lemma 2

We have

\displaystyle\big|f_{1}(x_{1})-f_{2}(x_{2})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|f_{1}(x_{1})-f_{1}(x_{2})\big|+\big|f_{1}(x_{2})-f_{2}(x_{2})\big|

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&[-\ell^{j}]^{*}(\mu_{i}^{j}-\nu_{i}^{j})+\sigma_{\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}(\nu_{i}^{j})-\big\langle\mu_{i}^{j},(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&\big\|\mu_{i}^{j}\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\hfill i=1,\dots,N,\,j=1,\dots,J,\end{array}

with variables
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
t\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\mu_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
, and
\nu_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
.
Note that
[-\ell^{j}]^{*}
is a convex conjugate with respect to variables in
{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle[-\ell^{j}]^{*}(\mu_{i}^{j}-\nu_{i}^{j})

\displaystyle=\underset{(G,F)\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}}{\sup}\;\left(\big\langle\mu_{i}^{j}-\nu_{i}^{j},(G,F)\big\rangle+\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),(G,F)\big\rangle+c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t&\textnormal{if }\mu_{i}^{j}-\nu_{i}^{j}+(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})=0,\\
\infty&\textnormal{o.w}.\end{cases}

The support function
\sigma_{\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}
of

\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}=\left\{(G,F)\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}\,\left|\;\mathcal{S}(G,F)\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|},\;\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),(G,F)\big\rangle+c_{0}\leq 0\right.\right\}

writes as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}(\nu_{i}^{j})

\displaystyle=\underset{(G,F)\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}}{\sup}\;\big\langle\nu_{i}^{j},(G,F)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\big\langle\nu_{i}^{j},(G,F)\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\mathcal{S}(G,F)\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}\\
&-\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),(G,F)\big\rangle-c_{0}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{j}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{j})+\tau_{i}^{j}(A_{0},b_{0})-\nu_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\},\end{array}

with variables
(G,F)\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
,
\tau_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
and
y_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
.
The last equality comes from the strong duality argument via Slater’s condition, which is valid for function classes
\mathcal{F}
in Table
2

[
70
, Theorem 6]
.
The resulting minimization problem becomes

\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{j}-\big\langle(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j}),\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{j})-(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})+\tau_{i}^{j}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\big\|(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\hfill i=1,\dots,N,\,j=1,\dots,J,\end{array}

with variables
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
t\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
y_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
,
\tau_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
, and
(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
.
∎

Next, we take the dual of the minimization problem to reach the maximization problem.

Lemma 4
.

Consider the setup of Theorem
2
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, the weak duality result holds true for the following pair of primal and dual problems:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{j}-\big\langle(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j}),\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{j})-(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})+\tau_{i}^{j}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\big\|(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\qquad(i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J),\end{array}

\displaystyle\geq\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\|(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon,\end{array}

with variables
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
and
(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in\Gamma
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma=\left\{(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{N\times J}\;\left|\;\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}=1/N,&i=1,\dots,N\\
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}=0\end{array}\right.\right\}.

In addition, the strong duality holds therefore the above inequality holds with equality.

Proof.

Consider the minimization problem in Theorem
2
.
Using the auxiliary variable
\lambda_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}
for
i=1,\dots,N
and
j=1,\dots,J
, the problem writes as

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{j}-\big\langle(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\,\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{j})-(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})+\tau_{i}^{j}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\lambda_{i}^{j}=\lambda,\hfill(i=1,\dots,N,\,j=1,\dots,J),\end{array}

with variables
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
t\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
y_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
,
\tau_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
, and
\big(\lambda_{i}^{j},(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\big)\in{\mathcal{K}_{\text{SOC}}}
.
The Lagrangian of this problem with dual variables

\alpha_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+},\quad(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1},\quad\varepsilon_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}},

writes as

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\left(s_{i},\lambda,t,y_{i}^{j},\big(\lambda_{i}^{j},(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\big),\;\alpha_{i}^{j},(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j}),\varepsilon_{i}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\,\alpha_{i}^{j}\left(c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{j}-\big\langle(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j}),\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon-s_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\,\big\langle\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{j})+(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})-\tau_{i}^{j}(A_{0},b_{0})+(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\,\varepsilon_{i}^{j}(\lambda_{i}^{j}-\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\left(\frac{1}{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\alpha_{i}^{j}\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i}^{j}\right)+t\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\,(y_{i}^{j})^{T}\left(\mathcal{S}(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\,\tau_{i}^{j}\left(\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle+\alpha_{i}^{j}c_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\,\left(\lambda_{i}^{j}\varepsilon_{i}^{j}+\big\langle(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j}),(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-\alpha_{i}^{j}(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle.

The corresponding dual problem is

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}=1/N,&i=1,\dots,N\\
&\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)\varepsilon-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\varepsilon_{i}^{j}\geq 0\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}=0\\
&\mathcal{S}(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|},&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J\\
&\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle+\alpha_{i}^{j}c_{0}\leq 0,&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J\\
&\left(\varepsilon_{i}^{j},\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-\alpha_{i}^{j}(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\right)\in{\mathcal{K}_{\text{SOC}}},&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J,\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}=1/N,&i=1,\dots,N\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big\|(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-\alpha_{i}^{j}(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}=0\\
&\mathcal{S}(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|},&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J,\\
&\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle+\alpha_{i}^{j}c_{0}\leq 0,&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J,\end{array}

with variables
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
and
\alpha_{i}^{j}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
.
Note that
\varepsilon_{i}^{j}
is removed using
\big\|(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-\alpha_{i}^{j}(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon_{i}^{j}
.
Using the change of variables
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\leftarrow\alpha_{i}^{j}(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})
, we get the problem

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}=1/N,&i=1,\dots,N\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\|(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{j}\right)c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}=0\\
&\alpha_{i}^{j}\mathcal{S}(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|},&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J,\\
&\alpha_{i}^{j}\left(\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle+c_{0}\right)\leq 0,&i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J.\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{lll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j}),\,(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{i}^{j}\big\|(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon\end{array}

with variables
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
and
(\alpha_{i}^{j})\in\Gamma
.
The equality holds due to the following reasons:
The case of
\alpha_{i}^{j}=0
allows
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})
to be any value in
{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}_{+}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
, with the same objective value.
Therefore, even with the constraint
\mathcal{S}(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
and
\big\langle(A_{0},b_{0}),(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})\big\rangle+c_{0}\leq 0
, the optimal value remains the same as these constraints are feasible with
(G_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j})=(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
.

Finally, we conclude the proof with showing the strong duality.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, the minimization problem satisfies Slater’s condition if there exists a feasible point with
\lambda=0
, due to following construction of strictly feasible solution: let
\lambda=(K+2)\varepsilon
and
(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})=(-\varepsilon I,0)\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
. If
y^{j}_{\star}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
and
\tau^{j}_{\star}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
satisfy

-\mathcal{S}(y^{j}_{\star})+\tau^{j}_{\star}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\},

this leads to

-\mathcal{S}(y^{j}_{\star})-(X^{j}_{i},Y^{j}_{i})+\tau^{j}_{\star}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{j})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{++}^{K+2}\times\{0\}.

Furthermore,
\big\|(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})\big\|=\sqrt{K+2}\varepsilon<(K+2)\varepsilon=\lambda
.
Therefore, if
\varepsilon>0
, we can always construct a strictly feasible solution
y_{i}^{j}=y^{j}_{\star}
,
\tau_{i}^{j}=\tau^{j}_{\star}
, and
(X_{i}^{j},Y_{i}^{j})=(-\varepsilon I,0)
.
And indeed, such feasible
y^{j}_{\star}
and
\tau^{j}_{\star}
exist, as they come from the solution of the dual PEP problem for the worst-case analysis.
∎

2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

According to Lemma
3
, the worst-case expectation problem is equivalent to the minimization problem in Theorem
2
.
Furthermore, Lemma
4
states that the minimization problem and the maximization problem in Theorem

are equivalent, with strong duality.
∎

2.1
.

Proof of Corollary
2.1
.

First, let
J=1
with
(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{1},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{1},c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{1})=(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}},0)
, which corresponds to
\mathcal{L}=\{\phi^{K}(G,F)\}=\{\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}}),(G,F)\big\rangle\}
.
Using Theorem
2
automatically gives
\alpha_{i}=1/N
for all
i=1,\dots,N
and results in

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{Q}_{Z}\in B_{{\varepsilon}}}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(G,F)\sim\mathbf{Q}_{Z}}\big(\phi^{K}(G,F))

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&-c_{0}\tau_{i}-\big\langle(X_{i},Y_{i}),\,(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i})-(X_{i},Y_{i})+\tau_{i}(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\big\|(X_{i},Y_{i})\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\hfill i=1,\dots,N,\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}}),(G_{i},F_{i})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\|(G_{i},F_{i})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon,\end{array}

with primal variables
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\tau_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
(X_{i},Y_{i})\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
, and
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}

and dual variables
(G_{i},F_{i})\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
.

Next, let
J=2
with
\ell(G,F)=\max\{\ell^{1}(G,f),\,\ell^{2}(G,F)\}
such that

\left(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{1},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{1},c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{1}\right)=\left(\alpha^{-1}A_{\mathrm{obj}},\alpha^{-1}b_{\mathrm{obj}},-(\alpha^{-1}-1)\right),\qquad\left(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{2},b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{2},c_{\mathrm{obj}}^{2}\right)=(0,0,1),

gives

\ell^{1}(G,F)=\alpha^{-1}\phi^{K}{(G,F)}-(\alpha^{-1}-1)t,\qquad\ell^{2}(G,F)=t.

Applying Theorem
2
results in

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{ll}\underset{\mathbf{Q}_{Z}\in B_{{\varepsilon}}}{\sup}&\mathbf{CVaR}_{\alpha}\big(\phi^{K}(G,F)\big)\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&-(\alpha^{-1}-1)t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{1}-\big\langle(X_{i},Y_{i}),(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&t-c_{0}\tau_{i}^{2}-\big\langle(X_{i}^{2},Y_{i}^{2}),(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\rangle+\lambda\varepsilon\leq s_{i}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{1})-(X_{i}^{1},Y_{i}^{1})+\alpha^{-1}\tau_{i}^{1}(A_{0},b_{0})-\alpha^{-1}(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y_{i}^{2})-(X_{i}^{2},Y_{i}^{2})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\big\|(X_{i}^{1},Y_{i}^{1})\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\quad\big\|(X_{i}^{2},Y_{i}^{2})\big\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\qquad(i=1,\dots,N,\;j=1,\dots,J),\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\alpha^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\big\langle(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}}),(G_{i},F_{i})\big\rangle\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\big\|(G_{i},F_{i})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\big\|\leq\varepsilon,\end{array}

with primal variables
s_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
t\in{\mathbf{R}}
,
\tau_{i}^{1},\tau^{2}_{i}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
,
y_{i}^{1},y_{i}^{2}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{|M|}
,
(X_{i}^{1},Y_{i}^{1}),(X_{i}^{2},Y_{i}^{2})\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
, and
\lambda\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
and dual variables
(\alpha_{i})\in\Gamma=\{(\alpha_{i})\in[0,1]^{N}\mid(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}=\alpha\}
and
(G_{i},F_{i})\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{X}
.
∎
