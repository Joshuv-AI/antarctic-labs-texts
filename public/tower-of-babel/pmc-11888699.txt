Correlation of Anthropometric Measurements with Insulin Resistance Assessed by HOMA-IR in Obese Normotensive Individuals: A Cross-Sectional Study in South India
Authors: S Swarupa Gnana Sudha Meriam, Karunya Varsha Ragavendran Susethira, B.S. Saravanan, Susethira Aruppukottai Rajendran, Panneerselvam Periasamy, Sasikala Gunasekaran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The study aims to evaluate insulin resistance in obese individuals with normotensive diabetes using Homeostasis Model Assessment and correlate it with anthropometric measurements, highlighting the link between poor lifestyles and metabolic syndrome.Methods:The research assessed the anthropometric, lipid, fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1C, serum insulin, and lipidosis profiles of 100 people using both traditional procedures and the Homeostatic model assessment (HOMA)-IR.Results:A study of 100 participants, including 43 males and 57 females, found a significant waist–hip ratio (HOMA-IR >2.5) among the group, indicating a high level of health.Conclusions:The research discovered notable connections between anthropometric measurements and clinical and biochemical factors associated with insulin resistance, with subscapular skinfold thickness demonstrating more robust linkages.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity increases the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, cancer, and osteoarthritis due to excessive body fat accumulation, potentially reducing lifespan.[1]

Insulin resistance (IR) is a physiological state in which cells do not adequately react to the typical functions of insulin, resulting in elevated levels of glucose in the bloodstream. IR, a prevalent condition in obesity, may be used as an indicator of health-related negative outcomes such as coronary artery disease and stroke. Typically, these outcomes are assessed via invasive and expensive procedures.[23]

The study aims to understand IR-related complications in obese individuals by examining the correlation between anthropometric indices and IR. It uses the Homeostasis Model Assessment to examine IR in obese normoglycemic normotensive individuals and determine its correlation with anthropometric data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The research included a sample of 100 individuals who were both obese and asymptomatic. These participants sought regular health check-ups at the General Medicine Out-Patient Department of the Government Erode Medical College and Hospital in Perundurai between January 2023 and March 2024.

The study focuses on asymptomatic normotensive, normaloglycemic, and obese individuals with BMI over 30 kg/m2. Excluded individuals consist of diabetic patients, individuals with malignancy, pregnant females, those using oral contraceptive pills, individuals with hypothyroidism, polycystic ovary syndrome, chronic renal and liver disease patients, individuals experiencing acute stress within 6 weeks of an illness, and individuals taking insulin-resistance medicines.

The study assessed body weight, sagittal abdominal diameter, waist girth, hip girth, and waist–hip ratio (WHR) using anthropometric measurements. Obese patients had a BMI over 30 kg/m2. Subcutaneous adipose tissue (seasonal affective disorder [SAD]) was measured using a sliding-beam caliper at the iliac crest level. Waist circumference (WC) was measured using non-elastic tape when the individual was upright and exhaled normally. The WHR is the ratio of the waist-to-hip circumference.

The study used the glucose oxidase-peroxidase coupled method (GOD-POD) technique to assess fasting blood glucose and plasma phosphate buffer, while HbA1c was quantified using HPLC. Insulin levels were assessed using CMIA. The Homeostasis Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was used to quantify IR, with patients with a HOMA-IR value over 2.5 indicating IR, and those below 2.5 indicating insulin sensitivity.

Photometry was used to evaluate the fasting lipid profile, which included total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, and LDL.

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, using measures like frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, with significance thresholds set at P values less than 0.05 for both normal and skewed data. The research was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee at Government Erode Medical College and Hospital in Perundurai, Tamil Nadu, ensuring informed consent and maintaining participant anonymity.

RESULTS

A sample of 100 patients was randomly chosen from an out-patient department, consisting of 43 males and 57 females. The average age was 37.83 for patients with HOMA-IR values up to 2.5 and 39.83 for those with values over 2.5. Anthropometric measures were likewise associated with HOMA-IR, with 17 patients in group 1 and 83 patients in group 2.

A research was done on 100 patients who had a body mass index (BMI) more than 30. These patients were divided into two groups: group 1 consisted of 17 patients with a HOMA-IR value less than 2.5 and group 2 consisted of 83 individuals. The findings indicated a P value of 0.3, which was not statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

Adiposity and fat accumulation location have a role in the impairment of IR caused by obesity, with abdominal obesity being associated with IR and metabolic disorders. There is a need for affordable and easily accessible methodologies in clinical practice and epidemiologic investigations, and SAD has been suggested as a relevant indicator.[4]

Depression is a powerful indicator of IR, cardiovascular death, and type 2 diabetes and is a reliable indicator of increased insulin production in nondiabetic individuals who are fat.[5]

SAD, initially measured on CT images, is a reliable indicator of visceral fat mass, providing accurate predictive values for assessing visceral adipose tissue.[6]

Magnetic resonance imaging studies have shown that an increase in WC may indicate an increase in subcutaneous fat storage in the supine position.[7]

The results of our group’s SAD measures in the supine position align with earlier studies, confirming their high level of accuracy. This research evaluated the severity of SAD using flexed legs, which enhanced the accuracy of the assessments in comparison to using straight legs.[8]

The research revealed a stronger association between SAD and IR as well as cardiovascular risk, compared to BMI, waist girth, and WHR in both males and females.[9]

The research examined the use of SAD (subscapular skinfold thickness and abdominal circumference) for infrared (IR) screening in a sample of 100 individuals. It then compared the results with traditional anthropometric measurements by using the HOMA-IR index. The results indicated that all anthropometric measurements were strongly connected with clinical and biochemical factors associated with IR. Among these measurements, SAD exhibited the strongest correlation with most variables, including IR indices, whereas WHR had less correlation.

CONCLUSION

HOMA-IR is a dependable measure of IR in overweight adults with a genetic predisposition to metabolic syndrome, while SAD is a straightforward and cost-efficient tool for diagnosing IR in individuals who might benefit from lifestyle modification. The sample size was limited, consisting of just one tertiary hospital. Additionally, the study did not take into account the mother’s socioeconomic status and dietary factors.
