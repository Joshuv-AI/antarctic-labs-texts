Mengke Wu, Weizi Liu, Yanyun Wang, Weiyu Ding, Mike Yao

Abstract
. Smart recommendation algorithms have revolutionized content delivery and improved efficiency across various domains. However, concerns about user agency persist due to their inherent opacity (information asymmetry) and one-way influence (power asymmetry). This study introduces a provotype designed to enhance user agency by providing actionable transparency and control over data management and content delivery. We conducted qualitative interviews with 19 participants to explore their preferences and concerns regarding the features, as well as the provotype ’s impact on users’ understanding and trust toward recommender systems. Findings underscore the importance of integrating transparency with control, and reaffirm users’ desire for agency and the ability to actively intervene in personalization. We also discuss insights for encouraging adoption and awareness of such agency-enhancing features. Overall, this study contributes novel approaches and applicable insights, laying the groundwork for designing more user-centered recommender systems that foreground user autonomy and fairness in AI-driven content delivery.



1.
INTRODUCTION

Behavioral targeting and predictive algorithms have revolutionized information dissemination by enhancing efficiency and fundamentally reshaping the ways media is produced and distributed. These systems draw on user data to anticipate their interests and automatically curate relevant content
(
Pandey et al. 2011
;
Yoneda et al. 2019
)
. Their use now spans across social media, news platforms, forums, streaming services, e-commerce, and more. While much research has examined the development and impact of these recommender systems (RS), growing concerns remain about their implications for human-AI interactions and the pursuit of human-centered design
(
Ehsan et al. 2021
;
Shin and Park 2019
)
. One persistent criticism is the opacity of algorithmic decision-making, often portrayed as a “black box” in which users can only access and consume final outputs
(
Pasquale 2015
;
Burrell 2016
)
, lacking explanations and involvement
(
Felzmann et al. 2020
;
Weller 2019
)
. This results in two imbalances:
1) information asymmetry
, which limits users’ perceptual agency by concealing the mechanisms behind recommendations
(
Kizilcec 2016
;
Rader et al. 2018
;
Sonboli et al. 2021
)
, and
2) power asymmetry
, which erodes users’ behavioral agency by restricting their ability to shape or intervene in algorithmic decisions
(
Cheng and Chouldechova 2023
;
Molina and Sundar 2022
;
Sundar 2020
)
. These asymmetries challenge user-centered design principles and risk manipulation, information blockage, or loss of trust
(
Conover et al. 2011
;
Kizilcec 2016
;
Nguyen et al. 2014
;
Rühr et al. 2023
)
.

Recent advancements in AI transparency, explainable AI (XAI)
(
Kizilcec 2016
;
Rader et al. 2018
)
, and human-AI collaborative decision-making (HACD) have sought to mitigate these issues. For example, cookie disclaimers attempt to address information asymmetry by making data collection more explicit and manageable
(
Segijn et al. 2021
)
, while permission controls
(
Burrell 2016
;
Segijn et al. 2021
)
and collaborative input mechanisms
(
Lai et al. 2023
;
Molina and Sundar 2022
)
redistribute limited power to users. However, such solutions often fall short: they provide partial insight without addressing deeper asymmetries, still leaving users with little ability to regulate the scope or outcomes of personalization. This underlines the need for more comprehensive approaches that integrate transparency and control, ensuring users can both comprehend and actively participate in the recommendation process.

Beyond these imbalances, RS face broader critiques of
"filter bubbles,"
where personalization amplifies itself by reinforcing existing interests while narrowing exposure to alternatives
(
Pariser 2011
;
Nguyen et al. 2014
)
. Over time, this narrowing can render feeds repetitive or predictable, constrain opportunities for exploration, and heighten risks of disengagement or even misinformation
(
Adamopoulos and Tuzhilin 2014
;
Aridor et al. 2020
)
. Attempts to counter these effects have often focused on backend algorithmic strategies, such as injecting diversity, novelty, or serendipity into recommendations
(
Kaminskas and Bridge 2016
;
Castells et al. 2021
;
Ziarani and Ravanmehr 2021
)
. While useful, these efforts remain largely system-centric: they tune outputs without asking how much or what kind of variation users themselves want. Moreover, most existing designs implicitly assume that users always value personalized content, overlooking scenarios where they might instead seek divergence from algorithmic predictions.

Building on these gaps, this paper pursues two goals:
1) to explore how interface-level features can enhance transparency and control in RS
, and
2) to investigate how such design interventions affect user perceptions, attitudes, and sense of agency (the ability to understand, evaluate, and meaningfully influence) toward the system.
To do so, we designed and evaluated a
provotype
(provocative prototype) that allows users to adjust data practices and navigate content discovery. Participants engaged with the provotype and shared feedback in semi-structured interviews. We use this process as a means to generate insights that can in turn inform broader design challenges. The findings contribute to ongoing debates on algorithmic fairness, user autonomy, and human–AI collaboration, while offering practical design guidelines for RS that balance algorithmic efficiency with user agency. Ultimately, this work advances a vision of RS that fosters greater understanding, control, and trust in the digital ecosystem.

2.
RELATED WORK

2.1.
Information Asymmetry

Information asymmetry in algorithm-based RS refers to the discrepancy between what users could observe (the recommendations) and what remains hidden (how recommendations were generated). Such asymmetry often prevents users from fully comprehending the system’s logic and undermines perceptual user agency, standing in contrast to the pursuit of "interpretability" and "explainability" in complex algorithms. Prior work has stressed the importance of making RS transparent and interpretable, especially from the user perspective
(
Cramer et al. 2008
;
Kizilcec 2016
;
Sonboli et al. 2021
)
. This is further linked to the situation awareness for autonomous and AI systems for effective interaction and oversight
(
Endsley 2023
)
. A range of strategies has been proposed to address this issue: 1) source transparency, which clarifies why and where such recommendations originate
(
Felzmann et al. 2020
)
; and/or 2) process transparency, often realized through Explainable AI (XAI) that elucidates the system behavior
(
Rader et al. 2018
;
Ras et al. 2018
)
. Familiar mechanisms, such as Cookie disclaimers, serve as forms of transparency by informing users about what data is collected and how it shapes personalized content
(
Millett et al. 2001
;
Segijn et al. 2021
)
.

Guidelines have also been developed to steer algorithmic systems toward greater transparency and usability.
(
Liao et al. 2020
)
proposed a "question bank" to identify user needs around explanations, while Microsoft’s HAX framework offers structured guidelines for building user-centered XAI
(
Amershi et al. 2019
)
. Usability guidelines for user interface and XAI design further advocate to address users’ needs for learning "what to explain" and "how to explain"
(
Eiband et al. 2018
;
Wolf 2019
)
.

2.2.
Power Asymmetry

While information asymmetry primarily limits perception, power asymmetry constrains agency behaviorally. In AI-driven RS, power asymmetry reflects the imbalance between user control and algorithmic authority.
(
Sundar 2020
)
highlights the inherent tension between human and machine agency, identifying that the loss of agency drives fears about automation and underscoring the importance of human-AI synergy, where users directly shape algorithms to align with their needs. In contrast, persistent power asymmetry can reduce system adoption, as lower perceived control diminishes both perceived usefulness and engagement intentions
(
Chen and Sundar 2018
)
. Proactive and reactive relationships between users and algorithms have also been discussed: proactive interactions involve machine agency predicting user needs and delivering content based on analyzed patterns, whereas reactive interactions exhibit user agency that responds to explicit user requests by personalized suggestions
(
Zhang and Sundar 2019
)
.

2.2.1.
Existing User Control in RS

User control mechanisms are essential for mitigating power asymmetry, improving transparency, trust, and user satisfaction. They also address ethical concerns, such as accountability and democratic participation
(
Harambam et al. 2019
)
. Emerging concepts like
"interactive transparency"

(
Molina and Sundar 2022
)
and human-AI collaborative decision-making (HACD)
(
Lai et al. 2023
;
Schemmer et al. 2022
)
emphasize respecting user feelings and involving users in algorithmic decision-making processes, thereby bolstering both trust and perceived control toward the system.

Behavioral agency manifests in three primary ways: 1) granting or withholding permissions (e.g., declining cookies)
(
Berens et al. 2024
;
Segijn et al. 2021
)
; 2) specifying preferences for personalized recommendations
(
Rashid et al. 2002
;
Zheng et al. 2022
)
; and 3) providing feedback to refine algorithmic decision-making
(
Cheng and Chouldechova 2023
;
Molina and Sundar 2022
)
. For example, studies have enabled users to understand and adjust how their data influences recommendations by inspecting and modifying their profiles, thereby boosting transparency and trust
(
Bakalov et al. 2013
)
. This has been further expanded by designing interfaces for preference specification, recommendation adjustments, and feedback, creating more interactive and responsive experiences
(
Jannach et al. 2017
;
Jannach et al. 2019
)
. Other efforts include adjustable parameters (e.g., popularity, recency), which offer immediate influence over outputs and enhance satisfaction
(
Harper et al. 2015
)
. Meta-recommender systems combine multiple recommendation sources to further empower users in personalizing and controlling the recommendation process
(
Schafer et al. 2002
)
. Figure
1
presents different forms of user agency in current practices.

Figure 1.
Examples of Different Types of Agency. Perceptual Agency (Left): (a) Source Transparency, (b) Process Transparency; Behavioral Agency (Right): (c) Executing Permissions, (d) Specifying Preferences, (e) Providing Feedback.

Research also examines how users perceive control and the psychological factors behind it. Value-added functions like tailoring control interfaces to individual traits can increase recommendation acceptance
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
, while combining control with visual explanations further enhances transparency and satisfaction
(
Tsai and Brusilovsky 2021
)
. In conversational RS, adaptability, understanding, and responsiveness are critical for fostering a sense of control
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
. Overall, well-designed control mechanisms can reduce cognitive load and improve engagement, directly strengthening users’ perceived control
(
Laban and Araujo 2020
)
.

2.3.
Filter Bubbles & Algorithmic Exploration

As RS have become more sophisticated and finely tuned for predictive accuracy (e.g.,
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
), a growing concern is that over-personalization produces
"filter bubbles"
: the system infers user preferences, ranks content accordingly, and based on the clicks it elicits, creating a feedback loop where past behavior narrows future exposure and suppresses alternatives. Such narrowing increases vulnerability to misinformation by removing countervailing sources
(
Pariser 2011
;
Nguyen et al. 2014
;
Adamopoulos and Tuzhilin 2014
)
. For users, filter bubbles compress the range of encountered information, leading to boredom, predictable feeds, and disengagement
(
Aridor et al. 2020
)
. HCI and RS communities have long cautioned that "being accurate is not enough", which can mask user isolation while inflating satisfaction metrics
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

2.3.1.
Mitigation to Filter Bubbles

To address beyond-accuracy objectives, prior work has introduced diversity, novelty, and serendipity into recommendation algorithms
(
Ziarani and Ravanmehr 2021
;
Kaminskas and Bridge 2016
;
Castells et al. 2021
)
.
Novelty
is the difference between present and past experience, i.e., how much a recommendation departs from a user’s prior consumption.
Diversity
, in contrast, concerns intra-list heterogeneity at a given moment, spanning distinct categories, styles, or attributes, regardless of user familiarity. However, neglecting unexpectedness could still make outcomes feel monotonous, and the assumption that users always want more of the familiar can be flawed
(
Iaquinta et al. 2008
)
. Therefore,
serendipity
incorporates both relevance and unexpectedness, typically achieving novelty with minimal accuracy trade-offs
(
Ziarani and Ravanmehr 2021
)
. However, most existing efforts focus on backend optimization that gauges and adjusts system outputs; few consider external user-driven control over how much AI-recommended content they need before intervening in algorithmic decisions. As a result, even when users seek broader or explorative information, they can not explicitly regulate it from the interface.

2.3.2.
Information Fairness

A related concern is user-side information fairness
(
Deldjoo et al. 2021
)
. Collaborative-filtering pipelines often overlook unobserved attributes and emerging user groups, meaning that some individuals are systematically exposed to less of the catalog even when they should have access to similar choices
(
Misztal-Radecka and Indurkhya 2021
)
. As user data accumulates and algorithms grow more complex, users will inevitably encounter divergent slices of content and may never see the "whole picture." To address this, mixing a small proportion of non-personalized or even provocative items into otherwise tailored slates could enhance information fairness and strengthen choice confidence by broadening exposure to items not yet signaled by the user. This parallels work on exposure fairness via randomized ranking to balance opportunity
(
Singh and Joachims 2018
;
Ubaid et al. 2021
)
, but shifts control to the interface, enabling users themselves to invoke broader perspectives when desired. To date, however, such front-end mechanisms remain rare for users, highlighting opportunities for design interventions and motivating our research.

3.
PROVOTYPE DESIGN

3.1.
What is a
Provotype
and Why We Use It

How can we enhance user empowerment in AI RS? To explore this, we developed features on both settings and feed pages for more explicit information disclosure and user intervention than typical RS, embedding them into a
provotype
: a provocative design artifact intended to spark discussion rather than advance toward final production
(
To et al. 2022
)
. Unlike standard prototypes, which imply refinement and usability testing, a
provotype
"feels out the boundaries of a problem" and encourages participants to question assumptions. This draws on IDEO’s notion of
"sacrificial concepts"
that are early-stage ideas used to provoke conversations and help imagine alternative futures
(
Carey and Howard 2009
)
. Prior work shows the value of
provotypes
in surfacing tensions and fostering co-creation by inviting people to respond to concrete ideas rather than abstract questions
(
Brandt et al. 2012
;
Boer and Donovan 2012
)
. As such, our
provotype
serves two goals: 1) gather user feedback on proposed features, and 2) serve as a tangible means to provoke reflection and discussion about user agency and control in RS.

We chose TikTok as the display platform for the
provotype
due to its global popularity and diverse content (e.g., lifestyle, learning, news, entertainment)
(
Boeker and Urman 2022
;
Alley and Hanshew 2022
)
, which facilitates engagement with and discussion of our features across different content scenarios. Nevertheless, since the core AI recommendation mechanisms are similar across platforms, TikTok was used solely to demonstrate functionality, not to imply the features are limited to TikTok or social media. Where applicable, our descriptions and findings were translated into insights generalizable beyond social media.

3.2.
Ideating Process

We aimed to introduce design elements that enhance user agency amid information asymmetry (transparency), power asymmetry (control), and filter bubbles. To guide this, we focused on two common aspects of RS: data collection and personalization, and created a dedicated design canvas for each. Within each canvas, co-authors engaged in a structured ideation process: reviewing literature-identified problems, drawing inspiration from prior design research (e.g.,
(
Storms et al. 2022
;
Luria 2023
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Bemmann et al. 2024
)
), and proposing new countermeasures. We also considered feature placement, either directly on the feed page for in-situ interaction or in settings for more deliberate customization. All proposed features were then collectively evaluated within the research team against criteria like expected user impact, novelty, understandability, and usability. To reduce participant cognitive load in a one-time study, the highest-scoring features from each canvas were selected for the provotype. Figure
2
shows the distribution of implemented features across focus (transparency vs. control) and placement (feed-level vs. settings-level), with Section
3.3
detailing each.

3.3.
Proposed Features in the Provotype

We implemented the provotype in Figma with interactivity to simulate a real application. It supported clicking on all essential chips and performing other gestures (e.g., drag sliders, hover to view information, scroll through entries), effectively conveying the visual structure and user flow of all features.

3.3.1.
Data Management

The Data Management suite enhances transparency and control over how personal data can shape recommendations. These features collectively make abstract algorithmic processes tangible, enabling users to understand, monitor, and influence the system. As such, users are empowered to actively manage and align recommendations with their preferences, interests, and privacy concerns. Figure
3
illustrates the suite and its functions.

•

Data Type Weighting:
Users gain fine-grained control over the types of data influencing recommendations. Five main categories are available: demographics (age, gender, location), search history (recent queries on the platform or linked services), interaction patterns (e.g., clicks, dwell time, comments, likes, shares), explicit interests (followed topics, creators, or channels), and social affiliation (friends’ activities and similar user profiles). Rather than relying solely on platform defaults, users can adjust how heavily each data type is weighted. Sliding scales allow users to increase, decrease, or disable a data type’s contribution, offering finer control than simple "on/off" toggles (see Figure
3
-1). This flexibility empowers users to prioritize their desired signals while reducing reliance on sensitive or less relevant data.

•

Data Usage % Indicator:
Building on the Data Type Weighting, this feature visualizes how data types contribute to individual recommendations, giving users clear feedback on their adjustments. Each recommended content has a color-coded “info” icon representing the dominant data source(s) (e.g., blue = demographics, green = interaction patterns). Users can hover over it to quickly grasp the main driver(s) and their percentage(s). To reduce cognitive overload, we avoid showing a full breakdown of all contributing types. Such indicators establish a direct feedback loop: changes in the backend weighting sliders will be reflected in the front-end feed. For example, users can observe a greater frequency of green icons if they increase Interaction Patterns to "high," making the impact of their control tangible and reinforcing their sense of agency over the system.

•

Data Aging Setting:
In addition to data types, users can adjust the time-wise focus of data used in generating recommendations. A slider enables users to prioritize long-term behavior or recent interactions (e.g., past few days, weeks, or months) to restrict recommendation data usage to that period (see Figure
3
-2). By making the system’s handling of temporal data explicit and adjustable, this feature enhances transparency and lets users tailor recommendations to their current interests and needs.

•

Data Diary:
Serving as a record of how user behavior shapes their recommendations over time, the Data Diary functions like a system log. It discloses user actions (e.g., searches, followed creators, content skips) and their effects on the type and frequency of recommended content (see Figure
3
-3). By showing how specific interactions increase or decrease recommendations, it helps users understand the dynamic link between their behavior and system response. For privacy and relevance, the diary retains data for a limited period (e.g., three months), offering a concise, meaningful view of recent activity without overwhelming historical information.

Figure 3.
Data Management Suite: (1) Data Type Weighting, (2) Data Usage % Indicator, (3) Data Aging Setting, (4) Data Diary.

3.3.2.
Content Discovery

To address challenges with filter bubbles and limited diversity in AI recommendations, we designed the Content Discovery suite to support intentional exploration and balance in users’ feeds. It empowers users to go beyond standard algorithmic outputs by encountering new perspectives, broadening interests, and reflecting on usual consumption patterns. Clear backend controls and front-end visual cues provide transparency into the system’s reasoning and let users consciously guide how, when, and what kinds of special content appear in their feed. Figure
4
illustrates the suite and its functions.

Figure 4.
Content Discovery Suite: (1) Adventure Content (A - Balance, B - Time), (2) Disruption Content (A - Frequency, B - Topic, C - Time), (3) Content Tagging.

•

Adventure Content:
Designed to help users step beyond their algorithmically tailored feeds, Adventure Content invites them to "wander off the path" and explore random, non-personalized recommendations for fresh ideas and unexpected discoveries. It introduces two core controls. The Adventure Balance slider adjusts the proportion of tailored versus random content, from a fully personalized feed (right end) to a non-data-driven targeting, similar to browsing as a visitor or in incognito mode (left end). Intermediate positions expose users to a mix of new perspectives while maintaining some familiarity (see Figure
4
-1A). The Adventure Time option adds further flexibility by letting users schedule designated windows for such exploration, such as following the proportion to show adventurous content on weekends from 10–11 p.m. (see Figure
4
-1B). Together, these controls provide a structured yet proactive way to break out of their content bubbles while preserving the benefits of personalization.

•

Disruption Content:
While Adventure introduces randomness, Disruption takes a more intentional approach to "breaking the bubble" by delivering content that challenges users’ typical interests, beliefs, and habits. Unlike random exploration, Disruption indeed leverages user data but surfaces deliberately different content to provoke reflection and broaden perspectives. The name
Disruption
is intentionally strong: it cautions users that enabling this feature could surface uncomfortable or even offensive content (e.g., in debates or news), prompting careful review and intentional use. Users can adjust the Disruption Frequency, ranging from completely off (for those who prefer no disturbance) to gentle nudges (e.g., one in fifty posts), noticeable mixes (one in ten), or bold shifts (every post diverging from the usual feed) (see Figure
4
-2A). To maintain comfort and agency, disruption can be limited to selected topics, such as political views, cultural content (e.g., from unfamiliar regions), or industry topics (cross-domain inspiration), allowing users to choose where they wish to encounter challenges (see Figure
4
-2B). Like Adventure, a Disruption Time option enables scheduling these experiences during specific windows (see Figure
4
-2C). This feature creates opportunities for meaningful exposure to difference and diversity while giving users granular control over when and how they step beyond their algorithmic comfort zones.

•

Content Tagging:
This feature labels special content (adventure or disruption) for users to distinguish it from regular recommendations. It can also be disabled, in which case all content appears unlabeled. When enabled, small icons mark such posts to signal their category. Clicking the icon opens a brief "why you see this" explanation to help understand the system’s reasoning and the diversity behind this content, with quick access to learn more or adjust settings for that content type (see Figure
4
-3). In doing so, the feature improves system transparency and surfaces backend settings, streamlining immediate in-feed access. We excluded feedback options for this function, as adventure and disruption aim to expose surprising or challenging content, and feedback could inadvertently suppress these experiences and undermine the exploratory and reflective value.

Figure 5.
Personal Modes: (1) Enrich Current Settings into a Mode, (2) Choose from Pre-Configured Modes, (3) Mode Switcher.

3.3.3.
Personal Modes

As a meta-layer over previous features, Personal Modes transforms granular settings into intuitive, scenario-based profiles (see Figure
5
). Rather than always following a single recommendation setting, it allows users to create, save, and switch between multiple configurations tailored to varying moods, goals, and contexts, such as preparing for work, unwinding in the evening, or seeking novelty. Modes can be created in multiple ways. When desired, users can directly
enrich their current settings into a mode
, then name and save it for future use (see Figure
5
-1). During setup, users can further refine the mode by specifying content tone (e.g., positive, neutral, challenging) and focus areas (e.g., work, learning, entertainment). Alternatively, the system offers
pre-configured modes
as a quick start and serves as inspiration for how to use Personal Modes. Examples include: 1) Motivational Mode (energizing content to boost confidence and prepare for tasks), 2) Focus Mode (streamlined, task-oriented, and distraction-free), 3) Relaxing Mode (comforting, interest-based content with light exploration to avoid boredom), and 4) Fresh Mode (thought-provoking, diverse content to inspire reflection and broaden perspectives) (see Figure
5
-2). Besides user-initiated setup, the platform can
auto-detect browsing patterns
(if any) and prompt users to save them as a mode through a system message. Correspondingly, on the front-end feed page, a visible
Mode Switcher
lets users view their current mode, switch modes, or return to the default setup (see Figure
5
-3). Overall, this function empowers users to flexibly adapt recommendation patterns to different needs, creating a dynamic and context-aware feed experience.

4.2.
Study Procedure

4.2.1.
Preliminary Survey

Before the study, participants completed a preliminary survey to provide demographic information, prior experiences, and attitudes toward existing in-market RS (as baseline data). Items from
(
Pu et al. 2011
)
assessed their perceptions of: 1) system transparency (e.g.,
"The system makes clear why the content is recommended to me."
), and 2) control, or how much they think they can influence the system (e.g.,
"The system allows me to influence the AI-recommended content"
). All items used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 5 = Strongly agree).

4.2.2.
Provotype Interaction and Semi-Structured Interview

To evaluate the effectiveness and potential impacts of our provotype, we took a qualitative, in-depth approach. This enabled us to gather nuanced reflections, assess the usability and desirability of proposed features, and explore its influence on users’ sense of agency. Sessions were conducted remotely via Zoom and audio recorded, with researchers facilitating interaction and providing support when necessary. Each 75-minute session consisted of three parts:

•

Warm-Up and Baseline Exploration:
Session began with an open discussion of participants’ general impressions of AI recommendations and their mechanisms. The facilitator then shared their preliminary survey responses for current RS, prompting elaboration on their ratings (e.g., clarity of recommendation, perceived control, factors influencing recommendations, strategies to manage them, and concerns about data usage). This helped contextualize participants’ existing mental models of RS and provide reference points to guide the provotype sessions and subsequent interviews.

•

Provotype Walkthrough and Feedback:
Participants were first presented with the TikTok provotype feed, where they could browse posts and access the Content Preference settings as usual to familiarize the context. Newly designed features were then introduced for free exploration. Think-aloud protocols
(
Van Someren et al. 1994
)
were encouraged to verbalize their observations and impressions, while researchers observed, took notes, and provided clarifications as needed. Participants were also invited to comment on the usefulness and clarity, and provide suggestions for each feature. After the walkthrough, participants were first asked to select their favorite feature with reasons. A brief survey mirroring the baseline questions was then administered to assess perceived transparency and control in our provotype. Drawing on these responses and any observed shifts, a semi-structured interview was conducted to probe their reasoning behind the ratings. The interview was then broadened to explore how the provotype, compared to existing ones, influenced their understanding of RS, trust, and comfort with data practices.

•

Co-Design for Adoption and Awareness:
Recognizing the potential stress from diverse settings, the final part invited participants to collaboratively brainstorm strategies for effectively implementing and promoting these features. Discussions centered on increasing adoption, designing approachable interfaces, and avoiding overload. Broader reflections further explored how to foster awareness of power dynamics and user agency in AI systems to encourage active engagement over passive acceptance.

4.3.
Data Analysis

We adopted a mixed-method approach for data analysis, combining quantitative comparison of perceptual measures with qualitative thematic analysis of user insights, allowing us to understand both what changes occurred and why.

Through two targeted surveys administered before and during the study, we quantitatively measured user perceptions of system transparency and control between current RS and our provotype. Paired-sample t-tests examined these rating differences, and effect sizes (Cohen’s
d
) assessed the practical significance of observed differences. The comparative survey data helped highlight areas where participants experienced perceptual shifts in transparency and control, guiding us in qualitative analysis to explore underlying reasons and experiences in depth.

Qualitatively, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed with participant consent. We employed inductive thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2024
)
and cross-referenced transcripts with observational notes. Two researchers independently open-coded five transcripts to develop a preliminary codebook, then iteratively refined it through discussion and reconciliation for the remaining data. These qualitative findings complemented the survey results by providing rich context into how and why participants experienced shifts, and surfaced additional strategies for system design.

5.
RESULTS

To address our research goals, we first briefly described participants’ general mental models of existing RS as context. Then, we discussed their specific feedback on provotype features, and how the provotype influenced their perceived transparency, control, trust, and data comfort toward the system. Finally, we present participants’ insights on strategies for encouraging feature adoption and enhancing agency awareness more broadly.

5.1.
Mental Models of Current Recommender Systems

5.1.1.
Transparency Gaps and Reliance on Inference

Participants generally found the current RS unclear or opaque, with 11 disagreeing in the pre-survey that systems clearly explained recommendations. Some stated the system
"never tells you why"
(P4, P17), while five recalled only generic labels like
"Recommended for you"
(P8). Several viewed this opaqueness as intentional, suggesting it was a business strategy to
"make profits"
(P11),
"avoid backlash"
(P5), or even to
"make systems seem intelligent and really in tune with you to keep you there"
(P5). In the absence of clarity, 13 participants inferred mechanisms by observing system behavior or guessing. They cited user behavior (e.g., clicks, watch time, search, 16 participants), demographics (ten participants), and social networks (five participants) as key drivers. Seven even suspected that those precise recommendations were because systems eavesdrop on their chats with friends, fueling uncertainty and fear. This lack of transparency underpinned participants’ skepticism about their ability to influence recommendations.

5.1.2.
Perceived Lack of Control and Desire for Proactive Influence

Although participants recognized that engagement could shape recommendations, most felt limited control over the systems (12 of 19). Algorithms were viewed as primarily driven by platform priorities and operating
"behind the scenes"
(P1). While 14 participants acknowledged that feedback such as likes/dislikes, blocks, or searches could affect recommendations, they expressed that
"understanding algorithm logic did not translate into feeling in control"
(P11). These actions were perceived as reactive, implicit, and ineffective (P2, P4, P18), with repeated exposure to unwanted or previously dismissed content further undermining the sense of control (P16, P18, P19). Settings offered little relief: few participants explored them, citing
"not expect the settings to help"
(P1, P5, P17), struggled to locate them (P6, P14), or were even unaware of them entirely (P18). Those who accessed found only basic controls (P8, P17, P19) and expressed a desire for more proactive or global settings (P7, P14), such as
"adjusting interests before generating [the recommendations]"
(P7). In practice, users relied on trial-and-error strategies, such as deliberately manipulating engagement to "train" the system (nine participants) or avoiding interactions to prevent leaving traces (four participants). Moreover, perceived ease of control depended heavily on visible cause-and-effect: participants felt empowered when their actions produced immediate and noticeable changes (P3, P7, P17), while hidden mechanisms and inconsistent performance often caused frustration (P4, P16).

5.1.3.
Personal Data Concerns

Participants reported widespread unease over data collection, profiling, and opaque data flows. Many participants feared hidden tracking or surveillance, with six worrying systems
"consistently and secretly watch [them],"
seven concerned about data leakage to third parties for misuse, and three critiquing
"data selling."
Several participants highlighted the risk of recommendations shaping behavior or influencing personal identity without explicit consent (P11, P12, P13), reflecting broader concerns about covert manipulation. While two participants accepted data collection as an inevitable trade-off for personalization (P10, P17), most still sought clearer disclosure and filtering options to understand what data is used and how.

5.2.1.
Data Management

Participants generally welcomed the
Data Type Weighting
as empowering. It let them suppress unwanted signals and emphasize preferred ones, particularly in contextual adjustments (e.g., increasing search history for focused tasks or decreasing social affiliation to reduce friends’ impacts) (seven participants). This feature was valued as a personalization tool rather than purely a privacy measure, encouraging experimentation and active "training" of the algorithm. Reactions were mixed: some expressed concerns and discomfort about
"feeling objectified"
by granular distinctions (P10, P13), while some desired even finer controls, such as numeric scales (0-100) instead of intervals (P11, P18) and clearer explanations for why each data type affects recommendations (P10).

Relevant to data weighting, the
Data Usage % Indicator
was seen as boosting transparency and particularly praised for making the effects of their adjustments tangible (four participants). Interest in this feature was split: some would actively check it (P16), while others doubted its notability and appeal (P5, P10, P11). Suggestions included using visual aids like bar charts for visualized clarity (P2, P11), proactive notifications triggered by user interactions (e.g., after "liking” content) to increase exposure without requiring manual clicking (P14), and periodic summaries (monthly/annual) to make insights more meaningful and shareable (P2).

The
Data Aging
setting was highly favored for its intuitiveness and alignment with users’ mental model of changing interests. Its time-based framing resonated, as P7 said,
"I wouldn’t want content from three months ago."
Participants valued its potential to correct algorithmic overreactions to temporary actions, such as
"undervaluing occasional clicks or likes that do not reflect trust interests"
(P3). It could also help explore older content and revisit past interests when needed (P9, P17). However, participants preferred situational use rather than daily, suggesting changing it to an on/off toggle for convenience (P10).

Participants found the
Data Diary
highly transparent and informative for
"knowing yourself better"
(five participants). While five felt
"watched"
or noted
"rebellious vigilance,"
others reconciled:
"at least you know"
(P16, P18) or
"it’s normal, just the system decides whether to tell us"
(P3). Participants tended to use it as a personal journey to review and reflect on interests or behaviors over time, akin to a self-tracking (P1, P18) or financial spending app (P14), which could also help guide recommendation adjustments (P16, P18). However, without actionable controls, some found it passive and of limited utility, requesting options to edit/delete/cancel entries to prevent unwanted influence (five participants). Other suggestions included more organized summaries (weekly/monthly) over exhaustive logs (P12, P17, P19) and using visualizations to aid comprehension (P2, P12).

5.2.2.
Content Discovery

Before the provotype session, four participants had already expressed desires to
"bypass past preferences and see non-personalized content,"
even imagining a balance control as
"3/4 familiar, 1/4 different"
(P3, P14). The introduced
Adventure Content
directly addressed this need and was strongly appreciated as refreshing, a way to counter monotony and
"get out of comfort zones"
(nine participants). Adventure was described as fun and exploratory, with envisioned use cases like boredom relief (P9, P12), news discovery (P10), or even dating apps (P19).

Disruption Content
was similarly valued for breaking filter bubbles and exploring diverse perspectives (four participants). Unlike the more random nature of Adventure, Disruption was praised for nuanced, opposite-but-relevant content, especially to
"know what people different from me see"
to evoke reflections (P8, P11). However, participants saw it as more situational: best for niche users or contexts like research or global events that go beyond seeking relaxation (P2, P3, P16), where the ability to limit topics was appreciated. Participants recommended renaming it to "exploration" or "contrast" to sound less negative and encourage acceptance rather than evoking fear (P10, P14).

Time-based scheduling
received mixed reactions: some found Adventure/Disruption Time to fit routines well (e.g., before and after work) (P14), while others resisted rigid timing given their fragmented usage patterns (five participants). Instead, they suggested quick toggles in the feed
"for easy adjustments when I want something"
(four participants), or occasional pop-ups like
“Are you satisfied with current recommendations?”
to help remind of the settings (P12).

Similar to Data Usage % Indicator,
Content Tagging
was appreciated for its transparency and connection to participants’ actions, helping them understand why certain content appeared, prompting self-reflection, and guiding adjustments to content ratios (P2, P3).

Overall, participants valued control over content discovery (six participants). They enjoyed the contrast between controllable randomness (Adventure) and intentional diversity (Disruption). For higher simplicity, some even suggested merging the two into a single slider with opposing ends (P7, P14).

5.2.3.
Personal Modes

Participants widely found Personal Modes appealing and innovative, appreciating how they simplified recommendation control and easily adapted feeds to different moods, tasks, or times of day to
"save time for users"
(12 participants). Some likened it to
"creating profiles,"
or a
"multiverse"
to
"having a bigger world"
(P5, P11). This feature offered flexibility and agency without needing to set multiple accounts or constant fine-tuning (P16, P18, P19).

However, concerns centered on complexity, learning cost, and potential over-control (P5, P13, P15). If modes were too time-consuming, hidden, or difficult to understand, participants (especially casual users for simple, playful experiences) might avoid exploring them (six participants). To reduce complexity and learning cost, most participants viewed pre-configured templates as easier and more approachable to start with, rather than from scratch (seven participants). These presets were also trusted as deliberate, logical choices from the platform and should be ideal starting points for personalization (P8, P9). Suggestions for reducing cognitive load included using visual metaphors (e.g., avatars, GIFs, emojis) to illustrate modes (P4, P5, P14) and surfacing modes during onboarding with intuitive explanations and quick-start settings (four participants). P14 also desired for content-based mode creation: allowing users to generate a mode directly from a piece of content, for example,
"pushing similar items for the next three hours."

Potential counter-effects were also considered: inappropriate usage of modes could reinforce filter bubbles if users stick to one preferred mode (P15), and interactions in one mode might unintentionally affect other ones to cause confusion or frustration (P3, P17). In response, participants emphasized data separation that allows users to choose whether to save or discard mode-specific data to avoid cross-mode contamination (P17).

5.3.2.
From Opaqueness to Understanding and Agency

Across sessions, participants reported deeper awareness of recommendation mechanisms and stronger feelings of agency, describing the provotype as reshaping their view of RS: from opaque forces into visible, controllable, user-driven tools. Eight participants reported that the provotype clarified the recommendations were not random but tied to their behaviors and preferences, replacing guesswork with a structured understanding of how recommendations were made and how their data was used. For example, P17 pointed out,
"it turns scattered knowledge into a structured view with subfields and modules."
Participants especially valued that even small indicators, such as those disclosing data usage or content type, helped link their actions to outcomes and signaled that they could shape their feeds (P4, P8, P18). This visibility made recommendations feel less arbitrary and encouraged more willingness to engage, while also helping participants understand
"how the settings can be used"
(P18), in turn, fostering greater agency in exploring control options (P8, P16).

Alongside increased awareness came a stronger sense of control. Proactive features, such as feed customization and switchable modes, made the system feel
"more user-centric"
(P4, P6) rather than
"controlling [users]"
(P9) or
"just applying the same algorithms to everyone"
(P8). Compared with real-world apps, which participants described as
"one-way and general,"
the provotype was seen as more innovative and granular (P19), as well as adaptive to build their own platform (P1, P4). P14 further described it as enabling
"a complete process of receiving, understanding, and controlling recommendations"
: first noticing indicators, using them to understand why content was recommended, and gradually being "educated" by repeated exposure; then having options to fine-tune recommendations; and finally using a portable Mode Switcher to simply align feeds with their current needs.

5.4.
Provotype-Driven Shifts in Trust and Data Comfort

Participants’ interactions with the provotype revealed how transparency, control, and data practices influence trust in RS, while also exposing limits to control, nuanced privacy concerns, and mixed emotions about data visibility. These findings illuminate the complex interplay between transparency, agency, and trust, showing both the benefits of clarity and the boundaries of user influence.

5.4.1.
Trust Built on Transparency and Control

The newfound clarity and control shaped participants’ evaluations of system trustworthiness. Seeing how recommendations were generated, coupled with meaningful levers of influence, fostered feelings of safety and empowerment (nine participants). For instance, P14 and P19 described having
"large decision-making power,"
while P16 perceived that he
"had a higher role toward the system and didn’t have to depend on or hope for certain outcomes."
Importantly, this sense of safety did not always about absolute guarantees, but rather about at least having visible options. Participants noted that, even if they did not use the features or were unsure about their real effects, simply knowing they existed contributed to greater trust and confidence in the platform (P8, P14). Consequently, six participants explicitly favored this provotype over existing platforms because
"it cares more about users"
(P18) and is run by a company
"more responsible and aware of people’s preferences"
(P15, P16), resulting in noticeably higher willingness to engage with such platforms.

5.4.2.
Hesitation on Full Trust

Still, trust could be conditional and nuanced. Despite positive shifts, participants hesitated to claim perfect control or trust. Three emphasized that such completeness is unattainable due to the inherent complexity and opacity of RS: P8 said,
"there must still be many algorithms behind it, you cannot overcome AI’s power,"
while P18 added,
"it would still be a black box because we don’t and can’t know the inner workings."
Others pointed to external limitations, such as third-party influence or advertising, that would continue to shape recommendations outside users’ control (P19). Additionally, participants stressed that trust ultimately depends on
"how well it works in practice,"
with accuracy, stability, and evidence of responsible data use all determining whether trust could be sustained (P11, P14, P15). Especially, presenting greater empowerment also raised user expectations:
"if platforms promise clarity and control, they must perform well, correspondingly, and consistently, otherwise frustration will deepen"
(P3).

5.4.3.
Data Concerns and Emotional Ambivalence

Beyond those structural limits of trust, participants drew distinctions between trusting the algorithm itself and trusting how their data is handled. Comfort of data usage largely improved when the provotype demystified the "black box" of recommendations (P13, P18, P19), but data concerns were not fully alleviated because
"it still collects and tracks my data"
(P10, P12, P16). For some, reassurance seemed superficial: P15 uniquely reported that abundant controls made her
"less think about data brokerage or selling but just how to tweak my feeds,"
suggesting that detailed options may overshadow, but not resolve, underlying data exploitation concerns.

Emotionally, participants expressed ambivalence. The provotype’s transparency made the system feel clearer, safer, and more empowering (P7, P14, P18), but explicitly seeing the extent of personal data use and realizing how much platforms know about them also heightened privacy concerns (P8, P14). Yet, although unsettling, participants preferred clarity over ignorance, as P18 observed,
"even if it is scary, I’d rather not be in the dark."

5.4.4.
Nuanced Value of Transparency

Participants consistently emphasized that transparency, while valuable, was insufficient on its own to build trust. Some described it as
"a minimum requirement rather than a guarantee of trust,"
with disclosure improving efficiency and clarity but not fundamentally altering confidence in the system (P2, P3, P12). In some cases, details metrics may even spark skepticism, triggering extra mental effort on users to question the authenticity of the displayed information (P15).

Three scenarios were commonly compared: no disclosure, disclosure without control, and disclosure with control. Participants agreed that the worst case was being unaware of any system workings, while merely revealing information created what P18 called an
"illusion of control"
that offers reassurance that the platform was not hiding things, yet
"still frustrating when powerless to act."
Others echoed this as
"static, non-editable disclosures may amplify the sense of surveillance"
(P4). By contrast, four participants highlighted that coupling transparency with actionable features unleashed its power, allowing them to feel both informed and empowered.

5.5.
Insights for Encouraging Feature Adoption

Participants widely recognized the value of provotype features for personalizing recommendations and safeguarding agency. However, six participants emphasized that the premise of benefiting from these features is awareness, as P15 noted,
"they need to notice it exists before planning to adopt it."
The next factor would be usability and ease of use, with participants cautioning that perceived complexity, technical jargon, and information overload would discourage regular interaction (as discussed in Section
5.2
). In response, participants co-designed a range of strategies to support this progression, making controls easier to discover, simpler to understand, and more engaging to use. While these discussions were initially anchored in our provotype, both the conversations and the resulting strategies extend beyond this context and offer broader implications for diverse platforms and scenarios.

5.5.1.
Onboarding and Embedded Guidance

Participants identified education and timely guidance as the first step toward adoption. Tutorials or onboarding flows were suggested by six participants as effective for initial awareness. Beyond this, eight participants proposed that guidance should also be seamlessly integrated into everyday workflows for timely help rather than buried in menus. Indicators and Tagging features in the provotype were cited as examples, with P15 marking,
"providing shortcuts to connect the front and back is a very easy way to access relevant settings when the need arises."
Four participants proposed contextual prompts as additional shortcuts, such as system messages that appear periodically or in response to user actions:
"Do you want some adventure?"
(P4) or
"If you need [certain things], set it up here!"
(P5). These subtle, embedded cues align more naturally with user behavior and their original intent (i.e., browsing content) than requiring deliberate detours to settings. For all those efforts, P13 specially noted,
"even if users do not actually use them, just seeing these settings is already an education"
and can foster future exploration.

5.5.2.
Layered and Adaptive Designs

Once aware, users need to understand how to engage without being overwhelmed. To lower this barrier, seven participants suggested layered controls: a basic, simplified version paired with an "advanced settings" option for deeper customization, described as
"a process of acceptance"
(P14). This also accounted for different user types, recognizing that casual and power users would have different needs (P4, P7, P11).

Building on Personal Modes, six participants recommended
"just packaging everything into several predefined modes"
to simplify decision-making, since
"one-click to switch mode is much simpler than adjusting detailed settings"
(P3, P4), and enthusiasts could still access a “see details” option for finer adjustments.

Layers could also be extended across devices. P18 proposed,
"after learning concepts on a full-featured platform, users could have a streamlined version on smaller devices, such as only keeping modes on mobile, and return to the desktop for complete settings,"
supporting progressive and context-appropriate engagement.

5.5.3.
Goal-Oriented Framing and Engaging Process

Beyond awareness and comprehension, willingness to use such controls could depend on how appealing they feel. Participants suggested presenting them as exciting, goal-driven tools rather than dry settings. For example, instead of segmented elements like drop-down menus (i.e., Mode Switcher) or proportion sliders (i.e., Adventure/Disruption), modes with different focuses could directly appear as front-end, overarching navigation tabs labeled "For You/Explore/Adventure" to trigger curiosity and invite interaction (P3, P5). Framing controls around practical benefits, such as ways to
"enhance efficiency"
or
"adapt to mood and needs throughout the day,"
was preferred over presenting them as plain settings (P8).

To sustain adoption over time, participants provided strategies to keep the experience engaging. Gamification elements, such as points and rewards for using these features, could encourage early and continued use (P1). Visual and content cues, including lively tones (P14), concise and bold wording (P18), clean design (P10), and playful videos or cartoons (P16), could all contribute to reducing perceived effort and maintaining attention.

5.6.
Insights for Agency Awareness and User Empowerment

Beyond adopting specific features, we broadened the conversation to how people often engage with AI systems passively without realizing their power to influence outcomes. Four participants attributed this to perceiving AI as an inscrutable "black box," which discourages curiosity and reduces willingness to engage with controls. To address this, participants proposed ways to prompt people to care more about their agency and power.

5.6.1.
Making Agency Visible Through Affordances

A recurring theme was the importance of making agency visible in daily interactions. Consistent with Section
5.5.1
, seeing controls itself increases agency (P6, P14, P16). As P6 stated,
"I need to see it, then know it."
Subtle but persistent affordances, such as small icons or embedded entry points in content flows, can invite curiosity and signal users’ ability to influence the system (P6, P16). Without such cues, P14 warned,
"it is actually the platforms that ’domesticate’ users into passive habits"
because even if users initially have awareness, it may fade over time when
"such cues appear too few."
This gradual erosion highlights the necessity of sustained visibility and affordance for building lasting agency habits.

5.6.2.
Education and Framing: Sustaining User Empowerment

While visibility can spark awareness, participants saw education as key to long-term empowerment. Four participants called for systematic efforts, such as onboarding guides, societal discourse, or formal AI literacy campaigns, to normalize the concept of user agency. As P16 noted,
"understanding motivates control and care."
Beyond adult education, P18 further recommended teaching AI literacy as a basic skill early to
"help younger users frame their sense of agency before passivity takes hold."

How agency is framed within these efforts is equally important. Participants suggested resonating with users’ personal goals and everyday enjoyment rather than abstract concepts like "privacy" or "agency." Users are more likely to engage when controls are tied to concrete goals like productivity, creativity, or fun (P7, P8). Framings should also be context-specific: for example, entertainment-focused systems could
"present ’agency’ as playful and part of the fun rather than a chore;"
while productivity-oriented systems might frame it as a way to
"streamline tasks and save time"
(P8).

5.6.3.
Responsibility on Designers and Regulators, Not Users

Notably, five participants asserted that agency awareness should not rest solely on users. Instead,
"the decision makers at higher levels are the ones who should take the initiative"
(P5, P19). For example, ethical product designers and system developers were described as
"essentially responsible for building these [features]"
(P13) and
"reminding users of their ability"
(P12). Participants even elevated it beyond business level to societal level, indicating regulators should exert external pressures (P5, P14, P19): privacy laws and cookie regulations were cited as examples of how regulation can force visibility and gradually normalize awareness, even if adoption remains slow. P5 specifically likened platforms and policymakers to
"adults"
tasked with guiding
"children"
(users) toward safe experiences and
"deciding what to show,"
rather than expecting users to carry the burden of understanding complex systems. Meaningful agency, therefore, requires shared responsibility beyond individual action.

6.
DISCUSSION

This study illustrates how actively involving users can inform the design of recommender systems. By exploring participants’ mental models, evaluating a provotype, and co-designing strategies, we gained insights into how transparency, control, and agency influence user experiences. While some findings are specific to our provotype, many extend more broadly to other personalized systems, offering both immediate design guidance and deeper theoretical insight. Practically, we highlight ways to encourage adoption, strengthen user empowerment, and balance transparency with trust. Theoretically, we also surface enduring dilemmas and socio-technical tensions, revealing the challenges of reconciling user agency with system design and business imperatives.

6.1.
Summary of Findings

Through provotype interaction and the user interview, we found that the proposed interface features targeting data management, content discovery, and personal modes can meaningfully surface hidden processes and create actionable points of influence in RS. The provotype prompted participants to reframe RS from opaque, unilateral systems into adjustable tools that align more closely with their goals and preferences.

These interventions produced notable shifts in user perceptions and attitudes. Participants reported heightened perceptions of transparency and control, stronger trust, and better engagement compared to existing RS. Such feedback points to promising directions for addressing information and power asymmetries, showing that user-facing features can reduce opacity, distribute agency, and mitigate the sense of helplessness often experienced in AI-driven RS.

Ambivalence was also revealed: disclosing data raised privacy concerns, excessive options risked cognitive overload, and trust remained conditional due to the inherent complexity of RS. This underscores that supporting agency requires careful calibration between clarity, control, and user comfort rather than simply adding more controls.

To make features effective in practice, participants proposed strategies such as raising awareness through onboarding, embedding subtle affordances in workflows, layering controls for progressive learning, and framing features around concrete goals, all of which could remind their sense of agency and facilitate adoption. Especially, agency responsibility was seen as shared: designers, developers, and regulators, not just users, play a role in enabling meaningful empowerment. Together, these insights provide a roadmap for informing RS design and empowering agency while acknowledging practical and socio-technical limitations.

As a proof-of-concept focused on enhancing users’ sense of agency, our provotype yielded promising results. These outcomes highlight concrete design opportunities for both RS and broader AI, showing how agency can be supported in ways that resonate with users’ natural behaviors and values. Figure

6.2.1.
From "Accuracy-Oriented" to "Agency-Oriented": Rethinking Personalization

A key motivation for our work was recognizing that accuracy-focused recommendations can narrow content exposure and reinforce filter bubbles
(
Pariser 2011
;
Aridor et al. 2020
)
. We explored how giving users direct control might offer a complementary path, allowing them to proactively introduce randomness or divergence. Consistent with prior work
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Cheng et al. 2017
)
, our study shows that users not only value such options but already desire them, even before encountering our provotype. This suggests that personalization should no longer be the sole goal of RS. While accuracy and relevance still matter, they are not universally desired: different users, or the same user at different times, may seek alternatives beyond tailored content. Unlike backend algorithm refinements (e.g.,
(
Wilhelm et al. 2018
;
Adomavicius and Kwon 2009
)
), our work tangibly demonstrates and evaluates external user control designs for shaping diverse recommendation outcomes. We argue that future systems must move beyond static notions of personalization toward a more
pluralistic and interactive paradigm
that lets users define what counts as meaningful content for them in context rather than remaining passive recipients, even when the system already introduces novelty, diversity, or serendipity. This approach reframes personalization as one option within a broader spectrum of user–system interactions, where controls are designed to invite adaptation, exploration, and responsiveness to the shifting needs while foregrounding user agency.

6.2.2.
"Interactive", not just "Declarative": Rethinking Transparency

Participants made clear that, while transparency remains vital in ethical AI design, static explanations of algorithms alone often fall short, or even frustrate, when users cannot act on the information provided. Prior work shows that explanations can boost awareness of algorithmic processes
(
Rader et al. 2018
)
, but awareness (the perceptual agency) does not translate into true agency. As exemplified by the Data Diary, users became acutely aware of system behavior and data collection, but still felt powerless when unable to intervene. This resembles cognitive dissonance theory
(
Harmon-Jones and Mills 2019
)
, where being informed yet unable to act creates discomfort and lowers perceived agency. Building on the human–AI synergy framework
(
Sundar 2020
)
and co-design insights stressing the pairing of transparency and user interventions during algorithm interactions
(
Storms et al. 2022
)
, we argue for reframing transparency
from a declarative model
(where systems merely tell users how they work)
toward an interactive model
(where users learn by doing and observing) to avoid paradoxically diminishing user experience. This extends research on
"interactive transparency,"
where systems
"afford users the option to provide feedback"

(
Molina and Sundar 2022
)
, by providing more novel approaches to equip users with an active role in managing recommendations, contributing to trust enhancement and future design inspirations in RS.

6.2.3.
Front-Back Feedback Loops Build Trust

Agency does not stop at providing controls (the behavioral agency). Even with abundant options, participants stressed the importance of connecting back-end actions to front-end outcomes to perceive tangible effects. When adjustments produce clear, visible changes in recommendations, such as through the Data Usage % Indicator, participants would feel empowered, whereas opaque or delayed results may raise doubts about system legitimacy
(
Herder and Atzenbeck 7
;
Amershi et al. 2014
)
. Performance also mattered: poor recommendation quality could undermine engagement and hinder participants from achieving full trust or agency. This aligns with prior work showing that perceptions of algorithmic fairness and trust depend critically on observed outcomes
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
and that feedback loops sustain user engagement and confidence in AI systems
(
Krauth et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2018
)
. Trust, therefore, is not a fixed property granted by controls but an ongoing state contingent on visible cause-and-effect feedback loops and system performance. Our findings demonstrate that effective agency combines both doing (the ability to act) and seeing (assurance that actions yield meaningful results). Rather than treating control as an isolated feature, designers should integrate performance, responsiveness, and usability to support a robust sense of agency.

6.2.4.
Designing Controls with Care: Embedded, Balanced, and Adaptive

Many participants admitted they rarely explored deep settings menus, instead preferring lightweight, in-context adjustments. This suggests that controls or explanations should not only be hidden at the periphery but surfaced directly into daily interactions, such as in feeds or via contextual hints. Such embedded cues not only reduce friction and enhance usability
(
Dodeja et al. 2024
;
Amershi et al. 2014
)
but also act as educational affordances, helping users stay aware and engaged
(
Thompson et al. 2013
;
Molina and Sundar 2022
)
. In contrast to prior approaches that focused on settings
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Bemmann et al. 2024
)
, our study advocates for a design paradigm where agency is also embedded into users’ natural workflow to align with their original intent and typical behaviors on the platform.

Yet, the balance is delicate. Our study reveals the challenge of operating agency without overwhelming users with complexity. Simply adding more control options does not necessarily increase users’ perceived control, but can plateau or even backfire (overload, frustration) to reduce adoption
(
Bemmann et al. 2024
;
Storms et al. 2022
)
. While transparency must go beyond simply exposing system processes to influence outcomes, the collaborative relationship should be made meaningful and effective by presenting clear and intuitive control mechanisms without requiring extensive learning burden. Moreover, while some participants welcomed detailed indicators and granular settings, others voiced concerns and unnecessariness, indicating that personality also influences control preferences
(
Harper et al. 2015
;
Rook et al. 2020
)
. Hence, adaptive or layered systems that tailor mechanisms to individual needs can ease the learning curve and maintain engagement. This aligns with the principle of progressive disclosure in interface design
(
Tidwell 2010
)
and customizable recommender interfaces
(
Knijnenburg et al. 2012
;
Zhang and Sundar 2019
)
, which manage complexity and improve accessibility by gradually introducing controls to match user expertise.

6.3.
Tensions and Dilemmas in Decision Making during Recommender System Development

Our study also surfaced several tensions that extend beyond immediate design choices, highlighting broader socio-technical dilemmas in developing RS. Derived from the design takeaways, these tensions reflect trade-offs between user empowerment, system performance, and business priorities, showing that designing for agency is rarely straightforward. Instead of prescribing solutions, examining these dilemmas helps illuminate the philosophical and strategic questions underlying personalization, transparency, and control, offering guidance for both research and practice. 6.3.1.
Who Decides What Users See? Designer Authority vs. User Autonomy

The challenge of balancing controls raises a broader dilemma: who decides what users see, and how should such decisions be made? Users have long criticized RS for making choices on their behalf. When developers fully determine what to present, user agency is clearly missing, as many scholars point out
(
Sundar 2020
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
. However, granting users full control is not a simple fix either. While seemingly empowering, the more aspects users can control, the more complex and overwhelming the framework becomes, which could worsen the service
(
Storms et al. 2022
;
Pennekamp et al. 2017
)
. Some participants argued that agency awareness should not belong exclusively to users but also to developers and regulators. They even framed this relationship as "adults" (platforms acting responsibly and simplifying the complexity) guiding "children" (users needing protection and support). Yet this analogy also exposes a core dilemma: even if platform-driven decisions lead to more moral or responsible outcomes, does treating users as "children" still strip away their agency? The challenge, then, lies in balancing establishment (risking paternalism) and autonomy (risking cognitive overload). Navigating this middle ground requires not only careful design but also a dedicated distribution of decision-making responsibilities across system actors.

6.3.2.
Transparency vs. User Comfort: The Paradox of Seeing More

Our findings on interactive transparency also surfaced an unresolved paradox: visibility into algorithmic processes is not universally comforting. Withholding information may simply not trigger users to think about underlying processes, producing delight or frictionless experiences. Conversely, disclosing data or system mechanisms can build trust but also raise anxiety and impose extra cognitive efforts to think, judge, and doubt, echoing previous studies
(
Kizilcec 2016
;
Ananny and Crawford 2018
)
. The visibility of options may also paradoxically heighten users’ awareness of what remains beyond their reach, thereby offsetting perceived gains in agency. This raises a challenge of what constitutes a good user experience: should systems reveal everything, or is selective disclosure preferable? Prior work frames the strategy as
"not too little and not too much"

(
Kizilcec 2016
)
, but what counts as sufficient visibility remains elusive. It may vary with users’ personalities, expectations, or tolerance for complexity, and may also shift across platforms and contexts. Thus, the ambivalence of "seeing more" reflects that transparency is not a straightforward variable to be optimized but a paradox to be navigated. As what paradox theory
(
Lewis 2000
)
describes as the simultaneous pursuit of contradictory logics: in this case, comfort through seamlessness and assurance through clarity, leaving design to negotiate trade-offs it may never fully resolve.

6.3.3.
Loyalty or Trust? Navigating Business Priorities

Our study also highlights a strategic tension for platforms between business priorities and user empowerment. Several participants perceived that existing systems intentionally obscure mechanisms to create a "magical" feeling and avoid backlash, ostensibly to drive engagement and maintain loyalty. However, trust issues have already emerged from information and power asymmetries
(
Rühr et al. 2023
;
Kizilcec 2016
;
Chen and Sundar 2018
)
. Our study shows that providing transparency and meaningful control, like in our provotype, can actually increase users’ willingness to choose the platform over existing ones, demonstrating its power to also foster loyalty and engagement and thereby produce benefits that align with both user and business value. This raises a critical question for businesses: is obscurity and unilateral control the best path to user retention, or can empowerment achieve equal or greater outcomes for both users and platforms?
In this light, trust can not only be positioned as a user experience outcome but also as an organizational asset for business framing, which can be directly tied to system design choices. Reconciling these logics may require balancing short-term business incentives with longer-term socio-technical considerations, creating opportunities for platforms to experiment with new mixes of product strategies.

7.
LIMITATION AND FUTURE WORK

This study has several limitations that point to opportunities for future research. First, the provotype was only displayed as the desktop version, and limited screen space (e.g., mobile) may constrain feature presentation and interpretation, requiring further design considerations. Second, although we used TikTok solely for demonstration and encouraged cross-platform thinking when applicable, the display environment may still have influenced how participants focused, recalled, or compared experiences, potentially limiting generalizability to other types of AI RS. Similarly, despite efforts for diversity, the study sample was primarily young adults with relatively high media and algorithm literacy, raising questions about applicability to older adults, individuals with lower digital proficiency, or other cultural contexts.

Furthermore, the provotype was non-executable, meaning that participants’ perceptions were largely based on imagination rather than actual system responses. As a result, observed shifts in perceptions and attitudes may not fully capture real-world dynamics. The findings are also based on short-term, self-reported perceptions, leaving longer-term behavioral effects and ecological validity unexplored.

Beyond practical limitations, our study surfaced valuable socio-technical tensions that warrant further exploration in Section
6.3
. Future work can systematically investigate these tensions to unveil their underlying dynamics by identifying design strategies, generating empirical evidence, and clarifying the unresolved trade-offs and considerations.

Nevertheless, these limitations and observed tensions frame the current study rather than diminish its contributions. The findings provide a valuable starting point for refining transparency and control features and point to promising directions for future research and design efforts aimed at creating more user-centered and responsive RS.
