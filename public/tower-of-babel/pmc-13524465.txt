Gut Faecalibacterium regulates host immunity and metabolic profiles to confer resistance against Salmonella infection in chicks
Authors: Xiang Li, Yaping Wang, Zixuan Wang, Man Deng, Jumei Zheng, He Geng, Guiping Zhao, Qiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo explore the pathogenic mechanism of Salmonella Enteritidis (SE) infection in chicks and the regulatory role of Faecalibacterium, we established a chick SE infection model, systematically analyzing intestinal damage, systemic inflammation, and cecal microbial community changes. Focusing on the differentially abundant Faecalibacterium, we elucidated its regulatory mechanism via metagenomics, transcriptomics, and serum metabolomics. Results showed that SE successfully colonized the chick cecum, causing significant reductions in the thickness of the intestinal mucosal and muscular layers, a decrease in gland depth, and a loss of goblet cells. Concurrently, serum levels of IgA and IL-6 were markedly elevated, indicating the induction of systemic inflammatory responses and severe intestinal damage. Microbiome analysis revealed SE significantly altered cecal microbiota β-diversity, increased Actinobacteria abundance, and decreased the abundance of beneficial bacterial families (Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae) and the key beneficial genus Faecalibacterium. Functional prediction indicated microbial function remodeling towards enhanced pathogen colonization and pro-inflammation. β-diversity analysis of Faecalibacterium gene set showed clear separation between the Ctrl and SE groups in two-dimensional space: the control group was enriched in immune-related pathways such as Th17 cell differentiation and IL-17 signaling pathway, while the infected group was enriched in pathways related to Salmonella infection and pathogenic Escherichia coli infection. Validation in uninfected chicks showed High_ Faecalibacterium abundance was associated with lower serum IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ and distinct gene expression profiles. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched in immune regulation pathways such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and intestinal immune network for IgA. LASSO regression screening identified 16 key associated genes including TM4SF4 and FABP4. Serum metabolomics showed distinct metabolic profiles between High_ and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups, with 26 differential metabolites; N-(2,4-dinitrophenyl) ethylenediamine and Val-Gly-Phe (AUC > 0.8) were potential biomarkers. In conclusion, SE induces pathogenesis by damaging intestinal barrier, triggering inflammation, and disrupting cecal microbiota. Faecalibacterium enhances chick resistance to SE via regulating immune and metabolic homeostasis, providing a basis for avian salmonellosis microecological control.Keywords: Salmonella infection, Gut microbiota, Faecalibacterium, Serum metabolomics, Chicks

Introduction

Salmonella is a common foodborne zoonotic pathogen. According to World Health Organization data, approximately 115 million people globally fall ill annually due to Salmonella infection, with about 370,000 dying from it (Balasubramanian et al., 2019; Chen, et al., 2024). Infections caused by Salmonella account for 70-80% of bacterial foodborne illnesses, ranking first among bacterial infections. Salmonella not only poses an ongoing threat to global public health but also serves as one of the primary pathogens responsible for bacterial enteritis in poultry. It may impair poultry growth and egg-laying performance, ultimately leading to death.

The gut microbiota provides colonization resistance against Salmonella infection (Rantala and Nurmi, 1973). Commensal bacteria suppress pathogen colonization and control its load by competing for key metabolites, producing inhibitory substances, and inducing host immune responses (Vogt and Finlay, 2017). Beyond this protective mechanism, they can also directly regulate pathogen virulence or enhance the host's resistance. Gut microbiota composition exhibits host specificity, undergoes continuous dynamic changes within individuals, and susceptibility to both exogenous and endogenous factors. Given its dual role in extensively supporting physiology while driving disease pathogenesis, maintaining microbial balance is crucial for intestinal barrier integrity and systemic homeostasis (Sekirov, et al., 2010). Further evidence indicates that host genes not only shape microbial composition (Zhang, et al., 2010), but the microbiota itself also contributes to the maturation of local and systemic immunity through molecular and cellular mechanisms (Mazmanian, et al., 2005). Within this symbiotic relationship, intestinal signaling plays a pivotal role in establishing and maintaining host-microbiota interactions (Hooper, et al., 1999).

Although studies confirm the gut microbiota's key role in resisting intestinal pathogens, the underlying molecular mechanisms by which the host–microbe interaction remain largely unexplored. To bridge this knowledge gap, this study utilized the "Guangming No. 2″ model to conduct an experimental infection with Salmonella Enteritidis. Subjects were grouped into an Ctrl group and an SE group. Utilizing 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metagenomic analysis, we identified bacterial—Faecalibacterium. To further investigate the potential function of Faecalibacterium, samples were stratified into High_ Faecalibacterium abundance and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups based on Faecalibacterium abundance, followed by integrated multi-omics analysis. The results revealed that Faecalibacterium modulate the immune response in local lymphoid tissue (cecal tonsil) by influencing host metabolic status, thereby contributing to host resistance against Salmonella. This mechanism elucidates a new dimension of host–microbe interaction and provides a novel direction for developing anti-Salmonella strategies targeting the host–microbiota interface.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

Ethical approval for animal care and experimental procedures was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IAS-CAAS, Beijing, China; approval number: IASCAAS2021-31).

Experimental design and sample collection

A total of 280 one-day-old "Guangming 2″ white-feathered broilers (mixed gender, male: female ratio 1:1; average body weight:43.98 ± 3.89 g) were used in this study. The chicks were randomly divided into groups using a random number table and housed in four negative-pressure isolation chambers (one chamber for the infection group and three for the control group), with a stocking density of 70 birds per chamber. All groups were reared under identical conditions (temperature: 26±1°C, humidity: 60±5%). During the experiment, the chicks were provided with specific pathogen-free feed purchased from Ke'ao Xieli Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and sterilized by cobalt-60 irradiation. The main nutritional composition of the feed was as follows: moisture ≤14%, crude protein ≥18%, crude fat ≥2.5%, crude fiber ≤6%, crude ash ≤8%, calcium 0.6%–1.2%, phosphorus ≥0.55%, lysine ≥0.85%, and methionine ≥0.32%. Filtered sterile water was provided, and the chicks had ad libitum access to both feed and water

At day 6 of age, chicks in the SE group were orally inoculated with 0.5 mL of PBS solution containing 8.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL of Salmonella enteritidis (strain 50335), while the Ctrl group received an equal volume of sterile PBS at the same time. On day 9 of age, all chicks were euthanized, and corresponding tissue samples were collected for analysis. Serum was collected for the determination of serum inflammatory cytokine levels. Cecal content was collected to evaluate the gut microbiota composition. Cecal tissue samples were preserved for histological examination and assess bacterial load. The experiment included 70 chicks in the Salmonella-infected group (SE group) and 210 chicks in the non-infected group (Ctrl group) The SE group was used to evaluate the inflammatory response, intestinal damage, and gut microbiota alterations induced by Salmonella infection. The larger Ctrl group was designed to capture natural inter‑individual variation in Faecalibacterium abundance and to facilitate subsequent selection of individuals with extreme abundance values. Specifically, the abundance of Faecalibacterium was ranked among the 210 control chicks, and 15 individuals were randomly selected from the upper and lower 15% of the abundance distribution to form the high_ and low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups, respectively. Consequently, subsequent transcriptomic and serum metabolomic analyses employed a balanced design with 15 chicks per group.

Salmonella inoculum preparation

The Salmonella Enteritidis strain 50335 (Institute of Veterinary drugs Control, Beijing, China) was used for the infection challenge. The strain was subjected to three serial passages in Luria Bertani broth at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. To prepare the inoculum, the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The final inoculum concentration was quantified by counting colony-forming units (CFU) on bismuth sulfite (BS) agar plates.

Phenotypic data determination

At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected via jugular venipuncture and placed in anticoagulant-free collection tubes to separate serum by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C). The concentrations of IgA, IL-6, IL-8, and IFN‑γ were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay ((Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) kits, strictly in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

A 2 cm cecal segment was taken, rinsed with PBS to remove contents, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After dehydration, embedding, and sectioning, the samples were subjected to H&E and AB-PAS staining. Whole slides were digitized using a PANNORAMIC scanner, regions of interest were selected with CaseViewer 2.4, and quantitative analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0. To determine goblet cell density, five intestinal crypts were randomly selected on each section; the epithelial length of each crypt was measured, and the number of goblet cells was counted. Goblet cell density was calculated as the number of goblet cells divided by the crypt epithelial length (goblet cells/mm). In addition, five different crypts were randomly selected on each section to measure the muscularis thickness, mucosal thickness, and gland depth, with the average value of each parameter used for statistical analysis.

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from cecal tissue using a modified phenol-chloroform method. Cecal tissue homogenates were mixed with 800 µl lysis buffer (Tris/EDTA/NaCl/SDS) and 30 µl proteinase K (20 mg/mL), followed by overnight digestion at 56°C. The lysate was sequentially extracted with Tris-saturated phenol-chloroform (1:1, v/v), precipitated with pre-chilled absolute ethanol, washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 100 µl ddH₂O at 37°C for 1 h. DNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically (A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0). Bacterial load in the SE group was quantified using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). The 20 µL reaction mixture: 10 µL of 2 × Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems), 0.6 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 2 µL DNA template, and ddH₂O to adjust the final volume. The amplification program was as follows: 95°C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 3 s and 60°C for 32 s. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The standard curve was generated by plotting the Ct value (y) against the logarithm of the Salmonella copy number (x), yielding the equation y = −3.3517x + 45.296 (R² = 0.99), which corresponded to an amplification efficiency of 98.8%. The bacterial load in cecal tissue was subsequently calculated using this standard curve (Salmonella-specific primers target a single-copy gene).

16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

At the end of the experiment, cecal content samples were collected from both Ctrl and SE groups of chicks. Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted using the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, Shanghai, China). DNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using barcode-tagged primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), with the extracted DNA as template. The 20 µL reaction mixture contained: 4 µL of 5 × TransStart FastPfu Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primers (5 µM), 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase, and 10 ng of DNA. Amplification was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min; 27 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; final extension at 72°C for 10 min; and hold at 4°C. Each sample was amplified in triplicate. The PCR products from the same sample were pooled, and target bands were recovered and purified via 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The purified products were quantified using a Synergy HTX system (Biotek, USA). Library construction was carried out with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, USA): adapter ligation; bead-based removal of self-ligated adapters; PCR enrichment of library templates; bead-based recovery of the final library. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 PE300 platform (Meiji Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Paired-end raw sequencing reads were quality-controlled using fastp (https://githu.cm/OpenGene/fastp, version0.19.6) and assembled with FLASH (http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash, version 1.2.11). The assembled sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity and filtered for chimeras using USEARCH (http://drive5.com/usearch/, version11). Sequences annotated as chloroplasts or mitochondria were also removed. All samples were rarefied to 20,000 sequences per sample, achieving a coverage of 99.09%. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance was performed to assess community similarity, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to analyze bacterial taxa with significantly differential abundance between groups from phylum to genus level.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, Shanghai, China). The extracted DNA was assessed for concentration, purity, and integrity, followed by fragmentation into approximately 350 bp fragments using Covaris M220 (Gene Company, China). These fragments were selected for library construction. A paired-end (PE) library was prepared with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, USA) through adapter ligation, bead-based purification to remove self-ligated adapters, PCR enrichment, and final product recovery. Sequencing was subsequently performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Meiji Biomedical, China) utilizing bridge PCR amplification.

Sequencing reads were first processed with fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.23.0) for adapter trimming and quality control, retaining reads with length ≥50 bp and average base quality ≥20. Host-derived sequences were removed by aligning reads to the host genome using BWA (http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net, version 0.7.9a) . Clean reads were then assembled into contigs with MEGAHIT (https://github.com/voutcn/megahit, version 1.1.2) , retaining those ≥300 bp for subsequent analysis. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from the contigs using Prodigal/MetaGene (http://metagene.cb.k.u-tokyo.ac.jp/) , and those encoding peptides ≥100 amino acids were translated. All gene sequences were clustered using CD-HIT (http://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit/, version 4.6.1) at 90% identity and 90% coverage to construct a non-redundant gene set. The abundance of gene was quantified by mapping reads from each sample to this gene set using SOAPaligner (http://soap.genomics.org.cn/, version 2.21) with 95% identity. Finally, taxonomic and functional annotations were performed by aligning the sequences to the NR and KEGG databases with Diamond (http://www.diamondsearch.org/index.php, version 0.8.35) under an e-value threshold of 1e-5, and the corresponding abundances of taxonomic groups and functional categories were calculated.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol method. RNA purity and integrity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), respectively. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and subjected to 150 bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Following quality control, clean reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (GRCg7b) using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were quantified in FPKM. Raw read counts were normalized (DESeq2 R package) and assessed with a negative binomial model to calculate the fold change (FC) and associated p-value for each gene between sample groups. p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with significantly differentially expressed genes defined by the thresholds of q-value (FDR-adjusted P-value) < 0.05 and |FC| ≥ 2. To identify stable candidate key genes associated with the experimental groups, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) obtained from the transcriptomic analysis were subjected to LASSO regression analysis. This analysis was performed using the glmnet package in R (version 4.4.0) with a binomial logistic regression model and an L1 regularization parameter (alpha = 1). Prior to model construction, the gene expression matrix was standardized. In each run, the optimal regularization parameter λ was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, and candidate genes were selected based on non-zero regression coefficients at the optimal λ. To improve the robustness of the selection, the LASSO model was run 200 times, and the number of times each gene was selected across all iterations was recorded. The final candidate key genes were identified based on their selection frequency.

Metabolomic sequencing of serum

Samples were mixed with a protein precipitation reagent containing an internal standard (4 μg/mL, methanol:acetonitrile = 2:1) at a volume ratio of 1:3. The mixture was vortexed for 1 minute, subjected to ice-bath ultrasonication for 10 minutes, and then kept at −40°C for 2 hours. After centrifugation at 13,000 rpm and 4°C for 20 minutes, 150 μL of the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm organic phase syringe filter, transferred to an injection vial, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of supernatant from all individual samples. Analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus system coupled with a Thermo Q Exactive High-resolution mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 45°C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water containing 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile, delivered at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The injection volume was 5 μL. Mass spectrometry detection was conducted in positive/negative ion switching mode with a scan range of m/z 100–1200. Full-scan MS at a resolution of 70,000 was followed by data-dependent acquisition (dd-MS²) at a resolution of 17,500 using stepped collision energies of 10, 20, and 40 eV. Key MS parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, +3.8 kV / −3.2 kV; sheath gas flow, 35 arb; auxiliary gas flow, 8 arb; capillary temperature, 320°C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350°C; and S-lens RF level, 50%.

Raw LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI (V3.0) for peak extraction, alignment, and normalization. Compound identification was based on accurate mass, secondary fragments, and isotopic distribution by matching against The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Lipidmaps (V2.3), Metlin, and a custom-built database. Data filtering was subsequently applied: peaks with a missing rate >50% in any group were removed, zero values were replaced by half of the minimum value, and unreliable identifications with a database matching score below 36 (out of 80) were excluded. Finally, data from positive and negative ion modes were merged into a single data matrix. The criteria for screening differential metabolites were set as P < 0.05, |FC| > 1, and VIP > 1. The discriminatory performance of each differential metabolite was evaluated using univariable logistic regression with stratified five-fold cross-validation. In each iteration, four folds were used for model training and the remaining fold was used for testing, until each sample had been included once in the test set. The AUC was calculated for each test fold, and the mean AUC and standard deviation across the five folds were reported. In addition, the out-of-fold predicted probabilities from all five test folds were pooled to calculate the pooled cross-validated AUC and its 95% confidence interval. To assess the combined discriminatory performance of the candidate metabolites, a random forest classifier was constructed using the selected metabolites as composite features and evaluated using the same stratified five-fold cross-validation procedure. ROC analyses were performed using the “pROC” package in R. Smoothed ROC curves were generated for visualization only.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and compared between groups using Student’s t-test for independent samples in SPSS software (V26.0). Differences were deemed significant at a two-tailed P < 0.05. Graphical representations were prepared with GraphPad Prism (V9.5.0).

Results

Salmonella infection induces systemic inflammation and intestinal damage in chicks

First, we examined the colonization of Salmonella in the cecum. The results showed that Salmonella successfully colonized the cecum of broilers in the SE group (Fig. 1A), while no Salmonella was detected in the Ctrl group, indicating successful establishment of the Salmonella infection model. To further investigate the effects of Salmonella Enteritidis infection on chicks, we assessed serum inflammatory factor levels and intestinal histomorphology alterations following infection. (B) Representative images of intestinal histological analysis (PAS staining; scale bar = 50 μm and H&E staining; scale bar = 100 μm). H&E staining shows mucosal damage and inflammatory cell infiltration (red arrows), while PAS staining shows goblet cells (blue arrows). (C) Differences in the intestinal barrier between Ctrl and SE groups. (D) Differences in the levels of serum inflammatory factors between Ctrl and SE groups. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

H&E staining revealed obvious damage to the intestinal mucosa in the SE group, including mucosal atrophy and inflammatory cell infiltration, while AB-PAS staining showed a reduction in goblet cell number and mucus secretion (Fig. 1B). Histological analysis showed that compared to the Ctrl group, the intestinal muscularis thickness, mucosal thickness, glandular depth, and number of goblet cells per unit length were significantly decreased in SE group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C). Concurrently, ELISA results indicated significantly increased serum IgA and IL-6 levels in SE group chicks compared with the Ctrl group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D), suggesting that Salmonella infection induces a systemic inflammatory response. Collectively, Salmonella infection induces systemic inflammatory responses and causes severe intestinal tissue damage in chicks.

Salmonella infection alters cecal microbiota structure and composition, leading to functional remodeling of the gut microbiota

Intestinal diseases are closely associated with alterations in the abundance or composition of the gut microbiota. Therefore, we systematically analyzed the characteristics of changes in the host gut microbiota following Salmonella infection. First, β-diversity analysis using Bray-Curtis distance was performed. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) showed a significant separation between the Ctrl and SE groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A), indicating that Salmonella infection significantly altered the microbial community structure. We further examined changes in the gut microbiota composition at different taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, Salmonella infection significantly increased the relative abundance of Actinobacteria at the phylum level (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B and Table S2). Subsequent finer-scale analysis identified 10 differentially abundant families between the two groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C and Table S2). Among these, beneficial families such as Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae were significantly enriched in the Ctrl group, wheras taxa like Erysipelatoclostridiaceae were more abundant in the SE group (P < 0.05). At the genus level, 12 genera exhibiting differential relative abundance (P < 0.05, Table S2). Among which, the genus Faecalibacterium showed the most pronounced difference, being almost undetectable in the SE group (Fig. 2D).

Next, we investigated the changes in microbial gene functions caused by Salmonella infection through comparative KEGG pathway analysis. Compared to the Ctrl group, 12 KEGG pathways were significantly altered in the SE group (P < 0.05). The enrichment of pathways, including “Salmonella infection”, “Bacterial invasion of epithelial cells”, “Phosphotransferase system (PTS)”, and “Shigellosis” indicates that Salmonella Infection shifts the functional profile of the gut microbiota towards enhanced pathogen colonization and a pro-inflammatory state. The marked activation of the Salmonella infection pathway reflects the pathogen's active disease process within the gut (Fig. 2E).

Structural and functional divergence of Faecalibacterium associated with Salmonella infection

At the genus level, we identified 12 bacterial taxa with significant differences in abundance, among which Faecalibacterium was significantly depleted after Salmonella infection. Therefore, we hypothesized that this genus may play a protective role against Salmonella infection. For further validation, we extracted data for the genus Faecalibacterium from the metagenomic data and performed a comparative analysis between the Ctrl and SE groups at the species level. β-diversity analysis of Faecalibacterium gene set showed clear separation between the Ctrl and SE groups in two-dimensional space (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A), confirming significant differences in the species composition of Faecalibacterium between the two groups.

To identify the specific microbial taxa underlying this structural divergence, we performed species level analysis of the Faecalibacterium gene set and identified 10 species with significantly different abundances between the two groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B and C and Table S2). Within this genus-specific gene set, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (F. prausnitzii) showed a significantly higher relative representation in the SE group (P < 0.05). Further LEfSe functional comparison demonstrated that the SE group was significantly enriched in pathways related to Salmonella infection and pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, while the Ctrl group showed predominant enrichment in immune-related pathways such as “Th17 cell differentiation”, “IL-17 signaling pathway”, and “NOD-like receptor signaling pathway” (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that the depletion of Faecalibacterium may be closely associated with the attenuation of the host immune response.

Faecalibacterium modulates host immunity to confer resistance against Salmonella infection

To further validate the protective role of Faecalibacterium against Salmonella infection in the host and avoid the confounding effect of Salmonella infection itself, we subsequently analyzed 210 uninfected control chicks. By comparing individuals with High_ versus Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance (the relative abundance of this genus in uninfected Salmonella chicks was ranked, and 15 chicks were randomly selected from the highest and lowest 15% extreme abundance values, respectively), we aimed to elucidate the potential mechanisms by which this commensal bacterium modulates host immunity and metabolism. A statistically significant difference in Faecalibacterium abundance was observed between the High_ and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups (P < 0.05, Table S3). ELISA results showed significantly increased serum levels of IL-6, IL-8, and IFN-γ in the Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A), which may be associated with anti‑inflammatory function. Further transcriptome analysis revealed distinct gene expression profiles between the High_ and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups, as shown by principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 4B). A total of 267 DEGs were identified, including 67 downregulated and 200 upregulated genes (Fig. 4C).

Feature selection was performed using the LASSO regression model to screen for key genes associated with Faecalibacterium abundance. After 200 iterations, 16 key genes were selected from the 267 DEGs including: TM4SF4, FABP4, and others (Fig. 4D–F). Subsequently, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on the 267 DEGs. The results revealed that the upregulated genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to host defense and immune regulation, such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, PPAR signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and the intestinal immune network for IgA (Fig. 4G). These findings provide strong evidence that Faecalibacterium plays a beneficial role by modulating host immune and inflammatory responses, thereby enhancing resistance against Salmonella infection.

Serum metabolomics identifies key metabolites linked to Faecalibacterium abundance

In addition to the immunomodulatory effects, Faecalibacterium may also be involved in regulating host metabolism. OPLS-DA analysis revealed a clear separation trend in serum metabolic profiles between the High_ Faecalibacterium and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups (Fig. 5A). A total of 26 differential metabolites were identified between the two groups, of which 11 were upregulated and 15 were downregulated (Fig. 5B). To screen for potential metabolic markers associated with Faecalibacterium abundance grouping, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and an area under the curve (AUC) > 0.8 as the screening criterion. We further constructed a random forest model integrating the candidate metabolites and internally validated its discriminatory performance using stratified five-fold cross-validation. The model achieved a mean AUC of 0.76 ± 0.22 across the five validation folds. The AUC values of N-(2,4-dinitrophenyl) ethylenediamine and Val-Gly-Phe both exceeded 0.80, with values of 0.82 and 0.82, respectively (Fig. 5C), indicating that these two metabolites exhibited good discriminatory ability between the High_ and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance groups. These findings suggest that changes in Faecalibacterium abundance may be accompanied by alterations in specific serum metabolite levels. The two metabolites identified through multi-step screening have the potential to serve as candidate biomarkers, providing a basis for further elucidating the mechanisms by which Faecalibacterium influences host health through metabolic pathways.

Discussion

Salmonella, a genus of Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic Enterobacteriaceae, causes enteritis, septicemia, and typhoid-like illnesses in poultry (Lacroix-Lamande, et al., 2023). This pathogen can persistently colonize avian cecal for months without clinical signs and be vertically transmitted to offspring via reproductive infection (Kogut and Arsenault, 2017). While vaccination is a common preventive measure for young chickens, its efficacy is limited. Antibiotic feed additives were once considered effective for preventing Salmonella, but they have now been prohibited in the European Union due to limited efficacy and antimicrobial resistance concerns. Therefore, it is imperative to develop alternative strategies for preventing and controlling Salmonella in poultry. Salmonella, as a Gram-negative bacterium, has an outer membrane in its cell wall that is rich in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)(Feyzi, et al., 2017; Lin, et al., 2020). This component is continuously or abundantly released throughout the bacterial lifecycle, upon external assault or during immune clearance. The liberated LPS activates the NF-κB signaling pathway via TLR4-mediated MyD88-dependent signaling, driving upregulation of various inflammatory cytokines(Chang, et al., 2020; Liu, et al., 2019; Zhou, et al., 2018). This aligns with the significant increase in IL-6 expression levels observed in the Salmonella-infected group in this study. Substantial pro-inflammatory cytokine production signifies an inflammatory response, and persistent inflammation damages intestinal tissue architecture(Miao, et al., 2020). Researchers have observed that LPS can significantly reduce the number of goblet cells in the intestine (Yu, et al., 2021). This study confirmed that inflammation triggered by Salmonella infection severely compromises intestinal structural integrity.

The chicken intestine is a multifunctional and highly complex organ, serving not only as the core site for digestion and absorption but also as a crucial immune defense bastion. The microbial community colonizing the chicken gut is crucial for the poultry's entire lifecycle, primarily because its colonization and maturation processes exert a profound influence on maintaining gut homeostasis. The gut microbiota establishes an anti-infection barrier by inhibiting pathogen colonization, secreting antimicrobial substances, and contributing to energy metabolism, while also modulating host immunity through initiating the innate-to-adaptive immune cascade (Clavijo and Flórez, 2018; Hooper, et al., 2012). Salmonella infection promotes the proliferation of pathogenic and facultative anaerobic bacteria in the chicken intestine, disrupting the homeostatic balance of the native microbiota (Zhang, et al., 2023). However, the development and progression of intestinal inflammation are also influenced by the structure of the microbial community, and targeted remodeling of microbial composition can alleviate inflammatory responses. At the genus level, this study identified a total of 12 differentially abundant bacteria. Among them, Faecalibacterium, as a key commensal bacterium in the gut, decreased abundance of Faecalibacterium has been widely correlated with several diseases (Martín, et al., 2023). Importantly, in the present study, Faecalibacterium was almost undetectable in the SE group, and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first observation of such severe depletion of this Faecalibacterium in an avian Salmonella infection model, suggesting that it may serve as a microbial biomarker for SE‑induced dysbiosis. It plays important roles in immune regulation, intestinal barrier protection, and microbiota modulation through mechanisms: (1) Metabolite-mediated regulation. Its principal metabolite, butyrate, inhibits histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity, upregulates the Wnt/JNK signaling pathway negative regulator Dact3, and thereby suppresses pathway activation and reduces inflammatory cytokine production. (2) Immune cell-mediated regulation. Studies have shown that Faecalibacterium can elevate IL-10 levels in antigen-presenting cells and specific T-cell subsets, which enhances regulatory T cell (Treg) function while restraining pro-inflammatory T cell responses (Martín, Rios-Covian, Huillet, Auger, Khazaal, Bermúdez-Humarán, Sokol, Chatel and Langella, 2023). Consistent with prior observations, Salmonella infection reduces the abundance of Faecalibacterium (Fig. 2E). Notably, although the overall abundance of Faecalibacterium was markedly reduced following Salmonella infection, the relative representation of F. prausnitzii within the Faecalibacterium-derived gene set increased. This pattern may reflect differential effects of Salmonella infection on individual species within this genus or a more pronounced reduction in other Faecalibacterium species, suggesting an intragenus compositional restructuring against the background of overall Faecalibacterium depletion (Morton, et al., 2019). Furthermore, F. prausnitzii exhibits substantial genomic and phylogenetic heterogeneity, and different strains may vary in their metabolic and immunomodulatory properties(Bai, et al., 2023; Fitzgerald, et al., 2018; Martín, et al., 2017). Previous studies have provided biological support for the potential protective role of Faecalibacterium. Viable F. prausnitzii, its culture supernatant, and the microbial anti-inflammatory molecule MAM have been shown to alleviate experimental intestinal inflammation and protect intestinal barrier function in murine models, partly through inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway (Breyner, et al., 2017; Martín, et al., 2014, 2015; Sokol, et al., 2008). In chickens, higher abundances of Faecalibacterium and an F. prausnitzii-related operational taxonomic unit have been associated with lower intestinal Salmonella abundance(Pedroso, et al., 2021). However, these findings were derived from mammalian inflammatory models or observational analyses in chickens and do not demonstrate that Faecalibacterium directly protects chickens against SE infection. Therefore, the specific functions of this taxon require further investigation in future studies. Collectively, SE-induced dysbiosis was characterized by the depletion of potentially beneficial bacterial taxa, particularly Faecalibacterium. Such depletion may weaken microbiota-associated metabolic and immunological functions, thereby contributing to impaired intestinal homeostasis and disease progression. Nevertheless, the present findings demonstrate an association rather than a direct causal relationship. The protective role of Faecalibacterium should therefore be further evaluated through bacterial strain isolation and controlled colonization experiments.

Based on the abundance of Faecalibacterium, this study selected individuals with extremely High_ and Low_ Faecalibacterium abundance, and subsequently integrated serum metabolomic and cecal tonsil transcriptomic data to systematically elucidate the potential functions and mechanisms of Faecalibacterium in regulating host metabolism and immunity. Metabolites are the ultimate products of gene transcription and protein expression, and changes in metabolites directly influence the growth and health of poultry (Fan, et al., 2020; Guo, et al., 2019). Blood contains various substances essential for normal physiological functions, and serum metabolomics provides a comprehensive snapshot of the systemic metabolic network dynamics (Tian, et al., 2023). Simultaneously, the cecal tonsil, as a key gut-associated immune tissue, provides transcriptional data that directly indicate local immune gene expression. By comparing differential metabolites and genes between the extreme Faecalibacterium abundance groups, we further elucidated key mechanisms underlying host–microbe interactions. Our study identified 267 genes influenced by Faecalibacterium abundance, and 16 key genes were further selected through LASSO regression analysis. Among these, FABP4 encodes a member of the intracellular lipid-binding protein family, which is highly expressed in adipocytes(Hertzel and Bernlohr, 2000). Recent studies have also revealed its expression in intestinal epithelial crypt cells. Previous research has shown that FABP4 knockout mice exhibit significantly enhanced clearance of enteric pathogens such as Salmonella Typhimurium and Escherichia coli, indicating that this gene plays an important negative regulatory role in host defense against infection. FABP4 participates in the host immune response to Salmonella infection by modulating the PPARγ signaling pathway, thereby influencing the expression of antimicrobial peptides(Su, et al., 2022). As a classic downstream target gene of the PPAR signaling pathway, its expression is directly regulated by core molecules such as PPARγ. The KEGG enrichment analysis in this study revealed that differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway. Therefore, we speculate that changes in Faecalibacterium abundance may indirectly influence FABP4 expression by modulating the activity of the PPAR signaling pathway, thereby contributing to the regulation of host intestinal immune responses and gut microbiota homeostasis. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, offering direct molecular evidence of its involvement in host immune processes.

Serum metabolomic analysis further identified N-(2,4-Dinitrophenyl) ethylenediamine and Val-Gly-Phe as metabolites significantly associated with Faecalibacterium. Val-Gly-Phe is a linear tripeptide composed of valine, glycine, and phenylalanine linked by peptide bonds. It is a small peptide fragment produced during protein digestion, absorbed through the intestine, enters the bloodstream, and participates in host metabolism. As a core beneficial bacterium, Faecalibacterium is characterized by butyrate production and high expression of ABC peptide transporters. First, intestinal bacteria encode ABC peptide transport systems that efficiently uptake small peptides as nutrients, reducing their entry into portal circulation(Freeman, 2015; Garai, et al., 2017; Smith and Macfarlane, 1998). Second, butyrate produced by Faecalibacterium lowers intestinal pH, thereby increasing the activity of intestinal digestive enzymes(Den Besten, et al., 2013; Kihal, et al., 2025) These combined effects may lead to changes in Val-Gly-Phe levels. The hydrolysis products of Val-Gly-Phe can serve as a nitrogen source for microbial nutrition, reflecting a bidirectional regulatory relationship (Jin, et al., 2025). However, this relationship still lacks relevant reports and validation. This study suggests that Faecalibacterium not only plays a critical role in short-chain fatty acid metabolism but may also participate in host peptide metabolism. As serum Val-Gly-Phe is readily detectable, it may serve as an indirect biomarker of gut microbiota health. Combined analysis of both offers a more comprehensive characterization of the gut microbiota–host metabolic axis. However, this study has several limitations. First, samples were collected only from 9-day-old broilers, which may not capture the dynamic changes of Faecalibacterium during different stages of Salmonella infection. Second, the protective role of Faecalibacterium requires further validation through colonization experiments. Additionally, the specific targets through which Val-Gly-Phe exerts its regulatory functions remain to be elucidated. Future studies should focus on these aspects to further refine the regulatory mechanisms by which Faecalibacterium mediates host resistance against Salmonella infection.

Conclusion

This study employed an integrated approach of animal infection models and multi-omics analysis to elucidate the critical role of the gut microbiome in host resistance to Salmonella infection. Salmonella infection significantly induced host inflammatory responses and intestinal tissue damage and reshaped the structure and function of the gut microbial community, characterized by significant enrichment of pathways related to Salmonella infection, bacterial invasion of epithelial cells, the phosphotransferase system (PTS), and Shigellosis pathogenicity. Among these, Faecalibacterium exhibited the most significant difference between the SE and Ctrl groups. Further analysis based on Faecalibacterium abundance under non-infected conditions revealed that high_ Faecalibacterium abundance reduced levels of inflammatory cytokines, and that the serum metabolites N-(2,4-dinitrophenyl) ethylenediamine and Val-Gly-Phe could serve as potential metabolic markers for distinguishing Faecalibacterium abundance. Collectively, this study deepens our understanding of the mechanisms underlying host–gut microbiome interactions during Salmonella infection, provides a novel theoretical framework for explaining inter-individual differences in disease resistance, and offers potential strategies for enhancing disease resistance in white-feathered broilers through modulation of the microbiota and metabolism.
