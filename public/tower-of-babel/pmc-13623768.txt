Understanding the neuroscience of communicating serious news
Authors: Katerina Rusinova, Robert Rusina, Steven Z. Pantilat
Published: 2026

Throughout their careers, physicians engage in more than 200 000 conversations about serious illness, yet communication remains one of the least formally taught clinical skills [1]. Good communication, especially in emotionally charged situations, can mitigate stress and improve outcomes and understanding [2, 3] for patients and families as well as clinicians who are also exposed to stress, uncertainty, and emotions [4].

We present a hypothesis-generating conceptual model that integrates understanding of neurobiological structures and pathways that may be activated during and after serious news delivery with best practice recommendations for communication. The goal is to offer potential neurobiological explanations for current communication guidelines and guide future research into improving communication.

Emotional perception before language comprehension: nonverbal and verbal communication

During communication of serious news, nonverbal communication is typically detected before any words are said [5]. The emotional salience of the situation is generally registered rapidly through limbic structures, including the amygdala (the brain’s sentinel for emotional relevance) and the insula (involved in the unconscious awareness of negative feelings of unease or distrust), even before the full semantic meaning of the message is consciously analyzed [6].

The processing of the words takes longer as it relies on a ventral stream involved in comprehension (posterior cortical areas, mainly Wernicke’s area, temporal lobes) and a dorsal stream linking temporal and frontal language regions supporting speech production (in anterior cortical areas, mainly Broca’s area)[7]. The hippocampus, where memories and prior experiences are stored and retrieved, is engaged [8] and emotionally charged encounters may be encoded with particular strength, especially if experienced as threatening or aversive (Fig. 1A). The stress response and the limits of understanding

When serious news is shared, the sympathetic–adrenal–medullary pathways and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis are activated, both in response to the news and in anticipation of it [9] (Fig. 1B). The resulting catecholamine and cortisol release narrows attentional focus, suppresses neuroplasticity, and impairs working memory and the recall of complex/neutral content. Paradoxically, emotionally salient fragments may be strongly encoded for the future [10]. What may appear as confusion, silence, anger, or disengagement upon the sharing of the news may instead reflect physiological and emotional overload triggered by early stress responses driven by catecholamine and cortisol release.

Regulation as the gateway to understanding

Only later do prefrontal networks modulate emotional responses, allowing semantic content to be accessed and better understood [6]. The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) inhibits both the activated amygdala and insula and resets the amygdala to “baseline” function [7] and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) engages in problem-solving (Fig. 1C). The OFC and DLPFC are prefrontal and limbic regulatory networks involved in coordinating social behavior, maintaining working memory, executing goal-directed behavior, planning, and the ability to attribute and understand one’s own and others’ mental states [6]. With these structures, engaged verbal output can occur using the dorsal language stream activating Broca’s area responsible for speech production [7].

Implication for communication with patients and families

In a common scenario, an intensive care unit (ICU) physician sharing bad news leans forward, attentive; the patient’s wife watches him closely, while her sister sits with her head down, her posture conveying distress and emotional burden (Fig. 1D). Emotion, memory, and cognition are deeply intertwined during such serious illness conversations. Meaning is first experienced and then processed. Understanding the neurobiological processes may help clinicians better interpret and respond to patients’ and families’ reactions.

Recommendation 1 – Share serious news simply and directly: stress limits comprehensionNeurobiology: Emotional salience from both nonverbal cues and the initial verbal message of serious news is rapidly registered and encoded, involving mainly the amygdala and insula, before the full semantic meaning is consciously analyzed through the ventral language stream.Recommendation: The first statement of serious news often carries disproportionate weight, received together with the clinician’s tone, posture, facial expression, and the surrounding setting. Acute stress may narrow attention and reduce the ability to absorb detail [10]; the headline thus should be clear, direct, and brief [11]. A confusing, prolonged, or emotionally mistuned opening may leave a lasting imprint difficult to repair later.

Recommendation 2 – Pause after the headline and address emotion: emotional arousal limits cognition and empathy can restore itNeurobiology: Serious news activates the listener’s acute stress response. Catecholamine and cortisol release narrows attentional focus, impairs working memory and recall of complex information, and limits the encoding of new information. Clinicians are also vulnerable to stress-related distortions [4].Recommendation: The initial disclosure should be followed by a deliberate pause. This period of silence acknowledges that the listener may be temporarily unable to process additional information and allows the clinician to observe the recipient’s emotional response. Once recognized, emotion can be addressed explicitly through empathic statements [12] to help reduce emotional arousal and allow neurobiological reset and cognitive processing to resume [13]. Pausing and responding to emotion help create the conditions needed for understanding and sound decision-making.

Recommendation 3 – Create an emotionally safe environment to reduce arousal and support comprehensionNeurobiology: Prefrontal–limbic regulatory networks act on the previously activated emotional circuits, and the OFC inhibits the activated amygdala and insula, facilitating semantic comprehension (ventral language stream). Emotional safety decreases the ongoing acute stress reaction.Recommendations: Carefully attending to the environment can signal safety and further reduce arousal to promote better understanding. Secure a quiet, private space when possible; close doors or draw curtains to create privacy; provide seating for everyone; and have tissues within reach [14]. Creating this sense of safety is not secondary to care; it is the precondition for it.

Recommendation 4 – Repeat and check understanding: memory under stress is fragileNeurobiology: Acute stress, by activating the amygdala and the insula and engaging the hippocampus, can impair working memory, reduce recall of complex information, and limit the encoding of new information. Elevated cortisol also impairs the encoding of new information by suppressing synaptic plasticity.Recommendations: Key information should be repeated in simple language and understanding should be checked. Rather than asking, “Do you understand?”, clinicians can say, “I know this is a lot to take in. Could you tell me what you are taking away from this so far, so I can make sure I explained it clearly?”.

Recommendation 5 – Respond empathically to support regulation and adaptive copingNeurobiology: A calm communication approach facilitates prefrontal–limbic regulatory network recruitment, reduces amygdala and insula activation, and gradually restores the capacity to encode new information (hippocampus) including through language circuits.Recommendation: Families who feel acknowledged and emotionally supported are better able to integrate information, make balanced decisions, and live with those decisions afterward [15]. Empathic engagement may help shift the listener from a threat-dominated state toward a state in which information can be processed, remembered, and used.

The way forward

Serious news will always be hard to give and harder to hear. The goal is to avoid adding injury through how it is delivered. Words, timing, tone, silence, environment, and empathy may well have a basis in neurobiology. Their value is already recognized in serious illness communication; further research into the underlying neurobiological mechanisms at play could provide evidence to support particular skills and help guide the development of others.
