Superior approach for pulmonary venous restenosis postcardiac-type total anomalous pulmonary venous connection repair in a 6-month-old infant
Authors: Kai Luo, Yanjun Pan, Xiaomin He, Xinrong Liu, Jinghao Zheng, Zhongqun Zhu, Huiwen Chen
Published: 2026

Enlarging the PV-LA anastomosis via the superior approach to shorten the drainage pathway.

Central Message

This report presents a successful superior-approach reoperation for pulmonary venous restenosis in an infant postcardiac-type TAPVC repair, offering a viable complex secondary repair strategy.

Pulmonary venous restenosis (PVRS) is a rare but severe complication after total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC) repair and is often associated with a poor prognosis. Although the cardiac subtype of TAPVC is generally considered low risk, postoperative restenosis may still occur because of technical limitations during the initial procedure. This report describes a 6-month-old infant who developed PVRS after previous surgical correction of cardiac-type TAPVC. The patient underwent successful reoperation that used a superior approach with enlargement of the pulmonary venous confluence anastomosis. This case highlights a feasible surgical strategy for complex secondary repair and provides a reference for the clinical management of similar cases.

Case Presentation

A 6-month-old male infant (height, 74 cm; weight, 10.6 kg) was admitted with progressive anorexia and shortness of breath of more than 2 months in duration. Five months earlier, he underwent corrective surgery for obstructive cardiac-type TAPVC, along with atrial septal defect repair and patent ductus arteriosus ligation at another institution. The initial surgery was technically successful; however, the patient did not undergo regular follow-up. During a subsequent outpatient visit, echocardiography revealed stenosis at the pulmonary venous confluence draining into the left atrium, with a flow velocity of 2.1 m/s. The infant was diagnosed with pulmonary venous restenosis after TAPVC repair. This case has been filed by The Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children's Medical Center and confirmed that formal approval was not required because it describes a single anonymized patient (proof of exemption available upon request). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for publication of clinical details and images was obtained from the patient's parents.

Preoperative Evaluation

Cardiac function was New York Heart Association class III, with a percutaneous oxygen saturation of 91% on room air. Contrast-enhanced cardiac computed tomography with 3-dimensional (3D) reconstruction revealed that all 4 pulmonary veins converged into a common confluence draining into the roof of the left atrium. The confluence orifice was significantly narrowed, with an internal diameter of 6.2 mm, although the individual pulmonary vein branches were well-developed. The surgical team analyzed the imaging data and performed procedural simulation by using a with 3D model. Enlargement of the pulmonary venous confluence anastomosis via a superior approach was planned.

Surgical Procedure

Reoperation was performed through median sternotomy as follows: (1) Adhesions were carefully dissected, a cardiopulmonary bypass was established, and the pulmonary venous confluence, pulmonary vein branches, left atrium, and left atrial appendage were sequentially exposed. (2) After aortic crossclamping, the interatrial septum was incised via the right atrium. The pulmonary venous confluence drained into the posterior wall of the left atrium through an orifice measuring approximately 5 mm. (3) With the junction of the pulmonary vein and the right atrium as the vertex, V-shaped incisions were made along the pulmonary vein and the left atrium, extending toward the distal end of the common confluence and the roof of the left atrium, respectively. (4) The posterior wall of the confluence was anastomosed to the posterior wall of the left atrial appendage. (5) The anterior wall of the confluence was then anastomosed to the anterior wall of the left atrial appendage, completing the reconstruction. The cardiopulmonary bypass time was 101 minutes, and the aortic crossclamp time was 58 minutes. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography revealed a common pulmonary vein confluence orifice diameter of 12.2 mm, with a flow velocity of 0.8 m/s and a mean pressure gradient of 2.6 mm Hg (Video 1).

Postoperative Recovery and Follow-Up

The infant was extubated on postoperative day 3 and transferred from the intensive care unit after 1 week. No complications, including arrhythmia or diaphragmatic paralysis, were observed. The patient was discharged on day 10. At the 2-month follow-up, cardiac function had improved to New York Heart Association class I. Echocardiography revealed a patent anastomosis measuring 15.8 mm, with a pulmonary venous velocity of 1.2 m/s and normal cardiac chamber dimensions. Follow-up computed tomography and 3D printed modeling confirmed the absence of stenosis.

Discussion

PVRS refers to progressive luminal narrowing at the pulmonary venous–left atrial junction or within any segment of the pulmonary vein after surgical correction of anomalous pulmonary venous return. PVRS may result from neointimal hyperplasia, thrombosis, or external compression and leads to pulmonary venous hypertension. PVRS remains a common and prognostically unfavorable complication after TAPVC repair, with an incidence of 5% to 15% and approximately 80% of cases occurring within 6 months postoperatively.1

Among the 4 anatomical subtypes of TAPVC, the cardiac type typically has the most favorable anatomy. Pulmonary veins are often symmetrically confluent, with a short common trunk and no diffuse hypoplasia or focal stenosis. These features contribute to a lower restenosis rate (approximately 1.1%) and more stable postoperative hemodynamics.2,3 Nonetheless, restenosis may still occur because of anatomical or technical factors. Studies have shown that a longer confluence segment, drainage into the right atrial roof, and a smaller confluence-to-total vein area ratio are significant predictors of restenosis.4

In this case, the 4 pulmonary veins formed a long-segment confluence and drained into the roof of the right atrium. During the initial surgery, only partial resection of the interatrial septum was performed, and the confluence was partitioned into the left atrium by using a pericardial patch without an adequate enlargement of the drainage orifice. This technical limitation likely contributed to postoperative stenosis.

Current surgical strategies for treating PVRS include sutureless repair and an anatomy-based reconstruction.5 Sutureless repair may be challenging during reoperation because of adhesions or previous pericardial resection. Anatomy-based repair is primarily indicated for focal branch stenosis. However, in this patient, the primary pathology here was a diffuse narrowing at the confluence orifice with an insufficient drainage area rather than isolated branch stenosis. Thus, neither conventional approach was ideally suited.

Preoperative 3D modeling facilitated procedural simulation and anatomical assessment. The superior approach was chosen because it has the following 3 advantages: (1) minimizes cardiac manipulation and reduces the risk of conduction tissue injury and arrhythmias; (2) provides excellent exposure, allowing the construction of a maximally sized anastomosis from the left atrial roof to the left atrial appendage; and (3) simplifies the procedure, reduces the bypass time, and may lower the complication rate.

Conclusions

The superior approach, which is traditionally used for supracardiac TAPVC, was successfully applied to this intracardiac-type variant with drainage into the right atrial roof. For patients with intracardiac or mixed TAPVC in which the pulmonary veins drain into the roof of the right atrium, it is insufficient to simply redirect the pulmonary venous flow into the left atrium during the initial operation. Maximizing the use of the pulmonary venous confluence and the left atrial tissue—by extending the anastomosis toward the distal confluence and the left atrial appendage—is essential for achieving an adequately sized drainage orifice. A sufficiently large anastomosis ensures early postoperative hemodynamic stability and provides a durable anatomical foundation for preventing long-term restenosis.
