Dynamic similarity framework and governing criteria for saturated rock masses under combined static and dynamic loading
Authors: Tianqi Nan, Linming Dou, Yanjiang Chai, Haobing Li, Zihao Kan, Jinzheng Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In deep underground engineering environments, the strong interaction between pore water and the rock skeleton constitutes the fundamental mechanical mechanism behind dynamic disasters such as rockbursts and water inrush. This study systematically develops a dynamic similarity criterion system applicable to saturated porous media under combined static and dynamic loading. Through rigorous dimensional analysis, four key dimensionless parameters are proposed: Cauchy number (CA), Euler number (EU), Darcy number (DA), and Biot number (BI), each characterizing solid inertia, fluid impact, nonlinear seepage, and coupling diffusivity, respectively. These parameters form a coupled system of similarity equations, revealing the paradox of similarity constraints in physical model design. The framework is further validated by applying it to experimental data, enabling analysis of how geometric scaling and loading intensity influence coupling responses. Specifically, results show that under dynamic loading, the CA value increased by over four orders of magnitude while the EU value dropped by seven orders, signifying a critical shift from a pressure-driven to an inertia-dominated regime. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that both the DA and BI values increase linearly with the geometric similarity ratio, fundamentally altering the hydraulic response and suppressing pore pressure build-up in downscaled models. This work provides critical theoretical guidance and a quantitative basis for improving hazard simulation and experimental model design in deep engineering applications.

Introduction

With the growing global demand for energy and resources, mining activities are increasingly extending into the Earth’s deep subsurface1,2. In rock formations at depths of one kilometre or more, rock masses are subjected not only to high in-situ stresses from overburden weight but also to intense dynamic disturbances induced by blasting, mechanical excavation, regional seismicity and other mining activities3–5. This extreme mechanical environment, characterized by the superposition of high static and strong dynamic loads, results in highly nonlinear mechanical responses with significant multi-physical coupling6,7. Particularly in water-rich formations, the strong solid–fluid interaction between the rock skeleton and pore fluids has been widely recognized as a key mechanism triggering dynamic disasters such as water inrush, rockburst, and large deformation in underground roadways8–11. Therefore, accurately simulating and predicting the solid–fluid coupling behaviour of deep rock masses under combined static and dynamic loading is of critical theoretical and engineering significance for ensuring the safety and sustainability of deep underground engineering.νν

Physical model experiments are a vital approach for studying complex geomechanical phenomena, enabling the controlled replication of key processes observed in in-situ engineering conditions12,13. However, the scientific validity of such models fundamentally depends on the mechanical similarity between the physical model and its engineering prototype14. This similarity must be established on a rigorous and systematic theoretical foundation. In current practice, when addressing solid–fluid coupling under combined static and dynamic loading, traditional similarity theory frameworks exhibit significant limitations15,16. The selection of key similarity parameters lacks standardized criteria, severely constraining both the scientific design and predictive capacity of such experiments17.

The widely used Froude criterion effectively describes gravity-dominated quasi-static processes, such as slope stability and seepage problems18–20. However, in the context of deep engineering involving high-frequency and high-acceleration dynamic disturbances, inertial effects become significantly amplified21,22. Traditional similarity criteria fail to adequately capture essential dynamic processes such as stress wave propagation, fracture evolution, and strain-rate–dependent material responses. Moreover, when simulating high-speed fluid surges, research has primarily focused on static hydraulic pressure while neglecting the inertial impact from fluid momentum changes23. Under high-pressure and high-velocity conditions, this effect may dominate rock mass destabilization, yet current similarity theories lack systematic treatment of this phenomenon.

On one hand, the unclear sources of water recharge and the poorly understood mechanisms of water inrush in deep underground engineering (Fig. 1) lead to inaccuracies in physical modeling24,25. On the other hand, most current model experiments either focus on the dynamic response of the solid skeleton or the fluid seepage behaviour, failing to comprehensively represent the strong coupling between pore pressure and structural stress–strain behaviour. In reality, dynamic loads act not only directly on the solid framework but also induce instantaneous compression and pressure fluctuations in the pore fluid26–28. The build-up and release of pore pressure, in turn, profoundly affect the strength, deformation characteristics, and failure modes of rock masses through effective stress mechanisms. Ignoring this bidirectional coupling results in similarity theories that fall short of accurately capturing rock mass behaviour under actual working conditions. Although various disciplines have proposed dimensionless parameters to characterize solid–fluid interactions, very few studies have integrated these into a unified theoretical framework29–32 There remains a lack of systematic derivation of their applicable forms under combined static–dynamic loading and a clear understanding of the intrinsic coupling relationships among these parameters.

In response to these challenges, this study aims to develop a unified similarity theory framework for modeling the solid–fluid coupling dynamics of saturated rock masses under combined static and dynamic loading. Building on the dynamic governing equations of saturated porous media, and through rigorous dimensional analysis and physical derivation, the study defines and elaborates on four key dimensionless control parameters: the CA, EU, DA, and BI. The internal mathematical coupling among these parameters is further revealed, resulting in a comprehensive and interdependent set of similarity criteria equations that provide a robust theoretical foundation for physical model design. Based on the proposed similarity theory, the study quantitatively predicts sample responses under varying geometric similarity ratios and dynamic loading paths, revealing the profound influence of scale effects on the solid–fluid coupling dynamics of saturated rock masses. This work advances similarity theory in the field of deep geomechanics toward a more integrated, high-dynamic, and multi-scale framework.

Traditional solid-liquid coupling similarity theory

Basic similarity criteria in static state

The similarity theory underpinning traditional physical model tests is primarily based on the assumption of static or quasi-static equilibrium. The central objective of this theoretical framework is to accurately and distortion-free reproduce the stress distribution, deformation patterns, and ultimate failure modes of a prototype geotechnical structure under self-weight and external loading, within a geometrically scaled model. To achieve this, a set of rigorous similarity conditions must be simultaneously satisfied between the model and the prototype, which are typically described from three key perspectives: geometric, material, and loading similitude33–35.

Geometric similitude is the fundamental prerequisite for any form of physical similarity, requiring the model to be a precise geometric replica of the prototype. Specifically, the ratio of the distance between any two points in the model to that in the prototype must remain constant, defined as the geometric similarity ratio CL=Lp/Lm, where subscripts p and m denote the prototype and model, respectively. Once CL is established, similarity ratios for other length-related quantities, such as area and volume, are accordingly determined. Moreover, since strain is dimensionless, its similarity ratio must be unity, meaning that strains in all directions must be identical in the model and prototype to preserve deformation fidelity.

Material similitude requires that the physical and mechanical properties of the model material maintain proportional relationships with those of the prototype under specific rules. This includes the basic density similarity ratio, as well as various mechanical parameters that describe the material’s resistance to deformation and failure, such as elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, cohesion, and internal friction angle. Among these, Poisson’s ratio and internal friction angle, being dimensionless, are typically assumed to be identical between the model and prototype to ensure similar shear and volumetric deformation behaviour. For other dimensional parameters, such as elastic modulus and cohesion, their similarity ratios must be determined based on the governing mechanical processes and similarity criteria.

Loading similitude serves as the critical link between geometric and material similarity and the mechanical response of the model. It requires that all external forces acting on the model maintain proportional relationships with those on the prototype. In most large-scale geotechnical engineering problems, gravity is the dominant body force and primary driving mechanism. Traditional seepage similarity criteria

When geotechnical problems involve pore fluid flow, traditional similarity theory must be extended beyond the solid mechanics framework described in Sect. 2.1 by incorporating seepage similitude. The core objective is to ensure that the seepage field in the model accurately replicates that of the prototype in a scaled manner. Classical approaches typically treat seepage as an independent or weakly coupled physical process with respect to solid deformation. The derivation of the corresponding similarity criteria is primarily based on maintaining the similarity of Darcy’s Law and the fluid continuity Equation38.

The similarity of seepage motion serves as the starting point for this theoretical framework. In most geotechnical seepage problems, fluid flow is assumed to be slow and laminar, governed by the classical form of Darcy’s law:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v= - K \cdot \nabla h= - \frac{{{k_0}}}{\mu }\left( {\nabla p+{\rho _f}g} \right)$$\end{document}

where v is the Darcy velocity, K is the hydraulic conductivity, ∇h is the hydraulic gradient, k0 is intrinsic permeability, µ is dynamic viscosity, ∇p is pressure gradient, ρf is the fluid density, and g is gravitational acceleration.

To ensure seepage similarity between the model and prototype, both the driving hydraulic gradient and the permeability that governs fluid transport must follow specific scaling relationships. Typically, the similarity ratio of hydraulic head is set equal to the geometric similarity ratio, ensuring that the hydraulic gradient remains consistent across the model and prototype. Under this condition, the permeability ratio must match the velocity ratio.

When the problem involves transient flow processes, such as soil consolidation or unsteady well flow in confined aquifers, similarity in fluid mass conservation becomes critical. The governing equation for such processes is the diffusion equation. Its similarity condition relies on maintaining an equivalent time factor between the model and the prototype. The consolidation coefficient, which incorporates medium permeability, compressibility, and fluid properties, characterizes the rate of pore pressure dissipation. Maintaining time similarity introduces an additional constraint on the time similarity ratio that is independent of the Froude criterion. This relationship forms the theoretical basis of traditional soil consolidation model tests, enabling the translation of laboratory test results over hours or days into field-scale settlement behaviour spanning years or decades.

To satisfy these seepage-related similarity conditions in practice, careful selection and adjustment of both the permeability characteristics of model materials and the physical properties of the fluid are required39. This includes similarity requirements for inherent properties such as permeability and specific storage of the porous medium, as well as the density and viscosity of the fluid40. For example, to simultaneously satisfy both mechanical similarity based on the Froude criterion and seepage similarity based on the diffusion equation, the permeability similarity ratio must be very precisely controlled. Furthermore, to ensure preliminary similarity of solid-fluid coupling effects, more refined models often require dimensionless parameters such as the Biot coefficient and Skempton’s pore pressure coefficient to remain consistent between the model and the prototype41,42. Traditional theory system of seepage similarity.

Although the traditional similarity theory described above has achieved widespread success in addressing problems such as slope stability, foundation bearing capacity, and steady-state seepage under static or quasi-static conditions, and its theoretical and practical maturity is well established, significant limitations emerge when it is applied to coupled solid-fluid disaster simulations in deep mines under combined high static and intense dynamic loading conditions. These limitations are rooted in the underlying assumptions and simplifications of the traditional framework and are primarily reflected in the following aspects:

In traditional similarity theory based on the Froude criterion, the so-called “inertial force” is limited to the momentum term associated with steady-state motion, specifically the velocity term. It does not account for the dynamic inertial forces induced by acceleration. As a result, the theory fails to capture critical inertia-dependent physical processes such as stress wave propagation, dynamic fracture evolution, and the strain rate sensitivity of materials, especially under high-frequency and high-acceleration dynamic loading conditions such as blasting or seismic events.The traditional seepage similarity criteria are typically derived based on Darcy’s law, which assumes slow, laminar, viscous flow and completely ignores fluid momentum variation and inertial effects. However, in high-speed transient processes such as water inrush or hydraulic fracturing, fluid kinetic energy and impact momentum may dominate the failure behaviour of surrounding rock. The traditional framework lacks the ability to quantify the inertial impact forces associated with non-Darcian flow, resulting in inadequate guidance when simulating such disaster mechanisms.Traditional theory typically overlays a gravity-dominated solid similarity system with a viscous-flow-based fluid similarity system, relying on the assumption of field separation to approximate multi-physical coupling processes. However, solid-inertia-dominated responses require high-frequency dynamic representation, whereas fluid-seepage-dominated processes are governed by long-term and slow variations. As a result, in simulating dynamic processes involving strong solid-fluid coupling, the existing theory cannot simultaneously satisfy both temporal and mechanical similarity requirements. This leads to conflicting constraints in material selection and loading path design. Moreover, the traditional framework lacks an integrated mechanical model capable of describing the bidirectional coupling between pore pressure accumulation and transmission and the stress response of the solid skeleton.

Kinetic solid-liquid coupling similarity theory

Fundamental theory of solid–fluid coupling similarity

To develop a similarity theory suitable for complex working conditions involving combined static and dynamic loads, it is essential to return to the fundamental physics governing the interaction between solid and fluid phases. In the field of porous media mechanics, this foundation is provided by Biot’s theory of saturated porous media. The strength of Biot’s theory lies in its ability to provide a fully coupled mathematical–physical framework, whose governing equations inherently and systematically account for all dominant physical interactions. Therefore, this theory serves not only as the theoretical foundation of our similarity approach, but also as the origin of the four key dynamic similarity parameters developed later in this study43,44. The key points of Biot’s theory can be summarized as two coupled momentum conservation equations and one mass conservation equation.

Firstly, the momentum conservation equation for the solid–fluid mixture is given as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla \cdot \sigma +{\rho _b}g={\rho _b}\frac{{{\partial ^2}u}}{{\partial {t^2}}}+{\rho _f}\frac{{{\partial ^2}w}}{{\partial {t^2}}}$$\end{document}

where ∇·σ is the divergence of the stress tensor, ρb is the density of bulk or skeletal, g is gravitational acceleration, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\partial ^2}u/\partial {t^2}$$\end{document} is the acceleration of the solid skeleton, and ρf is the fluid density, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\partial ^2}w/\partial {t^2}$$\end{document} is the acceleration of fluid.

In this equation, the total stress tensor σ is coupled to the pore pressure field through the effective stress principle. The right-hand side of the equation contains the inertial term of the solid skeleton, which is the fundamental distinction between dynamic and static analysis. In traditional static or quasi-static analysis, this term is typically neglected due to the assumption of zero acceleration. However, under dynamic loading, it represents the inertial force that resists deformation.

For fluid flow within the porous medium, Biot theory introduces a generalized Darcy’s law that includes inertial effects:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$- \nabla p+{\rho _f}g=\frac{\mu }{{{k_0}}}{v_r}+\frac{{{\rho _f}}}{\phi }\frac{{{\partial ^2}w}}{{\partial {t^2}}}$$\end{document}

where ∇p is the pore pressure gradient, ρf is the fluid density, µ is the dynamic viscosity, k0 is the intrinsic permeability, vr is the relative seepage velocity, ϕ is porosity, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\partial ^2}w/\partial {t^2}$$\end{document} is the acceleration of fluid.

Unlike the classical Darcy’s law, which accounts only for viscous resistance, this formulation includes an additional fluid inertial term. This term becomes dominant in high-speed flows and hydraulic impacts, and is absent in traditional seepage models.

Through the coupled mass conservation equation, Biot’s theory introduces essential parameters that characterize storage capacity and coupling-induced deformation, such as the Biot coefficient and Biot modulus:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla \cdot {v_r}+\alpha \frac{\partial }{{\partial t}}(\nabla \cdot u)+\frac{1}{M}\frac{{\partial p}}{{\partial t}}=0$$\end{document}

where ∇·vr is the divergence of seepage velocity, α is the Biot effective stress coefficient, ∇·u is volumetric strain, M is the Biot modulus, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial p/\partial t$$\end{document} is the pore pressure change rate.

This equation captures the interdependence between fluid flow, skeletal deformation, and pore pressure evolution. Under dynamic loading, the interplay between the inertial response timescale and pressure diffusion timescale governs the overall coupling behaviour. Collectively, the above equations constitute a complete mathematical model for solid–fluid coupling under combined static and dynamic loads. The central task of similarity theory is to ensure that the solutions to these equations in both the model and the prototype remain proportional. This system of governing equations provides an ideal theoretical starting point. The dynamic similarity indicators that we propose are derived through a detailed examination and mathematical abstraction of key physical terms in these equations, including solid inertia, fluid inertia, viscous resistance, and coupling-driven diffusion45.

Physical derivation of core dimensionless indicators

Criterion for solid dynamics: CAr

The derivation of the CA aims to address a fundamental shortcoming of classical similitude theory—its inability to represent inertial forces induced by acceleration. In engineering scenarios involving dynamic loading, it is essential to determine whether the system response is primarily governed by the material’s elastoplastic deformation or by inertial effects. This judgment determines whether a static framework or a dynamic model should be adopted for analysis, ultimately affecting simulation strategies, parameter selection, and material design. The CA is formulated to quantitatively assess the competition between inertial forces induced by acceleration and the material’s intrinsic elastic restoring forces.

When a rock element undergoes a characteristic strain, elastic stress develops internally, and the total elastic restoring force acting on the representative cross-sectional area is given by:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_e}\sim \sigma \cdot {L^2}=E\varepsilon {L^2}$$\end{document}

where Fe is the elastic restoring force, σ is stress; L is characteristic length, E is Young’s modulus, and ε is strain.

When the unit moves with a characteristic acceleration, and the effective density under solid-fluid coupling is considered, the resulting inertial force is:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_{id}}={m_{eq}} \cdot a={\rho _{eq}}{L^3}a \Leftrightarrow {\rho _{eq}}=(1 - \phi ){\rho _s}+\phi {\rho _f}$$\end{document}

where Fid is the dynamic inertial force, meq is the equivalent mass, a is the dynamic acceleration, ρeq is the equivalent density, L is the characteristic length, ϕ is porosity, and ρs and ρf are the densities of the solid and fluid phases, respectively.

The CA is defined as the ratio between these two forces:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CA=\frac{{{F_{id}}}}{{{F_e}}}=\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}{L^3}a}}{{E\varepsilon {L^2}}}=\frac{{\left[ {(1 - \phi ){\rho _s}+\phi {\rho _f}} \right]La}}{{E\varepsilon }}$$\end{document}

Thus, the Cauchy number serves as a fundamental criterion to determine whether the system enters a dynamic regime. When CA ≪ 1: the system is in an elasticity-dominated state. Inertial effects are negligible, deformation is nearly synchronous with external loading, and a quasi-static analysis is appropriate. When CA ≫ 1: the system becomes inertia-dominated. Energy propagates primarily as stress waves, the material response lags significantly behind the loading, and dynamic phenomena such as strain-rate hardening, dynamic fracture, and delamination may occur.

Fluid impact criterion: EU

The EU aims to compensate for the limitations of traditional seepage theory in capturing fluid inertia. In scenarios such as high-velocity water inrush, hydraulic fracturing, or blast-induced pore water oscillation, the momentum variation of the fluid can generate significant impact forces. The EU quantifies the interplay between total driving pressure and fluid inertia, allowing for the identification of whether the flow regime is dominated by pressure gradients or inertial impacts.

Under complex conditions involving both dynamic and static loading, both the driving force and inertial effects comprise multiple components. The total driving pressure includes both the original hydrostatic head and dynamic pressure waves induced in the fluid by stress waves propagating through the solid skeleton. The total fluid inertia effect manifests as the total kinetic energy of the system. The EU is the ratio of the two, and the formula is as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} EU=\frac{{Total driving pressure}}{{Total fluid inertia}}=\frac{{\Delta {p_s}+\Delta {p_d}}}{{{\rho _{eq}}v_{{eq}}^{2}}} \Leftrightarrow \left\{ \begin{gathered} \Delta {p_d}={\rho _{eq}}{v_w}{v_d} \hfill \\ {v_{eq}}=\sqrt {v_{s}^{2}+v_{d}^{2}} \hfill \\ {v_s}=\frac{Q}{A} \hfill \\ {v_d}=\frac{a}{{2\pi f}} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right. \hfill \\ EU=\frac{{\Delta {p_s}+{\rho _{eq}}{v_w}\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}}}{{{\rho _{eq}}\left[ {{{\left( {\frac{Q}{A}} \right)}^2}+{{\left( {\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}} \right)}^2}} \right]}}=\frac{{\Delta {p_s}+\left[ {(1 - \phi ){\rho _s}+\phi {\rho _f}} \right]{v_w}\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}}}{{\left[ {(1 - \phi ){\rho _s}+\phi {\rho _f}} \right]\left[ {{{\left( {\frac{Q}{A}} \right)}^2}+{{\left( {\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}} \right)}^2}} \right]}} \hfill \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

where Δps is the static pressure difference, Δpd is the dynamic pressure difference, veq is the equivalent characteristic velocity, vw is the P-wave velocity, vs is the steady-state seepage velocity, vd is the dynamic seepage velocity, Q is the volumetric flow rate, A is the flow cross-sectional area, and f is the characteristic frequency of dynamic disturbance.

The EU is the key indicator for evaluating disaster-inducing flow mechanisms. When EU ≫ 1, the system is in a pressure-driven regime. Flow is dominated by hydrostatic pressure gradients, and failure is primarily caused by static shear failure due to the reduction of effective stress under elevated pore pressure. When EU ≪ 1, the system transitions into an inertia-dominated regime, where the high kinetic energy of fluid flow dominates. In this case, damage occurs through direct scouring, erosion, or hydraulic fracturing induced by high-velocity fluid impacts.

Seepage state criterion: DA

Traditional Darcy’s law is only valid under very low Reynolds numbers. As flow velocity increases and fluid inertia becomes non-negligible, the flow regime deviates from Darcy’s law, and the relationship between pressure loss and flow velocity becomes nonlinear. The DA is proposed to characterize this deviation. It quantifies the ratio between inertial forces and viscous resistance during fluid flow through porous media.

According to Darcy’s law, the viscous resistance per unit volume is proportional to flow velocity and dynamic viscosity, and inversely proportional to permeability. The total viscous resistance acting on the representative volume is:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_v}=(\frac{{\mu {v_{eq}}}}{k}){L^3}$$\end{document}

where Fv is the total viscous resistance.

For fluid flowing at an equivalent velocity, its inertial force is:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_i}={m_{eq}}\frac{{d{v_{eq}}}}{{dt}}=({\rho _{eq}}{L^3})\frac{{{v_{eq}}}}{T}=({\rho _{eq}}{L^3})\frac{{v_{{eq}}^{2}}}{L}={\rho _{eq}}v_{{eq}}^{2}{L^2}$$\end{document}

where Fi is the inertial force of fluid.

The DA is defined as the ratio of fluid inertial force to viscous resistance:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} DA=\frac{{{F_i}}}{{{F_v}}}=\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}v_{{eq}}^{2}{L^2}}}{{(\frac{{\mu {v_{eq}}}}{k}){L^3}}}=\frac{{k{\rho _{eq}}{v_{eq}}}}{{\mu L}} \\ =\frac{{k\left[ {(1 - \phi ){\rho _s}+\phi {\rho _f}} \right]\sqrt {{{\left( {\frac{Q}{A}} \right)}^2}+{{\left( {\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}} \right)}^2}} }}{{\mu L}} \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

The DA serves as a key indicator of flow state under dynamic loading. When DA ≪ 1, the system lies in the viscosity-dominated regime. Flow is slow, and pressure losses are primarily due to viscous drag. When DA ≫ 1, the system transitions to an inertia-dominated regime. In this case, inertial penetration may occur, where the fluid, due to its large momentum, can open and sustain flow channels even under low pressure gradients.

Criterion for solid-liquid coupling characteristics: BI

The BI is designed to quantify the dynamic coupling characteristics between the solid and fluid phases. In static analysis, it describes the strength of coupling between pore pressure and stress. In dynamic scenarios, it characterizes the relative rates at which a physical disturbance propagates and diffuses through the system.

The inertial response time refers to the time required for a stress wave to propagate a characteristic distance through the solid skeleton, representing the rate at which the system mechanically responds to external loading:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_i}=\frac{L}{{{c_P}}}=L\sqrt {\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}}}{E}}$$\end{document}

where Ti is the inertial response time, and cP is the P-wave velocity.

The pressure diffusion time refers to the time required for pore pressure to diffuse over a characteristic distance within a porous medium, representing the rate of hydraulic adjustment in the system. It is calculated based on consolidation theory:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_d}=\frac{{{L^2}}}{{{c_v}}}=\frac{{\mu {L^2}}}{{kM}}$$\end{document}

where Td is the pressure diffusion time, and cv is the consolidation coefficient.

The BI is defined as the ratio of these two-time scales, representing the competition between mechanical and hydraulic responses:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BI=\frac{{{T_i}}}{{{T_d}}}=\frac{{L\sqrt {\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}}}{E}} }}{{\frac{{{L^2}}}{{{c_v}}}}}=\frac{{Mk}}{{\mu L}}\sqrt {\frac{{\left[ {(1 - \phi ){\rho _s}+\phi {\rho _f}} \right]}}{E}}$$\end{document}

The BI reveals the scale-dependent nature of solid–fluid coupling. When BI ≫ 1, pressure diffusion is much faster than mechanical response. In such cases, any transient excess pore pressure induced by dynamic loading dissipates rapidly, preventing significant accumulation. This condition typically occurs in small-scale, highly permeable systems. When BI ≪ 1, mechanical response is much faster than pressure diffusion. In this case, the stress variation due to dynamic loading occurs too rapidly for pore water to escape, leading to a sharp rise in local pore pressure and a significant reduction in the effective stress of the rock mass. This can trigger phenomena such as liquefaction or instability, typically in large-scale, low-permeability systems.

Unified framework for dynamic similarity criteria

In Sect. 3.2, we derived four key dimensionless indicators, each capturing the physical essence of the problem from a different perspective: solid dynamics, fluid impact, seepage regime, and solid–fluid coupling. However, actual engineering geohazards, like water inrush, are the result of the simultaneous and interacting contributions of all four mechanisms. Therefore, a comprehensive similarity theory must integrate these four indicators within a unified framework, revealing their intrinsic connections and mathematical constraints to construct a coupled system of similarity equations.

A complete dynamic similarity requires that all dominant dimensionless numbers in the model match those of the prototype. This forms the core of our theoretical framework: a constraint system composed of four governing equations:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ \begin{gathered} {(CA)_m}={(CA)_p} \Rightarrow {\left( {\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}La}}{{E\varepsilon }}} \right)_m}={\left( {\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}La}}{{E\varepsilon }}} \right)_p} \hfill \\ {(EU)_m}={(EU)_p} \Rightarrow {\left( {\frac{{\Delta {p_s}+{\rho _{eq}}{v_w}\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}}}{{{\rho _{eq}}v_{{eq}}^{2}}}} \right)_m}={\left( {\frac{{\Delta {p_s}+{\rho _{eq}}{v_w}\frac{a}{{2\pi f}}}}{{{\rho _{eq}}v_{{eq}}^{2}}}} \right)_p} \hfill \\ {(DA)_m}={(DA)_p} \Rightarrow {\left( {\frac{{k{\rho _{eq}}{v_{eq}}}}{{\mu L}}} \right)_m}={\left( {\frac{{k{\rho _{eq}}{v_{eq}}}}{{\mu L}}} \right)_p} \hfill \\ {(BI)_m}={(BI)_p} \Rightarrow {\left( {\frac{{Mk}}{{\mu L}}\sqrt {\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}}}{E}} } \right)_m}={\left( {\frac{{Mk}}{{\mu L}}\sqrt {\frac{{{\rho _{eq}}}}{E}} } \right)_p} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document}

Any changes in physical parameters imposes cascading constraints on all others through the coupled system. However, these constraints are highly stringent and nearly impossible to meet in practical similarity experiments. Suppose we conduct a model test with a geometric similarity ratio CL = 100, using water as the working fluid, and select a model material with density approximately equal to the prototype. According to Eq. (22), the requirement for the permeability and elastic modulus of the model material is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C_k}\sqrt {{C_E}} =100$$\end{document}.This implies that if the model material is 100 times softer than the prototype, then its permeability ratio must be 1/10 of the prototype. Yet this only satisfies the constraints from the CA and DA criteria, without considering the additional, more complex constraints imposed by Eu and BI.

Therefore, this complex theoretical system requires researchers to quantitatively assess, prior to similarity experiments, which similarity criteria are violated in a given solid–fluid coupling model and how such violations might affect the experimental outcomes. Secondly, which dimensionless numbers should be judged, so as to give priority to meeting their corresponding similar criteria, and weaken the requirements for other secondary criteria. For example, when simulating high-frequency blasting, the inertial effects of both solid and fluid phases dominate. In contrast, for simulating long-term seepage after high-pressure hydraulic fracturing, permeability and coupling effects dominate. DA and BI take precedence, and the CA can be neglected due to minimal acceleration, allowing for quasi-static or low-velocity flow models.

This paradox necessitates a pragmatic approach to physical modeling, where researchers must prioritize the dominant physical mechanisms for their specific problem. Theoretical application of similarity criteria

Following the establishment of a unified dynamic similarity theory and a corresponding set of dimensionless criteria, this chapter aims to demonstrate the practical application of the proposed framework through a specific theoretical case study. Drawing upon triaxial compression tests conducted by Li et al. on rock specimens collected from a phosphate mine in Yunnan Province46, we quantitatively compute and visualize how the dynamic solid–fluid coupling indicators of a theoretical similarity model vary under different geometric similarity ratios and dynamic loading accelerations, using the theoretical system developed in this study.

Theoretical application scheme

The selected rock samples were cut and polished into standard cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 100 mm, ensuring smooth and flat surfaces. The specimens were dried in an oven and subsequently divided into three groups: A, B, and C. Group A was designated as the dry condition group; Group B was saturated under vacuum and sealed with plastic film, representing the water-saturated condition group; Group C was first subjected to dynamic loading and then saturated and sealed using the same method as Group B. The experimental procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Preliminary uniaxial compression tests revealed that the uniaxial compressive strength of the specimens was 92.4 MPa. During dynamic loading, a sinusoidal stress disturbance with an amplitude of 9.24 MPa and a frequency of 4 Hz was applied once the axial stress reached 86 MPa. The applied stress wave is shown in Fig. 3.

Fig. 3Dynamic stress wave loading path.

Dynamic stress wave loading path.

The density and P-wave velocity of the three groups of specimens were measured. Group A exhibited an average density of 2.77 g/cm³ and an average P-wave velocity of 3093 m/s; Group B showed an average density of 2.818 g/cm³ and P-wave velocity of 2056 m/s; Group C had an average density of 2.825 g/cm³ and P-wave velocity of 1379 m/s. Subsequently, triaxial compression tests were conducted on all three groups under a confining pressure of 6 MPa. The resulting stress–strain curves are presented in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Stress–strain curves obtained from triaxial tests under the three different conditions.

The stress–strain curves reveal that all specimens underwent four typical stages during triaxial compression: compaction, elastic deformation, yielding, and failure. Specimens under both dry and water-saturated conditions exhibited a pronounced linear response prior to the peak stress, with well-defined peak points. In contrast, the water-saturated specimens subjected to seismic stress wave disturbance displayed markedly enhanced plastic behaviour. Their post-peak stress–strain curves featured multiple stepped drop stages, indicative of progressive structural degradation. Based on the curves, the calculated elastic moduli for Groups A, B, and C were 19.51 GPa, 17.16 GPa, and 15.95 GPa.

Calculation of solid–fluid coupling indicators

For the unknown permeability of the rock, we estimate its order of magnitude using the Kozeny–Carman equation. This equation is expressed as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=\frac{C}{{{S^2}}} \cdot \frac{{{\phi ^3}}}{{{{\left( {1 - \phi } \right)}^2}}}$$\end{document}

where k is the rock permeability, ϕ is porosity, C is the Kozeny constant, and S is the specific surface area of the rock particles.

To calculate the BI value, the Biot modulus of the rock is required, which is determined using its standard definition. The equation is given as follows:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{M}=\frac{\phi }{{{K_f}}}+\frac{{\alpha - \phi }}{{{K_s}}}$$\end{document}

where M is the Biot modulus, ϕ is porosity, α is the Biot effective stress coefficient, Kf is the bulk modulus of the pore fluid, and Ks is the bulk modulus of the solid grains.

After selecting appropriate flow parameters consistent with dense phosphate rock, the peak point of the stress–strain curve is used as the reference condition for all parameter calculations. Since Group B is subjected to static loading, there are no dynamic effects; both the imposed acceleration and the resulting equivalent velocity are approximately zero. However, to better illustrate the contrast between dynamic and static conditions, small representative values of acceleration and velocity are substituted into the calculations. The resulting solid–fluid coupling indicators for Group B under saturated conditions are: CAB ≈ 1.24 × 10⁻⁸, EUB ≈ 1.80 × 10¹⁰, DAB ≈ 2.82 × 10⁻¹³, and BIB ≈ 5.21 × 10⁻¹⁰. For Group C, which is subjected to dynamic loading, the dynamic acceleration and equivalent velocity are estimated based on the relevant equations. The computed indicators under dynamic loading and water saturation are: CAC ≈ 1.45 × 10⁻⁴, EUC ≈ 2.08 × 10³, DAC ≈ 1.27 × 10⁻⁷, and BIC ≈ 5.63 × 10⁻⁷.

Under dynamic loading, the CA value increases by more than four orders of magnitude compared to the static case, quantitatively demonstrating that inertial effects, negligible under static conditions, become a dominant factor when dynamic forces are present. The EU value drops by approximately seven orders of magnitude under dynamic conditions. This indicates that while flow inertia is negligible relative to static pressure in quasi-static states, it becomes significantly amplified during dynamic events, shifting the balance between pressure and inertia into a new, physically meaningful regime. The DA value increases by a factor of one million under dynamic conditions, implying that oscillatory motion induced by dynamic loading significantly enhances flow inertia and increases its relative importance in the system. The variation in BI value is more closely associated with changes in material properties and model scale, further illustrating how each of the four indicators captures different aspects of the solid–fluid coupling mechanisms.

Influence of geometric similarity on solid–fluid coupling characteristics

The inherent geometric downscaling in physical model tests is a critical factor contributing to the deviation between model behaviour and prototype physical mechanisms. This section aims to apply the unified similarity theory developed earlier to quantitatively reveal how the geometric similarity ratio, as a single variable, fundamentally alters the solid–fluid coupling behaviour in saturated rock masses. So, we take the previously defined Group B as the reference prototype and construct a set of hypothetical models. These models share identical material properties with the prototype, but differ in geometric scale, which is reduced by similarity ratios of 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100. The resulting values of the four solid–fluid coupling indicators are shown in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5Evolution of the four coupling indicators with varying geometric similarity ratios.

Evolution of the four coupling indicators with varying geometric similarity ratios.

As shown in the figure, geometric downscaling is not a straightforward linear process. It systematically diminishes solid inertial effects while drastically enhancing the “instantaneity” of fluid flow and coupling responses, thereby fundamentally transforming the underlying solid–fluid interaction mechanisms. At low geometric similarity ratios, the CA drops sharply with CL increasing; at higher CL values, the decline levels off and CA approaches zero. This indicates that solid inertial effects are significantly and systematically suppressed in downscaled models. A failure mechanism in the prototype that may involve inertial effects could degenerate into a purely static process in smaller-scale models. The EU remains independent of the geometric similarity ratio CL. This suggests that geometric scaling alone does not alter the relative strength of pressure versus fluid inertia, and the system’s driving force regime remains invariant across scales. Both the DA and BI increase linearly with CL increasing. The increase of DA implies that, in scaled-down models, fluid inertia gains relatively more significance compared to viscous resistance. The increase of BI reflects a shift in the dynamic response regime of solid–fluid coupling. This quantitatively explains the physical mechanism observed in small-scale models: extremely short drainage paths cause rapid pore pressure dissipation, preventing the pressure build-up that, in a full-scale prototype, could critically reduce effective stress and trigger hazards like liquefaction or water inrush. Consequently, small models may fail to capture these failure modes.

In deep underground engineering disaster scenarios, dynamic loading plays a decisive role in determining the mechanical response of rock masses. Therefore, building on the previous analysis, this paper further decouples and quantitatively reveals how the dynamic acceleration variable influences the solid–fluid coupling characteristics of the system. Using Group C as the dynamic reference prototype, we keep all geometric and material properties constant while systematically varying the dynamic loading strength by adjusting the acceleration similarity ratio, Ca = (2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100). By analysing the evolution of the four dynamic solid–fluid coupling indicators during this process, we aim to elucidate the transition in dominant internal physical mechanisms from quasi-static loading to high-intensity dynamic impact. The computed indicator values are presented in the Fig. 6.

Fig. 6Variation in the four indicators under different acceleration similarity ratios.

Variation in the four indicators under different acceleration similarity ratios.

The introduction of dynamic loading systematically alters the values of key indicators and effectively impacts the behavioural patterns of the solid–fluid coupling system. More importantly, the evolution of these indicators reveals an unconventional, pressure-dominated dynamic response regime. Within the proposed theoretical framework, the CA decreases inversely with the acceleration similarity ratio. This indicates that in strongly coupled systems, acceleration is not an independent control variable but is intrinsically linked to system deformation and energy dissipation. In the low-Ca region, the CA exhibits a sharp decline, followed by a gradual reduction toward zero in the high- Ca range. This behaviour indicates that under high dynamic loading, the model undergoes pronounced inertial–elastic oscillatory responses. However, as the acceleration diminishes, these inertial effects rapidly fade, and the deformation gradually transitions to a static or quasi-static elastoplastic regime. The EU increases linearly with the acceleration similarity ratio, clearly illustrating that reduced acceleration enhances the relative dominance of pressure-driven forces. In other words, under weaker dynamic loading, the pore pressure-induced driving effect becomes more prominent. Similar to CA, the DA also decreases inversely with the acceleration similarity ratio. This implies that during high-speed seepage, inertial or non-viscous flow components are suppressed by passive diffusion effects, whereas under low shear rate conditions, fluid flow tends to conform to traditional Darcy behaviour governed by viscous diffusion. The BI remains constant, suggesting that regardless of dynamic load intensity, the ratio between inertial response time and pore pressure diffusion time is fixed at a given scale and determined solely by the constitutive properties of the material. Overall, as the model’s dynamic loading continues to diminish, the system response gradually shifts from an inertia-dominated “rigid dynamics” regime to a “flexible quasi-static” mode, dominated by pore pressure-driven flow and viscous diffusion.

Discussion

The unified framework proposed in this study, while theoretically robust, reveals a significant practical challenge termed the similarity paradox. Achieving perfect similarity by matching all four dimensionless numbers (CA, EU, DA, and BI) between a small-scale model and a full-scale prototype is often impossible due to conflicting material property requirements. Researchers must adopt a problem-specific strategy, prioritizing the physical mechanisms most relevant to the engineering scenario under investigation.

Despite its significant advances, this study still has several limitations. The proposed theoretical framework is established under the assumptions of a homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic porous medium, while real rock masses are inherently heterogeneous, anisotropic, and exhibit nonlinear as well as inelastic behaviours. These simplifications, though necessary for analytical tractability, may constrain the direct applicability of the present similarity theory to complex geological environments.

Accordingly, future research should focus on three key directions.

First, the theoretical framework should be extended to incorporate nonlinear constitutive relations, damage evolution, and anisotropic mechanical responses, thereby developing a more generalized and realistic dynamic similarity theory. Second, there is an urgent need to develop novel similitude materials with independently adjustable physical properties. Such materials should allow the flexible control of stiffness, permeability, and density to simultaneously satisfy multiple dynamic similarity constraints, with particular emphasis on achieving tunable stiffness–permeability and strength–density ratios. Finally, large-scale, well-instrumented physical model experiments should be conducted based on the decision-making framework outlined in Table 3 to validate the prioritized similarity approach and to quantitatively evaluate the errors introduced by neglecting secondary similarity criteria. These future efforts will bridge the gap between theoretical predictions and practical engineering applications, enhancing the reliability and scalability of dynamic similarity modeling for saturated rock masses under combined static and dynamic loading.
