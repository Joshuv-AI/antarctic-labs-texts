Recovering polynomials over finite fields from noisy character values
Swastik Kopparty

Abstract
Let g ⁡ ( X ) g(X) be a polynomial over a finite field 𝔽 q {\mathbb{F}}_{q} with degree o ⁡ ( q 1 / 2 ) o(q^{1/2}) , and let χ \chi be the quadratic residue character. We give a polynomial time algorithm to recover g ⁡ ( X ) g(X) (up to perfect square factors) given the values of χ ∘ g \chi\circ g on 𝔽 q {\mathbb{F}}_{q} , with up to a constant fraction of the values having errors. This was previously unknown even for the case of no errors. We give a similar algorithm for additive characters of polynomials over fields of characteristic 2 2 . This gives the first polynomial time algorithm for decoding dual-BCH codes of polynomial dimension from a constant fraction of errors. Our algorithms use ideas from Stepanov’s polynomial method proof of the classical Weil bounds on character sums, as well as from the Berlekamp-Welch decoding algorithm for Reed-Solomon codes. A crucial role is played by what we call pseudopolynomials : high degree polynomials, all of whose derivatives behave like low degree polynomials on 𝔽 q {\mathbb{F}}_{q} . Both these results can be viewed as algorithmic versions of the Weil bounds for this setting.

Introduction

This paper is about efficient algorithms for some noisy polynomial interpolation problems over finite fields, where only a small amount of information about each evaluation is available.

Let
q
be an odd prime power, and let
\chi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
be the quadratic residue character (which takes values
0
on
0
,
+1
on nonzero perfect squares,
-1
on everything else). Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be an unknown polynomial of degree at most
d\ll q
. We consider the problem of computing
g
given a noisy version of
\chi\circ g:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
.
Concretely, given
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
, such that for
99\%
of the
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
we have
r(\alpha)=\chi(g(\alpha))
, can we efficiently recover
g(X)
?

There is an immediate obstacle to recovering
g
: it may not be uniquely specified by
r
. This arises because
\chi
cannot detect perfect square factors of
g
. Indeed, if
g_{1}(X),g_{2}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree at most
d
satisfy
g_{2}(X)=g_{1}(X)\cdot h^{2}(X)
for
h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, then
whenever
h(\alpha)\neq 0
:

\chi\circ g_{2}(\alpha)=\left(\chi\circ g_{1}(\alpha)\right)\cdot\left(\chi\circ h^{2}(\alpha)\right)=\chi\circ g_{1}(\alpha),

and so the Hamming distance
\Delta(\chi\circ g_{1},\chi\circ g_{2})\leq d\ll q
.

The deep and fundamental Weil bounds for character sums imply that this is essentially the only obstacle to uniqueness. It states that if
f(X)
is a polynomial of degree at most
d
that is not of the form
\lambda\cdot h^{2}(X)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
,
then:

\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi(f(\alpha))\right|\leq 2d\sqrt{q}.

(In words, if
d
is small, a random
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
has
f(\alpha)
being a perfect square with probability approximately
1/2
).

Thus if
g_{1}(X),g_{2}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
are distinct, monic (the leading coefficient is
1
), squarefree (no irreducible factor appears with multiplicity
\geq 2
), and of degree at most
o(\sqrt{q})
, then
\chi\circ g_{1}
and
\chi\circ g_{2}
are not only distinct, they are almost orthogonal:

\left|\langle\chi\circ g_{1},\chi\circ g_{2}\rangle\right|=\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi(g_{1}(\alpha))\cdot\chi(g_{2}(\alpha))\right|=\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi((g_{1}\cdot g_{2})(\alpha))\right|\leq O(d\sqrt{q})=o(q).

This implies that as
g
varies among the monic, squarefree polynomials of degree at most
d=o(\sqrt{q})
,
the vectors
\chi\circ g\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{q}
have all pairwise Hamming distances
\Delta(\chi\circ g_{1},\chi\circ g_{2})
at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\Omega(\frac{d}{\sqrt{q}})\right)\cdot q
. This defines an error-correcting code, and makes the problem of finding
g
given a noisy version of
\chi\circ g
that of unique decoding of this error-correcting code.

We give a polynomial time algorithm for recovering such
g
from
\chi\circ g
in the presence of a constant fraction of errors.

Theorem 1

Let
\epsilon>0
.
Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be monic, squarefree, with
\mathsf{deg}(g)\leq\epsilon\sqrt{q}
.

Suppose

is such that:

\Delta(r,\chi\circ g)<\left(\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon\right)q.

There is a
\mathrm{poly}(q)

when given
r
, computes
g(X)
.

If
g(X)
is a general monic polynomial of degree
d\leq\epsilon\sqrt{q}
and
r
is such that

\Delta(r,\chi\circ g)<(\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon)q
, then this algorithm will find the unique monic squarefree polynomial
\bar{g}(X)
of degree at most
d
such that

\Delta(\chi\circ g,\chi\circ\bar{g})\leq d
.

Such an algorithm was previously unknown even for the case of no errors. The previously fastest known algorithm was given by Russell and Shparlinski
[
RS04
]
and ran in
O(d^{2}\cdot q^{d+o(1)})
time. This algorithm made use of the Weil bounds as a black box, and improved upon the trivial
O(q^{d+1})
time brute force search.

In
[
VDHI06
]
, van Dam, Hallgren and Ip gave efficient

, which is sublinear in the input size. Russell and Shparlinski
[
RS04
]
showed that the general

quantum queries, but as far as we understand this does not lead to a fast quantum algorithm.
Further results in this direction were given in
[
BGKS12
,
IKS+18
]
.

We also prove an analogous result for additive characters over fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
2
. Here the underlying codes are the classical dual-BCH codes, which are the duals of the classical BCH codes

]
.

Let

, and let
\mathsf{Tr}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
be the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear field trace map given by
\mathsf{Tr}(\beta)=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\beta^{2^{i}}
. Additive characters

\mathbb{F}_{q}
are closely related to

\mathsf{Tr}
(they are of the form
\psi(\beta)=(-1)^{\mathsf{Tr}(a\cdot\beta)}
), and for notational convenience, we formulate our problem in terms of
\mathsf{Tr}
instead of

.

Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be an unknown polynomial of degree at most
d
. Suppose for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we are given a value
r(\alpha)
, such that for
99\%
of the
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
r(\alpha)=\mathsf{Tr}(g(\alpha))
. Can we efficiently recover
g(X)
?

Again, there is an immediate obstacle coming from the fact that

r
(or even
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
) does not uniquely identify
g(X)
. This arises because

\mathsf{Tr}
cannot distinguish between a polynomial and its square. Indeed,
if
g_{1}(X),g_{2}(X)
are such that
g_{2}(X)=g_{1}(X)+h(X)+h^{2}(X)
for some
h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, then
1
1

1

This is because
\mathsf{Tr}(\beta+\beta^{2})=0
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{2}(\alpha)=\left(\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{1}(\alpha)\right)+\left(\mathsf{Tr}\circ(h+h^{2})(\alpha)\right)=\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{1}(\alpha),

and so
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{1}=\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{2}
.

The Weil bound for additive character sums tell us that this is the only obstacle to uniqueness. It says that if
f(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is of degree at most
d
and not of the form
\lambda+h(X)+h(X)^{2}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, then:

\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(-1)^{\mathsf{Tr}(f(\alpha))}\right|\leq(d-1)\sqrt{q}.

(In words, if
d
is small, a random
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
has
\mathsf{Tr}(f(\alpha))
being roughly equally likely to be
0
and
1
).

Thus if
g_{1}(X),g_{2}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
are distinct polynomials with only monomials of odd degree, and of degree
d=o(\sqrt{q})
,
then
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{1}
and
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{2}
are not only distinct, they are almost as far apart as random vectors:

\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(-1)^{\mathsf{Tr}(g_{1}(\alpha)-g_{2}(\alpha))}\right|=\left|\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(-1)^{\mathsf{Tr}((g_{1}-g_{2})(\alpha))}\right|\leq O(d\sqrt{q})=o(q),

and so:

\Delta(\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{1},\mathsf{Tr}\circ g_{2})\in\left(\frac{1}{2}\pm O(\frac{d}{\sqrt{q}})\right)\cdot q.

This gives us the definition of the dual BCH code. For an odd integer
d
, codewords are indexed by polynomials
g(X)
of degree at most
d

g(X)=a_{1}X+a_{3}X^{3}+\ldots+a_{d}X^{d},

and the codewords themselves are the functions
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
This is a linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and has length
q
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-dimension
\frac{d+1}{2}\log_{2}q
.
The problem of finding
g
given a noisy version of
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
is thus the problem of unique decoding of this dual BCH code.

We give a polynomial time algorithm for recovering
g
from
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
in the presence of a constant fraction of errors.

Theorem 2

Let
\epsilon>0
.
Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be a polynomial with only odd degree monomials, and with
\mathsf{deg}(g)\leq\epsilon\sqrt{q}
.

Suppose
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is such that:

\Delta(r,\mathsf{Tr}\circ g)<\left(\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon\right)q.

There is a
\mathrm{poly}(q)
time algorithm, which
when given
r
, computes
g(X)
.

If
g(X)
is a general polynomial of degree
d\leq\epsilon\sqrt{q}

with
0
constant term, and
r
is such that

\Delta(r,\mathsf{Tr}\circ g)<(\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon)q
, then this algorithm will find the unique polynomial
\bar{g}(X)
of degree at most
d
with only odd degree monomials such
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g=\mathsf{Tr}\circ\bar{g}
.

There were several previously known results for this problem (namely, the problem of decoding dual BCH codes).

•

For all
d<O(\sqrt{q})
, if there are no errors at all, then
g(X)
can be found in time
\mathrm{poly}(q)
. This is because the map
g(X)\mapsto\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
is
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear; so
g(X)
can be found from
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
by solving a system of linear equations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

•

For all
d<O(\sqrt{q})
, if the number of errors is at most
O(\frac{q}{d})
, then there is a
\mathrm{poly}(q)
time algorithm to recover
g(X)
. This is a consequence of efficient decoding algorithms for Reed-Muller codes (multivariate polynomial codes over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{b}
). The key fact is that the function
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
is a
b
-variate
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree at most
\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil
when
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is viewed as
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{b}
via an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear isomorphism.

•

For small
d
, the work of Kaufman-Litsyn
[
KL05
]
, Kaufman-Sudan
[
KS07
]
and Kopparty-Saraf
[
KS10
]
gave
sublinear time
algorithms for decoding dual BCH codes from a constant fraction of errors. The running time of these algorithms is
O(d^{d}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log q))
. For
d=O(\frac{\log q}{\log\log q})
, they run in time
\mathrm{poly}(q)
, while for slightly smaller
d
they run in time
q^{o(1)}
.

These algorithms use the fact that there are many short linear dependencies between the coordinates of codewords; namely, that their duals have many low weight codewords.
Interestingly, they use decoding algorithms for BCH codes as a subroutine.

Theorem 2 gives the first algorithm to decode from a constant fraction of errors in

\mathrm{poly}(q)
time for
d>\omega(\frac{\log q}{\log\log q})
.

Our results can be viewed as algorithmic versions of Weil-type bounds for character sums. To the best of our knowledge, the only known proof that the error-correcting codes underlying Theorem 1 (for
d\geq 3
) and Theorem 2 (for
d\geq\Omega(\log q)
) have distance
\Omega(q)
go through the Weil bounds. Any algorithm that decodes these codes from
\Omega(q)
errors must either use a Weil-type bound or prove a Weil-type bounds. Our results fall in the latter category.

We use ideas from Stepanov’s polynomial method proof of the Weil bounds, as well as from the Berlekamp-Welch decoding algorithm for Reed-Solomon codes. A crucial role is played by what we call
pseudopolynomials
: high degree polynomials, all of whose derivatives behave like low degree polynomials on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

The number of errors that these algorithms can handle falls short of the unique decoding radius of these codes, which is about
\left(\frac{1}{4}-\epsilon\right)q
. It would be interesting to get efficient decoding from these many errors.

1.1
Related Work

The Weil bounds on character sums were proved by Weil
[
Wei41
,
Wei48
,
Wei49
]

as a corollary of his proof of the Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields. They capture some very basic pseudorandomness phenomena in finite fields in a very strong quantitative form. For this reason, they have found extensive applications in the explicit constructions of pseudorandom objects.
We mention some notable applications in theoretical computer science: the universality of the Paley graphs by Graham and Spencer
[
GS71
]
, the construction of epsilon biased sets by Alon, Goldreich, Håstad and Peralta
[
AGHP92
]
, and the construction of affine extractors by Gabizon and Raz
[
GR08
]
.

Another remarkable application is the dual-BCH code, mentioned above. Dual-BCH codes are
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear codes of length
q
with
q^{(d+1)/2}
codewords and minimum distance at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{q}})\right)\cdot q
. For small
d
these are unmatched codes: without the Weil bounds, there is no known proof of existence, for all constant
t
, of binary codes of length
n
with
n^{t}
codewords and minimum distance
(\frac{1}{2}-O_{t}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}))\cdot n
. (A nonlinear binary code with similar strong parameters can also be made out of the quadratic residue character applied to squarefree polynomials over fields of odd characteristic; one just has to replace the small number of
0
coordinates with either
+1
or
-1
, arbitrarily.) This also yields
n^{t}
unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with pairwise inner products all at most
O(t/\sqrt{n})
. This is an even smaller upper bound on the maximum inner product than what random vectors would achieve,
O(\sqrt{(\log n)/n})
.

The original proof of the Weil bounds used machinery from algebraic geometry. Many years later, Stepanov
[
Ste69
,
Ste70
,
Ste74
]
discovered an elementary proof of Weil-type bounds. This used a very clever version of what is now known as the polynomial method (with multiplicities) – understanding a set of interest by first interpolating a polynomial vanishing on that set (with high multiplicity), and then studying that polynomial.
Subsequently Schmidt
[
Sch76
]
, Bombieri
[
Bom73
]
and Stohr-Voloch
[
SV86
]
strengthened, simplified and generalized Stepanov’s method greatly. See
[
Sch76
,
HKT08
,
LN97
]
for excellent introductions to this and related topics. Our algorithms are heavily influenced by ideas from Stepanov’s method.

In recent years, a number of extensions of the Weil bounds for character sums were developed for applications in theoretical computer science and coding theory, see
[
Kop11
,
KL11
,
CGS16
,
KTSY24
,
CDGM25
]
and
[
Kop10
, Chapter 6]
.

The problems we consider in this paper are a kind of noisy polynomial interpolation problem. This is an old and very well studied topic in coding theory and theoretical computer science. Our algorithms are also heavily influenced by the polynomial method (with multiplicities) techniques from this domain, especially the “interpolation + algebra” framework of the fundamental Berlekamp-Welch and Guruswami-Sudan decoding algorithms for Reed-Solomon codes
[
BW86
,
Sud96
,
GS99
]
.

The Guruswami-Sudan algorithm solves a polynomial reconstruction problem of a very general type that nicely captures the problem of recovering polynomials from character values. We now describe this problem, known as the list-recovery problem for Reed-Solomon codes. In list-recovery of Reed-Solomon codes, for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
we are given a set
S_{\alpha}
of size at most
\ell
, and we want to find all polynomials
f(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree at most
d
with

|\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mid f(\alpha)\in S_{\alpha}\}|\geq 0.99q.

The Guruswami-Sudan algorithm
[
GS99
]
efficiently finds all such
f(X)
in time
\mathrm{poly}(q)
, provided
\ell<(0.99)^{2}\frac{q}{d}
(and in particular, proves that the number of such
f(X)
is at most
\mathrm{poly}(q)
).
When we are given
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
with

\Delta(\chi\circ g,r)\leq 0.01q
for some low degree
g(X)
, the problem of finding
g(X)
translates into a list-recovery problem as follows.
For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, let
S_{\alpha}=\chi^{-1}(r(\alpha))
.
Then the set of all
f(X)
of degree at most
d
satisfying

|\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mid f(\alpha)\in S_{\alpha}\}|\geq 0.99q,

contains
g(X)
(and in fact all such
f(X)
are of the form
\lambda\cdot g(X)\cdot h^{2}(X)
). Thus the problem of recovering
g
is a special kind of list-recovery problem.

In this setting,
|S_{\alpha}|\leq\ell=(q-1)/2
, and
d=\omega(1)
, so we are outside the range of where the Guruswami-Sudan algorithm applies. Our result can be viewed as a way of using the extra algebraic structure of the
S_{\alpha}
to nevertheless enable some kind of list-recovery.

Our algorithms use higher order derivative information about the polynomials being interpolated. In this sense, they are related in spirit to a recently flourishing line of work on decoding algorithms for multiplicity codes
[
RT97
,
Nie01
,
KSY14
,
GW13
,
Kop14
,
Kop15
,
KRZSW23

Tam24
,
BHKS24
,
GHKS24
,
CZ25
,
Sri25
,
GHK+25
,
AHS25
]
(Multiplicity codes are error-correcting codes based on evaluations of polynomials and their derivatives).

The dual-BCH code
C
with
d=\Theta(q^{0.1})

is a binary code that has:
(1) block length
n
, (2) dimension
k=\Omega(n^{0.1}\log n)
,
(3) distance
\left(\frac{1}{2}-o(1)\right)n
, and
(4) dual distance (i.e., distance of

)
\Omega(n^{0.1})
.
Theorem 2 shows that it also has: (5) efficiently decoding from
\Omega(n)

Explicit binary codes with distance
\frac{n}{2}-o(1)
, dual distance

,
and efficiently decodable from
\Omega(n)

were not known until quite recently. The first such codes were constructed by Kopparty, Shaltiel, and Silbak
[
KSS19
]
.
These codes, known as

, are closely related to dual-BCH
codes; their codewords are formed by taking juxtapositions of related dual-BCH codewords, and their minimum distance is proved by appealing to the same Weil bounds. The efficient decoding algorithm for these codes, which can actually list-decode from

If we relax the distance requirement on the code to simply be
\Omega(n)

[

discovered in the context of quantum codes (see also Guruswami’s appendix to

to be
h_{1}(X)\cdot(f(X))^{(q-1)/2+M}
also yields a valid solution to these equations.

The next step of the algorithm recovers
g(X)
from
F(X)
by factoring
F(X)
and inspecting the factor multiplicities of
F(X)
. Let

g(X)=\prod_{i}g_{i}(X)
be the factorization of
g(X)
into distinct irreducible factors. We show that the set of
g_{i}(X)
is exactly the set of all irreducible factors of
F(X)
whose factor multiplicity, after reducing mod
q
, is approximately
q/2
. This is clearly true if
F(X)=G(X)
. That it holds for any
F(X)
found by the first step of the algorithm is the main step of the analysis.

The proof of this is quite indirect. First, using a polynomial interpolation argument, we show that there are special polynomials
A(X),B(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
such that:

\displaystyle A(X)\cdot F(X)=B(X)\cdot G(X).

(2)

These special polynomials are what we call
pseudopolynomials
.
They are of the form
C(X)=\sum_{i}C_{i}(X)(X^{q}-X)^{i}
, with
C_{i}(X)
having
low degree
k
(much lower than
q
). The choice of this class of polynomials, as well as the idea of the interpolation argument, come from Stepanov’s method.
Pseudopolynomials have two key properties that we use:

•

For each
\ell
, there is a polynomial
C_{\langle\ell\rangle}(X)
of degree at most
k
such that the
\ell
-th derivative
C^{[\ell]}
and
C_{\langle\ell\rangle}
agree on all points of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This is used in the interpolation argument, to get the identity (
2
).

•

Every irreducible factor of
C(X)
has factor multiplicity approximately a multiple of
q
. This ensures, using (
2
), that the only factors of
F
with multiplicity approximately
q/2
mod
q
are the factors of
G
with multiplicity approximate
q/2
mod
q
, as we want.

This completes the sketch of the proof in the error-free case.

To handle errors, we use the idea of the Berlekamp-Welch decoding algorithm for Reed-Solomon codes. However, instead of using an error-locator polynomial to zero out the errors, we have to use a high multiplicity error-locator pseudopolynomial
E(X)
. Then ensures that the derivatives of
E(X)\cdot G(X)
also have a property
similar to the property of
G(X)
given in Equation (
1
).

For the additive character case, the algorithm becomes slightly more complicated.
To recover
g
given a noisy version
r
of
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
, we set up a system of linear equations capturing some special low-degree property of the derivatives of the polynomial
G(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}g^{2^{i}}(X)
. Specifically, we search for nonzero polynomials
F(X)
and
E(X)
, where
E(X)
is a pseudopolynomial, such that there exist low degree polynomials
U_{0}(X),\ldots,U_{M-1}(X)
with:

F^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=E^{[\ell]}(\alpha)\cdot r(\alpha)+U_{\ell}(\alpha)

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. If
E(X)
is a high multiplicity error-locator pseudopolynomial and
F(X)=E(X)\cdot G(X)
, then the above equations are satisfied. However the solution found by the algorithm may not be this one.

From this solution to this system of equations, we try to recover
g(X)
; however, unlike in the case of the quadratic residue character, where the relevant problem was factoring polynomials and there was an off-the-shelf algorithm known, we have to work a little harder. Here we only manage to use the solution of the system of equations to extract the largest degree coefficient of
g(X)
. We can then peel it off and repeat, and thus get the whole of
g(X)
. The analysis again uses an interpolation argument involving pseudopolynomials.

Our presentation of the algorithms and their analyses in Sections
3
and
4
is done from scratch and does not explicitly use the language of pseudopolynomials. Later, in Section
5
, we formally define pseudopolynomials and the pseudodegree, state some basic properties, and explain some ideas from previous sections in a cleaner context. We also formulate Stepanov’s proof of the Weil bounds in this language.

In the appendix, we briefly describe how these algorithms generalize to the
m
-th power residue character and additive characters over finite fields of small characteristic.

1.3
Questions

Below we highlight some interesting questions.

1.

Is there an efficient algorithm to decode these codes from a
(\frac{1}{4}-\epsilon)
-fraction of errors?
This is the unique decoding radius for these codes.

2.

More ambitiously, we could ask whether there is an efficient algorithm for list-decoding these codes from a
\left(\frac{1}{2}-O(\sqrt{\epsilon})\right)
-fraction of errors. This is the (binary) Johnson radius for these codes, so we know that there are only
O_{\epsilon}(1)
codewords within this radius of any given function
r
.

In some ways, our result is analogous to the Berlekamp-Welch algorithm decoding algorithm for Reed-Solomon codes. Is there an analogue of the Guruswami-Sudan algorithm for this context?

3.

Both the decoding problems that we give algorithms for are instances of the following more general problem.

Let
G(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]
be an unknown polynomial of degree
d
. Suppose we are given as input the projection
S=\Pi_{X}(G)
, where:

\Pi_{X}(G)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mid\exists y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mbox{ with }G(x,y)=0\}.

Can we find, in time
\mathrm{poly}(q)
, some polynomial
F(X,Y)
of degree at most
d
such that
|\Pi_{X}(F)\triangle S|\leq O_{d}(1)
?

Such sets are
definable
sets with complexity
O_{d}(1)
in the sense of Chatzidakis, van den Dries and Macintyre
[
CVDDM92
]
and Tao
[
Tao14
,
Tao13
]
.
The results of
[
CVDDM92
]
, which are based on more general forms of the Weil bounds, imply that the symmetric difference of any two such definable sets is of size either
O_{d}(1)
or
\Omega_{d}(q)
. This suggests that one could even hope to solve this given a noisy version of
\Pi_{X}(G)
.

4.

Let
q
be a prime, and let
H\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the set

\{0,1,\ldots,(q-1)/2\}
and let
1_{H}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,1\}
be its indicator function.
Suppose
g(X)
is an unknown polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree at most
d
, and we are given all evaluations of
(1_{H})\circ g:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,1\}
.

The additive character sum Weil bounds over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
imply that if
d<O(q^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon})
, then
g(X)
is uniquely determined, up to the constant term, by the function
1_{H}\circ g
.

Can we find
g(X)
(up to the constant term) in time
\mathrm{poly}(q)
?

To the best of our knowledge, the only elementary proofs of the uniqueness statement above are very indirect, involving the Stepanov method in multiple extension fields
\mathbb{F}_{q^{c}}
. It will be very interesting to find a more direct proof, and adapt it to the algorithmic problem above.

Preliminaries

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
denotes
\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
.

For two functions
r_{1},r_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
we define their Hamming distance to be the number of evaluation points where they disagree:

\Delta(r_{1},r_{2})=|\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mid r_{1}(\alpha)\neq r_{2}(\alpha)\}|.

We use
\mathrm{poly}(n)
to denote an expression bounded by
A\cdot n^{B}
for absolute constants
A,B
.

2.1
Polynomials, Hasse Derivatives and Multiplicities

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.

We use
\mathsf{deg}(A)
to denote the degree of a polynomial
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
. For polynomials
A(X),B(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
, we will use
A(X)\mod B(X)
to denote the remainder
when
A(X)
is divided by
B(X)
.

We now define Hasse derivatives, a notion of higher-order derivative that is suited for polynomials over finite fields.
See
[
HKT08
,
DKSS13
]
for more on this.

Definition 2.1

(Hasse Derivatives)
.

For a polynomial
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
,
we define the
\ell
-th Hasse derivative of
A(X)
, denoted
A^{[\ell]}(X)
, to be the coefficient
A_{\ell}(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
of
Z^{\ell}
in the expansion:

A(X+Z)=A_{0}(X)+A_{1}(X)Z+\ldots+A_{\ell}(X)Z^{\ell}+\ldots.

Definition 2.2

(Multiplicity)
.

For a nonzero polynomial
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
, we define the multiplicity of vanishing of
A(X)
, denoted
\mathsf{mult}(A,\alpha)
, to be the largest
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(X-\alpha)^{r}
divides
A(X)
.

Equivalently, it is the largest
r
such that
for all
\ell
with
0\leq\ell<r
,

A^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=0,

and
A^{[r]}(\alpha)\neq 0
.

Lemma 2.3

(Hasse derivative rules)
.

Let
A(X),B(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}
.
Then for all
\ell\geq 0
:

•

(\alpha A+\beta B)^{[\ell]}(X)=\alpha A^{[\ell]}(X)+\beta B^{[\ell]}(X).

•

(A\cdot B)^{[\ell]}(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=\ell\\
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}A^{[\ell_{1}]}(X)\cdot B^{[\ell_{2}]}(X).

Now we specialize to the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Here the polynomial

\Lambda(X)=X^{q}-X

plays a special role.

We recall some basic derivative calculations.

For
A(X)=X^{i}
:

A^{[\ell]}(X)={i\choose\ell}X^{i-\ell}.

For
\Lambda(X)
:

\Lambda^{[\ell]}(X)=\begin{cases}\Lambda(X)&\ell=0\\
-1&\ell=1\\
1&\ell=q\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
U(X),V(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
,
\mathsf{deg}(V)<q
,
i\geq 0
, and:

U(X)=V(X)\cdot\Lambda^{i}(X),

Then for
0\leq\ell<q
:

U^{[\ell]}(X)\equiv(-1)^{i}\cdot V^{[\ell-i]}(X)\mod\Lambda(X),

(where we adopt the convention that
V^{[j]}(X)=0
if
j<0
.)

Proof.

By the product rule for Hasse derivatives:

\displaystyle U^{[\ell]}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}\\
\sum\ell_{j}=\ell\end{subarray}}V^{[\ell_{0}]}(X)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{i}\Lambda^{[\ell_{j}]}(X).

If
\ell<i
, then in every term in the sum one of
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}
must equal
0
, and thus all the terms are multiples of
\Lambda(X)
.
So
U^{[\ell]}(X)
is a multiple of
\Lambda(X)
, as claimed.

If
\ell\geq i
, then we will see that all but at most one of these terms is a multiple of
\Lambda(X)
.

Since
\ell<q
, every term has all the
\ell_{j}<q
.

By our computation of derivatives of
\Lambda
,
the only terms that are nonzero must have

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}
all equal to either
0
or
1
.

If at least one of
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}
equals
0
, then
\Lambda(X)
is a factor of the term.

The only term remaining is
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}=\ldots=\ell_{i}=1
, and
\ell_{0}=\ell-i
, and this term equals:

V^{[\ell-i]}(X)\cdot(-1)^{i}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

2.2
The quadratic residue character over fields of odd characteristic

Let
q
be an odd prime power.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
be the quadratic residue character:

\chi(\beta)=\begin{cases}+1&\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\mbox{ is the square of an element of $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ }\\
-1&\beta\mbox{ is not the square of an element of $\mathbb{F}_{q}$ }\\
0&\beta=0.\end{cases}

Usually the range is viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{C}
, but we will view it as a subset of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then we have the formula:

\chi(\beta)=\beta^{(q-1)/2}.

Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
g
of degree
d
.
We will use
\chi\circ g
to denote the
function

from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\{0,\pm 1\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose values are given by:

\chi\circ g(\alpha)=\chi(g(\alpha)).

(We will never use
\chi\circ g
to refer to the polynomial
g^{(q-1)/2}(X)
.)

For an arbitrary nonzero
g(X)
of degree
d
, we can write it as:

g(X)=\lambda\cdot\bar{g}(X)\cdot h(X)^{2}

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
,
\bar{g}(X),h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, satisfy:

•

\bar{g}(X)
is monic and squarefree,

•

h(X)
is monic.

\bar{g}(X)
is the “squarefree core” of
g(X)
, and essentially determines
\chi\circ g
.

Indeed, for all
\alpha
where
h(\alpha)\neq 0
,

\chi\circ g(\alpha)=\chi(\lambda)\cdot\chi\circ\bar{g}(\alpha).

Thus, either
\Delta(\chi\circ g,\chi\circ\bar{g})\leq d
or
\Delta(\chi\circ g,-\chi\circ\bar{g})\leq d
.

2.3
Additive characters in fields characteristic
2

Let
q
be a power of
2
, say
q=2^{b}
.

Let
\mathsf{Tr}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
be the field trace map:

\mathsf{Tr}(\beta)=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\beta^{2^{i}}.

Let
\mathbf{TR}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be the polynomial:

\mathbf{TR}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}X^{2^{i}}.

For a polynomial
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, we will use
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
to denote the
function
from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}
whose values are given by:

\mathsf{Tr}\circ g(\alpha)=\mathsf{Tr}(g(\alpha)).

(We will never use
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
to refer to the polynomial

\mathbf{TR}(g(X))=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}(g(X))^{2^{i}}.
)

For an arbitrary nonzero
g(X)
of degree
d
, we can write it as:

g(X)=\lambda+\bar{g}(X)+h(X)+h^{2}(X),

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\bar{g}(X),h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
satisfy:

•

\bar{g}(X)
has only odd degree monomials,

•

h(X)
has
0
constant term.

This is proved by repeatedly taking the highest even degree monomial
a_{2i}X^{2i}
and writing it as the sum of
(a_{2i}^{1/2}X^{i})
and
(a_{2i}^{1/2}X^{i}+a_{2i}X^{2i})
– the former having lower degree, and the latter being of the form
h(X)+h^{2}(X)
.

\bar{g}(X)
is the “odd degree core” of
g(X)
and essentially determines
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
.

Indeed, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

\mathsf{Tr}\circ g(\alpha)=\mathsf{Tr}(\lambda)+\mathsf{Tr}\circ\bar{g}(\alpha).

Thus either
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g=\mathsf{Tr}\circ\bar{g}
or
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g=1+\mathsf{Tr}\circ\bar{g}
.

3
\chi\circ g

Let
q
be an odd prime power.

Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be a monic squarefree polynomial.
In this section, we give an efficient algorithm to recover
g(X)
given access to a noisy version
r
of
\chi\circ g
.

Algorithm A:

Parameters: degree
d\leq O(\epsilon\sqrt{q})
, error-bound
e\leq\left(\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon\right)q
.

Input
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}

1.

Set

•

M=\frac{16}{\epsilon}d

•

c=\frac{M}{2}

•

h=2\cdot e
.

•

D=d\cdot\left((q-1)/2+M\right)+cq=(1+O(\epsilon))\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot M\cdot q
,

•

u=h+dM=2e+O(\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon})
.

2.

Solve an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
system of linear equations to find polynomials

F(X),U_{0}(X),\ldots,U_{M-1}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, not all zero,
where:

•

\mathsf{deg}(F)\leq D

•

for each
\ell
,
\mathsf{deg}(U_{\ell})\leq u

•

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
0\leq\ell<M
:

\displaystyle F^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=r(\alpha)\cdot U_{\ell}(\alpha).

(3)

3.

Factor
F(X)
into irreducible factors:

F(X)=\lambda\prod_{j}H_{j}^{\mu_{j}}(X),

where the
H_{j}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
are distinct and monic, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.

4.

Define

J=\left\{j\mbox{ such that }\mu_{j}\in\left[\frac{3}{8}q,\frac{7}{8}q\right]\mod q\right\}.

5.

Set

f(X)=\prod_{j\in J}H_{j}(X).

6.

Return
f(X)
.

It is clear from the description of the algorithm (and the fact that factoring univariate polynomials of degree
D
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be done in time
\mathrm{poly}(q,D)

[
Ber70
]
), this algorithm can be implemented to run in time
\mathrm{poly}(q)
.

We now show correctness.

3.1
Correctness of the Algorithm

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
, and suppose:

•

d\leq\frac{\epsilon}{16}\sqrt{q}

•

e\leq\left(\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon\right)q
.

Suppose
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is a monic squarefree polynomial with degree
\leq d
such that
\Delta(\chi\circ g,r)\leq e
.

Then Algorithm A returns
g(X)
.

Proof.

The proof has three steps.

Let
G(X)=(g(X))^{(q-1)/2+M}
.

First, using the hypothesis that
r
is close to
\chi\circ g
, we show that
the system of linear equations in Step
2

has a nonzero solution.
Specifically, we carefully design polynomials
E(X)
(which is a kind of
high multiplicity error-locator pseudopolynomial) and
V_{0}(X),\ldots,V_{M-1}(X)

so that
F(X)=E(X)\cdot G(X)
and
U_{i}(X)=V_{i}(X)
satisfies
the linear equations.
Thus the algorithm will find a nonzero solution in that step.

The algorithm may not find the above mentioned “intended” solution.
In the second step of the proof, we show that whatever solution
(F,U_{0},\ldots,U_{M-1})
it does find,
F(X)
has some nontrivial relation to
G(X)
.

Finally, we use this nontrivial relation to show that the multiplicities of irreducible factors of
F
are closely related to those of
G
, and hence of
g
, and deduce the result from that.

We now proceed with the details.

Step 1: The system of linear equations has a nonzero solution.

The key observation is that if the received word
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0,\pm 1\}

had been exactly
\mathsf{Tr}\circ g
, then
F=G
would have given a valid solution to the system of equations. Instead, our received word
r
is merely close to
\chi\circ g
. So, following the idea of the Berlekamp-Welch decoding algorithm for Reed-Solomon codes, we will zero out
G
at the error locations to get a valid solution.

Let
S=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\mid\chi\circ g(\alpha)\neq r(\alpha)\}
be the error set. Let
Z_{S}(X)
be the error locator polynomial:

Z_{S}(X)=\prod_{\alpha\in S}(X-\alpha).

Recall that
c=M/2
and
h=\frac{eM}{c}=2e
.
We now take
E(X)
to be a nonzero multiple of
Z_{S}^{M}(X)
of the form:

E(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}E_{i}(X)\Lambda^{i}(X),

with
\mathsf{deg}(E_{i})\leq h
. (Recall that
\Lambda(X)=X^{q}-X
).
Such an
E(X)
exists, since vanishing mod
Z_{S}^{M}(X)
imposes
eM
constraints on the
c\cdot(h+1)
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear space
\{(E_{0}(X),\ldots,E_{c-1}(X))\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]^{c}\mid\mathsf{deg}(E_{i})\leq h\}
,
and
c\cdot(h+1)>ch=eM
.

This gives us two nice properties:

•

E^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=0
for all
\alpha\in S
and
\ell
with
0\leq\ell<M
. This is because
E
is a multiple of
Z_{S}^{M}(X)
, and thus of
(X-\alpha)^{M}
.

•

For each
\ell
with
0\leq\ell<M
, there is a polynomial
E_{\langle\ell\rangle}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
of degree at most
h=2e
such that for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

E_{\langle\ell\rangle}(\alpha)=E^{[\ell]}(\alpha).

Indeed, by Lemma
2.4
:

E^{[\ell]}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}(-1)^{i}E_{i}^{[\ell-i]}(X)\mod\Lambda(X),

and thus if we define:

E_{\langle\ell\rangle}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}(-1)^{i}E_{i}^{[\ell-i]}(X),

we have for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,:

\displaystyle E^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}(-1)^{i}E_{i}^{[\ell-i]}(\alpha)=E_{\langle\ell\rangle}(\alpha).

We will take our solution
F(X)
to be:

F(X)=E(X)\cdot G(X),

(recall that
G(X)=g^{(q-1)/2+M}(X)
).

Before we compute its derivatives, we need a quick lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
g(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be a polynomial of degree at most
d
.
Then the
\ell
-th derivative of
g^{j}(X)
is of the form:

(g^{j})^{[\ell]}(X)=H_{g,j,\ell}(X)\cdot g(X)^{j-\ell},

where
H_{g,j,\ell}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
has degree at most
d\ell-\ell
.

Proof.

Using the product rule for Hasse derivatives.
∎

Set
w=(q-1)/2+M
. Then by the above lemma:

G^{[\ell]}(X)=H_{g,w,\ell}(X)\cdot g^{w-\ell}(X)=H_{g,w,\ell}(X)\cdot g^{M-\ell}(X)\cdot g^{(q-1)/2}(X),

and so for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

\displaystyle G^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=H_{g,w,\ell}(\alpha)\cdot g^{M-\ell}(\alpha)\cdot g^{(q-1)/2}(\alpha)=\left(H_{g,w,\ell}(\alpha)\cdot g^{M-\ell}(\alpha)\right)\cdot\chi\circ g(\alpha).

(4)

Now we compute the derivatives of
E\cdot G
at
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

\displaystyle(E\cdot G)^{[\ell]}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=\ell}E^{[\ell_{1}]}(\alpha)\cdot G^{[\ell_{2}]}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=\ell}E_{\langle\ell_{1}\rangle}(\alpha)\cdot H_{g,w,\ell_{2}}(\alpha)\cdot g^{M-\ell_{2}}(\alpha)\cdot\chi\circ g(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=\ell}E_{\langle\ell_{1}\rangle}(\alpha)\cdot H_{g,w,\ell_{2}}(\alpha)\cdot g^{M-\ell_{2}}(\alpha)\right)\cdot\chi\circ g(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\chi\circ g(\alpha)\cdot V_{\ell}(\alpha),

(5)

where we defined:

V_{\ell}(X)=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=\ell}E_{\langle\ell_{1}\rangle}(X)\cdot H_{g,w,\ell_{2}}(X)\cdot g^{M-\ell_{2}}(X).

It is easy to check that:

\mathsf{deg}(E\cdot G)\leq d\cdot\left(\frac{q-1}{2}+M\right)+c\cdot q=D,

\mathsf{deg}(V_{\ell})\leq h+d\cdot M=u.

We now show that for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and all
\ell
with
0\leq\ell<M
:

\displaystyle(E\cdot G)^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=r(\alpha)\cdot V_{\ell}(\alpha).

(6)

For

4.1
Correctness of the Algorithm

Our main theorem about Algorithm

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

•

is an odd integer,

•

The key claim is about the behavior of Algorithm B.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\epsilon>0
, and suppose:

•

•

Given Theorem

The algorithm maintains the invariant that:

We just demonstrate the first step below.

4.2
A remark

Recall Step 2 of the proof of correctness of Algorithm B, where we deduced that
G
was related to
F,E
via a relation of the form:

A(X)\cdot(F(X)-E(X)\cdot G(X))=B(X),

with
A(X),B(X)
of a special form.

It turns out that this information already implies that
g
is uniquely deterimined by
F,E
.
However, we do not know how to
efficiently
find
g
from just
F
and
E
. This is why we had to take the longer route of just getting the leading coefficient of
g
, and then repeating (which involves solving for a new
F,E
).

Why do
F
and
E
uniquely determine
g
? Suppose not,
and that
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
are distinct polynomials with only odd degree monomials such that
G_{1}=\mathbf{TR}(g_{1}(X)),G_{2}=\mathbf{TR}(g_{2}(X))
both satisfy equations as above:

A_{1}(X)(F(X)-E(X)G_{1}(X))=B_{1}(X),

A_{2}(X)(F(X)-E(X)G_{2}(X))=B_{2}(X),

Eliminating
F(X)
, we get:

A_{1}(X)A_{2}(X)E(X)\bigg(G_{1}(X)-G_{2}(X)\bigg)+(B_{2}(X)A_{1}(X)-B_{1}(X)A_{2}(X))=0.

This kind of polynomial was explicitly considered by Stepanov, and he showed that it cannot equal
0
.

The key point is that
G_{1}(X)-G_{2}(X)
is a nonzero polynomial with degree in
q\cdot\mathbb{Z}+q/2
.

This means that the two polynomials

A_{1}(X)A_{2}(X)E(X)\cdot\bigg(G_{1}(X)-G_{2}(X)\bigg)
and
B_{1}(X)A_{2}(X)-B_{2}(X)A_{1}(X)
cannot have equal degrees: the former has degree in the interval
[q/2,q/2+2k+h]\mod q
, while the latter has degree in the interval
[0,2k+h]\mod q
, and these are disjoint intervals mod
q
.

Therefore their sum cannot be zero, and we get a contradiction to the assumption that
g
was not uniquely determined.

5
Theory of Pseudopolynomials

The results of the previous sections made extensive use of high degree polynomials, all of whose derivatives behaved like low degree polynomials on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We call these objects pseudopolynomials. In this section, we formally define them and build up some basic theory. We hope this will be useful for other algebraic and algorithmic problems involving polynomials over finite fields.

Definition 5.1

(Pseudoderivative)
.

Let
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
.

Define the
\ell
-th pseudoderivative of
A
, denoted
A_{\langle\ell\rangle}(X)
, to be
the unique polynomial of degree at most
q-1
whose evaluations on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
agree with the evaluations of
A^{[\ell]}(X)
.
Explicitly:

A_{\langle\ell\rangle}(X)=\left(A^{[\ell]}(X)\mod\Lambda(X)\right),

where
\Lambda(X)=X^{q}-X
.

Abusing notation, we will sometimes use
A_{\langle\ell\rangle}

to denote the function

A_{\langle\ell\rangle}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q},

defined by
A_{\langle\ell\rangle}(\alpha)=A^{[\ell]}(\alpha)
.

Definition 5.2

(Pseudodegree)
.

Let
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
.

We define the pseudodegree of
A
,
denoted
\mathsf{pdeg}(A)
to be:

\max_{\ell\geq 0}\mathsf{deg}(A_{\langle\ell\rangle}).

We say
A(X)
is a
k
-pseudopolynomial
if

\mathsf{pdeg}(A)\leq k.

With this notation in place, we now list an assortment of useful facts
about pseudopolynomials.

5.1
Basic Properties

With this notation in place, we now list an assortment of basic properties of pseudopolynomials.

•

\mathsf{pdeg}(A+B)\leq\max(\mathsf{pdeg}(A),\mathsf{pdeg}(B))
.

•

\mathsf{pdeg}(A\cdot B)\leq\mathsf{pdeg}(A)+\mathsf{pdeg}(B)
.

•

We have the following algebraic characterization of pseudodegree.

.

Let

, and

with each

,
be the base-

Then

\mathsf{pdeg}(A)=\max_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{deg}(A_{i}).

The proof appears in Appendix

•

For polynomials

of moderate degree, the pseudodegree can be approximately expressed in terms of the standard monomial expansion of

.

If

\leq k
and degree

In the reverse direction, if

For all the applications in this paper, we could have worked with the standard monomial basis with negligible losses.

•

Suppose

There is a natural error-correcting code here, closely related to multiplicity codes. There are 4 governing parameters:

.

Let

. The codewords of this code will lie in

When

\mathbb{F}_{q}

\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)q
.

Algorithm A
m
:

Parameters: degree
d\leq O(\frac{\epsilon}{m}\sqrt{q})
, error-bound
e\leq\left(\frac{1}{12}-\epsilon\right)q
.

Input
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\{0\}\cup\Gamma_{m}

1.

Set

•

M=\frac{16}{\epsilon}dm

•

c=\frac{M}{2}

•

h=2\cdot e
.

•

D=d\cdot\left((q-1)\cdot\frac{m-1}{m}+M\right)+cq=(1+O(\epsilon))\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot M\cdot q
,

•

u=h+(m-1)dM=2e+O(\frac{md^{2}}{\epsilon})
.

2.

For each
\nu\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
:

•

Solve an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
system of linear equations to find polynomials

F_{\nu}(X),U_{\nu,0}(X),\ldots,U_{\nu,M-1}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, not all zero,
with:

–

\mathsf{deg}(F_{\nu})\leq D
,

–

for each
\ell
,
\mathsf{deg}(U_{\nu,\ell})\leq u
,

–

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
0\leq\ell<M
:

\displaystyle F_{\nu}^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=r^{\nu}(\alpha)\cdot U_{\nu,\ell}(\alpha).

(23)

3.

Factor
F_{\nu}(X)
into irreducible factors:

F_{\nu}(X)=\lambda_{\nu}\prod_{j}H_{j}^{\mu_{\nu,j}}(X),

where the
H_{j}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
are distinct and monic irreducible polynomials, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.

4.

For each
j
, define
\mu_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
to be the unique number such that for all
\nu\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
, we have:

\mu_{\nu,j}-\frac{\nu\cdot\mu_{j}}{m}\cdot(q-1)\in\left(-\left(\frac{1}{12}-\epsilon\right)q,\quad\left(\frac{3}{12}-\epsilon\right)q\right)\mod q.

5.

Set

f(X)=\prod_{j}H_{j}^{\mu_{j}}(X).

6.

Return
f(X)
.

The analysis is very similar to the analysis in Section
3
.

Let
G(X)=g^{(q-1)/m+M}(X)
, which is a
(\chi_{m}\circ g)
-twisted
(O(dM),M)
-pseudopolynomial.

To show that the linear equations have a solution, we first interpolate a high multiplicity error-locator pseudopolynomial
E(X)
with
\mathsf{deg}(E)<cq
and
\mathsf{pdeg}(E)<h
. Then we show that taking
F_{\nu}=E\cdot G^{\nu}
yields a solution to Equations (
23
).

Next we show that whatever
F_{\nu}
the algorithm finds is related to
G
.
Exactly as in Section
3
, we find polynomials
A_{\nu}(X),B_{\nu}(X)

with
\mathsf{deg}(A_{\nu})<\frac{3}{8}Mq
,
\mathsf{deg}(B_{\nu})<\frac{7}{8}Mq
,
and
\mathsf{pdeg}(A_{\nu})\leq e
,
\mathsf{pdeg}(B_{\nu})\leq 3e+O(dM)
such that:

A_{\nu}(X)\cdot F_{\nu}(X)=B_{\nu}(X)\cdot G^{\nu}(X).

From these equations, we will be able to extract all the irreducible factors of
g(X)
.
Let
H(X)
be an irreducible polynomial, and let
\mu_{A_{\nu}},\mu_{F_{\nu}},\mu_{B_{\nu}},\mu_{G},\mu_{g}
be its factor multiplicity in
A_{\nu},F_{\nu},B_{\nu},G,g
respectively.
Then the above identity gives us:

\mu_{A_{\nu}}+\mu_{F_{\nu}}=\mu_{B_{\nu}}+\nu\cdot\mu_{g}\cdot\frac{q-1}{m}.

Thus for all
\nu
:

\mu_{F_{\nu}}-\nu\cdot\mu_{g}\cdot\frac{q-1}{m}=\mu_{B_{\nu}}-\mu_{A_{\nu}}\in q\cdot\mathbb{Z}+\left[-e,3e+O(dM)\right]\subseteq q\cdot\mathbb{Z}+\left(-\left(\frac{1}{12}-\epsilon\right)q,\left(\frac{3}{12}-\epsilon\right)q\right).

Thus
\mu_{g}
is a solution found in Step
4
of the algorithm.
Why is there no other solution? Suppose
\mu^{\prime}\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
is distinct from
\mu_{g}
and satisfies:

\mu_{F_{\nu}}-\nu\cdot\mu^{\prime}\cdot\frac{q-1}{m}\in q\cdot\mathbb{Z}+\left(-\left(\frac{1}{12}-\epsilon\right)q,\;\left(\frac{3}{12}-\epsilon\right)q\right)

for all
\nu
.
Then subtracting the two equations and setting
\mu^{\prime\prime}=\mu^{\prime}-\mu_{g}
, we get that for all
\nu\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
:

\nu\cdot\mu^{\prime\prime}\cdot\frac{q-1}{m}\in q\cdot\mathbb{Z}+\left(-\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\epsilon\right)q,\;\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\epsilon\right)q\right).

This is impossible: for any
\mu^{\prime\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
, there is a
\nu\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
such that
\frac{\mu^{\prime\prime}\cdot\nu}{m}\in\mathbb{Z}+\left[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right]
.

Appendix C
Additive characters over fields of small characteristic
p

We only give the analogue of the main subroutine, Algorithm B, from Section
4
.

Let
q=p^{b}
with
p
prime.
Let
\mathsf{Tr}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the field trace map.

Let
g(X)
be a polynomial of degree at most
d\leq O_{m}(\epsilon\sqrt{q})
, with only monomials of degree relatively prime to
m
.

The following algorithm can efficiently find the leading coefficient of
g(X)
given an
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
with
\Delta(\mathsf{Tr}\circ g,r)
being at most a small enough constant fraction of
\frac{1}{p}q
. For
p=O(1)
this is a constant fraction of
q
, but for growing
p
it is far from satisfactory. The distance of the underlying code is about
(1-\frac{1}{p})q
(it actually grows with
p
), and there ought to be an efficient unique decoding algorithm to radius half of that.

Algorithm B
p
:

Parameters: degree
d\leq O_{p}(\epsilon\sqrt{q})
(an integer not divisible by
p
), error-bound
e\leq\left(\frac{1}{4p}-\epsilon\right)q
.

Input
r:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}

1.

Set

•

M=\frac{16}{\epsilon}d

•

c=\frac{1}{2}\cdot M

•

h=2\cdot e

•

u=h+dM=2e+O(\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon})
.

2.

For each
h^{*}\in[0,h]

•

Try to solve a system of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear equations to find polynomials

E(X)
,
F(X)
,

(U_{\ell}(X))_{\ell\in[0,M-1]}
,
where:

–

F(X)
has degree exactly
(d/p+c)q+h^{*}
.

–

E(X)
has degree exactly
cq+h^{*}
, and is of the form:

\sum_{i=0}^{c}E_{i}(X)\Lambda^{i}(X),

where
\mathsf{deg}(E_{i})\leq h
.

–

each
U_{\ell}(X)
has degree at most
u
.

–

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
0\leq\ell<M
:

\displaystyle F^{[\ell]}(\alpha)=r(\alpha)\cdot E^{[\ell]}(\alpha)+U_{\ell}(\alpha),

(24)

3.

If no such
h^{*}
exists, return
0\cdot X^{d}
.

4.

Otherwise take one solution
E(X),F(X)
,
with degrees
D_{E},D_{F}
respectively.

5.

Let
a\cdot X^{D_{F}}
,
b\cdot X^{D_{E}}
be the leading monomials of
F(X),E(X)
respectively.

Set
a_{d}=(a/b)^{p}
.

6.

Return
a_{d}X^{d}
.
